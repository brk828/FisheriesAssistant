# Reintroduction of the Flannelmouth Sucker in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the population status of the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) in the lower [[Colorado River]] below [[Davis Dam]]. A single 1976 stocking of 611 adult fish, made by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] for black fly abatement, produced a self-sustaining population. The authors describe it as the first successful reintroduction of a native fish in the lower Colorado River. The study was conducted from 1998 onward, with main field sampling in 1999-2002. The authors argue that this species tolerates altered river conditions, while the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] does not, and that other native fishes might benefit from similar approaches in altered rivers.

## Background & Description

- **Historical status:** Nine endemic fishes once occurred in the lower Colorado River. Seven were listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The flannelmouth sucker was listed as a species of special concern (USFWS 1994).
- **Rarity and presumed extirpation:** Before 1976 the species had been reported only five times in the lower main stem. It was believed extirpated by about 1970-1975, based on surveys from the late 1960s and early 1970s. Earlier records were thought to reflect drift from tributaries or released baitfish.
- **Barriers:** [[Hoover Dam]] (1935), [[Davis Dam]] (1954), and [[Parker Dam]] (1938) blocked immigration from upstream populations.
- **Origin of the population:** Adults were moved from the Paria River confluence to the lower river near Bullhead City, Arizona. Young suckers were found by the mid-1990s during annual recreational fish surveys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** About 80 km of river from Davis Dam to Lake Havasu, sampled mainly December-May, which overlaps the native sucker spawning season.
- **Gear:** Trammel nets (small and large), hoop nets, minnow traps, and [[Larval Light Traps]]. Electrofishing was ineffective. Visual surveys were conducted from a boat in 2001 with water visibility greater than 4 m.
- **Habitat use:** Adults were common in the main channel, where depths exceed 2 m and velocities are 0.5-1 m/s. Spawning concentrations occurred off-channel, particularly near [[Laughlin Lagoon]] (about 1 km downstream of the dam) and near the [[Fort Mohave Ruins]] (about 25 km downstream).
- **Altered conditions:** Dam-regulated water, including cool tailwater releases from Lake Mohave, may have mimicked preferred tributary conditions (cooler, channelized, rockier substrates). This likely helped the species colonize the tailwater.
- **Competing and predatory species:** Nonnative predators, such as [[Common Carp]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], dominated the catch alongside native and nonnative fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking and population growth:** The 611 fish stocked in 1976 grew to an estimated population of 2,286 (95% CI 1,847-2,998) by mark-recapture in 2002, a number well above the initial stocking.
- **Visual survey estimate:** In 2001, 25 visual surveys along the 80-km reach recorded 301 suckers in the upper 40 km and none in the 40-80 km reach. Density was 8.6 fish/ha, giving an estimated 6,794 fish (8.6 fish/ha × 790 ha). The authors considered this more realistic than the mark-recapture figure but did not replicate it statistically.
- **Sample totals:** More than 8,000 larval fish and 8,095 larger fish were captured. Of these, 1,349 flannelmouth suckers were taken (11 larvae and 1,338 juveniles and adults).
- **Spatial distribution:** CPUE in trammel nets was roughly 50 times higher in the upper 40 km (3.91 vs. 0.08 fish/100 m²). About 99.2% of the catch came from within 40 km of Davis Dam.
- **Spawning aggregations:** Two spawning groups of more than 200 fish were observed. Of the suckers caught within 100 m of Laughlin Lagoon's entrance, 69% were taken there and 73% of those were female.
- **Age structure:** Aging used vertebrae and fin rays (n = 119 structures, 109 aged). Most fish (73%) were 8-13 years old, with growth mostly complete by age 16. The oldest fish was 24 years old.
- **Size structure:** Nearly all (94.8%) fish were adults over 35 cm. Immature fish made up less than 5%.
- **Recruitment:** Recruitment into the spawning cohort (ages 3-4) averaged slightly over 15% per year (range 9.4-31.3%). The authors calculate that this is sufficient to replace the spawning cohort if adult mortality is low. Recruitment was sporadic, with several low years (<10%) and one stronger year-class (31%).
- **Reproductive potential:** A mature female can produce about 15,000 eggs per year and may spawn for more than two decades. This supports a strategy that tolerates periodic recruitment failure.
- **Contrast with the razorback sucker:** More than 12 million small razorback suckers were stocked since 1980 with little measurable survival. Natural recruitment of that species has not been documented.
- **Conclusion:** One native fish species successfully tolerates altered river conditions where others cannot. The authors suggest that similar opportunities may exist in other altered rivers where native fishes were absent or historically rare.
