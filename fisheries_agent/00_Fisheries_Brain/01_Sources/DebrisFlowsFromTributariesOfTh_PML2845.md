# Debris Flows from Tributaries of the Colorado River, Grand Canyon National Park, Arizona

#aquatic-ecology #sediment-transport #debris-flow #geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey open-file report, prepared with the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and authored by Robert H. Webb (September 1987), documents how [[Debris Flow]] events in small tributary canyons deliver large volumes of coarse sediment to the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Debris-flow deposits were observed in 36 tributaries, and three were studied in detail: [[Lava-Chuar Creek]], [[Monument Creek]], and [[Crystal Creek]]. The flows are triggered by slope failures in Paleozoic sedimentary rocks of the [[Supai Group]] and [[Hermit Shale]]. Where they reach the river, they build fans that constrict the channel, form rapids, and supply sand to beaches. The report concludes that the magnitude and frequency of these events control river hydraulics and sediment budgets in the park.

## Background & Description

**Scope and purpose.** Little was known about the annual sediment yield of steep, high-relief tributary basins. Existing sediment-yield methods were not designed for such terrain. The stated goal was to document the occurrence of debris flows in Colorado River tributaries and to determine their frequency and the magnitude of recent events.

**Definition.** Debris flows are water-based flows of poorly sorted material ranging from clay to boulder size. They have a higher solid content than [[Hyperconcentrated Flow]] and [[Streamflow]], which the report cites as about 40 to 80 percent and 80 to 100 percent volumetric water content, respectively.

**Study area.** The study covered tributary canyons along the Colorado River within the park (see [[Grand Canyon Study Area Map]]). The work built on an earlier flood report by [[Cooley et al. Flood Study]] and on recent mapping of alluvial deposits.

**Methods (summary).**
- Field identification of debris-flow deposits, based on particle size, lack of sedimentary structures, and matrix-supported cobbles and boulders.
- Dating of deposits by [[Radiocarbon Dating]] of trees and by historic photographs, giving minimum frequencies only.
- Peak velocity estimated from simplified hydraulic formulas, boulder-size evidence, and [[Superelevation Method]] estimates, which the report considers conservative.
- Discharge calculated as velocity times cross-sectional area.
- Water content from samples of the debris-flow matrix, with stated uncertainty of 1 to 2 percent in the sample.
- Particle-size distributions from sieve data combined with point counts in the field, following standard sampling procedures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report focuses on geomorphology and sediment transport rather than biology. It contains no fish, invertebrate, or water-quality data. Its ecological relevance lies in the physical habitat changes it describes:

- **Fan-driven rapids.** Debris fans force the river against the opposite bank and create rapids, such as those at [[Granite Rapid]]. Reworking of fans by river discharge produces secondary riffles and rapids.
- **Depositional zones.** Flow-separation zones behind fans favor deposition and storage of sand on beaches. These beaches are a major recreational and habitat resource in the canyon.
- **Sediment supply.** Debris flows deliver large sand volumes to the river at discrete points, and the report notes that the events are infrequent.
- **Bedrock sources.** Failure-prone units include the [[Esplanade Sandstone]] (in the Supai Group), the [[Hermit Shale]], [[Kaibab Limestone]], [[Toroweap Formation]], [[Coconino Sandstone]], [[Muav Limestone]], and [[Quaternary Basalt]] in the western canyon. High relief combined with differential weathering of these rocks creates a high potential for slope failure.

## Key Findings

**1. Widespread occurrence.** Debris-flow deposits were found in 36 tributaries. Three tributaries were studied in detail for frequency and magnitude.

**2. Lava-Chuar Creek.**
- Evidence for at least five prehistoric and three historic debris flows.
- Historic flows occurred between 1966 and 1973, and between 1973 and 1984 (date ranges as read from the source).
- The 1966 flow began as slope failures in the Hermit Shale and Supai Group, traveled to the Colorado River, and carried boulders up to about 1 to 2 ft in diameter.
- Estimated water content was 22.5 percent. Sand content was 30 to 35 percent.
- The largest boulder transported weighed an estimated 9 tons.
- The estimated peak discharge near the river was about 4,000 (units as given in the source), with peak sediment discharge estimated at about 3,100 to 3,400.
- Prehistoric flows averaged roughly one event per 200 years. Historic flows were more frequent.

**3. Monument Creek.**
- Two debris flows in the last 25 years. Evidence suggests an earlier flow in the early 1960s.
- The 1984 flow began as an avalanche from the Esplanade Sandstone about 2,000 ft above the channel. It formed a 20-ft-high debris dam that had not been breached as of 1986.
- The flow traveled 2.8 mi to the Colorado River, with a peak discharge of about 3,800 ft³/s.
- Water content was 27 to 34 percent, and sand content was 30 to 40 percent.
- One boulder transported by the flow weighed an estimated 37 tons.
- The fan that formed significantly constricted [[Granite Rapid]].
- Most likely volume of sediment delivered to the river was about 300,000 ft³. Assuming 35 percent sand, sand input was about 84,000 ft³, with a range across scenarios given in the source.
- The fan was completely devoid of particles less than 16 mm in diameter by 1986, indicating that finer material was rapidly removed.

**4. Crystal Creek.**
- The drainage averaged about one debris flow reaching the Colorado River per interval the source states only ambiguously.
- A large December flow is cited in the report as 1956 in one passage and as 1965 in the detailed account. The discrepancy is not resolved in the text.
- The December 1965 flow began with 11 failures in the Hermit Shale and Supai Group and traveled 13 mi to the river.
- Calculated velocity ranged from 10 to 18 ft/s, and peak discharge from 9,200 to 14,000 ft³/s.
- Water content was 21 to 33 percent. Sand content was reported as roughly 40 to 50 percent.
- One boulder weighed an estimated 47 tons, and transported boulders exceeded 5 ft in diameter.
- The flow built a fan that significantly constricted the river.

**5. Similarities among events.** All three flows were initiated by slope failures in the Hermit Shale and Supai Group, especially the Esplanade Sandstone. All carried poorly sorted mixtures of clay to boulder-size particles, with water contents from about 21 to 33 percent by volume. Two of the three flows significantly constricted the Colorado River at the tributary mouths.

**6. Frequency.** Available data suggest that one debris flow reached the Colorado River every 20 to 50 years in these drainages. The report describes this as tentative. Compilation of historical information from Grand Canyon tributaries indicates that debris flows occur more often across the park.

**7. Hydraulic control.** The magnitude and frequency of debris flows control the hydraulics of the Colorado River in the park. Debris fans from small tributaries aggrade and force the river against the opposite bank. The report describes these small drainages as hydrologically significant because they create hydraulic controls on the river, including rapids.

**8. Sediment budget implications.** Knowledge of the magnitude and frequency of debris flows is necessary for any long-term estimate of sediment transport in the Colorado River through the park. The report regards these events as an important, but poorly quantified, source of sand for river beaches.
