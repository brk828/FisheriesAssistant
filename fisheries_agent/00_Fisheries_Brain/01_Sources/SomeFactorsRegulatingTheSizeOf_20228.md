# Some Factors Regulating the Size of Populations of Freshwater Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #percidae #population-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review by E. D. Le Cren (1965, *Mitteilungen Internationale Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie* 13) surveys the theoretical background of freshwater fish population ecology. It argues that fish differ from most other animals in their large size range, long generation times, repeated spawning and high early mortality, and that these traits shape population dynamics. The paper concludes that density-dependent regulation exists but is often masked by large density-independent fluctuations, particularly in early life. Fish populations compensate mainly through growth rate, fecundity and dispersion rather than through predation-driven mortality. Physical factors dominate near range limits. The author calls for more quantitative, long-term and comparative data.

## Background & Description

The review frames a debate in [[Population Ecology]] about whether animal numbers are regulated by [[Density-Dependent Regulation]], and whether such regulation evolved by [[Natural Selection]]. The author considers the controversy partly semantic but argues that empirical data from freshwater organisms should help resolve it.

Key biological features of fish that distinguish them from other aquatic animals include:

- **Size variability:** Brown trout spawning in Windermere tributaries may weigh 20 g, while lake trout reach 5000 g. Size depends mainly on growth rate, which is environmentally influenced.
- **Growth model:** Growth is described by the [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Model]], where the asymptotic size is environmentally controlled and the approach rate is largely genetic and physiological.
- **Generation time:** Most fish mature after one to four years, unlike many aquatic insects with one to three generations per year. Trout first spawn at age three, [[Perch]] at three years, and [[Bullhead]] at nearly one year.
- **Longevity:** Perch of up to 17 years were recorded. The white sturgeon (*Huso huso*) can live up to about 100 years. Repeated spawning produces multiple year-classes, which stabilises overall abundance.
- **Biomass:** Biomass (numbers × individual weight) is recommended as a more informative population measure than numbers alone, and it matches how catches are usually recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Eggs and fecundity.** Fecundity roughly scales with female weight but varies with genetics, season, age and previous food supply. Large eggs tend to give higher larval and juvenile survival, and smaller eggs are produced in greater numbers. Unripe eggs increase under slow growth or severe winters, as in Windermere perch after the 1947 and 1963 winters. Spawning success depends on migration timing, freshets, [[Spawning Grounds]] area, and redd superimposition by later spawners.

**Egg survival.** Survival in clean salmonid gravel can reach 97% or more. Floods, droughts and silting cause heavier losses, with mortality up to 30% reported by [[Hobbs]] (1940). High spawner densities can cause oxygen depletion, a density-dependent mortality. Egg-stage reproduction curves are largely unavailable.

**Young fish.** Large variation in year-class strength is a principal cause of population fluctuation. Perch year-class strength in the Windermere area did not correlate with the number of spawning females, and good years were synchronous across lakes. Warm summers appear linked to abundant broods. Predation by [[Northern Pike]] on perch was not a clear controlling factor, and Ullswater, which lacks pike, had perch populations about 5% as dense as Windermere.

**Trout fry experiments.** Screened sections of a flood-controlled beck were stocked with different egg densities over three years. Mortality was correlated with density and final populations levelled off above a low stocking rate. Most deaths occurred between days 20 and 40 after feeding began, mainly from starvation. Territorial behaviour, as described by [[Kalleberg]] (1958), appears to be the density-dependent mechanism. Final densities were about 7–10 fish per m² (about 10 g/m²). Survival in a sparse stocking reached 98%.

**Interspecific competition.** In experiments, trout fry were dominant over salmon fry. Salmon mortality depended on the total of both species, while trout mortality depended only on trout numbers.

**Adult mortality.** Mortality tends to be constant with age, except in cases such as whitefish. Perch instantaneous mortality in Windermere was about 0.44 before fishing (1941), about 1.96 during the trap fishery, and about 0.33 afterwards. Char mortality was 1.30 for males and 0.78 for females at Holbeck. Pike natural mortality was considerably lower than that of perch and char.

**Physical limits.** Near range edges, physical factors can dominate. About two-thirds of annual perch growth variation in Windermere correlated with summer temperature. Trout fry were killed by temperatures above 24 °C in small streams. Drought periodically decimates small streams. The survival of cisco at the southern edge of its range depends on cool, oxygenated metalimnion water.

## Key Findings

- Fish population dynamics are shaped by large size variation, long generation times, repeated spawning and high early mortality. These traits buffer populations against annual environmental change.
- Density-dependent regulation exists but is often obscured by density-independent fluctuations, especially in early life stages, where most of the variation in population size is thought to arise.
- Fecundity responds to density only partially. In Windermere, a roughly 94% reduction in adult perch numbers produced a threefold increase in average adult size and fecundity, a compensation that was incomplete.
- In salmonid fry, density-dependent mortality is linked to territoriality. Growth rate is inversely related to density, and a 20% growth reduction during a 40-day critical phase could cut survivors by about 33% (a hypothetical calculation).
- Adult mortality is generally age-independent. Fishing can be a major mortality cause, but predation is hard to quantify. A cannibalism calculation for Windermere pike suggested predation may account for mortality, though the assumptions were acknowledged as likely wrong.
- Stream and littoral species regulate density through territoriality and dispersion acting on the physical habitat. Schooling and pelagic species show less clear density dependence in mortality, though growth may be inversely density-dependent.
- Near range limits, temperature, drought and oxygen can limit abundance regardless of density.
- The review calls for quantitative, long-term data, study of small non-commercial species, research on schooling populations, and comparative analysis of trophic dynamics. It argues freshwater fish are good subjects for population ecology because fisheries provide large samples and freshwater units can be replicated experimentally.
