# Mesozoic Sedimentary and Volcanic Rocks of Southeastern Arizona

#geology #stratigraphy #mesozoic

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey guidebook paper by [[Philip T. Hayes]] and [[Harald Drewes]] synthesizes the stratigraphy of the Mesozoic rocks of southeastern Arizona. It correlates units across many mountain ranges using field relations, paleontology, petrology, and radiometric dates. The authors describe a sequence of Triassic and Jurassic volcanics and red beds, Early Cretaceous deltaic and marine sediments of the [[Bisbee Group]], and Late Cretaceous sedimentary and volcanic rocks. The region records repeated volcanism, plutonism, uplift, and erosion, culminating in the first phases of the [[Laramide Orogeny]]. The document contains no fish-specific data; its only faunal reference is a passing mention of fossil fish in Late Cretaceous shale.

## Background & Description

The study area is a region roughly between Tucson and the southeastern corner of Arizona. Its stratigraphy is difficult to interpret because most Mesozoic units lack datable fossils, and the area has undergone nearly half a dozen episodes of faulting and plutonism since the late Early Permian. The authors draw on mapping by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], university researchers, and minerals-industry geologists. The paper's correlations rest on local geologic relations, paleontologic data of variable usefulness, radiometric ages of variable reliability, petrologic studies, and the assumption that major geologic events were regionally continuous.

Dating methods include [[Lead-Alpha Dating]] on zircons from volcanics and [[Potassium-Argon Dating]] on biotite and other minerals. Upper Mesozoic horizons are considered reliable because replicate or concordant determinations are available. Lower Mesozoic dates from single determinations are considered reasonably trustworthy because most agree with field relations. The authors use Holmes' (1965) time scale for most of the Mesozoic and Gill and Cobban's (1966) scale for the Upper Cretaceous.

Sub-Mesozoic rocks include Precambrian metamorphic, plutonic, and sedimentary rocks, overlain by about 5,000 feet of Paleozoic marine strata (Late Cambrian to late Early Permian). The Mesozoic sequence has a composite maximum thickness of about 40,000 feet. It comprises rhyolitic to andesitic volcanics, mostly subaerial sediments, and some marine beds. It is divided into three major units separated by unconformities: Triassic and Jurassic rocks, Lower Cretaceous rocks, and Upper Cretaceous rocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses depositional and paleoenvironmental settings rather than living ecosystems:

- **Triassic–Jurassic:** Eolian crossbedded sandstones and quartzites occur in the Formation of Mount Wrightson. Red beds indicate subaerial conditions, and pillow lavas indicate subaqueous extrusion.
- **Early Cretaceous:** The [[Bisbee Group]] represents part of a large delta complex on the margin of a sea lying to the southeast, mostly in Mexico. Marine rocks increase toward the southeast, while brackish-water and more arkosic continental facies dominate the northwest.
- **Late Cretaceous:** Sediments were deposited in subaerial valleys cut into the Bisbee Formation and older rocks after early Laramide uplift. Drainage was probably toward the Late Cretaceous Mexican geosyncline. Freshwater mollusks, fish, turtles, and dinosaurs are reported from the shale unit.

## Key Findings

**1. Triassic and Jurassic rocks (three units)**
- **Formation of Mount Wrightson:** About 10,000 feet of mostly rhyodacite, with andesitic rocks near the top and bottom. It includes welded tuffs, pillow lavas, and eolian sandstone lenses. It is well exposed in the [[Santa Rita Mountains]].
- **Medial red-bed unit:** About 2,000 feet thick in the [[Canelo Hills]], Gardner Canyon, and Rodolfo Wash areas. It correlates with the Recreation Redbeds of the [[Tucson Mountains]] and the Walnut Gap Volcanics.
- **Upper Canelo Hills Volcanics:** Composite thickness exceeds 7,000 feet, consisting of silicic flows, tuffs, and clastic rocks. These are the youngest Triassic–Jurassic rocks.

**2. Age relationships**
- The Mount Wrightson formation is older than the red-bed unit, based on a lead-alpha date and cobbles of similar volcanics in the red beds.
- A monzonite intruding the Mount Wrightson formation yields a lead-alpha age of 184 m.y. A granite intruding the monzonite is Middle Jurassic.
- An andesite porphyry intruding the Recreation Redbeds gives a potassium-argon minimum age of 150 m.y.
- More than 40 radiometric dates are considered useful, though a few do not fit field relations.

**3. Major unconformities**
- **[[Permian-Triassic Unconformity]]:** Separates marine Paleozoic from subaerial Mesozoic rocks. The oldest Mesozoic rocks overlying it include Triassic-age volcanics and red beds in the Canelo Hills.
- **[[Jurassic-Cretaceous Unconformity]]:** Relief reaches hundreds of feet in the Mule Mountains and at least 1,000 feet locally in the Santa Rita and Huachuca Mountains. It places Cretaceous rocks on Jurassic granite.
- **[[Mid-to-Late Cretaceous Unconformity]]:** Relief is generally modest, but Upper Cretaceous volcanics overlie Jurassic, Triassic, or possibly Precambrian rocks in places.

**4. Lower Cretaceous rocks**
- **Volcanic and associated sediments:** The Temporal Gulch and Bathtub formations, each about 2,000 feet thick, are exposed in the Santa Rita Mountains. Their volcanics are so altered that datable zircon is scarce.
- **[[Bisbee Group]] and correlatives:** Up to 10,000 feet or more in places. Key sections include:
  - Mule Mountains: as much as 5,700 feet, with four formations.
  - Huachuca Mountains: 6,000 to about 10,000 feet.
  - Pedregosa and southern Chiricahua Mountains: nearly 8,000 feet.
  - Dragoon Mountains: may exceed 15,000 feet.
  - Patagonia Mountains (Patagonia Group): roughly 5,000 feet.
  - Whetstone Mountains: at least 6,500 feet.
  - Tucson Mountains (Amole Arkose): several thousand feet.
- **Age:** Marine fossils from the [[Mural Limestone]] correlate with the Trinity Group of Texas. The Bisbee Group may span much of Aptian and Albian time.

**5. Upper Cretaceous rocks**
- **Lower sedimentary sequence (Fort Crittenden Formation):** In the Adobe Canyon area, a basal conglomerate is followed by a 525-foot shale unit with freshwater mollusks, fish, turtle, and dinosaur remains dated Santonian to Maastrichtian (Miller, 1964). More than 6,000 feet of conglomerate and arkosic sandstone overlies the shale.
- **Upper volcanic sequence:** Up to 4,500 feet of andesite to dacite breccia, rhyodacitic tuff, and welded tuff. In the Salero area it includes about 400 feet of flows, 1,000 feet of dacitic breccia, 1,200 feet of welded tuff, and at least 2,100 feet of sedimentary and tuffaceous rock. The breccias contain exotic blocks up to 1,500 feet across.
- **Dating:** Potassium-argon dates confirm a Late Cretaceous age for the Salero volcanics. [[Richard and Courtright]] (1960) had considered similar rocks Tertiary.

**6. Plutonism at the end of the Cretaceous**
- Youngest Mesozoic plutons (diorite to granodiorite, some quartz monzonite) were followed in the Paleocene and Eocene by quartz monzonite, granodiorite, and quartz latite porphyry. These are associated with many of the region's ore bodies.

**7. Interpretive significance**
- The region records at least two Triassic–Early Jurassic volcanic episodes, Middle Jurassic plutonism and uplift, Early Cretaceous deltaic deposition, and early Late Cretaceous uplift that began the Laramide Orogeny.
- The authors caution that many correlations depend on lithology and single radiometric dates, and that unexposed areas may contain further evidence.
