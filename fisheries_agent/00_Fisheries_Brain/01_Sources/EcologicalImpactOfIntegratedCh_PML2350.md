# Ecological Impact of Integrated Chemical and Biological Aquatic Weed Control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This EPA Environmental Research Laboratory project summary (EPA-600/3-83-098, December 1983) reports a four-year study by J. V. Shireman, W. T. Haller, D. E. Colle, C. E. Watkins II, D. F. DuRant, and D. E. Canfield (University of Florida) on the physical, chemical and biological effects of [[Chemical Aquatic Herbicides]] and [[Grass Carp]] stocking for [[Aquatic Weed Control]] in Florida freshwater systems. The study examined [[Orange Lake]], [[Lake Pearl]] and 24 experimental ponds at Welaka. The central conclusion is that environmental effects of plant management are determined more by the amount of vegetation controlled than by the control technique used.

## Background & Description

Aquatic weed infestations, especially of [[Hydrilla]] and [[Eurasian Watermilfoil]], expanded sharply in recent decades owing to cultural eutrophication and species introductions. These infestations restricted navigation, agriculture, industry and recreation. Florida alone was reported to spend more than $7 million annually on chemical control. Environmental groups questioned long-term herbicide impacts, prompting interest in biological control with [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), which is effective but consumes submersed vegetation when stocked at high densities.

Study design comprised three projects:
- **Orange Lake** (untreated, naturally variable macrophyte abundance).
- **Lake Pearl** (small lake, about 56 ha in the original text as "ZS.6 ha"; herbicides and grass carp tested to assess integrated management).
- **Welaka ponds** (24 ponds; untreated controls and treatments with fertilizer, grass carp, endothall, diquat, 2,4-D or glyphosate).

Methods included monthly line transects for frequency and percent cover, water quality monitoring (dissolved oxygen, pH, nutrients, chlorophyll a, turbidity and others), periphyton slide sampling, benthos dredging, plankton netting, and fish sampling by pulsed DC electrofishing, with stomach analysis and radiotelemetry of largemouth bass in Lake Pearl.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrilla dominance:** Hydrilla comprised over 90% of vegetation biomass in Lake Pearl before treatment, so the lake was considered a near-monoculture. In Orange Lake, hydrilla was dominant between July and November, while coontail, naiad, spatterdock and maidencane were dominant at other times.
- **Light and carbon:** Hydrilla can eliminate native submersed vegetation, apparently because it uses free CO2 at lower light intensities. Its dense surface mats limit light penetration.
- **Water quality:** Macrophyte abundance influenced water chemistry. In Lake Pearl, reduction in macrophytes was linked to increases in bicarbonate, specific conductance, calcium, magnesium, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, turbidity and chlorophyll a. Lake water quality recovered as macrophytes returned, and changes in land use and hydrology often mattered more than vegetation removal.
- **Trophic communities:** Hydrilla expansion was associated with reduced algal cell numbers; a shift from bluegreen to green algae followed macrophyte reduction in Lake Pearl. Invertebrate and zooplankton communities also shifted with vegetation type and volume.
- **Conflicting user interests:** Fishermen generally favored vegetation, whereas homeowners wanted weed-free shorelines; Florida's aquabusiness was valued at about $525 million.

## Key Findings

**Vegetation and fish populations**
- In Orange Lake and Lake Pearl, large increases in fish number and biomass occurred when hydrilla coverage exceeded 60%, driven by small littoral species such as bluespotted sunfish, golden topminnow and bluefin killifish.
- High hydrilla density led to sportfish stunting through skewed length distributions and few recruits into larger size classes.
- Chain pickerel, a competitor of largemouth bass, increased in Orange Lake with the vegetation it prefers.
- When hydrilla was removed, young-of-the-year and intermediate sportfish declined, but the average individual weight rose. Three to five years were estimated for sportfish populations to stabilize.

**Grass carp and herbicides**
- Integrated management with grass carp and herbicides failed to hold Lake Pearl vegetation at prescribed densities; herbicides were needed frequently and grass carp did not prevent regrowth.
- Grass carp reduced hydrilla only in small areas until stocking reached 16 fish/ha, which eliminated all hydrilla, suggesting that intensive management is needed to hold submersed vegetation at a specific density.
- Floating-leaved plants increased in Lake Pearl despite grass carp stocking.

**Economics**
- Orange Lake fishery values fell by 45% in spring 1978 and 38% in spring 1982, relative to 1979 baseline creel data. Hydrilla reduced sportfishing income by 90% at its 1977 peak, mainly through lower angler use rather than lower success.
- The fall fishery contributed about one-quarter million dollars annually to the local economy.

**Impacts on water quality**
- The study could not definitively demonstrate direct effects of herbicides or grass carp on water quality or on invertebrate and fish populations.

**Recommendations**
1. Grass carp can eradicate submersed vegetation at a fraction of the herbicide cost, but long-term effects of complete removal are unknown and require multi-year research (at least 3 to 5 years).
2. Empirical lake models linking macrophyte density to nutrients, chlorophyll a, zooplankton and fish biomass should be developed across oligotrophic to eutrophic lakes to support cost-benefit analysis.
3. Fishery management and ichthyofauna reconstruction, shown in Russia, Poland and Israel to reduce eutrophication, should be tested in the United States with native and introduced species as weed control agents.
