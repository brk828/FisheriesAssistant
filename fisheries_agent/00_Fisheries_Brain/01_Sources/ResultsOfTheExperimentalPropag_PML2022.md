# Experimental Propagation of Razorback Suckers at the Niland Native Fish Ponds, 1986

#ichthyology #marine-biology #razorback-sucker

## Executive Summary

This January 29, 1987 memorandum from Kimberly A. Nicol, Fishery Biologist, Department of Fish and Game (Region 5), reports the first year of an experimental program to rear [[Razorback Sucker]] (RBS) at the [[Niland Native Fish Facility]]. The goal was to produce fish 100–152 mm total length (TL) for stocking into the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Of roughly 41,000 fry stocked in March 1986, about 4,163 fish were planted in the river. Only 1,952 of these (47%) met the 100 mm minimum size. Pond growth and survival varied widely, which the author attributes to stocking density, [[Lernea]] parasite losses, fertilization, and inherent pond differences. Total program cost was $15,887.62. The memo recommends a full-time onsite [[Fish and Wildlife Assistant]], better filtration, [[Catchment Basin]] installation, and measurable size-class goals.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Razorback sucker (RBS), a native fish of the lower Colorado River. The document does not give its scientific name, morphology, or life-history details.
- **Program start:** March 1986, with an experimental rearing goal of 100–152 mm TL. The number of fish desired was not specified.
- **Facility:** Niland Native Fish Ponds: five 1/4-acre ponds and two 1/2-acre ponds (Figure 1 not reproduced). Water was filtered with two swimming-pool filters.
- **Management:** Seasonal aids fed fish, treated disease, developed feed charts, and handled miscellaneous problems. The biologist spent roughly one-third to one-half of their time on pond management from March through August.
- **Measurements:** Fish were recorded as TL, fork length (FL), weight (g), and [[Condition Factor]] (K). Growth was tracked across sampling dates in April, May, June, July, and October 1986.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking and harvest timeline**
- March 1986: about 41,000 RBS fry stocked into seven ponds.
- 23–25 October and 25 November 1986: 5,163 RBS removed from ponds 1–6. About 4,163 were planted in the Colorado River, and the rest died in holding tanks or in transit.
- Ponds D and E were kept until spring, when the adjacent grass carp ponds would be drained (see [[Grass Carp]]).

**Release sites**
- About 1,000 fish were planted at [[Wilson's Crossing]], 10 miles south of the [[Parker Strip]].
- The remainder were planted at the boat ramp on the Parker Strip.

**Size range**
- Fish ranged 57–300 mm TL and 2–310 g.
- Fish that died were under 177 mm TL.

**Disease and water conditions**
- Fish were tested for [[Infectious Pancreatic Necrosis Virus]] (IPN). Fish later developed Lernea infestation, which was treated with 2 ppm [[Potassium Permanganate]] (KMnO4) and 0.5 ppm [[Dylox]].
- Treatment occurred in mid-August, when water temperatures reached about 21–33 °C. Moving fish at that temperature was judged too stressful.
- Lernea losses averaged about 50 dead fish per pond per day for two weeks in ponds 5 and 6. Dead fish were also found in ponds D, E, and 3, but no dead fish were found in pond 4.
- Dead fish that sank or were eaten by birds went uncounted.

**Density and crowding**
- Ponds 2 and 1 were not rotated as planned. Pond 2 was initially stocked with 25,000 fry, and the rotation was cancelled because of disease.
- Crowding likely reached carrying capacity around 1 July, and growth stopped by then. Density in October was about 1.4 fish per acre (reported as "L4? lr.glacre"; the OCR is ambiguous).

## Key Findings

**Survival and size outcomes**
- 1,952 of 4,163 planted fish (47%) met the 100 mm TL minimum, ranging 100–300 mm TL with an average of 191.4 mm TL.
- The author notes that many oversized fish were two to three times larger than the target minimum. Their survival compared with smaller fish is unknown.
- Heavier-stocked ponds had the highest survival, with the two most heavily stocked ponds reported at roughly 1.5–1.7%. The remaining three ponds were reported at about 0.8%–2.5%. Some values are unclear in the scan.

**Condition and growth**
- Condition (K) was the same in ponds 3, 4, and 6.
- Pond 1 had the lowest overall K but the individual fish with the lowest and highest K values.
- Pond 2 had the highest average K. Ponds 5 and 6 had similar numbers of dying fish, but pond 6 had about ten times as many fish recovered at the end.

**Factors affecting outcomes**
- Stocking density, Lernea-related losses, fertilization, and inherent pond differences all likely contributed to varied growth and survival.
- Fertilizer was added at different rates (alfalfa and superphosphate), but excess feed also acted as fertilizer. This made it impossible to compare fertilization effects.
- The author notes that identical ponds can produce fish differently under the same conditions.

**Cost**
- Total program cost was $15,887.62 (text figure; the table total reads $15,882.62). This is $3.83 per fish for five ponds. The cost per fish for all seven ponds could not yet be determined.
- Most expense went to labor: about 1.5 man-months of seasonal aid and 3 months of biologist time.
- Pond-pulling labor cost is reported as "$21107.95" in the text (OCR ambiguous; the table reads $2,572.44).

**Recommendations**
1. Assign a [[Fish and Wildlife Assistant]] with aquaculture knowledge to be onsite at all times, instead of relying on seasonal aids.
2. Install a better [[Filtration System]]. The existing swimming-pool filters did not stop Lernea from entering ponds from the reservoir.
3. Install [[Catchment Basin]]s in all ponds. These would reduce pulling time and stress, allow fish inventories, enable correct feed rates, and make it possible to combine low-numbered ponds.
4. Set measurable goals, such as target numbers of RBS in each size range. Without these, the author states the program's success cannot be judged.
