# Long-Term Retention of Implanted Transmitters in Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Holden And Tyus]] (as cited in the source, the author is H. M. Tyus of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) evaluates how long surgically implanted radiotransmitters stay in [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in the [[Green River]], Utah, and whether the implants affect growth and survival. No transmitter expulsion was detected during radiotracking of 97 Colorado squawfish and 9 razorback suckers implanted between 1978 and 1985. Of 14 transmitters recovered from recaptured fish, 12 had been retained for 3 months to 8 years in Colorado squawfish and 2 for 1 year in razorback suckers. The author concludes that implants may be retained for the life of the fish. Growth and mortality of implanted fish did not differ detectably from those of nonimplanted, dangler-tagged fish.

## Background & Description

Radiotelemetry was developed for underwater use in the 1960s and was widely applied to fishes in the 1970s. It is especially useful in turbid rivers where visual observation is impossible. Earlier work had examined external attachment methods, but few studies had tested the effects of surgically implanted transmitters on growth and survival in natural settings.

The two study species differ in status:

- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, family Cyprinidae) is listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1974).
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*, family Catostomidae) is a candidate for listing (USFWS 1985).

Transmitters had casings of polystyrene, polycarbamate, or acrylic resin. All except polystyrene were coated with [[Beeswax Coating]] before implantation, following manufacturer recommendations. Implantation used a lateral incision through the ventrolateral musculature. Recaptured fish were identified by incision scars or external dangler tags. They were re-incised over the transmitter, the transmitter was removed, and the incision was closed with sutures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study sits within the [[Colorado River Fishes Project]] and the broader [[Radiotelemetry In Fisheries]] literature. Its main ecological value is for rare fishes, where long-term monitoring may be the only way to obtain adequate samples. The paper also places its results against laboratory and pond studies of expulsion in other species:

- **Channel Catfish** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): Summerfelt and Mosier (1984) reported 71% intestinal expulsion of paraffin-coated dummy transmitters within 112 days in ponds. Marty and Summerfelt (1986) reported 53% expulsion within 23 days. Their expulsions occurred through the anus, the incision, and the body wall, with midline incisions linked to herniation and rupture.
- **Rainbow Trout** (*Salmo gairdneri*): Chisholm and Hubert (1985) reported 59% expulsion of paraffin-coated dummy transmitters over 175 days in aquaria.

The author attributes the contrast with these results to differences in surgical technique (lateral versus ventral midline incisions), transmitter coating, transmitter size, and holding stress, and suggests that the first three are the most likely explanations.

## Key Findings

**1. Transmitter retention**
- No expulsion was detected in 97 Colorado squawfish and 9 razorback suckers implanted 1978–1985.
- 14 transmitters were recovered from recaptured fish (10 Colorado squawfish with 12 implantations; 2 razorback suckers).
- Colorado squawfish retained transmitters for 3 months to 8 years. The mean retention was 2.2 years.
- Razorback sucker transmitters were retained for 1 year, the mean for that species.
- One Colorado squawfish was implanted in 1978 and again in 1985. Another had its transmitter replaced after 1 year.
- Transmitters were encapsulated in connective tissue, usually fixed to the body wall, and never free in the body cavity. The author treats this encapsulation as a normal foreign-body response.

**2. Long-term tracking context**
- Both species had been tracked routinely for 5–6 months.
- Of 112 Colorado squawfish tracked by U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service personnel, 5 were followed for 12–18 months.
- No expelled transmitter had been documented through 1985.

**3. Factors influencing retention**
- Lateral incisions may protect the wound better than ventral midline incisions, which can allow herniation and expulsion, especially in ripe females.
- [[Beeswax Coating]] was a likely contributor to retention. Tissue growth around the beeswax and polystyrene capsules appears to have hindered migration and expulsion.
- Average transmitter weight was less than 1% of fish body weight. This fits earlier recommendations: Marty and Summerfelt (1986) suggested less than 2%, and Mesing and Wicker (1986) and Miller and Menzel (1986) reported long-term retention at ratios below 1.5%.

**4. Effects on growth**
- Implanted Colorado squawfish (14 recaptured fish, size range 482–770 mm) grew 11.2 mm/year (SD = 10.2). Nonimplanted dangler-tagged fish of the same size range (n = 59) grew 10.2 mm/year (SD = 11.3). The difference was not significant (Student's t = 0.3; p = 0.76).
- Two implanted razorback suckers grew 2.5 mm/year, compared with 2.2 mm/year for 39 nonimplanted fish.
- The author concludes that retention had no discernible long-term effect on growth.

**5. Effects on survival**
- Recapture rates were 26% for implanted Colorado squawfish (N = 70) and 16% for nonimplanted dangler-tagged fish (N = 336).
- Recapture rates were 22% for implanted razorback suckers (N = 9) and 19% for nonimplanted fish (N = 323).
- The author concludes that differential mortality from implants did not differ from that of dangler tags. Lower catch rates for dangler-tagged fish may reflect tag loss.
- The result agrees with [[Minor 1981]], who reported no mortality or behavioral change in five implanted fish species.

**6. Recovery and handling**
- Two Colorado squawfish were necropsied in 1980, and their transmitters were removed after 3–5 months of retention.
- Fish were recaptured by electrofishing, netting, and angling.
- Earlier work (Mellas and Haynes 1985) reported a 33% loss of surgically tagged trout, which was attributed to 24-hour holding stress. Stasko and Pincock (1977) recommended prompt release after surgery. The author notes that expulsion may also be related to holding stress, though this has not been proven.

**Conclusion:** Surgically implanted radiotransmitters, coated with beeswax and placed through a lateral incision, can remain in Colorado squawfish and razorback suckers for years, possibly for life, with no detectable effect on growth or survival. The author recommends this approach for monitoring rare fishes.
