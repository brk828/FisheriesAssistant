# Largemouth Bass Mortality in a Tropical Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study used [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] to track 50 adult [[Largemouth Bass]] in [[Lucchetti Reservoir]], a 108-ha impoundment in southwestern [[Puerto Rico]], from January 1998 to June 1999. The authors sought to separate [[Fishing Mortality]] from [[Natural Mortality]] in a population with a reported annual mortality of 78–90%, and to link mortality timing to spawning and angling events. Fishing mortality (instantaneous rate 0.584) was substantially higher than natural mortality (0.313), giving a total annual mortality of 0.894 and an annual survival rate of 0.409. Mortality was concentrated in periods of active angling and was low during a post-[[Hurricane Georges]] closure. The authors conclude that harvest regulations, rather than natural causes alone, drive the short lifespan of this population and that more restrictive [[Size Limits]] could shift the population toward older, larger fish.

## Background & Description

**Species context.** Largemouth Bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) is an intensively studied sport fish of temperate North America. It was first introduced to Puerto Rico in the early 1900s and now occurs in reservoirs, small impoundments, and farm ponds across the island. Adult fish in Puerto Rican reservoirs are reported to be short-lived. Microtagging studies found most adults to be age 1 or age 2, with few surviving beyond age 3.

**Hypotheses for short lifespan.** Two competing explanations were considered:
- High natural mortality linked to an extended spawning season in the tropics (cited from prior work on tropical largemouth bass).
- High harvest, with little regulation of size limits or creel limits and a large consumptive angling fishery.

A telemetry study in La Plata Reservoir, Puerto Rico, found that seven of nine tracked fish that disappeared were confirmed captured by anglers, implying that harvest was the dominant loss.

**Fishing and release context.**
- Creel surveys at Lucchetti Reservoir found boat anglers identified largemouth bass as their preferred catch.
- [[Corujo-Flores]] (1991) estimated total fishing effort at nearly 20,000 h between August 1990 and July 1991, peaking May–July (6,331 h) and lowest August–October (2,947 h).
- Most Puerto Rican anglers lack the live wells and chemicals used by US tournament anglers. A Lucchetti study estimated 42% total mortality (initial plus delayed) for tournament-caught fish ([[Neal]] and [[Lopez-Clayton]] 2001), which the authors converted to a conservative 6% annual mortality from tournament releases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Lucchetti Reservoir is a storage impoundment supplying irrigation water to the arid Lajas Valley. It has steep shorelines and large annual water-level fluctuations (up to 17 m), which change shoreline length, surface area, storage volume, and littoral habitat. The reservoir is classified as mesotrophic to eutrophic based on nutrients, limnology, chlorophyll *a*, and phytoplankton biomass.

**Associated fishery.** The reservoir supports shoreline and boat-based sport fisheries for largemouth bass, redbreast tilapia (*Tilapia rendalli*), Mozambique tilapia (*Tilapia mossambica*), bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*), channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and marbled bullhead (*Ameiurus nebulosus*). Lucchetti has the best public access for anglers on the island, which the authors suggest increases the potential for overharvest.

**Water level and behavior.** Largemouth bass were consistently found offshore and became more mobile under low-water conditions. Low water may also reduce angler effort because anglers concentrate on the shoreline and littoral zone.

**Regulations.** There is no enforced size limit and a liberal creel limit of 12 fish per day. Angling is largely consumptive, with little catch-and-release.

## Key Findings

### Study design and methods
- **Tagging:** In December 1997, 50 adult largemouth bass were surgically implanted with Sonotronics CT-82-1 ultrasonic transmitters (40 × 15 mm, 4 g, 72–76 kHz, unique aural codes). Fish were collected by [[Electrofishing]].
- **Allocation:** Fish were divided among four lake areas and assigned equally to age 1 and age 2 classes and to sexes. Age was estimated from a length-frequency histogram because annuli do not form on scales or otoliths at this latitude.
- **Tracking:** Tracking began January 1998 and continued for 18 months. Fish were tracked once every 3–6 days during spawning (January–June) and once every 6–9 days otherwise, using a directional hydrophone and receiver, with positions recorded by GPS.
- **Angler reporting:** Anglers were asked to return tags and report harvested tagged fish, with the project publicized only to a limited extent.
- **Analysis:** A [[Cohort Survival Model]] (Pollock-type, with Jolly-Seber-style assumptions) estimated relocation probability (p), fishing mortality (F), and natural mortality (M) in biweekly periods, with estimates adjusted for fish that were not relocated.

### Sample characteristics
- Tagged fish ranged from 268 to 399 mm total length (mean 311 mm, SD 32).
- Six fish were excluded from survival analysis: three died before the first search, one was harvested before tracking began, and two were otherwise lost early. The remaining 44 fish were tracked for 1–536 days (mean 254 days, SD 171).
- 2,025 relocations were recorded over 92 tracking events.

### Fate of tagged fish
- Only 4 fish were known to have survived the full 18-month study.
- 7 fish (16%) were recaptured and harvested by anglers (tags returned).
- 11 fish (25%) were assigned to total or catch-and-release mortality because tags stopped moving. Ten of these were recovered as water levels receded, confirming their fate.
- 17 fish (39%) were lost to unknown causes, of which some were presumed harvested but unreported.
- 6 fish left the lake after [[Hurricane Georges]] (21 September 1998) and were censored.
- No transmitter failures occurred among the 21 tags assumed to represent unreported angling or hurricane losses before tracking ended on 11 June 1999.

### Mortality rates
- Mean (35 periods) instantaneous fishing mortality: **F = 0.584** (SE = 0.164).
- Mean instantaneous natural mortality: **M = 0.313** (SE = 0.122).
- Total annual mortality: **Z = 0.894**; annual survival rate: **0.409**.
- Mean annual exploitation rate: **0.38**, similar to 1980s US estimates but about double recent US values (typically 0.20 or lower).
- Relocation probability averaged 0.96 (typically 1.00).
- The full model (time-varying F, M, and p) fit better than a reduced constant-rate model (likelihood ratio χ² = 173.3, df = 33, P < 0.0001; AIC 138.8 vs. 186.1).

### Temporal patterns
- **Fishing mortality** occurred throughout the year but was high and fairly constant during the first 7 months, then effectively zero from mid-August 1998 to mid-February 1999. Causes include the closure of the lake from mid-September to early December following the hurricane, and lower water levels reducing shoreline angling.
- **Natural mortality** was seasonal, with deaths clustered around spawning (Feb–March 1998 and March 1999 in particular) and a smaller cluster in June–August 1998.
- The authors attribute the high spawning-period natural mortality to stress from a prolonged spawning season in the tropics, consistent with the first hypothesis.
- Fish handling and implantation appeared to cause no lasting harm. Recaptured fish were healthy, with transmitters encapsulated in fatty tissue.

### Management implications
- Fishing mortality was roughly twice natural mortality, indicating that harvest is a major driver of the short lifespan.
- Earlier work by [[Churchill]] et al. (1995) suggested that the short lifespan would preclude a trophy fishery. The present telemetry results challenge that view, suggesting that more restrictive harvest regulations could shift the age and size structure upward.
- [[Noble]] (2002) modeled minimum size limits of 279, 305, and 330 mm. A 305 mm limit gave the highest yield when M > 0.40. A 330 mm limit maximized yield when M ≈ 0.30 and F > 0.40. These models assume additive (not compensatory) natural and fishing mortality, a point supported by [[Allen]] et al. (1999).
- Other Puerto Rican reservoirs have produced largemouth bass over 3,000 g and 500 mm, indicating that trophy-sized fish are attainable on the island.
- The authors conclude that restructuring the Lucchetti population toward older, larger fish is feasible if fishery managers and resource users consider it appropriate.
