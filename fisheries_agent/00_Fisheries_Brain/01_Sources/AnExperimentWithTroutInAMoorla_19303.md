# An Experiment with Trout in a Moorland Fishpond: Part I

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted Salmon and Trout Magazine article (September 1966 and January 1967) by T. T. Macan, Jean C. McCormack and Rachel Maudsley reports fish-related data from a controlled experiment in [[Hodson's Tarn]], a small artificial moorland fishpond in which [[Brown Trout]] were stocked to study how predation shaped the rest of the tarn's fauna. The authors describe survival, numbers, growth, feeding activity and diet of the trout across the 1960 and 1961 year-classes. The data were by-products of a predation experiment, so the authors flag gaps, but they consider the findings worth recording.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** Hodson's Tarn is about one acre in extent and 9 feet deep at its deepest point. Dissolved salts are low and pH is just on the acid side of neutral. Heavy rain can replace all the water in 24 hours. Summer temperatures generally exceed 20°C, rarely by more than one or two degrees.
- **Vegetation:** [[Milfoil]] (Myriophyllum) grows in deeper water and [[Floating Pondweed]] (Potamogeton natans) toward the inflow. [[Lakewort]] (Littorella) carpets shallow water, with a [[Sedge]] (Carex) zone near the inflow.
- **History:** The tarn was drained in 1948. A few trout survived until the last was caught in early 1957. Fauna work began in 1955.
- **Stocking:** 500 trout hatched in spring 1960 were released in autumn 1960, and a similar number hatched in 1961 were released a year later. The 1961 fish were marked by clipping the adipose fin.
- **Sampling:** Fish were caught mainly by gill-net and partly by rod and line. Crews aimed for about 10 fish per sampling, to keep predation pressure high. A continuous 12-month series was not obtained until 1963–64.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predator-prey dynamics:** The study examined how [[Trout Predation]] altered invertebrate and plant-associated fauna. Some prey species persisted despite heavy predation, while others were heavily cropped.
- **Feeding ecology:** Diet was dominated by [[Leptophlebia]] (mayfly nymphs), [[Eurycercus]] (water flea), chironomid midges, and hydroptilid caddis larvae. Airborne insects contributed in the warmer months. Chironomids were eaten mainly during emergence.
- **Winter feeding:** Trout were caught with full stomachs even when temperatures stayed below 8°C from November to March 1963–64. This conflicts with [[Hickling]]'s (1962) statement that feeding stops at about 8°C.
- **Habitat structure:** Shallow-water plants Littorella and Carex harbored more fauna than Myriophyllum and Potamogeton natans. Leptophlebia and Pyrrhosoma were largely confined to margins at roughly one metre depth or less.
- **Population maintenance:** Some prey species, such as [[Gammarus Pulex]], breed continuously, and mayflies spread hatching over months. The authors liken these to "widow's cruse" species that sustain populations under predation.

## Key Findings

**Survival and numbers**
- No evidence was found that any stocked fish reproduced, though many reached sexual maturity.
- The 1960 year-class: 432 fish were captured, leaving 68 (14%) of the original 500 unaccounted for after about five years.
- The 1961 year-class suffered severe mortality. Only 52 specimens were captured. The authors infer that most of the second introduction died in its first winter, either eaten by larger fish or prevented from establishing feeding stations.
- Population estimates were assumed to be accurate, with an error the authors describe as small even under an inaccurate assumption about uncaught fish.

**Growth**
- Growth was negligible after the second winter until early 1964, when the population had fallen to about 200 fish.
- During 1964, when the population fell by about 75%, growth accelerated. Average weight and the weight of the heaviest fish were nearly two and a half times their starting values by autumn.
- The heaviest fish reached 390 g by 8 July 1965, from 38 g on 17 May 1961.
- The authors conclude that rapid trout growth occurs mainly where populations are much smaller than in most stocked tarns.

**Activity and feeding**
- Catches were fastest in May and June. Similar activity was recorded in 1964 from late April through May.
- Fish were caught at dusk with gill-nets set in the evening and lifted next morning. Most were alive, suggesting short time in the nets.
- Fish fed throughout the year, including at winter temperatures.
- Surface feeding was highly variable. Underwater feeding seemed linked to rain and wind, though no exact correlation was established.

**Diet and prey dynamics**
- Phryganea larvae were cropped heavily relative to their abundance. Enallagma and Pyrrhosoma were lightly cropped except in spring.
- Cloeon simile nymphs and newt larvae were barely eaten.
- Large, growing prey individuals tend to suppress the growth of smaller ones. The authors cite evidence from tadpoles and suggest a similar mechanism in snails.
- An "availability index" was abandoned because the data were too variable between seasons and years.

**Management recommendation**
- The authors suggest draining the pond and removing accumulated peat, down to the underlying boulder clay, would benefit the fishery by restoring vegetation cover and food supply.
