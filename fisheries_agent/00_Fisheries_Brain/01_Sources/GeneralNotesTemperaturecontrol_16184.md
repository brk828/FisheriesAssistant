# Temperature-Controlled Aquarium Tanks for Fish Laboratory Experimentation

#ichthyology #aquaculture #laboratory-methods #experimental-design

## Executive Summary

This technical note describes the design and construction of twelve 350-550 gallon temperature-controlled aquarium tanks at the [[New York State Conservation Department]] Fish Laboratory at [[Livingston Manor]]. The system was developed to conduct controlled experiments on the effects of chemicals on fish, featuring fiber glass-plastic construction, removable glass fronts, and independent aluminum grid heating systems capable of maintaining temperatures between 45-80°F with ±1.5°F precision.

## Background & Description

The aquarium assembly was designed to meet specific criteria including cost-effectiveness, efficient design, reliable glass front sealing, heating capability, minimal maintenance requirements, and chemical inertness. Each tank operates independently, allowing simultaneous experiments under different conditions.

**Tank Construction:**
- Material: Fiber glass impregnated with plastic
- Reinforcement: 3/4-inch pipe embedded in top edge, front edges, and around middle on three sides
- Smaller tanks: 60 x 36 x 39 inches (inside dimensions)
- Larger tanks: 96 x 36 x 39 inches
- Wall thickness: 1/8 inch on sides, 3/4 inch on bottom
- Rounded corners throughout
- 2-inch threaded drain in one corner
- Capacity: 350-550 gallons
- Full tank weight: 3,000-4,500 pounds

**Glass Front System:**
- One long side open with 2-inch flange
- Glass thickness: 1/4 inch (smaller tanks), 3/8 inch (larger tanks)
- Seal: Rubberized tape (1/8 inch thick) plus rubber pipe insulation (1/2 inch thick)
- Glass plate placed on rubber base on sill and pressed against flange seal
- Initial leakage stops when tank half full; complete filling creates tight seal
- Waste products removed by siphon or pump to prevent glass springing away from seal

**Heating System:**
- Closed copper pipe running over all tanks from large oil burner
- Standard circulator drives system
- Grid of 3/4-inch aluminum tubing along back of each tank
- Connected to intake and return pipes above
- Both sides of grid regulated by valves for removal
- Solenoid-controlled water flow regulated by thermostat (±1.5°F variation)
- Temperature range: 45°F (water supply temperature) to 80°F
- Temperature variation within tank: maximum 3°F despite lack of artificial circulation
- Heating rate: 45°F to 65°F in one hour
- Maintains temperature at flow of several gallons per minute

**Support Structure:**
- Tanks mounted on 1/2-inch plywood sheets
- Supported by 30-inch concrete block pillars
- Reinforced concrete floor 6 inches thick
- Removable glass allows complete disassembly and transport without leak development

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The laboratory facility was designed for controlled chemical exposure experiments on fish species. The photograph caption indicates the system was tested with [[Carp]] and [[Trout]] held at 65°F (±1.5°F), demonstrating capability for both warmwater and coldwater species research. The temperature control system allows simulation of various thermal regimes relevant to different aquatic habitats and seasonal conditions.

The facility supports [[Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration Project F-9-R]], indicating its role in broader fisheries management and conservation research programs.

## Key Findings

**Design Advantages:**
- Fiber glass-plastic tanks are strong, relatively light compared to steel, without sharp edges, and non-corrosive
- Material can be tapped and holds screws
- No maintenance required
- Cost little more than steel tanks
- Independent operation of twelve tanks allows multiple simultaneous experiments
- System convenient, efficient, and adaptable to general laboratory experimentation

**Performance Characteristics:**
- Temperature control: 45-80°F range with ±1.5°F precision
- Rapid heating capability: 20°F increase in one hour
- Stable operation at high water flow rates (several gallons per minute)
- Minimal temperature stratification (≤3°F) without artificial circulation
- Reliable glass seal after initial filling period

**Operational Notes:**
- Initial leakage between glass and seal compensated by high water volume during filling
- Leakage nearly stops at half-full; complete filling creates tight seal
- Small leaks may continue several days until seals fully compressed
- Emptying causes glass to spring from seal; siphoning or pumping recommended for waste removal
- Overhead water supply and heating systems maximize floor space and accessibility
