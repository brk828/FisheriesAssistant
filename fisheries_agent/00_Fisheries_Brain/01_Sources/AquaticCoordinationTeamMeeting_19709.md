# Cumulative Research Stress on Humpback Chub in the Grand Canyon (1992 GCES Memoranda)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-cypha

## Executive Summary

Two U.S. Bureau of Reclamation memoranda, dated July 7, 1992, come from the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program. The first announces the July 28, 1992 [[Aquatic Coordination Team]] (ACT) meeting and lists its agenda. The second, from the GCES Program Manager, assesses the cumulative effects of research-induced stress on the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and the adjacent mainstem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. Its data cover the first year of several overlapping research programs, and the authors describe the analysis as provisional. Across four research groups, researchers handled 12,115 humpback chub, documented about 8 incidental mortalities, and recorded recapture rates of roughly 15% overall. The memo concludes that the accumulated handling is manageable within permitted limits, but it recommends better data sharing, a review of radio telemetry, a quantified tapeworm survey, and early research on [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]].

## Background & Description

**Meeting memorandum (July 7, 1992).** The ACT was to meet at the GCES office in Flagstaff, Arizona, on July 28, 1992, at 9:00 AM. Its agenda covered:
- Research updates (10 minutes per group)
- Expansion of effort below Diamond Creek through the [[Hualapai Tribe]]
- LCR mapping needs and avoiding duplicated effort
- Review of a stress memorandum
- Quarterly and annual report contents
- [[PIT Tagging]] needs for the coming year
- Personnel changes
- An electrofishing safety seminar
- Other coordination needs

Attendees included representatives of the Bureau of Reclamation, [[Arizona State University]] (ASU), [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), GCES, [[Bio/West Inc]], the Hualapai and Navajo tribes, and the USFWS.

**Stress memorandum (cumulative effects).** The memo was written to John Ray of the Grand Canyon National Park Resources Unit after the National Park Service, the Navajo Nation, and other research entities raised concerns about the number of aquatic researchers and the cumulative effect of handling on native fish. Its three objectives were to:
1. Outline the scope of endangered fish research and its associated effects
2. Identify specific areas of concern
3. Recommend actions for the ACT

Areas of concern were incidental mortality, total fish handled, recaptures of previously handled fish, and fish taken for experimental or reproductive purposes. Permitted take was set by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). The authors state that the data came from annual reports and data requests rather than peer-reviewed publications, that population estimates rest on one year or less of data, and that no integrated population estimate had been produced. The analysis is therefore explicitly provisional.

**Research methods overview.** Each group used different gear:
- **ASU:** 1/8" and 1/4" hoop nets, trammel nets, and weirs. Native fish over 150 mm received PIT tags, and fish of 100–150 mm were fin-clipped. No anesthetics were used.
- **AGFD (LCR):** 6.4 mm hoop nets, trammel nets, seines, larval drift nets, dip nets, and minnow traps
- **USFWS:** 1/4" mesh hoop nets and minnow traps on 12-hour rotations
- **AGFD (mainstem):** Minnow traps, larval seines, and electrofishing, targeting young chub in backwaters
- **Bio/West:** Electrofishing, gill, trammel, and hoop nets, minnow traps, angling, and [[Radio Telemetry]] on adults

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The humpback chub is an endangered native fish of the Colorado River system. Its largest population is in the LCR, which joins the mainstem in Grand Canyon. Most adult chub were captured in the LCR reach from river mile 57 to 65.4. The memo describes several environmental stressors:
- **Flow and food:** Interim flows may have affected food availability.
- **Parasitism:** The [[Asiatic Tapeworm]] is present in the LCR population, but its infestation level has not been quantified.
- **Condition decline:** Mainstem chub lost about 9% of body weight on average over the year. The cause is unexplained and may reflect normal population cycling, flows, or tapeworm infection.
- **Flooding:** A December night flood sedimented fish held in nets, causing three ASU deaths.
- **Predation:** Non-native species were examined for stomach contents to document predation on natives.

Two other native catostomids are also of concern. The flannelmouth sucker is a category 2 species under the Endangered Species Act, and bluehead sucker populations are declining throughout the Colorado River Basin.

## Key Findings

**Totals and group results (first year, 1991 to early 1992)**

- **ASU (LCR):** 10 trips and 99 field days. 10,144 fish were captured, including 8,432 humpback chub, with 7 mortalities (<0.1%). USFWS permits allowed up to 35 mortalities from ASU methods, including 20 expected from weirs. ASU recorded 1,464 recaptures (40.2%): 1,002 ASU PIT tags (27.5%), 419 other PIT tags, and 43 Carlin or Floy tags (12.7%). The trammel net was changed from 2.5 cm to 3.4 cm mesh after four mortalities, and weirs were discontinued for poor success.
- **AGFD (LCR):** 8 trips and 80 days. 5,386 post-larval fish were collected from May to November 1991, including 2,025 humpback chub (250 larvae, 721 juveniles, 1,054 sub-adults/adults). 512 chub were PIT tagged, and 166 were recaptures (32.4%), 62 of them (37.3%) tagged by other researchers. A total of 244 chub had been taken from the LCR (126 larvae, 96 juveniles, 22 sub-adults/adults). 15 adults were taken for propagation, and all died at the Bubbling Springs hatchery.
- **USFWS (LCR):** 6 trips and 61 days. 2,489 fish were handled, including 506 humpback chub, with no mortalities or retained samples. Recapture data for the first year are unavailable because scanning for prior PIT tags did not begin until December 1991. Double sampling with ASU was resolved through exclusive reaches and data sharing.
- **AGFD (mainstem):** 5 trips and 92 days (March to November 1991). 188 humpback chub were handled, all under 150 mm, so no PIT tags were applied. Seven chub under 100 mm were taken for aging and diet analysis, within the USFWS limit of 100. One larva was taken, and no mortalities were reported.
- **Bio/West (mainstem):** 14 trips and about 210 days (October 1990 to December 1991). 964 chub were handled: 281 young-of-year, 77 juveniles, and 606 adults. There were 194 recaptures (20.1%). Four mortalities (0.42%) occurred against a USFWS limit of 10, including one chub that died after radio implantation surgery.

**Population and telemetry**

- Bio/West population estimates (95% CI) ranged from 919 <1,395> 2,254 in March to 1,102 <2,407> 6,564 in September. The memo stresses that these are unreliable.
- 53 adult chub were radio-implanted. Each transmitter weighed 11 g, and fish had to weigh over 550 g, so transmitter weight stayed within 2% of body weight. This group represents 22% of implantable-size fish captured and 82% of the USFWS permit allowance.
- Of 14 radio-tagged fish recaptured, 12 were checked by Bio/West for suture healing and health. Surgical protocols were changed as a result. Bio/West reduced implants per trip from ten to five because recontact rates were higher than expected.
- Tracking data suggested normal behavior, including spawning migration into the LCR, and no displacement from capture sites.

**Electrofishing**

- Early Bio/West settings of 15–20 amps caused burn marks on rainbow trout. Amperage was then lowered to 8–12 amps, which still produced good electrotaxis response.
- Three of 340 chub captured by electrofishing showed extended effects. All recovered and were released, but one was later recaptured downstream and never recovered.
- AGFD reported extended narcosis in larger fish such as trout, which had not been quantified.

**Cumulative summary**

- Total humpback chub handled by the four GCES groups: 12,115.
- Recaptures: 1,824 (15%). Of these, 1,165 (9.6%) were recaptured by the same group in the same year, and 659 (5.4%) were previously tagged or handled by different groups.
- Incidental mortalities: the memo reports 8 for humpback chub (<1% of handled fish). This figure does not appear to match the group totals (ASU 7 and Bio/West 4), and the 15 hatchery-bound adults are counted separately as mortalities. The discrepancy should be checked against the source reports.
- Samples taken: 100 chub under 99 mm and 22 over 100 mm from the Colorado and Little Colorado rivers for growth and aging, plus 127 larvae.

**Recommendations to permitting agencies**

1. Compile cumulative humpback chub handling and mortality each year, before collecting permits are renewed, and share it with permitting agencies and researchers.
2. Distribute monthly USFWS permit reports to ACT members and provide a quarterly calendar of planned research through GCES.
3. Decide when the otolith growth and aging project will finish and what additional take is needed, and complete any further analysis promptly.
4. Review the radio telemetry program alongside the next Bio/West annual report, weighing data value against impacts to the population and adjusting the design to fill data gaps.
5. Start life history and ecology research on flannelmouth and bluehead suckers early, so that work is spread over time at lower cost and with less stress than a later concentrated effort.
6. Quantify the level of [[Asiatic Tapeworm]] infestation in the Grand Canyon humpback chub population.
