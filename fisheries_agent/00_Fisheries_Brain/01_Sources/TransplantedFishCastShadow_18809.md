# Transplanted Fish Cast Shadow: Risks of Introducing Exotic and Genetically Engineered Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #biotechnology

## Executive Summary

This Biotechnology news feature (Vol. 10, October 1992, by Jeffrey L. Fox) examines whether the long history of damaging fish transplantations in the United States offers a useful model for assessing [[Genetically Engineered Fish]] before they are released into open environments. It draws on a workshop, "Native Fish, Introduced Fish: Genetic Implications," sponsored by the National Audubon Society, and on comments from researchers, federal and state officials, and environmental advocates. The article concludes that planned introductions of engineered fish need very careful assessment, that current U.S. policy is fragmented, and that once organisms enter open waters they are difficult to control and nearly impossible to eradicate.

## Background & Description

Foreign and domestic fish have been moved between environments across the U.S. for more than a century, often with serious consequences. The [[Zebra Mussel]] introduction to the [[Great Lakes]] is cited as a prime example, since the mussels colonize metal surfaces and clog water intake and treatment systems.

Genetic engineering in fish has so far focused largely on transferring hormone genes to increase growth rates. Other early work includes transferring [[Antifreeze Glycoproteins]] to enable cold tolerance and potentially widen habitats. Roughly a dozen U.S. laboratories are developing engineered fish, but only the group of [[Rex Dunham]] at [[Auburn University]] had sought federal approval for outdoor studies. Dunham proposed testing [[Channel Catfish]] engineered with [[Rainbow Trout]] growth-hormone genes. The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) gave a lukewarm approval, and officials later told him he could proceed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Scale of introductions:** Deliberate and accidental introductions have led to about 70 exotic fish species becoming established in U.S. waters, which the article states is 10 percent of overall fish species. More than 160 transplanted native species have also established in novel habitats.
- **Drivers:** Aquaculture growth is fueled by rising seafood consumption and by sport fishing growing at 15-20 percent annually, which pressures hatcheries to restock overfished native populations. Channel catfish cultivation accounts for about half of recent aquaculture growth.
- **Pathways:** Aquarium owners and hobbyists release exotic species arbitrarily, and ship ballast water acts as a global "conveyer belt" for aquatic organisms.
- **Mechanisms of harm:** Introduced species compete for space and nutrients and can carry parasites or pathogens that displace residents.
- **Case study, rud:** Introduced to [[Lake Oconomowoc]], Wisconsin, as bait, the [[Rud]] seemed to vanish, but geneticists found it was hybridizing with native [[Golden Shiner]]s, forming inter-generic hybrids.
- **Genetic diversity:** Declining genetic diversity among aquacultured species raises disease risk, since a homogeneous stock could be decimated by a new pathogen.

## Key Findings

- **Policy gap:** The 1990 [[Nonindigenous Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act]] is limited in scope, attempts at a broader federal policy have failed, and state efforts are minimal and inconsistent. Maureen Hinkle (National Audubon Society) called for a federal policy and uniform rules rather than a "free-for-all."
- **Growth efficiency unknown:** Recent reports show engineered hormone genes can significantly raise growth rates, but there is no definitive data on how efficiently engineered fish convert food to body weight (Anne Kapuscinski, University of Minnesota).
- **Risk assessment is low priority:** Kapuscinski says early risk assessment is "not a high priority," as research is mainly driven by building better aquaculture fish, though she argues it should begin early for planned outdoor tests.
- **Novel trait combinations:** Citing a 1989 [[Ecological Society of America]] white paper, Kapuscinski notes that "novel combinations of traits are likely to play novel ecological roles." Engineering a fish to span seawater and freshwater, or to tolerate extreme cold, could have large ecological impact.
- **No open-water release planned:** No researcher currently plans to release engineered fish into open waters; indoor hatcheries and outdoor facilities are under consideration.
- **Irreversibility:** Once released, organisms are hard to control and likely impossible to eradicate. Ellen Marsden (Lake Michigan Biological Station) said the public may need to learn "that introductions are forever."
- **Industry attitudes:** Awareness of genetic-diversity risk is growing, though market pressure has delayed action. Reggie Harrell (Horn Point Environmental Laboratory) reports fisheries managers shifting toward "restoring and enhancing" fish quality rather than simple restocking.
