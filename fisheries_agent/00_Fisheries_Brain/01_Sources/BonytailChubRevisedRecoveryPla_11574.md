# Bonytail Chub Revised Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This revised recovery plan, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 6), addresses the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), listed as endangered on April 23, 1980. The original plan was approved May 16, 1984. The species is very rare across the [[Colorado River Basin]]; few individuals have been found in the last decade, and recruitment is apparently nonexistent or extremely low. The largest known concentration is in [[Lake Mohave]]. Declines are attributed to dam construction and stream alteration, flow depletion from irrigation, hybridization with other *Gila*, and introduction of nonnative fishes.

The short-term goal is to prevent extinction, chiefly by establishing a genetically diverse captive population for reintroduction. Long-term goals (quantitative downlisting and delisting criteria) will be set once the extinction threat is removed. The plan is implemented largely through the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for the Upper Basin, which targets recovery by 2003 at an estimated cost of $59 million.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and history.** The species was described by Baird and Girard (1853) from the Zuni River, New Mexico, though Smith et al. (1979) suggested the original specimens came from the Little Colorado River at Grand Falls. "Bonytail chub" is the name used in the plan. The species is closely related to the [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]], and taxonomic status among these *Gila* remains unresolved.

**Morphology.** Adults are gray or olivaceous dorsally with silvery sides and white bellies. Breeding males show red-orange lateral slashes and head tubercles. Typical adults reach 300–350 mm TL, with specimens up to 600 mm recorded from Lakes Mohave and Havasu. Diagnostic traits include a narrow caudal peduncle (head length to peduncle depth ratio above five), dorsal and anal fin rays typically 10, and gill rakers averaging 18 (range 15–21). Juvenile identification is difficult and often "tentative."

**Hybridization.** The three *Gila* species may be sympatric with overlapping morphology. Artificial spawning has produced bonytail × roundtail and bonytail × humpback crosses (Hamman 1981).

**Recovery priority:** ranked 5C, indicating high threat and low recovery potential in conflict with economic activity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic distribution.** Records span the Colorado, Gila, Salt, Verde, San Juan, and Gunnison rivers, and the Green River in Wyoming, Utah, and Colorado. The species was abundant in parts of the late 1800s, but declined in the Lower Basin during the early 1900s and was absent from the Yuma area by 1950.

**Present distribution.** In Lake Mohave, 32 adults were collected from 1974 to 1987 and 16 more in 1988–1989. Individuals are occasionally taken in Lake Havasu. In the Upper Basin, the last riverine concentration was in Dinosaur National Monument (91 specimens collected 1962–1966). Recent captures are scattered, with several suspected fish from Cataract Canyon (1985–1987), and no recruitment confirmed.

**Riverine life history.** Adults occupy pools and eddies. Spawning was inferred in Dinosaur National Monument in late June to early July at about 18°C. Young reached about 55, 100, and 158 mm in their first three growing seasons. The largest handled was 388 mm and 7 years old. Diet shifts from chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs in juveniles to terrestrial insects in adults.

**Reservoir life history.** In Lake Mohave, adults prefer lacustrine habitat, and diet appears to be primarily plankton and algae. Shortly after impoundment, roughly 500 fish spawned over a gravel bar at up to 9 m depth.

**Hatchery biology.** Incubation temperature strongly affects survival. Hatching success was 35% at 15°C, 32% at 20°C, and 0.5% at 25°C, and none survived at 5, 10, or 30°C (Marsh 1985). Fecundity in 24 two-year-old females ranged from 1,015 to 10,384 eggs (mean 4,677), with 67.5% egg viability.

**Reasons for decline.**
- Dam construction, including Hoover, Parker, Davis, Roosevelt, and Coolidge dams, altered flow and temperature regimes.
- Cold water below Flaming Gorge Dam (after 1962) reduced summer temperatures and hatching success.
- Closure of Glen Canyon Dam altered flows, and populations in Lake Powell declined.
- At least 42 nonnative fish species have colonized Upper Basin rivers, with competition and predation implicated.
- Hybridization becomes more likely as the wild population shrinks.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** Bonytail chub is considered the most endangered fish in the Colorado River basin. No self-sustaining wild population has been confirmed.
- **Captive stocks:** A refugium exists at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico). The plan recommends at least one or two additional refugia, including sites at Page Springs Hatchery, Ouray NWR, Buenos Aires NWR, and Palm Lake (Hassayampa River Preserve).
- **Augmentation:** Stockings into Lake Mohave included about 41,000 marked fingerlings (1981), 13,320 (1982), about 28,000 fry (1983, embayment), 12,618 (1985), 13,971 (1987), and 20,000 (1988). Of 11 fish collected in 1988, one estimated at at least 10 years old may be a wild-recruited fish.
- **Green River trial (1988–1990):** 35 radio-tagged adults were released at Island Park and Jensen, Utah. Preliminary results suggest hatchery- or pond-raised adults may struggle in riverine conditions.
- **Priority tasks:** Establish refugia, evaluate captive genetics, obtain wild fish, and verify taxonomy (Tasks 111–114) are Priority 1, as are propagation tasks 1311–1313 and taxonomic resolution (Task 3).
- **Priority reintroduction sites:** Green and Yampa rivers at Dinosaur NM; Green River at Desolation/Gray Canyon; Colorado River at Cataract Canyon; San Juan River if suitable; and Lake Mohave as a wild refugium.
- **Protection measures:** Restrict warmwater nonnative stocking, enforce regulations, ensure Section 7 compliance, and pursue a multistate agreement to prohibit nonnative introductions.
- **Information gaps:** Research is needed on spawning, predation and competition, movement, essential habitat, food habits, aging, growth, and hybridization. Many cost estimates are listed as unknown.
- **Funding:** The Upper Basin Recovery Program has a projected annual budget of $2.3 million. A $10 million congressional request covers water rights purchases, and $5 million covers facilities. Water projects contribute $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion.
- **Public review:** The plan was open for public comment from July 21 to September 19, 1989. Five letters were received and considered.
