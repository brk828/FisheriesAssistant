# Hopi Buttes Volcanism: Geochronology and Geologic Evolution

#paleolimnology #volcanology #geochronology

## Executive Summary

This 1986 Arizona Geological Society Digest article by M. Shafiqullah and P.E. Damon (University of Arizona, Laboratory of Isotope Geochemistry) presents K-Ar geochronology and a geologic synthesis of the [[Hopi Buttes]] volcanic field in northeastern Arizona. The field forms the Middle member (Unit 5) of the [[Bidahochi Formation]], which separates a lacustrine lower member from a fluvial upper member. Volcanism ran from about 8.5 Ma to 4.2 Ma and produced [[Maar]] craters, [[Diatreme]] vents, plugs, and mesa-capping flows. The authors link the basin's lake history to the incision of the [[Little Colorado River]] and the establishment of a through-flowing drainage in the early Pliocene.

The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or other biological data. Its aquatic relevance lies in the paleolake deposits, the groundwater behaviour of volcanic rocks, and the fluvial base-level history.

## Background & Description

The [[Hopi Buttes]] are a Late Tertiary volcanic field in the southern [[Colorado Plateau]]. The Bidahochi Formation is divided into three parts (Repenning and Irwin, 1954; Shoemaker et al., 1957):

- **Lower member (Units 1–4):** Predominantly lacustrine. It consists of interstratified red and grey claystone and siltstone with minor sandstone. It rests unconformably on Mesozoic rocks along the flanks of the Defiance Plateau and the Zuni Uplift.
- **Middle member (Unit 5, White Cone member):** Intrusive and extrusive igneous rocks with intercalated sediments. Sediments grade from claystone at the base to sandstone and siltstone at the top, with pyroclastic and tuffaceous material locally present. The unit records a lacustrine-to-fluviatile transition marked by lateral and vertical lithologic change and cross-bedding.
- **Upper member (Unit 6):** Fluvial. It is composed of laminar and cross-bedded sandstone with minor intercalated claystone.

Augite-bearing ultrabasic [[Lamprophyres]] separate the lower and upper members.

Erosion since Unit 5 deposition has dissected the Bidahochi Formation and underlying Mesozoic sediments. The result is a landscape of [[Maar]] craters, [[Diatreme]] vents, flow-capped mesas, dikes, and volcanic necks. The most prominent features are steep-sided, nearly circular buttes formed by necks or plugs. Their dark volcanic rock contrasts with the reddish [[Wingate Sandstone]], which heightens their visual impact.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's environmental content centres on paleolake and paleodrainage history rather than living aquatic communities.

**Pliocene lakes.** Lakes formed in many maar depressions. The lake deposits include gypsum, chert, siltstone, water-laid tuffs, and travertine. The authors note that these sediments host syngenetic and epigenetic uranium mineralization (Wenrich and Mascarenas, 1982).

**Paired lakes.** If there was no differential tectonic movement between the north and south areas, two contemporaneous lakes must have existed:
- The northern lake was perched behind the Owl Rock Member of the [[Chinle Formation]], which dips 1–2° northeast toward [[Black Mesa]].
- The southern lake lay 150–200 m lower, within the already excavated [[Little Colorado River]] valley.
- Lower Bidahochi lake beds total about 90 m in the north and 45–50 m in the south.

**Groundwater.** Maars and diatremes play a significant role in the groundwater resource of the Hopi Buttes area. Explosive emplacement fractured the Mesozoic sediments, which have poor permeability. By contrast, tuff, agglomerate, and breccia are relatively porous.

**Drainage evolution.** Post-Bidahochi denudation was modest. About 82 m of pre-Bidahochi rock and the lake beds have been removed from the basin since Unit 5 time. Major washes such as Jeddito and Pueblo Colorado have cut 275–290 m below the pre-Bidahochi surface, and 30–180 m between the buttes. The authors interpret the increased gradient as the result of a new base level set by a modern through-flowing drainage in early Pliocene time.

## Key Findings

**Geochronology**
- K-Ar ages indicate volcanism from about 8.5 Ma to 4.2 Ma.
- The oldest rocks are a ring dike at [[Diatreme 14]] and a limburgite tuff at the [[Coliseum Maar]].
- A gap in ages exists between 4.4 and 6.0 Ma. The authors state this may be a sampling artefact.
- Mesa-capping [[Monchiquite]] flows (Roberts, Tesihim, Gray, Flat, and Deshgish mesas) yield K-Ar dates of 6.8–6.0 Ma. Their eruption surfaces lay at 1890–1950 m. Most of these mesas cover more than 2 km² and conceal dissected vents and maar craters.

**Landforms and relief**
- Relief around the buttes ranges from 274 m at Chimney Butte to 232 m at Round Top and Nipple.
- Butte crests share a similar altitude of 2010 ± 30 m.
- Coliseum Maar has a surface diameter of 2.7 km.
- Dissection exceeds 270 m at [[Diatreme 14]], exposing internal vent structure.
- Lacustrine sediments are preserved in about 25 percent of the northern maars, because erosion was less extensive at the greatest distance from the Little Colorado River.
- Rims of many large maars were high enough to act as topographic barriers, which explains the absence of mesa-capping flows over them.
- Some Cretaceous sedimentary blocks lie 200–300 m below their original stratigraphic positions (Sutton, 1974).

**Petrology and geochemistry**
- Monchiquite is a very fine-grained basaltic rock with 10–20 percent phenocrysts in a felty groundmass of analcime, augite, and minor feldspar.
- [[Limburgite]] tuffs contain volcanic clasts, minor augite, biotite, and olivine phenocrysts, a glassy matrix, plagioclase microlites, and calcite.
- Hopi Buttes monchiquites average 40 weight percent SiO₂. The [[Navajo Volcanic Field]] minettes are late Oligocene to early Miocene and range from 47 to 60 weight percent SiO₂.
- Hopi Buttes monchiquites have Na₂O about three times K₂O. Minettes have subordinate Na₂O.
- Hopi Buttes rocks are higher in water, P, Ti, and incompatible elements than the nearby alkalic basalts of the White Mountains, Mormon Mountain, and San Francisco fields. They are also enriched in light rare earths, strontium, and zirconium.

**Basin and river history**
- The Adamana 3 NE quadrangle contains the lowest Bidahochi beds, at about 1700 m, 5 km from the Little Colorado River, which runs at 1618 m there.
- Bidahochi beds lie at 1800 m at Greasewood, 1860 m at Pueblo Colorado Wash, 1815 m at Five Buttes, and 1900 m at Round Top Mountain.
- The [[Little Colorado River]] has cut down 82 m within the Adamana quadrangle and 212 m in the [[San Francisco Volcanic Field]] since emplacement of the 2.4 Ma Black Point flow (Damon et al., 1974).
- The Pre-Bidahochi erosion surface above 1800 m had low relief.
