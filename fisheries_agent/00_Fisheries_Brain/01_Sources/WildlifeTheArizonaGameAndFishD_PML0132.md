# Wildlife 2006: Arizona Game and Fish Department Wildlife Management Program Strategic Plan (2001–2006)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #wildlife-management

## Executive Summary

Wildlife 2006 is the six-year strategic plan (2001–2006) of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Department) and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]] (Commission) for the wildlife portion of their mission. It sets out nine general Challenges, shared Strategies, and goals, objectives and species-specific strategies for the Wildlife Management Program's three subprograms: [[Game Management Subprogram]], [[Sportfish Management Subprogram]], and [[Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Management Subprogram]]. The plan was approved by the Commission on January 19, 2001 and by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2 Office of Federal Aid on March 20, 2001. Its guiding philosophy is partnership: the Department commits to working with the broadest possible set of stakeholders to resolve conflicts and meet wildlife goals.

## Background & Description

The plan is a living document that links past and future management. Its stated purposes are to reflect public preferences and biological principles, to define the strategies and objectives that will drive annual operational (work) plans, and to balance traditional hunting and angling with emerging interests such as wildlife watching and photography.

The Department's mission is to conserve, enhance, and restore Arizona's diverse wildlife and habitats, and to provide wildlife resources and safe watercraft and off-highway vehicle recreation. Under [[Arizona Revised Statutes]] Title 17, most wildlife in Arizona is state property, and the Commission and Department hold management authority to set policies, hunting, trapping, and fishing rules, enforcement, and public information programs.

Arizona hosts more than 800 species of fishes, amphibians, birds, reptiles, and mammals (Table 1 in the source). Of these, 797 are counted as native or non-native (tallies listed below) and 74 are non-native. Native freshwater fishes number 30, saltwater fishes 2, amphibians 26, reptiles 103, raptors 42, non-raptor birds 460, and mammals 134. The state sits at the junction of four North American deserts (Chihuahuan, Great Basin, Mojave, and Sonoran) and at the terminus of the Rocky Mountains and Sierra Madre.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat and climate drivers:** Small game abundance tracks habitat quality and quantity, which in turn reflects climatic variation. Drought reduces small game numbers and hunter use.
- **Riparian loss:** Much lowland riparian habitat present a century ago has been destroyed, degraded, or fragmented. Upland watershed degradation worsens impacts downstream. Governor Rose Mofford's Executive Order 89-16 (June 10, 1989) directed agencies to pursue riparian restoration, and the [[Arizona Riparian Conservation Act]] of 1992 created an advisory committee.
- **Ecosystem approach:** The plan states that biological systems with greater diversity are more stable, and that many species act as biological barometers of ecosystem health. Single-species management is to shift gradually toward ecosystem-based planning.
- **Human-wildlife interactions:** Zoonotic disease concerns (plague, tularemia, hantavirus) affect hunter participation. The Department notes that deer mice, not rabbits or tree squirrels, are the more likely hantavirus carriers, and it is developing public health information.
- **Sportfish context:** Most sought-after sportfish were introduced; large inland reservoirs near Phoenix, such as [[Roosevelt Lake]], receive the most angling use. Water storage projects are often in conflict with fisheries objectives.
- **Partnerships across borders:** Migratory birds and bats require cooperation with other states and countries, including through the [[North American Waterfowl Management Plan]] and neotropical migrant initiatives.

## Key Findings

**Hunting (Game Management)**
- Hunting license sales declined from the 1986 peak to a low in 1992, then rose 12.4% from 1992 to 1993 and continued to climb with a slight drop in 1996–1997. Resident license purchasers were 3.4% of Arizonans in 1999.
- Women made up 6.4% of hunters in 2000. The mean hunter age rose from 36.8 (1987) to 44.7 (1999), indicating declining youth recruitment.
- Hunter education completion rose to 61.3% of Arizona hunters in 2000.
- Hunter expectations for success were 31.2% to 37.1%; actual success for big game was generally below expectations for deer and turkey.
- Among 1999 hunters, 66.7% rated their experience seven or higher on a ten-point scale.
- Juniors-only big game hunts were supported by 60% of respondents, with a mean suggested allocation of 9.1% of permits; the Commission set 2% of deer, 2.5% of general-season antelope, and 5% of antlerless elk permits for juniors in 2000.

**Sportfish (Sportfish Management)**
- In 1999, more than 350,000 resident and 35,000 non-resident anglers bought licenses. Residents logged about 7.8 million angler-days (2.49 million coldwater, 5.2 million warmwater).
- Anglers reported satisfaction as acceptable to excellent in 76% of responses (fishing experience) and 87% (outreach). The main reason for dissatisfaction was "not catching enough fish" (63%).
- Trout are the most sought-after group; 71% of 1999 license holders bought trout stamps. About 12% of anglers fish only for trout.
- Arizona anglers spent about $358 million in 1996, with an economic impact near $663 million.
- Current supply includes 159 stream reaches (1,470 miles) managed primarily for trout and 64 lakes (about 3,000 acres) managed primarily for trout.
- Projected resident demand through 2006 is 5.4 million warmwater and 2.6 million coldwater user days.

**Nongame and endangered wildlife**
- Arizona lists 113 species as Wildlife of Special Concern. The plan targets conservation projects for at least 25 of them and at least five reestablishment projects.
- Of the 32 native fishes, 27 still occur within their historic ranges; the Monkey Springs pupfish is extinct. [[Aravaipa Creek]] supports seven native species with virtually no non-natives.
- Of Arizona's 26 native amphibians and 103 native reptiles, about 20 are Wildlife of Special Concern. The Sonoran tiger salamander is federally listed as endangered; the Mojave desert tortoise is federally threatened.
- About 450 bird species are nongame. Of 42 raptor species, five are federally listed and 13 are on the state draft list.
- Arizona's population grew 53% from 1970 to 1980, 35% from 1980 to 1990, and 35% from 1990 to 2000 (4,961,953 residents in 2000).

**Key strategic commitments**
- Produce a Wildlife 2012 Strategic Plan by December 2006 and report subprogram accomplishments to the public in October 2002, 2004, and 2006.
- Pursue at least two watershed-based sportfish and native fish management approaches by 2006.
- Target at least 500,000 nongame wildlife recreation opportunities annually and at least 60% satisfaction among both hunters and anglers.
- Implement at least 20 cooperative or conservation agreements for nongame and endangered species.
