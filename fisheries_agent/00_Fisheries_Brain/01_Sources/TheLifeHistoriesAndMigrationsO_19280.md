# Life Histories and Migrations of the Ephemeroptera in a Stony Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ephemeroptera

*Note: the source is a freshwater invertebrate study, not an ichthyological one. The tag line is reproduced as the system format requires; the taxon-specific tag #ephemeroptera is the most relevant here.*

## Executive Summary

This paper by T. T. Macan (*Transactions of the Society for British Entomology*, 1957) reports three years of monthly collections of mayfly nymphs (Ephemeroptera) from a small, stony stream in the Lake District, [[Ford Wood Beck]], together with two tributaries, [[Sykeside Beck]] and [[Outgate Beck]]. Using coarse and fine nets and a shovel sampler, the author works out the life cycles of seven species. Most are univoltine (one generation per year) or show a split overwintering and summer generation, and the study argues that a summer resting period, likely spent in the egg stage, is linked to high summer temperatures. The paper also shows that delayed hatching and sampling-gear bias can seriously distort population size and growth estimates.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Ford Wood Beck, a stream with stones left after glacial fines were washed away, plus two tributaries. Outgate Beck flowed through an artificial channel and rose near a drained moss. Sampling stations were numbered 1 to 3, with tributary stations Ss (Sykeside) and Og (Outgate). Station 4 was added only in April 1951.
- **Sampling methods:**
  - Coarse net (20 threads per inch), used for 5 to 10 minutes per catch.
  - Phytoplankton net (160 threads per inch), added because many tiny nymphs passed through the coarse mesh.
  - [[Shovel Sampler]], which removed one-twentieth of a square metre of substratum. Organisms were separated by flotation in calcium chloride solution.
- **Sampling frequency:** Stations were visited monthly over three years. The effective start was early 1950.
- **Climate monitoring:** A thermograph in Outgate Beck had a defective clock, so continuous water temperature records were not obtained. Air temperature from Ambleside (about three miles away, average annual rainfall about 254 mm) was used instead. Rainfall was broadly similar across the three study years, and 1951 was the driest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature control:** The author argues that summer warmth drives the life-history patterns. Species with a summer resting period (Rhithrogena semicolorata, Heptagenia lateralis, Paraleptophlebia submarginata) are inactive mid-summer, so the stream is dominated by cold-water species.
- **Comparison with other streams:** [[Harker]] (1952) studied a colder stream where the resting period was shorter, with later emergence and earlier egg hatching. In that stream the nymphs grew larger.
- **Theoretical explanations discussed:**
  - [[Brinck]] (1949) on Swedish stoneflies: nymphs do not grow in summer heat.
  - [[Ide]] (1935) on a Canadian river: direct lethal temperatures restrict emergence.
  - [[Pleskot]] (1953) on *Habroleptoides modesta*: oxygen deficiency at high temperatures restricts emergence.
- **Habitat use:** Tiny nymphs seem to cling to smaller stones and gravel below the surface, where the coarse net misses them but the shovel sampler captures them.

## Key Findings

1. **[[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]]:** Univoltine. Main emergence in late May and June. New generation appears in August to October. Growth continues through winter, and nymphs reached the 10 to 11 mm group by March in the 1951-2 season. Tiny nymphs (1 to 2 mm) appear every month up to January, which the author attributes to delayed hatching of some eggs (possibly up to seven months). Sampling bias explains part of the step-like seasonal histogram, with the shovel sampler catching far more small nymphs early in the season.
2. **Delayed hatching:** The author argues this phenomenon is common. It invalidates growth-rate calculations based on monthly mean lengths (as used by Harker and Illies) and complicates production estimates. Allen's (1951) trout consumption estimate for the Horokiwi River may have missed unhatched eggs.
3. **[[Ecdyonurus Torrentis]]:** Single-generation-a-year life cycle in Ford Wood Beck, with emergence spread across the summer. Peak population at station 1 in most months. Harker found upstream migration of nymphs in November.
4. **[[Heptagenia Lateralis]]:** Univoltine. Grows less in winter than *R. semicolorata* and emerges later. Small nymphs first appear in September. Of 93 catches, 71 contained fewer than ten specimens. Most abundant at station 3 and in the tributaries.
5. **[[Baetis Rhodani]]:** The most complex cycle. The overwintering generation emerges April to July, and the summer generation emerges July to November. Each generation is probably split into autumn-hatched ("an") and winter-hatched ("wn") groups. Summer adults are smaller (about 5.5 to 8 mm, versus 8.5 to 10.5 mm for overwintering adults). Table 6 shows that at least 63% of large nymphs were at station 1 in one or more of May to September in each of the three years, suggesting congregation at the lowest station. The author warns that single-point emergence traps may give misleading population estimates.
6. **[[Baetis Pumilus]]:** Similar cycle to *B. rhodani* but with a shorter emergence period. Emergence of the overwintering generation starts in May and continues to July (table 7). Growth in August appeared to be about 3 mm in one month, faster than any other species studied.
7. **[[Paraleptophlebia Submarginata]]:** Univoltine. Adults emerge in May and June, tiny nymphs appear in August, and growth slows mid-winter. Too few specimens were collected for firm conclusions.
8. **[[Ephemerella Ignita]]:** Univoltine. Nymphs found only in June, July, August and occasionally September, and the author thinks it spends most of the year as eggs. This is the only true summer-growing species in the study.
9. **Summer resting period:** *R. semicolorata*, *H. lateralis* and *P. submarginata* rest during mid-summer, probably as eggs. The author proposes that this avoids unfavourably high summer temperatures or oxygen shortage, and that natural selection soon produces strains adapted to local conditions.
10. **Dispersal:** Larger nymphs of *R. semicolorata* were concentrated in the tributaries (chi-square test, highly significant in both seasons), suggesting immigration or downstream movement. A large April 1951 and May 1952 population rise in Sykeside Beck (about fivefold) was unexplained.
