# Desert Fishes Recovery Team Recommendations for Aravaipa Creek Red Shiner Invasion

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #threatened-species #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This letter from the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] leader to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Regional Director addresses the urgent threat posed by [[Red Shiner]] (Cyprinella lutrensis) invasion of [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona—essentially the last low-desert stream supporting a predominantly native fish fauna. The stream harbors two federally listed species: [[Spikedace]] (Meda fulgida) and [[Loach Minnow]] (Tiaroga cobitis). Despite internal team controversy regarding the severity of the threat, consensus was reached on four immediate management actions: installation of fish barriers, formal monitoring, establishment of refuge populations, and genetic studies. The letter emphasizes that [[Aravaipa Creek]] represents critical habitat, with [[Spikedace]] remaining in only four streams and [[Loach Minnow]] in only five streams across their entire range.

## Background & Description

[[Aravaipa Creek]] is a low-desert stream in Arizona with ownership divided among [[The Nature Conservancy]] (upper and lower reaches), and the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (central gorge, designated as [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area]]). As of December 1990, [[Red Shiner]] had attained a rare but relatively uniform distribution throughout the stream from above Turkey Creek (upper boundary of the BLM Wilderness Area) through the central gorge to near its mouth. A survey on 8 December found the extreme headwater above a temporary irrigation diversion to be free of [[Red Shiner]] and other non-native species, with all seven native species present in substantial numbers.

[[Spikedace]] now remains in only four streams: upper [[Gila River]] (New Mexico), upper [[Verde River]] (Arizona), [[Eagle Creek]] (Arizona), and [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Arizona). [[Loach Minnow]] persists in only five streams: upper [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] (New Mexico), [[Blue River]], [[East Fork White River]], and [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Arizona). The decline of both species to threatened status has been attributed by some workers mostly to habitat deterioration and loss, and by others to interactions with exotic fishes, or both.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] meeting in Phoenix on 7-8 November 1990 revealed significant controversy regarding the ecological significance of biologic interactions, specifically the negative impacts of [[Red Shiner]] on the decline of [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. Some team members believed the naturalness of [[Aravaipa Creek]] would preclude establishment of large shiner numbers, resulting in little impact on indigenous fishes. Others were convinced the invasion would be followed by shiner population explosion and subsequent declines in native species.

The controversy centered on whether to adopt a wait-and-see attitude or implement immediate action including potential renovation to remove invading shiners. Despite divergent views, consensus existed that [[Red Shiner]] represented either a potential or actual threat to the integrity of the [[Aravaipa Creek]] ecosystem. A downstream barrier, planning for which had been underway for almost two years, might have prevented the current situation had it been in place prior to mid-summer 1990 flooding.

Multiple agencies demonstrated high levels of concern and cooperation, including [[The Nature Conservancy]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and [[Arizona State University]].

## Key Findings

The [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] reached consensus on four immediate management actions:

1. **Fish Barriers**: Barriers must be placed in the stream to prevent and impede upstream movement of [[Red Shiner]] and other non-native fishes. Such barriers will prevent re-entry when exotics are displaced downstream by flood and compartmentalize the system for future management actions. The downstream barrier should be expedited, and one or more permanent upstream barriers should be created. If the headwater remains devoid of shiners and other non-natives, immediate installation of a semi-permanent gabion barrier would protect that section of stream.

2. **Monitoring Program**: Monitoring of the fish fauna should be initiated no later than 15 March 1991 and continued monthly until the extent and development of the potential shiner problem and its solution are identified, continuing into the foreseeable future to ensure ecosystem integrity. Informal monitoring was already underway collectively by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], and [[Arizona State University]] personnel, but required formalization and support.

3. **Refuge Populations**: Refuge populations of the [[Aravaipa Creek]] stocks of both [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] should be established immediately. Development of culture techniques and propagation should be given sufficient priority to ensure maintenance of captive stocks and availability of both species for research, restocking, and/or augmentation as needed.

4. **Genetic Studies**: Genetics of all remaining stocks of both [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] must be studied to assess levels of variability, differences and similarities among them, and provide baselines for genetic assessment of impacts of population changes that may occur in [[Aravaipa Creek]] and elsewhere. [[Arizona State University]] personnel had already commenced sampling and analysis for genetic characterization of both listed species.

The team leader emphasized that barriers are mandatory and already well along in the planning stage—a process requiring acceleration to fruition. Refuge populations must be established while both threatened species remain locally common. The letter stressed that dramatic alteration or loss of the [[Aravaipa Creek]] fauna is unacceptable.
