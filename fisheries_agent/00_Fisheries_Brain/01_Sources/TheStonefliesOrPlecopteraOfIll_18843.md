# The Stoneflies, or Plecoptera, of Illinois

#plecoptera #stoneflies #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1935 bulletin of the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] by [[Theodore H. Frison]] is a comprehensive survey of the aquatic insect order [[Plecoptera]] (stoneflies) in Illinois. Based on collecting and rearing from 1926 onward, the study records 36 stonefly species for the state, plus at least two and possibly a third known only as nymphs. The work revises the family classification, associates adults with nymphs through rearing, and documents seasonal emergence, habitat preferences, feeding habits and egg-laying behavior. It also includes a catalog of species, identification keys, and plates of nymphal and adult structures.

## Background & Description

The Illinois stonefly fauna had received little attention from entomologists. Adults were traditionally pinned, which shrinks the body and obscures genitalic characters, and adults and nymphs are hard to associate without rearing. The earliest major study was by [[Benjamin Dann Walsh]], whose 1862 paper recorded stoneflies mostly near Rock Island. [[Needham and Claassen]] (1925) later revised North American records; Frison notes that they credited Illinois with 14 species, while only 13 were actually supported by evidence.

Frison's 1929 report on the fall and winter fauna preceded this work, which extends the survey to spring and summer species. The present paper covers all seasons, so that classification is not limited by seasonal differences among taxa. Illinois holds only about one-fifth of the roughly 200 North American species, but it contains representatives of every family recognized by Frison except [[Peltoperlidae]].

The paper proposes a new classification that splits the traditional four families into eight: Pteronarcidae, Peltoperlidae, Taeniopterygidae, Nemouridae, Leuctridae, Capniidae, Perlidae, Perlodidae and Chloroperlidae. It is organized into a Holognatha/Filipalpia group (Pteronarcidae through Capniidae) and a Systellognatha/Subulipalpia group (Perlidae, Perlodidae, Chloroperlidae), based largely on labium structure. Frison argues that this grouping also aligns with biological habits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Most Illinois stoneflies belong to fauna common across the Mississippi Valley and eastern North America. A few are northern (*[[Togoperla media]]*, recorded once, in the Mississippi River in extreme northwestern Illinois) or southeastern (*[[Allocapnia forbesi]]* and *[[Allocapnia pygmaea]]*, in the [[Ozark Uplift]] region). *[[Hydroperla harti]]* is strongly tied to the main Mississippi River, and *[[Leuctra claasseni]]* is confined to Ozarkian streams in southern Illinois.

**Habitat.** Stream size is a major control. Large stoneflies with multi-year life cycles are excluded from small streams that dry out, so medium-sized streams often hold the greatest variety. Several species are restricted to small streams, while *[[Pteronarcys nobilis]]*, *[[Acroneuria ruralis]]* and *[[Taeniopteryx parvula]]* occur mainly in large rivers. Habitat preferences reduce competition among nymphs. *[[Neophasganophora capitata]]* favors rapid, rocky water, especially below dams. *[[Acroneuria ruralis]]* lives in trash in deeper, slower water, and *[[Perlesta placida]]* and *[[Neoperla clymene]]* hide in algae and vegetation on rocks in faster water. *[[Isoperla minuta]]* is abundant only in small, muddy, organic-rich streams. Bottom type matters too: *[[Isoperla decepta]]* and *[[Isoperla mohri]]* occur in small streams with sandy bottoms.

**Seasonal succession.** Roughly one-third of Illinois species emerge as adults, mate and feed during the coldest months. Adults are thus available from late fall through late summer. The paper charts 34 species (Figure 15) and 15 species at Oakwood (Figure 16). Herbivorous species concentrate in colder months, while the most widespread species tend to fly over longer periods. Northern Illinois emergence is later than southern.

**Feeding and behavior.** Adults of *[[Allocapnia]]*, *[[Taeniopteryx]]* and *[[Strophopteryx]]* feed on blue-green algae; *[[Leuctra]]*, *[[Nemoura]]* and some *[[Isoperla]]* also feed. Feeders have sclerotized mandibles, and non-feeders have flabby ones. Most nocturnal species do not feed. Nymphs of the spring and summer forms are largely carnivorous (*Perlidae*, *Perlodidae*, many *Acroneuria*), while the fall and winter nymphs are herbivorous.

**Life cycles.** Most small-stream and winter species complete their life cycle in one year. *Pteronarcys* and *Acroneuria* need two to three years. Frison notes that instar counts are poorly known (Wu gave 22 for *Nemoura vallicularia*; Schoenemund gave 36 for *Perla cephalotes*).

**Egg-laying.** Winter species from large streams deposit egg masses directly in rivers; *[[Taeniopteryx nivalis]]* egg masses can exceed 1,400 eggs. Small-stream *Allocapnia* crawl into the water to lay eggs. Spring and summer species generally drop egg masses from flight. Eggs differ in shape and sculpture among species; *[[Hydroperla]]* eggs are triangular in cross section, supporting its generic separation.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** 36 Illinois species are recognized, with at least two and possibly a third unnamed nymph. This is more than double the records of Walsh or Needham and Claassen.
- **Revised classification:** Four traditional families are split into eight, based on wing venation, genitalia, mouthparts, gills and habits. The Perlidae is restricted; *[[Perlodidae]]* and *[[Chloroperlidae]]* are separated out.
- **Adult–nymph associations:** Over half of the associations in this study are new, established by rearing and by collecting mating pairs.
- **Adult morphology:** Adults retain scars or remnants of nymphal gills (for example, on coxae of *Taeniopteryx* and *Pteronarcys*), which helps link adults to nymphs.
- **Sex determination in nymphs:** In most Illinois nymphs, the setal fringe on the posterior margin of the eighth abdominal segment is continuous in males and interrupted in females.
- **Taxonomic changes:** *Hydroperla* is proposed as a new genus, and *Megaleuctra* is placed in Leuctridae. Several names are synonymized: *Acroneuria evoluta* with *A. arida*, and *Perla producta* with *Atoperla ephyre*. Walsh's *Capnia minima* is referred to *Allocapnia vivipara*.
- **Pteronarcys nobilis:** The nymph is described for the first time. It is herbivorous, takes about two years to develop, and reaches body lengths up to 38 mm in males and 44 mm in females.
- ***Allocapnia illinoensis* is described as new:** Holotype and allotype are from Dolson (Clarksville), Illinois, and the species was found in a local ecological "pocket" near Charleston.
- ***Allocapnia forbesi* var. *cornuta*** is proposed for males with an extra raised tubercle on the sixth tergite.
- **Adult timing:** *Taeniopteryx nivalis* emerges from January and peaks February 15 to March 15. *Acroneuria* and *Pteronarcys* emerge from April to June. Summer-fauna species such as *Neoperla clymene* peak in early July, and some remain into late August.
- **Predation:** A robin was observed eating an adult *Perlesta placida* near Oregon, Illinois, when that species was extremely abundant.
- **Collecting methods:** Winter adults are found on light-colored bridges and tree trunks with algae. Nymphs are collected from leaf masses, and large river species from debris at hydroelectric bar racks at Rock Island and in the Rock and Kankakee rivers. Nymphs of some *Acroneuria* congregate under stone slabs at Mississippi River wing dams.
- **Preservation:** Specimens should be kept in 80 percent ethanol. Pinning shrinks bodies enough to obscure genitalic characters. Vials should be labeled with full collecting data and checked annually.
- **Conservation-relevant conclusion:** Frison concludes that few additional Illinois species are likely to be found, based on extensive watershed, seasonal and quantitative sampling supplemented by surveys in neighboring states. He stresses that a correctly named specimen gains practical value only when linked to its ecological context.
