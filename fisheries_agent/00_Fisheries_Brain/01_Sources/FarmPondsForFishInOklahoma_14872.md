# Farm Ponds For Fish In Oklahoma

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 bulletin, published by the [[Oklahoma Game and Fish Department]] and authored by H. C. Ward, E. M. Leonard, and J. Mayo Martin, is a practical guide to building and managing farm ponds for sport fishing. It estimates that Oklahoma holds about 100,000 farm ponds, with roughly 12,000 new ponds built each year. It argues that good site selection, a functioning drain, and deliberate fish management matter more than any single stocking decision. It recommends specific predator-forage stocking combinations, warns against unregulated fish transfers, and reviews chemical and fertilizer methods for controlling muddiness and aquatic plants.

## Background & Description

The bulletin was written to address the problems that arose as many new farm ponds created new habitats for fishes and other aquatic life. Its authors approached construction mainly from a fisheries biologist's viewpoint, intending it as a guide for pond builders who plan to stock fish. The management section is presented as the most important part, reflecting the best available practices at the time, while acknowledging that some statements would later become outdated.

The document is organized into sections on:

- Location and construction of the farm pond
- Pond construction (costs, dam, drain, spillway, diversion ditches)
- Management of farm ponds for fish
- Fish for the farm pond, including [[Stocking Ratios]] and the [[Fish Stocking Policy]]
- Fish production and the [[Food Chain]]
- [[Muddy Ponds]], [[Fertilization]], and [[Weed Control]]
- An appendix listing chemical suppliers

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site and water supply.** Ponds should be built in hollows or ravines, where topography supplies most of the basin walls, with a [[Pond Construction|dam]] as the only artificial side. The recommended depth is 8 to 18 feet at maximum; depths under six feet do not provide enough reserve water during drought, and the bulletin notes that shallow ponds (at least 8 feet maximum depth is the stated minimum for fish life) dry up in late-summer droughts. Subsoil must contain enough clay to hold water, since sand and gravel allow seepage. Springs are the most desirable water source, streams the least, because water exchange lowers fertility and lets undesirable fish enter.

**Silt and watershed.** Run-off from cultivated fields, roadways, and large watersheds carries silt, shortening pond life and creating muddy conditions. Pasture or woodland watersheds are preferred. Clay colloids in red clay soils can keep water permanently turbid.

**Food chain.** Fish production depends on a pyramid beginning with microscopic organisms that use nutrients, sunlight, air, and water. Algae follow, then small animals such as daphnia and copepods, then insect larvae, minnows, and young fish, and finally the larger game fish. Muddy water blocks sunlight, sharply limiting this base.

**Oxygen.** Algae and aquatic plants produce oxygen by day but consume it through respiration on hot, still nights and cloudy days. Decomposing vegetation, manure, and fertilizer can also deplete oxygen and cause fish kills.

## Key Findings

**Pond construction and drainage**

- Dams should have a 3-to-1 slope on the pond side and a 2-to-1 slope on the back side, with a top width of 10 to 12 feet and 3 to 4 feet of freeboard after settling.
- A [[Pond Drain]] is considered essential for fish management. Four-inch drains suit ponds up to three acres, and the drain pipe should be placed at the lowest point of the pond.
- Concrete cutoff collars, at least 2 x 2 feet and one foot thick, should be spaced no more than 15 feet apart along the drain pipe to prevent seepage.
- A wide, shallow, well-sodded [[Spillway]] is preferred. The bulletin advises against wire screens on spillways because they clog with trash and endanger the dam.
- Diversion ditches or [[Retaining Dams]] can control excessive run-off and keep ponds clear.

**Fish management principles**

The authors list seven principles, including that fishes are highly prolific, that adults need not be protected during spawning, that a pond cannot easily be overfished, that some species (trout, white bass, rock bass, green sunfish) are unsatisfactory for ponds, and that ponds should not be restocked yearly. They also note that a pond supports only a set number of pounds of fish per acre, comparable to pasture carrying capacity.

- Fish planted in fall should reach legal length by the following summer.
- Fishing should be encouraged for all species, since fishing only for bass can allow crappie and bluegill to dominate.
- When populations become unbalanced, often after five or more years, the recommended remedy is draining and restocking with fingerlings, or chemical removal under Department supervision.

**Species and stocking ratios**

- [[Largemouth Bass]] is the top predator and is not recommended for ponds of one acre or less. It requires clear water, cover such as stumps and brush, and sand-gravel or sand-mud spawning areas in about two feet of water.
- [[Black Crappie]] is preferred over [[White Crappie]] because it overpopulates less readily and reaches larger size. White crappie often become stunted and are not recommended for small ponds.
- [[Channel Catfish]] tolerate murky or clear water, can be fed artificially, and may need periodic restocking if they do not reproduce.
- [[Redear Sunfish]] overpopulate less readily than [[Bluegill]] and usually grow larger. Bluegill overpopulate clear ponds with abundant vegetation and can suppress bass spawning.
- Recommended combinations per surface acre include:
  1. 100 largemouth bass, 75 redear sunfish, 50 bluegill, 50 black crappie, and 75 channel catfish (optional), for ponds of about two acres or more.
  2. 100 largemouth bass and 75 bluegill or redear sunfish.
  3. 100 channel catfish, for murky ponds.
  4. 200 largemouth bass and 2,000 minnows (fathead, bluntnose), for clear bass ponds.
  5. 50 redear sunfish and 50 bluegill, for ponds of about one-half acre.

**Stocking policy**

- Pond owners must submit a completed application bearing their current fishing license number, and the Department must approve it after field inspection.
- Applications must arrive by August 15 to receive fish that year. Most deliveries occur in the fall, beginning in October.
- Private ponds are stocked only if owners permit reasonable public fishing.
- Turbid ponds are not stocked until fenced and cleared.
- Owners are strongly discouraged from seining fish from other ponds, creeks, or rivers, since this introduces undesirable fish.

**Muddy water and fertilization**

- Muddy ponds rarely produce good fishing. Causes include carp, goldfish, or crayfish; watershed erosion; missing silt basins; clay deposits; livestock wading; wave action; and oversized watersheds.
- Controlling watershed erosion is the first priority. Vegetation, commercial fertilizer, or manure can clear turbid water once run-off is controlled.
- Commercial fertilizer, such as 5-10-5, is applied by broadcasting over the watershed or pond. An initial application of 100 pounds per surface acre is suggested, followed by 50 pounds every 15 days in spring and fall, reduced to 15 to 20 pounds in hot summer months. The approximate seasonal cost is $32 per surface acre.
- Manure requires about 200 pounds every 15 days to match 50 pounds of commercial fertilizer.
- A safety test is given: if one cannot see a cupped hand when the arm is submerged to the elbow, do not add more fertilizer.

**Aquatic plant control**

- Emergent plants (cattail, bulrush, American lotus) can be controlled by drawdown, underwater cutting, hand pulling, or 2,4-D. An ester-form 2,4-D spray for one-fourth acre costs about $6.25, or $25 per acre.
- Submerged plants can be shaded out with [[Nigrosine Dye]] at 20 pounds per acre (up to 30 pounds in deeper ponds), applied after spring rains for about $12 per acre. The effect lasts 15 to 30 days and is seasonal only.
- [[Sodium Arsenite]] (one gallon of four-pound-strength solution per 30,000 cubic feet in soft water, or 16,000 in hard water) is highly toxic and was judged unsafe where livestock or swimmers are present.
- [[Copper Sulphate]] controls algae but not flowering plants, and repeated use reduces bottom fauna and kills fry.
