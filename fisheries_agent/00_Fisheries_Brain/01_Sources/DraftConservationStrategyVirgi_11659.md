# Conservation Strategy for Virgin Spinedace (Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #conservation #threatened-species #Virgin-River

## Executive Summary

This November 1994 draft conservation strategy addresses the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), a threatened cyprinid endemic to the [[Virgin River]] basin spanning southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southeastern Nevada. The species was recognized as threatened by the [[American Fisheries Society]] in 1979 and proposed for federal listing by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on May 18, 1994. The Virgin spinedace currently occupies only 37-40% (83.9-142.6 km or 52.1-88.6 mi) of its historic range, with 24% of occupied habitat experiencing adverse modification. Primary threats include water depletion, nonnative fish introductions, and habitat degradation from agricultural and recreational uses. The strategy aims to ensure the species occupies at least 80% of its historic range through flow restoration, habitat enhancement, nonnative species control, genetic management, and population monitoring.

## Background & Description

The [[Virgin Spinedace]] belongs to the endemic western cyprinid tribe [[Plagopterini]], which includes three genera: *Meda*, *Plagopterus*, and *Lepidomeda*. The genus *Lepidomeda* contains four species, with *L. mollispinis* divided into two subspecies: *L. m. pretensis* ([[Big Springs Spinedace]]) and *L. m. mollispinis* (Virgin spinedace). Most tribe members are rare; the [[Pahranagat Spinedace]] (*L. altivelis*) is extinct, while the [[Woundfin]] and [[White River Spinedace]] are federally endangered, and the [[Spikedace]], Big Springs spinedace, and [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]] are threatened.

The species derives its name from the fusion of two anterior, hardened spiny rays of the dorsal fin located just behind the pelvic fin. The specific epithet comes from Latin *mollis* (soft) and *spinis* (spine), referring to the soft-tipped second dorsal spine. The body is silvery with a brassy sheen and occasionally light sooty blotches on the dorso-lateral half. During breeding, bases of paired fins become reddish-orange. Body size typically ranges from 60 to 120 mm. The species has 77-91 lateral line scales, two rows of pharyngeal teeth (typically 2,5-4,2), eight dorsal fin rays, and usually nine anal fin rays (range 8-10).

Life history studies by Rinne (1971) indicate a lifespan of approximately three years, with sexual maturity reached at one year. Populations consist primarily of young-of-the-year (YOY) and one-year-old fish. Annual spawning occurs April through June at mean daily water temperatures of 13-17°C and day lengths of approximately 13 hours. One-year-old females average 459 eggs, while two- and three-year-old females show 42% and 34% increases in mean relative fecundity, respectively. Since populations comprise primarily one-year-olds, they often constitute over 90% of the spawning population.

Virgin spinedace are primarily insectivorous, feeding on a wide range of insects and occasionally plant material and organic debris. They feed on drifting prey in midwater and at the surface, maintaining equilibrium in the midwater column and darting up to capture prey similar to drift-feeding salmonids. Upper thermal preferences are reported at 23.1°C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Virgin River]] basin encompasses approximately 15,600 km² (6000 mi²), with headwaters in Kane County, Utah, flowing southwesterly to empty into [[Lake Mead]] on the [[Colorado River]] in Arizona. Elevations range from 3300 m (10,000 ft) above mean sea level near headwaters to less than 700 m (2000 ft) at Littlefield, Arizona. Mean annual precipitation ranges from about 4 inches at low elevations to about 40 inches at higher elevations. The diverse topography provides habitat for over 270 wildlife species, with 26 identified as sensitive.

Six fish species are indigenous to the basin: [[Woundfin Minnow]], [[Virgin River Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and Virgin spinedace. Five of these have been listed or are candidates for federal listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

Historic distribution is not well documented, but Holden (1977) speculated the species historically occurred in most clear water tributaries of the Virgin River and several mainstem reaches. Museum records from multiple institutions support this assessment. The earliest survey records (Tanner 1932, 1936) indicated the species was common in the [[Santa Clara River]] and [[North Fork Virgin River]], but probably less common in the mainstem Virgin River. Hubbs collected Virgin spinedace near Bunkerville, Nevada in 1938, but surveys in 1942 in the same area found none. The species was absent from subsequent surveys below Littlefield, Arizona between 1942 and 1975.

Current populations exist in the mainstem Virgin River and eleven tributaries/sub-tributaries: [[East Fork Virgin River]], [[Shunes Creek]], North Fork Virgin River, [[North Creek]], [[La Verkin Creek]], [[Ash Creek]], Santa Clara River, [[Beaver Dam Wash]], [[Coal Pits Wash]], [[Moody Wash]], and [[Magotsu Creek]]. The largest populations occur in the upper mainstem Virgin River above the [[Quail Creek]] diversion and in the Santa Clara River and Beaver Dam Wash drainages. Small populations exist in Ash Creek, La Verkin Creek, and the lower mainstem Virgin River below [[Pah Tempe Springs]].

Virgin spinedace typically inhabit clear, cool, swift streams interspersed with pools, runs, and riffles. They prefer pools with protection such as undercut banks, boulders, or debris, though habitat preferences vary by location. In Beaver Dam Wash, they utilize narrow, shallow runs with large amounts of emergent vegetation, while in the North Fork Virgin River they occupy quiet pools. They also prefer the shear zone between high (100 cm/second) and low (10 cm/second) velocities containing cover.

Thirteen nonnative fish species occupy the same habitat as Virgin spinedace, including [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Red Shiner]], [[Grass Carp]], Koi, Tilapia, Guppy, [[Mosquitofish]], [[Black Bullhead]], and crayfish (Astacidae). Several prey on Virgin spinedace, while others compete for limited resources. Crayfish may prey on larval and young-of-year life stages in lower reaches of several tributaries.

## Key Findings

**Threats to the Species:**

Approximately 6.8 km (4.2 mi) of historic habitat has been inundated by reservoirs including [[Quail Creek Reservoir]], [[Gunlock Reservoir]] on the Santa Clara River, and [[Schroeder Reservoir]] on Beaver Dam Wash. Approximately 59.9 km (38.1 mi) of historic habitat has been dewatered, and water depletions occur in approximately 30.9 km (19.2 mi) of currently occupied habitat. Dams and diversions act as barriers to fish movement, fragmenting habitat and populations.

Agricultural practices have modified habitat through riparian zone alteration. Crop production impacts occur in lower La Verkin Creek, lower Ash Creek, and middle Virgin River reaches. Livestock impacts occur in the Santa Clara River below Gunlock Reservoir, lower Santa Clara River, lower North Creek, lower La Verkin Creek, lower Ash Creek, and portions of the Virgin River mainstem.

Other limiting factors include drought, natural barriers, native species interactions, pollution from return flows, municipal drains, and agriculture. Recreational use (off-road vehicles) significantly impacts several reaches including the Santa Clara below Gunlock Reservoir, lower Santa Clara, and lower mainstem Virgin River.

**Conservation Objectives:**

The strategy focuses on two main objectives: (1) reduce and/or eliminate significant threats, and (2) enhance and/or stabilize instream flows in specific reaches of occupied and unoccupied habitat. The goal is to ensure the species occupies at least 80% of its historic range.

**Management Actions:**

1. **Flow Re-establishment and Maintenance:** Maintain existing channel-maintenance flows and minimum-low flows in all reaches. Establish target minimum-low flows in approximately 38.9 km (24 mi) of historic habitat. Priority areas include 27.1 km (16.7 mi) of the Santa Clara River between Gunlock Reservoir and the Virgin River confluence, and 4.5 km (2.8 mi) of the Virgin River between Quail Creek Diversion and Pah Tempe Springs. Flow requirements will be determined using an empirical approach incorporating the conceptual framework of Hill et al. (1991), defining channel maintenance flows and minimum-low flows. Target flows will be evaluated for five years before finalizing requirements.

2. **Habitat Enhancement:** Implement enhancement procedures in approximately 25.4 km (15.8 mi) of occupied habitat. Projects include maintenance and construction of boundary-line fences to control unauthorized grazing and recreational use, establishment of intensive grazing management programs on federal lands, and development of conservation easements and barriers within the Virgin River floodplain. Mitigation for future impacts will be on a one-to-one replacement basis of historic habitat.

3. **Nonnative Fish Control:** Examine nonnative fish species to identify detrimental effects. Control fish introductions through basin-wide procedures adapted from [[American Fisheries Society]] protocols. Stocking of native species will follow conservation agreements. Rainbow trout stocking allowed in areas with existing populations, avoiding direct conflict with native species of special concern. Brown trout generally not to be stocked except in limited circumstances above 5,000 feet elevation. Channel catfish, largemouth bass, and bluegill restricted to standing water impoundments. Prohibited species include nonnative minnows (Cyprinidae), [[Smallmouth Bass]], green sunfish, [[Black Crappie]], and crayfish species.

Selective removal projects include: (a) Upper Beaver Dam Wash: [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] (NDOW) will lead re-introduction efforts following removal of rainbow trout, with anticipated completion early summer 1995; (b) Santa Clara River: [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] (UDWR) will evaluate chemical renovation feasibility to control/eliminate green sunfish and other exotic fishes in workable segments; (c) Virgin River: Chemical treatments with rotenone to eradicate red shiners from Washington Fields diversion downstream to Mesquite diversion, scheduled for 1995 with follow-up treatments as needed. Approximately 20 drip stations will introduce rotenone, with Noxfish spraying in standing water areas and detoxification using potassium permanganate. Temporary fish barriers will be constructed at strategic sites.

4. **Population Genetics Management:** Establish and utilize protocols for introduction, re-introduction, and sub-basin transfer of Virgin spinedace.

5. **Population and Habitat Monitoring:** Implement cooperative monitoring by participating agencies using protocols similar to those established by the [[Virgin River Recovery Team]]. A minimum of 30 stations will be randomly chosen as monitoring points, with potential increase after re-establishment procedures. Annual fall sampling will use repeated hauls of a 15' wide x 6' deep mesh seine until depletion (≤10% of highest catch). Samples taken from preferred microhabitats approximately 10 meters in length. All native fish identified, counted, measured, and returned; first 100 nonnative fish measured. Additional data obtained from bi-annual Virgin River Recovery Team sampling. Annual assessment and evaluation by the Virgin spinedace conservation team using empirical criteria to measure management action effectiveness.

**Regulatory Context:**

Lands are administered by multiple entities including [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[National Park Service]], [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Paiute Indian Tribe]], States of Utah, Arizona, and Nevada, and private landowners. This mosaic ownership complicates regulation, monitoring, and basin-wide protection. In Utah, the species is protected against direct take but the state lacks authority to adequately protect habitat. Nevada prohibits illegal take, while Arizona provides no protection. According to USFWS (50 CFR Part 17), current protection levels are inadequate to prevent the species from becoming endangered and eventually extinct.
