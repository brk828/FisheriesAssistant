# Re: Analysis of concentrations of natural roadioactivity found in catfish and carp collected at the confluence of the LIttle Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae #radioecology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1988 memorandum and technical report, authored by John W. McKlveen (Arizona State University Radiation Measurements Facility) for the National Park Service and Bureau of Reclamation, presents results of a radioassay study on six [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]] specimens collected in June 1987 at the confluence of the [[Little Colorado River]] and [[Colorado River]]. The study was motivated by concern over slightly elevated natural radioactivity in the Little Colorado River and the possibility that bottom-feeding fish—including endangered species in the Grand Canyon reach—might bioaccumulate radionuclides, posing risks to fish health or entering the human food chain. Each fish was assayed for gross alpha activity, gross beta activity, Radium-226, and Uranium isotopes (U-238, U-235, U-234). The overall conclusion was that fish were **not** concentrating natural radioactivity to a radiologically significant degree, and no further research was recommended.

## Background & Description
This is not a morphological study; no anatomical, meristic, or morphometric data on the specimens are reported. Relevant identification notes:
- **Species sampled**: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*).
- **Sample size**: 6 individuals total (mixed species, unspecified split per species in the extracted text).
- **Tissue analyzed**: Whole fish, wet weight (frozen), reported in pCi/kg.
- **Feeding guild relevance**: Both species are noted as **bottom-feeding fish**, a trait directly relevant to the study's radionuclide-uptake hypothesis, as benthic feeding increases potential exposure to sediment-associated radionuclides.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Confluence zone of the [[Little Colorado River]] (a tributary with naturally elevated background radioactivity) and the mainstem [[Colorado River]] within [[Grand Canyon National Park]].
- **Trophic role**: Both [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]] are benthic/bottom-feeding omnivores, making them a key indicator group for assessing sediment-to-biota transfer of naturally occurring radionuclides.
- **Conservation context**: The study explicitly references concern for **endangered fish species** inhabiting this reach of the Colorado River (e.g., species such as [[Humpback Chub]], though not directly sampled here), as the confluence is a critical habitat zone. The catfish/carp served as surrogate bioindicators rather than the endangered taxa themselves.
- **Radiological source**: Elevated natural radioactivity in the Little Colorado River is attributed to natural geologic sources (background Ra-226 and Uranium), not anthropogenic contamination, per the referenced companion article "Radioactivity in the Waters of the Colorado River."

### Interpretive Summary
1. **Gross Alpha Activity**: Self-absorption corrections and interferences from calcium and potassium in tissue prevented accurate measurement — results inconclusive.
2. **Gross Beta Activity**: Beta activity is primarily attributable to naturally occurring **Potassium-40 (K-40)** and falls within normal biological ranges.
3. **Radium-226**: Measured concentrations (0.5–4.7 pCi/kg range across samples) were "somewhat higher than normal but not radiologically significant."
4. **Uranium Isotopics (U-238, U-235, U-234)**: Concentrations were within normal range compared to literature values.

### Comparative Literature Context
The report contextualizes findings against published reference ranges:
- **K-40 Beta Activity** (Klement 1982): 925–4540 pCi/kg — consistent with study's beta results.
- **Ra-226 in fish** (various cities, Klement 1982): 0.7–9.6 pCi/kg (New York), 0.7–8.3 (Chicago), 0.8–9.2 (San Francisco), 2.8–6.3 (Germany).
- **Gross Alpha** (Klement 1982): <1–310 pCi/kg.
- **Uranium** (Vohra et al. 1982): 2.03–8 pCi/kg.
- **Ra-226** (Vohra et al. 1982): 0.5–39 pCi/kg.

### Overall Conclusion
- **No evidence** that [[Channel Catfish]] or [[Common Carp]] are bioaccumulating/concentrating natural radioactivity above expected background levels at the Little Colorado River/Colorado River confluence.
- Radionuclide levels measured were generally consistent with, or only slightly elevated relative to, published values for fish populations elsewhere.
- **No additional research was recommended** by the investigating radiation facility.
- Findings support the assessment that natural radioactivity in this reach of the [[Colorado River]] does not pose a demonstrated radiological hazard to fish populations, including inferred risk to co-occurring endangered species, nor an elevated human food-chain risk via fish consumption.
