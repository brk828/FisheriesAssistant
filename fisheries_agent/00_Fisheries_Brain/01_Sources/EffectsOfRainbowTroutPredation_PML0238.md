# Effects of Rainbow Trout Predation on Little Colorado Spinedace

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether [[Rainbow Trout]] predation shapes the behavior, habitat use, and geographic distribution of the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], a threatened native cyprinid of northern Arizona. In field enclosures in [[Nutrioso Creek]], trout caused significantly greater loss of spinedace, even where natural refuges and abundant macroinvertebrate prey were present. Spinedace showed almost no predator avoidance, which the authors interpret as a lack of co-evolution with large nonnative predators. The authors propose that trout predation, possibly combined with [[Turbidity]] refugia, contributes to the disjunct distribution and decline of the species and recommend that stocking programs be scrutinized.

## Background & Description

The Little Colorado spinedace (*Lepidomeda vittata*) is endemic to the [[Little Colorado River]] system in Coconino, Navajo, and Apache counties, Arizona. It occupies stagnant pools and permanent flowing waters over substrates ranging from fine sediment to bedrock. Early surveys (Miller 1963; Minckley and Carufel 1967) found it abundant in localized sections of East Clear Creek. Later surveys reported very low numbers there, with the largest and most stable populations in the lower reaches of Nutrioso and Chevelon creeks. These declines led the [[Endangered Species Committee]] of the American Fisheries Society to list the species as threatened (Williams et al. 1989), using the definition from the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973.

Earlier explanations for the decline included reduced streamflow, repeated use of ichthyotoxins, and interactions with introduced fishes (Minckley and Carufel 1967). Later work proposed that predation plays a major role in the decline of native southwestern fishes (Minckley 1983; Meffe 1985; Rinne and Minckley 1991).

Nutrioso Creek offers a natural test case. The two species show an inverse distribution over about 40 km: trout occupy the upper ~10 km and spinedace the lower ~30 km, with minimal overlap.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Nutrioso Creek is a first- to third-order perennial stream (0.5–2.5 m wide, 0.1–1.0 m deep) draining the northern White Mountains in Apache County. It rises at 2,480 m in spruce-fir forest, crosses alpine meadows for about 40 km, and joins the Little Colorado River below 2,090 m.

- **Upper reaches (first ~10 km):** cobble riffles, temperatures below 20 °C, clear water (mean absorption coefficient 0.59; SE 0.04), and dissolved oxygen typically at or above 7.5 mg/L over a diel period.
- **Lower reaches:** pools with organic-rich sediments, temperatures above 25 °C, turbid water (mean absorption coefficient 1.81; SE 0.17) in summer, and dissolved oxygen below 1.5 mg/L at night in deep pools.

**Prey base.** Enclosure sampling by Surber net (two samples per enclosure) yielded a mean of 17,866 macroinvertebrates/m² (SE 283). The dominant groups were Elmidae adults and larvae (5,433/m²), Plecoptera (4,300/m²), Ephemeroptera (3,699/m²), Diptera larvae (2,267/m²), Trichoptera (1,367/m²), Hydracarina (400/m²), and Oligochaeta (167/m²).

**Other predators.** [[Belostomatidae]] (about 0.6/m²) were active predators on spinedace, and empty carcasses were seen on two occasions. A garter snake (*Thamnophis* sp., 540 mm) captured a rainbow trout (125 mm TL) in one enclosure.

**Habitat.** Within enclosures, spinedace used instream emergent vegetation (*Phalaris* sp., mean area 0.19 m²), large boulders (0.13 m²), undercut banks (0.36 m²), and open water.

## Key Findings

**1. Trout predation was high even with refuges and abundant prey.**
- Spinedace loss averaged 34% (SE 0.6) in trout enclosures versus 5% (SE 0.2) in controls (F = 17.7, df = 1,10; P = 0.002).
- Trout stomachs held a mean of 0.7 spinedace per fish (SE 0.2; N = 6).
- Trout consumed about 0.6 spinedace per day (SE 0.03) over the 10-day field exposures.
- In a 24-h trial (N = 4), one trout (185 mm TL) ate four spinedace (mean 48 mm TL, SE 1.6) within 16 h.

**2. Trout selected smaller spinedace.**
- Consumed fish averaged 53.2 mm (SE 0.63) versus 63.7 mm (SE 1.5) for survivors (t = 5.41, df = 56; P < 0.01).

**3. Trout changed spinedace habitat use.**
- Use of undercut banks fell from 77% (SE 5.3) in controls to 44% (SE 4.1) with trout.
- Use of open water rose from 21% (SE 4.7) to 52% (SE 4.2).
- Rainbow trout had a significant effect on behavior (F = 23.4, df = 3,24; P < 0.001).
- Use of rocks and vegetation was lowest and did not differ between treatments. Habitat use did not differ among early morning, midday, and late afternoon.
- The shift into open water likely increases exposure to visual predators.

**4. Schooling and "flipping" behavior.**
- Spinedace formed loose schools in all enclosures, with or without trout.
- When approached by larger fish, individuals performed a "flipping" motion that flashed their silver sides. No attacks were observed, and the function is unclear. The authors note the display would be highly conspicuous to a predator such as trout.

**5. Limited evolutionary experience with predators.**
- The authors argue that minimal avoidance reflects limited selective predation through evolutionary time. Spinedace probably used habitats now occupied by trout before exotic salmonids arrived, possibly in the mid-1800s.
- The species is adapted to harsh arid conditions (high summer temperatures, elevated salinity, floods, high suspended sediment) but not to nonnative predators.

**6. Turbidity as a possible refuge.**
- High suspended sediment reduces the reactive distance of fish predators (O'Brien 1976) and restricts salmonids (Sorenson et al. 1977; Newcombe and MacDonald 1991).
- Turbid habitats may offer a transient refuge but usually have limited food, so they are marginal for long-term populations.
- Turbidity in lower Nutrioso Creek pools during summer may help explain the inverse distribution of the two species.

**7. Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Trout predation influences the habitat distribution of spinedace within the stream and possibly their geographic distribution across overlapping drainages.
- Trout may have contributed to the disjunct distribution and threatened status of the species.
- The authors recommend scrutinizing stocking programs and considering native fishes and the functionality of potential refugia before future stocking.

**Methodological note.** Replication was kept to a minimum because the species is endangered. Fish were collected under permit PRT 676-811-AGFD. Experiments used four enclosures (2 m × 3 m; 64-mm hardware cloth), with two treatments and two controls, at a stocking density of 3.8 spinedace/m² (SE 0.5). Trout density was 0.3–0.6/m². Experiments were replicated on three dates. Proportions were arcsine-transformed and analyzed by one-way ANOVA, and habitat-use data by three-factor MANOVA with Student-Newman-Keuls comparisons (Zar 1984).
