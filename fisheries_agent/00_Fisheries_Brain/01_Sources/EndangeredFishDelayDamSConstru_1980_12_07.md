## Executive Summary
This 1980 news article documents a regulatory conflict between the State of Utah and the U.S. Department of the Interior over construction of the White River Dam and an associated 105,000 acre-foot reservoir in northeastern Utah. Three fish species native to the [[Colorado River Basin]] — the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (referred to in the source by its historical common name, "Colorado squawfish"), the [[Humpback Chub]], and the [[Bonytail Chub]] — were identified by federal wildlife authorities as endangered and potentially threatened by the dam project. The case is explicitly compared to the precedent-setting [[Snail Darter]]/[[Tellico Dam]] controversy in Tennessee, which led to amendments of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The article is primarily a policy and environmental-law account rather than a biological field study, but it contains embedded ecological and habitat data relevant to these three imperiled cyprinids.

## Background & Description
The source text provides no direct morphological descriptions (e.g., body measurements, fin counts, coloration) for the three species. However, known taxonomic identity can be cross-referenced:
- **Colorado Pikeminnow** — Large-bodied, elongate piscivorous cyprinid; historically North America's largest minnow species, adapted to fast, turbid river currents.
- **Humpback Chub** — Distinguished by a pronounced dorsal hump behind the head, an adaptation hypothesized to aid in maintaining position in swift, turbulent flows.
- **Bonytail Chub** — Streamlined, torpedo-shaped body; adapted to open, fast-moving river channels; among the rarest of the Colorado River's endemic big-river fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: All three species inhabit the "muddy waters" of the [[White River]], a tributary of the [[Colorado River]] system, located roughly 40 miles southeast of Vernal in the [[Uintah Basin]] of northeastern Utah.
- **River system context**: The White River is one of 18 tributaries within the broader Colorado River system under federal survey to assess cumulative impacts of water development on endangered fish populations.
- **Habitat knowledge gap**: The Interior Department explicitly acknowledged that "habitat requirements of the fish are not fully known," underscoring incomplete baseline ecological data at the time of the dam's environmental review.
- **Threat vector**: Proposed impoundment (105,000 acre-foot reservoir) and associated water withdrawal for oil shale and tar sand processing were identified as primary risks — likely through altered flow regimes, turbidity changes, temperature modification, and habitat fragmentation, all known stressors for [[Big-River Cyprinids]] adapted to free-flowing, turbid conditions.
- **Regulatory ecology**: The case exemplifies the intersection of the [[Endangered Species Act]] with regional energy development (oil shale and tar sand extraction), which required "billions of gallons of water" for heat- and solvent-based extraction processes.

## Key Findings
- The U.S. Department of the Interior determined that the Colorado squawfish (Colorado Pikeminnow), humpback chub, and bonytail chub are all federally endangered and inhabit the White River within the proposed dam and reservoir footprint.
- Utah's Bureau of Land Management right-of-way application faced delay after a three-year environmental impact study failed to resolve the "biological opinion" component addressing endangered species impacts.
- In August 1980, Utah requested an extension of the [[Endangered Species Act]] consultation period after learning the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service intended to issue an opinion finding the project would threaten the three fish species.
- A broader survey of the Colorado River system and its 18 tributaries was underway to determine cumulative effects of water projects on endangered aquatic species.
- Interior Department officials warned Governor Scott M. Matheson that immediate permitting could jeopardize the dam project's legal standing under the Endangered Species Act.
- The controversy is explicitly framed as analogous to the [[Snail Darter]] vs. [[Tellico Dam]] case in Tennessee, a landmark precedent for using the Endangered Species Act to halt or delay large water infrastructure projects.
- Political response included Governor Matheson's stated intent to seek Congressional amendment of the Endangered Species Act to grant states greater authority over such determinations, paralleling Senator Howard Baker's earlier amendment efforts during the Tellico Dam case.
- Project delay estimates shifted construction completion from an original 1984 target to potentially late 1984, 1985, or later, directly impacting private energy companies' plans to begin tar sand and oil shale production by 1985.
