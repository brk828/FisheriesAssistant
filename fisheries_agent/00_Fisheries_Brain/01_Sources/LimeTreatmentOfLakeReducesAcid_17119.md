# Lime Treatment of Lake Alma Reduces Acid Mine Waste Pollution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater #acid-mine-drainage

## Executive Summary

This 1957 article, reprinted from *Industrial Wastes* and written by [[Vernon W. Cole]], describes how [[Acid Mine Drainage]] from bituminous coal mines damaged [[Lake Alma]] in southeastern Ohio. Acid inflow caused a decline and loss of several fish species, including [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Carp]], [[Crappie]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. Direct application of [[Hydrated Lime]] to the lake raised pH and total acidity, improved spawning success, and was followed by three years of recovery. The author reports the treatment as quick and inexpensive, while noting that each acid-affected lake is a distinct problem.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** [[Lake Alma]] is an almost round lake of 72.5 acres, with an average depth of 9.6 feet and a shoreline of 9,050 feet. It lies about a mile from [[Wellston]] in [[Vinton County]], within the [[Raccoon Creek]] watershed, a tributary of the [[Ohio River]].
- **Watershed:** The 2.4 square mile watershed is mostly heavily wooded. The surrounding area is hilly, with coal mining and lumbering as the main industries.
- **Origin and ownership:** The lake was created in the early 1900s by a long dirt fill across an oxbow of Little Raccoon Creek. It was privately owned until 1927, when the city of Wellston bought it as an auxiliary water source.
- **Pollution sources:** The main feeder stream is less than a mile long and flows mainly from November to May. Acid drainage came from two drift mines, one combination drift and strip mine, and one strip mine. None were extensive, but the coal-vein slope and wetness caused acid drainage at each.
- **Author:** [[Vernon W. Cole]], Research Associate, Cooperative Fisheries Research Laboratory, Southern Illinois University, Carbondale. The work was financed by the [[Ohio Division of Wildlife]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Early good fishing:** [[Creel Census]] data from 1945 to 1947 showed good fishing. [[Test Netting]] in the same period showed good standing crops of fish of desirable size.
- **Decline:** Test netting from 1949 to 1952 showed decline and ultimate disappearance of gizzard shad, carp, crappies, and channel catfish.
- **Buffering loss:** The lake's [[Alkaline Reserve]] (natural [[Buffering Capacity]]) initially hid the effects of acid inflow. Local residents said fish kills were infrequent before the 1930s and became more frequent and severe later. The author attributes this to continued loss of the alkaline reserve through precipitation and loss of calcium from the water.
- **Fish kill:** A severe fish kill in May 1951, when the fish population was already low, eliminated several species.
- **Aquatic plants and algae (pre-treatment):** Only one submerged plant was prevalent in 1951, and emergent and floating forms were suppressed. Algae was so sparse as to be considered rare.

## Key Findings

1. **Failed control:** A filter of 100 tons of crushed [[Limestone]] was placed in the main feeder stream in 1951. It reduced acid inflow but did not stop the lake's pH from falling. By February 1952 the pH had dropped to 4.5 and total acidity had risen to 28.5 ppm. The filter had little or no effect and its cost was excluded from treatment totals.
2. **First lime treatment (1952):** One ton of hydrated lime raised pH from 4.5 to 5.8 over about a week and lowered total acidity to 16.0 ppm. In April, a treatment of three-fourths of a ton raised pH from 5.5 to 5.8 and lowered total acidity to about 14 ppm.
3. **Summer pH:** Summer 1952 reached a maximum pH of 6.9, compared with a maximum of only 6.1 in previous summers with available data.
4. **October 1952 treatment:** Three tons of lime were applied to increase buffering capacity before spring pollution. pH moved erratically, finishing at 7.1, with total acidity of about 11 ppm. The author considers the residual acidity to be due to carbon dioxide and acid-ionizing salts.
5. **Stability in 1953–1954:** No further lime was added. pH stayed at or above 6.9 except for a short period in June 1954, even though water draw-off ranged from 3.5 feet below spillway level in 1951 to 6.5 feet in 1953. In 1953 the lake lost nearly half its total volume, according to the capacity-elevation curve from the Ohio Division of Water. All major makeup water sources still carried acid mine wastes.
6. **Application methods:** Early treatments poured 50-pound bags of lime from a boat into the outboard motor's turbulence. In October, a 55-gallon drum and two pumps produced a slurry spread over a 14-foot width through a nozzled pipe. The slurry method used lime more efficiently but took more time. The author judged both acceptable.
7. **Spawning and fish recovery:** Before treatment, pH usually reached its lowest point during the spawning season, from early May to late June. After treatment, abundant young-of-the-year fish of nearly all species appeared in fall 1952, indicating more successful spawning. Improved catches of young fish by [[Shore Seining]] and of adults by test nets followed in 1953 and 1954.
8. **Crappie stocking:** Adult [[Crappie]] were stocked in April 1952. They appeared in test net catches until summer 1954. Crappies spawned in 1952 were taken in test nets and entered angler catches in 1954.
9. **Vegetation and algae response:** From 1952 to 1954 several new submerged aquatic plants appeared, and emergent and floating forms grew more vigorously. Algae became more abundant and new forms were noted.
10. **Costs as reported:**
    - Hydrated lime was bought in ten-ton lots at $17.00 per ton, including transport. Total lime cost was slightly over $80.00, or $1.10 per acre for materials. Prorated over the three effective years, this is $0.35 per acre.
    - Application took nine man-days at $12.00 per man-day. The article reports this as $2.60 per acre over the three-year period.
    - *Note:* Nine man-days at $12.00 is $108, which is about $1.49 per acre for one application, or about $0.50 per acre over three years. The reported $2.60 figure does not reconcile with the stated inputs and is reproduced here as given.
11. **Transferability:** The procedure may apply to any lake receiving acid mine waste, but results will vary with original pH, total acidity, and water volume. Each lake is a new and different problem.
