# Fish and Habitat Associations in the Gila River Basin: Improving Monitoring Protocols and Tracking a Source of Population Size Variation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #habitat-association

## Executive Summary

This draft technical report by Linda J. Allison of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) analyzes fish and macrohabitat data from the [[Gila River]], [[San Pedro River]] and [[Salt River]] collected between 1995 and 1999 under a monitoring protocol designed for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (USBR). The work was commissioned to address the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) Biological Opinion, which requires monitoring of non-native fish impacts on threatened and endangered species. The report characterizes habitat availability, describes how individual species and the fish assemblage associate with macrohabitats, examines links between fish species, and recommends how monitoring should be stratified by habitat and how flow effects should be tracked in future analyses.

The cover memorandum, dated November 16, 2001, asks reviewers to send comments by December 13, 2001, so that the text can be revised into a final technical report.

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory context:** CAP water delivery to the Gila River Basin, authorized in 1993, raised the risk of transporting non-native fishes from the Colorado River. The 1994 Biological Opinion (USFWS 1994) found that delivery would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*).
- **Monitoring mandate:** Reasonable and Prudent Alternative No. 2 directed USBR to develop a monitoring plan to determine baseline community composition and to track impacts of non-native fishes on native communities in the Gila and San Pedro rivers, including movement of non-natives from canals.
- **Protocol:** Clarkson (1996) drafted the monitoring plan. Its power to detect community change was assessed by Allison (2000), who argued that stratifying counts by habitat could reduce between-year variability for species with non-random habitat use.
- **Study design:** The report uses data from eight reaches (three on the San Pedro, four on the Gila, one on the Salt). Each protocol station was 200 m long, sampled by single-pass electroshocking. Macrohabitats (pools, riffles, runs and isolated pools) were the unit of analysis.
- **Species:** Table 1 lists the scientific and common names of fishes and acronyms used in the report. Native species discussed include the three speciose natives AGCH, CAIN and PACL. Non-natives include CYCA, CYLU, ICPU and others. Several uncommon species and hybrids (for example, a *Catostomus* hybrid in the text) were also recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Macrohabitat availability and dimensions**
- 379 macrohabitats were visited across the three rivers (Table 4). The Salt River had relatively fewer riffles and many more pools than the San Pedro River, and macrohabitat frequencies differed significantly among rivers (chi-square test, p < 0.0005).
- Within reaches, relative frequency of macrohabitat types was generally consistent between years. Within years, reaches on the same river were generally similar.
- Pools were the deepest and widest macrohabitats. Riffles were shorter than pools, and shallower than runs.
- Salt River macrohabitats were generally the longest, deepest and widest.

**Effect of discharge**
- Discharge was estimated from four [[U.S. Geological Survey]] gages (USGS 2001), using the mean discharge over the seven days before each station was sampled.
- A tenfold increase in discharge was associated with fewer pools (about one-fifth of the previous count), fewer riffles (about one-half), and more runs (about 1.6-fold, p = 0.097 but not significant at 0.05).
- Sampling was concentrated in October through December, near the end of the annual high-flow period.

**Habitat use by fish**
- Twelve species were present in at least 5% of macrohabitats and were analysed with logistic regression and canonical correspondence analysis (CCA). In total, 364 macrohabitats were sampled, and 37 contained no fish.
- The three native species (AGCH, CAIN, PACL) had reduced odds of occurring in pools. None of the non-natives had higher odds of occurring in non-pool habitats.
- Non-natives generally favored pools. Native species were more often found in riffles.

**Associations between species**
- Pairwise correlations were calculated for common species. AGCH had the most negative associations (five of six significant), indicating that it tends to occur apart from other common species.
- Most other significant correlations were positive.

## Key Findings

1. **Monitoring gaps:** Station sampling is systematic rather than random, the same stations were not always revisited, and sampling lengths are uncertain. Lyons (1992) suggested 35 times the stream width, while Paller (1995) suggested 100 to 450 times. Supplementary qualitative sampling is recommended for uncommon species.
2. **Habitat availability is variable:** Between-year variability is greater than between-reach variability on the same river. This implies that more years of data would be needed to detect trends in habitat availability.
3. **Flow matters:** Higher discharge reduces pools and riffles and increases runs. Because the study captured only low-flow conditions, the full seasonal effect of flow on habitat remains unknown.
4. **Native and non-native habitat partitioning:** Natives (AGCH, CAIN, PACL) favor riffles and non-pool habitats; non-natives favor pools. This partitioning may help explain regional coexistence.
5. **Reach-level differences:** Multivariate analysis showed reach-specific differences in fish assemblages. Reaches 1 and 2, both on the San Pedro, were unique enough to be distinguished from others. These differences were not simply river-level effects.
6. **Measurements are useful but costly:** Single-species logistic models found that most continuous habitat measures (length, depth, width) had significant effects. However, the CCA suggested that detailed measurements added little beyond reach and macrohabitat type. Maintaining the measurements was recommended only if they support specific species analyses (e.g., AMNA, CAIN, LECY, PACL).
7. **Stratification:** Stratifying counts by habitat reduced the coefficient of variation for many species. Species with improved precision were not among those judged hardest to track in the earlier power analysis, so the report concludes that stratification will not improve trend detection for those species.
8. **Recommendations:**
   - Describe habitat over the full year and across a wider range of discharges.
   - Use consistent discharge data for all three rivers, and build separate models for availability and for species abundance.
   - Consider a log-transformed model with river, reach (nested in river) and time as factors, so that parameters describe percentage change per year.
   - Reconsider the sampling unit: macrohabitats for habitat dimensions, but reaches for fish counts.
