# Over-Summering Strategies of Macroinvertebrates in Intermittent Streams in Australia and Arizona

#benthic-macroinvertebrates #freshwater-ecology #intermittent-streams

## Executive Summary

This 1992 comparative study, published in the Environment Canada symposium volume *Aquatic Ecosystems in Semi-Arid Regions: Implications for Resource Management*, examines how aquatic macroinvertebrates survive [[Drying Disturbance]] in intermittent streams. The authors compare two [[Werribee River]] and [[Lerderderg River]] sites in south-eastern Australia with [[Sycamore Creek]] in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of Arizona. They ask whether the fauna that returns when flow resumes survives drying in [[Over-Summering Refuges]], and they use the framework of [[Ecosystem Stability]] (resistance and resilience) to interpret the results.

The Australian streams had a diverse set of refuges that contributed to resistance, and most taxa found soon after flow resumed had survived in them. In Sycamore Creek, in-stream refuges contributed little, and the epigean fauna appeared to depend on [[Aerial Recolonization]]. The authors describe the study as preliminary and stress that results from more streams are needed.

## Background & Description

Drying is probably the most significant abiotic disturbance to surface-dwelling (epigean) aquatic biota in intermittent streams, although [[Flash Floods]] are major disturbances in desert streams with unstable beds. Earlier work on drying was mostly descriptive, covering physiological and behavioural responses of individual invertebrates or listing taxa from refuges. No previous study had assessed what drying implies for ecosystem stability.

The authors follow the definition of stability from Webster et al. (1975). A system is stable if its state variables are not deflected by a disturbance (resistance) or recover quickly (resilience). They apply this framework to drying in the spirit of [[Stanley and Fisher Framework]], specifically "Resistance II," which asks how much refuges contribute to resistance of the benthic fauna.

The study compares refuges at a broad taxonomic level, mostly family, to reduce the effect of [[Endemicity]] between continents.

**Study sites**

- **Australian sites:** Upper reaches of the Werribee and Lerderderg Rivers, about 100 km north-west of Melbourne. The Werribee ceases flow for about nine weeks each year. The Lerderderg flows through summer one year in three. Pools persist in both rivers except in drought years. Mean annual rainfall is about 950 mm, and maximum summer temperatures seldom exceed 40°C. The sites are heavily shaded by eucalypt woodland.
- **Sycamore Creek:** A spatially intermittent desert stream draining 505 km² of mountainous terrain with desert scrub. Flow is unpredictable, flash floods move the substrata, and the sites are unshaded. Average annual rainfall is 450 mm, and the mean maximum summer temperature is 41°C.

All reaches were riffles with heterogeneous gravel, pebble and cobble substrata. Drying at Sycamore Creek is faster and lasts longer than in the Australian streams. Crayfish burrows are absent there, and large permanent pools seldom persist nearby.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Refuges examined**

- [[Hyporheic Zone]] (subsurface water, sampled with the [[Karaman-Chappuis Technique]] three to six weeks after surface flow ceased)
- [[Crayfish Burrows]] (water-filled burrows, termed [[Pholeteros]] assemblages)
- Under dried litter and dried filamentous algae on the stream bed
- Dry surface sediments (upper 10 cm), re-inundated in the laboratory

**Sampling and analysis**

- Refuge samples were sieved (50 µm mesh) and live-sorted under 12–63× magnification.
- When flow resumed, benthos was sampled with a suction sampler in Australia and a core sampler in Sycamore Creek, supplemented by hand-netting.
- Abundance was recorded semi-quantitatively: present (1–2 individuals), common (3–10) or abundant (>10).
- Analyses used cumulative species curves, mean richness per refuge, [[TWINSPAN]] classification and [[Hybrid Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling]] (HMDS) ordination with the Kulczynski coefficient.
- A second, reduced data set used only taxa common to both continents, with some taxa grouped as [[Ecological Equivalents]].

**Key environmental contrast:** Sediment surface temperatures at Sycamore Creek frequently exceed 60°C, and moist sediments dry within days. Litter mats in Sycamore Creek are thinner and less well developed than in the Australian rivers.

## Key Findings

**Refuge richness (Australia)**

- 23 major taxa, mostly arthropods, were recovered from four refuges.
- Refuge totals: dry sediments 18 taxa (78%), litter 11 taxa (48%), hyporheic zone 8 taxa (35%), crayfish burrows 6 taxa (26%).
- Taxa with desiccation-resistant stages (e.g., nematodes, leptophlebiid mayfly nymphs) dominated the re-inundated sediments. Oligochaetes and elmid beetle larvae were also present.
- Nematodes and oligochaetes were the only taxa common in the hyporheic zone. Its fauna largely overlapped with that of the crayfish burrows.
- Amphipods (*Austrochiltonia australis*) were abundant under dry litter.
- Decapod crayfish (*Engaeus* sp.) and janirid isopods were among the taxa that survived in free water.

**Refuge richness (Sycamore Creek)**

- 13 major taxa were collected from three refuges.
- Refuge totals: dry sediments 13 taxa (100%), litter 11 taxa (85%), hyporheic zone 9 taxa (69%).
- Nematodes were abundant in all three refuges.
- Ceratopogonid larvae, oligochaetes and planorbid snails (*Helisoma* sp.) were numerous in the hyporheic zone.
- Peracarids, mayfly and stonefly nymphs, and caddisfly larvae were not recorded from refuges. Peracarids are absent from the Sycamore Creek surface water.

**Mean richness per sample (Table 1)**

| Location | Below dried litter | Dry substrata flooded | Hyporheic zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Australia | 6.7 ± 1.6 | 5.9 ± 0.2 | 3.4 ± 0.6 |
| Arizona | 4.6 ± 0.8 | 3.4 ± 0.6 | 5.8 ± 0.5 |

Australian refuges held more taxa per sample than Sycamore Creek, except in the hyporheic zone.

**Post-resumption faunas**

- Within two weeks of flow resuming, 20 major taxa were collected from the Werribee and Lerderderg Rivers and 9 from Sycamore Creek.
- Re-inundated dry sediments yielded 16 taxa in Australia and 13 in Sycamore Creek, from an equivalent number of samples.
- Cumulative species curves levelled off for all refuges on both continents, indicating adequate sampling.

**Taxa and endemicity**

- Dytiscid beetles were abundant in the surface waters of all three streams but were never found under litter in Sycamore Creek, where they were common under litter in Australia.
- Hydrophilid larvae and adults were common in moist Sycamore Creek sediments but absent from Australian refuges.
- In Sycamore Creek, two gastropod species favoured the hyporheic zone, whereas their Australian equivalents were found after flooding dry substrata.

**Multivariate results**

- Full data set: TWINSPAN and HMDS both showed clear endemicity. The Australian re-inundated sediments were most similar to the surface fauna at flow resumption. The Sycamore Creek refuges separated from the surface samples, which suggests little postwetting recolonization from them.
- Reduced data set (common taxa and ecological equivalents): The Sycamore Creek refuges still grouped apart from the surface samples. The Australian free-water refuges (hyporheic zone and crayfish burrow water) grouped together, distinct from the Sycamore Creek hyporheos.
- Common "early flow" taxa across all three streams included turbellarians, oligochaetes, simuliids, chironomids, tipulids, ceratopogonids, stonefly and mayfly nymphs, and nematodes. Overlap between continents in refuge use was still low after endemic taxa were removed.

**Main conclusions**

- In the Australian streams, many taxa that reappeared after flow resumed survived drying in the hyporheic zone, crayfish burrows, under litter and algae, and in surface sediments. Others probably survived in permanent pools, a refuge not measured in this study.
- In Sycamore Creek, in-stream refuges contributed little to resistance. Stability of the epigean fauna is likely maintained by [[Aerial Recolonization]]. Early colonists included simuliids, baetid mayflies and capniid stoneflies, which can recolonize from the air.
- The main reason for the difference is probably the severity of drying rather than time since inundation, which was similar in both studies. Sycamore Creek drying is faster and lasts months rather than weeks.
- The hyporheic fauna of Sycamore Creek (over 50 taxa, mostly below 30 cm depth, many blind and unpigmented) is largely made up of obligate hyporheos. It contributes little directly to the stability of the surface fauna.
- At the broad taxonomic level, the fauna present shortly after flow resumes is fairly predictable. This fits [[Equilibrial Community Concept]] applications, which allow resistance and resilience to be measured. Later Australian assemblages become less predictable and resemble nonequilibrial communities, which complicates the application of these concepts to later disturbances such as floods.
- The authors stress that comparisons should be limited to equivalent streams. Differences may reflect drying intensity within a single continent as much as between continents. Results from more streams under similar drying regimes on both continents are needed to test the hypothesis.
