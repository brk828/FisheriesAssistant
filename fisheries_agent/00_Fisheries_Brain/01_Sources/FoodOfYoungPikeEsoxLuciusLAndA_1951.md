#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Esocidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined stomach contents of 551 young [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), 345 small [[Yellow Perch]], and 431 fish of 18 other species collected from drainage ditches feeding Houghton Lake, Michigan — a primary spawning and nursery habitat for pike. The research documents a clear ontogenetic feeding succession in larval/juvenile pike: **Entomostraca → aquatic insects → vertebrates (fish and tadpoles)**. Cannibalism was significant, with 13.3% of pike ≥21 mm having consumed conspecifics. Yellow perch emerged as the most important fish predator of young pike, consuming pike in 20.6% of stomachs examined and comprising 62% of perch stomach content volume. The authors conclude that competition, predation, and cannibalism were major contributors to the extremely high (99.5–99.9%) mortality of pike eggs and fry on the spawning grounds.

## Background & Description

- **Study system**: [[Peterson's Ditches]], drainage channels entering Houghton Lake near Peterson's Resort, used by adult [[Northern Pike]] as spawning grounds and by fry as a nursery/rearing habitat before lakeward migration.
- **Sampling methods**: Seines, dip nets, minnow traps, and weirs; collections spanned May–July 1939 and May–June 1940.
- **Analytical approach**: Stomachs examined under low-power binocular microscope; food items identified, counted, and volume measured (water displacement) or estimated. Frequency of occurrence and percent of total food volume used as importance metrics. Pike were grouped into 5-, 10-, and 20-mm size classes for analysis.
- **Spawning population data** (from Carbine 1944):

| Year | Spawning Females | Spawning Males | Migrating Young | Egg/Fry Mortality |
|------|------------------|-----------------|------------------|--------------------|
| 1939 | 125 | 280 | 7,239 | 99.82% |
| 1940 | 65 | 81 | 1,495 | 99.93% |
| 1942 | 56 | 70 | 8,000 (est.) | 99.56% |

- Pike fry begin lakeward migration at ~20 mm total length, typically within 6 weeks post-hatch, though some remain in ditches until late summer.
- Associated species (small [[Yellow Perch]] and 2–4 inch fish of ~17 other species) co-migrated into the ditches from the lake, creating a natural assemblage that influenced pike survival and growth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Co-occurring species examined** (Table 1) included: [[Yellow Perch]], [[Bluegill]], [[Rock Bass]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Brook Stickleback]], [[Central Mudminnow]], [[Blackside Darter]], [[Iowa Darter]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Brassy Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Blackchin Shiner]], [[Blacknose Shiner]], [[Spottail Shiner]], [[Rosyface Shiner]], [[Mimic Shiner]], [[Redbelly Dace]], and others.
- **Plankton availability comparisons**: Four plankton samples (20–50 gallons strained) were compared against pike stomach contents to assess selectivity. Findings:
  - In 2 of 4 comparisons, Entomostraca were consumed roughly in proportion to their abundance in the water column.
  - **Nauplii**, though abundant in plankton samples, were never found in pike stomachs — suggesting a minimum prey-size threshold rather than strict avoidance.
  - Overall pattern suggests young pike are **not highly selective feeders**, consuming whatever entomostracans are most abundant and of suitable size.
- **Competition for invertebrate food**: Nearly all associated fish species (up to 40 mm) fed on Entomostraca, with occurrence in 47.3–90.0% of stomachs and comprising 16.0–57.7% of food volume — indicating direct dietary overlap with young pike.
- **Competition for insects (chironomids)**: Extensive overlap with pike (21–50 mm) documented in [[Yellow Perch]], [[Brook Stickleback]], [[Central Mudminnow]], [[Iowa Darter]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Redbelly Dace]], [[Common Shiner]], and multiple shiner species.
- **Vertebrate food competition**: Minimal — only [[Yellow Perch]], [[Central Mudminnow]], and [[Creek Chub]] consumed minnows/tadpoles to a limited extent, so little direct competition existed at this trophic stage.
- **Non-fish predators observed**: Birds, dragonfly and damselfly nymphs, beetle larvae, and water bugs ([[Belostomatidae]]) were noted capturing and consuming young pike fry.
- **Environmental/behavioral note**: Migration timing was weather-dependent — more fry migrated lakeward on bright days than cloudy days; the extended overcast period in 1940 likely prolonged fry exposure to ditch-based mortality sources (predation, cannibalism).

## Key Findings

### 1. Onset of Feeding
- Fry begin active feeding at **11–12 mm** total length, sometimes before yolk sac is fully absorbed.
- Earliest diet consists of small cladocerans (*Chydorus*, *Simocephalus*), copepods (*Canthocamptus*), and ostracods.
- Food items per stomach in early fry ranged from 2–66 organisms.

### 2. Three-Stage Feeding Succession (Table 2 & 3; Figs. 1–2)
| Pike Size | Dominant Food | Stage |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| Fry–25 mm | Entomostraca (cladocerans, copepods, ostracods) | Stage 1 |
| 26–50 mm | Immature aquatic insects (chiefly Chironomidae) | Stage 2 |
| >50 mm | Vertebrates (fish, tadpoles) | Stage 3 |

- Close agreement in food-group proportions between 1939 and 1940 datasets validates the generality of this succession.
- This Entomostraca→insect→fish sequence parallels patterns documented in [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Yellow Perch]], and [[White Bass]] (per cited literature: Adams & Hankinson 1928; Surber 1941; Tester 1932; etc.)

### 3. Crustacean Prey Details
- Small cladocerans (*Chydorus*, *Pleuroxus*, *Scapholeberis*) dominated early diet.
- Larger cladocerans (*Simocephalus*, *Eurycercus*) more common in 21–25 mm fish.
- *Daphnia* and *Bosmina* eaten infrequently; *Polyphemus* only appeared in June 1940 samples.
- Copepods (*Cyclops* most abundant; also *Canthocamptus*, *Diaptomus*, *Osphranticum*) major food up to 26 mm.
- Ostracods consumed consistently but in small quantities up to 70 mm.
- Stomachs with **Entomostraca exclusively**: 77.4% of pike <21mm and 39.1% of pike <60mm (1939); 68.7% and 43.4% respectively (1940).

### 4. Insect Prey Details
- Chironomid larvae/pupae formed the bulk of insect food, most important in 26–50 mm size class.
- Minor contributions: beetle larvae, mayfly nymphs (*Blasturus*, *Ephemerella*), dragonfly nymphs.

### 5. Vertebrate Prey & Cannibalism
- Tadpoles consumed by pike 26–152 mm, increasing with size: 7.6% (21–40mm), 13.3% (41–80mm), 37.4% (81–152mm) of total food volume.
- Fish prey absent in pike <51 mm; increasingly dominant in larger pike.
- Prey fish species identified in pike stomachs: [[Mimic Shiner]], [[Rosyface Shiner]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Brook Stickleback]], [[Brassy Minnow]].
- **Cannibalism**:
  - Onset at 21 mm total length.
  - 13.3% of 354 pike ≥21mm had eaten conspecifics.
  - Much more prevalent in 1939 than 1940 (only 7/233 stomachs in 1940 contained pike).
  - In 1939: 25% of 21–25mm group and 32.2% of 26–30mm group were cannibals.
  - Minimum size gap between cannibal (23mm) and prey (16mm) was just 7mm — prey often ~2/3 the cannibal's own length (Table 4).

### 6. Predation by Yellow Perch (Table 9)
- **[[Yellow Perch]] was the primary fish predator of young pike.**
- 20.6% of 345 perch stomachs (with food) contained pike; pike comprised **62.0%** of total perch stomach content volume.
- Pike prey ranged 12–36 mm (mean ~25mm), about 1/3 the length of predating perch.
- Predation rate declined once pike reached ~38–44mm average length (by late May/early June), suggesting a predation size-refuge threshold.
- Individual collection dates showed extreme variability: up to 66.7% of perch stomachs containing pike, with pike composing as much as 95.9% of food volume on peak dates.
- Other confirmed pike predators: [[Creek Chub]], [[Blackchin Shiner]], [[Mimic Shiner]].
- Notable trap incident (May 8, 1942): 5 [[Pumpkinseed]] contained 12 pike; 4 [[Central Mudminnow]] contained 16 pike — attributed to high fry availability in the trap itself rather than typical field rates.
- Non-fish predators: birds, dragonfly/damselfly nymphs, beetle larvae, and [[Belostomatidae]].
