# Lake Management Report: Lincoln Lakes and Sportsmen's Lake, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey *Biological Notes* report (No. 11, August 1939, by David H. Thompson and George W. Bennett) evaluates [[Lincoln Lakes]], a set of water-filled gravel pits near Lincoln, Illinois, and a 3.69-acre arm called [[Sportsmen'S Lake]]. Hook-and-line fishing in the main lakes was poor, although legal-sized fish made up about 19 per cent of the total fish weight. The Logan County Sportsmen's Club was given the arm to test management methods. Sportsmen's Lake was poisoned on May 20, 1938, and repoisoned on June 14, 1938, to remove the existing fish. A full census was then made, and the lake was restocked with [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. The report also covers bass growth and maturity, water chemistry and temperature, aquatic plants, and management recommendations.

## Background & Description

**Lincoln Lakes** are excavations dug since 1905 by the [[Lincoln Sand and Gravel Company]] in the floodplain of [[Salt Creek]], about a mile south of Lincoln. The source gives the water area as about 100 acres in one place and 92.5 acres in the detailed description, which excludes a new 10-acre pond dug after 1936. Other figures from the description:

- Shoreline: 7.3 miles
- Maximum depth: 42 feet (reported)
- Average depth: about 15 feet
- Shallow area: only 5 to 10 per cent of the water is less than 5 feet deep

The company developed a [[Bathing Beach]] (built 1930 to 1931 for $16,000), cottage sites, boating, and a bait concession. Fishing costs $1.00 per year plus the state license. Cottage sites rent for $35.00 per year on 10-year leases. Hunting is prohibited.

**Sportsmen's Lake** was dug in 1920 and 1921 and diked off in 1938 to keep it separate from the main lakes and protect it from Salt Creek floods. Its key dimensions are:

- Area: 3.69 acres
- Average depth: 9.8 feet
- Maximum open-water depth: given as 19.4 feet (the OCR reads "194 feet")
- Shoreline: 0.8 mile
- Volume: 36 acre-feet

In March 1939 a Salt Creek flood topped the levee and backed water from Lincoln Lakes into the lake, and a second flood followed in April.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish history.** No written stocking records exist. Sportsmen and company officials recalled that the [[Illinois Department of Conservation]] fish car stocked the lakes three or four times after 1925, and that trucks also brought fish. Species mentioned include largemouth bass, yellow bass, white and black crappies, warmouth, bluegill, green sunfish, yellow perch, sheepshead, channel catfish, black and yellow bullheads, and lake trout and walleye fry. Flood waters from Salt Creek probably brought in smallmouth bass, gar, gizzard shad, and several minnow species.

**Predators.** Herons, cormorants, kingfishers, gulls, fish-eating ducks, and a few turtles and gars that had come from Salt Creek were present. The authors judged that they had not noticeably affected the fish population.

**Water chemistry and temperature.** Key observations from 1938:

- Transparency (Secchi disc): 3 feet on May 20 (poisoning day), 9 feet on June 2 after the silt settled, 4 feet 3 inches on August 12 due to a heavy plankton algae crop, and 3 feet on November 16. Lincoln Lakes measured 3 feet 9 inches on August 12.
- Surface temperature: 70°F on May 20; 78.25°F at the surface on June 2, falling to 47.3°F at 18 feet.
- Dissolved oxygen: [[Largemouth Bass]], white crappie, black crappie, and bluegill need 2 to 3 ppm. In summer they cannot be expected to survive below about 15 feet in Lincoln Lakes. [[Bullhead]] species tolerate about 0.5 ppm.
- Chemical analyses by J. B. Swartz of the [[Illinois State Water Survey]] were run on samples taken April 30 and November 16, 1938.

**Aquatic plants.** Lincoln Lakes have very few submerged plants. [[Curly-Leaved Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton crispus*) was planted near the beach, spread so fast that bathers objected, and was pulled out. In May 1938 members of the club hauled a truckload of vegetation from strip-mine ponds near Oakwood. It included *Ceratophyllum*, *Myriophyllum*, *Ranunculus*, *Chara*, and several *Potamogeton* species. *Potamogeton foliosus* "took" the lake and was abundant by August 12, but it had disappeared by November 16. The other species persisted near the levee.

## Key Findings

**Census of Sportsmen's Lake (May–June 1938)**

- The first poisoning on May 20, 1938 used poison from a boat and a garden sprayer. Fish were sorted and counted over three days.
- The lake was repoisoned on June 14 at twice the original dose (1 lb poison per acre-foot). Survivors were mostly large fish, and many redmouth buffalo and bass fry (½ to 14 inches) had hatched after the first poisoning.
- Total catch: 7,008 fish weighing 1,256 pounds, or 1,899 fish and 340 pounds per acre. At least 29 species were recorded.
- Hook-and-line fish of legal or edible size made up 19 per cent of the weight (239.4 pounds). Largemouth bass and bluegill contributed most of the legal fish.
- Early hoop-net attempts (1937 and April 1938) caught very few fish. No bass were taken in either year. Muskrats cut holes in the nets.

**Largemouth bass**

- 167 bass were taken in the two poisonings: 146 in the first and 21 in the second. Only the first group was fully measured, aged, and sexed.
- Standard versus total length ratios fell as fish grew (1.236 for the smallest group, 1.192 for the largest), so the body grows faster than the tail.
- The most common age groups were one- and two-year-olds. The five- and six-year groups were larger than the three- and four-year groups, which suggests dominant broods every three to four years.
- Survival estimates: about 25 per cent per year for ages one to seven, and about 50 per cent per year for older fish.
- All bass four years and older were legal length (10 inches or more). Eight of 10 three-year-olds and 5 of 47 two-year-olds were legal length. The largest yearlings were 9 inches.
- Sexual maturity follows length more than age. All fish over about 10 to 10.5 inches were mature and all smaller fish were immature. Of 56 mature specimens, 19 were female and 37 male. Growth rates did not differ significantly between the sexes.
- Condition index C (weight in pounds divided by cubed total length in inches, multiplied by 10,000) rose with length, so larger bass are stockier.
- About half of the bass under five years old had formed new annual rings by May 20, 1938. None of the older fish had. Larger fish in an age group tended to form rings earlier.

**Other species**

- White crappies were numerous but small, and they grew very slowly. Hundreds of undersized fish were caught on June 16, 1937, and only four legal fish were taken (two bass and two crappies). Legal size for crappie is 8 inches.
- Most pan fish (crappies, bluegills) were small. Carp, buffalo, bass, and channel catfish were large.

**Restocking and management**

- Restocking in 1938 and 1939 added 227 largemouth bass and 255 bluegills from sources including Homewood Lake, Senachwine Lake, Chautauqua Lake, and Missouri stocks.
- The authors recommended removing at least 100 bass in 1939, removing at least 150 breeder bluegills, and no further stocking. They advised against minnow bait because of contamination risk and suggested artificial baits, worms, grasshoppers, and crayfish.
- They urged the club to keep detailed records of catches, transparency, vegetation, and predators, and to build a higher levee against Salt Creek floods. Levee erosion control with *Lespedeza* was recommended.
- For Lincoln Lakes, the report recommended levees to keep out turbid flood water, removal of legal-sized white crappies to reduce crowding and competition with bass, better bass-fishing methods, and wood duck nest boxes.

**Summary conclusions**

- Lincoln Lakes covered about half of Logan County's water area. Sportsmen's Lake's experimental clearing was successful as a management trial.
- Sportsmen's Lake's rebuilt fish community was expected to give better bass fishing, because bass were expected to be free of competition from crappies, with bluegills providing forage.
- Scale-based ages show that bass populations were shaped by variable year-class strength.
