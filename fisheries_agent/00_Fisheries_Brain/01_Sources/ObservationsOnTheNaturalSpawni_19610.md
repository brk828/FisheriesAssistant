# Observations on the Natural Spawning of Eastern Brook Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by [[Paul R. Needham]] documents all phases of natural spawning in [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) observed from an [[Underwater Observation Tank]] at the [[Sagehen Creek Wildlife and Fisheries Station]] in the Sierra Nevada, California, during late September and early October 1959. Observations centered mainly on one tagged breeding pair in two artificial spawning beds. The paper describes courtship, nest cutting, the spawning act, and post-spawning egg covering, and compares them with descriptions of [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Arctic Char]] and [[Brown Trout]]. It links spawning timing to water temperature and flow, and argues that knowing spawning requirements can guide stocking and the construction of spawning beds.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** Sagehen Creek station at 6,400 ft elevation, 27 miles north of Lake Tahoe, in the Truckee River drainage. Observations ran from September 4 to October 23, 1959 (Figure 1 caption).
- **Observation tank:** Two water-level control dams with flashboards allowed depth control over about one foot. Half-inch mesh screens prevented downstream escape, and a weir about four feet upstream confined the fish. The tank was previously described by [[Needham (1956)]] and [[Needham and Jones (1959)]].
- **Artificial spawning beds:** Two beds were built beside the two downstream windows. Each used a log anchored to the streambed, a canvas apron laid on the upstream side to force water up through the gravel, and stream-worn gravel of roughly 1/4 to 1 inch. Water depth over the beds was about 6 inches at the tank side, rising to about 1 foot at the upstream ends.
- **Focal fish:** The tagged male (about 12 inches, jaw tag placed in 1954) and a female (about 9 inches). Other stocked fish included five additional males and three females of Eastern Brook Trout, three sexually mature [[Brown Trout]] (one female about 10 inches, two males about 14 and 16 inches), and five [[Rainbow Trout]] (5 to 9 inches).
- **Stocking method:** Fish were collected by direct-current electroshocking from adjacent stream sections and placed in the tank pool on September 25, 1959. They recovered within about two hours.
- **Limitations:** Most observations were made through a small window, which gave a limited field of view. Fish frequently fled upstream into deeper water out of sight.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Timing cues:** The paper concludes that [[Day Length]] and [[Water Temperature]] are the main environmental factors inducing spawning readiness. A rapid temperature drop beginning September 15 may have acted as a releasing mechanism, and spawning began immediately afterward.
- **Temperature:** Ripe males appeared by September 15. Peak spawning occurred between October 1 and 15, when daily minimum water temperatures were 37 to 44 °F and daily mean temperatures were 43 to 46 °F. Females stayed near the redds until about October 25. The full season was estimated at September 15 to October 25.
- **Flow:** Discharge was low and steady, at 1.4 to 1.9 cubic feet per second (c.f.s.) for most of September and October. A storm on September 18 raised flow briefly to 6.1 c.f.s., and it returned to 1.9 c.f.s. by September 21. Peak-spawning discharge ranged from 1.7 to 1.9 c.f.s. No severe flooding occurred.
- **Reference temperatures:** [[Arctic Char]] spawned at 38.3 to 54.5 °F (3.5 to 12.5 °C), and salmon in Jones and King's work spawned between 36 and 42 °F.
- **Site selection:** Natural brook trout redds in Sagehen Creek (about half a dozen observed) were in fairly rapid currents at the lower ends (tails) of pools. Gravel size was the main cue, and the sites offered well-aerated water for incubating eggs. Eastern Brook Trout also spawn in lakes at upwelling gravel beaches.
- **Species interactions:** Brown trout spawned only at night. Eastern Brook Trout spawned both day and night. Rainbow trout did not harass spawners or eat eggs.

## Key Findings

1. **Courtship and nest preparation:** The male drove rivals away and courted the female by darting alongside, quivering, and rubbing fins. Only the female cut the redd, using rapid side-turned tail and body undulations. Cutting produced a pit about 4 to 6 inches deep. The male did not share in nest cutting.
2. **Testing behavior:** Before spawning, the female "anchored" by pushing her anal fin into deep crevices or against large stones to test pit depth. The cutting action resembled that of Atlantic salmon described by Jones (1959).
3. **Spawning act:** The first act was seen at 12:40 p.m. on October 10, 1959, and a second on October 11 at 11:05 a.m. Each lasted only about one second. Eggs and milt were released simultaneously, and the pair spawned once in a single pit. Smith (1941) reported two males sharing in one of three observed acts, but no other male shared here.
4. **Egg covering:** Immediately afterward the female performed a sinuous, weaving "post-nuptial dance," using the ventral tips of her caudal and anal fins to push about 1/4-inch gravel over the eggs. She finished covering in four minutes and then circled the pit for about 30 minutes. She drove away immature brook and rainbow trout aggressively. Fabricius and Gustafson (1954) reported that Arctic char spawned several times per pit, unlike the single act seen here.
5. **Day and night activity:** Brook trout spawned in daylight and at night. Nest shape changed overnight, indicating nocturnal activity. Brown trout spawned only at night.
6. **Redd detection:** Redds in small streams can be found by their clean, rounded gravel piles. Females' digging noise also helped locate spawning fish.
7. **Management implications:** The author argues that knowing species-specific spawning requirements and optimal densities would allow managers to install or improve spawning beds where natural gravel is scarce or crowded. Crowding can cause late spawners to uncover and expose earlier eggs to predators. The study shows that trout will use artificially constructed redds.
8. **Research gaps:** The author calls for more data on water volume and flow direction through redds, gravel size and position, spawning temperatures, and population density relative to hatching and survival.
