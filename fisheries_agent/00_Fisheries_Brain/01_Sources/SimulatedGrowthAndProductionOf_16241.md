# Simulated Growth and Production of Endangered Snake River Sockeye Salmon: Assessing Management Strategies for the Nursery Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates lake management options for [[Sockeye Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), an endangered Snake River stock whose historical nursery lakes lie in the Sawtooth Valley of central Idaho. The authors combined [[Limnology]] sampling, in situ experimentation, and simulation modeling to test [[Lake Fertilization]] and [[Kokanee Reduction]] as ways to improve survival and growth of juvenile sockeye released from a [[Broodstock Program]]. Results indicated that several lakes could receive broodstock progeny, and that fertilization combined with kokanee reduction was the option most likely to enhance survival of stocked juveniles in [[Redfish Lake]]. The authors suggest that the modeling framework could serve as a template for recovery planning for other endangered fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Historical range:** Snake River sockeye historically spawned and reared in five nursery lakes in the Sawtooth Valley. Today, anadromous fish return only to [[Redfish Lake]], the largest lake in the system and the only one without locally constructed barriers on its outlet streams.
- **Life history:** Juveniles generally smolt at age 1 and migrate down the Salmon, Snake, and Columbia rivers, passing eight dams and reservoirs before reaching the Pacific Ocean. Most spend two years at sea before returning through the migration corridor.
- **Conservation status:** The National Marine Fisheries Service listed the stock as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1991. Adult counts fell sharply: up to 5,000 anadromous adults returned to Redfish Lake in the 1950s, and perhaps 20,000 returned to the nursery lakes near the turn of the century. The stock is considered an evolutionarily significant unit because it is the most southerly and highest-elevation sockeye stock.
- **Recovery actions:** Full recovery depends on modifying mainstem dams on the Columbia and Snake rivers. In the interim, the Idaho Department of Fish and Game and NMFS established a short-term broodstock program. About 100,000 juveniles were released in 1995, and several hundred thousand more were slated for release from 1996 through 1998.
- **Competing kokanee:** Resident [[Kokanee]] (also *O. nerka*) in these lakes derive from the anadromous populations and may have absorbed much of the rearing capacity of the lakes, potentially compromising broodstock progeny.

The authors had three goals: (1) assess limnological suitability of the five lakes for sockeye growth and survival, (2) evaluate lake fertilization as a way to raise production, and (3) evaluate the effect of resident kokanee on introduced juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and productivity**
- The five lakes were oligotrophic. Mean epilimnetic total phosphorus and total nitrogen averaged less than 15 µg/l and 100 µg/l, respectively.
- Mean summer chlorophyll remained below 1.5 µg/l in all lakes. Stanley and Yellow Belly lakes had the highest phytoplankton abundance; Redfish and Pettit lakes had the lowest.
- Diatoms, chlorophytes, and dinoflagellates made up most phytoplankton biovolume in 1992 and 1993.

**Zooplankton**
- Crustacean zooplankton biomass varied tenfold among lakes, with mean summer epilimnetic values from 4 µg/l to 50 µg/l. This zooplankton is the primary food of juvenile sockeye.
- Dominant taxa were *Daphnia rosea*, *Bosmina longirostris*, and *Holopedium gibberum*.

**Fish community**
- Besides kokanee and sockeye, pelagic species include redside shiner, northern squawfish, bull trout, and brook trout.
- Lake trout occur in Stanley Lake, and cutthroat trout occur in Yellow Belly Lake. Rainbow trout are stocked annually into Redfish, Alturas, Pettit, and Stanley lakes.
- Piscivores in Stanley and Yellow Belly lakes reduced their appeal as stocking sites.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth potential differs among lakes.**
- A bioenergetics and foraging model, validated against age-0 kokanee trawl data from Redfish, Alturas, and Stanley lakes, predicted much higher sockeye growth in Stanley, Pettit, and Yellow Belly lakes than in Redfish and Alturas lakes.
- Alturas Lake was judged a poor candidate because of its low simulated growth. Its high kokanee density in 1993 appeared to exceed carrying capacity, with crustacean zooplankton populations having collapsed.
- Managers chose [[Pettit Lake]] for 1995 stocking. Redfish Lake remained a candidate because it had recently produced sockeye that returned from the sea, and its large size is an advantage for producing smolts.

**2. Density dependence limits stocking.**
- In carrying-capacity simulations, stocking 200,000 juveniles (325/ha) into Redfish Lake reduced individual growth by 30% relative to baseline (5/ha).
- In Pettit Lake, a similar density reduced growth by 38%, because lower nutrient levels mean lower primary and secondary production.
- Stocking 300/ha into Redfish and Pettit lakes would reduce growth to 70% of values currently observed in Redfish Lake.
- Simulated maximum stocking rates were 200 to 300 juveniles per hectare, which is two to three times lower than rates from empirical approaches used in Alaska and British Columbia. The difference is attributed to the larger smolt size in Redfish Lake (about 10 g, compared with 2 to 5 g elsewhere) and faster growth there.

**3. Lake fertilization raises production.**
- A 1994 in situ experiment used six limnocorrals (5 m diameter, 17 m deep) in Redfish Lake. Three were fertilized with ammonium phosphate and ammonium nitrate every 10 days from 13 June to 11 September, adding 5 µg/l P and 150 µg/l N. The fertilized corrals ended 66% and 260% above ambient levels for P and N, respectively.
- Fertilized treatments showed 160% of control chlorophyll, a 222% increase in zooplankton biomass, and a 20% increase in kokanee growth rate.
- Whole-lake simulations indicated that fertilization would allow an additional 180,000 juveniles into Redfish Lake while maintaining growth rates similar to 1992 to 1994 conditions. The model underestimated zooplankton and fish growth and overestimated chlorophyll, but broadly matched experimental trophic transfer.

**4. Kokanee reduction is needed to benefit sockeye.**
- Density-dependent effects on kokanee growth in both simulations and cage experiments led the authors to conclude that Redfish Lake kokanee were near carrying capacity (about 360 individuals per hectare, per Teuscher and Taki 1994).
- A one-year 50% reduction in age-0 kokanee had only a small effect on individual sockeye growth. Fertilization alone had a much larger effect, and the two measures were additive.
- Combined fertilization and kokanee reduction could support an additional 210,000 juvenile sockeye while maintaining growth rates similar to 1992 to 1994.

**5. Life-history simulations favor kokanee under current conditions.**
- Under current mortality and growth rates, more than 98% of *O. nerka* production goes to kokanee. The authors describe this as an extinction pathway that has likely operated in the Redfish Lake sockeye population for decades. The high mortality of downstream migration outweighs the growth advantage of marine sockeye.
- Phosphorus loading was 715 kg/year, and the lake fertilization scenario added 357 kg/year. Most benefits of fertilization went to kokanee because they survive better and gain four years of benefit versus one year for sockeye.
- The sockeye population benefited from fertilization only if age-0 kokanee survival fell by 50% to 70%. A 70% reduction shifted about 60% of production to the sockeye life history.

**6. Marine nutrient subsidies are small.**
- Under current migration and marine survival rates (smolt-adult survivorship of 0.002), more nutrients leave the lake as smolts than return in carcasses.
- Even under pre-dam conditions, nutrients from carcasses likely contributed less than 20% of the nutrient budgets for Sawtooth Valley lakes.

**7. Management recommendations.**
- **Pettit Lake** is an excellent candidate for a new sockeye population because it has high ambient zooplankton and is furthest from kokanee carrying capacity.
- **Modest fertilization** can offset the negative density-dependent effects of additional stocking in Redfish Lake.
- **Kokanee reduction** should accompany fertilization in Redfish Lake, because high kokanee densities would otherwise benefit more from fertilization than a nascent sockeye population would.
- Broodstock offspring can establish or augment anadromous populations in Redfish and Pettit lakes. Until migratory losses are addressed, these measures may help maintain Snake River sockeye in these lakes.
