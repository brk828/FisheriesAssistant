# Fishes of the Phoenix Metropolitan Area

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Marsh and Minckley describe the fish fauna and aquatic habitats of the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]] in the [[Salt River]] Valley of central Arizona. The fauna is dominated by introduced species, and the document reports that most of the original native fishes have disappeared. The authors attribute this to a canal-based irrigation system, urban pond development, and the establishment of non-native fishes. The text is an OCR scan and some passages, including the species checklist, are partly illegible.

## Background & Description

- **Historic river:** Before it was dammed, the Salt River flowed through a well-defined channel bordered by cottonwood, willow and mesquite forests. It was large enough to require a ferry crossing at Tempe. The channel is now dry except during floods, though standing water remains in deeper gravel pits and in some channel depressions.
- **Modern lentic habitats:** Numerous ponds and lakes built in parks and residential developments hold game fishes, often stocked on a [[Put-and-Take Fishing]] basis (Okamoto 1980). These ponds also contain species that reached them through connecting canals.
- **Planned changes:** Proposed additional ponds and lakes, and changes in the Salt River channel, were expected to increase the surface water available for recreational and aesthetic use (Anonymous 1979).
- **Canal system:** The [[Salt River Valley Water Users' Association]] system diverts the river at [[Granite Reef Dam]]. Water is conveyed throughout the Phoenix area, partly augmented by pumping. The canals are steep-sided, concrete-lined and of uniform depth, with riparian vegetation largely absent apart from [[Bermuda Grass]] on the banks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Primary producers and vegetation**
- Filamentous algae include *Cladophora glomerata*, which grows luxuriantly on solid surfaces, and *Nostoc* sp. Zygnematales (OCR "7vg?-matales") were also reported.
- Macrophytes establish in suitable substrate and slow current. Common species are *Potamogeton pectinatus*, *Potamogeton crispus*, *Zannichellia palustris*, *Myriophyllum spicatum* and *Myriophyllum brasiliense*. *Typha domingensis* is rare.

**Benthic invertebrates**
- Burrowing invertebrates are dominated by the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*) and the introduced [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*). A diversity of presumably native oligochaetes is also present.

**Fish assemblage**
- Many introduced fishes are present, mostly as bait or sport species. Their occurrence may reflect introductions rather than continuous occupation of these aquatic habitats.
- Established non-native species include carp, goldfish and golden shiner, which are considered potential problems. Other introduced fishes may also become problems, and recreational fisheries may depend on them.
- Diversity among introduced kinds is poor, and a large pool of potential species is available. The introduced species make predictable outcomes difficult.
- Native fishes have largely disappeared from the Phoenix system. The reported figure is nine of the original 15 native species (the OCR is partly garbled here). Several remaining native fishes are listed as of special concern by federal and/or state agencies. The [[Razorback Sucker]] is cited in the references.

**Human and historical context**
- Fish remains from archaeological sites in the Colorado River basin and [[Prehistoric Irrigation]] systems in Arizona show that fishes were used historically.
- The authors note that the use of introduced fishes for [[Mosquito Control]] has a long history in the western United States.

## Key Findings

1. The Salt River has been replaced by a managed canal and reservoir system. Riparian vegetation is largely absent from the canals, and the channel is dry except in floods.
2. Urban ponds and lakes are the main recreational fisheries. Many of these are stocked game-fish ponds, and fish can colonize them through connecting canals.
3. The aquatic community is dominated by introduced organisms: the Asiatic clam, the red swamp crayfish, and non-native fishes including carp, goldfish and golden shiner.
4. The assemblage is shaped by introduction history, not by continuous native occupation. The authors treat several introduced fishes as potential problems for the system.
5. Native fishes have declined sharply, with about nine of 15 original species reported as gone. Several remaining natives are of conservation concern.
6. Future changes to ponds and the Salt River channel are expected to increase surface water for recreation and aesthetics.
