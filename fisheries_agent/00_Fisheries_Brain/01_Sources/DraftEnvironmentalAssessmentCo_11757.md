# Draft Environmental Assessment: Construction of Fish Barriers on Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Environmental Assessment (EA), prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (lead federal agency, with the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] as cooperators), evaluates building two reinforced concrete drop structures near the lower end of [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona. The barriers are a reasonable and prudent alternative in the [[Central Arizona Project]] biological opinion (April 15, 1994). Their purpose is to block upstream invasion of nonnative fishes from the [[San Pedro River]] into reaches occupied by threatened native fishes, including [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. The EA concludes that permanent habitat loss is small (under 2 acres terrestrial, 0.3 acre aquatic), that temporary impacts are mostly sedimentation-related and recoverable, and that benefits to native fishes outweigh the genetic-isolation and drift losses.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory context.** Congress authorized the [[Central Arizona Project]] under PL 90-537 (1968); the CAP was declared complete in 1993. Concern that CAP canals could spread nonnative fishes led [[Reclamation]] to request formal consultation under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1991. The resulting opinion found that CAP operation would jeopardize several native fishes and adversely modify critical habitat. Aravaipa Creek's protected status includes the Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area and [[The Nature Conservancy]] reserves. Critical habitat for spikedace and loach minnow designated in 1994 was revoked on March 25, 1998.

**Proposed design.** Each barrier consists of:
- A 4-foot high reinforced concrete wall tied into rock abutments
- A concrete apron about 25 feet downstream
- An upstream key (estimated 15 to 20 feet deep) and a downstream key (estimated 12 feet deep)

The lower barrier includes a diversion feature requested by San Carlos Apache allottees. The second barrier sits about 800 feet upstream of the first, forming a [[Fish Management Zone]] for monitoring and removal of nonnatives. Construction is expected to take about 4 months and use roughly 1,500 cubic yards of concrete per barrier. The design life is 100 years.

**Alternatives.** The no-action alternative would leave nonnative access unimpeded. Of eight candidate sites examined between the wilderness boundary and the San Pedro confluence, sites E and F were dropped for lying within critical habitat, sites upstream of B2 for excessive road work, and sites B1 and B2 because landowners refused rights-of-entry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Aravaipa Creek flows are episodic. Monthly mean discharge at the project area ranges from 12 cfs (June) to 67 cfs (February) over 64 years of record. The record flood was 70,800 cfs (October 1, 1983), and the 1993 flood peaked at 13,000 cfs. Stream gradient in the project area is 31.7 feet/mile (0.6 percent). Modeled backwater effects extend upstream a few hundred feet, and [[Aravaipa Road]] would be affected at about a 4-year flood (versus 12-year now).

**Sediment and habitat.** The barriers act as sediment traps. Aggradation is projected to reach about 1,200 feet upstream, covering 7.5 acres with roughly 15,000 cubic yards of sediment. Lower gradient may reduce steep riffles and rapids. Abutments are soft [[Mudstone Conglomerate]], requiring armoring.

**Vegetation and wildlife.** Four community types were mapped in June 1998: [[Sonoran Riparian Deciduous Woodland]], [[Sonoran Riparian Scrubland]], [[Sonoran Interior Strand]], and [[Paloverde-Cacti Mixed Scrub]]. Seepwillow/burrobrush dominates the impact footprint. Riparian habitat is described as supporting 60 to 75 percent of Arizona's resident wildlife while covering less than 0.5 percent of the state. Special status species in the project area include [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]], [[Mexican Garter Snake]], and [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]].

**Cultural and social.** A Class III survey covered about 5 acres and found one tentatively prehistoric agricultural site. The area was confirmed as a [[Traditional Cultural Property]] by San Carlos Apache elders. The project area lies on Indian Trust Allotment 013736, and the population is 41 percent minority in Pinal County.

## Key Findings

- **Fish community:** Aravaipa Creek supports seven native species, described as one of the largest and most intact native fish communities in any Arizona stream. Introduced species include [[Green Sunfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Common Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Red Shiner]].
- **Red shiner threat:** Red shiner first appeared in 1990, disappeared after a flood exceeding 3000 cfs in 1991, and reappeared in 1997. It is a documented negative factor for spikedace.
- **Abundance of target species:** In the lower reach (1992-1994 surveys), median relative abundances of spikedace, loach minnow, and roundtail chub did not exceed 1 percent. Long-term data (1963-1997) show spikedace exceeded 4.5-5.0 percent in 12 of 32 years (37.5 percent), and loach minnow in 4 of 32 (12.5 percent).
- **Permanent impacts:** Less than 2 acres of terrestrial habitat (1.51 acres seepwillow/burrobrush) and 0.3 acre of aquatic habitat are permanently lost.
- **Temporary impacts:** About 9 acres of terrestrial habitat and 2.06 acres of aquatic habitat are temporarily affected, mainly by sedimentation.
- **Genetic and drift effects:** The EA judges genetic isolation of upstream populations and drift losses to be of no significance, given the small reach lost and limited larval drift.
- **Nonnative control:** If nonnatives enter the management zone, fish removal by nets or electrofishing is preferred. Chemical renovation with [[Fintrol]] (antimycin A) is the fallback. The toxin degrades within about 1/4 mile and is neutralized below the lower barrier with potassium permanganate.
- **Mitigation:** Reclamation proposes either acquiring 5 acres of riparian habitat on the San Pedro River or Aravaipa Creek, or acquiring a conservation easement. Mitigation for seepwillow/burrobrush uses a 1:1.7 ratio (7.63 acres affected); riparian mitigation uses a 5:1 ratio.
- **Monitoring:** A scientific monitoring program (including larval drift and emigration studies) is planned before construction.
- **Land and water:** Less than 3 acres (1.9 percent) of the allotment would flood through a 100-year event. Diversion provisions are included for tribal water rights.
- **Structural failure:** A "clear day" breach is estimated at about 1,100 cfs initial surge, decreasing over about 20 minutes, with attenuation within a mile.
- **Effect on fishing:** The barriers would eliminate potential sport or subsistence fishing above the lower barrier, which the EA notes does not currently support a quality sport fishery.
- **Compliance:** Required permits include a Section 404 CWA permit, Section 401 certification, an NPDES construction permit (disturbance over 5 acres), and NHPA Section 106 consultation.
