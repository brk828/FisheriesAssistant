# Proceedings of the Symposium on Fishes of the Upper Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

These proceedings (a 1981 American Fisheries Society symposium, co-sponsored by the [[Western Division American Fisheries Society]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region VI and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]]) assemble papers on the rare native fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The volume reviews the basin's hydrology and fish fauna, documents current distribution and abundance, and evaluates impacts from energy development, water depletion and non-native species. It presents life-history and recovery prospects for the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and discusses legal, political and mitigation frameworks under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The overall tone is pessimistic: speakers expressed little optimism for the four large-river endemics, and the editors emphasized that water supply, not biology alone, will decide their future.

## Background & Description

The symposium was organized by a committee chaired by [[Clarence A. Carlson]] and aimed to (1) present basic information on these fishes, (2) identify critical recovery needs, (3) emphasize life history and status, and (4) consider impacts of past and future human actions. Its focus was research conducted after 1975 on intermediate and large-river zones.

The Colorado River drains about 283,600 km² of the Upper Basin, divided into the Green, Upper Main-stem Colorado (Grand) and San Juan sub-basins. The basin has no broad connections with neighboring drainages since the Pliocene, which produced high endemism: [[Miller]] (1959) ranked the system second of seven endemism centers, with 87% endemism among primary freshwater fishes.

Key papers include:
- Shields (political and resource considerations; [[Conservation Plan]] concept)
- Tyus et al. (distribution, abundance and status of fishes)
- Jacobsen (new human impacts; energy and water development)
- Prewitt and Stalnaker (mitigation feasibility using flow-regime modeling)
- Lambertson (mitigation and Section 7 of the ESA)
- Harris et al. (water supply, legal constraints and political realities)
- Behnke (realities and illusions of species preservation)
- Miller (Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team)
- Holden and Wick, Valdez and Clemmer, Wick et al. (life histories of the four endemics)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream zonation.** [[Joseph et al. 1977]] recognized three zones: a cold, clear headwater zone (salmonid habitat); an intermediate zone with warming, turbid water and cyprinid/catostomid dominance; and a warm, turbid large-river zone whose energy base is largely allochthonous, with primary production virtually absent.

**Hydrology.** Lee Ferry, Arizona, divides the upper and lower basins. Virgin flow at Lee Ferry averaged about 18.3 billion m³ (1896–1980), and roughly 25% of natural flow was in use under [[Colorado River Storage Project]] development. Reservoirs such as Lake Powell, Navajo, Fontenelle, Flaming Gorge, Blue Mesa and Morrow Point trap about 75–80% of sediment that once reached Lake Mead.

**Water quality.** Increasing salinity (total dissolved solids) is the most serious general problem. The United States agreed in 1974 to deliver water to Mexico with salinity increases limited to 115 (±30) mg/L.

**Impoundment effects.** Flaming Gorge tailwaters became cold, halting native spawning on the Green River. Rotenone treatment before Flaming Gorge closure (1962) devastated the invertebrate and fish communities.

**Non-native fishes.** Of 55 fishes recorded in the Upper Basin, 42 are exotic, 13 native, 8 endemic, 1 threatened and 5 endangered (Tyus et al.). Only three exotics (common carp, red shiner, channel catfish) are abundant. Reservoirs such as Lake Powell support largemouth bass, striped bass and threadfin shad introduced for forage.

**Water demand.** Shields' catalog listed more than 226 projects able to deplete up to 2.9 million acre-feet annually; Jacobsen cites 3,963,000 acre-feet already depleted by 1980. Projected depletions reach 4.9 million acre-feet by 1990 and 5.4 million by 2000 (Table 1, Jacobsen).

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna and status**
- Tyus et al. report 55 fishes (52 species, 3 subspecies) in the Upper Basin; 49 are primarily riverine and 42 occur in reservoirs.
- Four threatened or endangered large-river endemics remain as remnant populations in mainstem rivers: Colorado squawfish, bonytail chub, humpback chub and razorback sucker. Two headwater taxa, the Kendall Warm Springs dace (endangered) and Colorado River cutthroat trout (threatened), are also listed.
- Federal listing: Colorado squawfish and humpback chub threatened in 1967 (later endangered); bonytail chub endangered on 23 April 1980; razorback sucker never federally listed, though Tyus et al. recommend protection as threatened or endangered.
- Tyus et al. conclude that endangered species are still declining with no sign of increase in known subpopulations.

**Species-specific findings**
- *Colorado squawfish*: reaches 1.5–2.0 m and 35–45 kg historically; matures at 5–7 years; reproduction driven by prespawning temperatures (about 20 °C) and spring flows. Populations remain relatively large only in the Green River System. A first spawning ground was discovered in lower Yampa Canyon in 1981, linking decline to blocked spawning migrations.
- *Humpback chub*: few individuals found in the Green River above Desolation Canyon in two years of sampling; new concentrations found in Westwater and Cataract canyons.
- *Bonytail chub*: now the rarest endemic in the Upper Basin; only occasional collections, including adults from Gray Canyon. Its recovery plan aims to prevent extinction rather than achieve recovery.
- *Razorback sucker*: adults hard to capture except during spring spawning; young seldom found, suggesting recruitment failure, possibly from predation by exotics or competition.

**Causes of decline**
- Dam construction and altered flows and temperatures, especially Colorado River Storage Project dams in the 1960s; non-native competition and predation; reduced spring flows; and habitat loss from channel modification, dredging and mining (Holden and Wick; Jacobsen).

**Conservation and mitigation**
- The [[Conservation Plan]] proposed by the Fish and Wildlife Service would fund recovery from developers in proportion to water depleted. Assessments included $550,000 for Windy Gap, $500,000 for Moon Lake and up to $180,000 for Cheyenne Water Project Phase II.
- Lambertson states that traditional mitigation is not permitted for listed species under Section 7 of the ESA, which requires prevention of jeopardy, not merely reduction of impacts.
- Prewitt and Stalnaker propose a phased, model-based approach (PHABSIM habitat simulation, macro water-budget and temperature models) to set flow regimes and evaluate trade-offs.
- Behnke argues the fishes' long-term survival requires an independent agency funded by a tax on environmentally altering benefits.

**Water supply and future outlook**
- Harris et al. project that the average annual flow at Lee Ferry will fall from 10.0 MAF (1990) to 8.2 MAF (2030), and that the Duchesne River could lose 70% of its volume by 2030.
- Holden and Wick judge biological prospects for the Colorado squawfish optimistic where habitat is adequate, but flow protection is "meager" and no longer being attempted by federal agencies.
- Symposium speakers were pessimistic overall; Behnke states, "At this time, I really don't know" whether the endangered species can be maintained in their present environment.

**Recovery planning**
- The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] (formed 1975) recommends self-sustaining populations, habitat protection, captive gene pools, reintroduction and public education. The humpback chub plan targets five self-sustaining populations by 1990.
