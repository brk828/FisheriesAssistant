# Tectonic Speleogenesis of Devils Hole, Nevada, and Implications for Hydrogeology and Long Paleoenvironmental Records

#ichthyology #marine-biology #N/A

## Executive Summary

This paper by Riggs, Carr, Kolesar and Hoffman (1994) argues that [[Devils Hole]], a deep, steeply dipping, fault-controlled fissure in Cambrian limestone and dolostone in southern Nevada, formed by [[Extensional Tectonism]] rather than [[Karst Processes]]. The authors base this on a continuous coat of dense [[Mammillary Calcite]] more than 30 cm thick, the absence of dissolutional morphologies, and the orientation of the opening relative to the regional northwest-southeast minimum principal stress. They further propose that extensional fracture networks can carry groundwater as effectively as karst conduits, maintain transmissivity while minerals precipitate, and accumulate exceptionally long, continuous paleoenvironmental records of the kind preserved in Devils Hole.

## Background & Description

Devils Hole is a roughly rectangular surface collapse measuring 23 by 7 m (Carr, 1988). It sits at the base of a steep carbonate ridge on the eastern edge of the [[Amargosa Desert]], one of the driest areas in the United States (75–100 mm/yr precipitation, pan evaporation about 2500 mm/yr, mean annual air temperature about 18.5°C). The water table lies about 15 m below land surface. Below it, the fissure dips 70° to 85° southeast, averages less than 2 m wide, is more than 100 m long at 70 m depth, and extends more than 150 m below land surface.

Devils Hole is a skylight into a branch of the [[Ash Meadows Flow System]], a regional, predominantly carbonate-rock groundwater system whose primary discharge is the 16-km-long Ash Meadows spring line. Spring discharge is a constant two-thirds m³/s. Recharge comes mainly from the [[Spring Mountains]] and high ranges of east-central Nevada more than 400 km away. Water entering Devils Hole is at 34°C, pH 7.4, about 400 ppm total dissolved solids, with a calcite saturation index of 0.16 to 0.18 at 25 to 55 m depth.

Regional history includes Paleozoic continental shelf/slope carbonate deposition (up to 12,000 m thick originally), Late Mesozoic to early Tertiary compression and thrusting, northwest-southeast strike-slip faulting (offsets up to 50 km), and mid-Tertiary east-west extension forming the [[Basin and Range]] province. Since about 10–5 million years ago, extension has oriented northwest-southeast.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is a geological and hydrogeological study with no biological survey, so its ecological content is limited. Notable environmental points include:

- Warm, moist air exhaled from some gaping fractures on Devils Hole ridge supports a small patch of green moss, contrasting with the surrounding desert (Fig. 8).
- The pool and nearby passages are part of a groundwater system with stable chemistry, which the authors cite as evidence that the water has been physicochemically stable for hundreds of thousands of years.
- Historical pumping for irrigation is noted as affecting water levels, and Dudley and Larson (1976) is cited in relation to desert pupfish habitats at Ash Meadows. The document does not analyze the fish themselves.

Local structure consists of Cambrian [[Bonanza King Formation]] limestone and dolostone (Papoose Lake Member about 300 m thick; Banded Mountain Member about 600 m thick). The [[Spotted Range-Mine Mountain Structural Zone]] is a northeast-striking zone of Quaternary faults, fractures, and persistent low-magnitude seismicity. Devils Hole lies just outside it but is developed on a northeast-striking fault.

## Key Findings

1. **Non-karst origin.** Devils Hole is not a karst feature. Evidence includes its near-vertical planar form cross-cutting bedding, the lack of differential solution in strata, the absence of speleothems deposited subaerially by percolating water, and a long history of calcite-supersaturated groundwater.

2. **Long-term calcite supersaturation.** Uranium-series dates from mammillary calcite 30 m below the water table show continuous supersaturation from 566,000 ± 20,000 to 60,300 ± 1,700 yr (Ludwig et al., 1992). A groundwater-deposited calcite vein about 175 m away was dated at 660,000 ± 75,000 to 890,000 ± 92,000 yr. Devils Hole may have formed after calcite-supersaturated waters were already circulating.

3. **Deposition and plugging constraints.** The average mammillary calcite deposition rate (0.72 mm/1000 yr from a 36-cm core) implies that passages under 72 cm would plug within half a million years. Devils Hole has not plugged, and the authors estimate it has opened at roughly 2–3 times the 1.4 mm/1000 yr average infilling rate.

4. **Tectonic evidence.** Supporting points include:
   - Alignment with the northeast-striking Spotted Range-Mine Mountain zone and northwest-southeast extension.
   - Single-plane development that cross-cuts joints, bedding and northwest-striking faults.
   - A 15-cm-wide pull-apart crack in the floor of [[Brown's Room]], opened in the past 20,000 yr, that could only open by spreading of the room.
   - Hundreds of mammillary-calcite-lined fissures in Pliocene fanglomerate near [[Furnace Creek Wash]], interpreted as an ancient Devils Hole homologue with no dissolution of carbonate clasts.

5. **Fault geometry.** The Devils Hole opening is controlled by the wedge between faults A–B and C. Vertical displacement is only about 0.6 m, but the fractures are gaping. Large unsupported blocks have slid into the opening as the walls separated.

6. **Collapse distribution.** Carr (1988) noted 11 other collapses within about 1.5 km of Devils Hole. Nearly all are associated with small northeast-striking faults or fractures. One collapse about 180 m above the regional water table, on Point of Rocks ridge, exhales warm moist air, indicating an open connection with the water table.

7. **Water-table record.** Devils Hole's water level has stayed within a quarter-meter range during historical time, apart from irrigation pumping and earthquake effects. Szabo et al. (1994) show a 9-m water-table range over the past 115,000 yr, including a major drop in the last 20,000 yr.

8. **Extensional flow systems.** Extension can form large-scale, integrated conduit networks that differ from karst systems: they form independently of climate, can develop in insoluble rock, have breakthrough times that do not lengthen with conduit length, can precipitate minerals contemporaneously with opening, and keep conduits open against plugging as long as they continue to spread.

9. **Paleoclimate potential.** The Ash Meadows system is probably more than 400 km long, drains an area probably much greater than 10,000 km², and has kept calcite-supersaturated discharge for more than four glacial-interglacial cycles (Winograd et al., 1992). Its precipitates hold a half-million-year proxy climate record (see [[Devils Hole Paleoclimate Record]]), along with records of water chemistry, water-level fluctuations, atmospheric circulation, precipitation and extension rates.

10. **Broader implications.** Similar mineral deposits in other extending terrains could yield long, well-dated continental paleoclimate records. The authors frame their conclusions as "hardly conclusive" but consistent with extension driving large-scale transmissivity in brittle, otherwise impermeable rock.
