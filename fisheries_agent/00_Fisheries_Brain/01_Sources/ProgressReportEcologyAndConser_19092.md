# 1991 progress report--ecology and conservation biology of humpback chub (Gila cypha) in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #ConservationBiology

## Executive Summary

This 1991 progress report from Arizona State University (Co-Investigators Michael E. Douglas and Paul C. Marsh) documents a tag/recapture study of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and associated native and non-native fish fauna in the Colorado River system near the confluence with the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), Grand Canyon. Ten field trips were conducted over 1991, spanning reconnaissance, base-camp establishment/weir construction, and seven dedicated data-collection trips across three sampling reaches: **Confluence**, **Powell**, and **Salt**. A total of 10,144 fish were captured, of which 9,977 (98.4%) were native species, underscoring the LCR confluence area's importance as a stronghold for native Colorado River fishes. The study employed PIT tagging alongside legacy Carlin/Floy tags to assess population dynamics and site fidelity of [[Humpback Chub]] and native sucker species.

## Background & Description

Fish were classified into two size classes for population structure analysis:
- **<150 mm TL** (sub-adult/juvenile size class)
- **≥150 mm TL** (adult size class)

Size-class breakdowns for [[Humpback Chub]] revealed reach-specific differences in population structure:
- **Confluence reach**: skewed toward smaller fish (897 <150mm vs. 468 ≥150mm), suggesting a nursery/recruitment function.
- **Powell reach**: also juvenile-dominated (2,681 <150mm vs. 1,012 ≥150mm).
- **Salt reach**: skewed toward larger, adult fish (1,207 <150mm vs. 2,167 ≥150mm), suggesting this reach may serve as adult habitat.

Similar size-class patterns were recorded for [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]], with juveniles predominating at Confluence and adults more common in deeper downstream reaches.

No morphometric (length/weight regression) or meristic data were reported in this progress letter; the focus was purely on abundance and mark-recapture metrics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study reaches represent distinct habitat types along the Colorado River/LCR confluence zone:

- **Confluence**: The LCR–Colorado River confluence, historically recognized as critical spawning and rearing habitat for [[Humpback Chub]], evidenced by high proportions of small (<150mm) individuals.
- **Powell**: A mainstem reach showing similarly juvenile-dominated chub demographics, potentially a secondary nursery/aggregation area.
- **Salt**: Reach dominated by adult chub, indicative of feeding/refuge habitat for mature fish.

**Native fish assemblage** (9,977 individuals; 98.4% of total catch) was dominated by:
- [[Humpback Chub]] (n=8,432; 84.5% of native catch)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (n=845)
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (n=396)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (n=303)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (n=1, indicating extreme rarity even in 1991)

**Non-native fish assemblage** (167 individuals; 1.6% of total catch) reflects the presence of introduced competitors/predators:
- [[Channel Catfish]] (n=85) — dominant non-native, potential predator of juvenile natives
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (n=40) — coldwater species linked to Glen Canyon Dam tailwater releases
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (n=20)
- [[Common Carp]] (n=14)
- [[Black Bullhead]] (n=3)
- [[Brown Trout]] (n=3)
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] (n=1)
- [[Green Sunfish]] (n=1)

The low non-native proportion (1.6%) in 1991 contrasts with later decades of the [[Colorado River]] ecosystem, where non-native fish invasion (especially [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] from cold, clear post-dam tailwaters) intensified substantially — making this dataset a valuable historical baseline.

## Key Findings

1. **Total catch**: 10,144 fish captured across 10 trips in 1991; natives vastly outnumbered non-natives (9,977 vs. 167).

2. **Species dominance**: [[Humpback Chub]] was overwhelmingly the most-captured species (8,432 individuals, 83% of total catch), confirming the Confluence/Powell/Salt reaches as critical habitat.

3. **Reach-specific demographics**: Juvenile chub concentrated at Confluence and Powell reaches; adult chub concentrated at Salt reach — suggesting ontogenetic habitat shift or differential reach use by life stage.

4. **Tag/recapture program**: A combination of new PIT tags, previously implanted PIT tags ("OLD"), and legacy Carlin/Floy tags ("V-OLD") were used to track individuals across trips:
   - Confluence: 274 new PIT tags issued, 160 PIT recaptures, 16 Carlin/Floy recaptures.
   - Powell: 553 new PIT tags issued, 437 PIT recaptures, 18 Carlin/Floy recaptures.
   - Salt: 1,304 new PIT tags issued, 824 PIT recaptures, 9 Carlin/Floy recaptures.
   - Reported overall recapture rate for chub: **27.25%** for new PIT tags; **~3.8%** for old tags (consistent with high recapture success typical of localized, non-migratory chub aggregations).

5. **Mortality**: Only 7 chub mortalities occurred during the entire 1991 study — 4 attributed to early use of 2.5 cm mesh trammel nets (subsequently replaced with less-lethal 3.8 cm mesh, eliminating further gear-related mortality), and 3 attributed to nets being buried under 18 cm of silt during a high-water event in December. This reflects a generally low-impact sampling protocol.

6. **Razorback Sucker rarity**: Only a single [[Razorback Sucker]] was captured across the entire study (Salt reach), foreshadowing this species' subsequent extirpation from the mainstem Grand Canyon population.

7. **Baseline value**: This dataset represents an important early (1991) baseline for long-term monitoring of [[Humpback Chub]] population dynamics in Grand Canyon, later used for comparison against post-Glen Canyon Dam operational changes and non-native fish control programs.
