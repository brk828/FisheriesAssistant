# Accumulation of Radioactive Materials by Fishery Organisms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bioaccumulation #radioecology

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Walter A. Chipman]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Radiobiological Investigations examines how marine fish and shellfish take up radioactive materials when [[Fission Products]] and other radioisotopes are present in sea water. Drawing mainly on laboratory work at the [[Beaufort Laboratory]] in North Carolina, the paper concludes that uptake depends strongly on the chemical form of the radionuclide (particulate or dissolved), the organism's feeding mode, and the target tissue. Strontium is concentrated mainly in shells and bone, cesium in edible muscle, and trace-metal isotopes such as [[Zinc-65]] and [[Cobalt-57]] are rapidly concentrated throughout the food web. The author argues that these pathways could affect the safety and availability of seafood.

## Background & Description

Expanding atomic energy use raised concern that radioactive contaminants released to the atmosphere, soil, and water could enter the food chain and build up in organisms, including humans. Isotopes of an element behave chemically alike, so a [[Radioactive Isotopes|radioisotope]] is incorporated into tissues as if it were a stable isotope of the same element (for example, carbon-14 among carbon-12 and carbon-13). Elements such as calcium and strontium concentrate in skeletal structures, while others accumulate in soft tissues and organs, and turnover rates vary among compounds.

Two source categories are identified:

- **Fission products** from uranium-235 fission, released mainly through weapons testing and, in small amounts, from reprocessing plant effluents.
- **Induced radionuclides** formed by neutron bombardment, including materials in reactor primary coolant water.

The paper notes that normal reactor operation releases only insignificant amounts, because primary coolant is held in a closed system and transferred via heat exchangers. Weapons testing in the Pacific Ocean added fission products to sea water. Notably, the largest contributions to fish radioactivity came from the non-fission product [[Zinc-65]], and to molluscs from [[Cobalt-60]] (Weiss et al., 1956).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fission product composition.** [[Table 1]] shows that between 20 days and about one year after fission, most biologically significant contributors are rare earth elements or chemically similar elements. [[Strontium-90]] and [[Cesium-137]] become especially important in aged mixtures.

**Physical state in sea water.** Fission products of about one year of age are mostly particulate, with only strontium and cesium isotopes remaining as important dissolved species. Particulate forms are associated with [[Phytoplankton]] and other particles, and many radionuclides adsorb strongly to the surfaces of living and non-living particles, which are abundant in inshore waters. Ruthenium behavior was uncertain, with [[Ruthenium-106]] precipitating at sea water pH.

**Plankton as primary concentrators.** Photosynthetic single-celled algae (phytoplankton) take up elements directly from water, and their large surface area promotes adsorption. In laboratory cultures, algae rapidly took up nearly all radioactivity from added fission products. [[Cerium-144]] was concentrated thousands of times above sea water levels; [[Yttrium]] isotopes were also strongly concentrated, and accumulation was largely related to surface area. Algae concentrated [[Cesium-137]] poorly, and many species took up only small amounts of [[Strontium-90]].

**Zooplankton and filter feeders.** [[Copepods]] and other filter-feeding zooplankton become radioactive by filtering particles. The copepod *Tigropus californicus* removed nearly all available cerium from water, and [[Brine Shrimp]] (*Artemia salina*) feeding on ruthenium-labeled algae became radioactive. Much of this accumulation is particulate, poorly absorbed, and lost quickly once particles clear from the digestive tract.

**Shellfish.** [[Oysters]], [[Hard Clam|clams]], and [[Bay Scallop|scallops]] filter large water volumes. Strontium is concentrated in shells, not soft tissue, and shells placed as [[Oyster Cultch]] can retain radioactivity. Cesium accumulates in soft tissues, reaching highest levels in muscle over time. Crustaceans such as [[Blue Crab]] and [[Shrimp]] accumulate strontium in shells, which is lost at molting, and cesium in edible meat.

**Fish.** Fish absorb only small amounts of most fission products from the gut. Strontium is concentrated in bone and scales, cesium in edible flesh, and rare earths in internal organs, especially the liver.

**Trace metals.** Zinc and cobalt isotopes are quickly taken up and passed through the food web. Pollution of estuaries or coastal waters with these isotopes could create hazards for seafood utilization.

## Key Findings

- **Particulate dominance.** Most radioactivity from one-year-old fission product mixtures is particulate; only strontium and cesium isotopes remain significantly dissolved ([[Table 2]]).
- **Phytoplankton concentration.** [[Cerium-144]] concentration factors in planktonic algae ranged from 314 to 2,610 after 0.5 hours and from 1,970 to 4,498 after 24 hours (Table 3). [[Zinc-65]] was concentrated by [[Nitzschia]] many thousands of times over sea water.
- **Cesium in algae.** Marine phytoplankton concentrated [[Cesium-137]] poorly (Table 4, Boroughs, Chipman, and Rice, 1957).
- **Copepod uptake.** *Tigropus californicus* retained about 100% of the cerium at start, falling to about 227 c.p.m. per copepod after one day and 123 c.p.m. after two days (Table 5, average of 22 individuals). Strontium concentration factor was about one; *Artemia* was below one (Figure 1).
- **Strontium in mollusks.** Hard clam meats showed a concentration factor of less than one, while shells accumulated strontium (Figure 2). Shell uptake from water continued beyond formation of new shell.
- **Scallop cesium.** In ten days, the adductor muscle of *Pecten irradians* reached ten times water concentration and was still rising (Figure 3). Scallops are considered the most hazardous shellfish for cesium ingestion because only the adductor muscle is eaten. Clams accumulated more cesium than oysters.
- **Crab and shrimp.** Only small amounts of administered fission product radioactivity entered blue crab and shrimp tissues. Shrimp heading removes much of the radioactivity from the internal organs and shell, but cesium remains in the marketed meat.
- **Fish gut absorption.** Bluefish and croakers retained only a small fraction of oral doses in the body (Table 6). Croakers dosed with [[Cerium-144]] had about 3–4% of the dose in tissues, and [[Menhaden]] fed ruthenium-106 cells retained about 1%.
- **Bone concentration.** Bluefish four hours after dosing had about 5% of the oral dose in the body, with considerable activity in internal organs, bone, and scales (Table 8). Repeated doses produced marked strontium-90 concentration in bone and scales, while cesium-137 accumulated in muscle.
- **Strontium in muscle.** Striped mullet (*Mugil cephalus*) exposed to strontium-85 for thirty days reached a concentration factor of 4.6 over water, mostly in bone and scales. Killifish (*Fundulus heteroclitus*) body muscle reached only 0.15 after thirty-two days (Figure 5).
- **Cesium in fish flesh.** Killifish muscle cesium-137 concentration was about 35 times that of water after 72 days, with uptake still continuing (Figure 6).
- **Trace metal accumulation.** Radiozinc reached over 200 times water concentration in copepods within 48 hours, and radiocobalt about 50 times. Oysters, clams, and scallops concentrated zinc thousands of times over water. Fish took up zinc-65 rapidly, with high levels in kidneys and internal organs.
- **Conclusion.** The author concludes that accumulation by fish and shellfish depends on radionuclide chemistry and feeding route, and that cesium in edible muscle and trace metals in coastal pollution warrant attention for seafood safety.
