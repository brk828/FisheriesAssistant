# Glen Canyon Dam Flow Operations and the Colorado River Conservation Dispute

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This 1991 newspaper column by the Arizona Republic's Outdoor Editor (Burkhart), published 24 March 1991, describes a conflict over how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] releases water into the [[Colorado River]] below Lake Powell. On one side is the [[Western Area Power Authority]] (WAPA), which markets power from the dam. On the other is a coalition of conservation groups, river runners and anglers. The [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] controls the dam and is conducting the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]], whose draft [[Environmental Impact Statement]] is due in July of the following year. The article reports that [[American Rivers]] named the Colorado River in Arizona the country's most endangered river, and it discusses a proposal to designate the river as a federally protected [[Wild and Scenic River]]. The author also outlines the operating alternatives presented at public meetings in April, and the column ends with criticism of state agencies and the Arizona Legislature.

## Background & Description

The column is opinion and news reporting, not a research paper. It contains no original field data, sampling results or population estimates. Its aquatic content centres on:

- **Glen Canyon Dam**, which holds back [[Lake Powell]] and is a large power-generating facility.
- A **world-class trout fishery** in a 16-mile stretch of river below the dam, which, according to anglers, is harmed by current generation practices. The trout species is not named.
- **Endangered fish species** that conservationists fear may be harmed downstream. They are not named in the text.
- The **Grand Canyon** downstream, which conservation groups say is being damaged by current release practices.
- The **Verde River**, which American Rivers named among the 25 most endangered rivers in the country.

The author notes that electricity is a major reason for the dam's existence but says environmental concerns are not automatically subordinate to it. He writes that the dam was built to deliver water at proper times downstream and that, after that purpose, priorities are "pretty even."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and sediment.** Lake Powell traps all sediment coming down the Colorado River. The article notes that this makes sediment management a central issue. A proposed sediment slurry pipeline would carry sand from Lake Powell or Lake Mead to near Lees Ferry. Its supporters argue that it could stabilize beaches and shorelines in the Grand Canyon and might supply nutrients to Lake Mead. The cost is described as "monumental."

**Pre-dam flow mimicry.** One alternative would approximate pre-dam flow by releasing water at the rate it enters Lake Powell. This would probably mean low flows in winter and high flows in summer. Releases would not exceed 45,000 cubic feet per second unless floods required otherwise.

**Temperature and depth control.** In many alternatives the dam could be modified to draw water from different lake depths and so control the temperature of released water.

**Recreation and wildlife management.** The article raises concern that wildlife management could be compromised if federal Wild and Scenic designation is adopted without proper legislation. It cites an earlier case in which the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] blocked wildlife management on a river stretch it managed. The author lists possible effects on duck hunting at Lees Ferry, trout management and management of endangered species downstream.

**Proposed secondary dam.** One alternative includes a secondary dam near Lees Ferry. The author says this would disrupt the river entirely and would have astronomical costs.

## Key Findings

The column makes the following main points, plans and conclusions.

### 1. Competing interests and institutional roles
- WAPA's priority is economical power generation.
- The Bureau of Reclamation runs the dam and will determine, through the studies, whether the current operating method is best.
- Former Arizona governor [[Bruce Babbitt]] reportedly called the Bureau "a rogue elephant, a bunch of engineers."
- The Arizona Game and Fish Department is reluctant to comment publicly because it receives substantial funding from the Bureau for studies.

### 2. National conservation designations
- American Rivers called the Colorado River in Arizona the country's most endangered river. It cited the dam's "enormous damage to wildlife, habitat and recreation opportunities" in the canyon.
- Observers expected that an attempt to designate the Colorado River from Glen Canyon Dam as a [[National Wild and Scenic River]] would succeed in Congress.
- The author describes this designation as "a tremendous club" against WAPA. Conservationists, however, worry that it could make the river unmanageable for wildlife unless proper legislation is passed.

### 3. Public meetings and the EIS timeline
- Meetings on the alternatives were scheduled for April 1 in Salt Lake City, April 2 at Little America Inn, and April 4 at the YWCA Leadership Development Center in Phoenix. All began at 7 p.m.
- The draft Environmental Impact Statement was due July 2 of the following year.

### 4. Operating alternatives (summary)
The article lists 10 alternatives, which it groups under steady flows and fluctuating flows, with the remainder described as modifications to the dam's structure and operation. Those described in the text are:

- **Pre-dam mimicry** with sediment slurry pipeline (the first alternative in the group described).
- **Refined mimicry** with tighter flow limits and more constant flows.
- **Year-round steady flows**, which could cause problems for seasonal downstream water users.
- **Seasonally adjusted steady flows**, with four different steady flows by season. The author suggests this would probably suit downstream wildlife better.
- **Steady flows set by monthly goals.**
- **Low-fluctuation flows** allowing plus or minus 25 percent of the mean daily flow. Minimum daily releases would be 8,000 cubic feet per second from December through February and 5,000 cubic feet per second for the rest of the year. Sand pumping would also be used.
- **Moderate fluctuation**, allowing plus or minus 45 percent of the mean (figure as printed), with beach protection measures.
- **High fluctuation with a reregulation dam**, which would turn Lees Ferry into a small lake. It would keep a steady flow through the Grand Canyon while allowing drastic power-generation fluctuations at the dam. This alternative would include a one-time sand pump and a "monumental price tag," and it is controversial among anglers.
- **No action**, which the author says "would be catastrophic to recreation below the dam."

### 5. Legislative and political situation
- In Congress, a dispute over interim flows below Glen Canyon Dam was already under way, pending the studies and final decisions.
- The author criticises the Arizona Legislature for failing to pass a basic bill allowing some instream water rights for the environment. He says the Legislature seems more interested in serving its financial contributors.
- Other state agencies are focused mainly on power generation and water delivery to agriculture.

### 6. Author's conclusion
The author says Arizona will not necessarily do its best on this issue. He concludes that the state picture is unhappy, while praising the public's strong desire to enhance Arizona's environment.
