# Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

The document is an October 2000 update, published by the [[Colorado Water Congress]] and written by Upper Basin water users' representative [[Tom Pitts]], on the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]]. The Program was established by a Cooperative Agreement signed in January 1988 to recover four endangered fish species while allowing water development to proceed under state law, interstate compacts, Supreme Court decrees, and the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It serves as the mitigation mechanism for water project depletions. According to the text, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) has found more than 600 water projects, depleting about 1,700,000 acre-feet per year, to be in compliance with the ESA, and no lawsuit has challenged these findings.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Humpback Chub]] were listed as endangered when the ESA passed in 1973. The [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] were added later. In the late 1970s the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] began consulting on water project impacts. USFWS held that depletions anywhere in the Upper Basin could "jeopardize" the fish. A 1983 draft USFWS report suggested that the only way to avoid jeopardy was one-for-one replacement of depletions. In December 1983, water users asked the Colorado Water Congress to form a Special Project on Threatened and Endangered Species. Its objectives were to resolve ESA conflicts while respecting state water law, recognizing interstate compacts, and equitably distributing costs. Negotiations with USFWS, USBR, the three Upper Basin states, environmentalists, and [[Western Area Power Administration]] began in 1984.

In May 1985 the Special Project proposed recovering and delisting the fish, with recovery actions serving as ESA mitigation. The Cooperative Agreement was signed in January 1988. The governing committee and technical committees included federal agencies, the three states, environmentalists, and water users. The Colorado River Energy Distributors Association and the National Park Service became voting members in September 2000 (the text gives "a000," which is most likely a scan error for 2000).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

By 1989 little was known about the biology and habitat requirements of the fish, and their numbers had declined sharply over the decades. The Bonytail was virtually extinct in the Upper Basin, and the Razorback Sucker continued to decline. Monitoring showed that 90 to 99 percent of fish in any given river reach were nonnative. Forty-six nonnative species had been introduced, in addition to the 14 native species. These nonnative warm-water species compete with the endangered fish for habitat and prey on their young. Fish screens were judged necessary at several major diversions on the Colorado, Gunnison, and Green rivers to prevent entrainment of adult fish.

Habitat work centered on flow management. Colorado and Utah agreed to use state instream flow laws, and USFWS agreed to pursue flow protection under state law and interstate compacts. The main focus was the [[15-Mile Reach]] of the Colorado River between the Highline diversion and the Gunnison River, where several major irrigation diversions substantially affect flows. Floodplain restoration (buying or leasing bottomlands to flood in spring and removing dikes and levees) has opened several hundred acres.

## Key Findings

**Program accomplishments (mid-1990s to 2000)**
- About $11 million (the scan reads "$ltl million," interpreted here as $11 million) was spent on stocking facilities at [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] (Vernal, Utah), the Wahweep facility (southern Utah), and Grand Junction, Colorado.
- Razorback Sucker populations had declined to less than 5 percent of their historical level in the Upper Basin. Stocked fish are now appearing on Green River spawning bars.
- The Colorado Pikeminnow population in the Green River is estimated at 5,000 to 7,000 adults. The Colorado River population is estimated at about 800 adults and sub-adults. Both are approaching downlisting and delisting levels.
- Humpback Chub populations are stable in the Colorado and Green rivers and nearing downlisting levels, so no stocking is planned.
- Fish passages were installed at the Redlands diversion (Gunnison) and the Grand Valley Irrigation Company diversion (Colorado). Additional passages are planned, which will open more than 100 miles of habitat.
- Fish screens are planned for major diversions, including Hartland and Redlands (Gunnison), Grand Valley Irrigation Company and Government Highline (Colorado), and Tusher Wash (Green). The Recovery Program will bear the cost.

**Nonnative fish control**
- Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming restrict stocking of nonnative fish in streamside ponds unless the ponds are bermed and have screened outlets.
- Predator-laden ponds in the Grand Valley are being poisoned and restocked with non-competing trout. Screens were installed at the Highline Reservoir outlet, with a similar screen planned for Elkhead Reservoir.

**Flow enhancement**
- In 1999, 93,000 acre-feet of water was delivered voluntarily for flow enhancement without reducing project yields. Because of this success, participants including USFWS agreed to drop the instream flow filings on the Colorado and Yampa rivers.
- The Grand Valley Water Management Project will install check structures on the 30+ mile Grand Valley canal. It is expected to reduce demand by 30,000 acre-feet per year, with the water stored at Green Mountain Reservoir for fish releases.
- In the Yampa Basin, a plan under development calls for expanding Elkhead Reservoir. The Recovery Program would fund the portion for fish needs.

**Biological opinions**
- A December 1999 programmatic biological opinion covered all federal and non-federal depletions on the Colorado River above the Gunnison River, including more than one million acre-feet of existing depletions and 120,000 acre-feet of new depletions.
- The programmatic approach replaced extensive individual analyses with a set of letters and "recovery agreements." It is expected to save hundreds of thousands, possibly millions, of dollars in study costs.
- Programmatic opinions are under development for the Yampa (late 2000 or early 2001), the Gunnison (about 2002), and the remaining tributaries of the Green and Colorado rivers.

**Funding and outlook**
- Capital project funding authorized by 1999-2000 federal legislation totals $46 million in appropriated funds, $17 million from the four Upper Basin states, $17 million from power users, $15 million in in-kind contributions, and $5 million from the Colorado River Water Conservation District. The legislation also authorizes $6 million per year for operation, maintenance, and monitoring through 2010. The bill passed the House (H.R. 2348) in July 2000 and was expected to pass the Senate in September 2000.
- Construction is expected to be complete by 2005, with monitoring continuing afterward. Draft recovery goals were expected in the Federal Register in late 2000 and adoption by USFWS in early 2001.
- The author concludes that the Program provides more certainty for all parties than any realistic alternative, and that continued water user and political support is essential.
