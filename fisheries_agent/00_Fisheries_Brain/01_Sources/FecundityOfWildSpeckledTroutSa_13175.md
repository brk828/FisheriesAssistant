# Fecundity of Wild Speckled Trout (Salvelinus fontinalis) in Quebec Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study by [[Vladykov]] of the Quebec Department of Fisheries examines the fecundity, ovarian ripening cycle and egg-size relationships of wild [[Speckled Trout]] from the [[Laurentide Park]], Quebec. Over 1,000 female trout were examined macroscopically, and 747 received detailed attention. The work establishes that egg counts are only comparable between populations once eggs are nearly mature, because a large fraction of maturing eggs is resorbed by [[Atresia]] during ripening. Fecundity rises with fish length and is higher in food-rich lakes. A simplified egg-counting method based on an [[Egg Diameter-Ovary Weight Method]] is introduced, along with a 7-stage maturity scale and a 6-digit flesh-colour scale.

## Background & Description

**Study species and setting.** [[Speckled Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) is a salmonid that remained abundant in Quebec's wild lakes. The author directed a [[Biological Station]] in the Laurentide Park, a region of more than 4,000 square miles north of Quebec City, where anglers' daily bag limit was 15 pounds plus one fish. Fecundity studies were run in two periods, 1938–1941 and 1949–1952.

**Material.** Fish came from 36 lakes and rivers at 1,000–3,000 ft elevation. Fork length was the standard size measure. Ovaries were preserved in 4–5% [[Formalin]] for periods from months to three years. Preservation slightly reduced egg size, with maximum preserved diameters (over 4.5 mm) somewhat smaller than fresh eggs.

**Literature context.** Earlier fecundity work on hatchery trout (von Bayer, Hayford, Davis and others) was extensive, but wild-stock fecundity had been little studied. Wild-stock references cited include Titcomb (1897), [[Ricker]] (1932), Stobie (1939), Smith (1947) and Allen (1956). Ricker's Ontario counts were likely made on fish with immature eggs, which may explain why his 22-inch fish carried more eggs than the Quebec fish of the same length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Lakes were very soft, acidic (pH 4.5–6.8), and underlain by Precambrian granite and gneiss.
- **Season:** Spawning in cold high lakes (above 2,500 ft) begins around August 20. Elsewhere it is mostly September, with the latest date near October 20. The effective growth season lasts three to four months. In many lakes the trout is the only fish species present.
- **Growth:** Tagging recaptures of 170–300 mm fish showed annual growth of only 25–50 mm, so scale-based ageing was judged unsatisfactory.
- **Regional contrast:** In southern Ontario, spawning occurs from October 15 to December 15. Light manipulation can shift spawning timing artificially.
- **Food and habitat:** Fecundity is greater in lakes richer in food. Beaver dams that blocked spawning brooks, and overstocking with small fish on extensive spawning beds, both altered local fecundity.
- **Parasites:** [[Diphyllobothrium]] plerocercoid cysts were found in immature adult trout from [[Lake Grelon]], where infected fish appeared to have delayed maturation.

## Key Findings

**1. Egg classes and atresia.** Three egg classes were recognised in adult ovaries:
- **Class a:** recruitment stock of small, yolkless eggs (0.1–0.9 mm).
- **Class b:** maturing yolky eggs, which increase in diameter (1.0–4.8 mm) as their number falls.
- **Class c:** atretic eggs, the resorbed remnants of maturing eggs.

Only 56–61% of Class b eggs eventually ripen. With Class a included, total reduction through atresia approaches 80%.

**2. Egg diameter and maturity.** Ripe eggs before spawning measured 3.8–4.8 mm, with a common size of 4.0–4.6 mm. Largest ova (4.8 mm) came from a 265 mm female in Lake Metascouillac. Eggs under 4 mm are probably not fully mature.

**3. Fecundity range.** Across 163 females (140–553 mm), the average number of mature eggs ranged from about 90 to nearly 4,800. Egg numbers rose curvilinearly with fork length. Fecundity was negatively related to egg diameter, so comparisons require fish with eggs of at least 3.5 mm, preferably 4.0–4.8 mm.

**4. Proportional increase.** Taking a 144 mm (5.7-inch) female as the base (about 100 eggs), larger fish carry roughly 2× eggs at 190 mm, 10× at 325 mm, and 50× at 565 mm (22.2 inches). The author argues that for management, 1,000 fish of 11–13 inches would produce as many eggs as 5,000 fish of 6–8 inches.

**5. Maturity index.** The ovary-weight-to-body-weight ratio (maturity index) averaged 1.2% at 1 mm egg diameter and 13.6% for ripe 4 mm eggs. A spawning fish never exceeded an index of about 20, whereas uncorrected calculations of early-season ovaries gave values of 30–45, which demonstrates the importance of atresia.

**6. Ripening cycle.** Transition from immaturity (Stage 0) to spawning (Stage 5) takes nearly two years in nature. Females usually mature at 3–4 years in Quebec lakes, but hatchery fish can spawn at 12–18 months. Ripening depends more on food intake than on age or size.

**7. Maturity stages.** Seven stages (0–6) were defined from ovary size, appearance and egg diameter. They condense into four: I (Stages 0–1), II (2–3), III (4–5) and IV (6, after spawning).

**8. Flesh colour.** A 6-digit scale using Séguy's colour code was devised. Flesh is brightest in July–August (Stages 3–5), fades around spawning, and is typically pale in fingerlings.

**9. Egg-counting method.** The earlier volumetric method was replaced by an egg diameter-ovary weight method. Counts per gram of ovaries (from 1,000 at 1.0 mm to 14.0 at 4.9 mm) are read from a curve and multiplied by ovary weight. Calculated counts averaged within about 2% of actual counts across 10 fish per diameter class. Of 261 comparisons, 13 showed differences above 14% and were attributed to counting errors.

**10. Spawning frequency.** Trout can remain in immature stages for several years. Some large adults in Lake Grelon did not spawn, and the author suggested parasitism or poor diet as causes.

**11. Population differences.** Fish from high-productivity lakes such as [[Hermine]] and Long carried more eggs than those from [[Grand Lac à l'Épaule]] or [[Grand Lac Jacques Cartier]]. Laurentide Park trout produced far fewer eggs at a given length than fish studied by Smith (1947) or Allen (1956).

**12. Hatchery selection.** Hatchery strains selected for growth can produce about four times as many eggs at first spawning as wild fish. The author notes that this may mainly reflect improved food use, which reduces atresia, rather than a purely hereditary change.

**13. Oviduct debate.** The author confirmed earlier reports that eggs can be expressed from the body cavity through the genital pore, disagreeing with Kendall (1921). Some females carried over 1,200 unspent eggs from previous seasons, for example a 405 mm female from Lake Héléne with 1,205 ripe eggs and 841 flattened residual ova.
