# Floodplain Wetland Suitability, Access, and Use by Juvenile Razorback Sucker in the Middle Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Modde]], [[Muth]], and [[Haines]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) tested whether floodplain wetlands of the middle [[Green River]] in Utah provide suitable nursery habitat for juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] and whether larvae can reach them. The authors studied [[Old Charley Wash]], a 147 ha floodplain wetland on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]], during the high-water years 1995 and 1996. Floodplain depressions offered warmer water, zooplankton densities well above those in the main channel, and adequate depth and oxygen for age-0 fish. Main channel habitats were poor rearing sites. Larvae entered the river during spring runoff, but floodplain connectivity lasted too briefly for all larvae to reach these sites, largely because of the flow regime set by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The authors recommend maintaining floodplain connectivity through levee removal and longer flood duration. They also raise concern that the very high biomass of nonnative fishes produced in the wetland could harm the river if released.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid once common throughout the major rivers of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1991. Its decline has been attributed mainly to lack of recruitment, which has been linked to habitat change and the introduction of nonnative fishes.

Dams have altered the basin's hydrology. Historically, the Colorado River system was warm, sediment-laden, and characterized by high spring snowmelt peaks followed by low summer baseflows. Mainstem dams have reduced seasonal and annual flow variation, lowered sediment loads, cooled water, and reduced the magnitude and duration of high flows. Together these changes have shortened floodplain connection to major rivers. The razorback sucker reproduces on the ascending limb of the spring hydrograph, so spawning timed before high flows gives newly emerged larvae access to inundated floodplains. Before this study, few juvenile razorback suckers had been recorded in the Upper Basin, and those were from main channel habitats.

The study had three objectives: (1) measure environmental variables (dissolved oxygen, temperature, depth, zooplankton density) in the wetland over the summer; (2) determine larval access to floodplains by comparing larval drift timing with bankfull flows; and (3) census the wetland's fish to see whether razorback suckers used it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Old Charley Wash (also called Wood's Bottom) is a 147 ha floodplain wetland. Only its 80 ha main unit was evaluated. Water enters by gravity through an inlet canal at river flows of about 240 m³/s, and through a levee notch at about 396 m³/s. Dominant vegetation is smartweed (*Polygonum* sp.) and sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), with bulrush (*Scirpus* sp.) and cattail (*Typha* sp.) at the margins. The wetland was drained each winter and refilled by spring floods. A modified outlet structure allowed efficient fish capture during draining.

**Physical conditions.** Wetland water was consistently warmer than main channel water through spring and summer in both years. Surface dissolved oxygen did not fall below 4.0 mg/L in 1995. In 1996, stratification appeared in late June and early July as macrophytes became abundant, but bottom oxygen was not considered limiting. Maximum depth exceeded 2.0 m whenever the wetland was connected to the river.

**Prey and macrophytes.** Zooplankton (mainly cladocerans and copepods) peaked around the third week of June in both years. Peak mean densities were 54.3/L in 1995 and 42.8/L in 1996, and the peaks came just after disconnection. Dense pondweed beds may both produce invertebrate prey and provide cover from predators.

**Nonnative fishes.** Carp, fathead minnow, green sunfish, black bullhead, channel catfish, black crappie, and red shiner all occurred in the wetland. Carp dominated biomass, and the wetland supported an "inordinate" nonnative fish biomass.

## Key Findings

**Access and hydrology**
- Larval razorback sucker were collected in the middle Green River in four years: 292 (1993), 1,277 (1994), 32 (1995), and 174 (1996). Collection periods lasted 51, 32, 39, and 24 days.
- Under regulated flows from Flaming Gorge Dam, larvae generally lacked access to most floodplain wetlands at river discharges above 575 m³/s. Under unregulated flows, they would have had access in all study years except 1994 (the low-flow year). In 1993 the access period was up to 17 days.
- Most floodplain habitat in the Ouray area dewaters below about 447 m³/s. Without floodplain access, larvae remain in the main channel as flows recede below that level.
- Old Charley Wash was inundated from 21 May to 2 July (48 days) in 1995, and from 8 May to 14 June (38 days) in 1996.

**Habitat quality**
- Zooplankton in the wetland were far denser than in the river. Mabey (1993) reported 311.4/L and 690.2/L in Old Charley Wash in July and August versus 7.1/L and 1.5/L in a riverine backwater, and 1.3/L and 0.3/L in high-velocity main channel habitat.
- Papoulias and Minckley (1990) found significant starvation mortality of larval razorback sucker at Artemia densities below 50/L.
- Main channel backwaters generally do not form until flows recede to about 109 m³/s, after most larvae have been collected.

**Fish composition and biomass**
- Fifteen species were collected in 1995 and 16 in 1996. Standing stock was 126.7 kg/ha in 1995 and 71.4 kg/ha in 1996.
- Carp and fathead minnow together made up 95.5% of weight and 79.4% of individuals. Carp contributed 91.2% of biomass, and fathead minnow were the most numerous fish (60.6%).
- Fish that accessed the wetland from the river made up 80% (1995) and 77% (1996) of biomass.

**Razorback sucker in the wetland**
- Razorback sucker was the most abundant native fish in the draining collections.
- 1995: 28 age-0 fish averaged 94 mm total length (range 74–125 mm) and 9.5 g (range 3–18 g). Two adults were also collected.
- 1996: 45 age-0 fish were collected in August, averaging 65.9 mm (range 44–83 mm) and 3.8 g (range 1–6 g). No adults were collected.
- Immature Colorado pikeminnow, flannelmouth sucker, and roundtail chub were also present in both years.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Larval razorback sucker are more likely to survive and grow in floodplain depressions than in main channel habitat.
- Floodplain connectivity was too brief for all larvae to reach the wetland. The authors recommend levee removal and longer flood flow duration to increase access.
- Timing and duration of Flaming Gorge Dam releases strongly control larval access to floodplain nursery habitat.
- Predation and competition by nonnative fishes likely reduced survival, but did not prevent it. If historical nursery habitat is restored, nonnative predation may become a secondary, rather than primary, threat.
- Open question: would releasing large numbers of wetland-produced nonnative fishes into the Green River harm native populations enough to offset the gains from razorback sucker recruitment?
