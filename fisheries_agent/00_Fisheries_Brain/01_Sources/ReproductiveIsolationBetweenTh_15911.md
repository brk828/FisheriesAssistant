# Reproductive Isolation Between Pumpkinseed and Bluegill Sunfish in Experimental Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by [[Francis W. Clark]] and [[Miles H. A. Keenleyside]] tested why [[Pumpkinseed]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) rarely hybridize in nature. Equal numbers of both species were stocked in two small ponds near London, Ontario, and observed through the 1964 and 1965 breeding seasons. Reproductive isolation appeared complete: no interspecific courtship or spawning was seen, and no clearly identifiable hybrids were found among several hundred yearling and 2-year-old fish. Behavioral (ethological) isolation, based on visual recognition of conspecific mates, was judged the main barrier. Seasonal and habitat isolation were judged inadequate or unimportant as explanations, although the two species differed in nesting timing, depth and nest-site location.

## Background & Description

Both species belong to the family [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes). Their ranges broadly overlap in eastern North America, and hybrids between them occur in the wild. The two species have also been crossed in the laboratory. Earlier work associated natural centrarchid hybridization with (a) competition for spawning areas, (b) unequal numbers of the two species, or (c) introduction of one species into an area holding the other.

The study was designed to remove those three factors. This allowed the authors to compare the relative effectiveness of [[Seasonal Isolation]], [[Habitat Isolation]] and [[Ethological Isolation]] as barriers, following the isolating-mechanism framework of [[Ernst Mayr]]. It also tested claims by [[R. M. Bailey]] (1938) that breeding time and nest-site choice are too broadly overlapping to act as barriers, so that behavioral incompatibility must be the main barrier.

**Study ponds**
- **[[Blackburn Pond]]:** surface area 0.11 ha, maximum depth 1.5 m, unused pasture with willows. It contained no fish before the experiment.
- **[[White Pond]]:** surface area 0.25 ha, maximum depth 1.4 m, surrounded by lawn, weeping willows and ornamentals. A few [[Speckled Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) were present during the study.
- Both ponds had constant-flow springwater inlets and standpipe outlets that stabilized water levels. Their bottoms were gravel overlain by silt, and [[Stonewort]] (*Chara* sp.) covered most of the bottom.

**Stock origin:** Pumpkinseeds were seined in October 1963 from Pinehurst Lake near Galt, Ontario. Bluegills came from a commercial trap net under the ice at Mitchell's Bay, Lake St. Clair, in January 1964. White Pond received more fish because it was larger and had more potential spawning area. A 1:1 sex ratio was assumed after chi-square tests showed no departure from it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and timing.** Nest-building was associated with rising spring surface water temperatures, which supports [[Breder]]'s (1936) hypothesis that temperature controls the start of sunfish reproduction. Pumpkinseeds began nesting at about 13 to 17 °C and bluegills at about 17 to 23 °C. In 1965 the first pumpkinseed nests were seen at 13 °C (White Pond) and 17 °C (Blackburn Pond), and bluegill nests at 23 and 17 °C respectively. Surface temperatures between the two ponds differed by a mean of 0.52 °C, which the authors regarded as within measurement error and not biologically meaningful.

**Nest occupancy.** Pumpkinseed nest occupancy tracked surface water temperature, and males often deserted nests that held eggs when temperature dropped suddenly. Bluegill occupancy depended mainly on the presence of eggs or fry. Bluegill males without eggs usually left their nests within 4 days.

**Nest sites.** Bluegills nested in deeper water and farther from shore than pumpkinseeds in both ponds and years (*t*-test, P < .01). Bluegill nests were also more clustered, while pumpkinseed nests were more scattered. Pumpkinseeds often nested close to shore even where equally shallow water lay farther out. In White Pond, most bluegill nests were concentrated under or near overhanging [[Willow Trees]], but this shade was absent in Blackburn Pond. Neither species showed a preference for particular substrate or vegetation.

**Disease event.** In spring 1965, 21 bluegills and 18 pumpkinseeds died in Blackburn Pond between April 3 and May 10. Fifteen bluegills and 13 pumpkinseeds of those deaths had been tagged. The cause was attributed to [[Saprolegnia]] (a fungus), which left white patches on the head, fins, and tag wounds. No fungus was seen in White Pond. On May 6, 1965, 27 bluegills and 30 pumpkinseeds were moved from White to Blackburn Pond to restore population numbers. No further dead fish were found after May 10.

**Territorial and courtship behavior.** Nesting males of both species chased and bit intruders, and bluegill males mostly defended only the nest itself. Males of both species first attacked any approaching female. A mature female threatened by a conspecific male either fled or settled lower and tolerated his attack, and the male then began courtship. Females approached by heterospecific males always fled.

## Key Findings

- **Reproductive isolation appeared complete.** No interspecific courtship or spawning was seen in either year. Conspecific courtship and spawning were frequent. Examination of several hundred juvenile fish found no hybrids.
- **Juvenile sampling.** Samples of 50 yearling and 50 two-year-old fish per tentatively identified group were examined from each pond in 1966. Fewer were available for some bluegill groups (11 yearling bluegills from White Pond, 7 two-year-old bluegills from Blackburn Pond). Internal characters, including gill rakers, pharyngeal bones and pharyngeal teeth, supported the field identifications, and none of the juveniles was considered a hybrid.
- **Nesting timing.** Pumpkinseeds began nesting about 3 weeks before bluegills in 1964, and 2 to 4 weeks earlier in 1965. Spawning of both species overlapped in time in each pond and year, so the authors judged seasonal isolation unimportant.
- **Nest duration.** Pumpkinseed males guarded nests for a mean of 18.5 days (SD 12.8) and bluegill males for 8.7 days (SD 5.0), based on 26 tagged males of each species in Blackburn Pond, 1965. The abstract gives the pumpkinseed figure as 8.5 days, which conflicts with the body text and may reflect a scan error.
- **Habitat isolation inadequate.** Nest sites differed in depth and distance from shore, but nests of the two species were close together, and some nests were used successively by males of both species. Females of both species moved through shallow water where they met males of both species, so behavioral barriers had to do the work.
- **Mechanism of isolation.** Pumpkinseed males attacked all approaching females but chased conspecifics most actively. Bluegill males began nest-rim circling with bluegill females and ignored or attacked pumpkinseed females. Female bluegills approaching pumpkinseed nests retreated when attacked, while female pumpkinseeds did not approach bluegill nests.
- **Sexing accuracy.** External sexing was 84.2% accurate for pumpkinseeds but only 56.6% for bluegills, based on specimens sexed by gonad examination. Bluegill estimated and actual sex ratios differed (P < .005).
- **Cue unknown.** The species-recognition cues were not identified. Unpublished aquarium observations by Keenleyside showed that males of both species mostly courted conspecific females.
- **Hybridization under crowding.** The authors proposed that hybridization in nature is linked to crowding of spawning areas. Under severe competition for nest sites, heterospecific females could enter nests, and species recognition might break down.
- **Mating mechanics.** Fertilization is external in Centrarchidae, so mechanical isolation does not exist between these species. Gamete mortality, hybrid inviability and hybrid sterility were not investigated but were not considered important, citing earlier work by [[Luce]] (1937) and [[Lagler]] and [[Steinmetz]] (1957).
