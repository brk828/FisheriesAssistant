# Metabolism of an Arid Region Canal Ecosystem

#aquatic-ecology #freshwater-ecology #limnology #ecosystem-metabolism #cladophora

## Executive Summary

This study measures whole-ecosystem metabolism in the [[Arizona Canal]], a concrete-lined irrigation canal in central Arizona, using diel changes in [[Dissolved Oxygen]] over a 12-km reach (stations A to E). Measurements were taken in June, July, September and November 1983. The canal behaves as a productive, autotroph-dominated system. Gross primary productivity (PG) averaged 6.70 g O2/m²/day across the four dates, and [[Ecosystem Respiration]] averaged 5.82 g O2/m²/day. The authors attribute most primary production to attached algae and macrophytes, and they cross-check whole-system estimates against the sum of subsystem measurements. The document does not assess fish or benthic invertebrate roles in ecosystem metabolism.

## Background & Description

The [[Arizona Canal]] receives water only from [[Granite Reef Dam]] and from runoff off a small desert watershed during storms. Regional precipitation averages 23.4 cm/year. Influent water is a mix of [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] water. Salt River water has higher conductance than Verde River water (Verde reference value 510 µS/cm). During the study period (December 1982 to November 1983), canal water was slightly basic (pH about 7.5), conductance ranged from 250 to 935 µS/cm, nitrate-N ranged from 0 to 1 µg/L-scale values as reported, and [[Soluble Reactive Phosphorus]] ranged from 4 to 68 µg/L. Water temperatures varied seasonally and daily, with summer maxima above 43°C reported in late June to August.

The canal is dredged one to three times per year to remove nuisance vegetation and is drained for about four weeks for cleaning and repair. Visual estimates suggest that maintenance removes little of the total system.

Sediment accumulates in patches (organic sediments to about 15 cm depth in places), mostly in areas where [[Macrophytes]] grow. Concrete sidewalls and floor are colonized by [[Epiconcretic Algae]], chiefly [[Cladophora Glomerata]] and an attached diatom assemblage, with blue-green algae in middle portions of the reach. Macroinvertebrates include Oligochaeta, Hydracarina, Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera (including [[Helicopsyche Mexicana]]), Chironomidae and the introduced [[Corbicula Fluminea]]. The clam is conspicuous in some areas, where dense aggregations occur. The canal supports a diverse and abundant fish community, but no sport fishery exists.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Whole-system metabolism is a useful integrator of producer and consumer activity in streams. The authors compare the canal with two other Sonoran Desert lotic systems with metabolic data, [[Sycamore Creek]] and [[Pinto Creek]]. Canal metabolic rates are only slightly lower than those of these creeks. The systems differ strongly in other respects: Sycamore Creek is small (1–4 m wide, flow below 0.05 m³/s), has metabolically active sand-gravel sediments, generally lacks macrophytes, and is subject to severe flood disturbance. Shading is low in both the creeks and the canal, and algae predominate as primary producers in all three.

The canal's productivity class fits the "intermediate-high productivity" category proposed by [[Fisher & Carpenter Stream Classification]]. In intermediate-sized rivers, macrophyte productivity may be an important component, though it is highly variable. Macrophyte contributions in the literature range widely.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Whole-system metabolism was estimated from diel dissolved-oxygen changes, measured by modified Winkler titration at stations A, C and E, in June, July, September and November 1983.
- Night-time oxygen-loss rates were used to estimate diffusion, assuming constant rates through the diel period.
- Light-dark bottle incubations (11:00 to 13:00) measured [[Epiconcretic Algae]] metabolism on colonized ceramic tiles and in canal water; [[Sediment Respiration]] was measured in cores in 0.5–1.1 hour incubations.
- Daily net primary productivity (PN) was computed from hourly measurements by multiplying hourly respiration by 24. Daily gross productivity (PG) was calculated as PN + R.
- Subsystem contributions (algae, macrophytes, sediments, water column) were summed and compared with whole-system values; the authors judged the sum to be a reasonable check.

**Whole-system metabolism**
- Daily PG in the 12-km reach averaged 6.70 g O2/m²/day over four dates. PG was highest in June and declined to a minimum in November.
- Ecosystem respiration (R) averaged 5.82 g O2/m²/day. The PG/R ratio averaged 1.16, meaning the canal produced more organic matter than it consumed.
- Across the study, PG ranged from 3.34 to 8.94 g O2/m²/day and R from 3.60 to 7.92 g O2/m²/day.
- Relative changes in PG, R and PG/R for upstream (A–C) and downstream (C–E) segments showed no pattern among dates.

**Subsystem metabolism**
- Epiconcretic algae were dominated by Cladophora glomerata and an attached diatom assemblage. Their PG/R ratio was consistently high (mean 2.84, SD 0.59, N = 18), indicating that the community was strongly autotrophic.
- Algal metabolism did not vary with incubation depth (0.2 to 1.5 m), location (upstream or downstream) or colonization period.
- Macrophyte coverage was mapped from nearly 3,000 observations. Macrophytes, including Potamogeton pectinatus and Zannichellia palustris, were measured for metabolism (excluding roots).
- The authors note that subsystem sums sometimes overestimated and sometimes underestimated whole-system values, so the subsystem approach is only approximate. Macrophyte and sediment estimates were also temporally variable.

**Longitudinal patterns**
- Among the parameters measured, only soluble reactive phosphorus showed a consistent longitudinal pattern. On 26–27 July 1983 it declined significantly downstream, falling 38% from 39 to 23 µg/L between stations A and E.
- On 23 other dates SRP varied little along the reach (average A–E difference below 2%).
- Ammonia-N showed no longitudinal pattern.
- Metabolic rates were largely independent of longitudinal position, suggesting that physical and biotic factors other than downstream position controlled production.

**Conclusions**
- The [[Arizona Canal]] is a productive, algae- and macrophyte-driven system with metabolic rates similar to nearby desert creeks, despite large differences in physical structure.
- Algal productivity in the canal is high and relatively constant, supporting the view that epiconcretic algae are the main autotrophic component on concrete surfaces.
- The authors call for further work on biotic interactions and on how upstream conditions affect downstream organisms, since the data do not explain all of the variation in metabolism.
