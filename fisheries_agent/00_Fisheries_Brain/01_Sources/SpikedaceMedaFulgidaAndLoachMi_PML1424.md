# Spikedace, Loach Minnow, and Red Shiner: Interactions Between Native and Introduced Fishes in the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by Paul C. Marsh, Francisco J. Abarca, Michael E. Douglas and J. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]] for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AZGF), [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] (NMGF) and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) (31 August 1989), investigates whether the introduced [[Red Shiner]] contributes to the decline of two federally threatened native cyprinids, [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], in the Gila River basin. The study combined comparative field surveys of habitat and food use across seven reaches of five streams with a controlled artificial-stream experiment at Arizona State University. Field data suggested that spikedace occupied significantly faster currents when syntopic with red shiners, and the artificial stream showed spikedace displaced by aggressive, spawning shiners. Food-resource overlap between the species was low. The authors conclude that competition for food plays a minor role at most, while behavioral displacement by red shiners is a plausible mechanism warranting longer-term study.

## Background & Description

**Project scope.** The 30-month contract addressed ecological interactions among natives and exotics. The report is organized into eight sections, covering species introductions, study sites and methods, physical stream characteristics, loach minnow studies, habitat relations of wild spikedace and shiners, food-use comparisons, habitat relations in an artificial system, and a summary. Abarca's thesis on benthic and drifting macroinvertebrates was reported separately.

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*).** A small, slender cyprinid endemic to the upper Gila River system of Arizona and New Mexico. It is listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). Historically widespread in moderate-sized, intermediate-elevation streams, it now persists in scattered populations in Aravaipa Creek, Eagle Creek, the upper Verde River, and the Gila River in New Mexico. Breeding occurs in spring (April to June) over shallow, gravelly riffles. Adults are carnivorous, feeding mainly on aquatic and terrestrial insects, with trichopterans and ephemeropterans most important.

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*).** A small, secretive, bottom-dwelling cyprinid endemic to the Gila basin and monotypic in genus. Its range has contracted to scattered tributary populations in Arizona and New Mexico, and it is believed extirpated from Mexico. It is also federally threatened. It inhabits turbulent, shallow riffles with gravel or cobble substrates, and is an opportunistic benthic insectivore; reproduction involves adhesive eggs attached to the undersides of flat rocks, guarded by the male.

**Red Shiner (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).** A moderately small cyprinid native to the Mississippi and Gulf of Mexico drainages of central and south-central North America. It was introduced to the lower Colorado River in the early 1950s and has become one of the most abundant fishes of the Gila basin. It tolerates intermittency, high turbidity, high temperature and low dissolved oxygen, and is an opportunistic omnivore with a protracted spawning season.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat degradation is the dominant cause of decline for both natives. Documented threats include dewatering, impoundment, channelization, groundwater pumping, livestock grazing, mining, agriculture, timber harvest and urban development. These activities alter flow regimes, increase sedimentation, and introduce contaminants. Natural flooding is considered important because desert native fishes withstand floods better than non-native forms.

Non-native predators and competitors also affect the natives. Channel catfish and flathead catfish prey on or use riffles occupied by spikedace and loach minnow. Largemouth and smallmouth bass, green sunfish, and salmonids at higher elevations may also interact with natives. Mosquitofish occupy habitats used by juvenile loach minnow. Red shiner is singled out because it occupies essentially the same habitats as spikedace, and the concurrent decline of spikedace and expansion of red shiner is considered circumstantial evidence of displacement.

Field sampling covered seven reaches: upper and lower Aravaipa Creek, Blue River, Gila River at Gila and Redrock (New Mexico), Sycamore Creek, and Verde River. Sampling used 2.0 × 1.2 m seines and bank electrofishing across 89 to 239 sample units per reach. Habitat variables included depth, current velocity and a weighted substrate index. Food analyses used volumetric, numerical and frequency measures, and an index of relative importance (IRI). Dietary overlap was assessed with Horn, Levins and Schoener indices.

Physical habitat differences among reaches were significant. Mean depths ranged from 19.0 cm (Blue River) to 28.1 cm (Verde River), and mean current velocities from 15.3 cm/s (Sycamore Creek) to 62.2 cm/s (East Aravaipa). The artificial stream comprised nine pool-run segments with ~20 fish per section. Water temperatures ranged 23–35 °C in mid-summer, 20–30 °C in autumn and 15–25 °C in winter.

## Key Findings

**Loach minnow habitat and food**
- Loach minnow occupied shallow riffles with mean depths of 14.7 to 20.8 cm and current velocities of 37.2 to 70.3 cm/s. Loach minnow was found at Blue River, Gila-Gila, and East Aravaipa (1 of 107 samples).
- Loach minnow co-occurred with red shiners only at Blue River, in too few samples (four samples with a single specimen each) for meaningful comparison. The authors concluded that shifts in loach minnow habitat use were unlikely to be explained by red shiner presence.
- Diets consisted mainly of immature rifle-dwelling ephemeropterans, chironomids, simuliids, and trichopterans. Feeding appeared crepuscular, with fullness peaking at noon and midnight.

**Spikedace habitat**
- Spikedace occupied significantly slower currents when syntopic with red shiners (Gila-Redrock and Verde) than when isolated (East and West Aravaipa, Gila-Gila). Spikedace in isolation averaged 35.3 cm/s versus 40.4 cm/s in syntopy (F = 4.34; p < 0.03; n = 141). Depth was significantly shallower in isolation (19.8 cm vs. 28.9 cm).
- Authors infer a degree of displacement: spikedace in syntopy occupied currents faster than those in which they occurred in isolation.
- Available habitat did not differ significantly in mean current velocity among four reaches (p > 0.05; n = 540).

**Red shiner habitat**
- Red shiner at Sycanore Creek (isolated) occupied deeper, slower water than where absent (depth F = 6.44, p < 0.01; velocity F = 6.98, p < 0.009).
- Shiners in the Gila-Redrock and Verde reaches occupied slow currents (19.4 cm/s where present vs. 37.6 cm/s where absent across three reaches; F = 86.2, p < 0.0001).

**Food habits**
- Spikedace diets (Verde River, June 1987, n = 11) were dominated by post-emergent adult trichopterans (75% of volume, 91% frequency of occurrence). Aravaipa Creek (August 1987, n = 50) was dominated by trichopteran pupae; Gila River (June 1987, n = 43) by ephemeropteran nymphs and trichopteran larvae.
- Red shiner diets were broad and opportunistic: amorphous organic matter, ephemeropterans, larval hydropsychids and chironomids, and unidentified insect parts. Sycanore Creek shiners (n = 205, October 1987) consumed 48 food items across nine categories.
- Dietary overlap was minimal. No overlap exceeded 0.50 in the Verde River, where the species co-occurred. Two out-of-system comparisons exceeded 0.50, but the authors judged them unjustified.

**Artificial stream experiments**
- Adult spikedace and red shiners were observed in nine replicate sections over six months. In August, when shiners were spawning, spikedace shifted toward slower currents and were chased frequently by shiners. Shiners stayed in pool headwaters and were more active and aggressive than spikedace.
- Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests showed that spikedace distribution differed significantly from random only in some months (p < 0.01 in August, September, October and December), and that spikedace distribution changed significantly in the presence of shiners in August (max D = 0.38; χ² = 11.23; p < 0.01).
- Red shiner distribution was consistent, whether or not spikedace were present.
- Physical harassment by shiners was repeatedly observed. Larval shiners were produced in the system in autumn; attempts by spikedace to prey on larval shiners were unsuccessful.

**Conclusions**
- Field and artificial-stream results were consistent: spikedace occupied different habitats when red shiners were present, shifting to faster currents in the wild and slower currents in the artificial system.
- Competition for food appears to play a minor role, if any, in spikedace declines where red shiners are established.
- Behavioral displacement by spawning red shiners is the most plausible mechanism, but population-level effects are unknown. The authors recommend long-term, life-cycle studies in controlled artificial systems to assess reproduction and recruitment with and without red shiners.
