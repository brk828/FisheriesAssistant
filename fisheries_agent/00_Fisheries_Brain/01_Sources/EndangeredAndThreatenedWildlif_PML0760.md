# Rio Grande Silvery Minnow Endangered Species Listing (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hybognathus

## Executive Summary

A 1994 final rule from the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] lists the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The rule was published in the Federal Register on July 20, 1994 and took effect August 19, 1994. The species now occupies only about 5% of its historic range, a 275 km (170 mi) reach of the [[Middle Rio Grande]] in New Mexico. Its decline is attributed to dewatering, channelization, flow regulation by dams, degraded water quality, and competition or predation by [[Non-Native Fish Species]]. Critical habitat was not yet determinable because the [[Economic Analysis]] was incomplete, so a final critical habitat decision was deferred to March 1, 1995. The surrounding documents (a 1994 cover letter, a notice to a researcher, and an announcement of an economic analysis contract) concern the same listing process.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and morphology.** The species is one of seven *Hybognathus* minnows in the United States. Girard first described it in 1856 from specimens taken near Fort Brown, Cameron County, Texas. It is a stout silvery minnow with moderately small eyes and a small, slightly oblique mouth. Adults reach about 90 mm (3.5 in) total length. The dorsal fin is distinctively pointed, and the body is silver with emerald reflections. The belly is silvery white, the fins are plain, and barbels are absent.

**Historic range.** Historically the species was one of the most abundant and widespread fishes in the Rio Grande basin. It ranged from Española, New Mexico, to the Gulf of Mexico. It also occurred in the [[Pecos River]] from Santa Rosa, New Mexico, to its confluence with the Rio Grande in south Texas.

**Current range.** Collection data indicate the species now occupies about 5% of its historic range. It has been completely extirpated from the Pecos River and from the Rio Grande downstream of [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]]. It is found only in the middle Rio Grande between [[Cochiti Dam]] (Sandoval County) and the headwaters of Elephant Butte Reservoir (Socorro County).

**Land ownership and governance.** Most land bordering the occupied river reach is owned by the [[Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District]], a quasi-public State of New Mexico agency. Other landowners include six Native American Pueblos, the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], the Fish and Wildlife Service, the Bureau of Land Management, New Mexico State Parks, the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, the New Mexico State Lands Department, and the [[Army Corps Of Engineers]]. River flow is governed by the [[Rio Grande Compact Commission]], established in 1929, together with the [[International Boundary And Water Commission]], the Bureau of Reclamation, and the Corps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Replacement by the plains minnow.** The species no longer exists in the Pecos River, where it was replaced by the introduced [[Plains Minnow]] (*H. placitus*). That fish was probably introduced around 1968 through the release of bait minnows collected from the Arkansas River drainage. The replacement was complete in less than one decade. The plains minnow may tolerate modified habitats better, and the two species are believed to have hybridized. Habitat alteration and flow modification may also have contributed to the Pecos extirpation.

**Dams and flow regulation.** Decline in the Rio Grande probably began in 1916, when the gates of [[Elephant Butte Dam]] were closed. Elephant Butte was the first of five major mainstream dams in the species' habitat. The dams allowed river flow to be manipulated and diverted for agriculture, often desiccating reaches and eliminating all fish. Construction of the dams also coincided with increased abundance of non-native species stocked into the reservoirs, which often replaced native fish.

**Water quality and disease.** Agricultural and urban development degraded water quality, which may have affected the species' range and distribution. Crowding in pools during drying periods stresses fish and often leads to disease outbreaks. The protozoan *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* is a notable example. Parasites such as the copepod *Lernaea* can also spread under these conditions.

**Non-native species and predation.** Native fish are preyed upon by introduced species, including northern pike (*Esox lucius*), walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*), white crappie (*Pomoxis annularis*), white bass (*Morone chrysops*), and black and brown bullheads. Introduced fish have largely displaced native species such as the silvery minnow.

**Bait harvest.** Licensed commercial bait dealers may sell bait minnows only from the drainage where they were collected. Recreational anglers may collect minnows for personal use, and the practice has encouraged introductions of non-native species. Platania's 1987–1989 sampling at 11 Pecos River locations failed to find any Rio Grande silvery minnows.

## Key Findings

- **Listing decision:** The species is listed as endangered throughout its historic range. The Service rejected threatened status because of the extremely limited habitat it occupies and the threats it faces. Without federal protection, the species was expected to become extinct in the foreseeable future.
- **Critical habitat deferred:** Critical habitat was "not determinable" because the economic analysis was not complete. The final designation was due by March 1, 1995 under section 4(b)(6)(C)(ii). A contract for the analysis was awarded to ECO Northwest of Eugene, Oregon, in September 1994.
- **Threats identified:** Dewatering, channelization, flow regulation for irrigation, degraded water quality from municipal, industrial, and agricultural discharges, and competition or predation by introduced fish.
- **Listing history:** The species was a Category 1 candidate in the November 21, 1991 Animal Notice of Review (56 FR 58804). A proposed rule followed on March 1, 1993 (58 FR 11821).
- **Public comment:** The comment period was extended from April 30 to August 25, 1993. Two public hearings drew 37 attendees in Albuquerque. Of 40 written comments, 13 supported the listing and 14 opposed it. Oral or written comments were received from 25 parties at the hearings, none of which directly supported the proposal. Respondents included 7 federal agencies, 5 state agencies, 14 local officials, and 36 private organizations, companies, and individuals.
- **Water rights:** Commenters asked whether the Service would buy water rights for instream flow. The Service said instream flow requirements were among several factors to be considered in recovery planning, and that it would explore purchases from willing sellers if they would enhance recovery.
- **Treaty obligations:** Under the 1906 Convention, the United States must deliver 60,000 acre-feet of water annually to Mexico. The [[International Boundary And Water Commission]] was concerned that the listing might impede compliance. The Service acknowledged the treaty obligation.
- **Federal protections:** Section 7(a)(2) of the Act requires federal agencies to ensure their actions are not likely to jeopardize the species. Federal actions affecting the species include dam operation and maintenance and water management by the Bureau of Reclamation and the Corps. The [[Section 7 Consultation]] process applies to these actions, and [[Take Prohibitions]] apply to all persons.
- **Regulatory action:** The rule amends 50 CFR 17.11(h) to add the Rio Grande silvery minnow to the list of endangered and threatened wildlife. The primary author is Gerald L. Burton. The rule was signed by Director Mollie H. Beattie.
- **Environmental review:** The Service determined that an environmental assessment was not required under NEPA for this listing.
