## Executive Summary

This document is a keynote/policy address by Angelo Incerpi, delivered at the American Fisheries Society (AFS) East Coast Trout Management and Culture Workshop II at Pennsylvania State University, and later published as a "President's Hook" column in *Fisheries* (Vol. 21, No. 5). Rather than presenting empirical ichthyological data, the piece is a management-philosophy essay arguing against the polarization of fisheries professionals into "pro-wild-fish" versus "pro-[[Hatchery Programs|hatchery]]" factions. Incerpi contends that hatchery-based [[Fish Culture]] is a legitimate management tool—not inherently comparable to destructive forces like habitat degradation, overfishing, or invasive species introductions—when applied responsibly and grounded in sound science. He calls for evidence-based, collaborative fisheries management that balances wild stock conservation with recreational and consumptive angling demands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although not a species-specific ecological account, the essay addresses broader ecosystem-management concepts relevant to [[Freshwater Fisheries Management]]:

- **Human role in ecosystems**: Incerpi argues humans are "an integral part of our ecosystems, not aliens," rejecting the notion that pre-settlement or pre-human baseline conditions are a realistic management target.
- **Habitat modification context**: [[Hatchery Programs]] are frequently grouped—unfairly, in the author's view—with genuinely destructive anthropogenic stressors including:
  - Channel modifications
  - Dams and reservoirs
  - Water diversions/withdrawals
  - Point and nonpoint source pollution
  - Global atmospheric change
- **Genetic and disease considerations**: Fish culture is proposed as a tool to develop genetic strains resistant to health issues such as [[Whirling Disease]], potentially improving survival of stocked or supplemented populations in the wild.
- **Coexistence framing**: The essay advocates that "natural and managed systems can coexist," positioning managed [[Wild Fish Stocks]] and hatchery-supplemented stocks as complementary rather than mutually exclusive within a sustainable fisheries framework.

## Key Findings

- **Polarization risk**: Citing Martin, Webster, and Edwards (*Fisheries*, Vol. 17, No. 1), the author warns that fisheries professionals are forming into "inflexible, polarized factions" over hatchery vs. wild fish stocks, risking erosion of public confidence in fisheries science.
- **Mischaracterization of hatcheries**: Equating hatcheries with habitat degradation, overfishing, and introduced species is described as a "biased, narrow, and unrealistic attitude" not supported by scientific evidence.
- **Poor design vs. inherent flaw**: The author distinguishes between *poorly designed* hatchery programs (which can threaten native stocks and reduce genetic variation) and hatcheries as a management tool in principle, which he argues can be applied responsibly.
- **Anti-hatchery ≠ pro-wild-fish**: A key critique is that the "pro-wild-fish equals anti-hatchery" framing is a false dichotomy often based on theoretical concerns lacking substantial empirical support.
- **Historical context**: References Roy I. Jackson's 1970 AFS centennial keynote ("Famine and the World Fisheries"), noting that at the time fish flesh supplied approximately 11% of humanity's animal protein intake, and that "controlled culture is our greatest opportunity for increasing fish production."
- **Call to action**: The essay urges renewed, unbiased evaluation of past hatchery programs, integration of current technology and evolving science into management plans, and broad-based public participation in fisheries decision-making, moving away from politically driven decisions (e.g., budget-driven hatchery closures without impact review).
- **Core thesis**: "Fish culture is as much a part of management as management is of fish culture" — the debate between hatchery and wild-fish advocacy should be replaced by collaborative, science- and public-input-driven fisheries management.
