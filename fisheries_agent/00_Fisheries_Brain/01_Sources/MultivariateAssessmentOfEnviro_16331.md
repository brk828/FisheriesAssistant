# Environmental Preferences of Cyprinid Fishes in the Illinois River, Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[James D. Felley]] and [[Loren G. Hill]] (University of Oklahoma) uses multivariate analysis to describe the habitat preferences of ten cyprinid species in the Oklahoma drainage of the [[Illinois River]]. Fish were collected at 15 localities in spring, summer, autumn and winter of 1978 and 1979. Each species' preference for each of 17 environmental variables was estimated as an abundance-weighted mean. [[Principal Components Analysis]] with [[Varimax Rotation]] then identified sets of covarying variables. Three recurring axes of habitat partitioning emerged: headwater versus downstream location, flowing versus quiet water, and food preference (herbivory versus predation). Movements tied to water temperature and breeding were also detected. The authors stress that the analysis is descriptive and cannot establish causation.

## Background & Description

The study addresses how species distributions reflect environmental tolerances, competition and predation, and preferences for particular conditions. A species' preference for a variable can be estimated as the mean of that variable across all collected individuals. The authors note that not every variable directly affects habitat choice. Some are only correlated with the causal variable (for example, substrate or debris may track current speed), and others have no effect at all. Confounding relationships among variables therefore have to be considered.

The earlier work of [[Baker And Ross]] (1981) on southeastern cyprinids used multiple discriminant analysis to examine habitat segregation. The present study follows a similar logic but uses components analysis on species means only.

The study area is the Oklahoma portion of the Illinois River, which drains the [[Boston Mountains]] and the [[Ozark Plateau]] in Arkansas and Oklahoma. Headwaters are heavily influenced by springs, and the fish fauna is typical of Ozark streams. Stream order at the collection localities ranged from 2 to 4.

The ten cyprinid species analyzed were:

- [[Campostoma Anomalum]] (stoneroller)
- [[Nocomis Asper]] (redspot chub)
- [[Notropis Boops]] (bigeye shiner)
- [[Notropis Nubilus]] (Ozark minnow)
- [[Notropis Pilsbryi]] (duskystripe shiner)
- [[Notropis Rubellus]] (rosyface shiner)
- [[Notropis Whipplei]] (steelcolor shiner)
- [[Pimephales Notatus]] (bluntnose minnow)
- [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]] (southern redbelly dace)
- [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]] (creek chub)

Each species was collected in at least seven of the eight collecting seasons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling.** Fish were seined (3.7 × 1.5 m, 3 mm mesh) at 15 localities once per season. Each seine haul covered a single microhabitat, defined as an area of no more than 40 m² (often smaller) that was homogeneous for clarity, substrate, current, cover, debris and emergent vegetation. Seven environmental variables were measured per locality or microhabitat in addition to the variables in Table 1: Secchi depth, substrate (coded 0–5 from mud to bedrock), current (seconds for an object to float 10 m, with still water set at 180 s), cover, debris and vegetation (each coded 1/0), stream width, maximum stream depth, capture depth, conductivity, pH, and temperature.

**Diet.** Up to 10 individuals per species per locality were examined for gut contents. Contents were sorted into terrestrial invertebrates, aquatic invertebrates, filamentous algae and detritus, and the first two were combined as "prey."

**Weighting and statistics.** Each variable value was weighted by the number of individuals of a species at a location. Presence/absence variables therefore became the percentage of a species' individuals found where the variable was present. Temperature was expressed as deviation from the drainage seasonal mean, and pH was averaged geometrically. Variable means were correlated across species within each season, and the correlation matrices were analyzed with principal components analysis and Varimax rotation. Only components with eigenvalues above 1 were rotated. Loadings of ±0.50 or greater were interpreted, and ±0.70 or greater was treated as high. A component was interpreted only if it had high loadings on at least two variables.

**Limits of the design.** The authors selected components analysis over discriminant analysis because species collected from a single location had zero variance, which violates discriminant assumptions. With only ten species, the correlations among variable means may not reflect true relationships. To check for this, the authors sampled over two years and looked for comparable components in the same seasons.

## Key Findings

**1. Three recurring axes of habitat partitioning.** Variables were examined for pairs that loaded highly on the same component in at least four of eight seasons. The resulting groups defined three component types:

- *Headwater versus downstream.* This component usually loaded on stream order, pH, temperature, capture depth and debris. It was the dominant gradient across seasons and reflects the influence of springs on headwater physicochemistry, which keeps headwaters cooler in summer and warmer in winter and more acidic.
- *Flowing versus quiet water.* This component loaded on substrate, debris, vegetation and current, with stream depth and width sometimes loading here instead of on the first axis.
- *Food preference.* This component contrasted herbivorous species with carnivorous species and appeared in four of the eight seasons.

**2. Two assemblages along the upstream–downstream gradient (Table 6).** The headwater group comprised [[Nocomis Asper]], [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]] and [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]], with average ranks of six or greater. [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]] and [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]] were never found away from springheads, while [[Nocomis Asper]] was ubiquitous but more prevalent in headwaters. The remaining species formed a downstream assemblage with average ranks below five. Exceptions were [[Campostoma Anomalum]] and [[Notropis Pilsbryi]], which were occasionally found in headwaters. In spring, breeding [[Notropis Pilsbryi]] were common over riffles in the extreme headwaters.

**3. Flowing versus quiet water preferences.** Species characteristic of pools and slow water were [[Notropis Boops]], [[Notropis Whipplei]] and [[Pimephales Notatus]] (average ranks of six or more). [[Campostoma Anomalum]], [[Notropis Nubilus]] and [[Notropis Rubellus]] preferred flowing water. [[Nocomis Asper]], [[Notropis Pilsbryi]], [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]] and [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]] used both. Preferences shifted seasonally. [[Notropis Boops]], normally a quiet-water species, bred over riffles in spring, as did [[Campostoma Anomalum]], [[Notropis Nubilus]], [[Notropis Pilsbryi]] and [[Notropis Rubellus]].

**4. Diet.** Using gut contents:

- Herbivorous or detritivorous: [[Campostoma Anomalum]] and [[Notropis Nubilus]] were almost exclusively herbivorous. [[Pimephales Notatus]] was primarily herbivorous.
- Omnivorous: [[Notropis Boops]], [[Notropis Pilsbryi]] and [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]].
- Predaceous: [[Nocomis Asper]], [[Notropis Rubellus]], [[Notropis Whipplei]] and [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]]. [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]] fed almost entirely on flying insects in summer and autumn.

**5. Temperature and breeding drive seasonal movement.** In winter, only [[Campostoma Anomalum]] and [[Pimephales Notatus]] had negative temperature means, meaning they lived in areas cooler than the drainage average. Upstream areas were warmer than downstream in winter because of spring-fed constancy. Downstream species moved toward these headwaters, which is shown by lower stream-order values in winter than in summer, most clearly in [[Notropis Pilsbryi]]. In summer, downstream species again sought warmer locations, some moving away from headwaters. Ontogenetic shifts also occurred. Young-of-year [[Nocomis Asper]] were always in vegetation or cover, while adults used open, current-influenced areas. Larger [[Notropis Pilsbryi]] favored upstream riffles and flowing water, while young fish occupied downstream slow-water habitats.

**6. Community structure.** The Illinois River cyprinid fauna was divisible into three assemblages: a headwater group ([[Nocomis Asper]], [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]], [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]]), a midstream group (the other species analyzed), and a large-river/lake group associated with [[Lake Tenkiller]]. The lake group comprised *Notemigonus crysoleucas*, *Notropis atherinoides* and *N. lutrensis*. These were collected only at the locality nearest the lake and were excluded from the analysis. This pattern supports [[Longitudinal Zonation]] of fish assemblages (Shelford 1911; Sheldon 1968).

**7. Methodological conclusions.** Varimax and oblique rotations gave the same interpretations, so the assumption of orthogonal axes was judged acceptable. The authors argue that multivariate reduction of 17 variables to three trends reveals patterns that are not evident in the raw Table 1 data. They also caution that the components are descriptive. Segregation attributed to current speed may in fact reflect debris or cover, and the longitudinal pattern could reflect unmeasured variables. Causal tests of tolerances and preferences are still needed.
