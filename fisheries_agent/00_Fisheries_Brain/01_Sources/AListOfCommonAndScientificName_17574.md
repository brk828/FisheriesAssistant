# A List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada (Second Edition)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-nomenclature

## Executive Summary

This document is the second edition of the standard reference list of approved [[Common Names]] and scientific names for fishes of the United States and Canada, prepared by the Committee on Names of Fishes of the [[American Fisheries Society]] (report presented at the 89th Annual Meeting, Clearwater, Florida, September 1959; printed 1960, reprinted 1966). It is a major revision of the 1948 first edition, retaining 560 of its 570 names and adding entries to reach 1,892 in total. The list aims at uniformity in vernacular nomenclature, sets out sixteen to nineteen guiding principles for choosing names, and provides a taxonomic checklist of accepted names with occurrence codes (Atlantic, Pacific, Freshwater) and authorities for scientific names.

## Background & Description

The Committee was chartered under the AFS and, from 1951, served jointly as the Committee on Common Names of Fishes of the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists. Members named in the report include Reeve M. Bailey (Chairman), Ernest A. Lachner, C. L. Hubbs, C. Richard Robins, Paul M. Roedel, W. B. Scott and Loren P. Woods. Financial support came from the [[National Science Foundation]] (NSF-G12424).

The document is organised as follows:

- **Part I:** Main taxonomic list (pages 6 to 50), arranged in phyletic sequence by class, order and family, with species alphabetised by scientific name within each family.
- **Part II:** Index to common names (pages 51 to 75), with one entry per species and cross-references to synonyms in wide use.
- **Part III:** Index to scientific names (pages 76 to 102).

Coverage includes all species from continental fresh waters and marine species in contiguous shore waters over the continental shelf to 100 fathoms, from Greenland and the northern Gulf of Mexico to the Mexico–US boundary on the Pacific coast, and the Arctic shores of Alaska and Canada. [[Hawaiian Fishes]] and most deep-sea fishes are excluded. Subspecies are generally excluded from separate naming.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The list is primarily taxonomic and nomenclatural rather than ecological, but it records habitat and distribution through occurrence codes: A (Atlantic, including eastern Arctic), P (Pacific, including western Arctic) and F (freshwater). Several entries note ecological context, such as:

- Lanternfishes (Myctophidae) are chiefly bathypelagic, with occasional stragglers over the continental shelf.
- Snipe eels (Nemichthyidae) inhabit the deep sea and are not treated.
- Many cavefishes, pupfishes and killifishes are restricted to single springs, drainages or desert basins (for example, the Devils Hole pupfish and Ash Meadows killifish), reflecting endemism in fresh water.
- Some sea-basin groups, such as the Bering Sea fauna and Caribbean species that sporadically reach southern Florida, are incompletely covered because of rarity or poor knowledge.

## Key Findings

**Scope and size of the list**
- The second edition contains **1,892 entries**, more than a threefold increase over the first edition.
- Of the 570 names on the 1948 list, **560 were retained**; the 10 removed proved to be synonyms or extralimital.
- Common names are provided for all native and successfully introduced species in the region, rather than only "better-known" fishes.

**Principles governing common names**
The Committee adopted 19 principles. The most important include:
1. One vernacular name per species or taxonomic unit; no two species may share an approved name. Only two fishes carry two approved names: redfish and ocean perch (*Sebastes marinus*), and cisco and lake herring (*Coregonus artedi*).
2. The word "common" should be avoided.
3. Hyphens, suffixes and apostrophes are omitted where possible (e.g., smallmouth bass), and names honouring persons are not admissible.
4. Common names should be stable and not tied to scientific names; changes in genus or species should not alter them.
5. Colourful, descriptive, American Indian and widely used vernacular names are favoured, even where they conflict with strict English usage or with other names (e.g., perch used for representatives of at least nine spiny-rayed families).

**Taxonomic checklist highlights**
- **Agnatha:** 3 hagfishes and 13 lampreys, including the Sea lamprey (*Petromyzon marinus*).
- **Chondrichthyes:** Sharks (about 60 species across more than 10 families), rays and skates (sawfishes, guitarfishes, electric rays, numerous *Raja* skates, stingrays, eagle rays, manta and mobulas), and chimaeras (ratfish).
- **Osteichthyes:** Among many groups, the list includes sturgeons, gars, bowfin, herrings and anchovies, salmonids (with notes on lake, landlocked and steelhead forms), minnows and shiners (the largest group, with well over 200 entries of *Notropis* and *Hybopsis*), catfishes, eels, killifishes, sticklebacks, cods and hakes, sea basses, sunfishes (including [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]), darters, perches, snappers, grunts, drums, mackerels and tunas, gobies, sculpins, flatfishes and pufferfishes.
- Notable species entries include the Atlantic and Pacific salmon (*Salmo salar*; *Oncorhynchus* spp.), the American eel (*Anguilla rostrata*), the Sea lamprey, the Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*) and the Bluefin tuna (*Thunnus thynnus*).

**Authorship convention**
- Scientific names include authorities. Where a species was described in a different genus, the author appears in parentheses (e.g., brook trout *Salvelinus fontinalis* (Mitchill)), following the International Rules of Zoological Nomenclature.

**Notes on the list**
- Footnotes flag taxa of special status: groups of cottids occurring only in Alaska, species assigned to alternative genera by some authors (e.g., *Coregonus* for *Leucichthys*, *Hybopsis* for several segregate genera), and species of recreational importance just outside the area (such as the black jack, *Caranx lugubris*).

**Editorial limits**
- The Committee states that decisions were made by majority vote, so no member endorsed every decision.
- Information the Committee possessed exceeded the published literature in some cases, but changes were generally withheld unless new data would appear shortly.
