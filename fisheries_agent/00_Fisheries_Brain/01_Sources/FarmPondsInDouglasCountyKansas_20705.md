# Farm Ponds in Douglas County, Kansas, and Their Use in Fish Production

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by [[Claude E. Hastings]] and [[Frank B. Cross]], published by the [[University of Kansas Museum of Natural History]], surveys the [[Farm Ponds]] of [[Douglas County Kansas]] and assesses their potential for fish production. It combines three data sources: [[Aerial Photography]] of pond numbers and sizes (1954), mail questionnaires to pond owners (1955–1956), and field sampling of 22 ponds in 1956. The authors conclude that although many owners wanted to produce fish, few ponds were managed for it. Only three of the 22 sampled ponds provided satisfactory fishing. [[Turbidity]] emerged as the most important limiting factor, and the study ends with recommendations for pond size, depth, fencing, and watershed management.

## Background & Description

The study was supported by the [[State Biological Survey of Kansas]]. It had three stated aims:

1. Count and size the farm ponds in Douglas County in 1954 using aerial photographs.
2. Collect additional pond information through questionnaires in 1955 and 1956.
3. Document fish species and growth rates from sampling 22 ponds in 1956.

**Methods**

- Pond inventory: all ponds were located and measured in October 1955 from photographs taken on June 29 and July 11, 1954, for the U.S. [[Soil Conservation Service]].
- Questionnaires: 100 of 484 sections were randomly chosen. Owners in these sections received one questionnaire per pond, with a follow-up mailing in February 1956.
- Field sampling: for each of 22 ponds, the team recorded area, average and maximum depth, turbidity (measured with a [[Jackson Turbidimeter]]), and the species, sizes, and ages of principal fishes. Samples were taken from May 24 to October 3, 1956, using seines, wire traps, trot lines, hoop nets, [[Fyke Nets]], gill nets, and [[Rotenone]] (5 per cent solution).
- Ages were determined from scales or pectoral spines.

Seines were the most-used gear. Wire traps and trot lines were ineffective in all ponds. Hoop and Fyke nets failed in turbid water but outperformed seines in one large, clear pond. Rotenone was very effective in the three ponds where it was used.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond inventory and distribution**

- At least 1,316 ponds existed in Douglas County in 1954.
- About 500 more were built between 1954 and 1960, giving almost four ponds per square mile by 1960.
- Table 1 groups ponds by size. The smallest class held 68.8 per cent of ponds and the next 20.7 per cent. Only 2.6 per cent exceeded one acre.

**Construction history and persistence**

- Construction rose progressively from 1915 to 1954. About 329 ponds (one-quarter of the total) were built in 1953.
- Ponds built in 1951 or earlier were more likely to dry: 40 of 72 (55.6 per cent) dried during 1952–1956. Only 9 of 50 ponds built after 1951 (18.0 per cent) dried. The authors attribute this to greater depth in newer ponds and to heavy silting of older ponds during the high runoff of 1951.

**Watersheds and water source**

- Surface drainage was the only water source for 80.0 per cent of ponds.
- Permanent pasture made up 75.2 per cent of watershed area. Cultivated land covered most of the rest, and woodland was insignificant.

**Use, management, and fencing**

- Livestock water was a purpose for 98.4 per cent of ponds, and the only purpose for 70 ponds (54.3 per cent). Fishing was a purpose for 51 ponds (39.5 per cent). Only one pond was built solely for recreation.
- Only 5.5 per cent of ponds were fenced, and livestock had access to some that were listed as fenced.
- About 16 per cent of ponds had been fertilized, usually to boost fish production.
- Overflow control was mostly by surface spillway. Vertical overflow pipes were present in 4 per cent of ponds and drainpipes in 8.1 per cent.
- Eighteen per cent of ponds had rooted aquatic vegetation, which the authors took as a sign of clear water. Livestock disturbance and turbidity were thought to exclude plants elsewhere.

**Fish stocking and owner interest**

- About 49.6 per cent of ponds contained fish.
- Stocking sources were state and federal hatcheries (36.0 per cent), streams (18.0 per cent), and other ponds (46.0 per cent).
- Owners were interested in fish production (77.6 per cent): 15.6 per cent for sport, 46.6 per cent for food, and 37.8 per cent for both.
- Pond fishing frequency averaged about 10 times per year, ranging from zero to 100.
- About 34.4 per cent of owners judged their ponds to be producing satisfactory fishing.

**Turbidity classification**

Ponds with turbidity of 50 parts per million (p.p.m.) or less were considered clear. Those above 50 p.p.m. were turbid. Turbidity among the 22 sampled ponds ranged from under 25 p.p.m. to 525 p.p.m.

## Key Findings

**Species composition**

- [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*) were the most frequent fishes, each found in 15 of 22 ponds.
- Eight ponds held no species other than these two.
- Ponds fell into two groups: 14 dominated by bullhead and/or green sunfish, and seven dominated by sport fishes such as [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and crappie.
- [[Channel Catfish]] dominated Booth 11-A Pond, which had been stocked with that species only about one year earlier.
- Other species recorded included carp, river carpsucker, fathead minnow, golden shiner, creek chub, [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Freshwater Drum]], and orange-spotted sunfish.

**Turbidity as the main limiting factor**

- The authors concluded that turbidity was the best single predictor of which fish species a pond would support.
- Turbidity was linked to shallow depth, wave action, cattle use, small size, and poorly vegetated watersheds.
- Buck (1956), cited in the paper, showed that high turbidity sharply limits growth and reproduction of bluegill and largemouth bass.
- Green sunfish and black bullhead dominated most turbid ponds. Crappie or bluegill dominated most clear ponds.
- Detwiler Pond had 525 p.p.m. turbidity. Its crappie and bluegill were abundant but stunted. After rotenone eradication, turbidity dropped to 25 p.p.m. or less.

**Depth and survival**

- Bass, bluegill, and crappie persisted in nine of ten stocked ponds with maximum possible depths of 8.0–20.5 feet (average 11.2 feet). They had apparently disappeared from three of five ponds at about 4.0–8.0 feet (average 5.2 feet).
- The authors recommended maximum depths of eight to ten feet.

**Growth and age**

- Bass and bluegill grew faster in clear than in turbid ponds. Bass reached a maximum age of six years.
- Black crappie grew fastest in Butell Pond (45 p.p.m.), which also held the best bass and bluegill growth in the study.
- White crappie grew more slowly than black crappie. Few exceeded seven inches.
- Green sunfish grew better in clear water and in ponds containing bass and crappie. The largest green sunfish, 8.3 inches at five years, came from turbid Kerns SW Pond.
- Black bullhead grew faster in clear ponds. Four-year-old bullhead averaged 8.3 inches and 0.25 pound.
- Channel catfish reproduction was documented only in Booth SW Pond, where young-of-the-year fish were found in 1956. Evidence on channel catfish growth in clear versus turbid ponds was described as inconclusive.

**Standing crops**

- D. Beeghley Pond: 142.60 pounds (about 428 pounds per acre).
- Walburn Pond: 33.36 pounds (about 400 pounds per acre).
- Kerns SW Pond: 37.17 pounds (about 409 pounds per acre).
- Standing crops per acre did not differ greatly between clear and turbid ponds. Species composition and growth did differ.

**Overall results**

- Only three of the 22 sampled ponds gave satisfactory fishing: Booth M, Butell, and Sportsmen's. Butell was probably the best.
- Few farm ponds met the conditions needed for sport-fish management. The authors listed them as one acre or more in size, eight feet or more in depth, turbidity under 50 p.p.m., stabilized watersheds of suitable size, proper initial stocks, drainage facilities, and livestock fencing.
- Most owners obtained stock from streams or other ponds rather than hatcheries. Some stocked species that conservation agencies did not recommend for ponds.

**Recommendations**

1. Research is needed on which food fishes and bait fishes suit small, turbid ponds. [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) was proposed for food production. Channel catfish grown in small ponds yielded 100 to 2,000 pounds per acre in State Biological Survey experiments, with higher yields from supplemental feeding and fertilization.
2. Owners of ponds meeting the requirements, and interested in sport fish, should consult state or federal fishery biologists and stock only recommended species.
3. Sport-fishing ponds should be at least one acre, with eight feet (preferably ten feet) or more of maximum depth, drainage facilities, livestock exclusion fencing, and permanently grassed watersheds that produce adequate but not excessive runoff.
