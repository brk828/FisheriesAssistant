# Northern Grand Canyon Catostomids: Population Dynamics and Hybridization

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined the sizes, movements, and potential hybridization of two indigenous [[Colorado River]] catostomids, the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), in the [[Grand Canyon]] and the [[Little Colorado River]]. Mark-recapture sampling from 1991 to 1995 used [[Cormack-Jolly-Seber Model]] estimates, seasonal comparisons, and [[Mitochondrial DNA]] analysis. Flannelmouth Sucker populations appeared relatively stable, while the putative hybrids were rare, small in number, and predominantly backcrossed toward the Flannelmouth Sucker. The authors argue that adult longevity may mask recruitment failure, so managers should not assume population stability reflects healthy recruitment.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin is a large, arid system that supports a native fish community with many endemic species. Catostomids are primarily benthic fishes of large, low-gradient rivers. The two focal species belong to subfamily Catostominae and are part of an indigenous but declining fish community. Both are of conservation concern, and the Razorback Sucker is federally listed as endangered.

The authors describe the Flannelmouth Sucker as a fish that spawns in tributaries and low-gradient streams in spring. Postreproductive adults remain in these habitats through summer and return to the mainstem in winter. Hybridization between the two species has been documented in the upper basin and is a conservation concern because hybrids can genetically swamp the rarer parent species.

Previous records of *X. texanus* in Grand Canyon are sparse:

- **1944:** first record, an angler catch at Bright Angel Creek (RM 87.8).
- **1963:** a single specimen caught between the Paria River confluence and Lee's Ferry.
- **1978:** a gravid female captured in the [[Paria River]] 100 m above its confluence with the mainstem, with two others observed.
- **1986:** the last reported capture, photographed and released above Bass Rapids (RM 107.7).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sampling took place in the Little Colorado River, a major tributary near the Grand Canyon, within the Navajo Nation in Coconino County, Arizona. Fish were captured mainly with hoop nets and passive gear, and effort was recorded as net-hours fished. Sampling occurred on 49 trips between July 1991 and June 1995, covering all four seasons.

The authors link the Flannelmouth Sucker's seasonal movements to habitat use. Captures were highest in spring and autumn, which they attribute to greater movement or use of shallower habitats. Most captures occurred well upstream of the confluence with the mainstem.

Longevity is an important life-history strategy for catostomids in western North American rivers. Adults can skip many spawning seasons and still contribute to a strong year class. However, a lack of recruitment can slowly drive a population toward extinction. The authors cite [[Lake Mohave]] Razorback Sucker as an example, where adult numbers stayed steady for over a decade while recruitment was chronically absent.

## Key Findings

### Population Size and Survival
- Flannelmouth Sucker population estimates (fish over 150 mm TL) were consistent across years. The authors describe the population as relatively stable during the study, with no significant differences among years in overall population size.
- Seasonal estimates showed a pattern of increase through spring, a peak in late spring or mid-to-early summer, and decline into autumn and winter. A smaller late-summer or early-autumn peak may reflect a response to late-summer rains.
- Of six seasonal comparisons, two were significant: summer versus autumn and autumn versus winter.
- Survival rates were relatively uniform across size classes in 1991–1993. Steep declines occurred in some size classes, and the largest fish (301–600+ mm TL) showed reduced survivability. Predation was judged an unlikely cause because the decline occurred in larger fish.
- The 2,578 Flannelmouth Sucker captures tallied across nine size classes ranged from 135 to 417 per class, with the largest counts in the mid-size classes.

### Hybrid Identification
- Forty-one putative hybrids were identified on morphology and recaptured a total of 60 times. Most individuals were recaptured only once (17 fish), while a single fish was recaptured seven times.
- Of the 41 fish, 85% (35/41) were male and 12% (5/41) were female; one fish was not sexed.
- Males expressed gametes in 46% (38/82) of captures, and 79% (65/82) were tuberculate.
- Mitochondrial DNA was evaluated in 12 putative hybrids. Seven (58%) carried *X. texanus* mtDNA.
- In synopsis, 8 of 9 individuals with hybrid-suggestive morphology evaluated electrophoretically and with RFLP analysis were hybrids. None was an F1 hybrid. Instead, they were backcrossed toward the Flannelmouth Sucker, at roughly 60–90% *C. latipinnis* ancestry.
- Hybrid population estimates were unadjusted for effort and consistently small, ranging from 0 to 136 (mean = 30) across 26 of 49 trips.
- Hybrid captures were 46% in spring, 32% in autumn, 12% in summer, and 10% in winter.

### Distribution and Movement
- Most captures (56%, 53/94) occurred approximately 3 km or more above the confluence, with one capture nearly 11 km upstream. The average capture distance above the confluence was 2.2 km.
- Groups of tuberculate male hybrids were captured with Flannelmouth Suckers at 3.1 km, 3.01 km, and 2.98 km above the confluence in November 1991, April 1992, and April 1995, respectively.
- Earlier electrofishing by Carothers and Minckley (1977–1979) and the 1990–1995 fieldwork of Valdez and Ryel produced no mainstem captures of *X. texanus*.

### Age and Longevity
- Scale-based age estimates reported eight or nine years for upper basin Flannelmouth Suckers. The authors note that scale regeneration and unreadable annuli make scales unreliable for maximum age.
- Opercular-bone estimates reached 10 years in Marble and Grand Canyons. Otolith data from the Green River estimated 30 years for a 530 mm TL fish, and another study suggested over 17 years for fish of 530–590 mm TL.
- The authors used body length groupings instead of age because aging methods were unreliable, and they stress that baseline longevity and age-specific survival must be defined.

### Conclusions and Management Implications
- The stable adult Flannelmouth Sucker population may reflect long-lived adults rather than extensive recruitment, so the apparent stability should be interpreted cautiously.
- Hybridization is occurring, but the hybrids are backcrossed toward the Flannelmouth Sucker and are rare. The authors note that the Razorback Sucker is nearly absent from the Grand Canyon mainstem.
- Long-term monitoring, age-based survival studies, and recruitment assessment are needed. The authors also suggest that managers should avoid "benign neglect" because adult senescence in long-lived catostomids can be rapid and drastic.
