# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes of Tonto National Forest, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1935 U.S. Bureau of Fisheries report by M. J. Madsen surveys the streams and reservoir lakes of the [[Tonto National Forest]] in Arizona. Its purpose is to collect physical, chemical, and biological data so that fish species, size, and numbers can be matched to each water body before stocking. The survey covers the [[Tonto Creek]], [[East Verde River]], and [[Cherry Creek]] watersheds, along with four reservoirs on the [[Salt River]]. It finds two stream types, a rapid headwater type that suits improvement and a slow lowland river type that does not. The reservoirs are chemically stressed, with almost no food for fish. The report closes with stocking tables for trout in streams and warm-water species in streams and lakes.

## Background & Description

**Scope and personnel.** The survey was a reconnaissance, not a complete study of any water body. The party consisted of M. J. Madsen (biologist in charge), Paul Klingenberg and Harry G. Moseley (assistant biologists), and Wm. Hudspeth (camp attendant). The text gives the field period as June 28 to July 22, 1954. This conflicts with the report date of May 1935 and the chemical data dated 1934, so the 1954 date is probably a scan or typographical error. The Arizona State Fish and Game Commission supplied deputy game agents and boats, and the [[Forest Service]] supplied maps.

**Forest and waters.** The forest lies south of the [[Mogollon Rim]] in the Tonto Basin, covers about 2,500 square miles, and ranges from 2,000 to 6,500 feet in elevation, with most land near 3,000 feet. Headwater streams are the only permanent waters that offer fishing. Many lower reaches dry up each summer because of irrigation diversion and evaporation. There are no important natural lakes, only four reservoirs on the Salt River, covering roughly 43 square miles at high water. These reservoirs supply much of the state's bass fishing. The road system is good, and most important waters can be reached by car.

**Survey methods.** A station was 200 yards of stream or an area that could be worked from an anchored boat on a lake.

- *Physical:* stream widths and depths were measured by rule and steel tape. Gradient and altitude came from U.S.G.S. maps. Water and air temperatures were taken with pocket Fahrenheit thermometers. Lake depth profiles used a Negretti-Zambra reversing thermometer. Turbidity was read with a 10 cm white disc.
- *Chemical (field):* pH by colorimetric tubes with bromthymol blue; free carbon dioxide by phenolphthalein titration against N/44 sodium hydroxide; [[Dissolved Oxygen]] by the [[Winkler Method]] (organic-matter removal step omitted); alkalinity by titration with 0.02 N sulphuric acid.
- *Biological:* fish were sampled with gill nets, seines, dip nets, wire traps, angling, and observation. Bottom fauna were collected with a square-foot net in streams and an [[Ekman Dredge]] in lakes. Plankton were taken with a standard net (11 cm opening, No. 20 bolting silk) and measured in cubic centimeters per haul.
- *Pool grading:* pools were scored for size, type, and frequency, then given an overall grade of A, B, or C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream types.** The forest's streams fall into two groups. The small, rapid headwater streams have pool and riffle relations near average, about 50% pools and 50% riffles. Some have been improved with artificial dams and deflectors by the Forest Service. The larger, meandering lowland streams have very large, shallow pools (usually under 4 feet), long wide riffles of fine gravel, shale, and boulders, and very low food production.

**Stream chemistry.** Stream temperatures were 76°F or less at higher elevations and up to 96°F at lower elevations. Gradient is usually under 700 feet per mile, averaging about 200 feet per mile. The report judged the streams generally chemically suitable for fish; full values are in Table 2.

**Aquatic food and plants.** Aquatic vegetation and algae were sparse. Slime algae formed a thin coat on riffles. [[East Verde River]] had considerable slime algae in larger still pools, and [[Haigler Creek]] had watercress near springs. Fish food, in order of abundance, was mayfly nymphs, caddisfly larvae, dipterous larvae, beetles, mollusks, dobsonflies (hellgrammites), mites, stoneflies, dragonfly larvae, roundworms, and flatworms. Terrestrial insects falling into the water were also an important summer food for game fish, but these were not quantified.

**Lake conditions.** The four reservoirs are primarily irrigation lakes, not fishing lakes. Water levels fluctuate greatly with irrigation demand. Shores have boulders, gravel, shale, mud, and bedrock. Turbidity is low, at 12 to 25 feet. Deep-water areas are mostly fine clay with no detritus, and spawning areas are stranded or smothered by fluctuating water. Chemistry shows low deep-water oxygen, high methyl orange alkalinity, high carbon dioxide, and a strong hydrogen sulphide odor near each outlet. Lake temperatures suit only warm-water fishes.

**Biological limits.** No rooted vegetation was found in any lake, probably because of water-level fluctuation. Ekman dredge samples did not produce a single bottom organism, and shore collections were also unproductive. Parasites and disease were rare in both streams and lakes.

## Key Findings

**Streams**
- Stream food counts ranged from 460 organisms per square foot (Haigler and Horton creeks, the highest) to 85 (station 2, Verde River). The average across all stations was 256. Mayflies and caddisflies averaged 93 per station.
- Horton and Haigler creeks had the richest food supply. Lower Tonto Creek and lower East Verde Creek were markedly deficient.
- Game fish in the streams, in order of abundance, were rainbow trout, European brown trout, eastern brook trout, catfish, and cutthroat trout. Suckers and bonytails occurred in all streams and were most abundant in the lower, warmer reaches. No [[Centrarchidae]] were taken, though the authors thought they might be present.
- Natural enemies (great blue heron, green heron, kingfisher) did no significant damage.
- The report recommends focusing development on the headwater streams and warns that too much effort went into building dams while cover and food were neglected. A good stream needs a balance of pools, riffles, and cover. Pools without cover or riffles are far from ideal.

**Lakes**
- No rooted vegetation or bottom food was found. The only food was scant plankton, terrestrial insects, and a few young fish.
- Fish, in order of abundance, were bonytails, carp, suckers, buffalo, [[Largemouth Black Bass]], sunfish, rock bass, crappie, and several unidentified minnows. All fish appeared in good condition.
- Lake maximum depths were given as 250 feet for Horse Mesa Lake in one section and 275 feet in another. Roosevelt Lake's depth was 40 feet at survey time.
- Because the lakes belong to an irrigation company, fishery regulation is largely impossible. The main recommendation is a closed season on largemouth black bass during spawning on the Salt River lakes. The survey did not gather enough spawning data to set dates, so the Arizona Game Commission should set them.

**Stocking plans**
- Stocking was based on a planting table developed by Dr. G. C. Embody and on guidance from Fred J. Foster of the Bureau of Fisheries. The authors stated that truly scientific stocking plans could not yet be drawn up.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] were recommended for cooler headwater streams, where fishing pressure is heavy enough to remove a planting in about two weeks, so natural reproduction is not expected.
- [[Brown Trout]] were recommended for streams with adequate food and spawning grounds but too warm for cold-water species.
- Lakes were stocked only with warm-water species. Warm-water stocking assumed that fish could sustain themselves for at least five years through natural reproduction.
- Table 6 lists about 4,750 rainbows annually for the East Verde River section, 5,975 rainbows for the Tonto Creek section, 14,000 rainbows annually for Haigler Creek, and 3,000 rainbows annually for Spring Creek. Lake bass were recommended at 384,000 for Roosevelt Lake (every fifth year), 300,000 for Apache Lake, 192,000 for Canyon Lake, and 108,000 for Stewart Mountain Lake. Marsh Creek, Fossil Creek, and Sharp Creek were judged unsuited.
