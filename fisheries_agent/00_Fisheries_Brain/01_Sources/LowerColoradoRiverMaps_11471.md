# Lower Colorado River Front Work and Levee System: Operation and Maintenance Program (1969)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This October 1969 booklet from the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (Region 3) presents facility maps and program status for the Colorado River Operation and Maintenance (O&M) Program on the lower [[Colorado River]]. The program is authorized under the [[Colorado River Front Work And Levee System Act]] of January 21, 1927, and was expanded in 1946. The lower river below [[Davis Dam]] is divided into ten operational Divisions. The document addresses river channel stability, sediment transport, water salvage, and fish and wildlife habitat, but it does not name any fish species, and it contains no fish population data.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The booklet compiles the program's facility maps, a symbol key, and a summary of problems, goals, and status for each Division.
- **Legal basis:** Work proceeds under the 1927 Act and later amendments. Authority was expanded in 1946 to permit an active O&M Program.
- **Historical driver:** Upstream dams, including [[Hoover Dam]], [[Imperial Dam]], [[Parker Dam]], and [[Davis Dam]], regulated flow but did not stabilize the channel. Each reservoir acted as a sediment trap. Clear water released downstream scoured the river below the dams, while reaches such as [[Topock Gorge]] and the reach above Imperial Dam aggraded. Sediment deposition raised water levels at [[Needles]], which led to emergency legislation.
- **Division structure:** The ten Divisions are [[Mohave Valley Division]], [[Topock Gorge Division]], [[Havasu Division]], [[Parker Division]], [[Palo Verde Division]], [[Cibola Division]], [[Imperial Division]], [[Laguna Division]], [[Yuma Division]], and [[Limitrophe Division]]. The Havasu Division was recently established to cover all of [[Lake Havasu]] and the river from Parker to Headgate Rock Dams.
- **Planning approach:** Comprehensive plans use [[Multiple-Purpose Planning]], which weights several objectives (water salvage, sediment control, fish and wildlife, recreation) to maximize overall public benefit, sometimes at the expense of any single objective.
- **Map set:** The booklet includes a location map (Plate 1) and facility maps (Plates 2 to 10) with drawing numbers, a list of drawings, and a symbol key covering dredge channels, riprap, levees, jetties, canals, drains, and background features.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sediment dynamics:** Reservoirs trapped sediment, and the clear water released downstream picked it up and redeposited it at the head of the next reservoir. Sediment trapping was a central concern across Divisions.
- **Backwater habitat:** Off-channel backwaters serve as fish and wildlife habitat. Sedimentation fills them, and once water depth falls to 2 or 3 feet, [[Cattails And Tules]] overrun open water. Isolated backwaters also risk stagnant and salty water without freshening flows.
- **Water salvage and phreatophytes:** Overwide, shallow channels lose water through evapotranspiration from wet soils and [[Phreatophytes]]. Lowering channel water levels reduces groundwater use by these plants.
- **Salinity:** The document identifies the need to minimize water-quality deterioration (increased salinity) within the Imperial Division.
- **Public use and safety:** Submerged trees (snags) in Lake Havasu, left after reservoir closure in 1938, became a hazard as boating and waterskiing replaced fishing as the dominant recreation. Fish and game agencies proposed marking the snags, but removal was judged necessary.
- **Stakeholders:** Work involved the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]], state fish and game agencies, the U.S. Coast Guard, the [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]], and, for the Limitrophe Division, the [[International Boundary And Water Commission]].

## Key Findings

**Channel and sediment control**
- Mohave Valley: constructed features reduce sediment transport into Topock Gorge and Lake Havasu by about 121,000 tons per year.
- Parker Division: sampling indicates about 73 percent of the sediment load is trapped in Imperial Reservoir. Bank erosion in some areas reached 45 feet laterally per year. The plan would reduce sediment arriving at Adobe Ruins by 788,000 tons per year.
- Palo Verde Division: sediment reduction of 310,000 tons per year.
- Cibola Division: sediment reduction of 350,000 tons per year into the Imperial Division.
- Imperial Division: about 73 percent of incoming sediment deposits outside the main channel, and about 27 percent is diverted at Imperial Dam. Trap efficiency of the reservoir may be declining.

**Fish and wildlife and recreation features**
- Topock Marsh is diked to hold water at elevation 455 feet, providing approximately 4,000 acres of open water for fisheries and wildlife management. It supplies water to the [[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]].
- Topock Gorge: deepening of 300 acres of selected backwaters is planned to improve fishery habitat.
- Parker Division: 500 acres of backwaters will be deepened, and about 100 acres of minor backwaters will be maintained for fish spawning and feeding.
- Cibola Division: inlet and outlet structures were completed at the newly formed [[Oxbow Lake]]. Plans for [[Three Fingers Lake]] and [[Cibola Lake]] were being prepared by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife.
- Yuma Division: dredging will deepen about 200 acres in [[Mittry Lake]] and 72 acres in the Yuma Island area.
- Havasu Division: snag removal across the reservoir area was targeted for completion in 1970.

**Status and decisions**
- Mohave Valley: channel stabilization is complete, and future work is mainly maintenance.
- Topock Gorge: a Secretarial directive suspended work in June 1968, after work began in October 1967 on the upper reach, which was never completed. Renewed opposition led to a modified plan limited to the upper 1.7 miles and lower 4.0 miles of the design channel. The Bureau of Reclamation and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife were directed to evaluate physical and ecological changes, especially below Blankenship Bend.
- Parker Division: the comprehensive plan was approved on September 19, 1969, but remaining work was deferred because the western boundary of the Colorado River Indian Reservation is uncertain. Work between Parker and Alligator Bend was completed in 1967.
- Palo Verde Division: channel stabilization is essentially complete. The dredge "Little Colorado" is deepening off-channel areas per fish and game agency plans.
- Imperial Division: no comprehensive program has been initiated. Ad hoc treatment continues for local problems, and the main emphasis is on reducing sediment inflow.
- Laguna Division: a settling basin traps sediment, which is pumped to dry-land storage. Removal will be needed for several years.
- Yuma Division: the May 1969 comprehensive plan was approved on August 15, 1969, by Acting Secretary of the Interior Dole. Work will begin as funds are made available.
- Limitrophe Division: channel capacity for floodflows is inadequate, but planning has not started because of higher-priority upstream work. Any work must be coordinated with Mexico through the International Boundary and Water Commission, since the river forms the International Boundary.

**Document limitations:** The booklet provides no fish species lists, population counts, or survey data. Its fish-related content concerns habitat features, dredging, and management intentions.
