# Pine Litter Import and Limnology of Arizona Trout Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #limnology #allochthonous-import

## Executive Summary

This University of Arizona master's-level thesis evaluates the physical, chemical, and trophic effects of ponderosa pine (*Pinus ponderosa*) litter on soft-water trout lakes in east central and southern Arizona. The principal study site was [[Rose Canyon Lake]], a 7.33-acre impoundment in the Santa Catalina Mountains, with supporting work at [[Hawley Lake]], [[Fool Hollow Lake]], [[Becker Lake]] and [[Woods Canyon Lake]]. The study was financed by the Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit. The author concludes that pine litter import, through its effects on alkalinity, color, oxygen demand and sediment buildup, likely outweighs its modest trophic value, and that litter is better leached on the watershed than washed into lakes.

## Background & Description

The thesis frames allochthonous (terrestrial) organic matter as an under-recognized input to fisheries ecosystems, citing earlier work on stream detritus, spring metabolism, pond fertilization and bacterial food chains. Arizona's desert climate, with dry spring and early summer periods followed by intense [[Monsoon Rainfall]] storms, and steep forested watersheds produce flash floods that carry litter into small impoundments.

The study area lies above roughly 7000 ft in ponderosa pine country, where most trout lakes in the southern half of the state are low in alkalinity and primary productivity. Fishery and biota noted at Rose Canyon Lake:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdneri*): the basis of the fishery, stocked in spring, summer and fall and largely "fished out" by winter.
- **[[Bluegill Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and **[[Golden Shiner]]** (*Notemigonus chrysoleucas*): present in large numbers.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides*): introduced from unknown sources.
- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis cyanellus*): reported by Kemmerer (1965) in 1964 but not collected in this study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake and watershed.** Rose Canyon Lake's dam was completed in 1958. Reported morphometry includes a maximum depth of 40 ft, shoreline development factor of 2.1 and a watershed of about 1500 acres. Granitic geology dominates, and canopy cover ranges from 100% in low areas to near zero on some slopes. Weather data came from Palisades Ranger Station, about 1.75 miles north of the dam; the author notes elevation-dependent temperature decline of 4.1 °F and precipitation increase of 4 to 5 inches per 1000 ft (after Shreve, 1915).

**Litter dynamics.** Most litter falls in October; litter drop is roughly 1000 lb/acre/yr for needles, implying about 1.5 million lb of needles subject to leaching and transport annually across the watershed. Buoyancy tests (six trials in 150-gal tanks) gave a mean floating time of 29.5 hours, after which needles sank rapidly. Because litter must remain buoyant until it reaches the lake, the author suggests high runoff may have carried floating litter over the spillway in summer 1966.

**Water chemistry.**
- Alkalinity was reduced by up to 50% in tap-water infusions, especially at high temperature, long duration and high litter concentration. Inflow alkalinity did not rise during peak litter drop, so the author concludes needles are not the source of lake alkalinity; dust deposition is the likely source.
- Pine infusions were yellow-brown, and optical density at 360 mµ was highest in fall lake water. Inflow color never reached high levels, suggesting color developed in the lake.
- pH dropped initially in infusions and then rose toward the original level with aeration.

**Oxygen demand and organics.** Oxygen was depleted below about 12 ft in fall 1965, with surface saturation near 60% after turnover. The author attributes high demand to allochthonous sources, since autochthonous production was low (gross photosynthesis estimated by Kemmerer at 155 g O₂/m²/yr). Antibiotic tests indicated that at least part of the oxygen demand is non-microbial.

**Phenolics and carbohydrates.** Phenolics accumulate in the hypolimnion during summer stratification and are depleted by bacteria and fungi after fall circulation. Carbohydrates were low in infusions and higher in newly fallen needles; glucose and xylose were the main sugars identified.

**Nutrients and trophic value.** Ammonia-nitrogen, nitrate-nitrogen and phosphate were released from needles. Dense bacterial and fungal growth developed in infusions, and McConnell (unpublished) showed higher growth of *Xenopus* larvae in infused pine water. Pine litter, however, has low trophic value: Nykvist (1959) reported only about 3% weight loss after 40 days of leaching, versus more than 10% for oak.

## Key Findings

- **Direct import was negligible.** Of 47 litter collectors set on 14 June 1966, 17 were recovered intact (35.8%). None contained pine needles; the only allochthonous material was one fir needle and one willow leaf. Import was judged insignificant as a food-chain base, at under 1% of Kemmerer's gross photosynthesis estimate.
- **Low alkalinity is a key constraint.** Watershed geology controls alkalinity, and pine litter reduces it further, potentially limiting primary production in Arizona trout lakes.
- **Color and oxygen demand are important.** Color was highest in fall, and oxygen demand may keep dissolved oxygen well below saturation for weeks after turnover, creating trout stress and possible hypolimnetic sulfide conditions.
- **Phenolics are variable indicators.** Tannin-type phenolics were highest in Rose Canyon's August 12, 1966 flash flood inflow; decaying [[Myriophyllum]] and [[Typha]] also produced phenolics, complicating interpretation.
- **Leaching on the watershed is preferred.** The author concludes that retaining litter on the watershed is more desirable than washing it into lakes, because slow decomposition and sediment buildup, combined with alkalinity loss, oxygen depletion and color, outweigh its trophic value.
- **Sampling limitations.** The thesis acknowledges that uneven litter distribution and loss over the spillway may underestimate import, and that dissolved organics may be more important than the macroscopic estimate indicates.
