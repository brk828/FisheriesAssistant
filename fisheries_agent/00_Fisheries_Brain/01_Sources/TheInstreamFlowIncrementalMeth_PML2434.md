# Instream Flow Incremental Methodology: A Primer for IFIM

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service / National Biological Service primer (Biological Report 29, March 1995) introduces the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM), a systems-analysis framework for evaluating how changes in streamflow affect fish habitat, recreation, and woody vegetation in regulated river systems. The primer explains the history of instream flow management, compares assessment techniques, describes the ecological basis of habitat-based modeling, and outlines a phased process for applying IFIM in water-resource decisions. Its central message is that no single "best" method exists; the appropriate tool depends on the decision context, and IFIM is intended for complex, multi-stakeholder negotiations.

## Background & Description

The primer was written by Clair Stalnaker, Berton L. Lamb, Jim Henriksen, Ken Bovee and John Bartholow for the [[National Biological Service]], Washington, D.C. It is organized into five sections: history of instream flow problems in the United States; choice of assessment methods; ecological underpinnings; philosophical foundations; and the practical application of IFIM. Its stated objective is to provide a short, accessible introduction and stepping stone toward certified training.

IFIM was developed by an interagency effort, with input from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], Soil Conservation Service, Army Corps of Engineers, Environmental Protection Agency, universities and state agencies. Over roughly 15 years it evolved from stream-reach hydrology into a river-network analysis integrating fish habitat, recreational opportunity and woody vegetation response. Key components include:

- [[Physical Habitat Simulation System]] (PHABSIM), the habitat-hydraulics model used to compute weighted usable area (WUA) versus discharge.
- Macrohabitat and microhabitat models, combined into total habitat.
- [[Habitat Time Series]] analysis, which displays available habitat across a historical hydrologic record.
- [[Suitability Curves]], which weight habitat attributes such as depth, velocity, substrate and cover by species and life stage.

The primer also describes the historical shift from standard-setting approaches (for example, the [[Tennant Method]]) toward incremental, multi-purpose analysis as hydropower, water supply and recreation demands grew.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The primer emphasizes several ecological principles underpinning IFIM:

- **Spawning and incubation:** Redd location, intragravel water exchange and dissolved oxygen govern embryo survival. Water velocity over nests above about 0.15 ft/s was cited as a significant cause of reproductive failure in smallmouth bass in lakes, and Lukas (1993) attributed most reproductive failure in North Anna River smallmouth bass to high velocities over nests.
- **Energetics and microhabitat selection:** Stream fish balance foraging gains against energetic cost, often occupying low-velocity resting areas adjacent to high-velocity feeding lanes. Predation risk also shapes habitat choice, following the Gilliam and Fraser (1987) framework of minimizing the ratio of mortality to foraging rate.
- **Habitat bottlenecks:** Adult populations are largely determined by recruitment, and limiting habitat events typically occur 1 to 3 years before maturation (Nehring and Anderson 1993; Bovee et al. 1994).
- **Longitudinal succession and the river continuum:** Macrohabitat zonation along a river reflects changes in channel structure, discharge, temperature and water quality, linking to the [[River Continuum Concept]].
- **Ecological bottlenecks:** Wiens (1977) concept of temporally variable constraints that depress populations below carrying capacity underlies the habitat bottleneck idea.

The primer also addresses managed flow regimes, including hydropeaking, flushing flows, ramping and the aquatic-terrestrial transition zone (ATTZ), and water-quality and temperature constraints on fish survival.

## Key Findings

- **No single best technique:** Experience and literature indicate that the choice of method "depends on the circumstances." Methods range from the simple [[Tennant Method]] and wetted perimeter approach (widely used in early planning) to PHABSIM (used for hydropower, controversial streams and federal permits) and full IFIM (for the most controversial project assessments).
- **Incremental versus standard-setting problems:** Incremental problems involve high-stakes, high-intensity negotiation over flow changes, while standard-setting problems seek a single fixed minimum flow. The primer frames these as a continuum rather than a strict dichotomy.
- **Cost:** The authors estimate that about 80% of IFIM studies for a single river segment could be completed within 12 months for under $45,000, though costs depend on model variables, sampling strategy, quality assurance, site accessibility and number of alternatives.
- **Research priorities:** A survey of IFIM users (Reiser et al. 1989) ranked as highest priority: defining the relation between flow, habitat and fish production; validating habitat-to-production relations; and developing new flow-requirement methods. Contemporary NBS research tests these concepts on anadromous salmonids in California and resident trout in Colorado.
- **Calibration and site-specific data:** Habitat simulations must be "calibrated" with samples of aquatic organisms and habitat use, analogous to calibrating physical and chemical models.
- **Habitat bottleneck hypotheses:** Bovee et al. (1994) found that multiple independent habitat events may affect adult populations, that limiting events occur over variable timescales, that both high and low flows may limit habitat, and that habitat for invertebrate food may matter more than habitat directly used by the fish.
- **Negotiation and planning:** The primer recommends structured phases (problem identification, study planning, study implementation, alternatives analysis, and problem resolution), emphasizing mutually acceptable decisions, the "no net loss" principle, and post-project monitoring.
- **Model limitations:** PHABSIM is criticized for using few variables (depth, velocity, channel index) and for ignoring biotic interactions such as competition and predation. The authors argue these variables were consistently important in riverine habitat studies, and that IFIM is more than a "brown model."

[[Interagency Collaboration]] and transparent, multidisciplinary teams are presented as essential to making IFIM results credible in contested decisions.
