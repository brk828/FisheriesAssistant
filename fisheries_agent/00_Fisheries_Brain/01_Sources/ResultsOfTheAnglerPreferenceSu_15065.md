## Executive Summary

This report presents results of the **1981 Angler Preference Survey**, conducted by the Arizona Game and Fish Department as the second phase of a three-year Arizona Angler Mail Survey Program. Unlike a taxonomic or morphological study, this document is a **socio-ecological fisheries management survey**, designed to quantify Arizona resident anglers' species preferences, trout-fishing behaviors, and facility/access preferences. Approximately 27,800 Class A and Class F resident license holders (~9% of the licensed population) were sampled, with a 41%+ response rate. Data were analyzed via SPSS and were intended to inform Arizona's **Cold Water Management Plan**, linking angler demand data to future fishery resource allocation decisions, particularly for [[Rainbow Trout]] and warm-water sportfish stocking and habitat management.

## Background & Description

No morphological, anatomical, or taxonomic data are provided in this source. The report is a demand/preference survey rather than a biological study. Species referenced (e.g., [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]], [[Common Carp]], [[Tilapia]]) are treated only as angling targets, not as subjects of physical description. The closest quantitative "size" data pertain to **angler-preferred creel size classes** rather than biological measurements:
- Two 8-inch + one 12-inch trout (preferred by ~45% of respondents)
- Six 8-inch trout (~32%)
- One 14-inch trout (~23%)

These three creel options were noted by surveyors as approximately equal in total biomass, reflecting a trade-off between trout **quantity vs. size** rather than actual growth or morphometric data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The survey implicitly maps the **recreational fishery niche structure** of Arizona waters rather than natural ecological relationships:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** dominate cold-water fisheries and are managed via Trout Stamp licensing (Class A + Trout Stamp, Class F). Trout habitat use is differentiated between **lake (lentic, boat-accessible)** and **stream (lotic, remote-access)** systems.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** and other warm-water species (**[[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Crappie]], [[Sunfish]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]]**) occupy multi-use reservoirs that often overlap spatially with designated "trout lakes," indicating shared habitat between cold- and warm-water assemblages in impounded Arizona waters.
- **[[Common Carp]]** and **[[Tilapia]]** represent the least-preferred and likely least economically/recreationally valued species in these systems, occupying a niche as tolerant, non-preferred warm-water species in reservoirs.
- Angler behavior data suggest a **niche partitioning of fishing effort**: boat-based anglers concentrate effort on lentic (lake) trout populations and catch more limits, while shore/stream anglers rely on bait-based methods in more remote, riverine habitats.
- Management implication: many Arizona "trout lakes" function as **mixed cold-water/warm-water habitats**, supporting both stocked trout and resident warm-water sportfish, with boating access influencing exploitation pressure on trout populations specifically.

## Key Findings

**A. Species Preference (Table 1)**
- Trout and Largemouth Bass were the top two preferred species overall (~75% and ~70% of respondents, respectively).
- [[Common Carp]] and [[Tilapia]] were least preferred.
- Preference for trout scaled strongly with Trout Stamp licensure: Class A w/ Stamp = 85% trout preference (lowest bass preference, 64%); Class A w/o Stamp = only 45% trout preference despite being unlicensed for trout; Class F = ~75% preference for both trout and bass.

**B. Trout Fishing Participation (Table 2)**
- 64% of all respondents fished for trout in 1980.
- Participation highest among Class A w/ Trout Stamp (88%), lowest among anglers without stamps (53%).

**C. Trout Limits**
- 78% of all respondents caught **no trout limits** in 1980; among trout-licensed anglers specifically, 74% caught none.
- Of anglers who did fish for trout: 66% caught no limit, 21% caught 1–3 limits, 13% caught 4+ limits.
- Boat anglers (especially those fishing lakes by boat) had the highest limit-catch success (41% caught ≥1 limit).

**D. Camping Facility Preferences**
- "Toilets and trash cans" most preferred (36%); "none/primitive" second (31%); full development (store/showers/RV) least preferred (13%).
- Preference for minimal facilities correlated with: high-limit anglers (>10 limits), fly/lure anglers, remote-access preference, and support for artificial-only water designations.
- Preference for developed facilities correlated with: boat-only lake anglers, easy-access preference, and bait anglers.

**E. Boating on Trout Lakes**
- Only 30% of all respondents used a boat on trout lakes; 44% fished trout lakes without a boat; 26% did not fish trout lakes at all.
- Class A w/ Trout Stamp had highest boating rate (37%).
- Notably, only 5% of non-trout anglers used boats on trout lakes despite many Arizona trout lakes also supporting warm-water fisheries and boating access — an "unexpectedly small" overlap in user groups.

**F. Access Preferences**
- 64% preferred "easy, automobile access within 1/4 mile."
- Class A w/ Trout Stamp had highest easy-access preference (71%); Class F anglers had strongest remote-access preference (44%).
- Remote-access preference correlated strongly with primitive-camping preference (66%).

**G. Trout Size/Number Preference**
- ~45% preferred two 8-inch + one 12-inch trout; 32% preferred six 8-inch trout; 23% preferred one 14-inch trout (roughly equal biomass options).
- High-limit anglers (>10 limits/yr) most strongly preferred the single large 14-inch trout (36%).
- Quantity-oriented anglers tended to use bait, oppose more artificial-only waters, and prefer developed facilities.

**H. Angling Method**
- Prepared bait was the dominant method (41%), followed by natural bait (28%), artificial lures (19%), and flies (12%).
- Class A w/ Trout Stamp anglers favored prepared bait most (48%).
- High-limit anglers (11+) had the greatest preference for fly fishing (21%).
- All-boat lake anglers had highest lure preference (34%).

**I. Artificial-Only Water Regulations**
- 69% opposed designating more artificial fly-and-lure-only waters; 31% supported expansion.
- Support for more artificial waters was elevated among: Class F licensees (35%), high-limit anglers (>10 limits, 44%), remote-access preference anglers (42%), single large-trout preference anglers (42%), and existing fly/lure anglers (77%/58% respectively).
- Bait anglers showed the lowest support for artificial-only water expansion.

**Management Relevance**
These findings were intended to feed directly into Arizona's **Cold Water Management Plan**, informing decisions on stocking policy (trout size/number trade-offs), facility development, access design, boating regulation, and expansion of artificial-lure-only water designations based on empirically measured angler demand segmented by license class and fishing behavior.
