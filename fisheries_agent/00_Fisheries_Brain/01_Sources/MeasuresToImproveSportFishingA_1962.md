## Executive Summary

This 1963 U.S. Department of the Interior report documents a large-scale chemical fish control ("rough fish eradication") operation conducted on the Green River in September 1962, prior to impoundment by the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The goal was to eliminate non-game ("rough") fish populations that would otherwise compete with introduced game fish (primarily trout) in the newly formed [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]]. Approximately 450 miles of river and tributaries were treated with 19,000 gallons of 5% rotenone solution. Potassium permanganate was used as a downstream detoxifying agent to protect aquatic life in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], located 45 miles downstream. Despite precautions, a toxic "block" of water passed through the Monument, causing fish kills and a measurable decline in aquatic invertebrates. All previously documented fish species were recovered in post-treatment surveys except the [[Humpback Chub]], which was locally extirpated from the affected reach. The report frames the operation as a net positive for future sport fishery development, projecting significant economic and recreational benefits from a resulting trout fishery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pre-impoundment river ecosystem**: The free-flowing Green River supported a diverse native non-game fish assemblage, which the report characterizes as being in direct competitive conflict with proposed introduced trout populations following dam closure.
- **Trophic/competitive dynamics**: Non-game fish were believed to threaten "optimum growth and survival" of introduced trout via resource competition, prompting the rationale for chemical eradication rather than co-management.
- **Rotenone mode of action**: Described as killing fish and aquatic invertebrates via suffocation (inhibition of cellular respiration), without harming birds or mammals; treated fish were considered safe for human consumption.
- **Detoxification chemistry**: Rotenone degrades via oxidation in flowing water; potassium permanganate was introduced as a supplemental oxidizing agent at Browns Park (29 miles below the dam, 16 miles above Dinosaur National Monument) to accelerate neutralization before the treated water reached protected downstream habitats.
- **Downstream impacts**: Despite detoxification efforts, a toxic "slug" of water passed through [[Dinosaur National Monument]], causing observable fish distress at Gates of Lodore, Echo Park, and Split Mountain campground, along with declines in aquatic insect populations.
- **Post-impoundment ecological trajectory**: The report acknowledges that dam-induced changes—flow regulation, altered water temperature regimes, and reduced downstream silt loads—would independently drive further "radical ecological changes," with unpredictable cumulative effects on native species' habitats.
- **Reservoir fishery niche**: The newly formed [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] was intended to support a stocked trout sport fishery, filling an artificial lentic niche created by damming a historically riverine (lotic) system.

## Key Findings

- **Scale of treatment**: ~450 miles of the Green River and tributaries treated with 19,000 gallons of 5% rotenone solution between September 4–7, 1962 (Pinedale, WY to a site 7 miles above the dam).
- **Detoxification protocol**: Potassium permanganate released at Browns Park from 6:10 a.m. September 8 through 5:00 p.m. September 11, timed to intercept the rotenone plume before it reached Dinosaur National Monument.
- **Unintended downstream mortality**: A toxic water mass unexpectedly passed through Dinosaur National Monument (45 miles downstream), killing fish and aquatic insects at multiple sites between September 9–14, despite detoxification measures — described in the report as an "unfortunate and unforeseen accident."
- **Species-level impact**: Post-treatment surveys (late September–early October 1962) recovered all previously known Green River fish species **except** the [[Humpback Chub]], which was not found in the Monument reach following the operation.
- **Invertebrate impact**: A documented decline in aquatic invertebrate abundance was reported, though insect populations were expected to recover relatively quickly based on precedent from similar rotenone operations.
- **Management rationale/controversy**: The operation was opposed by the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] (March–April 1961 resolution) on grounds it would destroy rare native fish populations, but was supported by state fish and game agencies and the [[Sport Fishing Institute]] as an established fisheries management technique.
- **Projected fishery outcomes**: Successful non-game fish suppression was expected to enable a trout fishery lasting an estimated **5 to 10 years**, supporting approximately **120,000 fisherman-days** of recreational use annually and generating over **$600,000** in annual economic benefit (based on the 1960 National Survey of Fishing and Hunting).
- **Broader project context**: This action was authorized under Section 8 of the Colorado River Storage Project Act (P.L. 458, 84th Congress, 1956), which mandated fish and wildlife mitigation measures across major storage units including [[Navajo Unit]], [[Glen Canyon Unit]], [[Flaming Gorge Unit]], and [[Curecanti Unit]]. A comparable, smaller rotenone treatment had already been completed at the Navajo Unit in fall 1961.
