# Desert Fish Conservation Reports: Tecopa Pupfish, Green River Rare Fishes and Sonoran Desert Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document gathers three short items from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] and related desert fish research: a 1977 survey of endangered [[Tecopa Pupfish]] habitats in California, a report on the status and habitat preferences of rare fishes in the [[Green River]] of Utah, and a Sonoran Desert report on native fish status, species translocations and aquatic habitat management in Arizona, Sonora and adjacent regions. The common theme is the decline of native desert fishes under drought, water withdrawal, reservoir construction and introduced species, and the need for better field data and conservation planning.

## Background & Description

**Tecopa pupfish survey (California).** All known aquatic systems in the Tecopa area of southeastern California were surveyed in summer 1977 to locate populations of the endangered Tecopa pupfish. Many artificial and natural habitats held populations that needed taxonomic evaluation, since some could be transplants rather than native stock. Habitats were assessed for basic water chemistry, physical conditions and invertebrate fauna. The results were compiled in a report to the California Department of Fish and Game.

**Rare fishes of the Green River (Utah).** Over 19,000 fish were collected by seines and electrofishing in August and September 1977. Adults and juveniles of four rare species were studied: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Habitat preference was described for each life stage (see Key Findings).

**Council administration and bibliography.** The Council's 1976 bibliography of desert fish literature (articles known as of March 1977) was proposed for printing in 1,200 to 1,500 copies, to be distributed through [[Death Valley National Monument]] and the Council membership. Pete Sanchez requested additional mailing addresses. A map of aerial coverage was pending final editing.

**Sonoran Desert fish fauna (Arizona/Sonora).** The report by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) reviews populations across the Rio Yaqui, Gila, Aravaipa and lower Colorado systems, with [[Razorback Sucker]] age and transfer data forming a major part of the content.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drought and water use.** Widespread drought in the American Southwest reduced flows in essentially all natural streams. Native fishes were persisting well, and declines were noticeable mainly where humans had altered habitats. Groundwater pumping had increased rapidly in both the United States and Mexico, and surface waters were declining.
- **Reservoirs.** [[Lake Mohave]] formed after [[Davis Dam]] closed (about 1951 to 1953 by the report's dating). [[Roosevelt Lake]] was nearly dry during the drought. The report notes that razorback suckers tend to spawn in newly filled reservoirs.
- **Lower Colorado River.** Mullet (*Mugil cephalus*) and ladyfish (*Elops affinis*) populations were considered doomed to extirpation as natural waters dried through [[Colorado River]] regulation.
- **Santa Clara Slough, Sonora.** This slough grew dramatically after increased canal inflow and tidal bore flooding from the [[Sea of Cortez]] during tropical storms.
- **Introduced species.** [[Tilapia]] (*Tilapia zillii*), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) and fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*) were recorded in several systems. Repeated trout stocking was a pressure on Mexican stonerollers in Chiricahua streams.
- **Habitat status.** Native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]] were in excellent condition despite low discharge. *Meda fulgida* was the most abundant species in many habitats. Data from the [[Gila River]] provided comparative insight on desert stream ecology, including aquatic invertebrates.
- **Rio Yaqui.** The fauna was judged of critical concern. The San Bernardino Ranch (east of Douglas, Arizona) was for sale, and its change in ownership could affect native fish protection.

## Key Findings

**Tecopa pupfish (California)**
- Surveys were designed to find all populations and evaluate whether they were native or transplanted. Taxonomic review was required.

**Green River rare fishes (Utah), habitat preferences**
- Over 19,000 fish were collected in August and September 1977.
- *Young-of-the-year* [[Colorado Squawfish]] preferred backwaters with low velocity, a silt substrate and 1 to 2 feet of depth.
- Juvenile squawfish preferred water deeper than 1 foot but were found mainly in eddies and runs.
- Young-of-the-year humpback chubs used backwaters and eddies with low velocities, a depth of about 2 feet and a silt and sand substrate. Juveniles used a wider range of microhabitats but preferred velocities less than 0.5 feet per second.
- Adult humpback chubs used a variety of habitats, with no clear preference for velocity, substrate or depth.
- One young-of-the-year squawfish was tagged in a backwater to study movement. Few recaptures were made, indicating movement, but its extent was not determined.
- Many introduced fishes were found, and the report stressed their impact on native species.

**Desert Fishes Council administration**
- The Council planned a bibliography of 1,200 to 1,500 copies and a newsletter built from annual reports of 12 Great Basin area coordinators.
- Resolution 76-1 (commendation of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service effort to develop [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as an endangered fishes refuge) and Resolution 76-2 (condemnation of *Tilapia zillii* in natural U.S. waters, with caution for other tilapias) were disseminated.
- A letter supported endangered status for a *Gila* species and threatened status for *Xyrauchen texanus* and was sent to the Colorado Division of Wildlife.
- On [[Collecting Permits]], agencies advised that applications include full justification of need. A permit may be issued within hours in California when sufficient data accompany the application.

**Sonoran Desert fauna (Minckley report)**
- Razorback suckers in [[Lake Mohave]] were aged by scales, with 1974 fish ranging from 17 to 23 years old and a mean age of 21 years. The large population likely hatched around 1953, about two years after [[Davis Dam]] closed.
- Adults made up more than 50% of fishes caught in nets and more than 50% of fishes seen by divers, but little or no recent reproduction was apparent, even with spawning observed.
- The report recommended transferring adult razorback suckers from Lake Mohave to the [[Salt River]] upstream of Roosevelt Lake this winter, to exploit spawning in newly filled waters.
- A Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) population at Cieneguita, Arizona, had a Bureau of Land Management habitat management plan accepted. Hatchery *P. o. sonoriensis* reproduced under hatchery conditions.
- A stock of *Poeciliopsis* (Santa Clara Slough, Sonora) was maintained jointly by Arizona Game and Fish and Arizona State University. Stocking into the Gila drainage was anticipated.
- Studies of Aravaipa Creek, funded by the Bureau of Land Management, were to finish in 1978.
- Plans to introduce [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) into the Gila River were supported by available information.
- Populations of Mexican stonerollers (*Campostoma ornatum*) persisted in Ruker Canyon and Leslie Creek, but the latter was threatened by low flows. Yaqui sucker (*Catostomus bernardini*) and an unidentified *Notropis* species were believed nearly extirpated.
