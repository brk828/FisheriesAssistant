# Trout Unlimited Advocacy Strategy: Interview with President Charles Gauvin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 *American Angler* interview with [[Charles Gauvin]], President of [[Trout Unlimited]] (TU), describes a shift in the organization toward a stronger national advocacy presence in Washington, D.C., while keeping its grassroots focus on [[Trout and Salmon Habitat]]. Gauvin argues that local stream victories can be undone by federal legislative or regulatory changes, so the national office must build legal, scientific, and policy capacity. The interview also covers a financial deficit inherited from the late 1980s, the membership base, how chapters and the national office share work and dues, and TU's stance on non-fly-fishing anglers. It does not present research data; it is a policy and organizational statement.

## Background & Description

**Leadership and background.** Gauvin grew up in the Providence, Rhode Island area near [[Narragansett Bay]], attended [[Brown University]], and earned a law degree from the University of Pennsylvania. Before joining TU's national staff, he worked on environmental law and policy in Rhode Island, practiced energy law in Maine (largely [[Hydroelectric Licensing]]), and at the firm [[Beveridge and Diamond]] handled [[Clean Water Act]] matters, especially [[NPDES Permitting]]. He had been a TU member for about 10 years and began volunteering on land-conservancy and legal matters in the late 1980s. He was hired as Executive Director after the position opened in late 1990.

**Organizational scale.** Using a conservative count of two people per family membership, TU had approximately 68,000 members. Counting individuals alone yields about 60,000, so family memberships were relatively few. Annual dues were $20 and had been raised to $25.

**Structure and funding.**
- Local [[Trout Unlimited Chapters]] are the entry point for volunteer activism. State councils let members advance into leadership, and some members reach the national board.
- The [[Embrace A Stream]] program funds habitat-improvement projects. Chapters submit proposals to national headquarters, which evaluates them, seeks outside funds from individuals, corporations, and foundations, and monitors results.
- About 10 to 15 percent of dues returns to chapters. The rest supports national membership services, a national conservation banquet program, a subscription to *Trout* magazine, and national resource advocacy. Administrative costs are roughly 20 to 25 percent, which Gauvin described as consistent with other conservation organizations.
- Fewer than 25 percent of members participate in chapter activities. Most join to support trout and salmon habitats.

**Financial history.** Gauvin said TU's financial problems were real, not rumor. Membership growth in the 1980s led to revenue projections that exceeded what later materialized as membership-based revenue declined at the close of the decade. He inherited a substantial deficit, which he believed had nearly been erased by the time of the interview. Remedies included staff reductions, unfilled vacancies, reduced travel, and a favorable new office lease. The board had already started expenditure reductions before he arrived.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The interview addresses several river systems and policy issues affecting trout and salmon habitat:

- **[[Glen Canyon Dam]] flow regime (Arizona):** The Arizona council pushed for changes in the dam's flow regime. Gauvin met with Interior officials to urge an interim flow regime pending completion of an environmental impact statement on a final regime. Related advocacy supported the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]].
- **[[Central Valley Project]] (California):** TU supported the [[Central Valley Improvement Act]], sponsored by Senator Bradley, to improve fish and wildlife conditions in the Central Valley Project, including the [[Sacramento River]] and other streams. Hearings were held in California, and TU was building a national base of support for this federal legislation.
- **Sacramento River spill:** TU issued a public statement on a spill affecting the Sacramento River. The organization was weighing legal action, negotiation, or another approach.
- **Policy threats:** Gauvin named the [[Clean Water Act]], wetland protections, and [[Hydroelectric Power Regulation]] as areas where congressional amendments or weakening could erase local and regional gains.
- **Fishing methods:** TU would continue to urge restrictions on baitfishing and other invasive techniques where resource conditions required them.

## Key Findings

1. **Dual-track advocacy model.** Gauvin's central argument is that grassroots stream restoration and national-level advocacy are complementary. Local successes depend on federal law staying intact.
2. **Expanded national capacity.** TU was in a strategic-planning exercise that could add up to about six full-time staff in Washington, D.C., focused on fisheries biology, resource economics, governmental affairs, and law and policy.
3. **Broader advocacy channels.** Advocacy will occur in Congress, before federal agencies, and in court. Gauvin stressed that TU is not a lobbying organization, because nonprofit rules limit substantial lobbying spending.
4. **Vision of a legal and science firm.** Gauvin described his goal as making TU "the hardest-hitting and most-effective public interest law and science firm," serving councils, chapters, and trout and salmon themselves. Current examples include reviewing fishing regulations for legal defects and advising on the administrative record for judicial review.
5. **Financial recovery.** TU reduced its deficit through staff cuts, unfilled positions, lower travel costs, and a lease renegotiation. Gauvin believed the deficit had nearly been erased.
6. **Membership base.** Membership was about 68,000 (60,000 individuals). The organization says it wants to serve both fly fishers and multi-method anglers. Gauvin acknowledged he had no empirical data on the fly-fishing share and said a non-purist segment is "small-but-significant."
7. **Responsiveness to breaking issues.** Gauvin said TU wanted to respond faster to newsworthy events. He said the main barrier in the past was staff capacity, not a lack of will.
