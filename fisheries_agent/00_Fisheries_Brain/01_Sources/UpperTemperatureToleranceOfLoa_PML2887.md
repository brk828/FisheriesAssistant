# Upper Temperature Tolerance of Loach Minnow under Acute, Chronic, and Fluctuating Thermal Regimes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Widmer]] and colleagues measures the upper thermal tolerance of the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitis*), a small, benthic stream fish listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986. Four laboratory methods were compared: the [[Lethal Thermal Method]] (LTM), [[Acclimated Chronic Exposure]] (ACE) at static temperatures, ACE with diel temperature fluctuations, and the [[Chronic Lethal Method]] (CLM). Tolerance depended on method and exposure time, with lethal temperatures as low as 28°C recorded under long exposure. The authors recommend managing loach minnow habitat to keep water temperatures below 28°C.

## Background & Description

The loach minnow is a small (<80 mm total length) stream fish endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona and New Mexico and the [[San Pedro River Basin]] in Arizona and Sonora, Mexico. Its range has contracted by more than 85% in the last 75 years. The [[Recovery Plan]] calls for research on habitat requirements and on tolerance to extreme temperatures.

Temperature affects nearly all biochemical, physiological, and life-history processes in fishes. Historically, small Gila basin streams reached 24–40.3°C in July and August, with daily fluctuations of 15–20°C. Groundwater pumping and irrigation diversions have reduced surface flows over the past 100 years, removing riparian shade and causing channel drying. These changes make southwestern streams more vulnerable to higher and more variable temperatures. Loach minnow have died in [[Aravaipa Creek]] at water temperatures of 30.5°C (Deacon and Minckley 1974) and 34.5°C (July 2002).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study fish:** Wild-caught and F1 hatchery-raised fish from Aravaipa Creek, held at the University of Arizona Environmental Research Laboratory for at least three months before experiments. Captive-bred fish were used to reduce collection impacts.
- **Husbandry:** Fish were fed a dry food mixture, with brine shrimp supplements. Water was partially changed with dechlorinated municipal water, and ammonia, pH, and dissolved oxygen were monitored.
- **Acclimation temperatures:** 25°C and 30°C, both common in natural loach minnow habitat.
- **Fluctuating regimes:** Sinusoidal 24-h cycles that mimic natural diel stream temperature changes, based on [[Stream Temperature Dynamics]].
- **Parasite context:** Some fish were infected with the [[Yellow Grub]] (*Clinostomum complanatum*), which affected LTM results.

## Key Findings

**Lethal Thermal Method (LTM)**
- Death at 36.8 ± 0.2°C (mean ± SE) for fish (37–49 mm TL) acclimated to 30°C, and 36.4 ± 0.07°C for fish acclimated to 25°C.
- LTMax rose 0.14°C (95% CI 0.04–0.23) per 1°C increase in acclimation temperature, and 0.09°C per 1 mm increase in TL (R² = 0.56).
- Fish infected with yellow grub had an LTMax 0.39°C lower than uninfected fish after accounting for TL. Infection severity (2–7 grubs) had no effect.

**Chronic Lethal Method (CLM)**
- Mean temperature at death was 33.4 ± 0.1°C for fish 25–35 mm and 32.9 ± 0.4°C for fish 45–50 mm.

**ACE with Static Temperatures**
- The 30-day LT50 was 30.6°C (95% CI 30.3–31.0).
- All fish died at 32°C, with none surviving beyond 6 days.
- No fish died at 28°C, but growth was slowed, suggesting sublethal stress.
- Growth declined by 1.27 mm (95% CI 0.65–1.89) for every 1°C increase.

**ACE with Diel Fluctuations**
- Fluctuations with a 32°C peak were tolerated better than static 32°C exposure: more than 80% of fish survived, versus none under static 32°C.
- Survival under fluctuating treatments was 83.3–96.7% (the highest mortality, 16.7%, occurred in the highest-mean treatment).
- All fluctuating treatments reduced growth relative to ambient (25–29°C) controls, though the three fluctuating treatments did not differ from each other.

**Conclusions and Management Recommendations**
- Loach minnow tolerate brief exposures to 32°C, but prolonged exposure is lethal.
- Fluctuations reduce stress but do not create stress-free conditions.
- Authors recommend managing habitat to keep water temperatures below 28°C to protect fish against temperature spikes and compounding stressors such as disease and competition.
- Laboratory methods differ from field conditions, so lethal temperatures from rapid ramping may not match natural exposures.
