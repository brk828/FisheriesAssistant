# Distribution, Migratory Behavior, and Habitat Use of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey open-file report uses [[Sonic Telemetry]] to describe the movements, habitat use, and thermal ecology of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], which currently supports most wild individuals of the species in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Ten adult and six juvenile fish were tracked from November 1994 to July 1997. Adults showed both pre- and post-spawning migrations, assembled in early December between river kilometer (RK) 26 and RK 90, and spawned between mid-January and March at multiple sites. Juveniles were cover-oriented and moved very differently from adults. The authors conclude that predation by [[Nonnative Predators]] is the main barrier to recruitment, and they recommend further study and a revised [[Stocking Protocol]].

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is a long-lived catostomid, with many individuals exceeding 30 years of age and believed to be nearing the end of their estimated lifespan. Most earlier research focused on adults, and little was known about early life stages. Lake Mohave was historically the main nursery for about 95 percent of the wild population. Population monitoring has shown a 60 percent decline over the past decade. Adults remain common, but juveniles are rarely collected.

The study was run with the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] in cooperation with [[Arizona State University]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[National Park Service]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and the Nevada Division of Wildlife. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] coordinated the recovery stocking effort.

**Study design (Methods)**
- **Adults:** 20 fish were captured at Yuma and Tequila coves (RK 34 and RK 39) in November 1994. They averaged 609 mm (555–680 mm) and 2.78 kg (1.75–4.21 kg).
- **Juveniles:** 25 fish from grow-out ponds were released in groups of four at Red Light Cove (RK 10), Dandy Crossing (RK 45), Owl Point (RK 47), Cove at RK 71, and Cross Current Rapids (RK 92). Juveniles averaged 338 mm (315–392 mm).
- **Transmitters:** Adults carried internally implanted tags. These were about 6 g, 16 × 55 mm, operating at 73 kHz, with an estimated battery life of about 14 months, a detection range of about 1,000 m, and body-temperature accuracy of ±0.25 °C. Five juveniles received externally attached 8 × 35 mm, 45-day transmitters.
- **Surveys:** A grid of 71 [[GPS Waypoints]] spaced 1,600 m apart was surveyed weekly by boat, with two complete surveys per week for 18 months. Vertical temperature profiles were taken with a Hydrolab.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir habitat.** Lake Mohave differs from riverine habitat in several ways. Water visibility exceeded 1 m, which made spawning aggregations easy to find and observe. Lake temperatures ranged from 12 to 30 °C, flows from none to 3 m/s, and depths could exceed 40 m. Fish could use both littoral and pelagic zones. Upstream of [[Willow Beach]], the water was cold because of hypolimnetic releases from [[Hoover Dam]]. Further downstream, the reservoir stratified in summer.

**Predation.** Juveniles use shallow, warm, vegetated shorelines. These are also the habitats used by nonnative predators: red shiner, green sunfish, bluegill, [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. Predator distributions and densities have expanded because of channel degradation, introduced fish populations, and sport-fishery management. The authors argue that these shorelines have become "lethal traps" for stocked fish.

**Boom-and-bust history.** Sucker populations previously rose and fell after the inundation of [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Roosevelt]], and [[Lake Mead]]. Meaningful recruitment has occurred only where non-native predators were largely absent.

## Key Findings

**Adult movement and range**
- Mean adult daily movement was about 521 m/day (range 242–788 m/day). Females (x̄ = 582 m/day) moved slightly faster than males (x̄ = 445 m/day). The OCR of the source is partly garbled at this value.
- The mean linear range was about 39 km (18–72 km). Females ranged 42 km (29–72 km) and males 35 km (18–51 km).
- One fish (#348) moved more than 17 km in fewer than 24 hours. Others stayed in the same general area for weeks.

**Spawning behavior**
- Adults assembled in early December between RK 26 and RK 90, with aggregations often numbering in the hundreds. Tagged adults spawned from mid-January to March.
- During the January 31–March 15 spawning peak, females were significantly more active than males (ANOVA F = 6.79, P = 0.0314). Males tended to remain at or near spawning sites, while females visited them only when ready to spawn. Females may therefore spawn with several males, which could help explain the high genetic diversity reported for this population.
- The authors estimate that the pre- and active-spawning period may last up to six months (November to late March or April), compared with 4–6 weeks in the Green and Colorado rivers.
- Nine of ten sonic-tagged adults used several spawning areas regardless of capture site. Post-spawning migrations were as common as pre-spawning ones, and about 60 percent of fish moved upreservoir after spawning.

**Summer and non-spawning habitat**
- Six fish (60%) used the area between RK 69 and RK 50, where cold hypolimnetic water from Hoover Dam mixes with warmer reservoir water. Two adults summered along the Nevada shoreline in [[Cottonwood Basin]] (RK 26–32), and two used both areas.
- [[Owl Point]] (RK 45) was the most heavily used one-mile reach. All ten study adults used it during the spawning season, and four used it during extended non-spawning periods.
- [[Chalk Cliffs]] (RK 69) to [[Cottonwood Basin]] area: seven fish used the Chalk Cliffs–Hoover mixing zone in summer.

**Habitat selection and thermal ecology**
- Fish were usually within 50 m of shore (chi-square test, P < 0.001). Fidelity was strongest during spawning, when mean distance from shore was less than 30 m.
- Fish were typically found at depths of 3.1–16.8 m, with most records under 10 m. One fish was observed deeper than 30 m. The reservoir's longitudinal temperature gradient did not affect distribution.
- Body temperatures for adults ranged from 12 to 28 °C overall, but summer preference was 18–22 °C. Adults seldom occupied water above 25 °C, even though surface temperatures peaked near 30 °C.
- Vertical movement occurred, but depth was not correlated with time of day. Adults appeared to regulate body temperature by moving in depth, and they showed a stronger preference for temperature than for depth.

**Data volume**
- Nearly 2,000 fish detections were recorded, including 637 daily detections for the 11 surviving fish. More than 1,000 hourly observations were added.
- More than 800 body-temperature readings were taken, 147 of which were matched to vertical temperature profiles.
- Of the internally implanted transmitters, about 50 percent of adults were still moving 14 months after release. Of the juvenile tags, four of five fish were tracked for the full 45-day life of the transmitter. The authors suggest transmitter retention was similar to or better than in other studies.

**Juvenile behavior**
- Young-of-year juveniles moved erratically for the first ten days after release, traveling 1,193 to 3,750 m/day across the pelagic zone. Afterward, they moved toward shore and sheltered in protected coves, often remaining sedentary for days or weeks.
- One young fish (#2273), reported as two years old in the Results and three years old in the Executive Summary, remained in one cove for 21 weeks. In November 1995 it began moving widely. It traveled a minimum of 145 km, reaching within 3 km of [[Davis Dam]] and later within 8 km of Hoover Dam. The authors call this "highly speculative" evidence of maturation-related roaming. They warn that dispersal of young adults could cause losses at stocking sites with surface withdrawal or major diversions. They also note the fish may have been eaten by a large striped bass.

**Management recommendations**
1. Study the migratory behavior of subadults, which may be vulnerable to entrainment, passage problems, and stranding.
2. Examine predator-prey relationships between early life stages and introduced predators. Identify habitat conditions and predator thresholds needed for measurable recruitment.
3. Develop a lower-basin stocking protocol. Prioritize areas by habitat, stream length, and threats. Stock fish larger than 40 cm when predators are less active (winter), and refine acclimation and release methods.
4. Determine whether reservoir fluctuations shape predator densities, and whether reservoir operations can create shallow vegetated habitat that reduces predators and improves survival of stocked fish.
