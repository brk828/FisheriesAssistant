# Range Expansion and Environmental Impact of the Introduced Walking Catfish in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clariidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two items. The main paper, by [[Walter R. Courtenay Jr]] and [[Woodard W. Miley II]], documents the spread of the introduced [[Walking Catfish]] ([[Clarias Batrachus]]) across southern [[Florida]] after escapes from an aquarium-fish farm in the mid-1960s. By 1974 the species occupied more than 8,750 km² in six Florida counties, later extended to ten. The authors judge the threat to native fishes and to fish-farming interests to be serious. A second item, by [[E. P. Lichtenstein]], [[T. T. Liang]] and [[B. N. Anderegg]] (University of Wisconsin), reports that herbicides can enhance insecticide toxicity under laboratory conditions, and that soils and turbulent water reduce this effect.

## Background & Description

*Clarias batrachus* is an air-breathing catfish native to Southeast Asia, from eastern India to Vietnam. It was prized in the [[Aquarium Fish Trade]], where albino individuals were imported from Bangkok, Thailand, at about 3 to 4 cm in length. The species grows to about 60 cm and 600 g and is not valued as food or sport fish. Imports rose sharply in 1964 and 1966. The dark colour phase is typical of Florida populations; the specimen pictured was not albino.

In 1964 or 1965, a fish farmer west of [[Deerfield Beach]] in northern [[Broward County]] stocked outdoor ponds beside drainage canals. From 1965 through 1967, fish escaped into the canals and began breeding. Florida's [[Drainage Canal Network]] is largely interconnected, and its flood-control and salinity structures do not stop a fish that can crawl over land or swim over barriers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range expansion.** Specimens were first found in 1968 in canals about 16 km from the escape site. Records then followed a steady pattern:

- 1969–1970: canals south of [[West Palm Beach]], and the eastern [[Hillsboro Canal]] and [[West Palm Beach Canal]] above salinity structures.
- 1970–1974: the [[Lake Okeechobee]] area, including the [[Indian Prairie Canal]], [[Taylor Creek]], and the [[St Lucie Canal]], which connected the species to the [[St Lucie River]] and Fort Pierce.
- Early 1974: 50 specimens in a Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission pond, which they reached by walking 1.2 km from [[Nubbin Slough]].
- October 1974: seven specimens in northeastern [[Glades County]], 26 km northwest of Lake Okeechobee.
- Late 1973–1974: spread southward into the [[New River Canal]], [[Water Conservation Area 3]], the [[Snake Creek Canal]], and the [[Tamiami Canal]], and across [[U.S. Highway 41]] near the [[Everglades National Park]].

The authors state that the range was over 8,750 km² in six counties in 1974, an invasion of more than 16% of Florida's area in under ten years. A footnote notes the figure was extended to ten counties in April 1975. Projected expansion within a year or two included [[Kissimmee]] and [[Osceola County]], [[Highlands County]], [[Polk County]], [[Fort Myers]] via the [[Caloosahatchee Canal]], the [[Big Cypress Swamp]], and the Everglades National Park.

**Human-mediated spread.** After Florida placed the species on its restricted list of exotic fishes in 1968, Tampa Bay fish farmers reportedly released illegal stock into nearby streams, and the species is now established in several drainages there. Farmers have fenced ponds to keep out Walking Catfish, which crawl in and prey on other exotic fishes.

**Impact on native fishes.** Destruction of native fishes is most evident in the dry season (November to April or May), when low water concentrates native species with the catfish in deep, isolated refuges. The catfish preys heavily on native fishes and may kill large bass without eating them. Reproduction occurs during wet months, and the authors fear that high fecundity combined with heavy rains could cause dramatic population increases. A severe drought could also magnify harm through the combined effects of reduced oxygen and habitat loss.

**Other North American records.** Clarias specimens were reported from reservoirs in [[Nevada]], the [[San Joaquin River]] in [[California]], and the [[Flint River]] in [[Georgia]] (three fish caught in 1973). None appear to be established. The authors suggest these were deliberate dumpings to avoid prosecution under new state bans.

**Broader exotic-fish context.** From 1970 to 1973, 38 exotic species and 5 hybrids were recorded in Florida waters, of which 20 species and 5 hybrids were established as reproducing populations (Courtenay et al., 1974).

**Insecticide synergism (second item).** The Wisconsin authors describe how mixtures of chemicals can interact in the environment. In glass-and-water laboratory tests, the herbicides [[Atrazine]], [[Simazine]], [[Monuron]] and [[2,4-D]] increased the toxicity of several insecticides to [[Drosophila Melanogaster]], [[Musca Domestica]] and [[Aedes Aegypti]] larvae. Atrazine was the most effective. Some newspapers reported this result without noting that the tests used glass surfaces and tap water. Further experiments tested soils and water turbulence with [[Parathion]], [[DDT]] and Atrazine.

## Key Findings

**Walking Catfish invasion (Courtenay & Miley)**

- Introduced in 1964–1965 through an aquarium-fish farm; escapes occurred from 1965 to 1967 and first wild records came in 1968.
- Range was over 8,750 km² in six Florida counties in 1974 (ten counties by April 1975), covering more than 16% of the state's area.
- Native fish losses are concentrated in the dry season, and the species' high fecundity raises concern for future population growth.
- Human movement of fish, including illegal dumping after the 1968 restrictions, has extended the range.
- Isolated records outside Florida (Nevada, California, Georgia) do not appear to be established populations.
- The authors call for stronger prevention of exotic introductions because of threats to native biota and to sport fisheries.

**Insecticide synergism by herbicides (Lichtenstein et al.)**

- Laboratory tests on glass showed that Atrazine enhanced insecticide mortality in insects. Insecticides alone at low doses caused only 7.5–10.5% mortality. Atrazine doses needed for 50% mortality were 23, 40, 6 and 10 µg when added to Carbofuran, DDT, Parathion and Diazinon, respectively.
- Soils significantly reduced insecticide toxicity and synergism. Parathion (2.3 µg/10 g soil) alone killed 19% of fruit flies in sandy soil and 3% in loamy soil.
- For 50% mortality with Parathion, Atrazine needed was 62 µg/10 g in sandy soil and 920 µg/10 g in loamy soil. With DDT (30 µg/10 g), the figures were 86 µg and 680 µg.
- The synergistic effect of Atrazine in soil declined within 4 days.
- In turbulent water with soil, 93% of mosquito larvae died in unsoiled Parathion water (0.3 ppm), but water shaken with loamy soil became non-toxic. Adding Atrazine gave 38% mortality.
- The authors conclude that chemical toxicity depends on concentration, synergists and environmental conditions, and that the fate of chemicals must be studied as part of a whole system.
