# Population Modelling Program for Humpback Chub in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This interim report from [[BIO/WEST Inc]] (Logan, Utah; authors Ronald J. Ryel and Richard A. Valdez, June 23, 1995) describes a population modelling program for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in Grand Canyon, prepared for the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. The report summarizes progress on five program elements, presents a conceptual population model dividing the Grand Canyon population into five subpopulations, reviews available parameter data, and outlines a framework for an age-structured model. It also includes an appendix estimating adult population size and survival using closed and open capture-recapture models.

## Background & Description

The modelling program was motivated by the [[Aquatic Coordination Team]] of GCES, whose integrated investigations aim to determine the ecological and limiting factors for all life stages of humpback chub and the effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. The work addresses two of seven conservation measures from the 1978 Biological Opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations.

Humpback chub was judged the best candidate among the four main-stem Colorado River endangered fishes (with [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]) for population modelling because:
- Sampling methods reliably capture all life stages.
- A sizable database exists for parameters and interactions.
- High site fidelity within a small geographic range permits closed-system analyses.
- Its life history resembles many freshwater forms, so prior modelling strategies apply.

The report frames models as organizing and integrating tools rather than precise predictors, and stresses that model outputs are of limited value unless validated by direct field measurements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The conceptual model divides humpback chub into five subpopulations:
- **Colorado River Upstream (CRU)**: about 5% of fish captured 1990–1993 occurred outside the 30-km zone around the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) inflow; small aggregations were found at several river miles.
- **Colorado River/LCR Inflow (CRI)**: about 95% of mainstem fish were found within a 30-km (19-mile) reach around the LCR inflow (RM 58–77). Adults ascend the LCR to spawn February–May and descend June–July.
- **Colorado River Downstream (CRD)**: fish found at numerous sites below the inflow, with the largest aggregation at RM 129 (about 100 fish).
- **Little Colorado River (LCR)**: considered a likely resident population, with reproduction from age 3+ fish; large numbers of young and adults remain there year-round.
- **Tributaries (TRI)**: small numbers captured in Bright Angel, Shinumo, Kanab, and Havasu creeks, but evidence of reproduction there is lacking.

Reproduction outside the LCR appears limited, largely because cold water released from Glen Canyon Dam prevents egg maturation and larval survival in the mainstem. Environmental uncertainty from flow regimes, temperature, food supply, predation, and parasites is identified as a key factor for viability.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and framework**
- Five program elements were defined: (I) feasibility evaluation, (II) conceptual population model, (III) identification and assimilation of state and rate variables, (IV) mathematical and logical relationships, and (V) development and implementation of the population model.
- The feasibility evaluation concluded the program was feasible; more demographic data exist for humpback chub than for any other native mainstem Colorado River fish.
- The recommended model structure is an [[Age-Structured Population Model]] using a [[Projection Matrix]] (Leslie-type), with simple birth-death models judged less suitable.

**Parameter data**
- Parameter needs fall into four categories: age-class population size, age-specific survival, age-specific reproductive rates, and age-specific movement rates.
- Data were drawn from the mainstem and LCR and from five other populations (Black Rocks, Westwater, Desolation, Cataract, and Yampa canyons).
- Estimates include about 3,000–3,500 adults in the LCR aggregation; annual adult survival of 0.755 (model B; 95% CI 0.627–0.886); and fecundity figures such as 2,500 eggs per female.

**Adult population estimates (Appendix A)**
- Adult (TL ≥200 mm) population in the LCR aggregation was estimated at 3,000–3,500, with 95% confidence intervals about ±20% of the estimate.
- Closed-model estimates for 1991–1993 averaged roughly 2,700–3,000 (Schnabel M_t: 2,994).
- Open-model seasonal estimates averaged about 3,080–3,192.
- The [[MGG Aggregation]] yielded 89–103 adults in 1993.
- Other aggregations (e.g., 30-Mile, Shinumo inflow) had fewer than 1/10 the LCR estimate; Pumpkin Springs had about 4 adults.
- Undersampling of fish 200–300 mm TL was suspected, since the length distribution fell well below a stable-population expectation.

**Viability and recommendations**
- Demographic and environmental uncertainties are the most likely threats to persistence; catastrophic events cannot be discounted, while genetic uncertainty is judged unlikely to matter at present population sizes.
- Seven recommendations include: sharing the program with Grand Canyon researchers, soliciting data, using an age- or stage-based model, defining GCES integration objectives, integrating the model with long-term monitoring, and conducting a viability analysis after program elements I–V are completed (including the effects of a second spawning population).
