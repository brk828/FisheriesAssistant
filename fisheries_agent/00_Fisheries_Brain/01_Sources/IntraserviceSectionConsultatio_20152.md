# Intra-Service Section 7 Consultation - Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction to Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #reintroduction #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal Section 7 consultation biological opinion dated June 1990 regarding the proposed reintroduction of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) into the [[Colorado River]] mainstream between [[Headgate Rock Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]] (approximately 130 miles) in Arizona and California. The species was listed as endangered in 1967 and has been extirpated from the lower basin. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] concluded that stocking juvenile Colorado squawfish as a nonessential experimental population under Section 10(j) is not likely to jeopardize the species and will promote its conservation. This cooperative project between the Service and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] aims to establish a self-sustaining population and trophy fishery through annual stocking of 100,000 fingerlings over 10 consecutive years.

## Background & Description

[[Colorado Squawfish]] is the largest cyprinid in North America, historically reaching weights exceeding 36 kg and lengths of nearly 1.8 m, though specimens over 7 kg have been rare in recent times. Early literature referred to the species as "white salmon," "Colorado salmon," or simply "salmon" due to its substantial size and migratory habits. The species is a voracious predator and the top native carnivore of the [[Colorado River]] system.

The evolutionary history spans oscillations between pluvial and arid habitats during the Miocene, Pliocene, and early Pleistocene epochs. The species adapted to swift water by the mid-Pliocene but fossil evidence indicates use of both lakes and rivers. Large size, great mobility, and spawning migrations represent adaptations to drier seasons when suitable spawning habitats are limited or far-removed from adult habitats.

Habitat preferences vary by life stage: young-of-the-year (up to 64 mm), juveniles (65-200 mm), and sub-adults (200-400 mm) inhabit shallow backwater areas with little current over silt and sand bottoms. A habitat preference change occurs at approximately 200 mm total length, with larger fish selecting deeper water with velocity. Adults occupy large-river habitats in various depths and velocities over diverse substrates including silt, sand, gravel, and boulders.

Thermal tolerance is broad. Summer water temperatures near [[Yuma, Arizona]] commonly approach or exceed 35°C and may drop below 10°C in winter. In the upper basin, temperatures generally range from 25°C in summer to freezing (0°C) in winter. Historically, Colorado squawfish occupied both temperature regimes.

Diet shifts ontogenetically. Stomach contents of young fish consist mainly of zooplankton and insect larvae. Juveniles become piscivorous at an early age; 60 mm fish begin feeding on fish, with 86% of juvenile diet consisting of fish (primarily [[Red Shiner]] *Notropis lutrensis*). By 200 mm total length, diet is almost exclusively fish. Adults feed on most native and many introduced fishes. Large adults are not limited to fish; historical accounts report jackrabbits used as bait, and observations include consumption of newly hatched [[American Coots]] (*Fulica americana*) and [[Mormon Crickets]].

Colorado squawfish are long-lived, with wild fish from the upper basin exceeding 30 years of age. Captive broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] are up to 16 years old, healthy, and producing viable offspring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] changed dramatically during the 1900s. More than 20 dams were constructed on the mainstream and tributaries since 1913. Declines of native fishes directly downstream from reservoirs are clearly related to colder water temperatures. Other physical factors include changes in stream nutrients, altered seasonal and daily discharge patterns, and lowered turbidity. Nutrients once occurring in rivers are now retained in phytoplankton and zooplankton populations of reservoirs. Sediments are trapped by reservoirs, transforming downstream channel bottoms from sand and silt to armored cobble and boulders. Channelization reduced the number and size of backwaters and sloughs used by Colorado squawfish for nursery and resting areas. Natural flood-drought cycles were replaced by consistent discharges and variable demands for irrigation water or hydroelectric power.

These factors eliminated Colorado squawfish and most native species from 105 km of the [[Green River]] below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and caused vast biological modification in essentially the entire 389 km reach of Colorado River mainstream in [[Marble Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Similar actions in the lower river excluded warm-water native and introduced fishes from long reaches below [[Davis Dam]].

Introduction of nonnative fishes is implicated in the decline. Quiet waters of early reservoirs were initially inhabited by native fishes, including Colorado squawfish. Substantial catches were made from [[Roosevelt Lake]] from 1913 through 1937, and mainstream reservoirs on the lower Colorado River yielded large Colorado squawfish until the 1960s. However, impounded waters became populated and managed primarily for introduced species. In Arizona, 28 freshwater and 3 saltwater native species have been joined by at least 60 introduced fish species since the turn of the century. Introduced fishes may have subjected Colorado squawfish to biological interactions to which they were poorly adapted due to previous isolation. Mortality from Colorado squawfish choking when preying on [[Channel Catfish]] has been documented.

Currently, Colorado squawfish occur naturally in three locations: the [[Green River]]/[[Yampa River]], the upper [[Colorado River]] mainstream, and the [[San Juan River]]—all confined to the Colorado River basin upstream from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. A refugium/broodstock population is maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], Dexter, New Mexico. Reestablishment efforts in the [[Verde River]] and [[Salt River]] sub-basins initiated in 1985 show encouraging early results with some fish surviving and potentially reaching sexual maturity.

The proposed reintroduction site between [[Headgate Rock Dam]] (River mile 524.5) and [[Imperial Dam]] (River mile 657.2) is a relatively free-flowing 130-mile section in La Paz and Yuma Counties, Arizona, and San Bernardino, Riverside, and Imperial Counties, California. One low-head dam, [[Palo Verde Diversion Dam]] (River mile 568.8), restricts upstream fish movements but allows downstream passage. Although modified for flood control and water supply, this section retains riverine characteristics somewhat similar to the early 1900s.

Three federally listed animal species occur on adjacent lands: [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] inhabits marsh areas along the lower Colorado River (no interaction expected with Colorado squawfish), [[Bald Eagle]] nests in the [[Bill Williams River]] and upper [[Gila River]] systems and winters in Arizona (no intensive feeding activity in the stocking area), and [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] occurs considerable distances east and northeast of introduction areas. No impacts on listed species are anticipated.

## Key Findings

**Biological Opinion**: The proposed stocking of juvenile Colorado squawfish into the mainstream Colorado River between [[Headgate Rock Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]] is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of Colorado squawfish but will promote conservation of the species.

**Project Design**: The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] will provide a minimum of 1.5 million Colorado squawfish fry (150,000 annually) from captive broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] will rear up to 1 million fingerlings (100,000 annually) for stocking over 10 consecutive years. Adjustments will be made when successful reproduction occurs or when information warrants changes. The goal is to establish a trophy fishery with severely limited angler harvest until stocked fish become sexually mature and have ample opportunity to spawn.

**Broodstock Status**: More than 400 Colorado squawfish are maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. In 1989, egg take exceeded 2 million eggs from 22 fish, producing more than 199,000 fry. No fish will be taken from wild stocks; therefore, production for this effort will have no negative impact on existing wild populations.

**Recovery Plan Support**: The project supports multiple objectives from the Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan, including: refining life history/spawning and recruitment information (121), assessing inter-/intra-specific interactions (122), developing aging techniques and determining age distribution and growth rates (123), identifying and assessing impacts of introduced nonnative species (212), identifying areas for reintroduction/augmentation (321), stocking and monitoring reintroduced populations (323), and assessing sport fishery potential (422).

**Anticipated Take**: Approximately 70% of yearly reintroduced fingerlings could be taken within a year of reintroduction, primarily through predation by fish, some angler activity (primarily after fish reach adulthood), and fish-eating birds. Total anticipated take is not judged to prevent adequate broodstock availability for recruitment needed to sustain a population.

**Reasonable and Prudent Measures**: (1) Authorized take will not be permitted until Colorado squawfish become sexually mature and have ample opportunity to reproduce; (2) An angler education program including Colorado squawfish identification and safe release techniques must be implemented; (3) A monitoring program must identify Colorado squawfish habitat requiring special management.

**Terms and Conditions**: (1) The Service will obtain agreement from [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] to prohibit unauthorized take, establish conditions constituting successfully self-reproducing population, and establish angler education program; (2) Annual reports evaluating introduction results and identifying habitat requiring special management; (3) Review of [[National Wildlife Refuge]] operations in relation to Section 7 consultation.

**National Wildlife Refuge Considerations**: The target area flows through approximately 40 miles of [[National Wildlife Refuge]] lands ([[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]). Although direct stocking into refuge waters is not contemplated, normal fish movement will take them into refuge waters. Section 10(j)(2)(c)(i) requires Section 7 procedures on National Wildlife Refuges; therefore, Colorado squawfish occupying these waters will be afforded full protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Environmental Baseline**: Adequate habitat and water flows associated with deep pools or races appear available for growth and maturation. Spawning habitat availability is unanswered; no native fish in the lower basin have the same spawning habitat requirements, and no surrogate species is available for comparison. Nonnative fish have been stocked throughout the area for more than 50 years.

**Information Value**: Whether stream flows modified by numerous upstream dams that stabilize flows, prevent spring freshets, and block historical migration routes, plus competition from introduced exotic fishes managed for sport fishery, will prevent reestablishment remains to be seen. This project will provide answers required to recover Colorado squawfish in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The utilization of two tributary streams ([[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]]) and the lower mainstream provides an excellent vehicle to determine feasibility of restoring Colorado squawfish to areas historically containing healthy populations.
