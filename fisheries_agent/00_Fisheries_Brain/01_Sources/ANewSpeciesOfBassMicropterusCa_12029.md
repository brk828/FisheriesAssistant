# A New Species of Bass, *Micropterus cataractae*, from the Apalachicola River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Bulletin of the [[Florida Museum Of Natural History]] paper by James D. Williams and George H. Burgess formally describes the **[[Shoal Bass]]**, *Micropterus cataractae*, a moderate-sized [[Black Bass]] endemic to the [[Apalachicola River Basin]] of Alabama, Florida, and Georgia. Long confused with the [[Redeye Bass]] (*M. coosae*), the species was diagnosed mainly by tongue dentition, pigmentation, and scale counts. It is morphologically most similar to the [[Spotted Bass]] (*M. punctulatus*). The authors argue that its habitat loss from impoundment and dredging justifies greater conservation attention, and they recommend a range-wide survey and microhabitat research.

## Background & Description

- **Naming history:** For roughly 40 years the species was treated as an undescribed species, a subspecies or variety of *M. coosae*, or part of the redeye bass complex. Early notices came from Hubbs and Bailey (1940), Bailey and Hubbs (1949), and Dequine (1949). The IGFA continued to list it under redeye bass.
- **Etymology:** The specific name derives from Latin *cataracta* ("waterfall"), reflecting its preference for shoals. The common name "shoal bass" has been used since the early 1970s (Ramsey 1973).
- **Size:** Attains about 64 cm (25 in) total length and 3.99 kg (8 lb 12 oz). The record 3.99 kg fish was taken in the Apalachicola River, Florida, on 28 January 1995, and the authors recommend transferring the redeye bass world record to this species.
- **Type material:** Holotype UF 92070, 387 mm SL, adult male, Chipola River, Jackson County, Florida, collected 8 May 1989. Paratypes were deposited in numerous institutions.
- **Diagnostic characters:**
  - Typically (92% of 136 specimens) lacks a tongue tooth patch, unlike *M. coosae* and *M. punctulatus*.
  - Adults show dark, vertically elongated midlateral "tiger-stripe" blotches, separated by spaces about equal to blotch width.
  - Caudal fin margin darkly pigmented.
  - Weak ventrolateral lines, compared with 5–7 well-developed lines in *M. coosae*.
  - Scales on the basal interradial membranes of the soft dorsal and anal fins, unlike [[Largemouth Bass]] (*M. salmoides*).
  - Juveniles (20–30 mm SL) have a narrow stripe ending in an oval to wedge-shaped basicaudal spot.
- **Meristics (162 specimens):** Lateral line scales 67–81 (mean 74.2); scales below lateral line 15–21 (mean 18.5); caudal peduncle scales 27–35 (mean 31.4); dorsal spines 9–11 (mean 10.0); dorsal soft rays 10–13 (mean 12.1); anal soft rays 9–12 (mean 10.1); pectoral rays 14–17 (mean 16.1); gill rakers 6–9 (mean 7.6); unbranched pyloric caeca 8–14 (usually 11–12).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Almost exclusively shoal areas of rivers and large creeks, consistent with its specific name.
- **Native range:** Apalachicola and Chipola rivers (Florida), the Chattahoochee River and larger tributaries (eastern Alabama, western and northern Georgia), and the Flint River (southwestern Georgia), including reaches above and below the [[Fall Line]].
- **Sympatry:** Co-occurs with redeye bass, largemouth bass, and introduced northern and Alabama [[Spotted Bass]] subspecies. The redeye bass is restricted to the Chattahoochee drainage above the Fall Line and does not occur in the Flint.
- **Habitat loss:** Shoal bass has disappeared from most of the Chattahoochee mainstem after impoundment (e.g., [[West Point Reservoir]], Sidney Lanier Reservoir/Buford Dam). It has been eliminated from the lower Flint by Jim Woodruff Dam backwaters and no longer occurs in Lakes Blackshear and Worth. Dredging for navigation has altered the Apalachicola main channel.
- **Threats:** Pollution and siltation degrade Chipola River habitat (Ogilvie 1980). Dredging, irregular flows from power generation, flood control, and lock operation affect the Apalachicola below Jim Woodruff Dam.
- **Introductions:** Stocked in the [[Ocmulgee River]] (Altamaha drainage), Georgia, in the mid-1970s, where it is now established in about 35 miles above and 25 miles below the Fall Line.
- **Life history:**
  - Diet shifts from insects in small fish (4–12 cm SL) to crayfishes, fishes, and insects in larger fish, with the transition in the third year.
  - Sexual maturity at age 3; fecundity 5,396 to 21,779 ova (Wright 1967).
  - Spawning from April to early June at 18–23°C; males guard saucer-shaped nests 30–92 cm in diameter.
  - Eggs about 2 mm in diameter, hatching in about 48 hours at 21.1°C.
  - Growth in the Chipola River: about 10 cm TL in year 1 and 29 cm TL in year 3; a five-year-old fish measured 38.4 cm TL and weighed 0.8 kg.
  - Maximum reported age: 8 years.
- **Conservation status history:**
  - Alabama: Rare-2 (Ramsey et al. 1972), later Special Concern (Ramsey 1976).
  - Florida: recommended Threatened (Gilbert 1978, 1992).
  - Range-wide: Special Concern (Deacon et al. 1979; Williams et al. 1989).
  - Never listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

## Key Findings

1. ***Micropterus cataractae* is a valid, previously unnamed species** distinguished from *M. coosae* and *M. punctulatus* by tongue dentition, lateral blotch pattern, pigmentation, and scale counts. Its nearest relative in morphology is the spotted bass.
2. **Redeye bass records are confounded:** Some redeye bass records, including the IGFA world record, refer to shoal bass. The authors recommend re-establishing redeye bass records for the smaller *M. coosae*, which rarely exceeds 25.5 cm (10 in) and 0.5 kg (1 lb).
3. **Non-native basses are established in the basin:** Northern spotted bass (*M. punctulatus punctulatus*) is established in the Apalachicola below Jim Woodruff Dam (about 50 river miles) and was likely introduced before 1941. Alabama spotted bass and smallmouth bass records from the Chattahoochee suggest unsuccessful introductions.
4. **Habitat fragmentation drives decline:** Impoundments and dredging have eliminated shoal bass from much of its historic range. Museum records and fishery information indicate a continued decline.
5. **Recommendations:**
   - A thorough range-wide survey.
   - Research on microhabitat requirements to guide habitat restoration.
   - Re-evaluation of conservation status, given the Alabama omission from the 1986 review and the Florida threatened recommendation.
6. **Data gaps:** The life history has never been thoroughly investigated, and no vertebral counts were made by the authors.
