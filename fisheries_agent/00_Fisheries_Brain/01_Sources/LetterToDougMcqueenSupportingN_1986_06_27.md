# Letter to Doug McQueen Supporting Native Fish Use of ASU Research Park Ponds

## Executive Summary
This 1986 letter from Bud Bristow, Director of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], to Doug McQueen (Planning Director, ASU Research Park) advocates for repurposing the newly constructed [[ASU Research Park Ponds]] as a native fish production, growout, research, and refuge facility rather than a conventional recreational put-and-take fishery. The letter supports a prior proposal by Dr. Paul Marsh ([[Arizona State University]], Center for Environmental Studies) and highlights a critical bottleneck in [[Native Fish Reintroduction]] efforts for endangered Colorado River Basin species: the lack of sufficient growout facilities to raise hatchery-produced fry to sizes viable for survival against predatory exotic fish species. The document is a policy/management communication rather than an empirical study, but it references internal agency monitoring data on stocking survivorship and predation.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological data is presented, as this is a management correspondence rather than a taxonomic or anatomical study. Species referenced by life stage include:
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — discussed at **fry**, **fingerling**, and **small adult** life stages; fry are described as highly vulnerable to predation immediately post-stocking.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, referred to in-text as "Squawfish") — also produced at fry stage by hatchery operations, facing similar growout limitations.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) — cited as an exotic/predaceous species in this ecological context despite being the target species of the Urban Fishing Program; noted specifically for documented predation on razorback sucker fry.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) — mentioned only as a seasonally stocked put-and-take sportfish, not a subject of native fish concern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Urban Lake Fisheries**: Existing Phoenix-area urban ponds/lakes (e.g., Canal Park, Kiwanis Park, Riverside Park, and residential lakes like those in Ahwatukee) function as intensively managed, seasonally rotated put-and-take fisheries for channel catfish and trout via the Department's **[[Urban Fishing Program]]**. These habitats are characterized as unsuitable for anything beyond heavily stocked, artificial fisheries.
- **Growout Habitat Bottleneck**: The primary ecological constraint on native fish recovery is insufficient pond surface area to rear fry to post-vulnerable sizes. [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] can mass-produce razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish fry but lacks capacity to grow them to reintroduction-ready sizes.
- **Predation Pressure from Exotics**: Nearly all potential native fish reintroduction sites harbor predaceous exotic fish (implicitly including catfish and other introduced species), rendering direct fry-stage stocking largely ineffective.
- **Tributary Stream Refugia**: High-elevation tributary streams with minimal exotic fish populations are identified as the most promising reintroduction habitat for razorback sucker, but only when stocked with fingerling-to-small-adult sized fish rather than fry.
- **Candidate Growout Sites**: In addition to the ASU Research Park ponds, the Department was pursuing growout capacity at **[[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]]** stock ponds (southern Arizona) and in coordination with the **[[Phoenix Zoo]]**, both of which required costly and often incomplete exotic fish removal prior to use.
- **Canal-Fed Water Source**: The Research Park ponds are filled with canal water, raising a specific ecological management concern regarding potential ingress of exotic fishes via the canal connection, which the letter proposes mitigating through screening/filtering devices at an early construction stage.

## Key Findings
- **Recruitment Failure in Fry Stocking**: Despite intensive monitoring, essentially no recruitment was observed from millions of stocked razorback sucker fry, directly implicating predation as the primary cause.
- **Documented Predation Rate**: Agency studies documented that catfish predation could decimate an entire cohort of newly stocked razorback fry within one week.
- **Strategic Shift in Reintroduction Method**: These findings support a management shift toward stocking fingerling-to-small-adult razorback sucker in tributary streams with low exotic fish densities, rather than fry stocking in exotic-dominated habitats.
- **Infrastructure as Limiting Factor**: The single greatest impediment to native fish reintroduction was identified not as hatchery production capacity, but as insufficient growout pond acreage to bridge fry to sub-adult life stages.
- **Value Proposition of Research Park Ponds**: Because the ponds were newly constructed and not yet colonized by exotic fish, they represented a rare "clean start" opportunity — avoiding the costly exotic-removal process required at other candidate sites (Buenos Aires NWR, Phoenix Zoo).
- **Multi-Use Potential**: The ponds were proposed to serve simultaneously as production, growout, research, and public refuge/display facilities, contingent on water treatment feasibility; even without full water treatment, all functions except production were considered achievable.
- **Public Education Value**: The letter emphasizes that public awareness and education, enabled by high-visibility native fish displays in the Phoenix metro area (where none currently existed), would be the most critical long-term factor in the success of [[Endangered Fish Recovery Programs]], since public opinion would ultimately determine the fate of the region's native fish fauna.
- **Research Accessibility**: Proximity to Phoenix and Tucson-based researchers was noted as a major logistical advantage, reducing travel burdens and hatchery production conflicts for ongoing recovery research projects.
