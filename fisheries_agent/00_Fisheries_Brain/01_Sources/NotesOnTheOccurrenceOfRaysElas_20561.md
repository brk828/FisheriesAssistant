# Rays (Batoidea) of the Indian River Lagoon, Florida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #batoidea

## Executive Summary

This study reports the occurrence, abundance, sex ratios and size structure of batoid fishes (rays) in the central Indian River Lagoon, Florida, based on net surveys made mainly during summers from 1982 to 1986. The abstract reports 369 individuals of seven species, while the body text and Table 1 give 358 rays of five regularly collected species. [[Bluntnose Stingray]] was the most abundant and [[Southern Stingray]] the least abundant of the regularly collected species. The study adds the [[Lesser Devil Ray]] as a first record for the lagoon system, finds no [[Smalltooth Sawfish]], and argues that [[Sebastian Inlet]] is important for the distribution of lagoon fishes.

## Background & Description

Earlier surveys showed that the northern Indian River Lagoon has far fewer fish species and less abundance than the southern section (Gilmore et al., 1981; Snelson and Williams, 1981; Snelson, 1983). The authors attribute part of this difference to the larger number of ocean inlets in the south, since inlets let oceanic species enter the lagoon regularly or occasionally. This paper is the third in a series on ray biology in the lagoon.

Rays were collected with large-mesh (30 cm stretch) nylon tangle nets, 190-350 m long and 3-4 m deep, fished in open water 2-3 m deep. Nets were set only in daylight, for an average of seven hours per day over 68 days. Water temperature in summer ranged from 26-32 °C and salinity from 29-39 ppt (mean about 34). Animals were sexed and measured as disk width (DW) to the nearest cm, and a subsample was necropsied in the field. Maturity was judged mainly from clasper length relative to DW, and from size-at-maturity values in the literature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area lies between Melbourne Beach and Sebastian, about 27 km of lagoon, with all sites within 25 km of [[Sebastian Inlet]]. The inlet is artificially maintained and provides tidal exchange between the ocean and the lagoon. Netting effort was concentrated in May-August (87% of netting and 90% of rays captured). Two broad explanations are offered for the greater fish richness in the south: latitudinal climate effects, greater habitat diversity, and greater ocean-lagoon exchange through inlets (Snelson, 1983; Gilmore, 1977).

The authors could not decide whether the species that were more common near Sebastian Inlet migrate through the inlet regularly or are carried in by strong tides or storms. They also noted an unusually large wedge of clear ocean water in the lagoon on the day the Lesser Devil Ray was caught, which suggests that the animal was a waif.

## Key Findings

**Species abundance and sex ratios**

- *[[Dasyatis sayi]]* (Bluntnose Stingray): over 165 individuals, the most abundant batoid in the collections. Of the sexed animals, 66 were males and 99 females, a 1:1.5 ratio that differed significantly from 1:1 (χ² = 3.3, p < 0.10). Male DW ranged 33-67 cm and female DW 34-75 cm. The reported size at maturity (about 40 cm DW for males, 50-55 cm for females) indicates that most captured animals were mature.
- *[[Rhinoptera bonasus]]* (Cownose Ray): 64 animals; 37 males and 26 females, not significantly different from 1:1 (χ² = 0.96). Forty-two were caught in one set on 22 June 1982, in a school sex-ratio of 1:1.5 in favor of males. The three schools in the catch were size-segregated (45-51, 61-75 and 65-69 cm DW). The authors judge these to be part of a migrating stock, not permanent residents.
- *[[Aetobatus narinari]]* (Spotted Eagle Ray): 56 specimens, the third most abundant batoid. There were 38 males and 18 females, a significant departure from 1:1 (χ² = 3.57, p < 0.10). Male DW ranged 48-188 cm and female DW 52-129 cm. Males probably mature at 100-150 cm DW, and the authors conclude that all collected females were probably immature.
- *[[Gymnura micrura]]* (Smooth Butterfly Ray): 39 individuals, 38 of them females (DW 38-96 cm). Thirty-four of the 38 females exceeded the reported female size at maturity of about 64 cm DW, so the sample was mostly adults. The one male (38 cm DW, mature by clasper length, seen attached to a larger female, possibly in courtship) was the first of its kind caught in the study.
- *[[Dasyatis americana]]* (Southern Stingray): 35 specimens, the least abundant regularly collected species, with 21 males and 14 females (no significant departure from 1:1, χ² = 0.70). Juveniles and adults were both present in summer.
- *[[Dasyatis sabina]]* (Atlantic Stingray): only nine large specimens (five males at 22-26 cm DW, four females at 23-27 cm DW). The net's mesh favored large animals, so these data do not reflect the species' real abundance. One 26 cm female was visibly pregnant.

**Notable records**

- *[[Mobula hypostoma]]* (Lesser Devil Ray) is reported for the first time from the Indian River Lagoon system. A small male (73 cm DW, with "tiny" claspers, probably immature) was caught on 7 August 1987, photographed, and released. Voucher photographs are deposited at the Florida State Museum (UF 46025).
- No [[Pristis pectinata]] (Smalltooth Sawfish) were caught, supporting the view that the species has been extirpated from the lagoon (Snelson and Williams, 1981).
- *[[Myliobatis freminvillei]]* (Bullnose Ray), a juvenile male of 39.5 cm DW (fresh wet weight 750 g), was netted on 12 January 1988 about 2 km south of Sebastian Inlet, as an addendum. It is the first record for the lagoon system (Florida State Museum, UF 46806).

**Life history and migration**

- *Dasyatis americana*, *D. sayi* and *A. narinari* include juveniles and adults in summer, which suggests they complete their life cycles in the lagoon and are year-round residents.
- The *R. bonasus* schools are probably migrants. Smith (1980) reported that the animals were mostly juveniles of 1-3 years, and all but the largest (83 cm DW male) were immature.
- Stomach contents of *G. micrura* were mostly empty (8 of 10). The two with food contained a pigfish (*Orthopristis chrysoptera*, 145 mm SL) and a puffer (*Sphoeroides* sp., at least 100 mm SL).

**Sex-ratio hypotheses for *Gymnura micrura***

The authors list three explanations for the female-skewed catch: (1) males are smaller and escape the large-mesh net; (2) an unequal sex ratio at birth or higher male mortality; and (3) sexual segregation by habitat. The capture of a small male supports the first explanation, but the authors call for experimental gill-net work to test it. Birth ratios are reported as 1:1 (Daiber and Booth, 1960), which makes the second explanation unlikely. The third cannot be evaluated with these data.

**Conclusions**

The authors conclude that inlets strongly affect the distribution of lagoon fishes. Sebastian Inlet appears to supply oceanic species, and the new records of *M. hypostoma* and *M. freminvillei* support this. Some species (*A. narinari*, *D. americana*) probably have resident populations that are supplemented by recruitment through the inlet.
