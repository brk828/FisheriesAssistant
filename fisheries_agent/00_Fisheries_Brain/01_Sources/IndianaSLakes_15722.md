## Executive Summary

This document is an early-1960s issue of the *ACRES Quarterly* (a northern Indiana natural areas conservation organization newsletter). It contains no direct fish species data, but it provides substantial **limnological and glacial-geomorphological context** for the freshwater lake habitats of northern Indiana — the very systems that support the region's fish and aquatic invertebrate communities. The centerpiece scientific content is William R. Eberly's essay "Indiana's Lakes," which catalogs lake typology, distribution, depth, and the ecological and geological processes of lake formation and decay via glacial action. Supporting material (Rex Potterf's talk, Richard H. Pough's essay) provides paleoecological and conservation framing for these aquatic habitats.

## Background & Description

- **Origin of Indiana's lakes:** Nearly all natural lakes in Indiana are relict features of Pleistocene glaciation (the Wisconsin Glacier), which shaped all but 15 of Indiana's counties.
- **Lake Maumee:** A former proglacial lake approximately 1,000 square miles in area and 60 feet deep, extending from Defiance, Ohio to New Haven, Indiana, formed as the last ice cap receded. Its drainage carved the modern **Maumee and Wabash River valleys**, with a watershed divide at Fort Wayne.
- **Lake typology (per [[William R. Eberly]]):**
  - **Ox-bow lakes** — isolated former river channel segments (e.g., lower Wabash valley).
  - **Kettle-hole / ice-block lakes** — formed when glacial ice chunks broke off, were buried by sediment, and later melted, leaving depressions of uniform shape and depth (e.g., McLish, Still, Shock, Myers lakes).
  - **Semi-artificial lakes** — natural basins raised by power dams (e.g., Manitou, Webster).
  - **Large shallow watershed lakes** — collect surface runoff and seepage (e.g., Chapman, Wawasee).
  - **Clogged drainage channel lakes** — e.g., Tippecanoe Lake, formed from an old channel blocked at its lower end.
- **Distribution:** A directory of the era lists **868 Indiana lakes** (≥5 acres). Glacial lakes concentrate in northern Indiana, especially the **Packerton Moraine** (NE corner to Cass County) and **Maxinkuckee Moraine** (through St. Joseph, Marshall, Fulton counties). Steuben and Noble counties each contain 97 lakes; Kosciusko 74; LaGrange 57.
- **Size and depth benchmarks:**
  - Largest natural Indiana lake: **Wawasee** (2,618 acres), followed by Maxinkuckee (1,650 acres) and James (1,034 acres); most lakes are under 500 acres.
  - Deepest Indiana lake: **[[Tippecanoe Lake]]** at 120 feet; only 2–3 others exceed 100 feet; many lakes average 50–60 feet.
  - Comparative global depths cited: Lake Baikal (5,600 ft), Great Slave Lake (2,500 ft), Crater Lake (2,000 ft) — contextualizing Indiana lakes as shallow by global standards.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat loss and lake senescence:** Lakes are framed as ephemeral geological and ecological features undergoing continuous infilling via sediment deposition, encroaching shore vegetation, and organic accumulation from biological productivity. **Winona Lake** was cited as approximately 40% filled by sediment at the time of writing.
- **Historical hydrological alteration:** **English Lake** (Starke County), once the largest lake in historic Indiana, was fully drained following dredging of the Kankakee River — an example of anthropogenic lake destruction relevant to habitat loss for aquatic fauna.
- **Wetland/aquatic habitat complexes:** The **Little River Wet Prairie** (south of U.S. 24 to Huntington) and the **black swamp/Maumee Lake bottom** east of Fort Wayne are described as extensive wetland–hardwood forest mosaics rich in aquatic bird life, illustrating pre-settlement wetland–lake–forest interconnectivity.
- **Land-use pressure:** Post-war suburban development and drainage are identified as major threats to remaining natural lake, bog, and swamp habitats in northern Indiana, motivating the conservation mission of [[Acres Inc]].
- **Limnology as a discipline:** The text explicitly names **[[Limnology]]** as the scientific study of aquatic life and lake systems, noting it was, at the time, a niche pursued mainly by large research universities.
- **Related habitat types mentioned for planned ACRES field trips:** **Cabin Creek Raised Bog** (Randolph County), **Hanging Rock** limestone outcrop (near Huntington), and **Barnes Swamp** (near Angola) — each representing distinctive wetland and aquatic-adjacent habitat types with unique flora, indirectly relevant to regional aquatic biodiversity.

## Key Findings

- Indiana lakes are almost entirely glacial in origin, formed via ice-block melting, moraine damming, or relict drainage channels — this geomorphic diversity produces varied depth profiles, shoreline structure, and basin morphology that shape aquatic habitat heterogeneity.
- Lake depth in Indiana is modest relative to global standards; **Tippecanoe Lake** (120 ft) represents the regional maximum, meaning most Indiana lentic habitats are shallow, warm, and likely support warm-water fish assemblages rather than cold, deep-water fish communities.
- Natural lakes are actively disappearing through natural sedimentation and succession as well as human drainage, with clear historical precedent (English Lake) — implying ongoing, measurable habitat loss for lake-dependent aquatic species in the region.
- The density and distribution of lakes is highly non-uniform, concentrated in specific moraine belts (Packerton, Maxinkuckee), meaning regional aquatic biodiversity and fish habitat availability is spatially clustered rather than evenly distributed across the state.
- No specific fish species, populations, or fisheries data are reported in this issue; the content is foundational and geomorphological and habitat-descriptive, useful as background context for future notes on specific Indiana fish species or lake fish surveys (e.g., [[Wawasee Lake]], [[Maxinkuckee Lake]], [[Tippecanoe Lake]]).
- The document underscores a conservation rationale — preserving lakes, bogs, and wetlands as "outdoor laboratories" — that is directly relevant to safeguarding aquatic habitat for fish and other freshwater organisms in northern Indiana.
