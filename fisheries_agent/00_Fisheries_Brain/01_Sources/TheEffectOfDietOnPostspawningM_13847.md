# Effect of Diet on Post-Spawning Mortality in Rainbow Trout at Mt. Whitney Hatchery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game report (Inland Fisheries Administrative Report No. 68-7, July 1968, by George K. Bruley and Robert J. Toth) describes a short-term feeding trial at the [[Mt. Whitney Hatchery]]. The trial tested whether diet affects losses among spawned-out female [[Rainbow Trout]] broodstock. Four diets were compared in groups of 100 two-year-old fish over 95 days. The [[Commercial Broodstock Pellet]] diet gave the lowest mortality, the greatest weight gain and the best physical condition. The authors recommended it as the exclusive broodstock diet at the station. They also stated that the study was incomplete, because egg production and egg quality had not yet been measured.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The test fish were two-year-old spawned-out female rainbow trout of the Mt. Whitney strain, reported as *Salmo gairdnerii*. Before the trial they had been raised entirely on a standard diet of beef liver and ocean fish.

**Problem.** Losses after spawning at the hatchery are unavoidable and are often not explained by gross examination. Records show the following:
- In 1965, 3,299 two- and three-year-old female broodstock were retained after spawning.
- In 1966, 2,785 remained (85%), so 514 fish were lost.
- At an average of 2 pounds per fish, the loss exceeded 1,000 pounds of costly fish flesh.
- Most losses occurred in the 60 days after spawning.

The authors noted that disease, trauma and predation probably explained only part of the loss. They suspected a [[Dietary Vitamin Deficiency]] and therefore included a vitamin-boosted diet.

**Experimental design.**
- Fish were moved into nursery tanks on March 20, 1967, and acclimated to diet until 100 fish per group had accumulated.
- The trial ran from April 13 to July 17, 1967 (95 days). On June 14 all groups were reduced to 80 fish to match the smallest group. On July 2 the fish were moved to spawning tanks with more space.
- Mean starting weight was 9.7 oz (range 9.5–10.0 oz).
- Stocking density was about 0.6 lb of fish per cubic foot. Water flow was 20 gallons per minute.
- Daily rations followed Circular 159 (*Trout Feeds and Feeding*, Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife).
- Fish were fed once daily. Feeding stopped for 10 days when water fell below 40 °F. Rations were not adjusted for temperature, which ranged from 38 to 60 °F.

**Diets tested.**
1. Ground beef liver with a commercial [[Vitamin Supplement]] (10 g per lb of liver, about twice the amount in commercial pellets)
2. Commercial 5/32-inch broodstock pellets
3. Equal-weight mixture of ground beef liver and broodstock pellets
4. Ground beef liver alone (control)

Ocean fish were left out of diets 1 and 4 because the authors thought they might degrade the vitamin supplement. Supplements and pellets were mixed into the liver just before feeding to limit leaching of water-soluble nutrients.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The hatchery operated a flow-through coldwater system. Water temperatures before and after the spawning season normally stayed in the low 30s °F, so little or no feed was given to broodstock at those times. The authors reasoned that this may have left fish without enough energy reserves to survive the metabolic demands of spawning.

Dry feeds were thought to digest better at low temperatures. Wet feed had long been considered poor for brood fish below 40 °F because of difficulty digesting hard fats. This suggested that a dry-pellet diet could help coldwater broodstock survive the post-spawning period.

## Key Findings

- **Diet 2 (commercial broodstock pellets)** produced the lowest mortality and the highest weight gain. Its fish also looked clearly better than those on the other diets.
- **Diet 1 (liver with vitamin supplement)** had the highest loss, at 28%. It still gained 23% in weight, compared with 9.2% for the liver-only control (Diet 4). Its physical appearance was better than that of the liver-fed fish.
- **Diet 3 (liver plus pellets)** gave intermediate results. The authors saw no need for it as a transition diet, because pellet-fed fish accepted dry feed immediately even though they had previously eaten only wet feed.
- **Diet 4 (liver control)** had the second-lowest mortality but the smallest weight gain and the poorest physical appearance. Exact mortality figures for diets 2–4 are not given in the text, and the results table is not legible in the source.
- **Conclusion on liver:** The authors concluded that beef liver is not a complete feed for trout. They suggested that adding ocean fish to the control might have improved results.
- **Recommendation:** Because of superior survival, growth and condition, plus lower initial cost and easier storage and handling, the authors recommended commercial pellets as the exclusive broodstock diet at Mt. Whitney Hatchery.
- **Hypothesis:** Better fat digestion of dry feed at low temperatures might reduce post-spawning losses in coldwater hatcheries. The authors proposed that withholding feed during the cold pre- and post-spawning period could contribute to losses.
- **Limitations and next steps:** The authors stated that 95 days is not normally enough for a valid feed test. They recommended continuing the trial through the next spawning season to compare egg production and egg quality between diet groups. The study was therefore not complete at the time of reporting.
