# The Homing Salmon: Odor Memory and Natal Stream Recognition

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 reprint from *Scientific American* by [[Arthur D. Hasler]] and [[James A. Larsen]] examines how salmon find their way back to their natal streams after years at sea. Drawing on laboratory conditioning experiments at the [[Wisconsin Lake Laboratory]] and field tests in North American rivers, the authors propose that each stream carries a stable, characteristic odor that young salmon learn before migrating and later use to navigate home. The authors frame the question as both a scientific riddle and an economic concern, since [[Dam Construction]] on Pacific Coast rivers has caused heavy salmon losses. The article presents the [[Olfactory Imprinting Hypothesis]] as a working model, supported by partial evidence, while acknowledging that it remains unproven.

## Background & Description

- **Homing behavior:** The [[Chinook Salmon]] of the U.S. Northwest hatches in a small stream, migrates to the Pacific as a smolt, lives at sea for up to five years, and returns to spawn in its birth stream, sometimes 800 to 900 miles away.
- **Species overview:** Of six common salmon species, the [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*, same genus as steelhead trout) can spawn repeatedly. The five Pacific species, namely Chinook (king), [[Sockeye Salmon]], [[Silver Salmon]] (coho), humpback and chum, spawn once and then die.
- **Life cycle:** Eggs hatch in stream gravel. Fingerlings feed on insects and small aquatic animals for a few weeks before migrating to sea. Mortality is high: an estimated 15 per cent of young salmon are lost at each large dam such as [[Bonneville Dam]], and others die in polluted streams or are eaten by larger fish at sea.
- **Olfaction in fish:** Fish possess a highly sensitive sense of smell. Bony fishes have nasal pits with separate inlet and outlet openings, and odorant molecules stimulate nasal receptors that send signals via the [[Olfactory Nerve]]. The article notes that in aquatic animals smell is always dissolved, unlike in land vertebrates.
- **Experimental apparatus:** Two designs are described: a conditioning tank in which fish learned to distinguish odors using food rewards and mild electric shocks (fish were blinded so that they could not associate reward with the experimenters' movements), and a four-runway [[Odor Choice Apparatus]] in which salmon fingerlings jumped waterfalls to enter arms scented with test odors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Plant odors:** Trained fish distinguished dilute rinses from all 14 aquatic plant species tested. The authors suggest plant odors guide fish to feeding grounds in turbid water or at night and keep young fish within protective cover.
- **Pollutant detection:** Trained minnows detected [[Phenol]], a common industrial pollutant, at concentrations far below human detection thresholds, suggesting fish could serve as bioassay organisms.
- **Dams and fisheries:** Dams have cut heavily into Pacific Coast salmon fishing. Fish ladders and lifts exist but lose large numbers of fish for reasons specific to each dam.
- **Stream chemistry:** Chemical analysis of two Wisconsin creeks found that the main difference between their waters was organic material. Distillation fractions indicated the discriminating substance was a volatile organic compound.

## Key Findings

- **Stream-specific odors exist:** Minnows and then salmon discriminated water from two Wisconsin creeks. Destroying nose tissue abolished this ability.
- **Odor-blinded salmon stray:** In a field test on the [[Issaquah River]] (Washington), silver salmon with unplugged noses mostly returned to their original stream. Fish with plugged noses (cotton-plugged) migrated at random, choosing the wrong branch as often as the right one.
- **Transplant evidence:** In 1949, eggs from the [[Horsefly River]] were reared at a hatchery on the Little Horsefly tributary, then flown and released in the main Horsefly River. Three years later, 13 fish returned to the Little Horsefly.
- **Large-scale marking study:** Pritchard, Clemens and Foerster (Canada) marked 469,326 young sockeye salmon from a Fraser River tributary and recovered nearly 11,000 in the parent stream after the ocean migration. None strayed to another stream.
- **Odor memory:** Trained fish retained odor discrimination for long periods, and young fish remembered better than old fish.
- **Artificial conditioning candidate:** Dilute [[Morpholine]] was neither attractive nor repellent to unconditioned salmon but was detectable at about 1 part per million and was chemically stable in stream conditions. It is proposed as a decoy odor to lead salmon to non-natal streams unobstructed by dams.
- **Ongoing work and caveats:** Federal collaborators were running field trials on the Pacific Coast to test whether morpholine-conditioned fry and fingerlings could be decoyed. The authors caution that a negative result might mean salmon respond to a combination of subtle odors rather than a single substance.
