# Fishes of the Refugium at Balmorhea State Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This guide, authored by [[Dr. Clark Hubbs]], describes the seven fish species that can be observed in the spring-fed refugium canal at [[Balmorhea State Park]], Texas. The refugium was built in 1975 to provide lasting habitat for the endangered [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]], whose only remaining wild populations are in the springs and irrigation canals of the Balmorhea area. A second endangered species, the [[Pecos Gambusia]], shares the refugium. The remaining five fishes are [[Largespring Gambusia]], [[Mexican Tetra]], [[Roundnose Minnow]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Green Sunfish]]. The guide focuses on identifying these fishes from the canal bank using morphological, behavioral and habitat cues.

## Background & Description

**Refugium purpose.** The Department's stated objective is to maintain the refugium as the best available habitat for the pupfish and the Pecos Gambusia.

**Comanche Springs Pupfish** (*Cyprinodon elegans*), about 2 in long, is the primary reason the refugium was built. The species once occurred in [[Comanche Springs]] at Fort Stockton but is now extinct there.

**Gambusia species.** Pecos Gambusia and Largespring Gambusia are about the same size but differ in markings:
- Pecos Gambusia females have a pronounced black spot near the anus.
- Largespring Gambusia have distinct markings on their scales and dark markings on their mouths, visible mainly at close range.
- Bank observation makes the two difficult to tell apart. Only about 1% of the Gambusia observed are Pecos Gambusia.
- Largespring Gambusia males are about 1 in long and females about 2 in long.

**Mexican Tetra** (*Astyanax mexicanus*) is the northern representative of the South American family [[Characidae]], many of whose members are popular aquarium fishes. In Texas it is native to the [[Nueces Basin]] and [[Rio Grande Basin]]. It is a pelagic, very active fish, about 100 mm (4 in) long, with a prominent black mark in front of the caudal fin. Its silvery flashes are visible when it turns quickly.

**Roundnose Minnow** (*Dionda episcopa*) is about 80 mm (3 in) long and widely distributed in Texas springs of the Rio Grande Basin. Each isolated spring area probably holds a unique population. It is omnivorous, feeds near the bottom, and moves slowly without silvery flashes.

**Channel Catfish** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) measures about 12 to 24 in. **Green Sunfish** (*Lepomis cyanellus*) measures about 3 to 5 in. Both are occasionally present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predators:** The Channel Catfish and Green Sunfish act as the top carnivores in the refugium, occasionally capturing a few individuals of the other fishes. The guide states that this predation does not currently threaten the survival of the other species.
- **Introduced Largespring Gambusia:** The population introduced into Comanche Springs went extinct when that system dried up. The remaining populations do not appear to have major adverse impacts on the endemic Gambusia there. The guide suggests this may reflect natural coexistence with the [[San Marcos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia georgei*) and the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) in the [[San Marcos River]].
- **Mexican Tetra distribution:** Outside its native range, the Mexican Tetra survives mainly in spring areas where winter temperatures do not reach lethal levels for warm-water fishes.
- **Isolated populations:** Texas spring systems hold isolated populations, so each spring area may contain genetically distinct fish.

## Key Findings

1. The refugium at Balmorhea State Park, built in 1975, is managed to maintain habitat for two endangered species: the Comanche Springs Pupfish and the Pecos Gambusia.
2. Seven fish species can be observed in the refugium canal: the two endangered species plus Largespring Gambusia, Mexican Tetra, Roundnose Minnow, Channel Catfish and Green Sunfish.
3. The Comanche Springs Pupfish is extinct at its original site in Comanche Springs (Fort Stockton). Its remaining populations are in the springs and irrigation canals of the Balmorhea area.
4. Pecos Gambusia and Largespring Gambusia are hard to tell apart from the bank. Only about 1% of the Gambusia observed are Pecos Gambusia.
5. Channel Catfish and Green Sunfish are the top predators but do not currently threaten the other fishes.
6. Introduced Largespring Gambusia died out where Comanche Springs dried up. Elsewhere, they coexist without major apparent impacts on endemic Gambusia.
7. Mexican Tetra persists in spring areas where winter temperatures remain above lethal levels for warm-water fishes.
