# The Lake Tahoe Sport Fishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 reprint by [[Almo Cordone]] (California Department of Fish and Game) and [[Ted Frantz]] (Nevada Fish and Game Commission) reports results of the cooperative [[Lake Tahoe Fisheries Study]], begun July 1, 1960. Using a coordinated [[Creel Census]] of boat anglers and the [[Cave Rock Census]], the authors describe angler categories, success rates, catch composition, fish sizes, use, and yield at [[Lake Tahoe]]. The fishery is dominated by [[Lake Trout]], which the authors judge stable, and they recommend no change in angling regulations. The lake may be near its potential game-fish yield, although further gains are possible through better use of the limnetic zone.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** [[Lake Tahoe]] is a deep, cold, extremely oligotrophic lake at 6,229 ft elevation in the Sierra Nevada. It averages 1,027 ft deep, has a maximum depth of 1,645 ft, and covers 123,300 surface acres.
- **Historical fishery:** Once famous for the native [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], which supported sport and commercial catches approaching 75,000 lb in good years (per Juday, 1907). The native cutthroat collapsed in the early 1930s and is considered extinct; occasional cutthroat are likely planted Heenan Lake strain fish.
- **Lake trout:** The first confirmed introduction was made in 1889 by the Nevada Fish Commission. By 1938 lake trout had come to dominate the sport fishery.
- **Fish fauna (Table 1):** Eight game-fish taxa and six nongame taxa. Apart from [[Mountain Whitefish]], all game fishes are introduced. The nongame fauna (e.g., [[Tahoe Sucker]], [[Tui Chub]], [[Lahontan Redside]]) is unchanged. Nongame species include the Lahontan speckled dace and Piute sculpin.
- **Regulations:** A year-round trout season has applied since January 1, 1955. Game fish may be taken from 1 hour before sunrise to 2 hours after sunset, with a bag limit of five trout, kokanee, or whitefish in any combination.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Productivity:** The authors cite Goldman and Carter (1965), who estimated net primary productivity at 36.22 g C/yr/m² and placed Tahoe among the ultra-oligotrophic lakes of the world. The euphotic zone averages about 60 m.
- **Limnetic zone:** Apart from scattered rainbow trout and kokanee, the limnetic zone is "virtually devoid of fish life." Tahoe's fishes are restricted to the littoral zone or are bottom-oriented, rarely descending below 500 ft. More than 75% of the surface (about 92,500 acres) lies over depths greater than 500 ft and is essentially lost to fishing.
- **Morphoedaphic index:** Using [[Ryder Morphoedaphic Index]] (mean depth divided by total dissolved solids), Tahoe's potential fish production is estimated at about 0.50 lb/acre, lower than any of the 33 lakes Ryder described.
- **Forage fishes:** Tahoe's forage fishes are poorly suited as food for lake trout. [[Mysis Relicta]] and the [[Bonneville Cisco]] were introduced to address this gap.
- **Comparative yield (Table 14):** Lake trout yields in other oligotrophic lakes are generally low, and Tahoe's yield was lower than that of almost all comparable lakes over 100,000 acres or with mean depths over 100 ft.

## Key Findings

**Angler categories and success**
- Four categories: [[Deepline Anglers]], [[Topline Anglers]], [[Shore Anglers]], and [[Pier Anglers]]. Each had a distinct success rate and catch composition.
- Deepliners were the most successful, with a mean deepline wild-fish catch of 0.209 fish/hour (99% lake trout). Topline was next at 0.082, then pier at 0.065, and shore at 0.036.
- Planted trout raised topline success to 0.110 and pier and shore success to 0.113 and 0.145 fish/hour. Planted trout were nearly all yearlings, mostly caught within three months of release.
- The census interviewed 28,318 anglers over 67,648 hours, who caught 11,871 game fish (July 1960–1964).

**Catch composition and size**
- [[Lake Trout]] comprised about 70% by number of all wild trout, and 82% by weight of the total game-fish harvest.
- Mean lake trout fork length was 18.5 inches (2.69 lb), based on 4,779 measured fish. Annual means varied by only half an inch.
- Lake trout were smallest in October, coinciding with spawning. Mean length rose abruptly in November and stayed between 18.5 and 19.5 inches until summer.
- Mean rainbow trout length was 13.4 inches (1.36 lb) from 792 fish. Mean brown trout length was 17.6 inches (59 fish). Mean whitefish length was 10.9 inches (57 fish).

**Use and yield**
- Annual mean use was about 1.1 angler hours per surface acre and 1,870 angler hours per shoreline mile. Estimated total use was 123,795 angler hours (1961), 144,147 (1962), and 133,217 (1963).
- Mean annual lake trout yield was 0.24 lb/acre, or 83% of the total game-fish yield of 0.29 lb/acre.

**Trends and management**
- Deepline anglers reported smaller, fewer lake trout than in earlier decades. The authors attribute this to probable sampling artifacts. Comparisons with historical data (1938–39: mean 19.7 in; 1948: mean 22.0 in and 0.125 fish/hour) gave no evidence of overfishing.
- A fishing guide's records (17 years, averaging 326 lake trout and 0.37 fish/hour) showed a stable fishery with a slight upward trend.
- **Conclusion:** The fishery is stable, and no changes to angling regulations are recommended. The authors recommend repeating the boat and Cave Rock census in 5 to 10 years.
- **Potential:** Tahoe may be close to its potential with existing species, but a historical cutthroat yield of 0.60 lb/acre suggests possible doubling. Yield could increase through kokanee management or new forage organisms.
