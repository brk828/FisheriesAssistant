# Distribution, Abundance and Entrainment of Larval Fishes in the Western and Central Basins of Lake Erie

#ichthyology #marine-biology #larval-fishes

## Executive Summary

This EPA Project Summary (EPA-600/S3-84-017, March 1984) reports a multi-year survey of [[Larval Fish]] distribution, abundance and [[Entrainment]] by steam-generating power plants in the [[Western Basin]] and [[Central Basin]] of [[Lake Erie]]. Conducted by researchers at The Ohio State University for a multi-agency assessment, the study sampled nearshore and open-lake waters from 1975 to 1978 and estimated the impact of cooling-water intakes on fish populations. Main results: shallow nearshore areas and river estuaries are important nursery zones; yellow perch, gizzard shad and white bass dominate larval catches; relict spawning populations of lake whitefish and sculpin persist in the Western Basin; and offshore intake locations cause markedly lower entrainment losses.

## Background & Description

The [[Great Lakes]] supply drinking water to roughly 70 to 75 percent of the basin's 29 million residents and support recreational boating, sport fishing, industry and electrical generation. Electrical generation is the largest user of Great Lakes water, and coastal power stations account for over 70 percent of basin water use. The document notes that an additional 17 power plants were planned for the basin by the mid-1980s.

Steam plants can harm aquatic life in three ways: (1) impingement of fishes on intake screens; (2) [[Entrainment]], which damages plankton, eggs and larvae drawn through the cooling system; and (3) thermal discharge that disrupts productive natural systems. This study focuses on entrainment of larval fishes.

Earlier Lake Erie larval studies were limited in area and circulation. This work is part of a broader program, begun in 1970 near Detroit Edison's Monroe, Michigan site, with larval studies from 1973 and distribution studies from 1975. It sampled Ohio, Michigan and Canadian waters.

**Methods (summary):**
- The open-lake area (about 7,740 km²) was divided into six depth zones using a two-metre contour interval.
- Stations were placed in a stratified random pattern: 56 stations sampled in 1975 and a number illegible in the scan in 1976.
- Nine cruises were run from May 12 to September 3, 1975, and fourteen from April 12 to September 3, 1976. All collections were made in daylight.
- In 1977, transects perpendicular to the shoreline were sampled in the nearshore Western Basin; in 1978 similar work was done in the Central Basin during midsummer.
- Samples were collected with [[Metered Plankton Nets]], preserved in buffered 5 percent formaldehyde, identified, counted, measured and transferred to 70 percent ethanol. Density estimates were volume-weighted by depth zone.
- Entrainment was estimated by multiplying larval density at the station nearest each plant intake by that plant's daily average flow. In-plant [[Ichthyoplankton Sampling]] was conducted weekly at shoreline plants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document states that nearshore Lake Erie is a valuable spawning and nursery zone. Larval fishes and buoyant eggs occur at high density near shore, and their movement is governed more by water currents than by swimming ability. This makes them especially vulnerable to high-volume intake flows.

Estuaries are critical. The [[Maumee River Estuary]] and [[Sandusky River Estuary]] are identified as important spawning and nursery sites for gizzard shad, white bass, walleye and freshwater drum. Eddy effects from the Maumee and Detroit Rivers were suggested to retain larvae in certain Michigan shoreline areas.

The authors caution that the sampling gear was selective for limnetic (open-water) species and may under-represent littoral (nearshore vegetated) species. Abundance estimates had large standard deviations and errors, which shrank when mean densities exceeded 100 fish per 100 m³.

## Key Findings

**Taxa and community composition**
- 19 larval taxa were collected with plankton nets in Ohio and Ontario waters of the Western Basin in 1975 and 1976.
- 17 taxa were collected in the Maumee River estuary across 1975 to 1977; 11 taxa were collected in the Sandusky River estuary in 1976.
- In the Maumee estuary, gizzard shad/alewife, white bass/white perch and freshwater drum made up 98 percent of larvae. In the Sandusky estuary they made up 91 percent.
- In 1977, gizzard shad/alewife, emerald shiner, white bass/white perch and yellow perch made up over 97 percent of larvae in Ohio and Michigan Western Basin waters.
- Central Basin Ohio waters yielded a larval taxa count in 1978 that is partly illegible in the scan. Gizzard shad/alewives, emerald shiners and spottail shiners were the dominant group.

**Distribution patterns**
- Gizzard shad/alewife and spottail shiner larvae were significantly more abundant immediately adjacent to shore than at 5 m offshore.
- Smelt larvae were significantly more abundant at 5 m offshore than at the shoreline.
- Walleye larvae were significantly more numerous along the Ohio shoreline than in Maumee Bay or along the Michigan shoreline. Freshwater drum larvae were significantly more numerous in Maumee Bay.
- In the Central Basin, larval gizzard shad, carp/goldfish, spottail shiner, troutperch and yellow perch densities were significantly higher in shallow (1 to 2 m) nearshore areas than at 5 and 10 m.
- Rainbow smelt and yellow perch larvae were almost entirely restricted to the lake proper.
- Rainbow smelt and emerald shiners were more abundant at deeper open-water stations than at shoreline stations.
- Yellow perch larvae in 1975 and 1976 were concentrated near the bottom over sandy or gravel nearshore substrates. Yellow perch densities in 1977 were highest along the Michigan shoreline north of Woodtick Peninsula and south of the River Raisin.
- Freshwater drum densities were highest in the Central Basin harbor breakwalls and sand shoals, and in quiet areas of these habitats. The document notes that water shallower than 12 m near Cleveland is limited to a narrow shoreline strip, which restricts drum habitat there.

**Evidence of relict populations**
- Capture of lake whitefish larvae on reefs and along Michigan and Ohio shorelines indicates a remnant spawning population in the Western Basin.
- Capture of sculpin larvae on island-area reefs indicates a remnant sculpin spawning population.

**Estuary productivity**
- The Maumee River estuary had higher larval densities and estimated numbers than the Sandusky River estuary.
- Maumee estuary production estimates for gizzard shad and freshwater drum often approached or matched Western Basin production estimates.
- Maumee and Sandusky Bays held higher densities of gizzard shad, white bass and freshwater drum than the Ohio or Michigan shorelines, indicating valuable nursery areas fed by both bay spawning and river transport.

**Entrainment impacts**
- Central Basin entrainment estimates were typically 2 to 4 percent of total estimated nearshore production.
- Yellow perch entrainment represented 3 percent of total yellow perch production.
- Carp had the highest proportion entrained: 36 percent of total nearshore carp production.
- All field-collection entrainment estimates were higher than in-plant estimates. Rainbow smelt, gizzard shad, carp and freshwater drum showed significant differences between the two methods.
- The authors argue that submersible pump samples give a better entrainment estimate than field nets, because net-based field sampling is less reliable.
- Entrainment was highest at the Avon Lake plant, which accounted for 60.1 percent of the total larvae entrained in the Central Basin study area. Cyprinids made up a large share of Avon Lake entrainment, but the exact percentage is illegible in the scan.
- Yellow perch entrainment was projected to be highest at the Avon Lake plant (about 130,500 larvae) and at the Ashtabula A and B plants (about 1,315,417 larvae).

**Offshore versus nearshore intakes**
- The [[Ashtabula Power Plants]] comparison showed the Ashtabula C plant, with an intake 1,200 m offshore, required 78 percent of the water used by the A and B plants, whose intake lies 425 m offshore.
- Entrainment at the C plant was much lower in all cases except rainbow smelt, which was about 10 times higher there.
- Yellow perch entrainment at the C plant was 20 percent of that at the A and B plants.

**Equivalent adult estimates**
- Enough data from 1975 and 1976 collections allowed an estimate of the impact of entrainment on adult yellow perch and emerald shiner populations using the [[Equivalent Adult Approach]].

**Management implication**
- Nearshore and estuary habitats are critical nurseries, so intake siting far offshore substantially reduces larval losses.
