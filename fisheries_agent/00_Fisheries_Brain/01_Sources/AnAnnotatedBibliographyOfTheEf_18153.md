# An Annotated Bibliography of the Effects of Temperature on Fish, with Special Reference to the Freshwater and Anadromous Species of New England

#ichthyology #marine-biology #thermal-biology

## Executive Summary

This Massachusetts Agricultural Experiment Station Research Bulletin (No. 605, July 1974), by James R. Beltz, James E. Johnson, Donald L. Cohen and Frank B. Pratt of the [[Department Of Forestry And Wildlife Management]] at the University of Massachusetts at Amherst, compiles an annotated bibliography on how temperature affects fish. Funded by [[Northeast Utilities Service Company]], it was prompted by rising electrical demand and the resulting heat discharged into surface waters from thermo-electrical generating facilities. The authors note that well over 7,000 references exist on fish and temperature, so the search was limited mainly to recent literature on freshwater and diadromous fishes of New England and to ecologically or physiologically similar species, with older classic papers included where still in common use. The bibliography contains roughly 457 numbered entries, and the authors acknowledge it is obsolete before publication.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Reduce time and effort spent on literature searches concerning [[Thermal Pollution]] and fish thermal biology.
- **Scope:** Two research directions are merged: (1) fish sensing of temperature and its influence on intralacustrine and geographic distribution, and (2) physiology of fish under different temperatures and life stages.
- **Taxonomy:** Appendix I phylogenetically lists the cited species, using names from the 1970 [[American Fisheries Society]] Special Publication (A List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada) wherever possible.
- **Format:** Each entry gives full citation followed by an abstract-style summary of the findings, often including numerical thresholds, sample sizes and lethal temperatures.
- **Coverage:** Entries span early work from the late 1800s to 1973 literature, including reviews such as [[Brett]] (1956), [[Coutant]]'s annual "Thermal Pollution - Biological Effects" reviews, and [[Krenkel and Parker]] (1969) symposium proceedings on [[Biological Aspects Of Thermal Pollution]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal discharge systems.** Power plant cooling systems (nuclear and fossil, including reservoirs, canals and river outfalls such as [[Savannah River Plant]], [[Par Pond]], [[Hanford Nuclear Station]], [[Connecticut Yankee Atomic Power Station]] and [[Oyster Creek Generating Station]]) were the most common study settings. Heated effluents create localized thermal plumes, and the bibliography repeatedly notes that these may be less damaging than expected because fish avoid lethal areas.

**Core physiological principles reported across the literature:**
- Temperature regulates metabolism, growth, feeding, digestion, reproduction, disease resistance, and tolerance to low oxygen and toxicants. Toxicity of many compounds (e.g., hydrogen sulfide, phenol, pesticides, cyanide) generally increases with temperature, although exceptions exist.
- Thermal acclimation is a key modifier: heat resistance is gained quickly and lost slowly, while cold acclimation is slower (e.g., [[Doudoroff]] 1942).
- Oxygen and temperature interact strongly; low dissolved oxygen reduces survival at high temperature, and a Bohr effect counterbalances low-temperature oxygen binding in salmonid blood.
- Endocrine and neural mechanisms (pituitary, thyroid, [[Acetylcholinesterase]] activity, [[Photoperiod]] interactions) contribute to thermal compensation and seasonal tolerance.

**Life-stage sensitivity.** Eggs and larvae are generally the most sensitive stages. Examples include:
- [[Striped Bass]] larvae and juveniles suffered significant losses in cooling passage above 30° C (Chadwick and Stevens, 1971).
- [[Atlantic Salmon]] smolts were more temperature sensitive than parr in fresh water.
- Sensitivity to thermal shock in [[Northern Pike]] embryos was linked to rapid temperature drops below 10° C.

**Fish–community responses.** Effects reported include species shifts, altered schooling, winter aggregation in heated areas, and increased susceptibility to predation and disease ([[Columnaris]], [[Ichthyophthirius]] and fungal infections).

## Key Findings

Selected findings reported in the bibliography (values as given in the source):

**Thermal tolerance and lethal limits**
- Brown trout, Atlantic salmon and roach were sensitive to summer discharges; laboratory tests showed lethality for brown trout and coarse fish, but field kills in Britain were uncommon because fish avoided plumes (Alabaster, 1962, 1963, 1969).
- Median survival time for sea trout smolts was 30 minutes at 26° C, 100 minutes at 24.7° C and 1,000 minutes at 23.2° C (Alabaster, 1967).
- Bluegill upper lethal temperatures were about 41.5° C under gradual warming (Hickman and Dewey, 1973); bluegill embryo maximum hatching occurred at 22.2 and 23.9° C (Banner and Van Arman, 1973).
- Ultimate upper lethal temperature for [[Tilapia]] (T. mossambica) was 38.2° C (Allanson and Noble, 1964).

**Reproduction and development**
- Walleye egg hatch was 28% for controls and 39% when fluctuation occurred at organogenesis (Allbaugh and Manz, 1964).
- Sockeye salmon egg upper and lower threshold temperatures were 55–57.5° F and 40–42.5° F (Combs, 1965).
- Largemouth bass egg survival was poor at 50° F and above 80° F (Kelley, 1968).
- Pike egg survival: maximum survival at 10° C; upper and lower lethal temperatures of 16.5 and 5.0° C (Muntyan and Reznichenko, 1966).

**Growth, feeding and metabolism**
- Optimum growth of juvenile sockeye occurred at 15° C for the highest rations, with no growth above 23° C (Brett et al., 1969).
- Brown trout fry fed daily grew slower at 10.5° C than at 12.5° C (Brown, 1951).
- Largemouth bass in heated areas ate less food than those in unheated areas (Bennett and Gibbons, 1972).
- Largemouth bass oxygen consumption rose markedly above 15° C (Clausen, 1933).

**Behavior and distribution**
- Chinook salmon and steelhead moved along the bank opposite the Hanford thermal discharge during peak river temperature (Coutant, 1969a).
- Juvenile American shad avoided rapid temperature rises greater than 4° C above acclimation temperature (Moss, 1970).
- Brook trout optimal temperature range was 11–16° C (MacCrimmon and Campbell, 1969).
- Rainbow trout acclimated at 5, 10, 15 and 20° C preferred 16, 15, 13 and 11° C respectively (Garside and Tait, 1958), an anomalous pattern.

**Notable applied and management findings**
- Thermal discharge sites in several reservoirs supported winter sport fisheries and higher game-fish production (Drew and Tilton, 1970; Marcy and Galvin, 1973).
- Winter aggregation of fish, including threadfin shad, in discharge areas was documented (Adair and DeMont, 1970; Strawn, 1963).
- Thermal effluents in Lake Erie and the Connecticut River showed mixed effects: some reductions in diversity, but no measurable mortality of migrating shad (Edwards, 1971; Merriman, 1970).
- Brett (1969) recommended mapping temperature tolerance, sustained swimming speed, metabolic rate and growth rate of key species, within a multidisciplinary approach.
- Sylvester (1972) noted that slight temperature increases can be lethal through synergism with other stressors.

**Conclusions of the bibliography**
- Temperature effects vary strongly with species, life stage, acclimation history, salinity, oxygen and pollutant interactions, so results cannot be generalized simply.
- Thermal water quality criteria should aim to sustain healthy fish populations above marginal abundance (Brett, 1969), and complete environmental surveys are needed before site selection (DeSylva, 1969).
