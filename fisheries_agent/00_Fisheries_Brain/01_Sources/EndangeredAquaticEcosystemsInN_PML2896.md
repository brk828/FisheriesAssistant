# Endangered Aquatic Ecosystems in North American Deserts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper, a Desert Fishes Council contribution published in the *Journal of the Arizona-Nevada Academy of Science* (Vol. 20, 1985), identifies 164 North American desert fishes as endangered, vulnerable, rare or indeterminate, and records 18 recently extinct fishes. The authors, the Endangered Species Committee of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], argue that habitat degradation and exotic species introductions are driving declines. They identify 15 aquatic ecosystems that each support at least four fishes of concern, together holding 83 vanishing fishes. Protection of remaining natural habitats and whole communities is presented as the best route to long-term survival.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Covers the [[Great Basin Desert]], [[Mojave Desert]], [[Sonoran Desert]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]], plus adjacent basins such as the Upper Colorado, Upper Klamath, Upper Pit and Malheur.
- **Ecosystem criteria:** An ecosystem qualifies if it supported at least four rare fishes in recent times. Boundaries follow native fish distribution, and endemism is a key indicator.
- **Status categories:** Follow the [[IUCN Red List Categories]] (endangered, vulnerable, rare, indeterminate), as applied by the Desert Fishes Council.
- **Context:** Mexican fishes and invertebrates are poorly known, and many taxa remain undescribed. The list of rare fishes records 182 fishes across 12 families. Cyprinids dominate with 84 fishes (46%), and 52 fishes (29%) are undescribed.
- **Status breakdown (per the list):** 46 endangered, 52 vulnerable, 46 rare, 20 indeterminate, 18 recently extinct.
- The issue also contains a frontispiece and dedication to Edwin P. Pister, and quotations from Alan Watts and Lao Tsu. These are not analysed here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Main threats**
- Groundwater pumping that lowers water tables and dries springs (e.g., Devils Hole, Comanche Springs, Ash Meadows).
- Impoundment and reservoir construction, such as [[Amistad Reservoir]] on the Rio Grande and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Hoover Dam]] on the Colorado River.
- Water diversion, channelization, and loss of riparian vegetation.
- Introduced exotic fishes, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Common Carp]], and [[Red Shiner]], which prey on or compete with natives and cause hybridization.
- Pollution from oil fields, agriculture and mining, and chemical vegetation control.

**Habitat character:** Many fishes are restricted to spring-fed systems with stable temperature and chemistry. In [[Cuatro Cienegas]], lagunas and pozos with karst geology support endemic biota. Several springs, such as Moorman (about 37°C, with very low dissolved oxygen), show that some endemics tolerate extreme conditions.

**Fifteen ecosystems identified** (each with at least four fishes of concern): Cuatro Cienegas, Gila River, Rio Grande, Pecos River, Railroad Valley, Colorado River, Green River, Pahranagat Valley, Parras Valley, La Media Luna, Ash Meadows, Upper White River, Moapa River, Rio Yaqui, and Upper Klamath Basin. Table 3 lists their approximate areas and fish counts; the largest is the Colorado River basin (about 631,936 km²) and the smallest is Ash Meadows (about 756 km²).

## Key Findings

- **164 fishes** are flagged as endangered, vulnerable, rare or indeterminate. **46** are endangered, and **18** are recently extinct.
- **15 ecosystems** support 83 vanishing fishes; each supports at least four fishes of concern.
- **Cuatro Cienegas (Coahuila)** is singled out as a natural experimental laboratory. It holds 12 endemic fishes, plus numerous endemic reptiles, at least six crustaceans and at least ten molluscs. The authors support a proposed national park and call for protecting whole communities rather than single species.
- **Gila River system:** Historically supported 12 fishes of concern. The Gila River and its tributaries have lost all populations of several native species, including *Ptychocheilus lucius* and *Xyrauchen texanus*. Aravaipa Creek is a key refugium, where seven native fishes still occur.
- **Rio Grande:** Amistad Reservoir inundated the Devils River and San Felipe Creek habitats. The phantom shiner (*Notropis orca*) and the Rio Grande bluntnose shiner are extinct. The Amistad gambusia is extinct, and the Big Bend gambusia (*Gambusia gaigei*) is endangered.
- **Pecos River:** Three species and one subspecies of fish are endemic. Groundwater overdraft caused failures of Comanche Springs and Leon Springs. The authors recommend maintaining spring flows for five taxa in the Diamond Y/Leon Creek system.
- **Railroad Valley:** Six fishes, including five *Gila bicolor* subspecies and the Railroad Valley springfish (*Crenichthys nevadae*), occur in springs and creeks. Carp and channel catfish threaten several populations.
- **Colorado River:** Hydrological change has favoured exotic fishes. Water losses from upper-basin reservoirs reached 5,172 cfs by 1980, about 27% of average flow at Lee's Ferry. The Las Vegas dace and Las Vegas leopard frog are extinct. The authors call for immediate recovery efforts.
- **Green River:** Introduced fishes are favoured after dam construction. The bonytail may be extinct in the system, and the humpback chub and Colorado squawfish are endangered.
- **Pahranagat Valley:** Five endemic fishes and two endemic invertebrates. Exotic fishes and molluscs have displaced natives; the Pahranagat spinedace (*Lepidomeda altivelis*) is extinct.
- **Parras Basin:** The stumptooth minnow (*Stypodon signifer*) and Parras pupfish (*Cyprinodon latifasciatus*) are extinct. Two surviving *Gila* forms are in imminent danger of extinction.
- **La Media Luna:** Five fishes and three crustaceans are endemic to 13 spring-fed locations. The Media Luna killifish (*Cualac tessellatus*) is endangered, and exotic cichlids and poeciliids threaten native species.
- **Ash Meadows:** Five fishes and at least 11 invertebrates are endemic. The Ash Meadows poolfish is extinct. The Devils Hole pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*), with about 150 to 400 individuals, is threatened by groundwater pumping. The Nature Conservancy acquired most private land in 1983, and it was transferred to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Upper White River:** Four endemic fishes and two molluscs in pluvial-relict springs. Exotic guppies and goldfish, and spring-channel modification, drive declines.
- **Moapa River:** Nine exotic fishes have been introduced. The Moapa dace is endangered, and the Moapa National Wildlife Refuge was established in 1979 and enlarged in 1983.
- **Rio Yaqui:** Holds the vast majority of habitat for two vulnerable, one indeterminate and one rare desert fish. The Yaqui topminnow is excluded from the list because of its wide distribution.
- **Upper Klamath Basin:** Three endemic sucker species and one subspecies are lacustrine, spawning in tributaries. Dams such as Chiloquin Dam impede spawning, and hybridization between *Chasmistes* and *Catostomus* is documented.
- **Overall recommendation:** Protect natural habitats and communities, maintain spring flows, control exotic species, and survey poorly known invertebrates before more species are lost.
