# What Have I Caught? Kansas Fish Identification Guide

#ichthyology #freshwater-fish #identification-guide #Kansas #game-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1947 educational bulletin by John Breukelman provides a practical field guide for identifying game fish, rough fish, and common bait minnows in [[Kansas]] waters. Published by the Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission, the guide uses simplified terminology and illustrated keys to help anglers and nature enthusiasts identify their catches without requiring technical ichthyological knowledge. The document covers approximately 50+ species across multiple families, emphasizing practical field characteristics over scientific nomenclature, though a technical checklist is provided. The guide addresses common naming confusion (e.g., "bream," "perch") and focuses on species most likely encountered by Kansas fishermen.

## Background & Description

The bulletin explicitly targets fishermen and nature enthusiasts rather than professional ichthyologists, using "plain English words" with minimal technical terminology. Author John Breukelman, Head of the Department of Biological Science at Kansas State Teachers College of Emporia, notes this represents a preliminary work, with plans for a complete technical paper on Kansas fishes including "all the small, obscure and doubtful ones" anticipated for future publication.

The guide employs illustrated keys organized by fish family, with detailed anatomical diagrams showing key identification features: gill covers, dorsal fins, tail fins, barbels, body depth, head length, jaw structure, and fin ray counts. A standardized fish anatomy diagram (page 3) introduces terminology including lateral line, pectoral fins, ventral fins, anal fins, and the "ear" (gill cover extension, clarified as unrelated to hearing—fishes detect vibrations via lateral lines).

Species coverage includes:
- **[[Catfish]] family (Ictaluridae)**: [[Channel Cat]], [[Blue Cat]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Mud Cat]] (flathead), [[Stone Cat]], mad toms
- **[[Black Bass]] and [[Sunfish]] family (Centrarchidae)**: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Orange-spotted Sunfish]], [[Long-eared Sunfish]], [[Warmouth]], [[Rock Bass]], [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]]
- **[[Perch]] family (Percidae)**: [[Ring Perch]] (yellow perch), [[Jack Salmon]] (walleye)
- **[[Minnow]] family (Cyprinidae)**: [[German Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Stoneroller]], [[Horned Dace]], [[Redfin Shiner]], [[River Chub]], [[Bullhead Minnow]]
- **[[Sucker]] family (Catostomidae)**: [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], [[Black Buffalo]], [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], [[Quillback]], [[Bluntnose Rivercarp]], [[Common Rivercarp]], [[Blue Sucker]], [[Fine-scaled Sucker]], [[Hogmolly]], [[Chub Sucker]], [[Spotted Sucker]], [[Common Redhorse]], [[Black Mullet]], [[Northern Redhorse]], [[Pavement-toothed Sucker]]
- **Other families**: [[Drum]] (Sciaenidae), [[Gizzard Shad]] (Clupeidae), [[Long-nosed Gar]] and [[Short-nosed Gar]] (Lepisosteidae), [[Sturgeon]] (Acipenseridae), [[Paddlefish]] (Polyodontidae), [[American Eel]] (Anguillidae), [[Mooneye]] species (Hiodontidae), [[Bowfin]] and [[Burbot]] (potential species)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Preferences and Distribution:**

The guide documents species-specific habitat associations across Kansas aquatic ecosystems:

- **[[Channel Cat]]**: Successfully propagated at Pratt hatchery since before 1947, widely distributed statewide, considered by many fishermen as the "State Fish"
- **[[Blue Cat]]**: Large river fish, common in [[Missouri River]], rare in [[Kansas River]] (Kaw) and [[Osage River]] (Marais des Cygnes)
- **[[Bullhead]]s**: [[Black Bullhead]] excellent for stocking small ponds and shallow lakes due to air-gulping ability when oxygen runs low; can survive weeks in mud after water evaporation; feed on animal and vegetable matter, tending toward vegetarian when choice available
- **[[Mud Cat]]** (flathead): Bottom feeder consuming varied foods including other fishes
- **[[Stone Cat]]**: Lives under rocks in rapid streams, reaches 10-12 inches; possesses poison gland at pectoral spine base (effect comparable to bee sting)

**Sucker Ecology:**

Suckers demonstrate complex ecological roles as both forage and competitor species:

- **[[Buffalo]]fishes**: Inhabit muddy waters, feed on snails, insect larvae, worms in mud; common in eastern Kansas, scattered westward; coarse flesh, limited food value
- **[[Rivercarp]]** (carpsuckers): More silvery than buffalo, prefer small creeks or ponds, swallow more mud while foraging
- **[[Blue Sucker]]**: Found in Missouri, Kaw, Neosho and other large streams; very small head, eyes set in hind part of head
- **[[Fine-scaled Sucker]]**: Most widely distributed sucker statewide, reaches over 12 inches, favored food fish in some localities; prefers clear cool water, feeds on small clams and snails with crushing teeth; essentially a small clear stream species adapted to wide Kansas conditions (eastern and western varieties exist)
- **[[Hogmolly]]**: Southeastern Kansas, lives in swifter stream sections with riffles, sucks food from plant growth on downstream sides of rocks using large head, thick lips, depressed skull top
- **[[Chub Sucker]]**: Southern Kansas, apparently extending range northward; young have broad black lateral band breaking into spots that disappear in adults
- **[[Spotted Sucker]]**: Most common in [[Neosho River]] and [[Verdigris River]], lives in tributaries rather than main streams; reported to reach 24 inches (largest Kansas record ~9 inches)
- **[[Redhorse]] suckers (mullets)**: Four Kansas species with varying fin coloration—bright red tail in northern redhorse, reddish/orange in pavement-toothed, slate to olive gray in others; [[Common Redhorse]] found throughout state, numerous in some localities

General sucker ecology: Primarily stream fishes preferring smaller streams with slow to moderate current; do not tolerate stagnation or oxygen deficiency well; prefer stream bottoms mixing sand/rock with mud rather than pure mud; value as forage fishes balanced against competition with forage minnows when suckers grow too large for game fish consumption.

**Minnow Family Ecology:**

- **[[German Carp]]**: European/Chinese native introduced 1880 (initial 160 specimens from St. Louis stock), now among most widespread Kansas fishes despite ~40 years of control measures; value disputed; originally regarded as good food fish
- **[[Goldfish]]**: Oriental introduction established in various Kansas streams; wild specimens usually silvery olive with variation; surface feeders taking almost any food, prefer insects, useful for mosquito control (feed on surface-breathing "wigglers")
- **[[Golden Shiner]]**: Largest native Kansas minnow (12+ inches), favorite bait minnow, greater value as forage fish for bass, crappie, carnivorous species; prefers quiet waters with vegetation in ponds and ditches; active all winter, taken through ice; feeds on varied plant and animal food, excellent indicator of general lake growth conditions

Minnow identification challenges: ~50 Kansas species with close resemblance; males and females differ in coloration and size; males change color during breeding season and develop tubercles (temporary or permanent "horns" or bumps). Common bait minnows include [[Stoneroller]] (horny-nosed chub, 4-6 inches, extensive tubercle development in breeding males), [[Horned Dace]] (creek chub, bluish olive to silvery, tail spot prominent in young, disappears in adults up to 12 inches), [[Redfin Shiner]] (bright red fins in breeding males with iridescent steel blue color, otherwise silvery with plain fins, 3-3.5 inches), [[River Chub]] (horny-head with tubercles mostly on swollen head during breeding, greenish to yellowish gray, no tail spot, similar size to horned dace), [[Bullhead Minnow]] (rarely exceeds 3 inches, breeding males almost black head with dusky body, short fleshy first dorsal ray, tail spot sometimes missing).

Minnows serve as critical forage fishes, converting microscopic plant and animal life into game fish food; reduced minnow populations decrease food supply for larger fishes unable to feed directly on algae and other basic foods.

**Other Species Ecology:**

- **[[Drum]]** (sheepshead): Common in eastern Kansas, found statewide; long dorsal fin, lateral line extends to tail fin end; general feeder with strong teeth for eating clams and snails (even large ones); successfully propagated at state hatchery, stocked in many state lakes; reaches 50-60 pounds (Kansas average 1-2 pounds); member of marine croaker family, makes croaking/grunting sounds
- **[[Gizzard Shad]]**: Common statewide, particularly in lakes and ponds; does not bite well, coarse flavorless flesh, but "one of the most valuable species" as forage fish due to ability to live on microscopic plants and animals from water or bottom silt; grows rapidly, important link between basic lake food and game fishes; does not withstand unfavorable conditions well, usually first to die during stress periods; travels in large schools, jumps and skips over surface when disturbed
- **[[Gar]]s**: Two Kansas species—[[Long-nosed Gar]] (most common, beak ~20 times as long as wide) and [[Short-nosed Gar]] (mostly in Kaw and Osage, beak ~5 times as long as wide); sharp-edged scales and beaks with sharp teeth make them "most destructive" to game fishes and fishing gear; "unqualified nuisance" though possible value suggested for controlling seriously overstocked lakes (needs experimental study; jack salmon likely more suitable predator)
- **[[Sturgeon]]**: Two species reported (lake and shovelnose), likely only [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] enters Kansas waters; large stream fish, fairly common in Missouri River, enters Kaw and Osage; feeds on snails, insect larvae, small bottom animals, seldom takes hook
- **[[Paddlefish]]** (spoonbill): Large stream fish apparently restricted to Kaw and Osage; feeds on small animal and plant life; "paddle" apparently used to work food from bottom mud
- **[[American Eel]]**: Found in all larger Kansas streams, apparently more numerous east of [[Flint Hills]]; general feeding habits, might take almost any bait; looks too snake-like for most Kansans, usually discarded
- **[[Lamprey]]**: Not true fish (gristle skeleton, no jaws, no paired fins, no gill covers); circular rasping mouth drills into other fish tissues; not common but may occur in almost any larger Kansas stream; lives in quiet pools, does not bite ordinary baits; occasionally hooked or found parasitizing catfish or larger fish

**Stocking and Management:**

The guide documents active fish management by Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission:
- [[Channel Cat]] propagation and wide distribution from Pratt hatchery (multi-year program by 1947)
- [[Drum]] successful propagation and stocking in state lakes
- [[Spotted Bass]] (Kentucky bass) widely distributed over state
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] planted outside native southeast corner range but "does not do well in Kansas waters"
- [[Jack Salmon]] (walleye) recently introduced into some state lakes with "considerable success, although it is too early to state definitely" (as of 1947)
- Various [[Pickerel]] family members introduced with possibility of occasional specimens; species would probably succeed in larger artificial lakes, though fishermen disagree on desirability due to indiscriminate predation

**Water Quality and Environmental Tolerance:**

Species demonstrate varying tolerances:
- [[Black Bullhead]] tolerates severe conditions including low oxygen (air-gulping), can survive in mud after water evaporation
- [[Gizzard Shad]] does not withstand unfavorable conditions well, first to die during stress
- Suckers generally do not tolerate stagnation or oxygen deficiency well

**Naming Confusion and Misidentification:**

The guide addresses extensive common name problems:
- "Bream" used for so many different fishes "it means practically nothing"
- "White perch" and "white bass" mistakenly applied to [[Drum]]
- "Flathead" should be restricted to [[Mud Cat]] but applied to large bullheads or old channel cats
- "Sun perch" applied randomly to sunfishes
- "Perch" correctly applies only to [[Ring Perch]] (yellow perch), not sunfishes
- "Jack salmon" = "wall-eyed pike" (Minnesota) = "yellow pikeperch" or "yellow pickerel" (Great Lakes)
- "Stoneroller" used for both [[Hogmolly]] and important bait minnow
- "Skip jack" mistakenly applied to [[Gizzard Shad]] (name belongs to herring family member not in Kansas, commonly applied to [[Silverside]] minnow)
- "Dogfish" should not be used for [[Mud Puppy]] (name accepted for other species)
- "Hell-bender" should not be used for [[Mud Puppy]]
- Checked specimens labeled "silver bass" turned out to be pale [[Largemouth Bass]] or [[Drum]]
- Checked specimen labeled "blue herring" was [[Gizzard Shad]]
- Reports of alligator gar probably based on exceptionally large [[Long-nosed Gar]]

## Key Findings

**Identification Keys and Diagnostic Features:**

The bulletin provides detailed morphological characters for field identification:

**[[Catfish]] Family Distinctions:**
- [[Channel Cat]] vs [[Blue Cat]]: Anal fin shape most reliable (channel has rounded lower lobes, blue has equal lobes); tail fork differences (channel has longer upper lobe, blue has equal lobes); eye position (channel higher); spotting (channel usually spotted, blue usually not); range (Kansas catches are channel except in Missouri, Kaw, Osage rivers where blue possible)
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] vs [[Black Bullhead]]: Anal fin length and shape most constant (yellow has longer anal fin with 24-27 rays, black has shorter with 17-21 rays); barbel color fairly reliable (yellow has white/light grey chin barbels, black has black/dark grey); tail fin shape (both rounded but differences disappear in large specimens); body proportions (yellow heavier, black more slender)
- [[Mud Cat]] (flathead/shovelhead): Flat head, wide mouth, mottled pattern, slender body relative to head width, large fatty fin, short anal fin; extremely variable in color, body proportion, lower jaw extension
- [[Stone Cat]]: Fatty fin attached to tail fin (not free at back end, only notch separating); lives under rocks in rapid streams, 10-12 inches; poisonous pectoral spine (effect like bee sting, not dangerous)
- Mad toms (tadpole cats): 3-4 Kansas species, similar to stone cat including poisonous pectoral spine, smaller (rarely over 3 inches)

**[[Black Bass]] Distinctions:**
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: Upper jaw extends back of eye, dorsal fins almost separate, plain coloration, more robust body, tail spot small and disappears in adults
- [[Smallmouth Bass]]: Upper jaw does not extend back of eye, dorsal fins joined, vertical streaks or bars, less robust, tail spot prominent
- [[Spotted Bass]]: Upper jaw even with back of eye, dorsal fin deeply cleft, rows of spots, intermediate robustness, tail spot prominent

**[[Sunfish]] Family Characteristics:**
- [[Green Sunfish]]: Large mouth, 10-12 anal rays, prominent spot on anal fin, dark spot on "ear"
- [[Orange-spotted Sunfish]]: Small mouth, about 8 anal rays, less prominent anal spot, orange spots on sides, dark spot on "ear"
- [[Bluegill]]: Small mouth, 10-12 anal rays, about 20-30 rays in soft dorsal, dark spot on "ear," no margin around ear spot
- [[Long-eared Sunfish]]: Small mouth, 8-10 anal rays, about 30-35 rays in soft dorsal, very long "ear" with light margin
- [[Warmouth]]: Large mouth, 3 anal spines, 8-10 anal rays, teeth on tongue (internal), mostly eastern quarter Kansas, spreading westward
- [[Rock Bass]]: 5-6 anal spines (vs 3 in sunfishes), shorter anal fin than crappie, large deeply red eye ("goggle-eye," "red-eye"), extreme color change ability (silver to dark green/black in minutes), eastern Kansas, decreasing
- [[White Crappie]]: Usually 6 dorsal spines, vertical bars pattern
- [[Black Crappie]]: Usually 7 dorsal spines, scattered irregular spots, often called "calico bass" or "giant crappie"

Sunfish hybridization: Much crossing occurs; collection from Jacob's Well included [[Green Sunfish]], [[Orange-spotted Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]] "and every possible combination of these three species."

**[[Perch]] Family:**
- [[Ring Perch]] (yellow perch, American perch): Separate spiny and soft dorsal fins, vertical bars ("rings") nearly always present; widely distributed but does not compete well with basses and sunfishes in most localities
