# Genetic Variation among Lower Colorado River Basin Gila (Teleostei: Cyprinidae): Conservation of a Species Complex

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #conservation-biology #population-genetics #hybridization

## Executive Summary

This study used protein electrophoresis to analyze genetic variation among morphologically divergent populations of [[Gila]] species from the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. The research examined [[Gila intermedia]], [[Gila robusta]], and phenotypically intermediate populations formerly referred to as "G. r. grahami". Results revealed that genetic divergence did not parallel taxonomic designations based on morphology. Highest levels of variation occurred among populations of G. intermedia rather than between nominal taxa. Substantial population subdivision characterized isolated stocks of G. intermedia, whereas G. robusta was genetically more homogeneous. Morphologically intermediate "grahami" populations may have arisen through introgression between G. intermedia and G. robusta during wetter climatic cycles. These findings have critical implications for conservation: protection of G. intermedia should involve preserving as many populations as possible to maintain overall genetic diversity, while concentrated protection of several G. robusta strongholds may be adequate.

## Background & Description

The genus [[Gila]] from the [[Colorado River Basin]] exhibits complex morphological patterns with an unstable nomenclatural history. Two extant nominal taxa represent morphological extremes in the lower basin: [[Gila intermedia]] (Girard, 1856), a thick-bodied, small-finned, coarse-scaled species from small streams and marshes, and [[Gila robusta]] Baird and Girard (1853), a slender, larger-finned, fine-scaled species inhabiting both small and large rivers. G. intermedia, endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]], was originally described as a member of genus Tigoma and has been assigned to five genera with at least nine synonyms. Currently recognized as a distinct species, it occupies a mosaic distribution with G. robusta in an almost-absolute geographic isolation pattern.

Morphologically intermediate forms exist between G. intermedia and G. robusta, previously referred to as "G. r. grahami" by Rinne (1976). These populations span the morphological hiatus between the two species rather than representing a discrete entity. The study employed protein electrophoresis to analyze 25 presumptive gene loci across 27 populations, using [[UPGMA]] and distance Wagner analyses to examine phenetic relationships and Wright's F-statistics to quantify distribution of genetic variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[American Southwest]] is an arid and topographically complex region where aquatic habitats tend to be isolated and ecologically dissimilar. The study area encompasses the lower [[Colorado River Basin]], including the entire drainage system downstream from [[Grand Canyon]]. Tributary basins drain the southern escarpment of the [[Colorado Plateau]] and highlands of the southern [[Basin and Range]] physiographic province.

Continuity of surface flow has been disrupted by increasing aridity from Tertiary to Recent times and accelerated by anthropogenic damming and dewatering in the past century. Permanent habitats sustaining native fish populations fall into two categories: (1) small headwater streams often receiving spring discharge; and (2) unmodified, intermediate-elevation sections of larger rivers such as the [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], and [[Gila River]]. Surface connections between habitats occur only during periods of exceedingly high runoff, and potential for upstream fish dispersal is often naturally constrained by barrier falls or interdicted by dams.

Habitat modification and introduction of exotic species have critically endangered these fishes. Only G. intermedia and G. robusta maintain several extant populations, though both are candidates for federal protection. G. robusta has dramatically declined in lower-basin rivers where it was once abundant, while G. intermedia populations in smaller tributaries have been less modified but remain vulnerable to catastrophe and introductions.

## Key Findings

**Genetic Structure and Taxonomic Relationships:**
- Allelic polymorphism was detected at 16 of 25 loci examined
- No fixed allelic differences or unique alleles unambiguously discriminated between G. robusta and G. intermedia
- Populations designated "grahami" did not constitute a single cohesive group but were interspersed among G. robusta and G. intermedia
- Lower basin populations formed a distinct cluster separate from [[Gila elegans]], [[Gila cypha]], and upper basin G. robusta
- Fixed allelic differences at CBP-1, CK-A, and AK-A loci distinguished upper basin species from lower basin forms

**Population Subdivision:**
- Mean F_ST values were significantly higher in G. intermedia (0.431) than in G. robusta (0.080)
- "Grahami" populations showed high F_ST values (0.458) similar to G. intermedia
- Hierarchical F-statistics revealed that the majority of genetic variation (41.0-43.7%) was accounted for by differences among localities rather than by differences among taxa, sub-basins, or stream order
- G. robusta populations were genetically far more homogeneous than G. intermedia populations

**Ecological Correlates:**
- Stronger genetic subdivision in G. intermedia and "grahami" correlates with their occurrence in smaller, more isolated headwater streams
- Weaker population subdivision in G. robusta reflects better connectivity between preferred habitats (larger streams and tributary mouths)
- Physical barriers (intermittent flow, steep gradients, barrier falls) and life-history factors promote isolation in headwater streams
- Strong habitat partitioning exists: G. intermedia and G. robusta have never been collected together even in contiguous habitats

**Hybridization Evidence:**
- Morphologically intermediate "grahami" populations likely arose through introgression between G. intermedia and G. robusta during wetter climatic cycles
- Ongoing hybridization detected in [[Eagle Creek]], the only stream where both species currently co-occur
- Significant allelic frequency difference at LDH-B locus between upper and lower Eagle Creek populations (P < 0.005)
- Middle Eagle Creek population exhibited intermediate allelic frequency, suggesting contemporary gene flow

**Conservation Implications:**
- Genetic and morphological homogeneity in G. robusta suggests concentrated protection of several strongholds may be adequate
- High variation among G. intermedia populations requires protection of as many populations as possible to preserve overall genetic diversity
- Morphologically intermediate forms ("grahami") represent important components of variation and should be afforded protection regardless of taxonomic status
- Substantial population subdivision indicates high potential for local adaptation
- Managers should not assume morphologically variant populations are interchangeable products of developmental plasticity
- Protection should encompass the entire species complex to maintain existing genetic diversity and evolutionary potential

**Virgin River Basin Populations:**
- [[Gila seminuda]] populations were polymorphic at AK-A, CBP-1, and CK-A loci
- Virgin River populations clustered with either lower basin or upper basin groups, or formed intermediate branches
- G. seminuda from [[Moapa River]] and G. r. jordani share an AK-A allele otherwise found only in G. cypha
- These patterns suggest complex evolutionary history involving hybridization
