## Executive Summary
This study by Henry van der Schalie examines the disjunct distribution of freshwater mussel species (family [[Unionidae]]) across northern Michigan river systems, using it as biological evidence for former paleohydrological connections. The presence of typically "southern" (Mississippian-affiliated) mussel species in isolated northern rivers—the [[Millecoquin River]], [[Carp River]], and [[Ocqueoc River]]—suggests these waterways were once part of a continuous dendritic drainage network. This connectivity is attributed to the existence of the ancient **Mackinac River**, which flowed through the basin of northern Lake Michigan during a significant postglacial low-water stage approximately 8,500 years ago.

## Background & Description
*Note: The source text is an abstract focused on biogeography and distribution; it does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of shell characteristics. The following are the taxonomic identities of the species referenced:*

- **[[Alasmidonta marginata]]** (Say) — Elktoe
- **[[Elliptio dilatatus]]** (Rafinesque) — Spike
- **[[Lasmigona costata]]** (Rafinesque) — Fluted-shell
- **[[Elliptio complanatus]]** (Dillwyn) — Eastern Elliptio

These species are classified into two distinct biogeographic groups based on their ancestral drainage origins:
1. **Mississippian Fauna**: *A. marginata*, *E. dilatatus*, and *L. costata* are typically associated with the Mississippi River drainage system.
2. **Atlantic Fauna**: *E. complanatus* is typically associated with Atlantic slope drainages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper focuses on the hydrogeological history required to explain the current niche occupancy of these mollusks:

- **Habitat Requirement**: As obligate freshwater mussels ([[Unionidae]]), these species require continuous flowing river or connected stream habitats for both adult residency and larval (glochidia) dispersal, which is typically host-fish dependent.
- **Dispersal Mechanism**: The presence of these species in geographically separated northern rivers today implies a historical dispersal event via a now-vanished river corridor, rather than independent colonization or introduction.
- **The Paleo-Corridor**: The "Mackinac River" is identified as the critical vector for this dispersal. It occupied the basin of northern Lake Michigan during a period of low water levels, effectively linking river systems that are now isolated by open lake waters.
- **Directional Migration**: 
    - The Mississippi-origin species (*A. marginata*, *E. dilatatus*, *L. costata*) migrated **northward and eastward** via this corridor.
    - The Atlantic-origin species (*E. complanatus*) migrated **westward**, reaching the Ocqueoc River from the opposite direction.

## Key Findings
- **Discontinuous Distribution as Evidence**: The disjunct (non-continuous) distribution of specific mussel species across northern Michigan rivers serves as biological proxy evidence for former river connectivity, corroborating findings from glacial geology.
- **Confirmation of the Mackinac River**: The mussel distribution patterns support the hypothesis of a former "Mackinac River" that occupied the bed of northern Lake Michigan during the Chippewa-Stanley low water stage (~8,500 years ago).
- **Bi-directional Faunal Exchange**: The data reveals a rare instance of bi-directional faunal exchange, where Mississippian species moved east/north while an Atlantic species ([[Elliptio complanatus]]) moved west, converging in the Ocqueoc River drainage.
- **Corroboration of Fox River/Green Bay Connections**: This biogeographic evidence for Michigan confluences also lends support to previously hypothesized connections in the region of the [[Fox River (Wisconsin)]] and Green Bay, suggesting a broader network of postglacial drainage connections across the Great Lakes region.
- **Interdisciplinary Methodology**: This work exemplifies the use of biogeography (specifically malacology) as a tool to validate and support geological reconstructions of postglacial landscapes and hydrology.
