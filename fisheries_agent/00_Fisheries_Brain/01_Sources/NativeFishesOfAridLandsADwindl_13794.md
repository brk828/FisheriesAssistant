# Native Fishes of Arid Lands: A Dwindling Resource of the Desert Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-206, 1991) combines photographs by [[John N. Rinne]] with a text by [[W. L. Minckley]] to document the native freshwater fishes of the arid American Southwest and northwestern Mexico. It describes the region's aquatic systems, the fishes they support, and the habitat those fishes require. The authors argue that native fishes are declining because of water diversion, dams, habitat degradation, and, above all, the spread of non-native species. Of 41 strictly freshwater species discussed, 28 (68%) were officially listed as threatened, endangered, or of special concern in 1989, and three further species had gone extinct. The stated goal is to raise public awareness so that these fishes are valued and conserved.

## Background & Description

- **Scope and purpose:** The report includes photographs of fish species, many published for the first time, and covers aquatic systems in the mountains and deserts of the region. The Preface gives the photograph count as 46 species, while the front-matter summary gives 44. Scientific names are largely left out of the main text and are listed in Appendix II.
- **Region:** The American Southwest spans parts of 12 states, including Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Texas, plus Mexican states such as Sonora, Chihuahua, and Baja California. Elevations range from sea level to more than 3600 m, and annual precipitation from less than 5.0 cm in the most severe desert to more than 128 cm at high elevations.
- **Geologic history:** Desert conditions are recent in geologic terms. Woodlands and prairies covered areas that are now desert only a few thousand years ago. Uplift of the [[Sierra Nevada]] and other ranges created a rain shadow, and Miocene faulting shaped the basin-and-range landscape.
- **Hydrology:** Water originates on high mountains and reaches the lowlands as perennial streams only where montane watersheds are large. The [[Colorado River]], [[Gila River]], and [[Rio Yaqui]] are the major through-flowing systems. Other basins are closed and drain only by evaporation or seepage.
- **Fish taxonomy:** Minnows (Cyprinidae) and suckers (Catostomidae) make up a large share of the native fauna. Common names are often confusing, so the report pairs English and Spanish names.
- **Conservation terms:** Status categories follow the American Fisheries Society: "endangered," "threatened," and "special concern."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human impacts.** Dams, diversions, groundwater pumping, channelization, and watershed abuse (overgrazing, logging, mining) have transformed the region's rivers. Pumping lowered water tables more than 50 m in most valleys and more than 200 m in some. Arroyo cutting drained riparian zones, and cottonwood-willow woodlands were replaced by introduced [[Saltcedar]] and Russian olive. Mexican systems are less developed but face similar pressures.

**Habitat zones.**
- *High-elevation creeks (above about 1800 m):* These are cool enough for trout, which exclude most warmwater species. Native trout live in pools and cover, and spawn after snowmelt. Beaver ponds once supported large trout populations.
- *Intermediate elevations (about 1800 to 900 m):* Most remaining native fish populations occur here. Water temperatures range widely, with afternoon summer temperatures near 30 °C, and flash floods supply most annual discharge. Groundwater seeps at bedrock dikes create refuges during drought.
- *Low desert rivers:* Historically, large rivers had seasonal floods, alluvial valleys, canyons, and marshes. The lower Colorado River had sand-bottomed deltas with dense riparian forests, and tides in the Gulf of California exceed 9.0 m.
- *Desert springs:* Constant temperatures and chemistry support specialized fishes with year-round reproduction. Some springs are hot, low in oxygen, and saline.

**Biological interactions.** The authors identify biological interactions, mainly predation and competition from introduced fishes, as the ultimate cause of native fish loss in the United States. Native fishes evolved without piscine predators comparable to those elsewhere in North America, so they had little defense against introduced predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Mosquitofish]]. Hybridization with non-native trout and chubs is another major threat.

## Key Findings

- **Species distribution:** Of 41 strictly freshwater fishes discussed, 10 occur only in Mexico, 9 only in the United States, and 22 in both countries.
- **Conservation status:** In 1989, 28 of the 41 taxa (68%) were recognized as threatened, endangered, or of special concern. Three species were reported extinct: the Las Vegas speckled dace, the Pahranagat spinedace, and the [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]].
- **Apache and Gila trout:** Both were pushed out of much of their ranges by non-native trouts. By the 1970s, hybridization had reduced [[Apache Trout]] to less than 5.0% of its former range. [[Gila Trout]] persists naturally in only five streams. Apache trout are federally listed as threatened and Gila trout as endangered.
- **Colorado River fauna:** The original fish fauna of the Colorado River has increased from 32 species to more than 80 through introductions, as native fishes were replaced by non-native competitors and predators.
- **[[Bonytail]]:** Fewer than 50 individuals were caught despite intensive efforts since 1975. Adults found in nature appear old (35–40 years), and no successful recruitment has been recorded for decades.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]:** A population of more than 50,000 individuals exists in [[Lake Mohave]]. Larvae are produced in large numbers but disappear before reaching the juvenile stage, and there is no evidence of recruitment for at least 30 years.
- **[[Sonoran Topminnow]]:** Now found in fewer than 10 natural localities in the United States, though its total population may number in the millions.
- **[[Woundfin]]:** Known from the Gila and lower Colorado rivers in the late 1800s, it is now restricted to the Virgin River mainstream.
- **[[Moapa Dace]]:** Restricted to thermal pools and outflows of the upper Moapa River. It is rarely found in water cooler than 30 °C, and its preferred range is 31–34 °C. It is listed as endangered.
- **Rio Yaqui diversity:** The Rio Yaqui supports 20 species, only three of which are shared with the Colorado system. Its canyon reaches have been poorly sampled, and new swift-water species may remain undiscovered.
- **Marine species:** Striped mullet, Pacific tenpounder, and [[Totoaba]] entered the lower river systems. Totoaba has nearly disappeared, likely from overfishing and dam construction. Some marine species, including striped mullet and Pacific tenpounder, persisted in the Salton Sea for a few years after it filled in 1905–07.
- **Management recommendations:** The authors recommend isolating and protecting selected native populations, using hatchery strains for restocking, and excluding non-native trout from native ranges. They also call for experimental research on how biological interactions drive species replacement, which they say remains largely untested.
- **Core conclusion:** Native fishes persist where physical and chemical modifications are minimal. In the United States, however, native species continue to disappear even from relatively natural streams, a pattern the authors attribute to introduced fishes. In Mexico, the same pattern is expected as reservoirs and introductions expand.
