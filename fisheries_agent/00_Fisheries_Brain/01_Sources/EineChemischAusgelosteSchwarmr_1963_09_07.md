## Executive Summary
This 1963 short communication by Wolfdietrich Kühme investigates whether newly free-swimming juvenile cichlids ([[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]] and [[Hemichromis Fasciatus]]) can chemically recognize and orient toward siblings, extending prior work on chemically-triggered parental brood-care behavior in cichlids. Using a controlled aquarium bioassay with neutral water versus "sibling water" (water conditioned by 50 conspecific juveniles), the author demonstrates a genuine **chemically elicited schooling (aggregation) reaction** in very young fry, which rapidly diminishes with age and is species-specific in its temporal window.

## Background & Description
This paper is behavioral/ecological rather than morphological; no anatomical descriptions of the fish are provided. Only developmental/life-stage descriptors are given:
- **Larval/juvenile stage tested:** 1–3 day post-free-swimming fry (freischwimmende Jungfische)
- **Locomotor behavior noted:** Very young *H. fasciatus* fry display a characteristic "hopping" or skimming locomotion along the substrate ("hüpfend über den Boden gleiten") during the first hours of free-swimming, transitioning later to more elevated, faster swimming.
- **Swimming capacity as a developmental constraint:** The author hypothesizes that limited swimming ability in the youngest fry may preclude optically-directed appetence behavior, which could explain why chemical orientation is most detectable at this early stage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Social structure:** Juvenile cichlids of both species form dense sibling schools under parental guard, consistent with known biparental brood care in [[Cichlidae]].
- **Chemical family recognition:** Building on earlier findings that adult cichlid parents can be induced to show brood-care behavior upon smelling water previously occupied by their own brood, this study asks whether juveniles reciprocally recognize siblings via **chemical cues** (kin-water discrimination), independent of visual contact.
- **Experimental arena:** A 60 × 60 × 60 cm swimming tank with a sand substrate, divided into a grid of 10 cm² "Planquadrate" (plan-squares) for spatial tracking of swim paths.
- **Stimulus delivery:** Two silicone tubes continuously delivered ~10 cm³/min of water: one delivering neutral (unconditioned) water, the other delivering water from a sibling group of 50 juveniles ("Geschwisterwasser"), positioned 20 cm apart on the tank floor.
- **Test subjects:** Individual fry drawn from a main school of ~500 individuals, released 15 cm from both tube outlets and tracked for up to 10–16 minutes.
- **Comparative species response window:** 
  - *H. bimaculatus* fry reliably oriented to sibling water on day 1–2 of free swimming, with only a single case (Trial 22) showing response on day 3.
  - *H. fasciatus* fry oriented to sibling water only during the first hours of free swimming, while still exhibiting the substrate-hopping locomotion; this response was lost once normal swimming behavior developed.
  - Fry older than ~15 days from either species never showed orientation to sibling water in any subsequent trials.
- **Species specificity of the cue:** In Trials 18–21, fry were offered water from *unrelated* two-day-old *H. fasciatus* larvae instead of true siblings; test animals did **not** orient to this water, indicating the cue is not merely species-generic but tied to specific sibling/kin group chemistry (or at least closely age/brood-matched chemistry). Trial 29 further showed an *H. fasciatus* juvenile actively rejecting/avoiding water from four-day-old *H. bimaculatus* juveniles.

## Key Findings
- **Chemically triggered schooling reaction confirmed:** Newly free-swimming cichlid fry actively seek out and preferentially remain within the chemical plume of sibling-conditioned water, demonstrating a non-visual, chemosensory basis for early kin/schoolmate attraction — a finding parallel to and extending previous demonstrations of chemically elicited parental brood-care behavior in the same genus ([[Hemichromis]]).
- **Behavioral trajectory after stimulus location:** Once a fry locates the sibling-water zone, it does not remain stationary but circles the area in progressively widening arcs at increasing swim speed, suggesting exploratory rather than purely attractive fixation.
- **Time-limited responsiveness:** The chemical "hold" of the sibling cue decays with separation time — after 8–15 minutes of isolation from siblings, fry can no longer be captured/held by the scent and instead show restless, undirected "appetence swimming" (Appetenzschwimmen) toward the tank walls.
- **Search-behavior interference effect:** If a newly introduced fry searches for more than 2 minutes without encountering the sibling odor, its subsequent excessive/overshooting search behavior appears to block further susceptibility to *any* schooling stimulus — whether optical or chemical — for the remainder of the trial.
- **Developmental narrowing of the response window:** The chemical orientation capacity is essentially restricted to the earliest 1–3 days of free-swimming life (species-dependent), disappearing entirely by ~15 days post-hatch.
- **Species-specific discrimination:** Fry discriminate between true sibling water and water from age-matched but non-sibling conspecifics of a related species, and reject/avoid water from a different species' older juveniles, indicating fine-scale chemical specificity in kin recognition cues.
- **Proposed explanation for restricted detectability:** The author suggests older juveniles may retain some chemosensory kin-recognition ability, but that improved swimming competence with age enables optically-directed appetence behavior, which may mask or override reliance on chemical cues — meaning the chemical schooling reaction could be more widespread developmentally but simply harder to detect once visual orientation becomes dominant.
- **Methodological contribution:** Establishes a replicable dual-source, gridded-tank bioassay (with neutral vs. conditioned water streams and spatial path-tracking) as a tool for quantifying chemosensory attraction in larval/juvenile fish, comparable to related paradigms used in [[Chemoreception in Fish]] research.
