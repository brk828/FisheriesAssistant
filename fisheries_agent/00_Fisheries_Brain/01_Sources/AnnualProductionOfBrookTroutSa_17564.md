# Annual Production of Brook Trout in Fertile and Infertile Streams of Pennsylvania

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares annual net production of [[Brook Trout]] (*[[Salvelinus Fontinalis]]*) in two Pennsylvania streams that differ sharply in fertility. [[Larry's Creek]], an infertile, softwater stream, produced 52 lbs per acre per year. [[Big Spring Creek]], a fertile, hardwater spring stream, produced 268 lbs per acre per year, more than five times as much. Growth rate and population density, not mortality or longevity, explain the difference. The authors also estimate that anglers could harvest only a small share of this production, and that the fertile stream would sustain roughly six times the yield of the infertile one.

## Background & Description

The brook trout is a popular angling species throughout its native range. In the study area it is abundant in infertile, softwater mountain streams of the Appalachians and occurs in a few fertile, valley streams. Trout in infertile streams grow slowly, mature at small size, and maintain sparse populations. Trout in fertile streams grow quickly, mature at the same age but at larger size, and maintain abundant populations.

The authors frame the study around the limits of production in ecosystems. Production is often constrained by mineral nutrient scarcity, the number of trophic levels, and the ability of consumers to use food energy. The two study streams are simple systems with only three steps from plants to arthropods to trout, which allowed the authors to attribute differences in trout production to differences in plant nutrients.

The paper is a reprint from the *Proceedings of the Pennsylvania Academy of Science* (Vol. 41, 1967), by Edwin L. Cooper (The Pennsylvania State University) and Robert C. Scherer (Lock Haven State College).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** The two streams were chosen to represent extremes in water chemistry:

| Parameter | Larry's Creek (infertile) | Big Spring Creek (fertile) |
|---|---|---|
| Total alkalinity | less than 5 ppm | about 130 ppm |
| pH | 6.6 (varies slightly) | 7.3 (varies slightly) |
| Conductivity (25 °C) | about 27 x 10⁻⁶ | about 374 x 10⁻⁶ |
| Setting | Wooded [[Allegheny Mountains]] drainage, sandstone and shale soils | Large rheocrene springs in an agricultural valley with limestone outcrops |

**Fish community.** Both streams are dominated by brook trout, with occasional [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) captured. The [[Slimy Sculpin]] (*Cottus cognatus*) is common in both. Larry's Creek also holds blacknose and longnose dace (*Rhinichthys atratulus*, *R. cataractae*) in small numbers. Big Spring Creek holds cutlips minnows (*Exoglossum maxillingua*) and pearl dace (*Semotilus margarita*) in small numbers.

**Vegetation and benthos.** Larry's Creek has little vegetation, with unidentified mosses dominant. Big Spring Creek bottoms are largely covered by watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*), waterweed (*Anacharis canadensis*), and water speedwell (*Veronica glandifera*), plus several other macrophytes. Benthic samples were not quantified, but a cursory check found a very sparse fauna in Larry's Creek and a very rich one in Big Spring Creek.

**Methods overview.** Fish were collected by [[Electrofishing]] at frequent intervals from September 1961 to October 1962. Fish were measured, weighed, and aged from scales. Standing crop was estimated in October 1962 using a [[Petersen Mark-Recapture Method]], with separate estimates by size group because large fish were recaptured at higher rates. Production was computed with the [[Allen Graphical Method]], which plots mean weight against numbers per acre and measures the area under the curves with a planimeter. Mortality was estimated from age composition, following [[Ricker Method]] tables. Because fry were not counted, first-year production relied on the mortality rates reported by Latta (1962).

## Key Findings

**1. Production differs about fivefold between the two streams.**
- Larry's Creek: 52 lbs per acre per year (net production)
- Big Spring Creek: 268 lbs per acre per year
- The authors attribute this to a higher standing crop and faster growth in Big Spring Creek, not to differences in mortality.

**2. Growth rate drives production.**
- At the end of two growing seasons, brook trout in Larry's Creek averaged 4.9 inches (0.042 lb) and those in Big Spring Creek averaged 7.0 inches (0.123 lb).
- Comparison with [[Horokiwi Stream]] brown trout (Allen, 1951), which reached 9.8 to 12.6 inches (0.375 to 0.840 lb) at the same age, supports the conclusion that growth rate strongly affects net production.

**3. Mortality and longevity are similar.**
- Some individuals in both populations lived as long as 6 years.
- Mean annual mortality from year 2 to year 6 was 35.5% in Larry's Creek and 40.5% in Big Spring Creek.

**4. Turnover is high and mostly occurs in young fish.**
- Annual production is about 1.3 times the initial standing crop (Larry's Creek 1.34; Big Spring Creek 1.30).
- The two youngest age groups produced 69.8% of production in Larry's Creek and 60.6% in Big Spring Creek.
- Much of this production is lost to natural mortality before fish reach a 6-inch legal size.

**5. Efficiency of net production is similar.**
- Efficiency, measured as actual versus potential net production (following [[Neess and Dugdale]]), was 20.7% for Larry's Creek and 21.5% for Big Spring Creek.
- Intermediate-age fish use available energy more efficiently than younger or older fish.

**6. Comparison with other salmonids.**
- [[Chapman Net Production Review]] (1965) reported salmonid net production from 1.9 to 18.1 g/m², with an outlier of 54.7 g/m² from Allen (1951) for brown trout.
- Larry's Creek (5.8 g/m²) and Big Spring Creek (30.0 g/m²) fall within the range typical of infertile and fertile waters, respectively.

**7. Both populations are lightly exploited.**
- Compared with the heavily exploited Wisconsin population studied by McFadden (1961), these streams have more fish in years 3 through 5.
- Big Spring Creek is a posted public fishing site, and its fish reach legal size in their third growing season. The authors still consider both populations typical of lightly exploited brook trout populations.

**8. Sustained yield to anglers.**
- Analysis used the [[Thompson and Bell Method]] (1934), assuming that population parameters stay constant under added exploitation.
- Larry's Creek: yield rises steadily with exploitation, from 7.1 lbs/acre at 30% to 9.9 lbs/acre at 90%.
- Big Spring Creek: maximum yield of 64.1 lbs/acre at 60% exploitation; 54.8 lbs/acre at 30% and 62.2 lbs/acre at 90%.
- The abstract summarizes yield at 90% exploitation as about 10 lbs/acre (infertile) and about 62 lbs/acre (fertile).

**9. Compensation is limited.**
- Reducing density in an infertile Pennsylvania stream produced only a slight increase in growth (Cooper, Boccardy and Andersen, 1962).
- Le Cren (1962) found that first-year survivors in small streams are similar in number regardless of egg numbers, consistent with [[Density-Dependent Mortality]] for young trout competing for feeding territories (Kalleberg, 1958).
- The authors therefore conclude that added angler harvest is unlikely to trigger compensatory growth or recruitment.

**10. Harvest efficiency is low.**
- Only 19 to 24% of total brook trout production can be harvested under optimal exploitation.
- Even in highly productive streams, harvest is unlikely to exceed about 65 lbs/acre (7.3 g/m²).

**Conclusion.** Nutrient-driven differences in basic productivity set the upper limit on brook trout production. Growth rate determines how much of that production reaches anglers, and the two trout populations share similar survival and turnover patterns. Managers should expect that even fertile streams yield only a fraction of their production to anglers.
