# Marsh Development After Large Floods in an Alluvial, Arid-Land River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This 1997 study in *Wetlands* examines how large floods reshape riverine marsh and riparian vegetation along the free-flowing [[Hassayampa River]] in central Arizona. Riverine marsh expanded fivefold, from 2% to 9% of the floodplain-channel area, after the severe [[1993 Flood]]. Floodwaters eroded aggraded terraces, widened the channel from about 3 to 50 m, and recharged the floodplain aquifer, lowering the floodplain surface relative to the water table. A second large flood in 1995 scoured the channel and sediment deposition raised the surface again, precluding marsh redevelopment. The authors conclude that riverine marsh is transitory in dynamic arid-land floodplains and that flood flows of varying magnitude are needed to maintain patch-type diversity.

## Background & Description

Riverine marshes ("cienegas") were once common in the southwestern United States but have declined sharply due to surface-water diversion, groundwater pumping, ditching, drainage, and overgrazing. Dewatering combined with floods, drought, and timber harvest contributed to channel incision and conversion of marshes to riparian forest in the late 19th century. Floods in arid-land rivers are especially effective at altering channel geomorphology and vegetation because their magnitude is large relative to base flow.

The 1993 Arizona winter flood was among the most severe in state history, probably surpassed only by a February 1891 event. The [[Hassayampa River Preserve]], managed by [[The Nature Conservancy]] for natural values, offered a site free of major human impacts, and the authors present its long-term data as a reference set for comparison with other regional rivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Hassayampa is an alluvial river with perennial, intermittent, and ephemeral reaches, flowing from mountainous headwaters to its confluence with the [[Gila River]] in the [[Sonoran Desert]]. In the 8-km Preserve reach (elevation ca. 600 m), shallow bedrock maintains perennial surface flow. Non-flood flow is typically below 0.11 m³/s, surface-water electrical conductivity is about 600–700 µS/cm, and surface sediments are predominantly sand. Mean annual rainfall at the Wickenburg station is 29 cm. The 1800 km² watershed supports Sonoran desertscrub, interior chaparral, semidesert grassland, and montane conifer forest.

Floodplains are vegetated mainly by [[Fremont Cottonwood]] and [[Goodding Willow]] forests, [[Velvet Mesquite]] woodlands, [[Burro Brush]] shrublands, and [[Seep Willow]] shrublands. Before the Conservancy purchase in 1986, the floodplain was used for grazing, recreation, and agriculture.

Vegetation patch types were tracked with aerial photographs, a GIS, and quadrat sampling. Obligate wetland species such as [[Olney's Bulrush]], [[Southern Cattail]], and [[Jointed Rush]] mark riverine marsh. Water-table depth relative to the floodplain surface is a key control on riparian plant composition.

## Key Findings

- **Flood magnitude:** The 8 January 1993 peak of 745 m³/s had an approximate 25-year recurrence interval. Maximum depth on low terraces was an estimated 3.0 m, with velocity of 2.1 m/s. The event was unusually long in duration, with multiple peaks.
- **1995 flood:** Peak flow of 566 m³/s (15–20 year recurrence interval) occurred in February 1995.
- **Groundwater:** Groundwater levels in the floodplain alluvium rose about 0.5 m after the 1993 flood pulse.
- **Channel change:** The active channel expanded from 2–5 m wide (0.6 ha/km) before 1993 to 40–50 m wide (5 ha/km) by May 1993. Abandoned channels were filled to depths over 1 m in places.
- **Terrace elevation:** Before 1993, less than 20% of floodplain-channel surfaces lay within 1 m of the water table, and over 80% were 1 m or more above it. In 1994, values were about 45% and 55%.
- **Sedimentation:** The 1995 flood deposited an average of 10 cm of sediment across the floodplain, mostly sand and gravel in the 1993 channel. Smaller floods (2–10 year recurrence) built banks and terraces at <1 to >8 cm per year.
- **Riverine marsh:** Marsh covered 2% of the floodplain-channel area in 1988 and was 0% in 1993. It rose to 9% (2.0 ha) in 1994, then returned to 0% in 1995. Marsh-dominated quadrats rose from 15–20% in 1993–1994 to 2% in summer 1995 and 3% in fall 1995.
- **Marsh succession:** Species composition shifted from short plants (jointed rush, water speedwell) to tall emergent graminoids (southern cattail, Olney's bulrush) between 1993 and 1994.
- **Cottonwood–willow:** The 1993 flood eroded about 30% of this patch type. Cottonwood–willow–salt cedar seedlings colonized about 40% of the 5.0 ha/km flood channel. The 1995 flood killed more than 95% of 1993 tree seedlings.
- **Seep willow:** Patch area fell 90% in 1993, with a 50% stem loss after a 1991 10-year flood. Recovery in 1991 came mainly through sprouting, while 1993 recolonization was from seed.
- **Burro brush:** About one-third of burro brush area was converted to channel in 1993, and 88% of stems were lost. Net area still rose 84% from 1988 to 1993 via vegetative spread, and burro brush did not establish from seed in any year.
- **Mesquite:** Mesquite woodland area fell 35% from 1988 to 1993, compared with 24% mature stem mortality. Juvenile mesquite had 77% flood-related mortality.
- **Cohort effects:** Cottonwood recruits roughly once every 12 years. Loss varied inversely with cohort age, with 1930s cohorts losing 10% and a 1991 cohort losing 90%.
- **Historical contrast:** Late 19th- and early 20th-century floods destroyed marshes through incision. The 1993 flood widened but did not incise the Hassayampa channel, allowing temporary marsh expansion and possible seed-bank replenishment.
- **Management implication:** The authors argue that maintaining flood flows of varying magnitude is essential for patch-type diversity in arid-land floodplains.
