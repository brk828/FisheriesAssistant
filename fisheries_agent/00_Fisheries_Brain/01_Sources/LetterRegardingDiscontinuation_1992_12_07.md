# Letter Regarding Discontinuation of Radiotelemetry Studies at Little Colorado River Confluence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #Humpback-Chub

## Executive Summary
This document is a formal letter from Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) to Dave Wegner of the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, dated 07 December 1992, expressing concern over a proposal by BIO/WEST to discontinue radiotelemetry research at the confluence of the Little Colorado River (LCR) and the mainstem Colorado River. The letter argues against redirecting research effort downstream, emphasizing the critical ecological importance of the confluence zone for the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). It is not a primary data paper but a research-management communication tied to the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) program and the Aquatic Coordination Team (ACT).

## Background & Description
No morphological, meristic, or anatomical data are presented in this source. The document is administrative/advisory in nature, pertaining to research protocol and study site prioritization rather than direct biological description of the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The letter identifies three interconnected habitat components critical to the [[Humpback Chub]] population dynamics in the Grand Canyon region:

1. **Little Colorado River (LCR)** — Identified as home to the **largest known population** of humpback chub, functioning as a core recruitment and refuge habitat.
2. **Colorado River mainstem (near LCR inflow)** — Habitat for what is described as a **potentially unique, remnant population**, distinct from the LCR population but geographically proximate.
3. **Confluence Zone** — The physical transition area linking the LCR and mainstem Colorado River, hypothesized to serve as a locale for **important life history processes** (e.g., movement, spawning migration, or habitat shifts between systems).

This tri-part habitat framework underscores the species' reliance on **connectivity between tributary and mainstem river systems**, a recurring theme in [[Colorado River Basin]] conservation biology and relevant to broader [[River Confluence Ecology]] concepts.

## Key Findings
- **Research Proposal in Question**: BIO/WEST proposed discontinuing radiotelemetry monitoring at the Little Colorado River/Colorado River confluence and redirecting tracking efforts to downstream mainstem reaches.
- **Institutional Research Division of Labor**:
  - BIO/WEST and Arizona Game and Fish Department (AZGFD) — primary data collection in the **mainstem Colorado River**.
  - Arizona State University (ASU) and AZGFD — primary data collection in the **Little Colorado River**.
  - No entity was clearly tasked with monitoring the **confluence zone** itself, which the author identifies as a critical gap.
- **Scientific Objection**: The author (Marsh) argues that while there may be justification for *adding* radiotelemetry studies downstream, this does not constitute justification for *discontinuing* work at the confluence, since the confluence is uniquely positioned to reveal inter-population connectivity and life-history linkages not observable in either system alone.
- **Procedural Concern**: The ACT meeting (01–02 December 1992) did not allow sufficient opportunity to evaluate the full implications of reallocating BIO/WEST's research effort away from the confluence.
- **Recommended Action**: The letter requests that the GCES Senior Scientist and Aquatic Advisor be consulted before any protocol modification is approved, to ensure a full cost-benefit assessment of the existing versus proposed research protocol.
- **Broader Significance**: This correspondence reflects contemporaneous (early 1990s) scientific concern for the conservation of the [[Humpback Chub]], a federally endangered [[Cyprinidae]] species endemic to the Colorado River Basin, and highlights institutional coordination challenges in large-scale, multi-agency [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) monitoring efforts under the Aquatic Coordination Team (ACT).
