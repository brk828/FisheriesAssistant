# Fishes and Changing Ecology of Mullet Pond, a Barrier Beach Island Pond on Shackleford Banks, North Carolina

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study reviews the fish fauna and ecological change of [[Mullet Pond]], a small pond on the western end of [[Shackleford Banks]], North Carolina. Historical collections by [[Samuel F. Hildebrand]] (1914–1930) recorded 26 species. A 1969 seine survey by [[Frank J. Schwartz]] found only four: [[Cyprinodon Variegatus]], [[Fundulus Confluentus]], [[Lucania Parva]], and [[Gambusia Affinis]]. The pond was isolated from [[Back Sound]] after the 1933 hurricane, and it has since become shallow, salinity-variable, and increasingly filled with cattails and sand. The author concludes that the pond is aging and dying. The once-prevalent [[Fundulus Luciae]] was not captured in 1969.

## Background & Description

Mullet Pond lies on the northwest corner of Shackleford Banks, a barrier island about 8 miles long in the [[Cape Lookout]] area near [[Beaufort Inlet]], Beaufort, and Morehead City. The island is shaped by hurricanes and by a coastline that shifts 4–12 feet per year northward (Lewis, 1917). Early surveys (1851) describe the pond as about 6 ft deep and roughly 300 yards in diameter. It connected to Back Sound, which received oceanic water through Beaufort Inlet. Its salinity was presumably oceanic.

Vegetation on the island changed markedly between 1851 and 1949. Engels (1952) reported a decline from about 1,250 wooded acres (live oak, red cedar, yaupon holly, loblolly pine) to about 180 wooded acres, with dune sand encroaching northward under prevailing southwest winds. By 1969 the open water was reduced to a band about 150 × 15 ft and about 5 ft deep, bordered by a peaty mud bank, live oaks, and a dense [[Cattail Encroachment]] zone. The bottom is covered with decayed cattail and other debris to about knee depth.

Hildebrand's historical collections were largely unpublished field notes, deposited with the U.S. National Museum. They include seine collections made mainly in 1914 (17 dates) and 1915 (9 dates), plus collections in 1926 and 1930. Hildebrand used a seine of unspecified mesh but captured specimens as small as 6 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Isolation and salinity.** Gray recorded salinities of 21–22‰ in 1931. The pond was apparently closed off from Back Sound following the September 1933 hurricane, though local residents could not confirm the date. In August 1969 the salinity was 1.015‰, with an air temperature of 35 °C and a water temperature of 29.4 °C. The author attributes the freshening to seasonal rain and subsurface water table fluctuations.

**Loss of species.** The author suggests that many marine and estuarine species could not tolerate the lower salinities. The loss of spawning habitat and of food for surviving young probably contributed further. Surviving species are small shore fishes that can feed largely on algae or plankton at all sizes.

**Species tolerance.** Gunter (1942, 1956) listed 19 of the 26 historical species as known from freshwater habitats. [[Fundulus Luciae]] was considered euryhaline. Several other species, including *Anchoa hepsetus*, *Opsanus tau*, and *Urophycis regius*, have not been considered to have freshwater affinities. Schwartz (1964) reported that [[Gambusia Affinis]] was abundant at 18.7‰ in [[Chesapeake Bay]]. The author cautions that occurrence records do not indicate a species' long-term salinity tolerance.

**Habitat decline.** Algal populations are densest in the deepest part of the pond. Dune encroachment and sediment filling continue to shrink the open water. The author expects [[Gambusia Affinis]] to persist longest as the pond fills in.

## Key Findings

- **Species decline:** Hildebrand's unpublished notes and literature records document 26 fish species in Mullet Pond from 1914 to 1930. The 1969 survey found only four, a loss of 22 species. Table 1 lists the full historical assemblage, which includes marine, estuarine, and some freshwater taxa such as *Elops saurus*, *Brevoortia tyrannus*, *Mugil cephalus*, *Leiostomus xanthurus*, and *Menidia menidia*.
- **1969 seine survey:** Using a 15 × 4 ft knotless nylon seine, Schwartz captured 671 individuals of four species:
  - *Gambusia affinis* (UNC 4518): 513 specimens, the most numerous.
  - *Cyprinodon variegatus* (UNC 4517): 77 specimens, the bulkiest fish.
  - *Lucania parva* (UNC 4516): 70 specimens.
  - *Fundulus confluentus* (UNC 4515): 11 specimens.
  - The same four-species ratio occurred in each haul.
- **Status of Fundulus luciae:** Once described as abundant and prevalent in the pond, *Fundulus luciae* was not captured in 1969. Its status in the pond is presumed lost.
- **Earlier collections:** A 1954 collection by Strasburg recorded 422 *Cyprinodon variegatus* and 125 *Gambusia affinis* on 1 July 1954.
- **Causes of change:** Isolation from Back Sound (probably after 1933), salinity drop from rainfall and water table fluctuations, filling by cattails and sand, and loss of spawning habitat. The author rates the pond's future as limited.
- **Future prospects:** The author considers renewed exchange with Back Sound, through a new hurricane breach or accidental marine flooding, an open question.
