# Colorado Basin Fishes of Arizona: A 1890 Collection Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #gobiidae #pleuronectidae

## Executive Summary

This note summarises a 1890 field survey and taxonomic report by [[Charles Henry Gilbert]] and [[Norman Bishop Scofield]]. The expedition, made by the senior author and A. B. Alexander of the [[U.S. Fish Commission]] during the late April and early May 1890 detached from the [[Albatross Expedition]], was primarily aimed at checking reports of [[American Shad]] in the [[Colorado River]]. Those reports proved erroneous: the fish concerned were [[German Carp]], a recent arrival. Shad were not found despite repeated netting and seining at Yuma and Horseshoe Bend.

The collections yielded a native freshwater fauna across the [[Colorado Basin]], Gila Basin and Salt River, plus two marine/brackish species from the Colorado mouth. The report describes three new species, reports range extensions for several taxa, and documents the highly endemic character of the Colorado fauna.

## Background & Description

The survey was undertaken in Arizona, Mexico and the lower Colorado. Collecting localities included the Colorado River and the mouth of the [[Gila River]] at Yuma; the [[Salt River]] from Tempe to near the mouth of the [[Rio Verde]]; the upper Rio Verde at Chino; and the Horseshoe Bend of the Colorado below Lerdo, Mexico.

The authors placed their work within the checklist of [[Evermann and Rutter]] (1894) on Colorado River fishes. That list included seven species supposed to occur in other basins. The authors eliminated two of these:
- *Lepidomeda vittata*: taken in southwestern Arizona by the Death Valley Expedition, but from a tributary now lost in desert sands before reaching the Rio Verde.
- *Cyprinodon macularius*: restricted to the Colorado and springs in the Colorado Desert, where drainage runs toward the Colorado.

Of the remaining five, three are widely distributed and their presence was set aside for faunal analysis. *Leuciscus lineatus*, shared with the Utah Basin, was described as "an unexplained anomaly". *Agosia chrysogaster*, described from the Rio Santa Cruz (a Gila tributary), is also listed from the Yaqui and Sonora rivers, which flow independently into the [[Gulf of California]]. The authors note these Gulf streams are practically unexplored.

### Species Accounts (summary)
The report gives detailed morphometric descriptions, meristic counts (fin rays, scale counts, teeth) and measurement tables for roughly 19 taxa. Key points:

- **[[Pantosteus arizonae]]** (new species, Catostomidae): four specimens from Tempe. Distinguished by unusually large scales.
- **[[Catostomus latipinnis]]**: five specimens from the Salt River, Tempe. Closely resembles *C. discobolus*, with which it was confused in earlier literature.
- **[[Catostomus discobolus]]**: described from Green River, Wyoming, and Delta, Colorado. Not found in the Lower Colorado by the authors.
- **[[Catostomus gila]]**: eleven specimens from Tempe. Lip papillae counts differ from the original description.
- **[[Xyrauchen cypha]]**: eight specimens. Abundant at Yuma and downstream to Horseshoe Bend.
- **[[Ptychocheilus lucius]]** (Colorado Salmon): several specimens; the most highly prized native fish, reaching 4 to 5 feet.
- **[[Gila elegans]]**: specimens 4 to 15 inches long. Most abundant in larger channels.
- **[[Gila robusta]]**: very abundant in the Salt River at Tempe. Not taken in the Colorado.
- **[[Leuciscus intermedius]]**: numerous specimens about 3 inches long at Tempe and Chino. Considered conspecific with *L. niger* by the authors.
- **[[Agosia osculus]]**: about thirty specimens at Chino, with very small scales and a definite lateral band.
- **[[Agosia couesii]]**: nine specimens from Tempe.
- **[[Agosia chrysogaster]]**: one specimen from Chino.
- **[[Tiaroga cobitis]]**: several specimens from Chino, a tributary of the Rio Verde. Not recorded since the 1851 type discovery (the authors' earlier date was 1831 in the text). Highly significant as a re-collection.
- **[[Plagopterus argentissimus]]**: a few specimens from the mouth of the Gila at Yuma and the Salt River at Tempe. Previously known only from the San Luis Valley, Colorado.
- **[[Meda fulgida]]**: "extremely abundant" in the upper Rio Verde near Chino. Previously known only from the Rio San Pedro.
- **[[Cyprinodon macularius]]**: numerous specimens from a spring-fed pond at Lerdo, Mexico.
- **[[Poecilia occidentalis]]**: several specimens from the Salt River at Tempe, agreeing with the type locality at Tucson.
- **[[Gillichthys detrusus]]** (new species, Gobiidae): several specimens, the longest 5 inches, from Horseshoe Bend. Occurs in muddy creeks connected to the river, and is eaten by local Indians.
- **[[Paralichthys aestuarius]]** (new species, Pleuronectidae): seven specimens (6 to 9 inches) from Shoal Point at the mouth of the Colorado, collected by the *Albatross*. Four are sinistral.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors' central ecological argument concerns the endemicity of the Colorado fauna. A "remarkably high percentage" of its fishes are peculiar to the [[Colorado Basin]], and they interpret this as indicating "a very long period of absolute isolation". Only a small residual of species is shared with other basins, and most of these are complicated by taxonomic confusion or unresolved distribution.

Several species (apparently *Plagopterus*, *Meda*, *Tiaroga*) showed highly restricted or disjunct ranges. The authors judged that some of these records extended known distributions. Three widely distributed species were said to cross barriers that stop other fishes, but the mechanism was unknown. The survey also documented habitat associations:
- Large channels: *Gila elegans*, *Xyrauchen cypha*.
- Salt River and smaller streams: *Gila robusta* (more abundant in smaller streams than the main channel).
- Muddy side channels and sandy shallows: *Gillichthys detrusus*.
- Springs and marshy ponds: *Cyprinodon macularius*.

Shad were absent from the river, which the authors considered "entirely unsuited" to them.

## Key Findings

1. **No shad present.** Earlier reports of shad in the Lower Colorado were erroneous. The fish were introduced [[German Carp]], and repeated netting and seining failed to find shad at Yuma or Horseshoe Bend.
2. **High endemism.** The Colorado Basin fish fauna is highly endemic. Only the species [[Leuciscus lineatus]] (shared with the Utah Basin) and a few others of uncertain status were thought to cross basins.
3. **Elimination of records.** Two species on the Evermann and Rutter list, *Lepidomeda vittata* and *Cyprinodon macularius*, were removed from the list as shared-basin species on the grounds given above.
4. **New species described.** Three species were described: *Pantosteus arizonae*, *Gillichthys detrusus* and *Paralichthys aestuarius*. The last two are marine/brackish taxa from the Colorado River mouth.
5. **Rediscovery of Tiaroga cobitis.** Specimens of *Tiaroga cobitis* were collected at Chino, Arizona, the first record since its type discovery, providing important new material.
6. **Range extensions.** *Plagopterus argentissimus* (previously known only from San Luis Valley, Colorado) and *Meda fulgida* (previously only from the Rio San Pedro) were recorded from Arizona.
7. **Taxonomic clarifications.** The authors resolved confusion between *Catostomus latipinnis* and *C. discobolus*, and considered *Leuciscus intermedius* and *L. niger* to be one species. They also judged *Xyrauchen uncompahgre* likely conspecific with *X. cypha*.
8. **Gulf streams unexplored.** Rivers of the Gulf of California (Rio Sonora, Yaqui) were flagged as poorly studied and potentially connected to the lower Colorado in the past.
