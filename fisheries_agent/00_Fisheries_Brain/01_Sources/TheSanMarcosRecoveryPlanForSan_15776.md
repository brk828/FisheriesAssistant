# The San Marcos Recovery Plan for San Marcos River Endangered and Threatened Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #percidae #plethodontidae #aquatic-plants

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan, prepared by the [[San Marcos Recovery Team]], addresses four federally listed species of the [[San Marcos River Ecosystem]]: the [[San Marcos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia georgei*), the [[Fountain Darter]] (*Etheostoma fonticola*), the [[San Marcos Salamander]] (*Eurycea nana*), and [[Texas Wildrice]] (*Zizania texana*). The overarching goal is to secure their survival and eventual recovery by protecting the ecosystem that sustains them. The plan is described as the first recovery plan to address multiple species through an ecosystem approach.

The most serious threat identified is cessation of flow from [[San Marcos Springs]], driven by groundwater overdraft from the [[Edwards Aquifer]]. Habitat modification, urbanization, pollution, recreation, and exotic species compound this risk. The stated recovery criterion is downlisting to threatened once flow in the San Marcos River is assured within its natural cycle of variation; delisting is not addressed in this phase.

## Background & Description

The San Marcos River arises from spring openings along the [[Balcones Fault Zone]] in San Marcos, Texas. The springs have historically shown the greatest flow dependability and environmental stability of any spring system in the southwestern United States, and the ecosystem has a greater known diversity of aquatic organisms than any other in that region. Many of its species are restricted to the first few kilometers of the springrun.

The plan covers four listed taxa:

- **San Marcos Gambusia (*Gambusia georgei*)**: a livebearing poeciliid first described in 1969, the most range-restricted native *Gambusia* in the United States. Males are distinguished by thickened upper pectoral fin rays and gonopodial characters.
- **Fountain Darter (*Etheostoma fonticola*)**: a small percid, usually under 25 mm SL, found only in the San Marcos and [[Comal River]] systems. It belongs to the subgenus *Microperca*.
- **San Marcos Salamander (*Eurycea nana*)**: a neotenic plethodontid that retains external gills throughout life and breeds in water. Adults are small, up to 59.6 mm total length.
- **Texas Wildrice (*Zizania texana*)**: an aquatic grass restricted to a 2.4 km reach of the upper San Marcos River within San Marcos city limits.

Conservation status is consistent across sources. In Table 1, the gambusia, darter, and wildrice are Endangered in all listed systems (FWS, TPWD, TOES, IUCN; IUCN lists wildrice as Vulnerable). The salamander is Threatened (FWS) and Protected nongame/Threatened (TPWD), and is listed as Rare by IUCN.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and hydrology.** The upper San Marcos River is a rapidly flowing, unusually clear springrun, 5-15 m wide and up to about 4 m deep. It flows about 110 km before joining the Guadalupe River near Gonzales. Average annual spring flow is 4.46 m³/s (161.0 cfs). The lowest recorded monthly flow was 1.53 m³/s (54 cfs) in 1956, and the lowest measured daily flow was 1.29 m³/s (45.55 cfs) on 15-16 August 1956. Headwater temperature varies by less than 1-2 °C annually. At the lower springrun, temperature ranged from 25.5 °C (August) to 20.4 °C (February). Water pH is 6.9-7.8.

**Recharge and drawdown.** The aquifer is recharged by runoff from the southern and eastern Edwards Plateau through Cretaceous limestones. Projections in the plan indicate that continuous flow from San Marcos Springs may be at risk. The Bureau of Reclamation estimates a 50-75 percent probability of continuous flow by 2020. Klemt et al. (1979) project cessation around 2010, and the plan summarizes all projections as indicating cessation around 2000.

**Human history.** The springs were a stop on El Camino Real and the Chisholm Cattle Trail. A federal fish hatchery was established near the springs in the late 1890s. Spring Lake was created by damming more than fifty years before the plan and hosts the Aquarena Springs amusement park. The population of San Marcos grew from 741 (1870) to 23,420 (1980).

**Habitat associations.** The darter and salamander share vegetated microhabitats in aquatic moss and [[Lyngbya]] mats. Associated fauna include crayfish, amphipods (*Hyalella azteca*), and snails. Predators and competitors include largemouth bass, sunfishes, cichlids, the characin *Astyanax mexicanus*, and introduced poeciliids.

**Threats.**
- Overdrafting of the Edwards Aquifer, the most serious threat.
- Urbanization, which increases flooding, erosion, pollution, siltation, and loss of species diversity (Edwards 1976).
- Herbicide use at the IH-35 crossing, which may wash into the river.
- Exotic species that compete with or prey on the listed taxa.
- Recreation (swimmers, tubers, canoeists) knocking over wildrice seed heads.
- Aquarena Springs management of algal mats and duck fecal pollution in salamander habitat.

## Key Findings

**Species-specific findings**

- **San Marcos Gambusia:** Only 18 *G. georgei* were found among 20,199 *Gambusia* collected in 1978-1979 (0.09%). Recent 1981/82 collections showed a slight decrease to 0.06% of all *Gambusia*. Hybridization with [[Gambusia affinis]] has continued, and 1981-83 collections indicated hybrids may be many times more abundant than pure *G. georgei*. Captive cultures held at Dexter National Fish Hatchery were contaminated by *G. affinis* and eliminated in April 1982.
- **Fountain Darter:** Schenck and Whiteside (1976a) estimated about 103,000 individuals in the San Marcos River; a 1973 estimate gave about 1,000 with no method stated. Mean fecundity was 19 (74 females with mature ova), and the sex ratio was 1.39:1 (325 males, 234 females). Natural populations show two ova-development peaks (August and late winter to early spring), suggesting year-round spawning. Darters were extirpated from the Comal River by the 1950s-1970s, and a reintroduction of 457 adults in 1975-76 produced an established population in the upper Comal River.
- **San Marcos Salamander:** Tupa and Davis (1976) estimated 17,000-21,000 individuals in the uppermost Spring Lake. Population structure was about 45 percent small juveniles, 30-40 percent larger juveniles, 10-15 percent mature males, and about 4 percent gravid females. Males mature at 19 mm SVL; gravid females carry large yellow ova (1.5-2.0 mm). Oxygen consumption peaked at 25 °C, and the species is intolerant of temperatures of 30 °C or greater.
- **Texas Wildrice:** Abundance declined sharply after 1932 discovery. Emery (1967) found one plant in Spring Lake and none in the uppermost 0.8 km. Emery (1977) estimated plants covered 1,131 m of river habitat in 1976. No recent seedlings have been observed in the native habitat. Greenhouse and raceway cultivation produced seed in some cases, but transplants to Salado Creek and the Comal River failed.

**Plan structure and priorities**

- The plan adopts a stepdown outline with five major tasks: maintain and enhance species in habitat (1.0), conserve habitat through management authority (2.0), law enforcement (3.0), public information and education (4.0), and recommend changes in listed status (5.0).
- Priority 1 actions prevent extinction; Priority 2 actions maintain current populations; Priority 3 actions provide for full recovery. Most tasks are assigned Priority 2.
- Short-term emergency actions include bringing species into protected refugia and supplementing river flows by pumping wells. Long-term actions include working with water agencies to assure flows.
- Downlisting is conditional on assured flow from San Marcos Springs and reasonable evidence that the species respond to recovery actions. Upon delisting, management authority would pass to Texas.

**Review comments (Appendix)**

- Reviewers from the University of Michigan, the EPA Ecological Effects Branch, and the University of California at Santa Barbara commended the plan but criticized it for emphasizing biology over feasibility, and for underemphasizing the subterranean aquifer fauna and regional groundwater use.
- The FWS Director requested that the plan rank tasks, quantify goals where possible, and clarify whether delisting was feasible. The Edwards Underground Water District submitted an objection to the draft's characterization of threats and reintroduction.
- The FWS responded that delisting is feasible and reworded the objective and Task 1.37 accordingly.
