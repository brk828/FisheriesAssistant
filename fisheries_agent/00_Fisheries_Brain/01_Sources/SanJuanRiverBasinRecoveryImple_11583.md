# San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #river-ecology #conservation-planning

## Executive Summary

This document establishes the San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program (RIP), a comprehensive cooperative framework designed to protect and recover endangered fish species—[[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]—while allowing water development to proceed in compliance with federal and state laws. Initiated in August 1992, the program involves federal agencies (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Indian Affairs), three states (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah), four Indian tribes (Navajo Nation, Southern Ute, Ute Mountain Ute, Jicarilla Apache), water development interests, and conservation organizations. The program addresses impacts from [[Navajo Dam]] operations, water depletions (baseline 632,200 acre-feet annually at Bluff, Utah), habitat degradation, water quality contamination (selenium, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), and non-native fish interactions. A seven-year research period will identify flow needs, habitat requirements, and recovery actions, with estimated annual costs of $1,980,000 and capital expenditures of $5 million. The program operates through three committees (Coordination, Biology, Operating) and commits to legal protection of habitat flows, Navajo Dam reoperation to mimic natural hydrographs, and implementation of recovery actions based on research findings.

## Background & Description

The [[San Juan River]] drains approximately 38,000 square miles across southwestern Colorado, northeastern Arizona, northwestern New Mexico, and southeastern Utah, flowing 357 miles from the San Juan Mountains to [[Lake Powell]]. The native fish fauna historically included eight to nine species: [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Mottled Sculpin]], and possibly [[Bonytail Chub]]. Three species are federally endangered (Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, bonytail), with additional state-level protections for other natives.

Construction of [[Navajo Dam]] in 1962 fundamentally altered the river's hydrology, reducing spring peak flows, increasing and stabilizing base flows, and decreasing water temperatures through hypolimnetic releases. Pre-dam flows at Bluff averaged 2,574 cfs (1914-1986) with extremes from 0 to 69,986 cfs. The natural hydrograph featured large spring peaks (mid-May to early June) and low base flows punctuated by storm events. Post-dam operations have moderated these extremes but disrupted natural flow cues critical for fish reproduction and recruitment.

Twenty-three non-native fish species have been documented in the basin since the late 1800s, with 21 present since 1987. [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are the most abundant non-natives in warmwater mainstem reaches, while [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Mosquitofish]] dominate low-velocity habitats. Rainbow, cutthroat (Snake River subspecies), and brown trouts are common in coldwater reaches. Most non-natives were introduced for sport fishing or became established through bait releases.

Current water development includes six major diversion structures in New Mexico (Hammond Canal, Fruitland Irrigation Canal, San Juan Power Plant, Four Corners Power Plant, Hogback Canal, Cudei Ditch), several of which impede fish passage during low flows. Total baseline depletions reach 657,200 acre-feet annually, reduced to 632,200 acre-feet at Bluff by return flows from [[Dolores River]] imports. The [[Animas-La Plata Project]] will add 57,100 acre-feet of depletion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population Status**: Since systematic surveys began in 1987, only 28 adult Colorado squawfish have been captured between Bluff, Utah and Hogback, New Mexico, with young-of-year documented in 1987, 1988, and 1990 downstream of Shiprock and near Bluff. Adults occupy habitats ranging from low-velocity tributary mouths to high-velocity deep runs. Young-of-year utilize shallow backwater and side-channel habitats with silt/sand substrates and minimal current. Spawning occurs in July-August but specific spawning sites remain unidentified.

Only one razorback sucker has been collected in the mainstem since 1987, though several specimens were captured in the Lake Powell inflow area, some in spawning condition. No reproduction or recruitment has been documented. Adults typically inhabit quiet eddies, pools, and mid-channel runs over sand or silt substrates. The population may be at critically low levels where natural recruitment to self-sustaining populations is impossible.

**Water Quality Concerns**: The San Juan Basin is naturally seleniferous, with selenium concentrations increasing downstream from <1 µg/L at Archuleta, New Mexico to 4 µg/L at Bluff, Utah. Tributaries carry higher concentrations than the mainstem. Irrigated agriculture contributes selenium through: (1) evaporative concentration, (2) pickup from irrigated soils, and (3) pickup from underlying shale beds. Fish sampling in 1990-1991 documented high levels of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (naphthalene, benzo(a)pyrene, phenanthrene) from oil and gas development, urban runoff, and petroleum spillage. Comparison of 1973, 1980, and 1984 data indicates elemental residues in the Farmington area are elevated and may be gradually increasing. The river may be approaching assimilation capacity for many contaminants.

**Habitat Degradation**: Young-of-year Colorado squawfish require low-velocity nursery habitats (backwaters, side channels) that can be created and maintained through flow manipulation or channel modification. Paucity of such habitats or poor water quality may limit recruitment. Six diversion structures in New Mexico create potential barriers to fish movement, particularly during low flows. The Hogback diversion, reconstructed in September 1987, diverts all flow into Hogback Canal at low flow with excess released downstream.

**Consultation History**: The 1991 Biological Opinion for the Animas-La Plata Project concluded that current and cumulative adverse conditions jeopardize the continued existence of endangered species, requiring a "reasonable and prudent alternative" based on Navajo Dam reoperation, research, and recovery actions. The NIIP Biological Opinion reinforced these requirements. Section 7 consultations since 1977 have evolved from no-jeopardy opinions based on limited information to recognition of the San Juan populations' importance to species survival.

## Key Findings

**Program Goals and Structure**:
1. Conserve populations of Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker consistent with ESA recovery goals
2. Proceed with water development in compliance with federal/state laws, interstate compacts, Supreme Court decrees, and tribal trust responsibilities
3. Implement recovery within 15 years through six major elements: essential research, genetic integrity/population management, habitat protection/augmentation, water quality enhancement, native/non-native interactions, and monitoring/data management

**Organizational Framework**:
- **Coordination Committee**: 13 voting members (federal agencies, states, tribes, water development interests, conservationists); chaired by USFWS Region 2; functions by two-thirds majority vote; reviews/approves annual work plans and budgets
- **Biology Committee**: Technical experts from participating entities; develops research plans, evaluates recovery actions, assesses progress; two-thirds majority vote with unresolved issues referred to Coordination Committee
- **Operating Committee**: Advisory to Biology Committee for Navajo Dam operations; coordinates release patterns considering senior water rights and endangered fish needs; meets before March annually to finalize release rules

**Research and Recovery Elements** (Section 3.0):

*Essential Research* ($500,000 annually):
- Intensive studies determining relative abundance, distribution, seasonal movements of endangered and other fishes
- Documentation of reproduction, recruitment, spawning areas, and nursery habitat characterization
- Evaluation of biological responses to test flows from Navajo Dam
- Identification of essential habitats and flow-habitat relationships

*Genetic Integrity and Population Management* ($275,000 annually):
- Genetic characterization of San Juan populations relative to other basins
- Evaluation of reproductive/recruitment potentials at current population levels
- Establishment of refugia with wild-caught stock if necessary
- Development of hatchery propagation programs for population augmentation if deemed necessary
- Evaluation of cryogenic gamete preservation

*Habitat Protection and Augmentation* ($300,000 annually):
- Characterization of existing geomorphic and habitat conditions
- Quantification of habitat availability at different flows
- Detailed habitat mapping and hydraulic modeling
- Assessment of geomorphology dynamics and flow effects
- Physical habitat modification including removal of fish passage impediments and creation of required habitats
- **Flow Management**: Bureau of Reclamation agreed to operate Navajo Dam to mimic natural hydrograph for life of Animas-La Plata Project if research shows benefit; habitat flows protected under state/tribal laws; participants will not use condemnation to acquire water rights; flows administered by State Engineers and tribal authorities

*Water Quality Protection* ($230,000 annually):
- Compilation and synthesis of historic water quality data
- Identification and characterization of contaminant types and sources
- Research on biological effects of selenium and PAHs on survival, growth, reproduction
- Systematic monitoring at series of sites characterizing water quality of used/potential habitats
- Enforcement of actions to bring about water quality improvements consistent with state/federal regulations
- Measures to diminish/eliminate contaminant sources limiting recovery

*Non-native Species Interactions* ($180,000 annually):
- Characterization of distribution, abundance, and habitat use by non-native species
- Definition of interaction modes between native and non-native species
- Response characterization of non-natives to flow regimes and habitat modifications
- Agreement among agencies to discontinue warmwater fish stocking in endangered species habitats
- Limitation of stocking to salmonids in habitats where endangered species do not occur
- Regulations banning bait-minnow seining and import of extra-basin bait minnows
- Implementation of flow manipulation or piscicide application if necessary to reduce non-native impacts

*Monitoring and Data Management* ($75,000 annually):
- Regularly scheduled systematic inventories using standardized methods
- Data management compatible with Upper Colorado River Recovery Program
- USFWS Region 2 assumes data management responsibilities
- Periodic review of monitoring activities

**ESA Compliance Process** (Section 4.0):

*Sufficient Progress Determination*: Service will assess whether positive response of endangered fish species and/or habitats to implemented steps offsets potential jeopardy impacts from proposed federal actions. Assessment proportional to action impacts—smaller impacts require lower progress levels. Examples of actions leading to sufficient progress include: Navajo Dam operation to mimic natural hydrograph, identification of flow needs, legal protection of reservoir releases, habitat/population improvements, water quality improvements, support/participation by parties, habitat modifications, exotic species control, population augmentation, and research completion.

*Milestones*: Stepwise accomplishment of recovery action increments outlined in Long Range Implementation Plan will be assessed as milestones marking progress toward recovery goals. Satisfaction of milestones will be considered in consultation process.

*Minor Depletions*: For federal actions resulting in minor depletions, Service will consider new information on project impacts, species status, and good faith Implementation Program implementation in determining sufficient progress. Aggregate of all minor depletions during 7-year research period may total no more than 3,000 acre-feet annually under these conditions.

*Reinitiation Requirements*: 50 CFR 402.16 requires reinitiation if: (1) incidental take exceeded, (2) new information reveals effects not considered in biological opinion, (3) action modified causing unconsidered effects, or (4) new species listed or critical habitat designated.

**Funding Structure** (Section 6.0):

*Annual Operating Budget*: $1,980,000 estimated, including:
- USFWS: $200,000 cash/in-kind plus $200,000 sought for administration
- Bureau of Reclamation: $670,000 (7-year commitment from Animas-La Plata consultation)
- Bureau of Indian Affairs: $175,000 direct plus $400,000 in-kind (habitat/flow research, operational modeling)
- New Mexico: $80,000 in-kind (committee participation, flow protection)
- Colorado: $50,000 in-kind (committee participation)
- Utah: $50,000 in-kind (committee participation, instream flow administration)
- Navajo Nation: $40,000 in-kind (committee participation, flow administration on reservation)
- Southern Ute: $25,000 in-kind
- Ute Mountain Ute: $25,000 in-kind
- Jicarilla Apache: $25,000 in-kind
- Water Development Interests: $20,000 in-kind
- Conservationists: $20,000 in-kind
- Administration: $180,000 ($20,000 per entity)

*Capital Expenditures*: Estimated $5 million for flow management equipment (gages), fish passages, barrier removal, hatchery construction/maintenance, and refuges. Federal funds anticipated from Land and Water Conservation Fund or other mechanisms requiring Congressional authorization/appropriation.

*Fund Administration*: Annual funds administered by responsible agencies according to individual regulations; Program Coordinator maintains records of distribution/expenditures; accounting provided end of each fiscal year. Wherever possible, funds placed in interest-bearing accounts (e.g., National Fish and Wildlife Foundation) with interest supporting recovery activities.

**Water Rights and Trust Responsibilities** (Sections 1.6, 1.7):

- Four federally recognized tribes have reservations in basin with reserved water rights under federal law, most unquantified
- Secretary of Interior has trust responsibility to assert and protect tribal trust water resources
- Specific federal plans exist for partial development: NIIP (Navajo Nation—6 of 11 blocks constructed, Blocks 7-8 under construction), Animas-La Plata Project (Southern Ute and Ute Mountain Ute—Settlement Agreement endorsed by Congress in 1988), Jicarilla Apache (lengthy litigation, recent negotiations)
- Nothing in Implementation Program construed to affect rights to use water under federal/state law, permit, contract, treaty, interstate compact, or adjudication proceedings
- Indian tribes do not recognize water rights limited by Upper Colorado River Basin Compact; participation does not waive claims to water exceeding state allocations or bind tribes to agreements they are not party to
- Order of water development may not reflect water right priority; successful project development does not create water rights greater/lesser than otherwise entitled
- Habitat flows administered by State Engineers and tribal authorities pursuant to state/tribal laws; signatories agreed to protect storage releases and acquired water for listed fish benefit so flows remain undiminished (except carriage losses) to Lake Powell

**Timeline and Procedures** (Section 5.3, Table 5.1):

- **January**: Biology Committee reviews preceding fiscal year Annual Work Plan (AWP), prepares detailed current FY AWP, develops succeeding FY AWP; Biology and Operating Committees develop preliminary flow regimes
- **February**: Principal investigators submit reports summarizing accomplishments, problems, required research to Program Coordinator by February 15
- **March**: Biology Committee forwards summary report and succeeding FY AWP to Coordination Committee; Operating Committee meets to finalize flows considering senior water rights and water supply; review/feedback completed by March 31
- **March/April**: Coordination Committee responds to Biology Committee on AWP
- **April 1**: Biology and Operating Committees finalize flows
- **May-September**: Field work; Bureau of Reclamation initiates annual habitat flow releases from Navajo Dam
- **August/September**: Biology Committee finalizes succeeding FY AWP
- **September**: Biology Committee forwards AWP to Coordination Committee; Coordination Committee responds

**Long Range Implementation Plan**: Draft provided within 12 months of program initiation, final submitted by January 1995. Plan establishes milestones for analyzing program progress, uses ALP and NIIP consultation research plans as basis. Coordination Committee approval based on accurate reflection of recovery goals and effective implementation of Sections 3 and 6.

**Program Modifications**: Modifications recommended by any participant become effective upon written concurrence by all participants. Participants recognize Service and federal agencies must reinitiate consultation when 50 CFR 402.16 circumstances occur.

**Coordination with Existing Efforts**: Activities closely coordinated with "Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin" (initiated October 1, 1988) covering Green River and Colorado River sub-basins above Glen Canyon Dam. Coordination reduces overlap, focuses resources on San Juan needs, enhances communication, and improves effectiveness. Service responsible for coordinating with Upper Basin Recovery Program and Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team.
