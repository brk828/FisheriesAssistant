## Key Findings

1. **Reach-scale distribution:** Spikedace and loach minnow occurred in Reach I, were absent from Reaches II and III, peaked in Reach IV, and declined in Reach V of the upper Gila River.
2. **Short-term abundance swings:** Spikedace abundance was tracked at several microhabitat sites sampled two to three months apart. Numbers generally decreased, by about 50 to 1,000 percent over three months (OCR-uncertain). One site fell 17-fold in just two weeks, while another site increased about 400 percent over three months (OCR-uncertain).
3. **Flood context:** Peak flows in the Gila River far exceeded those in the Verde River during the 1993 to 1997 period, and the 1997 tropical-storm flood was among the largest recorded. These high-magnitude flows are a plausible driver of rapid population change.
4. **Habitat scale:** The authors argue that fine-scale geomorphic and microhabitat factors are likely more important to these fishes than broad-scale gradient.
5. **Wildfire relevance:** The report's title frames these hydrologic and population data as context for assessing short-term wildfire effects, since post-fire floods can alter sediment and habitat quickly.
