# Draft Environmental Statement: Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #wilderness-designation

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management (BLM) draft Environmental Statement (1979) evaluates the proposed legislative designation of the [[Aravaipa Canyon Primitive Area]] (4,044 acres) and adjacent public lands (2,543 acres) as wilderness. The combined 6,587-acre "study area" lies in Pinal and Graham Counties, Arizona. The proposal would add the area to the National Wilderness Preservation System under the [[Federal Land Policy and Management Act]] (FLPMA) of 1976 and [[Wilderness Act]] of 1964.

The statement concludes that wilderness designation would cause no significant immediate or long-range environmental changes, because BLM's existing management plan already conforms to wilderness guidelines. Designation would mainly add permanence and legislative protection. Two alternatives were considered: enlarging the wilderness to 6,369 acres of inventoried land (Alternative 1), and no action (Alternative 2).

## Background & Description

- **Status:** The primitive area was established by Secretarial order on January 16, 1969 (the text also gives January 9, 1969 for the designation) after strong local, State and national support. BLM has managed it as a de facto wilderness for about 10 years.
- **Legal basis:** FLPMA Section 603 requires BLM to study public lands for wilderness potential. The recommendation was due to the President by July 1, 1980. Congressional designation is permanent, subject only to change by Congress.
- **Management:** The [[Aravaipa Canyon Management Plan]] (Appendix 1) sets objectives to protect natural beauty and primitive character. Measures include closure to motorized vehicles, a firearm ban in the canyon and side canyons, termination of canyon-bottom grazing since August 1974, a permit system limited to 50 visitors per day, and a 3-day/2-night stay limit.
- **Wilderness inventory:** BLM's intensive inventory (Appendix 2, unit AZ-4-1) found that the area meets the criteria of Section 2(c) of the Wilderness Act, including at least 5,000 acres, solitude, and supplemental values. Roughly 218 acres were excluded because of roads and impairments.
- **Public use:** Visitor use rose from 1,505 visits (1973, July-December) to 4,061 visits in 1977. Peak use falls in March-May and October-November.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The area lies in the Basin and Range province, where [[Aravaipa Creek]] flows as a perennial stream for 15 miles through canyon walls up to 1,000 feet high. The creek drains 541 square miles. The study area has 4 perennial and 10 perennial-spring tributaries among 14 major tributaries.

**Climate.** The climate is arid with about 15 inches of annual precipitation, 40 percent of it in July-September, and mean pan evaporation above 7 feet. The creek moderates local humidity and temperature.

**Water quality.** Untreated surface water is unfit for drinking because of bacterial contamination. A 1972 study found that all dissolved elements except mercury met recommended standards. Mercury often exceeded drinking-water and aquatic-life maxima, and its sources are unidentified.

**Vegetation.** The area supports three types: desert shrub (2,191 acres), mountain shrub (3,826 acres) and broadleaf riparian (570 acres). Of 16 candidate threatened or endangered plants, 3 are confirmed, 6 probable and 7 possible.

**Aquatic habitat.** Aravaipa Creek supports 8 of the 12 native fish species of the San Pedro River system, plus the introduced green sunfish. No other Arizona stream is known to hold as many native species without substantial introduced fish. Longfin dace and speckled dace make up more than 85 percent of fishes collected. The creek holds the last reproducing population of [[Loach Minnow]] in southern Arizona. A single illegally introduced largemouth bass was collected and could harm native fish if established. The Gila topminnow was reintroduced in early 1978 but had not been reported since.

**Fauna overall.** The area includes 9 fish, 6 amphibian, 35 reptile, 25 mammal and 202 bird species. Riparian habitat holds 21 of the 25 mammal species, 4 of 5 amphibian species and 21 of 35 reptile species.

**Threatened and endangered animals.** The southern bald eagle, peregrine falcon and [[Gila Topminnow]] are federally listed as endangered. Table 2 lists AG&FD state-threatened species, including the loach minnow, spikedace, desert tortoise and desert bighorn sheep. About 32 bighorn sheep inhabit the study area.

**Cultural resources.** The area has been occupied for about 9,500 years, with Hohokam and Salado sites. Of 18 known sites, one (Salazar Homestead) is eligible for the [[National Register of Historic Places]], and one prehistoric site is believed to be of National Register quality. Condition is poor at 22 percent, fair at 39 percent and good at 33 percent.

**Air and noise.** Air quality has never been measured but appears good. The area is Class II under the [[Clean Air Act]]. Nearby smelters at San Manuel, Hayden and Christmas are potential sources of pollution. Noise is mainly natural, though military jets and helicopters occasionally intrude.

## Key Findings

- **No significant impacts.** No impacts were found for topography, geology, soils, air quality, noise, water rights, vegetation or livestock grazing. Wilderness designation is expected to benefit wilderness values over the long term and to protect the area from future man-caused impairment.
- **Recreation effects.** Recreation quality and opportunity would not change. Visitor use might rise because of publicity, which could increase vandalism to cultural sites. Protective measures such as storm fencing may be disallowed in wilderness.
- **Land use constraints.** Wilderness designation would largely preclude power site development and new transportation or utility rights-of-way. Mineral resources would be further restricted but their physical characteristics would not change.
- **Social attitudes.** Of 38 informal interviews, all respondents were concerned about overuse, and none objected to a permit requirement. Only off-road vehicle groups and hunters opposed the management plan. The interviews were not statistically valid.
- **Alternative 1 (enlarged wilderness, 6,369 acres).** This would add 2,325 acres, including 150 acres of prime riparian habitat and 600 acres of crucial desert bighorn sheep concentration area. It would also protect seven additional known prehistoric sites. It would preclude mining on 840 acres of low mineral potential after December 31, 1983.
- **Alternative 2 (no action).** The study area would remain largely unchanged. Riparian habitat quality is expected to improve, and air quality may improve slightly.
- **Irreversibility.** Wilderness designation is a long-term commitment but is not irreversible or irretrievable, because Congress can change it.
- **Proposed conclusion.** BLM recommends wilderness designation of the 4,044-acre primitive area, with management continuing under the existing plan.
