# Surrogate Species Selection for Pharyngeal Teeth Damage Study in Gila cypha

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This document is an official U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service letter addressing a methodological concern in the [[Glen Canyon]] investigations involving the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The core issue is whether the **stomach pumping technique** used to sample gut contents of *G. cypha* causes physical damage to the fish's **pharyngeal teeth** during insertion, use, or removal of sampling equipment. Because *G. cypha* is a federally listed endangered species, direct experimentation on it to test equipment safety is discouraged. The letter recommends using a **congeneric surrogate species** with similar pharyngeal dentition to validate the sampling method before applying it to the protected species.

## Background & Description
- **Pharyngeal teeth**: The key anatomical structure of concern; located in the "gullet" region, used by cyprinids (including *Gila* spp.) for processing food since they lack oral (jaw) teeth, a hallmark trait of the family [[Cyprinidae]].
- Species within the genus *Gila* are noted to share **similar pharyngeal teeth morphology**, which is the scientific justification for using congeners as surrogates — damage assessment findings in one species should be reasonably transferable to another within the same genus.
- No detailed morphometric data (length, weight, tooth counts) are provided in this correspondence; the focus is procedural/methodological rather than descriptive taxonomy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Gila cypha ([[Humpback Chub]])**: Endemic to the Colorado River basin, notably studied within the [[Glen Canyon]] reach; federally listed as endangered.
- **Gila elegans ([[Bonytail Chub]])**: Also federally listed as endangered; explicitly excluded from experimental use, particularly the 1981 year class F1 stock derived from wild fish.
- **Gila robusta robusta ([[Roundtail Chub]])**: Not federally listed, but noted as "less rare" than *G. elegans* while still experiencing population declines across much of its range — a species of conservation concern despite lacking formal listing.
- **Gila atraria ([[Utah Chub]])**: Described as abundant and readily available, making it ecologically and logistically suitable as an experimental surrogate without conservation risk.
- The genus *Gila* occupies freshwater riverine habitats within the [[Colorado River]] drainage system, with varying degrees of rarity and range contraction among constituent species.

## Key Findings
- **Research Question**: Does the stomach pumping method used to study *G. cypha* diet in Glen Canyon damage the fish's pharyngeal teeth?
- **Conservation constraint**: Because *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* are endangered, direct testing on them (or on *G. elegans* 1981 F1 wild stock) is inappropriate for equipment validation purposes.
- **Recommended approach**: Use *Gila atraria*, or other abundant, non-listed *Gila* species, as a surrogate to assess pharyngeal teeth damage risk from stomach pumping equipment.
- **Rationale**: Congeneric species share sufficiently similar pharyngeal tooth structure, allowing extrapolation of damage/safety findings to *G. cypha* without endangering the listed species.
- **Institutional stance**: The FWS official expresses conditional support for using *G. r. robusta* if necessary (not prohibited by law despite declining status), but strongly prefers *G. atraria* as the least conservation-sensitive choice.
- **Broader implication**: This correspondence illustrates practical conservation-research trade-offs — balancing the need for methodological validation (sampling technique safety) against the imperative to avoid any experimental harm to endangered [[Gila]] species during ongoing endangered species recovery and monitoring efforts.
