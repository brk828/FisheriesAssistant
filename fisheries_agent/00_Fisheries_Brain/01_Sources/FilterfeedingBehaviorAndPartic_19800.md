# Filter-Feeding Behavior and Particle Retention Efficiency of Sacramento Blackfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study examines the pump filter-feeding behavior of [[Sacramento Blackfish]] (*Orthodon microlepidotus*), a North American cyprinid that uses a relatively uncommon suction-based feeding strategy. The authors quantified water processing rates as the product of [[Buccal Volume]] and [[Pumping Rate]], tested how temperature, fish size, and prey density affect feeding, and measured [[Particle Retention Efficiency]] using plastic microspheres and zooplankton. Fish from about 5 to 31 cm standard length all filter-fed, but their behavior and selectivity shifted with growth, with small fish relying more on visual feeding. Filtering rates were similar to those of other pump filter-feeding fishes, so the authors suggest the species' plankton impact should scale with its biomass.

## Background & Description

Fish use two general filter-feeding mechanisms: tow-net filtering, in which fish swim with mouths agape, and pump filter feeding, in which rapid buccal and opercular suctions draw in water and prey. The Sacramento blackfish is a pump filter feeder, a behavior the authors describe as relatively rare among temperate North American fishes. Juveniles typically begin as sight feeders and gradually or abruptly switch to filter feeding as they grow, a transition also documented in [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Filter-Feeding Cichlids]].

Prior work had related fish size to filtration rate in several pump filter feeders but had not examined how fish size, temperature, and prey density jointly affect filtration rate and feeding time. The authors note that such information is needed to estimate feeding rates in field populations. Two further constraints on plankton capture were identified: evasive zooplankton can escape suction currents, and the morphology of gill rakers or mucus systems limits the lower size of retained particles (approximately 7 to 65 µm across studies).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Field studies cited in the paper report that Sacramento blackfish consume a wide range of zooplankton, algae, and detritus. Fish under 4 cm SL feed largely on cladocerans, while fish over 20 cm SL have been regarded as nonselective generalists, eating copepods less often than expected. Adults often occupy turbid waters with clay substrates, juveniles use shallow vegetated areas, and young-of-the-year school near shore while older fish school offshore. The authors propose that habitat shifts accompany changes in feeding tactics as the fish grow.

Under clear, well-lit laboratory conditions the observed feeding rates likely represent maximum potential rates, because turbidity reduces sight-feeding ability in larger fish. The paper also places the species in a community context: filter-feeding fish may compete with zooplankton for particles larger than about 20 µm and with predatory copepods for large rotifers, nauplii, and early copepodids, and may affect nutrient fluxes. The magnitude of these community-level effects was described as currently unknown.

## Key Findings

**Feeding mechanics and rates**
- Buccal volume increased as a power function of standard length (BV = 0.0011·SL^2.86, r² = 0.99, n = 38).
- Pumping rate decreased with fish size at all temperatures (for example, PR2 = 32.29·SL^-0.78, r² = 0.90, n = 49, at 18.5–22.5 °C).
- Pumping rate generally rose with temperature, except that fish under 15 cm SL pumped more slowly at 25 °C than at 18.5–22.5 °C.
- Pumping rate was unaffected by prey density (r² = 0.086, n = 24).
- Time spent feeding was correlated with observation period, temperature, and the temperature–prey density interaction, but not with fish size.
- Filtering rates were very similar to those of other pump filter feeders, and when scaled per gram of fish weight, gizzard shad, Sacramento blackfish, and two tilapias showed broadly similar water processing rates at 20 °C.

**Particle retention**
- Fish from 5.1 to 14.8 cm SL retained particles larger than 35 µm.
- Larger fish (28.4–30.8 cm SL) retained particles larger than 25 µm.
- Small and medium fish did not differ significantly from each other, but both differed from large fish (P < 0.05).
- Retention efficiency increased slightly with growth (about 5–10 µm), which the authors judge unlikely to significantly change phytoplankton impacts.

**Feeding behavior and prey selectivity**
- Small fish fed visually and captured large zooplankters individually with single directed suctions; medium fish used series of suctions; large fish never oriented suctions at individual prey.
- Small fish showed higher selectivity for large zooplankton, and their feeding rates on live cladocerans (*Daphnia* spp.) exceeded those on dead prey, suggesting attraction to swimming motion.
- Large fish captured dead zooplankton and large microspheres with similar efficiency, but live cyclopoid copepods were taken less efficiently than dead ones (P = 0.043), indicating that evasion reduces vulnerability to nondirected suctions.
- Large fish may therefore obtain a growing share of nutrition from phytoplankton, consistent with gut-content data and rising gut-length to body-length ratios.

**Comparisons and implications**
- Sacramento blackfish parallel filter-feeding cichlids more closely than gizzard shad, passing through a transitional stage that combines passive filtering and active visual selection.
- Gizzard shad switch to filter feeding at a much smaller size, and their retention efficiency drops by about 40 µm as they grow.
- The authors expect direct effects to include reduced zooplankton and larger (>30 µm) phytoplankton densities, which could indirectly favor small algae.

**Methodological note**
- Feeding rate coefficients were calculated from prey density decline (Dodson 1975), and microsphere retention was compared against predicted exponential decline using analysis of covariance and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Control tanks showed no significant prey loss, while experimental tanks showed significant reductions in all trials (P < 0.05).
