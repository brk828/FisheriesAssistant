# Biological Investigation of Flathead Catfish in the Cape Fear River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by C. R. Guier, L. E. Nichols and R. T. Rachels of the [[North Carolina Wildlife Resources Commission]], documents the establishment and spread of the non-native [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) in the mainstream of the [[Cape Fear River]], North Carolina. Eleven adult flatheads (107 kg total) were released near Fayetteville in 1966, the only known introduction of this species into a North Carolina coastal stream. The population now occupies a 201-km section of river (the text elsewhere gives 230 km). Flatheads grew faster than in native riverine populations and at rates similar to reservoir populations. Their diet was almost entirely fish. Comparison with 1963 rotenone samples shows declines in native [[Ictaluridae]] species, most severely in bullheads. The authors attribute these declines to predation by the expanding flathead population.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The Cape Fear River forms near Moncure, North Carolina, where the Deep and Haw rivers meet. It flows about 274 km southeast to the Atlantic Ocean near Southport. Sampling covered only the mainstream, at three stations: [[Lillington]] (river km 238), [[Fayetteville]] (river km 196) and [[Riegelwood]] (river km 45). These sites were classed as Largemouth Bass, Carp-Catfish and Tidal zones, following Van Deusen's (1958) stream classification. Funding came from Federal Aid in Fish Restoration (project F22S).

**Collection methods.** The main method was [[Electrofishing]] from a shocking boat with a chase boat, run along the shoreline. This used a telephone-magneto unit following Morris and Novack (1968). Supplementary gear included [[Fyke Netting]] (1.1-m hoops, 6.1-m wings), [[Gill Netting]] (36 m long, 3 m deep, 44–77 mm bar mesh), and a DC electrofishing unit modified from the Wisconsin design. Each study area was sampled twice: a prespawn sample (May–June) and a postspawn sample (August–September). Per sampling period, each site received six gill nets and four fyke nets for at least two gear-days, plus at least two hours of electrofishing.

**Food habits.** Stomach contents were taken from flatheads over 1 kg using [[Gastric Lavage]] (Foster 1977), with a collection apparatus modified from Crossman and Hamilton (1978). Samples were preserved in 10% formalin. Heavily digested items were grouped as insect, fish or detritus remains.

**Age and growth.** Ages were estimated from [[Pectoral Spine Aging]] sections, using techniques based on Sneed (1951), Marzolf (1955) and Carlander (1969). Spines were embedded in epoxy, sectioned, polished and read under a dissecting microscope. Body length–spine radius data came from 121 fish (47–980 mm total length). Back-calculations used the Lee regression method (Lagler 1956).

**Condition and length–weight.** [[Condition Factor]] (K) and the [[Length-Weight Relationship]] were calculated for 161 flatheads, with weight in grams and length in millimetres.

**Reference fragment.** The first page of the document is a partial reference list from a separate channel catfish study (*Ictalurus punctatus*, Des Moines River, Iowa). It contains no extractable findings, so it is noted here only for context.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Introduction and spread.** The flathead population grew from a single release point. Establishment was first documented in 1976, when five flatheads of several size classes were collected. The authors say further upstream migration is probably blocked by a [[Lowhead Dam]], while downstream spread is halted by saline water in the lower river. Expansion into minor tributaries is unknown. Expansion into major tributaries is considered inevitable.

**Blue catfish.** [[Blue Catfish]] (*Ictalurus furcatus*) were also introduced in 1966. Their population has not expanded rapidly and does not appear to affect native fish as flatheads do. They are, however, used as forage by flatheads.

**Native community change.** Between the 1963 rotenone survey and the 1979 samples, the proportions of native bullheads, white catfish and [[Channel Catfish]] declined. [[Brown Bullhead]] showed the largest decline, followed by flat bullhead. Several species of bullhead were common in undocumented NCWRC samples from 1968 and 1972, but were absent or scarce by 1979. [[Centrarchidae]] increased in total numbers but declined in percent weight.

**Diet and forage base.** Fish dominated the flathead diet at every site. The most frequent forage families were [[Ictaluridae]], [[Centrarchidae]] and [[Clupeidae]]. Gizzard shad, [[Bluegill]] and black crappie were the most important fish taxa, and crayfish were scarce.

## Key Findings

- **Introduction and population expansion:** 11 adult flatheads (107 kg) were released near Fayetteville in 1966. The population now occupies 201 km of the Cape Fear River (Abstract), or 230 km (Discussion). It reached a significant population within about 10 years and was the dominant mainstream predator within 15 years.
- **Abundance in the 1979 samples:** Flatheads made up 10.52% of the fish by number and 64.7% by weight.
- **Growth:** Growth during the expansion exceeded rates reported for native riverine populations and was similar to reservoir populations.
- **Length–weight:** For 161 fish, log_e W = −5.2100 + 3.0973 log_e L. Reported exponents for other waters were 3.1809 and 3.1759 (Missouri River), 3.233 (Grand Lake), 3.421 (Watts Bar Reservoir) and 3.272 (Bluestone Reservoir).
- **Condition factor:** K increased with length class, from 0.89 (300–399 mm) to 1.46 (900–999 mm). Riegelwood flatheads had K = 1.25, higher than Lillington (K ≈ 1.16) and the other comparison area (K ≈ 1.15).
- **Diet sample:** Stomach contents were examined from 105 flatheads. Food was found in 66 stomachs and 39 were empty. Only 10% of fish were sacrificed, supporting the effectiveness of gastric lavage.
- **Diet composition:** Osteichthyes made up 45.6% of total numbers and 99.43% of total weight of major food groups, showing near-total reliance on fish for flatheads over 1 kg. Pelecypods (clams) were the most numerous item (38.2%) but were trace by weight. Crayfish were 3.75% of numbers and 0.38% of weight. Unidentified ictalurids were 8.06% of numbers and 11.55% of weight.
- **Frequency of occurrence:** Ictalurids occurred in 33.4% of stomachs, centrarchids in 22.7% and clupeids in 16.7%. At Riegelwood, ictalurids occurred in 65% of stomachs.
- **Native fish declines:** Native ictalurid populations declined between 1963 and 1979. Brown bullheads showed the greatest loss, followed by flat bullheads. The authors attribute this to flathead predation.
- **Aging bias:** Pectoral spine sections often lost the central annuli, and the loss increased with age. Back-calculations were adjusted for missing annuli, because uncorrected aging would have produced serious errors.
- **Conclusion:** The authors conclude that flathead predation has caused severe declines in native bullheads and in the previously introduced channel catfish population in the Cape Fear River mainstream.
