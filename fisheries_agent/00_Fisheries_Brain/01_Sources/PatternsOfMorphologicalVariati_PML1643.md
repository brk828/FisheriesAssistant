# Morphological Variation Among Endangered Populations of *Gila robusta* and *Gila cypha* in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 study in *Copeia* by [[McElroy and Douglas]] examines morphological variation in two endangered [[Colorado River Chub|Colorado River Gila]] species, [[Roundtail Chub|Gila robusta]] and [[Humpback Chub|Gila cypha]], across eight localities in the upper Colorado River Basin. Using geometric morphometric landmark data from 363 specimens, the authors tested whether morphology reflects population identity, geography, or species identity. They found significant population-level divergence in both species, a clear species-level separation, and an unexpected convergence of heterospecific populations at Desolation and Cataract canyons. The authors conclude that all populations should be treated as potential independent evolutionary and conservation units, and that management must consider hybridization, local adaptation, and habitat conservation.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** *Gila robusta* (Roundtail Chub) and *Gila cypha* (Humpback Chub), members of the [[Cyprinidae]] family, both endangered in the upper Colorado Basin.
- **Conservation context:** Native fish of the American Southwest are declining due to habitat destruction, disruption of natural flow regimes, and invasion by non-native species. Taxonomic uncertainty complicates the management of Gila populations.
- **Methodological background:** Earlier work relied on meristic and qualitative characters, with mixed success. Previous studies noted difficulties in identifying species and intermediate specimens, and some used non-destructive video imaging and landmark analysis.
- **Sampling:** Field work was conducted with the help of multiple agencies, and specimens were filmed and measured. Landmarks were digitized from video images and analyzed using [[Geometric Morphometrics]] and size-correction methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat sensitivity:** Desolation and Cataract canyons are described as extremely energetic habitats with rapid, turbulent flow. The authors suggest such conditions may impose strong selection for a common morphology in both species.
- **Dam influence:** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (1963) altered habitat and was previously proposed as a driver of hybridization, but the authors argue hybridization among Gila species predates known human modifications.
- **Spatial scale:** Populations separated by more than 600 river miles clustered together, while nearby populations (Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon, under 15 river miles apart) were most similar in some analyses. Geographic proximity did not predict morphological similarity.
- **Community context:** The authors stress that Gila should not be managed in isolation from congeners or from the environment, echoing [[Grant and Grant]] on maintaining intact communities.

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 363 specimens were analyzed from eight localities: Black Rocks (44), Cataract Canyon (17), Desolation Canyon (46), Debeque Canyon (20), Grand Canyon (28), Rifle (25), Westwater Canyon (113), and Yampa River (70). Of these, 215 were *G. robusta* and 148 were *G. cypha*.
- **Population divergence:** Significant among-population structure was found in both species at multiple levels of biological organization.
- **Population-level patterns:** In *G. robusta*, canonical variates analysis showed significant among-group differences. In *G. cypha*, population separation was also significant (Wilks' Lambda = 0.0014). Cataract and Desolation canyon populations were distinct from all others in both species.
- **No geographic signal:** Morphological similarity was uncorrelated with geographic distance (Mantel tests, non-significant for both species).
- **Species separation:** The two species were clearly distinguishable by canonical variates analysis (Wilks' Lambda = 0.202). All specimens were correctly classified with discriminant function analysis (DFA) at the species level.
- **Heterospecific convergence:** At Desolation and Cataract canyons, populations of the two species were more similar to each other than to allopatric conspecifics. Overall, over 92% (336 of 363) of specimens were correctly classified at the population level, with an overall error rate of 0.075; 89% of misclassifications occurred within species.
- **Possible causes of convergence:** The authors propose introgressive hybridization, local adaptation to high-energy flow, or shared ancestral (symplesiomorphic) traits. They consider these explanations not mutually exclusive and suggest genetic data (allozyme and mtDNA) are needed to test them.
- **Conservation implications:** Significant divergence in all populations suggests each may represent an independent evolutionary and conservation unit. Hybridization, if confirmed, does not imply introgressed populations are less valuable, because it may be a natural phenomenon and species remain distinguishable.
- **Recommendations:** Caution and biological foresight are needed in management decisions; habitat conservation should be emphasized; future work should include genetic studies and comparison with lower-basin Gila populations and museum collections made before habitat alteration.
