## Executive Summary
This study (Busdosh, McSorley, & Rinne, 1978) examines the age and growth of the [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) in a wild, unimpounded reach of the [[Salt River]], Arizona, upstream of Roosevelt Lake. *I. punctatus* is not native to Arizona, having been introduced into this river system in 1938. Based on 66 specimens collected via gill nets and seining in the summer of 1972, researchers used pectoral spine sectioning to determine age. The population exhibited significantly faster growth rates compared to most other North American rivers and lakes, which the authors attribute to an extended growing season and high total dissolved solids (TDS) in the desert environment. The population structure was heavily skewed toward Age Groups II and III, with young-of-year (Age 0) and yearlings (Age I) being notably underrepresented, likely due to sampling gear bias or habitat preferences of juvenile fish.

## Background & Description
* **Aging Structure:** The left [[Pectoral Spine]] was used for age determination via annular ring counts (following Sneed, 1951), as opposed to otoliths or scales.
* **Dentition:** Age groups II and III possess sharper, more recurved teeth on the trailing edge of the pectoral spine compared to older fish. This morphological trait likely increases their susceptibility to "snagging" during capture, potentially biasing sample collection toward these age classes.
* **Size Overlap:** Standard Length (SL) distributions for Age Groups 0 through III were distinct; however, overlap in size ranges occurred between Age Group III and Age Group IV.
* **Maximum Size:** While the sampled population was dominated by smaller/younger fish, anecdotal evidence included a sighting of a ~500mm SL individual and the recovery of skeletal remains from a very large catfish estimated at Age Group VII or VIII.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
* **Habitat:** The study focused on the reach of the Salt River above Roosevelt Lake, characterized as a nearly natural, undammed canyon environment flowing through the Upper and Lower Sonoran Desert.
* **Microhabitat:** Catfish were absent from Station 4, described as a "fastwater pool," though individuals were observed in nearby deep, slow-water refuges. This suggests a preference for low-velocity, deep water habitats over swift riffles.
* **Water Chemistry:** The river exhibits high Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) (~1000 mg/l), which the authors hypothesize contributes to increased biological productivity and subsequently faster fish growth rates (citing [[Ryder 1965]]).
* **Trophic Interactions/Predation:** The historical predator of *I. punctatus* in this system, the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), was found to be extinct or functionally absent from the Salt River during this period. Consequently, natural mortality (likely intraspecific, from larger catfish) is theorized to be the primary regulator of the older age classes, rather than predation.
* **Reproduction/Recruitment:** Dominant year classes (Age II and III) corresponded to hatching events following peak river discharges in December of the preceding years, suggesting a link between hydrology and spawning success (per [[Rinne 1975]]).

## Key Findings
1. **Rapid Growth Rate:** Salt River channel catfish grew faster in their first year of life than in subsequent years, following the typical decelerating growth curve for the species. By Age III, Arizona specimens were larger than counterparts from the Potomac River, Mississippi River, and Des Moines River (see comparative data below).
2. **Comparative Growth Data (Mean SL in mm):**

    | **Salt River (AZ)** | 120 | 195 | 260 | 310 |
    | Colorado River (CA) | 62 | 154 | 207 | 268 |
    | Potomac River (MD) | 144 | 196 | 242 | 293 |
    | Mississippi/Missouri R. | 120 | 177 | 231 | 270 |
3. **Climate vs. Other Factors:** Despite similar desert climatic conditions, Salt River catfish grew 15% to 100% longer at equivalent ages compared to those in the Colorado River, indicating that factors beyond climate (e.g., water chemistry/TDS, food availability) significantly influence growth rates.
4. **Skewed Age Distribution:** 52 of the 66 fish collected (and 24/29 in a subsequent verification sample) belonged to Age Groups II and III. Age 0 and Age I fish were rarely captured.
5. **Sampling Bias Hypothesis:** The researchers propose that juvenile catfish (Age 0/I) occupy waters too deep to seine effectively but are too small to be efficiently captured by gill nets, explaining their absence from the sample rather than confirming an actual population deficit.
6. **Capture Method Consistency:** A supplementary sample using hook-and-line and trammel nets confirmed the dominance of Age II and III fish, suggesting the skewed distribution is not merely an artifact of gill net mesh size selectivity alone.
