## Executive Summary

This document summarizes a technical review meeting (March 2, 1994) convened by the Bureau of Reclamation to evaluate the draft Biological Opinion for [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, with primary focus on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and other native fishes of the Colorado River in [[Grand Canyon]]. Endangered fish scientists, [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) advisors, and resource managers debated the ecological drivers affecting native fish populations and compared two dam-operation scenarios: the **Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA)** — emphasizing minimized daily flow fluctuations, seasonally adjusted hydrology, and periodic controlled floods — versus the **Modified Low Fluctuating Flow (MLFF)** alternative. The consensus favored the RPA as having the highest probability of removing jeopardy to the humpback chub, driven largely by concerns over unnatural thermal regimes, disrupted flow variability, and loss of natural hydrograph cues.

## Background & Description

The source is a policy/science meeting record and does not provide direct morphometric or anatomical descriptions of the fish. However, several life-history and physiological inferences relevant to species biology are noted:

- **[[Humpback Chub]]** — adults/subadults are physiologically robust to flow magnitude changes but require large peak/base flow ratios (three orders of magnitude) before adverse effects are observed; **eggs and larvae are vulnerable to washout** at much lower flow perturbations.
- All native fishes discussed are described as of **temperate origin**, and are **physiologically suppressed** in the artificially cold, thermally stable water released from Glen Canyon Dam — this affects spawning cues, egg yolk viability, and juvenile growth rates.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*) was specifically flagged as a possible exception among adult-sized native fishes with respect to food/drift limitation, implying different feeding ecology or metabolic demand relative to other native species.
- [[PIT Tag]] telemetry data showed **no upstream movement** of humpback chub in response to flow events, indicating limited large-scale migratory response to controlled floods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal Regime**
- Dam operations have converted the natural seasonal thermal cycle into an artificially dampened regime: **colder summers, warmer winters**, producing near-constant cold temperatures.
- This thermal homogenization: suppresses native fish physiology, removes spawning cues, impairs egg yolk development, reduces growth, and **favors cold-water nonnative predators** (e.g., trout).
- One benefit of cold mainstem temperatures: exclusion of warm-water nonnative competitors/predators.
- Because of persistent cold mainstem temperatures, the **entire below-dam humpback chub population is now dependent on the [[Little Colorado River]]** as the sole viable spawning and recruitment habitat — mainstem spawning is currently precluded by temperature.
- A proposed **multi-level intake structure** at the dam was suggested as a potential thermal-modification tool to enable future mainstem spawning.

**Flow Variability & Hydrograph**
- Native fishes are believed to rely on natural hydrograph cues (magnitude, duration, timing, frequency, rate of rise) for spawning and recruitment behavior; daily flow fluctuations are an evolutionarily novel stressor.
- Minimizing **diurnal fluctuations** while maximizing **seasonal fluctuation** (including randomness/natural variability) was broadly favored.
- Reduced food drift under interim flows may **limit young-of-year and juvenile fish** growth/survival, even if not limiting for most adult native fish.
- Turbidity/sediment load was identified as ecologically important, influencing feeding behavior and possibly explaining reduced fish productivity in **lower Grand Canyon reaches** compared to upper reaches (higher turbidity + cold water = lower productivity).

**Flooding & Habitat Maintenance**
- Controlled floods (<31,000 cfs, ~1:2 yr recurrence) and habitat-building floods (~45,000 cfs, ~1:8 yr recurrence) were distinguished from uncontrolled floods (>31,000 cfs, ~1:100 yr) plus tributary flood events.
- Flooding is considered **more important for habitat maintenance** (e.g., backwater creation/scour, shoreline diversity, return-current channel formation) **than for direct population gain or loss**.
- **[[Backwater Habitat]]** is critical nursery habitat for young native fishes; management should ensure backwater availability, though annual maintenance floods may not always be necessary.
- Historical mainstem floods were noted to pond the [[Paria River]] confluence for ~1 mile, with "bonytail" (*Gila elegans*) historically observed in such ponded tributary habitats — indicating floodplain/backwater use by this now critically rare species.
- Differential, diurnal use of diverse shoreline habitat types by fish was observed, underscoring the importance of habitat heterogeneity.

## Key Findings

- **RPA vs. MLFF**: The RPA (minimized daily fluctuation, seasonal hydrograph adjustment, periodic controlled floods) was judged by the majority of researchers present to have a **higher probability of benefiting the native fish community**, particularly the humpback chub, than the MLFF alternative.
- Some researchers cautioned that **neither alternative may fully remove jeopardy**, and that insufficient data existed to confidently choose between them.
- Given the **long generational timescales of native fish population responses** (a single generation ≈ 5 years; robust comparison may require 10–15+ years, or even up to 30 years for cyclic population dynamics), rapid side-by-side experimental comparison of flow regimes was viewed as impractical.
- The precautionary principle was emphasized: given uncertainty, the group recommended **erring on the side of the species** and adopting the alternative with the highest a priori probability of success (the RPA) rather than continuing to collect comparative data indefinitely.
- A researcher-authored white paper, *"Management of discharge, temperature, and sediment in Grand Canyon for native fishes,"* was distributed, reinforcing the same conclusions: minimize daily fluctuations, restore seasonal hydrologic patterns, and deliver periodic controlled floods under existing temperature/sediment regimes.
- The meeting explicitly reframed the comparison to avoid conflating alternatives, since the true comparison of interest was **RPA vs. MLFF**.
- Overall ecological driver categories identified for native/endangered fish management include: flooding, sediment/turbidity, tributary access and ponding, backwater availability, nutrient/food base, growth, predation, thermal regime, parasites/disease, nonnative species interactions, life-stage-specific habitat use, shoreline diversity, flow variability (daily/annual), climatic variables, and historical geomorphic change.
