# Interactive influence of turbidity and light on larval bluegill foraging

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

The document combines two sections of a journal issue. The first is a discussion of [[Ralston Plots]], a rapid graphical method for estimating the functional form of fishing gear selectivity, applied mainly to [[Hook Selectivity]] in longline and handline fisheries. The second, and main, part is a 1993 field study by Miner and Stein on how suspended-sediment [[Turbidity]] and [[Light Intensity]] jointly affect the foraging of larval [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) in [[Stonelick Lake]], Ohio. The study found that turbidity reduced prey consumption only when light fell below roughly 450 lx. Under high light, bluegill consumed more but smaller prey, so prey biomass did not change. Under low light, prey biomass consumed declined with turbidity. The authors argue that light-mediated turbidity effects help explain conflicting results in the larval fish literature.

## Background & Description

**Ralston Plots (selectivity section).** The first fragment discusses how Ralston Plots can reveal the shape of selectivity curves, such as asymptotic or domed (dome-shaped) functions. The authors note that Ralston's (1990) earlier attempt, on snappers (Lutjanidae), was unsuccessful, largely because of the low quality of available data sets. Plots built from field data nevertheless matched theoretical expectations. The authors reanalysed [[Atlantic Cod]] data from McCracken (1963), which had been taken as evidence for asymptotic hook selection. They found those data more consistent with some domed form. The authors caution that Ralston Plots should not be used as stand-alone analytical tools unless the curves are unambiguous and each point rests on many fish. They also recommend formal statistical comparison of competing functional forms.

**Turbidity and light study (bluegill).** The bluegill paper is a field study using in situ enclosures. Larval bluegill were the focus because they are a common limnetic species that feeds on zooplankton. The authors chose field enclosures over shallow aquaria because predators and zooplankton can select optimal depths in the field. The study addressed:

- Whether turbidity from suspended sediments reduces larval foraging on [[Copepods]] and [[Cladocerans]].
- Whether light intensity mediates any turbidity effect.
- Whether prey selection shifts with turbidity, using [[Chesson's Alpha]].
- Whether prey size and total biomass consumed change along the turbidity gradient.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Turbidity effects in the literature.** Turbidity from suspended sediment is common in lakes, particularly those in agricultural watersheds, and in rivers and estuaries, where it can reach 0 to 130 nephelometric turbidity units (NTU). Studies of juvenile and adult particulate feeders typically show reduced reaction distance and foraging with turbidity. Studies on larval fish have produced conflicting results: turbidity has been reported to decrease, increase, or have no effect on foraging and growth. Examples include [[Atlantic Herring]] (Johnston and Wildish 1982), [[Pacific Herring]] (Boehlert and Morgan 1985), and [[Striped Bass]] (Breitburg 1988; Chesney 1989). The authors propose that these contrasts arise from the interaction between turbidity and light intensity.

**Light and larval growth.** Light intensity reduces larval foraging and growth at levels below about 100 lx, although thresholds vary among species. For striped bass larvae in clear water, growth declined exponentially as light fell from 300 to 0.4 lx but did not change above 450 lx. Turbidity can alter [[Prey-Background Contrast]], and it can shift the vertical distribution of both fish larvae and zooplankton. Because light declines with depth, turbid water may push foraging into suboptimal light conditions.

**Study site.** Stonelick Lake is a 69-ha reservoir in southwest Ohio with an upstream-to-downstream turbidity gradient. Typical Secchi depths were about 20 to 120 cm. Peak larval bluegill density in the lake averages 2.2 fish m⁻³. Water temperature in the enclosures was 24 to 27 °C, and dissolved oxygen ranged from 8.9 to 11 mg L⁻¹ across treatments. These values were consistent across the four experimental days.

## Key Findings

**Ralston Plots and hook selectivity**
- Ralston Plots built from field data can reproduce theoretical selectivity shapes, but their usefulness depends on data quality.
- McCracken's (1963) [[Atlantic Cod]] data, previously read as asymptotic hook selection, fit a domed form better.
- The authors recommend that Ralston Plots be paired with statistical model comparison, and they warn that arbitrary point removal and subjective curve fitting can mislead.
- The method is equally applicable to bait size and hook size studies, provided the gears share a functional form and differ only in parameter values.

**Experimental design (bluegill)**
- Two transparent polyethylene enclosures (about 1 m diameter) were placed at each of three sites along the turbidity gradient (depths 1.7, 1.5, and 1.1 m).
- Low and intermediate treatments were ambient, about 10 and 20 NTU. The high treatment was raised to about 30 to 40 NTU by adding bottom sediment. One enclosure reached 64 NTU through an overaddition.
- Larval bluegill averaged 12.5 ± 0.08 mm total length. Stocking was 15, 13, and 10 larvae in the low, intermediate, and high enclosures, respectively, giving a consistent density of about 11 larvae m⁻³.
- Zooplankton densities ranged from 125 to 800 organisms L⁻¹ across enclosures.
- Experiments ran on four consecutive days. Sets 1, 2, and 4 were high light and set 3 was low light because of overcast skies.

**Light and turbidity thresholds**
- Light in set 3 at 1 m depth ranged from 5 to 460 lx across turbidities. Under high light, light stayed above 460 lx at all turbidities and depths.
- Turbidity reduced bluegill consumption of zooplankton only when enclosure light fell below about 450 lx.

**Prey numbers and biomass**
- At high light, the number of prey consumed increased with turbidity (r² = 0.35; p = 0.006; N = 18).
- At low light, fewer zooplankters were consumed as turbidity increased (r² = 0.86; p = 0.005; N = 6).
- Neither zooplankton density nor its interaction with turbidity improved these models.
- At high light, the increase in prey number was offset by smaller prey size, so biomass consumed was unchanged (r² = 0.00; N = 18).
- At low light, biomass consumed declined with turbidity (r² = 0.85; N = 6).

**Prey selection**
- Cyclopoid copepods and copepod nauplii were the main prey, together making up about 70% of the diet. Cladocerans *Bosmina longirostris* and *Ceriodaphnia* sp. were common in the environment but were negatively selected.
- At high light, selection for copepod nauplii rose with turbidity, from negative at low turbidity (α = 0.02 ± 0.01) to positive at the highest level (α = 0.33 ± 0.10).
- Selection for calanoid copepods declined with turbidity, to α = 0.05 ± 0.02 at about 10 NTU.
- Cyclopoid copepods were consistently preferred at high light (α = 0.43 ± 0.04) and at low light (α = 0.62 ± 0.05).
- Prey selection did not change with turbidity at low light, but the sample size was small (N = 6).

**Prey size**
- At high light, mean length of prey consumed declined with turbidity (r² = 0.43; p = 0.003; N = 18), falling about 30% to 0.19 mm at 40 NTU.
- At low light, prey length increased with turbidity (r² = 0.83; p = 0.012; N = 6). Prey averaged 0.28 mm at 11 NTU and 0.35 mm at 43 to 64 NTU, a reported 75% increase relative to high-light sets at high turbidity.

**Mechanisms and conclusions**
- The authors suggest that at low light, copepods used the dim lower strata of the enclosures as a refuge, which reduced bluegill foraging. Without that refuge, consumption rose with turbidity, but prey were smaller.
- Smaller prey at higher turbidity may result from increased prey-background contrast, which lets larvae detect smaller, more profitable prey.
- Turbidity effects on larval fish feeding are therefore light-dependent. The authors conclude that increasing turbidity may reduce larval foraging, growth, and [[Larval Fish Recruitment]] where light falls below the threshold.
- The study also cautions that results from juvenile and adult fish, which depend on [[Optimal Foraging Theory]] and reaction distance, cannot be directly extrapolated to larvae, which have poorer visual acuity.
- The authors note that [[Diel Vertical Migration]] by zooplankton and larval fish, as seen in [[Yellow Perch]] and other species, may affect exposure to light and to predators, and this may matter for field outcomes.
