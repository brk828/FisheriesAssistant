# Socio-Economic Impacts of the Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 2001 socio-economic study by Bernard E. Anderson, Ph.D., evaluates the [[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program]], an eight-year artificial habitat and angler-access partnership on [[Lake Havasu]] in the lower [[Colorado River]]. The study uses a 2001 angler survey (n = 406 usable interviews from 422 collected), license-based estimates of angler use, and the [[IMPLAN]] input-output model to estimate economic effects in [[Mohave County]]. The author reports that angler use rose from about 43,000 days in 1987/1989 to over 170,000 days per year, that non-resident anglers generate substantial annual value added, employment and tax revenue, and that the program was largely funded from outside the local area. Causation is described as "circumstantial" and not fully provable.

Key economic results for non-resident anglers (annual, direct plus indirect plus induced):
- Total output of about $33.8 million
- Value added of about $18.1 million
- 655 jobs
- Total taxes of about $5.7 million

Resident angler spending was estimated to support output of about $34.5 million and 639 jobs.

## Background & Description

The study places Lake Havasu in historical context. [[Parker Dam]] was built between 1934 and 1941 to store Colorado River water for the [[Metropolitan Water District of Southern California]] and later the [[Central Arizona Project]]. Lake Havasu is shallower than [[Lake Powell]] or [[Lake Mead]], with an average depth of 30 feet and a southern third averaging 70 feet. Original flooded [[Cottonwood]] groves provided early fish habitat, but they decayed and were removed in the late 1960s.

The decline in habitat, together with alteration of the river, reduced native fish. The [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] are listed as endangered. The partnership was formed in 1992, led by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], the Metropolitan Water District and two [[Anglers United]] chapters.

Three goals were set:
1. Improve game fish production through artificial reef habitat.
2. Enhance shoreline access, including trails, restrooms and docks.
3. Increase endangered native fish populations.

The plan was estimated at about $28 million over roughly 10 years. Proposed works included 67,482 bass shelters, 54,724 catfish houses, 3,484 ambush cover structures, 1,050 tire towers and 11,800 brush bundles across 42 sites (about 875 acres). The author reports over 800 acres of underwater habitat in place and the work completed under budget and on schedule. Volunteers, largely [[Anglers United]] members, built much of the habitat without pay.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat was the central mechanism. Early reservoirs rely on flooded vegetation and soils to support forage fish, crayfish and juvenile fish, which in turn support predator populations. As submerged habitat decayed, productivity fell. Artificial structures were added to replace that function.

Causation is complicated. Historical data are inconsistent because licensing rules changed across Arizona, California and the [[Chemehuevi Indian Tribe]] lands, and Colorado River stamps cover the whole river from Boulder Dam to Mexico. The author notes that over-use in the 1980s may have contributed to decline, and that tournament participation falls faster than general use when fishing worsens. The biologist [[Brad Jacobson]] notes that habitat improvements take years to mature and that single causes cannot be isolated.

Access was another issue. State-owned shoreline on the east side was leased for private development, and much of the accessible shore was cabled off. Chemehuevi reservation shoreline was reachable only by a narrow highway. Before the program, boats were effectively the only means of shoreline access.

Native fish recovery is a parallel goal. The partnership aims for over 30,000 razorback suckers of 12 inches and is working toward a comparable number of bonytail. The author estimates the Lake Havasu population could provide roughly a 40-year extension for learning about these species.

## Key Findings

**Economic impacts (annual, non-resident anglers)**
- Non-resident non-tournament anglers (n = 8,906 effective): total output about $24.4 million; value added about $13.1 million; 474 jobs.
- Non-resident tournament anglers (n = 2,547 effective): total output about $9.4 million; 181 jobs.
- Combined non-resident: 655 jobs, about $34 million in output, about $11.3 million in labor income, and over $5.5 million in federal, state and local taxes.
- Each 10% rise in non-resident visitation is estimated to support about 65 jobs and $3.4 million in output.
- Resident anglers (about 3,599 effective) support about $34.5 million in output and 639 jobs. The author argues their retirement-related presence depends on fishing quality.

**Angler use and catch**
- Angler use days: about 43,000 in 1987/1989 versus about 170,000-179,000 today, an increase the author describes as roughly 212%.
- Estimated 2001 use: 175,455 days (this study) versus 179,114 days (Arizona Game and Fish Department, 1999 data).
- Catch rates (Jacobson, 2001): about 0.35 fish per hour in 1987 rising to about 0.55 in 2000 (57% increase); keep rates about 0.20 rising to 0.35 (75% increase).

**Angler survey (spring 2001)**
- Judgement sample, not probability-based, so sampling error cannot be estimated.
- 84.2% of anglers were familiar with the program; 97.0% of those felt it improved fishing.
- Fishing quality rating: 4.1 out of 5 (all surveys).
- About 70% of anglers come from outside the Lake Havasu area; about 50% are California residents.
- Non-resident tournament anglers averaged 3.51 days per trip, and residents spent about 77% of their fishing days at Lake Havasu.
- Daily spending per angler was highest for non-resident tournament anglers across lodging, food, tackle and fuel.

**Tournaments and fish quality**
- Tournament boats launched at [[Windsor Beach State Park]] rose substantially between 1994 and 2000, with dips in 1997 and 1999 and a large preliminary increase in 2001.
- Winning bag weights reportedly rose from 10-12 lb to 16-17 lb.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] stock densities improved, with the proportion over 15 inches higher than before the program.
- [[Channel Catfish]] size increased; [[Black Crappie]] populations and [[Flathead Catfish]] populations appear to be increasing.
- [[Striped Bass]] abundance varies with year-class strength.

**Social and access benefits**
- Five fishing piers were completed, including Mesquite #1 and #2, Havasu Springs, Take Off Point and Site Six. Site Six is the most used, with about 50% of anglers reporting use.
- Piers serve families, birders and picnickers, and include shade, lighting, restrooms and interpretive trails.
- 11.4% of tournament anglers and 10.1% of all anglers reported a physical challenge.
- The author estimates that tournament anglers contribute money to local charities.

**Native fish**
- Razorback sucker and bonytail populations are being augmented through hatcherie. Average funding exceeded $100,000 per year since 1993, largely from outside the area.

**Limitations**
- Survey recall error may reach 50%; the author assumed 80% of anglers spent money at Lake Havasu.
- Guide, launch and rental expenditures were excluded due to extreme variability.
- Capital and storage expenditures were excluded from the economic impact estimates.
- The IMPLAN model assumes linear, constant coefficients.
