# Limnology and Fisheries of the Saskatchewan River in Saskatchewan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hiodontidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae #percidae #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1962 Fisheries Branch report (Fisheries Report No. 6), by [[Edward B. Reed]], presents the results of a two-year survey (January 1957 to September 1958) of the [[North Saskatchewan River]], [[South Saskatchewan River]] and the [[Saskatchewan River]] within Saskatchewan. The survey addressed three questions: the fisheries resource, how it is used, and the river's condition with respect to pollution. The study covers physical conditions, water chemistry, plankton, coliform bacteria, bottom fauna, fish fauna, populations, growth, food, parasites and pollution. It concludes that the North Saskatchewan is grossly polluted before entering the province, with near-anoxic winter conditions, while the South Saskatchewan is relatively clean on entry but is being measurably depleted of oxygen below [[Saskatoon]] by domestic and industrial wastes.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The river supports domestic water supply for people and livestock, industry, and recreation (angling, picnicking, boating).
- **Study design:** Detailed observations were made at selected stations, usually near ferry crossings or bridges. Saskatoon, Prince Albert and Borden were sampled most often; 33 stations are mapped (Fig. 1).
- **Scope:** The combined length of the three principal river portions in Saskatchewan is nearly 900 miles. Detailed study of the whole system was beyond the survey's scope.
- **Data custody:** Detailed data and biological collections are held at the Fisheries Laboratory, University of Saskatchewan, Saskatoon.
- **Drainage basin:** Headwaters rise in the Rocky Mountains on the British Columbia-Alberta boundary. The South Saskatchewan drains 65,970 sq. mi. and the North 58,700 sq. mi. (Thomas, 1956). The system drains 27 per cent of Saskatchewan's land area, where 42 per cent of the province's population lives. The estimated drainage population is 1,161,300.
- **Hydrology:** Two flood peaks normally occur: an April prairie snowmelt peak and a June (sometimes July or August) mountain meltwater peak. Summer maximum-to-minimum flow ratios reached 53:1 within a calendar year and over 100:1 between June 1957 and December 1956.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and thermal conditions**
- Rivers are shallow, usually 6 to 10 ft deep outside floods. The deepest sounding was 26 ft (South Saskatchewan at Outlook); a 40 ft hole was recorded in the Old Channel.
- Bottoms are hard sand and gravel in fast reaches, and soft sand or mud in slack water. Shifting sandbars are prominent.
- Water is turbulent and well mixed; no thermal stratification was observed.
- Maximum observed surface temperatures were 24.0°C (1957) and 26.9°C (1958, Empress Ferry). The largest diurnal swing was 5.4°C.

**Suspended solids and turbidity**
- Suspended load peaks during high water. The North Saskatchewan ranged from 244 to 518 mg/l in 1957 (April to August).
- At Fenton, suspended solids were 1,032 mg/l on May 14, 1957 and 133 mg/l on August 6.
- At Saskatoon, about 8,350 tons of suspended material passed each 24 hours (April 8, 1958, roughly 10,000 c.f.s.).

**Water chemistry**
- Water is hard to very hard. Hardness ranged 133–414 ppm (North) and 93–306 ppm (South), and roughly doubles from summer to winter.
- Bicarbonate rises sharply under ice cover. The authors attribute this to carbonic acid from decomposing organic matter dissolving carbonates.
- Sulphate ranged 15–163 ppm (South) and 14–158 ppm (North). Sulphate and bicarbonate increased together, suggesting organic sulphate sources are of little importance.
- Dissolved solids: 158–463 ppm (North), 128–400 ppm (South).
- Thomas's (1956) hardness classes: soft (1–60), medium-hard (61–120), hard (121–180), very hard (>180).

**Dissolved oxygen**
- Open-water values were usually 75–98 per cent saturation.
- Under ice, the North Saskatchewan fell to about 1.2 c.c./l (12 per cent saturation) at Borden in March 1958. At Prince Albert it was 1.4 c.c./l in March 1958.
- The South Saskatchewan was 7.6 c.c./l (about 75 per cent) at minimum in winter.
- Daily cycles at Empress (July 21–22, 1958) ranged from 4.3 to 6.6 c.c./l. Photosynthesis by submerged plants caused supersaturation in daytime, and a mid-morning minimum was recorded.

**Plankton and bacteria**
- Diatoms were low; green algae were much higher. Station totals ranged from about 60,000 to 1,200,000 cells/l.
- Yorath Island, above Saskatoon, had the lowest plankton crop. Clarkboro and Hague had three to five times as much phytoplankton, attributed to Saskatoon sewage.
- Coliforms peaked at 400/100 ml (Frenchman Butte, June 1957) in the North. Downstream of Saskatoon, the highest count was 15,000/100 ml (Clarkboro, October 29, 1957).

**Bottom fauna**
- Distribution is very uneven. Rock and gravel riffles were most productive, followed by muddy backwaters, mixed sand and mud, and all-sand bottoms (least productive).
- Mayflies, caddisflies and stoneflies dominate riffles. Oligochaetes and chironomids dominate mud.
- Standing crops (dry weight): North Ekman dredge 1.9 kg/ha; South Ekman dredge 3.3 kg/ha; North Surber 3.4 kg/ha; South Surber 5.7 kg/ha; Saskatchewan riffles 6.3 kg/ha (Table 12, which lists this value as 6.3 lb/ac).
- These values correspond to the lowest-yielding Saskatchewan lakes.

**Fish fauna**
- Twenty-four species were taken (gill nets and seines), plus one reported by licensees.
- Goldeye, longnose sucker and northern redhorse dominated gill-net catches. Pike, walleye and common sucker were secondary.
- Three species occurred only in side streams: mountain sucker, pearl dace and lake chub.
- Seven species were absent from the North Saskatchewan: sturgeon, silver redhorse, sauger, emerald shiner, river shiner, yellow perch and troutperch. The South and Saskatchewan rivers retain these species, but the North has few shiners and chubs.

**Pollution**
- Tarzwell's (1952) categories: inert inorganic, putrescible, toxic, radioactive, and heat wastes. Radioactive and heat pollution were not known to be problems.
- Red clay from Red Deer River badlands and storm-sewer silt turbidify the rivers.
- North Saskatchewan: domestic sewage and industrial wastes from Edmonton produce gross pollution. Winter oxygen is near nil, and the fish community suggests fish either die or migrate.
- Prince Albert discharges raw sewage; a sludge bed and sulphide-coated rocks extended about 15 miles to Cecil Ferry.
- Saskatoon discharges untreated sewage through the 23rd Street storm sewer and the main outfall. Oxygen at Hague fell 0.9 c.c./l relative to Yorath Island, about 16,000 lb of oxygen removed.
- Ice-covered winter oxygen at Forks: North 1.1 c.c./l; South 7.7 c.c./l (February 12, 1957).

## Key Findings

1. **Flow regime:** Flow varies from year to year and season to season; high water may exceed low water by about 100-fold. Two flood periods normally occur (spring and summer).
2. **Thermal regime:** Vernal warming and autumnal cooling are rapid. Surface temperatures stayed in the 19–22°C range from late June through August.
3. **Water chemistry:** Winter ice cover increases bicarbonate, chloride and hardness, lowers pH, and sharply reduces dissolved oxygen in the North.
4. **Plankton:** Populations are low but not excessive compared with other large, turbid rivers.
5. **Bottom fauna:** Standing crops are low, comparable to the least productive lakes. Riffles are the most productive habitat.
6. **Fish community:** Twenty-four species were recorded. Goldeye is the dominant gill-net species in all three rivers.
7. **Catch rates:** Three-inch mesh gill nets caught 16.6 fish (20.5 lb) per 50 yards in the North and 13.5 fish (17.0 lb) in the South.
8. **Sport fishing:** A substantial but unmeasured angling fishery exists. Goldeye and pike are the preferred species. Saskatoon has the heaviest pressure; an estimate of 8,000 lb per 100 days was proposed as a low-end harvest.
9. **Domestic gill-netting:** In 1958, 189 licensees were contacted and about 82 per cent fished. The average was 44 game fish per licence. The catch did not exceed angling limits on a daily basis.
10. **Goldeye biology:** River goldeye are longer and heavier at a given age than Cumberland Lake fish. River populations include older age classes (up to ten years). Downstream migration was indicated by tag returns (110 miles in four days).
11. **Pollution status:** The North Saskatchewan is grossly polluted before entering the province. The South Saskatchewan is relatively unpolluted on entry but is affected by Saskatoon wastes under ice cover.
12. **Management concern:** The report warns that uncontrolled waste release at Saskatoon could imperil the angling resource from the forks to the Manitoba boundary.
