# Fish Monitoring of Selected Waters of the Gila River Basin, 1995–1996

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This March 1998 report by [[Robert W. Clarkson]] of the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] presents fish monitoring results for 1995 and 1996 from canals and streams in the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona. The monitoring was required by a 1994 [[Biological Opinion]] for the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The opinion found that CAP water could transfer nonindigenous fishes from the Lower Colorado River into Gila Basin drainages, threatening four listed species: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The report documents species richness, distribution and general assemblage structure across six target reaches. It also identifies serious gaps in methodology, data quality and management response.

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory basis:** The Biological Opinion's Reasonable and Prudent Alternative directed Reclamation, with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), to develop a baseline study and long-term monitoring of non-native fish in the CAP aqueduct and selected river and canal reaches.
- **Goal:** Establish baseline data on the presence and distribution of nonnative fishes and detect changes in species composition or distribution.
- **Target reaches:** (1) CAP aqueduct; (2) [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) canals; (3) [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]] (FCG); (4) [[Salt River]] between Stewart Mountain and Granite Reef dams; (5) [[Gila River]] between Coolidge and Ashurst-Hayden dams; (6) [[San Pedro River]] downstream from the U.S.–Mexico border.
- **Plan history:** Monitoring began in October 1994 without a finalized plan. A draft plan was submitted in July 1995 and revised in June 1996, with the most recent revision in October 1996. The report notes that the plan may never be final because methods will keep evolving.
- **Methods:** Electrofishing was the primary gear. Other gears used included gill, trammel, drift and hoop nets, minnow traps, seines, dip nets, trot lines and angling. Canal sampling was opportunistic during low flow or dry-up, with fixed stations only on the CAP canal. Pumping plant forebays were the main CAP sampling sites.
- **Data handling:** Data were entered in Key Entry software, not the custom dBASE program specified in the plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nonindigenous species pathway.** CAP canals and SRP canals act as conduits for fishes from the Lower Colorado and Salt and Verde systems. Canal species assemblages reflect the source streams and canal management. Grass carp were introduced into SRP canals for vegetation control. Canal drying and the use of electrical fish barriers strongly shape which fishes persist.

**Dry-up and barrier effects.** SRP canals are dried for maintenance, and electrical barriers (see [[Electrical Fish Barrier]]) are operated at canal weirs. The author observed several thousand fish, apparently [[Tilapia]], descend over the South Canal weir after barrier shutdown in 1997. Small desert suckers and possibly red shiners were also seen moving upstream past the barrier. Fish can ascend weirs when water is about 8 cm deep or less, so barriers do not fully stop movement.

**Flood regime and red shiner invasions.** On the San Pedro River, red shiner (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) disappeared after a flood of more than 11,000 cfs in 1993. It was absent from lower San Pedro collections until 1995–1996. The species invaded [[Aravaipa Creek]] in 1990 and again in 1997. Discharge records (1985–1991) show no flows above roughly 1,500 cfs before a 3,000+ cfs flood in February 1991. The report links these invasions to long flood-free periods.

**Habitat condition.** The upper Gila River near Coolidge Dam has high conductivity (near 3,000 µS/cm), probably from a warm spring below the dam. Several Gila River sites were not sampled because of right-of-entry problems. At the San Pedro River's Hughes Ranch site, drying in summer 1996 likely caused the near absence of fish.

## Key Findings

**Species richness by reach (1995–1996):**

| Reach | Result |
|---|---|
| CAP canal (upper reaches) | 6 species at Bouse Hills, 4 at Harquahala, 7 at Salt-Gila pumping plants |
| CAP canal (Tucson Aqueduct, lower) | 13 species; max 8 at one station |
| Gila River (Coolidge–Ashurst-Hayden) | 10 species in 1995 (max 7 per station, min 4); 8 at Cochran in 1996 |
| San Pedro River | 9 species in 1995 across 8 stations; 11 in 1996 (2 nonnatives added) |
| FCG Canal | 5 species in 1995; 12 in 1996 |
| Salt River (Stewart Mountain–Granite Reef) | 13 species in 1995; 11 in 1996 |
| Arizona Canal (SRP, barrier reach) | 13 in 1995; 15 in 1996 |
| South Canal (SRP, barrier reach) | 13 in 1995; 10 in 1996 |

- **Canal totals:** 19 species recorded from the Arizona and South canals in 1990–1994 (17 from each).
- **New records:** CAP canal: [[Black Bullhead]] and [[White Bass]]. FCG Canal: [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Threadfin Shad]]. Salt River: [[Black Crappie]], mosquitofish and [[Smallmouth Bass]]. Common carp and goldfish were also new or newly confirmed in canals, and six goldfish were captured at San Xavier in 1995.
- **Absences:** In 1995, the CAP canal yielded no [[Razorback Sucker]], desert sucker, Sonora sucker, black crappie, fathead minnow or mosquitofish, although earlier surveys considered several of these rare or present.
- **Dominant species:** [[Longfin Dace]] was the numerically dominant San Pedro species in 1995 and 1996, with extremely high densities at lower stations. Native suckers dominated FCG Canal collections in 1995. Red shiner and mosquitofish dominated FCG catches in 1991–1994. Desert sucker was most numerous in the Salt River. Channel catfish and common carp dominated upper CAP catches.
- **Assemblage variability:** Dominance shifted markedly between years in the South Canal. Examples include shifts from red shiner to suckers, then to common carp and desert sucker, and later to tilapia.
- **Nonindigenous threats:** Grass carp, tilapia, red shiner, common carp and channel catfish were the main nonnative concerns. Striped and white bass were not yet established in the lowermost CAP segments but were expected to become established.

**Data quality limitations:**
- Standard field data (electrical settings, conductance, temperature, field notes, station maps and voucher specimens) were often omitted.
- Relative abundance was not computed. Only general conclusions on assemblage structure are presented.
- Sampling bias among gear types and year-to-year effort differences prevent direct comparisons.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- Early monitoring has already revealed new records, red shiner invasion patterns and large interannual assemblage variation.
- The most serious deficiency is the failure to develop a **management action plan**. This plan would define threshold criteria for species richness, distribution and assemblage indices, and would set contingency actions to return parameters to acceptable ranges.
- Without such a plan, monitoring becomes "mere surveillance and recording of events." Agencies must commit to action if native fish conservation is to succeed in the Gila River Basin.
