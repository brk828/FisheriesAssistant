# Water Quality Requirements of the Family Elmidae (Coleoptera)

#entomology #freshwater-ecology #elmidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 technical report by [[Ralph M. Sinclair]] of the [[Tennessee Stream Pollution Control Board]] (Tennessee Department of Public Health) assesses the water quality tolerances of the [[Riffle Beetles]] family [[Elmidae]]. It challenges the long-held view that elmids are a strict "clean-water" group. Field studies in Tennessee show that several species tolerate a wide range of effluents and can persist in poorly aerated sections of polluted streams. The report provides illustrated flow-chart keys to the larvae and adults of the eastern genera, summarizes each genus's response to specific pollutants, and identifies [[Stenelmis Sexlineata]] as the most resistant species.

## Background & Description

The report states that the sensitivity of Elmidae to sewage and industrial wastes, reported by several earlier investigators, was not confirmed by Tennessee Stream Pollution Control Board or [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] studies. It quotes [[Frank N. Young]] describing elmids as "extremely sensitive to even mild pollution," and cites [[Arden H. Gaufin]], who found [[Stenelmis crenata]] and [[Stenelmis sexlineata]] only in the cleanest Ohio streams. The author concludes that the clean-water classification holds for some species but not others.

**Morphology and life history**
- Both larval and adult stages are aquatic. [[Ancyronyx variegatus]] is wingless, [[Macronychus glabratus]] has vestigial wings, and [[Gonielmis dietrichi]] includes winged and wingless individuals. Winged forms of some species are attracted to lights.
- Adult body length ranges from about 1 mm in [[Limnius latiusculus]] to 3.5 mm in Macronychus glabratus. Their small size and habit of clinging to substrate make them easy to overlook.
- Dense spines, setae and tomentum make specimens difficult to handle. Trash must be cleaned from specimens with a closely trimmed camel's hair brush before identification.
- Larval identification relies on the terminal abdominal segment, which carries three retractile gill tufts and two hooks. In [[Dubiraphia]] this segment and its gills are notably elongated.

**Identification**
- The report uses diagnostic characters of the head, pronotum, prosternal leg, elytra, abdominal sternites and male aedeagus for adults, and of the frons, prothorax, meso- and metathorax, abdominal tergites and terminal segment for larvae.
- Adult and larval keys are provided for the eastern genera. Genus-level larval identification is possible for most taxa, but [[Stenelmis]] species had not been separated as larvae.
- Species-level Stenelmis adult identification follows [[Milton W. Sanderson]]'s 1938 monograph. Sanderson's technique of association and elimination is cited as the basis for a workable larval key.

**Genera and species covered (summary)**
- [[Ancyronyx variegatus]]: adults with red markings, a basal tooth on the tarsal claw and widely separated coxae; larvae with a stiff lateral fringe on the prothorax.
- [[Stenelmis]]: about 17 species; keyed mainly by elytral pattern and male aedeagus.
- [[Microcylloepus pusillus]]: four varieties (pusillus, aptus, perditus, lodingi) differing in elytral spotting and distribution.
- [[Macronychus glabratus]]: seven-segmented antennae, six abdominal pleura in larvae.
- [[Limnius latiusculus]]: three-segmented maxillary palpus; short, broad larval pleurites.
- [[Dubiraphia]]: two species (quadrinotata and vittata); larvae with an elongated terminal segment.
- [[Gonielmis dietrichi]]: distinctive adult elytral pattern; larvae with two dark marks per thoracic tergite.
- [[Optioservus]]: about five to six eastern species.
- [[Promoresia]]: three species (elegans, tardella and one other); larvae with humped abdominal tergites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution in Tennessee**
- West Tennessee streams drain to the [[Mississippi River]] over sand bottoms and muddy banks in the East Gulf Coastal Plain. Typical elmids there are [[Ancyronyx variegatus]] and [[Macronychus glabratus]], with occasional [[Dubiraphia]] and [[Stenelmis]].
- Middle and East Tennessee support a more varied fauna, including several Stenelmis species, Macronychus, Ancyronyx, [[Optioservus]], [[Gonielmis dietrichi]], [[Microcylloepus pusillus]] and Dubiraphia.
- The [[Clinch River]], [[Holston River]], [[Watauga River]] and [[Nolichucky River]] in East Tennessee have faunas similar to middle Tennessee.
- The lower [[Cumberland River]] and lower [[Tennessee River]] apparently lack an elmid component.
- [[Promoresia]] occurs in cooler small streams. [[Limnius latiusculus]] is largely confined to small, cold Appalachian streams, with records from the Alabama limestone lowlands.

**Dissolved oxygen**
- Elmids depend on dissolved oxygen. Adults remain submerged indefinitely, using a hydrofuge system of silvery tomentum that collects oxygen bubbles for the spiracles or stores them under the elytra. They do not need to surface.
- Larvae respire through retractile gill tufts. Elmids are excluded from septic reaches, although some species tolerate low oxygen.
- Dubiraphia larvae, with their elongated caudal filaments and gill chambers, are considered better adapted to lakes and slow rivers.

**pH**
- The [[Duck River]] has a varied elmid fauna and consistently high summer pH. Middle and lower sections average over 8.0 and sometimes reach higher values. Low-pH streams were not checked for elmids.

## Key Findings

**Pollutant responses by taxon** (summarized from the Correlation of Stream Conditions section)
- [[Macronychus glabratus]]: sensitive to sewage and industrial wastes, including plating, textile and viscose rayon effluents.
- [[Ancyronyx variegatus]]: sensitive to sewage and industrial wastes.
- [[Microcylloepus pusillus]]: sensitive to sewage and plating wastes. Absent for 20 stream miles below a plating effluent. Numbers fell from 67 to 2 per three-square-foot [[Surber Sampling]] sample below a rayon plant. Tolerant of [[Mica Mining]] wastes, including turbidity and siltation.
- [[Gonielmis dietrichi]]: absent for about 63 stream miles below a Kraft mill. Apparently unaffected by moderate organic enrichment, mica mining siltation or [[Phosphate Tailings]] turbidity.
- [[Dubiraphia]]: found in the recovery zone below sewage treatment plants. Absent from the North Fork Holston River down to mile 9.2, where chlorides may exceed 800 mg/L, then reappeared in 1962 at mile 4.6 alongside a varied elmid fauna.
- [[Promoresia]]: very sensitive to Kraft paper mill and viscose rayon wastes.
- [[Optioservus]]: common in the recovery zone of a stream receiving treated sewage, and present on the North Fork Holston River where chlorides exceed 800 mg/L.
- [[Stenelmis sexlineata]]: resistant to moderate sewage and phosphate tailing wastes. Ubiquitous in the Duck River despite past untreated sewage and elemental phosphorus pollution. Described as possibly the most resistant species in Tennessee.
- [[Stenelmis crenata]]: much more sensitive than S. sexlineata to sewage and phosphate wastes, but resistant to high chlorides.
- [[Stenelmis decorata]]: resistant to sewage and phosphate tailing wastes.

**Chlorides**
- On the North Fork Holston River, which carries high chloride loads from a soda ash plant, several elmid species showed notable tolerance. Table 2 lists average chloride values of 2,400 ppm and 825 ppm at the stations sampled, and records species presence at each site. Tolerant taxa included Stenelmis crenata, [[Stenelmis bicarinata]], Optioservus species, Dubiraphia, [[Microcylloepus pusillus]] and [[Macronychus glabratus]].

**Dissolved oxygen (Harpeth River, September 9–13, 1963, low-flow season)**
- At stations below a sewage treatment plant and chicken-processing wastes, average-maximum-minimum D.O. was 6.2–6.9–5.7 mg/L above the outfall, 3.0–5.1–1.4 mg/L at the U.S. Highway 431 station, and 4.6–5.5–4.0 mg/L above the mouth of the West Harpeth River. Stenelmis sexlineata and Dubiraphia vittata were abundant across the stations, and [[Stenelmis beameri]] was common at the upper and lower stations.

**Particulate matter and mining**
- Phosphate ore processing wastes (elemental phosphorus) severely suppressed the Duck River elmid fauna, with the exception of Stenelmis sexlineata. These wastes cause turbidity and siltation, and elemental phosphorus reached the river.
- [[Mica Mining]] wastes entering the Nolichucky River had little apparent effect on [[Gonielmis dietrichi]] or Microcylloepus pusillus. Siltation became severe enough that mica was dredged from deposits in [[Davy Crockett Reservoir]].

**Pre-impoundment records**
- Elmid samples were taken from reservoir areas in East Tennessee before impoundment, using Petersen and Surber samplers, drawing on data from [[F. Earle Lyman]] and [[Jack S. Dendy]] in the [[Watts Bar Reservoir]] and [[Cherokee Reservoir]] studies. Table 3 lists rare (R) and common (C) records by station, and reports ammonia values of 0.152 ppm and 6.60 ppm at two sites. Surber counts of 109.9, 104.3 and 65.1 are associated with mesosaprobic and oligosaprobic conditions.
- The elmid fauna recorded on the Holston River in 1941 closely resembled that found by Tennessee Stream Pollution Control Board biologists on the lower North Holston River in 1962. Riffle beetles were not found in samples from the upper Holston River at Churchill, and were common again near the mouth of Flat Creek at Mascot.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Elmids are not uniformly clean-water indicators. Their tolerance differs by species, so a detailed understanding of each species' requirements would allow finer assessment of stream conditions and of the benthic community.
- Because pre-pollution faunal records are scarce, the author recommends using elmids as water quality parameters, tracking the reappearance of fauna as conditions improve.
- Key identification characters for larvae and adults are still needed, especially for separating Stenelmis larvae to species.
