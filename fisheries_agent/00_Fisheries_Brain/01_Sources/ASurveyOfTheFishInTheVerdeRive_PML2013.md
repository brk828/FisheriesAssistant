# A Survey of the Fish in the Verde River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This student report from the Spring Semester of 1975 documents a one-semester survey of fishes in the [[Verde River]] drainage of Arizona. Seventeen taxa, including two subspecies of [[Roundtail Chub]], were collected with gill nets, bag seines, and small-mesh seines, then preserved and identified. Lengths and weights of three native species were compared across sampling sites. The author found no significant size differences among sites. The report also compares the current catch with the more than 20 species previously reported by [[Minckley]] (1973), notes that about ten of those species were not collected, and discusses hybridization, competition, parasitism, and drought as possible causes of decline. The author states that one semester is too short for publication and plans to continue sampling.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** Earlier work on Arizona fishes by [[Miller]] and [[Hubbs]] and later by Minckley and Rinne had sampled the Verde system, but no thorough survey of the drainage existed. The author framed the study as a check on previously reported species and as a direct comparison with Minckley's results. Sampling was limited to one semester, so the author did not consider the work publishable.

**Methods.**
- **Gear:** 150 ft gill nets, 6 x 20 ft bag seines, 4 x 20 ft bag seines, and 4 x 20 ft small-mesh seines (see [[Gill Net]] and [[Seine Net]]).
- **Preservation:** Specimens were preserved in 10–15% [[Formalin Preservation]]. Endangered species were measured and released, and are reported without being preserved.
- **Measurements:** Total and standard lengths and weights were taken with a simple balance and a meter stick.
- **Sites:** Thirteen sites were sampled across the Verde River and its tributaries. On the Verde these were Perkinsville, Clarkdale, Camp Verde, 10 miles south of Cottonwood, and 1 mile north of Sycamore Creek. Fossil Creek was sampled at three sites (Stehr Lake, 5 miles north of Irving, and 4 miles south of Irving). [[Oak Creek]] was sampled at two sites (Cornville and Northfork). Single sites were sampled on Dry Beaver Creek, Wet Beaver Creek ([[Beaver Creek]]), and [[Clear Creek]].

**Taxa collected.** The 17 taxa are listed below, grouped by family. Descriptions and localities appear in the original report.

- **Cyprinidae (minnows and chubs):** [[Carp]] (Cyprinus carpio), [[Roundtail Chub]] (Gila robusta robusta and G. r. grahami), [[Spikedace]] (Meda fulgida; endangered and released), [[Speckled Dace]] (Rhinichthys osculus), and [[Red Shiner]] (Notropis lutrensis).
- **Catostomidae (suckers):** [[Sonora Sucker]] (Catostomus insignis), [[Desert Sucker]] (Catostomus clarkii), and one undetermined Catostomus sp.
- **Ictaluridae (catfish):** [[Channel Catfish]] (Ictalurus punctatus) and [[Yellow Bullhead]] (Ictalurus natalis, as listed).
- **Poeciliidae (livebearers):** [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis) and [[Gila Topminnow]] (Poeciliopsis occidentalis).
- **Centrarchidae (sunfishes and basses):** [[Green Sunfish]] (Lepomis cyanellus), [[Bluegill]] (Lepomis macrochirus), [[Redear Sunfish]] (Lepomis microlophus), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (Micropterus salmoides).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Verde River system has been altered by introduced species, hybridization, parasites, and reduced flows. The report describes the following pressures:

- **Hybridization:** Hybridization has reduced populations of several native species. The Gila trout (Salmo gilae) was once abundant in Oak Creek. Extensive hybridization with rainbow trout has left pure strains only in Arizona hatcheries. Spotted bass (Micropterus punctulatus) have declined through hybridization with smallmouth bass (M. dolomieu), and the report says this has nearly caused the species' extinction from the Verde. See [[Hybridization]].
- **Competition:** Red Shiner reproduces faster than Spikedace. The report states that Red Shiner is now the most numerous fish in the Verde, while Spikedace is almost extinct from the system. The Spikedace specimen from near Clarkdale was the first collected in northern Arizona since 1972.
- **Parasitism:** The copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] (Lernaea cyprinacea) is a parasite introduced to the region. Wilson et al. (1966) reported that it causes drastic population declines in roundtail chub. Bartoszek reported that a 475 g chub in the Verde may carry many copepods. Minckley reported that both roundtail chub and smallmouth bass are susceptible to heavy infestation, and that smallmouth bass numbers have dropped since the parasite's introduction. The report notes that no smallmouth bass were collected in this study. See [[Parasitism]].
- **Drought:** The [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus) was once abundant in the Verde and made mass migrations. It has not been seen there since 1954. The author suggests that drought reduced flows in the Salt and Gila rivers, making the Verde too shallow for the species, which requires water deeper than about 1 m. See [[Drought]].

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** Seventeen taxa, including two roundtail chub subspecies, were collected. The report states that this is fewer than the more than 20 species previously reported by Minckley (1973).
- **Species not collected:** Minckley's list includes species not found in this survey, among them [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Gila Chub]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], and Razorback Sucker. The author attributes the gap to hybridization, competition, drought, and parasitism, and states that a longer study may add species to the list.
- **Size comparisons:** Lengths and weights of Roundtail Chub, Desert Sucker, and Sonora Sucker were compared across sites.
  - Roundtail Chub at Clarkdale reached 420 mm and 415 g. A chub at Perkinsville weighed 400 g, and the largest at Camp Verde weighed about 325 g.
  - Most chubs weighed roughly 200 g or more across the sites.
  - Desert Sucker and Sonora Sucker showed similar size ranges across sites. The largest Sonora Sucker measured at Camp Verde.
- **Main conclusion:** The author found no significant size differences among sites for the three native species, and concluded that these fishes can survive equally well throughout the Verde River.
- **Limitations and next steps:** The author notes that this single-semester survey is too short for publication, and plans to continue sampling the drainage for at least one more year to permit direct comparison with Minckley's data.
- **Reference points:** Key sources cited include Minckley (1973), *The Fishes of Arizona*; Rinne (1969), a thesis on cyprinid fishes of the Colorado River basin; and Wilson, Deacon, and Bradley (1966), on parasitism in fishes of the Moapa River, Nevada.
