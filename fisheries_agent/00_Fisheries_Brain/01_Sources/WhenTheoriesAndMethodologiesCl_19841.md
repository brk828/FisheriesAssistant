# When Theories and Methodologies Clash: A Phylogenetic Reanalysis of North American Ambystomatid Salamanders

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ambystomatidae

## Executive Summary

This is a critical commentary and reanalysis by Thomas R. Jones, Arnold G. Kluge, and Alan J. Wolf (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology). It responds to [[Shaffer Et Al. 1991]] (hereafter SCK), who studied the phylogeny of North American [[Ambystomatidae]] using [[Allozymes]] and morphology. The authors challenge SCK's use of [[Taxonomic Congruence]], their treatment of [[Character Independence]], their [[Bootstrap Resampling]] and [[Jackknife Resampling]] confidence claims, and their [[Outgroup Polarization]] method for allozymes. They argue for [[Total Evidence]] analysis instead. Their reanalysis of SCK's combined data yields a single best-fitting hypothesis (CI = 0.45) that preserves the monophyly of [[Linguaelapsus]] but differs in several species relationships.

## Background & Description

The paper is a theoretical and methodological critique rather than a field study. It addresses four issues:

- **Total evidence versus taxonomic congruence.** Total evidence analyzes an unpartitioned data set. Taxonomic congruence partitions data (here, allozymes and morphology), builds a cladogram from each partition, and then forms a consensus. The authors argue that consensus methods can be unresolved or even misleading (citing [[Barrett Et Al. 1991]]), and that partitioning rests on an artificial notion of "classes of evidence."
- **Character independence.** SCK suggested that clades supported by many synapomorphies are more likely to contain nonindependent characters. The authors argue that quality of evidence (homoplasy) is distinct from quantity of evidence (independence), and that quality should take precedence.
- **Resampling statistics.** The authors argue that the assumptions of bootstrap and jackknife resampling are violated in this context.
- **Outgroup treatment for allozymes.** SCK excluded alleles found in outgroups and rooted trees on a hypothetical all-zero ancestor. The authors regard this as a departure from accepted practice.

The study group is the ambystomatid salamanders, with [[Dicamptodon]] and [[Rhyacotriton]] as outgroups. The authors' comments also draw on examples from other taxa (unionid clams, ranid frogs, skinks, shrews, and others) to illustrate how coding and outgroup choices can change results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document contains no habitat, ecological, or water-chemistry data. Its ecological relevance lies in population-level sampling. The authors note that SCK's allozyme data came mostly from small samples from three or fewer populations per species, even for widespread species such as [[Ambystoma Gracile]] and [[Ambystoma Macrodactylum]]. Gene frequencies at the GAPDH locus illustrate geographic and temporal variability:

- SCK reported allele frequencies of 0.30 (allele 2) and 0.70 (allele 5) in [[Ambystoma Californiense]] from Contra Costa Co., California.
- Jones (1989), sampling within about 25 km of SCK's source, found frequencies of 0.50 at the same locus.
- Jones (1989) also found the GAPDH locus fixed for one allele in four other populations and fixed for the alternative allele in three others (sample sizes of 10 to 15 per population).

The authors argue that such geographic and temporal variation undermines the use of allozyme frequencies as phylogenetic characters.

## Key Findings

**1. Critique of taxonomic congruence**
- Consensus methods can yield unresolved hypotheses, and the more fundamental cladograms differ, the greater the loss of descriptive and explanatory power.
- Taxonomic congruence weights the data sets equally, so constituent characters are unequally weighted when data set sizes differ.
- Kluge and Wolf argue that subdividing evidence into "molecular" and "morphological" classes reflects tradition and technology, not natural classes of evidence.

**2. Saturation and allozyme rate classes**
- SCK divided their allozyme loci into rapid, medium, and slow classes (10-15, 7-8, and 4-6 alleles per locus). The authors argue this division is arbitrary and that polymorphism is not a sufficient measure of substitution rate.
- SCK's Fig. 2 showed a negative branch length, which the authors cite as evidence that the clock-like assumption does not always hold.

**3. Frequency data**
- The authors give three arguments against using allele frequencies as characters: frequencies are not heritable, small samples may not reflect geographically distant populations, and gene frequencies vary over time.

**4. Character independence**
- Simple correlation between characters does not establish nonindependence. Shaffer (1986) showed nonindependence among three cranial measurements in a paedomorphic form of [[Ambystoma Tigrinum]], but the authors argue this does not justify dismissing a whole suite of discrete characters.
- Reanalysis showed that the 147 informative noncranial characters outweigh the 13 cranial characters by more than 10,000 times to break the monophyly of Linguaelapsus. The authors conclude that the cranial characters do not unduly influence that clade.
- The authors contend that homoplasy concerns the quality of evidence, while independence concerns its quantity, and that quality has primacy.
- Some allozyme coding methods (e.g., [[Buth 1984]]; Swofford and Berlocher, 1987) assume nonindependence of alleles, which weakens SCK's assumption that allozymes are less vulnerable to nonindependence than morphology.

**5. Bootstrap and jackknife confidence**
- The authors argue that the assumptions of bootstrapping (independent and identically distributed characters, random character sampling, adequate sample size, and an algorithm that finds the best fit) are likely violated in this study. Characters are often selected deliberately or chosen for conservatism, and heuristic parsimony searches may miss the best-fitting tree.
- SCK's inconsistent interpretation is highlighted: the morphological clade Linguaelapsus appears in 96% of bootstrap samples, while the allozyme-based subclades (annulatum, cingulatum) and (barbouri, mabeei, texanum) have bootstrap support of only 45% and 48%. These values fall well below the 95% level recommended by [[Felsenstein 1985]]. The authors also note that SCK's number of replicates was only half the number required (Hedges, 1992).

**6. Outgroup polarization**
- SCK excluded "all alleles that occurred in any of the outgroups as well as the study group," coded the remaining alleles as 1s, and rooted the tree on a hypothetical all-zero ancestor.
- SCK inconsistently applied this criterion to several loci (e.g., sAAT, LDH-A, PP-2, MPI, GPI).
- The consistency index for informative characters was 0.38 under SCK's method and 0.36 with the three outgroups included, which the authors read as showing that absence of an allele is hardly less informative than its presence.
- Reanalyses of allozyme data sets from other studies (including Murphy et al., 1983; Hillis, 1985a, 1985b; Green, 1986; Hoeh, 1990; Zink and Avise, 1990) showed effects ranging from major topological shifts to no change. The authors conclude that the same distortion applies to any presence/absence coding, such as restriction site data.

**7. Reanalysis of SCK's combined data**
- The authors reanalyzed the combined allozyme and morphological data and accepted the best-fitting, completely resolved hypothesis (CI = 0.45, informative characters only). It closely matches SCK's Fig. 7.
- Consistency indexes were 0.38 for allozymes and 0.85 for morphology, indicating that the morphological data are more conservative.
- The reanalysis found that [[Ambystoma Texanum]] is paraphyletic, with [[Ambystoma Barbouri]] nested among western representatives. The authors suggest SCK's monophyletic depiction is likely an error.
- Correlations between alleles per locus and consistency indices were not significant, parametrically or nonparametrically, so the authors found no evidence of random homoplasy saturating the allozyme data.
- The monophyly of Linguaelapsus is preserved, consistent with [[Tihen 1958]] and [[Kraus 1988]]. Sever (1992) cloacal anatomy is cited as additional support.

**8. Overall conclusion**
- The authors argue that the best estimate of ambystomatid phylogeny comes from parsimony analysis of all available evidence, with inconsistencies arbitrated by character congruence. They also state that conflicts between molecular and morphological data are overstated, and that any data set can produce incongruent patterns.
- They conclude that discussion of "molecules and morphology clash" perpetuates the notion of classes of data and hinders evaluation of all evidence.
