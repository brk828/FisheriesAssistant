# Second Workshop on Ecology of Pumped Storage Research at Twin Lakes, Colorado (January 1975)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This workshop proceedings, held January 1975 in Denver, Colorado, reports progress on the environmental effects of [[Pumped-Storage Operation]] on aquatic systems. Three study sites represent different climates: the montane [[Twin Lakes]] in Colorado, the temperate [[Banks Lake]] in Washington, and the hot-desert [[Salt River Reservoirs]] in Arizona. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (USBR) leads the Twin Lakes work, which focuses on [[Lake Trout]] and their principal food, [[Mysis Shrimp]]. The workshop combines physical, chemical, biological, fisheries and modeling studies and sets a schedule of draft reports for 1975 to 1979. The stated aim is to make future pumped-storage planning faster and more accurate under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] of 1970.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and structure.** [[James F. LaBounty]], Principal Investigator of the Twin Lakes Ecological Study, introduced the workshop as the first step toward an annual meeting to keep stakeholders informed. Presenters came from USBR, the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit]], the [[University of Washington]], and [[Arizona State University]]. The agenda ran from 8:30 on 30 January 1975 to 4:00 p.m. and listed about 14 presentations, followed by a summary session. The proceedings include a list of about 50 attending persons and affiliations, mostly USBR staff, plus state and federal agencies.

**Twin Lakes setting.** Twin Lakes are glacial, montane lakes on Lake Creek at the eastern foot of the Sawatch Range in the upper Arkansas River Basin. Outflows have been regulated since about 1901, and inflows are augmented by a transmountain diversion from the Roaring Fork drainage. Surface areas and depths are 263 ha (650 acres) and 28 m (92 ft) for the upper lake, and 736.5 ha (1,820 acres) and 27.1 m (89 ft) for the lower lake. The maximum water surface elevation is 2802 m (9193 ft).

**Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant.** Marcotte reported that the [[Fryingpan-Arkansas Project]] was about 43 percent complete. The Mt. Elbert unit will have an initial nameplate capacity of 100,000 kW, with a second 100,000 kW unit about 3 years later. Pump-turbines will lift water from Twin Lakes into the Mt. Elbert Forebay during off-peak hours and generate during peak demand. Ultimate peaking capability is 211,000 kW, and the project is expected to be completed around 1983.

**Study design.** The Twin Lakes program covers chemistry, benthos, zooplankton, SCUBA observations, fish populations, primary productivity, physical and mathematical modeling, and instrumentation. Two additional sites, Banks Lake and the Salt River Reservoirs, provide comparison data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Twin Lakes physical and chemical conditions (May to November 1974).**
- Maximum surface temperatures were 14.4 °C in the upper lake and 16.1 °C in the lower lake. Maximum surface-to-bottom differences were 9.0 °C and 8.2 °C, respectively.
- Stratification peaked on July 22, about one month earlier than usual.
- Hypolimnetic dissolved oxygen minima were 3.6 ppm (upper lake, October 23) and 1.7 ppm (lower lake, September 24). Earlier summer minima were 5.0 ppm (1973) and 1.3 ppm (1972).
- Waters are soft and relatively pure. Calcium is the main cation and bicarbonate, followed by sulfate, is the main anion.
- Sediments contained significant copper, iron, lead, manganese, and zinc for the first time.

**Benthos.** [[Glacial Flour]] bottom sediment hosts midge larvae (Chironomidae, a fish food) and sphaeriid clams. Midge larvae numbered 1,700 to 2,800 per m² in the deepest lower lake and 3 to 12 per m² in the upper lake during the 1974 ice-free season. The upper lake is suspected to be affected by heavy-metal inflow from Lake Fork. Clams (4–5 mm) averaged 50 to 217 per m² in the lower lake and were never found in the upper lake.

**Zooplankton.** Apart from introduced Mysis shrimp, copepods are the only main zooplankton group now present (Cyclops and Diaptomus). Formerly abundant species may have been "cropped" off by Mysis. In July 1974, copepods numbered 18 to 24 per liter in the lower lake and 6 to 12 per liter in the upper lake.

**SCUBA observations (June 10–13 and August 26–29, 1974).**
- Glacial flour is very sensitive to disturbance; fin motion four feet above the bottom could cause turbidity within 10 feet of the bottom.
- Light penetration was about 45 to 50 feet (euphotic zone) in the lower lake.
- Pondweed (*Potamogeton*) grew from 3 to 6 feet in June to 8 to 12 feet in August.
- Mysis were mostly on the bottom below 10 feet during the day and migrated upward at night.
- Fish observed included rainbow trout, mackinaw, smelt and fry. More fish were seen on the night dive than all daytime dives combined.

**Mysis shrimp.** [[Mysis Relicta]] was introduced as a food source for lake trout in a previously unoccupied niche. Shrimp from Twin Lakes have been transplanted to many other lakes. Studies cover toxicity of glacial flour, feeding, life history, and diel vertical migration. Trawl densities rose from 11 per m² in May to 71 per m² in September, then fell to 50 per m² in November. Deep-water shrimp rose from 9 to 93 per m² between May and September.

**Lake trout.** Twin Lakes is a known lake trout fishery. Spawning is believed to occur along the south shore, at Rocky Point, and on the southeast shore, during late October and early November. Mortality was preliminarily estimated at 0.5 from 1971 to 1973 gill-net catches. Lake trout mostly eat Mysis; in 1974, 63 percent of stomachs contained Mysis. The catch in gill nets averaged 5 fish per net in the lower lake and 3 per net in the upper lake. Mean length fell from 15.2 inches in May to 14.6 inches in August and 13.2 inches in November.

**Fishing pressure and harvest.** Use was about 80,000 angler-hours over the ice-free months, down 20,000 hours from 1972. Stocking of half as many catchable rainbows contributed to the drop. Marked rainbows gave a 38 percent return. A total of 915 lake trout were caught, and 105 were caught below the dam during May and June irrigation drawdown, when lake trout escape through the outlet. Eighteen of these exceeded 30 inches.

**Banks Lake (Washington).** Banks Lake is the equalizing reservoir for the Columbia Basin Irrigation Project and a major sport fishery. Six pump-generating units at the Grand Coulee complex pump water to Franklin D. Roosevelt Lake from October to March. The lake remains largely homothermous in winter, reaches above 22 °C in August, and has specific conductivity of 80–100 µmho/cm and pH between 7.0 and 8.5. Eighteen fish species were identified, led by yellow perch, lake whitefish, kokanee and largemouth bass. Entrainment studies must handle canal velocities up to 10 ft/s.

**Salt River Reservoirs (Arizona).** Canyon and Apache Lakes were the first on-channel, pumped-storage systems in a North American hot-desert regime, with pump-turbines at both ends of one reservoir. Mormon Flat pumped on 43 days in 1971 and 250 days in 1974. Annual volumes were 6,048 ha-m in 1971 and 59,383 ha-m in 1974. In June 1974, pumping destroyed thermal stratification within 300 m of the dam. Canyon Lake maximum surface temperatures fell from 29 °C (1971–72) to under 25 °C (1974). Hypolimnetic minima ranged from 8 °C to 10.4 °C. The first observed inverse stratification occurred in October–December 1974. Threadfin shad, carp, channel catfish and walleye were most affected by fish entrainment.

**Physical and mathematical modeling.** Physical models showed that cold water (about 5 °C) pumped into a 14 °C lake creates stratification in about 2 weeks with one-unit operation or 1 week with two-unit operation. Mathematical simulations showed that forebay warming had little effect, but a diffusion coefficient six times the original value kept the lake nearly isothermal through the ice-free season.

## Key Findings

- **Lake trout and Mysis.** Lake trout rely heavily on Mysis, so changes that affect shrimp (currents, turbidity) could cascade to the trout fishery.
- **Low productivity.** Mean primary productivity was 78 µg C/m³/hr in the upper lake and 127 µg C/m³/hr in the lower lake. These values suggest oligotrophic conditions comparable to [[Lake Tahoe]]. Statistical tests did not find reliable relationships among productivity methods or physical parameters.
- **Thermal and oxygen effects.** Pumping mixes stratification within an hour, and generation releases cold water, with lasting thermal and oxygen effects documented at Salt River.
- **Fish mortality.** Highest entrainment mortality occurs in the first minutes of pumping; the Salt River data point to moribund fish carried to the surface throughout the cycle.
- **Sensitive sediments.** Glacial flour is easily disturbed, and reflow and channel design at the Mt. Elbert plant affect the movement of flour, based on movable-bed model tests.
- **Data gaps.** Recruitment and population estimates for lake trout remain uncertain. Mark-recapture was discontinued in 1974 after high gill-net mortality, and winter tagging reached about 130 of a 500-fish target.
- **Proposed reports.** Draft dates include pre-operation limnology (May 1975), pre-operation biology (May 1976), heat budget (May 1976), post-operation conditions (April 1979), and a comparative synthesis of all three lakes (September 1979).
