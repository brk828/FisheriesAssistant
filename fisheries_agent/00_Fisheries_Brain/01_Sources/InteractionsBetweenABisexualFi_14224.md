# Interactions Between a Bisexual Fish Species and Its Gynogenetic Sexual Parasite

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Bulletin of the Texas Memorial Museum paper by [[Clark Hubbs]] (1964) examines the reproductive interaction between the all-female [[Amazon Molly]] (*Mollienesia formosa*) and its bisexual host species, the [[Sailfin Molly]] (*M. latipinna*), in southern Texas. The amazon molly reproduces by [[Gynogenesis]]: it requires copulation with sailfin or related males to trigger embryonic development, but the offspring show no functional paternal inheritance. The study rests on roughly 30,000 preserved mollies (about 500,000 eggs) collected monthly from February 1960 to September 1961, plus more than 1,000 live fish tested in the laboratory. Hubbs concludes that the amazon molly's reproductive potential is roughly equal to the sailfin's, but that sailfins reach their potential more fully. Sailfin males court sailfin females preferentially, and this preference, together with differences in pregnancy and partial pregnancy, reduces amazon recruitment.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The amazon molly is thought to be of hybrid origin, derived from *M. latipinna* and a species of the *M. sphenops* group (most likely [[Mollienesia Mexicana]]). Hubbs notes that its electrophoretic muscle patterns are intermediate between these parents (Hewitt et al., 1963), and he suspects it is an allodiploid. Chromosome counts fall in the range 44 to 46 for the genus, and the Appendix by [[George E. Drewry]] estimates 2n = 46 for *M. latipinna* and an uncertain count for *M. formosa*.

**Phenotypic males.** Among the amazon mollies, 31 phenotypic males are catalogued in Table II. Most are genetic females with variably developed male traits. Many have aberrant gonopodia, and one (Male 7) proved to be an ovatestis. Only one male, possibly functional, is known from the Brownsville area, and its fertility is uncertain. Nine apparently normal males tested with virgin females produced no young.

**Sampling.** Collections were made at four stations with a 10-foot minnow seine and preserved in 10 per cent formalin:
- [[Lula Sams State Fish Hatchery]] (Station 1, Olmito, Cameron County)
- [[Central Avenue Ditch]] (Station 2, Brownsville)
- [[Rockport Pond]] (Station 3, six miles north of Rockport, Live Oak Peninsula)
- [[San Marcos River]] (Station 4), where amazon mollies were introduced in 1955 and are not native.

Hurricane Carla (September 1961) flooded the sites and altered habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and feeding.** Both mollies live in shallow, quiet water and feed mainly on [[Periphyton]]. Sailfins are more common among emergent vegetation and in cooler water, but Hubbs estimates the density difference at no more than 10 per cent. Sailfins can also tolerate more saline water, a habitat not sampled.

**Predation and competition.** [[Largemouth Bass]] are abundant at Lula Sams, where adult molly numbers were lowest and two-year-old amazons were scarce. Several other predators occur at the other sites. Hubbs suggests that food shortage may limit fecundity in both species, and that they reduce each other's food supply.

**Temperature and season.** Breeding runs from spring to fall, and temperature largely controls its start. The first young of 1961 appeared earlier than those of 1960 after a milder winter. Hubbs links cold exposure during development to masculinization of amazon mollies, although this is based on very small samples.

**Sperm and sex ratio.** Brownsville sailfin populations are strongly female-biased, which Hubbs attributes partly to the sympatric amazon molly. Sex ratio in *M. latipinna* differed significantly between sites, and laboratory-reared broods confirmed a genetic basis.

## Key Findings

**Sex ratio (sailfin molly)**
- All fish, Rockport (N = 5990): 31.95% male. Brownsville (N = 3826): 12.68% male. χ² = 469.88.
- Adult fish, Rockport: 38.79% male. Brownsville: 26.37% male.
- Differences persisted when analyzed by year and when immature fish were excluded.
- Cyprinodon variegatus, a control species, showed no comparable difference (44 to 60% males at Brownsville; 42 to 56% at Rockport).

**Courtship preference**
- Wild-caught males thrust at sailfin females 170 times and amazon females 55 times (24.4% toward amazons).
- Nibbles: 93 toward sailfins and 48 toward amazons (34.9% toward amazons). Both differences were significant (p < 0.01).
- Laboratory-reared males directed 25.0% of thrusts and 36.8% of nibbles toward amazons.
- Allopatric males (from outside the amazon range) discriminated less: amazons received 39.7% of thrusts and 38.4% of nibbles.

**Partial pregnancy**
- Partially pregnant females: 44.0% of pregnant amazons at Lula Sams, 36.5% at Central Avenue, 16.3% of Brownsville sailfins, and 11.8% of Rockport sailfins.
- Eggs with embryos among partially pregnant females: 59.9% (Lula Sams amazons), 57.4% (Central Avenue amazons), 68.2% (Brownsville sailfins), and 81.2% (Rockport sailfins).
- Hubbs attributes partial pregnancy to sperm depletion, since amazons are inseminated less often.

**Recruitment and productivity**
- In 1960 about 50% of Central Avenue sailfin eggs and 10% of amazon eggs contained embryos. At Lula Sams the figures were about 70% and 25%.
- July 1961, Lula Sams: 14 of 32 amazons produced broods (1:0.44); 24 of 31 sailfins did so (1:0.77).
- July 1961, Rockport: 23 of 33 sailfins produced broods (1:0.69).
- August 1961, Brownsville: 30 of 67 amazons (sailfins: 57 of 69).

**Superfoetation** was observed for the first time in *Mollienesia*. Six amazon females from July 1961 held eggs at two developmental stages, and two June 1961 females produced broods about a week apart (e.g., 84 young on June 28 and 66 on July 6).

**Egg counts and pregnancy.** Pregnant sailfins usually carry more eggs than nonpregnant females of equal size. Among amazons, nonpregnant females usually carry more eggs. Pregnancy frequency declines with size in amazons but not in sailfins, and reproductive senescence (decline in fecundity of the largest females) appears in amazons at Central Avenue.

**Masculinization.** Cold exposure of newborn amazon mollies (15 to 21°C vs 30°C) killed about 99% of the young. In each experiment one of three survivors was masculinized. Hubbs concludes that all males are probably genetic females with a variably developed male phenotype.

**Interpretation.** The breeding system includes two female types and one male type, all with the same chromosome number. Sailfin males are the "fathers" of amazon broods, but amazon females contribute no males. Hubbs argues that sailfin reproductive advantage must be maintained, or the three-way system would collapse. Sailfin males court sailfins first, then amazons, which keeps the amazons' recruitment in check.

**Limitations noted by the author.** Many size-class comparisons rest on very small samples. Hubbs states that he assumes full responsibility for the conclusions, and that the single functional male and the paternal inheritance question require further cytological study.
