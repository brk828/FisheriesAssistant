# Cutthroat Trout Biochemical-Genetic Assessment: Status and Systematics

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report, by [[Eric J. Loudenslager]] and [[Graham A. E. Gall]] (Department of Animal Science, University of California, Davis), uses [[Gel Electrophoresis]] of enzymes to assess the genetic structure, hybridization and taxonomy of [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*) and related western *Salmo*. The work began as a 1976 study of the origin of fine-spotted cutthroat in the [[Snake River]], Wyoming, and was expanded to sample over 2,000 trout from 78 locations across eight states and Mexico. Its stated audience is fisheries biologists, geneticists and the general public.

The main conclusions are that inland cutthroat subspecies are genetically distinct, that most variation lies among subspecies rather than among populations within them, that hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] ranges from none to complete, and that taxonomic recognition of some populations should be decided on systematic grounds rather than management convenience.

## Background & Description

The report was motivated by declines in native trout. Land use, dams, grazing, irrigation and introduced exotic species were identified as causes, and the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 prompted agencies to assess remaining native stocks. Funding came from the Nevada Department of Wildlife, the Wyoming Game and Fish Department and the California Department of Fish and Game (Dingell-Johnson Federal Aid to Fish Restoration), together with the University of Wyoming and the National Park Service.

Taxonomy of western *Salmo* has been contested. Miller (1950) grouped western trout into a cutthroat series and a rainbow series. Schreck and Behnke (1971) proposed three lineages, adding a "golden trout complex". Miller (1972) argued for a polyphyletic origin of the golden trout group. The report notes that the absence of diagnostic morphological characters and the lack of reproductive isolation complicate species definitions.

The methodological basis is that allozymes (alternative forms of enzymes encoded by different alleles) are codominant, Mendelian markers. Enzymes were scored at 36 loci using 19 enzyme systems, with [[Malate Dehydrogenase]] (MDH) and [[Peptidase]] (PEP) among the most informative. The authors stress that electrophoresis gives minimum estimates of genetic variation, because many DNA changes do not alter protein mobility.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Cutthroat and rainbow trout are largely allopatric but closely related. In coastal streams, ecological separation maintains species identity: coastal cutthroat favor low-gradient small streams, while rainbows favor large, steep streams. Inland cutthroat historically lacked contact with rainbows, but rainbow stocking for angling has created artificial secondary contact.

Geography shaped differentiation. Pleistocene glaciation and volcanism fragmented basins and drainages, and some were reconnected by pluvial lakes. The [[Lahontan Basin]] was once a large lake that repeatedly fragmented, and the [[Bonneville Basin]] received cutthroat by transfer from the upper Snake River after lava flows redirected the [[Bear River]]. Modern barriers are also measured: the barrier index (1 = total isolation, 0 = none) was 0.92 between the Lahontan and Bonneville basins, but only 0.32 between the upper Snake River and the Bonneville Basin.

Heterozygosity was low in inland cutthroat (mean 0.019) compared with rainbow trout (0.080). The authors suggest this reflects isolation above falls and in desiccating basins, but this explanation does not account for the higher variability of the desert-stream [[Snake Valley]] populations.

## Key Findings

**Sampling scope**
- Over 2,000 trout from 78 locations in Arizona, California, Idaho, Mexico, Montana, New Mexico, Nevada and Wyoming were analysed.
- The hybridization analysis covered 2,129 specimens from 78 populations representing 9 recognized taxa.
- The cutthroat analysis included 50 populations: 24 *henshawi*, 18 *bouvieri*, 4 *utah*, 2 *pleuriticus* and 2 *lewisi*.

**Genetic relationships among cutthroat subspecies** ([[Nei's Genetic Identity]], Table 3)
- Populations within subspecies had high identity, from 1.00 to 0.984.
- Divergent populations occurred within subspecies: Shoshone and Marysville creeks (*henshawi*) had identity 0.953 with the rest of the Lahontan populations (0.995 among themselves). Bear Creek (*bouvieri*) had identity 0.968 with other *bouvieri*.
- Identity between subspecies ranged from 0.969 (*utah*–*pleuriticus*) to 0.737 (*lewisi*–*utah*); the average among subspecies was 0.852.
- The *utah*–*bouvieri*–*pleuriticus* complex averaged 0.920. *Henshawi* averaged 0.880 with that complex, and *lewisi* was divergent at 0.779.
- Differences between subspecies exceeded differences between populations within subspecies.

**Three subspecies groups** ([[UPGMA Dendrogram]] and [[Wagner Network]])
- The inland complex divides into a Lahontan group (*henshawi*, with *seleniris* indistinguishable electrophoretically), an upper Snake–Colorado–Bonneville group (*utah*, *bouvieri*, *pleuriticus*, with *stomias* and *virginalis* possibly included), and an upper Columbia–Missouri group (*lewisi*).

**Chromosomal support** ([[Karyotype]])
- *Henshawi*, *utah*, *bouvieri* and *pleuriticus* share 2n=64, NF=104; *lewisi* has 2n=66, NF=104; coastal *clarki* has 2n=68–70, NF=104–106.
- The authors note that the relationship of the 64-chromosome and *lewisi* groups to coastal *clarki* remains unresolved.

**Snake River fine-spotted cutthroat**
- Two fine-spotted populations were compared with large-spotted Snake River cutthroat at 24 loci. No loci distinguished them, and identity was 0.995.
- Divergence was estimated at about 20,000 years ago, which places the origin in the post-glacial period.
- The authors recommend treating the fine-spotted form as part of a variable *S. c. bouvieri*, not as a new subspecies.

**Bonneville Basin populations** ([[Genetic Identity]], Table 4)
- Bear River populations averaged identity 0.997, and Snake Valley populations 0.984. Between the two groups the average was 0.888.
- Bear River populations were most similar to *bouvieri* (0.996), and Snake Valley populations to *pleuriticus* (0.969).
- The authors conclude that Bonneville cutthroat are polyphyletic, probably the result of multiple invasions.

**Taxonomic recommendations**
- The Snake Valley populations should retain the *utah* designation, because of morphological differences from *pleuriticus* and distinctive regional genetic polymorphisms.
- Bear River populations should be treated as a subgroup of *bouvieri*, because of high genetic identity and the absence of diagnostic morphological criteria.
- The authors argue that *utah* receives management priority as a dwindling resource, while *bouvieri* does not, and that management needs should be met through flexible policy rather than proliferating subspecific names.

**Hybridization between cutthroat and rainbow trout** ([[Hybridization]])
- Analysis used the [[Peptidase]] loci PEP-1 and PEP-3 and [[Malic Enzyme]] (ME) as diagnostic markers. CK-2 was dropped because heterozygotes were hard to identify, and IDH-3&4 because of high variability in rainbow broodstock.
- Of 22 populations examined, 12 showed no hybridization: nine contained only cutthroat and two only rainbow trout.
- The North Fork Humboldt River population showed no hybridization despite stocking of nearly 500,000 rainbows from 1900 to 1970.
- Long Canyon Creek, Nevada, held cutthroat and rainbows in nearly equal numbers with no hybrids. The last recorded rainbow plant was in 1947, which the authors cite as evidence of stable sympatry.
- At [[Wolf Lake]], Yellowstone, a self-sustaining cutthroat population was replaced by rainbows after stocking, and the lake now contains only rainbow trout.
- [[Introgression]] was found in Soda Butte Creek, Trout Creek (Utah) and Heenan Lake (California). No rainbow or F1 hybrids were observed, and backcross hybrids ranged from about 10% (Trout Creek) to 30% (Heenan Lake).
- [[Hybrid Swarm]]s occurred in lower Slough, Rose and Buffalo Fork creeks in Yellowstone, and in Silver King Creek, California. In these populations backcross hybrids dominated, which the authors treat as evidence that hybrids are fertile.
- Dunderberg and East Fork Desert creeks contained cutthroat and F1 hybrids but no backcrosses. The authors cannot tell whether these are recent hybridization or incompletely sampled swarms.
- Hybridization outcome appears to depend on ecological variables, not genetic incompatibility, since no prezygotic isolation was found. Behnke's model proposes that hybridization is limited in marginal habitats where native populations are adapted to harsh conditions.

**Genetic variation** ([[Heterozygosity]], Tables 6 and 7)
- Total diversity (HT), within-population diversity (HS), among-population diversity (DST) and the coefficient of gene differentiation (GST):

| Analysis | HT | HS | DST | GST |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *S. c. henshawi* | 0.027 | 0.016 | 0.011 | 0.41 |
| *S. c. bouvieri* | 0.022 | 0.016 | 0.006 | 0.27 |
| All *S. clarki* | 0.118 | 0.019 | 0.099 | 0.84 |

- About 80% of allelic heterogeneity in cutthroat lies among populations, and about 20% within them. Most differentiation is therefore among subspecies.
- Lahontan *henshawi* populations show significant allele-frequency heterogeneity at IDH-3, PH1-1, SDH-1 and SDH-2 (P<0.005).
- Mean within-population heterozygosity was 0.019 across 50 inland populations. Values ranged from zero to 0.075 (Pine Creek, *utah*). Subspecies means were *henshawi* 0.016, *bouvieri* 0.016, *utah* 0.063, *pleuriticus* 0.000 and *lewisi* 0.021.
- Inland cutthroat were significantly less heterozygous than the 41 rainbow populations analysed by Allendorf and Utter (1978) (P<0.005). The authors attribute the difference to population size, structure and gene flow, but note the explanation does not fit the variable *utah* populations.
- *Henshawi* differentiation is explained by its Lahontan history: the lake fragmented repeatedly over the last 50,000–100,000 years and the populations have been isolated since desiccation about 8,000 years B.P. *Bouvieri* populations occupy glaciated regions and are of more recent origin.

**Systematic conclusions** (Section V)
- Phenetic (UPGMA) and cladistic (Wagner) analyses across 78 populations of nine taxa show that genetic identity is uniformly high (above 0.95) within taxa.
- Excluding *lewisi*, cutthroat subspecies are more similar to each other than to other taxa. *Lewisi* is about as divergent from other cutthroat as from other trout.
- *Gila* and *Arizona* trout are more similar to each other than to other taxa. *S. apache*, *S. gilae*, *S. aguabonita* and Rio Mayo trout are more similar to *S. gairdneri* than to cutthroat.
- Western *Salmo* form two superspecies groups (cutthroat and rainbow). Cutthroat subspecies differ as much genetically as full species in the rainbow group, and these subspecies groups may be older than some rainbow-series species.
- The authors conclude that classification should follow systematic principles, not management needs, while acknowledging that many "best effort" classifications are possible.
