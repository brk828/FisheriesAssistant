# Razorback Sucker Nursery Suitability and Use of a Floodplain Wetland in the Middle Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service draft final report (May 1997, Recovery Program Project CAP-6, prepared by Tim Modde) evaluates whether [[Floodplain Depression Wetlands]] provide nursery habitat for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Middle Green River, Utah. The study centred on [[Old Charley Wash]] on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]], sampled over three years (1994–1996). The author concludes that depression wetlands offer favourable temperature, zooplankton density, water quality and cover for age-0 razorback sucker, even though nonnative fishes dominate the wetland. Because larval razorback sucker in the river were left without floodplain access once flows fell below 447 m³/s, the report recommends managing depression wetlands and extending high-flow duration through [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] releases.

The document also includes the author's cover correspondence (July–August 1997) with reviewer Paul Marsh and the reviewer request letter, which explains the three-reviewer peer-review requirement of the [[Recovery Implementation Program]].

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** [[Razorback Sucker]], an endangered catostomid of the Upper Colorado River Basin. Adults were collected in floodplain reaches, and the study tested whether wetlands can support recruitment.
- **Study site:** [[Old Charley Wash]] (also Wood's Bottom), a 1.47 ha natural depression wetland (main unit evaluated ~90 ha, as stated in the text) adjacent to the Green River at river kilometre 402, 2.5 km upstream of the Duchesne River confluence. Dikes, inlet and outlet water control structures were installed in the 1960s–1990s. The wetland was drained each autumn and refilled in spring.
- **Study period:** three years, with 1994 a low-flow year and 1995 and 1996 high-flow years. The wetland was connected to the river in the high-flow years.
- **Acknowledged context:** the work was funded by the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin. An initial aim of the first year (hatchery grow-out capability) was dropped late in planning, and first-year data collection was described as largely unproductive but instructive.
- **Correspondence:** the appended emails and letter record that reviewer Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) was unable to meet the review timeline; the author arranged an alternative reviewer. Reviewers Peter Bayley and Carol McIvor were also named.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and zooplankton**
- Dissolved oxygen remained high in all three years. Surface–bottom differences in late June–early July 1996 were attributed to macrophyte photosynthesis.
- Turbidity was generally higher in spring; the highest recorded value (212) occurred on 13 June, and values rarely exceeded 50 NTU.
- Alkalinity ranged from about 150 to 10 mg/l CaCO₃; conductivity and alkalinity were not inhibitory.
- Peak zooplankton means were 54.3/l (1995) and 42.8/l (1996), peaking around the third to fourth week of June. Zooplankton densities in the wetland were one to two orders of magnitude above main-channel values cited from earlier studies.
- Macrophytes (*Polygonum* sp. at the margins, *Potamogeton pectinatus* in deeper water) expanded rapidly in July, coinciding with zooplankton decline.

**Fish community**
- Native fishes made up no more than 10.1% of the catch of any gear type.
- Nonnative fishes dominated draining catches: carp and fathead minnows together made up 94.4% of weight and 78.9% of fish collected across all years. Carp accounted for about 90% of biomass, and fathead minnows were the most numerous (59.6% of individuals).
- Biomass standing stock was 12.6 kg/ha (1994), 126.7 kg/ha (1995) and 71.4 kg/ha (1996).
- Species counts during draining were 5 (1994), 15 (1995) and 15 (1996).

**Habitat use**
- Red shiners were captured in significantly higher numbers in deeper open-water fyke net sets.
- Small carp favoured shallow emergent and flooded terrestrial vegetation.
- Fathead minnows showed little habitat preference.

## Key Findings

1. **Razorback sucker recruitment:** the draining of Old Charley Wash produced 28 age-0 razorback sucker in 1995 (mean 94 mm TL, range 74–125 mm; mean 9.5 g) and 45 in 1996 (mean 65.9 mm, range 44–83 mm; mean 3.8 g). Two adults were collected in 1995 and none in 1996. The report calls these juveniles the greatest number of juvenile fish reported in the Upper Colorado River Basin.
2. **Successive-year use:** capture in two successive years suggests a pattern of wetland use by larvae. Eight wild yearling razorback sucker (235–365 mm TL) were also collected from Leota Bottoms ponds in 1994.
3. **Growth and survival:** razorback sucker grew and survived at rates similar to carp in the wetland, despite high densities of nonnative predators and competitors.
4. **Timing mismatch:** in 1995, floodplains disconnected at about 447 m³/s, before most backwaters appeared (109 m³/s). In 1996, larvae were first collected after flows fell below 447 m³/s. The interval from first to last larval collection (3–27 June 1996) and the time to reach 109 m³/s was 37 and 13 days, respectively.
5. **Starvation threshold:** citing [[Papoulias and Minckley]] (1990), larvae die if not fed within 20–30 days of hatching, and zooplankton densities needed for survival were not documented in main-channel habitat.
6. **Nonnative biomass concern:** an inordinate biomass of nonnative fishes (especially carp, green sunfish, black bullhead and black crappie) was produced in the wetland, raising the question of their impact if released into the Green River.
7. **Draining catch differences:** the 1995 nonnative biomass removed was nearly double that of 1996, which the author partly attributed to the longer 1995 connection (48 days vs. 38 days in 1996).

**Management recommendations**
- **Priority 1:** maximise management of large depression wetlands to produce and release native fishes. Three options were given: (a) water control and draining of large connected wetlands such as Leota Bottoms, Johnson Bottoms, Stewart Lake and Little Stewart Lake; (b) partial breaching of depression wetlands for more frequent connection, lowering dikes to allow connection at 382 m³/s, which reduces isolation time but risks summer drying and nonnative spill into the main channel; (c) a combination of these.
- **Priority 2:** restore historical frequency and duration of floodplain inundation through [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] releases. In years when the Yampa River and Green River flows are between 0 and 50% exceedance, releases should hold flows at Jensen at or above 447 m³/s for at least one month after peak.
- **Priority 3:** use Old Charley Wash as a grow-out facility for hatchery fish in low-flow years, when the wetland is not connected (about one year in three).

**Historical flow context**
- Pre-Flaming Gorge (1947–1962): 0–50% exceedance flows coincided 35.7% of the time, with average peak 809 m³/s and flows above 453 m³/s lasting 6.2 days on average.
- Post-dam (1968–1995): coincidence fell to 25.9%, average peak 639 m³/s, and duration above 453 m³/s to 2.3 days.

**Author's reviewer correspondence**
- Modde regards the razorback sucker in the Green River subbasin as habitat limited rather than competition or predation limited, while acknowledging a major suppressive effect from nonnative fishes.
- The three-reviewer requirement is a prerequisite for submission to the program approval process, and reviewers were asked to assess methodology, design, data interpretation, conclusions and recommendations.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Census of the 1994 draining was incomplete because of erosion through the outlet structure in spring and difficult muddy capture conditions. Fish collected in 1994 therefore do not represent all fish present.
- Gear differed among years, which prevented direct annual comparison of catch per unit effort.
- Zooplankton densities may have been underestimated because cladocerans undergo vertical migration.
