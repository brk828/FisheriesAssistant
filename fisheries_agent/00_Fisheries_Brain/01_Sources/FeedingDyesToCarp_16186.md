# Feeding Dyes to Carp

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #fish-marking #hatchery-science

## Executive Summary
This 1961 study from the New York State Conservation Department's Fish Laboratory (Livingston Manor, NY) investigated the use of ingested dyes to (1) increase palatability/consumption of fish food pellets and (2) mark or color [[Common Carp]] and other species for research and management purposes. Researchers tested numerous water-soluble and oil-soluble dyes fed via doughy or shellacked flour baits, evaluating palatability, internal staining of feces (useful for tracking bait consumption in wild populations), and external epidermal coloration. The most successful dye for producing durable external coloration was **Sudan black** (Solvent Black 3), which turned carp tissues blue in a dose-dependent, reversible manner. The work was conducted under Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration Project F-9-R and has direct applications for tracking poison-bait consumption rates and for mass-marking hatchery-reared trout.

## Background & Description
- **Target tissues affected by dye uptake:** epidermis, mouth, chin, head, belly, musculature, intestine/feces. Tooth enamel was notably **resistant** to Sudan black staining.
- **Coloration progression (Sudan black):** first appeared in the mouth ~24 hours post-ingestion at sufficient dose, then spread to the chin, and subsequently throughout the body (head, belly).
- **Dose-response relationship established:**
  - <19–37 mg/kg: no external coloration (though intestine stained blue).
  - 39–58 mg/kg: coloration limited to mouth (lowest dose) and mouth+chin (higher doses); faded within ≤2 weeks.
  - 67 mg/kg: heavy coloration of entire head, persisting 10 days (fish removed due to fungal infection).
  - 75 mg/kg: threshold for **reliable/sure external coloring**.
  - 78–143 mg/kg: intense coloration of head and belly, lasting up to 30 days.
- **Accumulation/depletion dynamics:** Sequential feeding demonstrated temporary tissue accumulation — repeated sub-threshold doses (e.g., 37 mg/kg + 16 mg/kg) produced delayed but darker coloration, with color loss occurring in a tissue-specific order (chin faded before mouth).
- **No effect on internal injection:** Sudan black suspended in peanut oil and injected into body cavity/musculature remained localized and inert at the injection site even after 10 days — ingestion, not injection, was required for systemic distribution.
- Comparative palatability/coloration in other species: [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] colored blue within 4–6 days at 47°F via force-feeding; [[Goldfish]] readily accepted dyed baits and colored; [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]] rejected black-colored baits outright (suggesting bait color, not just dye chemistry, drives acceptance).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Experiments were conducted in **controlled hatchery/laboratory settings** (tanks and ponds) rather than open water, reflecting the applied fisheries-management context of the New York Conservation Department.
- **Color preference behavior** varied by species and life stage:
  - Adult [[Common Carp]] showed **no strong color preference** in laboratory conditions.
  - Carp **fingerlings** preferred bright colors, especially red.
  - [[Brook Trout]] were strongly attracted to **red and yellow** baits, consistent with prior work (Wolf 1953) showing trout selectively consume red pellets over tan, green, blue, or yellow ones.
- Dye palatability differed by chemical class: water-soluble food dyes were uniformly palatable; some oil-soluble dyes (e.g., F D & C Yellow #4, F D & C Red #27) were **slightly unpalatable** at higher concentrations (~2%).
- Ecological/management application: dye-marked feces allowed researchers to estimate the **percentage of a wild carp population consuming a particular bait** — directly relevant to designing poison-bait control programs for invasive/nuisance carp populations in ponds.
- Potential food-web/human health consideration: Sudan black fed to white rats produced dark stained feces and some apparent physiological effect, and citing New (1959), the dye stains fat in mice — raising concern about bioaccumulation risk if dyed fish were consumed by humans or other predators, warranting further toxicological testing before field application.

## Key Findings
1. **Water-soluble food dyes** (e.g., certified red and yellow dyes) are inexpensive, palatable, and effective for staining intestinal contents/feces, enabling researchers to quantify bait consumption rates in carp populations — red dye worked best against dark natural foods, yellow against light-colored foods.
2. **Sudan black** was the only dye tested that reliably produced durable **external** blue coloration in carp, with effects lasting from several days to over 60 days depending on dose; a minimum ~75 mg/kg was needed for consistent external marking.
3. Coloration is **fully reversible** — dye is progressively lost from or metabolically altered within body tissues over time, following a predictable fade sequence (chin before mouth).
4. Several other dyes tested (alizarin red S, indigotin, cadmium red/yellow, Sudan III, Rhodamine B in shellacked particle form) were **palatable but produced no useful or lasting external coloration**, either due to poor uptake, rapid loss, or interference from natural food coloration (e.g., indigotin's blue too closely resembled natural dark foods).
5. **Rhodamine B** fed in a flour-based bait (rather than shellacked particles) produced a dark pink coloration lasting up to 7 days in a carp that consumed multiple baits, though most fish rejected these baits via shredding behavior.
6. Species-specific bait acceptance varied: carp, [[Goldfish]], and some [[Brown Trout]]/[[Brook Trout]] individuals accepted Sudan black baits, but [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]] and most brown trout rejected them, likely due to visual aversion to black-colored bait.
7. Boiling dyed carp flesh for 10 minutes did **not** destroy the dye, though natural decomposition eliminated the color — a food-safety consideration if dyed fish enter the human food chain.
8. Injection of Sudan black (in peanut oil) was **ineffective** for producing coloration, confirming that oral ingestion is the necessary route for systemic dye distribution in carp.
9. The authors suggest Sudan black or similar dyes could be used for **mass-marking hatchery trout** via feed, offering a practical, low-cost alternative to injection or fin-clipping methods, pending further safety evaluation (see [[Lawrence 1941 Madder Root Marking]] for a comparable historical approach using alizarin/madder root in rainbow trout bone staining).
