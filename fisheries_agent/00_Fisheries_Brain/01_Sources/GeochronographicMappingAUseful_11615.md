# Geochronographic mapping:  A useful technique for understanding species distribution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #conservation-biology #species-distribution-mapping

## Executive Summary
This USFWS Research Information Bulletin introduces **geochronographic mapping**, a spatiotemporal data visualization technique used to track changes in species distribution over time, particularly for rare, threatened, or endangered aquatic species. The technique was first applied in 1982 to the declining [[Roanoke Bass]] (*Ambloplites cavifrons*) and the threatened [[Hybopsis monacha]] (spotfin chub). The bulletin's primary case study concerns the endangered [[Okaloosa Darter]] (*Etheostoma okaloosae*), endemic to the Rocky Bayou stream system in northeastern Florida, and its apparent ecological replacement by the introduced, ecologically similar [[Brown Darter]] (*Etheostoma edwini*).

## Background & Description
The source document does not provide detailed morphological data for the species discussed. It notes only that:
- [[Brown Darter]] (*Etheostoma edwini*) is described as **"ecologically similar"** to the [[Okaloosa Darter]], implying niche and likely morphological overlap sufficient to enable competitive replacement.
- No meristic, morphometric, or coloration data are presented in this bulletin; the focus is methodological (distributional mapping) rather than anatomical.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: The [[Okaloosa Darter]] and [[Brown Darter]] inhabit the lower reaches of major tributary systems within the Rocky Bayou drainage, a geographically confined freshwater stream system in northeastern Florida.
- **Range dynamics**: The [[Brown Darter]] was first documented in the westernmost tributary at river kilometer (RKM) 0.3 and has since expanded through the lower reaches of the major tributary systems, apparently displacing the native [[Okaloosa Darter]] from portions of its historic range.
- **Interspecific interaction**: The pattern documented is one of **species replacement**, where an introduced species with an overlapping ecological niche gradually supplants an endangered native congener. This is analogous to competitive exclusion dynamics documented in other freshwater darter systems.
- **Research context**: The National Fisheries Research Center–Gainesville was, at time of publication, actively studying the ecological interaction between these two darter species to clarify the mechanism of replacement.

## Key Findings
- **Geochronography defined**: A mapping technique that graphically references all known collection records for one or two species to specific collection localities, arranged in chronological order, to reveal spatial and temporal distribution patterns (stasis, decline, or expansion) within a defined geographic unit (typically a stream system).
- **Advantages over tabular/textual data**: Patterns are more easily detected; data can be rapidly referenced; geographic subsets are readily compared; and such maps serve as durable summaries for future research.
- **Data requirements**: Robust geochronographic maps depend on exhaustive data compilation from museums, university collections, published and "gray" literature, state agency permit files, and validated personal collections.
- **Case study dataset**: For the [[Okaloosa Darter]] manuscript in preparation, researchers compiled **273 collection records** spanning **~240 stream miles** over **51 years (1938–1989)**.
- **Primary conservation finding**: The data revealed a clear pattern of the endangered [[Okaloosa Darter]] being progressively replaced by the introduced [[Brown Darter]] in the Rocky Bayou system, with the invasion front tracked from its initial detection point (RKM 0.3) through subsequent spread into major tributaries.
- **Historical precedent**: The technique was originally developed and applied in 1982 for the [[Roanoke Bass]] and later adapted for [[Hybopsis monacha]], establishing geochronographic mapping as a validated tool for conservation-status assessment of declining or range-shifting freshwater fishes.
