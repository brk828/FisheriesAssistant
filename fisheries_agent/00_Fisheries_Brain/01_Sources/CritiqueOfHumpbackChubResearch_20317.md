# Critique of Humpback Chub Research Funded by the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #humpback-chub

## Executive Summary

This critique by [[Hiram W. Li]] of the [[Oregon State University]] [[Oregon Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] reviews the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Program]], which funds research on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the [[Grand Canyon]] of the [[Colorado River]]. The author argues that the program lacks a systematic research framework. Projects appear to be a collection of individual interests rather than work directed at two central goals: (1) identifying [[Glen Canyon Dam]] flow operations that prevent negative impacts on the humpback chub, and (2) establishing a second spawning population within the Grand Canyon. The critique recommends a logical, flow-chart-based research pathway, outside peer review, broader expertise, and changes to the review process. It also identifies specific studies with design or justification problems.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Written critique and review memo. It is not a primary research study, and it reports no original field data, sample sizes, or statistics.
- **Study species:** [[Humpback Chub]], described as rare and endangered. Population decline is treated as part of a broader syndrome, and the species' requirements are described as poorly known.
- **Regulatory context:** The research addresses [[Endangered Species Act]] recovery objectives. The author notes that time is limited: if conditions continue as they are, extinction is "just a matter of time."
- **Program structure:** The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Program]] funds a large research effort, including a knowledge synthesis, and several agencies and institutions participate, including the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Pinetop office), [[Arizona State University]], and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].
- **Review process:** Research groups were interviewed in a panel format. Each group gave a presentation of about 30 minutes, followed by questions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat distinctions:** Adult humpback chub are reported to occupy distinctive macrohabitat or channel units, which may be detectable through [[Aerial Photography]] and remote sensing. Microhabitat analysis is considered most relevant for locating suitable spawning and rearing habitat. Early life stages (young-of-year, YOY) have requirements that are better known for other fishes.
- **Disturbance ecology:** Research by [[Gary Meffe]] and [[Bill Minckley]] indicates that native fauna are adapted to periodic disturbance such as flash floods and big spates, while exotic fauna are not. The author considers this important for the program.
- **Watershed variation:** The author questions why the Colorado watershed appears unique within the wider Colorado River Basin, and asks whether watershed characteristics can identify candidate reintroduction sites and suitable watersheds above fish barriers.
- **Little Colorado River (LCR):** The LCR is a key tributary. A hypothesis holds that the LCR is at carrying capacity and that chub there may be potential colonists of other tributaries. Reported tidal-bore-like disturbance flows affect YOY habitat availability.
- **Exotic species:** The author discusses a hypothesis that alien species limit native species, with [[Alien Fish Removal]] in tributaries of the Colorado River proposed as a test through reintroduction of native fishes.

## Key Findings

1. **Lack of a systematic framework.** The program is described as "an amalgam of pet projects" rather than a systematic approach. The author recommends setting research priorities and developing a flow chart that links each project to the two program objectives.

2. **Recommended logical pathway.** The author recommends [[Systematic Problem Solving]] methods (such as [[Strong Inference]], [[Multiple Hypothesis Testing]], [[Step-Down Planning]], or [[Critical Path Analysis]]) and the [[Bayesian Approach]], in which information gained determines what is gathered next. The "need to know" should be separated from the "nice to know."

3. **Knowledge synthesis.** The author commends the funding of a knowledge synthesis but asks whether it is complete and whether field research was designed to use it.

4. **Two analytical approaches.** Limiting factors can be sought from the bottom up ([[Autecology]], [[Life History Analysis]], [[Key Factor Analysis]], and risk assessment, seeking life-history bottlenecks) or from the top down ([[Hierarchical Analysis]] and [[Disturbance Theory]] at ecoregional, regional, local, and microhabitat scales).

5. **Adaptive management.** A stronger adaptive management approach is recommended. The author suggests testing the alien-species hypothesis through reintroduction and notes this would bring immediate benefits to the research program.

6. **Expert involvement and peer review.** Scientists familiar with the system should be involved in developing the flow chart. Outside referees should review the flow chart and reports to protect the program and the scientists, as happened in the early [[Spotted Owl]] listing process. Each referee panel should mix disciplines, and membership should rotate (e.g., half previous attendees and half new participants) to maintain institutional memory.

7. **Conflict of interest.** The author did not detect any conflict of interest. However, Arizona State University is well represented in upper levels of the program, and the author notes that this could invite accusations. Outside peer review is proposed to address the issue.

8. **Political interference.** Attempts reportedly were made by politically appointed officials to suppress sections of the report and misuse data. Outside referees reportedly resolved the changes and rectified the problem.

9. **Broader expertise.** Relying only on regional scientists risks overlooking new approaches. The author suggests consulting outside experts, including [[Bob Hughes]] (EPA Corvallis), [[Jim Sedell]] (PNW), [[Frank Davis]] (Santa Barbara), [[Brad Harvey]] (Weber State), and [[John Hunter]] (NMFS Hawaii).

10. **Landscape and GIS approach.** The landscape approach and [[Geographic Information Systems]] are described as among the most valuable tools for selecting reintroduction sites. Aerial photography may greatly speed up inventory, and GIS specialists with aquatic experience are needed.

11. **Need for a research coordinator.** A scientist with expertise in ecological theory and adaptive management, and with authority over program direction and funding, should be appointed as chief of research. The author suggests someone with experience similar to leaders of the Desert Fishes Council, Pacific Northwest tribal treaty groups, or the Animal Resource Ecology program at the University of British Columbia or the University of Alaska.

12. **Research contractor review.** Research groups were asked two questions: (a) how their research directly related to humpback chub recovery, and (b) how it would directly relate to flow operations that protect the species. The author notes that the questions were not clearly understood by all participants and that the panel's briefing was not clearly communicated.

13. **Little Colorado River studies.** A hypothesis is that the LCR is at carrying capacity and that chub there are potential colonists. Work proposed includes radiotracking chub induced to spawn with an [[LHRH Analogue]] injection, moving them to spawning sites, and microhabitat characterization of those sites. Richard Valdez of BioWest is described as an astute and valuable scientist.

14. **FWS Pinetop habitat work.** Habitat mapping at multiple scales in the LCR is considered valuable for documenting habitat availability at the reach and microhabitat levels. Findings on shifts in activity cycles during ontogeny are considered important for defining habitat needs.

15. **Sampling design concerns.** The LCR is considered too high in flow for some netting methods during certain periods. Nets are said to be placed at "hot spots," which biases mark-recapture statistics. The author recommends random grid points, with the caution that population estimates depend on randomly distributed sampling.

16. **Methods the author considers worth improving:**
   - Use of [[PIT Tags]] and radio tags. The author suggests these are costly and that fewer animals may be needed.
   - [[Otolith Analysis]] for age and growth. The author suggests calibrating length-based back-calculation and considering other parameters.
   - Endocrine studies. The author notes that fish do not need to be sacrificed to examine endocrine levels.

17. **Flawed or low-priority studies.** The author considers these to be weak:
   - Studies of algal and gammarid drift in the mainstem, which are not well designed.
   - Water quality studies designed as "shotgun analysis," which need better justification and design.
   - Primary production work, where time-of-production analysis in lotic conditions and the effects of chemical spikes are considered limiting factors.
   - Organic carbon studies.
   - Monitoring by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] that is reported to be maintained by a study of the same kind.
   - Research described as "laden with superfluous studies," with a "shopping list" character.

18. **Interview process concerns.** The author recommends that research groups be given time to speak privately, and that panel members, principal investigators, and mid-level administrators be handled differently. The author was disturbed that certain agreements seemed to be made before the panel, and notes a later meeting with David Wegner and a break during a council meeting.
