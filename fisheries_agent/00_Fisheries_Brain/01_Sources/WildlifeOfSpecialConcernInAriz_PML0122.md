# Wildlife of Special Concern in Arizona (Public Review Draft, June 1996)

#ichthyology #herpetology #ornithology #mammalogy #wildlife-conservation

## Executive Summary

This public review draft from the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AZGFD) reissues the 1988 "Threatened Native Wildlife in Arizona" list as the **Wildlife of Special Concern in Arizona (WSCA)** list. The change responds to public and agency confusion: the old title was often read as carrying regulatory or legal status. The WSCA list is a non-regulatory management policy guide. It identifies taxa whose occurrence in Arizona is or may be in jeopardy, based on habitat loss, population decline, and probability of extirpation. The draft covers fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. Public comment was requested before July 26, 1996, and a revised list was planned for adoption by the end of 1997 after a "Diversity Review" with expert input.

## Background & Description

**Origins and purpose.** Arizona's varied topography, climate, and geologic history support more than 200 major plant communities and aquatic features, and more than 800 species of vertebrate wildlife. Most WSCA-listed taxa have suffered significant habitat losses, and many have declined appreciably. The authors attribute these losses mainly to human-caused habitat fragmentation, degradation, and destruction. Listing depends on the degree of habitat or population impact and the probability of extirpation. Limited distribution alone does not justify listing. When data are scarce, the list relies on professional inference and informed opinion.

**Scope rules.**
- The list includes species and subspecies. Where several subspecies occur in Arizona, only the subspecies of concern is listed. Where only one form occurs, the species as a whole is usually listed.
- **Extirpated** taxa are eliminated from Arizona but survive elsewhere, which offers hope for restoration. Examples include the [[Black-Footed Ferret]] and the [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- **Extinct** taxa are believed gone everywhere. Examples include Merriam's elk and the Mexican wolf (*Canis lupus* subspecies). The document notes that "extinct" species are sometimes rediscovered, as with the relict leopard frog in northwestern Arizona.

**Why some species are fished or hunted.** Some WSCA taxa can be legally harvested. The [[Apache Trout]] is federally threatened and on the WSCA list, yet it can be taken as a sportfish in east-central Arizona. Its problems are habitat-related, and regulated harvest is said to support management and restoration funding. Conversely, some protected species are absent from the list. The banded rock rattlesnake is protected from hunting but is not on the WSCA list, because its habitat is more extensive and less threatened than that of the [[Ridge-Nosed Rattlesnake]].

**Revision process.** The March 15, 1996 Commission meeting received the first draft. A preliminary comment period closed May 31, 1996. A second period was planned for September to November 1996, with public meetings in regional office cities (Pinetop, Flagstaff, Kingman, Yuma, Tucson, and Mesa). The announcement was mailed to 1,191 addresses from the agency's strategic planning exercise.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies a recurring set of threats across taxa:

- **Aquatic systems:** dewatering and drought; dams and channelization; stream diversion; and predation by or competition with non-native fishes such as the red shiner, largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*), and mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*). Hybridization with introduced trout and parasites are also noted.
- **Riparian systems:** clearing, grazing, wood-cutting, groundwater depletion, and urban or agricultural development. Many riparian bird and bat species depend on cottonwood, willow, and sycamore woodlands.
- **Desert and grassland systems:** habitat fragmentation, off-highway vehicles, agriculture, pesticide drift, and invasive plants such as a non-native mustard (*Brassica*) that may impede lizard movement.
- **Montane and forest systems:** timber harvest, especially intensive even-age management and removal of large trees, which affects the [[Northern Goshawk]] and [[Mexican Spotted Owl]].
- **Disease and contaminants:** lead poisoning in waterfowl, which affects [[Bald Eagle]] populations; pesticides in Mexico that affect wintering grounds for migrants.
- **Non-native species:** bullfrogs (*Rana catesbeiana*) are repeatedly cited as predators of native amphibians and fishes. Starlings compete with owls for cavities.

Several taxa are remnants of formerly widespread populations, such as the [[Desert Pupfish]], which was eliminated from much of the lower Gila Basin and survives in reintroduced or restricted sites.

## Key Findings

The draft lists taxa in five groups. Entries below are representative; the full list is in the source.

### Fishes
- **Colorado River natives:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is extirpated. Reintroductions into the Salt and Verde drainages began in 1985. The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) population is largely non-recruiting, and massive reintroductions began in 1981. The bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) survives mainly through hatchery stocking.
- **Gila basin natives:** Gila chub (*G. intermedia*) persists in fewer than 20 streams and is declining. Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) is known only from the upper Verde, Eagle, and Aravaipa creeks. Loach minnow is restricted to six streams.
- **Virgin River:** the woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) is restricted to the Virgin River, as are the Virgin River chub and spinedace.
- **Trout:** Apache trout is threatened but supports sportfishing. Gila trout (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) is extirpated in Arizona, and reintroductions depend on stock from New Mexico.
- **Pupfish and topminnows:** Monkey Springs pupfish is extinct. Quitobaquito pupfish survives in a single natural population in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument. Of 191 reintroductions of Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) since the late 1910s, only 35 still persisted in 1987. Twenty-nine of those 35 were first stocked in 1982, and 20 natural localities remain in southern Arizona.

### Amphibians and Reptiles
- **Amphibians:** Sonoran tiger salamander is restricted to a few ponds in the Sonoita area. Chiricahua leopard frog (*Rana chiricahuensis*) survives in scattered localities. The Tarahumara frog is extirpated; its last Arizona individual was found dead in 1983. Relict leopard frog was rediscovered in the Virgin River drainage.
- **Turtles and lizards:** desert tortoise (*Gopherus agassizii*) declined sharply in Mohave Desert populations. Flat-tailed horned lizard and Mojave and Colorado fringe-toed lizards face habitat loss and off-highway vehicle impacts.
- **Snakes:** Ridge-nosed rattlesnake (*Crotalus willardi*) is limited to a few southeastern mountain ranges. Massasauga is known from very few grassland populations. Narrow-headed garter snake (*Thamnophis rufipunctatus*) is threatened by stream alteration and declining native fishes.

### Birds
- **Waterbirds:** Clark's grebe breeds at only two Colorado River locations and Many Farms Lake. American bittern, least bittern, great egret, and snowy egret breed locally, and wetland loss is their main threat.
- **Raptors:** Mississippi kite, Bald Eagle (200 to 250 wintering birds, with many nests along the Salt, Verde, and Bill Williams rivers), northern goshawk, gray hawk, and common black-hawk are of concern. The Northern Aplomado Falcon and California Condor are extirpated. Peregrine falcon has recovered to more than 200 known Arizona eyries, though some sites need seasonal closures.
- **Riparian songbirds:** Southwestern willow flycatcher, yellow-billed cuckoo (extirpated from most lowland sites), buff-breasted flycatcher, and tropical and thick-billed kingbirds depend on riparian woodlands.
- **Owls:** Mexican spotted owl depends on large trees, snags, and multistoried conditions, with logging as its main threat. The cactus ferruginous pygmy-owl has declined substantially since 1950.

### Mammals
- **Bats:** California leaf-nosed bat, lesser long-nosed bat (declined drastically in roost counts), and Mexican long-tongued bat are sensitive to roost disturbance and loss of agave and saguaro nectar sources.
- **Carnivores:** Jaguar and ocelot are extirpated as residents. The Mexican wolf and red wolf-related taxa are extinct or extirpated. The grizzly bear is extirpated, last confirmed in 1939 on Mount Baldy. The southwestern river otter is uncertain, with genetic swamping risk from a Louisiana subspecies introduced in 1981 to 1983.
- **Small mammals:** Arizona shrew and water shrew are restricted to a few sites, and only one water shrew population was verified in the last 20 years. Mount Graham red squirrel is restricted to high-elevation spruce-fir forest.
- **Ungulates:** Sonoran pronghorn persists in a remnant population in southwestern Arizona. Rocky Mountain elk, introduced in 1913, is abundant, while the native elk is extinct.

### Program Conclusions
- The WSCA list is a management policy tool, not a regulatory list, and its contents are expected to change as data improve.
- Many taxa remain poorly known, so several listing decisions rest on informed opinion.
- The revision aims for a final, Commission-approved list by the end of 1997. Public comment with data and expert information is explicitly solicited.
