# Woody Debris and Nest Site Selection in Pumpkinseed Sunfish; River Discharge and Gulf of St. Lawrence Landings

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two short journal notes from the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada* (vol. 30, no. 6, 1973), with French abstracts. The first, by [[Colgan and Ealey]] of Queen's University, is an experimental field study of how woody debris affects nest site selection in [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]]. The second, by W. H. Sutcliffe Jr. of the [[Bedford Institute Of Oceanography]], reports positive correlations between monthly [[St. Lawrence River]] discharge and regional landings of [[American Lobster]] and [[Atlantic Halibut]]. The main result of the nesting study is that pumpkinseeds prefer cleared areas early and late in the breeding season, but this preference disappears at peak nesting because of limited space.

## Background & Description

**Nest site selection study (Colgan and Ealey).** Nest site choice is recognized as important in fish breeding, but few experiments have tested it. The pumpkinseed was chosen because much background information on its breeding biology exists. Earlier work reported that pumpkinseeds prefer shallower water than [[Bluegill]], avoid dense shade (though partial shade from overhanging trees does not affect nesting), and avoid pure mud substrates. In the eutrophic study lake, woody debris is a major feature of nest sites.

**Fish-landings study (Sutcliffe).** The paper examines links between seasonal river discharge and local catches of American lobster and Atlantic halibut in the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Lag periods were used to represent age at maturity or time to commercial size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** In early May 1972, eight coves on Lake Opinicon were each divided into two sections. In one section ("cleared"), all woody material over 25 cm was removed. That wood was scattered in an overdispersed but irregular pattern across the other section ("cluttered"). Nest counts ran from June 9 to July 18, 1972, with visits every second day when weather permitted. Between June 28 and July 6, nest diameters, nearest neighbor distances, and nearest cover distances were measured at sites 1 to 4. At the end, each site's area and proportion of open substrate (free of rocks and wood) were estimated.

**Measures of selection.** The authors used four measures:
- Nest numbers at the beginning and end of breeding, when nests were scarce and density was less constrained.
- Nest density (nests/m²) during heavy nesting, split into peak (three highest counts) and nonpeak values.
- Spawning activity, recorded as a pair circling over a nest.
- Substrate type, tested by checking whether cleared and cluttered sections of a site ranked similarly (Spearman rank correlation).

**Substrate.** The two lowest-ranking sites (6 and 7) had thick silt over soft clay. Site 5 had thick silt over hard clay or gravel. The four highest-ranking sites had thin silt over rock or gravel. Substrate type therefore appeared to shape nest density.

**Sutcliffe's study area.** The analysis covers monthly St. Lawrence River discharge and annual regional landings. The excerpt does not give sample sizes or correlation coefficients.

## Key Findings

**Pumpkinseed nest site selection (Colgan and Ealey, 1973)**

- **Early and late preference for cleared areas.** More nests appeared in cleared sections at the start and end of breeding. Spawning activity was observed six times, always in a cleared section.
- **No preference at peak nesting.** During heavy nesting, nest densities in the two section types did not differ significantly. The authors attribute this to a [[Density-Dependent Constraint]], since a high breeding population limits available nesting space. Their conclusion is that breeding density cannot be increased by adding environmental boundaries.
- **Open-area density.** Using total area, density was higher in cleared sections except at site 4. Based on open (rock- and wood-free) area, there were no differences (P > .10, two-way ANOVA). The authors argue this result is not an artifact of overdispersed wood creating unusable patches.
- **Site 4 anomaly.** Site 4 went against the general pattern. The authors suggest a large, immovable tree stump blurred the distinction between sections, so the habitat manipulation likely failed there.
- **Substrate effects.** Substrate influenced density. Thick silt was avoided, consistent with [[Breder]] (1935, 1936) and [[Ingram and Odum]] (1941).
- **Nest dimensions.** Nests were significantly smaller in cluttered sections (P < .01). Nearest neighbor distances did not differ between section types. Nests in cleared sections were farther from cover.
- **Nest size relative to fish length.** Mean nest diameter divided by mean breeding fish length gave a ratio of 3.5, close to the 3.2 reported by Ingram and Odum. This supports the idea that nest size scales with the length of the builder.
- **Comparison with smallmouth bass.** Unlike [[Smallmouth Bass]], where added boundaries can raise breeding density, pumpkinseed density was not raised this way.
- **Behavioral parallel.** The authors link the lack of preference under crowding to the lack of feeding preference in hungry pumpkinseeds (Colgan 1973). They conclude that fish must be free of interfering constraints to show preferences.
- **Open questions.** The authors ask whether males choose clear areas to attract females or to avoid egg predators, and whether females choose males or the nest itself.

**Discharge and fisheries landings (Sutcliffe, 1973)**

- Monthly St. Lawrence River discharge is positively correlated with annual regional catches of American lobster and Atlantic halibut.
- Lag periods that reflect age at maturity or commercial size allow some predictive use of the correlations.
