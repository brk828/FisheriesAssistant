# Gila Crassicauda (Thicktail Chub)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 paper by Robert Rush Miller provides a comprehensive taxonomic redescription of the **[[Thicktail Chub]]** (*Gila crassicauda*), a now-presumed-extinct California minnow, based on 101 specimens representing an estimated 98% of all known museum material. The paper resolves a complex 19th-century synonymy involving five separate original descriptions under three genera, and documents the first confirmed evidence of natural hybridization between *G. crassicauda* and the **[[Hitch|Lavinia Exilicauda]]**, based on only five known hybrid specimens collected between 1872 and 1926. The thicktail chub is presented as a cautionary case study of extinction driven by anthropogenic habitat alteration in California's Central Valley.

## Background & Description

*Gila crassicauda* was historically a common freshwater fish of the **[[Sacramento-San Joaquin Drainage Basin]]**, inhabiting lowland streams, overflow ponds, marshes, and lakes of California's Central Valley. Notable populations occurred in **[[Clear Lake (California)]]** (1,325 ft elevation, Lake County) and **[[Coyote Creek]]**, a southern tributary of San Francisco Bay; during periods of heavy runoff (e.g., 1862) it likely occupied surface waters of San Francisco Bay itself.

The species attained a total length "somewhat greater than one foot" and was abundant enough in the late 19th century to appear regularly in the San Francisco fish market. Archaeological midden material from an Indian mound indicates the species was also utilized as a food source by indigenous peoples.

### Taxonomic History (Synonymy)
The species was described five separate times under three generic names:
1. *Leuciscus gibbosus* Ayres (1854a) — rejected as junior homonym of *Leuciscus gibbosus* Storer (1845)
2. *Lavinia crassicauda* Baird & Girard in Girard (1854) — based on San Joaquin River specimens
3. *Lavinia conformis* Baird & Girard in Girard (1854) — based on Poso Creek specimens (later shown to be a large-scaled variant, or possibly hybrid material)
4. *Lavinia gibbosa* Ayres (1854b)
5. *Tigoma crassa* Girard (1856) — based on Sacramento River (Redding) specimen

Girard (1856) additionally erected the genus ***Siboma*** for *Lavinia crassicauda* and *Siboma [Gila] atraria*, with *S. crassicauda* erroneously listed from the Mohave River. Generic placement fluctuated substantially over the following century (*Leuciscus*, *Siboma* as genus vs. subgenus), with Uyeno (1960) ultimately arguing that *Siboma* should be regarded merely as a species group within **[[Gila (genus)]]**.

### Diagnostic Characters
- **Body form**: Caudal peduncle short, very deep, and thick (source of the specific epithet "crassicauda")
- **Head**: Short, cone-shaped; dorsal profile ascends steeply from snout to nape in adults
- **Scales**: Large and regular, 49–60 lateral line scales (ave. 54.47); apical radii only; predorsal scales 27–36 (usually 31–32)
- **Fins**: Dorsal and anal fins almost always with 8 rays; pelvics typically with 9 rays; origin of dorsal fin over or slightly behind pelvic insertion
- **Gill rakers**: Short, denticulate, 8–14 (usually 10–12), ave. 11.20
- **Pharyngeal teeth**: Dental formula 2,5-4,2 (rarely 2,5-5,2 or 2,4-4,2); major row teeth hooked, lacking grinding surfaces; arches strong
- **Intestine**: Single S-shaped loop with characteristic restriction at the second loop (Kafuku Group 2 type) — diagnostic against *Lavinia*, which has ≥4 S-loops
- **Peritoneum**: Silvery, with sparse-to-numerous brown punctulations
- **Dermosphenotic bone**: Enlarged, width ~2.5–3× length, occupying more than half of the lateral temporal fossa (distinct from the small, divided dermosphenotic typical of other *Gila*)
- **Vertebrae**: 41–43 (ave. 41.67)

### Coloration
- Back: greenish-brown to bluish/purplish-brown to purplish-black
- Sides: lighter, yellowish ground color
- Venter: unicolor, silvery to dull yellowish
- Opercular bones tinged purple
- Males: body clouded with small melanophores (puncticulations), especially prominent during spawning
- Juveniles: black spot at caudal fin base

### Sexual Dimorphism
Females attained larger maximum size. Dorsal fin origin was slightly more anterior in males than females — a pattern typical of American cyprinids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

*Gila crassicauda* occupied sluggish lowland waters — the same habitat type preferred by the sympatric **[[Hitch|Lavinia Exilicauda]]**. The two species likely partitioned resources via diet: *G. crassicauda* was probably chiefly **carnivorous**, inferred from its hooked pharyngeal teeth lacking grinding surfaces, silvery peritoneum, short single-looped intestine, and moderate gill raker counts. In contrast, *L. exilicauda* relies largely (as adults) on **planktivory**.

### Decline and Extinction
The species' decline is attributed to drastic anthropogenic habitat alteration in the Central Valley, including:
- Lake drainage
- Marsh reclamation
- Dam building
- Irrigation practices
- Possible contribution from introduction of exotic fish species

The last verified capture was in 1950 in the Sacramento River near Rio Vista, Solano County (CAS 20456, 156 mm SL). Shapovalov, Dill, and Cordone (1959) listed the thicktail chub among California fishes "now either extinct or extremely rare." This paper represents one of the earliest formal scientific documentations of a freshwater fish extinction in North America.

### Hybridization Habitat Dynamics
Miller hypothesizes that *G. crassicauda* and *L. exilicauda* interbred because both occupied similar sluggish/slough habitats but avoided competitive exclusion through differing feeding ecology. Although *L. exilicauda* was historically thought to require gravel-bottomed streams for spawning (Murphy, 1948), Kimsey (1960) demonstrated it is not an obligatory stream spawner and can reproduce in lakes over mud or gravel. This behavioral flexibility may have facilitated spawning overlap and accidental gamete mixing with the declining *G. crassicauda*, with increasing rarity of the latter potentially further promoting hybridization — a pattern noted broadly in cases where a rare species co-occurs with an abundant congener/relative.

## Key Findings

### Hybrid Specimens (*Gila crassicauda* × *Lavinia exilicauda*)
Only **five hybrid specimens** are known, collected across three localities within a 10-mile radius, all in the Sacramento River system, over a 54-year span:
1. **MCZ 35692** — 1 adult, 115 mm SL, Sacramento River at Sacramento, collected by Steindachner, October 1872 (the specimen underlying Hubbs' 1955 graphical evidence of *Gila* × *Lavinia* hybridization, though species were unspecified at the time)
2. **SU 37856** — 1 adult, 130 mm SL, Sacramento River 20 mi below Grimes, Colusa County, collected by Rutter & Scofield, May 28, 1898 (gonad appeared grossly abnormal)
3. **UMMZ 94166** — 3 juveniles (50, 52, 66 mm, all immature), Putah Creek (tributary to Sacramento River), 4 mi west of Davis, Yolo County, collected by Hubbs & Schultz, July 29, 1926

A sixth putative hybrid from the Kaweah River (St. John Channel), described by Rutter (1908) under *Leuciscus conformis*, is treated as a probable hybrid based on its dental formula (1,5-5,1) and matching meristics, though the specimen itself could not be relocated.

### Evidence Supporting Hybrid Status
| Character | *G. crassicauda* | Hybrids | *L. exilicauda* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gill rakers | 8–14, ave. 11.20 | 16–20 (intermediate) | 20–27, ave. 23.53 |
| Lateral line scales | 49–60, ave. 54.47 | 50–61 (intermediate) | 57–67, ave. 60.25 |
| Predorsal scales | 27–36, ave. 31.29 | ~33–36 | 34–43, ave. 38.05 |
| Pharyngeal teeth (main row) | 2,5-4,2 | 4–5 teeth main row, 1–2 lesser row | 5-5 |
| Intestinal loops | Single S-loop | 2–3 S-loops (intermediate) | ≥4 S-loops |
| Dermosphenotic | Enlarged, undivided | Resembles *Gila* (single, ~1/2 fossa) | Divided, ~1/4–1/3 fossa |
| Caudal peduncle depth/head length ratio | 1.65–2.05, ave. 1.96 | 2.2–2.4 (intermediate) | 2.5–3.1, ave. 2.82 |
| Fin margins | Rounded | Straight-edged (intermediate) | Weakly to strongly falcate |
| Caudal fin | Shorter, less cleft | Intermediate length/forking | Longer, more deeply cleft, upper lobe > lower |

Nearly all meristic and morphometric characters examined (fin rays, scale counts, gill rakers, pharyngeal teeth, proportional measurements) showed values intermediate between the two parental species, providing strong corroborating evidence for hybrid origin despite the small sample size.

### Taxonomic Resolution
- *Lavinia conformis* is confirmed (via holotype re-examination) to be a large-scaled variant of *L. crassicauda* [= *G. crassicauda*], not a distinct species, resolving nearly 40 years of erroneous persistence in the literature.
- Rutter's (1908) two "*Leuciscus conformis*" specimens (Sacramento R. and Kaweah R.) are reinterpreted here as natural hybrids rather than a valid taxon or variant of *G. crassicauda*.

### Material Examined
The redescription draws on 101 specimens across 19 museum collections (USNM, ANSP, MCZ, SU, UMMZ, CAS, Sacramento State College Museum), including the holotypes of *Lavinia conformis* (USNM 231), *Tigoma crassa* (USNM 216), syntypes of *Lavinia crassicauda* (USNM 235/ANSP 4197), and the presumed type of *Leuciscus gibbosus* (ANSP 14738).

### Broader Significance
This account stands as a rare documented instance of interspecific, intergeneric hybridization associated with the terminal decline of one parental species (*G. crassicauda*), illustrating how the breakdown of reproductive isolation can accompany population collapse and habitat degradation — a phenomenon of continued relevance to conservation biology of rare freshwater fishes such as those discussed in [[Man and the Changing Fish Fauna of the American Southwest]] (Miller, 1961).
