# Lawsuit Filed Against Ft. Huachuca to Save San Pedro River

#conservation #riparian-ecology #environmental-law #water-resources

## Executive Summary

The [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]] (SWCBD) and Dr. Robin Silver filed a lawsuit in U.S. District Court (Phoenix) against [[Ft. Huachuca]] and the Defense Department for violations of the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA). The suit alleges illegal failure to evaluate, disclose, and mitigate environmental damage from a proposed military base expansion that threatens the [[San Pedro River]], described as "the last living river in the Southwest." The expansion could bring nearly 5,000 additional military personnel and dependents, potentially increasing the regional population by approximately 15,000 people. The military concluded the expansion would not significantly impact environmental resources, proposing only water-conserving fixtures as mitigation.

## Background & Description

The [[San Pedro River]] is characterized as the last living river in the Southwest and hosts the most extensive surviving expanse of the rarest forest type in North America: the [[Cottonwood-Willow Gallery Forest]] (broadleaf riparian association forest). In 1988, Congress established the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] in recognition of the river's ecological significance. The area is acknowledged as one of the last great relatively intact surviving ecosystems on Earth.

The river system supports 489 species of birds, mammals, fish, amphibians, and reptiles. Twenty-four San Pedro species are already in need of federal protection. The ecosystem represents a critical biodiversity hotspot in an otherwise arid landscape.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

For twenty years, Ft. Huachuca's own studies have documented the devastating effect of ongoing excessive water pumping on the [[San Pedro River]]. The proposed base expansion threatens to exacerbate these impacts through increased groundwater extraction to support a substantially larger human population.

The [[Cottonwood-Willow Gallery Forest]] depends on consistent groundwater levels and surface flow. Excessive pumping lowers water tables, reducing or eliminating surface flows and threatening the riparian forest structure that provides habitat for the diverse vertebrate community. The riparian corridor functions as a critical migration pathway and breeding habitat in the arid Southwest.
