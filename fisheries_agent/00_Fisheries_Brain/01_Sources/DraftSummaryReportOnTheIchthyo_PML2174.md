# San Juan River Ichthyofauna Summary, 1987–1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This provisional draft, transmitted by the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] in a December 20, 1989 cover letter, summarizes the 1987–1988 ichthyofaunal investigations of the [[San Juan River]] in New Mexico and Utah. The work was funded under a cooperative agreement with the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] and focused on five target taxa: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. Non-native species dominated catches, and only a few target-species individuals were captured. The appended hydrology report evaluates how [[Navajo Reservoir]] operations affect downstream flows under three alternative water-supply scenarios. All data are described as provisional, and the final report was still pending.

## Background & Description

**Study scope.** The [[Colorado River Basin]] drains about 2.5 million km² of the western United States. The San Juan River is the second-largest sub-basin of the Upper Colorado River Basin, draining about 38,300 mi² (99,200 km²) across Colorado, Utah, New Mexico, and Arizona. Its main tributaries include the Animas, La Plata, Los Pinos, Mancos, and Chaco rivers. The river originates in the San Juan Mountains of southwestern Colorado, flows through [[Navajo Reservoir]] and into [[Lake Powell]], and drains about 39% of its basin in New Mexico and 17% in Utah.

**Historical records.** Documented collections are sparse:
- Target species were first reported in the 1870s to 1890s, with much of that reporting later shown to be unreliable.
- Three juvenile squawfish were collected at [[Alcove Canyon]], Utah, on 4 July 1935 (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology specimens). These were the first substantiated San Juan record.
- Two squawfish were collected near Rosa, New Mexico, in 1959, and three young fish were taken at Mexican Hat, Utah, in 1960.
- A 1961 pre-impoundment survey by the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] (NMGF) provided the most extensive pre-dam baseline.

**Methods (1987–1988).** Sampling followed a study plan agreed among the Bureau of Reclamation, NMGF, and the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife Resources]]. Methods included:
- [[Electrofishing]]: standardized one-mile reaches, with raft-mounted DC units and netters.
- [[Seine Sampling]]: low-velocity habitats and backwaters, including young-of-year (YOY) surveys in spring and fall.
- [[Trammel Netting]] and [[Gill Netting]]: deep pools, eddies, and the Lake Powell arm.
- Target fish were measured, weighed, tagged with uniquely numbered Carlin tags (fish over 200 mm TL), and photographed for identification.

**Study area.** The emphasis was a 199-mile (319 km) reach from Farmington, New Mexico, to Piute Farms Marina, Utah. The reach includes diversions near Fruitland, the Four Corners pump station, Hogback, and Ardec. Several structures were barriers to upstream fish movement. Flows were highly variable historically, and floodplain soils contribute heavy silt loads during runoff.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regulation.** Navajo Dam reduced peak spring flows and raised winter baseflows. Post-dam spring and summer flows differ from pre-dam flows by about 10%. Water temperatures are moderated, and temperature fluctuations are reduced.
- **Habitat.** Riverine habitat in the New Mexico reach is braided, with sand and silt substrate. Canyon reaches near Mexican Hat are confined and bedrock-dominated. Salt cedar and Russian olive are common on the floodplain. Backwaters and side channels provide YOY nursery habitat.
- **Non-native species.** Common carp, red shiner, fathead minnow, channel catfish, and mosquitofish dominated many collections. Native fish, especially flannelmouth and bluehead suckers, comprised most of the electrofishing catch.
- **Mixed water quality.** Upstream of Shiprock, the river was subject to diversions and irrigation return flows. Sewage treatment effluent enters the river below Shiprock.

## Key Findings

### Fish community and abundance

- **New Mexico:** Total catch was 64,415 specimens (electrofishing 10,865, seining 53,452, trammel nets 98), with 17% from electrofishing. Non-native species made up 57.3% of individuals. Seven Colorado squawfish and 11 roundtail chub were captured.
- **Utah:** Total catch was 20,856 specimens across 551 collections (Table 3). Non-native species were prominent, though exact percentages are not reproduced here.
- **Dominant natives:** Flannelmouth and bluehead suckers together made up about 74.5% of electrofishing catch in New Mexico. Flannelmouth suckers were found at all 185 electrofishing sites.
- **Dominant non-natives:** Common carp and channel catfish made up about 21% of the electrofishing catch. Red shiner and fathead minnow were most abundant in seine samples.
- **Species richness:** Electrofishing species counts declined from 13 in 1987 to 9 in 1988.
- **New record:** Sand shiner was recorded in the Utah reach, where it had not been previously reported in the San Juan drainage. It was not collected in New Mexico seining.

### Target species

- **Colorado Squawfish (New Mexico):** Five squawfish were collected by electrofishing in 1987–1988 in New Mexico. The 1988 specimens included a female (730 mm TL) taken at RM 145.2 and one at RM 154.3 that was the most upstream record during this study. Its location was 4.4 km downstream of Shiprock.
- **Colorado Squawfish (Utah):** Two adults and 16 YOY were captured in Utah. Nine YOY fish were taken in the lowermost reach near Grand Gulch. A single YOY fish near RM 6.0 was the most downstream record. Six YOY were taken near RM 9.4.
- **Colorado Squawfish YOY spawning:** Using the Haynes et al. (1984) equation, the document estimates spawning between about 16 July and 7 August for New Mexico YOY, with slightly earlier dates in Utah. A 1988 YOY hatching date near 26 July was also estimated.
- **Roundtail Chub:** Roundtail chub were captured in several tributaries and the mainstem. A single adult was taken on 11 October 1987 at RM 139.5. The study verified reproduction in 1987 and 1988 through 15 YOY collections, but no adults of reproductive size were captured in New Mexico.
- **Razorback Sucker:** The first verified San Juan record was two adults in an irrigation pond near Bluff in 1976. In Utah, 10 adults were collected at Piute Farms Marina in March 1988, with half reproductively active. Six of these had been caught in 1987, suggesting a spawning aggregation.

### Flow management alternatives (appended hydrology report)

- **Alternative 1:** Minimum release at the Archuleta gage of 260 cfs (October–March) and 525 cfs (April–September). The minimum was met in at least 80% of years at Archuleta.
- **Alternative 2:** Minimum flow at Bluff of 30,000 acre-feet per month. Reservoir content stayed above about 980,000 acre-feet in 90% of years.
- **Alternative 3:** Minimum flows at Bluff of 30,000 acre-feet (October–March) and 60,000 acre-feet (April–September). Reservoir content dropped below the inactive pool (659,900 acre-feet) during parts of the study period.
- **Conclusion:** The study period included a flow regime that largely met the Bluff minimum under these alternatives. The document notes that further biological information is needed to refine flow recommendations.

### Overall conclusions

- Target species are rare in the San Juan drainage, with fewer than 40 squawfish and razorback suckers captured across all sampling in 1987–1988.
- Non-native fish dominate most habitats, and reservoir-induced flow changes may favor them.
- Verified reproduction of roundtail chub and squawfish YOY indicates some successful recruitment. Survival of adult populations is uncertain.
- Results remain provisional pending completion of the final report.
