# Grass Carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella): Evaluation of Release into U.S. Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #invasive-species #aquatic-vegetation-control

## Executive Summary

This 1973 review by David W. Greenfield synthesizes existing literature on the biology of the [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), evaluating the advisability of releasing this species into U.S. natural waters as a biological control agent for [[Aquatic Vegetation]]. The species, native to the [[Amur River Basin]] and Chinese river systems, had already been introduced experimentally into several U.S. states (Arkansas, Alabama, Arizona, Oregon) by the early 1970s, with wild specimens already captured in the [[Mississippi River]] as far north as southern Illinois by 1971. While Grass Carp show strong efficacy in controlling nuisance aquatic macrophytes and offer economic/food value, the paper raises significant concerns regarding indirect competition with native species, potential uncontrolled reproduction in large rivers, and disruption of habitat structure — ultimately recommending extreme caution before further release, echoing similar warnings from contemporary researchers (Stevenson, Ling, Cross, Kuronuma).

## Background & Description

- **Size/Growth**: Reaches approximately 1.5 meters in length within 5–6 years in river systems; growth rate is 2–3× that of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) under similar conditions (Kuronuma, pers. comm.).
- **Growth benchmarks** (Stevenson 1965):
  - 6 months: 4 g, 8 cm
  - 12 months: 372 g, 28 cm
  - 18 months: 1,816 g, 50 cm
- **Sexual dimorphism**: Females grow larger than males; a mature female can reach ~3 feet in length.
- **Digestive tract**: Notably short for a herbivorous fish; food passes completely through the gut in under 8 hours at 28–30°C (Hickling, 1966), with roughly one-third of ingested material passing through **undigested**.
- **Fecundity**: A 16-lb female was documented to contain ~100,000 eggs (Lin, 1935), though not all are believed to be spawned simultaneously.
- **Eggs**: Semi-pelagic, drifting in mid-water layers downstream; hatch in 24–34 hours post-fertilization depending on temperature (~25°C).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native habitat**: Large rivers, lakes, and ponds of the [[Amur River Basin]], Manchuria, and China, southward to the Chu River in South China.
- **Feeding ecology**:
  - Broadly **omnivorous** as juveniles, shifting toward strong **herbivory** in subadults/adults.
  - Diet includes aquatic macrophytes, tree leaves, small fish, earthworms, silkworm pupae, insects, chironomid larvae, [[Daphnia]], tubifex worms, and *Asellus*.
  - Juveniles feed primarily on **zooplankton, rotifers, and chironomid larvae** — placing them in direct dietary competition with young native fish.
  - Auburn University studies (Kilgen & Smitherman, 1971) found adult pond diets consisted of ~84% macrophytes, 9% insects (mostly chironomids), 7% commercial feed — indicating minimal dietary overlap with co-occurring [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Redeye Bass]], [[Israeli Carp]], and [[Channel Catfish]], which fed mainly on insects.
- **Spawning requirements**:
  - Requires river current of 3.3–5.5 ft/second, typically below rapids.
  - Water temperature 26–30°C (Lin) or above 20°C (Nikolskii).
  - Requires a sudden river rise (>4 ft within 12 hours), typically post-heavy rain — likely reducing egg predation via turbidity.
  - Spawning migration occurs in large shoals, April to mid-August (peak late May–mid-June in the West River, China).
  - Largely fails to reproduce naturally in pond conditions outside native range, though documented spawning occurred in Japan (1961) and Taiwan (Tang, 1960); artificial spawning induced via pituitary extract injections.
- **Physiological tolerance** (Cross, 1969 summary):
  - Temperature range: 0–35°C
  - Salinity tolerance: up to 10,050 ppm
  - Low oxygen tolerance: as low as 0.5 ppm
  - Survived a 5-week heavy ice cover at 0°C with no ill effects (Stevenson, 1965).
- **Interspecies interactions**:
  - No significant negative interaction observed with [[Carassius Auratus]] (goldfish), shrimp, [[Tilapia Zillii]], or trout in mixed-culture experiments (Japan, Arizona).
  - Growth of channel catfish and striped bass unaffected when stocked with Grass Carp at 40–50/acre (Auburn studies).
  - Possible indirect competitive effect on [[Largemouth Bass]]–[[Bluegill]] biomass (159.73 lb/acre in controls vs. 119.15 lb/acre with Grass Carp at 40/acre), attributed by researchers to loss of vegetative cover rather than direct food competition.

## Key Findings

1. **Effective weed control**: Grass Carp can achieve near-total aquatic macrophyte control within 2–3 weeks at sufficient stocking density (Avault, 1965), and represent a chemical-free alternative to herbicide-based aquatic vegetation management.
2. **Incomplete digestion → nutrient loading**: Roughly 1/3 of ingested plant material passes through undigested, promoting plankton growth (Stroganov, 1963) and raising concern about **eutrophication** risk in receiving waters.
3. **Reproductive risk**: Because spawning requires specific river current, temperature, and flood-pulse conditions, natural reproduction had not yet been confirmed in U.S. rivers as of publication — but large river systems like the [[Mississippi River]] could theoretically provide suitable conditions, raising fears of an uncontrollable population akin to the [[Common Carp]] invasion.
4. **Adult vs. juvenile competition risk differs**:
   - Adults show little direct dietary/ecological overlap with native U.S. game fish.
   - Juveniles (zooplanktivorous/invertivorous) pose a more direct competitive threat to young native fish, including non-game "trash species" (suckers, minnows, darters).
5. **Habitat-mediated indirect competition**: Removal of aquatic vegetation may eliminate spawning substrate and invertebrate habitat relied upon by native fish and waterfowl, even though it benefits pond managers seeking weed clearance.
6. **Economic/food value**: Reared as a food fish in Eastern Europe; reported to have excellent flavor and sport-fishing qualities in Alabama trials, despite bony flesh.
7. **Documented wild establishment risk**: Three Grass Carp (~20 lbs, age 4) were already captured in the Mississippi River (including southern Illinois) by 1971, despite no confirmed established breeding populations — highlighting that escape from containment had already occurred prior to formal risk assessment.
8. **Consensus recommendation**: Multiple contemporaneous experts (Stevenson, Ling, Cross, Kuronuma) and the author urge **extreme caution** and further controlled study — including development of monosex populations to prevent reproduction — before further release into open U.S. waters.
