# El Carácter Interdisciplinario de la Conservación

#ichthyology #marine-biology #natural-resources-conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1966 essay by [[Enrique Beltran]], published by the [[Instituto Mexicano de Recursos Naturales Renovables]] (IMRNR), is the closing chapter of the institute's 1965 round-table series on the contribution of various professions to the conservation of renewable natural resources. The author argues that conservation is inherently interdisciplinary, that no single professional can be a complete "conservationist," and that biology should occupy a central position in coordinating the contributions of agronomy, veterinary zootechnics, economics, geography and other fields. He proposes short "Conservation Seminars" as a practical training route.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Essay (sixth round table, "El carácter interdisciplinario de la conservación"), from the proceedings of a series held 25–29 October 1965 at the IMRNR auditorium, Mexico City.
- **Author:** [[Enrique Beltran]], a biologist and conservationist who taught the first Mexican course on renewable natural resources at the [[Escuela Normal Superior]] (from 1945) and the [[Escuela Nacional de Ciencias Biológicas]] of the IPN.
- **Professions examined in the series:** agronomist, veterinary zootechnician, economist, geographer and biologist.
- **Core terminology:** the author separates renewable resources (biotic resources, soil and water) from non-renewable ones (minerals, petroleum, coal), and distinguishes *protection* (avoiding human impact, e.g., in a forest) from *conservation* (sustainable use without destroying the resource). He stresses the "maximum sustained yield" as the ultimate goal.
- **Historical context:** the author notes that before about 1950 Mexico had almost no practitioners interested in this field, and that US centres at [[University of Michigan]] and [[Yale University]] pioneered conservation training led by biologists [[Stanley A. Cain]] and [[Paul B. Sears]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The essay frames natural resources as a single system with close mutual relations, in which biology supplies the knowledge of living organisms and their relations with the environment (mainly soil and water).
- Protection is justified by mystical, aesthetic and scientific motives; conservation is justified by sustained use of living resources. The author argues that a strictly protectionist approach is impossible in modern society, citing India's sacred cows as an example of a policy he considers unacceptable.
- The author adds an ethical dimension, invoking [[Aldo Leopold]] and his "land ethic," and argues that resource use should avoid unnecessary damage to the environment, to natural beauty, and to the scientific value of intact ecosystems.
- The essay presents a schematic diagram linking disciplines such as geography, geology, biochemistry and engineering (base level), human sciences and education (lateral), and agronomy, zootechnics, economics and law (upper level), with biology at the centre. The diagram itself is not reproduced here.

## Key Findings

- **Interdisciplinarity is necessary:** renewable resource problems are multifaceted and cannot be solved by isolated disciplines. Specialists need a panoramic view ("visión panorámica") of how their field connects with others.
- **No single expert exists:** the author concludes that no professional can be a complete conservation expert, and that such a person could not be trained without becoming superficially encyclopaedic.
- **Biology as the central discipline:** all management policy for living resources depends on knowledge of their biology and ecology, which the author places at the core of the interdisciplinary framework.
- **Role of economics and law:** economics is responsible for resource evaluation, planning and supervision; law translates biological, social, technical and economic information into binding norms.
- **Proposed solution:** the author proposes short "Seminarios de Conservación" for graduates (holding a degree above Bachiller) with core courses on the History of Conservation, the Philosophy of Conservation and Techniques for Conservation Planning. The aim is to create teams of specialists with a shared panoramic view, led by biologists.
- **Critique of existing courses:** the author's semester-long courses lacked laboratories, specialised libraries and funds for field trips, limiting their impact. He calls for longer, better-equipped courses in all preparatory and professional schools, and for conservation concepts to be diluted into primary and secondary curricula rather than taught as a separate subject.
- **Pending work:** the author reported that he was writing a textbook on the subject under IMRNR sponsorship.
