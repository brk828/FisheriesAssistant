# Characteristics of Fish Populations in Upper Mississippi River Backwater Areas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service report (Circular 212, March 1965) by Lyle M. Christenson and Lloyd L. Smith quantifies the fish populations of three backwater areas on the upper [[Mississippi River]]: [[Miller Lake]], Area A and Area B. Populations were sampled by rotenone poisoning of blocked-off areas in 1947–1952 under the [[Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee]]. The study measured standing crop, species composition, size and age structure, growth rates, and the influence of environmental factors, including water fertility, bottom soils, food availability and water-level fluctuation.

## Background & Description

The study was prompted by a lack of detailed data on warm-water fish populations in river backwaters, which had been created by [[Navigation Dams]] on the upper river. At normal pool levels these backwaters connect to the main and side channels and form an important part of the total fish-producing water.

- **Miller Lake:** 11.9 acres in pool 8, about 2 miles below La Crosse, Wisconsin; sampled August 15, 1957 (as printed in the source tables; the text also gives 1947 as the collection date). Bottom silt; heavy coontail growth; turbidity 320 ppm; water temperature 80°F.
- **Area A:** 1.35 acres, maximum depth 4.5 ft, average 2.4 ft; sampled July 6, 1948; sparse vegetation except Sagittaria at the closed end.
- **Area B:** 3.36 acres in 1948 (about 3.0–3.1 acres in later years); maximum depth 9 ft; sampled July 1948, August 1949 and 1951, and September 1952. Vegetation was more diverse, dominated by Sagittaria, with Potamogeton natans and Vallisneria present.

Methods: a 0.75-inch mesh seine closed each area, and toxicant (derris root at 5 percent rotenone, or emulsified rotenone in 1951–1952) was applied at 0.50–0.75 ppm. Fish were recovered for 3 days, with unrecovered young estimated. Fish were grouped into six classes: predators, game fish, panfish, catfish, rough fish and forage fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Collection efficiency:** Marked fish and cage tests in Area B in 1951 indicated a near-complete kill and about 100 percent recovery by weight. At Miller Lake, netting before poisoning allowed fish to escape. Only 15.9 percent of marked fish were recovered, so Miller Lake data are used with limitation.
- **Water fertility:** Total phosphorus in and near Area B ranged from 0.091 to 0.114 ppm and total nitrogen from 0.69 to 1.43 ppm. Total alkalinity was 100–115 ppm. The author concludes that chemical fertility of the upper Mississippi River is high.
- **Bottom soils:** Area B had a silty clay with 7.49 percent organic content, compared with 2.64 percent in the adjacent side channel and 0.7 percent in the main channel (coarse sand).
- **Food availability:** Bottom fauna in three sloughs near Trempealeau was dominated by Cyclops, cladocerans, ostracods and mayflies (Hexagenia). Mayflies were abundant in Area B in 1948. Production compares favorably with Lake Mendota.
- **Water-level fluctuation:** Standing crop in Area B could not be related to water-level changes. Fish move into and out of backwaters as levels change, and recolonisation occurs after drawdown or winter-kill.

## Key Findings

**Standing crop**
- Six collections gave standing crops of 39.1 to 604.9 pounds per acre, averaging 248.4 pounds per acre (about 225 pounds without adult gizzard shad).
- Miller Lake produced 3,798 fish and 153.8 pounds per acre; Area A produced 1,617 fish and 39.1 pounds per acre.
- Area B standing crops were 217.0 (1948), 604.9 (1949), 202.4 (1951) and 272.6 pounds per acre (1952). Adjusted average is about 285 pounds per acre.

**Species composition (adjusted Area B average, by weight)**
- Rough fish 38%, predators 27%, panfish 13%, catfish 11%, game fish 12%, forage fish 1%.
- Average pounds per acre: rough fish 112, predators 77, panfish 36, catfish 30, game fish 29, forage fish 2.
- Most important species: bowfin (predators), northern pike and largemouth bass (game fish), bluegill, black and white crappie (panfish), channel catfish, gizzard shad, and carp, spotted sucker and freshwater drum (rough fish).

**Size and age**
- Very few fish were older than 5 years. Most game fish and panfish were under 3 years old.
- Largemouth bass, bluegill, black crappie, white crappie and sauger were each aged; the oldest black crappies were of age groups IV and V.
- Young bowfin were rare in backwaters in summer.

**Growth**
- Growth of most species exceeded that reported for most north-central waters and downstream Mississippi River sections.
- Exceptions: carp in Clear Lake, Iowa; largemouth bass in Onized Lake, Illinois; and black crappie in Onized Lake and Red Haw Lake.
- Growth of channel catfish and sauger was faster in the lower river, so species, not just river section, must be considered when assessing growth potential.

**Comparisons**
- Standing crops in southern Mississippi River backwaters were larger, with more rough fish and fewer predators and game fish.
- Standing crops in area B exceeded those of northern Wisconsin and Minnesota lakes (about 50 pounds per acre in northern waters, per Thompson).

**Conclusions**
- Standing crop fluctuated between years, and the rough fish group varied most. Catfish were the most stable group.
- Differences in standing crop could not be linked to water level.
- Benthic fauna has changed since the locks and dams were built, but production per unit area is comparable to pre-dam levels.
- Overall, the upper Mississippi backwaters are highly productive for their latitude.
