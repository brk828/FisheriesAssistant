# South America West of the Maracaibo, Orinoco, Amazon, and Titicaca Basins: Horizontal Distribution of Fresh-Water Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae #loricariidae #pimelodidae

## Executive Summary

This Indiana University Studies monograph summary (Study No. 45, June 1920, Contribution No. 176 of the Zoological Laboratory) by [[Carl H. Eigenmann]] reports the freshwater fish fauna of the Pacific slope of South America west of the Andes of Bogota, from eastern Panama through western Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru south to Pacasmayo. The study is based on several expeditions (1912–1919) and on earlier collections by the [[Smithsonian Institution]] and [[Field Museum]]. It presents a checklist of 345 numbered taxa, tabulated by river basin, and argues that the [[Andes Mountains]] divided an ancient continental fauna into cis-Andean and trans-Andean sections, which then evolved independently.

## Background & Description

The paper continues earlier Indiana University Studies (Nos. 16, 18, 19, 20, 23, 24, and 25). It is a summary; fully illustrated chapters were planned for journals and a monograph was "ready for the printer."

**Collecting expeditions** (seven sources):

1. **Eigenmann "Reconnaissance of Colombia" (1912):** collections from Cartagena up the [[Magdalena River]] to Bogota, west to Buenaventura, and along the [[San Juan River]] and [[Atrato River]]. Manuel Gonzales collected supplementary material around Bogota.
2. **Landon-Fisher Expedition to Colombia (Henn and Wilson):** Rio Telembi ([[Patia River]] basin), upper San Juan, Atrato, and Truando (1913).
3. **Landon Ecuadorian Expedition (Henn):** Patia headwaters, lower San Juan, and the Chone, Portoviejo, and [[Guayas River]] basins, plus Ecuadorian highlands (1913–1914).
4. **Irwin Expedition to Peru, Bolivia, and Chile:** only partly relevant; Chira, Piura, Jequetepeque, [[Rimac River]], and Chili rivers in Peru (1918–1919).
5. **University of Michigan Expedition to Santa Marta:** material lent by Dr. A. G. Ruthven.
6. **Collections from Colombia** made under Hermano Apolinar Maria of the Instituto de la Salle, Bogota.
7. **Seth E. Meek and S. F. Hildebrand collections** for the Smithsonian Institution and Field Museum.

Types and series are held by the [[Carnegie Museum]] and Indiana University. Many species recognized were new to science.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The Pacific slope is about 4,000 miles long and rarely more than 100 miles wide, moving from wet tropical conditions in the north to dry and cold conditions toward Cape Horn. Rainfall shows a steep [[Rainfall Gradient]]:

- Canal Zone: over 200 inches per year; Buenaventura: reportedly 250–400 inches.
- South of the Esmeraldas River the climate becomes progressively arid.
- Coastal Peru to Copiapo: under 1 inch per year. Serena, Chile: about 2–8.5 inches (1869–1910); Santiago: 4–31 inches; Concepcion: 26.6–40 inches (1876–1910); Valdivia: 73–143 inches (1872–1910); Puerto Montt: 71–128 inches.
- Rain shadows occur within wet Colombia (upper Dagua and Cauca).

**Topography and drainage.** The crest of the western Cordilleras forms the continental divide over most of Peru, exceeding 14,000 feet except at a dip near Paita (about 6,700 feet). Peruvian Pacific rivers are short, swift, seasonally fluctuating (maximum in March), and often exhausted by irrigation or lost in coastal sands. Desert stretches between them restrict fish movement. The [[Lake Titicaca]] basin occupies the interandean area of southern Peru. In Ecuador, two cordilleras enclose highland parks (6,000–10,000 ft) that drain to both oceans, and the divide shifts repeatedly between the eastern and western chains. The [[Guayas River]] basin was a gulf as late as the Quaternary before being filled with debris.

**Flow examples:** the Rimac at Chosica ranges from about 10 m³/s (September) to 115 m³/s (March); the Jequetepeque ranges from about 5 to 220 m³/s. The Piura, in dry season, shrinks to stagnant pools where fishes concentrate and starve, though some survive.

**Geological history.** The coast Cordillera of Colombia is the youngest major chain (maximum about 1,800 m); the [[Cordillera de Bogota]] is largely Cretaceous sedimentary rock. The [[Cauca River]] is the oldest present western Colombian river; the [[Magdalena River]] developed with the Cordillera de Bogota; the San Juan and Atrato are the youngest. Lowering the land about 200 feet would link these valleys into bays or a strait.

## Key Findings

**Checklist.** The horizontal distribution table lists 345 numbered taxa, each marked by presence in Panama (Canal Zone, Chepo, Chagres, Tuyra basins), the Atlantic (Magdalena, Atrato, San Juan, Dagua basins), and Pacific-slope basins (Esmeraldas to Portoviejo, Guayaquil, Patia to Santiago, Paita–Chira–Piura, Pacasmayo). Notable groups include:

- Catfishes (Pimelodidae, Doradidae, Auchenipteridae, Aspredinidae) and Loricariidae, including [[Pygidium]] (entries 59–79) and [[Astroblepus]] (entries 38–55, 18 taxa).
- Characins (Characidae, Curimatidae, Prochilodontidae, Characidium), including many [[Astyanax]], [[Brycon]], and [[Hemibrycon]] species.
- Electric knifefishes (Gymnotiformes), killifishes and livebearers (Poeciliidae), and Rivulidae.
- Marine or estuarine taxa (e.g., sawfishes *Pristis* and several Centropomidae and Mugilidae) listed as entering river mouths, with the Centropomus records noted as very incomplete.

**Answers to the nine distribution questions:**

1. Fish composition west of the Bogota Cordillera and on the Ecuador–Peru Pacific slope is given in the checklist.
2. The ancestral fauna came partly from Central America, partly from the ocean, and largely from a common origin with the present Orinoco and Amazon faunas.
3. The ancient South American fauna was split by the rising Andes into cis-Andean and trans-Andean sections, which then evolved independently into many peculiar genera and species.
4. The Guayas and Magdalena faunas had little or no intermigration. The Guayas fauna was separated from the Amazon, and the Magdalena from the Orinoco.
5–7. The [[Chagres River]] fauna arrived partly from the north and partly from the south (via the Atrato through the Tuyra and Chepo), mostly during the lifetime of its present species.
8. Western Peru north of the Rimac is a relict of the Guayas fauna; south of the Rimac it partly reflects the Chilean fauna.
9. The San Juan and Atrato are separated by a divide only slightly above 300 feet, yet their fishes differ more than the Paraguay and Amazon faunas. The Atrato-San Juan valley has served as a limited highway between the Atlantic and Pacific drainages.

**Other conclusions:**

- Some genera peculiar to the west and widely distributed north and south are likely older than the Andes.
- Many Magdalena species are identical on both sides of the Cordillera de Bogota, suggesting their markings predate the mountains.
- Interandean drainage patterns reflect either headward erosion by rivers such as the [[Patia River]] or late uplift of the Cordillera de Bogota; the author leaves this question open.
