# Rainbow Trout Fishery of the Kennebec River Between Wyman Dam and Solon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report documents a 1954–1955 study of the [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery in the [[Kennebec River]] between [[Wyman Dam]] and [[Solon Dam]], Maine, with attention to temperature, flow, spawning tributaries, age and growth, and the effects of hatchery stocking. Rainbow trout were introduced to the system in 1933 and now maintain themselves through natural reproduction, producing a sport fishery that yields a small total catch. The author recommends no further stocking of any fish species in the study section, discontinuing [[Brook Trout]] plantings, and no new angling restrictions, and warns against introducing [[Largemouth Bass]] or [[White Perch]] upstream of Solon Dam.

## Background & Description

Rainbow trout were introduced into [[Pleasant Pond Stream]] in 1933. Pleasant Pond is an impounded area of the Kennebec River. The study area covered the Kennebec River and its tributaries from Wyman Dam to the Solon flowage above the town of Solon. The main fishery is concentrated in the first four miles below Wyman Dam.

The Kennebec River at Bingham drains 2,710 square miles, and its flow is regulated by reservoirs and two power plants. U.S. Geological Survey records cited in the report give a minimum of 110 c.f.s. in December 1947 and a maximum of 58,800 c.f.s. in March (year not legible in source).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Main river habitat.** The river between Wyman Dam and the head of Solon Slough offers excellent salmonid habitat in terms of water quality. Its bottom is mainly rubble, from small to fairly large, with some fine sand, and it contains many good trout spawning and nursery areas. Spawning occurs in the main river, but no redds were observed there during the study because of the large volume of flow.

**Temperature.** Water below Wyman Lake is cold for a river of this type. Temperatures were nearly optimal for rainbow trout production. The cold summer water is attributed to withdrawal from the bottom of Wyman Lake, which is cooler than the surface layer. On July 2, surface water in Wyman Lake was 61°F, and the river below Wyman Dam was warmer at about 71°F (OCR-ambiguous reading).

**Human impacts.** The annual pulpwood drive was observed during the study. Mechanical damage to spawning areas was not seen, because of the high flow, but large deposits of bark and some wood chips were noted.

**Tributaries.**

- **[[Austin Stream]]**: The largest tributary. Rainbow trout are present throughout its lower portion and are reproducing well. Its wooded sections and tributaries are considered favorable for trout production. A falls about two miles above the Gulf Stream confluence blocks upstream movement. Low summer flows were recorded, and a tributary reached a low of 9.5 c.f.s. in July 1955.
- **[[Joe Foss Brook]]**: A spring-fed brook entering the Kennebec about one-half mile below Wyman Dam. Its entire length is used by both rainbow and brook trout. Temperatures stayed at or below 60°F in July, with random readings ranging from 40°F in April to 61°F on July 20.
- **[[Meadow Brook]]**: Bottom composed mainly of mud and sand, which is less productive than the rubble bottoms of other streams. It maintains a fair flow and produces fish for the fishery.
- **[[Jackson Brook]]**: About five miles long, with an annual spawning migration and the greatest flow fluctuation of the streams studied. Flow ranged from about 3 c.f.s. to 20 c.f.s. in 1954. In June 1955 the brook held only seepage, and small rainbows were trapped in isolated pools. Its habitat is limited by an impassable falls about one mile above the mouth. Its contribution to the main fishery is likely minor in normal years.
- **Unnamed tributaries (1–6)**: Tributaries 1 and 3 have impassable falls near their mouths, and no rainbows were seen below them. Tributary 2 (fed by Merrill Pond and springs) produced no rainbows and was dropped from the study. Tributaries 4 and 5 are very small and do not carry sufficient flow in spring. Tributary 6 was not surveyed.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and reproduction**

- Spawning began around April 20 (summary) and was observed in the spring of both years. Redds were counted on June 17 in a 300-yard section of Joe Foss Brook in 1955.
- Fry were seen in large numbers by July 15 in all streams accessible to rainbows.
- One redd was excavated on Joe Foss Brook on April 30, 1954. Of the eggs recovered, 699 (about 90 percent) were fertilized. The report notes that this single sample cannot support definitive conclusions.

**Age and growth** (19 fish sampled by Warden Service, 1954–1955; ages read from scales)

- Age Group II: 8.0 to 14.3 inches (approximately)
- Age Group III: 12.3 to 19.2 inches
- Age Group IV: 15.7 to 17.6 inches
- Age Group V: 16.7 to 24.1 inches
- Age Group VI: 21.0 to 21.3 inches
- Spawning usually occurred at the beginning of the fourth growing season. Fish 1, 5, 12 and 16 had spawned twice, and fish 9 had spawned three times. The author describes the growth as very good, though the sample is too small for firm conclusions.

**Stocking history**

- From 1933 to 1937, 100,219 rainbow trout were stocked in the watershed above the study area (Pleasant Pond Stream, Kennebec River at Caratunk, Wyman Lake).
- A final stocking of 856 large adult rainbows (15–16 inches) was made in the Kennebec at Bingham in November 1954. Fish had completed three growing seasons in the hatchery. Their ventral fins were removed to mark them.
- Creel checks from April 15 to June 19 recovered 41 marked fish. Warden Walker estimated 150 to 200 marked fish were taken, a return of about 17 to 23 percent.
- Rearing costs were about $0.80 per pound, and the report estimates the cost to the angler at over $6.50 per trout caught. The author doubts many of the fish will be caught after the 1955 season.

**Recommendations**

1. No stocking of any fish species in the Kennebec River or its tributaries from Wyman Dam to Solon Dam. Natural reproduction is sufficient to support good angling.
2. Discontinue brook trout stocking, since past plantings did not materially raise the brook trout harvest and competing salmonids are not considered wise management.
3. No new restrictions on time or method of rainbow trout angling, given the success observed under current regulations.
4. Take extreme care to prevent introduction of bass or white perch above Solon Dam, because these species are not known to occur there and could endanger the important upper Kennebec salmonid waters.

**Overall conclusion.** The rainbow trout population is maintaining itself through natural reproduction, and the sport fishery, though small in total catch, is considered well established.
