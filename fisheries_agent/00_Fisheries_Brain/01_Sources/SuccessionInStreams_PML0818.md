# Succession in Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This chapter by [[Stuart G. Fisher]] (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University) argues that stream ecology and [[Ecological Succession]] theory can inform each other. It extends the classical definition of succession, change in community composition after disturbance, to two dimensions: site-specific temporal succession and [[Longitudinal Succession]] along the stream gradient. The author concludes that streams offer unusually good settings for testing succession hypotheses, that stream succession is stochastic and strongly shaped by disturbance timing and severity, and that upstream communities can strongly influence downstream ones through unidirectional transport of materials. The document is a book chapter; it contains no original field dataset, and most quantitative statements are drawn from cited studies.

## Background & Description

The chapter traces succession theory from early phytosociology ([[Cowles]], [[Clements]], [[Gleason]]) to the modern debate between the Gleasonian individualistic school and the Clementsian holistic school. Fisher notes that the concept has been largely botanical and that [[Drury and Nisbet]] (1973) restricted generalization to temperate forests, while [[McIntosh]] (1980) questioned whether any general pattern exists.

Key conceptual points:

- **Definition:** Succession is change in community composition after disturbance. Recovery can be measured by species composition, richness and dominance.
- **Two basic elements:** colonization and subsequent change. Colonization is stochastic because colonists are not initially present at the site.
- **Causes (Tansley 1929):** allogenic forcing functions (external, abiotic, such as seasonal, climatic and geomorphic cycles) versus autogenic changes (biotic, such as competition and predation).
- **Connell and Slatyer (1977):** their null hypothesis holds that succession follows from individual life-history traits; alternative interactive hypotheses hold that communities inhibit or facilitate their successors. Fisher argues these are not mutually exclusive.
- **Schools of thought:** the Gleasonian individualistic school (e.g., [[Connell and Slatyer]], [[Pickett]]) versus the Clementsian holistic school (e.g., [[Odum]], [[Margalef]]), which views succession as an analogue of organismal development.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Streams are presented as favourable but under-used systems for succession research:

- **Disturbance regime:** scouring floods occur at statistical frequencies; severe washout return intervals usually extend two years or more, allowing multiple generations to develop. Colonization by aerial vectors, drift or upstream movement can be measured.
- **Constraints:** large rivers rarely flood severely enough to reset biota; small temperate streams flood too frequently; in frequently flooded streams, return periods can be shorter than organism life cycles.
- **Seasonality:** seasonal change in streams can confound succession patterns. Fisher's ideal stream for succession study has (1) severe flood disturbance, (2) several generations between disturbances, (3) no minor disturbances between catastrophes, and (4) minimal seasonal effects. Hot desert streams approximate this because succession can be completed within a single season.
- **Disturbance types:** flooding, dredging, desiccation, pesticide application, coal ash, drought and hail storms, plus colonization of newly inundated substrates.
- **Recovery rates:** flood-damaged streams recover in a month to several years. Diatom communities recover in a month or two, fishes may need several years (Hanson and Waters 1974: four to five years for a brook trout population without immigration), and insects are intermediate. Recovery is accelerated when colonization routes are open. Larimore et al. (1959) reported a two-week recovery for a fish community where all population increase was by immigration.
- **Sycamore Creek, Arizona:** post-flood colonizers are always diatoms, later replaced by bluegreen or Cladophora-dominated assemblages depending on local conditions (Fisher et al. 1982).
- **Longitudinal context:** fish distribution has been linked to slope, depth, stream order and temperature; invertebrates change downstream with substrate, temperature, discharge, current velocity and food availability.
- **Long-term cycles:** detrital turnover in small northwestern streams approaches 100 years; desert cienegas in the Southwest supported large marshes until roughly the turn of the century, when overgrazing and climate change led to arroyo cutting and drainage.

## Key Findings

1. **Succession applies to streams in two dimensions.** Site-specific temporal succession follows disturbance; longitudinal succession is the sequence of communities from headwaters to large rivers. Fisher proposes a redefinition: succession is "a sequence of communities in which the ascendancy of each is influenced by its predecessors," resolved in time or space.
2. **Colonization is stochastic.** Initial colonization depends on timing, magnitude and spatial extent of disturbance, and on abiotic conditions during colonization. Egler's (1954) initial floristics model may apply, but is hard to test in systems dominated by small, easily overlooked organisms.
3. **Interactive autogenic succession is poorly tested.** Diatom inhibition altered pioneer algal communities (Dickman 1974). Spontaneous detachment of algal mats is required for later communities to establish (Moore 1976; Eichenberger and Wuhrmann 1975). Evidence for or against interactive models in streams is lacking.
4. **Collective properties are under-studied.** Biomass, chlorophyll-a and primary production increase during succession, a pattern Peters (1976) called tautologous. Ecosystem generalizations from Odum (1969) mostly hold at Sycamore Creek, except for attributes influenced by the open nature of streams (e.g., P/R does not approach unity; net production is lost as fluvial export).
5. **No evidence of increased resistance.** Dominant organisms at all stages remain characteristic of frequently disturbed ecosystems; flooding is too severe to be biologically ameliorated.
6. **Upstream communities can control downstream ones.** Through organic matter processing, nutrient spiralling and transport, upstream communities alter downstream nutrient concentrations and food quality (e.g., Kaushik and Hynes 1971; Vannote et al. 1980 river continuum concept). Influence is asymmetric, with stronger effects downstream.
7. **Streams are useful for hypothesis testing.** Artificial uniform channels and recirculating "Round River" designs could separate physiographic from biotic effects. Partial scouring experiments could test Connell and Slatyer's facilitation and inhibition models. Under facilitation, streams recover from the bottom up; under inhibition, from the top down.
8. **Patchiness and time scales complicate analysis.** In Sycamore Creek, algae form distinct patches with predictable but probabilistic transitions. Fisher suggests extending Horn's (1975) Markovian matrix approach to algal communities, and notes that a community is truncated when disturbance interval is shorter than organism life span.
9. **Spectrum of lotic succession.** Heterotrophic New England streams with weekly spates may show little succession, whereas autotrophic Arizona streams flooding annually show marked succession. Longer cycles, such as cienega formation and drainage, also occur.
