# Strategies for Reducing Risks from Introductions of Aquatic Organisms: A Philosophical Perspective

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-introductions

## Executive Summary

This 1986 perspective paper by [[Walter R. Courtenay Jr.]] and [[Jeffrey N. Taylor]], published in *Fisheries* (Vol. 11, No. 2), argues that introductions of aquatic organisms in North America have often been made as expedients rather than on sound ecological grounds. The authors state that the risk of any introduction lies in the species itself and its impact on the receiving system, not in whether it is exotic or a native transplant. They call for introductions to be decided only as biological decisions, supported by research, independent review, and education, and for a policy from the [[American Fisheries Society]] to guide state and provincial agencies. The paper focuses on fishes, though it notes that many plants and invertebrates were introduced in similar ways.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** North American aquatic introductions include plants, invertebrates and fishes. Most are transplants of native forms, and many others are exotics from other nations.
- **Agricultural precedent:** Growth of agriculture was built largely on introductions, which led to the assumption that introductions are inherently good. The authors note that cultivated organisms are usually far removed from wild ancestors, need human care, and are poor wild competitors, so their introductions were generally beneficial. Management of these cultivars (deforestation, overgrazing, pesticides, water withdrawal) has sometimes harmed native biota.
- **Nonagricultural introductions:** These can be beneficial but also destructive. Sought-after traits such as sport fishing or biological control may not prove viable, and unpredicted side effects are common.
- **Terminology:** The authors move away from the "accidental vs. purposeful" classification toward a new system they expect will be better understood.
- **Pathways:** Unintentional releases include [[Aquarium Trade]] and [[Bait Fish]] releases, ship ballast water, contaminated shipments, canals and water diversions, and escapes from [[Aquaculture]] facilities. Intentional introductions include stocking, range extension, and releases for sport, weed or insect control, forage, and species protection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Receiving-system readjustment:** Every introduction requires some readjustment in the receiving system. Effects can be minor or major and may appear decades later.
- **Risk categories:** Taylor et al. (1984) identified habitat alteration, introduction of parasites and diseases, trophic alteration, hybridization potential, and spatial alteration.
- **Decline of native species:** Introductions have been implicated in, and in some cases proven responsible for, declines and extinctions of native fishes, including in the Southwest (Minckley and Deacon 1968).
- **Hybridization:** Trout gene pools were altered through aerial stocking of western trouts beyond historical ranges. Northern largemouth bass populations were affected by introductions of the Florida subspecies (Philipp et al. 1981). The authors note that intracontinental introductions carry higher hybridization risk than intercontinental ones (Hocutt 1984).
- **Replacement of native fauna:** Some tilapias in the Gila and lower Colorado rivers near Yuma, Arizona, have largely replaced previously transplanted native fishes.
- **Aquaculture escape risk:** Growing aquaculture is expected to increase escapes. Regulation should be biologically sound. For example, restricting tilapia makes no sense where escapees could not overwinter in the wild.

## Key Findings

- **Scale of introductions (U.S. and Canada):** Of 41 established exotic fishes in the U.S., 31 were introduced unintentionally. Of 743 native freshwater fishes in Canada and the continental U.S., at least 168 species (151 plus 17 added later) had been transplanted beyond their natural ranges. The authors consider this figure conservative, and up to 79 of these transplants may have been unintentional.
- **Mexico:** Of 55 introduced fishes, 29 were transplants and 26 were exotics. Most introductions there were for aquaculture (32 species: 15 exotics, 17 transplants), while only four were for recreational fishing.
- **Motivations:** U.S. and Canadian introductions were mostly for game species or came from bait-bucket releases, reflecting the importance of recreational fishing. In the U.S., an estimated 53 species were moved as game fish and perhaps 58 were released unintentionally from bait buckets, and 11 were moved for species survival and protection.
- **Mixed-fish transfers:** Moving target and non-target fishes together, as "incidentals" during seining, complicated analyses and spread species across drainages.
- **Grass carp and tilapia:** Grass carp used for weed control have shown no major disasters so far, though results are not yet in. The authors found little evidence that tilapia introductions solved weed problems.
- **Core principles:** Introductions should be approved only as biological decisions, never as management or political ones. The agency researching a candidate species should not evaluate its own work. Where species may cross state, provincial, or international borders, independent review panels should be mandatory.
- **Recommendations:** The authors call for education for biologists, managers and the public, protocols that reduce political interference, regulation of imported culture species and escape-proof facilities, and a policy from the [[American Fisheries Society]] that aligns with [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] and [[International Council for the Exploration of the Seas]] efforts. The AFS Introduced Fish Section was drafting such a policy (Kohler and Courtenay 1985).
- **Key quotation (Regier 1968):** "We choose exotics on the basis of what they can do for us and not primarily on what they can do for the non-human system."
