# History of Fishes of the Great Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 lecture abstract by G.R. Smith (Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan) reviews the history, distribution, and conservation of the fishes of the [[Great Basin]] in Utah and Nevada. The region holds 36 native species, a small number that nonetheless reflects its geological history and isolation. Smith argues that the fauna is shaped by long-term isolation, Pleistocene climate fluctuations, and recent human impacts, and that native species deserve protection for scientific as well as economic reasons. He also contends that stability, not fluctuation, promoted diversification in the region's fish history.

## Background & Description

- **Discovery:** Fishes were first collected by railroad-route expeditions in 1853. The [[Gunnison and Beckwith Surveys]] sent specimens to the [[Smithsonian Institution]], where they were described in 1856.
- **Definition of the region:** Smith defines the Great Basin hydrologically as waters with no outflow in the last several thousand years. This includes [[Lake Bonneville]], [[Lake Lahontan]], [[Death Valley]], south-central Oregon lakes, and isolated basins in central Nevada. Drainages recently connected to the [[Snake River]] and [[Colorado River]] are excluded.
- **Taxonomic composition:** The 36 native species are assigned to six families. The text names five: Salmonidae (trouts and whitefish), Cyprinidae (minnows), Catostomidae (suckers), Cyprinodontidae (pupfish), and Cottidae (sculpins). The sixth family is not named.
- **Major groups:**
  - **Trouts:** Native [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Redband Trout]], and a dolly varden trout were important food fishes. Overexploitation had mostly eliminated them by the 1930s. They were replaced largely by [[Rainbow Trout]] from California and Yellowstone cutthroat trout from Wyoming.
  - **Whitefish:** Three of four species are endemic to [[Bear Lake]], described as the last large, unspoiled remnant of Great Basin waters. Two of these lived in Lake Bonneville.
  - **Minnows and suckers:** 21 species, valued mainly as forage and hatchery chow. Their fossil record in the Great Basin extends back more than 15 million years.
  - **Sculpins:** Originally four species. Two were endemic to [[Utah Lake]] and Bear Lake. The Utah Lake species was lost to pollution about 50 years before the lecture. The Bear Lake species survives.
  - **Pupfish:** Seven species in small springs and desert streams in Death Valley and southern Nevada, including the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Isolation:** Great Basin fishes have no aquatic connections to surrounding drainages. Species entered or left isolated basins only through rare events such as high-water spillovers and headwater stream captures. This has produced localized, isolated species to a degree Smith describes as unmatched over most of the world.
- **Aridity and fossils:** Arid conditions have eroded soils and sediments, exposing lake beds with fossils of the ancestors of living fishes. Fossils from the [[Pleistocene]] are generally similar to living species. Older lake-bed fossils from before the ice age usually differ from modern species.
- **Pliocene lakes:** Ancient lakes north of the Great Basin, from Cache Valley to Boise, Idaho, were stable for around one million years. They supported more species of fishes, snails, clams, ostracods, and other aquatic organisms than any other lake in western North America. Most of these species went extinct during the Pleistocene, which Smith attributes to water loss during hot, dry interglacial periods rather than cold climates.
- **Drainage history:** Several species are found across the divide in the upper Snake River, which reflects former drainage connections. The [[Bear River]] once flowed north to the Snake. Its diversion into the Great Basin raised Lake Bonneville until it spilled over past Pocatello into the Snake.
- **Diversity comparison:** The 36 native species occupy an area of over 100,000 square miles. An area of similar size in the [[Great Lakes]] would hold no fewer than 150 species. Smith attributes the difference to Pleistocene isolation and climatic instability.
- **Human impacts:** Water removal and water-quality degradation from cultural activity continue to drive fish extinctions. Shoreline development around Bear Lake may threaten its four endemic species.
- **Introduced species:** Species such as [[Largemouth Bass]], sunfish, perch, [[Walleye]], brook trout, and lake trout were introduced from the Midwest in the past 100 years. Introduced species now outnumber the native fauna. [[Carp]], originally from Asia, are described as more harmful than useful. Smith notes that many native species are unaccustomed to competitors and are therefore vulnerable to them.

## Key Findings

1. **Low native diversity with high endemism:** The Great Basin's 36 native species are small in number but highly localized, with most found nowhere else.
2. **Stability over fluctuation:** Smith's reading of the fossil record suggests that stable conditions promoted diversification, while the repeated fluctuations from great lakes to desert environments over the past two million years caused extinction and reduced diversity.
3. **Limited support for one theory:** Smith finds little evidence that fluctuating lake levels and intermittent isolation drove large-scale speciation, contrary to a popular theory.
4. **Dispersal matters:** Biogeographic evidence shows that dispersal, which another current theory discounts, is significant in how Great Basin fishes adapt and repopulate their environments.
5. **Subdued Pleistocene change:** Pleistocene changes in fossil fishes are small compared with the scale of environmental change. Smith suggests the intervals between climate reversals may have been too short for lasting adaptation, possibly producing generalized tolerances for both wet and dry conditions.
6. **Conservation priorities:** Smith argues that the endemic Bear Lake species deserve protection comparable to the Devil's Hole pupfish. He grounds conservation in scientific value, applied value in resource management, ecology, and pharmacology, and respect for natural populations, not only economic value.
7. **Natural experiment:** The Great Basin's isolation and documented geological and fossil history make it a long-term "natural experiment" for studying survival, adaptation, and extinction.
8. **Competing pressures:** Recreational stocking of introduced species and protection of native fauna are in tension, because native species are vulnerable to new competitors.
