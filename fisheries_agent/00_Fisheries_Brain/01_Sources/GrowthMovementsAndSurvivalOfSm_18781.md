# New York Fish and Game Journal Vol. 8 No. 2 (1961): Smallmouth Bass, Otter Diet, and Canvasback Literature

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #micropterus-dolomieu #lutra-canadensis

## Executive Summary

This compiled journal issue contains three items. The first is only a literature-cited page from a [[Canvasback]] distribution and mortality study. The second is a full research paper by John L. Forney on the growth, movements, survival and exploitation of [[Smallmouth Bass]] in [[Oneida Lake]], New York, based on tagging from 1954 to 1958. The third is a paper by V. T. Hamilton on the late fall, winter and early spring diet of the [[North American River Otter]] in the [[Adirondack Region]]. The bass study is the most substantial: it finds localized bass populations, growth comparable to northern lakes, a 0.55 catch-curve survival, and a natural mortality estimate of 0.08. The otter study shows that fish dominate the diet of otters but that crayfish and frogs are also important.

## Background & Description

**Smallmouth bass study (Forney).** Smallmouth bass ranked second only to [[Pikeperch]] as the most sought game fish in Oneida Lake. Tagging was begun in 1954 by New York State Conservation Department biologists and continued from 1956 to 1958 by the Warm Water Fisheries Investigations Unit at [[Cornell University]]. The five-year program aimed to assess the population's status and provide a basis for evaluating future changes in fishing regulations.

Oneida Lake covers 51,000 acres and is about 20 miles long and 5 miles wide. Its maximum depth is 55 feet, and 25 per cent of its area is under 12 feet deep. Wave-exposed shorelines are mainly rubble and boulders, with sand or mud at depths over 12 feet.

Bass were collected in May by [[Oneida Fish Hatchery]] personnel using trapnets, held in hatchery ponds for spawning, and released in early June. Each fish was measured, scale-sampled and tagged with a monel jaw strap (about 2½ × 1 inch, supplied by Salt Lake Stamp Company). The strap loosely encircled the mandible and was shortened for 10- to 12-inch bass, the smallest size tagged.

**Otter diet study (Hamilton).** The otter (*Lutra c. canadensis*) has a fairly extensive distribution in the Adirondacks and occurs sporadically elsewhere in New York. It appears to have extended its range and increased in numbers over the past 20 years. Anglers often blame otters for poor fishing on trout waters. No published New York data on otter food existed at the time, so the author analyzed stomach and intestine contents from 141 otters taken from late fall to early spring.

**Canvasback literature (DeGraff et al. and others).** The first page contains only the literature-cited list for a study of canvasback distribution, migration, banding and mortality in New York and North America. The cited works date from 1944 to 1959 and include [[Waterfowl Banding]] reports and studies of hunting kill and sex vulnerability. No body text is included, so no canvasback findings can be reported.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bass habitat and population structure.** Bass at Oneida Lake form localized groups with limited summer dispersal. Recoveries clustered near tagging sites. Three populations were sampled: Constantia, [[Shackelton Point]] and [[Shackelton Shoals]]. Constantia and Shackelton Shoals are separated by about 3 miles of open water, yet exchange between them was minimal.

**Growth comparisons.** Constantia bass grew substantially faster than those in [[Cayuga Lake]], eastern [[Lake Ontario]] and [[Manitoulin Island]] waters of [[Lake Huron]). They grew slightly slower than bass in [[Lake Erie]]. Growth fluctuated annually and followed the same year-to-year pattern seen in pikeperch scales from Oneida Lake.

**Exploitation and fishing pressure.** Angling pressure changed between seasons. Boat rentals at three Oneida Lake liveries fell from 4,599 in 1956 to 3,024 in 1957, a 33 per cent decrease. Exploitation of bass fell even more sharply, reflecting a change in angling vulnerability (see Key Findings).

**Otter prey base.** Otter food reflected local prey availability. Fish were the most frequent item, but crayfish, frogs, tadpoles and insects were also common. Earlier studies in Michigan, North Carolina and Montana reported similar mixes of forage fish, crayfish and amphibians.

## Key Findings

**Smallmouth bass (Oneida Lake)**

- **Localized populations:** Most tag recoveries occurred near tagging sites. Of 342 first-year recoveries from Constantia releases, 219 had adequate location data, and about 63 per cent of those recoveries were in Zone 1, within 2½ miles of Constantia.
- **Displacement and return:** Bass moved to distant release points (5 to 15 miles away in 1955) mostly returned to the Constantia area by 1956. Bass released at Sylvan Beach, Walnut Point, Cleveland and Lakeport traveled an average straight-line distance of 4.2 to 7.7 miles before capture, compared with 2.4 miles for Constantia bass released in 1954. A chi-square of 43.50 showed the 1955 recovery pattern differed significantly from other years.
- **Growth at Constantia (mean total length, inches):** age III, 9.8; age IV, 12.3; age V, 13.5; age VI, 14.7. Shackelton Shoals bass grew consistently more slowly at every age.
- **Age at recruitment:** Under the 10-inch minimum size limit, bass were recruited to the fishery in their third and fourth growing seasons.
- **Annulus validation:** In a June 1957 sample of 110 bass, an annulus had formed on 97 per cent of II+ and III+ fish, 55 per cent of IV+ fish and 15 per cent of older fish. The scale-length regression came from 577 scales from 3.0 to 19 inches, with non-significant deviation from linearity at the 5 per cent level.
- **Year-class dominance:** The 1948, 1949 and 1952 year classes dominated the Constantia spawning populations from 1954 to 1957. The 1952 class made up 74 per cent of the 1956 spawning population and 65 per cent in 1957.
- **Catch-curve survival:** Geometric mean survival for ages V to X was 0.55. The author noted that this approximates average survival from about 1950 to 1955.
- **Tag-based survival:** Annual survival estimates were 0.48 (1955), 0.42 (1956) and 0.82 (1957). A separate calculation gave 0.32 (1954), 0.51 (1955), 0.41 (1956) and 0.73 (1957). The 1954 value was judged an underestimate.
- **Minimum exploitation:** Rates were 0.18, 0.21 and 0.05 for the three years tested. Exploitation fell from 21.4 per cent in 1956 to 4.6 per cent in 1957, paralleling a drop in pikeperch exploitation from 17.5 to 4.2 per cent.
- **Vulnerability:** Angling vulnerability appeared to drop as length increased, but size-class differences were not significant (chi-square = 4.37). Tagging before the July 1 season opening did not noticeably change angling vulnerability.
- **Natural mortality:** A regression of total mortality on minimum fishing mortality (based on 1955–1958 tag returns) gave an instantaneous natural mortality rate of 0.08. The slope of 2.5 suggests true exploitation may have been 2.5 times the minimum estimate. The author calls the natural mortality estimate a crude approximation.
- **Tag loss and mortality:** No mortality occurred during or right after tagging, and tag loss through mandible erosion seldom occurred. Of 103 tagged bass recovered in nets at Constantia from 1956 to 1958, tags showed no sign of wearing through the jaw.
- **Comparative survival:** Survival was 0.43 in Potomac River bass (Sanderson, 1958) and 0.42 in Lake Michigan bass (Latta, 1958), with an instantaneous natural mortality of 0.54 in the Lake Michigan population.

**North American river otter (Adirondacks)**

- **Sample:** Stomach and intestine contents from 141 otters, taken late fall to early spring.
- **Fish:** Found in 70 per cent of specimens. Minnows were most common at 35.5 per cent, followed by sunfish and black bass (12.1 per cent), perch (9.3 per cent), suckers (6.4 per cent), trout (5 per cent), catfish (2.1 per cent), sculpins (2.1 per cent), mudminnows (1.4 per cent) and pickerel (0.7 per cent). Undetermined fish made up 12.8 per cent.
- **Other prey:** Crayfish were eaten by 34.7 per cent, frogs and tadpoles by 24.8 per cent, aquatic insects by 13.5 per cent, and mammals (including two muskrats) by 4.3 per cent. One otter had eaten a bird.
- **Trapping effort:** An average of 285 otters were trapped and legally tagged per season from 1955–56 to 1958–59.
- **Predation and trout:** [[Ryder]] (1955) concluded that otters take fish in proportion to their abundance and may benefit trout by removing slower, less desirable competitors.
