# Woundfin Transplant Dispute: Historic Range in the Gila Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1985 correspondence file from the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]]. It contains a letter from Director [[Harold F. Olson]] to [[US Fish And Wildlife Service]] official John L. Spinks Jr., a memorandum from John P. Hubbard to Olson, and the relevant citations. It disputes a federal proposal to transplant [[Woundfin]] from the [[Gila Basin]] into the Gila River in southeastern Arizona. The Service had argued that the area lay within the species' historic range. Olson concludes that the woundfin's historic range was confined to the lower Gila River, between the [[Colorado River]] and the Salt River junction, so the transplant cannot be justified and should not proceed. He also argues that the transplant could allow woundfin to move upstream into New Mexico, where habitat is marginal and non-native fishes are present.

## Background & Description

- **Subject species:** [[Woundfin]], a small Gila Basin minnow. Its scientific name is garbled in the scan and is not clearly stated in the text.
- **Proposal:** Published by the Service on April 10, 1984. It proposed transplanting woundfin into southeastern Arizona, with the transplanted population classified as a nonessential experimental population.
- **Service final rule:** Published July 24, 1985 (50 FR 30188–30195), approving the transplant over New Mexico's objections.
- **Olson's earlier letter:** Dated May 7, 1984, it raised three concerns. Olson's November 20, 1985 letter states that these concerns remained after the Service's response.
- **Native fish assemblage:** The historic Gila Basin ichthyofauna comprised 15 species (per [[Miller 1961]] and [[Minckley 1973]]). The memo cites Miller (1961) and Minckley (1973) for the basin's fish records.
- **Author experience:** Olson states he has been involved in such range determinations for over 25 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and elevation (Lower San Francisco River vs. transplant area):**
- Summer water temperatures in the upper Lower San Francisco Box were reported as lower than those in the proposed transplant area. Olson cites [[Montgomery 1985]] for the transplant area. Some summer values are illegible in the scan.
- Winter temperatures in the upper reaches of the Box were 12.2–17.0 °C, versus 7.2–9.6 °C in the lower reaches.
- The lowest point at the upper end of the Lower San Francisco Box is 4600 ft (Pleasanton Valley). The New Mexico–Arizona boundary lies at about 3600 ft, a drop of about 1000 ft over a short river distance.
- Olson argues that habitat in the Box resembles habitat in the transplant area, so temperature and elevation differences are unlikely to limit fish.

**Irrigation dams and flooding:**
- Irrigation diversion dams in New Mexico are low (4–6 ft). They become submerged during floods, and woundfin could move over them during high water.
- Of three barriers on the Gila River in New Mexico, two were apparently destroyed by flooding in 1984; one was an insurmountable barrier still in place on October 2, 1985.

**Competition and non-native fishes:**
- Non-native fishes in southeastern Arizona include [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Red Shiner]]. Olson identifies their abundance as a likely barrier to woundfin establishment.
- Olson argues that woundfin could invade the habitat of [[Spikedace]] in New Mexico, creating management conflicts with that species.

**Historic-range evidence:**
- Surveys in the 19th century covered perhaps nine sites (Minckley 1973). Surveys from 1901–1940 covered more than twice as many sites.
- Woundfin were recorded at only two sites in the Gila Basin: near Yuma (1890–1895) and in the Tempe area (Miller 1961).
- Olson argues that the lack of woundfin records is not due to poor survey coverage, since the same surveys documented all 15 native species.
- The Virgin River in Nevada, where woundfin are periodically abundant, is cited for comparison.

**Lower Gila River decline:**
- The lower Gila River was navigable in 1846 and was largely a dry bed by 1920 (Miller 1961).
- A drought in 1891–1894 compounded intermittency in the 1890s.
- Olson concludes that the loss of habitat in the lower Gila River is the most likely cause of woundfin extirpation.

## Key Findings

**1. Historic range disagreement**
- The Service argued that woundfin occurred widely throughout acceptable Gila River habitats, citing [[Minckley 1973]], [[Minckley et al. 1979]], and Arizona biologists.
- Olson concludes instead that the species was limited to the lower Gila Basin, from the Colorado River to the vicinity of the Salt–Gila junction.
- He says the Service's view is "not tenable" because the historic survey evidence does not support it.

**2. Records and survey adequacy**
- Woundfin were recorded at only two Gila Basin sites: near Yuma (1890–1895) and the Tempe area.
- Surveys documented all 15 native species, so Olson argues the lack of woundfin records is not due to poor sampling.

**3. Coexistence with related fishes**
- Six native minnows occurred in the Tempe area alongside woundfin (Miller 1961). Olson notes that only some of these coexisted with woundfin, and their niches differed.
- Olson argues that competition with [[Spikedace]] probably limited woundfin in most of the Gila Basin.

**4. Elevation error**
- The Service implied native woundfin occurred in the Verde River up to 3400 ft. Minckley (1973) mapped the only known Verde River occurrence as a transplant.
- Olson states the natural altitudinal maximum in Arizona is about 2300 ft lower than the Service's figure. Tempe lies at about 1100 ft.

**5. Recommended course**
- The transplant into the eastern Gila Basin "cannot be justified and therefore should not be made."
- Olson suggests alternative sites, including the [[Hassayampa River]], [[Agua Fria River]], and [[Williams River]], Arizona.
- He warns that, given the small number of woundfin available, placing them in the eastern Gila Basin is "a case of poor judgment at best."

**6. Consistency concern**
- Olson warns that accepting probable historic range as a basis for transplants would require similar reasoning for other species:
  - [[Gila Trout]] in the Mimbres and/or Rio Grande basins
  - [[Socorro Isopod]] in the Rio Alamosa drainage
  - [[Whooping Crane]] in New Mexico
- He argues that restoration should be "confined to areas that are known or highly likely to have been naturally occupied" and that determinations should "not go against nor far beyond the evidence."

**7. Documentation**
- The file includes the Service's final rule (50 FR 30188–30195) and the Hubbard memorandum of November 5, 1985. Copies went to Michael Spear (USFWS), Bud Bristow (Arizona Game and Fish Department), and other New Mexico staff.
