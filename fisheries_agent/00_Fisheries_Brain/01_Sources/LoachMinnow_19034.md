# Determination of Threatened Status for the Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis)

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #threatened-species #gila-river-basin #native-fish-conservation

## Executive Summary

On October 28, 1986, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service published a final rule determining the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) to be a threatened species under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended. This determination was effective November 28, 1986. The loach minnow historically occupied approximately 2,600 kilometers (1,600 miles) of stream habitat in the [[Gila River]] system upstream from Phoenix, Arizona, but now persists in only 380 kilometers (236 miles)—approximately 15 percent of its historic range. The final rule includes a special provision allowing take for certain purposes in accordance with state laws and regulations. Designation of critical habitat was postponed until June 1987 to allow completion of economic analysis. The species faces ongoing threats from habitat destruction, water diversion, exotic predatory and competitive fish species, and proposed dam construction.

## Background & Description

*Tiaroga cobitis* was first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro in Arizona and described by Girard in 1856. It is the only species in the genus *Tiaroga*. The loach minnow is a small (less than 80 millimeters or 3 inches), slender, elongated fish, olivaceous in color with dirty white spots at the base of the dorsal and caudal fins. It has a highly oblique terminal mouth and markedly upward-directed eyes. Breeding males develop vivid red-orange markings.

The species inhabits small to large perennial streams, using shallow turbulent riffles with primarily cobble substrate, swift currents, and growths of filamentous algae. Recurrent flooding is very important to *Tiaroga* biology, keeping the substrate free of embedding sediments and helping to maintain the competitive edge over invading exotic fish species.

Historically, the loach minnow was locally common throughout much of the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, and Gila River systems (upstream from Phoenix), occupying both mainstream and perennial tributaries up to about 2,200 meters (7,200 feet) elevation. The historic range included the upper San Pedro River in Sonora, Mexico, but habitat no longer exists there due to dewatering of the river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Current distribution is restricted to:
- Approximately 24 km (15 miles) of [[Aravaipa Creek]], Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona
- Approximately 93 km (57 miles) of the upper [[Gila River]], upstream from the Middle Box canyon through the Cliff-Gila Valley and the confluence area of the East, West, and Middle Forks, Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico
- Approximately 167 km (103 miles) of the [[San Francisco River]] and [[Tularosa River]], Catron County, New Mexico
- The lower 1.5 km (1 mile) of [[Whitewater Creek]], a tributary of the San Francisco River, Catron County, New Mexico
- Approximately 95 km (59 miles) of the [[Blue River]], Greenlee County, Arizona
- A short stretch at the confluence of the East Fork and mainstream of the [[White River]], Navajo County, Arizona

Land ownership is mixed, including USDI Bureau of Land Management (including [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]]), Defenders of Wildlife (George Whittell Wildlife Preserve), The Nature Conservancy, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, National Park Service (Gila Cliff Dwellings National Monument), U.S. Forest Service (Gila National Forest, Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest, including wilderness and primitive areas), Fort Apache Indian Reservation, and private lands.

The native fish fauna of the Gila River system has been drastically affected by human alteration, with 35 percent of native fish presently federally listed as endangered and another 35 percent considered threatened or endangered by Arizona and New Mexico and/or the American Fisheries Society. *Tiaroga* was last found in the San Pedro River (except Aravaipa Creek) in 1961 and in the Verde River drainage in 1938. It has retreated at least 60 km (37 miles) upstream in the Gila River in the last 50 years.

## Key Findings

**Listing Status**: The loach minnow is determined to be a **threatened species** under the Endangered Species Act. Threatened status is appropriate because of the greatly reduced and fragmented range (85 percent loss of historic habitat) and ongoing threats, though the species is still extant in 380 km of stream and does not appear to be in danger of extinction within the foreseeable future.

**Threats to the Species** (Section 4(a)(1) factors):

*Factor A - Habitat Destruction*: Of approximately 2,600 km (1,600 miles) of historic habitat, 2,220 km (1,364 miles) no longer support the species. Major causes include:
- Conversion of flowing waters to still waters by impoundment
- Alteration of flow regimes (conversion of perennial to intermittent flow, reduction/elimination of natural flooding)
- Channel downcutting and erosion
- Water diversion and excessive groundwater pumping
- Riparian destruction
- Sedimentation from watershed disturbances (grazing, mining, timber harvest)
- Channelization

Specific habitat losses: The San Pedro River is now severely downcut with only intermittent flow; the lower Salt and Verde Rivers have limited or no flow during portions of the year due to diversion and impoundment; the San Francisco River often goes dry near Glenwood due to upstream diversion.

Ongoing threats include:
- Groundwater pumping upstream of [[Aravaipa Creek]] lowering water table
- Pesticide use on agricultural lands upstream from Aravaipa Canyon
- Proposed [[Conner Dam]] on the Gila River (Bureau of Reclamation's Central Arizona Project Upper Gila Water Supply Study): A high dam could inundate up to 23 km (18 miles) of river (31 percent of existing Gila River range) and adversely alter downstream habitat through changes in temperature, bedload, and flow regimes, including elimination of natural flooding
- Increased timber harvest on steep slopes in Gila National Forest may increase sedimentation

*Factor C - Predation*: Introduction of exotic predatory fishes has increased predation pressure. Potential predators include native [[Roundtail Chub]] and exotic species: [[Green Sunfish]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], black and yellow bullhead, [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Predation appears more significant under altered habitat conditions. Dam and reservoir construction exacerbates predation by increasing habitat favorable to exotic predators and supplying a ready source from reservoirs.

*Factor D - Inadequacy of Regulatory Mechanisms*: 
- New Mexico lists *T. cobitis* as endangered (Group 2), prohibiting take except under scientific collecting permit
- Arizona lists it as threatened (Group 3), with no special protection beyond general prohibition of take except by angling
- Neither state provides habitat protection
- New Mexico water law does not include provisions for instream water rights for fish and wildlife protection; Arizona only recently recognized such rights
- State regulations allow use of [[Red Shiner]] and other live minnows as bait in areas containing loach minnow, encouraging spread of detrimental exotic species

*Factor E - Other Factors*: Continued introduction and dispersal of exotic species, particularly *[[Notropis lutrensis]]* (red shiner), throughout the Gila River system. The spread of exotic species correlates closely with declining numbers and distribution of *Tiaroga* and other native species. *N. lutrensis* may compete for habitat and adult red shiners may prey on larval *Tiaroga*. In suitable unaltered habitat, *Tiaroga* may hold its own against invasion, but this balance is destroyed in extensively altered habitat. Alteration of natural flooding patterns is a major factor in displacement, as native species thrive under frequent moderate to severe flooding while exotics do not. A 1983-84 study found flooding in fall 1983 increased the proportion of native fish in the San Francisco River from 30 percent to 90 percent. *N. lutrensis* is found in the San Francisco River as far upstream as Kelly Canyon and in the upper Gila River as far upstream as Highway 180 bridge near Cliff, New Mexico (first collected in Gila River, New Mexico in 1978).

**Public Comment Summary**: Ninety-five letters received from 89 parties; 67 letters from 66 parties in support, 12 letters from 10 parties in opposition, 16 letters with economic information only. Three public hearings held (Silver City NM, Thatcher AZ, Phoenix AZ) attended by 107 people with 33 statements (16 support, 12 opposition, 5 neutral). Support received from Bureau of Land Management, Desert Fishes Council, American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists, International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources, Arizona Game and Fish Department, multiple Audubon societies, The Nature Conservancy, and numerous biologists and citizens. Opposition primarily from water development interests and local governments concerned about flood control and water supply projects.

**Special Rule (50 CFR 17.44(q))**: A special rule under Section 4(d) of the Act allows take without Federal permit if:
1. Take is for educational, scientific, propagation/survival enhancement, zoological exhibition, or other conservation purposes consistent with the Act, AND a state collection permit is obtained and all state laws are satisfied, OR
2. Take is incidental to state-permitted recreational fishing, provided the individual fish is immediately returned to its habitat

This special rule recognizes that the species is threatened primarily by habitat disturbance, not intentional taking or commercialization, and that state scientific collection permit systems are adequate.

**Conservation Measures**: Section 7 consultation required for Federal actions that may affect the species. Potentially affected Federal activities include:
- Bureau of Reclamation's Upper Gila Water Supply Study (dam construction alternatives)
- U.S. Forest Service grazing, mining, timbering, and recreational activities
- Bureau of Land Management recreational management in Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness
- Federal Emergency Management Agency flood control work
- U.S. Army Corps of Engineers flood control projects
- Federally funded highway/bridge construction and irrigation projects

**Critical Habitat**: Designation postponed until June 18, 1987 under Section 4(b)(6)(C)(ii) to allow completion of economic analysis. Prompt determination of threatened status deemed essential to provide full Section 7 protections.

**Historical Context**: The species was included on the American Fisheries Society's 1972 list of threatened freshwater fishes and their 1979 list as a species of special concern. Listed in IUCN Red Data Book (Vol. 4) in 1977. Included in Service's December 30, 1982 Vertebrate Notice of Review in Category 1. Proposed rule published June 18, 1985.
