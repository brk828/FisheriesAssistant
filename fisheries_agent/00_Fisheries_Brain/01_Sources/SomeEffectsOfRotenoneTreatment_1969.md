# Some Effects of Rotenone Treatment on the Fauna of the Green River, Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #river-ecosystem #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This study, conducted by the [[Wyoming Game and Fish Department]] from June 1962 to December 1964, examined the ecological consequences of a large-scale [[Rotenone]] treatment applied to the [[Green River]] in Wyoming/Utah in September 1962. The treatment was undertaken prior to the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] to reduce coarse fish populations (especially [[Common Carp]]) and establish a viable sport fishery, primarily for [[Rainbow Trout]] and later [[Brown Trout]]. The rotenone treatment produced a near-total collapse of both the invertebrate and fish fauna in treated river sections, followed by a multi-year, spatially uneven recolonization process. The project was ultimately judged successful in meeting its fisheries management objectives, though coarse fish (suckers, shiners, carp) re-established populations more quickly than large predatory or competitive species like [[Mountain Whitefish]] and [[Brown Trout]].

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily an ecological/fisheries management study rather than a morphological one, some growth and size data are reported for key species:

- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]**: Reached 7–8 inches in the first year post-treatment (1963 year-class); grew to 14–16 inches by two years of age — an unusually rapid growth rate attributed to reduced competition and predation.
- **[[Common Carp]]**: One-year-old individuals averaged ~7 inches, compared to a typical pre-treatment length of 5 inches; averaged 10–11 inches by two years of age.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]**: Stocked fish included individuals up to 5 lbs; stomach content analysis showed a diet dominated by snails (notably *Physa*) rather than other fish, indicating minimal piscivory.
- **[[Brown Trout]]**: Selected for stocking in 1964 (700,000 fingerlings) due to its characteristically strong piscivorous behavior and ability to withstand heavy angling pressure, unlike rainbow trout.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat context**: The Green River system transitions from torrential mountain stream to a mature, slow-moving river, supporting a successional invertebrate community that shifted with habitat maturity.

**Pre-treatment fish community**: Included [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Common Carp]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Brown Trout]], and various cyprinid shiners — a mixed coarse-fish/salmonid assemblage typical of a large regulated river.

**Invertebrate community & succession**:
- Post-rotenone, **[[Tubificidae]]** (annelid worms) became strongly dominant in upper treated reaches through the following spring, favored by their burrowing habit which conferred partial resistance to the toxicant.
- A midge–mayfly assemblage ([[Tendipedidae]]/[[Ephemerella]]) succeeded the worms in fall/summer.
- **[[Simuliidae]]** (blackflies) rose to dominance in summer 1963, characteristic of smaller, intermediate mountain creeks — suggesting a compressed successional sequence following disturbance.
- **[[Hydropsychidae]]** (net-spinning caddisflies), a historically common taxon, was among the last groups to return (not reappearing until summer 1964), recolonizing primarily via downstream drift from unaffected upstream populations.
- Burrowing/vegetation-dwelling taxa (dragonflies, snipe flies) showed the greatest resistance to rotenone exposure.
- Recolonization strategies varied by taxon: passive transport (snails, e.g. *[[Physa]]*, via surface drift), egg-bank survival (e.g. *[[Ephoron]]* mayfly, whose eggs were not present upstream yet the species reappeared from surviving in-situ eggs), and active dispersal (strong-flying adults such as *[[Baetis]]* mayflies).

**Predator-prey dynamics**: The post-treatment fish community lacked an efficient predator-prey structure. Stocked rainbow trout fed predominantly on snails rather than fish, allowing unchecked increase of cyprinids. This gap motivated the deliberate introduction of piscivorous brown trout as a biological control on coarse fish.

**Water quality shifts**: Rotenone-induced fish and invertebrate die-off led to increased water clarity (reduced turbidity from feeding activity), which in turn promoted diatom blooms (e.g. *[[Gomphonema]]*) and dense growths of the filamentous alga *[[Cladophora]]* within weeks of treatment. Phytoplankton showed no significant change, while zooplankton (except Protozoa) were sharply reduced.

## Key Findings

- **Near-total faunal collapse**: Rotenone virtually exterminated fish populations (except in sloughs/backwaters) and drastically reduced invertebrate fauna across the treated river reaches.
- **Differential recovery rates by distance**: The upper 10 miles of treated river recovered invertebrate populations (quantitatively and qualitatively) within about one month; areas farther downstream remained essentially devoid of invertebrates until spring 1963.
- **Species-specific recolonization mechanisms**: Recovery depended on dispersal mode — passive drift (snails), egg survival in situ (certain mayflies), and active downstream/upstream movement (adult insects, fish).
- **Fish recolonization primarily via migration**: The most significant fish-related finding was active immigration from untreated river sections rather than in-situ survivor reproduction alone, though surviving adults did spawn successfully in 1963 and 1964.
- **Accelerated growth in surviving/young fish**: Reduced competition and predation produced markedly faster growth in flannelmouth sucker and carp year-classes.
- **Absence of predation control**: Stocked rainbow trout did not effectively prey on smaller fish, allowing rapid proliferation of non-carp cyprinids; this prompted the strategic 1964 introduction of 700,000 brown trout fingerlings as a biological control agent.
- **Incomplete/patchy kill required retreatment**: Some areas were ineffectively treated initially and required retreatment in fall 1962 and spring 1963.
- **Carp distribution asymmetry**: Post-treatment, carp were found below [[Fontenelle Dam]] but not yet re-established above it by the end of the study, likely due to incomplete kills in isolated backwaters serving as refugia.
- **Successional pattern disruption**: By the end of the study period, the invertebrate community in upper treated reaches had not returned to its pre-treatment successional pattern, indicating lasting ecosystem alteration beyond simple recovery.
- **Overall project evaluation**: The Wyoming Game and Fish Department judged the rehabilitation project successful — fishing success in the river and in [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] exceeded pre-impoundment expectations, fulfilling the stated management objectives of coarse-fish reduction and sport fishery establishment.
