# Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This species account describes the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*), a large [[Catostomidae|catostomid]] of western North America. The account covers its morphology, coloration, reproduction, feeding, habitat preferences, and historical and recent distribution. The species has been extirpated from the [[Gila River Basin]], has not been recorded in the [[Salt River]] reservoirs since about 1950, and is nearing extinction in the lower [[Colorado River]] below [[Lake Mohave]]. Surviving populations in the Colorado River impoundments consist mainly of large adults, which may indicate low reproductive success.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and nomenclature.** The document lists a long synonymy under several names, including *Catostomus texanus* (Abbott, 1860) and *Xyrauchen cypho* (Lockington, 1881). The current name is *Xyrauchen texanus*. The species is also known as the humpback sucker.

**Morphology.**
- Head and body elongated.
- A sharp-edged keel develops in adults behind the occiput, supported by greatly produced underlying bones.
- Lateral-line scales are moderately small, numbering 68 to 82.
- Dorsal fin rays number 13 to 16, usually 14 or 15.
- Gill rakers are numerous, with 44 to 50 on the first arch.
- Size: one of the largest catostomids in western North America, sometimes approaching one meter in length and weighing 5 to 6 kg.

**Coloration.**
- Olivaceous to brownish-black above, lighter below (often yellow).
- Sides with light gray to pinkish or reddish stripes.
- Caudal fin light yellow-brown.
- Breeding males are black or dark brown on the dorsum and upper sides, orange laterally, and bright yellow on the belly.

**Common names.** Older residents of the Phoenix area called the fish "buffalofish" or "buffalo," either for its keeled back or by association with the Mississippi buffalofish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Adults occur in water more than one meter deep, often deeper than 15 m in reservoirs, over sand, mud, or gravel. Where adults were taken, the current was usually strong, but older observers who knew the species in the lower [[Salt River]] and [[Gila River]] before those rivers were dried reported that large adults tended to remain in eddies and backwaters beside the strongest currents. They often concentrated behind obstructions, in deep holes, along undercut banks, or among fallen trees. Young fish travel in large schools along the margins of streams or reservoirs.

**Historical distribution.**
- The species formerly occupied all the larger streams of the [[Gila River Basin]], where it has now been extirpated.
- It was abundant in the Gila and Colorado rivers, where Indigenous peoples took it for food, and later Caucasoid settlers did the same.
- It undertook upstream migrations at times, presumably to spawn.

**Current distribution and status.**
- The [[Salt River]] reservoirs held populations until at least the early 1950s. In 1949, commercial catches at [[Saguaro Lake]] reached "six tons" during the spawning season, but no specimens have appeared in collections since about that time.
- The species disappeared from [[Roosevelt Lake]] just before the 1930s.
- It persisted in the [[Verde River]] basin, at [[Peck's Lake]] near Clarkdale, at least until 1954. Remains from an Indigenous site near Perkinsville, Yavapai County, show that it was once present in that system as well.
- In the lower Colorado River, individuals were taken 1968 miles below [[Davis Dam]] in Topock Gorge and directly below the dam. The fish are reported as locally abundant in [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Lake Mead]], but all individuals are large, which may indicate low reproductive success. Few records are available from the lower river between 1962 and 1972.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction.**
- Spawning occurs along shorelines or in bays in the Colorado River impoundments.
- One female is attended by 2 to 12 males. The group moves in circles less than two meters in diameter, randomly spiraling over the bottom.
- Males "herd" the female by nudging her genital region with their heads and predorsal keels.
- When a site is selected, the female settles to the bottom with a male pressed to each side. Vibrations then lead to a convulsive finale, when gametes are presumably released. The fish then move forward and upward, leaving a cloud of silt and sand.
- Females spawn repeatedly with several males.
- Eggs are transparent and adhesive, attaching to the substrate where they are deposited.
- Spawning runs from late winter (February, based on gonadal condition of specimens taken in 1965) to early summer. Young fish have been collected along Lake Mead shorelines in June.

**Diet and feeding.**
- Diet includes algae and dipteran (fly) larvae.
- Specimens from Lake Mohave collected in May had intestinal tracts entirely filled with planktonic crustaceans.
- Feeding behavior: the mouth projects forward, and the fish moves in a "bouncing" up-and-down pattern produced by alternating strokes of the caudal fin. The pectoral fins are held stiffly extended, producing a planing effect, with little lateral head movement. The keel-like anterodorsal surface may serve as a lateral stabilizer.
- Hubbs & Miller (1953) also recorded plankton feeding in riverine populations of the northern part of the range. They noted that the gill rakers are long and "fuzzy," approaching those of the plankton-eating suckers of the genus [[Chasmistes]].

**Conservation concerns.**
- The species is extirpated from the Gila River basin.
- Its status in the lower Colorado River is near extinction, with few records from 1962 to 1972.
- Adult-dominated populations in the reservoirs suggest a lack of recruitment, although the document does not report direct recruitment data.
