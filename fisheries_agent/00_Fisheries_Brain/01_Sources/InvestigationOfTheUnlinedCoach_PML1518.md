# Benthic Macroinvertebrate Community of the Unlined Coachella Canal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This baseline study by [[Paul C. Marsh]] (Arizona State University) and Carolyn Stinernetz (Yuma Projects Office, Bureau of Reclamation) characterizes the benthic macroinvertebrate community of the unlined [[Coachella Canal]] in October 1980, shortly before the southernmost 49 miles were replaced by a [[Concrete-Lined Canal]]. The community was depauperate, dominated by the [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*), [[Chironomidae]] midges, oligochaetes and the [[Hydropsychid Caddisfly]] (*Smicridea utica*). The authors argue that the lining will change habitat and food supply, with implications for the [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] fishery.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Canal invertebrate resources in North America were largely unknown, although canals may support substantial fisheries that depend on macroinvertebrate production.
- **Prior work:** Earlier studies covered European canals, Californian canals (ectoprocts, concrete-lined canal sampling) and individual fish species (tilapia, tilapia mossambica, Acanthogobius flavimanus, Ictalurus punctatus). A 1974 unpublished California Department of Fish and Game report estimated 1,500–3,000 game fish per mile, mainly largemouth bass, but largely ignored forage species.
- **Purpose:** To provide baseline data on relative abundance, biomass and community structure of the unlined canal benthos, to evaluate trophic relations between fish and macrobenthos, and to predict community dynamics in the new lined canal.
- **Study area:** The canal delivers Colorado River water via the All-American Canal to irrigation districts in the Imperial, Coachella and Indio Valleys. Seepage losses led to replacement of the southernmost 49 miles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design:** Nine stations were sampled on 23–24 October 1980 along east and west banks (about 1 m depth) and mid-channel, using a 0.02 m² Eckman dredge, sieved through 425 µm mesh and preserved in 10% formalin. Substrate, current (about 1–2 feet per second), depth and vegetation were recorded at each station.

**Habitat:** Substrates were mainly fine to medium sand with sparse pebble gravel, silt and clay in quiet nearshore zones, and concrete structures at check drops, siphons and bridges. Rooted macrophytes (*Phragmites*, *Typha*, *Potamogeton pectinatus*, *Myriophyllum spicatum*) occurred in shallow water, and filamentous algae grew only on a concrete apron at "check drop 5."

**Trophic structure:** Filter feeders (*C. manilensis*, Chironomidae, *S. utica*) and sediment-detritus feeders (Oligochaeta) dominated. Scrapers were nearly absent, suggesting very low attached microalgal primary production. Mayflies and beetles were poorly represented or absent, which the authors attribute to the shifting-sand substrate.

## Key Findings

- **Taxa richness:** 17 invertebrate taxa were collected on 23–24 October, and one odonate (*Anisoptera*) was added in November qualitative samples, for at least 18 taxa in total.
- **Dominance:** Corbicula manilensis, oligochaetes, Chironomidae and *S. utica* made up over 90% of individuals.
- **Near-shore vs. mid-channel:** Near-shore habitats had over 20 times as many individuals and nearly four times the biomass as mid-channel, and more than twice as many taxa (17 vs. 7). Mean totals were 3,678 per m² near shore versus 158 per m² in mid-channel.
- **Biomass:** Biomass ranged from roughly 7,625 to 2,015 mg dry weight per m² across the study summary, and was overwhelmingly dominated by *C. manilensis*. Large clams (shell width 8–33 mm) reached a mean of about 1,277 mg/m² at Station 8 (west bank), the only site with very low current and silt/organic substrate.
- **Size classes:** Juvenile clams (under 4 mm) had a mean dry weight of 0.009 mg; larger clams ranged from 10 to 550 mg. Mean individual dry weights of other dominant taxa were 0.10 mg (*S. utica*), 0.07 mg (Oligochaeta) and 0.02 mg (Chironomidae).
- **Caddisfly on concrete:** Densities of *S. utica* on concrete structures were estimated at about 25,000 per m², nearly 15 times the highest quantitative density, with a biomass of nearly 2,500 mg. Lepidoptera larvae (*Parargyractis confusalis*) reached about 1,500 per m² on the concrete walls at check drop 5.
- **Fish diet:** About 90% of over 100 channel catfish from siphon number 2 contained *S. utica* larvae. Other fish species had empty stomachs or contained fish remains.
- **Low diversity:** Chironomid densities were under 21,300 per m², far below the typical 50,000 per m² reported by Merritt and Cummins (1978), so the community is considered depauperate. The authors attribute this to harsh conditions on shifting-sand substrate.

**Predictions for the lined canal:**
- *S. utica* production may greatly exceed that in the unlined canal, owing to stable concrete substrate, and may support a larger fish population, notably channel catfish.
- Loss of [[Aquatic Macrophyte]] beds, which are a primary food source and fish habitat, could reduce the caddisfly food supply and fish recruitment, eventually depleting the recreational fishery.
- Losses of *C. manilensis* and Oligochaeta are expected to matter less, because these taxa are unlikely to be major canal fish food.
- Invertebrates will colonize the new canal quickly, while fish communities may take years to establish, so early invertebrate monitoring can forecast fishery outcomes.

**Summary conclusion:** Benthic distribution tracks habitat characteristics (substrate size and stability, organic matter). Establishing a substantial fishery in the lined canal may depend on vegetation establishment, which is unlikely, and on caddisfly production on concrete.
