# Fish Introductions in the American Southwest: A Case History of Rogers Spring, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

Rogers Spring, a desert spring in Clark County, Nevada, has received at least 14 fish introductions since the 1930s, including cyprinids, a clariid, poeciliids and cichlids, most of them aquarium species. The spring is inside the [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]] and drains to [[Lake Mead]]. In October 1980, underwater observations revealed a resident population of [[Tilapia Mariae]], a species not previously recorded as established in the United States outside Florida. The authors argue that the spring also acts as a source of introductions to other southern Nevada springs, threatening endemic desert fishes. They recommend immediate eradication of the tilapia, public signage, and continued monitoring.

## Background & Description

Rogers Spring lies above the Overton Arm of Lake Mead, approximately 68 km from Las Vegas. For at least two decades it has been used as a release site for non-native fishes, mostly aquarium species. The historical record is summarized below:

- **Early record:** Only one native species, [[Rhinichthys Osculus]], has been recorded there, but its natural occurrence is questioned. Collections on 19 April 1952 yielded eleven specimens, which R. R. Miller identified as *R. osculus*. No native fish were found when the spring was examined on 23 March 1961. High salinity (specific conductance 3.72 x 10^-3 µmhos at 25°C) and temperature (26–36°C range given) suggest the species could not maintain a viable population.
- **Introductions:** On 1 May 1955, the Nevada Department of Wildlife released ten *Dorosoma petenense* into the spring.
- **Surveys:** [[Larry Rivers]] (1962) recorded *Xiphophorus helleri*, *X. maculatus* and *Poecilia latipinna*. [[Deacon Et Al 1964]] found the xiphophorins absent in 1961 and 1963 but *P. latipinna* persisting, and reported new introductions of *Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*, *Clarias batrachus* and *Gambusia affinis*.
- **Eradication:** [[Rotenone Treatment]] was applied in late 1963. Among the dead fish were *Cichlasoma severum* and *Poecilia mexicana*. Two *C. batrachus* were later collected by the Nevada Department of Wildlife.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The native freshwater fish fauna west of the Rocky Mountains and north of Mexico comprises approximately 100 species (Miller, 1961). This depauperate fauna has many relicts, monotypic genera and a high degree of [[Endemism]], and many species have limited ranges, some confined to habitats smaller than a few hectares. Human environmental change has led to many of these fishes being listed as threatened or endangered.

Isolated desert populations probably evolved without competitors or predators and therefore lack the behavioral defenses of fishes from more saturated faunas, which makes them vulnerable to introduced species. Rogers Spring has also been a source of introductions to other springs. Hubbs and Deacon (1965) suggested it as the likely source of *C. nigrofasciatum* and *P. mexicana* introduced into [[Ash Springs]], about 146 km NNW, from which these exotics may have spread to [[Crystal Spring]]. The outflow of Ash Springs holds the only extant population of *Gila robusta jordani*, an endangered fish. Native fishes shared by Ash and Crystal springs include *Crenichthys baileyi* and *Rhinichthys osculus*, both threatened by exotics (Hardy, 1980).

Deacon (1979) identified hybridization and competition with introduced fishes as threats to 22 of the 33 endangered and 19 of the 30 threatened fishes in the western U.S. Habitat destruction threatened all but two endangered species, and introduced fishes were the only threat to those two.

Threat mechanisms described in the paper include:
- **Hybridization** with introduced poeciliids, a major threat to localized endemic poeciliids.
- **Predation:** Sport fishes such as *Micropterus salmoides* cause direct predation on endemic fishes. [[Gambusia Affinis]] feeds on the eggs and larvae of desert fishes, and *Poecilia reticulata* and *P. latipinna* may share these habits.
- **Competition:** Cichlids such as *C. nigrofasciatum* may prey on eggs and young and compete for spawning sites and territory.
- **Algal grazing:** *Tilapia mariae* prefers green algae, a key food for small endemic fishes and for the snails and ostracods they eat, so it can damage food webs.

## Key Findings

1. **Establishment of Tilapia mariae:** On 18 October 1980, underwater observations found a resident population of [[Tilapia Mariae]] in Rogers Spring. Individuals were as large as typical spotted adults, but many had lost fins and showed incomplete juvenile color patterns. Collections on the same date included *Poecilia mexicana* (abundant), *C. nigrofasciatum* (now rare) and two *P. reticulata*.

2. **Status of earlier introductions:** Collections immediately after the 1963 rotenone treatment indicated that *Gambusia affinis*, *Poecilia latipinna* and *C. nigrofasciatum* had been eliminated. They had not re-established by 1980.

3. **Ongoing introductions (November 1981):** Further underwater observations recorded several albino *C. nigrofasciatum*, barred and marmalade variants of *Pseudotropheus zebra*, and other cichlids (some names are illegible in the scan). These had been released after the 1980 visit.

4. **Threat to endemic fishes:** The authors state that dispersal of tilapia into Nevada and adjoining states could cause multiple extinctions of endemic fishes, since the species could establish and dominate predator-free desert springs.

5. **Recommendations:**
   - Immediate eradication of *Tilapia mariae* and other introduced fishes at Rogers Spring.
   - Signs at this and other springs in the Lake Mead National Recreation Area stating that releasing non-native fishes violates Nevada law. The authors doubt these signs will prevent releases but expect them to reduce releases.
   - Continued monitoring of fish populations at these springs.

6. **Wider implications:** Similar aquatic habitats with depauperate faunas and high endemism occur in the Cuatro Ciénegas region of Mexico and the Anatolian Basin of Turkey. The authors predict that introductions of exotic fishes into these areas would cause habitat destruction, predation and competition with endemic fishes.
