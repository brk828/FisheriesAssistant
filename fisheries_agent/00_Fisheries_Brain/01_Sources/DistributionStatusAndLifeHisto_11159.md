# Distribution, Status, and Life History of the Bluestripe Darter (*Percina cymatotaenia*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Missouri Department of Conservation report (Aquatic Series No. 18, Jefferson City, October 1984) by William L. Pflieger documents the distribution, abundance, habitat, diet, growth, reproduction, and conservation status of the [[Bluestripe Darter]], an endemic fish of the [[Osage River]] and [[Gasconade River]] systems in central Missouri. The study draws on collections from 228 localities sampled between July 27, 1974 and April 23, 1982, compared with earlier records back to 1884.

The species occupies at least 240 miles of stream channel in six streams and is confined to the [[Salem Plateau]] section of the [[Ozark Uplands]]. The author concludes that the species has declined since the early 1900s but has been relatively stable in recent decades. He does not support listing it as nationally endangered or threatened, and recommends periodic monitoring, habitat studies, and stronger water-quality protection.

## Background & Description

*Percina cymatotaenia* is a slender darter with a broad dusky lateral stripe that ends in a small, triangular black spot at the base of the caudal fin. The dorsum is pale with brownish streaks, and the lateral stripe shows an iridescent greenish tinge in bright light. Maximum length is about 4 inches (approximately 82 mm SL in this study's largest adult sample range).

The species belongs to the subgenus *Odontopholis*. Its closest relative is an undescribed species from Kentucky, and the two are the only members of the subgenus (Page 1974). The species was first described by Gilbert and Meek from 1884 specimens collected in the [[Niangua River]], [[Osage Fork]], and Sac River.

The study followed methods similar to those used for the [[Niangua Darter]], a related species with overlapping range. Key methods included:

- **Seining:** a 25 × 8 ft, ¼-inch mesh drag seine for pools and deeper riffles, and a 6 × 4 ft, ⅛-inch mesh kick seine for riffles and pool margins ([[Seining]]).
- **Direct observation:** snorkel and face mask observations of habitat and behavior.
- **Age determination:** scale reading, with back-calculated growth.
- **Diet analysis:** stomach contents, sorted and counted, with volume estimated visually.
- **Fecundity:** total ova counts from females collected in February and March.
- **Laboratory spawning:** adults held in a 140-gallon recirculating fiberglass tank during 1981 and 1982.

Fish were grouped into benthic, nektonic, and large-fish ecological categories to reduce sampling bias.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range and streams.** The species is found in the [[Niangua River]] (Osage system) and the [[Osage Fork]], [[Gasconade River]], [[Whetstone Creek]], [[Roubidoux Creek]], and [[Big Piney River]] (Gasconade system). These are clear, medium-sized streams draining hilly terrain underlain by chert-bearing limestones and dolomites. The species was largely restricted to stream orders V and VI, at gradients of 1 to 12 ft/mile, and at elevations of about 800 to 1,180 ft. It avoided high-gradient creeks, spring branches, and large Ozark rivers.

**Habitat.** Bluestripe Darters were almost always found along quiet pool margins, associated with sticks, leaves, and other organic debris or dense aquatic plants. Depths ranged from about 10 to 54 inches (mean 27 inches). Current velocity averaged 0.3 ft/s, with a maximum of about 0.8 ft/s. The species showed a strong affinity for vegetation such as [[Justicia]], along with *Ranunculus*, *Myriophyllum*, *Potamogeton*, and *Heteranthera*. In winter and early spring, when plants were dormant, it used mats of dead Justicia stems and organic debris. Substrates ranged from bedrock and rubble to sand, with silt rarely dominant.

**Associated fauna.** A total of 78 fish species were identified at stations where the darter occurred, with 22 to 53 species per station (mean 37.2). The fauna was decidedly Ozarkian, with 85.4% of species occurring at ten or more darter localities belonging to Ozark, Ozark-Lowland, or Ozark-Prairie faunal groups. The [[Longear Sunfish]], bleeding shiner, largescale stoneroller, and bluntnose minnow occurred at every darter station. The [[Rainbow Darter]] and [[Orangethroat Darter]] were the most abundant darters and together made up 65.9% of all benthic fishes collected.

**Diet.** The diet consisted mainly of immature aquatic insects, especially [[Mayflies]] (Ephemeroptera: Baetidae, Heptageniidae, Ephemerellidae) and midges (Diptera, principally Chironomidae). Lesser amounts of microcrustacea (Cladocera and Copepoda), amphipods (*Hyalella azteca*, the only amphipod identified), and other invertebrates were also eaten. No terrestrial items were found. Diet varied only slightly with season and size. Odonata and Trichoptera became important in late summer and fall, and stoneflies appeared only in late winter and spring.

**Threats.** The author links the historical decline to deforestation, repeated burning, and chert gravel influx in the early 1900s, which eliminated pool habitat and reduced surface flow. Current concerns include sedimentation, channel instability, and nutrient enrichment from urbanization and conversion of forest to pasture. Sewage effluent from Hartville (Woods Fork) and Cabool (upper Big Piney River) was suspected as a factor in local declines.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- 117 individuals were collected at 17 localities since 1974, about the same distribution as in 1940–1974.
- Seining averaged 2.3 individuals per hour, all in pools. Catch rates were highest in Whetstone Creek, Niangua River, and Gasconade River.
- Density estimates at two intensively sampled stations ranged from 0.7 to 5.2 individuals per 100 linear feet of stream.
- The species is estimated to inhabit about 240 miles of channel in six streams. The Big Piney River estimate assumes its distribution matches earlier surveys.
- The darter occurs at higher densities and in larger streams than the [[Niangua Darter]], and its range is more continuous, which the author argues makes it less vulnerable to extinction.

**Life history**
- Five age groups (0 to IV) were recorded from 79 scale-aged fish. Females predominated in all groups except IV, but only the age I sex ratio differed significantly from 1:1.
- Males and females grew at similar rates and had similar longevity.
- Fish reached about 55% of maximum adult size (about 82 mm SL) by the end of the first year. Mean SL at that age was 46.0 mm for males and 47.9 mm for females.
- Of 25 age I fish, 52% were sexually mature in early spring: 64.7% of females and 25.0% of males.
- A total-length to standard-length conversion was derived: SL = −0.8395 + 0.8706 TL (r = 0.997).

**Reproduction**
- Sexual dimorphism: males have longer soft dorsal and anal fins, a triangular genital papilla (females have a lobed, truncate one), and a ctenoid keel on the caudal peduncle.
- Breeding males change rapidly from a longitudinal stripe to about 10 black lateral bars and about 10 dorsal bars. Females show only slight blanching.
- Spawning occurs in early spring, peaking in March and early April. Water temperatures during spawning were roughly 46–56 °F. Spawning was observed in the laboratory between March 16 and April 10.
- Fecundity: 12 females (SL 44–69 mm) had 81–351 mature ova. Neither length nor age significantly predicted ova number (P = 0.15 and 0.14).
- Eggs are about 2 mm in diameter, buried in fine gravel, and hatch in 9–10 days. Newly hatched larvae average 8.5 mm TL. Larvae in the laboratory never fed and died about 10 days after hatching.
- Trematode flukes were found in the digestive tract, body fat, and ovaries of dissected specimens.

**Status and conservation**
- The species declined sharply between the 1880s and the 1930s–1940s, but has been relatively stable since 1930. A possible recent decline was noted in the upper Big Piney River and Woods Fork.
- Recommendation 1: Do not list the species as nationally threatened or endangered at this time.
- Recommendation 2: Sample populations periodically, especially in the upper Big Piney River and Woods Fork.
- Recommendation 3: Study habitat changes responsible for local declines.
- Recommendation 4: Maintain and improve habitat quality through water-quality enforcement, landowner education, cost-sharing for soil conservation, and easements along prime stream reaches.
