# Predator-Prey Relationships in Pikes (Esocidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 review by E. J. Crossman, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, examines how predator-prey relationships vary among pikes ([[Esocidae]]). Across the family, prey choice ranges from selection based only on relative abundance or size to a more definite association between predator and prey. The clearest case of association is the [[Grass Pickerel]] and [[Central Mudminnow]] in Ontario, contrasted with the [[Redfin Pickerel]] and [[Eastern Mudminnow]] in North Carolina, where the association is essentially absent.

## Background & Description

The note opens by placing predator-prey interactions in context. Associations among fishes are well documented in warm seas, including parasitism, symbiosis, commensalism and cleaning behaviour. The most common association between fish species is a habitual predator-prey relationship, defined here narrowly as the pursuit, capture and consumption of one fish by another. Such associations are much rarer among temperate freshwater fishes, where predation is generally non-specific and governed by relative abundance.

Within the Esocidae, the author describes a gradient of relationships, from those driven by a critical predator-prey size ratio to those with a more definite pairing. Several pike species are discussed:

- [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*): diet of perch, trout and suckers, closely matching prey availability (Allen 1939; Johnson 1959).
- [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*): yellow perch and suckers prominent, but a wide range of prey suggests selection by size rather than species (Hourston 1952).
- [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox niger*): conflicting reports. Wich and Mullen (1958) found selection tracked abundance; Raney (1942) found young bullheads (*Ictalurus nebulosus*) were the most frequent prey despite sunfish and perch being more abundant.
- [[Amur Pike]] (*Esox reicherti*): diet of cyprinids, depending mainly on which species was most abundant or migrating through the river (Nikolsky 1956).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The core observations come from habitats of muddy, narrow, heavily vegetated creeks. In Leeds County, Ontario, about 80 collections of grass pickerel were made, and 43 yielded both grass pickerel and central mudminnow. Many locations without either species had temperature, depth and vegetation conditions similar to those where both were caught, so the association appears to exceed shared habitat requirements.

In North Carolina, redfin pickerel were sampled in streams, sloughs and swamps with habitat similar to the grass pickerel sites. Eastern mudminnow occurred in only 3 of 11 collections alongside redfin pickerel, even though it was sometimes as abundant as centrarchids, the predominant prey of redfin pickerel.

## Key Findings

- **Grass pickerel and central mudminnow:** Of 277 grass pickerel of 100 mm and over, 89 had eaten mudminnows, almost five times as many as the next most frequently eaten fish. Of 21 other exploitable fish species present, only four besides the mudminnow were preyed on to any extent. Several cyprinids, darters and sunfishes were more abundant than *Umbra limi* in most collections.
- **Exception case:** One seine haul had 4 pickerel and no mudminnows, but one pickerel regurgitated a recently ingested mudminnow when captured.
- **Contrast in North Carolina:** Of 11 collections, only 3 contained both redfin pickerel and eastern mudminnow. Fourteen fish species were found in redfin stomachs, yet *Umbra pygmaea* was completely absent.
- **Synthesis:** Two closely related pairs in similar habitats differ sharply. One pair shows a high degree of association, the other almost none. Other esocids select prey mainly or solely by relative abundance or size.
- **Broader implication:** Predator-prey choice in pikes is not uniform across the family, and apparent specificity may reflect mechanisms beyond abundance that are not yet identified.
