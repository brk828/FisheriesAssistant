# A Portable Core Sampler for Lake Deposits

#limnology #paleolimnology #sediment-core #sampling-equipment

## Executive Summary

This 1958 methods paper by F. J. H. Mackereth, of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], describes a pneumatically operated [[Lake Sediment Cores|sediment core sampler]] that two people can carry and operate from a rowing boat. It takes undisturbed cores about 6 m long and works in water depths up to roughly 250 m. The device uses [[Hydrostatic Pressure]] to anchor itself in the sediment and compressed air to drive the coring tube in. Buoyancy from an air-filled anchor chamber then extracts the apparatus, so no heavy weights or lifting tackle are needed. The design was aimed at sampling the deep-water sediments of lakes in the [[English Lake District]].

## Background & Description

Lake-bottom sediments preserve a record of biological and chemical change over the period of deposition, so undisturbed sampling matters for [[Paleolimnology]]. Mackereth reviews earlier devices:

- **[[Kullenberg Sampler]]** and **[[Jenkin Sampler]]**: both use heavy weights to drive the corer. They need heavy lifting gear and large vessels that most lakes lack.
- **[[Livingstone Piston Sampler]]** (Livingstone, 1955): a lighter piston sampler pushed by a jointed rod. It gives good cores but becomes hard to operate in water deeper than about 20 m because the rod flexes.

Shallow-water sediments often differ from deep-water sediments in the same basin, so a portable device usable to about 80 m was needed. The paper also credits the workshop builders, W. C. Findley and J. B. Anderson, and reviewers, including divers of the East Lancashire Subaqua Club who checked the penetration depth.

**Main components** (per the paper's Figures 1–4):

- **Stainless steel tube (A):** 20 ft long, 3 in. external diameter, with a flange bolted to the anchor chamber.
- **Anchor chamber (G):** sheet steel cylinder, 18 in. diameter and 4 ft high, open at the bottom and closed at the top by a plate. Its upper plate has an area of 250 in².
- **Coring tube (B):** 20 ft of "Duralumin" scaffold tubing, about 2 in. external diameter, with a ground cutting edge at the lower end.
- **Driving piston (C) and internal piston (F):** the upper piston is driven by compressed air, and the lower piston is joined to a brass rod (D) that runs the full length of the apparatus.
- **Release valve:** a spring-loaded valve (about 40 lb pull) that vents air during ascent. It is opened by a detachable weight on a chain.

**Equipment:**

- Air hose rated to about 400 lb/in², in 30 m sections coupled with gas-nipple fittings.
- A PVC hose for pumping water out of the anchor chamber.
- A [[Air-Lift Pumping|pumping method]] that injects compressed air into the PVC hose through a hypodermic needle, avoiding any pump in the field.
- A hand-operated "stirrup" pump (about 200 lb/in²) for hydraulic core extraction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sampler works in the lake's deep-water sediment, with the anchor chamber sinking into soft flocculent mud. Mackereth's theoretical calculation for a 50 m operating depth gives a withdrawal force of about 22,500 lb, assuming the mud cannot flow. He notes that real sediment does flow, so the actual force should be much lower. The paper also states that the force required to lift the chamber was not measured.

The cores contain palaeoenvironmental material. The paper reports that the varves in glacial clays lie flat where the sediment has not been disturbed by slumping on underwater slopes. The cores also contain wood fragments, hazel nuts and leaves, which are large enough to yield enough carbon for [[Radiocarbon Dating]].

Lake-specific details include the reference to [[Windermere]] as a deep-water site where a core could be taken "almost as quickly as one would obtain a deep water sample."

## Key Findings

- **Core length:** undisturbed cores about 6 m long (reported as 19 ft 6 in., or 595 cm) with no evident distortion of strata.
- **Core diameter:** cylindrical cores, reported as "114 in." in the OCR text, which is most likely 1¼ in.
- **Operating depth:** the device is "substantially independent of water depth" up to about 250 m (abstract). A practical limit of about 280 m is given, based on a 500 lb/in² working pressure with a 100 lb/in² margin above hydrostatic pressure.
- **Target depth range:** designed for lakes of the English Lake District up to 80 m.
- **Crew:** operated by two people from an ordinary rowing boat, with a second boat used for towing.
- **Anchoring:** the chamber's hydrostatic grip anchors the apparatus, so no separate anchor is needed.
- **Lift force:** an excess of 10 lb/in² over external pressure gives a lift of about 1 ton on the 250 in² plate. The air-filled chamber has a buoyancy of about 450 lb.
- **Release valve:** the spring is set to about 40 lb, which requires about 50 lb/in² above hydrostatic pressure to open. This corresponds to a lifting force of about 5 tons.
- **Pumping time:** 5–15 minutes to embed the chamber, depending on water depth. Deeper water gives more efficient pumping.
- **Core-driving time:** the core tube is driven in "less than 1 minute" normally.
- **Compressed air use:** at about 50 m, a standard 240 ft³ cylinder takes 3 or 4 cores.
- **Shallow water (25 m or less):** the release valve is not used. A 5-gallon oil drum float is attached to the rope to control the ascent, which prevents a violent surfacing.
- **Length limit:** core length is set by the 20 ft coring tube. Handling difficulties would become serious above about 9 m.
- **Comparison:** the paper states that the Kullenberg corer is more advantageous for very deep water where a large vessel is available, and that this apparatus is best for easy transport and speed.
- **Summary:** the sampler gives deep-lake cores without the heavy weights, lifting tackle or large vessels that the [[Kullenberg Sampler]] and [[Jenkin Sampler]] need. It is transported on a small vehicle, with the anchor chamber removed for transport.
