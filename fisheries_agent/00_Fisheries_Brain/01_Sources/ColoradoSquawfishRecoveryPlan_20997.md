# Colorado Squawfish Recovery Planning and Bonytail Handling Protocol

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This source is a 1988 [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) correspondence packet with two attachments. It covers three linked items:

1. A request to the [[California Department Of Fish And Game]] recovery team for comments on proposed revisions to the [[Colorado Squawfish]] Recovery Plan. The revisions concern how habitat fragmentation is treated and the role of the [[San Juan River]].
2. An excerpt (page 19) of the recovery objectives, including downlisting and delisting criteria.
3. A field protocol for handling and transporting wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) for hatchery broodstock and refugia.

A fourth item is a short figure caption from [[Trautman 1957]] showing spiny-rayed fish measurement points. The note keeps the emphasis on policy and handling procedures, since the document contains no original field data or population statistics.

## Background & Description

**Correspondence (August 10, 1988).** [[John Hamill]], Colorado River Recovery Coordinator, wrote to [[Dr. James St. Amant]] to request recovery team comments before the technical review draft of the plan is released. The letter asks for a reply by October 1, 1988, and states a preference for responding without a formal team meeting.

**Target species.** Two fishes are addressed:
- *Colorado squawfish*, the main subject of the recovery plan.
- *Bonytail*, a *Gila* cyprinid for which a separate recovery plan was in agency review. The bonytail protocol is attached.

The source does not give scientific names for [[Humpback Chub]], which is mentioned only as a companion species in sampling guidance.

**Legal framework.** The plan relies on the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Section 7 applies equally to threatened and endangered listings, which is the stated reason fragmentation was moved to a delisting criterion. Section 10 is cited for designating nonessential experimental populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Recovery reaches (upper basin).** Reclassification to threatened is tied to naturally self-sustaining populations in:
- (a) the Green River from its confluence with the Colorado River to Echo Park, the lower 199 km of the [[Yampa River]], and the lower 240 km of the [[White River]]; and
- (b) the Colorado River from Palisade, Colorado, to [[Lake Powell]].

**White River fragmentation.** The [[Taylor Draw Dam]], above Rangely, Colorado, was impounded in 1984. The recovery team still considers the lower 240 km important. It recommended either restoring upstream and downstream passage at the dam or presenting scientific data showing that losing this range will not impair recovery.

**San Juan River.** Recent studies indicate the San Juan supports one of only three remaining natural Colorado squawfish populations. In 1988, New Mexico and Utah were to conduct further fishery surveys. The FWS and the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] were evaluating present and future hydrology. Results were due by March 1989. Until recovery potential is clearer, the reach from Shiprock, New Mexico, to Lake Powell is to be treated as a high-priority recovery area with full Endangered Species Act protection.

**Lower basin.** The areas with the greatest recovery potential are the [[Salt River]], the [[Gila River]], and the mainstem Colorado below Davis Dam. These are under consideration as nonessential experimental populations. A 10-year stocking program is underway in the Salt and Gila rivers with the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]. Stocking below Parker Dam is planned to create a sport fishery. Self-sustaining populations there are not required for downlisting or delisting, but the objectives would be revised if they establish.

**Bonytail sampling zones.** The protocol lists river reaches where bonytail are most likely to be captured, mostly those with recent humpback chub records (UCRBCC Sensitive Reach document, last five years). Examples include:
- Upper Colorado: Westwater Canyon (river miles 111–125), Moab Canyon (72), Black Rocks (135–136), and Cataract Canyon (the river mile figure is illegible in the scan).
- Green River: Gray Canyon (146–171) and Whirlpool Canyon (342).
- Yampa River: Yampa Canyon to Cross Mountain (18–56).

**Holding facilities.**
- *Temporary:* [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (Vernal, Utah; Green River subbasin) and [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] (Hotchkiss, Colorado; Colorado River subbasin).
- *Permanent:* [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Dexter, New Mexico). Transfer from the temporary facility should occur within two weeks or sooner.

## Key Findings

### Recovery objectives and criteria (revised draft)
- **Reclassification to threatened** requires self-sustaining populations in the Green, Yampa, and White river segments and the Colorado River from Palisade to Lake Powell.
- **Delisting** requires that:
  - (a) downlisting criteria are met;
  - (b) significant fragmentation is removed. This is defined as fragmentation that would impair reproductive success or limit adult population size; and
  - (c) essential habitats, migration routes, required flows, and water quality are legally protected.
- **Fragmentation** was moved from a downlisting to a delisting criterion. The commenter argued that fragmentation is not scientifically shown to make recovery impossible, and that the draft implied this for the White River.
- **San Juan River:** the specific recovery location was removed from page 19 and replaced with language on ongoing studies. The FWS asked the team whether this change is appropriate.

### Bonytail field identification criteria
A bonytail must meet all of the following criteria (strictly applied):
1. Minimum total length of 300 mm.
2. Dorsal/anal fin-ray count of 10/10 or greater.
3. Either (a) caudal peduncle length / head length > 1.0, or (b) head length / caudal peduncle depth > 5.0. Both ratios follow [[Minckley 1973]].

Measurements should be taken on anesthetized fish, using [[MS-222]] or [[Quinaldine]], following [[Lagler Bardach And Miller 1977]].

### Handling and transport protocol
- **Retention:** all wild bonytail encountered should be retained alive for refugia. Researchers may need to hold a fish for two or more days.
- **Discretion:** field researchers decide whether transport is feasible. If risk is too high, the fish is returned to the river.
- **Equipment (supplied by the FWS):** one 100–150 quart cooler; one or two oxygen cylinders with regulator and diffuser; a live net; a vernier caliper; a morphometric key; and an anesthetic (MS-222 or Quinaldine).
- **Container care:**
  - Fill with fresh water, preferably from the capture site.
  - Aerate only if water temperature exceeds air temperature.
  - Cool with ice in sealed containers or river water, keeping temperature stable or slightly depressed.
  - Partially submerge the container if holding exceeds 12 hours.
  - Do not use tarred or knotted nets, which are abrasive.
  - Exchange water by pouring at intervals of 1 to several hours. Replenish only if the fish shows respiratory stress.
- **Remote capture:** the fish should not be left for later retrieval. Transport takes top priority.
- **Records:** capture date, time, location, and notes should accompany the fish.

### Notification and custody
- Cooperators must notify the appropriate [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] station one week before each trip. Green River subbasin trips go to the Vernal office under [[Harold Tyus]]. Colorado River subbasin trips go to the Grand Junction office under [[Lynn Kaeding]].
- Transport from the river to temporary facilities uses a 250-gallon fish distribution tank in a pickup truck. Vernal handles Green and Yampa fish. Grand Junction handles Colorado River fish.
- At the temporary facilities, fish are held in an eight-foot fiberglass tank with groundwater (Hotchkiss) or in isolated earthen ponds or circular tanks (Ouray). Staff must treat abrasions, fungal infections, and bacterial invasion. Lernaea parasites are removed manually, and removals are recorded for treatment at the permanent facility.
