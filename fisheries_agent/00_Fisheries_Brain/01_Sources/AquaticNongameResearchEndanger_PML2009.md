# Non-Native Gamefish Impacts on Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin (1988-1989 Job Progress Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife Job Progress Report (Federal Aid Project SE-3, May 1990, by Thomas P. Nesler) covers 1988-1989 field work on non-native gamefish in the [[Yampa River]] and [[Colorado River]]. The work tested whether non-native gamefish, mainly [[Northern Pike]], [[Channel Catfish]] and centrarchids, prey on or compete with the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The findings are explicitly tentative, pending 1990 field work and final analyses. The study was affected by a low water year and an oil spill in the Yampa River in spring 1989.

## Background & Description

The report's objectives address concern that non-native gamefish may hinder recovery of native Upper Basin species. The stated goals were to:

- Document 3-year trends in abundance, distribution, reproductive success and size structure of non-native, non-salmonid gamefish.
- Assess predation on endangered fishes and measure predator-prey selection.
- Examine habitat use by northern pike, including radio-tracking movements related to spawning.
- Evaluate whether removal of adult northern pike and channel catfish could reduce predation pressure (removal experiments were planned for 1990).

The report also included a Job 5 on a key to sucker larvae and juveniles in the Upper Colorado River Basin. The document provides only a placeholder for this job, with no results.

Study reaches on the Yampa River (river miles 45-170) included Lilly Park-Deerlodge, Maybell-Sunbeam, [[Juniper Springs Reach]] and [[Hayden-Craig Reach]]. On the Colorado River, sampling covered RM 128-160 (reported as RM 185-140 in the methods). Sampling used electrofishing, trammel and gill netting, seining and angling. A "[[Block-And-Shock Technique]]" was developed to capture fish: nets were set across backwater mouths, the backwater was electrofished, and the net then blocked the exit.

Radio-tagged northern pike were implanted with 30 MHz transmitters (surgery through an incision posterior to the pelvic fin girdle, with anesthesia). Fish were tracked by triangulation and fixed-station monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Spring runoff created side-channel and tributary backwaters, which supported northern pike and other fishes. Rapidly declining flows in 1989 dewatered these backwaters by early June, forcing fish into mainstem pools and eddies.
- **Oil spill:** An oil spill in late spring 1989 (estimated at approximately 10,000 gallons) contaminated the Yampa River throughout the study reaches. Oil accumulated in tributary backwaters, but its effects on fish behavior were uncertain. No immediate mortality attributable to it was observed in the following week.
- **Water temperature:** Short periods of warm water appear to limit centrarchid nesting and juvenile development in the upper mainstem Colorado River.
- **Barriers:** [[Cross Mountain Canyon]] and Juniper Canyon on the Yampa River may restrict fish movement. No comparable barriers were identified in the Colorado River study reach.
- **Downstream connections:** Northern pike from the Yampa River may emigrate into the [[Green River]], where juvenile endangered fishes occur in nursery backwaters.

## Key Findings

### Northern Pike (Yampa River)

- Northern pike were captured in all four Yampa reaches in 1989. Catch in the Hayden-Craig reach was more than three times higher than in 1988, partly due to improved sampling coverage and partly due to a real increase.
- Of 36 pike in the Juniper Springs reach, 31 were collected in May and June during spring runoff. Twenty-six of these came from two tributary backwaters at RM 106.8 and 103.4. All were 374-470 mm TL, a size range not previously seen below Craig, which suggests downstream displacement.
- The population remains relatively small and composed mostly of adults in the reaches inhabited by Colorado Squawfish. Successful reproduction and juvenile pike appear to occur only in the Hayden-Craig reach.
- Spawning occurs mainly in April through June, when spring flows inundate suitable vegetated habitat.
- Radio-tagged pike preferred mainstem pools, eddies and backwaters. Two tagged pike (tags 140.232 and 142.242) appear to have expelled or passed their transmitters during runoff. One transmitter was later found in a residual pool that may have been spawning habitat.
- Stomach samples: 24 of 35 northern pike contained fish prey. No prey selectivity was detected, so availability probably determines diet composition. Colorado Squawfish were not a common prey item, and stomach samples from 37% of the 1989 sample did not indicate predation on squawfish.
- Growth and condition appear best in the Hayden-Craig reach, possibly due to habitat quality differences.

### Channel Catfish (Yampa and Colorado Rivers)

- In 1989, Yampa catfish ranged from 283 to 881 mm TL (mean 489 mm) and weighed 150 to 10,880 g (mean 1,175 g). The length-weight relationship was log W = 3.7?4 log L - 6.974 (R² = 0.934, n = 73), and condition factors ranged from 0.397 to 1.617 (mean 0.875), indicating a healthy population.
- Catfish were relatively few above Cross Mountain Canyon, were mostly large adults, and juveniles were concentrated in the lower Yampa (Lilly Park and Echo Park).
- No sexual maturation was found in Echo Park catfish (233-570 mm TL) sampled on 13 July, which may reflect a short growing season.
- Colorado River catfish ranged from 390 to 2,900 g (mean 515 g) in length-weight analyses, with a similar size-frequency pattern (peaks at 380-460 mm). Catfish numbers per backwater averaged 8 in April and 22 in July; 95 fish (52%) came from two backwaters at RM 135.5 and 128.1. Mean condition factor was 0.841.
- Stomach samples: only unidentifiable fish and fish parts were found in 13 of 80 catfish stomachs. Decomposed prey suggests scavenging rather than predation on live fish.

### Centrarchids and Other Warmwater Gamefish

- **Smallmouth Bass:** Two adults (156 and 186 mm TL) were found near Morgan Gulch in May 1989. Twenty-nine young-of-the-year (31-47 mm TL, mean 41 mm) were collected at RM 145.1 in August. An adult female with eggs (307 mm TL) was caught in October at RM 134.5. Smallmouth bass are highly piscivorous as young, but occur rarely in the river.
- **Largemouth Bass (Colorado River):** Only two adults and three juveniles were captured in April and July 1989. Seine data show a three-year declining trend in young-of-the-year, and reproduction appears unsuccessful. Half of the young bass sampled had eaten fish.
- **Green Sunfish:** Mostly collected from shallow, clear-water backwater tips. Sunfish were rarely caught, and about one-third of the larger individuals were piscivorous.
- **Black Bullhead:** Present in backwaters from RM 179.2 to 128.1. A notable catch at RM 128.1 in July included more than 1,000 young-of-the-year (10-12 mm), which were not found in other backwaters.

### Occurrence and Abundance Trends

- Warmwater gamefish young-of-the-year occurred in 53%, 90% and 64% of backwaters sampled in 1987, 1988 and 1989, respectively, but over half of specimens came from only 1-3 of 19-22 backwaters.
- Frequency of occurrence in seine samples was 41%, 73% and 40% for 1987-1989.
- Centrarchid YOY are probably immigrants from adjacent ponds that connect with the river during high spring flows, and survival within the river appears very low.

### Predation Risk to Endangered Fishes

- The report concludes that predation risk to endangered fishes from centrarchids appears minimal at present, given low predator abundance and the scarcity of squawfish and razorback sucker young-of-the-year.
- Northern pike predation on squawfish is not considered significant. The size difference between pike and squawfish, and the displacement of small pike downstream, were noted as limiting factors.
- Northern pike migration into the Green River may increase predation risk on juvenile endangered fishes there. This is described as a small but chronic problem.

### Conclusions (Tentative)

1. Northern pike abundance in the Yampa River is relatively low. Pike below Craig are maintained largely by emigrants from the Hayden-Craig reach, rather than by reproduction in squawfish habitat.
2. Spawning is constrained by spring spawning habitat (immersed vegetation), which flow regime influences.
3. Long-distance movement occurs, with some adult pike emigrating from the Yampa to the Green River.
4. Channel catfish are more abundant in the Colorado River than the Yampa, but do not reach as large sizes. Catfish successful reproduction appears to occur in Yampa Canyon.
5. Centrarchid YOY in the Colorado River result mainly from emigration from adjacent ponds. The flow and temperature regime is not conducive to successful centrarchid reproduction.
6. Removal experiments involving northern pike and channel catfish were not evaluated due to flow conditions and oil contamination, and were planned to resume in 1990.
