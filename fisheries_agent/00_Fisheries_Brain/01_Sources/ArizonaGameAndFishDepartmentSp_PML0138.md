# Public Comments on the Arizona Game and Fish Department Sport Fish Stocking Draft Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 2011 comment letter from fishery scientist [[Paul Marsh]] (Marsh & Associates, LLC) on the Draft Environmental Assessment (EA) for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) [[Sport Fish Stocking Program]], prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) under the [[Dingell-Johnson Sport Fish Restoration Act]]. The letter argues that sport fish management and native fish conservation are incompatible in the southwestern United States and that the two must be spatially segregated. It rejects the proposed expansion of stocking and the Reduced Stocking Alternative as inadequate, and calls for an acceptable alternative, meaningful mitigation, and substantially greater funding. The attached email exchanges among Marsh, Jerry Stefferud, and Robert Clarkson contain internal critiques, mitigation budget calculations, and Endangered Species Act (ESA) consultation notes. The document also includes the EA's own tables and mitigation program text.

## Background & Description

- **Commentator:** Paul C. Marsh, PhD, a fishery scientist with more than thirty years' experience with native and non-native fishes of the American Southwest, and a long-time Arizona angler.
- **Proposed Action:** Continued AGFD sport fish stocking for a 10-year period, funded in part by FWS. It covers operation of five AGFD hatcheries (including [[Page Springs Hatchery]], [[Tonto Creek Hatchery]], [[Silver Creek Hatchery]], [[Canyon Creek Hatchery]], and [[Sterling Springs Hatchery]]) and stocking at 167 sites (58 cold-water, 23 warm-water, 86 cold- and warm-water). The EA cites 18 stocked species in the Proposed Action and 17 in the Reduced Stocking Alternative.
- **Alternatives in the EA:**
  - *Proposed Action* (167 sites).
  - *Reduced Stocking Alternative* (101 sites; 66 eliminated, mainly where listed species occur).
  - *No Action* (no stocking).
  - *Eliminated:* Expanded Stocking (about 386 sites, including 219 historical sites) and Minimal Stocking (19 sites).
- **Mitigation program:** A $5 million, 10-year Sport Fish Stocking Conservation and Mitigation Program, about $500,000 per year, covering 24 program species (7 focal, 16 additional conservation, and sensitive species).
- **Scoping:** Roughly 130 public responses, with general support for stocking and concern about native fish, plus agency concerns about non-native fish.

Key terms: [[Consultation Species]], [[Biological and Conference Opinion]] (BCO), [[Angler User Day]] (AUD), [[HACCP Plans]], [[Triploid Rainbow Trout]], [[Meaningful Mitigation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Arizona has 36 native fish species, about 20 of which are listed under the ESA or otherwise imperiled. The letter describes non-native fishes as the most significant factor in the loss and decline of native fishes.
- Introductions of non-native fishes in Arizona were most frequent between 1940 and 1960. Sixty-five non-native species have been introduced, most through authorized stockings. Of these, about 60 were likely unauthorized or unintentional (bait bucket and aquarium releases).
- Stocked fish can disperse both upstream and downstream, and intermittent reaches are not reliable barriers during flow, according to Marsh's reading of the case history (for example, [[Striped Bass]] in the lower Colorado River and [[Gizzard Shad]] arriving unexpectedly).
- The EA records non-native aquatic species at 132 of the 167 proposed stocking sites.
- Stocking effects on the environment include predation, competition, hybridization, disease and parasite transmission, invasive species spread (such as [[Quagga Mussel]] and [[Giant Salvinia]]), and angler disturbance. The EA also discusses effects on fish-eating birds such as bald eagles and ospreys, and on amphibians such as the [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]].
- Climate change and drought are identified as additional stressors.

## Key Findings

**Marsh's main objections to the EA:**

1. **Incompatibility:** Sport fish management and native fish conservation cannot both succeed in the same waters. Spatial segregation is the only option.
2. **No need case:** The EA does not justify the need to meet future demand for angling through stocking, unlike the permitted allocation used for terrestrial game.
3. **Unacceptable alternatives:**
   - The Proposed Action is a significant, unnecessary expansion of stocking.
   - The Reduced Stocking Alternative is illogical. For example, it removes urban lakes, which have no potential to affect native fishes.
   - The No Action Alternative is included only for formality.
4. **Declines:** Native fish status would deteriorate under the Proposed Action. Effects include new ESA listings, uplisting from threatened to endangered, range contraction, and population losses.

**Recommended acceptable alternative:**
- Substantially fewer sites, focused on high-use areas without native fish.
- No stocking in habitats of listed or imperiled species, critical habitat, or habitat needed for recovery.
- No stocking where self-sustaining sport fish populations already exist.
- No stocking where five or fewer angler users per day occur.
- Native [[Apache Trout]] or [[Gila Trout]] to be used instead of non-native trout where within historic range.
- Limit channel catfish, bluegill, redear sunfish, and largemouth bass to urban lakes.

**Recommended meaningful mitigation:**
- Meaningful mitigation means direct, measurable benefits to native fishes, such as isolating existing populations, establishing new populations in protected sites, and removing non-native fishes where practicable. Planning, monitoring, and outreach do not qualify.
- Minimum requirements: no net loss of any native population; compliance with the 1995 and 2001 incidental take statements for [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]; and no stocking in or upstream of waters occupied by listed species.
- Existing actions should not count as new mitigation.
- Stocking should not occur where the Gila complex occurs (Gila chub, headwater chub, roundtail chub).

**Funding concerns:**
- The $500,000 annual mitigation fund is 0.038% of the estimated annual economic impact of recreational fishing in Arizona. Marsh estimates that most of it could go to salaries for about seven AGFD positions, leaving about $170,000 for project costs.
- Marsh compares this with the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) program ($550,000 per year for six species, roughly $111,833 per species). He recommends $1.5 to $3 million per year.
- The EA does not identify the funding source, annual allocation, or species allocation.

**Specific technical comments:**
- The EA understates dispersal. Fishes are known to move long distances both up- and downstream.
- The EA's distinction between direct and indirect effects "seems trivial."
- The use of 2001 angler survey data is questionable.
- Rio Grande sucker is not native to Arizona, so the citation is incorrect.
- The EA states that 27 native fish species occur in Arizona; the correct number is 36.
- Literature cited lacks species recovery plans, which should be incorporated.
- Hybridization and competition from triploid trout remain concerns.
- Mitigation actions in Table 35 are not guaranteed, while stocking impacts are certain.

**Attached internal correspondence:**
- Stefferud's notes suggest bridging the EA's inconsistency between opposing stocking in principle and proposing mitigation for it.
- Marsh's budget analysis estimates per-species costs and a fallback mitigation budget of about $917,831 per year.
- Emails question whether the LCR spinedace ITS mitigation from 1995 and 2001 is being double-counted.
- The Roundtail chub and Headwater chub analyses note that triploid rainbow trout still prey on native fishes, and that their effect on wild populations remains uncertain.

**EA conclusions criticized:**
- The EA concludes the Proposed Action is "not likely to jeopardize" any consultation species. The letter argues that this rests on uncertain assumptions and on mitigation that is not guaranteed.
- The letter concludes the EA must choose between two incompatible goals, and that AGFD "needs to confront the reality" and develop strategies to spatially segregate the two activities.
