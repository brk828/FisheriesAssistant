## Executive Summary

This 1947 study by Justin W. Leonard analyzes the stomach contents of 322 rainbow trout ([[Salmo gairdneri irideus]]) collected over a six-year period from Birch Lake, Michigan, to infer the burrowing behavior and habitat availability of two co-occurring species of burrowing mayfly nymphs: [[Ephemera simulans]] and [[Hexagenia occulta]]. Although both species occur in similar dense concentrations in bottom dredge samples, they show a striking disparity in their frequency of occurrence in trout diets. *H. occulta* was consumed steadily throughout a 26-week collection period, while *E. simulans* appeared in stomachs on only six dates, overwhelmingly concentrated during a single mass emergence event. The paper concludes that this dietary disparity reflects differing burrow depths/behaviors of the two mayfly species rather than differences in their abundance, life-cycle length, or trout feeding selectivity.

## Background & Description

- **Fish sampled**: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri irideus* Gibbons), ranging from 7.75 to 22.25 inches in length.
- **Sample size**: 322 stomachs collected via angler creel census (with a few gill-net exceptions) over a six-year period, spanning 31 weeks (May 21–December 19).
- **Mayfly nymphs**:
  - *Ephemera simulans*: burrowing nymph and subimago stages recorded; nymphs described as reaching maturity (females with well-developed eggs) by late June.
  - *Hexagenia occulta*: nymphal stage only found in stomachs; subimaginal/adult winged stages were never eaten by trout, despite emergence occurring within the sampling window.
- **Comparative note**: A related species, [[Hexagenia recurvata]], observed by the author in a sand-bottom Michigan trout stream, was rarely found less than four inches below the stream-bed surface — supporting the inference of deep burrowing habits in this mayfly genus/family.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Location**: [[Birch Lake]], an oligotrophic lake in Cass County, southwestern Michigan.
- **Substrate interaction**: Both *E. simulans* and *H. occulta* are burrowing mayfly nymphs that inhabit soft lake-bottom substrate, frequently co-occurring in Ekman/Peterson dredge samples in approximately equal numbers.
- **Trout diet composition (volumetric basis)**:
  - Fish: 43%
  - Aquatic insects: 23%
  - Vegetable matter: 6%
  - Mollusks: 3%
  - Decapod crustaceans: 3%
  - Terrestrial insects (87 species): 3%
  - Unseparated organic debris, entomostracans, water mites, spiders: 19%
- **Dietary breadth**: The rainbow trout diet included 170 species of invertebrates, indicating a highly opportunistic, generalist feeding strategy — a key contextual factor supporting the conclusion that differential nymph occurrence reflects prey **availability**, not trout selectivity.
- **Seasonal feeding pattern**: Terrestrial insects were rare in trout stomachs except during October and early November; *H. occulta* nymphs were consumed consistently across almost the entire seasonal sampling window; *E. simulans* was essentially only consumed during mass emergence/migration to the surface.
- **Related ecological observations**: [[Ephoron leukon]] nymphs (Ide, 1935) in Ontario stream riffles were found to retreat deep into their burrow tubes during the day and rise to the tube opening in the evening to feed — a light-avoidance behavior cited as a comparative model for burrowing mayfly vertical movement.
- **Broader fishery context**: Prior literature (Neave, 1932) identified *H. occulta* nymphs as an important food source for [[Sturgeon]], [[Whitefish]], [[Tullibee]], [[Goldeye]], and [[Sauger]] in Lake Winnipeg, reinforcing the ecological importance of *Hexagenia* nymphs as a year-round prey base for bottom-feeding fishes.

## Key Findings

1. **Disparate occurrence despite co-abundance**: Even though *E. simulans* and *H. occulta* nymphs occur together in similar densities in bottom dredge samples, they show radically different frequencies in trout stomachs, implying differing degrees of vertical accessibility within the substrate rather than differing overall abundance.
2. **Mass emergence spike for *E. simulans***: On May 23, during a mass emergence event, trout stomachs averaged 507 nymphs and 106 subimagoes each, with one 18-inch rainbow trout containing 789 nymphs and 232 subimagoes — a dramatic outlier compared to the otherwise negligible occurrence (~3 nymphs/stomach) on other dates.
3. **Steady occurrence of *H. occulta***: Unlike *E. simulans*, *H. occulta* nymphs appeared consistently in trout stomachs across a continuous 26-week span beginning June 25, despite winged (subimaginal/adult) stages never being consumed.
4. **Behavioral inference over life-cycle explanation**: The two-year life cycle of *H. occulta* versus the annual cycle of *E. simulans* cannot explain the observed disparity, since sampling spanned six years and seven months — ruling out a phenological artifact.
5. **Proposed explanation — differential burrow depth**: The author concludes that *E. simulans* likely burrows too deep in the substrate to be regularly accessible to bottom-feeding trout, while *H. occulta* nymphs either periodically leave their burrows or remain close enough to the surface to be consistently vulnerable to predation.
6. **Trout as opportunistic generalist feeders**: The extremely catholic diet (170 invertebrate species, 87 terrestrial insect species) supports the interpretation that prey occurrence in trout stomachs is governed primarily by prey **availability** in the substrate, not trout dietary preference — validating the use of stomach content analysis as a proxy for benthic invertebrate microhabitat behavior.
7. **Thermal barrier to feeding on winged stages**: Trout failure to consume subimaginal/adult *H. occulta* is attributed to the fact that emergence occurs in late June/early July when surface waters are too warm, causing trout to avoid the lake's warmer upper strata.
