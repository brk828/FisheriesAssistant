# Marine Reserves as Reef Fishery Management Tools

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reef-fishes

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[Garry R Russ]] of James Cook University, examines whether [[Marine Reserves]] (permanently closed no-fishing areas) can function as effective management tools for [[Coral Reef Fisheries]]. The author treats reserves as an "insurance policy" against traditional effort and catch controls, which have repeatedly failed (e.g., North Atlantic cod). He argues that empirical evidence for reserve effects on density, size, biomass and reproductive output remains equivocal, mainly because no well-designed Before-After Control-Impact Pairs experiment has yet been completed. Reserves are not presented as the only or optimal management approach, and the author stresses that fishing-effort reduction and alternative livelihoods are also needed in developing nations.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** A chapter in *Coral Reef Fishes* (Elsevier, 2002), pp. 421 onward, that reviews seven basic expectations of reserves as fisheries tools.
- **Definition:** "Marine reserves" means areas permanently closed to fishing.
- **Motivation:** Subsistence and artisanal fishers in developing nations cannot practically be restricted by size or catch limits, and traditional management has largely failed globally.
- **Literature base:** Tables 1 and 2 list numerous reviews and modeling studies; Table 3 summarizes studies of large predatory reef fish density and Table 4 summarizes nine studies with spatial and temporal comparisons.
- **Life cycle:** Reef fishes have a benthic adult phase and a pelagic larval phase, dispersing over tens to thousands of meters (adults) and tens to hundreds of kilometers (larvae), creating two distinct spillover and recruitment pathways.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fishing mortality (F):** Reserves are assumed to have lower F, but few studies measure it. Kelleher et al. (1995) found management effectiveness was known for only 29% (383 of 1306) of world MPAs, and almost 70% of those had only moderate to low effectiveness.
- **Poaching:** Russ and Alcala (1996a) showed that 7–12 years of uncontrolled fishing at Sumilon Island, Philippines, removed density and biomass accumulated over 5–10 years of protection.
- **Great Barrier Reef Marine Park:** About 5% of the 350,000 km² park is no-fishing. Ferreira and Russ (1995) and Adams et al. (2000) found no significant age-frequency differences in [[Coral Trout]] between green (no-take) and fished (blue) reefs in three of four regions.
- **Reproductive output:** Fecundity increases exponentially with body size. A 61 cm female red snapper was estimated to equal the fecundity of 272 females of 42 cm (PDT 1990). Florida groupers had spawning potential ratios mostly below 20% of unfished levels (Bohnsack and Ault 1996).
- **Movement and spillover:** Tag-recapture and telemetry studies show most reef fish stay close to home. Davies (1995) found less than 1% of 4,627 tagged [[Plectropomus leopardus]] moved between reefs. Kramer and Chapman (1999) in Barbados detected no tagged fish moving out of the reserve. Movement is generally within a few hundred meters to 1 km, so spillover benefits are likely to operate at about 100–500 m.
- **Larval dispersal:** Palumbi (2000) estimated average dispersal distances of 4–30 km from genetic isolation-by-distance modeling. Otolith tagging (Jones et al. 1999) and trace-element studies (Swearer et al. 1999) indicate substantial self-recruitment, though GBR modeling suggests self-recruitment is modest for individual reefs.
- **Reserve coverage:** Closed proportions cited include 5% (GBR), 6% (Florida Keys) and 2% (Bermuda). Modeling suggests 40–50% of reef regions may be needed for a sufficient recruitment hedge (Lauck et al. 1998; Sladek-Nowlis and Roberts 1999).

## Key Findings

1. **Seven basic expectations** of reserves are defined: five inside reserves (lower F; higher density, mean size/age, biomass, and egg/larval production) and two outside (adult spillover and recruitment effect).
2. **Evidence for density, size and biomass effects is equivocal.** Spatial comparisons suggest density is on average about three times higher inside reserves. The few long-term temporal studies suggest a mean density increase of 3.9-fold over 9 years.
3. **Study design is weak:** Of 25 studies reviewed, 16 (64%) used spatial comparisons only, and only 4 (16%) included BEFORE data. Many were pseudoreplicated, with only one reserve and one control per location.
4. **Recovery takes decades:** Continuous protection of 5–20 years is likely needed, and for many reef fishes biomass buildup may take decades.
5. **Reproductive output:** Only one of 25 studies estimated reproductive output per unit area. Paddack and Estes (2000) found rockfish reproductive potential 2.9 and 2.7 times higher inside Hopkins and Point Lobos reserves (36 and 12 years of protection), but nearly identical inside Big Creek (2 years).
6. **Spillover is real but small-scale:** Adult emigration is demonstrated (e.g., Attwood and Bennett 1994, 17,022 fishes tagged, 1,008 recaptures), but net export is unproven because immigration was not measured.
7. **Recruitment effect remains untested:** An unequivocal test would require a regional network experiment over 10–20 years, which the author considers nearly impossible to carry out.
8. **Proposed experimental design:** A BACIP design with replicated reserve and fished pairs, sampled before and after establishment, measuring F directly, and run regionally over 5–20 years, with tagging to detect net export.
9. **Main conclusion:** Marine reserves should not be rejected or delayed because the optimal design is unknown. They serve as an insurance policy and a spawning-stock reserve even if they do not fully offset lost fishing area.
