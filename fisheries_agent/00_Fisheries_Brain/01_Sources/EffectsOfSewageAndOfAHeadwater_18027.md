# Effects of Sewage and of a Headwaters Impoundment on the Fishes of Stillwater Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1950 study by Frank Bernard Cross, published in *The American Midland Naturalist*, compares a 1947–48 fish survey of [[Stillwater Creek]] with an earlier 1938 survey by Moore and Mizelle. Two changes occurred between surveys: construction of [[Lake Carl Blackwell]] (a 3,300-acre flood-control impoundment about 16.37 river miles upstream) and a rise in the city's sewage load from under 750,000 to 1,600,000 gallons daily, of which 850,000 gallons were untreated. Of the 28 species recorded in 1938, 26 were retaken, mostly in greater abundance, and 14 additional species were reported as first official records for the system. The author concludes that sewage and impoundment effects together have increased fish abundance, while sewage-driven oxygen deficiency caused recurrent fish kills.

## Background & Description

The survey covered about 38.37 miles of mainstream running through Permian red-bed from Blackwell Lake to the junction with the [[Cimarron River]]. The channel is 20 to 30 feet wide, has steep banks and a mud bottom, and is generally less than four feet deep. Sand bottoms occur in [[Spring Creek]] and parts of Little Stillwater Creek. Near the mouth the channel widens to about 80 feet, with flow usually under half a foot per second.

Collections used a 10-foot ¼-inch mesh seine as the primary gear. A gill net was tried unsuccessfully and abandoned. Water-quality measurements were limited to temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen (Rideal-Stewart modification of the Winkler method). Six of the 1938 stations were used, with stations A, C, D, and F sampled at roughly three-week intervals and B and E less often. Station descriptions cover the quiet mouth area (A), an intermittent tributary (B), a sewage-influenced reach (C, D), an artificial pond series at [[Boomer Creek]] (E), and the tailwater below Blackwell Lake dam (F).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Impoundment release maintained flow in formerly intermittent reaches and stabilized flow, while reducing turbidity through settling.
- **Nutrients and food:** Sewage increased fertility and fish food production. Red-bed soils otherwise keep natural waters highly turbid, with stream turbidity reported as high as 30,000 ppm in the Southwest.
- **Oxygen:** Low dissolved oxygen, especially when lake valves were closed, caused fish kills in December 1947 and April 1948.
- **Introductions and escapes:** Exotic and stocked fishes entered the lake and escaped downstream through outlet valves, adding species such as [[White Crappie]] and [[Channel Catfish]] to the creek fauna.
- **Migration and spawning:** Low-water dams at station D blocked upstream spawning migrations. The author found no evidence that the dam inhibited spawning overall, but reproduction was lost during the spring kill.
- **Sewage tolerance:** Gizzard shad, carp-suckers, and minnows benefited most. Black bass, bluegill, and some other centrarchids appeared intolerant of sewage.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness and abundance:** 26 of 28 species from 1938 were retaken, most in higher numbers. Fourteen species were first official records for the system, including [[Gizzard Shad]], [[White Bass]], and [[Channel Catfish]] in increased abundance.
- **Red shiner dominance:** [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) was the most abundant species at stations C, D, E, and F, with the largest populations at the sewage-influenced stations C and D.
- **Plains minnow:** [[Plains Minnow]] (*Hybognathus placita*) produced the largest total catch, mainly from extreme fall abundance at station A, and most likely migrated to the river to spawn.
- **Arkansas River shiner:** *Notropis girardi* was extremely abundant at station A and spawned only in the river, with eggs found only in flood-turbid river hauls.
- **White bass:** [[White Bass]] (*Lepibema chrysops*) became the most important sport fish in lower Stillwater Creek, with anglers reporting catches of 30 to 40 per fisherman-day.
- **Channel catfish:** Abundance rose markedly, with 96 taken in one ten-foot seine haul on December 18 and extensive upstream migration during high water.
- **Sunfish:** [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) declined in relative dominance, while [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] (*Lepomis humilis*) was the most tolerant centrarchid. [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) was scarce outside Boomer Creek and appeared intolerant of sewage.
- **White crappie:** 139 were tagged between November 25 and January 8, with only two recaptured in spring and summer, suggesting downstream displacement during high flows.
- **Fish kills:** Oxygen depletion after the lake valves closed on about November 24, 1947 killed fishes at stations C and D. A second kill occurred in April 1948. Phenacobius spawning attempts failed during the second kill.
- **Sewage benefit, with limits:** The author concludes sewage benefited the fish fauna overall. However, sludge deposition exceeded removal, and the large populations are not self-sustaining and depend on replenishment from the river and lake.
- **Taxonomic notes:** Ceratichthys specimens were treated together as *C. perspicuus* and *C. vigilax* because of hybridization. A putative hybrid, *Hybognathus placita* × *Notropis girardi*, was recorded from station A.
