# Status of Nebraska Fishes in the Missouri River: Burbot (*Lota lota*), Gadidae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae

## Executive Summary

This Nebraska Game and Parks Commission report, prepared by [[Larry W. Hesse]] under the [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] (F-75-R-10, February 1993), reviews the status of [[Burbot]] in the [[Missouri River]]. Burbot were once common in the [[Gavins Point Dam]] tailwater but declined sharply after the mid-1950s dam closures. They are now very rare in Nebraska's portion of the river. The author recommends that burbot be listed as endangered under the [[Nebraska Nongame and Endangered Species Conservation Act]].

## Background & Description

**Physical description.** Burbot are slender, minutely scaled fish with a single barbel at the center of the chin and two dorsal fins. They can reach a maximum weight of 34 kg (Keleher 1961). The anterior dorsal fin is short and non-spinous, and the posterior dorsal fin is nearly half the body length and more rigid. The anal fin is nearly as long as the posterior dorsal fin. The caudal fin is rounded and separated from the dorsal and anal fins. In life, burbot are mottled olive, shading through brown to black, with a white belly shading to yellow (Pflieger 1975).

**Taxonomy and distribution.** Burbot are the only freshwater member of the mostly marine cod family [[Gadidae]]. They are a northern species that occurs as far south as Missouri (Pflieger 1975). Nebraska lies on the southern edge of their range. Historical records include the Missouri River mainstem near Kansas City, Leavenworth, Atchison, St. Joseph, and White Cloud (Cross 1967). Burbot were also reported from the Kansas River after large Missouri River floods (Cross 1967), the Niobrara River (one specimen, Johnson 1942), the Platte River (Johnson 1942, uncertain), and the Cheyenne River system east of the Black Hills (Bailey and Allum 1962). Their range extends across interior Alaska, Canada, the Great Lakes Basin, and the northern Rocky Mountain states.

**Conservation status in neighboring jurisdictions.** Iowa lists burbot as threatened (Iowa Administrative Code 57, Chapter 77). Burbot populations were reported as healthy in Montana and Wyoming (personal communications cited in the text).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.**
- Burbot are secretive and hide by day among snags, aquatic vegetation, rock piles, and other underwater structure (Pflieger 1975; Harlan et al. 1987).
- Young burbot forage actively at night in crevices and holes among snags and banklines, feeding mainly on amphipods such as *Hyalella* sp. and *Gammarus* sp. (Hanson and Qadri 1980).
- Adults feed on fish, crawfish, and crustaceans, especially in summer (Bailey 1972).
- Cross (1967) found burbot among large rock, collapsed bridges, fallen trees, and erosional areas where overhanging banks provided cover.
- Burbot are sedentary except during spawning movements, when they undertake lengthy upstream migrations (Breeser et al. 1988).
- In Alaska, burbot preferred turbid glacial rivers and moved into clear tributaries only late in summer, after velocities declined (Chen 1969; Mecum 1984; Breeser et al. 1988).

**Spawning.** Burbot spawn communally at night in winter, in water about one meter or less deep, over gravel or compacted sand. Young burbot occupied weed beds on gravel bottoms in swift current (Cross 1967). Cahn (1936) observed spawning under ice at an air temperature of -28 °F. A single mature female may lay more than one million eggs (Cross 1967).

**Historical and biogeographic context.** Pflieger (1971) proposed that burbot entered the Mississippi River valley during the Pleistocene, when glaciation rearranged drainages and allowed faunal exchange with more northerly systems.

**Threats and factors affecting burbot (Hesse).**
- **Dams and migration barriers:** Dam construction on the Missouri mainstem and most tributaries has blocked burbot migration to suitable spawning grounds.
- **Channelization:** Channelization of the Missouri River from Ponca, NE to St. Louis, MO eliminated off-channel habitat that was important for young burbot and rich in aquatic insects.
- **Loss of large woody debris:** More than 100 years of snagging removed large woody debris, which provided habitat, substrate for aquatic insects, and attraction for adult burbot and native predators such as sauger and catfish.
- **Reduced organic matter:** Settling of coarse particulate organic matter (CPOM) in reservoirs and reduced flooding have reduced amphipod shredder populations.
- **Overfishing in tailwaters:** Burbot were stopped in their migration below dams and became vulnerable to harvest. They were likely overfished between [[Fort Randall Dam]] and [[Gavins Point Dam]] shortly after closure in the mid-1950s.
- **Loss of turbidity:** Reduced sediment transport may make young burbot more vulnerable to sight-feeding predators such as walleye, smallmouth bass, and northern pike.
- **Unstudied disease and parasites:** Whether disease or parasites significantly limit survival in Nebraska has not been investigated.
- **Regulatory gaps:** Existing regulations do not adequately protect burbot from habitat loss, the elimination of snags and CPOM, loss of turbidity, reduced aquatic insects, or dam barriers.

## Key Findings

**1. Historical decline in tailwater harvest (Gavins Point Dam)**
- Burbot were 5.1% of the harvest in the Gavins Point Dam tailwater in July 1956, one year after closure (Abstract).
- Harvest rose from 510 burbot in 1956 to 4,280 in 1958 (Abstract). The body text gives 4,780 (2%) for 1958, a discrepancy in the source.
- Burbot were 0 in 1961 (out of 539,945 fish harvested) and 0 in 1962 (out of 710,389 fish harvested) (Orr 1962).
- By 1961 burbot were no longer routinely caught in the tailwater.

**2. Declines at other Missouri River tailwaters and reservoirs**
- **Lake Sakakawea (Garrison Dam, ND):** Commercial harvest was 1,500 kg in 1960, none from 1961 through 1974, 11 kg in 1975, and none through 1984.
- **Oahe tailwater (1959–62):** Burbot made up 2.0% (1959), 1.7% (1960), 0.3% (1961), and 0.4% (1962) of the catch. A total of 4,408 burbot weighing 5,333 kg were caught among 372,913 angler-caught fish.
- **Fort Randall Dam tailwater (1955–59):** 7,950 burbot weighing 6,567 kg were harvested among 410,078 other fish. The percent composition was 1.3% (1955), 2.0% (1956), 1.2% (1957), 2.9% (1958), and 2.9% (1959). In 1990, no burbot were harvested among 11,199 other fish caught (Stone and Wickstrom 1991).
- **Lewis and Clark Lake:** Burbot were collected in 1955–57 but not in 1958–74 collections (Walburg 1964, 1976). Walburg attributed this to conversion of riverine habitat to reservoir and to water release patterns.
- **Tailwater gill netting:** Walburg et al. (1971) collected 11 burbot among 5,486 large fish (0.2%) from the Gavins Point Dam tailwater in 1968–69.

**3. Nebraska sport fishery surveys**
- A 1972 creel survey (Groen 1973) recorded no burbot harvest in the Gavins Point tailwater or the unchannelized reach downstream. Six burbot (1% by composition) were harvested downstream from Omaha.
- User surveys in 1978, 1984, and 1992 reported no burbot harvested or caught and released in Nebraska's Missouri River.

**4. Electrofishing and biological surveys**
- **Channelized Missouri River, eastern Nebraska (1970–75):** 20 burbot among 29,493 large fish (0.07%) (Hesse and Wallace 1976). This is the largest burbot sample in the report.
- **Same locations, since 1983:** 2,019 large fish electrofished, no burbot collected.
- **Unchannelized reaches, northeast Nebraska:** 7,024 large fish collected, including 4 burbot (0.06%).
- A 1970–75 analysis of length-frequency, length-weight, and condition factors by month is in Hesse et al. (1978).

**5. Evidence of recent reproduction (larval fish)**
- Larval burbot were collected in northeast Nebraska in 1984 (2 specimens, tailwater of Gavins Point Dam), 1985 (3 larvae: 2 in the tailwater and 1 upstream at Niobrara), and 1986 (1 larva, 16 km downstream from Gavins Point Dam).
- The six larvae were very rare among more than 150,000 fish larvae collected from nearly 400,000 cubic meters of river water.
- The author notes that these records suggest a few adults remain in the river.

**6. Individual specimen records (Table 1, 1970–92)**
- Table 1 lists all burbot collected from the Missouri River in Nebraska, 1970–92, by hoop net, electrofishing, explosive, and plankton drift net.
- The 20 specimens from 1970–75 range from 125 mm to 448 mm in length. Most were from Blair and Brownville.
- Later adult records include a Niobrara River specimen (1978), a Nebraska City specimen (291 mm), and Gavins Point specimens (1983–88, 327–434 mm), plus two Sunshine Bottom specimens (1989, 314 and 316 mm).
- Additional records include a 102 mm burbot trawled near the bottom of the old river channel at Lewis and Clark Lake during 1987–88 (personal communication).

**7. Conclusion and recommendation**
- The author concludes that burbot are very rare in Nebraska and should be listed as **endangered**.
- The report recommends that burbot be listed as endangered under the [[Nebraska Nongame and Endangered Species Conservation Act]] (37-430 to 37-438).
- The author states that it is unlikely any new projects would rapidly diminish remaining burbot. Restoration is still needed, including hydraulic reconnection of cut-off backwaters, return to a semblance of a natural hydrograph, restoration of sediment and organic matter dynamics, recovery of large woody debris, and construction of fish bypasses at selected dams.
