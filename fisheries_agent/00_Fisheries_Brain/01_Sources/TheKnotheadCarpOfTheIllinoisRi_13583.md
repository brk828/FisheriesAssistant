# The "Knothead" Carp of the Illinois River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1928 bulletin from the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]], by David H. Thompson, describes a skeletal and growth malformation in [[Cyprinus Carpio]] (common carp) of the middle [[Illinois River]]. Fishermen called the affected fish "knotheads." The study was begun with [[Ludwig Scheuring]] during his 1926–1927 visit. The author links the condition to water pollution that began during the 1916–1918 increase in sewage load. He argues that the malformation is a rickets-like metabolic disorder, probably caused by loss of green plant food and, by inference, a dietary lack of vitamin D. The author states this is probably the first known instance of rickets among fishes, but he notes that feeding experiments were still needed to confirm the cause.

## Background & Description

- **Economic context:** Carp were introduced in 1885 and became the most important commercial fish in the Illinois River. In 1908 the river yielded 23,896,000 pounds of commercial fish, of which carp made up 64 per cent (15,400,000 pounds). After pollution and drainage, the river's annual yield fell by about half, and carp later made up 80 to 90 per cent of the total commercial catch.
- **External form:** Knotheads are slender, with malformed, bulged or curled [[Opercle]] bones (average curvature like a spoon bowl), sunken cheeks, a narrowed pectoral girdle, an emaciated head, and a "drooping," backward-inclined dorsal fin. The large serrated dorsal and anal spines are shortened, often to less than one-quarter normal length, and the dorsal spine is usually shorter than the anal spine (the reverse of normal).
- **Internal and skeletal features:** Gill lamellae are smaller and fewer (average 11.0 mm on the malformed head versus 15.5 mm on the normal head). The skull bones are thickened and dense over ossification centers but thinner in the deep bones. The skull is smaller overall, yet its nerve and vessel foramina are larger. Vertebrae showed no visible malformation.
- **Comparison specimens:** Table II contrasts a normal and a knothead carp, both 4 summers old. The normal measured 14.2 inches, weighed 57 ounces, and had a body depth of 5.0 inches. The knothead measured 12.4 inches, weighed 30 ounces, and had a body depth of 3.9 inches.
- **Age:** Knotheads were generally older than normals of the same size. The author describes some as three times the age of normals of the same weight.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pollution gradient:** Knotheads ranged from [[Utica]] (Starved Rock), the upper limit of fish life, down to the [[Copperas Creek Dam]], about ninety miles. Above Utica, [[Dissolved Oxygen]] was too low for fish. Frequency fell downstream, consistent with a pollution-linked cause. Within the zone the average was over 50 per cent, and near the upper end it exceeded 90 per cent.
- **Selected station data (1926–1927):** [[Peoria]] had 256 carp examined with 78% knotheads. [[Henry]] had 316 examined with 75%, [[Chillicothe]] 117 with 64%, [[Averyville]] 149 with 57%, and [[Spring Bay]] 302 with 46%. [[Pekin]] had about 500 carp with about 30%, and knotheads there were often pot-bellied. [[Havana]] had 539 examined with none (only occasional stragglers).
- **Other waters:** Fewer than a dozen knotheads were seen in many thousands of [[Rock River]] carp. Indirect reports came from [[Lake Pepin]] on the Upper [[Mississippi River]]. Other species of the middle river (about twenty larger species) showed no similar malformation.
- **Food supply changes:** Between 1915 and 1920 the [[Potamogeton]] and other coarse aquatic plants in [[Peoria Lake]] were almost wiped out, and partial recovery began around 1922. Summer [[Phytoplankton]] shifted from green algae and diatoms to non-chlorophyll protozoa and [[Blue-Green Algae]]. The water turned blackish in 1920. Changes in the [[Bottom Fauna]] were also large, and the author suggests they may have further restricted the diet of fish.
- **Winter oxygen stress:** When dissolved oxygen fell to about 3 ppm or less under ice, fish retreated to backwaters and spring holes. At [[Spring Bay]], about 200,000 pounds of carp were taken in December 1917 and January 1918. The early hauls were almost all knotheads.

## Key Findings

1. **Malformation and growth:** Knotheads grow far more slowly than normals. Calculated average growth is about 11 ounces per year for normals and about 6 ounces per year for knotheads. Knothead growth ranged from 3.8 ounces per year at Depue to 9.1 ounces per year at Pekin. Growth stunting was cumulative, with knotheads stopping growth just when normals grew fastest (between the third and sixth years).
2. **Pot-bellied knotheads:** Downstream, about 30 per cent of knotheads between Pekin and Copperas Creek Dam were pot-bellied. The author interprets this as partial recovery, or downstream movement, after exposure to the causative conditions.
3. **Bone chemistry:** Ash content was similar in both groups (for example, opercle ash 56.0% normal versus 54.7% knothead). Calcium content was similar or slightly higher in knotheads (opercle 21.2% versus 21.3%). Bone weight ratios were opercle 1:0.81, cleithrum 1:0.64, and pharyngeal 1:0.46. The author concludes that the X-ray density differences reflect bone thickness and mineralization distribution, not a simple calcium deficit.
4. **Respiration and viability:** In a September 1927 holding pond, knotheads began dying when dissolved oxygen fell to about 2.8 ppm, while normals began dying at about 2.45 ppm. Respiration in five knotheads averaged 61 to 87 movements per minute, while normals were estimated at about 50. Four of six knotheads died within three days, and none of three normals died.
5. **Behavior:** Knotheads are sluggish and less responsive than normals. They are caught in greater numbers by [[Hoop Net]]s during major environmental shifts, such as upstream winds. Extreme "grass carp" lie quiescent in shore vegetation and are often found dead.
6. **Flesh quality and market impact:** Knothead flesh is softer and tears easily during dressing. Knotheads cause a "gassy" taste that lowers value, so they are culled from shipments to New York. A spring-fed storage pond at Lower Peoria Lake, holding about 120,000 pounds by mid-September 1927, was used to purge the taste.
7. **Etiology:** The author rules out heredity and infection (after several months of histological work). He argues that the malformation most resembles [[Rickets]] in higher vertebrates, and that the probable cause is a loss of [[Vitamin D]] through the loss of chlorophyll-bearing food. He notes that feeding experiments are still needed to confirm this.
8. **Related experimental evidence:** Brown trout fry fed diets lacking vitamins A and D did not grow well ([[Coward and Drummond]]). Davis and James found high mortality in carp fed vitamin-deficient diets. A German "Knochenweiche" bone-softness disorder in cereal-fed carp has normal bone calcium content.
9. **Conclusion:** The author calls the knothead malformation the first known instance of rickets among fishes. He states that the depletion of green aquatic vegetation and the increase in pollution occurred over the same period, and that other fishes were unaffected, probably because they are less tolerant of pollution. He concludes that the retardation of knothead growth adds to pollution-related yield losses, which "probably ran into millions of pounds per year," but he sees no reason to think the fish is less valuable as human food.
