# Sustaining and Enhancing Riparian Migratory Bird Habitat on the Upper San Pedro River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1999 final draft, prepared for the [[Commission for Environmental Cooperation]] (CEC) Secretariat by an interdisciplinary San Pedro Expert Study Team, assesses how base water flows can be maintained to sustain the riparian corridor of the upper San Pedro River. The corridor is a critical migratory stopover and breeding habitat for songbirds moving between Mexico and Central America and the United States and Canada. The team treats the upper basin as a single hydrologic unit spanning the border.

The central finding is that groundwater pumping is drawing down the regional aquifer and capturing river baseflow. The net deficit in the US sub-basin is estimated at about 7,000 acre-feet per year, projected to reach roughly 13,000 acre-feet by 2020 under current trends. The team concludes that conservation, recharge and retirement of irrigation are necessary, but that these measures alone will not fully close the deficit. The report also stresses that the basin's main obstacle is less hydrologic disagreement than the allocation of costs and the absence of a coordinating institution.

## Background & Description

The San Pedro flows about 240 km (145 miles) from near Cananea, Sonora, north into Arizona, where it joins the Gila River at Winkelman. The river is uninterrupted by dams, with its only major diversion downstream of the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] (SPRNCA) at St. David. Congress established the SPRNCA in 1988, and the CEC recognized it as an "Important Bird Area" in 1996. The American Bird Conservancy, with Partners in Flight and the National Audubon Society, designated it a Globally Important Bird Area in 1995, the first such designation in the Western Hemisphere.

Key biological features include:
- Over 20 biotic communities in the upper basin, three of them considered endangered. These include [[Fremont Cottonwood]]/[[Goodding Willow]] forests, riverine ciénegas, and big sacaton grasslands (declined by over 90 percent).
- Nearly 390 bird species recorded in the upper valley (more than 350 within the SPRNCA), 250 of them neotropical migrants.
- An estimated one to four million migrating songbirds use the riparian zone annually.
- More than 15 percent of the world's known western [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]] population breeds along the river.
- Three federally endangered species: [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Huachuca Water-umbel]] and [[Jaguar]]. Critical habitat is designated for the flycatcher.
- In Mexico, about 260 vertebrate species, 64 of them threatened, endangered, rare or specially protected, and only 4 of 10 native fish species remaining.

The report explicitly does not seek a return to historic conditions. It advocates maintaining or modestly improving current SPRNCA riparian communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Baseflow is the main water source during the dry season, especially April through June. It derives from the [[Regional Aquifer]], which holds roughly 50 million acre-feet in storage in the Sierra Vista sub-basin. Storm flows recharge a shallow floodplain aquifer. Winter and spring floods establish new cottonwood and willow seedlings.

**Groundwater thresholds.** Goodding willow and Fremont cottonwood require the shallowest groundwater. Most high-density stands occur where depth to groundwater averages less than about three meters. Salt cedar tolerates drier conditions better and tends to displace cottonwood as conditions dry. Loss of perennial flow is identified as the key ecological threshold.

**Pumping impacts.** Models by the Arizona Department of Water Resources and others indicate that pumping has reduced groundwater discharge to the river at one point to perhaps 30 percent of its initial amount. Impacts probably began in the 1960s and 1970s. In some reaches the water-table gradient is now flat or reversed.

**Flow trends.** Using moving 10-year flow-duration curves at the Charleston and Palominas gauges, low flows have steadily declined. At Palominas the median flow fell from about seven cfs in 1940 to about two cfs by 1965. The team attributes this largely to irrigation on both sides of the border.

**Land cover.** Riparian vegetation in the Palominas-to-Charleston reach fell from 1,143 ha in 1970 to about 800 ha by 1986, and stayed near that level through 1997. Salt cedar, livestock removal from the SPRNCA in the late 1980s, and the planned beaver reintroduction are all reshaping vegetation.

**Water use and economy.** Fort Huachuca and the city of Sierra Vista are the largest consumers in the basin. Irrigated agriculture is a small part of the local economy, but its effect on the aquifer is disproportionate. Birdwatching in the upper basin is economically significant, with revenues approaching US$6 million per year according to the report.

**Governance.** Legal rules in Arizona separate surface water and groundwater. The federal reserved water right for the SPRNCA carries a 1988 priority date but faces delays in the Gila Basin General Adjudication. No coordinating body currently integrates the many federal, state, local, private and Mexican interests.

## Key Findings

**Water balance**
- The US sub-basin deficit is estimated at about 7,000 acre-feet per year (2030 projection: about 12,570 acre-feet without intervention, versus about 6,770 with the package of measures in Table 11).
- Current recharge credits from effluent and fort recycling are roughly 2,000 acre-feet per year from Sierra Vista and about 600 acre-feet from Fort Huachuca.
- Previous model estimates of baseflow reduction vary widely (from about 2,400 to 7,000 acre-feet per year). The team's own estimate is about 2 to 3 cfs.

**Priority measures (judged both effective and economically achievable)**
- Limit irrigation extractions on the US side, preferably through voluntary acquisition of development rights and exchanges with state lands rather than regulation or adjudication.
- Retire irrigated agriculture in the Mexican floodplain near the border. The report judges this the single largest hydrologic benefit: Palominas median flow could rise from about 2 cfs to about 7 cfs, yielding roughly 3,500 acre-feet per year, and it could support a binational riparian conservation zone.
- Pursue aggressive water conservation, recycling and recharge in domestic use, including xeriscaping, tiered pricing, and recharge of effluent.

**Measures with uncertain benefit:** mountain-front recharge along the Huachuca Mountains, beaver reintroduction, valley-floor vegetation management, local runoff retention, and emergency pumping to sustain streamflow. Emergency pumping could keep the river flowing at Charleston, but it does not solve the deficit and may mask it.

**Economically problematic options:** closing Fort Huachuca (estimated loss in excess of US$150 million per year to the local economy), importing water from the [[Douglas Basin]] (about US$400 per acre-foot, legally blocked without new legislation), and importing [[Central Arizona Project]] water (about US$870 per acre-foot). Redistributing pumping northward would protect the Charleston reach at the expense of the reach north of the Babocomari.

**Residual deficit:** Even with all measures, a deficit of over 3,000 acre-feet per year remains by 2030. The report concludes that the aquifer cannot be brought into long-term balance at current development levels without importing water, and that deficit spending on storage is "irresponsible" thinking.

**Legal and institutional gaps**
- Arizona's Groundwater Management Code does not directly protect aquatic environments. Active Management Area designation or an Irrigation Non-Expansion Area is possible but requires strong local support, which is absent.
- The Endangered Species Act could eventually force action, as in the Edwards Aquifer litigation in Texas, but causal links between individual pumpers and habitat loss are hard to prove.
- A local Water Issues Group proposal and the 1995 Cochise County plan both failed amid local opposition.

**Recommendations (Considerations for Action)**
1. Create a binational coordinating body, possibly formalized by joint presidential proclamation and evolving into a river basin commission, modeled on California's [[Coordinated Resource Management Planning]].
2. Pursue voluntary, compensated development buffers around the SPRNCA, with highest priority on the Hereford–Palominas area.
3. Establish a long-term monitoring program for birds and vegetation, with Mexican counterparts monitoring restored sites.
4. Restore grassland and riparian zones in Mexico, consider retraining for agricultural workers, and study the effects of mine tailings on water quality.

**Research gaps:** storm-flow modeling, recharge processes in mountain-front washes, valley-floor evapotranspiration, the clay layer near the Sierra Vista recharge site, beaver effects, and almost absent Mexican monitoring.
