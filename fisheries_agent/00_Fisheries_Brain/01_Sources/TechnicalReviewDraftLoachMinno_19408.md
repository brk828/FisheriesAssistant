# Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) Recovery Plan: Technical Review Draft

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is an unapproved technical review draft of the recovery plan for the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), prepared under contract for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region II. The loach minnow is a small, secretive, bottom-dwelling cyprinid endemic to the upper [[Gila River]] basin. It was listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), largely because of habitat destruction, impoundment, water diversion, groundwater pumping, sedimentation, and the spread of non-native predatory and competitive fishes. Its range and numbers have declined sharply, and it survives only in scattered tributary populations in Arizona and New Mexico. The plan's stated objective is to protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated populations and their habitats, and secure the species for self-sustaining existence, which would justify delisting. The draft stresses that habitat modification and interaction with exotic fishes both contribute to decline and must both be addressed.

## Background & Description

The loach minnow is a monotypic genus known to science for more than a century. It was not considered imperiled by Miller (1961), but rarity was noted by Barber and Minckley (1946) and Minckley (1973). The species was proposed for listing in 1985 and listed as threatened by FWS in 1984/1986 (dates as cited in the source). Critical habitat was proposed in 1985, but designation was deferred to 18 June 1987 and then lapsed, so no critical habitat is designated.

State status differs:
- **New Mexico:** Group 2 endangered; take prohibited except by scientific collectors.
- **Arizona:** Group 3 threatened; take prohibited except by angling or special permit.
- Neither state protects occupied habitat.

**Morphology:** A small, elongated, slightly compressed, ventrally flattened minnow resembling darters (*Etheostoma*). Dorsal fin has 8 rays, anal fin 7, and the lateral line about 65 scales. The mouth is small, terminal, and oblique, with no barbels. Pharyngeal teeth are in two rows (formula 1,4-4,1). The body is olivaceous and blotched, with whitish depigmented spots at fin bases. Breeding males show red-orange coloration; breeding females turn yellowish on fins and lower body.

**Life history:**
- *Habitat:* Reduced gas bladder restricts it to a bottom-dwelling habit; it occupies shallow, moderate-to-swift riffles with pebble-to-cobble substrate.
- *Reproduction:* First spawning at age I, from late winter to early spring in Aravaipa Creek and late March to early June in New Mexico. Adhesive eggs are laid on the undersides of flattened rocks, with 52 to 63 mean eggs per rock (range fewer than 5 to more than 250). Fecundity is about 150 to 250 mature ova, increasing with female size. Hatching occurs in 3 to 6 days.
- *Growth:* Larvae are about 3 mm at hatching. Age 0 fish reach 30 to more than 40 mm SL by mid-summer and just over 50 mm SL by year's end. Age I fish average about 55 mm SL; maximum about 68 mm SL at age II. Most live 15 to 24 months, with rare individuals reaching 36 months.
- *Diet:* Opportunistic benthic insectivore feeding mainly on riffle-dwelling mayfly (ephemeropteran) and black fly (simuliid) larvae, with seasonal roles for other aquatic insects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution:** Historically recorded in the Salt, White, Verde, Gila, San Pedro, Aravaipa, San Francisco, Blue, and Eagle Creek systems in Arizona, and in the Gila, San Francisco, and Tularosa systems in New Mexico. Today it persists in the White River, North Fork White River, Aravaipa Creek, San Francisco and Blue rivers, and Campbell Blue Creek in Arizona. In New Mexico it persists in the upper Gila (East, Middle, and West forks), San Francisco, Tularosa, and lower Whitewater and Dry Blue creeks. Populations transplanted to Sonoita Creek (1968) and Zinc Springs Wash (1976) were extirpated.

**Co-occurring fishes:**
- Native species: speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), desert sucker (*Catostomus clarki*), longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*), and native suckers.
- Non-native species: channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), flathead catfish (*Pylodictis olivaris*), red shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*), and mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*).

Red shiner is strongly implicated in declines; loach minnow decline zones coincide with shiner range expansion. Flathead catfish, which are piscivorous even when small, pose a predation threat in riffles.

**Reasons for decline:**
- Dewatering from groundwater pumping, channelization, diversion, and damming.
- Impoundment, which creates lentic habitat and alters flow, temperature, and organic drift.
- Grazing, mining, timber management, agriculture, and development, which increase turbidity, sedimentation, and contaminants.
- Non-native fish invasion, which often follows habitat alteration.

The authors state that habitat alteration and non-native interactions usually occurred together, so their individual effects cannot be separated. Aravaipa Creek, which lacks established exotic fishes, supports a native community of seven kinds including loach minnow.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective:** Protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated populations, and ensure self-sustaining populations, with quantitative delisting criteria to be developed as data accumulate.

**Recovery strategy (stepdown outline, summarized):**
1. **Protect existing populations:** designate critical habitat; identify, acquire, and protect private lands; discourage detrimental uses; ensure perennial flows; enforce laws and [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation.
2. **Monitor populations and community:** set standard monitoring sites and techniques; determine natural variation in abundance and age structure; conduct bi-annual (spring and autumn) population estimates.
3. **Clarify exotic-fish interactions:** investigate direct (predation, displacement) and indirect effects through field, experimental, and lab work.
4. **Quantify habitat effects:** study substrate siltation and armoring, velocity and depth, water temperature, and their interactions.
5. **Augment depleted populations:** restore habitat, identify donor stocks (wild adults), and monitor success.
6. **Reintroduce to historic range:** priority sites are Eagle Creek and the San Pedro River, with barrier dams, exotic removal (possibly piscicide), and donor stock from Aravaipa Creek or upper Gila.
7. **Define self-sustaining criteria:** absolute numbers, age structure, reproduction, recruitment, minimum stock size, and physical, chemical, and biological environmental variables.
8. **Contingency captive propagation:** not deemed necessary now, but hatchery planning is advised in case of rapid decline.
9. **Information and education:** public outreach, open exchange among agencies, peer-reviewed publication.

**Notable data points:**
- Eagle Creek: 13 individuals collected in 1950 (A.R. Miller); species apparently no longer present.
- San Pedro River: type locality of the species (Girard 1857), with 10 other native fishes extirpated over the last century.
- Aravaipa Creek: largest remaining Arizona population.
- 1982–1985 surveys: loach minnow locally abundant in scattered New Mexico reaches but small in Whitewater and Dry Blue creeks.

**Critical habitat (proposed 1985, not designated):** Arizona reaches total about 24 km (Aravaipa Creek), 78 km (Blue River), 14 km (Campbell Blue Creek), and 6 km (San Francisco River). New Mexico reaches include about 3 km (Dry Blue Creek), 15 km and 24 km (San Francisco and Tularosa rivers), 24 km, 17 km, and 18 km (East, West, and Middle Fork Gila rivers), and 37 km (Gila River). The appendix contains further reach listings.
