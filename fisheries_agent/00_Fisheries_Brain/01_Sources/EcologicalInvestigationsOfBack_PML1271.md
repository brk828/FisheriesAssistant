# Ecological Investigations of Backwaters Along the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Clupeidae #Ictaluridae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 doctoral dissertation by David Millard Kennedy, submitted to the University of Arizona, evaluates four man-made [[Backwater]] habitats on the east side of the [[Lower Colorado River]] to provide baseline data, describe riverine-backwater ecological relationships, and improve fishery potential. The backwaters were constructed in the 1960s as mitigation for habitat lost to river channelization and water salvage. The study compares physical, chemical and biological parameters across the backwaters and the river, and concludes with management recommendations and a proposed interagency master planning process.

## Background & Description

Prior to dam construction, the Lower Colorado River meandered widely, with seasonal floods and low flows. Completion of [[Parker Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]] in 1918 began intensive control; the river is now described as one of the most altered, man-controlled riverine systems in the world. Water salvage, channelization, backwater filling and phreatophyte control drastically changed the system, causing loss of most endemic fishes and their replacement by exotic species. Subsequent federal legislation (including the Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act and the National Environmental Policy Act) increased demands for fish, wildlife and recreation consideration.

The four study backwaters were:
- **A-7**: dredged in 1969; surface area and depth varied, fed by two culverts (91 cm diameter) and one 10 m channel; highly flushed.
- **A-7B**: undredged oxbow remnant separated from A-7 by a levee; shallow with a deeper eastern channel; one channel connection to the river.
- **A-10**: dredged in early 1970; fed by culverts only, with beaver debris intermittently plugging them; longer water residence time.
- **No Name Lake**: undredged, cut off from the river in 1954 by a levee with no surface opening; seepage only.

Surface areas ranged from 37.4 ha to 5.1 ha, with maximum depths of 2.5 to 4.0 m. Deer Island Lake, a fifth comparison system with a single small opening, was drawn from earlier work by Saiki.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Backwaters are essentially fed by river water, so their conditions largely reflect river conditions. Daily water-level fluctuations in the river, driven by irrigation-linked releases from Parker and Palo Verde dams, produced changes of up to about 60 cm daily in some backwaters, while No Name Lake varied only about 5 cm. Annual fall drawdowns exposed large proportions of backwater bottoms and removed much submergent vegetation. Climate is hyperarid: average rainfall about 10 cm per year and evaporation about 218 cm per year, which concentrates dissolved salts. Dominant vegetation included [[Typha]] (cattails) along shorelines, and submergents such as [[Myriophyllum exalbescens]] (water milfoil), [[Najas marina]] and [[Potamogeton pectinatus]].

Water quality data included temperature (0.0 to 31.0 °C range across sites), dissolved oxygen (mean monthly 3.9 to 12.8 mg/L), pH (7.2 to 8.4 mean), total alkalinity (about 100 to 216 mg/L), conductivity (about 940 to 1600 µmhos/cm at 25 °C in backwaters), and total dissolved solids (546 to 1941 mg/L in backwaters; 503 to 1223 mg/L in the river).

## Key Findings

**Water quality**
- Water quality was suboptimal for good fish production only in poorly flushed backwaters. Low winter temperatures, particularly as they affect threadfin shad survival, were the most significant water quality problem.
- Dissolved oxygen was adequate for aquatic fauna in nearly all areas; depressions occurred near bottom sediments with high organic detritus, mostly May through October.
- Total alkalinity and TDS were lower in highly flushed backwaters due to dilution. TDS in backwaters and river (546–1941 mg/L) exceeded the range McKee and Wolf considered suitable for good mixed fish faunas (about 400 mg/L), and the river's dissolved sulfate (about 500 mg/L) was at least 16 times the North American average.
- Blue-green algae dominated epibenthic growth; the author notes these are a dead end in the food chain for most organisms.

**Water level and flushing**
- Water-level fluctuation caused by river releases was probably the most important factor affecting backwater biota.
- Flushing rate was related to the width of openings and distance from the river. Estimated flushing times were about 5.7 days (A-7), 3.7 days (A-7B) and 18.5 days (A-10), versus an estimated average of 244.4 days for Deer Island Lake.
- Fall drawdowns exposed about 80% of the No Name Lake bottom during one low-river period, and killed roughly 80–90% of submergent vegetation in A-7 and A-7B.

**Plankton and invertebrates**
- Net plankton standing crops were inversely related to flushing rate; Deer Island Lake had at least ten times the standing crop of the other backwaters.
- Benthic invertebrate communities were similar across backwaters, dominated by [[Chironomidae]] (midges), and Oligochaeta; Deer Island Lake had the most diverse fauna (19 taxa).
- Macrophytic invertebrate biomass paralleled submergent vegetation abundance.

**Fishes**
- A total of 20 species were collected; all were exotic except the [[Humpback Sucker]], of which only two individuals were captured. The most abundant species were threadfin shad, carp, channel catfish, bluegill, largemouth bass and redear sunfish.
- Threadfin shad survival was limited by winter temperatures and drawdowns; shad did not persist in closed No Name Lake even with repeated stocking.
- Channel catfish did poorly in No Name Lake (mean condition coefficient 0.6?? versus 0.756 in A-7), with few fish over 40 cm and no evidence of natural reproduction in backwaters. Catfish appeared to migrate out of open backwaters after fall drawdowns.
- Largemouth bass in closed backwaters showed poor juvenile survival; starvation followed shad loss in Deer Island Lake.

**Recommendations**
1. Keep all backwaters open to the Colorado River.
2. Reduce diurnal and annual water-level fluctuations.
3. Regulate flushing with culverts fitted with sliding gates.
4. Investigate alternative forage such as crayfish (*Orconectes causeyi* and *Procambarus clarkii*).
5. Develop a predictive regression model linking opening size and location to water chemistry and biota.
6. Create an interagency Master Plan using the Great River Environmental Action Team (GREAT) model, with public participation, to resolve conflicts among water salvage, fish and wildlife, and recreation.
