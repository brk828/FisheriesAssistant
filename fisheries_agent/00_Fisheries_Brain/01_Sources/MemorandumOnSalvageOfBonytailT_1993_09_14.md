## Executive Summary
This document is an internal Smithsonian Institution memorandum (dated 14 September 1993) from W. Starnes, Manager of the [[Gila Taxonomy Project]], addressed to collaborators at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, USFWS Region 2, Arizona State University (ASU), and GAI. It does not present primary biological data but instead outlines a **tissue salvage protocol** for frozen [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) specimens collected from [[Lake Mohave]]. The memo coordinates logistics for extracting frozen tissue, otoliths, and other biological material to support downstream **allozyme studies**, **DNA/enzyme preservation**, and **morphological analysis**. As such, the "data" herein is procedural/methodological rather than morphometric or ecological in the traditional sense.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological measurements or descriptions of the [[Bonytail]] are provided in this memo. However, the document references planned downstream morphological work:
- **Morphology assessment**: To be conducted by M. Douglas / P. Marsh / T. Dowling (ASU), described as "Mike's morphology" work, to be performed in concert with otolith extraction on ASU-held specimens.
- **Otolith extraction**: Planned as a later-stage process, to be coordinated by W. Starnes, for age/growth or taxonomic verification purposes (methodology and personnel for this component were still being identified at time of writing).
- Specimens described as being in a **frozen, "rock-hard"** state, necessitating specialized dissection technique (see Key Findings/Methods below).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Source population**: Bonytail specimens salvaged from **[[Lake Mohave]]**, a reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]], historically one of the last strongholds of wild-origin bonytail.
- **Conservation context**: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) is a critically endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], and this salvage effort reflects broader recovery and genetic conservation work under an interagency recovery program (referenced as "RIP" — Recovery Implementation Program).
- **Specimen custody split**: Individuals/tissues were distributed between two institutional repositories — Dexter National Fish Hatchery (frozen specimens, tissue removal stage) and ASU (specimens undergoing morphological/otolith study) — reflecting a distributed, multi-institution conservation genetics framework typical of endangered Southwestern cyprinid management.

## Key Findings
- **Protocol Initiation**: Following a FAX request (9/9/1993) from G. Carmichael and J. Hutchison, W. Starnes was tasked with coordinating salvage of tissues, otoliths, and related materials from frozen Lake Mohave bonytail specimens.
- **Staged Workflow**:
  1. **Tissue removal** from frozen Dexter specimens (first priority, to be done immediately).
  2. **Storage** of dissected tissue in Nunc tubes, returned to ultracold storage (-80°C or similar) at Dexter pending shipment.
  3. **Shipping coordination** to GAI (for [[Allozyme Electrophoresis|allozyme studies]]) and ASU, once removal is complete.
  4. **Later-stage work**: Morphological analysis and otolith extraction (ASU specimens), handled separately and on a later timeline.
- **Preservation Priority**: Emphasis placed on keeping tissue **maximally frozen during dissection** to preserve DNA and enzyme integrity for genetic analyses — this is the central technical constraint discussed.
- **Proposed Dissection Method**: Use of a **flame-sterilized serrated knife** (rather than a scalpel) suggested as more effective for sawing through frozen tissue, reflecting an ad hoc solution to a stated practical difficulty ("rock-hard frozen specimens").
- **Labeling / Chain of Custody**: Strong emphasis on cross-referencing tissue vials back to original carcasses via careful labeling, to maintain traceability across the three participating institutions (Dexter, GAI, ASU).
- **Downstream Use**: Salvaged tissues were destined for incorporation into ongoing **[[Allozyme Electrophoresis|allozyme studies]]**, indicating this effort supported a broader population-genetics/systematics research program on Gila species (the "[[Gila Taxonomy Project]]").
- **Institutional Coordination Gaps**: The author (Starnes) notes unfamiliarity with some personnel involved in the otolith work, indicating this was an evolving, multi-institution collaborative effort still being organized at time of writing.
