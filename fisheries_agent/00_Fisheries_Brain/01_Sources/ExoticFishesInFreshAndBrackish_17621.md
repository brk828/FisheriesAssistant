# Exotic Fishes in Fresh and Brackish Waters of Florida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #poeciliidae #exotic-species

## Executive Summary

This 1974 study, published in *Biological Conservation*, is the first comprehensive survey of non-indigenous fishes in Florida's fresh and brackish waters. The authors recorded at least 38 exotic species and several hybrids, of which 20 species and five hybrids were established as reproducing populations. The [[Ornamental Aquarium Fish Trade]] was the main source of introductions, and the authors argue that Florida's mild climate, well-water supply and interconnected canal network enable establishment. They oppose an import ban, recommend effluent containment and labelling requirements, and call for dispersal risks to be considered in [[Channelization Projects]] and environmental impact statements.

## Background & Description

Biological pollutants, meaning exotic species, are treated as comparable in impact to chemical and physical pollutants. Unlike chemical or physical additives, an established biological pollutant generally cannot be removed. Permissive or weakened habitats are most susceptible to establishment.

Collections (62 in central and southern Florida, July 1970 to July 1972) used minnow seines up to 24 ft (7.2 m), electrofishing, dip nets, block nets and 5 per cent emulsified rotenone. Species were grouped by family, and established species are marked (E) in the species checklist (Table I):

- **Anabantidae:** 8 species; 2 appear established.
- **Characidae:** 3 species; none established.
- **Cichlidae:** 11+ species; 8 established.
- **Clariidae:** 1 species (established).
- **Cyprinidae:** 6 species; carp established.
- **Loricariidae:** 1 species listed as *Hypostomus* sp. (established).
- **Poeciliidae:** 14 taxa including hybrids; 12 established.

Most fish stocks entered Florida through aquarium fish farms. Florida hosts more than 250 fish farms, which supply nearly 80 per cent of US demand, and roughly 110 million live fish were imported into the US in 1972, with nearly 50 per cent entering through Miami and Tampa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Five release pathways from fish farms were identified: unscreened effluent pipes, draining of contaminated ponds, flooding of low-lying farms, surplus stock from successful breeding, and illegal dumping of stocks. Home aquarists were judged a minor source. Several species were released deliberately, including blue tilapia (for plant control, and later by fishermen) and pike killifish (50 specimens, 1957, after a research project ended).

Key ecological impacts documented include:

- **[[Black Acara]]:** the most widely distributed exotic in southeast Florida. It was present in 24 of 44 collections there, and comprised up to 64 per cent of fish biomass in some canals and 80 per cent in one canal near its suspected introduction site. It is established in [[Everglades National Park]].
- **[[Blue Tilapia]]:** comprised 20 per cent of fish biomass in Lake Parker (1966), rising to 69.1 per cent in 1972; in Lake Effie it rose from 0 per cent (1966) to 93.26 per cent (1972). Authors note it competes directly with native species.
- **[[Blackchin Tilapia]]:** comprised about 90 per cent of fish biomass at Lithia Springs, where vegetation was greatly reduced and native bass and bluegill appeared diseased.
- **[[Congo Tilapia]]:** in a Lake Wales pond, nearly all vegetation was eliminated; stocks were destroyed in 1970.
- **[[Walking Catfish]]:** spread rapidly. It is established over about 36 miles (57.6 km) from West Palm Beach Canal to New River Canal, tolerates salinities of 18 ppt and anoxic water, and eats insects, fish and plants.
- **[[Pike Killifish]]:** the only established poeciliid considered to have significant ecological effects. It occupies about 160 square miles (400 km²) in southern Dade County, and comprised 20 per cent of fish biomass in some canals there.
- **[[Grass Carp]]:** not known to be established. Authors warn that if it reproduced, it could denude vegetation and harm waterfowl habitat.

Environmental conditions: the mild southern Florida climate and constant-temperature well water favour the industry. Mozambique tilapia tolerates salinities up to 40 ppt and can spread along the Intracoastal Waterway. Walking catfish can survive cold, dry periods by burrowing.

## Key Findings

- At least 38 exotic species and several hybrids were found, with 20 species and five hybrids established.
- The ornamental aquarium trade is the major source; the authors estimate that nearly every species held in Florida farms can reach open water.
- Established species with the most ecological impact include black acara, blue tilapia, blackchin tilapia and walking catfish.
- Florida Statutes 372.26 and 372.265 and Rule 9.07 already regulate imports, permits and farm overflow protection, but are largely unenforceable due to limited staff.
- Recommended measures: warning labels on fish bags, retailer signage, and diversion of farm effluent through sand and gravel filters or dry wells. Dikes should be built around floodable outdoor ponds.
- The authors oppose a total import ban as ineffective, and argue that existing established exotics cannot be eradicated.
- Canal channelization, such as the [[Tampa Bypass Canal]] project linking Six-Mile Creek to the Hillsborough River, should consider dispersal of exotics in environmental impact statements.
- Armoured catfishes (*Hypostomus* spp.) were judged innocuous, with minimal niche overlap with native fishes, but their range may expand into the Hillsborough River.
