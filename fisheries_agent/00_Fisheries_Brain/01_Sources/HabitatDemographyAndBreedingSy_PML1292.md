# Habitat, Demography and Breeding Systems of Five *Cyprinodon* Pupfish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This scanned paper (OCR text, heavily garbled in places) compares five populations of small desert pupfish in the genus [[Cyprinodon]]. The populations occur in isolated spring and lake habitats in [[New Mexico]], [[Nevada]] and [[California]]. The authors describe each population's habitat, population size and density, breeding system and seasonal breeding period. They also report mating success in representative populations and the effects of experimental habitat manipulation on breeding systems. The main finding is that the breeding system varies among populations and tracks population density and habitat size. Within these populations, [[Territoriality]], [[Consort Pair]] mating, [[Dominance Hierarchy]] and [[Group Spawning]] all occur.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** The populations are assigned to *Cyprinodon pecosensis* (Mirror Lake, New Mexico), *C. nevadensis* (Big Spring and Mexican Spring, Ash Meadows, Nevada; Amargosa River, Tecopa, California; with subspecies names partly garbled, including *mionectes*) and *C. diabolis* (Devils Hole, Ash Meadows, Nevada). Exact subspecies assignments could not be confirmed from the OCR.
- **Study system:** The fishes live in isolated desert aquatic habitats, many of them small and spring-fed. This isolation makes the populations useful for comparing breeding systems under different conditions.
- **Methods:** The authors used field observations of mating behaviour, population censuses and density estimates, and experimental manipulations. In the manipulations, adults were introduced into artificial pools and habitat size was reduced. Sources cited include [[Kodric-Brown]] (1975, 1977 and unpublished data), [[Soltz]] (1974), [[James]] (1969) and [[Minckley]] and [[Deacon]] (1973, 1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Table 1: Habitat, demography and breeding characteristics (summary of OCR text)**

**Table 2: Mating success in representative populations**

- The table lists the number of females mated per male per hour for each breeding system. Values appear to range from about 0.6 to 3.7 (OCR-uncertain).
- Observation effort varied, with totals of about 4 to 15 hours per system. Territoriality, consort pair and group spawning are the main systems compared.
- The highest values appear in systems with territoriality or dominance hierarchies, though the OCR makes exact pairings uncertain.

**Table 3: Effects of environmental manipulation on breeding system**

- **Big Spring, Ash Meadows (*C. nevadensis*):** A decrease in population density from about 150 to about 10 individuals per m² was associated with a change from consort pair to territoriality. Adults were introduced into Jackrabbit Spring (30 m²) in January 1970.
- **Big Spring, Ash Meadows:** A reduction in habitat size and an introduction of 30 adults into a wading pool (12.5 m²) were associated with a change from territoriality to dominance hierarchy (March 1970).
- **Amargosa River, Tecopa (*C. nevadensis*):** A change from group spawning and some territoriality to dominance hierarchy was recorded (July 1970), after habitat reduction and the introduction of 30 adults into a wading pool (12.5 m²).
- The authors conclude that density and habitat area directly influence which breeding system the fish use.

## Key Findings

1. **Breeding systems differ among populations.** Territoriality, consort pair, dominance hierarchy and group spawning occur across the five populations. Several populations use a mixed strategy.
2. **Density and habitat size shape the breeding system.** Experimental reductions in density or habitat area shifted populations between systems within months, as shown in Table 3.
3. **Mating success varies by system.** Table 2 suggests higher mating rates under some systems than others, though the exact values are uncertain in the OCR.
4. **Population sizes and densities differ widely.** Reported populations range from a few thousand to more than 50,000 individuals, and densities span about 4 to over 30,000 per m² across sites.
5. **Isolated desert habitats are fragile.** Small, spring-fed sites with limited area are vulnerable to density changes, which underlines the conservation significance of these populations, including [[Devils Hole]] and [[Ash Meadows]].
