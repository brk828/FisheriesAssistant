# The Relation of Hydrological Conditions to Speciation in Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #speciation

## Executive Summary

This symposium paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], curator of fishes at the Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan, argues that the hydrological environment, including its temperature, oxygen, light, pressure, flow, salinity and chemistry, is a primary driver of [[Speciation]] in fishes. Hubbs contends that water is far from a stable environment and that fishes, bound to it in body fluids, respiration and reproduction, are more closely tied to their surroundings than terrestrial vertebrates. He organizes the evidence into two patterns: [[Parallel Adaptation]], in which unrelated fish groups evolve similar forms under similar conditions, and [[Diverse Adaptation]], in which fishes diverge into specialized forms for different niches. He concludes that across both patterns, habitat and character evolution are correlated, so any complete theory of speciation must account for environmental control.

## Background & Description

Hubbs opens by framing fishes as organisms "perpetually bathed" in their medium. Key points from his introduction include:

- **Physical coupling:** Fish body density is close to that of water, and the blood is separated from the surrounding fluid only by a thin epithelium. The chemical link between marine fish body fluids and seawater is so close that moving to freshwater or land has been a rare evolutionary event.
- **Thermal regime:** Fishes are poikilothermic, whereas terrestrial vertebrates are homoiothermic.
- **Reproduction:** Oviparity is the rule among aquatic vertebrates, whereas most land vertebrates practice some form of parental care or viviparity.
- **Hydrological instability:** Heavy rain turns pool-and-riffle creeks into torrents, rolls cover and spawning beds, buries eggs in silt, floods habitats and can convert estuaries and reefs from brackish or saltwater to freshwater.
- **Oxygen limitation:** Dissolved oxygen is very low in water, so small environmental changes often cause oxygen depletion.
- **Physical gradients:** Light is absorbed in differential patterns (work of Edward A. Birge and colleagues), pressure rises steeply with depth, and sharp temperature and chemical gradients can occur within a meter or two in lakes.

Hubbs also notes that the sensory contrasts between lotic (flowing) and lentic (still) water have no close parallel in air.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hubbs describes adaptation as a response to specific microhabitats and water types. The main ecological contexts he covers are:

- **Pelagic and surface waters:** Streamlined bodies, blue-green dorsal and silver ventral coloration, and prolonged transparent larval stages. Examples include the Pacific Coast rockfishes and sculpins, mullets, eels (leptocephalus larvae), smelt and skipjack.
- **Vegetated waters:** Compressed bodies and a lengthwise black streak on greenish fishes, including several *Notropis* species, young golden shiners and young largemouth bass.
- **Sandy and soft bottoms:** Flattened ventral profiles and inferior mouths in species such as *Notropis deliciosus*, *Hybognathus* (*Hyborhynchus*) *notatus* and *Boleosoma nigrum*; sand-burrowing darters (*Ammocrypta*, *Crystallaria*) resemble marine sand-dwelling fishes.
- **Streams and rivers:** Fishes in fast, large rivers are more streamlined and have more fin rays and smaller scales than creek forms. Some western North American forms show clines linked to stream type.
- **Isolated spring holes:** Spring-dwelling forms (*Rhinichthys*, *Siphateles*, *Hesperoleucus*) have reduced sense organs, lost barbels, reduced lateral lines and altered scales.
- **Intertidal reefs (Pacific Coast):** A narrow ecotone exposed to surf, tidal emersion, heat, freshwater rain and predators, supporting varied specialists (see [[Intertidal Reef Fishes]]).
- **Rapids and torrents:** Streamlined swimmers and flattened, bottom-hugging forms with widespread pectoral fins and reduced air bladders; cave-dwelling and sheltering forms such as catfishes, darters, sculpins and burbot.
- **Low-oxygen swamps and ditches:** Tropical swamps, pools and ditches, where many fishes evolved air-breathing structures (see [[Dissolved Oxygen]]).

## Key Findings

**1. Parallel adaptation is common and frequently correlated with environment.**
Hubbs states that such modifications are probably more frequent in fishes than in terrestrial vertebrates. He notes that Dice found little evidence of parallel adaptation among mammals beyond soil-matching coloration.

**2. Trophic specialization shows parallel evolution.**
- Nutrient-rich water is associated with gill rakers adapted to filter plankton, as in *Xystrosus* (Mexico), *Siphateles* (Nevada) and a Lake Biwa species (Japan).
- Racial differences in East Indian cyprinids follow habitat productivity: fish from sterile mountain waters are slender and small-headed, while lowland river fish are deep-bodied with large heads, eyes and fins.
- Herbivory has evolved repeatedly within several families, with changes in jaw, lip and tooth structure, an elongated intestine and a black peritoneum.
- Parasitic lampreys lost their adult feeding stage, with intermediate stages forming clines. Non-parasitic brook lampreys arose repeatedly in streams where food is scarce or seasonal.

**3. Reduced light drives sensory evolution.**
Fishes in silty or deep waters have smaller eyes and enlarged non-visual senses, such as the lateral line. Hubbs cites George A. Moore's study of this relationship. Deep-water and cave fishes show degeneration of eyes and pigment. Hubbs suggests this may result from repeated mutations with no selection acting against them, and he computed possible speciation rates on that basis.

**4. Temperature and salinity correlate with meristic and body characters.**
Alfred Perlmutter's doctoral research on Atlantic Coast fishes showed gradients in vertebrae, scales, fin rays, body proportions and dermal growths linked to average physical and chemical habitat features. Hubbs proposes these changes are secondary responses to physiological adaptation.

**5. Diverse adaptation to varied microhabitats.**
Intertidal reef fishes meet extreme conditions through specialization: the blind goby (*Typhlogobius*) lives commensally with a crustacean in burrows; *Caularchus* clings with a sucking disk; several finless cottids and stichaeids hide in crevices; *Xiphister* lives among mussel beds; *Scytalina* burrows in gravel. Hubbs stresses that the apparent overlap of these habitats masks distinct niches with different selective forces.

**6. Oxygen-depleted waters drove respiratory innovation.**
Hubbs describes a range of adaptations:
- A true lung (lungfish) or a rebuilt air bladder (osseoglossids, *Lepisosteus*, tarpon, *Megalops*).
- Independently evolved lung-like structures in *Heteropneustes*, Labyrinthici and *Amphipnous*.
- Gut respiration in loaches (Cobitidae).
- A sieve-like gill in *Amia calva*. Sailfish and swordfish (Istiophoridae and Xiphiidae) evolved similar gills independently, because their large size and high activity demand more oxygen, not because surface waters are oxygen-poor.
- Accessory external gills in young tropical fishes, sharks, surfperches (Embiotocidae) and goodeids.

**7. Egg-oxygenation strategies.**
Hubbs lists five strategies: hanging eggs from aquatic plants; floating bubble or stick nests; laying eggs on leaves just above water; fanning or cleaning eggs; and viviparity. Viviparity is common in tropical cyprinodonts (Goodeidae, Poeciliidae, Fitzroyidae, Anablepidae) and in Asian Hemirhamphidae, because embryos can absorb oxygen from the surface film.

**8. Environmental control is central to speciation.**
Hubbs concludes that correlated gradients between characters and habitat recur in the same direction. Whether this reflects environmental determinism or selection of habitat by newly speciated forms is, in his view, less important than recognizing a definite interplay between organism and environment. He notes that speciation in cave systems continues under uniform conditions, so pre-adaptation followed by habitat selection is not the only pathway.

**9. Cited examples of climatic stress.**
Hubbs cites a cold snap in Florida as a case where a slight temperature drop caused vital effects on fish life, referencing Mullan's report of cold-related fish mortality on the Florida Gulf coast.
