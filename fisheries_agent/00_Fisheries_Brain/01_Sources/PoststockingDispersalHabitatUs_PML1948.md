# Post-Stocking Dispersal, Habitat Use, and Behavioral Acclimation of Juvenile Razorback Suckers in Two Colorado River Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This USGS Open-File Report (98-301), prepared with [[Arizona State University]], the [[National Park Service]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], tracks stocked juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered [[Catostomidae]] fish, in [[Lake Powell]] (San Juan Arm) and [[Lake Mohave]] during May–August 1997. Juveniles were tagged with externally attached [[Sonic Telemetry]] transmitters on the dorsal keel, a method the authors present as a short-term alternative to surgical implants. The study tested whether holding fish in blocked coves for three days (acclimation) influenced short-term dispersal. Post-stocking dispersal was rapid and widespread, habitat use concentrated in shallow, vegetated, flooded backwaters, and the authors recommend further testing and predator-focused research.

## Background & Description

- **Species of focus:** Razorback Sucker, a large-bodied, endangered Colorado River fish with a dorsal keel that is pronounced in adults and poorly developed in young.
- **Life-stage gap:** Wild populations are dominated by old individuals, and young fish are rarely encountered. Earlier stocking of larval and fingerling suckers in large numbers largely failed to establish populations.
- **Knowledge gap:** Juvenile habitat use and movements were poorly documented because of sampling difficulties in remote riverine habitats, fish disappearance from dispersal, and unknown survivorship. Surgically implanted radio or sonic tags are generally infeasible at small body sizes.
- **Motivation:** Behavioral stress and acclimation after repatriation were largely unstudied. The authors argued that acclimation could improve the ability to keep fish in target reaches and improve survival by reducing predation exposure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Powell (San Juan Arm):** Fish were stocked from Ouray National Fish Hatchery, with about 300 fish transported and 30 captured from golf course ponds for tagging (mean length 358 mm). Release occurred at Castle Creek Canyon, and surveys covered roughly RK 51–85 initially, extending to Slickrock Rapids (RK 106). Rising spring waters inundated hundreds of hectares of flooded tamarisk.
- **Lake Mohave:** Fish were supplied by Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery and were smaller (mean about 241 mm). Releases occurred at Owl Cove (RK 45), Tequila Cove (RK 32), and Mesa Cove (RK 29). The lake was surveyed on a GPS grid with 71 listening stations initially, expanded to more than 125 stops.
- **Cover and habitat:** In Lake Mohave, fish used flooded tamarisk (about 48–86%), submergent vegetation (about 40%), and rock ledges or rubble (12–14%). Cover was used for concealment, and cover types were similar in Lake Powell except for emergent vegetation.
- **Water temperature:** Backwater temperatures ran 1–3 °C warmer than the main lake, which likely supports growth.
- **Reservoir fluctuation:** Annual elevation changes of up to about 20 m in large reservoirs such as Powell and Mead can shift flowing and non-flowing habitats substantially.
- **Predators:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Striped Bass]] (inferred from a possible predation event) are present in shoreline and cover habitats used by juveniles.

## Key Findings

**Methods and tagging**
- Externally attached ultrasonic transmitters were attached to the dorsal keel with a single suture and no backing plate, which the authors designed to allow shedding and faster healing. Processing took under one minute per fish.
- Of 30 Lake Powell transmitters, 28 functioned properly, one was weak, and one gave no signal. Groups of five fish were held three days behind blocking nets (15 fish) or released immediately (13 fish).
- Lake Mohave releases comprised 27 tagged fish, with 17 detected initially.

**Dispersal and range**
- Lake Powell: 23 of 28 fish were contacted. 21 of 23 (92%) moved uplake, and only two moved downstream, the farthest 2.7 km. Maximum detected distance from release was 4.6 km upstream and 2.7 km downstream. Mean maximum distance travelled was reported as more than 2,080 m/day (range 400–5,000 m/day), with one fish (#438) covering about 5 km/day.
- Lake Mohave: 17 of 27 fish were detected. Most moved downreservoir (about 84% reported as moving downlake in the discussion); the authors note only two moved upstream. One fish (#238) travelled about 63 km through pelagic zones over four days, possibly eaten by a predator.
- Roughly 50% of stocked suckers emigrated out of the 46 km Lake Powell study area during the first month.

**Habitat use**
- Fish were typically within 10 m of shore (about 62% of Lake Mohave sightings versus about 15% at distances over 50 m in Lake Powell, per the reported figure) and in depths under 2 m.
- Juveniles strongly preferred flooded backwater complexes at Castle Creek Canyon and Mike's Canyon, and were seldom found in narrow, deep canyon habitat.
- Dispersal appeared strongly influenced by habitat and cover availability.

**Behavioral acclimation**
- Of the 14 Lake Powell fish that remained in the study area for more than one month, cove-held fish made up 64% of detections, about twice the share of fish simply released. The authors caution the sample size was small.
- Lake Mohave data were judged inadequate to draw conclusions on acclimation.

**Transmitter retention and survival**
- Field fish retained transmitters for a mean of 3.8 weeks (range 2 to more than 8 weeks) in tank tests of five fish; suture wounds healed within about 2–4 weeks.
- In a second tank test (ten larger fish, one control), five fish including the control died of unknown causes, and the first transmitter was lost in week 5.
- Initial tag failure rate was 2.5% (2 of 80). Losses of about 80% of transmitters in Lake Mohave were attributed partly to dense vegetation blocking signals, and to possible shedding, predation, or flooded habitat.

**Management recommendations**
- Test acclimation (three-day holding) further in both reservoir and riverine habitats.
- Consider external keel attachment for short-term behavioral studies of razorback sucker and similar Colorado River fishes.
- Focus research on reducing predation on early life stages, and evaluate non-native predator control.
- Stock larger fish (>30 cm, with a 40 cm size recommended for the San Juan River) and delay stocking until winter, when predators are dormant.
- Manipulate reservoir elevations as a possible means of supporting young natives.
