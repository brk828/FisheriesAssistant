# Proposed Listing Rules for Two Southwestern and Californian Plants: Rumex orthoneurus (Threatened) and Phlox hirsuta (Endangered)

#botany #plant-conservation #polygonaceae #polemoniaceae

## Executive Summary

This document is a fragment of the *Federal Register* (Vol. 63, No. 62, April 1, 1998, Proposed Rules) issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. It contains two proposed listing rules for plants:

- **Chiricahua dock (*Rumex orthoneurus*)**, a rare Southwest endemic of riparian and cienega wetlands, proposed for listing as **threatened**.
- **Yreka phlox (*Phlox hirsuta*)**, a perennial subshrub endemic to serpentine slopes near Yreka, California, proposed for listing as **endangered**.

Both proposals cite habitat loss and degradation (livestock grazing, recreation, water diversion, road construction, and wildfire for the dock; urbanization and small population size for the phlox) as the main threats. The Service invites public comment and information on distribution, population size, and threats, and notes that the Act's listing provisions and regulatory mechanisms are insufficient to prevent continued decline.

A fragment of the rule amending [[50 CFR Part 17]] also appears in the document, referring to a species table entry for Pecos sunflower (Asteraceae). This text is heavily garbled by OCR and is not described further here.

## Background & Description

### Rumex orthoneurus (Chiricahua Dock)

- **Taxonomy:** Herbaceous, robust perennial in the Polygonaceae (buckwheat family). First described by Rechinger (1936) from a [[Blumer's Collection]] in the Chiricahua Mountains, Barfoot Park. Plants from the White, Mogollon, and San Francisco mountains were originally thought to be *R. occidentalis* but are now assigned to *R. orthoneurus* (Mount and Logan 1993; Friar et al. 1994; Bellsey and Mount 1995).
- **Morphology:** Plants reach 1 m (3.3 ft) in height, with flowering stalks up to 2 m (6.6 ft) on vigorous specimens. Large basal leaves reach 50 cm (19.7 in) long and 18 cm (7.1 in) wide. Distinguishing features include creeping underground rhizomes (rather than taproots), lateral leaf veins nearly perpendicular to the midvein, and no swellings on the midribs of fruiting capsules.
- **Reproduction:** Spreads largely asexually through creeping rhizomes, which complicates population counts.
- **Associated species:** Common associates include [[Sneezeweed]] (*Helenium hoopesii*), larkspur (*Delphinium andesicola*), monkeyflower (*Mimulus* sp.), and sedges (*Carex* spp.). Adjacent communities include [[Douglas Fir]], ponderosa pine, big-tooth maple, and white fir.

### Phlox hirsuta (Yreka Phlox)

- **Taxonomy:** Perennial subshrub in the Polemoniaceae (phlox family). Type specimen collected by Edward Green in 1876, 8 km (5 miles) southwest of Yreka. Described by Elias Nelson in 1899. Jepson (1943) reduced it to variety (*P. stansburyi* var. *hirsuta*), but Wherry (1955) restored full species status.
- **Morphology:** Grows 5 to 15 cm (2 to 5.9 in) tall from a stout, woody base; hairy throughout. Leaves are 1.5 to 3 cm (0.6 to 1.2 in) long and 4 to 7 mm (0.2 to 0.3 in) wide, with glandular margins. Pink to purple flowers (corollas 12 to 15 mm) bloom April to June.
- **Similar taxa:** [[Showy Phlox]] (*P. speciosa*) and [[Northern Phlox]] (*P. adsurgens*) are taller, and *P. adsurgens* blooms June to August and is glabrous. *P. stansburyi* occurs about 112 km (70 miles) farther east.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat and Soils

- **Rumex orthoneurus** occurs in high-elevation riparian and wetland habitats in moist soils or shallowly inundated areas adjacent to springs and streams, mostly in open meadows or open-canopy streamsides. The Arizona Game and Fish Department (1993) estimated that riparian vegetation along perennial streams makes up about **0.4 percent** of Arizona's total land area. Its habitat is therefore rare in the desert Southwest.
- **Phlox hirsuta** grows on [[Serpentine Soils]], which are high in magnesium, chromium, and nickel and low in calcium, nitrogen, potassium, and phosphorus. Serpentine soils strongly shape local vegetation, and few plants are fully restricted to them. The species occurs at **880 to 1,340 m (2,800 to 4,400 ft)** in association with Jeffrey pine, incense cedar, and juniper.

### Distribution and Population Status

**Rumex orthoneurus**
- About **10 natural sites** in Arizona in the Chiricahua, Pinaleño, Huachuca, and Sierra Ancha mountains, plus populations on the Gila National Forest (Mogollon and San Francisco mountains, including Willow and Silver Creek drainages and SA Creek).
- Presence in the White Mountains is still being assessed.
- Roughly **three** natural sites in Arizona are believed extirpated.
- Approximately **24 introduced populations** were established in the 1980s. Many are now extirpated or unlikely to persist. The Tonto National Forest (1993) identified **15 transplant sites** as Priority 1 populations expected to be extirpated within 50 years, and now considers **six** introduced populations extirpated.
- Overall numbers are declining, but precise counts are unknown because of clonal growth and differing survey methods.
- Recent research indicates the species extends into northern New Mexico (Pecos Wilderness) and once occurred on the Lincoln National Forest.

**Phlox hirsuta**
- Known from only **two locations** near Yreka. The first is an open ridge in juniper woodland within Yreka city limits (estimated at roughly 15 to 36 ha, or 37 to 90 acres). The second lies about 8 to 10 km (5 to 6 miles) southwest of Yreka along California State Highway 3 in open Jeffrey pine forest, with about 65 ha (160 acres) of occupied habitat.
- A third location near Etna Mills was last reported in **1930**. It was searched without finding plants or suitable habitat, and the record may be erroneous.
- Surveys covered **80 percent** of potential habitat on Klamath National Forest and BLM Redding Resource Area lands, with no new populations found.
- Land ownership is mixed among private owners, the City of Yreka, and the U.S. Forest Service. The Yreka occurrence is the larger and denser of the two.

### Threats

- **Livestock grazing** is the main threat to *R. orthoneurus*, due to high palatability and occurrence in wetlands attractive to cattle. Grazing can prevent seed production, compact soils, trample rhizomes, and destabilize streambanks. Trespass cattle and horses have been a recurring problem.
- **Recreation** causes soil compaction, trampling, and unstable banks, especially where [[Campground Use]] and hiking are concentrated. The Tonto National Forest receives the highest recreational use of any U.S. National Forest.
- **Water development and diversion**, **road construction and maintenance**, **logging**, **mining** (including a proposed uranium mill near Workman Creek), and **wildfire** (the Dude, Bray Creek, and Rattlesnake fires) have all reduced habitat or caused extirpation.
- **Phlox hirsuta** is threatened by urbanization, inadequate state regulatory mechanisms, and extirpation from random events due to its small number of populations and limited range.

### Legal Protection Context

- [[National Environmental Policy Act]]: No Environmental Assessment is required for listing rules. NEPA does not require agencies to select the least harmful alternative, and actions affecting listed species may proceed if effects are disclosed.
- [[Clean Water Act]] (Section 404) and Executive Orders 11988 (Floodplain Management) and 11990 (Protection of Wetlands) offer some protection to wetlands but have not stopped declines.
- [[Lacey Act]]: Prohibits trade in plants taken in violation of federal, tribal, or state law. Its protection is limited by enforceability.
- [[Arizona Native Plant Law]]: *R. orthoneurus* is "highly safeguarded." Collection requires an Arizona Department of Agriculture permit, but landowners may clear land after giving notice for plant salvage.
- *R. orthoneurus* is not listed under [[CITES]].
- [[National Forest Management Act]] and the [[Federal Land Policy and Management Act]] direct federal agencies to consider listed and sensitive species, but forest plans have not eliminated adverse effects.

## Key Findings

### Regulatory History

- Rumex orthoneurus was listed as an endangered species in the Smithsonian Institution's House Document No. 94-51 (January 9, 1975), and included in the June 16, 1976, proposal of about 1,700 plant taxa. That proposal was withdrawn on December 10, 1979, after the 1978 amendments.
- The species was a Category 1 candidate from 1980 onward. It was identified as a candidate in the February 28, 1996, and September 19, 1997, notices.
- A petition from the Southwest Forest Alliance and the Southwest Center for Biological Diversity (received May 7, 1996) requested listing and critical habitat designation. A civil action filed in the District Court of Arizona on October 2, 1997, alleged failure to make a 90-day finding.
- The proposal falls under **Tier 3** of the Service's Listing Priority Guidance for FY 1997 and was delayed by work on higher-priority species.

### Proposed Actions

- **Rumex orthoneurus** is proposed for listing as **threatened** under the Act. The Service concludes it is not in immediate danger of extinction but is likely to become endangered in the foreseeable future if threats continue.
- **Critical habitat** is not designated in this proposal. The Service has not identified specific areas as essential, and this is a key open question.
- **Phlox hirsuta** is proposed for listing as **endangered** to implement federal protection and recovery provisions.

### Notable Conclusions

- Both plants are small-range endemics that are vulnerable to chance extirpation because of small, isolated populations.
- Habitat degradation, not direct harvest, is the principal threat. No commercial, recreational, or scientific use of *R. orthoneurus* is known.
- Existing federal and state laws do not provide sufficient protection for either plant, according to the Service's analysis.
