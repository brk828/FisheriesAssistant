## Executive Summary

This study by Dieleman investigates the endogenous ("circatidal") control of swimming activity and rheotaxis in an estuarine population of **[[Gammarus Zaddachi]]**, a brackish-water amphipod, sampled from the River Slack (Pas-de-Calais, France). Building on prior field studies of anadromous (upstream) and catadromous (downstream, "drift") migrations linked to tidal cycles, temperature, salinity, and stream velocity, this paper uses a laboratory current chamber to demonstrate that swimming intensity and current-orientation behavior are governed by an internal biological clock synchronized to the tidal cycle, independent of external stimuli. The findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the previously observed mass anadromous migrations occurring during spring tides in autumn and spring.

## Background & Description

This paper is behavioral/ecological rather than morphological in focus; no new anatomical descriptions are provided. Relevant biological notes include:

- **Species**: *Gammarus zaddachi* Sexton, 1912 — a brackish-water gammarid amphipod.
- **Sympatric congener**: *[[Gammarus Chevreuxi]]* Sexton, 1913, co-occurring in the River Dourduff, also exhibiting endogenous swimming rhythms (notably intense in current-chamber trials).
- **Behavioral morphology of note**: During peak nocturnal activity near high water, individuals leave the substratum and swim/circle actively, frequently making antennal and gnathopod contact with conspecifics, often attempting precopulatory pairing ("precopula") — indicating a link between the migratory activity peak and reproductive behavior.
- **Rheotactic behavior states**:
  - *Low activity*: animals adhere to substrate, orient head-into-current (positive rheotaxis), occasionally washed downstream as passive "drifters," then re-settle.
  - *Peak activity (around nocturnal high water)*: rheotaxis is effectively abolished; animals swim indifferently to current direction, resulting in net displacement with the flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Estuarine/brackish zones of small French coastal rivers (River Slack, River Dourduff); occupies the lower estuary in summer where daily tidal incursions raise salinity.
- **Seasonal migration pattern**:
  - **Autumn**: High spring tides can reverse stream direction in the riverbed, triggering mass **anadromous** (upstream) migration into normally limnic (freshwater) river reaches, exploiting tidal current reversal.
  - **Spring**: A secondary, smaller anadromous invasion occurs during equinoctial high tides.
  - **Year-round**: **Catadromous** ("drift") downstream migration occurs continuously at varying intensity, peaking in early spring and summer.
  - Both migration types are restricted to hours of darkness.
- **Co-occurring species**: *[[Gammarus Chevreuxi]]* shares habitat and migratory ecology in the River Dourduff; driftnet catches there have recorded up to 300,000 individuals of *G. chevreuxi* in a single nocturnal high-water period.
- **Comparative species**: The pink shrimp *[[Penaeus Duorarum]]* shows an analogous endogenous, tidally phase-locked control over swimming activity, level, velocity, and direction — supporting the broader concept of **[[Circatidal Rhythms]]** across estuarine/marine crustaceans.
- **Trophic note**: In laboratory maintenance, animals were fed the terrestrial weed *Stellaria media* (chickweed), which was available ad libitum during experiments.
- **Environmental drivers of migration** (per Girisch & Dennert, 1975): rising salinity, decreasing stream velocity, and rising temperature act as external triggers, especially potent in combination during a simulated natural tidal cycle.

## Key Findings

1. **Endogenous (circatidal) control of activity confirmed**: Under constant laboratory conditions (fixed temperature 8.5°C, chlorinity 0.19‰, current velocity ~8 cm/sec), estuarine *G. zaddachi* still displayed swimming activity rhythms tightly correlated with the timing of nocturnal high water (H.W.) in the original sampling area — confirming an internal biological clock rather than purely reactive behavior to external cues.

2. **Modulation by tidal amplitude (spring vs. neap tides)**:
   - During a **neap tide period** (23 March 1975), activity progressively increased alongside rising tidal coefficients (74→92), peaking near maximal coefficient.
   - During a **spring tide period** (1 December 1974), activity dropped rapidly as the tides waned toward the following neap phase.

3. **Phase relationship to high water**: Activity increased sharply ~1 hour before nocturnal H.W. in the sampling area, peaking 1–2 hours after H.W. — a pattern distinctly different from that reported in driftnet field studies (Dennert et al., 1969; Girisch et al., 1974), which showed activity increases a few hours after sunset, independent of tidal phase. This discrepancy underscores that estuarine populations are entrained to the *tidal* clock rather than the *light/dark* cycle.

4. **Light/dark transition has minimal influence** on the activity rhythm compared to the tidal phase relationship.

5. **Behavioral switch in rheotaxis around nocturnal H.W.**: A quantifiable net transport of animals occurred "with the current" specifically during the hours bracketing nocturnal high water (from ~1 hour before to several hours after H.W.), attributable to loss of positive rheotaxis during the peak activity window. On 17 March 1975 (equinoctial spring tides), net transport exceeded 200 individuals per 5 minutes over several hours.

6. **Quantitative agreement with field displacement data**: Estimated average displacement per individual in the current chamber (~30–40 m during peak transport) closely matches field-based anadromous migration distances reported by Dennert et al. (1969) (commonly ~50 m, up to 80 m maximum).

7. **Proposed integrated mechanism for anadromous migration** (five-step model):
   - Loss of positive rheotaxis near nocturnal H.W. during spring tides enables passive transport with reversed (upstream) currents.
   - Rising tides drive progressively increasing swimming activity, reinforcing upriver movement.
   - Falling tides sharply reduce activity, preventing high catadromous drift-rate immediately after upstream migration.
   - Neap tide periods show minimal activity and negligible drift.
   - Repeated spring tide cycles (e.g., equinoctial periods) allow cumulative upstream migration over several kilometers.

8. **Explains prior tagging results**: The internal clock model accounts for Dennert et al.'s (1969) observation that individuals tagged while migrating upstream continued moving in that direction — their activity window is endogenously restricted to nocturnal high-water periods during spring tides, minimizing catadromous drift risk.

9. **Cross-species relevance**: The endogenous tidal-clock mechanism parallels findings in *[[Penaeus Duorarum]]*, suggesting a broadly conserved biological timing strategy among tidally migrating crustaceans, and reinforces the theoretical framework of **[[Circatidal Rhythms]]** described by Palmer (1973).

10. **Unresolved contribution of external cues**: While Girisch & Dennert (1975) showed rising salinity, falling stream velocity, and rising temperature can each independently trigger swimming activity in simulation chambers, the present study argues that mismatches between an animal's endogenous tidal phase and the imposed artificial tidal cycle likely explain the large inter-experimental variability observed in their earlier work.
