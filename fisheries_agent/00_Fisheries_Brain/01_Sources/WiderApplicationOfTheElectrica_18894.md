# Wider Application of the Electrical Method of Collecting Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1947 paper (published 1949 in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*) by John L. Funk of the [[Missouri Conservation Commission]] reviews the limitations of the [[Electrical Shock Method]] of fish collection and describes modifications that extend it to wider, deeper and more turbid streams. The central innovation is a simplified, largely permanently wired "electric seine" powered by a small [[Portable Generator]] and used in [[Fishery Census]] work in Missouri. Preliminary stunning trials suggest most species are stunned by about 5 to 6 amperes at 110 volts and recover within minutes. Three blocked-section efficiency experiments gave recovery rates of 40.9%, 15.9% and 25.2%, which the author regards as incomplete and inconclusive. The method is judged qualitatively excellent for sampling areas that cannot be seined.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Electric-shock sampling spread after [[Haskell 1940]] and [[Haskell and Zilliox 1941]]. Improvements were reported in few journals, so the author aimed to document field practice and encourage discussion.

**Common limitations identified by the author:**
1. Equipment is cumbersome and costly (generators, cable, meters, rheostats, fuses, and at least two operators).
2. The method works mainly in narrow, shallow, clear streams, such as [[Northern Trout Brooks]].
3. Effectiveness was thought to vary with water and soil chemistry.
4. The method is dangerous, as stressed by Haskell and Zilliox.

**Equipment design (the "electric seine"):**
- **Power:** 110 V, 60-cycle AC generator of 500 W, air-cooled, with light-metal castings and an automatic cut-off that replaces fuses. Units weigh under 150 lb.
- **Electrodes:** Eight "floating grids" (2 × 3 in white pine, 3 ft long, wrapped in ¼-inch-mesh hardware cloth), spaced 10 ft apart on one line. Seven 7-ft "drag wires" of uninsulated cable, weighted with lead seine weights, hang between them on the other line. Two 7-ft [[Probe Pole]]s with copper-sheathed lower ends close each end.
- **Wiring:** Flexible insulated copper cable (No. 10 or 12 recommended). The author used army-surplus two-wire No. 18 rubber-covered cable. Connections are permanent except at the generator, which removes most start-up and operating delays.
- **Simplifications:** Meters and rheostats were judged unnecessary once potentials are set. A boat-mounted power plant trailing a line of 50 ft or less reduces snarls.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and soil chemistry.** Records from waters with calcium carbonate between 75 and 177 ppm showed similar success. The author argues that most running stream water carries enough dissolved minerals to conduct current, and that little current passes through soil, so chemistry is not a major limit with this apparatus.

**Physical factors affecting efficiency (ranked by the author):**
- **Turbidity:** Reduces visibility and recovery. Large redhorse, carpsucker, buffalo and carp were nonetheless taken in highly turbid water.
- **Depth:** Limits crew movement and visibility. Large fish are disproportionately recovered from deep water.
- **Bottom type:** Mud, clay and fine sand roil easily. Gravel and rubble can hold cover and algal debris.
- **Current velocity:** Swept-out stunned fish are often recovered in quiet water below riffles.

**Cover and refuge.** Undercut banks, submerged logs, boulders and drift piles hold most of a stream's fish. Stunned fish often flee into cover before fully stunning and are easily overlooked. Undercut trees and stumps are especially hard to work. Heron tracks suggested predation on stunned fish at one site.

**Size selectivity.** Larger fish are stunned and recovered more readily. Small fish often pass through the field unaffected unless they come within 1 to 2 ft of an electrode, whereas moderate and large fish are stunned at distances of about 4 ft.

## Key Findings

**Equipment and operation**
- A 500 W, 110 V plant with automatic cut-off works well; higher voltages or wattages were unnecessary.
- Crew of at least two operators in water (tied to a rope and wearing waders and electrician's rubber gloves), plus a boatman and additional recovery staff.
- The seine covers streams up to about 100 ft wide in one pass, and wider streams are worked along both margins at an acute angle to the bank.
- Work proceeds upstream to downstream. The author states the method was effective on streams 90 to 100 ft wide and usable, though less efficient, in turbid water.
- Safety was mainly managed through supervision. The only casualties were minor shocks from waders flooding or falling inside the field.

**Stunning and recovery**
- Preliminary tests suggested most species are stunned by about 5 to 6 A at 110 V and recover within a few minutes.
- Larger fish are stunned more readily than fingerlings.

**Efficiency experiments (1947, incomplete)**
- Method: Sections blocked with 14-inch-mesh seines, stocked with marked fish (weighed, measured, tagged or fin-clipped), then sampled with the seine.

| Site | Planted | Recovered | Recovery | Key conditions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Swan Creek]], Osage County (about 200 × 60 ft) | 22 | 9 | 40.9% | Turbidity 9 to 11 ppm; about 65% good cover |
| Upper [[Gasconade River]], Wright County (420 × 35.8 ft) | 63 | 10 | 15.9% | Clear; one-third cover; herons likely killed some fish |
| Upper [[Meramec River]], Dent County (190 ft long) | 42 | 11 | 25.2% | Turbidity 7 ppm; boulders provided all cover |

- Prior work by [[Schuck 1945]] on trout in a New York stream reported recovery from 95% (age-groups III to V) down to 50% (age-group 0). Schuck identified fish size as the main determinant.
- Haskell and Zilliox found more than 80% recovery for trout over 4 in long.
- The author considers the low figures at Upper Gasconade and Upper Meramec partly due to unmeasured losses (predation, burial in cover, deep water), so they do not represent true efficiency.

**Species and sampling yield**
- Almost all Missouri fish species were collected, including lampreys, paddlefish, many catfishes, eels and gars.
- On the Meramec River (May 31, 1947, 765 ft × 87.6 ft, depth to 5.75 ft), a single sweep took 138 fish in under 2 hours, including 20 smallmouth bass, 48 longear sunfish, 24 green sunfish and 28 redhorse. The author calls this a good qualitative, not quantitative, sample.

**Conclusions**
- Efficiency depends on local physical conditions, so each stream section is a separate problem.
- The method is highly effective for qualitative sampling where seining is impossible, but quantitative estimates would require further study of turbidity, depth, cover and size effects.
