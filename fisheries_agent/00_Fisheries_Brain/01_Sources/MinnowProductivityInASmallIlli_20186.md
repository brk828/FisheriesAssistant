# Minnow Productivity in a Small Illinois Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 reprint from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Volume 84, 1954) by R. Weldon Larimore of the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] evaluates the effects of harvesting minnows from a small stream. Nine electric-shocker collections were made from a 0.9-mile section of [[Jordan Creek]], east-central Illinois, between August 1950 and October 1953. Despite continuous cropping, minnow yields rose each year, and the population showed strong natural cycles that made the effects of harvest hard to separate from natural fluctuation. The study concludes that electric shocking removed minnows efficiently but did not eliminate any species or reduce the population for more than a few months.

## Background & Description

Minnow harvest restrictions in the United States were adopted for several purposes: preserving bait supplies for anglers, protecting food for game fish, preventing losses of young game fish during seining, and reducing competition with commercial minnow dealers. Some states ban minnow taking entirely, while others close only certain counties, drainage systems, or seasons.

The study formed one phase of a larger investigation of the fish population of Jordan Creek. The study section was 0.9 mile long, averaged about 22 feet wide, covered 2.4 acres at average summer water level, and had an average depth of about 1 foot. Collections used a 30-foot [[Electric Fish Shocker]] powered by a 115-volt A.C. generator, operated by three men, with minnows dip-netted (1/4-inch mesh) and sorted, counted, and weighed in the laboratory. The habitat and fish population were previously described by [[Larimore, Pickering, and Durham (1952)]].

Species highlighted in the study include the [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Pimephales notatus*), the [[Silverjaw Minnow]] (*Ericymba buccata*), and the [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*). The paper also references seining-damage literature by Markus (1934, 1939) and Surber (1940).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Annual cycle:** Minnow numbers were low in spring, rose through summer as new broods entered, and declined over winter. Average weight peaked in spring when large breeders were numerous and fell during summer as small fish were added.
- **Environmental drivers:** Weather affecting reproduction, survival, growth, and movement strongly influenced cycles. Each species responded differently, so populations fluctuated independently.
- **Shocking vs. seining:** Seining is restricted to smooth, snag-free bottoms and is usually limited to occasional pools. Electric shocking covered every pool and shallow riffle, and the author judged it many times more effective than seining in this habitat.
- **Conditions where seining may harm populations:** The paper lists isolation of the cropped area, extensive seinable habitat such as dredge ditches, year-round favorable conditions with continuing demand, habitat change from pollution, dredging or land use, and low natural productivity with a short growing season.

## Key Findings

- **Harvest yields rose over time.** A total of 76,217 minnows weighing 522.0 pounds (217.5 pounds per acre) were removed across nine collections. August yields increased from 15.0 lb/acre in 1950 to 52.1 lb/acre in 1953. The 1953 total was 40,645 minnows weighing 243.5 pounds (101.5 lb/acre).
- **Unharvested mortality was substantial.** The shocker killed about 50 percent of minnows present. Operating at roughly 50 percent collecting efficiency, the author estimated about 25 percent of the population was left dead in the creek. Adding this to the 1953 crop gives an estimated 152 lb/acre total production.
- **Species responded differently.** The silverjaw minnow declined sharply in 1952 after a weak brood, while the bluntnose produced a very strong 1952 year class (about three-fourths of that species' catch). Both species had suitable reproduction in 1953.
- **Cropping only shifted timing.** The short life cycle, high reproductive potential, and fast growth of minnows meant shocking mainly removed each brood earlier than natural decline. No species disappeared.
- **Selectivity by size and spawning date.** The 1/4-inch mesh dip nets restricted harvest largely to minnows above 1.8 inches. Larger species such as the stoneroller entered collections earlier in their life cycle, and early spawners appeared in August while late spawners appeared in October.
- **Natural fluctuation masked harvest effects.** The author concluded that natural population swings were too large to isolate cropping effects, but no evidence of adverse effects from electric shocking was found.
- **Management implication.** Seining damage should not be assumed without examining all factors influencing a population, and the paper cautions that the conditions listed above are needed for seining to be detrimental.
