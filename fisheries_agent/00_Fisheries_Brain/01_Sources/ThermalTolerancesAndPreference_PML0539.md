# Thermal Tolerances and Preferences of Fishes of the Virgin River System (Utah, Arizona, Nevada)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares critical thermal maxima (CTM) and acute and final thermal preferenda of the common native fishes of the [[Virgin River]] and the introduced [[Red Shiner]]. Species differ in thermal lability, and these differences correspond with their distribution and abundance along the river. Acclimation temperature strongly shapes preferred temperatures in some species but little in others, and the authors argue that combining the influence of acclimation temperature with CTM gives a better picture of distributional relationships than CTM alone. The work is framed by the decline of native desert fish stocks caused by water use, altered flow and water quality, and non-native fish introductions.

## Background & Description

The Virgin River is a desert stream system with shifting sandy bottoms, steep gradients, high sediment loads, variable flows, and large daily and seasonal temperature swings. Natural barriers include Pah Tempe Springs, a series of more than 100 saline hot springs along the Hurricane Fault in Utah, and the Virgin River Gorge in Arizona, where flow often goes underground.

The native fish fauna comprises six species:

- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus (Pantosteus) clarki*)
- [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), an endemic subspecies
- [[Virgin Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*), an endemic subspecies
- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), an endemic species listed as endangered (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1986)

Of 13 introduced fish species recorded from the lower Virgin River, only the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) has become well established. It was introduced into the Colorado River system as a bait fish in the early 1950s. The roundtail chub has been recommended for endangered status and the spinedace for threatened status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Agricultural diversion and groundwater use since 1900 have reduced mainstream flows so that long stretches may be dry in summer. After completion of the Quail Creek Reservoir project in early 1985, discharge from Pah Tempe Springs increased unexpectedly, raising both temperature and salinity in the downstream segment in Utah. Summer river temperatures fluctuate by 15–20 °C daily and reach 36 °C in some areas.

Before this study, the temperature responses of the native fishes had not been examined, apart from the roundtail chub (Schumann 1978) and Virgin spinedace (Espinosa and Deacon 1978).

Distribution patterns described in the paper:
- Woundfin is dominant in moderately altered middle and lower mainstream sections with extreme temperature variability.
- Desert sucker is the most widely distributed species, most abundant in middle and lower tributaries and the upper mainstream.
- Speckled dace has the most upstream distribution, tied to cool tributaries and springs.
- Virgin spinedace is most abundant in lower tributaries and the upper mainstream.
- Flannelmouth sucker reaches its greatest abundance in the upper mainstream.
- Virgin roundtail chub is confined to the middle and lower mainstream below Pah Tempe Springs.
- Red shiner dominates the deeper water of the highly modified lower reach, where summer temperatures more often exceed 30 °C.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Fish were collected in May and June with 10-m nylon seines (6.4-mm mesh); roundtail adults were collected April to October.
- Fish were acclimated for at least two weeks at 10, 15, and 25 °C, on a 12-hour photoperiod, and fed commercial trout chow. All experiments ran June to August.
- CTM was measured on six individuals per species per acclimation temperature, heating at 0.2 °C per minute until loss of equilibrium.
- Acute preferenda were measured in a horizontal thermal gradient of six chambers (roughly 6–35 °C), with six individuals per species (except suckers and roundtail chub).
- Roundtail were reared from Moapa River adults and tested at 8, 22, 25, and 30 °C; suckers were tested at incomplete acclimation sets, so their results were not compared statistically.

**Critical thermal maxima (Table 1)**
- CTM increased linearly with acclimation temperature in all species.
- At 15 °C and 10 °C acclimation, CTMs did not differ significantly between species.
- At 25 °C acclimation, woundfin and red shiner CTMs each differed significantly from all other species but not from each other.
- Slopes of CTM versus acclimation temperature ranged from 0.28 (desert sucker) to 0.59 (woundfin). Other slopes: speckled dace 0.42, flannelmouth sucker 0.38, Virgin spinedace 0.45, Virgin roundtail 0.48, red shiner 0.58.

**Thermal preference**
- Mean acute preferenda increased significantly with acclimation temperature for all species, with one exception: speckled dace at 15 and 25 °C did not differ.
- At 10 and 15 °C acclimation, mean preferenda equaled or exceeded acclimation temperature in all species. At 25 °C this held only for flannelmouth sucker and red shiner.
- Preference distributions were generally skewed, which affected the choice of central-tendency measure.

**Final thermal preferenda (Figures 2–3, fitted by eye)**
- Red shiner: approximately 27.0 °C (literature values for Texas populations were 30.0 and 23.3 °C)
- Flannelmouth sucker: 25.9 °C (reported as 25.1 °C in the text)
- Virgin roundtail: 23.8 °C
- Virgin spinedace: 23.1 °C
- Woundfin: 17.5 °C
- Desert sucker: 19.5 °C
- Speckled dace: 15.8 °C (the text also lists a 19.5 °C value in the same sentence sequence; the figure label gives 15.8 °C)

**Interpretation**
- Woundfin has a high CTM, relatively low final preferendum, and the most labile acute preference of the species examined. Its thermal lability suggests it occupies the warmer, variable mainstream.
- Red shiner has a high CTM and the highest final preferendum. Its CTM rises markedly with acclimation, which may give it an advantage over woundfin in the warm lower mainstream during summer.
- Flannelmouth sucker has a high final preferendum, but its CTM at 25 °C is significantly lower, which may partly explain its near absence below Mesquite, Nevada.
- Virgin roundtail has the lowest CTM of any native species and a thermal curve similar to the spinedace. Its restriction below Pah Tempe Springs, plus the lack of suitable tributaries, may explain its precarious status.
- The authors estimate that desert fishes have thermal niche breadths of 5–10 °C, broader than many temperate freshwater fishes.
- Laboratory thermal patterns correspond closely with field distributions in the Virgin River, which the authors describe as striking.

**Conclusions**
- Combining acclimation-dependent preferenda with CTM provides better insight into distribution than CTM alone.
- Warming from altered flows and discharges may favor the red shiner and disadvantage native fishes such as the woundfin and roundtail, which are already threatened.
