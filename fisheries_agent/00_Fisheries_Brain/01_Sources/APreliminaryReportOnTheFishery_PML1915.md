# Preliminary Report on the Fishery of Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service progress report describes the early fishery of [[Lake Mead]], a reservoir created by the construction of [[Boulder Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]]. Bass fishing was initially popular, but catches declined in 1941 as the fish grew thin and lost condition. The study covered the reservoir's physical setting, temperature and chemistry, plankton and bottom fauna, and the fish species present, and it included fish growth data for [[Largemouth Bass]]. The report concludes with six management recommendations, the most important of which are against further stocking and against a closed season. The document also contains a short preface on farm-raised pond fishing and a transcribed discussion among fisheries scientists.

## Background & Description

Creation of Lake Mead opened recreational fishing in an area that had previously been largely undeveloped. Bass fishing quickly attracted anglers, and requests for stocking were sent to state and federal agencies. Fishing went well from roughly 1935 to 1940, but in 1941 bass were thin and in poor condition. Concern among sportsmen prompted the Fish and Wildlife Service to begin a study of the fishery in fall 1941. The reservoir was filled to the spillway level of Boulder Dam for the first time during the summer of 1941.

The lake is irregular in shape, with many coves and bays. Most tributaries are small, warm, and muddy streams with highly variable flow. The [[Virgin River]] is the main tributary apart from the Colorado, and the [[Muddy River]] is smaller. Both carry heavy silt loads during floods. The Colorado supplies most of the water. Its drainage area of about 167,000 square miles is mostly semiarid, easily eroded desert land.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and temperature.** Colorado River discharge at Bright Angel Gaging Station showed large fluctuations. Peak discharges were 46,000 cubic feet per second and 122,100 cubic feet per second, the latter in 1941. Estimated mean annual runoff was 15,000,000 acre-feet, with a maximum of 25,200,000 acre-feet and a minimum of 4,300,000 acre-feet. River temperatures reached a maximum of 81.0 °F in both 1940 and 1941. A clear [[Thermocline]] did not form near Boulder Dam in summer 1941, although thermographs showed a zone of rapid temperature decline. A vertical profile on 1 July 1941 showed a thermocline 30 feet thick, 50 feet below the surface. Surface water temperatures around the intake towers fluctuated by an average of about 27 °F per year. Maximum yearly temperatures ranged from 79.1 °F in 1941 to 84.7 °F in 1940. Storage increases had added 313 feet to the reservoir depth and may have lowered maximum temperature by 5.6 °F.

**Substrate and water chemistry.** Silt is rapidly covering the reservoir bottom. Water is alkaline (pH about 7.1 to 8.4) with methyl orange alkalinity of 117 to 140 ppm. Dissolved solids reached as much as 797 ppm, mainly from calcium, magnesium, sodium chloride, and other minerals.

**Plants and plankton.** No aquatic plants were found except a blue-green alga covering the bottom to considerable depths. A fluctuation of 65 feet in water level and poor soils were judged to make plant establishment very unlikely. In November 1941, net plankton samples from three locations averaged 0.0023 cubic inch per cubic foot of water. [[Ceratium]] was the most abundant organism, followed by Diaptomus, Cyclops, Microcystis, and Daphnia. Plankton is the main food of many young fish.

**Bottom fauna.** Twelve square-foot bottom samples at Detritus Wash and Temple Bar (depths 20 to 107 feet) contained no organisms large enough to be retained by a 50-mesh screen, except one midge larva. Bottom production is very poor.

**Fish species.** Native species include [[Bonytail]], the Colorado "white salmon," and [[Humpback Sucker]]. Introduced species include [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Carp and channel catfish were considered probably the most abundant fish in the lake, although no data were given. Largemouth bass, bluegill, and black crappie were ranked in that order of abundance by visual observation. Crappies were increasing rapidly. [[Crayfish]] are also present in the lake.

## Key Findings

- **Bass growth was fast.** The report compared Lake Mead largemouth bass with those in Michigan, Indiana, and Wisconsin. Lake Mead fish grew much faster, and Michigan largemouth bass typically took three years to reach 10 inches. Lake Mead bass of the same age averaged 13.4 inches and over one pound. The largest bass on record weighed 13 pounds 14 ounces. Average sizes were declining, judging from angler catches.
- **Growth appears to be slowing** as the reservoir stabilizes. Eschmeyer and Jones reported a similar trend in the game fish of [[Norris Reservoir]].
- **The 1940–1941 decline was probably environmental.** Bass were in poor condition in 1941, probably because something disrupted spawning or egg incubation in spring 1940, which reduced the number of young fish available as forage. Bass recovered after the 1941 spawn.
- **Reproduction was strong in 1941.** Thousands of young bass and bluegills were seen in every cove. Using [[Carbine]]'s Michigan figures, each bluegill nest produces an average of 17,914 fry and each bass nest 4,977 fry. The potential natural reproduction far exceeds any artificial stocking capacity.

**Management recommendations (summarized):**

1. Forage fish introduction is unnecessary under present conditions, because largemouth bass grow well without other fish.
2. No closed season should be applied, since fishing does not remove the annual yield and restriction would let bass overpopulate relative to their food supply.
3. No further stocking is advisable.
4. Aquatic plants should not be introduced, because fluctuating water levels prevent establishment. Invertebrate food organisms should not be introduced because basin fertility is very low.
5. [[Smallmouth Bass]] are well suited to the lake but should not be introduced while largemouth bass are doing well.
6. Working with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to regulate water levels could help. Drops of 5 to 10 feet during April, May, and June should be avoided to protect [[Spawning]] bluegill and bass nests.

**Discussion highlights.** Participants discussed [[Crayfish]] as forage. Largemouth bass ate crayfish only moderately well, while smallmouth bass fed on them more readily. One Illinois study stocked only largemouth bass in a small artificial lake, where growth was fast, but the spawning run was nearly wiped out and production appeared to depend on the absence of other forage. The preface, on [[Fee Fishing]] ponds, notes that trout are raised for sale and that the ponds are a source of revenue that could be developed before commercial shipping of farm-raised fish is permitted.
