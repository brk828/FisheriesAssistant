## Executive Summary

This document is a formal expert critique authored by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University, Department of Zoology) and Paul C. Marsh (Center for Environmental Studies) in response to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's (FWS) 1993 draft **Colorado River Endangered Fishes Critical Habitat: Biological Support Document**. Rather than presenting novel primary field data, the letter provides a scientific and policy-oriented review of proposed critical habitat designations for four federally endangered "big-river" fishes of the Colorado River Basin: the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (referred to as Colorado squawfish, CSF). The authors support the general concept of critical habitat designation but strongly criticize the inconsistent application of "primary constituent elements" and "additional selection criteria," particularly the near-total exclusion of the lower Colorado River basin from proposed habitat. They recommend specific additional river reaches for inclusion and present a quantitative analysis showing no significant statistical relationship between degree of species endangerment and the proportion of historic range or river miles proposed as critical habitat — raising concerns about the scientific rationale underlying the draft's habitat allocation decisions.

## Background & Description

This document does not present new morphological data. It references historical taxonomic and distributional works (e.g., [[Smith Miller and Sable 1979]] on *Gila* species relationships in the upper Colorado River basin) but focuses on habitat, distribution, and management status rather than anatomical description. No morphometric, meristic, or physical trait data for [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], or [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] are provided in the source text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **River mainstem and floodplain habitats**: All four species historically occupied large, low-gradient, turbid "big-river" mainstem and floodplain habitats throughout the Colorado River Basin, including the lower basin (Gila, Salt, and Verde river systems) — an area the authors argue is systematically underrepresented in the draft's proposed critical habitat.
- **Reservoir/lacustrine habitats**: Species show differential use of impounded (lacustrine) habitats:
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] appears to use large reservoirs (e.g., [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Powell]], [[Roosevelt Lake]]) for adult habitat and possibly recruitment, with larvae documented from Lake Havasu and the Central Arizona Project Canal (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation 1986, 1987; Marsh & Papoulias 1989).
  - [[Bonytail Chub]] historically inhabited both riverine and reservoir habitats in the lower basin (Parker Dam to the U.S.-Mexico border), an area supporting historically the largest known populations of Bonytail, Colorado Pikeminnow, and Razorback Sucker (Dill 1944; Follett 1961; Miller 1961; Minckley 1973, 1985; Minckley et al. 1991).
  - [[Humpback Chub]] is largely restricted to riverine (non-reservoir) canyon habitats, consistent with its exclusion from lacustrine reaches.
  - [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] historically ranged into free-flowing canyon reaches (e.g., Grand/Marble Canyon), with the last wild individual in the lower basin taken near Havasu Creek in Grand Canyon in 1975 (Smith et al. 1979).
- **Spawning habitat cues**: Warm-water tributary inflows are identified as critical spawning habitat, e.g., the [[Paria River]] mouth, noted as the only sufficiently warm-water inflow supporting native fish reproduction within the Glen Canyon reach.
- **Riverine corridors as genetic linkages**: The authors emphasize that unimpounded river reaches between fragmented "islands" of proposed critical habitat function as essential corridors for genetic exchange among remaining populations, particularly for [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]].
- **Threats to niche integrity**:
  - **Non-native fish invasion** is identified as a primary, likely insurmountable barrier to recovery absent active management against aliens.
  - **Sediment starvation** downstream of dams disrupts natural hydraulic and geomorphic conditions essential to riverine habitat structure.
  - **Water temperature regimes**, altered by dam operations (e.g., cold hypolimnetic releases), may render some historically suitable reaches (e.g., San Juan River near Navajo Dam) currently unsuitable.
  - **Water management practices** (depletions, releases, power/water marketing, state entitlements) are implicated as underlying, insufficiently transparent drivers of habitat inclusion/exclusion decisions.

## Key Findings

- **Historic range vs. proposed critical habitat mismatch**: A quantitative comparison shows [[Bonytail Chub]] — considered the most biologically imperiled of the four species — had only ~15% of its historic range (344 of 2293 river miles) proposed as critical habitat, whereas [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (the least imperiled) had ~26% (1148 of 4415 river miles) proposed, nearly three times the river mileage.
- **Statistical analysis**: A strong linear correlation (R² = 0.77) was found between the number of pages devoted to a species in the draft and the number of river miles proposed as critical habitat — attributed to expected habitat redundancy in reporting, not biological justification.
- **No correlation with endangerment**: No significant relationship (R² consistently < 0.16) was found between degree of biological endangerment and any of the other variables examined (pages, % historic range proposed, river miles proposed, historic river miles) — a central critique of the draft's apparent lack of a coherent, biologically-driven rationale.
- **Lower Colorado River Basin underrepresentation**: The near-total absence of proposed critical habitat for [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] in the lower basin is called "unacceptable," given documented historic populations in the Gila, Salt, and Verde river systems.
- **Specific recommended additions** include:
  - Colorado River mainstem/floodplain, Parker Dam to U.S.–Mexico border (BTC, RBS, CSF)
  - Gila River, Ashurst-Hayden Diversion to AZ-NM border (BTC, CSF)
  - San Juan River and lower Animas River, CO–NM, upstream of Navajo Reservoir (RBS, CSF)
  - Roosevelt Lake and lower Tonto Creek, and extended Salt River reach to the Black/White river confluence (RBS, CSF)
  - Lake Powell, AZ–UT (RBS)
  - Lowermost Paria River, AZ (RBS spawning habitat)
  - Verde River, Horseshoe Reservoir to Sullivan Lake (CSF)
  - Colorado River, Glen Canyon Dam to upper Lake Mead (CSF)
  - Lower Colorado River/floodplain, Davis Dam to U.S.–Mexico border (CSF)
- **Reservoir inclusion inconsistency**: The draft excludes reservoir reaches for [[Humpback Chub]] (biologically justified, as HBC is riverine) but also for [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (less justified), while including reservoirs for [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] — an inconsistency attributed partly to "experimental, non-essential" population designations that create management and legal ambiguity.
- **Recovery program critique**: Despite [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Humpback Chub]] being federally listed as endangered since 1967 (26+ years) and [[Bonytail Chub]] since 1980 (10+ years), all three continue to decline. The upper basin Recovery Implementation Program, despite substantial funding (tens of millions of dollars) over roughly one-third of its planned 15-year term, had not demonstrated measurable improvement in the status of any of the four species by the time of this review.
- **Biodiversity paradox**: The authors note that gross ichthyofaunal biodiversity in the Colorado River Basin has increased due to non-native species introductions, even as native species diversity and abundance have collapsed toward extinction — a distinction the draft's "Biodiversity and Ecosystem Change" section fails to adequately define or address.
- **Systemic driver of decline**: The cumulative, compounding effects of water resource development (dams, diversions, water marketing) and non-native fish stocking are identified as the primary drivers of the imperiled status of the entire native Colorado River Basin fish fauna, with continuation of current policies predicted to result in eventual "faunal collapse."
