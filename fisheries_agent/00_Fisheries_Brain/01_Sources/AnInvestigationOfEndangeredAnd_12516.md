# Investigation of Endangered and Threatened Fish Species in Upper Colorado River Related to Bureau of Reclamation Projects

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This final report documents a 10-month field investigation (February–November 1976) by [[Northwest Fisheries Research]] examining the status of four endangered and threatened fish species in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The study covered approximately 50 miles of the [[Gunnison River]] (Delta to confluence with Colorado River) and 130 miles of the [[Colorado River]] (DeBeque Canyon to Moab, Utah). Sampling employed graduated mesh gill nets, trammel nets, hand seines, and electrofishing gear across 30 sampling trips (80 man-days in Colorado, 25 man-days in Utah). Results indicate drastic population declines of endemic species, with no federally endangered species captured in the Gunnison River despite intensive effort, though small self-maintaining populations persist in the Colorado River. The [[Colorado River]] exhibits 74% endemism, but introduced exotic species now dominate most sections. [[Walker Wildlife Area]] emerged as the only location supporting significant populations of both squawfish and razorback suckers.

## Background & Description

### Study Area Characteristics

The [[Upper Colorado River]] originates at the Continental Divide near Grand Lake, Colorado. The study section between DeBeque Canyon and Moab, Utah encompasses 130 miles with mean flow of 3,775 cfs (maximum 22,500 cfs, minimum 1,050 cfs at Cameo). The river drops 448 feet over this distance (average 7.8 feet/mile) draining 7,320 square miles with approximately 158,000 acres of upstream irrigation diversions.

The [[Gunnison River]] study area covered 50 miles from Delta, Colorado to its confluence with the Colorado River near Grand Junction, dropping an estimated 380 feet (7.2 feet/mile average). Mean flow at Whitewater is 2,562 cfs with historic range of 106–35,700 cfs. The [[Grand Valley]] is classified as arid with mean annual precipitation of 8 inches and mean annual temperature of 52°F. Water temperatures approach 80°F (27°C), near the upper tolerance limit for coldwater species.

### Historic Fish Fauna

Pre-colonization fish assemblages showed distinct elevational zonation. Higher elevations supported [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Mottled Sculpin]], [[Eagle Sculpin]], and [[Speckled Dace]]. Mid-elevations were dominated by [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. In lower elevation large-river habitat above Delta, [[Colorado Squawfish]] were common and [[Razorback Sucker]] abundant. [[Roundtail Chub]] occurred throughout except highest mountain streams. Evidence for significant [[Bonytail Chub]] or [[Humpback Chub]] populations in the Gunnison is limited, with nomenclature confusion in early literature (Bonytail/Roundtail names used interchangeably).

Historical references (Jordan 1891, 1896; Beckman 1952; Miller 1961) and unpublished correspondence from Robert R. Miller indicate fish collections were primarily endemic until approximately 1940. Commercial fisherman Ralph Vernon reported taking 50 squawfish and large numbers of razorback suckers in better years from the Gunnison River between 1930–1950, operating until the early 1950s.

### Anthropogenic Alterations

Major modifications to the river system include:

**Dams and Diversions**: First major dam constructed 1937; first irrigation diversions pre-1900. The [[Gunnison River]] has three main-stem dams (Blue Mesa part of Curecanti Unit) plus tributary dams. The [[Redlands Power Diversion]] (built 1907) spans the entire Gunnison River 1.5 miles above its mouth, considered an effective fish migration barrier except during brief maintenance shutdowns (April 1, November 1). On the Colorado River, three major diversions exist: [[Government Highline Canal]] (roller dam, effective barrier most periods), [[Palisade Irrigation District Diversion]] (effective barrier at all times), and [[Grand Valley Canal]] (deflector dam, barrier only at extremely low flow).

**Exotic Species Introductions**: Stocking records show [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] planted in upper Gunnison for many years; only rainbow trout in recent years (Brown trout fingerlings planted near Delta in 1970). Catfish planted in lower sections 1964–1965. Additional unreported introductions include [[White Sucker]], [[Longnose Sucker]], and [[Fathead Minnow]]. A salvaged backwater isolated from the river for 6 years (post-1970) contained only exotics: 5 largemouth bass, 50 bluegill, 75 carp, 20 bullheads (8–12 inches)—no endemic species survived.

**Flow Regime Alterations**: Releases from Curecanti Unit peak in winter months with low points in early summer, reversing natural flow patterns. The 1976 water year peak flow of approximately 1,850 cfs on the Gunnison was below optimal for backwater formation. Maximum 1976 Colorado River flow nearly equaled 1975 levels but occurred in early June, approximately one month before prime spawning time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Sampling Methodology

The [[Gunnison River]] was divided into five sections (A–E) based on accessibility and habitat characteristics:
- **Section A**: Delta to Escalante crossing (14 miles), wide valley with agriculture/industry
- **Sections B, C, D**: Canyon environment (26 miles total), biologically uniform
- **Section E**: Whitewater bridge to Colorado River confluence

The [[Colorado River]] was divided into two basic sections: DeBeque to Loma (agricultural land, excellent access) and Loma to Moab (mostly canyon, limited access except last 36 miles).

Sampling gear included:
- Graduated mesh gill/trammel nets (125' × 6', bar graduations 1"–2")
- Straight trammel nets (90' × 6', middle bar 3", outside bars 8")
- Hand seines (20' × 4' × ¼" mesh; 50' × 6' × ⅛" mesh)
- Electrofishing gear (2,500-watt generator)

Standard net sets were 24 hours. All fish were identified, enumerated, and subsampled for size range. Periodic samples were individually weighed and measured for age-growth statistics. Electrofishing covered the entire Gunnison and 10 miles of Colorado River at least once, though considered least effective for large-river endangered species.

### Water Quality

Water quality data from key locations (1975) showed wide variation:

**Colorado River sites** (Table 5):
- Flow: 130–10,836 cfs
- TDS: 194–1,680 ppm (mean 495–742 ppm)
- Temperature: 48–60°F mean
- pH: 7.91–8.06

**Grand Valley irrigation return drains** (Table 6):
- Flow: 0.7–190 cfs
- TDS: 641–5,064 ppm (mean 2,042–3,122 ppm)
- Temperature: 55–59°F
- pH: 7.52–7.89

Despite high TDS in return drains, fish survived in these areas. [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] successfully reproduce at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] where TDS is approximately 820 ppm. Study area waters are generally below this value during late spring–early summer spawning periods. The report concludes flows and fluctuating water chemistry within observed ranges are not limiting factors for endemic species.

### Macroinvertebrate Communities

Macroinvertebrate sampling used Surber square-foot bottom samplers in riffle areas (3–10 inches depth), collecting approximately 9 square feet per sample. Dominant groups (Table 9):

**Gunnison River**:
- Delta area (spring): Diptera (abundant), Damselflies (common), Stoneflies (common), Caddisflies (rare)
- Below Redlands Power Dam: All four groups common to abundant across seasons

**Colorado River**:
- Connected Lakes area: Diptera (abundant to common), Damselflies (common), Stoneflies (rare to absent), Caddisflies (common to rare)
- [[Walker Wildlife Area]]: Diptera and Damselflies (abundant to common), Caddisflies (common to rare)

Agricultural drains supported Diptera, aquatic earthworms, Planaria, and freshwater shrimp. Earthworms from irrigation runoff are considered a major food source, particularly early summer to late fall. Upper stretches of drains serve as nursery areas for most species. Only 2 native fish species were collected in drains; the balance were introduced species tolerant of high TDS.

### Backwater Habitat

Backwater areas emerged as critical habitat for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Physical characteristics of key areas (Table 4):
- Palisade Labor Camp: 150 feet × 2,000 feet
- [[Walker Wildlife Area]]: 100+ acres
- Clifton backwater: 200 feet × 2,600 feet
- Black Rocks area (Humpback Chub): 150 feet wide, 30–50 feet deep

Permanent connection to the main river appeared critical for utilization by endemic squawfish and razorback suckers. [[Walker Wildlife Area]] was the only location consistently supporting significant populations of both species since first investigated in 1973.

### Tagging Studies

Tagging data (1974–1976) indicated limited movement when habitat requirements are met locally. Of 70 [[Razorback Sucker]] tagged with numbered spaghetti tags at [[Walker Wildlife Area]], only 1 of 19 recaptures moved out of the area (recaptured at Clifton backwater, 1975). Tagged squawfish showed similar limited movement, though insufficient numbers were tagged for conclusive results. Detailed tag numbers and locations are provided in Appendix B.

## Key Findings

### Species-Specific Status

**Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):
- Small, apparently self-maintaining population scattered throughout Colorado River portion of study area
- Readily captured in Colorado section in 1975 but almost nonexistent in 1976 catches
- Four specimens taken in Utah section (1976) with limited sampling
- **Zero captured in Gunnison River** despite intensive, directed effort
- Historically fairly common in Delta area of Gunnison River until late 1950s
- Self-maintaining populations persist above fish barriers (e.g., Plateau Creek population isolated since ~1908; Gunnison River population above Redlands Power Dam documented until at least 1971)
- Migration barriers not considered primary factor in decline

**Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*):
- Life history parallels Colorado Squawfish; similar decline period
- Presently more common than squawfish in study area
- One specimen taken from Gunnison River near Delta in 1975; observed in lower reaches near Grand Junction
- Migration barriers do not appear to pose major threat
- **Direct competition from introduced sucker species** may be significant: readily hybridizes with introduced [[White Sucker]] and [[Longnose Sucker]], both genus *Catostomus*, as well as native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]
- Curecanti Project altered downstream conditions favoring introduced species
- Competition for spawning beds and increased hybridization considered significant decline factors

**Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*):
- "Perhaps one of the rarest fish in Colorado"
- Found in only one Colorado location: Black Rocks area of Colorado River (~13 miles upstream from Colorado/Utah state line)
- Very little known about this species
- Immediate conservation action recommended

**Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*):
- No specimens taken recently in Colorado or Utah by author
- Holden (1975) found them very rare in Green River–Yampa River confluence area
- Not considered historic Gunnison River inhabitant

### Population Trends and Distribution

**Gunnison River** (Table 1):
Most common large species in decreasing order: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]]. No federally classified endangered species captured. With exception of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker, native species are "faring quite well." Exotic species abundant to common in all sections:
- Smaller species (dace, shiners, minnows) most numerous introduced species
- Game fish (trout) found only in uppermost section near Delta
- Catfish common only in lowermost section near Grand Junction

**Colorado River** (Table 3):
Comparison of relative abundance across river sections (Rifle to Moab) shows endemic species rare to occasional, while introduced species common to abundant throughout. The river from DeBeque, Colorado to Westwater, Utah "seems to still have self maintaining population of squawfish, Humpback chub, and Razorback suckers," though the situation is described as "tenuous" and "could be reversed almost over night."

### 1976 Sampling Anomaly

Fish did not return to many backwater areas in 1976 where major concentrations were recorded in 1975. **Exception: Walker Wildlife Area**. Low water conditions in 1976 restricted fish from utilizing backwaters readily occupied in higher water years. Intensive netting effort failed to yield razorbacks or squawfish except at Walker Wildlife Area. Low early summer flows did not enlarge backwater areas to sizes comparable to areas where fish are found in the Colorado portion of study area.

### Limiting Factors Analysis

**Migration Barriers**: Theory that damming and impoundment eliminated spawning migrations is not entirely supported by existing data. Self-maintaining populations persist above fish barriers for several decades without upstream migration benefit:
- Plateau Creek population isolated between two Colorado River barriers since ~1908
- Gunnison River population above Redlands Power Dam (built ~1900) documented for at least 70 years

**Flow Regime Alteration**: Curecanti Unit releases peak in winter, low in early summer—reversing natural patterns. Most significant during normal or below-average water years. 1976 Gunnison peak flow (~1,850 cfs) was below optimal for backwater formation. 1976 Colorado River peak occurred early June, approximately one month before prime spawning time. Alteration of flow regimes "may be a limiting or causative factor decline of these species."

**Exotic Species Competition**: Demonstrated by salvaged backwater (isolated 6 years post-1970): only exotics survived (largemouth bass, bluegill, carp, bullheads)—no endemic species. For razorback suckers specifically, competition for spawning beds and hybridization with introduced *Catostomus* species (White Sucker, Longnose Sucker) plus native Flannelmouth and Bluehead suckers "may be a significant factor influencing the decline."
