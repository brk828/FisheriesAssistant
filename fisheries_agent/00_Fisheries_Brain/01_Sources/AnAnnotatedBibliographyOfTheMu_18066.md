# An Annotated Bibliography of the Muskellunge, *Esox masquinongy*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This Royal Ontario Museum (Life Sciences Miscellaneous Publications, 1978) bibliography by [[E. J. Crossman]] and [[Cheryl D. Goodchild]] compiles and annotates the literature on the [[Muskellunge]], the largest member of the family Esocidae and a fish endemic to North American fresh waters. The compilation draws on a file built over 24 years at the ROM, supplemented by biologists and angler contributions, and searches closed in June 1976. It is a source book rather than an evaluation: the authors state that many works were not examined directly, that some citations are incomplete, and that the list is not claimed to be complete. The body contains well over 1,000 numbered entries, arranged alphabetically by author, followed by a 28-category subject index.

## Background & Description

- **Size and records:** The muskellunge is described as the largest esocid. The accepted angler record is 69 lb 15 oz (31.7 kg), 64.5 in (163.8 cm), caught in September 1957 by Arthur Lawton in the [[St. Lawrence River]]. Unauthenticated catches above 100 lb (45 kg) are reported from the late 1800s and early 1900s.
- **Maturity:** Sexual maturity takes three to five years, which is why it is often the only sport fish protected by local minimum-length regulations.
- **Angling significance:** Angling is considered among the most avidly pursued North American freshwater sport fisheries, after salmon and trout. A legal fish can require up to 100 man-hours of angling. Dedicated anglers form clubs that support hatchery programmes and record keeping.
- **Nomenclature:** Literature before 1900 is confusing. Authors used names such as *E. estor*, *E. nobilior*, *E. nobilis*, *E. lucioides*, *E. ohioensis*, *E. immaculatus* and *E. amentus*, often interchangeably with [[Northern Pike]]. Weed (1927) listed 94 common names, including at least 40 spellings, and the most accepted name is "muskellunge" (maskinongé is still a statutory name in Ontario and Quebec).
- **Subspecies:** Historical "forms" (*E. m. masquinongy*, *E. m. ohioensis* / Chautauqua muskellunge, *E. m. immaculatus* / northern muskellunge, and the "tiger" *E. amentus*) are discussed extensively, with many authors doubting their subspecific status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** The recent native range runs from the St. Lawrence River near Quebec City, through western Vermont, west of the Appalachians, to the Tennessee River system, around the southern shores of lakes Erie, Huron and Michigan, into Iowa, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Manitoba, northwestern and northern Ontario, and around Lake Superior. Introductions have occurred in impoundments to the east and west, with many failures.
- **Sympatry with pike:** Where both occur, natural hybrids are known and muskellunge populations are often less successful.
- **Historical exploitation:** Commercial fishing by net and hook-and-line was common until the early 1900s. The species is now a sport fish across its range. In Ontario, commercial sale was prohibited from 1898.
- **Habitat:** Literature describes weedy, shallow areas and submerged vegetation (e.g., Lake Saint-Louis, Quebec), clear, slightly acidic rocky streams (Tennessee), and large, well-vegetated rivers. Water quality, mercury and cadmium levels, and pollution (including surface-mined coal runoff in Tennessee) are recurring concerns.
- **Predator-prey role:** Studies consider muskellunge as predatory control on other fishes, with work on perch and bass reductions in small lakes (e.g., [[Gammon and Hasler]] 1965). Diet studies report yellow perch and common suckers as prominent prey.
- **Hatchery and stocking:** Culture programmes (Chautauqua Lake, Woodruff, Deer Lake, Lachine, Wolf Lake) and stocking programmes are extensively documented, including survival studies showing high early mortality of stocked fingerlings.

## Key Findings

The bibliography is organised into the following features rather than empirical conclusions:

- **Scale of literature:** Entries span the 1815 Clinton citation to 1977 and include scientific papers, Dingell-Johnson project reports, hatchery notes, angling magazine articles and popular books. Around 1,000 or more numbered entries are included, many annotated.
- **Subject index (28 categories):** Accounts by Geographical and Political Areas; Age and Growth; Anatomy; Angling and Record Catches; Behaviour; Commercial Fishing; Creel Census; Culture and Artificial Propagation; Dingell-Johnson Project Reports; Distribution and Range; Food and Feeding Habits; Fossils; General Accounts; Habitat and Environmental Factors; Hybrids; Introduction and Stocking; Length-Weight Relationships; Life History and Habits; Management; Marking and Tagging; Parasites, Diseases, Pathology; Physiology; Popular Accounts; Population Studies; Predator-Prey Relationships; Proteins, Enzymes, Electrophoresis; Spawning (natural); Taxonomy, Nomenclature, Systematics.
- **Key quantitative records cited:**
  - Eight-pound muskellunge containing about 30,000 eggs (Anon., Fishes of Ohio).
  - Length-weight data for 264 fish from Lake St. Clair (Krumholz 1949; 30–54 in, 6.5–37 lb).
  - Age and growth of 576 muskellunge (Devitt 1958, Ontario).
  - Nogies Creek population estimate of 769–1,122 fish in July 1953 with about 70% annual mortality after age III (Crossman 1956).
  - Chautauqua Lake creel census: 80 muskellunge caught in five days, 0.05 per angler per day (Moore and staff 1938).
  - Tennessee streams: 169 miles of habitat; growth about 5.8 in per year; males mature near 22 in and 3 years (Parsons 1959).
  - Lake Simcoe, Ontario and Wisconsin stocking survival ranging from 25% to 95% (Johnson 1974a).
- **Key methodological and physiological findings:**
  - Karyotype of 2n = 50 acrocentric chromosomes across Esocidae (Davisson 1972), though McGregor (1970) reports 2n = 50 with terminal centromeres in muskellunge.
  - Stress from capture by seining was greatest, with fin-clipping and transport showing little additional effect (Miles et al. 1974).
  - Holding fish in a lake for 48 hours before release did not reduce stress indicators (Miles et al.; Johnson 1972a).
  - Cleithral and scale methods are validated for age determination (Johnson 1971a; Casselman 1975).
- **Hybridization:** Muskellunge hybridize readily with northern pike (the "tiger muskellunge") and with other esocids. Crossman and Buss (1965) review these crosses.
- **Parasitology:** Muskellunge hosts include the monogenean *Tetraonchus loftusi* n. sp. (Dechtiar 1972c) and the trematode *Cestrahelmins laruei* n. g., n. sp. (Fischthal 1957). Choquette (1951) found 88% of St. Lawrence muskellunge infected with helminths.
- **Conservation status:** Crossman (1969) lists the muskellunge as "endangered" in the Canadian fauna. Riddle (MS 1975) reports pollution from surface-mined coal as causing decline of the Cumberland Plateau population.
- **Authors' caveats:** Annotations are brief, some works were not seen, and entries are included even when incomplete. Angling periodicals were often not annotated.

Overall, the bibliography serves as a comprehensive reference for researchers and anglers seeking [[Muskellunge Biology]], management, culture, and historical literature across North America.
