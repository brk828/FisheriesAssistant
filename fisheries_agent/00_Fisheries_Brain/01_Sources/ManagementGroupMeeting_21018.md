# Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program: Management, Research, and Habitat Planning Documents

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of program materials from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for endangered fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin. It includes a September 5, 1989 Management Group meeting memorandum, a list of barriers to protecting instream flows, action items from the August 30, 1989 Implementation Committee meeting, a call for fiscal year (FY) 1990 research proposals, and guidance for five recovery elements: non-native fish control and sportfishing, research/monitoring/data management, stocking of native fishes, habitat management, and habitat development and maintenance. The program is directed at recovering the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]] for delisting, and managing the [[Razorback Sucker]] so it does not need listing. Central constraints identified are the scarcity of flow-response data, limited availability of water rights, and inconsistent state instream-flow laws. Approximately $2.4 million in FY 1990 program funds was expected to become available in October 1989.

## Background & Description

**Program structure.** The Recovery Implementation Program identified five recovery elements: (1) habitat management; (2) habitat development; (3) stocking of native fishes; (4) non-native fish control and sportfishing; and (5) research, monitoring, and data management. The parties include three Federal agencies and three States.

**Target species.** The program's objective is to recover and delist the Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, and bonytail chub, and to manage the razorback sucker so it will not need listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Recovery goals are described in species-specific recovery plans, with a recovery period of about 15 years.

**Management Group memorandum (September 5, 1989).** John Hamill, Program Director, scheduled a Management Group meeting for September 13, 1989, in Lakewood, Colorado. Agenda items included Recovery Program updates, follow-up on the August 30 Implementation Committee meeting, [[Water Rights Acquisition]] (including [[Ruedi Reservoir]] releases and the [[Cross Mountain Ranch]] acquisition), and the next meeting.

**FY 1990 research solicitation.** Jim Bennett, Chairman of the Technical Group, requested proposals from researchers, managers, and private individuals, using prescribed forms, by May 1, 1989. Proposals outside the guidance documents were allowed and would be assessed case by case. The Technical Group would prioritize ongoing and new projects in June. Submission did not guarantee selection, and selected work would still be subject to State and Federal procurement rules.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow needs and uncertainty.** Quantifying flow needs has been difficult because of scarce data on the endangered fishes and logistical problems on large, turbid, and inaccessible rivers. Specific flow needs had been identified only for a few river reaches during a few months of the year, and those determinations were partly based on professional judgment that water development interests had not readily accepted. The [[Recovery Program]] notes that correlating fish populations with instream flow rates and volumes remains uncertain, so research is intended to be integrated continually into flow decisions. The program uses the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) and [[Physical Habitat Simulation]] (PHABSIM) as analytical tools.

**Flow-temperature effects on non-native fishes.** During higher-than-average flow years, such as 1983 and 1984 in the Yampa River, reproductive success of native species exceeded that of non-native species. The program proposed assembling existing data to test whether flows and temperatures could be managed to favor the endangered fishes.

**Competition and predation.** Predation and competition were hypothesized to be significant limiting factors. Studies were needed to determine which species and life stages are competitors or predators, which variables influence interactions, and how interactions could be manipulated. Physical and chemical habitat alone was considered unlikely to produce recovery without biological habitat considerations.

**Stocking and propagation.** Hatchery-reared fish are to be used for research and augmentation, not indiscriminately. Key concerns include disease, genetic variability, and survival of hatchery-reared fish after release. Grow-out ponds, such as gravel pits, were suggested for rearing. Observations indicated that Colorado squawfish grow rapidly when competition is absent and forage is adequate. Bonytail were reintroduced into the Green River system. An Asian tapeworm, which may occur in hatchery and wild stocks, was identified as a management concern.

**Sediment and channel change.** Sediment transport and changes in channel geomorphology were recognized as needing closer study, to be addressed in the five-year road map and FY 1991 budget.

**Section 7 consultation.** Depletion impacts and reservoir operations are governed by Section 7 consultation. The Sandstone project illustrated disputes over conservation measures beyond depletion fees and over the Service's definition of "sufficient progress," including water-for-water offsets. The Muddy Creek draft biological opinion was considered acceptable, while most Recovery Implementation Committee members found the Sandstone draft unacceptable.

## Key Findings

### Barriers to Protecting Instream Flows (September 5, 1989)

- **Flow-quantity uncertainty** may delay appropriation or acquisition of water rights for many years. Progress depends on the Service quantifying flow needs in a timely manner and on acceptance of its recommendations. Many more years of research were expected to be needed.
- **Colorado:** The Colorado Water Conservation Board (CWCB) had discussed delivering water withheld from Ruedi Reservoir to occupied habitat below the Grand Valley Irrigation Diversion Dam for about two years, with a final plan expected within months. The CWCB was also concerned about appropriating instream rights before flow needs were defined for all Upper Basin rivers. Federal enforcement of a State instream right is limited if the State does not defend or administer it, which is especially problematic for new CWCB appropriations. An original instream right in the 15-mile reach faced opposition from upstream interests and could take six years or longer, possibly requiring a State Supreme Court decision.
- **Water availability:** Acquisition is limited to willing sellers, with emphasis on a few "controlling" rights. On the Yampa River, the Juniper and Cross Mountain rights were identified as principal rights to acquire. No formal process existed beyond word of mouth to find other available rights.
- **Wyoming:** Instream flows cannot be protected within one mile of the State border, which may prevent delivery of Wyoming water to occupied habitat in Colorado and Utah.
- **Utah:** Instream appropriations require State legislative approval, and it was unclear whether they could be made for anything other than "sport" fish.

### Action Items from the August 30, 1989 Implementation Committee Meeting

- Establish technical committees of biological and hydrological researchers to advise the Technical Group on research priorities.
- Prepare five-year "road maps" for each program element, to guide the FY 1991 budget and researchers and be updated yearly.
- Prepare summary reports on each FY 1989 and subsequent project, showing expenditures and results, to improve accountability.
- Review the FY 1990 work plan to increase water-acquisition activities.
- Write to Utah and Wyoming to request meetings on stocking of cool- and warm-water exotic species.
- Involve the Bureau of Reclamation (Missouri Basin) in discussions on the Green Mountain Reservoir and Orchard Mesa check operations affecting 15-mile reach flows.
- Use a consistent, scientifically defensible, basin-wide methodology for instream flow recommendations, with some flexibility.

### Funding Decisions

- The Bureau of Reclamation's proposal to add $20K to Vernal's FY 1990 budget was approved.
- CWCB's $312.4K toward a Colorado hatchery feasibility study will be credited to Colorado if the RIC deems the end product useful.
- CWCB's $100K for the FY 1990 Yampa Basin Reservoir study will be credited as $60K, at $20K per year over three years, with no credit if the project is built.
- Everyone except Utah had FY 1990 funding shares fairly well assured.
- Section 7 funds not earmarked for specific purposes will go to water acquisition, with exceptions authorized by the Management Group.
- The RIC supported The Nature Conservancy's proposal to appraise Cross Mountain Ranch water rights on the Yampa River.
- Approximately $1 million was available for purchasing water rights when they became available.

### Non-Native Control and Sportfishing (Element 4)

- Research to determine whether competition with non-native fishes affects endangered fish abundance and distribution, and whether control is warranted (high priority).
- Evaluation and testing of flow and temperature effects on exotic fishes.
- A Colorado squawfish experimental sportfishery based on hatchery-reared fish, already being implemented at Kenney Reservoir on the White River near Rangely, Colorado, as a substitute for species such as bass and pike.
- Mapping sources of exotic fishes in Utah and Colorado.
- Regulatory actions: Colorado restricted non-native stocking in the Upper Basin, and similar measures were recommended for Utah. Utah and Colorado initiated a creel survey in FY 1989 to determine the extent of incidental take.
- Enforcement and public education, including a five-year plan for an information and education effort.
- Removal of exotic fishes from grow-out ponds, backwaters, and nursery areas, and an issue paper on management options (high priority).

### Research, Monitoring, and Data Management (Element 5)

**FY 1990 research priorities:**
1. Complete research identified in the Gila taxonomy work plan (due February 16, 1990).
2. Evaluate the effect of tagging and handling on mortality of rare fishes.
3. Assess inter- and intraspecific interactions among Colorado River fishes.

**FY 1990 monitoring priorities:**
1. Review techniques for establishing quantifiable, scientifically based recovery goals.
2. Develop quantitative recovery goals for the rare Colorado fishes.
3. Maintain and update the interagency standardized monitoring program, ensuring statistically valid trend detection.
4. Use video image interpretation to track channel morphology and habitat conditions.

Data management aims to improve efficiency, coordination among parties, and long-term preservation of program information.

### Stocking Native Fishes (Element 3)

- **Goal:** Enhance recovery of the four rare fishes by providing research fish, disease-free and genetically healthy fish for augmentation toward self-sustaining populations, and refugia.
- **Objectives:** Test artificial rearing in backwaters, study hatchery-reared fish introduced to the wild, augment populations if successful, develop propagation and genetics plans, establish refugia, and reintroduce bonytail into the upper basin.
- **FY 1990 tasks:** Food-habits studies of larval fish in hatchery ponds, grow-out ponds, and backwater nurseries; refugia criteria and site evaluation; marking/tagging methods for augmentation; culture research on loading densities, feeding, and reproduction; Threatened and Endangered Fish Propagation Plan tasks; a genetics management plan; and razorback sucker research at the Ouray National Wildlife Refuge facility.

### Habitat Management (Element 1)

- **Objective 1:** Identify and quantify habitat needs and flow levels for all life stages of the four species.
- **Objective 2:** Legally protect instream flows under State water law, including acquisition of stored water and willing-seller water rights.
- **Flow priorities:** Yampa River first; White River and 15-mile reach second. Preliminary flow recommendations for the Yampa and 15-mile reach were scheduled for completion by FY 1990.
- **Reservoir operations:** Flaming Gorge's biological opinion was scheduled to conclude in June 1990. Aspinall Unit studies on the mainstem Colorado River were to begin in FY 1990 and continue through FY 1992, with emphasis on humpback chub year-round flow needs in the Black Rocks Region and Westwater Canyon, and on flows that enhance young Colorado squawfish growth and survival.
- **Stored water:** 10,000 acre-feet from Ruedi Reservoir was earmarked to supplement deficient habitat.
- **San Juan River:** Studies were predicated on a 1989 summary report, a forthcoming management plan, and New Mexico's willingness to participate.

### Habitat Development and Maintenance (Element 2)

- **Categories:** Backwater development (connecting gravel pits or constructing habitat), spawning habitat improvement, habitat created by structures, and fish passage facilities.
- **Status:** Habitat development had not been practiced for rare fishes in the Upper Basin, though it had been applied successfully for sport fish. The Service planned to complete an assessment of fish passage feasibility in FY 1989, building on a 1986 report on the Redlands Diversion.
- **FY 1990 priority tasks:** A literature review on habitat development (priority 1); non-flow habitat alternatives for adult squawfish and razorback sucker in the 15-mile reach (2); life-stage limitations for young fish in the 15-mile reach (3); backwater opportunities that avoid harboring exotic fishes, with a demonstration project (4); spawning habitat expansion with a demonstration project (5); and evaluation of grow-out ponds as rearing sites (6).
