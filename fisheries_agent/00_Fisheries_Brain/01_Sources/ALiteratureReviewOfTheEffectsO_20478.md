# A Literature Review of the Effects of Turbidity and Siltation on Aquatic Life

#aquatic-ecology #water-quality #sediment-pollution #turbidity #siltation #chesapeake-bay

## Executive Summary

This 1964 staff report from the [[Department of Chesapeake Bay Affairs]] comprehensively reviews scientific literature on the detrimental effects of turbidity and siltation on aquatic ecosystems. Turbidity is defined as light restriction from suspended inorganic particulate matter (excluding organic matter, stains, and plankton), while siltation refers to deposition of inorganic matter and trapped organic material. The review synthesizes findings across multiple taxa including fish, benthic invertebrates, phytoplankton, aquatic plants, and shellfish, documenting impacts on reproduction, survival, species composition, and ecosystem productivity. Critical turbidity thresholds are identified for various species, with particular emphasis on spawning failures and habitat degradation in [[Chesapeake Bay]], [[Potomac River]], [[Lake Erie]], and other North American water bodies.

## Background & Description

### Physical Properties of Sediment

Sediment particles are classified by size following Twenhofel (1961):
- **Clay particles**: <1/256mm (0.00015 inches)
- **Silt**: 1/256 - 1/16mm (0.00015 - 0.0025 inches)
- **Very Fine Sand**: 1/16 - 1/8mm
- **Fine Sand**: 1/8 - 1/4mm
- **Medium Sand**: 1/4 - 1/2mm
- **Coarse Sand**: 1/2 - 1mm
- **Very Coarse Sand**: 1 - 2mm
- **Granule**: 2 - 4mm
- **Pebble**: 4 - 64mm
- **Cobble**: 64 - 256mm
- **Boulder**: >256mm

Clay and silt particles are of particular concern due to their small size permitting suspension and transport by tidal currents, enabling wide dispersal depending on currents, silt load, and precipitation rate.

### Transport and Resuspension

Current velocity of 0.08 m/sec (0.18 mph) resuspends precipitated brick clay, while 0.32 m/sec (0.72 mph) moves fine loam and mud (Twenhofel 1961). Bartsch (1959) documented turbidity of 5,000 ppm at a [[Potomac River]] tributary mouth, decreasing to 550 ppm at 0.4 miles downstream and 82 ppm at 2.3 miles. Strong winds can elevate [[Lake Erie]] turbidity from average 0 ppm to over 200 ppm (Langlois 1941).

### Sedimentation Rates

Between 1843 and 1938, [[Kent County]] lost 1,101 acres to erosion with net loss of 970 acres after deposition (Singewald 1945). The head of [[Chesapeake Bay]] experienced average depth reduction of 2.5 feet over 32 square miles (85 million cubic yards of sediment) between 1846 and 1938 (Gottschalk 1945). The [[Potomac River]] transports 40 million cubic feet (1.48 million cubic yards) of silt annually (Wolman 1958). The [[Mississippi River]] carries 500 million tons of silt annually (Galtsoff 1954), with Gulf of Mexico sedimentation potentially reaching one billion tons per year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Natural Turbidity Levels

Near [[Chesapeake Biological Laboratory]] at Solomons, Maryland, Secchi disc readings ranged from 97cm (3.2 feet) in June to 258cm (8.5 feet) in November-December (Nash 1947). A 1936 [[Potomac River]] flood maintained turbidity at 6,000 ppm for 15 days (Kemp 1949). Streams with watershed forest removal reached maximum turbidity of 56,000 ppm versus 15 ppm in control areas (Eschner 1963). Oklahoma streams with colloidal clay reach 30,000 ppm (Cross 1950). Concentrations of 3,000-4,000 ppm seldom occur naturally except during heavy floods and channel dredging (Loosanoff and Tommers 1948).

### Interaction with Organic Matter

Siltation traps organic matter on the bottom, creating oxygen demand resulting in gas ebullition and anaerobic conditions (Ellis 1940, Bartsch 1948). Turbidity delays self-purification and allows distant transport of organic wastes (Tarzwell 1953). Silt flocculates planktonic algae, carrying it to the bottom to die (Jackson 1963). Particulate matter absorption increases bacterial settling rate (Weiss 1951). Silt absorbs and precipitates oil, with bottom sediment remaining a pollution source upon agitation (Galtsoff 1936).

### Bottom Organism Habitat Quality

Tarzwell (1937) rated stream bottoms by organism population density: sand - 1; silt - 10.5; rubble - 30; coarse gravel - 32; plant beds - 52. Unstable silts and shifting sands form a false bottom that typically will not support benthic animals.

## Key Findings

### Effects on Fish Production

Buck (1956) investigated 39 farm ponds stocked with [[Largemouth Bass]] and sunfish. After two growing seasons, fish production was:
- **Clear ponds** (turbidity <25 ppm): 161.5 lbs/acre
- **Intermediate ponds** (25-100 ppm): 94 lbs/acre
- **Muddy ponds** (100-185 ppm): 29.3 lbs/acre

Average total fish weight was 5.5 times greater in clear versus muddy ponds due to faster growth and better reproduction. [[Largemouth Bass]] in clear ponds increased 6.9 inches in length and 14 times in weight over two growing seasons, while bass from muddy ponds increased only 2.4 inches and 2.5 times in weight.

Fishes in [[Whitewood Creek]], South Dakota, were completely eliminated where mine wastes raised residual turbidity above 720 APHA units (Ellis 1948). Silt from gravel washings eliminated trout food and cover in [[Wynoochee River]], Washington, even at low turbidities of 91-102 ppm (Wagner 1959). Siltation destroyed the whitefish population of [[Detroit River]] and [[Maumee Bay]] (Troutman 1957).

### Critical Thresholds for Fish Reproduction

**Spawning Success**: Buck (1956) determined critical turbidity levels for [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Redear Sunfish]], and [[Bluegill]] appeared between 75-100 ppm, with 100 ppm as the approximate level above which spawning was severely restricted or non-existent. Young-of-year [[Largemouth Bass]] were found in 7 of 12 clear ponds, 4 of 12 intermediate ponds, and 0 of 9 muddy ponds. The most turbid pond producing young bass averaged 83 ppm turbidity.

**Egg Survival**: Stuart (1953) conducted laboratory experiments with [[Brown Trout]] (loch trout) ova exposed to natural stream sediments. All early development ova in turbid water died without hatching. The chorion attracted suspended silt particles, becoming covered with tenacious dark sediment coating. Only late development ova subjected to silt for ≤48 hours hatched into healthy larvae.

Cambell (1954) found 211 trout eggs below a gold dredging operation (turbidity 1,000-2,500 ppm) died within 6 days, while eggs in clear tributary suffered only 6% mortality after 20 days. Shapovalov and Berrian (1940) showed 4% survival of [[Silver Salmon]] eggs subjected to silt versus 50% in controls. Shaw and Maga (1943) received 4.16% fry yield from trout and salmon eggs in silt-laden water versus 86.2% in normal hatchery water.

### Effects on Fish Larvae

Stuart (1953) demonstrated high mortalities in [[Brown Trout]] larvae when >1mm depth of natural stream silt was present in holding trays. Inhalation of silt particles resulted in gill membrane inflammation and death. With <1mm silt, larvae succeeded in keeping gills clean by dislodging particles with mucus and expelling material from gill openings. Continuous additions of fresh silt could not be tolerated and larvae died from gill inflammation. Older larvae were slightly more resistant.

### Direct Effects on Fingerling and Adult Fish

Rainbow trout subjected to 30, 90, and 270 mg/l of china clay and diatomaceous earth for 5 months showed no adverse effects at 30 ppm, some mortality at 90 ppm, and >50% mortality at 270 ppm (Anon. 1961). Examination revealed gill epithelial cell thickening and fusion of adjacent lamellae. At 270 ppm diatomaceous earth, median survival period was 11 days, with death from gill irritation (Klein 1962).

Pautzke (1937) found steelhead trout placed in stream polluted with coal washings died within 2.5 hours. Dead fish were heavily coated with mucus and coal/slate particles. Mullet, carp, and [[Fundulus]] exposed to high suspended mud concentrations were killed by mechanical gill injury and exhibited avoidance reactions (Ingle 1955). Wallen (1951) concluded concentrations below 70,000 ppm were not harmful to adult warmwater fishes.

Kemp (1949) considered turbidities of 3,000 ppm dangerous if maintained for 10 days and attributed a large [[Potomac River]] fish kill in 1936 to flood-produced turbidity of 6,000 ppm for 15 days. Ellis (1935) stated silt particles too large to pass through 1,000 mesh per inch screen will cut and injure fish and mollusk gills.

### Species Composition Changes

Troutman (1957) documented that habitat changes primarily from siltation in the Ohio region "considerably modified the fish fauna, changing it from a species complex dominated by fishes requiring clear and/or vegetated waters to one dominated by those species tolerant of much turbidity of water and of bottoms composed of clay silts." The shift was from large fishes of great food value to smaller species unfit as human food or large fishes of inferior quality.

Before siltation, Ohio streams supported pikes, [[Walleye]], catfishes, buffalo fishes, suckers, drums, and sturgeons. Environmental changes in Ohio lakes shifted populations from [[Largemouth Bass]]-[[Bluegill]]-pumpkinseed-yellow bullhead associations to white crappie-brown and black bullhead-channel catfish associations.

[[Whitewood Creek]] in South Dakota was so heavily polluted with rock powder (22% by volume settleable solids) that all fishes were driven out. Fish populations only reappeared when pollution was greatly reduced to 3% by volume (Ellis 1948). [[Powder River]], Oregon, below gold mining operations showed 1,700 ppm turbidity with only rough fishes present, while clear water (5 ppm) above the dredge supported trout and whitefish (Wilson 1956).

Survey records from 1887-1938 of two Ohio streams showed little change in undredged sections, while dredged sections experienced drastic decrease in abundance of species requiring clear water, constant flow, pools, or aquatic vegetation (Troutman 1939). In Middle Harbor, Ohio, silt elimination of fish habitats resulted in disappearance or reduction of 51 fish species. Rotenone sampling showed >90% of remaining population consisted of carp, goldfish, their hybrids, and dwarfed bullheads (Troutman 1957).

### Lake Erie Case Study

Langlois (1941, 1954) attributed decline of [[Cisco]] and whitefish in [[Lake Erie]] to siltation of spawning areas. Spawning grounds were limited to clean, hard bottoms surrounding islands in western [[Lake Erie]]. South shore tributary currents carried silt onto these grounds, resulting in deposits several feet deep. Other species limited by siltation include [[Yellow Perch]], [[Walleye]], and chain pickerel. [[Yellow Perch]] spawn in and over water plants along south shore; dearth of aquatic plants from siltation may be the most important limiting factor. Walleye production failure is attributed to heavy silt loads carried by [[Maumee River]] during spring spawning runs. Less desirable turbid-tolerant species (saugers, sheepshead, catfish, carp) greatly increased in abundance.

Cisco depletion may be due to siltation of eggs laying on bottom approximately 115 days before hatching (Langlois 1954). Butler (1936) reported serious walleye egg losses at Swan Creek hatchery when gale winds stirred up mud and raised water temperatures.

### Spawning Ground Destruction

Siltation in [[Nechako River]], a [[Fraser River]] tributary, destroyed spawning area formerly accommodating 5,000-7,500 salmon (Wickett 1959). Smith (1940) reported salmon spawning in muddy [[Yuba River]], California, was largely restricted to clear streak below a clear tributary. Heavy siltation in [[Wynoochee River]], Washington, covered salmon spawning gravel, while light siltation filled interstitial spaces retarding seepage and oxygen (Ziebell 1960). Siltation destroyed whitefish spawning areas in [[Maumee Bay]] (Troutman 1957). Large areas of spawning grounds in various estuaries have been destroyed or shifted downstream by sedimentation (Mansueti 1961).

### Effects on Fishing Success

Creel census data from Fork Lake, Illinois, showed striking correlation between water transparency and catch rates (Bennett, Thompson, and Parr 1940):
- **0.5-2.0 feet transparency**: 2.04 fish per man-hour
- **2.0-3.5 feet**: 2.86 fish per man-hour
- **3.5-4.5 feet**: 6.53 fish per man-hour

Buck (1956) contrasted turbid Heyburn Reservoir with clear Upper Spavinaw Reservoir, finding the clear reservoir attracted more anglers, yielded more fish per unit effort and more desirable species, and was immeasurably more appealing aesthetically. [[Meramec River]] watershed study in Missouri showed recreational attendance (mostly fishermen) dropped 1/3 when water was muddy. Based on 25 muddy water days per year, this amounted to $49,000 annual revenue loss (Brown 1945). Gunter (1938) noted trawl fishing was very effective in turbid water, presumably because reduced visibility lowered avoidance.

### Effects on Bottom Organisms

At least 27 papers record adverse effects of turbidity and siltation on bottom organisms. Unstable silts and shifting sands form a false bottom that will not usually support benthic animals.

Bartsch (1953) documented effects of sand-glass crushing effluents on [[Potomac River]] bottom organisms. At tributary mouth (5,000 ppm turbidity), bottom fauna were absent. At 0.4 miles downstream, right side showed dearth of bottom fauna while left side was unaffected. Bottom fauna volumes for right side did not equal amounts above confluence until 13.1 miles downstream. Turbidities on right side did not decrease to <25 ppm until 4.9 miles downstream.

Rock powder action is similar to sand sediments, blanketing stream bed and smothering bottom life. Ellis (1945) observed six miles of [[Bear Butte Creek]] rendered unfit for fish and food organisms through mine silting. Ellis (1937) stated clay precipitates cover bottom and exclude life except low oxygen-tolerant fauna.

Jackson (1953) summarized that benthic population reductions may approach 75-80% or greater for distances up to 10-50 miles below silt pollution sources. Wagner (1959) observed gravel removal pollution reduced bottom insects 75-85% where turbidity measured 91-102 ppm.

### Effects on Phytoplankton and Plant Production

Algae is the basis of the aquatic food chain. Silt destroys planktonic algae by absorbing light needed for photosynthesis and by flocculating and precipitating algae cells. Encrusting algae are smothered by silt coating (Cordone and Kelley 1961). Inorganic particle abrasive action may damage algae cells. Aquatic plants essential for egg attachment and cover may be destroyed by silt through light elimination and interference with gas exchange through leaves (Phinney 1959).

Reid (1961) stated major effects of high turbidity in estuaries are: (1) quenching light penetration, thereby inhibiting photosynthesis and plant production, and (2) building up deep zones of mud, silt, other sediments and detritus.

**Light Penetration**: Inorganic particulate matter suspended in water decreases light penetration (Anderson 1956, Reid 1961, Chandler 1942, Tarzwell 1953). Greater silt concentration produces greater light exclusion. In western [[Lake Erie]], Chandler (1942) found turbidities from bottom-derived materials varying from 5-230 ppm. One percent of light penetrated 9.7m at 5 ppm turbidity, 0.8m at 115 ppm. Greatest light loss occurred within first 10cm, varying from 43-94% of surface light. In many estuaries with voluminous tributary flow, light is reduced to 1% of surface radiation at <3m (Reid 1961). By contrast, in open sea certain regions, yellow-green components are not diminished to 1% until nearly 100m depth.
