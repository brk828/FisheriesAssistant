# Temporal and Spatial Patterns in Littoral-Zone Fish Assemblages of Lake Texoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the littoral-zone fish assemblage of [[Lake Texoma]], a 36,000 ha reservoir on the Washita and Red rivers, using [[Electrofishing]] from January through December 1986. The authors asked whether abundance, assemblage structure and species associations in this reservoir resemble those of naturally evolved littoral communities. Total abundance differed significantly among seasons, but only one common species (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) showed significant seasonal movement into or out of the littoral zone. Overall assemblage structure was persistent across seasons and habitats, while within-season and within-habitat structure varied with habitat availability and physicochemical conditions. Persistent species associations were weak, which the authors attribute to [[Water-Level Fluctuation]] in a young, non-coevolved reservoir fauna.

## Background & Description

Most natural aquatic systems support characteristic fish assemblages that have developed over evolutionary time. Littoral-zone studies exist for marine, estuarine and natural lake habitats, but few have covered all seasons in a single system. North American reservoir littoral studies have focused mainly on game and forage species and their management. Reservoirs contain native and introduced species in a non-natural habitat with fluctuating depth and human regulation. Their assemblages have existed only briefly in evolutionary time, which raises the question of whether reservoir assemblages resemble those of natural habitats.

The study area was the near-shore littoral zone of the Red River arm of [[Lake Texoma]], which contains shallow coves, sand beaches and rocky shorelines. [[Lake Texoma]] was constructed in 1944 by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Its watershed is primarily agricultural, Secchi depth typically ranges 50–125 cm, and conductivity is high for freshwater (reported as 750 to 1200 µmhos).

Sampling covered three habitat categories:
- **Wood:** standing dead willow trees on mud or sand.
- **Vegetation:** submerged or flooded grasses and rooted plants, such as button bush and cocklebur, on mud or mud/sand.
- **Open:** open shoreline with clay, sand or cobble-boulder substrate.

Each collection lasted 10 minutes in one habitat type at depths to 2 m along 80–150 m of shoreline. A 220 V electrofisher (Coffelt VVP-15) operated in DC pulse mode, and identifications were made by experienced observers. Schooling species (*Dorosoma* spp. and *Menidia beryllina*) were visually estimated and capped at 200 individuals per collection. Only species occurring in at least 5% of collections and with mean abundance at least 5% of the total were included in analyses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and water level.** Fluctuating water level limits rooted aquatic macrophytes. When the lake falls below normal, vegetation is progressively excluded and open habitat increases. When it rises, emergent terrestrial plants become submerged and habitat heterogeneity increases. [[Wood Habitat]] in deeper water is less affected by level changes, and this is reflected in its persistent assemblage structure.

**Temperature and refuge.** Open shallow-water habitats regularly exceed 30 °C in summer and may be unfavorable to many species. In summer 1986, fish densities were highest in and near shaded creek channels, which likely served as thermal refuges.

**Reservoir context.** The authors describe reservoirs as hybrids of lotic and lentic systems. No "reservoir-adapted" species exists because of the short life expectancy of reservoirs, so coevolved-type associations would not be expected. Comparable studies in natural lakes and marine surf zones show seasonal and spatial patterns that were largely absent here.

**Between-year stability.** Using data from [[Harper and Namminga]] (cove rotenone collections, 1976–1982), the authors calculated [[Schoener's Index]] (0.58–0.83, mean 0.72) and [[Morisita's Index]] (0.69–0.97, mean 0.87) for consecutive years. These values indicate at least moderate between-year stability in the shallow-water fish community.

## Key Findings

### Abundance across seasons
- Total littoral abundance differed significantly among seasons (Kruskal-Wallis H = 33.716, p < 0.001, all species; H = 15.794, p < 0.01, with *M. beryllina* and *Dorosoma* spp. omitted).
- Mean total abundance per collection was highest in spring (2466.7) and lowest in winter (47.2). Annual mean was 640.5 (autumn 107.6, summer 139.7).
- Of twelve common taxa, only *Dorosoma cepedianum* differed significantly in littoral density across seasons (H = 26.646, p < 0.05).
- Most common species remained in the littoral zone in winter and summer, showing no marked seasonal movement.

### Assemblage structure
- Species ranks were concordant across seasons (pooled among habitats) and across habitats (pooled among seasons). The authors therefore consider the whole-year littoral assemblage persistent across seasons and habitats. This may reflect numerical dominance by *D. cepedianum*, *D. petenense* and *M. beryllina*.
- Within vegetation habitats, rank abundance was not concordant across seasons.
- Within wood habitats, rank abundance was concordant across seasons, although *M. beryllina* dropped from among the four most abundant species to the fourth most abundant in autumn.
- Within open habitats, rank abundance was not concordant across seasons.
- Within seasons, ranks were not concordant among habitats in winter or autumn, but were concordant in spring and summer.

### Statistical methods
- Kendall's W was computed for the ten most abundant species. Table 4 values are partly garbled in the scan; the reading consistent with the text is W ≈ 0.62 (chi-square 22.32, df = 9, p < 0.01) for pooled seasons.
- Because summer open-habitat collections were omitted and Kendall's W needs at least three rankings, Spearman's rank correlation was used to compare summer wood and vegetation habitats.
- The authors found that using 15 species instead of 10 increased the chance of significant concordance, because the larger set included many rare taxa. Ten species were retained to focus on likely ecological dominants.

### Species associations
- Principal coordinates analysis explained 59% (winter) to 72% (summer) of variance on the first two axes. Associations were partially segregated but strongly blurred.
- Across the year, axis 1 separated small fishes, juveniles and schooling taxa (mostly open-water) from large centrarchids and benthic fishes (mostly vegetation).
- Seasonal patterns differed. In winter, associations followed habitat and trophic group. In spring, they followed age class and size. In summer and autumn, they followed habitat and age or size class.

### Ecological relationships
- *Carpiodes carpio*, *Ictiobus bubalus* and *Cyprinus carpio* grouped together in winter and summer, and *I. bubalus* and *C. carpio* stayed close in autumn. These two species occurred together throughout the year.
- Adult and juvenile *Lepomis* were persistently segregated across seasons, whatever their habitat use.
- *Micropterus salmoides* size classes were not consistently associated with prey taxa across seasons. Only in autumn was a close proximity to *M. beryllina* consistent with that species' frequency in the predator's diet.
- Interspecific segregation of predators (*M. salmoides*, *M. punctulatus* and *Morone saxatilis*) was consistent with differences in primary habitat use in autumn.

### Conclusions
- Fluctuating water level, which controls vegetation availability and open-habitat extent, appears to deter persistent, well-structured species associations in this reservoir.
- Intraspecific and interspecific habitat use by size and age classes was consistent with patterns in natural stream fish communities.
- The authors call for further comparative and whole-assemblage studies in reservoirs, with attention to [[Non-Native Species]] and to whether theories from natural systems apply to reservoirs.
