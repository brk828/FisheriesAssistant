# Phase II Flow Recommendations - Yampa River

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #flow-ecology #habitat-management

## Executive Summary

This 1989 memorandum from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service transmits interim streamflow recommendations for the [[Yampa River]] to protect four rare and endangered fish species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The recommendations convert biological findings from the study "Habitat Use and Streamflow Needs of Rare and Endangered Fishes, Yampa River, Colorado" into quantified flow requirements by season. The [[Yampa River]] is the last free-flowing river in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] and is considered critical to maintaining viable populations of these endangered species. Flow recommendations are based on maintaining natural hydrologic patterns that support spawning migrations, reproduction, larval transport, nursery habitat maintenance, and overwinter survival. The Service emphasizes that these interim recommendations represent the minimum flows needed and that experimentation with reduced flows should not occur in this critical habitat.

## Background & Description

The [[Green River]] basin represents the last remaining stronghold for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in native riverine environments. The [[Yampa River]], as the last free-flowing tributary in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], maintains natural flow patterns critical to these species' life histories. Typical annual hydrology begins with winter baseflows, rising flows from late March through May, peak flows at [[Deerlodge Park]] (typically 7,748 to 20,476 cfs, 75-25% exceedance values) during late May to early June, followed by steady decline through August and return to baseflows by September.

The document describes a three-step process established by the Recovery Implementation Program: (1) evaluation of habitat use and flow needs, (2) quantification of flow requirements, and (3) identification of future studies. This report provides Step 2, quantifying flows based on biological and hydrological data. Critical river reaches are delineated in the document, with [[Colorado Squawfish]] utilizing river miles 0-140 year-round, spawning in miles 4-31 during June-August. [[Humpback Chub]] concentrate in miles 4-40, while [[Razorback Sucker]] spawning occurs in miles 0-4 during April-June.

The environmental baseline condition includes all existing water projects and those with favorable biological opinions: [[Hayden Power Station]] (7.1K acre-feet depletion), [[Craig Power Station]] (19.2K acre-feet), private actions (5.9K acre-feet), [[Stagecoach Reservoir]] (12.8K acre-feet), and [[Cheyenne Stage I and II]] projects on the [[Little Snake River]] (23.8K acre-feet combined), totaling 68.8K acre-feet depletion above the [[Green River]] confluence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Reproductive activities of target fishes are closely associated with flow, temperature, and parameters linked to normal spring runoff conditions. [[Colorado Squawfish]] migrations in 1981-83 and 1988 were associated with highest spring flows (average 4,276-8,516 cfs), increasing temperatures (average exceeding 14°C, range 9.2-19.4°C), and long photoperiod. Spawning generally occurred earlier in low water years and later in high water years, with temperatures ranging 19-24°C during spawning.

[[Razorback Sucker]] spawning in the lower [[Yampa River]] and upper [[Green River]] (1975, 1981, 1988, 1989) occurred with increasing flows and temperatures during highest spring runoff (average 14°C, range 9-17°C). Curtailment of high spring flows in the [[Green River]] due to [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations may be associated with loss of effective recruitment to juvenile stage. Seasonal inundation of low-lying lands provides food base for adults and may be important for larval dispersal and rearing.

[[Humpback Chub]] spawning occurred shortly after highest spring discharge at temperatures around 19.5°C (range 14.5-23°C) in 1987-1989. High spring flows promote development of shoreline eddy habitat and deepening of occupied habitats. The document notes that physical habitat modeling has not demonstrated predictive capability for endangered fish distribution and abundance, attributed to environmental variability, logistical challenges in large turbid rivers, and nonnative fish introductions.

High spring flows mobilize sediments delivered downstream, creating and maintaining nursery habitat for [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the upper [[Green River]]. Native fishes are adapted to high sediment loads, and resultant turbidity presumably reduces efficiency of visually dependent predators like [[Northern Pike]] and [[Walleye]]. High flows also restrict growth of introduced [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix ramosissima*), which competes with native riparian vegetation and alters important habitat through shoreline stabilization.

## Key Findings

**Spring Flow Recommendations (March 21 - June 21):**
- Uninterrupted flow with no stream blockage required from river mouth to Craig, Colorado for spawning migrations
- Maintain environmental baseline flows (50% exceedance values): April 3,139 cfs, May 7,856 cfs, June 6,198 cfs
- Major deviations from current environmental baseline flows will likely eliminate recovery hopes for rare fishes
- Spring peak flows associated with reproduction of all target species during ascending (razorback sucker), peak (Colorado squawfish), and descending (humpback chub, Colorado squawfish) flows
- High flows provide seasonal bottomland habitats, maintain nursery areas, mobilize sediments, limit salt cedar growth, and reduce proliferation of introduced fish species

**Summer Flow Recommendations (June 22 - September 22):**
- Gradual decline from spring peaks needed to maintain favorable habitat conditions
- Maintain present flows June 22-August 1, followed by flows near 50% exceedance value
- August-September flows around 1,800 cfs at Ouray, Utah for nursery habitat maintenance
- Gradual decline prevents siltation of spawning substrate, maintains sediment transport equilibria
- Supports larval transport, downstream drift, and nursery area productivity

**Autumn Flow Recommendations (September 23 - December 21):**
- Stable flows at 50% exceedance value recommended
- Maintain flows favoring nursery habitats until at least November 1
- Higher flows (50% exceedance or more) desirable for adults and juveniles until ice cover
- Reduce frequency of ice breakup, ice jams, and shoreline scouring
- Flows lower than 75% exceedance not recommended (caused poor habitat conditions in 1989)
- Avoid high flows causing ice breakup and jamming

**Winter Flow Recommendations (December 22 - March 20):**
- Stable winter flows near 50% exceedance value (approximately 400 cfs) recommended
- Reduce scouring of shoreline habitats by ice
- Flows at 50% exceedance acceptable for adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] habitat during 1986-88 winter study
- Flows lower than 75% exceedance caused habitat loss and potential winterkill of smaller fish in pools
- Stable conditions reduce stress on overwintering [[Razorback Sucker]]

**Potential Limiting Factors Identified:**
- Spring peak flow alterations
- Overbank flooding loss
- Siltation of spawning substrate
- Competition and predation by nonnative fishes
- Stream blockage
- Low flows in late summer, fall, and winter
- Flow instability during winter
- Lack of substantive [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment to juvenile stage
- Availability of shoreline eddy habitat for [[Humpback Chub]]

**Management Context:**
- Reduction in 1989 year-class of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in upper [[Green River]] suggests additional factors influence recruitment beyond flow conditions
- Decline of [[Colorado Squawfish]] continues in other upper basin rivers (very low numbers of young, failure to detect spawning areas)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment insufficient to sustain species anywhere, but higher numbers and two spawning areas in [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] offer recovery promise
- Experimentation with flows should be conducted elsewhere, not in [[Yampa River]]
- Future development scenarios using off-channel impoundments and non-consumptive water options may be viable but require future studies
- Service will evaluate ongoing consultations and projects against interim recommendations on case-by-case basis
- Comments requested by December 15, 1989, with Service response by January 15, 1990
- Studies for 1991 work plan to be identified immediately, particularly addressing spring flows
