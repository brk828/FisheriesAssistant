# Cryopreservation as a Tool for Genome Preservation in Apache Trout, *Oncorhynchus apache*

## Executive Summary
This research proposal from the Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery (NFH) Complex outlines a conservation genetics initiative for the [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), a threatened salmonid endemic to the White Mountains of east-central Arizona. The species survives as nine relictual, genetically isolated populations. Because the captive broodstock (derived from the East Fork White River population) shows relatively low heterozygosity, the project proposes collecting and cryopreserving milt from wild, pure endemic populations to cross-fertilize with captive-stock eggs. This approach aims to increase genetic diversity in hatchery-reared fish used for restoration stocking, without disturbing wild population structure through live broodstock transfer. The study builds on cryopreservation techniques developed in parallel research at the University of Idaho and began field trials in 1992, continuing into 1993.

## Background & Description
The source document does not provide detailed morphometric or meristic descriptions of *Oncorhynchus apache*. Key biological/reproductive characteristics noted include:
- **Reproductive mode:** Gonochoristic, external fertilization typical of salmonids; male gametes (milt) collected via aspiration during natural spawning.
- **Spawning asynchrony:** Wild population spawning periods (May–June) do not coincide with captive broodstock spawning periods (March–April), necessitating gamete storage rather than direct co-spawning.
- **Genetic status:** Biochemical systematics indicate the East Fork White River population (used for hatchery propagation since 1983–1984) is the most homozygous of the nine remaining pure populations, suggesting reduced allelic diversity relative to other relictual stocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Native to headwater streams in the White Mountains, Arizona; a coldwater lotic (flowing-water) specialist.
- **Adaptation limits:** The species has adapted successfully to reintroduction in lotic environments but shows limited success adapting to lentic (static/reservoir-type) water bodies, a key constraint for restoration planning.
- **Population distribution:** Nine surviving pure, endemic populations occupy isolated headwater tributaries, primarily within the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] and the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest:
  - Boggy Creek
  - Crooked Creek
  - Deep Creek
  - Elk Canyon Creek
  - East Fork White River (type locality)
  - Firebox Creek
  - Flash Creek
  - Loafer Creek
  - Soldier Creek
- **Threats:** Competition with introduced/exotic salmonid species, habitat degradation from land-use practices, and declining population trends across multiple relictual populations.
- **Genetic conservation context:** These nine populations collectively represent the entire remaining specific genome of *O. apache*, making each population a critical unit for the species' [[Genetic Diversity]] and long-term viability.

## Key Findings
- **Historical propagation:** Apache Trout culture began in 1983–1984 at Alchesay-Williams Creek NFH using fertilized eggs from the East Fork White River (type locality); over 2 million fish have since been produced for restoration/enhancement stocking.
- **Genetic bottleneck concern:** The sole broodstock source (East Fork population) is the most homozygous among the nine relictual populations, raising concern about limited adaptive potential in hatchery-propagated fish.
- **Proposed solution — Cryopreservation:** Wild milt would be aspirated from males in the nine source populations, temporarily stored at 4°C under a pure oxygen interface for transport, then cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen for long-term storage using methods adapted from University of Idaho research.
- **Asynchronous fertilization strategy:** Wild milt collected in May–June would be thawed and used to fertilize captive-stock eggs during the March–April captive spawning season the following year, circumventing the mismatch in spawning timing between wild and captive fish.
- **Evaluation metrics:** Success would be measured via fertilization rates, hatch rates, survival, and growth of resulting offspring — a direct test of [[Cryopreservation]] viability for salmonid gamete banking.
- **Interagency collaboration:** The project involves the Alchesay and Williams Creek NFH Units, Arizona Fisheries Research Office, Pinetop Fish Health Center, Arizona Department of Game and Fish, and the White Mountain Apache Tribe Department of Game and Fish — reflecting a multi-stakeholder approach to tribal and federal fisheries co-management.
- **Timeline:** Initial fieldwork on collection, transport, cryopreservation, and fertilization trials began in spring/summer 1992, with continued cross-fertilization trials planned for 1993.
- **Broader significance:** This effort represents an early applied use of gamete cryopreservation as a genome banking strategy for an endangered/threatened salmonid, aiming to preserve remnant [[Genetic Diversity]] without requiring translocation of live wild fish, a concept relevant to broader [[Ex Situ Conservation]] strategies in ichthyology.
