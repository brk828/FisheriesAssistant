# Middle to Late Cenozoic Geology, Hydrography, and Fish Evolution in the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Geological Society of America Special Paper (No. 439, 2008) by Jon E. Spencer, Gerald R. Smith, and Thomas E. Dowling combines geological and biological evidence to reconstruct the poorly understood Cenozoic drainage history of the American Southwest. The authors argue that closed basins between the Mesozoic [[Continental Divide]] and the rising [[Laramide Orogeny]] ranges held fishes of Atlantic affinity, that Oligocene-Miocene extension fragmented the landscape and allowed exchange of Pacific and Atlantic faunas, and that the arrival of the modern [[Colorado River]] at about 5 Ma was probably the largest Cenozoic hydrographic change in the region. Fish evidence, including late Miocene fossils from the [[Bidahochi Formation]] and modern mitochondrial DNA differences, is used to test where ancestral river flows did and did not go.

## Background & Description

The paper synthesizes four major stages of the region's geologic history spanning about 100 million years:

1. Late Jurassic and Cretaceous magmatism and faulting built a mountain belt along the southwestern edge of North America, probably forming the continental divide.
2. Eastward migration of mountain building produced the [[Laramide Orogeny]] uplift of the Rocky Mountains (Late Cretaceous to early Tertiary).
3. Oligocene-Miocene extension in the [[Basin and Range Province]] fragmented the western mountain belt and created closed basins that gradually filled with sediment.
4. Late Cenozoic integration of drainages, culminating in the arrival of the Colorado River at about 5 Ma.

The authors emphasize that the study area's river history is poorly documented because the record is obscured by faulting, volcanism, and sediment burial. Fish fossils and living fish distributions are therefore used as independent evidence for past drainage connections.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Closed basins and fish faunas.** Eocene closed basins between the Rocky Mountains and the western mountain belt held freshwater fishes of Atlantic affinity. Fish fossils from these basins are described as fundamentally Atlantic, with no evidence of connection to the Pacific. Eocene to early Miocene river flow near the southwestern edge of the [[Colorado Plateau]] was generally northeastward, toward the Baca and probably Claron basins.

**Late Miocene exchanges.** Miocene and Pliocene fossils in the northern [[Great Basin]] record fish influx from adjacent areas. DNA and species diversity data suggest a long period of diversification, dating to at least the late Miocene, in the greater [[Lake Mead]] area or the ancestral Bill Williams-Verde-Gila drainage, followed by migration into the Lake Mead area after the Colorado River arrived.

**The Snake River connection.** The Bidahochi Formation fishes (upper Miocene) have features of large, swift-moving rivers and are closely related to fishes of the [[Snake River]] and [[Sacramento River]] drainages. Because large fishes require large rivers, this exchange implies a significant stream transferred from the upper ancestral Snake River to the ancestral upper Colorado drainage. The authors suggest this was caused by uplift linked to late Miocene magmatism along the [[Snake-Yellowstone Hotspot]] track, which created an eastward regional slope in southern Idaho.

**Colorado River integration (about 5 Ma).** The Colorado River arrived abruptly in the Basin and Range Province, filled formerly closed basins, spilled over basin divides, and reached the [[Gulf of California]], carving the lower Colorado River valley. The [[Gila River]] underwent similar basin filling and spilling at about the same time. Evidence includes the [[Bouse Formation]], which contains marine fossils in the Blythe Basin but shows no seawater Sr isotope signal.

**Lake overflow and Grand Canyon.** Lake highstand modeling suggests that a lake filling the Little Colorado River valley to about 1830 m (6000 ft) probably did not spill at Telegraph Flat (elevation about 1675 m, or 5500 ft, about 40 km east of Kanab, Utah). Instead, the authors infer overflow over the Kaibab uplift at a lower elevation, which initiated incision of the [[Grand Canyon]].

**Faunal dispersal to the Los Angeles region.** Pliocene delta deposits of the [[Imperial Formation]] were derived from the Colorado River. Headwater captures could have moved freshwater fishes from these deltas to the [[Los Angeles Basin]], probably explaining the presence of the Santa Ana mountain sucker and Los Angeles speckled dace. Displacement of about 170 km on the [[San Andreas Fault]] is restored in the reconstruction.

## Key Findings

- **Closed-basin origin:** Early Cenozoic closed basins held fishes of Atlantic affinity and show no Pacific connection.
- **No Rio Grande or Platte link:** Mitochondrial DNA and fauna differences indicate that late Miocene runoff from the Colorado Plateau did not flow down the ancestral [[Rio Grande]] or Platte River.
- **Snake River exchange:** Significant faunal exchange occurred with the Snake River, supported by modern fishes, Bidahochi fossils, Snake River Plain fossils, and Sacramento River lineages. Zircon evidence shows Colorado Plateau runoff did not reach the western Snake River until the Pliocene.
- **Major 5 Ma event:** Integration of the Colorado River (about 5 Ma) was probably the largest Cenozoic hydrographic change in the region.
- **Dispersal routes after integration:** New river connections let [[Rainbow Trout]] move from coastal areas to central Arizona and southwestern New Mexico, let [[Cutthroat Trout]] move down the upper Colorado to the Virgin River, and facilitated dispersal of [[Speckled Dace]] and suckers to the Los Angeles Basin, likely aided by faulting along the San Andreas system.
- **Separated faunas:** Miocene parts of the Colorado system produced distinct faunas, which mix only where upper-basin fishes migrated downstream through the Grand Canyon.
- **Ongoing implications:** The 5 Ma integration created aquatic conditions that still offer opportunities and challenges for fish dispersal and adaptation to uncolonized environments.
