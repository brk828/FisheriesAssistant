# Hydrogeologic and Hydrochemical Framework, South-Central Great Basin, Nevada-California, with Special Reference to the Nevada Test Site

#hydrogeology #groundwater #aquatic-ecosystem

*Note: This document is a hydrogeology report. It does not address fish, invertebrates, plankton or aquatic species, so the taxonomic fields are N/A.*

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Professional Paper (712-C, 1975, by Isaac J. Winograd and William Thordarson) was prepared for the [[U.S. Atomic Energy Commission]]. It characterizes how ground water moves beneath the [[Nevada Test Site]] and the surrounding south-central [[Great Basin]], to assess the risk of radionuclide contamination from underground nuclear tests. Data were collected from 1957 to 1964.

The study divides the local rocks into 10 hydrogeologic units. Three of them control regional ground-water movement: the [[Lower Clastic Aquitard]], the [[Lower Carbonate Aquifer]] and the [[Tuff Aquitard]]. The authors conclude that a roughly 4,500-square-mile area, including 10 intermontane valleys, drains into a single integrated flow system, the [[Ash Meadows Ground-Water Basin]]. Discharge from this system is at least about 17,000 acre-feet per year at a fault-controlled spring line in east-central [[Amargosa Desert]]. Within the test site, ground water moves south and southwest toward Ash Meadows.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The study had three objectives: (1) define the hydraulic character and subsurface distribution of the major aquifers and aquitards; (2) identify areas of recharge and discharge; and (3) determine the rate and direction of ground-water movement. The third objective was of prime importance for evaluating radionuclide transport.

**Scope and history.** The fieldwork ran in three phases: 1957–59, 1960–61 (overlapping a testing moratorium from November 1958 to September 1961), and 1962–64. The work included more than 5 miles of tunnels beneath [[Rainier Mesa]], and a second drilling program of 10 test holes ranging from 900 to 5,500 feet deep. Well numbers follow the Nevada coordinate grid (for example, well 67-73), and the study area covers about 7,100 square miles across Clark, Lincoln and Nye counties, Nevada, and Inyo County, California.

**Setting.** The area lies in the [[Basin and Range Province]] and is among the most arid parts of Nevada. Valley precipitation averages 3 to 6 inches per year, while potential evaporation is 60 to 82 inches. Precipitation rises to about 10 to 30 inches on the [[Spring Mountains]] crests. Climate-driven pluvial periods (wetter climates) occurred over the past 70,000 years, with the last major pluvial thought to have ended about 9,000 years ago.

**Geology.** About 37,000 feet of Precambrian and Paleozoic marine sediments were deposited in the [[Cordilleran Geosyncline]]. Tertiary volcanic rocks locally exceed 13,000 feet. Structure includes thrust faults, the [[Las Vegas Valley Shear Zone]] (an estimated 15 to 40 miles of displacement), and widespread Basin and Range normal faults.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document's "aquatic" relevance is groundwater-dependent discharge rather than biota.

- **Springs.** [[Big Spring]] at Ash Meadows discharges about 1,000 gpm at 82°F (28°C). [[Crystal Pool]] discharges 2,800 gpm at 88°F (31°C), the largest pool spring in the area. Valley-level springs have uniform discharge and temperatures of 24.0 to 35.0°C. Perched springs in the mountains are cooler (usually below 21.0°C) and highly variable.
- **Devils Hole.** This water-filled solution cavern at Ash Meadows has passages extending more than 315 feet below the pool level. Solution notches show that water levels once stood as much as 4 feet above the 1966 level, and possibly about 20 feet higher.
- **Gypsum Cave.** This archaeologically important cave in Permian carbonate rock is about 300 feet long.
- **Spring Mountains.** More than 60 springs were recorded, with discharges ranging from a few gpm to more than 16,000 gpm. Only about eight exceeded 100 gpm. Total spring flow measured in summer 1964 was about 1,430 gpm.
- **Water use.** Ground water was the sole source of supply for the region in 1967 (apart from some water piped from Lake Mead to Las Vegas). Las Vegas pumped about 42,000 acre-feet in 1964.

## Key Findings

**Hydraulic properties of the controlling units**

- **Lower clastic aquitard:** fracture transmissibility generally below 1,000 gpd per ft. Interstitial permeability is negligible. A test well (89-68) gave a specific capacity of 0.27 gpm per foot of drawdown and an apparent transmissibility of 600 gpd per ft.
- **Lower carbonate aquifer:** transmissibility ranges from about 1,000 to 900,000 gpd per ft. Six wells with clear drawdown curves had a median of about 7,000 gpd per ft and a mean of 13,000 gpd per ft. Specific-capacity estimates from 10 wells had a median of 5,000 gpd per ft.
- **Tuff aquitard:** transmissibility generally below 200 gpd per ft. Ground-water velocity is less than 1 foot per year.
- **Lower carbonate aquifer velocity:** estimated at 0.02 to 200 feet per day, depending on position in the flow system.

**Depth of circulation.** Open fractures are present to at least 1,500 feet below the top of the lower carbonate aquifer and to 4,200 feet below land surface. No consistent decrease in fracture yield was found with depth.

**Matrix properties.** Matrix (intercrystalline) porosity of carbonate cores is very low: total porosity has a median of 5.5 percent (range 0.4 to 12.4 percent, 16 samples), and permeability is 0.00002 to 0.1 gpd per sq ft. Fracture porosity estimated from geophysical logs ranged from 1 to 12 percent. Core-based effective fracture porosity was much lower, probably a fraction of 1 percent.

**Major conclusions**

- An area of at least 4,500 square miles is hydraulically integrated into the Ash Meadows basin through interbasin flow in the lower carbonate aquifer. Annual discharge is at least 17,000 acre-feet.
- Intrabasin movement is controlled by the tuff aquitard. Yucca and Frenchman flats are semiperched and drain downward into the carbonate aquifer. The southern Amargosa Desert and southern Indian Springs Valley receive upward leakage.
- Perched water in the tuff aquitard is poorly connected. Water perched hundreds of feet above the regional saturated zone was found beneath Rainier Mesa, and perched water levels range from about 80 to 1,400 feet below the surface.
- Hydrochemical and isotopic evidence supports regional flow, and hydrochemistry is used as an independent tool for determining flow direction.
- Precipitation east of about long. 115°45′ is 1.5 to 2.5 times greater than at similar altitudes to the west.

**Limitations and open questions.** The authors note that pumping tests show an anomalously rapid water-level recovery after pumping. The cause was not identified, so recovery-curve transmissibility values were excluded from the main estimates. Geophysical porosity estimates have large uncertainties in low-porosity rock. Velocity estimates depend on the flow-system position, and the study recommends further work on possible solution channels or highly permeable fracture zones.
