# Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans) Search and Captive Breeding Effort at Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This newspaper feature (The Arizona Republic, Western Arizona Bureau) reports a weeklong search by roughly 50 state and federal researchers at [[Lake Mohave]] for wild [[Bonytail Chub]]. The bonytail is described as one of the rarest vertebrates in North America. Wild bonytails are nearly absent, and the surviving animals are held mainly in captivity at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico. The goal is to find even one fish to improve the genetic diversity of the captive broodstock. The article frames the decline as a result of damming the [[Colorado River]], the conversion of the river to regulated reservoirs, and the introduction of non-native predatory fish. It also reports that captive breeding is the main recovery strategy, and that surviving stock may be too genetically limited to secure the species.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Bonytail chub, *Gila elegans*, a large-river fish of the lower [[Colorado River]] basin, including the [[Salt River]] and [[Gila River]].
- **Status:** Listed as endangered in 1980. Among the four native "big river" fishes now threatened with extinction, along with the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], the [[Razorback Sucker]], and the [[Humpback Chub]].
- **Historical abundance:** Fifty years before the article, all four natives were common throughout the lower basin. Razorbacks supported a commercial fishery at [[Saguaro Lake]] as late as 1949, when they were harvested with pitchforks.
- **Captive origin:** All captive bonytails at Dexter descend from 10 adults netted in Lake Mohave in 1981. Only one or two wild fish have been captured per year since then, and none of those survived. The original adults are now gone.
- **Comparison:** [[W.L. Minckley]] and federal biologists liken the bonytail to the [[California Condor]], which survives only in captivity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline (as stated in the article):**
- **Damming and habitat change:** The [[Colorado River]] was transformed from fast, muddy rivers into regulated waterways linking idle reservoirs. The article characterizes this as the event that "forever changed the waterway's face."
- **Non-native fishes:** Introduced "sport" fishes, mainly trout and several bass species, along with catfish and striped bass, have eliminated native fishes. [[Jim Brooks]] notes that the bass, catfish, and sunfish that now dominate are not native to the basin. They were transplanted from the Mississippi drainage across the Continental Divide.
- **Predation:** Brooks argues that bonytails would probably do well in a reservoir and would spawn and recruit successfully, were it not for predation by [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Catfish]].
- **Reproduction failure:** None of the native big-river fishes has reproduced successfully in the wild in at least 35 years.
- **Salton Sea:** The text begins a comparison involving the [[Salton Sea]], a depressed basin, but the passage is cut off in the source.

**Management context:** Young bonytails have been stocked into Arizona and California waters every year since 1983. In one recent year, about 19,000 fish measuring 3 to 5 inches went to the [[ASU Research Park]], Lake Mohave, and the [[Imperial Valley Warmwater Fish Hatchery]] at Niland, California. The article says it is doubtful that these fish survived.

## Key Findings

**Population status**
- The bonytail is among the rarest vertebrates in North America, according to [[Bill Kepner]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].
- Since 1974, 36 adult bonytails have been observed in the wild.
- Minckley estimates the average age of surviving bonytails at more than 40 years, which suggests a senescing population with no recruitment.
- There has been no evidence of successful reproduction in Lake Mohave.
- Only the bonytails that exist today, mostly in captivity, are the remaining genetic resource.

**Search effort**
- About 50 researchers and wildlife experts, the largest team assembled in Arizona to date, worked from five radio-connected camps along the Arizona and Nevada shores of Lake Mohave.
- An airplane was on standby for immediate transport of any fish found.
- Finding a single fish would be a success, and additional finds would be welcome.

**Genetic concerns**
- The captive broodstock comes from very few founders. Brooks warns that the stock may never reach the population sizes often recommended for viable populations, which range from 50 to 200 individuals of each sex depending on the source.
- Brooks says that the team would be comfortable with one more wild fish, and even more comfortable with each additional one, but he states that the population will "probably never be 100 percent sure" of having good stock.

**Recovery strategy**
- The soon-to-be-released [[Recovery Plan]] for the bonytail, drafted by federal researchers, emphasizes [[Captive Breeding]]. It is informally described as "sort of a condor plan."
- The strategy follows from the conclusion that recovery in the wild is otherwise doubtful.

**Historical context**
- The article notes that the decline went unnoticed for a long time. Brooks says "nobody paid any attention to these fish earlier."
