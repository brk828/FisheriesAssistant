# Eradication of Fish by Chemical Treatment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-eradication

## Executive Summary

This Michigan Department of Conservation pamphlet, written by [[Frank Hooper]] of the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]], gives practical guidance for eradicating unwanted fish from ponds, lakes, and streams using [[Rotenone]]-based toxicants. It covers toxicant selection, lake suitability, timing, dosage calculation, application methods, toxicity testing, human safety, and the permit process in [[Michigan]]. The core recommendation is a concentration of 0.5 p.p.m. of rotenone material, applied in late summer under warm, calm-then-windy conditions, with careful volume mapping and thorough distribution across the basin.

## Background & Description

- **Program history:** Since 1934 the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]] has used chemicals to eliminate undesirable fish from ponds, lakes, and streams. Most treated waters have been public lakes, and treatment has been carried out by [[Fish Division]] personnel.
- **Growing private interest:** Riparian owners of private lakes and ponds have shown increasing interest in rehabilitation by poisoning and restocking.
- **Document type:** Fish Division Pamphlet No. 19. The printed date is partly illegible in the scan and is not reported here.

### Fish Toxicants

- **Active ingredient:** [[Rotenone]], derived from the roots of plants in the pea family. Marketed fish toxicants are made from [[Derris Root]] or [[Cube Root]] (Barbasco).
- **Powdered root:** Usually contains 5% rotenone. It is less expensive than extract formulations but must be mixed into a suspension, and it gradually sinks while releasing rotenone.
- **Emulsified form ([[Nox-Fish]]):** Contains 5% rotenone plus 15% other cube root extractives, with [[Methylated Naphthalene]] as emulsifier. It is a dark brown liquid that forms a white suspension in water and is often preferred for convenience. The manufacturer claims it disperses vertically and horizontally.
- **Stability:** Rotenone products deteriorate rapidly, especially in direct sunlight, and should be used fresh.
- **Unreliable product:** One marketed toxicant gave variable results. Some trials failed to eradicate fish, and some treated lakes stayed toxic for several years. The active ingredient is undisclosed but is believed to be the insecticide [[Toxaphene]]. The pamphlet advises using only rotenone products from reliable manufacturers.
- **Supplier noted:** Rotenone products from S. B. Penick and Company (New York) were thoroughly tested in Michigan and found satisfactory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Lake Suitability

- **Large, deep lakes:** Generally impractical to treat because of the high cost of chemical. Deep lakes with oxygenated bottom water are difficult to poison because the toxicant is hard to mix into that water.
- **Deep lakes with low oxygen:** Easier to treat if water deeper than 15–20 ft contains little or no oxygen.
- **Depth threshold:** Lakes deeper than 30 ft require measurement of temperature and dissolved oxygen at all depths. Persons unfamiliar with these measurements should hire a competent fisheries biologist.
- **Shallow lakes and ponds:** Maximum depth under 15 ft, usually treatable with little difficulty. If surface and maximum-depth temperatures differ by only one or two degrees, wind is assumed to mix the toxicant top to bottom within about a week.
- **Marshy or boggy shorelines:** Present difficulties. Small fish can survive in shallow, boggy, or marshy backwaters.
- **Springs and streams:** Eradication is seldom possible or practical where large bottom springs or sizable inflowing and outflowing streams exist, because untreated water keeps entering.
- **Reinvasion:** Trash fish can migrate back after treatment unless [[Weirs]] are installed and maintained on tributaries and outlet streams.
- **Dammed outlets:** Treatment may be feasible if a sufficiently high outlet dam prevents fish migration back in, the outflow is small, and water can be stored during the toxic period.

### Time of Treatment

- **Temperature effect:** Rotenone is most toxic at high temperatures. Treatment should not be attempted when surface water is below 60° F unless the dosage is increased considerably.
- **Season:** Warm seasons are favored. Spring and early summer are less desirable because fertile spawn may be present, and surviving eggs can repopulate the lake unless the water stays toxic until hatching.
- **Best window in Michigan:** Usually late August or early September. The first few cool nights create ideal conditions, since surface water sinks at night and carries the poison into deeper layers. A few windy days after treatment help distribute it.
- **Hot, calm weather:** Unfavorable.
- **Detoxification:** Rapid at high temperatures. Shallow hard-water lakes and ponds above 80° F may lose toxicity within three days. Detoxification is slowest in soft-water lakes and at low temperatures.
- **Deep anoxic strata:** Deep layers with minimal late-summer oxygen may already lack fish and need not be treated.

### Environmental and Human Safety

- **Fish-food organisms:** Many are killed at higher concentrations, so higher strengths should be used only when essential.
- **Human and domestic animal exposure:** Rotenone compounds are not poisonous to man at the strength used. Lake water treated at the recommended strength is safe for bathing and is not harmful if taken internally by man or domestic animals.
- **Dust hazard:** Inhaling fine powder during mixing can cause severe headache and nausea. A wetting agent reduces this hazard.
- **Edibility:** Unspoiled fish recovered after treatment are edible, and their flavor does not appear to be affected by rotenone.

## Key Findings

### 1. Dosage Calculation

- **Standard concentration:** 0.5 p.p.m. (1/2 lb of rotenone material per million lb of water). This applies to both emulsifiable rotenone and dry cube or derris root, and the material is assumed to contain about 5% rotenone.
- **Powdered root:** 1.36 lb per acre-foot of water. Total powder = 1.36 × lake volume in acre-feet.
- **Emulsifiable rotenone:** 1/6 gallon per acre-foot. Dividing the volume in acre-feet by 6 gives the emulsion dosage in gallons.
- **Volume estimation:**
  - Small ponds: average depth (ft) × surface area (acres).
  - Large natural lakes: hydrographic map required, because irregularities cause large errors.
  - If volume is uncertain, increase chemical by 50% or more.
- **Strength limits:**
  - Concentrations below 0.5 p.p.m. are not recommended, since bullheads tolerate nearly that level for short periods.
  - Hard-water, vegetation-choked lakes above 80° F may need 1.0 p.p.m. for a complete kill.

### 2. Application Methods

- **Powder preparation:** 10–20 lb of powdered root mixed in a 30-gallon garbage can to a thick mash, then thinned to a paste or suspension. Adding 1 oz of a wetting agent (detergent such as Tide is satisfactory) per lb of powder aids mixing. Apply immediately, since the powder loses strength quickly after mixing.
- **Large-lake spraying:** Centrifugal pump with gasoline motor, able to throw a stream 30–40 ft. Both powder suspensions and emulsions are diluted at least ten-fold before spraying, using a 45° Y connection with valves to control chemical and dilution-water proportions.
- **Small ponds:** A garden-type hand sprayer or knapsack fire-fighter's pump is sufficient.
- **Burlap-bag method:** Mash in weighted burlap bags towed behind a power boat, recommended for deeper strata.
- **Weighted spray nozzle:** A 29–30 lb weight may be needed at slow outboard speed to pump chemical to depth. Nozzle depth is checked by towing it toward shore until it strikes bottom and sounding there.
- **Sectioning large lakes:** Divide into sections with chart markers and buoys. Allot chemical in proportion to each section's volume. Deep areas are typically sprayed two or three times; shallow shore zones once.
- **Spiral pattern:** Recommended for small lakes, starting at shore and spiraling inward, with whorls no more than 30 ft apart.
- **Special attention:** Shallow boggy backwaters, weedy areas, inlet channels, and known subaqueous springs must be treated thoroughly, since wind and waves do not carry chemical to them effectively.

### 3. Results and Species Tolerance

- **Onset:** Distressed fish appear at the surface within one hour of treatment. Fish continue dying for two days or more, depending on lake size and mixing.
- **Temperature effect:** Fish die more slowly in cold water, so deep-water fish die last. Small fish die more quickly than large fish.
- **Species differences:** Bullheads, suckers, and mudminnows die more slowly than bass, pumpkinseeds, bluegills, and trout.
- **Recovery:** Only part of the kill surfaces. Many fish decompose in deep water unseen.

### 4. Toxicity Testing

- **Method:** Live fish held in containers suspended in lake water. Recommended containers are about 12" × 12" × 12" made of door screen, or minnow bucket liners. Containers are hung from a wooden float anchored to the bottom and may be placed at several deep-water points.
- **Criterion:** Fish should die within 4 days if the water is toxic.
- **Detoxification:** Most rotenone-treated lakes detoxify within a month. A toxicity test should be done before restocking.
- **Test species:** Use only the species to be restocked, to avoid accidentally reintroducing an unwanted species.
- **Temperature caution:** A sudden change of 10° F or more is often lethal to test fish.

### 5. Permits and Legal Procedure

- **Public waters:** Eradication may be undertaken only by Department of Conservation personnel.
- **Landlocked private ponds and lakes:** Not connected by a flowing stream to other waters. These can be treated without a Conservation Department permit, though the District Fisheries Supervisor and/or local Conservation Officer should be notified.
- **Transport:** All game laws must be observed when moving live or dead fish off private property. A permit from the Conservation Department in Lansing is required to transport fish in numbers above the possession limit, fish of illegal size, or fish during closed seasons.

### 6. Core Recommendations

- Use only reliable rotenone products, and use them fresh.
- Measure volume accurately, and add 50% or more chemical when uncertain.
- Treat late summer, ideally late August to early September, after cool nights and before windy days.
- Ensure complete basin coverage, since failure to reach all water leads to incomplete kill and reinfestation.
- Test toxicity before restocking.
