# Feasibility of Augmenting the Grand Canyon Humpback Chub Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) report, prepared by [[David R. Van Haverbeke]] and [[Robert L. Simmonds Jr.]] in July 2004 at the request of the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring And Research Center]] (GCMRC), assesses the feasibility of three actions to conserve the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in [[Grand Canyon]]: (1) a [[Captive Broodstock]] program, (2) supplemental stocking of wild-caught age-0 fish grown out in captivity, and (3) translocation of fish within the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and into other tributaries. The report is explicitly a feasibility study, not an implementation proposal or USFWS endorsement.

Broodstock development is judged a possible genetic refugium with little risk in itself, but release of captive-propagated fish carries serious genetic risks (introgression, inbreeding depression, domestication, reduced effective population size) and should be a last resort under [[Captive Propagation Policy]]. Modeling suggests that capturing roughly 960 to 1,400 age-0 fish per year from the LCR and growing them to about 170 mm could arrest decline, while roughly 4,300 to 6,350 per year could restore age-4+ abundance to 1990 levels; these figures may need to be doubled to account for post-release mortality. Translocation within the LCR, especially above [[Chute Falls]], is presented as the most promising first step, with manageable genetic risks if monitored.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and was listed as endangered in 1967. The Grand Canyon population uses the mainstem Colorado River for part of its life cycle but spawns in the LCR, a saline tributary, which is unusual habitat for the species. Threats include habitat loss, watershed mismanagement, cumulative environmental variation, parasite loads (notably the [[Asian Tapeworm]]), and predation by non-native fishes.

Historical abundance was much higher. [[Kolb Brothers]] described spawning in the LCR mouth as sounding like "a slide of shale," and the [[Rust Expedition]] photograph shows numerous large fish near Bright Angel Creek. [[Minckley et al. 2003]] estimated about 200,000 adults in the basin during pre-dam times. Population estimates for fish over 150 mm in the LCR fell from about 4,500 in the early 1990s to 2,482 (SE = 242) in spring 2001, 2,666 (SE = 463) in spring 2002, and 3,419 (SE = 480) in spring 2003. Modeling by [[Coggins et al. 2003]] confirmed the declining trend. The 1994 [[Biological Opinion]] on [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations called for a second spawning population downstream of the dam, which had not been achieved by 2004.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and temperature:** The mainstem is a cold, hypolimnetically derived, fluctuating-flow environment unsuitable for all life stages. The LCR hydrograph varies strongly between years, which affects capture logistics and recruitment.
- **Genetics:** Captive populations founded from few individuals have low effective population size (Ne). [[Tringali and Bert 1998]] suggest hatchery contributions above about 45% would drive Ne below 500 even with 100 effective breeders. [[Lande 1995]] argues minimum broodstock sizes of about 5,000 for quantitative variation.
- **Domestication and behavior:** Fish held in hatcheries lose anti-predator responses, feeding efficiency and migratory cues. [[Conservation Aquaculture]] techniques (environmental enrichment, pre-release training, soft release) can reduce but not eliminate these effects.
- **Predation and competition:** Non-native trout and other fishes prey on juveniles. Predator removal, such as brown trout removal in Bright Angel Creek, is proposed to open niche space.
- **Chute Falls habitat:** Above Chute Falls (14.9 km), the water is high in dissolved CO2 near Blue Springs, with dense algal communities and higher invertebrate densities than below the falls. [[Robinson et al. 1996]] found neither food nor water chemistry precludes humpback chub there.
- **Alternative tributaries:** [[Valdez et al. 2000]] estimated Havasu Creek could sustain about 462 adults and Shinumo Creek about 110, both below genetic viability guidelines.

## Key Findings

**1. Captive broodstock (last resort)**
- Broodstock is a possible genetic refugium with no direct wild risk, but releasing its fish is the highest genetic-risk option in the report (Table 4).
- Prerequisites: completed genetics studies, a formal broodstock management plan, a quarantine-equipped hatchery holding roughly 2,500 to 3,000 fish, a reintroduction plan, and long-term funding.
- Candidate founders: about 80 LCR fish held at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (from about 450 age-0 fish collected in July 1998, from a short LCR reach) and a remnant aggregation of about 50 adults at 30-mile. Genetic fingerprinting is required before use. A 2004 USFWS work plan proposes microsatellite analysis of Willow Beach fish against reference samples, with completion around September 2005.
- Recommended collection of age-0 fish over 5 to 10 years, spread across time and space (Boulders, Coyote and Salt reaches), in moderate lots of about 300 fish.

**2. Supplemental stocking of wild-caught age-0 fish**
- Targeting age-0 fish of 50 to 70 mm in July and August is recommended for capture, with a minimum grow-out to 150 mm before release so fish can be [[PIT Tagging]] and monitored.
- [[Age Structured Mark-Recapture Model]] (designed by C. Walters, UBC) results (Table 2):
  - Arrest decline (hold age-4+ at 2000 level): about 960 to 1,400 age-0 fish removed per year (1,350 in the base model).
  - Recover to 1990 level: about 4,300 to 6,350 age-0 fish removed per year.
  - Doubling may be needed for post-release mortality, which could exceed 50%.
- Without stocking, the spawning population was predicted to stabilize at about 1,900 to 2,300 fish.
- Removal of 960 to 1,400 fish equals about 2 to 3% of annual age-0 production (estimated at about 50,000); 1,300 to 6,350 equals about 9 to 13%.
- Full effects would take until about 2020 with stocking from 2005, and depend on recruitment remaining at mid-1990s levels.
- Release site options: the LCR (risk of exceeding carrying capacity, since fall abundance of fish over 150 mm averaged 1,729), or the mainstem between river miles 58.8 and 64.5, the preferred first option because of lower density, cooler water and reduced tapeworm risk.
- Growing to 200 mm would require roughly half as many fish, but adds about six months and additional hatchery space.

**3. Translocation**
- **Within the LCR above Chute Falls:** A 2002 Biological Opinion called for 300 fish (50 to 100 mm) in 2003, with a second 300 planned for summer 2004. The first translocation was deemed successful. Mitigation is intended for Glen Canyon Dam flow experiments and non-native fish removal. Risks include a small reproductively isolated founder group (below 500 Ne), one-way gene flow, possible genetic change through selection, and unknown ecological effects. The potential gain is roughly 312 fish over 200 mm (about 30% of the presumed adult LCR resident portion, or about 16% of the average 1,631), plus about 5 km of range expansion.
- **Other tributaries (Bright Angel, Shinumo, Havasu):** Genetic risk to the main population is minimal because these tributaries are far downstream of the LCR, and migration from them to the LCR is very limited (two fish identified out of several hundred). Viability depends on predator removal, possibly in combination with mainstem removal near Bright Angel (river miles 81.7 to 90.2) and Shinumo (river miles 106 to 112.1).

**4. Recommended priority and conclusion**
- Priorities: (1) least genetic harm to the wild population, and (2) best potential for a self-sustaining wild population. The report suggests translocation first, then supplemental stocking of wild age-0 fish, and captive broodstock as a last resort.
- Across all options, habitat improvement and natural recruitment are the long-term solution. The report warns that small demographic boosts with high genetic risk should not be the goal, and that the actions would require decades of commitment and funding.
