# A Bibliography and Host List for the Yellow Grub, Clinostomum marginatum (Rudolphi, 1819)

#parasitology #trematode #fish-disease #Clinostomidae #digenetic-trematode

## Executive Summary

[[Clinostomum marginatum]], the yellow grub, is a digenetic trematode distributed across most of North America with economic importance in commercial and sport fisheries. The metacercarial stage (0.5–1.0 inch) encysts under the skin or in musculature of fishes, rendering them esthetically undesirable for human consumption. This parasite occurs in 15 families and 67 species of North American freshwater fishes. Feasible control methods for breaking the life cycle are not presently available for wide application. The document provides a comprehensive host list and bibliography compiled by Lee W. Miller (California Department of Fish and Game, 1966).

## Background & Description

### Taxonomy and Synonymy

[[Clinostomum marginatum]] (Rudolphi, 1819) is a member of the Family [[Clinostomidae]]. Nigrelli (1936) considers *C. marginatum* synonymous with *C. complanatum* (Rudolphi, 1814) based on similar morphology, hosts, and geographical distribution. Price (1936) also lists the two as synonyms. The name *Clinostomum complanatum* has taxonomic priority (1814 vs. 1819), but *C. marginatum* has been commonly used in North America.

### Distribution

The metacercarial stage has been reported throughout North America. Under the synonymy with *C. complanatum*, the distribution extends to Central and South America (Nigrelli, 1936) and is cosmopolitan, including Europe, Australia, and Africa (Chapham, 1945; Yamaguti, 1958).

### Life Cycle

The life cycle was first described by Hunter and Hunter (1935b) and involves three hosts (Figure 1):

1. **Definitive Host**: Wading birds, primarily [[Great Blue Heron]] (*Ardea herodias*) and other herons. Adult trematodes reside in the mouth and esophagus of birds.
2. **First Intermediate Host**: Snails of the genus [[Helisoma]]. Miracidia penetrate snails, developing through sporocyst and redial stages in snail livers.
3. **Second Intermediate Host**: Fish of many species. Cercariae released from snails penetrate fish skin and encyst as metacercariae throughout body tissues.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat and Environmental Factors

- **Shallow, Weedy Waters**: Hunter and Hunter (1930) and Van Cleave and Mueller (1934) found highest infestation in [[Yellow Perch]] from shallow, weedy water. Nigrelli (1936) noted warmwater pond fishes had the highest number of infested species.
- **Littoral Zone**: Snails of genus [[Helisoma]] are littoral and seldom found below 15 feet (Baker, 1945), indicating greatest cercaria-to-fish contact in shallow areas.
- **Lacustrine vs. Lotic**: Fish hosts are primarily lacustrine. Intense parasitism in stream fishes occurs when confined to lake or pond situations (Linton, 1911; Uzmann and Douglas, 1966). Infestations in strictly stream habitats are usually not severe.

### Ecological Factors

Klaas (1963) studied ecological factors in eastern Kansas ponds and found no correlation between environmental factors and degree of parasitism. Heavily infested fish populations existed in ponds with very low or no infected snails. The production of cercariae by infected snails is such that a few infected snails can infect large numbers of fish (Edney, 1950).

Factors potentially involved include:
- Water depth
- Thermal and chemical stratification
- Abundance of weeds, snails, and herons
- Fish behavior and habitat preference
- Snail population dynamics (Edney, 1950; Klaas, 1963)
- Physiological condition of host

### Host Specificity

Fish host specificity is nearly nonexistent. Klaas (1963) found highest infestation rates and cyst numbers in [[Black Bullhead]]. Heavy infestations have been observed in [[White Catfish]] at Lower Otay Reservoir and [[Threadfin Shad]] at El Capitan Reservoir, San Diego County, California. High infestations appear related to extrinsic environmental factors, as the same species may be lightly infested elsewhere.

**Notable Observations**:
- [[Carp]] has not been reported as a host despite being common in lacustrine environments and shallow water. Heavy scales may be a barrier to cercaria penetration.
- Lack of scales in Family [[Ictaluridae]] may partially explain severe infestations in that group.
- Severe parasitism of [[Steelhead Trout]] reported by Uzmann and Douglas (1966) occurred in a lake stocked with fingerlings, apparently related to confinement in a lacustrine environment favorable to cercaria contact (Nigrelli, 1936). This is a rare epizootic; [[Steelhead Trout]] had been reported as a host only once before (Haderlie, 1953).

## Key Findings

### Effects on Fish

**Epizootics** have been reported for:
- [[Yellow Perch]] (Elliot and Russert, 1949; Van Cleave and Mueller, 1934)
- [[Black Bullhead]] (Meyer, 1958; Forney, 1955)
- [[Channel Catfish]] (Edney, 1940)
- [[Steelhead Trout]] (Uzmann and Douglas, 1966)

Swartz (1956) observed death of *Noturus miurus* in captivity attributed to *C. marginatum* infestation. Forney (1955) found infestations in [[Black Bullhead]] in Clear Lake, Iowa, exceeding 500 cysts per fish. Young-of-the-year fish were found emaciated and dying in some areas.

**Condition and Parasite Burden**:
- Forney (1955) found [[Black Bullhead]] emaciated by *C. marginatum*.
- Elliot and Russert (1949) and Rabideau and Self (1953) found no correlation between parasite burden and condition factors.
- Elliot and Russert found older, larger fish had heavier parasite burdens than younger, smaller fish.

**Overwinter Persistence**:
Early reports (Van Cleave and Mueller, 1934; Smallwood, 1914) suggested fish lose parasites during winter in temperate climates. Fischthal (1949) invalidated this, showing overwinter loss of metacercariae is insignificant.

### Fish Host List

A comprehensive check list documents *C. marginatum* in **15 families** and **67 species** of North American freshwater and estuarine fishes:

**Families with Multiple Species**:
- **Cyprinidae** (minnows): 15 species including [[Golden Shiner]], [[Common Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]]

### Bird Hosts (Definitive Hosts)

Hunter and Hunter (1935) listed several known definitive hosts, nearly all wading birds:

- [[Great Blue Heron]] (*Ardea herodias*) – most commonly reported
- [[Black-crowned Night Heron]] (*Nycticorax nycticorax*)
- [[Wood Stork]] (*Mycteria americana*)
- [[Black Tern]] (*Chlidonias niger*)
- [[Common Tern]] (*Sterna hirundo*)
- [[American Bittern]] (*Botaurus lentiginosus*)
- [[Green Heron]] (*Butorides virescens*)
- [[Herring Gull]] (*Larus argentatus*)
- [[Double-crested Cormorant]] (*Phalacrocorax auritus*)

### Snail Hosts (First Intermediate Hosts)

Reported snail hosts all belong to genus [[Helisoma]]:

- *Helisoma campanulatum* Say 1821
- *Helisoma antrosa* Conrad 1834
- *Helisoma trivolvis* Say 1817
- *Helisoma tenue* (Phillipi) Dunker 1850 (probable intermediate host in San Diego County)

### Control Measures

Possible methods of breaking the life cycle discussed by Klaas (1963):

1. **Elimination of Submerged Aquatic Weeds**: Suggested to limit snails to pond edges and reduce contact. However, impractical on most bodies of water. Heavy infestations have been observed in reservoirs without abundant aquatic weeds (e.g., [[Threadfin Shad]] in El Capitan Reservoir, San Diego County). Effectiveness untested.

2. **Elimination of Great Blue Heron Populations**: Neither feasible nor desirable.

3. **Elimination of Snail Host**: Probably the most feasible method. Uzmann and Douglas (1966) successfully eliminated [[Helisoma]] from a 23-acre lake using copper sulfate (0.5 ppm as Cu²⁺) but did not recommend routine use due to toxicity to other aquatic organisms. Klaas (1963) discussed molluscicides; problems include incomplete elimination and rapid reestablishment due to high biotic potential.

4. **Biological Control**: Avault and Allison (1965) showed [[Redear Sunfish]] reduce snail (*Physa*) numbers in experimental ponds, reducing parasitism of [[Bluegill]] by the strigeid trematode *Posthodiplostomum minimum*. Under natural conditions, control of snail hosts may not sufficiently reduce parasitism since only a few infected snails produce numerous cercariae. This approach warrants further exploration.

**Conclusion**: Feasible methods of breaking the life cycle and controlling the parasite are not presently available for wide application.
