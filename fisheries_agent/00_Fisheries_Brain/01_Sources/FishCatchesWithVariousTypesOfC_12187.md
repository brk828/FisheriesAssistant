# Commercial Fishing Gear Catches in T.V.A. Reservoirs, June 1954 to January 1955

#ichthyology #marine-biology #commercial-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1955 field study by District Fisheries Biologist C. E. White, Jr. evaluated the catch composition of six types of [[Commercial Fishing Gear]] in the four main-stream reservoirs of the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] (T.V.A.) system from June 1954 through January 1955. Biologists sampled gear directly at landings and on the water. The study tested a concern raised by sport fishermen and camp operators that commercial netting was depleting [[Game Fish]] stocks.

Of a total recorded catch of 7,666.35 pounds, 98.8 percent was [[Commercial Fish]] and only 1.2 percent was game fish. The author concluded that the gear was highly selective when used under the existing regulations. The paper also suggested that removing large commercial poundages, which compete with game fish for food, may benefit sport fisheries.

## Background & Description

The study was reprinted from the Proceedings of the Southeastern Association of Game and Fish Commissioners (Daytona Beach, Florida, October 1955). It was conducted with T.V.A. biologists and the Alabama Department of Conservation. Records cited in the paper show that 2,400,000 pounds of commercial fish were removed from T.V.A. lakes in Alabama in the 1954 fiscal year. T.V.A. records also show that the [[Crappie]] catch had risen steadily since 1945, when netting was first permitted.

The gear examined and its sample sizes were:

- **[[Gill Net]]:** 46 nets, knot-to-knot mesh 4 to 5 inches, length 240 to 525 feet (average 500 feet).
- **[[Rip Rap Net]]:** 74 nets, mesh 3 to 4 inches, length 90 to 600 feet (average 315 feet). This is a fine-woven straight net with heavy diagonal twine tied from the lead line to the float line.
- **[[Stationary Set Trammel Net]]:** 96 sets, length 60 to 3,000 feet (average 508 feet). Outer walls were 14-inch mesh and the inner wall 3-inch mesh. Nets were set roughly perpendicular to the bank and left overnight or longer.
- **[[Whip Set Trammel Net]]:** 28 sets, length 240 to 1,350 feet (average 770 feet). The net was set in a semicircle in 4 to 6 feet of water, and the fisherman "whipped" the water with a pole to drive fish into it. Each set took 15 minutes to 1 hour.
- **[[Trammel Net]] (total observed):** 124 nets, mesh 3 to 3.5 inches.
- **[[Baited Line]]:** 25 lines, averaging 190 hooks (range 100 to 800).
- **[[Snag Line]]:** 22 lines, averaging 828 hooks (range 600 to 2,000).

Biologists contacted fishermen without notice and accompanied them while they removed fish. Catches were recorded by number, weight, and percentage of total weight (see Table I in the source).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addressed competition between commercial and game fish. The author argued that the large removals of commercial species, such as [[Buffalo]], [[Carp]], and [[Catfish]], reduce competition for food with sport species. Under this reasoning, net removals of small amounts of game fish by unlawful operators were minor by comparison.

Water conditions were recorded in field data sheets, including water temperature, air temperature, water color, and whether water levels were rising or falling. One sample entry, for example, noted dingy, muddy water and rising water levels. Because catches varied with distance from the bank, the study also examined sets 25 to 49 feet, 50 to 99 feet, and 100 or more feet from shore.

## Key Findings

**Overall catch composition**
- Total catch: 7,666.35 pounds of fish (2,349 individuals) recorded across all gear types.
- Commercial species made up 98.8 percent of the total weight (7,572.35 pounds, 2,292 fish). Game fish made up 1.2 percent (94.00 pounds, 57 fish).
- Monthly commercial percentages ranged from 99.9 percent (a 1954 month, partly illegible in the scan) to 96.7 percent in January 1955.
- The largest commercial components by weight were [[Buffalo]] (3,355.0 lb, 43.7%), [[Catfish]] (2,029.1 lb, 26.4%), and [[Carp]] (1,133.5 lb, 14.8%). Drum (530.7 lb, 7.0%) and Spoonbill (200.0 lb, 2.6%) were also common.

**Commercial share by gear type**
- [[Snag Line]]: 100 percent commercial.
- [[Gill Net]]: 99.6 percent.
- [[Rip Rap Net]]: 99.3 percent.
- [[Stationary Set Trammel Net]]: 98.9 percent.
- [[Baited Line]]: 98.6 percent.
- [[Whip Set Trammel Net]]: 97.5 percent.

**Effect of set distance from bank**
- Sets 25 to 49 feet from shore: 98.4 percent commercial.
- Sets 50 to 99 feet: 97.9 percent commercial.
- Sets 100 feet or more: 99.4 percent commercial.
- Distance did not produce large game fish catches.

**Effect of mesh size**
- Stationary set trammel nets, 3-inch mesh (88 sets): 1.2 percent game fish by weight.
- Stationary set trammel nets, 3.5-inch mesh (8 sets): 0.0 percent game fish.
- Rip rap nets, 3-inch mesh (49 sets): 0.6 percent game fish.
- Rip rap nets, 3.5-inch mesh (17 sets): 1.3 percent game fish.
- Rip rap nets, 4-inch mesh (8 sets): 0.0 percent game fish.
- Mesh size made no appreciable difference to the game fish percentage.

**Catch per set (text values)**
- Stationary set trammel net: 40.39 lb of commercial fish per set.
- Whip set trammel net: 42.82 lb per set. The paper describes the trammel nets as the most effective commercial gear.
- Baited line: 34.57 lb per set.
- Snag line: 25.92 lb per set.
- Rip rap net: 10.92 lb per set.
- Gill net: 5.41 lb per set.
- The summary table gives slightly different totals (for example, 40.86 and 43.89 lb for the two trammel types), a minor internal inconsistency.

**Conclusions and regulatory context**
- The author concluded that these gears are "very selective" when used according to the laws in effect during the study.
- The paper also summarized the regulations then in force. Resident commercial licenses cost $10 and non-resident licenses cost $20 per year. The main rules were:
  - Minimum gill net mesh of 4 inches (knot to knot, after shrinking).
  - Minimum trammel net mesh of 3 inches.
  - Nets set no closer than 25 feet from the water's edge, and no closer than 50 feet to another net.
  - Nets may not extend more than halfway across any creek, slough, or stream.
  - Fish may be removed and nets set or removed only between sunrise and sunset.
  - Nets must be marked with buoys bearing the license number, and at least one buoy must show the owner's name and address.
  - Game fish caught incidentally must be released unharmed. Selling game fish is unlawful.
- Game fish were defined as black bass, bream, crappie, perch, and other sunfish; white bass, yellow bass, and trout, pickerel, and perch families.
