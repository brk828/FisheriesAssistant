# Life History of the Santa Ana Sucker, *Catostomus (Pantosteus) santaanae*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 study (Greenfield, Ross and Deckert) describes the life history of the [[Santa Ana Sucker]], a small stream-dwelling catostomid of the subgenus [[Pantosteus]], in the [[Santa Clara River]] from April 1968 to August 1969. A total of 3,359 individuals was collected at two stations. Spawning peaked in May and June, with some spawning as early as March/April and as late as early July. Fecundity in six females ranged from 4,423 to 16,151 eggs. The species matures at age 1+, and few fish survive past the 2+ group. A vertebral deformity (kyphosis) occurred in up to 3.47% of monthly samples without apparent survival cost. The species is well adapted to flood-prone streams, through current tolerance and rapid repopulation after heavy losses.

## Background & Description

- *C. santaanae* was first described by Snyder (1908) as *Pantosteus santa-anae* from the [[Santa Ana River]] near Riverside. [[Gerald R. Smith]] (1966) corrected the spelling and reduced *Pantosteus* to a subgenus of *Catostomus*.
- It is the only native catostomid in the coastal drainages of southern California and one of three native primary freshwater fishes there, along with the [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) and the [[Arroyo Chub]] (*Gila orcuttii*).
- Remaining populations are in the Santa Ana River (Orange County), the San Gabriel and Big Tujunga rivers (Los Angeles County), and the Santa Clara River (Ventura and Los Angeles counties). Numbers have been greatly reduced by human effects (Miller, 1961).
- Miller (1968) suggested the species was not native to the Santa Clara River, but on negative evidence only. Santa Clara River fish have been influenced by genes from the [[Owens Valley Sucker]], so results may differ from other drainages.

**Methods:**
- Monthly [[Seining]] at two stations (April–December 1968 except May and August; February–August 1969 except July). The river there is about 6 ft wide or less and generally under 1 ft deep, with water temperatures of 10–26 °C.
- Age was estimated from [[Otolith Analysis]] and length-frequency data. Scales lacked clear annuli. Otoliths gave satisfactory ages for about 20% of fish examined (50 fish).
- Spawning time was inferred from egg diameter, the [[Gonadosomatic Index]] (34 specimens) and the first appearance of young.
- Fecundity was assessed from six females (77.8–158.2 mm). Stomach contents came from 48 fish (27–57 mm) collected October 4, 1968. Artificial spawning was carried out April 17, 1968.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Shallow, rock-and-sand stream reaches with little aquatic vegetation, and a second station with more streamside vegetation.
- **Diet:** By weight, 97.53% of stomach contents was detritus, algae and diatoms; 1.16% hydrophilid larvae; 1.31% other insect larvae (stoneflies, Odonata, Helodidae, Hydraenidae, Hydroptilidae, Empididae, Culicidae, among others), fish scales and eggs. Insect larvae increased in importance with fish size.
- **Associated fishes:** [[Arroyo Chub]], [[Unarmoured Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni*) and the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Large predatory fish are lacking in the river.
- **Flooding:** The river flooded in late January and mid-February 1969. Mosquitofish were not collected again after the first flood, and the chub and stickleback declined sharply.

## Key Findings

- **Development:** Hatching took 360 hours at 13 °C. Eggs were demersal and adhesive, averaging 2.2 mm in diameter. Prolarvae were about 7 mm TL at hatching. Eyes were pigmented by 315 hours. The mouth shifts from terminal to subterminal at about 16 mm.
- **Fecundity:** 4,423 (8.57 g, 77.8 mm) to 16,151 (69.35 g, 158.2 mm), linearly related to body weight. No females under 49 mm or 2.05 g contained eggs. This exceeds *C. platyrhynchus* (990–3,710) but is below the larger-bodied *C. catostomus* (14,000–35,000) and *C. commersonii* (31,200).
- **Spawning:** Peak in late May and June, with young first appearing in early June. Mean egg diameter rose from 0.10 mm (December 1968) to 1.25 mm (May 1969). Fish of 25 mm in April and 31 mm in May 1968 indicate early spawning, and 10–11 mm young on July 21, 1968 indicate spawning into early July. The evidence supports protracted rather than dual spring/fall spawning.
- **Age and growth:** Mean length of the 0+ group in December was 44 mm. Maturity occurs at 1+, and a second spawning at 2+. Most fish die in the 2+ group, with a few reaching 3+. Otolith weight is curvilinear with length and less reliable beyond about 65 mm SL. Scale width increases linearly with SL. The sex ratio was 1:1 with no growth differences between sexes.
- **Flood response:** One week after the first flood, 120 fish were taken versus 225 in December. Catches then rose to 316 (June 20, 1969) and 1,308 (August 22, 1969). Two large fish (141 and 153 mm), likely escapees from a private recreational lake, suggest slower river growth than in ponds.
- **Vertebral deformity:** Kyphosis occurred in 0–3.47% of monthly samples; over 60% of deformed fish had crowded, shortened centra. Post-Weberian vertebral counts (39–42, median 40) were unchanged. The deformity appeared across year classes. Deformed fish up to 54 mm survived the floods (August 1969: 20 of 1,308, 1.52%), so selection against them appears absent. The cause is unknown; high water temperature and pesticides have been proposed elsewhere, but no pesticide data exist.
- **Adaptive interpretation:** Prolonged spawning, high fecundity, early maturity (1+, versus age 4–5 or older in subgenus *Catostomus* suckers) and algae/detritus feeding of young allow rapid recovery from flood losses within a single breeding season.
