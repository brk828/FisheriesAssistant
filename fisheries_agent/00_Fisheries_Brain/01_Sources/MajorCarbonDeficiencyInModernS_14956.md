# Major Carbon-14 Deficiency in Modern Snail Shells from Southern Nevada Springs

#malacology #freshwater-ecology #radiocarbon-dating

## Executive Summary

This compiled JSTOR extract from *Science* (1984) contains three papers. The main aquatic study, by Alan C. Riggs (U.S. Geological Survey), reports carbon-14 (¹⁴C) contents as low as 3.3 ± 0.2 percent modern in shells of the exotic snail [[Melanoides Tuberculatus]] from artesian springs at [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada. The shell carbon is in isotopic equilibrium with dissolved bicarbonate, so the deficiency reflects the spring water, not the snail's metabolism. The paper warns that such deficiencies could cause freshwater shells to be dated hundreds of years to tens of thousands of years too old. A second paper, on Holocene sediments from [[Lake Superior]], infers reduced bottom-current and wind intensity after about 9500 years ago. The third, by J. M. LoSecco, is an astrophysics note on using the IMB water-Cherenkov detector to observe neutrinos from stellar collapse.

## Background & Description

**Study organism.** *Melanoides tuberculatus* (Miller) is an exotic mollusk introduced into Big Spring around 1970. The population is composed entirely of parthenogenetic females that bear postmetamorphic young about 2 mm long. Adults are most abundant under rotting algal mats, where they apparently feed on decaying organic matter at the mat–sediment interface.

**Study area.** The Ash Meadows discharge area (about 16 × 3 km) lies between the Amargosa Desert and Paleozoic carbonate ridges in southern Nevada. The three springs studied are:

- **[[Big Spring]]:** A glory-hole spring about 15 m in diameter and 5 m deep, with a single orifice.
- **[[Crystal Pool]]:** A similar glory-hole spring, about 15 m in diameter and 5 m deep.
- **[[King Spring]]:** Smaller and shallower (about 8 m × 1 m), with multiple small orifices in a tufa floor.

All three are fed by a regional [[Paleozoic Carbonate Aquifer]]. Recharge occurs in mountains at least 100 km to the north and east, and some groundwater branches take thousands of years to travel.

**Carbon reservoirs.** Snails could draw on three carbon sources: (1) old lake-bed and carbonate rock with no measurable ¹⁴C; (2) atmospheric CO₂ and subaerial plants at 95–100 percent modern ¹⁴C; and (3) dissolved HCO₃⁻ plus algal mat carbon, which ranged from 1.7 percent modern (King Spring) to 11 percent modern (Crystal Pool).

Shell δ¹³C values were about 2 per mil heavier than the water in all springs (for example, Big Spring water −4.6 ± 0.1 versus shell −2.7 ± 0.1 per mil PDB, with other values also reported). The text notes that water and shell values for the other springs follow the same pattern.

**Flushing and contamination.** Big Spring would replace its pool water about every 2 hours without mixing, and Crystal Pool in less than half an hour. Average annual precipitation is 10 cm. All three springs catch organic litter, wind-blown dust, and human debris. Rapid flushing masks surface contamination, except at King Spring, where perennial inflow from a marsh overwhelms flushing. Irrigation headworks at the King Spring outlet block downstream migration of snails.

**Regional context.** The author argues that ¹⁴C deficiencies are common in aquatic and terrestrial life. Submerged plants and a turtle from [[Montezuma'S Well]], Arizona, show ¹⁴C values of 11.61 ± 0.57 (Chara), 4.72 ± 0.2 (*[[Potamogeton Illinoensis]]*), and 16 ± 2.6 percent modern (*Kinosternon sonoran*), indicating that organisms draw much of their carbon from water. Groundwaters and springwaters below 5 percent modern ¹⁴C (apparent age about 22,000 years) have been reported from the United States, Hungary, Great Britain, Libya, Morocco, and Chile. Some Qatar groundwater is indistinguishable from 0 percent modern.

**Lake Superior setting.** The second study examined sediments from a deep-water site north of the Keweenaw Peninsula in water depths of 220–260 m. Wind-driven bottom currents intensify during spring and fall storms, when the lake is isothermal and the Keweenaw current may reach beyond 200 m depth.

## Key Findings

**Riggs (1984): Carbon-14 deficiency in spring snails**

- *Melanoides tuberculatus* shells from Big Spring had 3.3 ± 0.2 percent modern ¹⁴C, the lowest value recorded for freshwater biogenic carbonate at the time. The largest previous deficiency was 4.72 ± 0.2 percent for *Potamogeton illinoensis*.
- The apparent age of the 3.3 percent value is about 27,000 years.
- Shell carbon is in isotopic equilibrium with dissolved HCO₃⁻, based on the δ¹³C pattern. Algal photosynthetic fractionation was not evident in the shells.
- No ¹⁴C fractionation was detected between shells and water in Crystal Pool and Big Spring. Measurement uncertainty overshadowed the expected small (about 4 per mil) fractionation.
- King Spring shells (5.9 ± 0.2 percent) contained more ¹⁴C than the water (1.7 ± 0.4 percent). The author attributes this to marsh-derived inflow reaching the pool, with some snails possibly migrating upstream.
- Ignoring the deficiency could cause large errors. If the Big Spring depletion were attributed only to the [[Hard Water Effect]], its shells would date at least 22,000 years too old.
- The author concludes that deficiencies are not limited to snails, that they occur widely, and that the potential for misdating in arid areas has no clear upper limit. Many living organisms lacking ¹⁴C may simply be untested.
- Mollusk shells can serve as dating material if the initial ¹⁴C level and the postdepositional alteration are known.

**Lake Superior study (Holocene paleoclimate)**

- Downcore median grain sizes of the silt fraction coarsened with burial depth in five of six cores. Core 14G was the exception, possibly due to local eddies.
- The results indicate a substantial reduction in bottom-current velocity, and corresponding wind velocity, from about 9500 to 6500 years ago. The trend suggests decreasing wind intensity or storm frequency beginning 9500 years ago.
- Surface silt median grain size declined from 36 µm in the scoured trough to 15 µm farther offshore.
- Dating relied on [[Paleomagnetic Dating]] of cores 23P and 24P, which is the best available method for Holocene sediments in the Great Lakes.
- Analytical precision: average deviation between duplicate median grain-size analyses was 0.05 phi (about 1.0–1.5 µm).
- The sampling base comprised 2 piston, 10 gravity, and 12 box cores, plus seismic profiles and side-scan sonar over 500 km of shiptrack.
- The authors are not identified in the extracted text.

**LoSecco (1984): Stellar collapse neutrino detection (brief)**

- The IMB detector in an 8000-ton water tank could detect neutrinos from stellar collapse, but individual signals are too weak. Events would be recorded through the proton-decay electronics.
- Adding NaCl (8000 tons of water dissolving 3200 tons of salt) would increase density and Cherenkov photon yield, with some offset from shorter range.
- Neutrino mass time-of-flight differences of about 50 ms are predicted for m = 10 eV, p = 10 MeV, and 1 kpc distance.
- Interaction counts were given in the text. The exponents are garbled in the OCR, so treat them as unverified.
- The paper concludes that the 8000-ton IMB detector would be an effective stellar collapse detector.
