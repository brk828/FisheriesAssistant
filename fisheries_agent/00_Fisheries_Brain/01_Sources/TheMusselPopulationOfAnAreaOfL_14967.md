## Executive Summary

This 1956 study by Riggs and Webb quantifies the freshwater mussel (Unionidae) population inhabiting a loamy-sand bottom area of **[[Lake Texoma]]**, an impoundment of the Red River formed by Denison Dam. Taking advantage of a natural "die-off" event caused by lake-level fluctuation exposing a peninsula near **[[Big Mineral Creek]]**, the authors used shell-based quadrat sampling to reconstruct mussel community composition and density. The study is notable not for fish taxonomy per se, but for its ecological linkage between mussel reproduction/dispersal and host fish species — particularly the **[[Freshwater Drum]]** (*Aplodinotus grunniens*), the primary glochidial host for the dominant mussel species, *Leptodea laevissima*. The paper provides one of the few quantitative mussel density datasets for a reservoir system and situates Lake Texoma's mussel fauna within the broader depauperate mussel assemblages of the **[[Red River]]** and **[[Washita River]]** systems.

Notable shell/growth traits described:
- ***[[Leptodea laevissima]]***: thin, rapidly-growing shell; large individuals (four+ years old) hypothesized to represent post-1949/1952 migrants into the exposed basin.
- ***[[Quadrula quadrula]]***: thick, heavy shell — a defensive trait against fish predation and a factor enabling survival in deep water during lake drawdowns.
- Growth-ring analysis indicated most specimens were >1 year old, with very few juveniles (<1 inch) except in *Carunculina parva* and *Truncilla donaciformis*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat context:** The study area was a ~60-acre flat peninsula immediately downstream of Big Mineral Creek's confluence with Lake Texoma, historically a peanut field, later inundated to become loamy-sand lake bottom. A shallow basin within this peninsula trapped mussels as water levels fell, effectively creating a natural mortality/population "snapshot."

**Host-fish relationship:** The dominant mussel species, ***[[Leptodea laevissima]]***, relies on the **[[Freshwater Drum]]** (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) — an abundant fish in Lake Texoma — as its principal glochidial host (per Baker, 1928). This host abundance is proposed as a primary driver of *L. laevissima*'s dominance in the reservoir, illustrating a direct trophic/parasitic linkage between fish community composition and mussel recruitment success.

**Riverine origins:** Both parent rivers (Red River, Washita River) were historically classified as poor mussel habitats due to heavy silt loads and shifting substrates (Isely 1924; Coker et al. 1922). Isely's pre-impoundment surveys recorded only 52 living mussel specimens (8 species) from the Red River bed sites now submerged under Lake Texoma, and 12 species from the Washita River.

**Impoundment effects:** The paper frames Lake Texoma as a case study in how dam impoundment reshapes benthic invertebrate communities — potentially favoring species tolerant of fluctuating water levels (e.g., deep-water-retreating *Quadrula* spp.) while disadvantaging others. Water-level history (1944–1953) shows the site underwent repeated inundation/exposure cycles, with the sampled population interpreted as having established between February 1949 and April 1952 (~3 years), based on shell size/age analysis.

**Substrate preference patterns:** Quadrats with fine gravel yielded higher shell densities; those with less sand yielded fewer. Species such as *Anodonta grandis* and *Leptodea fragilis*, more typical of mud-bottom habitats, were likely undersampled in this sandier area relative to their true lake-wide abundance.

**Species inventory for Lake Texoma:** Seven mussel species were confirmed present via quadrat sampling: *Anodonta grandis*, *A. imbecillis*, *Carunculina parva*, *Leptodea fragilis*, *L. laevissima*, *Quadrula quadrula*, and *Truncilla donaciformis*. All were previously documented within Isely's (1924) list of 43 species from Red River tributaries; only *A. imbecillis* had not been recorded directly from the Red or Washita main channels.

## Key Findings

- **Density estimate:** The exposed peninsula population averaged **0.36 mussels per square foot (15,647 per acre)** — substantially lower than comparable stream mussel densities reported elsewhere (e.g., 7.5/ft² in Clark & Wilson 1912; up to 16.5/ft² in the Clinch River per Coker et al. 1922).
- **Dominant species:** *[[Leptodea laevissima]]* was overwhelmingly dominant (536 of 898 total specimens, ~60%), followed by *[[Quadrula quadrula]]* (220 specimens, ~24%).
- **Mortality event as natural experiment:** The 1952–1953 lake drawdown exposed a shallow basin where mussels appear to have died in place, with high water temperatures (>80°F) implicated as the probable lethal mechanism during the preceding exposure period.
- **Population turnover timeline:** Historical lake-level records (1944–1953) allowed the authors to bracket the studied mussel population's establishment to a ~3-year window (Feb 1949–April 1952), demonstrating relatively rapid post-impoundment colonization despite the parent rivers' historically poor mussel-supporting capacity.
- **Species associations:** All seven species co-occurred within quadrats, though the homogeneous sandy substrate limited the ability to draw strong conclusions about interspecific habitat partitioning.
- **Host-fish dependency:** The success of *L. laevissima* is attributed partly to the abundance of its fish host, the [[Freshwater Drum]], underscoring the ecological interdependency between reservoir fish communities and unionid mussel recruitment.
- **Biogeographic note:** Two species found in Lake Texoma (*Anodonta corpulenta* and *A. imbecillis*) were not recorded by Isely (1924) from the Red or Washita rivers directly, suggesting either underrepresentation in historical river surveys or post-impoundment range shifts via tributary sources.
