## Executive Summary
This 1963 paper by Gerald A. Cole reports on calanoid copepods recovered from 18 archived plankton samples (1933–1934) collected by C. M. Armack from four small, likely man-made ponds (Saginaw/Cement Pond, Santa Fe Pond, Rock Pond, City Pond) near Williams, Arizona, on the Colorado Plateau (~6,750 ft elevation). Although the study is not fish-focused, it is notable to aquatic ecology for documenting **four species of *Diaptomus*** (*D. siciloides*, *D. nudus*, *D. sanguineus*, *D. clavipes*), including the first Arizona record of *D. sanguineus*. The work reconstructs seasonal succession, congeneric coexistence patterns, body-size trends, and reproductive (clutch-size) dynamics in these calanoid populations, framed partly around competitive exclusion theory (Gause's Axiom). A single incidental mention of a **ctenoid fish scale** found in a Rock Pond vial is the only direct ichthyological data point, suggesting at least intermittent fish presence in that pond.

## Background & Description
Data pertains to copepod (not fish) morphology, with one incidental fish-scale note:

- **Ctenoid fish scale**: A single ctenoid-type scale was recovered from a Rock Pond sample vial, taken as indirect evidence that [[Rock Dam|Rock Pond]] may have been a permanent (fish-supporting) water body, distinguishing it from the more clearly intermittent [[City Dam|City Pond]].
- **Copepod body length** (measured via ocular micrometer, rostral tip to base of caudal setae) was used as the primary morphometric parameter for species and sex comparisons:
  - *D. siciloides* males: mean 1.00–1.21 mm depending on season/population.
  - *D. nudus* males: mean 1.11–1.29 mm.
  - *D. sanguineus*: larger-bodied, means up to ~2.55 mm (females).
  - *D. clavipes*: largest species, means up to ~2.27–2.55 mm, described as having a "large, bulky metasome and relatively short urosome and caudal rami" compared to the slender *D. sanguineus*.
- Body size decreased in both *D. nudus* and *D. siciloides* as population density increased seasonally (spring → midsummer), a pattern also reported for *D. siciloides* in [[Severson Lake]], Minnesota (Comita, unpublished data).
- Mass/volume was noted as potentially more ecologically meaningful than length alone; *D. clavipes* was calculated to be roughly 8× heavier than *D. siciloides* in North Dakota populations (Comita & Schindler 1963), versus only ~1.4–1.7× by length-based estimates — highlighting a mismatch between linear and mass-based size comparisons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Four probably artificial impoundments near Williams, AZ — ranging from a possibly permanent, fish-bearing pond ([[Rock Dam|Rock Pond]]) to a clearly intermittent basin ([[City Dam|City Pond]], dry for ≥2 months prior to sampling).
- **Co-occurring plankton community**: cladocerans (*Daphnia pulex*, *Daphnia similis*, *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*, *Bosmina*, *Chydorus*, *Simocephalus*, *Leydigia quadrangularis*), cyclopoid copepods (*Eucyclops agilis*), ostracods, testate amoebae (*Difflugia corona*), and diverse rotifers (*Keratella valga*, *Asplanchna*, *Polyarthra*, *Brachionus calyciflorus*, *B. bidentata*, *Filinia*). Blue-green algal blooms (*Aphanizomenon holsaticum*, *Anabaena circinalis*) and *Volvox* were also recorded, indicating eutrophic to mesotrophic pond conditions.
- **Seasonal succession**: 
  - *D. sanguineus* and *D. clavipes* behaved as cool-season/marginal-habitat specialists, present November–May but absent in mid-summer samples.
  - *D. nudus* was a "late-winter-to-early-summer" form, dominant in spring but apparently displaced by *D. siciloides* by mid-summer.
  - *D. siciloides* emerged as the most successful, perennial species, becoming dominant across all four ponds by July, consistent with findings from Lake Erie (Davis 1961) and Severson Lake, MN (Comita 1963), where it produces four summer generations plus an overwintering egg-diapause generation.
- **Congeneric coexistence and competition**: Up to four *Diaptomus* species (three subgenera) co-occurred in single samples from Saginaw Pond. Analysis of body-length differences among coexisting species (Table 3) supports a size-partitioning framework consistent with **[[Gause's Axiom|Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle]]**. *D. nudus* and *D. siciloides* — both in subgenus *Leptodiaptomus* — showed the least size separation and greatest length overlap, implying they are the closest ecological competitors, with *D. siciloides* achieving dominance during warmer months.
- **Reproductive ecology**: Clutch sizes varied strongly by species and season. *D. sanguineus* showed exceptionally large clutches (up to 65 eggs) in early spring, hypothesized as a genetic adaptation for persistence in ephemeral/marginal habitats (per Gotto 1962 and Ravera & Tonolli 1956 frameworks linking high fecundity to habitat instability). *D. nudus* and *D. siciloides* showed more moderate, declining clutch sizes as populations peaked, with only weak/inconsistent correlations between body size and egg number (contrasting with stronger correlations reported for European diaptomids and some Lake Erie species).

## Key Findings
- **First Arizona record of *Diaptomus sanguineus***, extending the known state list of calanoid copepods to eight species (previously seven, per Marsh 1929, Kincaid 1953, Wilson 1955, Cole 1961).
- Documented apparent **seasonal replacement of *D. nudus* by *D. siciloides*** in the same pond systems, interpreted as competitive succession between closely related, similarly sized congeners.
- Found **inverse relationship between population size and mean body length** in both *D. nudus* and *D. siciloides*, with size minima coinciding with population peaks in mid-summer — paralleling trends independently observed in Minnesota populations.
- Large clutch sizes in *D. sanguineus* (up to 65 eggs) support the hypothesis that **high fecundity is an adaptive response to habitat unpredictability** (temporary/intermittent ponds), consistent with broader copepod life-history theory (Gotto 1962; Ravera & Tonolli 1956).
- Body-length-based species separation (Table 3) provides indirect ecological evidence for **niche partitioning among coexisting *Diaptomus* species**, with the two most taxonomically similar species (*D. nudus*, *D. siciloides*, both *Leptodiaptomus*) showing the least differentiation and greatest inferred competitive pressure.
- Discrepancy between **length-based vs. mass-based size ratios** (e.g., *D. clavipes* vs. *D. siciloides*: 1.7× by length but ~8× by dry mass in other studies) flags a methodological caution for using linear measurements alone to infer competitive or trophic differences among copepod species.
- Incidental finding of a **ctenoid fish scale** in Rock Pond is the sole direct evidence in this paper of fish presence, suggesting at least episodic fish occupancy in one of the four studied ponds — relevant context for any food-web or predation inferences regarding the zooplankton communities described.
