# Growth, Energy Utilization and Reproduction in Meso- and Bathypelagic Fishes off Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #myctophidae #bathylagidae #life-history

## Executive Summary

This 1980 study by [[Childress]] and colleagues compares growth, metabolism (energy use) and reproduction in 4 mesopelagic and 5 bathypelagic fishes captured by midwater trawl off Southern California. All mesopelagic species performed diurnal vertical migrations; no bathypelagic species did. The mesopelagic and bathypelagic fishes are benchmarked against two epipelagic planktivores, the [[Pacific Sardine]] and [[Northern Anchovy]], using otolith-based ageing and calorimetric energy budgets. The authors conclude that deep-living fishes grow rapidly and reproduce late, a pattern that challenges the traditional view of slow growth in the deep sea, and that energy conservation is not the primary selective force shaping their physiology.

## Background & Description

The study is framed against the common assumption that physiological rates and growth are slower in the deep sea, supported by work on [[Deep-Sea Metabolism]] and the decrease of biomass with depth ([[Vinogradov]], 1968). Earlier work by [[Childress and Nygaard]] suggested that the low caloric density of bathypelagic fishes could permit relatively rapid growth. The present study addresses three life-history parameters together: growth, metabolism and reproduction.

**Sampling and ageing.** Fishes were collected with a 3 m Isaacs-Kidd midwater trawl or a 3.1 m [[Tucker Trawl]] between 1971 and 1978 from research vessels including the *Alpha Helix*, *Oconostota*, *Velero IV*, *Agassiz* and *Proteus*. Mesopelagic species were taken in the Santa Barbara Channel (550 m maximum depth) and the San Clemente Basin (2000 m maximum depth). Ages were read from otolith (sagitta) concentric zones, with each translucent zone taken as one year. Only species with distinct, alternating opaque and translucent zones were analyzed.

**Energy budget method.** Energy used for growth (G) and metabolism (M) was calculated per year, with caloric content estimated from proximate composition and caloric equivalents of 5.7, 9.3 and 4.1 kcal g⁻¹ for protein, lipid and carbohydrate. Oxygen consumption was converted at 4.63 kcal per litre O₂. The budget is partial, excluding reproduction, specific dynamic action and excretion.

**Study groups:**
- Epipelagic comparison: [[Pacific Sardine]] (*Sardinops caerulea*) and [[Northern Anchovy]] (*Engraulis mordax*), taken from literature.
- Mesopelagic migrators: *Leuroglossus stilbius*, *Stenobrachius leucopsaurus*, *Triphoturus mexicanus*, *Lampanyctus ritteri*.
- Bathypelagic non-migrators: *Poromitra crassiceps*, *Borostomias panamensis*, *Lampanyctus regalis*, *Bathylagus milleri*, *Bajacalifornia burragei*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Bathypelagic fishes in this study occur between about 400 and 1500 m and do not rise above 400 m once they exceed roughly 50 mm length. Mesopelagic migrators spend daytime at about 400 to 700 m and move toward the surface at night. The authors describe the deep realm as dark, stable and homogeneous, which removes many selective forces. They argue that seasonal fluctuations in micronekton biomass and the nutritional status of deep predators still justify treating otolith rings as annual marks.

The paper also places its findings in the context of [[Life-History Strategies]]. Epipelagic sardines and mesopelagic migrators show iteroparous, repeated reproduction, consistent with variable juvenile survival in the unstable Southern California borderland. Bathypelagic species show late, probably single reproductive events (semelparity), similar to the bathypelagic mysid [[Gnathophausia ingens]], which the authors interpret as a response to stable deep conditions.

## Key Findings

**Growth**
- The epipelagic sardine and anchovy show the fastest early growth, with asymptotic curves and lifespans of about 6 yr.
- Bathypelagic species grow linearly or, in *Poromitra crassiceps*, exponentially, and never clearly reach an asymptote. They grow nearly as large as the epipelagic species.
- Mesopelagic migrators have the lowest growth rates and smallest maximum sizes, approaching asymptotes quickly.
- *Lampanyctus ritteri* grows faster in calories than its deeper congener *L. regalis* in early years; the difference is explained by roughly threefold higher caloric density in the migrator.
- Lifespans reported: epipelagic and bathypelagic species 4 to 8 yr; mesopelagic species 5 to 8 yr.

**Energy use**
- Lifetime energy totals (G + M) are highest for the sardine (lifetime sum 7378 kcal for the sardine, with metabolism 7126 kcal).
- Weight-specific energy use ranks sardine > mesopelagic migrators > bathypelagic species, reflecting low bathypelagic metabolic rates and low caloric densities.
- Relative growth efficiency rises from 0.03 in the sardine to 0.68 in the bathypelagic species, with the bathypelagic group investing heavily in growth throughout life.
- Bathypelagic metabolic rates are low (e.g., about 0.005 to 0.017 mL O₂ mg⁻¹ wet wt h⁻¹ in the bathypelagic group, compared with 0.3 for the sardine).

**Reproduction**
- *Leuroglossus stilbius*, *Stenobrachius leucopsaurus* and *L. ritteri* appear to mature in their third year and continue reproducing.
- Bathypelagic species defer maturity; four species had large oocytes only in their final year, and *Bathylagus milleri* in its last two years.
- *Poromitra crassiceps* shows little ovarian development until about the seventh year and mature oocytes only in the ninth year (>140 mm SL).
- *Leuroglossus stilbius* and *S. leucopsaurus* spawn in winter and spring; *B. milleri* weakly suggests summer spawning.

**Interpretation**
- The bathypelagic strategy favours maximum size per unit energy input rather than energy conservation. Larger size carries metabolic costs, and the authors estimate that *Bajacalifornia burragei* would need only about 6% of its energy if it stayed as small as *Triphoturus mexicanus*.
- The authors find no evidence of an "energy bonus" for downward migration, since migrators use more energy per unit body than deeper fishes and fare worse than the sardine on most criteria.
- The authors propose that reduced locomotory demands with depth, rather than food limitation, drive the lower metabolic rates and structural reduction of deep-sea animals.

**Methodological caveats**
- Energy budgets are partial, and caloric estimates from large individuals may overestimate caloric content in young fish (about 20% for the smallest *T. mexicanus*).
- Growth rates were validated against independent studies of *S. leucopsaurus* by [[Fast]] (1960) and [[Smoker and Pearcy]] (1970).
