# Blue Desert: The Yaqui Topminnow and the San Bernardino Ranch

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliopsis

## Executive Summary

This literary nonfiction passage, the "Fish" chapter of Charles Bowden's *Blue Desert* (University of Arizona Press, 1986), follows a journalist's visit to the [[San Bernardino Ranch]] in southeastern Arizona. The ranch's springs and ponds shelter the [[Yaqui Topminnow]], an endangered fish of about an inch or two in length. The text uses the fish as a lens on the history of ranching, the arrival of [[Mosquito Fish]] and [[Russian Thistle]], and the wider extinction crisis. Its central argument is that species are worth saving because "a world with less life is less of a world."

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The ranch lies seventeen miles east of Douglas, Arizona, on the Mexican line. It was once part of a 65,000-acre Spanish land grant and [[John Slaughter]]'s holding. It was later reduced to 2,400 acres. Slaughter's herd once reached 50,000 head. Today no cattle are permitted.
- **Ownership:** John Slaughter bought the property in 1884 and served as Sheriff of Cochise County from 1887 to 1890. The [[Nature Conservancy]] bought the ranch in 1979 to save the fish. It later transferred the land to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], which plans habitat restoration.
- **Fish morphology and biology:** The Yaqui topminnow runs from tan to olive, darkest on top and yellow to white below. Males are larger than females. Broods range from six to forty-nine young, and gestation takes about two weeks.
- **Native fish community:** Besides the topminnow, the ranch became a refuge for the [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Catfish]], Beautiful Shiner, and Mexican Stoneroller.
- **Range:** The topminnow once thrived across the [[Yaqui River Basin]], which drains to the Gulf of California near Obregón, Sonora. It now survives in a few places, including the ranch's artesian wells.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat loss:** Farms, people, and cattle dried up rivers, and the [[Cienegas]] (swamps) have vanished in southern Arizona. The fish has retreated to a few springs and wells.
- **Cattle impacts:** Cattle trampled the Astin Spring area in 1969, during a dry year, and wiped out a large minnow population there.
- **Introduced predator and competitor:** [[Mosquito Fish]] were introduced in the twentieth century to control mosquitoes. They "wiped out many of the native fish" by outcompeting them for food.
- **Chub recovery:** When the Nature Conservancy bought the ranch in 1979, the Yaqui chub was down to about twenty survivors. The population now numbers 30,000.
- **Land transformation:** Former marshes and grasslands became [[Mesquite]] thickets and bare, trampled earth. The [[Black Draw]] creek, once lined with cottonwoods, became a dry ditch with banks 10 to 20 feet high.
- **Drought and disaster:** Drought in 1892 and 1893 killed large herds, and bone gatherers later hauled skeletons away for fertilizer.
- **Global extinction context:** The text cites a current rate of about one hundred species a year or more. It projects 100 a day for the next twenty years, or about 40,000 a year, out of an estimated 5 to 10 million species on Earth. It also notes that a million species may disappear in the next twenty-five years.
- **Evolutionary context:** Since life began 3.5 million years ago, about 100 to 250 million species have existed. Between 90 and 98 percent of those efforts failed and became extinct.

## Key Findings

1. **The Yaqui topminnow is endangered** because it has lost most of its habitat across the Yaqui River basin and in Arizona, and it survives only in a few springs and wells, including those at the San Bernardino Ranch.
2. **Historical land use drove decline.** Cattle grazing, water diversion, and drought reduced water and fish numbers, and introduced mosquito fish later displaced native species.
3. **Conservation success is partial but real.** The Yaqui chub population rose from about twenty survivors in 1979 to 30,000, showing that protected habitat can allow recovery.
4. **Restoration is planned.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service intends to restore tall grass, cane thickets, and mucky bogs, and to remove cattle.
5. **Biodiversity is being homogenized.** The text argues that a few species increasingly dominate ecosystems, citing the replacement of Great Plains grasses with wheat and the spread of thistle and mesquite on the San Bernardino.
6. **Arguments for conservation:** The text lists aesthetic, stability, moral, and economic (genetic and medical) reasons for saving species, but its own preferred argument is that "a world with less life is less of a world."
