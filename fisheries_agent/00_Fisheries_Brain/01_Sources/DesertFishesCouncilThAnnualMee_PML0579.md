# Desert Fishes Council 2008 Annual Meeting Abstracts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is the abstract book of the 40th Annual Meeting of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (November 2008), a volume of oral and poster presentation abstracts arranged alphabetically by first author. The abstracts cover desert fish conservation, population biology, habitat ecology, genetics and taxonomy, and aquatic invertebrates and amphibians that share desert waters. The bulk of the work concerns freshwater fishes of the Desert Southwest and northern Mexico. Recurring themes include the threats posed by non-native species, the effects of altered flow and climate, and the value of captive propagation, habitat restoration and multi-agency partnerships.

## Background & Description

The Desert Fishes Council is a professional society devoted to the conservation of desert fishes. The meeting volume is organized as a series of short abstracts from agencies (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Park Service, state wildlife agencies), universities, non-governmental organisations (such as [[World Wildlife Fund]] Mexico and Trout Unlimited) and tribal authorities. Most contributions are one-paragraph summaries. A few are non-research items: a partnership call-to-action ("Desert Fish Habitat Partnership"), a Mexican conservation program report, and a state status report. Several abstracts are in Spanish with English parallels.

Taxa addressed include native sucker, chub, pupfish, minnow, trout, sculpin, killifish and livebearer species, with a smaller number of invertebrates (giant water bug, endemic springsnails and Cuatro Cienegas stromatolites) and amphibians (leopard frog, Mohave toad).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Many abstracts describe desert aquatic systems as isolated and highly modified. Spring-fed oases and cienegas (for example [[Ash Meadows]], [[Cuatro Cienegas]] and the Sierra El Aguaje) host endemic biota that are restricted to small areas. Threats repeatedly named include:

- **Non-native species:** [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], red swamp crayfish, brown trout, and tilapia.
- **Habitat loss and alteration:** dams, groundwater pumping, flow regulation, grazing, mining and geothermal development.
- **Climate change and drought:** projected warming and drying in the desert Southwest.
- **Disease and water quality:** golden alga (*Prymnesium parvum*) outbreaks on the lower Pecos River and salinity effects on pupfish.

Several studies document recovery where flow was restored or exotics removed, for example at Fossil Creek and in Ash Meadows.

## Key Findings

### Restoration and Management

- **School Spring, Ash Meadows, Nevada:** Concrete refuge ponds were removed and a semi-natural stream channel built. Monitoring showed that the red swamp crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*) and mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) were eradicated, and the [[Warm Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*) was flourishing. Reintroduction of thermal endemic invertebrates was in progress.
- **Fossil Creek and Cuatro Cienegas:** Restoring flow with exotic fish removal produced a reported fifty-fold increase in native fish, versus about threefold from flow alone where exotics never invaded. Native fish did not increase where exotics remained.
- **Upper Rio Yaqui watershed (Arizona and Sonora):** The Cuenca Los Ojos Foundation used gabions, vegetation establishment and upland restoration. These produced a dramatic increase in perennial wetland extent and the expansion of native fish populations.
- **Devils Hole Pupfish:** Captive propagation was moved to the University of Arizona, with work on water temperature, egg isolation to avoid cannibalism, and algae and bacteria effects.
- **Ash Meadows *Ambrysus* species:** Habitat additions raised densities of *A. amargosus* two- to three-fold in restored channels, while *A. relictus* declined or remained stable.
- **Ash Creek, Arizona:** Five native fish species were reintroduced. The project took over five years and led to a policy change making future reintroductions easier.
- **Apodaca Monterrey Platy (*Xiphophorus couchianus*):** The first reintroduction of this extinct-in-the-wild subspecies took place in Apodaca, Nuevo León.
- **Spinedace in Beaver Dam Wash:** After flood-driven overtopping of Schroeder Dam, translocated adult spinedace expanded upstream and showed evidence of recruitment.
- **Conchos trout (*Oncorhynchus* sp.):** Conservation strategies and a management plan at Ejido Panalachi included stream closure to fishing and a participatory committee. CONANP and SAGARPA support continues, including broodstock at the Guachochi aquaculture center.
- **California golden trout (South Fork Kern River):** A recovery program started in 1959 removed non-native brown trout, which had outnumbered golden trout more than 100:1 in some areas.

### Population Biology and Behaviour

- **Razorback sucker, Lake Mead:** Recruitment continues despite non-native fish. Lake elevation appears to influence recruitment, and 24 recruits had been produced at the time of reporting.
- **Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*), Marble Canyon:** Young fish overwintering in the mainstem Colorado River grew larger from dam-tailwater warmth, suggesting a survival advantage from dam-regulated water.
- **Rio Grande silvery minnow:** In a PIT-tag study, control survival at day 32 was about 99%, fish implanted by incision about 81%, and by injection about 50%. A 280-fish incision group held 49 days had survival and tag retention of about 90%. Tag retention was not related to size, and PIT tags were judged reliable for fish greater than 60 mm standard length.
- **Mohave tui chub:** In a mesocosm trial with eight chub and 75 mosquitofish over four months, chub growth was significantly higher with mosquitofish present, contrary to expectation.
- **June sucker (*Chasmistes liorus*):** Spawning adults and larvae were documented in smaller tributaries of Utah Lake. Stream rehabilitation of these tributaries may increase spawning habitat and recruitment.
- **Gambusia mating behaviour:** Males became more aggressive as the sex ratio became more male-biased. Males spread copulations across more females when females were in the majority.
- **Coahuilan box turtle:** Mark-recapture showed frequent inter-wetland dispersal locally. Nine microsatellite loci across seven subpopulations gave a global F_ST of 0.01 with isolation by distance.
- **Predator recognition in June sucker:** June sucker did not innately recognise bass odour but could learn to associate it with predation risk, though the association was short-lived.
- **Trammel net stress (Arizona fishes):** Plasma cortisol was used to assess stress in razorback sucker, bonytail and roundtail chub at 15, 20 and 25 °C after two hours in trammel nets.

### Genetics, Taxonomy and Diversity

- ***Micropterus* phylogeny:** Eight major clades were recovered, and eight putative hybridisation or introgression events were identified. Sequence divergence was 1–2%. Cryptic diversity was suggested in Gulf Coast *M. punctulatus*.
- ***Ictalurus* hybridisation:** Only three Yaqui catfish populations are known in the US portion of the range, and the Twin Pond and Big Tank populations were confirmed uncontaminated.
- **Cyprinodon body shape:** Salinity was an important factor in streamlining, but ecological variation within species was more pronounced than between species.
- **Fundulus sp. nov.:** 498 specimens were examined from the Río Soto La Marina basin. Morphometric and meristic data supported a new species distinct from *F. grandis*.
- **Sierra El Aguaje oases (Sonora):** Eight oases yielded more than 150 aquatic invertebrate species and four vertebrates (two frogs, one snake, one fish) from four habitat types. Several new invertebrate species were likely collected.

### Inventories and Status Reports

- **Coahuila freshwater fishes:** 41 species in 26 genera and 13 families were recorded from 48 locations. Thirteen species are listed under NOM-059 and several new exotic records were noted.
- **Zacatecas (NW):** 35 collections at 30 localities over three years yielded 22 species in 17 genera and 8 families, including about 10 exotics. Several species are listed as threatened or endangered under NOM-059.
- **Texas fishes database:** 5,876 freshwater records and 20,664 localities, reflecting uneven sampling that peaked between 1950 and 1970.
- **Standard Methods for Sampling North American Freshwater Fishes:** Drawing on over 4,000 datasets from 42 states and provinces, the volume provides standardised sampling methods.
- **Native fish, New Mexico (2007–2008):** Monitoring covered Rio Grande silvery minnow, Pecos bluntnose shiner, gray redhorse and blue sucker, with captive propagation and restoration projects.
- **Lower Pecos catostomids:** Blue sucker has been extirpated from the mainstem Pecos, and gray redhorse persists only in a short fragment near Carlsbad. Golden alga outbreaks are a continuing threat.
- **Borax Lake chub (Oregon):** Mark-recapture estimates achieved about ±14–20% precision and could detect a 26–34% decline in abundance, while snorkel surveys were insensitive.
- **Moapa dace:** Water rights applications for Coyote Spring Valley led to a Section 7 consultation with the USFWS and a monitoring programme. Dace numbers declined despite stream restoration.
- **Least chub:** About 9,000 were reintroduced to five sites in Box Elder and Cache counties, and a pilot trial tested its use in mosquito abatement.
- **June sucker recovery (Utah):** About 68,000 juveniles (about 200 mm TL) were reintroduced to Utah Lake, with 37,000 reared at FES and 31,000 in grow-out ponds.

### Other Notable Results

- **Lake Malawi fish fossils:** Stable carbon isotope values ranged from about −9.5‰ to −27.5‰, suggesting fish fossils can serve as palaeoclimate and palaeo-lake indicators.
- **Salinity and bullfrog control:** Exposure of bullfrog eggs and tadpoles to 6 ppt salinity reduced survival, whereas 3 ppt had no effect, suggesting a method to remove bullfrogs from ponds with native fishes.
- **Leopard frogs in mountain canyons:** Occupied pools had more perimeter vegetation, more canopy cover and more refugia than unoccupied pools.
- **Partnership call:** The Desert Fish Habitat Partnership, spanning Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Nevada, New Mexico, Oregon, Texas, Utah and Wyoming, invited data sharing and funding partners.
- **Devils Hole Pupfish climate risk:** Models projected warming and drying in the desert Southwest, which may increase pupfish metabolic demand and lower water levels through groundwater pumping.
