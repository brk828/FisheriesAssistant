# Studies of Smallmouth Black Bass (*Micropterus dolomieu*) in the Columbia River Near Richland, Washington

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper (reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, vol. 86, 1956) by [[Croswell Henderson]] of the [[U.S. Public Health Service]] and [[Richard F. Foster]] of the [[General Electric Company]] reports on a population of [[Smallmouth Bass]] in a cold-water reach of the [[Columbia River]] near [[Hanford Site]], Washington. The bass population arose from a transplantation program by the [[Richland Rod and Gun Club]] that moved adult fish from restricted reactor-area sloughs to the lower [[Yakima River]] (1950–1953). The same work supplied data on age, growth, spawning, movement and radionuclide uptake, which were collected to assess the effects of [[Reactor Effluent]] on river organisms.

## Background & Description

- **Study population:** Smallmouth bass were rare in the region. Anglers were few before World War II. Construction of the [[Hanford Reactors]] from 1942 restricted public access, and the new workforce, many of them avid bass anglers, depleted the unrestricted population within a few years.
- **Transplant project:** Adult bass from four sloughs near the old Hanford townsite were caught on weekends in late April and May, marked, measured, sexed, sampled for scales, and released in the lower Yakima River. The project served three purposes: removing fish from restricted areas, adding brood stock to the Yakima, and providing life-history data.
- **Marking:** Left ventral fin clipping (1950), right ventral (1951), upper caudal (1952), left ventral (1953), plus aluminum disk tags in 1951. Disk tags performed poorly. Fin clips remained recognizable for about three years before regeneration obscured them.
- **Sport fishery context:** Largemouth bass had been widely introduced to semi-desert lakes and potholes. Smallmouth bass were restricted mainly to the Columbia and mouths of large tributaries. Fishing was concentrated in spring slough areas, and [[Trout]], [[Steelhead]] and [[Salmon]] dominated regional angling interest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions (main stem, as summarized from [[Water Quality Studies]]):**
- Discharge: about 300,000–500,000 cfs May–July; 50,000–70,000 cfs for the rest of the year. Tributary peaks occur about a month earlier.
- Turbidity: generally below 7 ppm except during high flow.
- Temperature: about 36°F (February–March) to 68°F (August–September) in the main stem; tributaries reached 75°F.
- Dissolved oxygen: at or near saturation year-round.
- pH 7.6–8.2; alkalinity 51–81 ppm.

**Food base:**
- Phytoplankton: over 90 percent diatoms (*Asterionella*, *Cyclotella*, *Fragilaria*, *Synedra*). *Asterionella* dominates in spring; *Cyclotella* in late summer and fall, pulsed by tributary inputs.
- Benthos: clean-water forms, including mayfly nymphs, midge and caddisfly larvae, and mollusks. Tributaries held about twice the main-river abundance. The river was classed as average to rich in fish food organisms.
- Fish community: migratory salmonids (Chinook, sockeye), steelhead, [[White Sturgeon]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], and abundant forage and rough fish including suckers, carp, shiners, [[Columbia Squawfish]], chiselmouth, dace, sculpins and chubs.

**Spawning and early life:**
- Adults entered slough areas in April–May, where water warmed to 55–60°F compared with 45–50°F in the main channel.
- Early nests (April) failed when a rise in river level brought colder water (54°F on May 3). Eggs became fungus-coated and were abandoned.
- Successful hatching occurred July 15–August 1 at 60–75°F. Poor fry survival was attributed to predation by squawfish and other rough species.
- Some successful spawning occurred in the lower Yakima (fry observed early July at 66°F) and in several Columbia River reaches.

## Key Findings

**Growth and population structure**
- The average bass caught was 15.7 inches long and weighed 40 ounces.
- Ages ranged from 3 to 11 years (based on 225 fish, 1952). Seven- and eight-year-old fish made up about half the catch.
- Females were larger and more numerous in the catch. Females averaged 16.3 in and 44 oz, males 14.9 in and 33 oz. In 1952, 129 of 219 spring fish were female. In 1953, 109 of 173 were female.
- Growth was rapid despite a short season and water temperatures below the species' optimum. Most growth occurred July–October. The authors linked this to abundant benthos, a large forage fish base, and low competition from spiny-rayed species. Growth was faster than most reported populations, with few faster in lakes.
- Fifteen largemouth bass taken in spring 1952 averaged 15.5 in and 38 oz. Their growth was similar to smallmouth, which the authors attributed to cold water.

**Transplant results (Table 1 summary, 1950–1953)**
- Fishing effort: 270–642 man-hours per period across four seasons.
- Total catches: 191–313 bass per season, mainly smallmouth in 1951–1953.
- Catch per man-hour ranged from 0.30 to 0.82. Catch per man-day ranged from 1.8 to 4.0.
- The 1951 tagged group (35 recaptured, about 20 percent) showed mostly downstream movement during high water. Some fish moved over 40 miles and many returned to their original capture sites. In 1953, about 11 percent of fish were previously marked individuals, suggesting a low local population.

**Spawning**
- Spawning success was very poor overall, mainly due to fluctuating stages and temperatures, and predation by squawfish.

**Radionuclide uptake (1952 data)**
- Radiophosphorus (³²P, half-life 14.3 days) was the principal isotope. It made up less than 1 percent of water activity but over 90 percent of fish activity, a concentration of many thousandfold.
- Scales and bone were about ten times more radioactive than skin and muscle (the edible tissues). Stomach contents were higher than all tissues.
- Bass activity peaked in September and was lowest in April. It tracked temperature and food organism activity rather than water activity.
- Radioactivity was higher in phytoplankton than in herbivorous insect larvae, then juvenile fish, and lowest in adult bass at the top of the food chain.

**Conclusions**
- No adverse effects of reactor effluent on bass or food organisms were detected.
- Radioactive contamination of bass was far below levels hazardous to human consumers.
- Because the isotopes have short half-lives, no buildup occurred year to year. The authors nonetheless stressed that bioconcentration must be considered when setting safe operating practices for atomic energy installations.
