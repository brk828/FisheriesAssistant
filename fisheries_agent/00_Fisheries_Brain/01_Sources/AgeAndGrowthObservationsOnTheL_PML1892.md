# Age and Growth of Largemouth Bass in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 University of Nevada, Las Vegas master's thesis by C. O. Minckley estimates age, growth, condition and length-weight relationships of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in [[Lake Mead]], Nevada. Using scale-based aging on 876 fish from three locations and three collection years, the study finds that growth in the first year of life rose sharply after the 1954 introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]], while growth in the second and third years fell. Growth depended on food availability, water temperature and reservoir level, and the thesis concludes that Lake Mead largemouth bass grow fast in their first year but by age group II are similar in length to bass in other U.S. waters.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Largemouth Bass]] was introduced into Lake Mead between 1935 and 1942 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Jones and Sumner, 1954). It supplies the majority of the angler catch and was expected to be exploited more heavily in future years.
- **Rationale:** Aging and growth data are described as essential to understanding fish population dynamics. The study used the [[Scale Method of Aging]] to determine age groups, size intervals, a [[Length-Weight Relationship]] and a [[Condition Factor]].
- **Study sites:** Samples came from [[Las Vegas Bay]] (UNLV collections, September 1970 to June 1971), and from [[Temple Bar]] (1959) and [[Boulder Beach]] (1963), both collected by the Nevada Department of Fish and Game during creel census.
- **Funding:** Partial support came from a Nevada Department of Fish and Game grant to Dr. J. E. Deacon on effluent from the [[Las Vegas Wash]] and its effect on bass in Las Vegas Bay.

### Methods

- **Collection:** UNLV used [[Electrofishing]] at night from a 20-foot boat with a 2,500-watt generator, copper electrodes and two 150-watt lights. Scales were taken from the left side above the lateral line.
- **Measurement:** Nevada Department of Fish and Game fork lengths were converted to total length with a factor of 1.025 (Jones and Sumner, 1954).
- **Aging:** Scales were impressed on cellulose acetate slides, projected at 32X, and three to six scales were read per fish. Measurements were averaged per fish.
- **Back-calculation:** Length at each annulus was calculated from a [[Body Length-Scale Relationship]] fitted by least-squares regression, a method related to the [[Lee-Fraser Method]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Annulus Formation

- Annuli were read from the anterior scale margin. A distance of 50 mm or less was taken to indicate recent [[Annulus Formation]].
- In all three collections, annulus formation began between January and May. The primary period was February in 1959 and March in 1971.
- Most Las Vegas Bay bass had not yet formed an annulus by May 1971, so much of the apparent first-year growth included winter and spring growth.

### Temperature and Food

- Annulus formation was linked to [[Water Temperature]], food supply and reproductive condition. The thesis cites [[Swingle 1949]], who gave 15.6 °C as the temperature at which warmwater fish growth begins.
- Winter diet in Las Vegas Bay was mainly chironomids. From spring through fall, bass switched to threadfin shad, which they ate most heavily in spring, fall and summer, their periods of maximum growth (Deacon et al., 1972).
- Surface temperatures at the intake tower of Lake Mead often stayed above 15 °C for 10 months or more, so a near year-round growing season was possible in some years.

### Water Level

- Water levels, as shown in [[Lake Mead Water Levels]], were exceptionally high in 1952. Growth of age group 0 fish was nearly twice that of any other pre-shad year.
- In 1963, lake levels dropped sharply, and growth in length in older Boulder Beach fish increased.
- The thesis suggests rising water through spring and summer benefits young-of-year growth but is not essential.

## Key Findings

- **Sample base:** Scales from 876 largemouth bass were used. Three age groups were represented in all samples, and the largest sample was from Las Vegas Bay (1970 to 1971).
- **Body length-scale relationship:** Linear for all locations. Correlation coefficients were 0.93 (Temple Bar), 0.93 (Boulder Beach) and 0.91 (Las Vegas Bay). Slopes differed significantly at the 95% confidence level (t-test, seven degrees of freedom).
- **Oldest fish:** The oldest fish reported from Lake Mead was eight years old (Jones and Sumner, 1954). Jones and Sumner also reported fish up to five years old fairly often, and the average age entering the angler catch was 2 to 3 years.
- **Shad effect on first-year growth:** After the threadfin shad introduction in December 1954, age group 0 growth nearly doubled. The thesis attributes this to larval shad, which are within the size range of food for small bass.
- **Shad effect on later growth:** Age group II growth before shad introduction was about twice that observed afterward. Pre-1953 age group II increments were 84 to 157 mm, versus 9 to 50 mm after 1953.
- **Mean lengths, 1970 to 1971:** Age group 0 fish averaged 133 mm in October 1970 and 199 mm in February 1971. About 200 mm was reached by 1 January 1971.
- **Statistical tests:** Differences in growth increments among Temple Bar, Boulder Beach and Las Vegas Bay (1956 to 1971) were not significant. Differences among age groups were significant at all locations. Sex differences in Las Vegas Bay were not significant. Pre- and post-shad growth differed significantly at the 95% level.
- **Comparison with other waters:** Pre-shad growth was lower than in other U.S. areas for the first two years. After 1955, growth was comparable. Post-shad first-year fish were larger than fish from other areas, but second- and third-year fish were similar.
- **Condition factor:** The optimal value is 3.0. Literature values range from 0.92 (Kramer and Smith, 1960) to 1.40 (Clugston, 1964). Lake Mead bass fall within this range. Las Vegas Bay relative condition was 1.018, and overall values were roughly 1.01. Condition did not differ significantly among areas, but Boulder Beach had the highest mean.
- **Length-weight relationships (W = aL^n):**
  - 1959 (Temple Bar): log W = −4.5970 + 2.9957 log L (n ≈ 3.00, near isometric).
  - 1963 (Boulder Beach): log W = −1.0068 + 2.2477 log L (n = 2.2477, the lowest; interpreted as more length than weight growth).
  - 1971 (Las Vegas Bay): log W = −1.9398 + 2.6208 log L (n = 2.6208).
  - The n value increased with age in most collections. Differences among age groups, sexes and locations were statistically significant only between sexes.
- **Monthly variation:** The constant n dropped in March and April, which the thesis attributes to spawning and weight loss.
- **Conclusion:** Total lengths varied among age groups but not significantly among locations. Growth was always highest in age group 0 fish. Growth slowed in age groups I and II relative to earlier studies, and the thesis suggests food conversion, prey selection and food availability as causes.
