# Monitoring Plan for Native Fish Sanctuaries in the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a draft monitoring plan, with accompanying review correspondence, for [[Native Fish Sanctuaries]] in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. It was prepared by Jeanette Carpenter, Joan Thullen and Gordon Mueller of the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] (Denver Field Unit) and circulated for comment in July 2006. The plan aims to identify the physical, chemical and biological factors that make an off-channel habitat succeed for [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]], and to give managers the information needed to refine management, operating costs and construction design. Monitoring is planned annually or semi-annually (fall to winter) to limit cost and disturbance. The plan is built on the success of the [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] and uses fish communities as the main indicator of sanctuary health. Reviewers (notably Paul Marsh of [[Arizona State University]]) praised the plan but asked for a clearer ranking system, a summary table of resource needs, better explanation of how historical data will be used, and added detail on sampling design.

## Background & Description

- **Focal species:** [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Catostomidae) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*, Cyprinidae). Both are native to the Colorado River and are the subject of recovery efforts.
- **Conservation rationale:** The [[Conservation Plan for Native Fishes of the Lower Colorado River]] (Minckley et al. 2003) argues that stabilizing native populations requires isolated, secure off-channel habitats free of non-native fish. The plan states that native fish of the basin do not persist where non-natives are present.
- **Historical context:** Oxbow habitats historically supported large native fish numbers. They ranged from a few square meters to tens of hectares, and differed in depth, complexity, water quality, predator presence and isolation.
- **Sanctuary types:** Developed sanctuaries range from *repositories* (where survival of stocked adults is the goal) to *sanctuaries* (where spawning and recruitment support an expanding community). Table 1 lists 15 potential sites, including Davis Cove, Office Cove, Mohave Community College Pond, San Pedro Pond, Cibola High Levee Pond, Bulkhead Cove, Palm Lake, Hippie Hole, Iceberg Canyon, Stillman Lake, BHC Nature Center, Cocopah Tribe, Floyd Lamb State Park, Sahara Estates and Three Fingers Lake. Status ranges from "Complete" to "Incomplete" and "Nearly Complete."
- **Current fish-holding sites:** Only Davis Cove, Office Cove, Mohave Community College Pond, San Pedro Preserve Pond and Cibola High Levee Pond (Cibola HLP) hold native fish (the text lists four names in the Objectives section and five in Table 1 context; the source is inconsistent on this count).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Key water-quality stressors.** Historical fish kills or disappearances at grow-out ponds (Davis Cove, Palm Lake, Ducks Unlimited Ponds) point to dissolved oxygen (DO) and temperature as limiting factors. DO is expected to be lowest and temperatures highest in July and August. Night-time respiration by aquatic plants can also drive low DO, as can plant die-off.

**Physical requirements.** Evidence from grow-out ponds suggests a minimum depth for thermal refuge. [[Mueller (2006)]] recommends a maximum depth of at least 4 m. Adult bonytail at Cibola HLP need daytime cover. Cover and suitably sized substrate at appropriate depths may also affect spawning and recruitment.

**Food base.** Zooplankton are key food for both species, especially young-of-year fish. Phytoplankton (indicated by chlorophyll) supports zooplankton. Macroinvertebrates are important for adult fish.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Vegetation provides cover, food and habitat. Shifts in plant community (observed at Davis Cove) may affect the food web and native fish.

**Isolation and contamination.** Sites differ in isolation from non-natives. Some are separated from reservoirs full of sportfish only by a small berm (Office, Bulkhead and Davis Coves). Others, such as Iceberg Canyon and Mohave Community College Pond, are more geographically isolated. Public access, historical fishing use and land ownership also influence contamination risk. Sites with gravity drainage or pump-out capacity can be renovated without chemicals ([[Mueller 2006]]).

**Renovation.** The degree to which non-native fish can be removed depends on timing, sanctuary volume, habitat complexity and spring inflow. Reviewers recommended adding removal ease, cost, success likelihood, and needed permits and environmental compliance to the list of factors.

## Key Findings

### Monitoring Objectives and Questions
The plan poses seven questions for each site:
1. Are physical characteristics and water chemistry sufficient for long-term survival?
2. What are the growth rate and condition of the fish, and is the population stable and free of disease and non-natives?
3. Is the population spawning and recruiting?
4. What is the abundance of non-natives, are they reproducing, and what is their likely source?
5. Are spawning and nursery habitats adequate, or do they need active management?
6. Is there an appropriate food base?
7. Is aquatic vegetation healthy and stable?

### Sampling Design (Highlights)
- **Fish populations (highest priority):** Snorkel surveys in spring after stocking, where clarity allows. Fall (October–November) collections use electrofishing, minnow traps and/or trammel nets. Trammel nets in two mesh sizes target multiple size classes. Fish are weighed, measured (TL mm), sexed and checked for spawning condition and parasites. Larval light taps (4 taps, 2-hour sets, at least 3 nights) are used in spring if no young-of-year are found.
- **Water quality:** DO profiles in July–August, with MiniSondes overnight in secure habitats (including a sunrise reading to capture the DO minimum). Habitat suitability is defined as percent volume below 30 °C with DO above 3 mg/L. Hydrolab measurements of DO, temperature, pH, conductivity and salinity are taken at 0.5 m intervals. Hobo loggers record temperatures hourly for one year at 0.5 m and 1.5 m depths.
- **Physical quality:** Bathymetric mapping (existing Bureau of Reclamation maps ground-truthed or new maps made), with surface area, maximum and mean depth and volume recorded. Substrate and cover are mapped at low water. Permanent photo stations document change.
- **Plankton and macroinvertebrates:** Two 2-m vertical tows (64 µm mesh) in early spring and mid-summer. Chlorophyll is sampled in spring, summer and fall. Invertebrates are sampled at six locations in March and October to estimate abundance.
- **Aquatic vegetation:** Annual or biennial mapping of plant species and coverage by snorkel transects. Three specimens of each plant species are analyzed for contaminants such as selenium, mercury and arsenic.
- **Historical and management factors:** Review of historical records and interviews with local managers, and ranking of sites by renovation potential, isolation and public access.

### Scheduling
- **First year:** February/March (larval and zooplankton sampling, Hobo installation, macroinvertebrates); July/August (DO profiles, chemistry, zooplankton, chlorophyll); October/November (fish collection, plant and substrate mapping, aquatic plant chemistry).
- **Second year and beyond:** Optional February/March survey timed to 14–16 °C for razorback spawning. Optional April survey timed to 18 °C for bonytail spawning. Fall fish and plant surveys continue.

### Data Analysis, Reporting and Products
- Sites will be ranked numerically by current and historical success, and rankings updated annually for clients and partners. Doak et al. (2003) statistical tools may be used to assess the power and cost-effectiveness of monitoring.
- Jeanette Carpenter maintains all datasets. Annual reports are issued.
- Products include a biological database, annual reports, adaptive management plans, scientific papers and presentations.
- The plan is reviewed annually.

### Reviewer Comments (Marsh, August 2006)
- Cite additional plans: the USFWS lower river plan, the LCR-MSCP and the BioMesa backwater plan.
- Clarify the ranking system (described on pages 3 and 9) and how historical and interview data will be applied.
- Note that growth estimation becomes harder for older fish with slowed growth. Growth data can also identify missing age classes.
- Add renovation and permitting factors to the list of other factors.
- Specify who reviews the program.
- For mark-recapture, use one gear for the initial sample and another for the recapture (electrofishing for the first, netting for the second).
- Add a column to Table 1 showing which species are planned for each facility.
- Provide a summary table or timeline covering tasks, duration, personnel, dates and costs for the 15 sites.
- The flow chart's "survival" criterion may be insufficient. Survival must meet or exceed a target for the population to persist.

**Survival scenario flagged for review:** The email uses an example of 1,000 fish at 50% survival, giving "62 fish in the first 5 years" and "1–2 fish in 10 years." Annual 50% survival would give about 31 fish after 5 years. The 62-fish figure matches four years of survival, so the interval should be checked.
