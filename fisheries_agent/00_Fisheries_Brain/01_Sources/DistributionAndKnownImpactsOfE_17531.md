# Distribution and Known Impacts of Exotic Fishes in Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Salvador Contreras-B. and Marco A. Escalante-C. reviews the introduction, distribution, status and known impacts of nonnative fishes in Mexico. The authors compile **55 exotic fishes**: **26 of foreign origin** and **29 transplants** (fishes moved within Mexico or from a foreign part of a range that extends into the country). Most introductions were made for food (aquaculture), bait, ornamental (aquarium) release, sport, or mosquito control, and were rarely evaluated for ecological risk. Evidence of negative impacts is described as circumstantial and usually entangled with habitat loss, dams and other stressors. The best-documented consequences are the replacement of native fishes in northern Mexican drainages, hybridization with endemics, and predation that pushed endemic species toward extinction. The authors conclude that a national protocol for introductions is needed to balance protein-supply goals with protection of native biota.

## Background & Description

**Scope and sources.** Prior compilations were limited: Arredondo (1973) and Escalante and Contreras (in press) covered 19 introduced food and aquarium species, mostly from official records. Early references include Chazari (1884) on culture of [[Rainbow Trout]], brown trout and carps; Meek (1904) on [[Goldfish]] and [[Common Carp]] in Valley of Mexico lakes and a trout hatchery at Lerma; and De Buen (1941), the first Mexican paper on impacts, documenting the effect of [[Largemouth Bass]] on pescado blanco (*Chirostoma* spp.) in [[Lago De Patzcuaro]].

**Terminology.** The authors define *exotic* as introduced or nonnative fishes beyond historical range due to human movement. They subdivide these into *foreign-origin* fishes and *transplants*, a distinction they argue is useful when communicating with agencies that manage stocking.

**Purpose categories.** Introductions were classified using the four primary reasons treated by [[Hubbs Purpose Categories]] (bait release, ornamental disposal, aquaculture including forage and weed control, and sport), plus two added by the authors: species protection and mosquito control.

**Status categories.** Species were rated as *reproductive* (breeding in at least one site), *nonreproductive* (failed to establish a breeding population), *unsuccessful* (established and later died out), or *unknown*.

**Taxonomic framework.** Accounts follow the phylogenetic order of Robins et al. (1980). Each account gives native range, Mexican records, release date, source, purpose, status and impact.

**Table 6-1** lists all 55 species with origin, status and purpose codes. Families represented include Clupeidae, Salmonidae, Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Cobitidae, Ictaluridae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Atherinidae, Percichthyidae, Centrarchidae and Cichlidae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Niche concept.** The authors reject the common claim that introduced fishes fill a "vacant niche." In their view, a species' niche is the sum of its activities and responses, so an absent species leaves at most vacant trophic levels, never a vacant niche. The Mexican species list is also acknowledged to be incomplete, because many records name only genera (e.g., *Tilapia*, *Lepomis*), species groups such as *charal* and *blanco*, or aquarium fishes mentioned in newspapers.

**Native fish replacement.** Contreras (1969, 1975, 1978) and Contreras et al. (1976) documented declines of native fishes at multiple sites, including [[Casas Grandes Basin]], [[Bustillos]], [[Cuatro Cienegas]], [[Camargo]], Lerdo, Peña del Águila, Parras, San Juan del Río and [[Monterrey]] area localities. **Table 6-2** summarizes native and exotic species counts through time at nine localities. The authors caution that dams, channelization, drying, intense agriculture, urbanization and highway construction were also present, so exotics may not be the main driver at every site.

**Gene swamping and hybridization.** Introduced [[Xiphophorus Hybridization]] species (*X. helleri*, *X. maculatus*, *X. variatus*) produced massive hybridization with the endangered endemic *X. couchianus* at La Peñita near Monterrey, one of its three surviving populations. [[Cichlasoma Cyanoguttatum]] hybridized with an endemic *Cichlasoma* at Cuatro Ciénegas.

**Predation and displacement.** Largemouth bass introduced at El Potosí, Nuevo León, had a major impact on *Megupsilon aporus* and, to a lesser degree, *Cyprinodon alvarezi*, bringing endemics close to extinction (Contreras 1978).

**Unexpected biological effects.** A tilapia introduced into the Balsas basin was attacked by a nematode parasite that was native to *Cichlasoma istlanum*. The parasite nearly destroyed the planned fishery, while the tilapia almost eliminated the native cichlid (Rosas 1976b). Some introduced tilapias also shifted from herbivory to carnivory when infested by parasites.

**Dispersal into the Rio Colorado and Rio Grande.** Species such as [[Red Shiner]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Threadfin Shad]] are linked to the disappearance of most native fishes in the lower [[Rio Colorado]], though causation is not proven.

## Key Findings

**1. Scale of introductions.** Mexico has at least 55 exotic fishes (26 foreign, 29 transplants). The conclusion section reports reproductive populations for 41 species, 1 nonreproducing species, 3 unsuccessful species and 11 species of unknown status. These figures total 56, one more than the 55 species listed, a discrepancy present in the source.

**2. Principal sources and purposes.**
- Aquaculture food fish dominate, including [[Common Carp]], [[Grass Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Blue Tilapia]], [[Redbelly Tilapia]], and *Chirostoma* silversides.
- Bait release (e.g., [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]]) is a recurring pathway, usually from fishermen.
- Ornamental releases (*Xiphophorus* spp., *Poecilia* spp., *Barbus*, *Carassius*) occurred in cities such as [[Monterrey]]. In 1967 children released aquarium fishes into the Río Santa Catarina during a municipal aquarium opening.
- [[Mosquitofish]] was introduced for mosquito control, and [[Rainbow Trout]] and brook trout for sport.

**3. Exotic status by species.**
- [[Common Carp]] (established) is linked to native fish disappearance at multiple Chihuahua, Durango, Coahuila and Querétaro sites. It was introduced in 1872-73, with Israeli carp imported in 1956.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (established) caused damage to the Patzcuaro fishery and predation and displacement of endemics at Potosí, Cuatro Ciénegas and several Chihuahua and Durango localities.
- [[Grass Carp]] (established only at Cupatitzio, Michoacán, since 1974-75) was introduced in 1964 or 1965 from the People's Republic of China for weed control and food.
- [[Goldfish]] was judged "may not be so detrimental as common carp," though it co-occurs with carp.
- [[Mosquitofish]] (established) is associated with native fish replacement in the Río Casas Grandes.
- [[Channel Catfish]] (established) appears associated with replacement of native fishes in the Río Colorado. Escapes from aquaculture facilities are the source.

**4. Failed or unsuccessful introductions.** Rosy barb and cherry barb (released 1967 in Monterrey) were unsuccessful. Golden shiner was unsuccessful, and two Gila chubs introduced to Baja California from California were failed (*Gila bicolor mohavensis*, *G. orcutti*). The oriental weatherfish was introduced before 1961 to Chapingo but has not been seen since the hatchery closed.

**5. Impact evidence.** Negative impacts are documented as circumstantial (Contreras and colleagues) and rarely tested directly. Clear cases include gene swamping in the *Xiphophorus* complex at La Peñita, Cichlasoma hybridization at Cuatro Ciénegas, and predation-driven declines at El Potosí. The authors note that little direct study of exotic-native interactions has been conducted.

**6. Official perspective.** The Mexican government measures success of introductions in economic and social terms, especially the supply of protein for a largely protein-deficient population, and does not systematically assess impacts on native biota or habitats.

**7. Recommendation.** The authors call for a **protocol for introductions** developed by Mexican biologists. It should serve citizens' nutritional needs while protecting native communities, and an approach is outlined in Kohler and Stanley (chapter 18). They also recommend that stocking agencies record release dates, sources and purposes, since many early records are incomplete.

**8. Future risk.** Additional species can be expected in Mexican waters because several exotics are already established near the border.
