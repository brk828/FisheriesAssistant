# Trout Angling Under Special and Normal Regulations on the North Branch Au Sable River, 1958-63

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares angling and trout populations in three sections of the [[North Branch Au Sable River]] in [[Michigan]] under two sets of rules. [[Normal Regulations]] (7-inch minimum size, ten-fish creel limit, any lure) and [[Special Regulations]] (9-inch minimum size, five-fish creel limit, artificial flies only) were alternated across 3-year periods from 1958 to 1963. The special rules halved fishing pressure, and the fish larger than 9 inches were not harvested in greater numbers. Higher adult populations under special rules shrank to normal levels by the next season because of nonangling mortality. The authors conclude that streams with adequate natural reproduction give the most sport under normal rules.

## Background & Description

The study site is the [[North Branch Au Sable River]], about 225 miles north of Detroit in Michigan's Lower Peninsula. The headwaters lie east of Otsego Lake at roughly 1,275 feet elevation, and the river drops about 200 feet over 33 miles to its junction with the main Au Sable. Above an old lumber dam (Dam 2), the river receives outflows from several warmwater lakes, so it is described as marginal trout water.

The study species were [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*). Brook trout made up most fish under 9 inches, while most trout over 9 inches were brown trout. Catches of the two species were combined for most analyses.

The experiment tested whether stricter rules improved angling quality and trout numbers, and whether any differences reflected regulation or habitat. Three sections were compared:

| Section | Length | 1958-60 | 1961-63 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper (Dam 2 to County Line) | 4.2 mi | Normal | Special |
| Middle (County Line to Eaman's) | 6.9 mi | Special | Normal |
| Lower (Eaman's to Kellogg Bridge) | 8.7 mi | Special | Special (control) |

Reversing the rules in the upper and middle sections allowed the authors to separate regulation effects from habitat differences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The middle section was described as having the best all-around trout habitat, and the authors found its angling quality was best regardless of the regulation in force. The river's trout reproduction appeared sufficient to stock the available habitat up to its present carrying capacity, which limited how much extra survival better regulations could produce.

Population estimates were made with [[Petersen Mark-And-Recapture]] using [[Direct-Current Electrofishing]] in sample sections 700 to 1,300 feet long. Minnows, muddlers and some rough fish were present but excluded from the estimates. The number of sample sections rose from one per section in 1957-58, to two in 1959-62, to three from fall 1962.

Creel data came from a [[Stratified Random Creel Census]]. The stream was divided into 18 subsections of about one mile each. Angler counts were taken in three 5-hour periods per day, with 50% of samples on weekends and holidays. Clerks also interviewed anglers about catch and effort.

The authors cite [[Hooking Mortality]] of sublegal trout, nonangling mortality, and overwinter losses as factors that offset regulation gains.

## Key Findings

**Angling pressure and harvest**

- The abstract reports fishing pressure reduced by 50% in special-regulation sections.
- In the upper section, angling effort fell from 1,007 to 541 hours per mile (the authors estimate the drop was significant at about 81%).
- In the middle section, effort rose 82% under normal rules, from 1,939 to 3,427 hours per mile (significant at the 99.5% level).
- In the upper section, the catch of trout over 9.0 inches was about the same under both rule sets (116 per mile under special versus 123 per mile of fish over 9 inches under normal). The total creel under normal rules was 541 trout per mile, about four times the special-rule figure.
- In the middle section, the total creel was 1,333 trout per mile under special rules and 1,542 under normal rules. The difference between creeled totals was significant at the 92% level.
- The lower section's pressure and catch varied little between periods. Its pressure changes were not statistically significant.

**Catch per hour**

- In the upper section, catch per hour of 7.0-8.9 inch and 9.0-inch-plus trout was significantly higher during the special-rule years (1961-63).
- In the middle section, catch per hour of 7.0-8.9 inch trout was higher under special rules. Catch per hour of 9-inch-plus trout did not differ significantly between periods.
- Catch per hour of trout over 7 inches was measurably higher under normal rules than catch per hour of trout over 9 inches under special rules.

**Fish size**

- Trout over 16 inches were seen in the catch from normal waters more often than from special waters.
- In the upper section, trout over 9 inches averaged about 11.0 inches under normal rules and about 10.2 inches under special rules (values read from the OCR table). The difference was statistically significant but small.
- Length-frequency comparisons of brown trout across the 9.0-21.9 inch classes found no significant difference between water types (chi-square = 5.316, 4 df, P = 74%). Brook trout in the 9.0-12.9 inch range also showed no significant difference (chi-square = 0.824, 1 df, P = 61%).

**Fall populations**

- In the upper section, the 7.0-8.9 inch population rose from 305 to 786 trout per mile under special rules (P = 99%). Changes in other size groups were small and not significant.
- In the middle section, trout over 9.0 inches were significantly more numerous in 1958-60 under special rules (P = 88%) than in 1961-63 under normal rules.
- Trout 6.9 inches and under showed no significant change after the reversals in either section. The authors read this as evidence that young-of-the-year abundance is controlled by factors other than adult numbers. They cite [[Latta]] (1965), who found no relationship between adult brook trout and surviving young in the Pigeon River.

**Conclusions**

- Higher adult populations under special rules did not translate into higher angler yield. Lower pressure and winter losses to natural mortality were the main reasons.
- Special rules produced a temporary stockpile of trout. Without better carrying capacity or reduced natural mortality, the stockpile was short-lived.
- Fly-only restrictions probably had little effect on fall populations, because reduced hooking mortality did not change them measurably.
- The authors conclude that Michigan trout streams with adequate natural reproduction provide the most sport for the most anglers under [[Normal Regulations]]. Bag-limit differences will not significantly affect the total creel until nonangling mortality is reduced.
