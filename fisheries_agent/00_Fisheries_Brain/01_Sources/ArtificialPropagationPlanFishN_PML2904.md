# Artificial Propagation Plan: Endangered Fish Needs, Upper Colorado River Basin (1993–1998)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This October 1992 draft, issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Mountain-Prairie Region) through its Propagation Coordinator, J. Holt Williamson, is a skeletal draft operational [[Artificial Propagation]] plan for four endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. It defines five-year fish needs (1993–1998) for artificial refuges, back-up refuges, broodstocks, research/development, and stocking. It also summarizes current and potential holding facilities.

The plan was built at a work group meeting in Grand Junction, Colorado, on September 1, 1992. Its numbers rest on assumptions the participants themselves flagged for scrutiny, chiefly a 20 percent annual mortality rate. The document explicitly states that its figures are minimum, first-cut estimates. It was submitted for review to the [[Biology Committee]] and the [[Propagation Subcommittee]].

## Background & Description

The work group was convened to develop a comprehensive artificial propagation work plan under the [[Recovery Program]] for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin. Phase one defined fish needs. Phase two, to be completed later, would address facilities capacity. The memo accompanying the draft (dated October 15, 1992) asks the Biology Committee to reconcile the plan with other documents, including the [[Genetics Management]] plan, the Colorado DOW draft Interim Recovery Objectives, and Nelson's prioritization document.

The plan's logic, drawn from the Propagation Coordinator's framework, runs in a sequence:

1. Define genetics management goals and artificial propagation goals.
2. Identify high-priority populations for artificial refuges and broodstocks.
3. Determine founder numbers (about 25 paired matings, with a 5 x 5 breeding matrix).
4. Set production targets per family lot by life stage.
5. Estimate the number of young and facility needs.

Key assumptions:
- Founder populations are drawn from wild adults, with the aim that founders mirror the donor population's genetics.
- A [[Paired Mating]] (family-lot) strategy is favored, to maximize genetic diversity within each population.
- Annual captive mortality is set at 20 percent. Utah representatives argued for a higher rate, around 30 percent. The work group adopted 20 percent as provisionally conservative, to be revised as experience with [[Captive Breeding]] accumulates.
- A target of 500 young-of-year (YOY) fish per family lot is set, with about 250 age-I, 102 age-V, and 3 age-XX fish surviving to the end of the 20-year cycle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Many Colorado River fish populations have been extirpated or have lost recruitment. The document describes the situation as urgent: populations of razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish, and humpback and bonytail chubs are at risk, and some genetic reserves could be lost within a few years.

Population status was assessed using criteria such as:
- Number of reproducing adults (N less than 250 flagged as endangered).
- Evidence of recruitment.
- Annual decline rates.
- Probability of extinction within a set time frame.

Populations were ranked into priority tiers (for example, priority 1a, 2b, and 3). The Upper Colorado River razorback sucker population (Mile 110–185) was rated the highest priority. The work group treated all razorback sucker populations as high priority for captive rearing.

Facilities are scarce. Current or interim holding sites include Horsethief Canyon, Bellevue, Wray, Ouray, and Dexter. Facilities at Ouray include the Ouray Fish Hatchery, with 27 rearing cages, and new ponds being built by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

## Key Findings

**Priority ranking of species (for recovery potential):**
1. Razorback sucker
2. Bonytail chub
3. Colorado squawfish
4. Humpback chub

**Production targets (five-year, 1993–1998), as printed:**

| Category | Young-of-year | 1+ fish | Adult (3+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total production needed (refuge needs) | 130,400 | 65,200 | 16,400 |
| Total fish needs incl. research and stocking | 196,900 | 65,900 | 16,500 |

Species breakdown for refuge production (Table 2): razorback sucker about 47,200 YOY; humpback chub about 30,000 YOY; Colorado squawfish about 53,200 YOY. Bonytail needs were "as available."

**Fish needs by activity category:**
- **Artificial refuges and back-up refuges:** primary focus; the largest share of need. Razorback sucker is the top priority.
- **Research/development:** Table 3 lists about 20 studies (chemoreception, nursery habitat, contaminants, morphology, and CS marking) for 1993–1994. Most require small numbers of fish, but some call for thousands of YOY.
- **Stocking:** three categories, with [[Experimental Stocking]], [[Supplemental Stocking]], and augmentation/restoration. Table 4 proposes roughly 100 razorback sucker, about 31,000 Colorado squawfish, and about 52,700 bonytail for augmentation over five years (the plan lists several uncertain year entries).

**Facilities and current holdings:**
- Dexter, Ouray, and other facilities can spawn fish. Adult razorback suckers from the upper basin are held at Ouray and Ouray SFH.
- Table 6 lists holding ponds and tanks at Horsethief Canyon, Bellevue, Wray, and Ouray.
- The Ouray Fish Hatchery inventory (August 10, 1992) recorded about 5,334 estimated razorback sucker fry and 446 actual Colorado squawfish fry.
- Adult inventory (August 10, 1992) included large round tanks and outside tanks with razorback sucker lots from the San Juan, Colorado River, and Lake Powell populations. Tags, mortalities, and retags were recorded.

**Concerns and conclusions from the work group:**
- The 1987 Recovery Program table (Table 6-4) projecting 15 years of fish needs was judged to have no basis in reality, and was dropped from discussion.
- Population estimates for most wild stocks are lacking. The one exception is the Green River razorback sucker. Donor broodstock estimates in the literature range from 20 to 4,000 wild individuals.
- Founder populations must be built gradually, removing only a few wild adults at a time, to avoid overcrowding and adverse impacts on wild populations.
- Risks noted include inbreeding, loss of genetic diversity, and [[Inbreeding Depression]] in small captive populations.
- The work group said the Recovery Program's data and status information are not yet advanced enough for precise numbers, so the estimates should be treated as minimums.

**Disposition of surplus fish (Appendix III):** The draft calls for planned production to minimize surplus fish. Surplus fish should be disposed of through permitted transfer, stocking into approved projects, or humane euthanasia consistent with American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines. Endangered fish produced in excess of program needs become the responsibility of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

**Next steps:** Participants were asked to review and comment by September 28, 1992. The Biology Committee was to review the document, identify gaps, and pass it to the Propagation Subcommittee. A companion facilities capacity assessment was to follow, and the Propagation Coordinator was to consolidate the fish needs and facilities phases into a formal operational plan.
