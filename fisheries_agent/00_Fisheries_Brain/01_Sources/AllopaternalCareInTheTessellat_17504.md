# Allopaternal Care in the Tessellated Darter, Etheostoma olmstedi

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This paper examines allopaternal care in the [[Tessellated Darter]] (*[[Etheostoma Olmstedi]]*), a freshwater percid in which males guard and clean eggs they did not fertilize. Large males defend flat-bottomed rocks whose undersides serve as [[Spawning Substrate]]s. Because females attach eggs to bare stone, each spawning bout reduces the space left for later clutches. Fathers therefore abandon nests to search for surfaces that are more open, and they sequentially monopolize breeding patches that are temporarily most attractive to females. The author argues that the costs of abandoning eggs are low, and that caring for other males' eggs is incidental to a father's own mating opportunities. On this view, allopaternal care evolved through selfish pursuit of spawning opportunities rather than through [[Parental Investment]].

## Background & Description

The paper's framing is that parental care usually increases the reproductive success of adults at some cost to themselves, which is termed parental investment (Trivers 1972). The tessellated darter appears unusual because males care for eggs fertilized by other males. The study was designed to (a) describe social behavior, (b) test hypotheses about its causes, and (c) propose an evolutionary explanation for the mating system and allopaternal care.

Key biological features reported:

- **Males:** Large, darkened males hold territories around flat-bottomed rocks. Small roaming males, called "floaters," search for unoccupied nests. Males show three breeding patterns: territoriality, floating, and [[Female Mimicry]].
- **Females:** Smaller than males. They lay eggs on bare rock toward the centre of the nest ceiling. Several females may contribute to one clutch, and some spawn sequentially with different males.
- **Parental behaviour:** While eggs water-harden, fathers repel intruders, aerate eggs by undulating, and scrub ceilings by inverting and wiggling. Within one day eggs are hard enough to resist cannibalism.
- **Cruising:** Fathers may make brief excursions, lasting a mean of 7.5 min (range 0.2–33 min, n = 21), and return to the original nest. Others relocate for days or permanently after fertilising eggs at a new rock.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites and methods**

- **Field site:** Pools of a third-order reach of [[White Clay Creek]], Chester Co., Pennsylvania.
- **Experimental tank:** A flow-through plexiglas tank (2.4 m × 1.2 m) supplied with creek water. It mimicked natural pools in silt fallout, resident fauna and flora, and diel physico-chemical cycles.
- **Tagging:** Individually colour-coded polypropylene beads on monofilament nylon. Tags stayed in place through the breeding season without causing ulceration.
- **Artificial spawning substrates:** Removable transparent plexiglas ceilings with plastic beads matching egg size and colour. Ceiling patterns were Blank, A, B, C, and All Full (bead areas of 29, 104, and 201 cm² for A, B, and C). Males guarded these nests and performed normal cleaning and aeration.

**Sampling periods**

- Tank experiment: 2 May–1 July 1979, with ten resident males, a male:female ratio of 1:2.5, and hourly recording of male locations and silt levels.
- Creek nests: 25 May–5 July 1980, with three nests in each of seven stream pools. Ceilings were photographed daily and cluster areas were estimated by polar planimetry.
- Female visitation in the creek was about 0.4 visits per nest per hour.

**Nest abandonment and replacement**

Orphaned eggs, whether lost to predators such as the [[Northern Water Snake]] (*Nerodia sipedon*) or to collecting, were tended by a new male within 5–15 min. The author infers a large pool of subordinate floaters. Non-parental males did not eat untended eggs and instead defended, cleaned, and aerated them.

## Key Findings

**1. Male size and dominance**

- Males in the tank ranged from 33 to 63 mm standard length.
- Dominance was inferred from displacement ratios among males. The inferred hierarchy was bl-w > bk-r = w-r > bk > y-bl > five smaller males, which could not be ranked.
- Body size and social rank were positively correlated (r = 0.92, p < 0.01, n = 10).
- Only the four largest males spawned, with the exception of y-bl, which was the same size as bk.

**2. Reproductive success**

- Among spawning males, ceiling area fertilized was not clearly related to body size.
- Ceiling area fertilized was positively correlated with the number of spawning bouts at nests other than the original rock (r = 0.95, p = 0.05, n = 4).
- The author notes that sample sizes are small. The data suggest that reproductive success depended on the number of nests at which a male spawned.

**3. Nest choice and occupancy**

- Occupancy was non-random with respect to ceiling quality. The largest male (62 mm) occupied bare ceilings in 53% (46/86) of observations, against a random expectation of 10%.
- Ceilings with intermediate spawning area (A and B Full) were mostly tended by intermediate-sized males (42–56 mm).
- Ceilings with little (C Full) or no (All Full) spawning area were occupied only occasionally and by males of all sizes.
- Occupancy frequency increased with the amount of available spawning surface. Observation counts across the five ceiling types (Blank to All Full) were 86, 60, 65, 39, and 27, for a total of 277.

**4. Ceiling maintenance**

- Males brushed nest floors with their dorsal and caudal fins while inverted, and this was used as an index of cleaning effort.
- Bare ceilings had the least silt on the floor in 53% of observations, consistent with greater cleaning on open surfaces.

**5. Measurement reliability**

- Duplicate readings of cluster area differed by 8.0% on average (range 0.9–38.4%, n = 21).

**6. Egg survival and cannibalism**

- Eggs become invulnerable to cannibalism within one day of deposition, which lowers the cost to fathers of abandoning clutches.
- The planned test of whether male cleaning improves egg survival compared with screened, male-isolated clusters is described, but the results of that comparison are not reported in the text provided.

**7. Overall interpretation**

- Large males maximise fertilisations by sequentially monopolising the nests that are temporarily most desirable to females.
- Non-parental males maintain abandoned eggs as part of their own cleaning and breeding behaviour. The author argues that viable eggs also attract females, so the behaviour is adaptive without requiring parental investment costs.
- Allopaternal care may therefore have evolved through selfish pursuit of [[Spawning Opportunities]]. The author notes that the behaviour is unlikely to result from reciprocal altruism (Trivers 1971), because males that scrubbed widely concentrated on peripheral bare substrate.
