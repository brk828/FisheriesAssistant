# Annotated List of the Fishes of Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annotated checklist by [[James E. Deacon]] and [[Jack E. Williams]] (Proceedings of the Biological Society of Washington, vol. 97, no. 1, 1984) records all fishes known from Nevada during historical times, both native and introduced, whether or not established. The list comprises 160 fishes referable to 108 species, 56 genera and 19 families, including 67 subspecies within 15 species. Introduced fishes are increasingly burdensome to the native ichthyofauna. The checklist updates [[La Rivers]] (1962) and highlights where further work is needed.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Native and introduced taxa, with subspecies recognised according to current understanding of distribution and geographic variation.
- **Lineage of the list:** Begins with La Rivers and Trelease (1952), followed by La Rivers (1962), *Fishes and Fisheries of Nevada*. Since 1962, 29 introduced fishes have been added.
- **Nomenclature:** Families are ordered phylogenetically; Cyprinodontiformes follow [[Parenti]] (1981); common names follow the American Fisheries Society list ([[Robins et al.]], 1980). The name "poolfish" is retained for *Empetrichthys*, reflecting its placement in the Goodeidae.
- **Drainage systems:** Great Basin (including [[Lahontan Basin]], Bonneville and other endorheic basins), Colorado River, Snake River, and Amargosa River (including [[Ash Meadows]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native diversity by basin:** 46 native fishes occur in the Great Basin (three also in the Snake River). The Lahontan drainage has the most diverse ichthyofauna of the Great Basin with 12 native fishes; 29 native fishes occur in the Colorado River system, 13 in the Snake River system and 6 in the Amargosa River system.
- **Extinctions and extirpations:** At least seven native fishes are extinct throughout their range; four others are extirpated from Nevada but persist elsewhere.
- **Threats:** Habitat modification, extirpation of populations, and competition or predation from introduced species. Many endemics are restricted to single springs, such as the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] at Devils Hole, Ash Meadows.
- **Introductions:** Introductions occurred through official stocking and unofficial means, including bait-fish use, aquarium release and illegal fish farming (e.g. an arowana in Forest Spring, Ash Meadows).

## Key Findings

- **Totals:** 160 taxa (108 species, 56 genera, 19 families); 67 subspecies within 15 species.
- **Origin split:** Including subspecies, 69 taxa (43%) are introduced and 91 (58%) are native. At the species level, 63 species (58%) are introduced and 45 (42%) are native. Only six of the 19 families are native to Nevada.
- **Dominant family:** Cyprinidae (minnows and chubs) is the most diverse, with 31 species, 18 genera and 39 subspecies.
- **Historical comparison:** La Rivers and Trelease (1952) reported 33 introduced taxa and La Rivers (1962) reported 40.
- **Imperilled genera:** Several endemic lineages are at risk, including [[Gila Bicolor]] (tui chub; many undescribed or extinct subspecies), [[Lepidomeda]] (spinedace), [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (speckled dace), [[Crenichthys baileyi]] (White River springfish) and [[Empetrichthys]] (poolfish).
- **Notable native species:** [[Chasmistes cujus]] (Cui-ui), endemic to Pyramid Lake; [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (Razorback sucker), now abundant only in Lake Mohave; [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii henshawi*), native to Pyramid, Tahoe and Walker lakes.
- **Extinct natives:** The Alvord cutthroat trout, Chinook salmon, Humpback chub, Colorado squawfish, Pahranagat spinedace and several pupfish and tui chub populations are recorded as extinct or extirpated.
- **Common introduced species:** Common carp, rainbow trout, brown trout, mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), largemouth bass, bluegill and channel catfish are widely established. Aquarium species such as guppy, convict cichlid, sailfin molly and tilapia are established in springs.
- **Taxonomic changes:** Increased subspecies recognition since 1962 reflects better understanding of distribution and geographic variation; *Lepidomeda mollispinis* is named Virgin River spinedace.
- **Knowledge gaps:** The tui chub complex in Railroad Valley "remains an enigma pending detailed investigations"; the authors call for further work where status is uncertain.
