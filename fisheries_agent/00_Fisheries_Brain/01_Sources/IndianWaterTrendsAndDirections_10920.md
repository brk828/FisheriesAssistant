# Indian Water—1997: Trends and Directions in Federal Water Policy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report summarizes the March 17–18, 1997 Phoenix forum of the [[Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission]], titled *Indian Water—1997: Trends and Directions in Federal Water Policy*. Prepared by Todd Olinger for the Commission, it condenses a 240-page transcript into four sessions covering tribal water law, watershed governance, the federal water settlement process, and a federal action agenda for Indian Country. Fish are a recurring concern rather than a research subject: the document addresses [[Salmon]] declines in the Columbia and Snake basins, the [[Cui-ui]] at Pyramid Lake, and the [[Loach Minnow]] as an endangered species that stalled a Colorado River project. Its core argument is that tribal water rights remain unquantified, underfunded, and subordinated to non-Indian uses, and that federal trust obligations require negotiated, funded, and enforceable remedies.

## Background & Description

The forum was authorized under the Western Water Policy Review Act of 1992 (Pub. L. 102-575), which directed a federal review of activities in nineteen western states that affect water allocation and use. The Commission comprised twenty-two members: ten appointed by the President (including the Secretaries of the Interior and the Army) and twelve members of Congress serving ex officio. The legislation noted that at least fourteen federal agencies had water-related responsibilities, producing "unclear goals and an inefficient handling of the Nation's water policy."

The proceedings were organized into four sessions:

- **Session I – Western Water Trends and Directions:** Keynote addresses by [[David H. Getches]], [[Susan M. Williams]], and [[Chelsea Congdon]], followed by statements from tribal chairmen including [[Billy Frank Jr.]], [[Ronnie Lupe]], and [[Albert Hale]].
- **Session II – Watershed Governance and the Management of Indian Water Resources:** Case presentations from the Flathead, Deschutes, Missouri Basin, Ute Mountain Ute, Wind River, and Lummi tribes.
- **Session III – The Water Settlement Process and Resolution of West-Wide Water Issues:** Perspectives from practitioners, Congress, the Clinton Administration, and the Gila River, Fort Belknap, and Nez Perce tribes.
- **Session IV – Enhancing Tribal Leadership and Participation:** Congressional, Interior, and Bureau of Indian Affairs testimony on funding and personnel, followed by [[Secretary of the Interior Bruce Babbitt]]'s closing comments.

The document also reproduces letters from the Walker River Paiute Tribe, the Owens Valley Indian Water Commission, the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission, the Colorado River Indian Tribes, and four New Mexico tribes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water policy and aquatic ecology are tightly linked throughout the proceedings. Several themes recur:

- **Salmon and instream flows.** Presenters tied the Northwest salmon crisis to dam operations, water depletion, and degraded quality. Getches described the crisis as caused by "all these causes" together. The Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission reported that hundreds of streams in Oregon, Washington, and Idaho are listed as "water quality limited," and that Columbia Basin salmon runs are listed as endangered or threatened. [[Samuel N. Penney]] of the [[Nez Perce Tribe]] described the tribes' proposal to breach some Snake and Columbia dams to restore more natural river flows.
- **Pyramid Lake.** Water diversion from the Truckee and Carson system pushed the tribe's legendary fishery "to near-extinction." The endangered [[Cui-ui]] in Pyramid Lake later helped bring urban interests into the [[Fallon Paiute-Shoshone and Truckee-Carson-Pyramid Lake Settlement]].
- **Missouri River mainstem.** Five Corps of Engineers dams inundated reservation land. Getches reported that the dams destroyed 550 square miles of reservations in North and South Dakota and displaced more than 900 reservation families, and that Fort Peck displaced 350 families. Richard Bad Moccasin reported that eight main-stem tribes lost 350,000 acres.
- **Groundwater and forest hydrology.** [[Ronnie Lupe]] argued that pumping from the [[Coconino Aquifer]] would deplete surface flows on the White Mountain Apache lands, and that the tribe's forests and streams depend on groundwater. The Owens Valley Indian Water Commission reported that Los Angeles operates 360 wells pumping about 108,000 acre-feet per year, which causes declining water tables.
- **Watershed management.** Speakers described multi-stakeholder efforts, including the Zuni River watershed, the Rio Puerco Management Committee, the [[Chelan Agreement]] on Washington salmon, and the [[Deschutes Basin Resource Conservancy]].
- **Endangered Species Act conflicts.** Tribes and federal officials disagreed on whether the [[Endangered Species Act]] should limit tribal water development. The Jicarilla Apache Tribe reported that the Act currently precludes it from diverting water from the Navajo River.

## Key Findings

**1. Policy trends in western water.** Getches identified five trends shaping western water policy: conservation and efficiency, a changing role for structural solutions, water marketing, environmental protection and sustainability, and institutional reform. He argued that demographic shifts (more than half the national population lives west of the Mississippi) and changing attitudes drive these trends.

**2. Unquantified rights and the trust responsibility.** Williams argued that tribal reserved water rights vest at the creation of each reservation (or at "time immemorial" for aboriginal rights) but remain largely unquantified, so non-Indian users have diverted water the tribes own. She cited [[United States v. Mitchell]] (1983) as the standard for money damages for breach of trust, and argued that the [[McCarran Amendment]] of 1955 is the vehicle that allows the United States to be joined in [[General Stream Adjudications]]. She also questioned whether state administrative agencies should play any role in these proceedings.

**3. Structural solutions and infrastructure.** Tribes were largely excluded from federal water development benefits. The [[National Water Commission]] (1973) found that federal projects were built on tribal streams "with little or no regard for Indian rights." The Corps estimated that Missouri River projects contribute $1.3 billion annually to the national economy, with about $655 million from hydroelectric generation. Tribes have asked for revenue sharing from that power generation.

**4. Water marketing.** Virtually all of the twelve most recent Indian water settlements contain limits on marketing. Getches argued that this limitation has "no principled basis" because non-Indian rights under [[Prior Appropriation Doctrine]] are transferable. Commission testimony called for clear tribal authority to market water. The Colorado River Indian Tribes noted that marketing could fund their irrigation system rehabilitation, but that several Lower Basin states oppose it.

**5. Watershed governance and tribal capacity.** The Flathead Reservation (population about 20,000, of whom about 7,000 are Indian) reported adversity across technical, administrative, social, political, and legal dimensions. Its recommendations include consistent federal management objectives and adequately funded negotiation teams. The Warm Springs Tribe recommended a "proactive stakeholder collaboration" approach to reserved rights, and it identified 42 state and 18 federal agencies with overlapping water responsibilities within the Deschutes Basin.

**6. Funding gaps.** The Colorado River Indian Tribes reported that their irrigation system, which irrigates about 80,000 acres and supports up to $80 million in crop revenues annually, requires a $30 million rehabilitation plan of which only $3.5 million has been appropriated. The Ute Mountain Ute Tribe reported a Farm and Ranch Project originally projected at $50 million, with about $15 million received and $10 million still needed. The Pueblo of Santa Ana received $15,000 for FY 1997 negotiations. The Jicarilla and Pueblos described funding as "primarily linked to the adjudication process," which forces litigation to obtain funds.

**7. Settlement process.** Michael Jackson reported that the settlement process has produced fourteen water settlements involving twenty tribes, bands, and communities since 1978; six are fully implemented, four are on schedule, and four are stalled. No settlements were produced after 1992. Jackson and Timothy Glidden described the process as broken, with Glidden noting that Congress was considering options that included moving negotiating authority to a new entity. [[Robert T. Anderson]] stated that the Interior Department was committed to settlements that can be implemented in the short term.

**8. Key settlement and litigation cases.**
- The [[Snake River Basin Adjudication]], begun by Idaho in 1987, has more than 175,000 claims filed to date; the United States and tribes have spent over $10 million preparing the Nez Perce cases.
- The [[Big Horn Adjudication]] in Wyoming, begun in 1977, is the only general stream adjudication to reach the U.S. Supreme Court.
- The Gila River tribes, with a negotiated water budget of 653,500 acre-feet per year, have litigated since 1935.
- The Little Colorado River negotiations have stalled over non-Indian parties' demands for full compensation.

**9. Recommendations to the Commission.** Recurring recommendations included: adequately funded and stable federal negotiation teams with authority to bind the United States; reform of the water-code approval moratorium in place since June 1975; use of hydropower revenues for tribal benefit; federal enforcement of senior tribal rights before junior uses are curtailed; and clear tribal authority to market water. The [[Colombia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission]] urged that salmon be managed as a treaty-protected resource, not as a cost to power revenues.

**10. Secretary Babbitt's assessment.** Babbitt attributed the decline of settlements to lost leverage (the end of large reclamation projects such as the [[Central Arizona Project]]), weakened perceptions of water scarcity, and reduced litigation risk for non-Indian parties. He proposed regional, basin-wide solutions, including reviewing Bureau of Reclamation budget mechanisms, the Justice Department Judgment Fund, and possible revenue from hydropower contracts. He also acknowledged that the Animas-La Plata Project "has taught us a melancholy lesson."
