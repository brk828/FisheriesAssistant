# 7 Species Project: Section 7 Consultation and Land and Resource Management Plan Direction for 63 Listed Species, Southwestern Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a Forest Service (FS) correspondence and technical package, dated 1997, covering formal Section 7 consultation with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) on the Land and Resource Management Plans (LRMPs) of National Forests and Grasslands in the [[Southwestern Region]]. Consultation began May 15, 1996, covered 63 listed species, and was driven by FWS concern for seven species: [[Pima Pineapple Cactus]], [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], and [[Sonoran Chub]]. The "7 Species Project" was organised into specialised teams, funded from an initial request of $1.35 million that was later reduced to $900,000 for FY97 and $500,000 for FY98, and it issued Regional Direction, short-term funded actions, and an effects analysis. The package is framed as an attempt to remove on-the-ground threats while strengthening LRMP language, with the aim of obtaining a non-jeopardy biological opinion.

## Background & Description

**Scope and origin.** The consultation was initiated by mutual agreement of FS and FWS and was intended to produce a non-jeopardy biological opinion covering the amended forest plans. The Regional Forester's cover letters (1996–1997) describe a schedule set November 8, 1995, targeting completion by March 1, 1997, with the goal of removing on-the-ground threats to the seven species and strengthening Forest plan protections for all listed species.

**Organisation.** A project coordinator (John Peterson, Mimbres/Santa Fe district ranger) led four teams: Pima pineapple cactus; cactus ferruginous pygmy-owl; southwestern willow flycatcher; and fish (the latter covering the four aquatic species). Team members included staff from the FS, the FWS, state agencies and the [[Rocky Mountain Research Station]], with research fishery biologist John Rinne and project leader Debra Finch among the named scientists.

**Desired outcomes.** The briefing to the Secretaries of Agriculture and Interior set six outcomes, including consultation extended by mutual agreement, resolution without a jeopardy opinion, and resolution without injunction on significant numbers of ongoing activities. A Region-wide [[Riparian Restoration]] initiative was proposed as the long-term ecosystem strategy.

**Budget.** An additional $150,000 was identified for project assessment, with up to $1.2 million for implementation. A Regional allocation of $763,000 (from carryover funds, expiring 9/30/97) funded short-term actions, and a further $107,000 went to potential flycatcher habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species accounts (fish).** The fish team's narratives cover four aquatic species:

- **Little Colorado spinedace (*Lepidomeda vittata*)**, listed threatened in 1987. Endemic to the Little Colorado River system in Arizona (East Clear, Chevelon, Silver, Nutrioso creeks). Populations are cyclical with water years, and decline is linked to impoundment (about 150 impoundments in the basin), dewatering, and predation by non-native trout.
- **Loach minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)**, listed threatened in 1986. Once widespread in the Gila basin; current range is under 17% of historic range. Riffle-dwelling benthic insectivore sensitive to sedimentation and to non-native predators and competitors (e.g. red shiner).
- **Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)**, listed threatened in 1986. Restricted to under 9% of historic range in Arizona and New Mexico; lives in midwater runs, pools and eddies. Its decline is attributed to impoundment, groundwater pumping, channel downcutting and non-native fish.
- **Sonoran chub (*Gila ditaenia*)**, listed threatened in 1986 (state endangered). Endemic to the Rio de la Concepcion drainage; in the U.S. it occurs only in Sycamore Creek and California Gulch on the Nogales Ranger District, the only known U.S. locale. Vulnerable to drought, grazing, mining, roads, and cattle entering through the cut international fence.

**Critical habitat.** Designations for loach minnow and spikedace (1994) were set aside by a New Mexico court pending NEPA analysis, and the spikedace rule was under appeal.

**Plant and bird species.** Pima pineapple cactus (*Coryphantha scheeri* var. *robustispina*) is endangered (listed October 25, 1993), occurring in Santa Cruz and Pima counties, Arizona, and northern Sonora. The forest had surveyed approximately 2,000 acres, and 58 plants were documented on Forest land; the main threats are urbanisation, mining, the Central Arizona Project, off-road vehicles and invasion by [[Lehmann's Lovegrass]], which raises fire intensity. The cactus ferruginous pygmy-owl was listed endangered in Arizona (final rule March 10, 1997, effective April 9, 1997). Only about 14 individuals were confirmed in recent surveys, all in Sonoran desertscrub. Southwestern willow flycatcher occupied 10 known sites on National Forest lands, with [[Brown-Headed Cowbird]] parasitism and livestock grazing identified as main pressures.

**Cumulative stressors.** Reviewers stressed that riparian degradation results from many small, chronic effects over a century of land use. Livestock grazing in riparian pastures, road crossings, dispersed recreation and ORV use were the most frequent direct effects identified.

## Key Findings

1. **Consultation status and Regional response.** The Region issued Regional Direction for all seven species (June 1997), including recreation/OHV closures, fire protection, grazing exclusions and monitoring for the cactus; survey requirements and biological review for the pygmy-owl; and habitat, grazing, cowbird and recreation controls for the flycatcher.
2. **Short-term funding (FY97).** The Regional allocation was $763,000, distributed across the Apache-Sitgreaves, Carson, Cibola, Coconino, Coronado, Gila, Prescott, Santa Fe and Tonto forests and the Rocky Mountain Station. Fish projects received the largest share (about $0.55M in summary tables). A separate $107,000 went to potential flycatcher habitat.
3. **Fish team project review.** The team reviewed 199 projects on five national forests in calendar 1997. Of these, 125 posed no direct short-term effect and 74 did (50 range, 11 recreation, 10 roads, 3 special use permits). The Apache-Sitgreaves had 44 projects with potential effects (28 direct), Gila 31 direct effects, Prescott 9, Coconino 2 and Coronado 3.
4. **No single fix.** The fish team concluded that no single localised action would significantly relieve the four fish; impacts are broad, subtle and indirect, so long-term guidance must focus on watershed and riparian health.
5. **Pima cactus.** Off-road driving near plants, grazing with non-native seeding, and fire interacting with lovegrass were identified as threats. A closure order and fire plan were put in place, with a recovery plan to follow.
6. **Flycatcher recommendations.** Include exclusion of livestock year-round from occupied habitat, trapping of cowbirds where parasitism is documented, fencing, recreation closures, fire planning, and a Regional annual review under adaptive management.
7. **Pygmy-owl.** Surveys of at least 5,000 acres per forest per year, biological review of projects in mesquite bosques and desertscrub, and a Conservation Assessment by Debra Finch were required; Forest staff noted that the FWS concern was the absence of habitat assessment and protective measures in the forest plans.
8. **Sonoran chub.** Regional Direction required obliteration of roads in Sycamore Canyon, closure to off-highway vehicles, elimination of grazing in riparian corridors of Sycamore Canyon and California Gulch, and annual population monitoring.
9. **Riparian strategy.** The fish team called for a shift in focus to riparian area health, noting that over 130 Regional sensitive species depend on riparian habitat, and recommended removal of direct impacts, Regional funding of about $1,000,000 per year for TES/riparian projects, and a Regional policy that riparian areas are not suitable for grazing capacity.
10. **Concerns about analysis quality.** The team found that most FY97 project clearance relied on the Programmatic BA, that individuals not trained as journey-level fisheries biologists were signing effects determinations, and that the Gila and Apache-Sitgreaves forests lacked sufficient fisheries expertise.
11. **Other risks.** The 1997 contingency list includes injunctions, funding gaps, interagency disagreements on thresholds, legal challenges to annual operating plans from the livestock community, and employee burnout.
