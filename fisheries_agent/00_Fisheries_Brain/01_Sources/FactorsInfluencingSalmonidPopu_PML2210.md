# Factors Influencing Trout Populations and Habitat in Six Headwater Streams, Central Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined how physical habitat and biotic variables influence trout populations and aquatic macroinvertebrates in six first-order headwater streams of the [[Tonto National Forest]] in central Arizona. The streams were sampled from 1985 to 1987 as a pretreatment phase before a proposed watershed treatment. Stream depth emerged as the strongest predictor of fish numbers and standing stock. Fine substrate sediment was the second most important physical habitat factor. Multiple regression models explained most of the variation in trout density and invertebrate density. The authors argue that livestock grazing is a probable source of increased substrate fines, and that water depth is essential to salmonid survival in these drought-prone headwaters.

The authors frame the work around a shift in U.S. fisheries management. Put-and-take stocking of hatchery trout had long dominated recreational angling. Budget cuts at state and federal levels had reduced hatchery production, so agencies were moving toward naturally sustaining wild trout fisheries. The authors state that reliable estimates of fish abundance and standing stock in wild populations are scarce and highly variable, which limits management planning.

The six study streams are Pine, Horton and Christopher creeks (large, first-order systems) and Dude, Banita and Ellison creeks (small systems). Fish and macroinvertebrates were sampled in the Rim streams, which are fed by springs such as Horton Spring. Fish were captured with a backpack [[Electrofishing]] unit, measured to the nearest millimeter of total length and weighed to the nearest gram. Macroinvertebrates were collected with a Surber-type sampler, and substrate sediment, water depth, width and gradient were measured. Water quality was analyzed for alkalinity, hardness, sulfate, nitrate and phosphate. Statistical analysis used t-tests, [[Multiple Regression Analysis]] and [[Stepwise Regression]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The streams lie on the downthrust side of a fault block at roughly 1550 to 2100 m elevation. The area lies in a chaparral and ladder-fern (oak-woodland) transition zone with a bimodal rainfall pattern. Intense convectional storms, which can produce flash floods, cause brief, local stream channel scouring. Average annual precipitation is given as roughly 50 to 80 cm (OCR-derived reading).

**Fish assemblages.** Trout are the only native-range salmonids present in the sampled reaches, and the streams are inhabited only by introduced salmonids in single or multiple-species assemblages:

- Pine Creek is predominantly rainbow trout, with brown trout sparsely present.
- Christopher Creek is predominantly brook trout.
- Dude Creek has a small rainbow trout population at its downstream end and brook trout in the rest of the stream.

Recreational fishing occurs on all six streams, most intensively on Christopher and Horton creeks because of accessibility, history of put-and-take fishing, and the relative size of the fish. The streams are not stocked regularly with hatchery-reared trout.

**Land use.** Watersheds have experienced differing grazing regimes. The Fine Creek watershed had not been grazed for over 25 years. Christopher Creek had not been grazed for over 5 years. The remaining watersheds were grazed continuously in a seasonal May-through-October pattern. Timber harvest was also considered as a possible contributor to substrate fines, but its records were not yet analyzed.

**Substrate.** Substrate fine sediment is a key environmental factor. Its effects on fish and invertebrates were described in earlier literature as negative. Sediment-related changes could reduce spawning habitat and invertebrate production.

## Key Findings

- **Trout abundance and size.** Mean fish size averaged about 147 mm total length. Only rainbow trout in Pine Creek and brook trout in Christopher Creek differed significantly in mean size between streams. Fish numbers per meter were significantly higher in the three large streams than in the three small streams. Fish numbers in Pine Creek were significantly higher than in Dude, Ellison and Banita creeks. Standing stock differences followed a similar pattern.
- **Stream depth as the dominant factor.** Stream depth explained most of the variation in fish density. Stepwise regression showed depth first, followed by substrate sediment and stream gradient. Together, these three physical habitat variables explained the bulk of the variation in fish numbers, and the overall model was highly significant. Predicted and observed values agreed closely.
- **Fish standing stock.** Stream depth explained the largest share of fish standing stock. Substrate sediment and an invertebrate index added further explanatory power. Fish standing stock was also highly significant in its regression model.
- **Macroinvertebrates.** Invertebrate densities were significantly lower in Pine Creek than in the other streams in 1985 and in Horton Creek in 1986 (as reported by the authors). Invertebrate density was best predicted by stream gradient, sulfate and fish density. The final model explained a substantial portion of invertebrate variation, with the predominant influence coming from physical habitat factors.
- **Substrate fines.** Mean substrate sediment was significantly lower in Pine Creek than in the other streams. Fine sediment in the small streams was significantly higher than in the large streams. Sediment in upper Pine Creek was significantly lower than in the other streams, and the authors note that sediment samples collected above the treatment area were lower than those downstream.
- **Water quality.** Alkalinity and hardness varied significantly among streams, seasons and reaches. Sulfate was highest in Christopher and Horton creeks. Phosphate was significantly higher in Banita Creek. Nitrate was more variable in winter and did not vary significantly among seasons. Water quality was not identified as a controlling factor for trout populations in these streams.
- **Likely cause of increased fines.** The authors conclude that differences in soil origin and composition were similar across the six watersheds, so a land use factor is probably responsible for increased substrate fines. Domestic livestock grazing is their leading hypothesis, supported by simulated grazing experiments in Fine Creek. Timber harvest remains a secondary possibility.
- **Management implications.** Depth and substrate conditions should be considered in managing wild trout in these headwater systems. The authors recommend additional habitat data from other headwater streams to refine predictions, and note that introductory remarks on habitat requirements are essential for effectively managing naturally reproducing populations of salmonids in the southwestern United States.
