# Taxonomic Status and Morphology of Isolated Populations of the White River Springfish, *Crenichthys baileyi*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines [[Crenichthys Baileyi]], a springfish endemic to a chain of isolated warm springs in the pluvial [[White River]] system of southeastern Nevada. Drying of the pluvial river and the species' obligate spring existence have isolated populations, and differing spring temperature and dissolved oxygen have produced morphological divergence. Based on morphometric, meristic, and color analyses, the authors describe four new subspecies, bringing the formerly monotypic species to five subspecies. The paper also documents population losses caused by [[Exotic Fish Introductions]] and habitat alteration, including one extirpated population (Hiko Spring) and continued threats to others.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Crenichthys Baileyi]] (Gilbert, 1893), originally described as a subspecies of *Cyprinodon macularius*. The genus *Crenichthys* was erected by Hubbs (1932) for *C. nevadae*. The genus is closely allied with [[Empetrichthys]], differing mainly in having uniserially bifid rather than biserially conical teeth and herbivorous feeding.
- **Type locality:** Probably Ash Spring, Pahranagat Valley, Nevada. Gilbert's 1893 description was based on 11 specimens under 20 mm collected in 1891 by C. H. Merriam and V. Bailey. Two syntypes are held at the California Academy of Sciences (SU 709).
- **Common name:** The authors recommend replacing "killifish" with "springfish" in common-name lists for *C. baileyi* and *C. nevadae*.
- **Habitat:** Warm springs. Spring temperatures range from about 21 °C to 37 °C, and minimum dissolved oxygen from 0.7 to 5.8 ppm among sampled sites (Table 1).
- **Pre-study status:** The species was considered monotypic before this work. Earlier research focused on tolerance of high temperature and low oxygen ([[Hubbs and Hettler]], 1964), activity cycles ([[Deacon and Wilson]], 1967), and early life history ([[Kopec]], 1949). Reider (1971) compared four populations but did not separate data by sex or describe new taxa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Isolation and geography.** The pluvial White River, which drained to the [[Colorado River]] about 10,000 to 30,000 years ago, is now disjunct. Its remnants are the White River Valley, Pahranagat Valley, and the Moapa River. Because the species requires springs, isolated populations persist along the former river course. Robert R. Miller (pers. comm.) suggested these springs have been isolated for about 10,000 years. The authors argue that continued isolation results from dry river channels, obligate spring use, long distances between springs, and springs located near but not in the river bed.

**Environmental divergence.** [[Preston Big Spring]] (21.0 °C, 3.3 ppm O₂) and [[Mormon Spring]] (37.0 °C, 0.7 ppm O₂) represent opposite ends of the environmental gradient. In a transplant experiment by [[Sumner and Sargent]] (1940), fish from Preston Spring did not survive in Mormon Spring, while fish moved from Mormon to Preston survived. The authors measured gill filaments as a possible explanation. Mean longest gill filament length was 0.046 SL (range 0.040–0.054) in 10 Mormon Spring fish, compared with 0.0362 SL (0.034–0.042) in 10 Preston Big Spring fish. Gill filament number did not differ significantly. The authors attribute the difference to adaptation to oxygen availability, consistent with [[Deacon and Minckley]] (1974).

**Exotic species and habitat threats.**
- **[[Hiko Spring]]:** Largemouth bass ([[Largemouth Bass]], *Micropterus salmoides*), shortfin mollies, and mosquitofish were introduced in 1965. Springfish were rare by February 1966 and extinct by June 1967. An introduced parasite, *Lernaea*, also contributed to the decline.
- **[[Hot Creek Spring]]:** Largemouth bass from Dacey Reservoir reached the spring in the early 1970s and were thought to have extirpated the population. A surviving group isolated by dense emergent vegetation was later found. Fish were relocated after the spring was poisoned and a barrier repaired. One bass was still observed in 1979.
- **Other springs:** Exotic species, including *Poecilia reticulata*, *Gambusia affinis*, and *Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*, have reduced activity and population numbers. Parasites *Lernaea* (Copepoda) and *Contracaecum* (Nematoda) were linked to exotic fishes in Moapa headwater springs.

**Associated native fauna.** Co-occurring native fishes include [[Lepidomeda albivallis]], [[Rhinichthys osculus]] subspecies, [[Gila robusta]] jordani, and [[Catostomus clarki]] intermedius, depending on the spring.

## Key Findings

1. **Five subspecies are recognized.** The authors describe four new subspecies and retain *C. b. baileyi* for the Ash Spring population. Diagnoses rely on head length, least bony interorbital width, anal-to-caudal length, snout length, fin-ray counts, and male coloration.
2. **Morphology follows environmental gradients.** [[Mormon Spring]] fish have the largest relative head size index (41.14). [[Preston Big Spring]] fish have the smallest (27.61). Values for other springs range from 32.87 (Hiko) to 37.16 (Crystal).
3. **Meristic differences are documented.** Typical dorsal ray counts are 11 in *albivallis*, 10 in *thermophilus*, 10–11 in *grandis*, and 10 in *baileyi*. Typical anal ray counts are 12–13 in most subspecies, and *grandis* averages 13.2 rays. Vertebral counts were determined from radiographs and reported as means of 27.5 to 28.8 total vertebrae across subspecies.
4. **Subspecies distributions are defined.**
   - *albivallis*: northernmost springs near Preston and Lund, White Pine Co.
   - *thermophilus*: three springs in Nye Co. (Mormon, Hot Creek, Moon River).
   - *grandis*: Hiko and Crystal springs, Pahranagat Valley.
   - *baileyi*: Ash Spring only, Pahranagat Valley.
   - *moapae*: headwater springs of the Moapa River, Clark Co.
5. **Recent population status varies.**
   - *albivallis* is reduced in some springs, possibly because of *Poecilia reticulata*.
   - *thermophilus* has viable populations in its three springs but has been extirpated from the Hot Creek outflow.
   - *grandis* at Hiko became extinct in 1967. Crystal Spring is affected by *Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*.
   - *baileyi* is rare due to exotic species in the main pool.
   - *moapae* remains abundant in many headwater springs.
6. **Gill morphology may explain tolerance differences.** The longer gill filaments in Mormon Spring fish are offered as a physiological explanation for their survival under low oxygen.
7. **Conservation implications.** The authors call one population extirpated and others endangered by exotic introductions and physical alteration. They note continuing risk from bass near Hot Creek, which could cause further introductions.
8. **Taxonomic nomenclature is updated.** The authors propose "springfish" as the standard common name, replacing "killifish."
