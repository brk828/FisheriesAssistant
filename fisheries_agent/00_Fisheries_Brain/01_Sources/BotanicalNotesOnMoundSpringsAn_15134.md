# Botanical Notes on Mound Springs and Bores

#botany #plant-ecology #wetland-ecology #mound-springs

## Executive Summary

This document presents a botanical survey of [[Mound Springs]] and flowing bores in arid South Australia, written as three linked contributions: D.E. Symon's site-by-site account of springs and bores and their wet-habitat flora, an illustrated identification key, and a full regional plant checklist, plus C.R. Alcock's account of alien and weedy plants. Symon documents the vegetation of more than 20 springs, bores and spring complexes, with particular attention to species that depend on free water. The survey finds that the best-preserved spring complexes support rare plants absent from the bores, including the first South Australian records of several disjunct species. Grazing and trampling by stock are the dominant threat, and the author recommends a minimal list of springs for conservation.

## Background & Description

The survey visited springs and bores between roughly 28° and 29° S. Sites are described individually, noting vegetation on vents, outflow "tails," mound slopes and surrounding plains, together with signs of stock damage, fencing and peat. Plant material was collected by C.R. Alcock, K. Chorney, P. Horton, D.E. Symon and J.Z. Weber. Specimens are lodged at the [[State Herbarium Of South Australia]] and the Waite Institute herbarium. Symon's key is built on growth form, leaf and inflorescence characters, and stem structure, so that the wet-habitat species can be identified in the field.

Notable taxa described in the species accounts include:

- **[[Cyperus Gymnocaulos]]**: a tough, rhizomatous sedge to 1 m, more drought-tolerant than other wet-habitat sedges, and a major component of tails at northern sites.
- **[[Cyperus Laevigatus]]**: a short (10–30 cm), strongly rhizomatous sedge, the most regularly collected species, and possibly an indicator of degradation.
- **[[Eriocaulon Carsoni]]** (Button Grass): the most significant find, recorded only at [[Hermit Hill]].
- **[[Gahnia Trifida]]** (Cutting Grass): a sedge to 1 m, confined mainly to larger, less damaged spring complexes.
- **[[Machaerina Juncea]]** (Bare Twigrush): a wiry perennial to 35 cm, also linked to surviving peats.
- **[[Juncus Kraussii]]** (Sea Rush): a stout rush to about 1 m, occurring where degradation was not severe.
- **[[Phragmites Australis]]** (Common Bamboo Reed): 2–3 m, palatable and vulnerable to grazing, and likely to recover strongly if protected.
- **[[Typha]]** (Bulrush/Cumbungi): found in free water and at hot springs such as [[Purni Bore]].
- **[[Scirpus Maritimus]]** and **[[Scirpus Litoralis]]** (Clubrushes): the latter is soft-stemmed and persists mainly in deep pools out of stock reach.
- **[[Sporobolus Virginicus]]** (Salt Couch) and **[[Polypogon Monspeliensis]]** (Beard Grass): widespread on sandy or subsaline sites.
- **[[Spergularia Rubra]]** (Spurry): very common on the survey and found equally on bores and springs.
- **[[Potamogeton Pectinatus]]** (Pondweed): submerged, recorded only from [[Coward Bore]] and [[Blanche Cup]].
- **Samphires (Halosarcia spp.)**: four species, identified by P. Wilson, important indicators of saline soils.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Mound springs are small, discrete wetlands on a dry landscape, built around artesian vents and fed by water that flows out over mound slopes to form tails. Zonation is clear: free-water vents and tails carry sedges, rushes and reeds, while mound slopes carry [[Nitraria]], samphires and trees such as *Acacia* and *Myoporum*. Salinity and evaporation concentrate salts around running water, which limits the number of species present. Site condition varied widely. Several springs were dry, dead or extinct, while others had strong flows.

**Grazing and trampling** were the main pressures. Many springs were heavily pugged, and fences were often derelict. The author notes that the single springs had suffered most, reduced to one to three species, whereas larger complexes spread or concentrated grazing damage across multiple vents and retained more species and peat.

**Peat remnants** were found only in the better-preserved complexes, including [[Strangways Springs]], [[Peake Ruins]] and [[Hermit Hill]]. These peats may be dated and may hold pollen records of past vegetation change.

**Bores** differ from springs. Flowing bores such as [[Coward Bore]], Umbum Bore and Eitzens Bore support some wet-habitat species, notably *Cyperus* spp., *Scirpus maritimus*, *Typha* and *Polypogon*, but lack the springs' key species, peat and *Phragmites*. The author concludes that flowing bores cannot be botanically equivalent to mound springs.

**Alien and weedy plants** (Alcock) were sparse at wet sites, mainly *Spergularia* and *Polypogon*. Dominant weeds along waterways were Sonchus oleraceus and Erodium spp. Of 51 alien and weedy species collected, only two are proclaimed pest plants: [[Emex Australis]] (Three-Corner Jack, with long sharp spines) and [[Tribulus Terrestris]] (native caltrop, with short spines). The alien flora is most common along disturbed sites such as railway sidings, road verges and stock yards. Control is considered impractical across the large, low-rainfall region, although removing Three-Corner Jack from stock yards and railway sidings was suggested.

The [[Dalhousie Spring Complex]] was not visited, but the author records additional restricted species there, such as *Eleocharis geniculata* and *Machaerina huttonii*, none of which occur at the surveyed springs or bores.

## Key Findings

- **Rare and disjunct species:** *Eriocaulon carsoni* was found only at Hermit Hill. Two earlier collections are known, from a spring in western New South Wales and from Murnpeowie, both made in the previous century. It is the only member of its family in South Australia, and its small size makes it vulnerable to trampling, so the author recommends priority conservation.
- **New locality records:** *Gahnia trifida* and *Machaerina juncea* are significant range extensions, widely disjunct from previous records. Both occurred only in larger, less damaged spring complexes still retaining peat, and neither was found on any bore.
- **Interpretation of disjunction:** These species may be relics of a wetter past or examples of long-distance dispersal from southern high-rainfall districts.
- **Indicator value:** *Cyperus laevigatus* may indicate degradation and heavy grazing. *Phragmites* and *Juncus kraussii* appear mainly where degradation is not severe. *Potamogeton* suggests undisturbed water of some depth.
- **Degradation gradient:** Single springs (e.g., Beresford, Warburton, Elizabeth, Jersey, The Bubbler, Blanche Cup, Old Mt Hamilton) are the most degraded, with one to three species, whereas larger complexes retain peat and more species.
- **Recovery potential:** If protected from stock, reeds and rushes could regain full height and density, restoring bird habitat. Recovery of eroded mound slopes is expected to be very slow.
- **Conservation recommendations:** Protect the larger complexes completely. Elsewhere, fence some springs and leave others for stock, using piping where feasible. The author stresses landholder cooperation and notes that broken fences can undo years of protection. A minimal conservation list comprises Beresford Hill, Peake Springs complex, [[Hermit Hill]] complex, The Bubbler, [[Blanche Cup]], [[Coward Springs]], and possibly Twelve Mile Springs. Listing on the Heritage Register is also recommended, and [[Strangways Springs]] is proposed for preservation on geological, botanical and historical grounds.
- **Flora overview:** The regional checklist (Symon) lists the plants of the survey area as a whole, with an asterisk marking introduced species. Numerous families are represented, including Asteraceae, Poaceae and Caryophyllaceae. Specimens were collected by several botanists.
- **Alien flora:** Of 51 alien and weedy species, 18 are grasses. Weeds are uncommon on open gibber plains, and the main infestations of Brassica tournefortii occur in sandy areas.
