# Lake Powell Quality Studies for the Navajo Plant

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reservoir-limnology #water-quality #fisheries

## Executive Summary

This document presents comprehensive water quality and aquatic ecology investigations conducted at [[Lake Powell]] on the [[Colorado River]] above [[Glen Canyon Dam]] during 1965-1969. The studies were undertaken to evaluate the feasibility of discharging cooling tower blowdown from the proposed [[Navajo Generating Station]] (2,310,000 kilowatts capacity) back into the reservoir. The investigation examined physical limnology, water chemistry, stratification patterns, density currents, aquatic biology, and dispersion characteristics. Despite findings indicating minimal environmental impact from the proposed discharge scheme, the project participants ultimately decided against returning concentrated blowdown to the lake to avoid setting a precedent for increased salinity in the [[Colorado River]] system. The study provides valuable baseline data on reservoir behavior, penstock-induced withdrawal currents, and the transformation of a riverine ecosystem into a reservoir environment.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Powell]] is one of the world's largest man-made reservoirs with an ultimate gross capacity of 27,000,000 acre-feet at normal full pool elevation 3,700 feet. Impoundment began in March 1963 behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]], constructed under the Colorado River Storage Project Act. The reservoir occupies deeply incised canyons of the Colorado and San Juan Rivers in northern Arizona and southern Utah. The reservoir is Y-shaped in plan: 63 miles from the dam to the Colorado-San Juan confluence, then 163 miles upstream in the [[Colorado Arm]] and 64 miles in the [[San Juan Arm]] at full pool.

The canyon geometry is distinctive: 900-1,500 feet wide at the bottom with nearly vertical walls to elevation 3,600 feet, then transitioning to gentler slopes. The dam features four 8-foot diameter river outlets at elevation 3,374 feet and eight 15-foot diameter penstocks at elevation 3,470 feet. Since reaching minimum power pool (elevation 3,490 feet) in 1964, all releases have been through the penstocks, with average weekly discharge typically 11,000-13,000 cfs (range 5,000-17,000 cfs).

The [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and [[Utah Division of Fish and Game]] maintain 21 water quality sampling stations in the main stem plus additional stations in bays and tributaries. Data collection includes temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, major ions (Na, K, Ca, HCO₃, CO₃, Cl, SO₄), boron, total dissolved solids (TDS), conductivity, turbidity, hardness, alkalinity, phosphates, nitrogen, and other parameters, typically on a quarterly basis with monthly temperature and dissolved oxygen monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-Impoundment Conditions**: Prior to dam closure, the Colorado and San Juan Rivers were highly turbid and erratic in discharge, flowing through narrow steep-sided canyons with few backwaters and unstable sandy bottoms. A specialized aquatic biota adapted to current-dominated conditions was present, plus some introduced eastern species. Phytoplankton was essentially absent due to high turbidities and physical action of currents.

**Post-Impoundment Transformation**: Establishment of the reservoir immediately interrupted life cycles of current-adapted species while greatly benefiting quiet-water organisms. Major ecological shifts included:

- **Primary Production**: Rapid shift from attached benthic algae to rich phytoplankton communities. Common species include the diatom *Fragillaria*, desmid *Staurastrum*, and increasingly *Asterionella*. Algal blooms are frequent, mostly in the upper lake in spring-summer and downstream portions in July.

- **Invertebrates**: Transition from bottom-dwelling insects to abundant zooplankton. Planktonic crustaceans are exceedingly abundant and comprise major food for fish species. Rotifers also contribute substantially to plankton biomass. The upper 25 feet (photic zone) is most productive, but some species occur to 50-100 feet depth. A crayfish species, not known in the area prior to impoundment, has invaded from upstream and is abundant in the upper reservoir.

**Fish Community Dynamics**: Native Colorado River fishes, adapted to turbulent conditions, have largely been replaced by introduced species better suited to lentic environments:

- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides*): Range at 10-50 feet depth during stratification where plankton and forage fish concentrate in warm, highly oxygenated water. Extend to 100 feet during unstratified periods. Abundance per unit area is now uniform throughout the lake. Diet consists mostly of other fishes downstream but increasingly crayfish upstream. Growth rates were initially higher upstream except near the plunge point.

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdneri*): Second in importance to fishery in early years, now surpassed by [[Channel Catfish]]. Do not reproduce in Lake Powell but are repeatedly stocked. Concentrate in lower 2-5 miles near [[Wahweap Bay]]. In summer, occupy the metalimnion at 52-70°F and 60-90 feet depth. In winter, found in upper 40 feet within maximum plankton concentrations. Correlation between trout numbers and penstock withdrawal depth suggests positive response to withdrawal-induced currents. Trout pass through penstocks and appear in tailrace.

- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): Second in importance to fishery since 1967. More ubiquitous than other species, occurring throughout reservoir at various depths with greater abundance in middle and upper reaches.

- **[[Walleye]]** (*Stizostedion vitreum*): Appeared "naturally" from upstream reservoirs, now moving through dam into [[Lake Mead]]. Populations increasing with confirmed reproduction. Expected to concentrate at trout level but with greater horizontal distribution.

- **[[Black Crappie]]** (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*): Introduced at Wahweap in 1964, spread to 30 miles above dam by 1966, 75 miles by 1967, now essentially throughout reservoir. Young ranged from surface to 140 feet in winter 1967-68.

- **[[Threadfin Shad]]** (*Dorosoma petenense*): Introduced in 1968 as forage. Cold-sensitive but has overwintered and reproduced. Expected to attain massive populations and markedly increase game fish production.

- **Non-game Species**: [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) most abundant large fish, concentrating in upper reaches and middle areas in warm upper water during summer. [[Green Sunfish]] decreasing in abundance and size while [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) becoming more abundant and widespread, particularly in upper lake.

**Tailwater Fishery**: Radical changes in thermal regime (narrowed from 34°F in January-83°F in July historically to 51-68°F post-dam) and discharge patterns severely affected indigenous fauna. Rainbow trout stocked in cool tailrace have established substantial fishery. Food supply includes plankton carried through penstocks and fish (including trout) passing through the dam. Stocking of invertebrates and algae undertaken to supplement food supply.

## Key Findings

**Water Quality and Salinity Dynamics**:

1. **Inflow Characteristics**: For water years 1965-1969, mean annual flows at principal stations were: Green River at Green River 4,401,000 acre-feet, Colorado River at Cisco 4,424,000 acre-feet, San Juan River at Bluff 1,577,000 acre-feet, totaling 10,402,000 acre-feet (86% of 1914-1957 adjusted average). Flow-weighted average dissolved solids: Green River 427 ppm, Colorado at Cisco 547 ppm, San Juan 361 ppm.

2. **Salinity Trends**: Combined inflows at main stations were 71% of 1914-1957 average while weighted inflow dissolved solids concentration increased 28% compared to 26% increase in outflow at [[Lees Ferry]]. The 52% increase in San Juan salinity is particularly notable. During water years 1963-1968, inflow stations "explained" 95.85% of dissolved solids at Lees Ferry compared to 89.93% historically (1914-1957).

3. **No Precipitation/Solution**: Unlike [[Lake Mead]], preliminary results indicate no significant precipitation of dissolved solids (e.g., CaCO₃) or solutioning of materials in Lake Powell. Between March 1963 and January 1969, the difference between inflow and outflow dissolved solids accumulated to approximately 9.5 million tons, closely matching the estimated 9.7-10.0 million tons in the reservoir proper. Bank storage (estimated 4.6 million acre-feet as of December 1968) contained approximately 4.9 million tons of dissolved solids, roughly equaling the 5.1 million tons contributed by local drainage between main inflow stations and Lees Ferry.

4. **Ionic Composition**: Trilinear plots of 63 lake samples from Wahweap (January-September 1969) plot within zones representing mixtures of the three main inflows for the preceding five water years, confirming minimal precipitation or solutioning. Three analyses taken at elevation 3,500 feet during July-September 1969 plot intermediate between May-July inflow composition polygons, suggesting direct feeding of inflow into the penstock-withdrawal density current. The San Juan waters, characterized by much lower salinities, appear not to be entrained but rather float in a large surface "patch" in mid-reservoir.

5. **Evaporation Effects**: Mean annual evaporation varies from 44 inches in Colorado headwaters to 60 inches at dam. Evaporation averaged 250,000 acre-feet annually from 1965-1969, expected to range from 420,000-620,000 acre-feet at future lake levels. During May-October (74% of annual evaporation), surface waters are characteristically warmer and 25-60% less saline than waters at depth, leaving behind less dissolved solids than annual-weighted average inflow salinity.

**Temperature Regime**:

1. **Inflow Temperatures**: Minimum inflow temperatures of 32°F occur December-February, rising to 75-80°F in July-August. Spring runoff (late May-early June) temperatures range 50-60°F on three main tributaries.

2. **Reservoir Stratification**: In lower reservoir during July-August, epilimnion is typically 30 feet thick with maximum temperatures 80-83°F; thermocline approximately 100 feet thick; below, temperatures grade uniformly from 50°F to 44°F with increasing depth. In fall-winter, downstream portion approaches nearly isothermal condition; in upper arms, colder inflowing waters progress downstream as density currents.

3. **Maximum Density Water**: January 13-15, 1969 data show near-maximum density water (38-40°F) between Hite and Bullfrog Stations, corresponding to 85,300 acre-feet passing Cisco and Green River gages 50 days earlier (November 23-27, 1968) when flow-weighted temperature rapidly declined from 41°F to 37°F. Lighter 32-33°F water followed as surface flow in piston-like progression.

4. **Discharge Temperature**: Below dam at Lees Ferry, temperature regime narrowed from historic 34-83°F to 51-68°F since 1965, creating favorable trout environment. Expected to narrow further with rising reservoir, but drawdown to minimum power pool would result in higher median and broader range.

**Dissolved Oxygen**:

Cold winter inflows of high DO entering as heavy bottom-lying density currents are significant oxygen sources for deep layers, while warmer surface-lying spring-summer inflows have less DO and higher BOD due to higher organic matter. Lowest values (4-5 ppm) occur in winter only in lower reservoir at depths exceeding 250 feet. Above this depth, DO ranged 5-12 ppm, satisfactory for most aquatic species. High DO at or near saturation evident in most of reservoir in July due to interflow-type density currents in deeper water and photosynthesis in upper layers.

**Other Parameters**: pH typically 7.7-7.8 at inflow stations with little change between flood and low flows. In reservoir, pH ranges 7.6-8.3, largely 7.7-8.1, with tendency for higher values near surface in summer (photosynthesis). Alkalinity almost entirely bicarbonate type, about 120-160 ppm at surface increasing with depth. Total hardness ranged 200 ppm at surface to 450 ppm at depth. Phosphates 0.30-0.80 ppm. Total organic solids 120-140 ppm throughout reservoir. Organic nitrogen 0.18-0.33 ppm.

**Stratification and Density Currents**:

1. **Penstock-Withdrawal Control**: Penstock-induced withdrawal currents are the most significant currents in lower Lake Powell, easily traced throughout much of the reservoir by temperature, TDS, ionic composition, and dissolved oxygen. These currents control geometry for at least 25 miles upstream, sometimes beyond San Juan confluence, due to nearly uniform canyon cross-section and lack of significant inflow over this reach.

2. **Mathematical Modeling**: An approximate procedure combining King's expression for local densimetric Froude number with turbulent prototype withdrawal layer relationships (Brooks and Koh) and velocity profiles (Bohan and Grace) was developed and programmed for electronic computation. Model validation against Fontana Reservoir data showed satisfactory reproduction of main withdrawal velocity profile. At Lake Powell, computed flow-weighted temperature and dissolved solids generally agreed well with tailrace measurements (e.g., July 1969: computed 60°F vs. measured 56°F; computed 632 ppm vs. measured 636 ppm TDS).

3. **Density Current Tracers**: Water quality parameters serve as effective tracers for density currents. The July 1969 profile shows a continuous density flow at elevation 3,500 feet extending the entire length of the Colorado arm, with isohalines and isotherms indicating inflow directly feeding the outflow density current.

**Future Projections**:

1. **Upstream Development Impacts**: Interior Department projections (based on 1941-1966 period) estimate future conditions after completion of contemplated developments: Green River salinity increase 24% (to 566 ppm), Colorado at Cisco 30% (to 794 ppm), San Juan 114% (to 920 ppm), Colorado at Lees Ferry 35% (to 735 ppm). At future annual average of 920 ppm, San Juan water would flow at depth most of the time, usually at reservoir bottom, causing significant redistribution of currents.

2. **Lake Level Effects**: Rising lake levels will cause penstock withdrawal to strip less better-quality surface water and more dense, saline water at depth, increasing salinity at Lees Ferry regardless of upstream development. At higher levels, less-saline flood flows will spread over greater area and thinner proportion of depth. This effect will be accentuated by any increase in inflow salinity but limited by continuity and forced mixing between less-saline flood flows and more-saline late-season flows.

3. **Island Formation**: When water surface reaches elevation 3,610 feet, Wahweap and Warm Creek bays will merge across a saddle to form large island, affording two channels to dam. Submerged topography suggests little penstock-withdrawal current should follow this path; spillway operation (crest elevation 3,658 feet) would draw from both channels but such discharges expected to be extremely rare and brief.

4. **Biological Projections**: Next few years will see tremendous ecological changes. Further filling and inundation of broad, gently-sloping reaches in upper canyons and bays should greatly benefit sunfishes, largemouth bass, crappie, and bluegill. Shallow areas will be warmer, allowing carp and other warm-water species to increase. Lake will "evolve" more toward warm-water fishery quantitatively, though cold-water and mid-water fishes will remain in substantial numbers. Anticipated salinity increases do not enter range directly detrimental to existing fish fauna.

**Cooling Tower Blowdown Disposal**:

1. **Original Proposal**: Return approximately 3,000 gpm (6 cfs average, 4,500 acre-feet annually) of blowdown water at 6X concentration after consumptive use of 34,000 acre-feet annually for cooling towers. This would remove net 15 tons/day (5,500 tons/year) of dissolved solids from Colorado River system due to acid addition for pH control and drift loss.

2. **Jet Dispersion Studies**: Preliminary studies using Ditmars' program (based on Fan and Brooks research) showed that with high-velocity jet dispersion and nozzle at elevation 3,450 feet, maximum centerline concentrations would be 20% of outlet values within 75 feet. With full entrainment into withdrawal current, maximum increase in concentration at Lees Ferry would be less than 3 ppm (assuming maximum consumptive use and return of all diverted dissolved solids). Under more reasonable assumptions, increase would be 1-2 ppm at Lees Ferry, 1.3-2.6 ppm at Imperial Dam.

3. **Decision**: Despite findings indicating disposal could be achieved without measurable economic effect on downstream interests or harmful effects to reservoir ecology, objections were raised by Federal Water Quality Administration and downstream interests to even nominal salinity increases. To avoid setting precedent for further Colorado River quality deterioration, Navajo Project Participants decided to abandon scheme in favor of more expensive alternatives not requiring return of concentrated blowdown to Lake Powell.

**Research Value**: The data already available on water quality and lake's unique geometry make Lake Powell an ideal candidate for research on reservoir density currents. A quantitative sequential study of variation in chemical composition and heat content from inflow stations through reservoir holds particular promise for advancing understanding of density current parameters and behavior. An additional year of data with velocity measurements and perhaps additional stations would greatly enhance value of such investigation.
