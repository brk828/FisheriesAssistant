# Status of Fishes from Sinkholes and Caves in Namibia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #clariidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 review by P.H. Skelton, published in the Namibia Scientific Society's Journal 42, assesses the conservation status of fishes living in the dolomite karstveld of northern Namibia. The fishes occur in two sinkhole lakes near [[Tsumeb]], [[Lake Guinas]] and [[Lake Otjikoto]], and in one cave lake, [[Aigamas Cave]], near Otavi. The author argues that these fishes are of high scientific and aesthetic value, that several are threatened, and that conservation depends on legal protection, landowner engagement, and monitoring of water levels and introduced species.

## Background & Description

- **Study scope:** The paper draws on the author's involvement in revising the [[Red Data Book]] for fishes (Skelton, 1987).
- **Karst formation:** Caves and sinkholes form where water dissolves dolomite fissures. Sinkholes form when cave roofs collapse. Some caves hold large underground lakes, such as Dragon Breath Cave.
- **Historical records:** Sir Francis Galton first recorded fish in Otjikoto Lake in 1861. Karl Jordan's 1933 collections were described by [[Ernst Trewavas]] in 1936, yielding *Tilapia guinasana* (new species) and a subspecies of *Pseudocrenilabrus philander*.
- **Key taxa:**
  - *Tilapia guinasana* (Guinas tilapia): notable for striking colour variation in Lake Guinas. Listed as Endangered.
  - *Pseudocrenilabrus philander* (southern mouthbrooder): the only known native fish in Lake Otjikoto; the most common fish below about 12 m there.
  - *Clarias cavernicola* (cave catfish): the only true phreatobic (cave) fish in southern Africa, known only from Aigamas Cave. Listed as Endangered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Guinas and Lake Otjikoto**
- Lake Guinas has no known alien fish introductions and holds only two native species, *T. guinasana* and *P. philander*.
- Lake Otjikoto has been altered by man-made structures (pump platforms, water gauges) and by introductions of *Oreochromis mossambicus*, *T. guinasana*, guppies (*Poecilia reticulata*) and swordtails (*Xiphophorus helleri*). The author notes that a World War I dump of weapons and ammunition lies in the lake.
- Threats to Guinas include exploitation of its water supply, its restricted range, and public access to a privately owned lake.
- Otjikoto's native mouthbrooder survives despite introductions, pollution and water extraction, according to [[Twentyman-Jones]] (1988). The author notes that no pre-introduction studies exist, so the impact of introductions cannot be quantified.

**Aigamas Cave**
- The cave is a narrow slit that drops about 20 m to the water surface. Animal carcasses (a kudu and a baboon were observed) and bat droppings provide food for the fishes.
- Fish congregate on a shelf a few metres deep and were not observed below about 15 m. The lake drops a further 30 m to the cave floor, where old pump pipes lie at about 50 m.
- Pumping for irrigation after about 1900 lowered the water level. The water level is now lower than when [[Jaeger and Waibel]] visited in 1921, possibly due to regional groundwater depletion. The population is believed to be small.
- The cave's remoteness and the owner's protection currently limit collecting pressure.

## Key Findings

1. **Endangered status:** *Tilapia guinasana* and *Clarias cavernicola* are both listed as Endangered in the Red Data Book (Skelton, 1987).
2. **Genetic value of Lake Guinas:** Colour variation in Lake Guinas is striking, with nearly every individual differently coloured or patterned. Otjikoto populations show little variation, so they hold only a fraction of the genetic diversity of the source population.
3. **Introduction risk:** The author stresses that introduced fishes can prey on, compete with, hybridise with, or carry disease to natives. Because Guinas holds the only natural *T. guinasana* population, no introductions can be tolerated.
4. **Otjikoto as a study site:** The lake offers a rare opportunity to study the ecology and impact of introduced species, though the lack of baseline data limits conclusions.
5. **Aigamas water-level risk:** Water-level decline may threaten the cave catfish. A detailed study is needed to confirm this.
6. **Captive breeding:** *T. guinasana* breeds readily in captivity. The author suggests maintaining captive stocks at a reliable facility such as the [[National Aquarium]] in Pretoria. Attempts to breed the cave catfish have failed due to disease.
7. **Recommended actions:**
   - Educate landowners and the public on the scientific and aesthetic value of the fishes.
   - Establish legal protection and possibly special conservation site status for Lake Guinas.
   - Prohibit introductions and pollution in karstveld waterbodies.
   - Monitor water levels and conduct limnological and ecological studies.
   - Adopt a heritage scheme similar to South Africa's National Heritage Scheme.
8. **Conclusion:** Ignorance is possibly the greatest threat to these species. Survival requires a coordinated programme involving landowners, conservation authorities and the people of Namibia.
