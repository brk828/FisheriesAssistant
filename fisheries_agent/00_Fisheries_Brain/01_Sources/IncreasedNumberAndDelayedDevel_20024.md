# Increased Number and Delayed Development of Scales in Abnormal Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1940 paper by [[Carl L Hubbs]] (published 1941 in the *Papers of the Michigan Academy of Science, Arts, and Letters*) describes teratological suckers collected on August 25, 1939, from [[Nash Bog Pond]] and its outlet, [[Nash Stream]], in the [[Connecticut River]] system of New Hampshire. The abnormal fish, identified as [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii commersonii*), showed retained larval features, delayed development of squamation, and roughly 50 per cent more scales than normal conspecifics. Hubbs attributes the scale increase to delayed lepidogenesis: scale foci form at approximately constant absolute distances, so a larger unscaled body base yields more scales. The paper also reports that parasitism and silt diet were not supported as causes, and proposes a genetic basis, while not excluding environmental causes.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** The specimens were referred to Hubbs by [[Joseph R Bailey]] and [[James A Oliver]], who were surveying the fishes of the Connecticut River watershed in New Hampshire (Bailey and Oliver, 1940).
- **Identification:** Abnormal fish were identified as *C. c. commersonii* rather than the fine-scaled [[Fine-scaled Sucker]] (*Catostomus catostomus nannomyzon*), based on snout shape, juvenile color pattern (three dark blotches), and dorsal ray counts. Scale size resembled the fine-scaled form.
- **Body condition:** Fish appeared well fed, thick, deep, and pudgy, not emaciated. Stomachs contained cladocerans, a common food of young suckers.
- **Morphology of abnormal fish:** Soft, weak texture; oblique mouth; flabby, transparent body wall with visible heart and ribs; pectoral fins retaining traces of larval pedunculation; and gill membranes free from the isthmus much longer than in normal development.
- **Behaviour:** Abnormal fish, 21 to 37 mm standard length, swam jerkily near the surface, as postlarval and very young suckers do, rather than occupying the bottom.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Site conditions (August 25, 1939):** Rather clear, light-brown water at 70° F in midafternoon, air temperature equal; moderate current in the outlet; bottom of rubble, boulders, silt, and muck with sparse vegetation.
- **Local conditions:** Nothing unusual was found to explain the aberrancy. Internal parasite searches in two abnormal fish revealed relatively few infestations, which Hubbs took as negating a helminth cause proposed for parallel abnormalities (Hubbs, 1927) and the silt-diet hypothesis of Bailey and Oliver.
- **Seasonality and temperature:** Lower dorsal ray counts in smaller normal fish of the August collection may reflect seasonal variation, in which fish hatched in warmer water show fewer segments (Hubbs, 1922, 1923, 1925, 1926a, 1934).
- **Reference to growth in relation to scales:** Normal *C. commersonii* begin scale formation at "somewhat less than 22 millimeters" (Stewart, 1926).

## Key Findings

- **Scale count increase:** Normal fish from this locality had 58 to 67 lateral-line scales. Abnormal fish of larger size had about 88 to 101 scales (counts of 96, 97, and 101 were near certain; 88 to 92 were less sure and treated as minimums).
- **Dorsal rays:** Abnormal fish averaged 11.79 principal dorsal rays (21 to 37 mm, mostly 24 to 35 mm). Their average is close to other-locality *C. commersonii* and to normal conspecifics of like size, not to *C. c. nannomyzon* (average 9.97 elsewhere, 10.33 at Nash Bog Pond from 3 fish).
- **Squamation development:** Table III staged scale development from 0 (no scales) to 7 (complete). Abnormal fish showed retarded stages compared with normal conspecifics of the same length.
- **Gill membranes:** Retention of free gill membranes was strikingly prolonged in all aberrant fish compared with normal development (Table IV). Hubbs had previously seen a similar retention only in *Margariscus margarita nachtriebi* from near the northern limit of its range (Cowan, 1939).
- **Causal interpretation:** Hubbs proposed that delayed differentiation while general growth continues yields a larger scale-bearing area, so more scales form. He notes a genetic basis is suspected, but an environmental cause "is by no means ruled out."
- **Geographic parallel:** In [[Northern Redbelly Dace]]-related studies of *Notropis cornutus* (cited as Hubbs and Brown, in Ortenburger and Hubbs, 1927), protracted scale formation toward the north correlates with more predorsal scales.
- **Broader conclusion:** Modifications in the rates of growth and differentiation alter fish characters and play an important role in speciation.
