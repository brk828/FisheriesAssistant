# Use of Genetic Tags to Evaluate Stocking Success for Reservoir Walleyes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Brian R. Murphy]], [[Larry A. Nielsen]], and [[Bruce J. Turner]] (Transactions of the American Fisheries Society, 1983, vol. 112, no. 4, pp. 457–463) uses allozyme genetic markers to separate natural recruitment from supplemental stocking in [[Walleye]] populations of [[Claytor Lake]], Virginia. Using [[Malate Dehydrogenase]] (MDH) allele frequencies, the authors estimate that stocked juveniles contributed an average of 67% of year-class strength to augmented cohorts during 1976–1979. The approach is presented as a practical tool for evaluating stocking efficiency and cost, even where stocked fish lack unique genetic characters.

## Background & Description

Walleyes (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) have been widely introduced across North America, often in habitats marginal for natural reproduction. Stocking is therefore used both to maintain non-reproducing populations and to augment poor year classes. Conventional marks such as fin clips or chemical tags are detectable for no more than about one year, and alternate-year stocking comparisons risk losing an entire year class if natural reproduction fails.

Genetic markers based on heritable enzyme phenotypes offer a permanent, non-invasive alternative. Earlier work by [[Schweigert et al. 1977]] in West Blue Lake, Manitoba, used fixed MDH phenotype differences as markers. The present study addresses the more common situation in which stocked and resident fish share alleles but differ in allele frequencies.

[[Claytor Lake]] is a 1,820-hectare hydroelectric impoundment of the [[New River]], formed in 1939. It is deep (mean depth 37.5 m), riverine in shape, with limited littoral area (<5 m depth). Its stocking history is complex: fish from Pennsylvania (1939–1946, probably Lake Erie origin), and later from Nebraska (1974, 1975) and Kansas (1977, 1979), the latter two ultimately derived from the Red River area of Minnesota. The authors note the lake may hold three geographic strains: a native Ohio River drainage strain, a Great Lakes strain, and a Hudson Bay drainage strain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Walleye recruitment in Claytor Lake declined after 1972, and surveys found no evidence of natural reproduction during 1973–1975, prompting resumed stocking in 1974. The authors could not collect any native-origin walleyes during surveys of southwestern Virginia streams, so the native strain's contribution could not be sampled directly.

Mixing of genetic stocks through egg, larval, and juvenile exchange among drainages is identified as a general management problem for North American gamefish. Walleyes are also known to mature a year earlier in warmer, more fertile lakes, which the authors invoke to explain possible reproduction by Nebraska fish in 1976 and 1978.

Relevant comparison sites include [[West Blue Lake]], Manitoba (stocked at 16 juveniles/hectare-year), [[Clear Lake]], Iowa, and [[Pymatuning Reservoir]], Pennsylvania, which holds a Lake Erie-strain population.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- 217 walleyes were collected from Claytor Lake at three sites (March–May and October–December 1979; April and July 1980).
- 31 adult [[Webster Reservoir]] (Kansas) walleyes were sampled for reference.
- Muscle tissue was analysed by starch gel electrophoresis with MDH histochemical staining, and genotype proportions were tested against Hardy–Weinberg expectations.
- The [[Widrig and Taft Method]] (modified) estimated the percentage of an augmented cohort derived from stocked fish, with 95% confidence limits.

**Allele frequencies (Mdh-B² frequency, sample size in parentheses)**

| Cohort | Stocking | Reference | Claytor cohort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1976 | Unstocked | — | 0.57 (35) |
| 1977 | 114,000 Kansas juveniles | 0.84 (139) | 0.73 (98) |
| 1978 | Unstocked | — | 0.53 (44) |
| 1979 | 55,000 Kansas juveniles | 0.84 | 0.76 (36) |
| 1976 + 1978 | Natural only | — | 0.55 (79) |
| 1977 + 1979 | Augmented | — | 0.74 (129) |

- Pennsylvania reference frequency was 0.80 (100); Nebraska reference frequency was 0.54 (120).
- Allele frequencies varied among cohorts (P < 0.002); homogeneity across all groups was rejected (P < 0.001).
- No Hardy–Weinberg deviation was found (P > 0.1), and no sex effect was detected (P > 0.9).

**Stocking contribution**
- Stocked fish contributed an estimated **62%** (95% CI 52–72%) of the 1977 year class at ages II and III.
- Stocked fish contributed an estimated **72%** (57–87%) of the 1979 year class in its first two years.
- The authors report a mean contribution of **67%** across stocked year classes.
- This estimate is comparable to the 66% contribution of stocked larvae in Clear Lake (Carlander et al. 1960), and far above the 7% reported for juveniles in West Blue Lake (Schweigert et al. 1977). After adjusting for stocking density, Claytor Lake juvenile stocking was about three times more effective.

**Economic implications**
- Claytor Lake anglers harvested an annual average of 2,300 walleyes during 1976–1978.
- With 67% of recruitment from stocking, about 34% of harvested walleyes were attributed to the stocking program.
- An annual average of 46,800 juveniles were stocked during 1975–1979, at $0.46 per 4.5-cm fish, giving average annual costs of $21,528.
- The estimated cost per stocked walleye harvested during 1976–1978 was $27.35. The authors stress this estimate depends on the reliability of its inputs and that age composition of the harvest is needed.

**Conclusions**
- Allozyme markers can assess stock structure, natural reproduction, stocking effectiveness, and economic efficiency, even where allele frequencies rather than fixed differences separate stocks.
- Genetic divergence can persist after introduced stocks have died out, enabling tracking of later generations.
- The authors call for further research on intralacustrine genetic dynamics to understand the effects of introducing new genetic strains.
