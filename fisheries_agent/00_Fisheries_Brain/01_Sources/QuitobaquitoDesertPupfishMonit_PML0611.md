# Quitobaquito Desert Pupfish Monitoring Protocol

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a near-final draft report by [[Michael Douglas and Marlis Douglas]] (Arizona State University) that proposes a standardized annual census for the endangered [[Quitobaquito Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*) at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] and pond in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. It is accompanied by a cover note from [[Tim Tibbitts]] and by annual Threatened, Endangered and Sensitive Species summaries for 1996 to 1999 from the [[National Park Service]].

The proposed protocol replaces the [[Depletion Sampling]] method used from 1985 to 1995. It calls for marking fish with [[Fluorescent Grit Marking]] applied by compressed air, resampling one week later, checking captures for marks under ultraviolet light, and analyzing counts with a [[Lincoln-Petersen Estimator]] under a [[Closed Population Model]]. Trial censuses from 1997 to 1999 exposed several problems, including residual marks, a failed UV light source, and a pooled estimate that was biased by unequal recapture rates between the channel and pond.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The genus *Cyprinodon* comprises about 30 species, 20 of them desert or semidesert forms. The [[Desert Pupfish]] (*C. macularius*) is of Late Pliocene origin and was described from the San Pedro River by Baird and Girard (1853). Its original range exceeded 40,000 km² (50 to 1,500 m altitude). The Quitobaquito form was named a subspecies in 1987 by Miller and Fuiman, based on reduced pelvic fins and fin-ray counts. The 1999 summary reports that mitochondrial DNA work by [[Anthony Echelle]] supports elevating the Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta pupfish to a distinct species, *C. eremus*.

**Life history.** The Quitobaquito population is fed by a groundwater spring with a mean springhead temperature of 26.0 °C (SD 0.86) over 18 months. Pupfish grow rapidly in their first year (mean 29.6 mm TL), then more slowly: about 44.2 mm in year two (+10.6 mm) and 48 mm in year three (+7.8 mm) (Kynard and Garrett, 1979). Breeding begins in late April to early May, when daily maxima exceed 24 °C. Fish in the springhead and channel stay active year-round, while those in the pond aestivate in detritus during colder months, when pond temperatures can fall to 13 °C.

**Concerns and history.** Early censuses (1975 to 1981) gave estimates of 7,294 in 1975 and 1,800 in 1981, with intervening years at 3,000 to 6,700. NPS questioned their reliability. Methods then changed: [[Fin Clipping]] mark-recapture (1982 to 1984), depletion sampling (1985 to 1995), and the proposed fluorescent-grit method. Depletion sampling caused injury and death from crowding in holding tanks and seemed to underestimate the population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Quitobaquito is the species' sole critical habitat apart from a refugium at Arizona State University. Threats include:

- Groundwater pumping, pesticide drift and agriculture in the Sonoyta Valley, Sonora, just south of the spring.
- [[Mexican Highway 2]], about 100 m south of the pond, which serves as a rest stop and starting point for illegal border traffic.
- Visitation of more than 250,000 people per year, plus litter, erosion and vegetation loss, worsened by budget and staffing cuts.
- Non-native fish, including [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus melas*). A catfish was removed from the southwest spring in August 1993, and no others have been found.

The pond is much larger than the channel, which makes mark-recapture harder. The channel is about 240 ft³, while the pond is about 55,000 ft³ (the 1998 annual summary gives the channel as 0.47% of pond volume). The channel is a pseudo-closed system that is easy to trap, but most of the fish are in the pond. Stream and pond have both been modified over time to improve water delivery and pond volume.

The site is also a habitat for the [[Rio Sonoyta Mud Turtle]], and it contains cultural and archaeological features. Management aims to protect the pupfish, provide shallow-water habitat for the turtle, and conserve other native aquatic and riparian species.

## Key Findings

**Proposed protocol**

- **Timing (Recommendation 1):** Census in early October each year, after the reproductive season and before pond fish aestivate. The 1999 summary refers to mid-October.
- **Sampling effort (Recommendation 2):** At least 31 minnow traps: 2 at the springhead, 10 in the channel, 7 at the island, and 12 in the pond. No traps are placed in the open pond interior, which would require a boat.
- **Marking (Recommendation 3):** Fluorescent grit applied by low-pressure air (under 100 psi), with red, orange and yellow as available colors. Marks are retained for 4 to 12+ months. Other marking methods were rejected: fin clipping is labor-intensive, PIT tags are too costly for such small, short-lived fish, and latex injection is impractical for small bodies.
- **Analysis (Recommendation 4):** A closed-population model using the Lincoln-Petersen estimator, because immigration, emigration and mortality can be assumed negligible over the short recapture window. Open models require far more data.

**Trial and monitoring results**

- **October 1997 mark:** About 640 fish marked in springhead, channel, island and pond. Recapture failed because of a UV light malfunction, so no estimate was produced.
- **Spring 1998 (springhead/channel):** 353 fish marked in February with yellow pigment. Estimates rose as spring progressed. Marked fish fell by 43% and unmarked fish rose by 63% from February to May. Movement from springhead/channel to the pond was limited January to May, while movement from pond to channel increased as fish emerged.
- **September 1998 trial census:** Channel estimate 978 (95% CI about 935 to 1,044); pond estimate about 7,845 (very wide CI); pooled estimate 2,231 (CI 2,082 to 2,391). The combined separate estimates total 8,823, which is in line with depletion-era estimates. The authors judged the pooled figure too low because channel and pond recapture rates differed greatly, and they concluded that pupfish remained abundant.
- **1996 census:** 5,463 fish captured (pond 4,743 in three runs; springhead/channel 720). The census was ended after one day because holding tanks were overcrowded, causing aggression and 9 deaths.
- **October 1999 pre-survey:** 807 fish caught, compared with 680 in the same locations in 1998. Of these, 49 (about 6%) carried residual orange and yellow marks, some about 18 months old. In the channel, 48 of 413 fish (11.6%) were marked. Because of residual marks, the planned 1999 mark-recapture survey was not carried out. Two juveniles (about 12 mm) died during capture.

**Size and reproduction**

- Mean TL in the springhead was 34.7 mm and in the channel 36.8 mm. The authors concluded there was no meaningful size difference between these reaches.
- Histograms show small fish (under 10 to 11 mm TL) in March and May 1997 in the springhead/channel but not in the pond. This suggests local reproduction, and possibly late-season reproduction in October 1997.

**Non-native fish surveillance**

- No non-native fish were captured in 1995 to 1999 despite repeated baited hoop-net and minnow-trap sampling. Weekly inspections found none.

**Management and research priorities**

- Use microsatellite DNA markers, sampled by non-lethal pelvic-fin clipping, to test reproductive isolation between the springhead and the pond.
- Run a finer mark-recapture study to see whether pupfish move between pools within the stream.
- Establish refugium populations of *C. eremus*, since the ASU refugium is the only one.
- Address litter, fire risk from dense vegetation and non-native grasses, possible chemical contamination from Highway 2, and removal of seedling trees from the dam.
