# Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This technical review draft, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 6), is a recovery plan for the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest cyprinid in North America and the top native carnivore of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The plan summarizes the species' description, distribution, life history, and reasons for decline, and sets out a stepdown outline of recovery actions with a costed implementation schedule (1988 values). The document is explicitly a draft: it has not been approved by the Service or other agencies, and its goals depend on appropriations and budget constraints.

The species has declined from its historic range across the basin to a small number of reproducing populations, concentrated in the upper basin (the Green, Yampa, White, and Colorado rivers) with a small reproducing population in the [[San Juan River]]. Natural populations are considered extirpated from the lower basin, where stocking of hatchery fish is under way.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish is an elongated, pike-like minnow with a long, flattened head and a large, nearly horizontal mouth. Diagnostic counts include a pharyngeal tooth formula of 2,5-4,2, a dorsal fin with nine rays, and 80-95 scales in the lateral line. Adults are strongly countershaded (dark olive back, lighter sides, white belly), and young often bear a dark, wedge-shaped spot at the base of the caudal fin.

Maximum weights above 36 kg and lengths near 1.8 m have been recorded, but specimens above 7 kg have been rare in recent times. Early settlers called it "white salmon," "Colorado salmon," or simply "salmon." The species is the largest of four living members of the genus, the others being the [[Sacramento Squawfish]], [[Northern Squawfish]], and [[Umpqua Squawfish]], which remain abundant and are regarded as "rough fish." Fossil evidence shows the species used both lakes and rivers, and its large size, mobility, and spawning migrations were adaptations to drier climatic periods.

Legal status: listed as endangered by the Service on March 11, 1967 (Federal Register Vol. 32[43]:40001), with full protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] from January 4, 1974.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Historically the species was abundant in the main stem Colorado River, most major tributaries, and the Colorado delta in Mexico, with records from eight jurisdictions. Present natural populations are restricted mainly to the Upper Colorado River Basin. The most abundant reaches are the Green River below the Yampa confluence, the Yampa below Craig, the White River from Taylor Draw Dam to the Green, and the main stem Colorado from Palisades to Lake Powell. Catches of young, juveniles, and adults were reported to be an order of magnitude higher in the Green River basin than elsewhere. The species was extirpated from Wyoming (no fish found in 1986 surveys) and from the Gila River system; the last wild adult from the main stem lower Colorado was taken in 1967 in [[Lake Mohave]].

**Habitat and temperature.** Young (up to 64 mm TL) and juveniles (65-200 mm) use shallow backwaters with little current over silt and sand. Fish shift to deeper, faster water at about 200 mm, the same size at which they become piscivorous. Adults use varied depths and velocities. During peak runoff they occupy flooded backwaters; as flows decline they move to run-riffle, eddy, and pool habitats to spawn. Thermal tolerance is broad: Yuma summer temperatures approach or exceed 35°C, while the upper basin ranges from about 25°C in summer to 0°C in winter. Marsh (1985) found optimal embryo hatching near 20°C.

**Tributaries.** Tributaries such as the Yampa, White, Duchesne, Gunnison, and Dolores rivers support long-distance movements between main-stem spawning areas and feeding and overwintering habitat. Holden (1980) linked high spring Yampa flows (often above 12,000 cfs, approaching 20,000 cfs at Jensen) to juvenile success downstream in the Green River; in dry 1977, flows under 8,000 cfs coincided with the absence of young-of-year fish in nursery areas.

**Reasons for decline.** The plan attributes decline to a combination of factors: more than 20 dams built since 1913, altered temperatures and flows, loss of nutrients and turbidity, channelization that reduced backwaters, and interactions with [[Introduced Nonnative Fishes]]. Of 55 fish species in the upper basin, 42 are introduced, and Arizona has received at least 60 introductions. A 1962 accidental rotenone treatment is noted as a possible short-term impact in Dinosaur National Monument. The authors state that the absolute cause is not fully understood.

## Key Findings

- **Life history:** Males mature at 400 mm TL or more and at least 6 years of age; females mature a year later. Ripe males on Green River spawning grounds averaged 559 mm TL (n=120) and ripe females 710 mm (n=7).
- **Spawning:** Gonads ripen during spring runoff (May-June). Spawning begins near 21°C and peaks at 22-25°C, generally between late June and mid-August. Adults have moved up to 320 km to reach spawning areas.
- **Spawning sites:** Radiotelemetry suggests spawning is limited to two major sites in the Green basin, the lower 32 km of Yampa Canyon and Gray Canyon. Spawning is also suspected in Labyrinth Canyon and in several Colorado River reaches. Collections on the two known grounds in 1981-85 yielded 208 fish, of which 127 were ripe.
- **Growth:** Adults tagged in the Green River grew 9.9 mm/yr (n=53). Back-calculated lengths for 182 fish ranged from 44 mm at age I to 600 mm at age X. Pond-stocked fingerlings reached 185-304 mm in one year, versus about four years for wild fish, which the authors attribute to warmer water and abundant food.
- **Food:** Young feed mainly on zooplankton and insect larvae. Juveniles are about 86 percent fish by diet (mainly red shiner), and adults are almost exclusively piscivorous.
- **Recruitment:** Larval abundance was 233 and 271 per 1000 m² in 1979 and 1980, but only 5-8 per 1000 m² in high-flow years 1983-84. The plan recommends avoiding abnormally high summer and fall water levels in nursery areas.
- **Lower basin:** Stocking of 96,000+ fingerlings and 442 larger fish (355-405 mm TL) at six Salt and Verde River sites in 1985 produced preliminary evidence of survival. Salt and Verde populations are designated nonessential experimental populations.
- **San Juan River:** A small reproducing population exists. In 1987, 17 young-of-year fish were captured, and the plan calls the river one of only three remaining natural populations, a high-priority area under full ESA protection.
- **Hatchery production:** The species was first propagated at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery in 1974. Broodstock at Dexter National Fish Hatchery spawned after pituitary injection, with production limited by holding facilities.
- **Kenney Reservoir:** About 17,000 fingerlings were stocked in April 1988 as part of a three-year experimental sport fishery.
- **Recovery objectives:** Reclassification to threatened requires self-sustaining populations in the lower Green River to Echo Park, the lower 199 km of the Yampa, the lower 240 km of the White, and the Colorado from Palisade to Lake Powell. Delisting also requires removal of fragmentation threats and legal protection of essential habitat, flows, and water quality. The Upper Basin Recovery Implementation Program targets delisting by 2003.
- **Budget:** The Upper Basin program has a projected annual budget of $2.3 million. Requested funds include $10 million for water rights and $5 million for facilities. Private water projects would contribute $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion.
- **Implementation schedule:** Tasks are ranked in three priorities (Priority 1: prevent extinction; Priority 2: prevent significant decline; Priority 3: full recovery). Example Priority 1 costs for monitoring populations are $90,000 per year for FY-01 to FY-03, and for identifying essential habitat $200,000 per year. The schedule assigns tasks to the Service, the Bureau of Reclamation, and state agencies in Colorado, Utah, Arizona, California, New Mexico, and Nevada.
