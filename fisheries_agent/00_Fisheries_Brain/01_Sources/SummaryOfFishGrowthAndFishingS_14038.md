# Summary of Fish Growth and Fishing Success in Managed Strip-Pits Through Spring 1956

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #reclamation #warmwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This report documents fisheries management activities in reclaimed coal strip-pit lakes across southeastern Kansas (Crawford, Cherokee, and Linn Counties) from approximately 1951 through spring 1956. The [[Kansas Forestry Fish and Game Commission]] conducted reconditioning and restocking programs in 106 strip-pits totaling 600 acres to restore public fishing opportunities. Management involved complete fish population removal via rotenone poisoning or partial reduction, followed by strategic restocking with [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]], [[White Crappie]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. Fish growth rates in reconditioned pits dramatically exceeded expectations, with bass reaching 1-2 pounds within one year and channel catfish growing 6-8 inches in eleven months. Creel census data showed fishing success averaging approximately 1.0-1.2 fish per hour, matching national warmwater fishery averages. Three coal companies donated strip-land for public fishing development. The biologist's time was split between direct fisheries work (less than 50%) and extensive public relations activities including speaking engagements, technical assistance to private pond owners, and coordination with Kansas State Teachers College at Pittsburg.

## Background & Description

Strip-pit lakes are water-filled excavations remaining after surface coal mining operations in southeastern Kansas. Prior to management intervention, most pits exhibited severely degraded fisheries characterized by stunted, overabundant sunfish populations (often exceeding 7,700 fish per surface acre with only 30 keeper-size individuals), poor bass and crappie spawning success, and extremely low fishing success rates (considerably less than 1 fish per hour). The primary management technique involved complete eradication of existing fish populations using rotenone, followed by restocking with balanced predator-prey assemblages.

Managed species included:
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (Micropterus spp.) - primary predator
- **[[Bluegill]]** (Lepomis macrochirus) - forage and sport fish
- **[[Black Crappie]]** and **[[White Crappie]]** (Pomoxis spp.) - sport fish
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (Ictalurus punctatus) - sport fish, some pre-reared as "shorts" (6-10 inches)
- **[[Black Bullhead]]** (Ameiurus melas) - present in some systems
- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (Lepomis cyanellus) - incidental
- **[[Warmouth]]** (Lepomis gulosus) - incidental

The [[Kansas State Teachers College]] at Pittsburg provided laboratory facilities in Science Hall for routine water chemistry analyses, fish food studies, and disease/parasite investigations. Graduate students conducted coordinated research supporting management objectives.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water Chemistry**: Acidity testing was critical for determining pit suitability for fish life. Some waters required lime treatment to neutralize acid conditions resulting from coal mining operations. Water clarity (Secchi depth) typically exceeded 6 feet in most managed pits, which influenced crappie spawning success.

**Habitat Characteristics**: Pits ranged from 1 to 30 acres in surface area. Many featured complex morphology with finger-like extensions, interconnected basins, and variable depths. The [[Miner's State Park]] complex contained multiple interconnected pits (Fireplace Pit, Long Pit, Round Pit, Thousand Islands Pits) totaling approximately 15 acres. Drought conditions during the study period caused water level fluctuations that temporarily separated interconnected systems.

**Aquatic Vegetation**: Excessive growth of "skunk moss" (likely filamentous algae or aquatic moss) in some pits (e.g., Fireplace Pit) retarded fish growth and reduced fishing success. Copper sulfate treatment was recommended for control.

**Food Web Dynamics**: Successful bass growth depended critically on bluegill spawning during the first year post-stocking to provide forage for young bass. Stomach content analyses confirmed bluegill fry as primary bass food. [[Gizzard Shad]] were present in some systems (e.g., West 4th Street Pit) and supported crappie populations. The abundance of natural food organisms was enhanced in experimental fertilization trials.

**Stocking Strategies**: Pre-rearing channel catfish fingerlings to "short" size (6-10 inches) with supplemental feeding before transfer to strip-pits accelerated growth by one full season compared to stocking stunted fish of similar size. Healthy, well-conditioned fish began growing immediately upon stocking, while stunted transfers required most of their first season to recover before showing rapid growth.

## Key Findings

**Growth Rates - Channel Catfish**:
- "Short" channel catfish (6-10 inches at stocking, December 1953) grew to 18 inches and nearly 2 pounds in 11 months ([[23 Pit/Foxtown Pit]])
- Fingerlings (3 inches) reached 12 inches in 15 months, 15.5 inches and 1 pound in 39 months ([[East Pit]], Miner's State Park)
- 10-inch fish grew to 18.5 inches in 12 months ([[Partial Poison Pit]])
- Three-year growth from 10-11 inches to 14 pounds documented ([[Fireplace Pit/Long Pit/Round Pit]] complex)
- Average growth to 13-15 inches in first year common across multiple sites

**Growth Rates - Largemouth Bass**:
- Stocked bass reached 1 pound 3 ounces to 1.5 pounds and spawned successfully within one year ([[32 Pit]])
- 12-14 inch bass weighing 1 pound 6 ounces to 1 pound 12 ounces produced in one year following stocking when bluegill spawned first year
- 9-12.5 inch bass (up to 1.5 pounds) achieved in first year ([[Fertilized Pit]])
- Bass in [[East Pit]] reached 12 inches in 1954 (two years post-stocking), with larger individuals in 1955
- Trophy bass documented: 7 pounds 8 ounces (June 1954), multiple 5-7 pound fish across sites

**Growth Rates - Crappie**:
- Black crappie reached 6.5 inches in one year, 7 inches by second year ([[East Pit]])
- 7.75 inches documented in [[College Farm Pit]]
- Record black crappie: 19 inches, 4.5 pounds ([[Box Pit]], May 1954, caught by Mrs. Dorothy Kirk) - believed largest black crappie on record
- Multiple 2-3 pound crappie documented across sites

**Growth Rates - Bluegill and Sunfish**:
- Bluegills reached 5.75 inches in summer 1955 ([[East Pit]])
- 5.5-7 inch bluegills in June 1953 ([[College Farm Pit]])
- [[Warmouth]] reached 8.5 inches and 0.75 pound ([[East Pit]])
- [[Green Sunfish]] to 7 ounces ([[Transformer Pit]])

**Growth Rates - Bullheads**:
- [[Black Bullhead]] reached 9 inches and just under 0.5 pound in July 1954 ([[Bullsnake Pit]])
- 11.25 inches and 9 ounces documented ([[Pumphouse Pit]])

**Fishing Success Metrics**:
- Pre-reconditioning: considerably less than 1 fish per hour, no fish larger than 4 inches caught
- Post-reconditioning: approximately 1.0-1.2 fish per hour (matching U.S. warmwater fishery average)
- [[East Pit]] averaged 1.0 fish per hour (bass, crappie, bluegill combined)
- [[Fireplace Pit/Long Pit/Round Pit]] complex: 1.2 fish per hour
- Heavy fishing pressure documented at multiple sites, particularly [[Transformer Pit]] (attracting Missouri anglers)

**Population Dynamics**:
- Pre-treatment populations severely unbalanced: 7,700 fish per surface acre with only 30 keeper-size fish ([[East Pit]])
- Stunted bluegill populations with very low bass and crappie spawning success typical of unmanaged pits
- Bluegill spawning during first year post-stocking critical for bass forage and growth
- Bass spawned successfully at age 1 in multiple reconditioned pits
- Crappie spawning success variable; successful reproduction documented in [[Fertilized Pit]] (1955) despite typical expectation of poor crappie reproduction in clear strip-pits (Secchi depth >6 feet)

**Management Program Scale**:
- 106 strip-pits stocked and reconditioned, totaling 600 acres
- Sites distributed across Crawford, Cherokee, and Linn Counties
- [[Miner's State Park]] (Crawford County, Section 32-T29S-R25E) served as major public fishing destination
- Three coal companies donated strip-land: [[Hume-Sinclair Coal Mining Company]], [[Mackie-Clemens Fuel Company]], [[Apex-Compton Coal Company]]
- [[Pittsburg and Midway Coal Mining Company]] preparing deed for ~70-acre tract

**Experimental Work**:
- Fertilization experiment conducted in three [[Linn County]] pits ([[Ideal Pit]], [[Fertilized Pit]], [[Pumphouse Pit]] as control) by Hume-Sinclair Coal Company with department supervision
- Regular limnological tests documented fertilization effects on food organism abundance
- Definitive conclusions compromised by unauthorized fish transfers from drying ponds by "well-meaning" fishermen
- Age analysis confirmed some bass were one year older than stocked cohorts, proving external introductions

**Walleye Stocking**:
- 3,000 walleye fry stocked experimentally in [[Transformer Pit]] (1953) - no apparent survival
- 6 walleyes (6-8 inches) stocked in [[West 4th Street Pit]] (November 1953); 3-pound walleye caught March 1956

**Public Relations and Extension**:
- Biologist spent >50% time on public relations: speaking engagements (Chambers of Commerce, Kiwanis, Rotary, Lions Clubs, Boy Scouts, sportsmen's clubs)
- Technical assistance provided statewide: farm pond rotenone treatments, turbidity control, fish rescues, acid water liming, aquatic weed control, fish disease/die-off investigations
- Routine management on state lakes in eastern Kansas
- Activities credited with greatly improved public attitudes and cooperation

**Problem Identification**:
- Poor fishing in new pits historically due to improper stocking methods (now correctable)
- Poor fishing in old pits due to unbalanced, stunted populations (correctable via population removal/reduction and restocking)
- Unauthorized fish transfers by public compromised experimental work and potentially disrupted balanced populations
- Excessive aquatic vegetation (skunk moss) in some pits reduced fish growth and fishing success

**Notable Individual Catches** (newspaper-documented):
- Channel catfish: 14 pounds ([[Fireplace Pit]] complex, April 1956), 8.5 pounds ([[Maupin's Whitmer Pit]], August 1953), 6 pounds ([[Partial Poison Pit]])
- Bass: 7 pounds 8 ounces (State Park, June 1954), 7 pounds ([[Eagle-Cherokee Pit]], March 1954), 6 pounds 8 ounces ([[West 4th Street Pit]], April 1956), 6 pounds 2 ounces ([[32 Pit]], February 1956), multiple 5-6 pound fish
- Crappie: 4.5 pounds/19 inches ([[Box Pit]], May 1954), 3 pounds ([[Eagle-Cherokee Pit]], May 1953), 2 pounds 14 ounces ([[32 Pit]], February 1956), 2 pounds ([[College Farm Pit]], May 1954)

**Geographic Coverage**:
State-managed strip-pits represented less than one-fourth of fishable strip-pits in the area but provided a large majority of documented quality fishing catches, demonstrating management effectiveness.
