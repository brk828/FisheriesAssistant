# First North American Record of Cichlasoma Managuense (Pisces: Cichlidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

An established population of the neotropical cichlid [[Cichlasoma Managuense]] (jaguar guapote) was found in Boiler Spring, a thermal spring pool in the [[Virgin River]] basin of southwestern Utah, on 13 September 1988. The record is the first documented feral occurrence of this species in natural waters of North America. The authors argue that this large predator adds to the threats facing an already stressed native fish fauna, which includes the federally endangered [[Woundfin]]. A removal attempt by the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife]] killed several hundred fish but did not eradicate the population, and the authors recommend complete elimination of the species from the system.

## Background & Description

The study was carried out by Marsh, Burke, DeMarais and Douglas, affiliated with Arizona State University and the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation. The authors frame the find within a long history of [[Nonnative Fish Introductions]] in [[Southwestern Springs]], where introduced fishes have often led to the local extirpation of native and endemic populations, mainly through predation.

**Species profile**

- *Cichlasoma managuense* (Günther) belongs to the "true guapotes," large predatory cichlids of Central America and the Caribbean. Ecologically, it is comparable to North American black basses of the genus [[Micropterus]].
- Adults reach about 40 cm standard length (SL) in females and 50 cm in males.
- It has large, oblique, strongly protractile jaws with enlarged pseudocanine teeth.
- Its natural habitat is turbid, slow-moving water over soft bottoms, though it is also associated with rocky substrates.
- Its native range covers Atlantic drainages of Central America, from southern Costa Rica north to Río Patuca, Honduras.
- It has been introduced to [[Lake Amatitlan]] (Guatemala), the Río Chamelecón basin (Honduras), and lakes Ilopango and Coatepeque (El Salvador).
- The species is popular with aquarists and was first imported into the United States in 1964.
- Breeding occurs in varied settings, from small tributaries of large rivers to flooded marshes. Fecundity is reported as 50,000–60,000 ova per kg body weight (Villa 1982).

**Specimens examined**

Thirty-three *C. managuense* were seined from the spring pool. They measured 5.0–22.0 cm SL and weighed 4–278 g. Specimens were deposited at Arizona State University. The site is adjacent to [[Interstate 10]] in the community of Washington, Washington County, Utah.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Boiler Spring habitat**

- The spring is a natural limnocrene that discharges about 0.015 m³/s through a modified outlet channel.
- Mid-pool water temperature was 20 °C in January.
- The pool is nearly circular, about 10 m in diameter, with steep sides sloping to a maximum depth of 1.5 m.
- The bottom is sand in the center and organic material at the margins.
- Nonnative salt cedar (*Tamarix* sp.) and palms (*Washingtonia filifera*) shade the pool.
- The only other fish known from the pool is the introduced [[Poecilia Reticulata]] (guppy), which was not collected.
- The main channel of the Virgin River lies 2.75 km downstream. Whether the two waters are connected was unknown.

**Native fish fauna of the Virgin River basin**

Six native fishes occupy the basin:

1. [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
2. [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), federally listed as endangered
3. [[Virgin River Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda* sp.)
4. [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), a candidate for federal listing
5. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
6. [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarkii*)

The dace and two suckers occur as distinct, not yet formally described "forms" in main-channel versus spring and spring-fed tributary habitats. Several other nonnative fishes are also recorded from the system.

**Threats**

- Recent invasion of the Virgin River by the [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) has produced sharp declines in the distribution and abundance of woundfin.
- Water development has added to the pressures on the native fauna.

**Thermal limits and establishment risk**

- Cichlids generally do not survive temperatures below about 10 °C for more than a few days (Chervinski and Dor 1982).
- *C. managuense* has probably been in Boiler Spring for several years. Warm pool temperatures allowed it to overwinter.
- If it reached the Virgin River, it could spread during summer and survive winter in warm-water inflows along the channel.
- Thermal tolerance data for the species are lacking. Its persistence in the relatively cool, high-elevation Lake Amatitlan suggests a lower lethal limit than other cichlids, which may aid its spread.

## Key Findings

1. **First North American record.** The established population in Boiler Spring is the first documented feral occurrence of *C. managuense* in natural North American waters.

2. **Eradication attempt failed.** On 14 September 1988, the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife]] attempted removal. Several hundred fish were killed, but treatment with both explosives and [[Rotenone]] did not eradicate the population (T. Burke, unpublished data).

3. **Wider establishment.** Local testimony indicated the species was present in other habitats. At least one breeding population was established in a private pond nearby.

4. **Potential impacts.** The authors note that impacts have not been demonstrated. However, the species' predatory habit and high reproductive potential, linked to advanced [[Parental Care]], pose a risk of predation and other negative interactions with native fauna.

5. **Management recommendation.** The authors recommend locating and completely eliminating *C. managuense* from the system. They stress that the Virgin River fauna is already imperiled and that predatory guapotes pose an added threat.

6. **Historical context.** Introduced fishes have repeatedly caused declines or extirpations of native and endemic fishes across the Southwest, most often through direct predation (Meffe 1985).
