# Investigation of the Lakes of Anderson Mesa and Related Wildlife Surveys (1952–1953)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Commission Wildlife Restoration Division report, Project FW-1-R, compiles four completion reports by Project Leader [[Richard A. Wagner]] covering the high-elevation lakes of [[Anderson Mesa]] (Work Plans 1 and 2), a herbicide and buffering trial at [[Ashurst Lake]] (Work Plan 3), and wildlife surveys of the [[Colorado River]] (Work Plan 4). Data were collected mainly in 1952. The fisheries work aimed to identify fish species and abundance, food supplies, and the most suitable fishery for each lake. The waterfowl work documented migrant use of the lakes in the Pacific Flyway.

The central conclusion is that water temperature, water-level instability and aquatic plant overgrowth limit fishery potential. Most lakes are best suited to cold-water trout, a few to panfish, and several to waterfowl. Recommendations focus on increasing water supply, controlling vegetation, and managing stocking and harvest.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The report notes a shortage of permanent, high-quality fishing water in Arizona and rising summer recreation pressure around Anderson Mesa. Limnological and ecological data for these lakes did not exist, so the project set out to provide a basis for restoration and management.

**Study area.** Anderson Mesa lies in southeastern Coconino County at roughly 7,000–7,250 ft, less than ten air miles from the Sonoran life zone. Its malpai and clay soils shed most rainfall, so the lakes depend heavily on snowmelt. Spring terrain is boggy and difficult to travel.

**Lakes surveyed.** Seven main lakes were studied: [[Upper Lake Mary]], [[Lower Lake Mary]], [[Ashurst Lake]], [[Kinnikinick Lake]], [[Long Lake]], [[Soldier Annex Lake]] and [[Stoneman Lake]]. Secondary lakes, which appear only in wet years, were [[Soldier Lake]], [[Mormon Lake]], [[Marshall Lake]], [[Vail Lake]] and [[Rogers Lake]].

**Fish community.** Surveys recorded 13 fish taxa across the lakes, including trout, black bass, crappie, sunfish, catfish, bullhead, carp and golden shiner. The summary counts six game fish and seven panfish species. Key species are [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Yellow Perch]].

**Methods.** Fish were sampled with experimental gill nets (one- to four-inch mesh), seines, rotenone (spot poisoning) and cube-root extract. Food was assessed with plankton nets, square-foot counters and dip nets. Limnological variables were recorded monthly, including temperature, Secchi turbidity, dissolved oxygen, pH and total soluble salts.

**Aquatic biota.** Dominant plants included [[Parrot Feather]], pondweed (*Potamogeton*), waterweed (*Elodea*), muskgrass (*Chara*), spike rush and smartweed. Common invertebrates included Physa snails, midges, damselfly and dragonfly naiads, backswimmers, water boatmen and caddisflies. Zooplankton was mostly cladocerans and copepods, and phytoplankton was mainly blue-green algae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and climate.** Air temperature drives water temperature, which controls food production and fish feeding and growth. Cloudiness and humidity affect light availability, photosynthesis, evaporation and, in turn, pH and dissolved salts. Several lakes stratify thermally, and wind strength strongly controls mixing. Upper Lake Mary's August surface temperature was about 75°F, but its bottom water stayed below 56°F. At Long Lake the pool bottom was below 65°F even though surface temperatures were higher.

**Water chemistry.** Upper and Lower Lake Mary and Kinnikinick are described as soft water. Stoneman is hard because evaporation has concentrated its salts. In Ashurst Lake, plant metabolism raised total soluble salts from below 40 ppm in spring to over 130 ppm in August. Surface pH in Ashurst fluctuated from 8.75 to 11.15 within a single 12-hour period.

**Algal blooms.** "Green-paint" algae blooms in Lower Lake Mary and Upper Lake Mary are dense in summer. Wind windrows the algae toward the shore, and the bloom's metabolic products shift surface and bottom pH. This mortality affected trout in Lower Lake Mary.

**Water-level instability.** Evaporation, municipal pumping and variable inflow cause large fluctuations. Upper Lake Mary drops six to eight inches a week, stranding fish on shore, and over 10,000 fish may die per event. Ashurst Lake fluctuates about five feet in some years. Stoneman's watershed is so small that the lake cannot hold water reliably.

**Waterfowl use.** Anderson Mesa lakes serve as feeding and resting areas for waterfowl of the eastern Pacific Flyway during migration. Pintails are the most abundant duck (over 65% of ducks). Few geese use the area, and none were seen at several lakes. Waterfowl feed on plants, snails, insects and probably fish fry.

## Key Findings

### Fisheries Findings

- **Upper Lake Mary** (1,058 surface acres, maximum depth about 40 ft, elevation about 6,850 ft) is most suited to trout. Its warm-water fish are stunted and forage fish are abundant. Bass fry were not captured, though spawning was not ruled out. Stomach analysis showed brown trout and bass fed on golden shiners, damselfly naiads and small fish.
- **Lower Lake Mary** (826 surface acres, maximum depth 20.9 ft, elevation about 6,800 ft) supports a crappie-dominated fishery. Black crappie made up over 65% of the catch, with a split of about 80% black to 20% white. Anglers took over 110 crappie per hour at times. Fishing pressure would need to rise at least 300% to harvest the surplus, or stunting is likely. Juvenile bass, crappie and bluegill indicated successful 1952 spawning.
- **Ashurst Lake** (165 acres, about 17 ft, elevation about 7,150 ft) yielded no trout, although it had supported trout earlier. Warm-water fish were in excellent condition but grew very slowly, which was attributed to a short feeding season. Smallmouth bass were four to five years old at 16–18 inches. The fish probably came from Lower Lake Mary via sportsmen's transplants in 1951.
- **Kinnikinick Lake** (110 acres, about 16.5 ft, elevation about 7,050 ft) holds rainbow and cutthroat trout, yellow perch, bluegill, carp and bullheads. Wind-driven turbidity from volcanic silt is a key problem. A 1952 chemical control program removed yellow perch, at densities from one to over 60 per linear foot of shoreline.
- **Long Lake** (about 200–268 acres, depth 18–25 ft, elevation about 6,800 ft) is a mixed warm-water community with bass, bluegill, crappie, bullhead, yellow perch, catfish and carp. Its lack of suitable forage limits its fishery.
- **Stoneman Lake** (160 acres, about 12 ft, elevation about 6,800 ft) is dominated by bullheads. Its watershed is too small to sustain a long-lived fishery.
- **Soldier and Soldier Annex lakes** have no fish. Water is very turbid, blue-green algae is common and water levels fluctuate too much for a fishery.

### Vegetation and Herbicide Findings

- At Ashurst Lake, over 90% of the permanent bottom supports plants. Parrot feather is about 70% of the plant population and waterweed about 25%.
- Herbicide trials tested sodium arsenite, chlorinated benzenes and copper sulfate. [[Trichlorobenzene]] applied with a centrifugal pump gave the fastest and most lasting results, and no regrowth was seen in treated areas.
- Alum lowered pH by about 2.1 units in an afternoon trial and 1.5 units in a morning trial, but wind restored original pH within about an hour. Alum gave the most satisfactory buffering result, but the root problem was the plant-driven pH source.
- At Kinnikinick, gypsum (10 lb per 50 gallons) formed effective floc that settled turbidity. The report raised the question of whether this would increase weed problems.

### Waterfowl Findings

- The Anderson Mesa lakes provide feeding and resting habitat for migrating ducks and geese during the Pacific Flyway migration.
- On the Colorado River, the peak goose count was 5,850 birds in 1952 (about 42% [[Snow Goose]], 56% Canadian honker and 2% speckled goose). The peak duck count was 101,510, down from about 125,000 in 1951.

### Recommendations

- **Upper Lake Mary:** Continue unfed rainbow fry stocking, and stop warm-water plantings. Investigate a fish screen on Newman Canyon.
- **Lower Lake Mary:** Manage for crappie and panfish with no closed season, bag or possession limit, and promote the fishery to anglers.
- **Ashurst Lake:** Control parrot feather chemically or biologically. Rehabilitate the lake for trout by renovating the canal and increasing depth by about ten feet. Review limnological and herbicide results.
- **Kinnikinick Lake:** Build a dam to increase capacity, modify the canal to reduce turbidity, and maintain trout fry stocking.
- **Long Lake:** Control the population, then stock crappie and largemouth bass and introduce golden shiners as forage.
- **Stoneman Lake:** Establish no game fishery until depth reaches 20 ft, and remove non-game fish first.
- **Soldier and Soldier Annex:** Manage for waterfowl by planting sedge, plantain and smartweed.
- **Marshall, Vail and Rogers:** Manage for waterfowl, since they have no fishery value.
- **Mormon Lake:** Do not establish a warm-water panfish fishery until depth reaches at least 10 ft.
- **Waterfowl policy:** Set hunting seasons concurrently with Utah, because ecological zones shape migration more than geography. Establishing sanctuaries on Anderson Mesa is not recommended. Study nesting requirements.
- **Colorado River:** Establish wildlife reservations at Topock Swamp, lower Bill Williams River and Cibola Valley. Continue unfed trout fry stocking below Davis Dam and in Black Canyon, and manage water control for aquatic life. Add a forage fish to the warm-water fishery.
