# Foods of Buffalofishes, Genus Ictiobus, in Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study analyzed the intestinal contents of 541 buffalofishes of the genus [[Ictiobus]] (428 [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], 57 [[Black Buffalo]], and 56 [[Smallmouth Buffalo]]) collected in 1966–69 from [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]] in central Arizona. Bigmouth buffalo fed mostly on planktonic algae and crustaceans, with evidence of a near-bottom feeding habit. Black and smallmouth buffalo were benthic feeders, with molluscs (the introduced Asiatic clam [[Corbicula]]) being the most important group utilized by both species. The authors interpret the dietary differences as reflecting feeding position and habitat use within the reservoirs.

## Background & Description

The buffalofishes were introduced to the Salt River reservoirs as "eyed eggs" by the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries in 1918, most likely sourced from the Mississippi River near Fairport, Iowa. The four Salt River reservoirs were built between 1905 and 1930 and stocked with a variety of mostly eastern U.S. species because few native fishes were suitable for a desired fishery. The work was funded in part by a grant-in-aid to the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] under P.L. 88-309 and is part of a 1966–69 series of studies on the commercial fishes of these lakes.

Three buffalofish species are present in Apache Lake, but only the bigmouth buffalo is known from Roosevelt Lake, where its population is described as aberrant. The absence of black and smallmouth buffalo from Roosevelt Lake is attributed to past drought and desiccation of the reservoir.

Specimens were collected by gill nets, trammel nets, and 220-volt AC electrofishing. Guts were preserved in 10% formalin and later stored in isopropanol. Because buffalofishes lack a well-defined stomach, food samples were taken from the anterior gut or from the entire gut when nearly empty. Two slides were prepared per fish and read independently by different observers. Relative volumes were estimated using an ocular grid method, and detritus and inorganic material were visually estimated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir characteristics (Table 1):**

| Feature | Roosevelt Lake | Apache Lake |
|---|---|---|
| Dam construction | 1905–1911 | 1924–1927 |
| Maximum depth | 63.7 m | 77.7 m |
| Mean depth | 24.8 m | 28.7 m |
| Surface area | 72.2 km² | 10.5 km² |
| Shoreline length | 142.2 km | 66.8 km |
| Slope of basin | 5.2% | 25.4% |

Roosevelt Lake is the most upstream impoundment in the Salt River system. It trapped organic and inorganic debris and has fluctuating water levels and turbidity. Apache Lake lies immediately downstream in a narrow, fjord-like canyon, is far more stable, and is maintained by inflow from Roosevelt.

The lakes are generally clear, warm, and seasonally stratified, with surface summer temperatures above 30 °C and rarely any freezing. They are somewhat saline (specific conductance > 500 µmho/cm at 25 °C), with ion dominance of Na⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ > HCO₃⁻ > SO₄²⁻.

Native biota is sparse. The bottom fauna consists of presumably native chironomids, the introduced Asiatic clam [[Corbicula Manilensis]], and an unidentified introduced crayfish (*Orconectes* sp.). Of 24 commonly found fish species, all but three are introduced. Plankton has not been thoroughly studied but is presumed indigenous.

## Key Findings

**Bigmouth Buffalo (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*)**
- Morphology (enlarged head and mouth, fine and numerous gill rakers) indicates plankton feeding, but multiple lines of evidence point to near-bottom feeding.
- Detritus and inorganic sand were present in nearly all fish, with detritus averaging 24.4% relative volume in Apache Lake and 25.7% in Roosevelt Lake. Frequency of occurrence of these components was essentially 100% in both lakes.
- Algae were more important in Apache Lake. [[Diatoms]] were dominant, mainly planktonic genera (*Melosira*, *Asterionella*), with some aufwuchs forms (*Tabellaria*, *Synedra*). Chlorophytes were common in autumn.
- Crustaceans, mainly [[Cladocera]] (*Bosmina* dominant, followed by *Daphnia*) and [[Copepoda]] (mostly calanoids), dominated animal foods year-round. Chironomid larvae and juvenile *Corbicula* suggest some bottom feeding.
- Gut contents varied strongly between fish caught in the same net on the same date.
- A sponge later discovered in Canyon Lake may have been confused with diatoms in the analysis, though the authors consider the resulting error minimal.

**Black Buffalo (*Ictiobus niger*) and Smallmouth Buffalo (*Ictiobus bubalus*)**
- Both are benthic feeders that search the bottom, stirring sediments and probing under debris.
- Clams were the major volumetric component of black buffalo diets. Detritus averaged 30% and sand 10.3% by volume.
- Smallmouth buffalo differed by eating more diatoms and dipteran larvae, and ostracods were a numerically important component. Sand comprised 20.6% of gut contents, suggesting shallow-water or shoreline feeding.
- Smallmouth buffalo gut contents showed greater diversity than those of black or bigmouth buffalo.

**Comparison with Other Studies**
- Studies in Saskatchewan (Johnson, 1963), Iowa (Moen, 1954), Minnesota (Scidmore and Woods, 1960), and the Missouri River (Walburg and Nelson, 1966; McComish, 1967) broadly agree that bigmouth buffalo feed on crustaceans and near-bottom organisms.
- The authors conclude that bigmouth buffalo move in a "bouncing" motion near the bottom, and that their feeding stirs sediments, making small benthic organisms available. The feeding is likely more efficient when schools of fish feed together.
- Smallmouth buffalo in other studies were considered opportunistic and shoreline-feeding.
