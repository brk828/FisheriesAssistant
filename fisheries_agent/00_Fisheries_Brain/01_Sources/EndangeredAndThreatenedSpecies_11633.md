# Endangered and Threatened Species of Arizona (Summer 1991 Federal Listing Summary)

#ichthyology #conservation #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) summary, compiled by the Phoenix Ecological Services Field Office in Summer 1991, catalogs the plants and animals of Arizona that are federally listed or proposed as threatened or endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). It covers roughly 49 taxa: 15 plants, 18 fishes, 2 reptiles, 8 birds and 6 mammals. Each species account gives federal status, description, habitat, historic and current range, threats, land ownership and recovery notes. The document is a general-knowledge reference, and it notes that State-listed species are covered separately by the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Arizona Department of Agriculture.

The fish accounts dominate the aquatic content. Most aquatic taxa are declining because of dams and impoundments, water diversion and groundwater pumping, channelization, and introduced predatory or competitive non-native fishes. Several are now confined to a few refugia, hatcheries or reintroduced populations.

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory framework:** Federal agencies must ensure that actions they authorize, fund or carry out do not jeopardize listed species or adversely modify their critical habitat. The summary includes only species listed or proposed in the Federal Register.
- **Sources and contacts:** The USFWS Phoenix office handles federal listings. The Arizona Game and Fish Department handles State-listed animals and the Arizona Department of Agriculture handles State-listed plants.
- **Document structure:** Each species has a status line (with Federal Register citation), description, habitat, range (historic, current, potential), reasons for decline, land management and notes. Species are indexed by common and scientific names.
- **Cover and illustrations:** Drawings of *Asclepias welshii* and *Cycladenia humilis* var. *jonesii* are reprinted from *Intermountain Flora* (1984). Range maps are included for each species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic systems.** Many fishes are tied to the [[Colorado River Basin]], the [[Gila River]] system and the [[Virgin River]]. Key threats include:
- Impoundment and dam construction, which fragment habitat and block recruitment (e.g., [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]]).
- Flow depletion, dewatering and groundwater pumping (e.g., [[Spikedace]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]).
- Introduced non-native fishes that prey on or compete with natives, and hybridization with introduced trouts or catfish (e.g., [[Apache Trout]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Yaqui Catfish]]).
- Flood events, which can both sustain habitat (e.g., [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]]) and destroy small populations.

**Desert and riparian systems.** Plants and reptiles are limited by specialized substrates (Tertiary limestone lakebeds, gypsiferous soils, Navajo sandstone seeps), slow recovery from surface disturbance in arid climates, and threats from off-road vehicles, mining, pesticides, grazing and illegal collection. Several cacti are withheld from precise location data to deter collectors.

**Terrestrial and montane systems.** Birds and mammals are affected by pesticide-related reproductive failure (e.g., raptors and pelicans), loss of old-growth or riparian habitat, and logging, grazing and human disturbance.

**Protective measures.** Several taxa are covered by [[Arizona Native Plant Law]] and by CITES. Captive breeding and hatchery programs, including [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and the Arizona Game and Fish Page Springs Hatchery, support reintroductions.

## Key Findings

**Fishes (18 taxa, most listed as endangered or threatened)**

- **[[Apache Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus apache*): Threatened (originally endangered in 1967). About 30 sites support native or reintroduced populations. Hybridization with introduced trouts is a threat. Over 250,000 are produced annually for reintroduction.
- **[[Beautiful Shiner]]** (*Cyprinella formosa*): Threatened, with critical habitat at San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge. Extirpated from the US in 1968. Reintroduced in 1989 from Mexican stock.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): Endangered and very close to extinction. Only a few very old wild fish are known, such as those from [[Lake Mohave]]. Minimal wild reproduction. Fingerlings from Dexter National Fish Hatchery were stocked in Lake Mohave in 1981–1990.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Endangered. Largest American minnow (up to 6 ft, 80 lb). Extirpated from the lower basin, with the last naturally occurring Arizona specimen collected in 1969. Experimental nonessential populations exist in the Verde and Salt basins.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*): Endangered, with critical habitat. No naturally occurring wild populations remain in Arizona. The Quitobaquito pupfish is at one spring in Organ Pipe National Monument.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): Endangered. Natural populations persist in several Santa Cruz, Graham and Pima County waters.
- **[[Gila Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus gilae*): Endangered. Loss of habitat, hybridization with rainbow trout and predation by brown trout.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): Endangered. Populations occur in the Little Colorado and Colorado Rivers (Grand and Marble Canyons) and in Utah and Colorado reaches.
- **[[Little Colorado Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda vittata*): Threatened, with critical habitat of 18 miles of East Clear Creek, 8 miles of Chevelon Creek and 5 miles of Nutrioso Creek.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Threatened. Once common in the upper Gila system; current populations include Aravaipa Creek and the Blue River. A recovery plan was approved in September 1991.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Endangered (listed October 1991). Lake Mohave holds about 60,000 fish, the largest Basin population, but it is non-recruiting. Old adults spawn but young do not survive to adulthood.
- **[[Sonora Chub]]** (*Gila ditaenia*): Threatened, with critical habitat in Sycamore Canyon, Santa Cruz County.
- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*): Threatened. Current range includes Aravaipa Creek, the upper Gila and the upper Verde. Recurrent flooding is important to its life history.
- **[[Virgin River Chub]]** (*Gila robusta seminuda*): Endangered. Restricted to about 50 miles of the Virgin River of 134 historic miles.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): Endangered. Now found only in the Virgin River, from Utah through Mohave County to Nevada. Introductions into experimental habitat were unsuccessful.
- **[[Yaqui Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus pricei*): Threatened. Extirpated from the US; the sole US population is held at Dexter National Fish Hatchery.
- **[[Yaqui Chub]]** (*Gila purpurea*): Endangered, with critical habitat at [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]. Also introduced to Leslie Creek and West Turkey Creek.
- **[[Yaqui Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): Endangered. Competition with introduced mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) is a major threat.

**Plants (15 taxa)**

- Notable examples include Arizona agave (*Agave arizonica*), Arizona cliffrose (*Purshia subintegra*), Jones cycladenia (*Cycladenia humilis* var. *jonesii*), Kearney's blue-star (*Amsonia kearneyana*), Navajo sedge (*Carex specuicola*), Sentry milk-vetch (*Astragalus cremnophylax* var. *cremnophylax*), San Francisco Peaks groundsel (*Senecio franciscanus*) and Welsh's milkweed (*Asclepias welshii*).
- Kearney's blue-star: 181 individuals were transplanted in 1988–1989 to create a reserve. A 1990 flood removed many, and only 33 of the original 181 survived by mid-1990.
- Sentry milk-vetch: The two extant populations are on the South Rim of the Grand Canyon. The largest has fewer than 500 individuals in under one acre. A fence built in spring 1990 has largely stopped trampling.
- San Francisco Peaks groundsel: Restricted to alpine tundra above 10,900 ft; threats include hiker trampling and ski developments.

**Reptiles (2 taxa)**

- **[[Desert Tortoise]]** (*Gopherus agassizii*, Mohave population): Threatened. Threats include habitat loss, grazing, off-road vehicles, disease (Upper Respiratory Tract Disease), and predation of juveniles by common ravens. The Sonoran population is a Category 2 candidate.
- **New Mexican ridge-nosed rattlesnake** (*Crotalus willardi obscurus*): Threatened. Only one recent Arizona record, from the Peloncillo Mountains.

**Birds (8 taxa)**

- **[[Mexican Spotted Owl]]** (*Strix occidentalis lucida*): Proposed threatened. Estimated ownership of owl habitat: National Forests 90%, Indian reservations 5%, National Parks 4%, BLM 1%.
- **[[Yuma Clapper Rail]]** (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*): Endangered. Threatened by stream channelization and marsh drying and flooding.
- **American peregrine falcon, bald eagle, California brown pelican, masked bobwhite, northern aplomado falcon and whooping crane** are also listed. Pesticide-related reproductive failure is a recurring cause of decline.

**Mammals (6 taxa)**

- **[[Mexican Gray Wolf]]** (*Canis lupus baileyi*): Endangered. Eliminated from Arizona, Texas and New Mexico by predator control programs by the 1920s. A captive breeding program is underway.
- Other listed mammals include black-footed ferret, Hualapai Mexican vole, Mount Graham red squirrel, Sanborn's long-nosed bat and Sonoran pronghorn.

**Management and planning**

- Recovery plans have been completed or revised for most aquatic taxa. Several (e.g., Rio Yaqui fishes, Virgin River fishes, Gila topminnow revision) are still in preparation.
- The Yaqui fishes are the focus of a combined recovery effort at San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge.
- The Sonoran pronghorn working group sought a US–Mexico census and an International Recovery Plan.
