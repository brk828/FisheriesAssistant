# Transition Monitoring for Riparian Vegetation in the Colorado River Corridor of Grand Canyon National Park

#riparian-ecology #vegetation-monitoring #dam-impacts #wetland-ecology #flow-regime

## Executive Summary

This proposal by [[Michael J.C. Kearsley]] and [[Tina J. Ayers]] from [[Northern Arizona University]] requests funding to continue monitoring riparian and wetland plant assemblages in the [[Colorado River]] corridor downstream of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The 12-month study (starting October 1, 1995) addresses vegetation changes under the Bureau of Reclamation's Interim Flows regime. Previous monitoring revealed that channel margin marshes increased in number but decreased in total area, with riverward displacement of wet marsh species ([[Typha]], [[Juncus]], [[Phragmites]]) and water stress in [[Salix exigua]] (coyote willow) inversely related to discharge volumes. The proposal employs aerial photography mapping and ground-truthing methods developed during prior Interim Flows monitoring to track assemblage development, focusing on fluvial marshes and the "New Dry Zone" (between 20,000 and 28,000 cfs stage elevations). Budget: $150,970 over 12 months.

## Background & Description

Riparian vegetation in the [[Colorado River]] corridor has been profoundly affected by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations since 1963. Water releases determine timing, magnitude, and duration of flows, which in turn control substrate deposition, scouring, and groundwater-mediated influences on plant assemblage identity, diversity, and productivity. Historical studies documented rapid increases in riparian vegetation cover in the new high water zone (NHWZ) between 1965 and 1982, followed by significant losses during 1983-1984 spillover flooding. Post-dam substrate changes in soil texture, water availability, and nutrient status drove successional development of phreatophyte communities.

Phase II Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) by [[Larry Stevens]] and colleagues (1989-1993) established that reduced flooding frequency after dam completion allowed fluvial marshes to form in low-velocity channel margin environments. By 1991, approximately 253 wet marshes and 850 dry marshes existed in the park corridor, totaling ~25.0 ha (30% wet marsh dominated by [[Typha domingensis]], [[Juncus]] spp., [[Scirpus]] spp.; 70% dry marsh with [[Phragmites australis]] and [[Equisetum X ferrissii]]). More than 95% of wet marsh vegetation occurred between 10,000 and 20,000 cfs stages. Marsh assemblage composition and succession related to sediment distribution and daily inundation frequency (0.4 to 0.9 for wet marshes).

Interim Flows monitoring (1992-1995) revealed continuing rapid changes: marshes became drier (measured by ordination and basal area of obligate wetland species), the "New Dry Zone" (between 20,000-30,000 cfs elevations) was rapidly colonized by diverse species, and invasive exotics including [[Erianthus ravennae]] (Eurasian bunchgrass), [[Lepidium latifolium]], [[Eragrostis curvula]], and annual bromes expanded. By 1993, marsh patch density increased 3.5-fold to 895 patches, but total marsh area decreased 22% to 7.03 ha. Reaches downstream from the [[Little Colorado River]] that experienced January-February 1993 floods showed marsh area losses up to 90%.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study focuses on riparian and wetland plant assemblages in nine established monitoring sites between [[Lees Ferry]] and [[Diamond Creek]]. These assemblages occupy distinct geomorphic zones: fluvial marshes in return-current channels, channel margins, back beaches, and the New Dry Zone. Vegetation composition reflects complex interactions among flow regime, substrate characteristics, groundwater availability, and disturbance history.

Fluvial marshes represent critical wetland habitat in an otherwise arid canyon environment. Obligate wetland species (sensu Reed 1988) including cattails, rushes, and reeds provide habitat structure for wildlife. The New Dry Zone—newly exposed between the 20,000 cfs Interim Flows maximum and the 28,000 cfs pre-Interim Flows normal maximum—represents a novel habitat undergoing rapid colonization.

Exotic species pose management concerns. [[Tamarix ramosissima]] (tamarisk) continues low-level establishment under fluctuating flows. [[Erianthus ravennae]] and [[Lepidium latifolium]] are rapidly colonizing riparian habitats. These invasions have implications for native plant diversity and wildlife habitat quality.

The study employs detrended correspondence analysis (DCA) to quantify successional trajectories of marsh plots through time, with plots scored on moisture availability gradients. Two-way indicator species analysis (TWINSPAN) classifies broad-scale vegetation patterns into 26 major vegetation types (sensu Brown et al. 1979). Integration with the GCES Geographic Information System (GIS) enables spatial analysis of vegetation change patterns.

## Key Findings

**Research Questions and Hypotheses:**

1. **Assemblage Change Under Extended Interim Flows:** Testing whether riparian plant assemblages below the old high water zone continue changing, specifically:
   - a) Testing null hypothesis of no change in species composition or abundance of important wetland/riparian species
   - b) Testing null hypothesis that areal extent and location of obligate wetland species remain unchanged
   - c) Testing null hypothesis of no change in foliar cover or spatial distribution of exotic species

2. **Return-Current Channel Morphology:** Testing hypothesis that return-current channel morphologies were unaffected by water year 1995 flows (coordinated with NAU sandbar studies)

**Monitoring Approach:**

- **Mapping:** Vegetation polygons mapped from 400% enlargements of Memorial Day constant flow aerial photographs at nine study sites
- **Ground-Truthing:** Complete species lists for each polygon; up to 5 circular plots (≤6 m diameter) per polygon for percent foliar cover census; vertical structure measured via point-contact methods
- **Analytical Methods:** 
  - Species turnover analysis (presence/absence)
  - Paired t-tests comparing total foliar area (polygon size × percent cover) between 1995 and 1996 for obligate wetland species and important riparian species ([[Tamarix]], [[Salix]], [[Populus]], [[Baccharis]])
  - Similar analysis for invasive exotics
  - TWINSPAN-derived vegetation type area comparisons
  - Cross-sectional area comparisons of marsh transects (1995 vs. 1996)

**Previous Findings Informing This Study:**

- Between 1991 and 1993: 3.5-fold increase in marsh patch density (253 to 895 patches) but 22% decrease in total marsh area (9.0 to 7.03 ha)
- Reaches downstream from Little Colorado River flooded in January-February 1993 showed up to 90% marsh area losses
- Unflooded upstream reaches showed slight marsh area gains
- New Dry Zone quadrats, devoid of vegetation at Interim Flows inception (1991), became nearly as densely colonized as bar platform zones by 1992 (no significant difference in basal area: F₁,₂ = 2.979, p = 0.099)
- Repeated measures ANOVA on DCA scores showed both spring and fall censuses had more dry-site species, fewer wet-site species, and more dry-site individuals in second year of censusing
- Higher elevation marsh areas lost significant numbers of obligate wetland species during first three years of Interim Flows; these same species colonized New Dry Zone during that period

**Deliverables:** Quarterly reports, final technical and administrative report (October 1, 1996) including attributed vegetation maps with multi-year comparisons and integrated data from fiscal years 1993-1995 for GCES-GIS.
