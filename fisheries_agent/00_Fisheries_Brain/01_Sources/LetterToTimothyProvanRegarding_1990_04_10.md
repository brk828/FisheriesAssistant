## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment letter from the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), signed by Director Duane L. Shroufe, responding to a proposal from the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources to introduce a forage fish species (referred to as "smelt," likely [[Rainbow Smelt]]) into [[Lake Powell]] as a management tool to address an overpopulated [[Striped Bass]] population. AGFD raises significant scientific, regulatory, and ecological objections, arguing that the proposal constitutes a "major federal action" requiring compliance with the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) and Section 7 consultation under the [[Endangered Species Act]] due to potential impacts on the Lower Colorado River Basin, particularly the endangered [[Humpback Chub]]. The letter systematically critiques the technical assumptions of the proposal regarding striped bass recruitment biology, zooplankton distribution, thermocline depth, smelt habitat tolerance, and species interaction risk near the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence. AGFD ultimately declines to support the introduction, citing an unacceptable risk-benefit ratio and referencing the striped bass introduction itself as a cautionary precedent for well-intentioned but ecologically risky species introductions.

## Background & Description

The letter is management-focused rather than morphologically descriptive; direct morphological data is absent. However, it does implicate species-specific physiological/behavioral traits relevant to management:

- **[[Striped Bass]] (Morone saxatilis)**: Described as existing in a "stunted," hungry, overpopulated condition in Lake Powell. AGFD notes that improved forage availability would likely produce **larger, better-nourished adults with greater reproductive output**, not reduced recruitment as the Utah proposal assumed.
- **[[Rainbow Smelt]] (proposed forage fish, referred to generically as "smelt")**: Noted for known **thermal and turbidity tolerances** — smelt reportedly avoid "warm, silty water" typical of Colorado River tributaries in summer, but can tolerate and reproduce in clearer, cooler tailwater environments such as the San Juan River below Navajo Reservoir.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha)**: Endangered cyprinid whose habitat overlaps the Little Colorado River confluence zone, raising concern for competitive interaction with introduced smelt under altered flow regimes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary habitat under discussion**: [[Lake Powell]], a large reservoir on the [[Colorado River]] impounded by [[Glen Canyon Dam]], within the Lower Colorado River Basin.
- **Thermal stratification**: The proposal's own data cited a thermocline depth of **60–100 feet**, while **70% of zooplankton biomass was concentrated in the upper 10 m (~30 ft)** of the water column. AGFD flags this as a critical mismatch — smelt behavior patterns predicted for the reservoir would place them below the primary zooplankton food layer, risking starvation or poor body condition during summer stratification.
- **Selenium contamination**: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service studies cited as finding **unusually high selenium levels in fish tissue in Lake Powell**, proposed by AGFD as a possible confounding factor in **shad reproductive failure**, rather than assuming forage limitation alone drives striped bass problems.
- **Tributary vs. tailwater habitat distinction**: The proposal asserted smelt do not persist in "warm, silty water" characteristic of Colorado River tributaries in summer. AGFD counters that the **San Juan River tailwater below Navajo Reservoir** and the **Colorado River reach between Glen Canyon Dam and the Little Colorado River confluence** (a slow-moving, lake-like reach at ~1 mph flow) present atypical, favorable conditions (cool, clear, low-velocity) that could support a **self-sustaining, reproducing smelt population** — a scenario without precedent in the Missouri or Mississippi River systems but considered plausible here.
- **Species interaction zone**: The Little Colorado River confluence is highlighted as a zone of potential niche overlap between introduced smelt and native [[Humpback Chub]]. Under current fluctuating dam flows, competition is minimal, but AGFD warns that proposed **flow stabilization (higher constant flows)** could increase spatial and trophic overlap, elevating competition risk for an endangered species.

## Key Findings

1. **Regulatory framing**: AGFD asserts the proposed introduction, tied to federally funded fisheries management, constitutes a major federal action requiring **NEPA compliance** and **ESA Section 7 consultation**, coordinated through the USFWS Endangered Species Office (Albuquerque).
2. **Historical precedent risk**: AGFD draws a direct analogy to the original introduction of striped bass — a "new species into a new environment" once considered low-risk — as a cautionary parallel to the new smelt proposal.
3. **Flawed recruitment assumption**: The proposal's claim that "striped bass recruitment should be reduced in a system where sizeable numbers of adult stripers are present" is challenged; AGFD argues **improved forage leads to healthier, larger adults and greater reproductive output**, not natural population control.
4. **Regulatory alternative**: AGFD recommends **removing harvest limits** on striped bass and adopting a "**probability angling**" framework (managing angler expectations via average catch-success statistics) as an alternative to species introduction.
5. **Selenium as confounding variable**: High tissue selenium levels are proposed as a possible contributor to shad reproductive decline, independent of smelt introduction.
6. **Zooplankton/thermocline mismatch**: Cited data (70% zooplankton in upper 10 m; thermocline at 60–100 ft) suggests introduced smelt could face starvation risk during summer stratification, contradicting an implicit assumption of foraging success.
7. **Habitat suitability underestimated**: AGFD argues smelt could establish self-sustaining populations in atypical Colorado River Basin habitats (San Juan tailwater; Glen Canyon Dam–Little Colorado River reach), a risk the original proposal downplayed based on non-analogous precedent from other U.S. river systems.
8. **Endangered species conflict risk**: Potential future flow management changes (stabilized flows) could increase competitive overlap between smelt and the endangered **Humpback Chub** at the Little Colorado River confluence, elevating ecological risk beyond what the proposal considered.
9. **Alternative untested strategy**: AGFD suggests testing a **"stripercide" or spawning-site disruption technique** (analogous to the Lake Mead fertilization project) to directly control striped bass reproduction before pursuing a novel species introduction.
10. **Final risk determination**: AGFD formally declines to support the proposal, concluding that potential negative environmental consequences (including possible smelt establishment in the Grand Canyon reach) outweigh the uncertain benefit of resolving the striped bass overpopulation problem.
