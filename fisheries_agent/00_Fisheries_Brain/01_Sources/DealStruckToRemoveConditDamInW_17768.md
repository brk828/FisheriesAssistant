## Executive Summary
This is a news article (not a primary scientific study) reporting on a negotiated agreement between PacifiCorp, federal fishery agencies, and the [[Yakama Nation]] to demolish the [[Condit Dam]], a 125-foot-high, privately owned hydroelectric dam on the [[White Salmon River]] in Southwest Washington. The dam, built in 1913, blocks the only significant impoundment between Mount Adams and the Columbia River and has been identified as a major barrier to [[Pacific Salmon]] migration and habitat restoration. The removal, agreed upon for 2006, is framed as a lower-cost alternative ($17 million) to installing federally mandated fish ladders ($30 million), and represents a notable early precedent for dam removal as a fish conservation strategy in the Pacific Northwest, contrasted against more politically contentious proposals (e.g., Snake River dams, Elwha River dams).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat blocked**: [[Condit Dam]] is the sole human-made impoundment on the [[White Salmon River]] between Mount Adams and the Columbia River confluence, located ~3 miles upstream of the Columbia River.
- **Migratory barrier**: The dam's presence without fish passage infrastructure (fish ladders) has prevented anadromous salmonids from accessing upstream spawning and rearing habitat, a key ecological niche requirement for [[Pacific Salmon]] life-history completion.
- **Restoration significance**: Removal is intended to reopen historic salmon migration corridors between the Columbia River (mainstem migratory highway) and upper White Salmon River spawning habitat, restoring longitudinal river connectivity essential to salmonid population viability.
- **Broader watershed context**: Positioned within a larger regional pattern of Pacific Northwest hydropower infrastructure (Columbia and Snake River systems) that fragments salmon habitat, compared here to the also-discussed [[Elwha River]] dam removal efforts in Olympic National Park.

## Key Findings
- PacifiCorp agreed to remove Condit Dam by 2006 rather than retrofit it with federally required fish ladders, citing lower cost (~$17M demolition vs. ~$30M for fish passage compliance).
- The dam produces ~15 megawatts of electricity, enough to power roughly 13,000 homes — a relatively small hydropower asset weighed against fishery restoration value.
- Because Condit Dam is privately owned, its removal required no direct government funding and bypassed many of the political/bureaucratic obstacles facing larger, publicly controversial dam-removal proposals (e.g., Snake River dams opposed by Sen. Slade Gorton).
- The [[Yakama Nation]] will receive $1 million for fisheries management as part of the settlement; tribal officials (Randy Settler) described dam removal as a longstanding goal for restoring salmon runs.
- Conservation groups (American Rivers, via spokeswoman Katherine Ransel) characterized the agreement as avoiding protracted litigation and as the best outcome for the White Salmon River and its salmon populations.
- The case is presented as a comparatively low-conflict model relative to other Pacific Northwest dam-removal debates, including the costlier and more politically fraught Elwha River dam removals (~$130M estimated cost, only ~$30M appropriated by Congress at time of writing) and the highly contentious proposals to breach federal dams on the Snake and Columbia Rivers for salmon run restoration.
- Agricultural and irrigation interests are noted as consistent opponents of dam removal generally, citing threats to water availability for the state's agriculture economy, illustrating the socio-political tension between [[Fish Conservation]] efforts and water resource management.
