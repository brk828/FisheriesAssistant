# Comparative Study of the Bones Forming the Opercular Series of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1919 comparative anatomy paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], reprinted from the *Journal of Morphology* (Vol. 33, No. 1), consolidates evidence on the opercular series and branchiostegal rays across a wide range of fishes. Hubbs argues that these bones take one of two basic forms: a generalized type in the primitive malacopterygians (soft-rayed fishes) and a constant, specialized type in the higher teleosts, including the spiny-rayed Acanthopterygii. The study also supports several classification refinements, particularly those proposed by C. Tate Regan.

## Background & Description

The opercular series is a row of membrane bones that supports the outer wall of the branchial cavity and protects the gills. In the extinct ganoid family [[Palaeoniscidae]], the series consists of upper opercular bones, lower branchiostegal rays, and the anterior branchiostegals enlarged as branchigulars, which meet a median gular plate.

In [[Amia Calva]], the bones are modified in several ways:

- Each of the upper five plates overlaps the one below.
- The gular plate partly covers the anterior branchiostegals of the left side, which overlap those of the right side. This asymmetry, caused by folding of the branchial membranes, persists across the teleosts.
- The operculum has a socket that receives a peg-like condyle of the hyomandibular, allowing lateral movement.
- A **branchioperculum** is named here for a free plate lying in a conspicuous fold below the sub- and interoperculum.

Hubbs concludes that the arrangement of Amia, which lies between ganoids and teleosts, is confirmed by these bones.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats the opercular apparatus as a structural and evolutionary character, not an ecological study. Its only explicit ecological link is the observation that a low number of branchiostegals in certain malacopterygians is "usually correlated with the broad union of the branchial membranes and with a fresh-water habitat." Examples include the Cyprinidae, which have three branchiostegals, and Haplochiton, Phractolaemus, Kneria, and Cromeria, which have three. Hubbs also notes that the strong development of the [[Musculus Geniohyoideus]] modifies the hyoid arch and the attachment of branchiostegal rays.

## Key Findings

**1. Two broad structural types.**
- *Generalized type* (primitive malacopterygians, including [[Isospondyli]], [[Ostariophysi]], [[Stomiatoidea]], [[Apodes]], [[Heteromi]], [[Lyopomi]], [[Synentognathi]], [[Haplomi]], and [[Iniomi]]): variable in form and number, with wide variation in branchiostegal counts.
- *Specialized type* (higher teleosts, including [[Microcyprini]], [[Labyrinthici]], [[Hemibranchii]], [[Symbranchii]], [[Opisthomi]], [[Salmopercae]], and the [[Percoidea]] and related groups): a peculiarly constant branchiostegal arrangement.

**2. Branchiostegal counts in the generalized fishes are inconstant.**
- [[Elops]]: 24 to 36 total branchiostegals across its species.
- [[Megalops atlanticus]]: ten rays on the epihyal. [[Elops affinis]] and [[Harpodon microchir]]: nine.
- Hubbs counted rays on the epihyal in roughly 50 species, from one (*Bathylagus pacificus*) to ten, and noted that the number varies widely.
- Nematognathi: six in *Arius gagora*, seven in *Saccobranchus fossilis*, nine in *Ictalurus punctatus*, and eleven in *Macrones aor*.
- [[Synodus intermedius]] and [[Esox lucius]]: seven on the epihyal. Iniomi overall: six to twenty branchiostegals.

**3. Branchiostegal patterns in the higher teleosts.**
- The Acanthopteri are usually "four plus two" or "four plus three" (four upper saber-shaped rays on the epihyal and ceratohyal, plus two or three lower rays), rarely "four plus one" or "four plus four."
- The Labyrinthici (Ophicephalidae and Anabantidae): four plus two.
- The Poeciliidae ([[Microcyprini]]): six or fewer, with the upper four on both hyoid elements, which Hubbs links to the Acanthopteri rather than the Haplomi.
- [[Fistularia]]: four plus one. Lophobranchii: usually two, and one in *Nerophis*.
- [[Mastacembelus Pancelas]] (Opisthomi): four upper rays, with the two lowest anterior rays inserted on the inner surface of the hyoid arch.
- [[Salmopercae]] (*Percopsis omiscomaycus*, *Columbia transmontana*, *Aphredoderus sayanus*): six branchiostegals, arranged as in the Acanthopteri.

**4. Taxonomic implications.**
- The Synentognathi (Belonidae, Scombresocidae, Hemirhamphidae, Exocoetidae) resemble the Isospondyli. This supports Regan's view that their resemblance to the Percesoces is "purely fictitious," and they belong among the typical soft-rayed fishes.
- The [[Taeniosomi]] (*Regalecus*, *Stylephorus*, *Trachypterus*) have six or five slender rays, which Hubbs considers doubtfully consistent with their low placement.
- [[Ammodytoidea]] (*Ammodytes personatus*): six rays, with the upper four resembling the Acanthopteri. Hubbs treats their placement as uncertain.
- The [[Symbranchii]] (*Monopterus javanensis*, *Symbranchus marmoratus*): four rays in an upper cluster plus a lower inner pair. This supports placing them higher than the true eels.
- The [[Opisthomi]] fit the view of Boulenger and Regan that they are higher teleosts.

**5. Stable features across the acanthopterygian series.** Hubbs states that the basic branchiostegal structure "has been retained, almost without deviation" across spiny-rayed fishes, including Atherina, Toxotes, Tetraodon, Diodon, Cyclopterus, Solea, Callionymus, Echeneis, and Antennarius. The examples differ widely in body form, but their branchiostegal characters remain essentially unchanged. The paper's species list appears in an appendix of 30 families of spiny-rayed fishes and their derivatives, and it names the families examined for each taxon.

**6. Methods and limits.** The study draws on specimens and figures from earlier anatomists (including Parker, Starks, Meek, Ridewood, and Jungersen), so some counts rely on published descriptions. Hubbs notes that variation within the higher-teleost families examined was "so slight" that detailed descriptions were unnecessary.
