# Classification of Running-Water Sites in Great Britain Based on Macro-Invertebrate Communities

#macroinvertebrates #freshwater-biology #running-water-classification

## Executive Summary

This 1984 paper in [[Freshwater Biology]] presents a national classification of unpolluted running-water sites in [[Great Britain]] based on macro-invertebrate species lists. Sampling covered 268 sites on forty-one river systems plus nine additional tributaries, sampled qualitatively in spring, summer and autumn between spring 1978 and spring 1980. The classification, built with [[Detrended Correspondence Analysis]] (DCA) and [[Two-Way Indicator Species Analysis]] (TWINSPAN), produced sixteen site groups. [[Multiple Discriminant Analysis]] (MDA) using twenty-eight environmental variables correctly predicted the group of 76.1% of sites. An independent test on forty held-out sites reached a 50% success rate at the sixteen-group level. The authors conclude that the approach is a useful basis for biological classification and for predicting the expected community at a site, though it requires further testing before management use.

The paper does not discuss fish as a study group. Fish appear only in the historical review, where early zonation schemes relied on dominant fish species.

## Background & Description

**Aims.** The project had two objectives: (i) to develop a biological classification of unpolluted running-water sites based on macro-invertebrates; and (ii) to assess whether the macro-invertebrate community at a site can be predicted from its physical and chemical features. The authors argue that a predictive tool is valuable as a baseline against which to judge attempts to improve polluted rivers.

**Historical context.** Earlier zonation schemes were reviewed, including those of [[Huet Zonation]], [[Illies Zonation]] and [[Pennak Classification]]. Many early schemes were built on fish zones for fishery management, and studies gave conflicting evidence on whether invertebrates followed these zones. The authors note that Britain's invertebrate fauna is comparatively limited and well known, and that physical and chemical data were available for many rivers. These conditions made a national classification feasible.

**Study design.**
- Sites were restricted to rivers largely free from serious pollution, so that groupings reflected "natural" species assemblages.
- Sampling points were placed at 5, 10, 20, 30 and 40 km from the source, then at 20 km intervals downstream, following the spacing used by [[Verneaux Classification]] in France.
- Roughly 100 candidate rivers were compiled with Water Authority and River Purification Board (WA/RPB) staff. Forty-one rivers plus nine tributaries were selected.
- Biological sampling was done by WA/RPB biologists using guidelines from the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] (FBA), and identifications were done by FBA staff.

**Field methods.** Standard [[Pond-Net Sampling]] and kick-and-sweep sampling covered a total of about 3 minutes across the main habitats of each site. Air-lift, grab and core samplers were used at deep-water sites where pond-nets were ineffective. Samples were preserved in formalin or alcohol.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical variables.** Twenty-eight environmental variables were used in the analyses (Table 1 of the paper). They covered distance from source, slope, altitude, discharge, channel width and depth, velocity, substratum (dominant particle size and diversity), and macrophyte cover. Some variables were recorded for each of three seasons, with minima, maxima and means used. Water temperature was excluded because complete records were unavailable.

**Chemical variables.** Only 137 of the 270 biological sites had coincident chemical sampling. A further thirty-one sites lay within 10 km of a chemical sampling point. No chemical data were available for the remaining fifty-one sites. Thirty-three determinands were compiled, and six were selected for analysis after examination of their importance. These included [[Alkalinity]], pH, chloride, oxidised nitrogen and orthophosphate. Missing values were estimated from the nearest relevant site or from WA/RPB advice.

**Gradients.** The ordination revealed two main environmental gradients:
- **Axis 1** separated types of river. It was most strongly correlated with mean substratum size (r = 0.779) and alkalinity (r = 0.739). Upland, Scottish, northern English, Welsh and south-western rivers occupied one end. Midland, East Anglian and south-eastern rivers occupied the other.
- **Axis 2** reflected position along the river. It was most strongly correlated with log distance from source (r = 0.781) and discharge category (r = 0.740).

**Anomalous sites.** Tidal and brackish influence, as on the River Rother, and saline intrusion, as on the River Dane, pushed some sites toward the lowland end of Axis 1. Sewage and industrial effluent affected the lowest site on the River Weaver, which had a very limited fauna. Such outliers were excluded from the analysis.

**Biological indicators.** Plecoptera, many Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera, and many Diptera characterised upland groups. Oligochaeta, Gastropoda, Tricladida, Hirudinea, Crustacea and Hemiptera dominated lowland groups. Groups 24 and 27 contained taxa common in organically enriched sites, and their BMWP scores suggested mild enrichment. Only seventeen sites had [[Biological Monitoring Working Party Score]] values in the 50–100 range; the rest exceeded 100.

## Key Findings

**Dataset.**
- 268 sites were used after excluding two outliers from the original 270. The text also refers to 267 and 265 locations in different places, reflecting shared sampling points.
- The combined three-season analysis recorded 599 taxa.
- Identification was to species for most taxa, but Diptera and some other groups were identified only to genus, species group or family.
- A further fifteen classifications were built from single seasons and different taxonomic levels (see [[Furse Et Al 1984]]).

**Ordination.** Eigenvalues were 0.283 (Axis 1), 0.078 (Axis 2), 0.054 (Axis 3) and 0.046 (Axis 4). Sites did not form sharply separated clusters. This was expected, because physical gradients along rivers produce gradual changes in assemblages.

**Classification.** TWINSPAN produced sixteen groups at Level 4. Group sizes varied considerably. Group 7 and its subgroups were small. Groups 17–19 were largely upland, and groups 20–24 were progressively more lowland. The Swale/Ure/Ouse system showed the widest range of Axis 1 scores. An appendix provides a dichotomous key, using scores for differential species, that allows new sites to be assigned to a group. Table 4 lists 130 of the 599 taxa that occur in at least 80% of samples in at least one group.

**Prediction.**
- At Level 1 (two groups), MDA placed 91.4% of sites in the TWINSPAN group assigned by biology.
- At Level 4 (sixteen groups), 76.1% of sites were correctly predicted. A further 15.3% were wrongly predicted, but the correct group was the second most probable.
- In an independent test, forty randomly selected sites were withheld. The remaining 228 sites were reclassified and the MDA equations re-derived. The forty test sites were then predicted at 95.0% correct at Level 1 and 50.0% at Level 4. The authors expect a success rate of roughly 50–76% for new sites at Level 4.

**Key predictors.**
- In Function 1, alkalinity had the highest standardised coefficient (0.553).
- In Function 2, mean % macrophyte cover (0.872), log chloride (−0.545), minimum % macrophyte cover (−0.528) and log mean water depth (−0.445) were most important.
- Group 31 was an outlier on Function 2 because of its high macrophyte cover and chloride concentration.
- Of the twenty-eight variables, only five were not among the top ten in any of the first four functions.

**Sequence along rivers.** Most sites follow a logical sequence down each river. Roughly 10% break the pattern. Causes include slow-flowing pools, local inputs such as the Bassenthwaite lake outflow on the River Derwent, and changes in gradient near the mouth of the River Torridge. The Welland and Glen showed the least consistent pattern.

**Authors' conclusions.**
- The classification is more reliable than the fifteen alternatives, and it is intended as the main product of the study.
- Any classification of a continuum is somewhat arbitrary, but it is useful if it suits the user's needs.
- The classification is of sites, not river stretches. Extending it to stretches would require sufficient sampling and would be valid only if no atypical features occurred between sites.
- Pool samples are not yet included. Future work should add pools, [[Base Flow Index]] values (a measure of stored-water runoff), temperature (estimated from air temperature) and food resources.
- The system must be tested further before use as a management tool.

**Planned applications.** The [[Nature Conservancy Council]] could use the classification to check the adequacy of its Nature Conservation Review of rivers. The authors also planned to compare it with a parallel classification based on macrophytes (Holmes, 1980/1989), since over 150 river lengths overlapped between the two surveys.
