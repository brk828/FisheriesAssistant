# Razorback Sucker Recruitment in Lake Mead, Nevada-Arizona: Why Here?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This peer-reviewed study (Albrecht et al., published in *Lake and Reservoir Management*, 2010) investigates why [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) recruitment is occurring in [[Lake Mead]] while most other lower [[Colorado River Basin]] reservoirs show no sustained recruitment. Using a nonlethal [[Pectoral Fin Ray Aging]] technique validated against [[Otolith Aging]], the authors show that the Lake Mead population is young and that natural, wild recruitment has occurred regularly since the 1970s. Back-calculated spawning years were compared with historical lake elevations, and habitat variables were compared among bays. The authors conclude that lake-level management that allowed shoreline vegetation to establish, together with high turbidity, probably provides nursery cover for larval and juvenile fish, and that recruitment can persist alongside abundant [[Nonnative Predators]].

## Background & Description

The Razorback Sucker is a federally endangered catostomid historically widespread in the large rivers of the Colorado River Basin. Its distribution and abundance have declined sharply. Major causes include mainstem dam construction, which converted a warm riverine environment into cool tailwater and reservoir habitat, and competition and predation by nonnative fishes. Razorback Sucker persisted in several early reservoirs, but those populations consisted mainly of adults recruited in the first few years after impoundment. Populations then declined about 40-50 years after reservoir creation.

Lake Mead was formed when [[Hoover Dam]] closed in 1935. Razorback Sucker was relatively common lakewide in the 1950s and 1960s, apparently from reproduction soon after filling. Numbers fell noticeably in the 1970s. From 1980 through 1989, neither the [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] nor the Arizona Game and Fish Department collected the species in Lake Mead. In 1990, anglers reported fish in [[Las Vegas Bay]] and [[Echo Bay]], which prompted limited NDOW sampling. From 1990 to 1996, 61 fish were collected: 34 from Blackbird Point (Las Vegas Bay) and 27 from Echo Bay. Two larvae were also collected in 1995 near Blackbird Point, suggesting spawning in that area.

The study was initiated in 1996 by [[Bio-West Inc]] personnel to determine whether natural recruitment was occurring and what factors explained it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Sampling focused on Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay, with the [[Muddy River Virgin River Inflow]] added in 2005. Limited sampling also occurred near the Colorado River inflow in Arizona and in [[Lake Mohave]] for physicochemical comparison. Five locations were sampled for habitat in 2000-2002: Las Vegas Bay, Echo Bay and Trail Rapids Bay in Lake Mead, and Arizona Bay and Tequila Cove in Lake Mohave. Trail Rapids Bay was the only site without observed razorback sucker spawning. Each location had five 200 m x 20 m sites, with measurements taken at three points per site.

**Capture methods.** Adult and subadult fish were collected with [[Trammel Netting]]. Nets were 91.4 m long and 1.8 m deep, with inner panels of 2.5, 3.8 or 5.08 cm mesh and outer panels of 30.48 cm mesh. Nets were set in the late afternoon in about 3-9 m of water and pulled the next morning. Sampling was generally monthly from September through May. Net locations were guided by [[Sonic Telemetry]] habitat-use data and prior larval capture sites to limit sampler bias. Fish were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] (Destron/Fearing TX1400, 400 kHz), measured for standard length and weighed, then released at capture.

**Aging validation.** Two carcasses were aged with both otoliths and pectoral fin rays, and the methods agreed. Both fish were relatively young (ages 5 and 8). Eight known-age (age-3) fish held at the Lake Mead hatchery were also correctly aged by fin rays. Fin ray sections from fish stocked and later recaptured in the lake were also correct. From 1999, a roughly 6 mm segment of the second left pectoral fin ray was surgically removed from selected fish under anesthesia. Sections were embedded in epoxy, cut, mounted and read by at least two independent readers.

**Water quality and plankton.** Dissolved oxygen, nitrate, ammonium, total phosphorus, total dissolved solids, temperature and turbidity were measured, along with zooplankton density (organisms per liter). Little to no discernible difference was found among sites for these parameters. Zooplankton was present at variable but similar abundances across sites, suggesting larval fish were not food-limited at the sites tested.

**Lake elevation history.** Lake Mead was about 1200 ft AMSL at the start of the study. It peaked at 1215-1220 ft in 1998-1999 and then declined more than 100 ft, reaching a month-end low of 1105 ft AMSL in May 2008. The lake was filling in the 1930s and was drawn down about 100 ft in the mid-1960s to fill [[Lake Powell]]. Since then, Lake Mead has operated with small annual fluctuations but larger multiyear fluctuations.

## Key Findings

- **Capture totals:** BIO-WEST personnel captured 517 individual adult and subadult razorback suckers in Lake Mead from 1996 to 2008. The reported split is 218 at Las Vegas Bay, 249 at Echo Bay and 51 near the Muddy/Virgin River inflow, which sums to 518, a discrepancy in the source. Fin ray sections were taken from 186 newly captured wild fish.
- **Historical decline:** An estimated population of about 75,000 (partly illegible in the scan) was reported in the 1980s at [[Lake Mohave]]. That population declined to approximately 30 wild individuals by 2008 (Marsh et al. 2003; pers. comm.). Predation by nonnative bass, channel catfish and sunfish was identified as the main reason for lack of recruitment in other reservoirs.
- **Age structure:** Ages of razorback suckers ranged from 2 to 36 years. Most aged fish were spawned between 1974 and 1998, and the oldest was spawned around 1966. Recruitment pulses occurred from 1974 to 1998, notably in 1985-1987 and 1989. In 2005, 35% of aged fish were 10 years old or younger, indicating recent wild recruitment.
- **1997-1998 cohort:** Four of six fish aged in 2005 (67%) were recruited in 1997-1998, coinciding with high lake levels early in the study. One fish from the Muddy/Virgin inflow was also 6 years old, reinforcing that cohort. All five-year-old fish collected in 2005 were sexually mature (expressing gametes), and subadults at about 5 years did not appear mature, suggesting maturity near 6 years.
- **Recruitment and lake level:** Recent data through 2006 show that strong year classes (2000-2006) occurred during declining lake levels, so recruitment no longer coincided with high or rising elevations. The authors conclude that recruitment has occurred regardless of lake level during the study period.
- **Vegetative cover:** Mean percent cover of vegetation was significantly higher at Echo Bay than at other sites in several years (ANOVA, p < 0.04 to p < 0.004). Cover at recruitment locations declined from 2000 to 2002 as lake levels dropped. The authors propose that long-term, lake-elevation-driven establishment of vegetation, combined with reduced predation, drives recruitment pulses.
- **Turbidity:** Las Vegas Bay had significantly higher turbidity than all other locations in March (ANOVA, p < 0.001), and all but Echo Bay in May (p < 0.001). Echo Bay was significantly higher than other sites in May only. Turbidity did not appear to decline with lake level, unlike vegetation.
- **Mechanism:** The authors hypothesize that the drawdown for filling Lake Powell, followed by drawdown in the 1990s, let terrestrial vegetation establish on shorelines. Later inundation then created cover in coves and littoral habitats. Small annual fluctuations since then have preserved this cover. Pre-1970 large annual fluctuations likely prevented such vegetation from establishing. Limited recruitment may also have occurred between 1966 and 1978, when lake levels rose toward their highest point.
- **Nonnative predators:** Lake Mead supports large populations of largemouth, smallmouth and striped bass and channel catfish. Despite this, natural recruitment is occurring. The authors suggest that limited recruitment may be possible when razorback suckers and nonnative fish co-occur, if suitable environmental conditions prevail.
- **Management implication:** The authors recommend isolating the conditions that allow recruitment in Lake Mead. Active management could then facilitate similar conditions elsewhere in the basin to support wild and stocked populations.
- **Funding and cooperation:** The study was funded by the Southern Nevada Water Authority and the US Bureau of Reclamation, with support from NDOW, the National Park Service and the US Fish and Wildlife Service.
