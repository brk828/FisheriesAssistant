# Bonytail Chub Capture, Radio-Tagging and Handling Protocol Review, Lake Mohave (12-17 May 1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bonytail-chub

## Executive Summary

This memorandum, dated 21 May 1996 from the Project Coordinator of the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] Parker Fish and Wildlife Resource Office (FRO), reports a week of interagency [[Bonytail Chub]] research on [[Lake Mohave]] from 12 to 17 May 1996. Crews from the FWS, [[Arizona State University]] (ASU), the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR), the [[National Biological Service]] (NBS) and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) netted native fish, captured bonytails, radio-tagged them and released them. The memo also reviews the chemical and transport handling protocol used during the trip and recommends changes. The author judged the trip successful and the interagency effort a good example of cooperation.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** [[Bonytail Chub]], a native fish that is the focus of recovery work. The memo refers to it as "bonytail chub" or "bonytail" and treats it as a valuable, sensitive species. Scientific nomenclature is not given in the text.
- **Incidental natives:** [[Razorback Sucker]] (about 15 taken, roughly a third from the cove).
- **Non-native and other fish taken in nets:** [[Common Carp]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Threadfin Shad]]. The memo gives estimates, not counts.
- **Study area:** Carp Cove and Cottonwood East on Lake Mohave, the upstream side of Carp Cove, Tequila Cove waters in Nevada, and the North AJ backwater. Holding and transfer occurred at [[Willow Beach]] and [[Katherine's Landing]].
- **Method:** [[Trammel Net]] sets, checked every six hours and pulled during daytime from 15 May onward. Captured bonytails were held in chemically treated water, implanted with [[Radio Telemetry]] tags (by ASU, observed by Parker FRO and Ron Majors of Pinetop Fish Health), or identified as [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]] (PIT) fish.
- **Personnel:** 13 people in camp (6 FWS, 5 ASU, 2 NBS), plus the BOR and AGFD camps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The trip involved several fish species coexisting in Lake Mohave. Trammel nets captured native fish alongside large numbers of non-native species, and the memo's estimates indicate that the nets were dominated by carp. Native fish were a small share of the catch, which the memo notes as a reason to keep handling brief. Bonytails were captured in the lake, the cove and a backwater (North AJ), so they used several habitats within the reservoir. Several fish were held briefly at Willow Beach before release or transfer, and some fish that had been held or recaptured were at risk from repeated handling and surgery.

## Key Findings

**Catch and tagging results**
- Four bonytails were collected from North AJ by ASU and Parker FRO staff. All were implanted with radios and released.
- A total of about 7 bonytails were taken during the trip: the first lake fish taken by BOR, three more taken by the Carp Cove camp, and fish taken by AGFD (three of which were transported to Willow Beach). The memo's exact final tally is not separated by camp.
- Nine fish were radio-tagged and released into Lake Mohave: four cove fish and five lake fish.
- At least three bonytails remained at Willow Beach at the end of the trip.
- Estimated catch in the nets: about 15 razorback suckers, "some" 1,500 carp, 150 striped bass, roughly 20-26 largemouth bass (count garbled in scan), channel catfish, and 10 threadfin shad.
- Most fish came from the AGFD crew.

**Coordination problems**
- Cell phone contact between camps generally failed. Contact with Willow Beach worked. A landline with the AGFD camp worked.
- A bonytail was briefly missed by BOR staff due to poor communication, and was later picked up by Ron Majors and Parker FRO personnel.

**Recommended protocol changes**
1. Add the chemical treatment to water and turn on oxygen at the moment of capture, when the fish is brought to the holding boat. This takes about one minute and avoids wasting chemicals.
2. Use oxygen rather than aeration in small containers. The aeration system overworked and could abrade and stress the fish during extended holding; a smaller air stone would not supply enough oxygen.
3. The Parker pontoon served as the pickup boat for bonytails and stayed near the net boats. The other boats carried containers of 0.5% NaCl solution rather than the full chemical suite. The memo states this was the most logical arrangement given the boats' speed to the pontoon.
4. The protocol did not address minimum size or how to handle fish too small to implant. A small fish was taken to Willow Beach and released after a second opinion.
5. Each captured fish should be briefly inspected on capture. One radio-tagged fish was recaptured after about two hours, re-anesthetized and prepared for surgery before the team learned it had already been tagged. Checking for a radio or tag takes seconds, and the "no handle" protocol put a valuable fish at unnecessary risk. PIT-tagged bonytails should also be scanned, measured and returned to the habitat, and the memo leaves the exact handling time to the Region.

**Conclusions and next steps**
- The author considers the trip successful: several bonytails were captured, transported and tagged, and the chemical protocol was followed with slight modifications.
- Comments on the protocol were requested from the Recovery Coordinator, George Divine, by 1 June 1996, from camp leaders and from Tom Liles (FWS), Tom Currie and Glen Gould (BOR), Gordon Mueller (NBS), and Paul Marsh (ASU).
