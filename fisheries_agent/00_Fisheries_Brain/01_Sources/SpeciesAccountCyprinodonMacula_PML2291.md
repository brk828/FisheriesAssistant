# Desert Pupfish (Cyprinodon macularius) Species Account

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This student species account reviews the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*[[Cyprinodon Macularius]]*), a small cyprinodontid fish of the [[Sonoran Desert]] region. The document covers morphology, historical and current distribution, environmental tolerances, territorial and spawning behavior, growth, diet, threats, interactions with nonnative fishes, and conservation status. The species was listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986, and the account concludes that delisting is unrealistic in the foreseeable future, while downlisting to threatened could be considered once extant populations are protected and replicated within their historic range.

## Background & Description

- **Size and body form:** Small fish (< 75 mm TL) with an elliptically elongated body, a short, smoothly rounded head, and a superior, highly protractile mouth with tricuspid teeth in each jaw.
- **Scales and fins:** Scale circuli bear spine-like projections, with usually 26 lateral line scales. Dorsal and anal fins have 9 to 12 rays; pectoral fins 14 to 18 rays; pelvic fins usually 7 rays; caudal fin 14 to 20 rays. The dorsal fin origin lies halfway between the snout and the caudal fin base.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males have a deeper, more laterally compressed body. During breeding, males turn bright iridescent blue, with the caudal fin and peduncle becoming yellow to orange-red, and may show a dark terminal band on the caudal fin.
- **Females and juveniles:** Coloration is tan to silvery-olive with 5 to 8 narrow, disrupted vertical bars. Males may also show these bars, but less prominently.
- **Taxonomy:** The population at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] is considered a separate subspecies (*C. m. eremus*), though it is most morphologically similar to the Río Sonoyta population ([[Miller and Fuiman Subspecies Description]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution**

- **Historical range:** The lower [[Colorado River Basin]] and delta (including the lower Gila River and its tributaries, Salt, Santa Cruz, and San Pedro rivers), the [[Salton Sink]] basin, and the Río Sonoyta basin, including Quitobaquito Springs in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. The species likely also reached Needles, CA, and the Agua Fria, Hassayampa, and Verde rivers, though no collections exist from these areas.
- **Current range (excluding transplants):** Colorado River delta (particularly Laguna Salada), the [[Salton Sea]] and some of its tributaries, and the Río Sonoyta basin including Quitobaquito Springs.

**Habitat**

Habitats range from marshes, springs, and sloughs to irrigation drains, small streams, and shallows of larger waters. The species is usually found in shallow water with soft substrates. Aquatic vegetation and invertebrate abundance vary seasonally.

**Environmental tolerances**

- **Temperature:** 7 to 45 °C; thermal tolerance varies with acclimation temperature, and fish from constant-temperature habitats show narrower ranges.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** As low as 0.13 mg/L for some individuals, with a 50% survival rate at 0.2 mg/L in laboratory conditions.
- **Salinity:** Up to 70 g/L for eggs and adults and 90 g/L for juveniles in laboratory conditions, depending on temperature and dissolved oxygen.

The document describes the species as having some of the widest environmental tolerances of any freshwater fish.

**Territoriality and habitat use**

Males are aggressively territorial, with territory size smaller in aquaria than in the wild (where males typically defend one to two square meters). Territory size is inversely proportional to population density. Territories occur in shallow areas (typically 5 to 10 cm deep) centered on topographic irregularities such as submerged debris. At the Salton Sea, males left territories to forage in late afternoon and at sunrise, when territorial and non-territorial fish alike gathered in the deepest pool areas.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction and life history**

- **Courtship:** A characteristic sequence in which the female approaches the male with an arched body, the pair circles and charges, and the female nips at the substrate to induce physical contact. The male wraps his anal fin around the female's vent, and the female deposits eggs that the male fertilizes.
- **Spawning:** Up to five eggs may be spawned in rapid succession. A spawning bout lasts 30 minutes to two hours. Spawning begins in spring when water reaches about 20 °C and continues through the warm season; in constant-temperature habitats it is continuous.
- **Fecundity:** Females may lay 50 to 800 eggs or more in a single season.
- **Eggs and hatchlings:** Eggs are 1 to 2 mm in diameter and hatch in about 10 days at 20 °C. Hatchlings are 4 to 5 mm TL, and size can double in less than 8 weeks.
- **Maturity:** Sexual maturity can occur at 15 mm TL (about 2 to 3 months), so the life cycle can be completed within one year. More commonly, maturity occurs at about one year, around 45 mm TL.

**Growth**

- Temperature is the main environmental factor influencing growth ([[Kinne Growth Studies]]). At 35 g/L salinity, young fish grew fastest at 30 °C, with declining rates at 25 °C, 35 °C, 20 °C, and 15 °C.
- Salinity also affects growth: at 15–20 °C, preadult growth was fastest in fresh water, while at 25–35 °C it was fastest at salinities of 35–55 g/L.
- The optimum temperature for growth decreases with age; for fish older than 28–30 weeks, 24 °C was optimal.

**Diet**

The species is an opportunistic, largely unselective omnivore. Aquarium-reared larvae eat [[Brine Shrimp]] on the day of hatching. Gut analyses show chironomid, other dipteran, and lepidopteran larvae, adult and pupal insects, ostracods, algae, mollusc eggs, and detritus. At Quitobaquito Springs, fish have been reported to eat their own eggs and larvae. Males grab food from the substrate and return to patrolling; females graze more slowly.

**Threats to the species**

- **Habitat loss and degradation:** Loss of perennial waters has been documented since at least the 1890s. Overgrazing by livestock contributed to channel incision, lowered groundwater levels, and drying of springs and marshes. Other causes include groundwater extraction (particularly in the Río Sonoyta basin), impoundment, and diversion.
- **Other degradation:** Airborne pesticide spraying, invasion of nonnative vegetation (e.g., *Tamarix*), and especially introduction of nonnative fishes.
- **Natural resilience:** Although the species tolerates large fluctuations in water level and discharge, complete elimination of large habitat areas has been detrimental.

**Nonnative fishes and interactions**

The account lists numerous nonnative fishes recorded with or near desert pupfish habitat, including [[Red Shiner]], [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Tilapia]]. Three mechanisms are most often cited: competition, hybridization, and predation.

- **Competition:** Interference competition has been documented with [[Sailfin Molly]], which court both male and female pupfish and thereby disrupt reproduction. Adult tilapia are too large to interfere, but juveniles are frequently chased by territorial males, passively disrupting breeding.
- **Hybridization:** Of concern only if unauthorized introductions of other *Cyprinodon* species or subspecies occur. Geographic isolation has historically prevented hybridization.
- **Predation:** Considered the most serious concern. Largemouth bass introduction caused the extinction of an undescribed *Cyprinodon* population at Monkey Springs, Arizona. Mosquitofish, a less obvious predator, are highly carnivorous and have been linked to extirpation of [[Sonoran Topminnow]] where present.
- **Mosquitofish in the Salton Sea:** Long suspected to reduce desert pupfish populations, but this has been disputed. Laboratory work by Jennings (1986) showed the two can coexist briefly in systems open to egress, and a long-term study in closed systems was underway at the time of writing.

**Conservation status and recommendations**

- The species was listed as endangered in 1986 under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- Critical habitat has been designated at Quitobaquito Springs and parts of the Salton Sea tributaries (San Felipe Creek, Carrizo Wash, and Fish Creek Wash, Imperial County, California).
- Delisting is not considered realistic due to limited habitat and difficulties in establishing secure habitats.
- Downlisting to threatened should be considered if all extant populations are protected and replicated in natural habitats within the historic range, with genetic purity of replicated stocks assured.
- The account cites the [[Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan]] (USFWS, 1993) as the current management framework.
