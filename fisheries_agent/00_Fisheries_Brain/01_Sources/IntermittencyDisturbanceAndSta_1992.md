## Executive Summary

This theoretical/conceptual paper by Stanley and Fisher (1992) argues that stream **drying (intermittency)** is a widespread but understudied natural disturbance, especially in arid and semi-arid regions where intermittent streams are the dominant channel type. The authors propose a hydrology-based framework that partitions intermittency into three discrete phases — **drying, dryness, and rewetting** — and extend the classical ecosystem stability model of [[Resistance And Resilience]] (sensu Webster et al. 1975) to accommodate prolonged ("press") disturbances rather than only instantaneous ("pulse") ones. While the paper is not fish-centric, it situates fish populations (e.g., rainbow trout fry, upland stream fish communities) alongside macroinvertebrates and algae as biota subject to drying-induced disturbance, and it calls for standardized, phase-explicit sampling designs to better quantify differential ecosystem responses to intermittency.

## Background & Description

No taxon-specific morphology is described; the paper is a conceptual/ecological framework rather than a species account. However, it references generalized **biotic traits relevant to surviving drying disturbance**, applicable across fish, invertebrates, and algae:

- **Desiccation-resistant life stages** (e.g., cysts, resting stages) that must persist in situ through all three hydrologic phases to permit reactivation upon rewetting.
- **Mobility/behavioral plasticity**, such as the ability of organisms (including fish, per [[Erman And Leidy 1975]] on rainbow trout fry) to track receding wetted margins during flow loss.
- **Physiological tolerance thresholds**, since dry sediment temperatures in desert stream channels can exceed 65°C (Hadley 1970), representing extreme thermal and desiccation stress on any residual aquatic life stages.
- **Refuge-seeking traits**, allowing organisms to persist in remnant wetted pools/reaches or within hyporheic sediments during phase 2 (dryness).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Intermittent and ephemeral streams, which dominate arid/semi-arid landscapes worldwide (Graf 1988; Ward and Robinson 1990) and are subject to extreme flow variability across diel, annual, and multi-year (e.g., Sahelian drought cycles, [[El Nino Southern Oscillation]]) timescales.
- **Hydrologic phase structure**:
  1. **Phase 1 – Drying**: Discontinuous longitudinal flow develops; shrinking wetted margins intensify biotic density and competition, and water quality deteriorates.
  2. **Phase 2 – Dryness**: Surface water is absent; organisms may persist in [[Hyporheic Zone]] sediments, where evaporation rates are far lower than surface rates, potentially sustaining water storage and refugia.
  3. **Phase 3 – Rewetting**: Flow resumes, either gradually (via reduced evapotranspiration) or catastrophically (via flash flooding), which can scour or export desiccation-resistant propagules, risking a "false start" and premature, fatal reactivation.
- **Refugia**: Remaining wetted pools and hyporheic water bodies serve as critical refuges for fish, invertebrates, and algae during drying and dryness phases.
- **Community-level differential responses**: Algal communities, macroinvertebrates, and fish are expected to respond differently to the same drying disturbance, underscoring the need for taxon-specific and state-variable-specific comparisons (e.g., [[Boulton Et Al 1992]] found greater macroinvertebrate resistance to dryness in Australian streams vs. Sycamore Creek, Arizona, attributed to refuge quality).
- **Trans-ecosystem comparative context**: The framework draws on comparisons between desert stream ecosystems (e.g., [[Sycamore Creek]], Arizona) and Australian intermittent systems (Werribee and Lerderderg Rivers, Victoria), highlighting how regional differences in refuge quality and dry-period harshness shape survival outcomes.

## Key Findings

- **Intermittency as disturbance**: Drying meets the formal definition of a disturbance (White and Pickett 1985) — it is discrete, alters population/community/ecosystem structure, and changes resource, substrate, and physical-environment availability — yet has been far less studied than flood disturbance despite comparable ecological importance.
- **Three-phase resistance framework**: The authors propose partitioning "resistance" (per Webster et al. 1975) into **Resistance I** (to drying), **Resistance II** (to dryness), and **Resistance III** (to rewetting), plus **Resilience** (post-rewetting recovery rate), enabling quantitative, comparative hypothesis testing across sites, events, and antecedent conditions.
- **Press vs. pulse disturbance**: Borrowing from [[Bender Et Al 1984]], the paper distinguishes "press" (prolonged, e.g., drying) from "pulse" (instantaneous, e.g., flash floods) disturbances, arguing that classical stability models—built around instantaneous disturbance—must be modified to accommodate the temporal, multi-phase nature of drying.
- **Hyporheic buffering**: Subsurface hyporheic water may persist through phase 2 due to much lower subsurface evaporation rates than surface rates (Thomsen and Schumann 1968), potentially buffering organisms and nutrients and accelerating post-drying recovery, as shown by faster algal biomass recovery in upwelling zones of a Sonoran Desert stream ([[Valett 1991]]).
- **Comparative resistance evidence**: A cross-continental comparison (Boulton et al. 1992) found Australian stream macroinvertebrates exhibited greater Resistance II (survival through dryness) than those in Sycamore Creek, Arizona, generating a testable hypothesis that dry-period survival is a function of sediment moisture during dryness.
- **Research gap**: Virtually nothing is known about ecosystem-level (as opposed to organism- or community-level) changes during intermittency, or about biological/biogeochemical processes occurring during the "dry" phase itself (phase 2), representing a major frontier for stream ecology.
- **Management relevance**: Anthropogenic water consumption, desertification, and global climate warming are expected to increase the frequency and severity of stream intermittency, elevating the practical importance of understanding drying as an ecological disturbance for water resource management in semi-arid regions ([[Grimm And Fisher 1992]]).
- **Methodological recommendation**: Studies of intermittent streams should be explicitly designed around the three hydrologic phases, with high-frequency sampling during rapidly changing drying and rewetting phases, to avoid blurring disturbance–response relationships.
