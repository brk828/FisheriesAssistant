# Dissolved Nitrogen Concentrations in the Columbia and Snake Rivers in 1970 and Their Effect on Chinook Salmon and Steelhead Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] technical report by [[Wesley J. Ebel]] (Seattle, August 1971) summarizes 1970 water sampling of [[Dissolved Nitrogen]] supersaturation in the [[Columbia River]] and [[Snake River]] and reviews its effects on juvenile and adult [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) and [[Steelhead Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). The data were provided to the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] for a mathematical model predicting dissolved nitrogen levels and for spillway design and operation.

Dissolved nitrogen was generally lower in the Columbia River in 1970 than in 1968-69, but concentrations were high in parts of the Snake River in spring and early summer, mainly because of spill at [[Little Goose Dam]]. [[Gas Bubble Disease]] was widespread in Snake River juvenile and adult salmon and steelhead, and substantial losses of fish, particularly juveniles, occurred during periods of high supersaturation.

## Background & Description

**Problem:** Supersaturation of dissolved nitrogen gas associated with spilling water at dams in the [[Columbia River Basin]] prompted studies to build a predictive model, to design spillways that avoid supersaturation, and to modify spill operations to reduce gas concentrations.

**Scope:**
- Dissolved nitrogen sampled twice a month, 7 April to 18 August 1970
- Lower 200 km of the Snake River and lower 640 km of the Columbia River
- Effects (unusual mortality and gas bubble disease symptoms) examined in juvenile and adult chinook salmon and steelhead trout

**Sampling methods:**
- Stations ran from the forebay of [[Little Goose Dam]] to Astoria, Oregon, reached by aircraft (21 stations, Table 1)
- Samples were collected in a single day, usually the second and fourth Tuesday of each month
- Forebay samples were taken at the surface and at 10 m; tailrace samples at the surface only
- Measured variables: dissolved nitrogen, dissolved oxygen and water temperature
- Methods followed [[Ebel 1969]] and [[Beiningen and Ebel 1970]]; argon in air is included in the nitrogen values

**Biological sampling methods:**
- Caged juvenile fall and spring chinook at various depths in the forebay of [[Ice Harbor Dam]], 50 fish per cage, observed for 7 days
- Control cage at 3-4 m depth, where hydrostatic pressure was judged to compensate for nitrogen up to about 135% of saturation
- Juvenile chinook and steelhead from the gatewell bypass trap at Ice Harbor Dam (100 or more fish per sampling)
- Adult chinook examined at [[Rapid River Hatchery]], Riggins, Idaho
- Aerial and riverside searches for dead adult salmon and trout, related to nearby nitrogen concentrations

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and migration:** Both species migrate through the Columbia and Snake River systems, passing a series of dams and reservoirs. Spill at dams produces supersaturated water that is carried downstream and into forebays and tailraces.

**Gas supersaturation:** Water supersaturated with dissolved nitrogen causes gas bubble disease in fish. The report notes that depth provides some compensation, since hydrostatic pressure offsets supersaturation at depth.

**Dams and spill:**
- [[Little Goose Dam]]: spill of about 1,981 m³/s (70,000 cfs) on 7 July, reduced to 368 m³/s (13,000 cfs) on 21 July
- [[Lower Monumental Dam]]: site of the 146% high on 8 June
- [[Ice Harbor Dam]]: site of caged fish experiments and gatewell sampling

**Hatcheries and tributaries:** [[Rapid River Hatchery]] (Idaho), [[Dworshak Hatchery]] (steelhead source), and the Salmon River at Whitebird, Idaho.

**Temporal pattern:** Snake River supersaturation was present in early April, while high Columbia River concentrations appeared only in early June, during peak flow in the first week of June.

## Key Findings

### Dissolved Nitrogen Concentrations
- Columbia River concentrations were generally lower in 1970 than in 1968-69, except during peak flow in the first week of June.
- Early May, McNary to Bonneville, nitrogen ranged:
  - 100-133% of saturation in 1968
  - 128-144% in 1969
  - 96-107% in 1970
- The Snake River had extremely high concentrations: 129% below Little Goose Dam on the first survey (7 April).
- Concentrations rose between Little Goose and Ice Harbor Dams over the next two months, reaching 146% of saturation in the forebay of Lower Monumental Dam on 8 June.
- Levels dropped markedly by 21 July, attributed to reduced spill at Little Goose Dam, and returned to about 100% by 18 August.
- Mean nitrogen at Lower Monumental rose sharply between 5 May and 19 May as spill at Little Goose increased.

### Mortality and Gas Bubble Disease in Caged Juveniles (Ice Harbor Dam)
- **Table 3, wild spring chinook, 21-28 May 1970** (water 11.9-12.4°C; 127% saturation): surface cage mortality was 60% at 24 hours, 92% at 48 hours and 100% at 7 days. Of survivors, 17% and 94% showed gas bubble disease in the reported cages.
- Mortality in the 0-4.5 m cage (the fish could sound freely to 4.5 m) ranged 45-68% from late May to early July. The author considered this cage most representative of migrating juveniles.
- **Table 4, hatchery fall chinook (Wahkeena Pond), 4-11 June 1970** (water 12.4-13.8°C; 127-132% saturation): surface cage mortality was 98% at 24 hours and 48 hours and 100% at 7 days. Survivors showed gas bubble disease at 92-100%.
- **Table 5, wild fall chinook, 15-22 June 1970** (water 13.8-15.8°C; 132-127% saturation): surface mortality was 58% at 24 hours, 82% at 48 hours and 100% at 7 days. Survivors showed gas bubble disease at 25-88% depending on cage.
- **Table 6, wild fall chinook, 24 June-2 July 1970** (water 17.1-19.4°C; 127-134% saturation): mortality at 7 days was 34% at the 3.0-4.0 m control cage and 238% recorded as "2" in the OCR for a second cage (values garbled in the scan); survivors showed 0-12% gas bubble disease.

### Gas Bubble Disease in Migrating Juveniles (Gatewell Samples, Ice Harbor Dam)
- 25-45% of juvenile chinook salmon showed symptoms of gas bubble disease.
- 30-58% of juvenile steelhead trout showed symptoms.

### Population Losses of Juvenile Migrants
- Raymond (1971) estimated about 70% loss of migrating juvenile chinook between the Salmon River at Whitebird, Idaho, and Ice Harbor Dam.
- For steelhead from Dworshak Hatchery: about 15% loss to Ice Harbor Dam and about 90% loss to McNary Dam.
- Independent observations and Raymond's estimates indicated substantial losses of juvenile steelhead and chinook from supersaturation in the Snake River.

### Effects on Adult Fish
- Adult chinook at Rapid River Hatchery: after 6 July, about 30% of arriving fish showed gas bubble disease.
- No significant dead-fish mortality was found in searches until 7 July.
- On 7 July, 27 dead adult chinook were counted between Little Goose and Lower Monumental Dams. Symptoms were observed on 3 fish; most were too decomposed to examine. Gas bubble disease was judged the primary cause.
- Mortality appeared in early July, probably from additional exposure of later-migrating fish to high concentrations in both rivers, with a delay between exposure and death.
- Mallet et al. (1971) estimated that 45% of adult spring chinook in the Snake River were lost before spawning, attributed to delayed effects of nitrogen supersaturation.
- The author notes that limited adult observations cannot measure the full extent of losses.

### Overall Conclusions
- Snake River supersaturation was high and widespread in spring and early summer 1970, largely due to spill at Little Goose Dam.
- Gas bubble disease was widespread in juvenile and adult chinook and steelhead.
- Substantial losses of fish, particularly juveniles, occurred during periods of high dissolved nitrogen.
- Data were intended to support the Army Corps of Engineers' predictive model and spill management changes.
