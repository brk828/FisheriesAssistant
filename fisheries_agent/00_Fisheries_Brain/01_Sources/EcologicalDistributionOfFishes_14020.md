# Ecological Distribution of Fishes of the Moapa (Muddy) River, Clark County, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study by [[James E. Deacon]] and W. Glen Bradley (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) maps the fish fauna of the Moapa River, a spring-fed desert stream in southern Nevada, using collections from nine stations sampled between 1963 and 1968. The river hosts four common native species, a rare fifth species, and a growing community of non-native fishes. The authors argue that the stream's unsaturated environment has allowed exotic species to establish readily, and that their arrival, especially of [[Poecilia Mexicana]], has reduced native populations and narrowed their spatial range.

## Background & Description

The Moapa River arises from numerous warm springs and flows about 40 km southeast to [[Lake Mead]]. Before [[Hoover Dam]] was built it drained into the [[Virgin River]]. It is a remnant of the [[Pluvial White River]] system, and its only tributary is [[Meadow Valley Wash]], which is intermittent and flows in only during floods. The river is notable because its usual temperature pattern is reversed: the headwater springs are warmer than downstream reaches, which cool under a low head dam and then track ambient air temperature.

Previous work included the description of the endemic [[Moapa Coriacea]] (Hubbs and Miller, 1948b), studies of headwater ecology and breeding by Kopec (1949), and investigations of parasitism, activity cycles and exotic introductions by [[Wilson et al. (1966)]], [[Deacon and Wilson (1967)]] and Hubbs and colleagues.

**Methods (summary):** Nine stations were established (two spring sources combined as Station 1, giving stations 1–8). Sampling used tied seines (10 hauls per seine, reported per 20 hauls) and a 115 V, 600 W AC shocker, later a 2650 W alternator, reporting fish per 30.5 m of stream. Seining was dropped after 1963–64 because it was judged to be the more biased method. Water temperature was measured with a YSI telethermometer and chemistry with a Hach kit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow:** Average stream gradient is 1.6 m/km. Spring discharge is remarkably constant. Flow at Station 3 is larger and more stable than at Station 6, reflecting losses to irrigation, groundwater and evapotranspiration. Summer is the lowest-flow season. Most collections were made in February to April.
- **Temperature:** Headwater springs range from 29–32 C, and Station 2 holds near 29 C. The minimum recorded was 10.5 C at Station 7 near Logandale (2 January 1966). Overall ranges reported were 27–32 C for [[Crenichthys Baileyi]] and 19.5–32 C for Moapa.
- **Water chemistry:** Springs are clear. Turbidity, alkalinity, sulfate, pH, total dissolved solids and oxygen increase downstream. In November–December 1965, total alkalinity rose from 190 to 265 ppm between stations 1 and 3, sulfate from 155 to 240 ppm, and total dissolved solids from 656 to 1060 mg/L.
- **Vegetation and habitat:** Spring pools support Najas, Nasturtium, Eleocharis, Chara and algae, with dense growth to 1.2 m depth. Vascular plants are largely absent from the main channel below Station 3. Streamside canopy was removed at Station 4 (by the owner) and Station 5 (by highway construction). Station 3's canopy was removed in 1965.
- **Lower river:** Station 8 has no native fish. Turbidity and deep silt there result from slowed flow during Lake Mead inundation, and irrigation affects flow strongly.
- **Middle river:** Stations 4–6 have a firm clay bottom with scattered rock riffles. Their turbidity, velocity, oxygen and temperature resemble the lower extent.

## Key Findings

**Native fauna**
- Four native species are ecologically segregated into headwater and middle-stream types: [[Moapa Coriacea]] and [[Crenichthys Baileyi]] in the headwaters, and [[Gila Robusta]] and [[Rhinichthys Osculus]] in the middle stream.
- [[Gila Robusta]] is most abundant in the middle river, in undisturbed deep riffles. It ranged from about 15–30 C. Its abundance has declined since 1942. At Station 4 in 1964, 104 specimens were taken, and only 34 were taken at Station 5 in 1964–67. The University of Michigan collected 193 specimens near Station 4 in 1938.
- [[Moapa Coriacea]] prefers flowing water. Its greatest abundance occurs at 28–30 C, yet it can survive at cooler temperatures, and one specimen was taken at 19.5 C. Hubbs and Miller (1948b) collected 268 specimens at Home Ranch in 1938. Numbers were 13 and 11 in April 1964 and 1965, then 50 and 121 in April 1966 and February 1967, when it was considered common again.
- [[Crenichthys Baileyi]] is most abundant in the warm spring pools at the source. Its distribution reflects a preference for warmth and a competitive edge in low-oxygen water.
- [[Rhinichthys Osculus]] is uncommon but peaks at Stations 3 and 4. It is restricted to marginal habitat, which may be due to competition with Moapa.
- [[Plagopterus Argentissimus]] is extremely rare. A single specimen (UNLV F-290) was taken at Station 5 on 26 October 1963, probably a straggler from the Virgin River. [[Pantosteus Clarki]] was not collected by the authors, but one specimen was taken near the Union Pacific Power House in April 1950, probably a stray from the Virgin River.

**Non-native fauna**
- The authors list nine introduced species, seven of which are common to abundant in some part of the river. The effective species diversity has roughly tripled.
- The earliest recorded introduction was [[Gambusia Affinis]], established by 1938. [[Poecilia Mexicana]] was introduced in 1963 and became established in the headwater springs in January 1965. Notropis lutrensis probably invaded in the late 1950s or early 1960s.
- [[Poecilia Mexicana]] has become the dominant headwater species and has also reduced native populations. It occupies much of the niche of the four native species.
- Non-native fishes increase in number and relative abundance from the headwaters to the lower river. [[Cyprinus Carpio]], Notropis lutrensis and Lepomis cyanellus dominate the lower river, which now has no native species.

**Spatial overlap and competition**
- Percent spatial overlap ranged from 93% to 28% between native species. The Moapa–Crenichthys overlap of 93% is the only value above 46% among native species, suggesting possible competition, though sampling differences complicate this interpretation.
- Poecilia, Gambusia and Gila all show high overlap, as does Poecilia with Rhinichthys.

**Conclusions and conservation concerns**
- The unsaturated habitat of the Moapa River allows exotic species to establish easily, compared with saturated habitats in Malaya (Johnson and Soong, 1963).
- Establishment of exotic fishes has been a major factor in the deterioration of the river as a native fish habitat. Parasitism (Wilson et al., 1966) and reduced fecundity (Espinosa, 1968) are suggested mechanisms.
- Moapa Coriacea, then classified as rare and endangered, survives only where at least one exotic species also occurs. Further habitat or biotic change could threaten the species, and the authors suggest the same for Rhinichthys.
- Hydrologic and water-quality trends have been stable since the early 20th century. Irrigation has mainly affected the lower river. Habitat alterations such as vegetation clearing, road construction and bank cutting have been relatively minor.
