# Striped Bass Decline and the Debate Over a Federal Moratorium

#ichthyology #marine-biology #striped-bass

## Executive Summary

This article reports on the collapse of [[Striped Bass]] (striper) stocks along the U.S. Atlantic coast and the policy response in the early 1980s. Recreational and commercial anglers, state and federal biologists, and conservation groups debated a federal moratorium on striped bass possession, while the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) led a multi-year study of the causes of decline. The piece also includes a short sidebar on renewed interest in wind-assisted "sail-power" for commercial ships as a response to fuel costs. Note that the scanned text is partly garbled; figures are reproduced as legible in the source.

## Background & Description

- **Historical reputation:** Striped bass has been praised since the seventeenth century. [[John Smith]] (1614) called it "an excellent Fish," and [[Robert B. Roosevelt]] (1865) described it as the main attraction of saltwater angling on the northern Atlantic coast.
- **Life history:** The species is an "offspring of the Ice Age" that moved from freshwater lakes and rivers to the sea. It stays close to shore, using surf, tidal currents and salt marsh shallows. Its streamlined, torpedo-shaped body, powerful tail and protected gills suit this habitat.
- **Growth:** Per the article, a striper grows from about five to six inches at the end of its first year to roughly forty inches and an average of fifty pounds by about fourteen years of age.
- **Record fish:** A 103-pound striper was caught in Maine's Middle Bay nearly a century before the article.
- **Maturity:** Females can reach maturity in their fourth year and need at least one spawning season before harvest to sustain the population.
- **Stakeholders:** Millions of recreational and commercial fishermen fish for striped bass from beaches, jetties, breakwaters and small boats. Advocacy groups include [[Stripers Unlimited]] and the [[National Audubon Society]], whose board member [[Donal C. O'Brien]] has fished Nantucket waters for over thirty-five years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Chesapeake Bay:** The [[Chesapeake Bay]] is the species' most important spawning and nursery system. The article reports it supplies about 85 percent of the striped bass taken, as described in the source. Sharp declines in the young-of-the-year (YOY) index here drove the crisis. The bay produced a strong year class in 1970, but recent spawning has been poor.
- **Hudson River:** The [[Hudson River]] stock recovered because [[PCB Contamination]] led to a ban on commercial fishing for striped bass, reducing harvest pressure. Its most recent YOY index was among the best in two decades.
- **Causes of decline:** Scientists could not yet determine whether fishing pressure, environmental/pollution factors, or both were primarily responsible. The article states that the population cannot recover unless fishing pressure is sharply reduced while the causes are studied.
- **Youth index benchmarks:** NMFS considers a YOY index under 4 as poor, and a drop below 2 signals an alarm. In the 1960s the index averaged 15 (with a higher peak), it was small in 1980, and it fell to 1.4 in 1983.

## Key Findings

**Population and fishery trends**
- Commercial striped bass landings along the Atlantic fell steeply from a peak of more than four million pounds to about two million pounds in 1982. The 1983 figures were still being compiled at the time of writing.
- The species supports an annual recreational and commercial fishery valued at about $200 million (per NMFS).
- The 1983 Chesapeake YOY index was 1.4, down from 1980 levels; the article describes this as alarmingly low.

**Research and policy**
- Senator [[John Chafee]] of Rhode Island sponsored a 1979 measure, the [[Chafee Amendment]], calling for a three-year study of the decline, funded at $41 million under the [[Anadromous Fish Conservation Act]]. NMFS and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service led the research.
- Representative [[Gerry Studds]] of Massachusetts added a two-year extension via the [[Studds Amendment]], which tied federal funds to states adopting a uniform management plan.
- Congressional staff said a federal moratorium was unlikely in the current session because of [[States' Rights]] concerns. [[Dick Russell]] expected progress if states did not make significant changes.
- A [[Minimum Size Limit]] of twenty-four inches (measured nose to tail fork) is the core of the proposed uniform plan. It is meant to protect spawning females.
- Of eleven northern Atlantic states, nine had adopted the twenty-four-inch minimum by the time of writing. Massachusetts was the only state with no addenda or conditions. Delaware and North Carolina had not yet adopted it, though Delaware was expected to act that year.

**Petitions and pressure**
- Stripers Unlimited (executive secretary [[Avis Boyd]]) petitioned the Department of Commerce, under the [[Endangered Species Act]], to list striped bass as "endangered" for fish over fifty pounds and "threatened" for smaller fish. A decision was expected in the spring.
- [[John Boreman]] of NMFS (Woods Hole) said the priority was a moratorium on the entire fishery until management plans were in place. The moratorium would continue if YOY indices stayed low and would be lifted once plans were effective.
- Russell cited the Hudson River as proof that reduced pressure enables recovery.

**Sidebar: Sail-assist shipping (non-fish topic)**
- A short sidebar reports renewed interest in wind-assisted [[Sail-Assist Shipping]] after oil price spikes. Bunker C fuel oil rose from about $2.20 (1971) to $29.28 (early 1983), according to the article.
- A 1981 report for the U.S. Maritime Administration by the Wind Ship Development Corporation predicted fuel savings of 20 to 30 percent for sail-assisted ships.
- If 20 percent of the world's merchant fleet used sail, fuel bills would fall by about $3 billion a year, according to the article.
- Sail-assist conferences were held in 1982 and 1983, and [[Jacques Cousteau]] tested a sail rig on a new research ship.
