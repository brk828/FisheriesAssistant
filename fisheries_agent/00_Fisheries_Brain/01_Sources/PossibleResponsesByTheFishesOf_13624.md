# Multi-Level Intake Structure Warmed Releases and Native Fish Spawning in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Richard A. Valdez of [[BIO/WEST Inc]], argues that cold hypolimnetic releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] currently prevent mainstem spawning by native fishes in the [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]]. It proposes that a [[Multi-Level Intake Structure]] (MLIS) allowing warmed releases could create suitable mainstem spawning conditions, helping to establish a second spawning population of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] as required by [[Conservation Measure 7]]. The author stresses that thermal regulation must be combined with flow management and that warming would also affect algae, macroinvertebrates, non-native fishes, and parasites.

## Background & Description

**Native fishes.** Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*), flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*), bluehead sucker (*Catostomus discobolus*) and speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*) are reduced in number, and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is very rare. Only five of the original eight mainstem native species remain in Grand Canyon (Minckley 1991). The humpback chub and razorback sucker are federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Endemism.** The paper cites Miller (1959) for the statement that 87% of Colorado River fish species occur nowhere else on earth. In the upper basin, 13 native and endemic species share habitat with 42 non-native species (Tyus et al. 1982).

**Population status.** Grand Canyon supports one of only six known humpback chub populations (Valdez and Clemmer 1982). The 1978 biological opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations produced seven conservation measures. Conservation Measure 7 calls for establishing a second spawning population in Grand Canyon, which the paper argues can only occur through mainstem reproduction because the other tributaries are too small or have limited fish access and spawning areas.

**Dam impacts.** Glen Canyon Dam, completed in 1963, reduced spring runoff magnitude and duration, increased daily flow fluctuations, reduced nutrients, depleted sediment, and stabilized cold water releases. Non-native species were introduced for sportfishing and food.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species assemblage.** The number of fish species reported from Glen Canyon to Separation Canyon rose from 8 to 22 between the late 1950s and 1990–92, probably reflecting both invasion and more intensive surveys. In 1990–92, 22 species were recorded, of which only about a dozen were abundant, common or locally common. The region also contains nine lake-dwelling species, including striped bass, walleye, largemouth bass and smallmouth bass.

**Spatial distribution.** Humpback chub concentrate in a 13-km reach around the [[Little Colorado River]] inflow, with smaller numbers at Bright Angel, Shinumo, Tapeats and [[Havasu Creek]], and at RM 120 and RM 213 (Pumpkin Springs). Only two razorback suckers were reported in the five years before the study. Rainbow trout are the most common salmonid above the Little Colorado River, and common carp are among the most abundant fish throughout the region.

**Thermal classes (Table 1).** Warmwater species (>15.6°C), coolwater species (4.4–15.6°C) and coldwater species (<12.8°C) are classified, but the author notes these categories overlap too much to be useful, so each species is assessed individually.

**Temperature regime.** In 1991, water released from Glen Canyon Dam ranged from about 8 to 10°C, peaking in June through August. Mainstem temperature rose by roughly 1°C per 35 miles, reaching about 12°C at the Little Colorado River inflow (77 miles downstream) and about 16°C at [[Diamond Creek]] (241 miles downstream). Predam temperatures at Phantom Ranch (103 miles downstream) ranged from about 2°C in January and December to about 23°C in July and August.

**Spawning timing.** Humpback chub currently spawn in the Little Colorado River in March through May, when that tributary reaches 16–22°C. Historical evidence (a Kolb brothers observation of an aggregation at the Little Colorado River mouth in May 1911, and Suttkus and Clemmer 1977, who inferred June–July spawning) suggests predam spawning was one to two months later than at present, likely from late May to early July. Three hypotheses are considered: (Ho1) humpback chub did not spawn in the mainstem before the dam; (Ho2) cold releases forced mainstem spawners to shift to the Little Colorado River; and (Ho3) the population was split between mainstem and Little Colorado River spawners, with mainstem reproduction failing after dam closure. The text for Ho3 is partly illegible in the source.

**Temperature requirements.** Adults of native mainstem species can survive roughly 0–30°C, but eggs, larvae and juveniles are more sensitive. Native spawning ranges are 16–22°C for humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker and speckled dace, and 10–22°C for razorback sucker. Non-native warmwater species have overlapping or higher ranges: red shiner and fathead minnow 15–30°C, common carp 20–26°C, channel catfish 23–29°C, striped bass 15–24°C, and walleye 3–12°C.

**Trout thermal limits.** Upper lethal temperatures are 25°C for rainbow trout, 23°C for cutthroat trout and 24°C for brown trout. Optimum growth occurs at 12–17°C for rainbow and cutthroat trout and 10–16°C for brown trout, so warming favoring native fishes would be detrimental to salmonids.

**Non-native species.** [[Channel Catfish]] are considered the greatest predation and competition threat to native fish. [[Common Carp]] consume large numbers of recently deposited eggs, but their spawning is limited to warm quiescent areas with vegetation or structure. [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]] are controlled by flow, although red shiners may exclude other species, possibly through temperature and the absence of stable backwaters. Striped bass ascend to spawn each May to July, with nine captured in 1991, all with empty stomachs. Their upstream distribution appears limited by velocity and lack of deep lentic habitat rather than temperature. [[Flathead Catfish]] spawn at 24–28°C and are not expected to invade the canyon.

**Parasites and disease.** The Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) was found in 80% of young humpback chub (13–35 mm TL) examined in 1990 but was absent in 1989. Egg maturation occurs at 25–30°C, with highest worm densities at 20°C, so mainstem maturation is unlikely even under warming, although survival would be possible. The anchor worm (*Lernaea cyprinacea*) is common on Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker and humpback chub. Naupliar development fails below 20°C, and egg production does not occur below 24°C. Its effects in the upper basin do not appear to cause significant mortality.

## Key Findings

- **Current releases are too cold for mainstem spawning.** Water temperatures of 8–10°C at the dam and about 16°C only at Diamond Creek in July and August fall short of the 16–22°C range native fishes need. Survival of humpback chub eggs and larvae was below 15% under 16°C, and no hatching occurred below 12°C (Hamman 1982).
- **Proposed thermal regime.** Releases of 11°C in May and 15°C in June through September are proposed to warm the reach between the Little Colorado River and Diamond Creek to 16–22°C. Warming to 19–20°C is expected from Elves Chasm to Havasu Creek (131–172 miles below the dam) in June and July. Early March and April releases would not be augmented, to protect current Little Colorado River spawning.
- **Second population goal.** The author concludes that an MLIS is a viable element of the preferred alternative and that mainstem reproduction by humpback chub is the only realistic path to a second spawning population.
- **Warming risks.** Higher temperatures could alter algal and diatom communities, macroinvertebrate composition, parasite and disease incidence, and facilitate invasion by predaceous non-native fishes from [[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mead]] and tributaries. The author notes that other investigators will address algae and macroinvertebrates, while this paper addresses non-native fishes and parasites.
- **Non-native fish outlook.** Warming is expected to benefit some non-native species, but flow management and limited life-history requirements (for example, backwater stability and velocity limits) may restrain expansion. Table 2 summarizes positive and negative effects but several entries are marked "?" (unknown), including algae and macroinvertebrates.
- **Trout trade-off.** Thermal regulation could improve growth for tailwater trout, but downstream trout populations would be harmed by the warmer conditions favoring native fish.
- **Data limitations.** The author states that a definitive evaluation is not possible in this paper and that existing physiological data are limited.
