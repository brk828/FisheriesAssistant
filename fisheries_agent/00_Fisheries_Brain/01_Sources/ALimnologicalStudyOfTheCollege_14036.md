# A Limnological Study of the College Farm Strip-Mine Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 study by Charles C. Burner and Claude Leist, published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science*, is the first phase of a research program on improving fish production in [[Strip-Mine Lake]]s of Southeastern Kansas. It describes the [[College Farm Strip-Mine Lake]] near [[Pittsburg Kansas]] over 10 months (July 1950 to April 1951). The lake has favorable traits, including clear water, a thriving growth of [[Higher Aquatics]], alkaline pH, and oxygenated water. It also has serious limitations: very low [[Plankton]] and [[Benthos]] abundance, steep banks with little shallow habitat, and a fish catch dominated by small [[Forage Fish]]. The authors conclude that the fish population is unbalanced and that the lack of shallow areas is the most serious handicap.

The issue also contains two other items. A brief fragment on a chick embryo virus-inoculation technique, which survives only as its closing text and references, and a note on color aberrations in the [[Prairie Vole]], a terrestrial mammal, which is not aquatic.

## Background & Description

The authors note that strip-mine pits, widely regarded as good reservoirs, have yielded far less fish than expected. The study was undertaken by the [[Kansas State Teachers College]] Department of Biological Science in cooperation with the [[Kansas Forestry Fish and Game Commission]]. It was intended as a preliminary survey of a pit likely to contain a large variety of organisms.

**Lake morphology and history**
- Formed by strip-mining in 1932. Most of the pit and adjacent spoil banks were deeded to the Kansas Forestry, Fish, and Game Commission in 1936. The Commission deeded them to the college in 1950.
- The main body is about one-fourth mile long and 120 feet wide, with a maximum depth of 15 feet. Its long axis runs north to south.
- East-west arms, 2 to 12 feet deep, extend from both sides. Total area is about 7 acres.
- No streams enter the lake. Inflow comes from surface runoff, seepage from spoil banks, and overflow from small side pits. The watershed is a little over 15 acres. Under ordinary conditions there is no outlet. During heavy rain, water drains from the south end into an old creek bed.
- Banks are precipitous, typical of strip-mine lakes, and are slowly eroding. Only one shallow area exists in the main body, a 50-foot-long zone at the south end.
- Cattails grow in shallow arms and the south-end meadow, with sedges and rushes in the mud. Beaver and muskrat activity is evident.

**Methods**
- Five stations (I to V) were sampled at surface and bottom. Station I was sampled monthly as a reference. The others were sampled in rotation to test how well station I represented the whole lake.
- Water was collected with a [[Kemmerer Water Sampler]]. Plankton was taken by straining 24 liters of water through a Wisconsin plankton net. Bottom organisms were separated from debris with a screen of 30 meshes per linear inch.
- Secchi disc readings and water color were recorded. pH was determined with a Hellige color comparator. Other chemistry was done by titration in the field.
- Standard procedures followed [[Limnological Methods]] from Welch (1948), Lagler (1950), Ellis et al. (1948), and the American Public Health Association (1946).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physico-chemical conditions**
- **Temperature:** 3.5 to 26 °C in the main body, with the minimum in January and the maximum in August. Surface and bottom temperatures differed little, so there was no [[Thermal Stratification]]. Arms closely matched the main body.
- **Light and color:** Secchi visibility ranged from 24 to 102 inches, highest in October and lowest in December. The water was never murky. Color ranged from green to brownish green, attributed to algae and submerged plants.
- **pH:** Averaged 7.8 (alkaline). The only low reading was pH 6.9, from a bottom sample at station II in September.
- **[[Dissolved Oxygen]]:** Never absent. The July low was 1.3 ppm at the bottom of station I, versus 4.95 ppm at the surface. The winter maximum was 12.55 ppm. Oxygen was nearly uniform through the water column except in July.
- **Free carbon dioxide:** Usually higher near the bottom. Absent in December, March, and April. Maximum was 8.5 ppm at station II in September.
- **[[Carbonates and Bicarbonates]]:** Carbonates were probably present as bicarbonates, since pH never exceeded 8.3. Bicarbonates ranged from 96 ppm (July) to 140 ppm (September), staying near 130 ppm.

**Biological communities**
- **Higher aquatics:** Submerged plants were [[Chara]], *Potamogeton foliosus*, and *Najas flexilis*. The floating plant was *Jussiaea repens*. Emergent and wet-soil plants included two cattail species (*Typha latifolia* and *T. augustifolia*), several sedges, and several rushes. Submerged plants grew at 8 to 10 feet along the shore and across most arm bottoms.
- **Plankton:** Most frequent phytoplankton were desmids, diatoms, *Peridinium*, and *Ceratium*. Most frequent zooplankton were *Difflugia*, rotifers, *Daphnia*, *Cyclops*, and Cyclops nauplii. Total abundance peaked at 1210 per liter in November and 1050 in February, fell to 20 per liter in January, and stayed at or below 550 per liter in other months.
- **Benthos:** Most abundant larval insects, found at all stations, were Corethra, Chironomus, Tanypodinae, and Ceratopogonidae. Hexagenia occurred at station I from November to April. Distribution was patchy. In December, station I held 606 organisms (about 2.8 cubic centimeters) versus 108 at station V. Crayfish were present but were not quantified.
- **Fish:** 155 specimens were seined. Bluegill sunfish made up 58%, topminnow 23%, green sunfish 10%, longear sunfish 8%, and largemouth black bass 1%. Average lengths were about 2.25 to 2.87 inches. Stomachs averaged a little under half full. Large fish were rarely seen.

## Key Findings

- **Favorable traits:** Clear water, alkaline pH, adequate oxygen for most of the year, and a thriving [[Higher Aquatics]] community that provides cover for many organisms. Temperatures can support warm-water fish.
- **Summer risk:** Dissolved oxygen fell below the 4 ppm standard in July, though briefly and near the bottom. Prolonged hot spells could push oxygen too low for fish. The authors judged that the surface layer's higher oxygen would prevent this from limiting the population.
- **Trophic limits:** Plankton and benthos counts were low, so the lake lacks the food base that [[Plankton]] and [[Benthos]] provide to fish and other animals.
- **Unbalanced fish community:** 154 of 155 fish were forage fish. The authors concluded that forage fish greatly outnumber predators, indicating an unbalanced population.
- **Habitat handicap:** Steep, nearly vertical sides leave little shallow area for food production and breeding. Only the south end and several arms have less than 3 feet of water suitable for spawning and feeding.
- **Lake classification:** Following Welch (1935), the pit is a temperate lake of the third order because it shows no pronounced stratification.
- **Research direction:** This study is an initial survey intended to guide later work on improving fish production in Southeastern Kansas strip pits.

**Other items in this issue**
- **Chick embryo technique:** Page 2 contains only the closing lines of a "Simplified Technique for Virus Inoculation" and its references. These cite the [[Chick Embryo Technique]] for culturing viruses and rickettsiae. The technique's details are not in the surviving text.
- **Prairie vole color aberrations:** Cockrum reports that three of twelve prairie voles trapped in November 1947 in northeastern Kansas and southeastern Nebraska had abnormal coloration. A review of about 900 *Microtus ochrogaster* specimens, mostly from Kansas, at the [[University of Kansas Museum of Natural History]] found two more aberrant individuals, for five in total. Patches of white hairs on the dorsum, venter, or tail tip were described for each specimen. Aberrations were previously reported in other *Microtus* species, including *M. pennsylvanicus*, *M. californicus*, *M. montanus*, and *M. oeconomus*.
