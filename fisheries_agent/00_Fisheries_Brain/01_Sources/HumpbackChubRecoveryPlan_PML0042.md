# Humpback Chub Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the draft-stage Humpback Chub ([[Gila Cypha]]) Recovery Plan, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] in 1978 under the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The plan's goal is to restore and maintain a minimum of five self-sustaining Humpback Chub populations in the Colorado River system by 1990. It sets out seven major objectives: habitat requirements, habitat protection and management, a captive propagation program, information and education, and an agency-coordinated recovery effort. The document also contains the written review comments from federal, state, and tribal agencies and the Recovery Team's responses.

## Background & Description

The Humpback Chub is one of the most bizarre-looking North American fishes, with a prominent nuchal hump, a flattened head with a fleshy snout, and small eyes. It is one of the last large fish species to be described in North America, based on a specimen from the Grand Canyon (Miller 1946). Its taxonomy is unsettled: intermediate forms between the Humpback Chub, [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Roundtail Chub]] have been reported, and some authors suggest many specimens are hybrids or intergrades. The Recovery Team treats the Humpback Chub broadly to include these intermediate forms, pending taxonomic studies.

Key diagnostic traits include a nuchal hump with lateral grooves, a flat head, dorsal rays typically 9 (occasionally 10), anal rays typically 9, and a caudal peduncle intermediate in depth between the Bonytail and Roundtail. Maximum length is about 45 cm. Adults are olivaceous or brownish dorsally and silvery ventrally. Young fish (about 70 mm total length) are difficult to distinguish from other young chubs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and abundance.** Historically the species occupied the Colorado River basin, but was little reported before the 1940s because of inaccessible canyon areas. Recent records show a patchy distribution in several canyon reaches:

- Grand Canyon (the largest known population, per the plan)
- Westwater Canyon and Black Rocks (Colorado)
- Desolation and Gray Canyons (Green River)
- Little Colorado River and Marble Canyon
- Dinosaur National Monument (Green and Yampa Rivers), where chubs are relatively rare

Only four populations were known at the time of writing. Earlier surveys in the Flaming Gorge and Glen Canyon reservoir areas found chubs, but Glen Canyon Dam's cold, fluctuating tailwaters appear to have eliminated them from much of their former habitat.

**Life history.** No specific life-history research had been conducted. Information was extrapolated from related species and occasional observations. Spawning appears to occur at higher water temperatures, likely June to July, with young-of-year appearing in September. Adults were observed feeding at the surface; juveniles may feed on plankton and crustaceans.

**Causes of decline.** The plan identifies several threats:

- Habitat alteration by Flaming Gorge and Glen Canyon dams, including cold tailwaters and altered hydraulics that reduced spawning and rearing habitat
- Competition and predation by exotic fishes
- Hybridization with related species, which may be accelerated by habitat modification
- Eradication of fishes in the Green River before Flaming Gorge Dam closure, possibly affecting Dinosaur populations
- Potential effects of upper-basin water development and energy development

## Key Findings

**Goal and objectives.** The plan aims for five self-sustaining populations by 1990 and is organized into seven objectives:

1. Determine habitat requirements and areas of essential habitat, including spawning and rearing areas.
2. Locate and monitor all extant populations and suspected habitats; determine population status and levels needed to maintain five secure populations.
3. Propagate and reintroduce chubs, with facilities for about 10,000 Humpback, 10,000 Roundtail, and 10,000 Bonytail chubs, and a minimum of 20,000 Humpback Chubs produced through 1990.
4. Eliminate unfavorable activities affecting key habitats, including Section 7 consultation under the Endangered Species Act.
5. Prepare habitat management plans and manage habitats.
6. Establish a long-term captive propagation program for three Gila species, with brood stocks of 10 pairs of Humpback Chubs at each of two facilities.
7. Conduct information and education programs.

**Budget and implementation.** The plan proposes a five-year budget and an agency responsibility matrix. Several subobjectives were unassigned in the draft.

**Agency review.** Most reviewers supported the plan but raised concerns:

- The Bureau of Reclamation and Bureau of Land Management noted budget and funding uncertainty; some proposed budget figures were judged highly optimistic.
- The Navajo Area Office noted the high cost (about $1,400,000 over five years) for propagating a fish of doubtful value, and possible effects on the Navajo Indian Irrigation Project.
- The New Mexico Department of Game and Fish stated the species does not occur in New Mexico.
- The Nevada Department of Fish and Game and the Nevada BLM office declined to participate because the species does not occur in Nevada.
- The U.S. Forest Service stated its lands in Arizona and New Mexico are unlikely to support the species.
- Reviewers suggested clarifying that many populations are unknown, that goals should prioritize maintaining existing populations over establishing new ones, and that an environmental statement may be required.
- Reviewers also suggested an electric weir as an alternative capture method and starch-gel electrophoresis for taxonomic work.

**Team responses.** The Recovery Team accepted several comments, including reordering priorities so that habitat maintenance (Task 24) receives top priority, adding a habitat-protection emphasis, and increasing recommended funding levels. It rejected some, including an economic analysis of the species' value (considered arbitrary) and a suggestion to defer the goal of five populations.

**Conclusion.** The plan emphasizes that the Humpback Chub's decline is linked to habitat change from dams and that recovery depends on protecting existing populations, restoring habitat, and building captive stocks as a safeguard against catastrophic loss.
