# Thermal Requirements of Tilapia zillii

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[W. J. Hauser]] (Division of Biological Control, University of California, Riverside) combined laboratory experiments and field observations to define the thermal requirements of [[Tilapia Zillii]], an African cichlid proposed as a [[Biological Herbicide]] for aquatic weeds in [[Southern California]] irrigation canals. The optimal range for feeding, growth, and spawning lies between 16 and 32°C. Deaths occurred at average maximum-minimum temperatures above 39.5°C and below 11.2°C, while the extreme upper and lower [[Lethal Temperature Limit|lethal limits]] were 42.5°C and 6.5°C. Because of these thermal requirements, the author concluded that the value of *T. zillii* as a biological herbicide in southern California canals is diminished, since winter water temperatures would leave it marginal or unable to survive.

## Background & Description

*Tilapia zillii* had been introduced into southern California as a biological control agent for aquatic weeds in irrigation canals. The aim was to reduce the high cost of mechanical weed control and improve water delivery efficiency. Earlier expectations that the fish could survive water temperatures as low as 7°C and persist year-round in canals were not well supported by the literature. Published tolerance values varied widely, for example:

- [[Chimits 1957]] reported a minimum tolerated temperature of 9°C.
- [[Sterba 1963]] reported tolerance of 14 to 16°C.
- [[Welcomme 1964]] reported a maximum tolerance for juveniles of 41°C.

Preliminary field observations suggested the species was well adapted to summer conditions but not winter, which motivated the present study on its lower thermal tolerance.

**Methods.** One laboratory test examined increasing water temperatures, and four examined gradual lowering. Tests ran in a temperature-controlled refrigerator box with fish held in aerated 3 or 22-liter containers under a constant 12 h light / 12 h dark photoperiod. Specimens came mainly from a drainage ditch fed by warm seepage water, with some from an [[Imperial Irrigation District]] rearing pond. Temperature changes were gradual, roughly 1°C per interval, with one test slowed to about 1°C per 72 hours below 15°C. Diel fluctuations were usually about 2°C, but reached about 5°C in two tests. Field temperatures were recorded with maximum-minimum thermometers or thermographs, and laboratory thermometers were calibrated against a reference thermometer from the University of California, Riverside chemistry department.

Behavioral categories used in the study were:

- **Lethargic:** movements became sluggish and fish could be caught by hand.
- **Lost equilibrium:** reduced fin and opercular movement, with abnormal orientation.
- **Moribund:** resting on the bottom while still alive, with some opercular movement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish inhabit managed irrigation systems in the [[Imperial Valley]] and nearby districts, including the [[Central Main Canal]], drainage ditches, seepage drains, and a 13 × 10 m pond. Field studies covered caged fish, fish confined in canal and ditch enclosures, and free-swimming fish. Winter conditions in these canals limit the fish's annual activity window, and the author noted that aquatic weeds begin their annual growth by April, before the fish can be effectively stocked.

Egyptian studies provide a comparison. In [[Lake Quarun]], Egypt, the growing season begins when water temperatures exceed 19.9°C ([[El-Zarka 1961]]), and spawning in Egypt occurs at temperatures above 23.1°C ([[El Bolock and Koura 1960]]; [[El-Zarka 1956]]; [[El-Zarka 1962]]).

## Key Findings

**Laboratory: rising temperatures**
- Feeding decreased and fish became lethargic at an average temperature of 32°C.
- Loss of equilibrium occurred at 39°C.
- Deaths occurred at average temperatures between 39.5 and 40.2°C.
- The maximum temperature recorded when the last dead fish was found was 42.5°C.

**Laboratory: falling temperatures**
- Feeding declined at about 16°C.
- Fish became lethargic at about 13°C and lost equilibrium at 11.4°C.
- Deaths occurred between 10.9 and 7.1°C (reported as 11.2 to 7.0°C in the discussion).
- The minimum temperature recorded when the last dead fish was found was 6.5°C.
- Starting acclimation temperature had no apparent effect on the results.

**Field observations**
- No mortality was attributed to high water temperatures, with a maximum observed of 39°C.
- Confined fish died when average water temperatures fell to between 11.8 and 14.1°C.
- No fish survived average water temperatures below 10°C.
- The minimum temperature recorded when the last dead fish was found was 7.2°C.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning was first observed in a drainage ditch in 1974, after average temperatures rose to 20°C in spring.
- Successful spawning followed once average temperature reached 22.5°C.
- Nesting continued through summer until average temperatures fell to 21°C.
- Most spawning occurred in spring as temperatures rose from 22.5 to 25°C.

**Thermal requirements summary** (from the study's Table 3)

| Condition | Temperature (°C) |
|---|---|
| Extreme lethal (lower) | 6.5 |
| First death (low temperatures) | 11.2 |
| Last death (low temperatures) | 7.0 |
| Moribund (low temperatures) | 13.0 to 14.5 |
| Lethargic (low temperatures) | 14.5 to 16.0 |
| Feeding (lower limit) | 16.0 to 18.9 |
| Growth | 18.9 to 31.0 |
| Spawning | 22.5 to 31.5 |
| Lethargic (high temperatures) | 32.0 |
| First death (high temperatures) | 39.5 |
| Last death (high temperatures) | 40.2 |
| Extreme lethal (upper) | 42.5 |

- The optimal range for life processes is 16 to 32°C, making the species thermophilic.
- Few fish would survive exposure to 14.5°C for longer than two weeks.
- Winter survival would be marginal from December through late March.
- Active feeding and growth could be expected only from April through mid-November, and reproduction from May through October.
- Extensive or complete mortality would be expected whenever average water temperature drops below 10°C.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Control of aquatic weeds with *T. zillii* would require annual restocking, as with any herbicide. Rearing operations would need to stock high densities early in the season, before water warms in spring.
- The species could be a useful biological herbicide in other California waters where temperatures stay high and there is no chemical residue.
- Tolerance estimates vary across studies because of differences in acclimation, experimental technique, and exposure duration. The author found no clear correlation between fish size and temperature-related mortality.
