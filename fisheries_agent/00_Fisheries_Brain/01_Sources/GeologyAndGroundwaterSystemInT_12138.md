# Geology and Ground-Water System in the Gila River Phreatophyte Project Area, Graham County, Arizona

#hydrogeology #groundwater #phreatophytes

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey professional paper (PP 655-D, by William G. Weist, Jr.) describes the geology and ground-water system along a 15-mile reach of the [[Gila River]] at the head of [[San Carlos Reservoir]], on the San Carlos Indian Reservation. The study supports a wider investigation of how much water is consumed by [[Phreatophytes]], chiefly [[Saltcedar]], and how much could be saved by replacing them with more beneficial vegetation such as Bermuda grass. The report covers the [[Basin-Fill Deposits]] and the [[Alluvial Deposits]], their aquifer properties, recharge and discharge, and water-level behavior. It contains no fish or aquatic-fauna data.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** About 60 square miles of flood plain and terraces along the Gila River, Graham County, Arizona.
- **Motivation:** Dense saltcedar (*Tamarix pentandra* Pall.) growth along Southwestern rivers was thought to consume large volumes of water.
- **Investigation timeline:** Begun in 1962. This report is the fourth in the series. Geologic mapping was by E. S. Davidson (1964–65), modified photogrammetrically by Weist (1966–67). No exact geologic ages were assigned.
- **Monitoring network:** Observation wells were first drilled in November 1962, with 72 wells in cross sections about 2 miles apart. About 35 more were added in March–April 1966. In 1966, water levels were measured monthly in 20 basin-fill wells.
- **Well numbering:** A four-digit system in which the first two digits give the nearest downstream cross section and the last two are an arbitrary unique number (e.g., well 0102).
- **Data handling:** Size analyses were done at the University of Arizona sedimentation laboratory. Aquifer-test data were analyzed with the modified nonequilibrium formula (Ferris and others, 1962).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic units**

- **[[Basin-Fill Deposits]]:** The most widespread unit, underlying the alluvium and extending to about 1,000 feet depth. Divided into:
  - [[Silt And Sand Facies]]: reddish-brown to light-brown, thin-bedded silt and sand with clay interbeds. Fine-grained and arkosic, with low hydraulic conductivity.
  - [[Limestone Facies]]: thin-bedded white limestone with minor silt and tuff, cropping out in the western part of the area and cut by northwest-trending faults.
- **[[Alluvial Deposits]]:** Poorly sorted lenticular clay, silt, sand, and gravel filling a trough carved into the basin fill. Divided into:
  - [[Terrace Alluvium]]: 20–100 feet above the river bed, covering the full trough width, generally 40 feet thick or less beneath the flood plain.
  - [[Flood-Plain Alluvium]]: occupies the channel centre, supports most of the saltcedar, is frequently flooded, and is generally coarser than the terrace alluvium. It is up to about 50 feet thick, with the greatest penetrated thickness 48 feet.
- A volcanic neck about 400 feet in diameter intrudes the silt and sand facies in the western part of the area.

**Aquifer behaviour**

- **Basin fill:** Water is generally under artesian pressure. Wells yield only a few gallons per minute, with a reported maximum of about 20 gpm. Dissolved solids are generally 500 mg/L or less, sometimes 100–200 mg/L.
- **Alluvium:** Wells yield up to 600 gpm. Water is mostly unconfined. Total dissolved solids range from about 1,000 mg/L to more than 15,000 mg/L, with sodium and chloride as the principal ions and very hard water.
- **Limestone facies:** One well (2374) taps it. Its sodium chloride water has about 5,000 mg/L dissolved solids, the poorest quality in the basin fill.

**Recharge and discharge**

- Basin-fill recharge comes mainly along the mountain-front contact and from coarse ancient tributary deposits.
- Basin-fill water flows toward the Gila River and moves into the alluvium at about 5,400 acre-feet per year.
- Alluvial recharge comes from basin-fill inflow (~5,400 acre-feet/yr), [[Underflow]] from upstream (~610 acre-feet/yr), river percolation, and precipitation.
- Alluvial discharge is mainly downstream underflow, [[Evapotranspiration]] by phreatophytes (more than 10,600 acre-feet/yr, preliminary), and minor seepage into the river at low flow.

**Water-level behaviour**

- Levels are highest from mid-February to the end of April, after winter rains, and lowest from mid-July to mid-September.
- Annual range is typically 1.5–2 feet, and more than 5 feet near the river in dense vegetation.
- Wells near the river respond rapidly to streamflow changes, and some show diurnal fluctuations of a few hundredths to two tenths of a foot.

## Key Findings

1. **Basin-fill aquifer is poor:** The [[Aquifer Test]] on Calva Tribal well 1 gave a [[Transmissivity]] of about 50 sq ft/day (recovery data 20 sq ft/day) and a [[Storage Coefficient]] of 0.00043. Average [[Hydraulic Conductivity]] was estimated at 0.5 ft/day.

2. **Alluvial aquifer is productive:** An aquifer test (pumped at 152 gpm for 5,021 minutes) gave an average transmissivity of 10,700 sq ft/day (range 9,250–12,500) and an average storage coefficient of 0.12. Hydraulic conductivity is about 275 ft/day, a value used for the whole alluvial aquifer.

3. **Directional anisotropy:** Transmissivity was about 8 percent higher in the north-south direction, attributed to imbricated gravel that reduces flow along the stream direction.

4. **Vertical leakage:** Temperature-gradient analysis (Bredehoeft and Papadopulos method) gave vertical flow of 0.78 ft/yr at Dewey Flat and 1.08 ft/yr at cross section 17. The estimate implies about 0.9 acre-feet per acre per year, or about 5,400 acre-feet per year over the 6,000-acre project area. The authors note that the assumptions were not fully met.

5. **Gravel content:** Terrace alluvium averages 33 percent gravel (range 0–98 percent). Flood-plain alluvium averages 37 percent (range 0–95 percent). The coarsest material lies in the central trough. Where gravel exceeds 50 percent, hydraulic conductivity is expected to be higher.

6. **Streamflow losses:** Losses between cross sections 1 and 9 were 12,000, 7,300, and 4,100 acre-feet for water years 1963, 1964, and 1965. A daily loss of 1,400 acre-feet occurred on September 25, 1964. Most loss returns to the stream as bank storage recedes.

7. **Water-table gradient:** About 9 feet/mile in the upper area, falling to about 2.5 feet/mile in the lower area affected by backwater from [[San Carlos Reservoir]]. In December 1966, the river was seeping into the alluvium over most of the reach, except near Calva.

8. **Unsaturated zone:** Neutron soil-moisture measurements showed no downward movement below about 1 foot in summer or 4 feet in winter, so most precipitation replenishes root-zone deficits or is used by plants.

9. **Storage coefficient uncertainty:** Comparable Arkansas River values were 0.17 at 4 days and 0.25 extrapolated to 20 days. The authors suggest a true Gila River value near 0.20, and preliminary soil-moisture studies suggest about 0.25. Cyclic-fluctuation estimates (0.29 and 0.04) were inconclusive.

10. **Management implication:** The paper underpins phreatophyte-replacement planning by quantifying the aquifer resource that saltcedar consumes. Further analysis (computer modelling) was reserved for later chapters of the series.
