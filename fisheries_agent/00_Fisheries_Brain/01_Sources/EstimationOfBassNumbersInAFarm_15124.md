# Bass Population Estimation in a Farm Pond and Chironomid Bioassay of Industrial Pollution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two studies from Alabama freshwater research. The first, by Swingle, Smitherman and Spencer, estimated the [[Largemouth Bass]] population of a 3.5-acre experimental pond at the [[Auburn University]] Agricultural Experiment Station using [[Mark-Recapture]] methods. It compared the [[Schnabel Method]] and [[Peterson Method]], with sampling by [[Electric Shocker]] and angling, and checked the estimates against a full pond drain. Both methods were off by roughly 50 percent or less in the cumulative estimates, and the authors classed the results as management or preliminary rather than research-grade.

The second study, by Walter M. Tatum, tested a low-cost [[Bioassay]] using [[Masonite Plate Sampler]]s colonized by [[Chironomids]] to detect effects of industrial effluent on the [[Black Warrior River]] near [[Tuscaloosa, Alabama]]. Chironomid counts were consistently lower below the outfalls than at upstream control stations, which the author interpreted as evidence that the waste harms fish food organisms over more than nine miles of river.

## Background & Description

### Study 1: Bass Estimation in Pond S-9

- **Study site:** Pond S-9 at the Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station, Auburn, Alabama. It covered 3.5 acres with about 575 yards of shoreline.
- **Stocking history:**
  - 2,625 [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), 22 February 1960
  - 875 overwintered [[Tilapia Mossambica]] (*Tilapia mossambica*), 4 April 1960
  - 350 [[Largemouth Bass]] fingerlings, 2 May 1960
  - 87 [[Israeli Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), added in 1962
- **Sampling tools:** A boat-mounted [[Electric Shocker]] (3,000 W rated output, 12-foot boom with five electrodes) and spinning-tackle angling with artificial lures, with minnows used for the last three angling samples.
- **Marking methods:**
  - Period 1: caudal fin clips on bass in the 6- to 12-inch groups ([[Caudal Fin Clip]]).
  - Period 2: numbered Monel tags on the lower jaw, with right pelvic fin removal on 88 of the 103 tagged fish to estimate [[Tag Loss]]. Bass smaller than 8 inches received a right pectoral fin clip instead.
- **Estimation formulas:** The multiple-census Schnabel estimator with the Chapman correction (Ricker, 1958), and Bailey's form of the Peterson estimator.

### Study 2: Chironomid Plate Samplers

- **Sampler design:** Two 3 × 3-inch masonite plates separated by one 1-inch square plate, bound with nylon rope. Materials cost about three cents per sampler.
- **Colonization:** Samplers were suspended in a fertilized pond at about two feet depth for roughly one month to build a dense larval chironomid population.
- **Industrial setting:** Four industries (a sulfuric acid and resin chemical company, a tall oil and binder company, an asphalt company, and a pulp-paper mill) discharge within a 2.9-mile reach of the river.
- **Test periods:** October 1963; May and November 1964; July 1965. Five to ten stations per period, two samplers per station, deployed for one week.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond dynamics.** The bass population was a closed system during the study, with recruitment removed analytically. Bass under the 8-inch group were excluded from estimates because they were unlikely to be recruited from the 1962 year-class. The pond was drained on 20 December 1962 and all fish were removed for direct counts.

**Fish food organisms.** Chironomids are abundant in non-polluted Alabama waters and are important forage for many fishes (Howell et al., 1941). Their decline below outfalls is therefore presented as a proxy for broader impacts on the riverine food web and on fish.

**Pollution gradient.** Sampler counts were compared with control stations upstream of the uppermost outfall. A control station 100 yards above a navigational lock and dam also showed higher chironomid densities than the reach below the lock, suggesting that conditions there were more favorable for the organisms.

**Sampling artifacts.** Samplers were lost in several instances. In the fourth period, a five-hour delay in transport left floating larvae in the containers, which probably reduced densities at the first stations sampled.

## Key Findings

### Bass Population Estimates (Study 1)

**First period (23 May to 20 December 1962)**

- Actual N, derived from the draining count and the 65.7 percent survival of marked fish, was **604 bass**.
- Survival calculation: 71 marked fish recovered (69 at draining plus 2 angled) out of 108 marked, giving 65.7 percent. The adjusted population was 397 / 0.657 ≈ 604.
- Schnabel estimate: **304 bass** (−49.7%). The 95% confidence interval (195–502) did not include N.
- Peterson estimate (cumulative): **469 bass** (−22.4%). The 95% confidence interval (364–626) included N.
- The authors concluded that the Peterson estimate was the more reliable of the two for this period.

**Second period (1 August to 20 December 1962)**

- Actual N was **500 bass**, based on 102 tagged fish alive after one mortality, 79.4 percent tag-based survival, and 397 bass counted at draining.
- Schnabel estimate: **381 bass** (−23.8%). The 95% confidence interval (269–706) included N. The no-mortality assumption was violated, yet accuracy was not clearly reduced.
- Peterson estimate (cumulative): **313 bass** (−37.4%).

**Criteria and precision**

- Robson and Regier (1964) suggested that M × C should exceed four times N for a valid Peterson estimate. The first-period product was 40.94N.
- Only the first-period Peterson estimate met Robson and Regier's criteria for management applications (precision 0.95, accuracy 0.25).
- None of the censuses met the stricter research criteria (accuracy 0.10). Achieving that would have required about 425 bass captured, roughly 70.4 percent of the population.

**Effort and conclusions**

- Total effort was 23.5 man-days, including 4.0 days for marking and 21.5 days for subsequent sampling. This was enough to estimate the population within 22.4 percent of N.
- The authors reported that the estimates were fairly uniform, which gave a false sense of accuracy, since they were off by about 50 percent on draining.
- The estimates consistently underestimated N, which suggested that marked bass were more vulnerable to shocking and angling than unmarked bass.
- The [[Electric Shocker]] was an effective capture tool. The authors called the work preliminary and recommended intensive sampling within a few weeks of draining.

### Chironomid Bioassay Results (Study 2)

- **Period 1:** Chironomid counts on samplers were 86–98 percent lower at five stations below the outfalls than at the station 0.5 miles above the uppermost industry.
- **Period 2:** Counts at four stations below the outfalls were 47–79 percent lower than at the control 1.7 miles above the uppermost industry.
- **Period 3:** Nine stations were used. Counts at seven stations below outfalls were 32–77 percent lower than at the control.
- **Period 4:** An additional control was added. Counts below five outfalls were noticeably lower than at the upstream stations, though the transport delay complicated comparisons.
- The author concluded that the waste materials adversely affect fish food organisms in more than nine miles of the Black Warrior River.

### Recommendations for the Bioassay

1. Locate a dense chironomid pond (or other non-burrowing insect larvae) as close as possible to the test area.
2. Submerge each sampler during transport, and leave air space in sealed containers.
3. Avoid jolting and temperature changes during transport.
4. Minimize delays during transport.
5. Deploy enough samplers per station, since sampler loss is unavoidable.
