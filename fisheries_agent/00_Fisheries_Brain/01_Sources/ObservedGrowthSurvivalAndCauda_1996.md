# Observed Growth, Survival, and Caudal Fin Ray Deformities of Intensively Cultured Razorback Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #freshwater-ecology #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This study investigated the cause of severe caudal and other fin ray deformities observed in ~65% of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae hatched at the Colorado Division of Wildlife's Fish Research Hatchery (FRH) in 1993. Using a controlled 1994 feeding trial with four known parental crosses fed three different commercial diets (B-250, HEG1, HEG1+), researchers tested nutritional, environmental, and genetic hypotheses. Although diet affected growth and survival rates, **all larvae subjected to repeated handling during examinations developed fin deformities regardless of diet or parental lineage**, while unexamined siblings reared under identical conditions rarely showed deformities. The study concludes that **handling stress during critical fin ray developmental stages**, rather than nutrition, environment, or genetics, was the primary cause of the 1993 deformity outbreak, with implications for [[Endangered Species Aquaculture]] protocols.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a monotypic, endemic Colorado River [[Catostomidae|catostomid]].
- **Fin ray developmental sequence** (onset by total length, TL):
  - Principal **caudal fin rays**: first observed at 10 mm TL, typically apparent by 11 mm TL (consistent with Snyder and Muth 1990).
  - **Dorsal and anal fin rays**: onset at 14 mm TL.
  - **Pelvic and pectoral fin rays**: onset at 15 mm TL.
- **Deformity characteristics observed in 1993 year-class**:
  - Absence of all but one principal caudal fin ray.
  - Dorsal fins missing all rays, appearing as a small lump of flesh.
  - Pectoral and pelvic fins shriveled and cup-shaped.
  - Multiple concurrent fin deformities common in individual juveniles.
- **Deformity onset in 1994 trial**: No deformities in larvae 10–13 mm TL; deformities began appearing at 14 mm TL and became predominant at ≥15 mm TL — coinciding precisely with the onset of fin ray formation, irrespective of diet or parental cross.
- **Growth rates by diet** (at study completion, ~47 d):
  - B-250 (Fry Feed Kyowa): greatest average growth, 19.1 mm TL.
  - HEG1 (Hatchfry Encapsulon Grade 1): 16.0 mm TL.
  - HEG1+ (HEG1 + Spirulina 9:1): 15.7 mm TL (no added growth benefit from Spirulina).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native range/status**: Endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]; listed as **endangered** under the U.S. Endangered Species Act (1973) and protected under statutes in Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming.
- **Threats to wild populations**:
  - Habitat loss (Carlson and Muth 1989; Tyus and Karp 1989, 1990).
  - Competition and predation by nonnative species (Minckley 1983; Marsh and Brooks 1989; Bestgen 1990).
  - Lack of recruitment (Tyus 1987; Papoulias 1988; Lanigan and Tyus 1989; Tyus and Karp 1990).
  - Possible hybridization with sympatric native catostomids, notably [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Buth et al. 1987; Tyus and Karp 1990), though disputed (Minckley et al. 1991).
- **Conservation context**: Cooperative hatchery agreements (1993–1994) between CDOW and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service established the FRH as a **refugia and broodstock source** for potential population augmentation.
- **Broodstock origin**: Four spawners (2 male, 2 female) collected from wild Colorado River populations near Grand Junction, CO, and one gravel pit pond (Etter Pond) near De Beque, CO — spawned at [[Horsethief Canyon State Wildlife Area]].
- **Rearing environment**: Heated, degassed well water; average flow 6.04 L/min; temperature 21.1°C; DO 7.4 mg/L; pH 7.8; total hardness 265 mg/L as CaCO₃; alkalinity 215 mg/L as CaCO₃; total gas pressure 102.08%. Optimum reproductive/hatching temperature ~20°C (Hamman 1981; Marsh 1985).
- **Developmental timeline**: Hatching began at 96 h postfertilization, completed by 129 h; swim-up began day 4 posthatch, completed by day 7.

## Key Findings

1. **Deformity cause identified as handling stress, not nutrition/genetics/environment**: Larvae subjected to repeated physical examination (tranquilization, measurement) during critical fin ray formation stages developed deformities across all diets and all four parental crosses, while unexamined "surplus" siblings reared identically (same trough, same feed, same water) rarely showed deformities.
2. **Growth-survival trade-off across diets**:
   - HEG1 and HEG1+: highest survival (63% and 66%) but least growth (16.0 mm, 15.7 mm TL).
   - B-250: greatest growth (19.1 mm TL) but lowest survival (39%).
   - Diet composition (proximate analysis) was consistent with prior studies (Tyus and Severson 1990; Severson et al. 1992), ruling out diet formulation as the driver of differential survival.
3. **Mortality timing**: Survival was similar across all diet groups up to and immediately following onset of caudal fin ray formation (13–14 mm TL, ~19 d old); mortality diverged sharply afterward, coinciding with deformity onset.
4. **Deformities are stage-specific, not diet-specific**: Deformities appeared consistently at 14–15+ mm TL regardless of diet or genetic cross — implicating a developmental "window of vulnerability" during fin ray formation rather than a nutritional deficiency.
5. **Feeding regime ruled out**: Ad libitum feeding (surplus tray) vs. calculated 10% body weight ration (test trays) was not the cause, since 1993 fish (which developed deformities) were fed similarly to the low-stress surplus tray method.
6. **Historical comparison**: Growth on B-250 (19.1 mm TL) was comparable to live *Artemia salina* nauplii diets reported by Papoulias (1988; 18.8 mm TL), and exceeded prior Encapsulon-based studies (Tyus and Severson 1990; Severson et al. 1992), likely due to a higher daily ration (10% body weight vs. 0.9–5% in earlier work).
7. **Practical recommendation**: To prevent recurrence of deformities in future [[Endangered Species Aquaculture]] efforts, the paper recommends minimizing handling stress via: automatic feeders instead of hand-feeding, hatching jars/aquaria instead of trays/troughs, isolating larvae from human activity, and reducing frequency/intensity of tray-cleaning disturbance — implicating the labor-intensive manual suction-cleaning method used in 1993 as the likely original stressor.
8. **No evidence for gas supersaturation as a general cause**: Isolated gas bubble disease was observed only in Lot D (B-250), temporarily arresting growth, but was not evident in other lots/diets and thus not a generalizable deformity cause.
