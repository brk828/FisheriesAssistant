## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the Arizona Game and Fish Department's 1996 "Wildlife of Special Concern in Arizona" draft catalogs the conservation status of six imperiled freshwater fish taxa native to Arizona and adjoining regions of Sonora, Mexico. Covered taxa include the extirpated [[Yaqui Catfish]], two salmonids of high-elevation streams ([[Apache Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]]), two pupfish subspecies adapted to extreme desert spring habitats ([[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]]), and the historically dominant [[Gila Topminnow]]. All species have suffered severe range contraction driven by a consistent suite of threats: habitat degradation from grazing, erosion, and water diversion; hybridization and competition with nonnative salmonids; and predation/competition from introduced fishes such as [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Mosquitofish]], and nonnative catfishes. The document also references ongoing [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroduction efforts in the Verde and Salt rivers, threatened by altered flow hydrology and cold tailwater releases.

## Background & Description

- **Yaqui Catfish (*Ictalurus pricei*)**: Native ictalurid catfish; capable of hybridizing with nonnative [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Blue Catfish]] (*Ictalurus furcatus*), indicating close congeneric morphology and genetic compatibility.
- **Apache Trout (*Oncorhynchus apache*)**: Salmonid endemic to Arizona headwater streams; morphologically and genetically susceptible to hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*).
- **Gila Trout (*Oncorhynchus gilae*)**: Closely related congener of Apache Trout with a similar life history; also hybridizes readily with nonnative salmonids.
- **Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*)**: Small cyprinodontid; physiologically tolerant of extreme abiotic stress, surviving salinities up to twice that of seawater and temperatures up to 100°F (38°C).
- **Quitobaquito Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*)**: A subspecies of Desert Pupfish, morphologically and ecologically similar, isolated to a single spring/pond system.
- **Gila Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*)**: Small live-bearing poeciliid; ecologically and morphologically convergent with nonnative [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), a factor facilitating competitive displacement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Yaqui Catfish**: Inhabits larger rivers with medium-to-slow current; historically occupied the Yaqui and Casas Grandes basins and Rio Fuerte system, with Arizona range limited to San Bernardino Creek.
- **Apache Trout**: Cold headwater streams of the Salt, San Francisco, and Little Colorado drainages in the White Mountains; spawns March through mid-June depending on elevation; diet of aquatic and terrestrial insects.
- **Gila Trout**: Historically occupied headwaters of the Gila and Verde river drainages, including the upper Verde and possibly Agua Fria River and Eagle Creek; ecology parallels Apache Trout.
- **Desert Pupfish**: Marshes, springs, backwaters, and slow-flowing rivers/streams below ~5,000 ft elevation in the Gila River basin and lower Colorado River; omnivorous diet of invertebrates, aquatic plants, algae, and detritus; extreme environmental tolerance suited to desert spring/marsh ecosystems.
- **Quitobaquito Pupfish**: Single isolated spring/pond ecosystem (Quitobaquito Springs, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument); life history parallels Desert Pupfish.
- **Gila Topminnow**: Historically the most abundant native fish in low-to-mid elevation streams of the Gila River Basin, extending into Sonora's Rio de la Concepcion and Rio Sonora drainages; niche now heavily overlapped and displaced by invasive [[Mosquitofish]].
- **Razorback Sucker** (contextual mention): Riverine species affected by reservoir-altered flow regimes and cold tailwater releases in the Verde and Salt rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Yaqui Catfish**: Extirpated from Arizona; eliminated by overgrazing-driven habitat degradation, erosion, water diversion, and aquifer pumping (late 1800s–early 1900s); a reintroduced population at Monkey Springs was lost when the pond was drained; remains imperiled in Mexico due to habitat modification and hybridization with nonnative catfishes.
- **Apache Trout**: Now restricted to White Mountains streams (upper Salt, Blue, Little Colorado drainages) plus introduced populations in North Canyon, North Kaibab, and Grant Creek (Mount Graham); active recovery program includes habitat renovation and reintroduction.
- **Gila Trout**: Fully extirpated from Arizona; a 1974 reintroduction into Gap Creek (Verde River drainage) has since also failed; future Arizona reintroductions depend on stock from New Mexico's more advanced recovery program.
- **Desert Pupfish**: Extirpated from Arizona in the wild but reintroduced since 1977 into 13 localities (2 outside historic range); currently persists at only one reintroduced natural site; management goal is to maintain a minimum of 55 sites in Arizona.
- **Quitobaquito Pupfish**: Survives in a single natural population; faced major threats from illegal introduction of [[Golden Shiner]] (1960s, required intensive eradication) and [[Black Bullhead]] (1993); also threatened by pesticide wind-drift from Sonora, Mexico.
- **Gila Topminnow**: Persists in 11 natural localities, 7 of which are contaminated by nonnative Mosquitofish; of 180 reintroduction attempts since the early 1960s, only 18 persisted by 1996 — reflecting an extremely low long-term reintroduction success rate.
- **Cross-cutting threat pattern**: Every species account cites nonnative fish introductions (predation, competition, hybridization) alongside anthropogenic hydrological alteration (diversion, aquifer pumping, reservoir operations, drought) as primary drivers of decline.
- **Cross-cutting management pattern**: Recommended actions consistently include habitat protection/enhancement, control or removal of nonnative species, monitoring of extant populations, and expansion via reintroduction into historic or refugium habitats.
