# Ecology and Conservation of Humpback Chub in the Little Colorado River - ASU/NNHP Research Program Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document comprises correspondence and progress reports from [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) and the [[Navajo Natural Heritage Program]] (NNHP) to the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program regarding endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) research in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), Arizona, during 1991. The research team established helicopter-supported base camps at multiple locations along the lower 14 km of the LCR to conduct intensive mark-recapture studies using [[PIT Tags]] (Passive Induced Transponder tags), hoop nets, and trammel nets. Between March and November 1991, researchers completed nine field trips, capturing 4,793 individual chub across multiple size classes. The study aims to quantify adult chub movement patterns, estimate population abundance by season, and track distribution within the LCR system. Preliminary findings suggest adult chub remain in the LCR throughout summer rather than departing after spring reproduction, challenging previous assumptions about their life history.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is an endangered cyprinid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. This research program, conducted under Cooperative Agreement No. 1-FC-40-10490, focuses on the population residing in the [[Little Colorado River]], a tributary of the Colorado River within [[Grand Canyon]]. The study was initiated in response to concerns about the species' conservation status and the potential impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on chub populations.

The research team established three camps along the LCR: a confluence camp at river kilometer (RK) 0.6 near the mainstream [[Colorado River]], Powell Canyon camp at RK 3.0, and Salt Trail Canyon camp at RK 10.75. Each upper camp consisted of two 16×14 canvas wall tents with wooden floors, generators, and equipment to support four researchers (two ASU/NNHP, two USFWS). A 1 km reach around the Sipapu (a [[Hopi]] shrine) at RK 7.6 was excluded from sampling out of respect for religious and cultural sensitivities.

The standardized fish handling protocol involved measuring, weighing, and processing all captured fish. Individuals <100 mm total length (TL) were measured and released. Fish 100-149 mm TL received fin clips (one lobe of caudal plus left or right pectoral fin, depending on capture location). Fish ≥150 mm TL were measured, weighed, sexed, examined for damage or existing tags, scanned for PIT tags, tagged if untagged, and released near the point of capture. Any external tags (Carlin or Floy tags used prior to 1990) were removed and recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Little Colorado River]] provides critical habitat for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], serving as a spawning and rearing area. The LCR's warmer, turbid waters contrast sharply with the cold, clear waters released from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in the mainstem [[Colorado River]]. This thermal difference may influence chub distribution and behavior.

Previous assumptions held that adult chub entered the LCR during spring (late April through early June) in response to environmental cues such as a descending hydrograph, spawned, and then departed by late spring or early summer. The closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963 dramatically altered the thermal regime of the mainstream Colorado River, cooling it year-round and potentially affecting chub life history strategies.

The study area encompasses diverse riverine habitats including pools, runs, and riffles. Sampling revealed differential habitat use, with substantially higher chub densities in upper reaches (Salt camp area, below RK 14) compared to lower reaches (Powell camp area). This disparity suggests greater availability of preferred chub habitat in the upper LCR, despite equivalent sampling effort across reaches.

Non-native species present in the system include [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), which were examined for predation on humpback chub. Stomach content analysis revealed occasional predation, though it remained unclear whether predation occurred before or after net entrapment. The research team implemented daily inspection protocols to remove channel catfish from nets and minimize potential predation.

## Key Findings

**Field Operations (1991):**
- Nine LCR trips completed: two reconnaissance trips (March, May), one camp establishment trip (June), and six data collection trips (July through November)
- Three base camps established with helicopter support along lower 14 km of LCR
- Sampling conducted in three primary reaches: confluence area (1.5 km), Powell Canyon area (6 km), and Salt Trail Canyon area (6 km)

**Capture Summary (Summer 1991):**
- Total chub captured: 4,793 individuals
- Chub <150 mm TL: 2,195 individuals
- Chub ≥150 mm TL: 2,598 individuals
- Capture by reach: Confluence (775), Powell (2,011), Salt (2,007)
- Capture by gear: Hoop nets (4,606), trammel nets (175), angling (6), seine (6)

**Tagging and Recapture Results:**
- New PIT tags deployed: 1,669
- Total recaptures: 899 (34.6% recapture rate)
- New PIT tags recaptured: 523 (20.1% recapture rate)
- Old PIT tags recaptured: 334
- Carlin/Floy tags recaptured: 42
- Iterative recaptures (3+ captures): 110 individuals (12.2% of recaptures)
- Multiple recaptures: 17 individuals captured 5-7 times over 8-week period

**Mortality:**
- Four total mortalities occurred due to initial use of 2.5 cm trammel net mesh
- Zero mortalities after switching to 3.8 cm mesh
- No mortalities from hoop nets or other gear types

**Movement and Residency Patterns:**
- Preliminary results indicate adult chub remain in LCR throughout summer (at least until late September), contrary to previous assumptions of post-spawning departure
- Adult chub appear to remain within specific river areas during summer but move within those areas, primarily during crepuscular and early nocturnal periods
- Extent of movement areas and duration still being elucidated
- Uncertainty remains whether extended summer residency represents evolutionary life-history strategy or recent behavioral response to cooling of mainstream Colorado River following Glen Canyon Dam closure (1963)

**Habitat Distribution:**
- Upper reaches (Salt camp area) yielded approximately twice as many new PIT tags as lower reaches (Powell camp) despite equivalent sampling effort
- Disparity suggests greater abundance of preferred chub habitat in upper LCR below RK 14
- Speculation exists that upper-reach chub may be year-round residents

**Protocol Modifications:**
- Research team requested modification to run selected hoop nets at 72-hour intervals rather than 12-hour intervals
- Justification: (1) reduced physical demands on field personnel, (2) increased data quality and quantity
- Evidence supporting modification: fish showed no damage when held 12-72 hours; many fish recaptured multiple times at same sites, indicating minimal trauma from net confinement
- Hoop nets create "quiet pocket water" that fish appear to tolerate or even prefer

**Research Questions for Future Investigation:**
- Will chub exit LCR during winter or become dormant in deeper pools while remaining resident?
- Will spring catch increases result from immigration from mainstream Colorado River or increased activity of resident population as temperatures rise?
- What environmental cues trigger chub movements into and within the LCR?
- What is the relationship between habitat characteristics and chub density in different LCR reaches?

The research program demonstrates successful establishment of a comprehensive mark-recapture database for [[Humpback Chub]] in the [[Little Colorado River]], with high recapture rates indicating effective sampling protocols and minimal impact on the endangered population. The unexpected finding of extended summer residency challenges existing understanding of chub life history and has implications for management of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations and endangered species conservation in the [[Colorado River]] system.
