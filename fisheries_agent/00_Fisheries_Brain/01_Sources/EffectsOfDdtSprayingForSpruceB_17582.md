# Effects of DDT Spraying for Spruce Budworm on Fish in the Yellowstone River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by Oliver B. Cope, of the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, examines the effects of aerial [[DDT]] spraying against [[Spruce Budworm]] in the [[Yellowstone River]] drainage during July 1957. The spray covered 71,678 acres at 1 pound per acre. The study addressed a suspected link to widespread fish die-offs observed in 1955, for which no fish studies had been made at the time. In 1957 the study measured invertebrate abundance, drift, water and vegetation residues, and fish tissue residues over 1957–1959.

Key results: no acute fish mortality attributable to DDT was found, but DDT residues were detected in every fish sample analysed, and invertebrate communities were reduced and altered.

## Background & Description

The 1955 die-off involved [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Longnose Sucker]] in the Yellowstone River below [[Yellowstone National Park]], with thousands of dead fish observed from October to December. Earlier invertebrate collections had documented sharp declines after spraying. The 1957 program, planned by the National Park Service and Forest Service, offered an opportunity for fish and fish-food studies. Funding came from the National Park Service, Forest Service, and Fish and Wildlife Service, with chemical analyses by Montana State College and the Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation.

The spray area lay in the northern part of Yellowstone National Park and extended about 65 miles downstream outside the park. Terrain was mountainous, with tributaries such as the [[Lamar River]] and [[Soda Butte Creek]] draining high country. The fish fauna of the Lamar comprised cutthroat trout, longnose sucker, and longnose dace, with some rainbow trout and mountain whitefish. The Yellowstone River held the same species plus brown trout.

The spray operation ran from July 10 to 21, 1957, over 26 plots. Application was by aircraft in oil solution at 1 lb/acre, with an average of 0.18 lb/acre reaching the ground on six plots, measured by spray cards. Average application was 0.93 lb/acre across the schedule, with 66,671 acres recorded as the sprayed total in the schedule table.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water in the study streams was generally cold, slightly alkaline (pH about 7.2–8.0), and free of phenolphthalein alkalinity. Turbidity ranged from low to high. Tributaries were steep and fast-flowing, with turbulent mixing even at low water. The Yellowstone supported more aquatic vegetation and richer invertebrate fauna than the Lamar. Some tributaries were rich in invertebrate fish food, and small lakes held cutthroat and rainbow trout among abundant rooted vegetation.

Pelican Creek, a tributary to Yellowstone Lake and separated from the spray area by impassable falls, was used as an apparently untreated control. Its cutthroat trout nevertheless contained DDT, so no clean reference population was established.

## Key Findings

- **Fish mortality:** No mortality attributable to DDT was found in the limited observations. Thousands of dead fish had been seen in 1955, but the cause was not confirmed here.
- **Fish residues:** All 80 fish samples (mountain whitefish, rainbow, cutthroat and brown trout) contained DDT, up to 14.0 p.p.m. in trout. Of 57 samples tested for DDE, all but four contained it, up to 6.53 p.p.m.
- **Highest trout values:** Cutthroat trout from Cache Creek reached 14.0 p.p.m. DDT, and Lamar River cutthroat reached 11.0 p.p.m. on August 14, 1957.
- **Downstream residues:** DDT was found in trout about 85 stream miles below the spray area (near Livingston) and about 55 miles below (at Emigrant). Fish taken more than 2 years after spraying still contained DDT.
- **Control fish:** Pelican Creek cutthroat trout (June 1958, n = 6) all contained DDT, confirmed by fly bioassay on three fish. The source was not identified.
- **Water residues:** DDT reached up to 0.03 p.p.m. in several samples, typically as brief pulses. Trout Lake outlet held 0.50 p.p.m., suggesting the lake acted as a reservoir. A trace was found 55 miles downstream at Emigrant. Yellowstone at Corwin Springs had DDT on only 2 of 9 sampling days.
- **Vegetation residues:** Potamogeton samples contained up to 2.3 p.p.m. DDT, consistently higher than co-located water samples.
- **Invertebrates:** Bottom invertebrate numbers fell sharply after spraying. Tower Creek recovered to near pre-spray numbers by mid-1958. Soda Butte Creek showed no insects one week after spraying, and total numbers were similar a year later but with altered composition. Slough Creek and the Lamar River had reductions that persisted into 1958.
- **Drift:** In Tower Creek, drifting invertebrates rose from 85 pre-spray to 613 by 7:30 a.m. on July 14 after the second spray, indicating dead and dying organisms in the current.
- **Mechanisms:** The author concluded that DDT moved downstream in pulses over hours rather than lingering, and that fish probably accumulated DDT through food, including contaminated invertebrates and vegetation, at concentrations well above those in water.
- **Comparison:** Residues in Yellowstone fish were considered comparable to those reported by Graham and Scott (1959) from the Ruby River, Montana (1.30–5.80 p.p.m. in trout).
