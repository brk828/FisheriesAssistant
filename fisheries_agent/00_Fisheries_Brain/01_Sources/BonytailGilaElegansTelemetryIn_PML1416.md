# Bonytail Telemetry in Lake Mohave and Native Fish Rearing Ponds in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This two-part document covers conservation work on endangered native fishes of the [[Colorado River]] system. The first part is a short-term [[Sonic Telemetry]] study of [[Bonytail]] ([[Gila Elegans]]) in [[Lake Mohave]], the reservoir where the largest remaining bonytail population persists. The study tracked tagged fish for about three months to measure movement patterns. The second part is a project report on a new facility to raise native fish, mainly suckers, in ponds on the [[Colorado River Indian Reservation]] near Parker, Arizona. The source is bilingual (English and Spanish). Its English abstracts are partly garbled by OCR, so details below are limited to what can be read reliably.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail]] is a large-river minnow (family Cyprinidae) endemic to the Colorado River system of western North America. It is federally listed as endangered. The source states that its biology is poorly understood and that nothing was known about its movements before this study.

The Spanish abstract names "matalote jorobado" among the native fishes to be raised. This is the common Spanish name for the [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus), so suckers are likely the species involved, although the English text is too degraded to confirm the exact species list.

The rearing project followed a renovation of about twenty acres of ponds. Work began on 22 July 1996, and the project was developed under a Memorandum of Understanding with the [[Colorado River Indian Reservation]] tribes, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and the [[Bureau of Land Management]]. The source credits multiple private, state, and federal agencies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave is a Colorado River reservoir on the Arizona–Nevada border. It is the main stronghold of wild bonytail. Tagged fish mostly used shallow shoreline areas, and some were found within flooded stands of [[Riparian Vegetation]]. The source links this habitat use to the bonytail's conservation needs.

The rearing ponds are part of efforts to prevent further loss of native fishes in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] while recovery work proceeds. The source notes that the ponds were cleared of non-native vegetation, including saltcedar and cola de gato (cat's-claw), to restore habitat before stocking. The English text says six ponds had been cleared or renovated to date, with the rest still being prepared. It also mentions fish stocking planned for 1 December; the year is garbled but is most likely 1996.

## Key Findings

**Telemetry study (Lake Mohave, May to August 1996)**

- **Sample:** 10 fish were implanted with 90-day sonic tags in May 1996. Five were "wild" fish from the lake and five were "cove" fish from a hatchery, with five of each sex. Fish were released immediately after surgery and tracked weekly through August.
- **Dispersal:** Eight fish dispersed immediately after release. Two moved up-lake and six moved down-lake. Two fish were never relocated after release.
- **Wild fish:** Five wild fish moved down-lake as much as 42 km. Four of these (all male) stayed in the lowest 5 km of the reservoir. The fifth (a female) occupied an area about 10 km below the release site.
- **Hatchery ("cove") fish:** Two moved up-lake, 15 km and 43 km. A third moved 4 km down-lake.
- **Habitat use:** Most locations were in shallow shoreline water, sometimes within inundated riparian vegetation.
- **Movement behavior:** Bonytail can move tens of kilometres in a few days but may remain in favored areas for weeks.
- **Capture method:** Unmarked fish were captured by setting nets in areas used by tagged fish. The authors consider this significant for monitoring reintroductions, locating spawning, studying habitat use, and obtaining [[Brood Stock]] for a critically imperiled species.

**Native fish rearing facility (Colorado River Indian Reservation)**

- A facility was developed to raise native fish, mainly suckers, in renovated ponds.
- Six ponds were cleared or renovated to date, with more under preparation.
- The facility's purpose is to rear fish to about 12 inches before release into Colorado River waters.
- The report describes the project as a cooperative effort aimed at preventing extinction of native fishes in the lower basin and supporting recovery.

**Publication context:** The telemetry study appears in the proceedings of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (1996 symposium). The rearing report is a companion item in the same proceedings. The source gives no further numerical results for the rearing project.
