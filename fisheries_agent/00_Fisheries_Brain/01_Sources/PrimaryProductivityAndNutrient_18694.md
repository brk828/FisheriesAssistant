# Primary Productivity and Nutrient Balance in a Lower Colorado River Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1982 study by [[John C. Priscu]], [[Jacob Verduin]] and [[James E. Deacon]] examines phytoplankton primary productivity and nutrient budgets in [[Lake Mohave]], a 115.3 km² warm-monomictic reservoir on the [[Lower Colorado River]] below [[Lake Mead]] and [[Hoover Dam]]. Sampling at five stations from October 1976 to September 1977 showed strong spatial and temporal variation in productivity, driven by nutrient-rich hypolimnetic water released from Lake Mead. Less than 5% of total nitrogen and phosphorus loads were retained, and hypolimnetic discharge at [[Davis Dam]] was the dominant regulator of the nutrient balance.

The document is a full research article (with an German-language abstract, "Zusammenfassung"), not a fish study. It contains no fish data; the biology described concerns phytoplankton and water chemistry.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** Lake Mohave is a reservoir filled in 1953. It receives essentially all its water from about 61 m depth in Lake Mead, and it was built as a buffer for highly variable discharges from Lake Mead.
- **Inflow:** Hypolimnetic inflow is high in dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus, low in phytoplankton, and relatively uniform at about 13 °C. Hoover Dam hydroelectric demands make inflows highly variable daily and seasonally.
- **Outflow:** Hypolimnetic outflow (about 40 m) at Davis Dam fluctuates with power production, causing seasonal changes in reservoir elevation. Watershed precipitation is below 13 cm/yr, so edaphic nutrient inputs are small.
- **Sampling design:** Four reservoir stations (M1–M4) and one Colorado River station (M5) were sampled monthly or semi-monthly. Methods included the ¹⁴C method for productivity, nutrient analyses, chlorophyll-a, phytoplankton enumeration, Hydrolab profiling, and transmissometry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Productivity:** Primary productivity was highest just below the river underflow zone, averaging 3474 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹. The rest of the reservoir averaged about 1700 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹, and the reservoir mean was 1770 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹. The authors describe Lake Mohave as among the most productive freshwater systems studied.

**Hydrodynamics:** During stratification, cold river water underflows the lake epilimnion. Shear instability at the plunge point generates intense turbulent mixing (eddy diffusion coefficient 38 cm² s⁻¹), which supplies nutrients to the zone just below the plunge point.

**Nutrient inputs:** Nutrient loading was high (total-N = 19.37 × 10³ kg day⁻¹; total-P = 725 kg day⁻¹).

**Phytoplankton succession:** Winter communities were dominated by [[Cryptomonas erosa]] and [[Cyclotella meneghiniana]]. Spring diatom pulses of [[Fragilaria crotonensis]] were followed by early summer dominance of the cyanophyte [[Gomphosphaeria lacustris]], then [[Synedra delicatissima]] through summer and fall. Upper stations showed distinct river-influenced assemblages, including [[Phormidium]] sp. and [[Navicula tripunctata]]. A bloom of [[Chlamydomonas globosa]] reached 21.0 × 10² org ml⁻¹ at M4 in May.

**Nutrient limitation:** Dissolved inorganic nitrogen to dissolved phosphorus (DIN:DP) ratios indicated nitrogen limitation in summer and phosphorus limitation in winter. Blue-green dominance occurred when N:P fell below about 10, and diatom dominance when it exceeded 10.

## Key Findings

1. **Hypolimnetic loading drives productivity.** Water from the Lake Mead hypolimnion supplied nutrients that produced high productivity, especially at M4 and just below the underflow. At M4 productivity varied almost 100-fold between winter and summer.

2. **Discharge controls interface position.** Once stratification developed, the interface between river and lake was best predicted by Hoover Dam discharge (r² = 0.69, n = 14, p < 0.01). Adding lake elevation did not significantly improve the fit.

3. **Discharge controls biomass.** Chlorophyll-a maxima at M4 were significantly correlated with the previous month's Hoover Dam discharge (r² = 0.61, n = 8, p < 0.05). The authors attribute this to dilution followed by nutrient-driven growth.

4. **Low nutrient retention.** Less than 5% of nutrients were retained. Total-P retention was about 1.9% (14 kg day⁻¹ of 725 kg day⁻¹). Dissolved P was transformed to particulate form and lost via the hypolimnetic penstocks at Davis Dam.

5. **Nitrogen transformation.** A net DIN gain of 2.33 × 10³ kg day⁻¹ was nearly balanced by a TON loss of 1.84 × 10³ kg day⁻¹. This indicates that DIN is converted to organic nitrogen before discharge.

6. **Flow management matters.** Normalizing Davis Dam losses to mean Hoover Dam discharge (324 m³ s⁻¹) increased retention of all fractions. The authors conclude that reservoir elevation management strongly affects nutrient balance.

7. **Davis Dam is the key regulator.** Multiple regression showed that only DIN was significantly correlated with the independent variables (R² = 0.97, F = 34.4, p < 0.01). Davis Dam discharge had the largest standardized partial coefficient (−1.39).

8. **Bioassays confirm phosphorus and dilution effects.** Enrichment bioassays at M4 (May) and M3 (August) showed that phosphorus addition stimulated growth. River water addition initially diluted growth, then enhanced it after 234 hours.

9. **Management implications.** The authors argue that deep-water discharge reduces nutrient retention, consistent with [[Wright Hypothesis]] on deep outflow. They call for biogeochemical knowledge in multi-purpose reservoir management, balancing productivity with water quality for fisheries, power, irrigation and drinking water.
