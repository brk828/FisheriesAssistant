# Colorado Squawfish Predation on Channel Catfish, Roundtail Chub Larval Growth, and Painted Lady Migration Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Southwestern Naturalist note (vol. 30, no. 1, 1985) contains three separate contributions. The first describes larval growth and development in [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila*) and compares it with [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. The second is a laboratory feeding experiment testing whether endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) prey on and choke on [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), with supporting field records from the Green River, Utah. The third is a brief set of field observations of a September painted lady (*Vanessa cardui*) migration in Yellowstone National Park. The fish-related sections are the core of the note.

## Background & Description

**Larval roundtail chub.** Larvae were measured to the nearest 0.1 mm and assigned to developmental phases following Snyder (1976). The authors note that identifying young-of-the-year roundtail chub, humpback chub and bonytail is a persistent problem for biologists in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Reliable discriminating characters were needed before larval and early juvenile *Gila* could be used for population monitoring and life-history studies. The developmental series was intended as a component of ongoing taxonomic work by the Colorado Division of Wildlife and Colorado State University.

**Colorado squawfish.** The Colorado squawfish is the largest cyprinid native to North America and is endemic to the Colorado River basin. It declined after major water development and is listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Federal Register, 1974). Historically it preyed on soft-rayed fishes. Channel catfish were introduced to the Colorado River system early in the twentieth century as a sport fish. Earlier anecdotal reports of squawfish dead with catfish lodged in the esophagus had been published, but only one documented case existed before this study (McAda 1983: an adult of 550 mm TL with a 120 mm TL catfish lodged head-first behind the gill arches).

**Study objectives.** The laboratory experiment asked whether adult squawfish would prey on channel catfish without prior conditioning, whether catfish could be swallowed or tended to lodge in the esophagus, and whether squawfish were injured while attempting to eat catfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing and holding conditions.** Squawfish were hatched in 1974 and reared at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Sixty individuals (360 to approximately 512 mm TL, mean 444 mm, SD 29 mm; the upper bound is partly illegible in the scan) were used. Squawfish were fed pelleted commercial trout feed or [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) on alternating days. Channel catfish were hatched in June 1982 at a commercial hatchery in southern Idaho. Catfish ranged from 51 to 125 mm TL (mean 77 mm, SD 10.5 mm) at the time of testing. Both species were held at 15 °C under a 14L:10D photoperiod in dechlorinated Logan City water.

**Feeding arena.** Trials were conducted in a flow-through circular tank. Squawfish were tested singly at first, then in groups, because individuals appeared reluctant to feed when isolated. Spines of catfish were clipped in some trials (CCSPL, catfish with spines removed) and left intact in others (CCSP). Catfish were anesthetized with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222) for measurement and spine clipping, then allowed to recover. Squawfish were tranquilized and examined after trials to check for lodged prey.

**Post-trial monitoring.** Following feeding trials, squawfish were segregated into three groups for long-term observation over two months: (1) fish that had eaten CCSPL but not CCSP, (2) fish that had eaten CCSP, and (3) fish that had eaten neither. No disease was evident in any fish. Behavior and appetite did not differ among the groups.

## Key Findings

### Roundtail Chub Larval Growth
- Larval period: 54 days.
- Mean daily growth: 0.3 mm (range 0.0 to 0.7 mm per day).
- Mean daily growth by phase: 0.2 mm in mesolarvae, 0.4 mm in prolarvae.
- Comparison: 0.5 to 0.6 mm per day in hatchery-reared larval humpback chub and bonytail, respectively.
- Culture temperatures: 12.9 to 25.5 °C (Hamman 1982a, b).
- Total length at selected developmental events correlated closely with Snyder's (1976) field-collected roundtail chub data.

### Colorado Squawfish Feeding Behavior
- Squawfish consumed considerably fewer spineless channel catfish (CCSPL) than trout.
- Catfish with intact spines (CCSP) were eaten more often than catfish with spines removed; spineless catfish were still eaten far less often than trout.
- Two CCSP individuals were eaten across the trials (630 hours of exposure).
- Squawfish usually seized catfish by the head and spent several minutes "mouthing" the water to free spines. CCSPL were eventually eaten, but only after several attempts.
- Trout were the most-preferred prey. Trout of 90 mm length were significantly larger than catfish of 77 mm (P = 0.01), though body lengths of prey actually eaten and not eaten were similar.
- Squawfish eating catfish showed no detectable change in behavior or appetite afterward, and no injuries were reported in the surviving fish held for observation.

### Interpretation of Prey Choice
- Authors suggest several reasons for the low catfish utilization: spine-clipping may still feel harsh to squawfish; catfish formed dense schools along the tank bottom, whereas trout formed loose surface schools that scattered quickly when a predator approached.
- Squawfish may simply prefer other prey, or may have been conditioned to trout through years of feeding on them. Prior conditioning did not affect prey preference in a comparable study (Reist 1971).
- Squawfish were classified by Moyle (1976) as roving predators. The authors suggest that a single fish near the surface may appear more attractive than a group of bottom-dwelling fish.

### Field Records
- Three squawfish captured live on the Green River, Utah, in 1982 and 1985 had channel catfish lodged in their throats.
- One fish, captured 15 July 1982 at Km 235, had a catfish lodged in its throat and was released after removal.
- A second fish, approximately 595 mm long (OCR-uncertain), was choking on a 135 mm channel catfish; both survivors were released in apparent good health.
- Authors note that the lodged catfish in nature were relatively large, and the fish were likely more vulnerable to predators while impaired.
- A similar case involved a Lepomis species (suckerfish-related species) dying after attempting to swallow an oversized fish; it was cited to show that oversized prey can make a predator vulnerable.

### Conclusions on Squawfish Predation
- With only eight attempts in the laboratory, the data were judged insufficient to determine the frequency of choking.
- Nonetheless, the authors conclude that Colorado squawfish will prey on channel catfish, will feed on other fish species when catfish are unavailable, and that channel catfish lodgement is a real, if uncommon, source of mortality that may affect the endangered species.

### Painted Lady Migration Observations
- Observations were made in early to mid-September in Yellowstone National Park and nearby areas, with migrating painted ladies moving generally south.
- Numbers varied greatly from day to day. On some days, large numbers (estimated in the hundreds per minute) passed; on others, few or none were seen.
- The movement was considered part of a regional migratory event, linked to a large wave of painted ladies crossing the park.
