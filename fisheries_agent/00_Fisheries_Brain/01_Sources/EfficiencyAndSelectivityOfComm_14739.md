# Efficiency and Selectivity of Commercial Fishing Devices Used on the Mississippi River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #commercial-fisheries #Ictaluridae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Volume 26, Article 4), by William C. Starrett and Paul G. Barnickol, statistically evaluates the efficiency and selectivity of commercial fishing gear used on the [[Mississippi River]] between Caruthersville, Missouri, and Dubuque, Iowa. Data came from field collections at 31 stations in 1944 and 1946 under the [[Technical Committee for Fisheries]]. The paper concludes that gear type, mesh size and fishing site all shape catch composition, and that mesh size can be used to control the size of fish taken, which would reduce the labor of measuring catches under minimum-size laws.

## Background & Description

- **Survey context:** A 1944 and 1946 fishery survey was financed by the Illinois Department of Conservation, the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]], the Iowa Conservation Commission and the Missouri Conservation Commission. The Natural History Survey laboratory boat was used as field headquarters.
- **Prior work:** The survey built on [[Barnickol and Starrett 1951]], a report on the status of commercial and sport fishing in the section. At the time, law enforcement was the chief form of fish management in the river section.
- **Study area:** The 689-mile section was split into the MR-C section (Missouri River mouth to Caruthersville, 306 miles; free-flowing, more turbid and swifter) and the D-MR section (Dubuque to the Missouri River mouth, 383 miles; canalized by 14 locks and dams).
- **Field stations:** 19 stations in 1944 (Illinois–Missouri) and 12 in 1946 (Illinois–Iowa), listed in Table 1 with dates, pool numbers and river distances.
- **Gear terminology:** Gear was classified following the [[Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee]] (1946) into four capture methods: angling, encompassment, entrapment and entanglement.
- **Mesh convention:** Mesh size is given as square measure in inches; stretch measure is roughly twice the square measure.
- **Species list:** Table 2 gives accepted common, scientific and local names for the fishes recorded. Its full checklist is not reproduced here.
- **Commercial fishery scale:** In 1950, 248 Illinois residents worked as professional commercial fishermen on the Mississippi, landing 2,788,073 pounds valued at $297,045.23. Missouri's 590 licensed fishermen took 330,488 pounds, and Iowa's 2,200 licensed fishermen took 839,211 pounds.
- **Key commercial species:** carp, black buffalo, smallmouth buffalo, bigmouth buffalo, channel catfish, flathead catfish, blue catfish, freshwater drum, paddlefish and shovelnose sturgeon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat differences:** The MR-C section is turbid and swift, with no locks or dams. The D-MR section is canalized, and sport fishes were more numerous there in test-net collections. Bigmouth buffalo were more common in the D-MR section, while small- and black buffalo dominated the MR-C section.
- **Catfish distribution:** Blue catfish reached upstream to Lock and Dam No. 19 at Keokuk, Iowa, and were more numerous below the Missouri River mouth. Flathead catfish were common in both sections.
- **Habitat-linked gear use:** Hoop nets fish deeper, current-swept water, while wing nets fish quiet backwaters and sloughs. Backwaters and sloughs of the canalized upper river held more abundant sport fish populations than the main channel.
- **Seasonal and winter use:** In the D-MR section, large carp and buffalo hauls were taken under ice in winter. Floated trammel nets are limited to the upper ends of pools with enough current.
- **Sport fish concerns:** Anglers suspected commercial fishing for sport fish declines. The authors argue that environmental change over the past century is a more plausible cause.

## Key Findings

**Methods and statistics**
- Efficiency was measured as fish or pounds per net-day (one net fished 24 hours). Chi-square tests (p = 0.05, 1 df) assessed catch differences. t-tests assessed length differences.
- The 1-inch-mesh wing net without a lead was the only entrapment device fished at all stations, so it served as the standard for comparison.

**Gear results**
- **Seines:** 25 hauls with 1-inch-mesh seines (100, 150 and 200 yards) between Winfield, Missouri, and Cordova, Illinois. Commercial and predatory fishes made up 89.3% of total weight. Sport fishes were 11.7% of numbers and 3.3% of weight, with crappies 82.1% of sport fish.
- **Basket traps:** 74 sets in 1944 (74 net-days). Catfish were 80.8% of 73 fish, with channel catfish 54.2% of numbers. No sport fish was taken. Basket traps were among the most selective devices tested. The flathead catfish catch was significantly higher in 1-inch wing nets (chi-square 26.85).
- **1-inch wing nets with and without leads:** Leads increased crappie and bluegill catches of desirable size. Black crappies were slightly but significantly larger in nets without leads.
- **1 1/4-inch vs. 1-inch wing nets (1944, 156.10 vs. 508.12 net-days):** Commercial-sized carp were significantly more numerous in the 1 1/4-inch nets (chi-square 21.09). Commercial-sized carp averaged about 15 inches in both nets, and the 1 1/4-inch mean was significantly larger (t = 2.908, 561 df).
- **2 1/2-inch wing nets vs. 1-inch wing nets (1946):** The 2 1/2-inch nets caught more commercial-sized carp and buffalo per net-day. Sport fishes were only 2.4% (1944) and 1.9% (1946) of the catch. The 1-inch nets were far more efficient for catfish, and the 2 1/2-inch nets had few sport fish.
- **Hoop nets vs. wing nets (1-inch, 1946):** Wing nets caught more commercial-sized carp and more usable bluegill and crappie. Sport fishes were 53.4% of the wing-net catch against 10.4% in hoop nets.
- **2 1/2-inch hoop nets vs. 2 1/2-inch wing nets (1946):** Hoop nets were most efficient for commercial-sized freshwater drum (1946) and flathead catfish. Hoop nets caught fewer commercial-sized bigmouth buffalo than wing nets.
- **Trap nets (1 1/4-inch):** Fished at three stations for 50.53 net-days, too few for firm conclusions.
- **Trammel net sets:** 116 sets (1944 and 1946) were dominated by carp and buffalo (commercial fishes). Only three channel catfish and two flathead catfish were netted, so the method is inefficient for catfish. Sport fishes were 17.3% of the catch, and 84.6% of those were crappies.
- **Selective trammel netting (table 25):** Single-species sets of carp and buffalo, with almost no sport fish.
- **Experimental trammel net at Grafton (October 1944, 27 sets):** The 2-inch inner-net section gave the highest commercial catch, while 1 1/4-inch inner nets caught 3.7 times as many sport fishes. Crappies averaged 9.3 inches in the 1 1/4-inch section.
- **Gilling:** Gilling was highest among buffalo (up to 38.5% in the 2-inch section). Crappies gilled at 15.1% (1 1/4-inch) and 30.8% (2-inch) for black crappies.
- **Trammel net floats (108 floats):** Mainly shovelnose sturgeon (67.9%) and freshwater drum; only five sport fishes (saugers). Floats were the only method that took sturgeon in numbers.
- **Experimental floats at Quincy (November 1944, 33 floats):** Shovelnose sturgeon made up 51.3% of numbers. Gilling was 4.2% (1 1/4-inch) and 5.6% (2-inch).

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Gear type, mesh size and site all shape species composition, so gear can be chosen selectively for target species.
- Mesh size can control the size of fish in commercial catches, removing the need to measure each fish for minimum-size laws. Missouri had dropped size limits for all commercial fish except catfish, while Illinois and Iowa retained them.
- Commercial fishing of legal-mesh gear is unlikely to deplete the sport fishery. The authors cite [[Bennett 1948]], where heavy cropping of a 1.38-acre lake with illegal small-mesh wing nets failed to reduce the sport fish population.
- The authors call for management laws that are regularly reviewed as science changes, not treated as permanent.
