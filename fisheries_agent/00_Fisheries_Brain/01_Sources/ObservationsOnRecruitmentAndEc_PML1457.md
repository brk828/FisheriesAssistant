# Razorback Sucker Recruitment and Ecology in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Marsh and Minckley (1989) examines whether the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is recruiting naturally in the lower [[Colorado River]] downstream of [[Davis Dam]]. Adults persist in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]], but young fish are scarce. The authors separate wild-produced juveniles and larvae from those derived from hatchery reintroductions (begun in the late 1970s and substantially from spring 1986), using collection records from 1962 to 1988. They conclude that the wild juvenile captures provide convincing evidence of potential natural recruitment, but that most young fish found since 1980 were of hatchery origin or came from irrigation canals, where they are vulnerable to loss.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The razorback sucker is an endemic fish of the Colorado River basin of western North America. It was once abundant but now occurs naturally in only a few places.
- **Upstream populations:** Those above the Grand Canyon are small and apparently declining.
- **Downstream population:** A substantial remnant persists only above Davis Dam in Lake Mohave. This stock consists mainly of old individuals that, despite annual reproduction, apparently failed to recruit for nearly four decades.
- **Historical status:** In the 1950s and earlier, the species was a reproducing population in the river below Davis Dam, in Lake Havasu, and farther downstream. It is now rare or sporadic in those reaches.
- **Reintroduction programs:** [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[California Department of Fish and Game]] began reintroduction programs. Substantial stockings of hatchery fish into the mainstream began in spring 1986, after a few earlier experimental releases.
- **Study rationale:** Larvae or juveniles collected before reintroductions, or in places inaccessible to stocked fish, represent natural production. Comparing collection sites before and after reintroduction shows that wild and hatchery young distribute themselves similarly.
- **Agencies:** The text uses acronyms for agencies including [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Funding came from USFWS, USBR, and Arizona State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Mohave**
- Adults are common, comprising an average of roughly 25% of fishes in annual trammel net samples from 1975 to 1988. (The scanned value is partly garbled.)
- Larvae (<15 mm TL) are seasonally abundant in the littoral zone, where 10 or more can be attracted to a light in a few minutes at night. They rarely occur in open water.
- Between the larval and adult stages, size classes are essentially absent despite intense sampling.
- Four juveniles (33 to 54 mm TL) collected in July 1987 are the only naturally produced juveniles recorded from Lake Mohave since the 1950s.

**Lake Havasu and riverine reaches**
- Larvae are rare. Razorback suckers made up 0.56% of 6,617 larval specimens caught in tow-net samples in Lake Havasu and upstream riverine reaches in 1985 and 1986 (8 fish in 1985 and 29 in 1986).
- No larvae were found in shoreline surveys in Lake Havasu in 1988.
- Abundances were similar in riverine and reservoir habitats, and no larval concentration areas were evident.

**Irrigation canals and drains**
- Canals such as the [[Central Arizona Project]] [[Granite Reef Aqueduct]] (withdrawing water from the [[Bill Williams Arm]] of Lake Havasu since 1983) and other diversion canals capture larvae and juveniles.
- Canal captures are a major source of wild juveniles. Canal habitats are unstable: they are dewatered for cleaning and repair, and fish are often killed by mechanical removal and chemicals.
- Drains in the Imperial Valley may also hold razorback suckers. Their slow, vegetated water and seasonal change in water quality can support populations, but they are less permanent than canals.

**Predation and competition**
- Nonnative predators such as [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and ictalurid catfish are present. These predators may limit juvenile survival, though the authors note that juveniles may be too small for some predators.

## Key Findings

**1. Adult population and records**
- Only 42 adult razorback suckers have confirmed records since 1962 from the lower Colorado River mainstream and associated habitats downstream of Davis Dam (excluding [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], California).
- These comprise 16 from Lake Havasu, 23 from riverine reaches (including [[Laughlin Lagoon]], Nevada), and 3 from the Central Arizona Project canal (October 1986).
- Adult numbers are not evidently lower in the 1980s than in the 1960s. Low adult mortality and long life (at least 44 years) probably explain this.
- Otolith analysis showed two canal adults, about 25 years old, that originated from Lake Havasu.

**2. Wild juvenile recruitment**
- Twenty-four juveniles (about 15 to 37.1 cm TL) were captured in recent years at sizes not otherwise known from Lake Mohave or elsewhere in the system. Most came from canals.
- Only one juvenile was taken from a natural (non-canal) habitat in the 1980s.
- The authors conclude that these juveniles provide convincing evidence of potential natural recruitment to the population. Their age, size, and location place them outside the hatchery release zones.
- Two larvae (about 15 mm TL) were among 5,036 larvae sampled in the CAP canal in 1987.

**3. Reintroduction results**
- Stocked fish have grown rapidly; one report gives growth to about 25 cm in six months (unpublished data).
- Some hatchery-produced fish were recaptured in the lower river, and a few reproduced in isolated ponds or reservoirs. The authors judge that reintroduced fish have contributed to subsequent recruitment in some areas.
- Juvenile distribution of wild and hatchery fish is similar, so canal captures cannot easily separate the two origins.

**4. Recommendations and research needs**
- **Marking:** Reintroduced fish should be marked so that wild and stocked fish can be distinguished.
- **Sampling:** Absence of recruits may partly reflect inadequate sampling of drains, backwaters, and other habitats.
- **Canal management:** Canal habitat is unfortunately unstable. The authors recommend timing dewatering and mechanical removal to minimize losses of young fish.
- **Predator control:** Nonnative predators should be controlled or managed to protect young razorback suckers.
- **Population monitoring:** Larval and juvenile surveys should continue to determine whether natural recruitment becomes self-sustaining.
