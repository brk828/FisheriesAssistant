# Quantitative Survey of the Bottom Fauna of Lake Wawasee (Turkey Lake)

#benthos #limnology #freshwater-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1928 Indiana Division of Fish and Game publication (Publication No. 77) reports a quantitative survey of the benthic invertebrates of [[Lake Wawasee]] (Turkey Lake), Kosciusko County, Indiana. The work was based on two summers of field collections in 1926, carried out to support the state's broader study of lake productivity. The authors estimate that the lake bottom holds slightly more than 18 billion invertebrates, with a total wet weight of 2,028,961 lbs and roughly 182,606 lbs of dry organic matter. The fauna is grouped into littoral (0-2 m and 2-4 m) and deep-water zones, and results are reported by weight per acre and by number per square meter. Fish are not studied directly; the fauna is discussed mainly as food for fish. The author line on the title page is partly garbled in the scan.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The study was initiated when the state provided equipment, field and laboratory space, and staff to investigate how lake productivity relates to the bottom fauna. The authors state that fish growth can be read more accurately when the food supply is known, so the benthos was measured quantitatively.

**Study lake.** Lake Wawasee was chosen because it is the largest natural lake in Indiana and because the authors had access to a field base there. The lake lies in the St. Lawrence drainage basin, near the continental divide, and has a long axis of roughly 2.8 miles (length value partly garbled in scan). Its basin is irregular, with several bays and promontories; the bays are cited as examples of wave-eroded versus sheltered shores (Plate I).

**Morphometry.** The lake has a central basin with a maximum depth reported in the text as roughly 20-25 m. Slope was calculated as the average change in depth per 100 ft of horizontal distance, using a formula given in the paper (S = (C1 + C2) × I / A, where C are contour lengths, I is contour interval and A is area between contours). The authors note that the lake's gentle slope between 2 m and 4 m contains a large share of the lake's area.

**Sampling design.**
- Littoral zone (0-2 m): hoe dredge and a homemade sample cutter, with stations placed according to habitat variation rather than a grid. Two series of collections, each taking about six weeks, were made in the summer of 1926.
- Deep water (below 2 m): Foerst-Ekman dredge, with two catches per station. Collections were made along transect lines on the odd-numbered contours.
- Samples were washed through a No. 40 silk net, sorted and counted. Biomass was determined by wet weight, then dry weight after drying at 100 °C, and ash-free dry organic matter after ignition at about 550 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Zonation.** The authors divide the lake into three benthic zones:
- **Open littoral (S-2 m):** exposed to waves and shore currents; partly barren sand and marl, with patches of Chara. Area 568 acres.
- **Weedy littoral (2-4 m):** densely covered with submerged vascular plants, mostly Potamogeton. Described as very productive even when plants are excluded. Area about 923 acres (928 acres in one passage).
- **Deep water (4 m to bottom):** area about 1,376 acres.

**Slope and area.** The 2-4 m contour interval has a gentle slope (1.45-1.46 per cent, values differ between passages) and covers about one-third of the lake's area. The authors compare Wawasee with about twenty other Indiana lakes and state that the 2-m contour gives the lowest slope among the examined lakes. Slope is treated as a key factor in the lake's productivity.

**Volume.** About 85 per cent of the lake's water lies above 10 m, so the upper depth levels are the most important for plankton and for the economy of the lake. The paper defers a full plankton and turnover discussion to a second section.

**Predator-prey link.** Annelids and insect larvae form part of the diet of small fish, and the authors interpret the distribution of the fauna in terms of fish food supply.

## Key Findings

**Overall totals**
- Total invertebrates on the lake bottom: slightly more than 18 billion.
- Total wet weight: 2,028,961 lbs; dry organic matter about 9 per cent, or 182,606 lbs.
- Estimates are based on two years of collections. The authors stress that accuracy would scale poorly with more years of work.

**Annelids (oligochaetes and leeches).** Small in total biomass but widely distributed and an important food item for small fish. Leeches include about six [[Glossiphonia]] species and [[Nephelopsis obscura]], identified by J. Percy Moore.

**Insect larvae**
- Open littoral: mayfly (Ephemeroptera) nymphs, dragonfly (Odonata) nymphs and chironomid midge larvae dominate. Total insect larval weight: 68.72 lbs per acre.
- Weedy littoral: chironomids decline to 11.61 lbs per acre; damselfly (Odonata) nymphs are few.
- Deep water: the large brood worm [[Chironomus tentans]] is the most important insect. A sample of 100 large specimens (collected in August) was used to estimate weight. Its numbers peak at 881 per square meter at one depth (exact depth partly garbled in scan).

**Crustacea**
- [[Crayfish]] (Cambarus) and [[Amphipoda]] (scuds, including [[Hyalella azteca]] and [[Eucrangonyx gracilis]]) are important to about 6 m.
- Crayfish in the open littoral were nearly uniform in size (2-3 inches), suggesting a single year class.
- Crayfish biomass exceeds amphipod biomass in both littoral zones. The authors note that amphipods breed about every 25 days, whereas crayfish breed once a year, so amphipod turnover is higher than biomass alone suggests.

**Snails (Gastropoda)**
- [[Goniobasis]] livescens is the most important snail: about 97 per cent of snail biomass in the open littoral and 74 per cent in the weedy littoral.
- [[Physa]] rises to about 90 lbs per acre in the weedy littoral, which lowers Goniobasis's share there.
- [[Amnicola]] is evenly distributed inshore, at about 4-5 lbs per acre in both littoral zones.
- [[Planorbis]] reaches about 4.69 lbs per acre in the weedy littoral. Planorbis peaks near 5 m and Valvata near 9 m.
- About 17 gastropod taxa were identified by Herman P. Wright.

**Bivalves (Sphaeriidae)**
- [[Sphaerium]] peaks at 9 m; [[Pisidium]] increases with depth until about 11 m, then levels off.
- Both bivalves are less abundant in the weedy littoral than in the open littoral or deep water, with the ratio of zones close to 6:7:20. The authors link the weedy-littoral deficit to dense submerged plants, but the mechanism is unexplained.
- About 16 bivalve taxa were recorded, including about 10 to 11 Pisidium species.

**Mites (Hydrachnidae).** About 20 taxa identified by Dr. Ruth Marshall, including common species of Arrhenurus, Piona and Lebertia.

**Taxonomic approach.** Species names were omitted from the body of the paper because identifying large numbers of individuals was impractical and species-level data were not needed for the main question. Specialists identified selected groups (leeches, mites, amphipods, mollusks), and the authors hope to expand these authoritative lists.

**Methods limitations.** The authors acknowledge that counts are approximate, that leeches ball up and are hard to count, and that the data came from only two summers.

**Reference works cited.** [[Ekman]] (1917), [[Muttkowski]] (1918, Lake Mendota), [[Juday]] (1922, Lake Mendota), and [[Adamstone]] (1924, Lake Nipigon).
