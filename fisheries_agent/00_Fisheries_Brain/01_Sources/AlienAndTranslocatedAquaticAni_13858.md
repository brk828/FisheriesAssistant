# Alien and Translocated Aquatic Animals in Southern Africa: A General Introduction, Checklist and Bibliography

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #cichlidae #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This 1985 report, compiled by M N Bruton and S V Merron of the [[J L B Smith Institute of Ichthyology]] for the [[Committee for Nature Conservation Research]] under the [[National Programme for Ecosystem Research]], provides a general introduction, a checklist and an annotated bibliography of alien and translocated aquatic animals in southern Africa. It cites 587 publications and identifies at least 93 established invasive species: 44 alien and 49 translocated indigenous. Fishes make up the majority (68.8%). The authors argue that invasive animals, especially fishes, are widespread across all major river systems, that impact assessment and control are urgently needed, and that introductions should be evaluated with caution before they are made.

## Background & Description

The report forms part of South Africa's contribution to the [[SCOPE Project]] on the ecology of biological invasions, administered by the [[Invasive Biota Working Group]]. The scope is restricted to natural and semi-natural habitats, including small and large impoundments. Purely artificial settings such as aquaculture ponds and aquaria are excluded unless they contribute to wider invasion of natural waters.

Key definitions:
- **Alien species:** a species remote from its natural distribution, usually from another continent or major catchment outside the southern African region.
- **Translocated indigenous species:** a species native to southern Africa but moved by humans into catchments where it does not naturally occur.
- **Invasive species:** a species able to establish self-sustaining populations in natural or semi-natural communities and cause ecological imbalance.
- **Endemic species:** restricted to a particular lake, drainage system or biogeographic region.

The first recorded alien aquatic animal in the region was noted by [[Valentyn]] in 1726, who saw goldfish in the Governor's house at the Cape. The report also records carp in the Eerste River in the early nineteenth century, well before the commonly accepted introduction date of 1896. Trout and other European fishes followed in the late nineteenth century.

The bibliography (sections on invertebrates, Osteichthyes, Amphibia, Reptilia, Aves and Mammalia) is organised alphabetically with a keyword index. Geographic coverage includes South Africa, South West Africa/Namibia, Botswana, Swaziland and Lesotho, but excludes Zimbabwe and Mozambique.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reasons for introduction** (Table 3) include accidental introduction of parasites and free-living invertebrates, aquaculture, sport fishing, hunting, restocking, biological control of nuisance plants and animals, ornamental ponds and the [[Aquarium Trade]].

**Other invasive categories** noted in the report:
- 6 alien fish species that have died out locally (Table 4, for example [[Northern Pike]] and [[Striped Bass]]).
- 10 suspected invasive species in inland waters, awaiting confirmation (Table 5).
- 11 suspected alien species whose status needs confirmation (Table 6).
- 19 alien species held in captivity that pose a threat if they escape (Table 7).

**Documented impacts** (Taylor et al., 1984, applied to the region) include:
1. Habitat alteration: removal of vegetation, increased turbidity, siltation, substrate erosion and eutrophication.
2. Introduction of parasites and diseases.
3. Trophic alteration: forage supplementation, competition and predation.
4. Hybridisation, which may reduce the fitness of indigenous stocks.
5. Spatial alteration: aggression and overcrowding.

Of these, the authors consider genetic pollution through hybridisation the most damaging in the long term.

**Pathways of spread:** all major southern African river systems are inhabited by alien animals. An intricate network of intercatchment irrigation canals, pipes and tunnels, such as the [[Orange-Fish Tunnel]], further facilitates transfer.

**Marine invasives:** only four marine species are recorded in the surrounding seas, including [[European Shore Crab]] (*Carcinus maenas*) and [[Black-Brown Mussel]] (*Mytilus galloprovincialis*). The authors note this area has received little attention.

**Global comparison:** the report cites [[Nile Perch]] (*Lates niloticus*) introduced into [[Lake Victoria]], where it decimated the haplochromine cichlids that had supported a lucrative fishery.

## Key Findings

- At least **93 established** aquatic animal species occur in southern Africa: **44 alien** and **49 translocated indigenous**.
- **Fishes** comprise **68.8%** of invasive species. Of the 20 established alien fish species, **4** are regarded internationally as pests and **12** as nuisance animals under certain circumstances.
- Table 2 breakdown: Osteichthyes 20 alien and 44 translocated indigenous species (the translocated figure is partly illegible in the scan); Amphibia 0 alien and 2 translocated; Reptilia 1 and 1; Aves 5 and 2; Mammalia 1 alien; Arthropoda 6 alien; Mollusca 7 alien; Ciliophora 2 alien; Cnidaria 1 alien; Platyhelminthes 1 alien.
- **Rainbow trout** (*Salmo gairdneri*) is both valuable (aquaculture and sport fishing) and harmful, having been implicated in the local extinction of rare minnows and kneriids.
- **Red-eared terrapin** (*Chrysemys scripta*), introduced through the aquarium trade, is linked to human Salmonella infections and is regarded internationally as a pest.
- The fish parasites *Argulus japonicus*, *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* and *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* have been associated with disease outbreaks in aquaculture and aquaria.
- Trout and [[Largemouth Bass]] are implicated in the reduction or local extinction of several indigenous minnows and cyprinids, including *Oreodaimon quathlambae*, *Barbus burchelli* and *Kneria auriculata*. However, the authors state that very little is known about these effects.
- **Four** marine invasives are known; the report explicitly warns that more may be found.
- The authors conclude that the introduction of any aquatic animal should be preceded by criteria for impact assessment and a scientific evaluation. Where uncertainty exists, a taxon should not be introduced. They also recommend checking whether an indigenous species could serve the same economic or recreational role.
- The report notes that the bibliography and checklists aim to stimulate research into the natural history and ecological impact of these species, and that a synthesis of current knowledge was in preparation.
