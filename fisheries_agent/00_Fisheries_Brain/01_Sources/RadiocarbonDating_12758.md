# Radiocarbon Dating

#paleoecology #geochronology #radiocarbon-dating

## Executive Summary

This 1952 *Scientific American* article by [[Edward S. Deevey Jr.]] reviews [[Radiocarbon Dating]], a method developed by [[Willard F. Libby]] that measures the age of organic remains from their residual [[Carbon 14]]. The author argues that the method has largely fulfilled its promise for the last few thousand years and has been checked against geological and archaeological evidence. He also identifies weaknesses: statistical uncertainty, unexplained discrepancies between dates, and uncertain chemistry of carbon in lakes and peat. The article uses the Two Creeks and Alleröd interstadials to show that radiocarbon dates from North America and Europe agree with each other and with stratigraphic evidence. The document contains no fish or aquatic-species data; its aquatic content concerns lake sediments, peat, and aquatic plant carbon.

## Background & Description

- **Physical basis:** Cosmic rays produce carbon 14 in the upper atmosphere. It enters carbon dioxide, is absorbed by plants, and passes to animals. Living organisms take it in until death, after which the carbon-14 decays.
- **Half-life:** About 5,568 years. Three half-lives (about 16,700 years) leave one eighth of the original carbon 14, which the author describes as close to undetectable.
- **Initial abundance:** About one carbon-14 atom per trillion ordinary carbon atoms at death.
- **Scope:** Radiocarbon covers only the last few thousand years to roughly 20,000 years. By contrast, [[Uranium]] (half-life 7.6 billion years) is suited to much longer timescales.
- **Early calibration:** A giant sequoia heartwood (about 8,000 years old) gave a satisfactory check. Egyptian mummy cases and house beams, the oldest about 4,600 years old, were also dated accurately.
- **Open assumptions:** The method assumes past cosmic-ray intensity was stable and that carbon 14 mixes rapidly through the atmosphere. Glacial-age climate may have violated both.
- **Laboratories:** By 1952 the Chicago laboratory had published about 300 dates. Laboratories existed or were planned at Columbia, Michigan, Yale, Cambridge, Copenhagen, Pennsylvania, the U.S. Geological Survey, and New Zealand.

### Comparison methods

- **[[Pollen Analysis]]:** Pollen percentages in stratified lake and bog sediments indicate past vegetation and climate. Spruce dominance suggests cold climate and oak suggests warmth. The author notes that pollen time is not constant: pine pollen time spreads over about 3,000 years (9,000 to 6,000 years ago) in its northward march from West Virginia to Maine.
- **Glacial chronology:** Ice-sheet advances and retreats were dated by geologists. The [[Mankato Glaciation]] was traditionally estimated at about 25,000 years old, based on the Niagara River gorge and later questioned.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Glacial retreat:** Ice sheets retreated intermittently. Readvances brought colder conditions into previously temperate regions. Birch woodland in Denmark was replaced by tundra, and in Wisconsin a tree-growing phase was destroyed by a returning glacier.
- **Asymmetry of ice sheets:** The North American ice sheet during the last glacial age was about three times as large as the Scandinavian one, yet both retreated in a similar pattern according to radiocarbon data.
- **Interglacial timing:** The past 20,000 years make up only about 2% of the total estimated duration of the ice ages.
- **Carbon chemistry in aquatic systems:** Aquatic plants take up bicarbonate from water. Some of it comes from ancient limestone, which is radiocarbon-dead. This makes the carbon-14 content of lake peat hard to predict. The chain runs from atmospheric carbon dioxide to carbonic acid to bicarbonate to aquatic protoplasm to lake-bottom mud.
- **Fresh shell:** Fresh shell contains about 10% more radiocarbon than fresh wood, and the reason is unknown.
- **Groundwater contamination:** Ancient shell, wood, and charcoal may exchange carbon with groundwater carbon dioxide after burial.
- **Pleistocene context:** The article refers to four major glacial ages in the [[Pleistocene]] across North America, Europe, and Asia.

## Key Findings

- **Two Creeks vs. Alleröd agreement:** Five Two Creeks wood and peat specimens from Wisconsin averaged 11,400 ± 700 years. Four Alleröd lake-mud samples from Europe averaged 10,800 ± 1,200 years. The author calls the agreement "essentially perfect." Three of the four Alleröd samples came from England and Ireland.
- **Contamination test:** Spruce wood and peat from Two Creeks were dated separately and gave the same age, which argues against contamination by younger groundwater carbon.
- **Old-date check:** At least nine samples known to be a little older than Two Creeks returned "older than 16,000 years," which argues against a systematic error affecting Two Creeks.
- **Last ice advance:** Several independent dates cluster around 10,000 years ago. These include a charred extinct bison bone from a [[Folsom Culture]] site (9,900 ± 700 years), charcoal from [[Scottsbluff Culture]] camps (10,500 ± 3,000 years), and ground sloth dung in Chile and Nevada (about 10,000 years).
- **Oldest date:** The oldest date determined so far was 15,500 ± 1,800 years for charcoal from [[Lascaux Cave]] in France. The author notes that its association with the painters cannot be proved.
- **Peruvian discrepancies:** Two samples from one level gave 3,600 ± 440 years (shell) and 4,400 ± 540 years (plant remains). Wooden artifacts from two graves that had originally been one set of pots differed by about 900 years.
- **Danish bog problem:** Dates for objects in old houses stratified in a Danish peat bog were so far off that the author concludes the bog sediments were disturbed by the ancient Danes.
- **Statistical error:** Of Chicago laboratory dates, about one in three is "wrong" by more than its published error, though which ones cannot be identified. Each count uses roughly 24-hour averages. Quadrupling the count time only doubles accuracy, because the standard deviation scales with the square root of the number of observations. Improving [[Geiger Counter]] efficiency is named as the most immediate route to better precision.
- **Conclusion:** Radiocarbon dating is a constant, absolute timescale, unlike pollen chronology. Its remaining errors may be due to chance or to unresolved chemistry in lakes, peat, and groundwater. The author argues that measuring those chemical pathways will advance knowledge of lake, atmospheric, and ocean chemical history.
