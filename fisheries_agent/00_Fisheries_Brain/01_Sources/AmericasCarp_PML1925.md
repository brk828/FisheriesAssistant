# America's Carp: Introduction, Spread, and Controversy of a Widespread Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This essay by [[Peter B. Moyle]] traces the history of the [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) from its domestication in China through its spread across Europe and into North America. It describes how carp became established through deliberate stocking, most notably by the [[U.S. Fish Commission]] in the 1870s and 1880s, and why the species has been both valued as a food and game fish abroad and widely disdained in America. The text also examines the ecological impact of carp on aquatic habitats, the broader American prejudice against [[Rough Fish]], and the more recent introduction of [[Grass Carp]] for weed control. The author concludes that carp are now permanently established and that Americans should learn to live with and appreciate them.

## Background & Description

- **Origins:** Carp were first domesticated in China, where the species is native, sometime between 800 and 300 B.C. The Romans obtained stock around the first century A.D., likely from the [[Danube River]], the western limit of the native range. During the Middle Ages carp spread through Europe, largely for monastery ponds and fast-day meals.
- **Early literature:** [[Izaak Walton]] described carp in *The Compleat Angler* (1653) as a naturalized fish in England.
- **Physical traits:** Carp have [[Barbels]], whisker-like sensory projections at the mouth corners, used to locate prey in soft mud. They have a flexible lip and molarlike pharyngeal teeth behind the gills. Wild carp have large scales and many small bones; [[Domestic Carp]] are deep-bodied, often scaleless, and have fewer bones.
- **Fecundity and longevity:** A large female can produce up to two million eggs per season. Carp can live up to fifteen years in the wild (forty-seven years is the captive record, as printed in the text). A carp tagged in the [[Missouri River]] moved more than 650 miles in a little over two years.
- **Etymology:** Aristotle called the fish *kyprinos*, linked to Aphrodite (Kypris). The name persists in the genus *Cyprinus* and the family Cyprinidae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat tolerance:** Carp tolerate low oxygen, high temperatures, and high turbidity, allowing them to thrive in polluted or murky waters that are lethal to native fishes. Their populations are largest in shallow, warm lakes and muddy river sloughs, but they also occur in clear mountain lakes, reservoirs, and estuaries.
- **Keystone effects:** Carp uproot aquatic plants and stir up organically rich bottom muds. This can produce turbid water and blue-green algal blooms, reducing habitat for [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]], and [[Yellow Perch]], which depend on clear water and vegetation cover.
- **Waterfowl impacts:** Loss of aquatic plants affects waterfowl such as the [[Canvasback]]. Refuges including [[Malheur Lake]] (Oregon) and [[Lake Mattamuskeet]] (North Carolina) recovered their waterfowl populations after carp were controlled.
- **Spawning:** Carp spawn in shallow water on or near submerged vegetation. Young remain under cover until about three to four inches long. Reproductive failures occur in reservoirs when water levels drop suddenly, but population persistence is maintained by occasional successful spawning.
- **Native rough fishes:** Many native species, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]], are threatened by habitat change and by past poisoning programs. The carp is one of the few rough fish still thriving in the Colorado River.

## Key Findings

- **Introduction history:** The U.S. Fish Commission imported 345 carp from Germany. Demand peaked in 1883, when about 260,000 carp were distributed to 298 of 301 congressional districts, transported partly by specially fitted railroad cars carrying nearly 20,000 fish each. The program ended in 1897. The Illinois River commercial fishery harvested more than eight million pounds of carp in 1899.
- **Public perception:** Carp were regarded as the ultimate trash fish in America, and some states required them to be killed if caught. Spawning-run "fishouts" and spearing events remain common. In contrast, carp are prized in Europe and Asia as game and food fish.
- **Ethnic and commercial demand:** Carp are valued by Jewish, Chinese, and Black American communities, often sold live for dishes such as gefilte fish and steamed carp. Canned and processed carp, including fish sticks, have also been eaten widely.
- **Grass carp introduction:** Grass carp were imported from Malaysia and Taiwan in 1963 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to control aquatic weeds. By 1978 they had been collected more than 1,000 miles upstream of stocking sites, and thirty-five states had recorded their presence. Their impact on native juvenile game fish remains uncertain.
- **Colorado River rotenone treatment:** In 1962 state and federal biologists treated a 45-mile stretch of the Colorado and Green rivers in Utah and Wyoming with rotenone to remove nongame fish. Most nongame populations partly recovered, but several target species are now federally endangered.
- **Changing attitudes:** The author notes a recent shift toward less removal of native nongame fish and occasional state promotion of carp as a sport and food fish. Carp are described as tasty if properly prepared and challenging to catch.
- **Conclusion:** Carp are permanently established in North America, and the author argues that the public should learn to live with and enjoy the species.
