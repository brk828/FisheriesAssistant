# Nutrition in Commercial Catfish Culture

#ichthyology #aquaculture #ictaluridae #catfish-nutrition #feed-formulation

## Executive Summary

This 1969 review synthesizes the first decade of research on commercial [[Channel Catfish]] nutrition in the United States. Despite only four research stations intensively studying catfish feeding, excellent practical rations have been developed yielding 1 pound of gain per 1.5-2.0 pounds of feed. The document covers nutrient requirements (protein, energy, vitamins, minerals, essential fatty acids), ration formulation guidelines, physical feed characteristics, feeding practices, intensive culture systems (cage culture, raceways, biofilters, pond aeration), and off-flavor problems. Research was conducted primarily at the Fish Farming Cultural Station (Marion, Alabama), Fish Farming Experimental Station (Stuttgart, Arkansas), Kansas State University, and Auburn University. The work was jointly supported by USAID/csd-1581 and Rockefeller Foundation Project 65061.

## Background & Description

Commercial freshwater catfish culture experienced unprecedented growth in the 1960s. U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries statistics show production increasing from 1.1 million pounds (1962) to 16.5 million pounds (1967) and 27.5 million pounds (1968), with projections of 42.0 million pounds for 1969 and 112.5 million pounds by 1975. Major factors enabling this expansion included the adaptability of [[Channel Catfish]] to supplemental feeding of dry, pelleted formulations and rapid advancements in fish nutrition science.

The channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) is the primary species cultured commercially. Research focused on year-old fingerlings grown to marketable size in earthen ponds, though studies also examined newly-hatched fry, small fingerlings, and second-year fish. Most experiments used stocking densities of 2,000-13,900 fish per acre in ponds ranging from 0.1 to 1/7 acre, with water temperatures typically 65-88°F.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Commercial catfish culture occurs in managed freshwater pond systems, representing a departure from natural riverine and lake habitats. Water quality parameters are critical: dissolved oxygen levels, pH, temperature, and organic matter loading directly affect fish health and growth. Natural food organisms in ponds provide some nutrition, but this becomes less significant with intensive stocking.

Off-flavors in catfish, described as "musty" or "algae-like," have presented serious problems. These are likely related to aquatic flora, particularly the algae Oscillatoria tenuis, O. prolifica, and O. limosa identified in Israeli carp ponds. Actinomycetes (filamentous microorganisms) in pond muds produce malodorous compounds, especially when oxygen tension increases during pond turnover or water level drops. The organisms Streptomyces griseoluteus and Eutreptia sp. have been associated with off-flavors. Industrial and agricultural effluent wastes also contribute to off-flavors in some fishing grounds.

Intensive culture systems being researched include cage culture (fish stocked in 1 m³ wire cages suspended in ponds), raceway culture (flowing water systems), biofilter systems (water circulated through gravel columns coated with microorganisms to oxidize organic matter), and mechanical pond aeration (air dissipated through submerged perforated pipes). These systems support higher biomass concentrations than conventional ponds.

## Key Findings

**Protein Requirements:**
- Optimum crude protein levels for growing catfish range from 25-40% of dry ration, depending on multiple factors
- [[Nail]] (1962): 25% crude protein adequate for 6-7 inch yearling channel catfish when sufficient energy provided (dextrin 18%, corn oil 8.4%)
- [[Dupree]] and [[Sneed]] (1966): Linear increases in weight gain with protein increments up to 40% (casein/gluten) or 35% (soybean) at 76°F; performance decreased at 69°F
- [[Kansas State University]] researchers recommend 25% crude protein with 850 kcal metabolizable energy per pound for commercial rations
- [[Hastings]] (1969) at Fish Farming Experimental Station: 30% protein most economical, though 40-50% provided best conversion
- [[Auburn University]] Auburn No. 2 formulation: 46% crude protein (soybean meal 35%, peanut cake 35%, fish meal 15%, distillers dried solubles 15%)
- Fry and small fingerlings require higher protein (32-35%) than larger growing fish (25%)

**Biological Value of Proteins:**
Relative biological value for channel catfish fingerlings (casein = 100):
- All-animal protein mixture: 133.5
- Whole egg: 114.8
- Fish meal: 93.7
- Soybean: 52.3
- Wheat gluten: 32.4
- Corn zein: 4.0

**Essential Amino Acids:**
[[Dupree]] (1966) determined qualitatively that channel catfish require the same 10 essential amino acids as rats: arginine, histidine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine. Lysine and methionine are most limiting in plant proteins. [[Deyoe]] et al. (1968) showed that low-animal-protein diets (2.5% of ration) performed as well as high-animal-protein diets (17.3%) when supplemented with lysine and methionine.

**Energy Requirements:**
- [[Kansas State University]]: 850 kcal metabolizable energy per pound in 25% protein ration optimal (energy-protein ratio 34:1)
- [[Hastings]]: recommends 1200 kcal metabolizable energy per pound
- Carbohydrate digestibility varies: glucose 99%, maltose 93%, sucrose 73%, dextrin 64.3%, cooked corn starch 47%, raw corn starch 38% (trout data)
- [[Dupree]] and [[Sneed]] (1966): Best growth with 12.5-14.0% corn oil in diet
- [[Dupree]] (1969): 1 g partially hydrogenated corn oil spared 0.73 g protein; 1 g liquid corn oil spared 0.38 g protein; 1 g beef tallow spared 0.27 g protein
- High levels of digestible carbohydrates (>40-50% dry matter) may cause pathological "high-glycogen" liver in some fish species
- Cellulose (nonnutritive bulk): optimal at 21% of purified diet ([[Dupree]] and [[Sneed]], 1966)

**Vitamin Requirements:**
Essential vitamins identified for channel catfish:
- Water-soluble: pyridoxine, pantothenic acid, riboflavin, thiamine, folic acid, nicotinic acid, B-12, choline
- Fat-soluble: A, D, E, K
- Not required: biotin, inositol, ascorbic acid, para-aminobenzoic acid
- Channel catfish relatively inefficient at converting beta-carotene to vitamin A (45 units vitamin A palmitate per 100 g sufficient; 1.2 mg beta-carotene equivalent to 2,000 rat units was insufficient)
- [[Hastings]] (1967): vitamin supplementation increased catfish growth in earthen ponds by 15%

**Mineral Requirements:**
No specific research conducted on catfish mineral requirements. Recommendations include:
- Calcium: 1.4-1.5% of ration
- Phosphorus: 0.9-1.0% of ration
- Salt: 0.5-1.0% of ration
- Iron particularly essential for young marine fish

**Protein Digestibility Coefficients:**
Selected nitrogen digestibility percentages for channel catfish vs. livestock:
- Corn gluten meal: 80% (catfish), 85% (livestock)
- Cottonseed meal: 76% (catfish), 80% (livestock)
- Fish meal (menhaden): 73.5% (catfish), 81% (livestock)
- Fish meal (sardine): 85% (catfish), 82% (livestock)
- Soybean meal: 72% (catfish), 84% (livestock)
- Dehydrated alfalfa meal: 12% (catfish), 73% (livestock)
- Blood meal: 23% (catfish), 71% (livestock)
- Meat scraps: 40% (catfish), 80% (livestock)

**Ration Formulation Guidelines:**

Fish Farming Experimental Station recommendations:
- Crude protein: ≥32% (28% digestible)
- Animal protein: ≥16% of ration
- Fish meal protein: ≥8% of ration
- Crude fat: 3-4%
- Crude fiber: >8%, <20%
- Metabolizable energy: 1,200 kcal/lb
- Vitamin premix: yes

Kansas State University recommendations:
- Crude protein: 25%
- Animal protein: 17.3% of ration
- Crude fat: 3.5-4%
- Crude fiber: ≤10%
- Metabolizable energy: 850 kcal/lb
- Methionine: 0.52%, Lysine: 1.33%, Arginine: 1.48%
- Calcium: 1.4-1.5%, Phosphorus: 0.9-1.0%
- Vitamin premix: yes

**Physical Feed Characteristics:**
- Pelleted feeds markedly superior to meal form ([[Swingle]], 1958)
- Optimal pellet size: 3/16-inch diameter, approximately 1/4 inch length for yearling fingerlings to marketable size
- Water stability test: ≤10% dry weight loss after 10 minutes in still water indicates good pellet
- Minimum 8% crude fiber important for pellet binding
- Floating pellets gaining acceptability; advantages include easier observation of feeding, detection of disease, potentially reduced disease transmission
- Floating pellets priced 20-40% above conventional pellets
- Manufacturing process: conditioning with steam (moisture +5-6%, temperature 85°C), compression through die, cutting, drying/cooling
- Low-density floating pellets: high pressure (440-520 x 10⁴ kg/m²), temperature 107-127°C, sudden pressure release expands material

**Feeding Practices:**
- Most efficient feeding rate: 2-4% body weight per day, fed once daily
- Maximum safe feeding rate in nonmoving water: 30 pounds pelleted feed per acre per day (to prevent oxygen deficiency)
- [[Tiemeier]] et al. (1969): 2.5% feeding rate more efficient than 3.0%, but higher rate more economical due to increased yield
- [[Hastings]] (1969): 3% body weight through June, reduced to 2.5% remainder of summer
- [[Deyoe]] and [[Tiemeier]] (1968): splitting daily ration (50% morning, 50% afternoon) more efficient than single feeding
- Feed conversion inversely proportional to feeding rate
- More frequent feeding increases consumption and favors efficient growth

**Intensive Culture Systems:**

Cage Culture ([[Schmittou]], 1969, Auburn University):
- 34,000+ catfish stocked in 98 cages over 2 years
- Densities up to 500 fish per m³
- Fed 40% protein floating pellets
- Overall feed conversion: 1.25
- Highest standing crop in 1 m³ cage: 421 lb
- Maximum daily gain per cage: 4.94 lb

Raceway Culture (Fish Farming Experimental Station):
- 5,000 fish per acre stocking rate
- Fed 4% body weight until July, then 3% through September
- Average production: 3,150 pounds per acre
- Average feed conversion: 1.8
- Growth curve increased exponentially throughout 180-day period

Biofilter Systems (Auburn University, Greene 1968):
- Water circulated through gravel columns coated with microorganisms
- Stocking rates up to 4,000 pounds fingerlings per acre
- Fed 3% body weight mid-May through mid-October
- Production up to 19,756 pounds per acre
- Constant growth rate indicated maximum capacity not reached

Pond Aeration ([[Jeffrey]], 1969, Auburn University):
- 0.1-acre ponds, 8,000 fingerlings + 90 bass fry per acre
- Air flow up to 100 ft³ per minute through submerged perforated PVC pipe
- Maximum feeding: 116.4 pounds per acre per day
- Aerated yield: 4,311 pounds per acre
- Non-aerated yield: 3,321 pounds per acre
- Benefits: higher dissolved oxygen, higher pH, lower dissolved CO₂, eliminated stratification

**Off-Flavor Problems:**
- Fall 1968 incident in western Alabama: distinct "musty," "algae-like" odor in 1-2 pound channel catfish
- Odor most pronounced in visceral and dorsal fat, least in lean tissue
- Euglenoid algae (Eutreptia sp.) predominant; no actinomycetes found
- Off-flavor removed after 2 months by stopping feeding and allowing winter rains to flush ponds
- Israeli research: Oscillatoria tenuis caused severe off-flavor in carp; also O. prolifica and O. limosa
- Actinomycetes (Streptomyces griseoluteus) produce malodorous compounds with threshold >1 part per billion
- Odors most intense after algal cell decomposition or mechanical disruption
- Industrial/agricultural effluent wastes contribute to off-flavors in some areas
- Excessively fat catfish have objectionable sensory attributes and poor frozen keeping quality
- Properly packaged catfish maintain flavor for 16 months at ≤0°F storage
