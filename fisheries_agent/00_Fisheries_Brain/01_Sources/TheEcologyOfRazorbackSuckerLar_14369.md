# Ecology of Razorback Sucker Larvae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This student special-problems report investigates the nighttime catchability of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. It combines the author's own 1995 dip-net surveys with 1994 larval collection records from the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (USBR). The study tests whether [[Lunar Phase]] or time of night drives larval [[Phototactic Response]] and catch rate. The author concludes that lunar phase has little or no detectable effect, that catch rates decline with observer fatigue rather than falling larval numbers, and that certain coves are consistently productive. The author recommends further research on the phototactic behaviour of this endangered species.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a rare and endangered fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its historic range extended from Wyoming to northwestern Mexico (Holden 1980a). The species declined sharply in both range and abundance from the mid-twentieth century, largely for reasons still unknown (Minckley and Deacon 1991). Today the largest known population is in Lake Mohave. A recovery plan has been developed and implemented, but many aspects of its life history remain poorly understood.

Larval records are sparse in the early literature. Larvae were first noted in Lake Mohave around 1954 (Jonez and Sumner). Mueller recorded a few in plankton tows in 1982, and Marsh and Langhorst recorded infrequent captures in midwater larval trawls in 1988. Larval collection became much easier after spawning areas were identified and sampling improved. A key advance was the discovery that larvae show a positive phototactic response to bright light at night. This allows collectors to obtain hundreds or sometimes thousands of larvae in a few hours (Bozek et al. 1984).

**Field methods (author's 1995 surveys):**
- Equipment: one fine-mesh dip net per person, one Q-Beam fish light per side of the boat, a water bucket for holding larvae, and a boat.
- Procedure: collecting began soon after dark and continued until dawn. Work was done from the boat in two to three feet of water with the bow beached. At least two people were needed, organized into early and late shifts.
- Recording: catches were tallied in 15-minute increments, with one tally mark per larva. Each tally was logged by person, time, location, weather, water conditions and lunar phase on a standard data sheet.
- Sampling dates: March 14–16 and April 1–2, 1995. The author sampled under near-full moon (89–98% illumination) and near-new moon (1–4%) to test lunar effects.

**USBR 1994 dataset:** Records from February through April 1994 list date, cove, start and end times, and larvae captured per attempt. The author notes that this dataset was harder to analyse because it lacked consistent information on the number of lights and crew.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Larvae were concentrated in specific coves. The most productive sites were [[Tequila Cove]], [[Yuma Cove]] and [[Arizona Bay]]. Other sites included [[Hog Farm Cove]], [[Nine Mile Cove]] and [[Willow Cove]].
- **Light response:** The strong phototactic response means that artificial light, rather than natural moonlight, is probably the main factor driving capture. The author suggests this is because a [[Fish Light]] is far brighter than the moon.
- **Spawning:** The author suggests that Tequila Cove may be a "hot" spawning ground, based on high catches on several dates.
- **Observer effects:** Catch rates varied with the person collecting and the time of night. This makes [[Human Fatigue]] an important confounding factor in field surveys.

## Key Findings

1. **Lunar phase:** No consistent pattern was found between [[Lunar Phase]] and catch rate. Comparisons covering 1% to 96% illumination (graphs D, G, H, I, L) showed no steady increase toward full moon. The author therefore concludes that moonlight has little or no effect on larval activity.
2. **Nightly pattern:** Catches were fairly steady through the night. In the author's 1995 data, catch rate dropped after about 2:00 am. The author attributes this to observer fatigue rather than fewer larvae. Groups with shift changes (B and C) performed better in early morning hours than groups without them (A, D and E).
3. **Location effects:** Of 6,047 larvae in the combined USBR and author data, Tequila Cove produced 2,107 (34.8%) and Yuma Cove 1,870 (30.9%). Arizona Bay produced 765 (12.7%). Many other coves produced few or no larvae.
4. **Seasonal and annual variation:** Catch totals varied widely between dates. For example, April 1–2, 1995 produced about 1,014 and 1,016 larvae, the highest in the comparison (graph K). No clear seasonal pattern emerged across February–April 1994 samples (graph J).
5. **Total catch in the author's hourly data:** The author's per-hour comparison totals 2,693 larvae caught across all groups and nights.
6. **Recommendation:** The author recommends further research into the causes of larval phototaxis, noting that many variables were not controlled.

**Limitations (as stated by the author):** Data were collected by different people with different equipment. Light numbers, crew fatigue and site selection were not standardised. The author states that numerous factors affecting phototactic behaviour were not considered.
