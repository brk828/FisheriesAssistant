# Governor's Riparian Habitat Task Force: Final Report and Recommendations (Arizona, 1990)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 final report from the [[Governor's Riparian Habitat Task Force]] was submitted to Governor Rose Mofford of Arizona under [[Executive Order 89-16]], "Streams and Riparian Resources." The Task Force was charged with developing a definition, classification system, inventory, list of key riparian areas, policy, legislative recommendations, and agency actions to protect, conserve, enhance, and restore Arizona's riparian resources. The report describes riparian areas as the most threatened natural communities in the state, with an estimated loss, alteration, or degradation of over 90% of native riparian areas along major desert watercourses. Its central recommendation is a new executive order establishing a state riparian policy, an interagency coordinating council, and a set of priority actions, including riparian protection legislation and a watershed enhancement board.

## Background & Description

The Task Force was created on June 10, 1989, and met with the Governor on October 25, 1989. Its membership included directors of the Arizona Department of Commerce, Department of Transportation, Geological Survey, State Land Department, Office of Tourism, Department of Water Resources, Game & Fish Department, State Parks, and Department of Environmental Quality, chaired by [[Sue Lofgren]] of the [[Commission on the Arizona Environment]]. A Steering Committee Work Group of agency staff prepared issue papers and draft recommendations, and public, federal, tribal, and local participation was invited. The Task Force held 11 full meetings and 10 Steering Committee meetings.

The report defines a riparian area as "an aquatic or terrestrial ecosystem that is associated with bodies of water, such as streams, lakes, or wetlands, or is dependent upon the existence of perennial, intermittent or ephemeral surface or subsurface water drainage." It notes that riparian zones cover less than 0.5% of the southwestern landscape but hold the state's richest plant and animal productivity and diversity. Because wetland law under the [[Clean Water Act]] requires hydrology, hydric soils, and hydrophytic vegetation together, most Arizona riparian areas may fail to qualify for federal wetland protection even though they function as wetlands.

The report reviews federal instruments relevant to riparian lands, including [[Executive Order 11988]] (Floodplain Management), [[Executive Order 11990]] (Protection of Wetlands), the [[National Environmental Policy Act]], and [[Section 404 Permitting]] under the Clean Water Act. It also summarizes the history of riparian decline, drawing on the [[Arizona Wetlands Priority Plan]], the 1989 [[SCORP]] Rivers, Streams & Wetlands Study, and the 1988 [[Riparian Resource Report]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Riparian areas are ecologically central to Arizona's arid landscape. The report states that all 27 remaining native freshwater fish species depend on streams, riparian areas, and wetlands, and that the [[Arizona Game & Fish Department]] estimates 75% or more of native wildlife depend on healthy riparian systems for some part of their life cycle. It further estimates that riparian areas are critical to about 60% of fish and wildlife species at risk of extirpation in the state.

Riparian areas improve water quality by processing chemical and organic pollutants, reducing sediment and turbidity in floodwaters, and taking up nutrients. They also provide flood protection, erosion control, groundwater recharge, and streamflow regulation. Historically, the Gila, Salt, and Santa Cruz Rivers flowed year round, and the San Pedro supported a commercial fishery. Arizona receives an estimated 80 million acre-feet of precipitation annually, with more than 95% lost to evaporation and transpiration, and average annual runoff of 0.4 inch, the lowest in the nation (ADEQ, 1988).

Threats documented in the report include overutilization and conversion (sand and gravel extraction, grazing, timber harvest, agriculture, exotic species), irrigation and water storage (groundwater pumping, impoundment, diversion), and urbanization and flood control (channelization, pollution, residential and commercial development). Nonpoint source pollution is a leading cause of water quality degradation. Over 90% of assessed surface waters fail to meet designated protected uses, and over 40% of groundwater wells sampled from 1979 to 1987 were impacted or exceeded recommended contaminant limits.

## Key Findings

**Status of native fishes and wildlife**
- Native fishes are the fastest-disappearing wildlife group in the United States. About 90% of Arizona's native fish species are on the Arizona Game & Fish Commission's Threatened Native Wildlife list. Six have been extirpated and one is extinct. Eighteen are listed as Endangered or Threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- Five of 32 native fish species present around 1900 no longer occur in Arizona. Twenty-one of the remaining 27 are listed by the state as endangered or threatened or are under study for listing.
- Riparian-dependent birds such as the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and [[Yellow-Billed Cuckoo]] are on the state threatened list. Historic records include large [[Colorado River Squawfish]] taken along the lower Gila River.

**Gaps and obstacles identified**
- No agency has a specific statutory mandate to conserve riparian ecosystems, and Arizona has no state statute protecting riparian areas.
- Management authority is fragmented among agencies, with no integrated or centralized statewide riparian authority.
- Threats include over-appropriation of rivers, lack of instream flow maintenance, groundwater-surface water connection problems, residential construction in floodplains, and proliferation of non-indigenous phreatophytes.
- A statewide riparian inventory and classification system had never been completed, and funding for acquisition and agency management was lacking.

**Accomplishments and limits of the first year**
- The Task Force adopted a mission statement and a riparian definition used by all state agencies.
- It adopted the [[Handbook of Riparian Measurements]] as a first step toward a common classification system. The Arizona Riparian Council developed the handbook.
- It could not complete a classification system, statewide inventory, or key riparian area list within the time frame. It decided to use criteria from the [[Arizona Rivers Assessment]], projected for completion by 1992.
- It stated that over 90% of native riparian areas along major desert watercourses had been lost, altered, or degraded.

**State riparian policy (Figure 4)**
The policy states that riparian protection and restoration are of critical importance, and it commits the state to: maintaining existing riparian areas and restoring degraded ones; promoting public awareness; supporting cooperative and citizen efforts; encouraging instream flow preservation; and requiring that any loss be balanced by restoration of areas of equal values and functions.

**Priority recommendations (Figure 6)**
- Legislation to create an Arizona Watershed and Riparian Enhancement Board, modeled on Oregon's Senate Bill 23.
- A State Riparian Protection Law, drawing on a model statute and New Jersey's Freshwater Wetland Protection Act.
- Statewide instream flow reservations and controls on groundwater and surface water transfers, modeled on Colorado's legislation.
- Funding for a statewide inventory, classification, and riparian management plan.
- Funding for purchase of key riparian areas and public/private partnerships.
- Funding for river corridor planning and riparian education under the Environmental Education law.
- Support for designating qualified rivers to the [[National Wild and Scenic Rivers System]].

**Draft Executive Order (Figure 7)**
The draft order contains 15 sections. Key provisions:
- **Section 1:** Adopts the state riparian policy.
- **Section 2:** Requires all state agencies to enforce existing authorities to protect riparian areas.
- **Section 3:** Commission on the Arizona Environment to develop legislation for the Watershed and Riparian Enhancement Board, a non-regulatory body running a cost-sharing or grants program.
- **Section 4:** Creates a Riparian Areas Coordinating Council, replacing the Task Force, with a rotating chair and annual reports to the Governor.
- **Section 5:** Game & Fish Commission to draft a statewide riparian management plan.
- **Section 6:** Department of Environmental Quality to develop riparian protection legislation.
- **Section 7:** Department of Water Resources to complete rules for instream flow water right applications and protect riparian water use.
- **Section 8:** Game & Fish Commission to conduct a statewide riparian inventory and classification.
- **Section 9:** Game & Fish Commission to develop methods for determining equal functions and values.
- **Section 10:** Game & Fish Commission and State Parks Board to acquire and manage key riparian areas and water rights.
- **Section 12:** Department of Environmental Quality to consider riparian protection in Section 401 water quality certifications.
- **Section 14:** State agencies to help identify rivers suitable for National Wild & Scenic designation.
- **Section 15:** Department of Water Resources to develop a model local riparian protection ordinance.

**Strategy inventory (Figure 5)**
The Briefing Paper lists 39 potential programs and strategies. Examples include an Arizona Watershed and Riparian Enhancement Board, a State Riparian Area Protection Law, [[Conservation Easements]], [[Purchase of Water Rights]], [[Statewide Water Reservations]], and [[Riparian Mapping]] with the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service. Each has a time frame (short- or long-term) and a cost category, ranging from under $50,000 to over $1,000,000.

**Coordination**
The Task Force recommends that the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service help map riparian areas alongside its National Wetlands Inventory, which would cover much of a statewide inventory. It also recommends encouraging federal, local, tribal, and private groups to adopt its recommendations.
