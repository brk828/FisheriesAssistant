# Humpback Chub Population Monitoring in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon (1989 Field Season)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 report by C. O. Minckley (Biology Department, Northern Arizona University, Flagstaff) presents the fourth year of monitoring the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in the lower [[Little Colorado River]], Grand Canyon, Arizona. Fieldwork in May 1989 used hoop, trammel and seine nets at confluence and upstream sites, with the aims of documenting spawning and recruitment and estimating population size and movement. The study also records native and non-native fish assemblages, [[Parasitism]] by anchorworm, and a short [[Thermal Tolerance]] experiment that was not completed because no fry were produced. The report closes with recommendations to continue monitoring, document spawning requirements, and investigate the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Striped Bass]] situation.

## Background & Description

*Gila cypha* is an endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its Little Colorado River population is monitored because the confluence area functions as a major staging and spawning area. The report notes that the [[Humpback Chub]] was historically among the most abundant fish in the system, and that it is a long-lived species, with at least 20 years of life suggested for it in the text.

The study area extended from the confluence with the Colorado River up to about 15 km upstream in the Little Colorado River. Two camps were established: a "Salt Trail" camp about 1 km upstream of the mouth of the Salt Trail Canyon and a "Confluence" camp. The research team consisted of six people, three at each camp. Fieldwork involved helicopter-supported gear transport and a multi-week schedule, roughly 19 April to 30 May 1989 for the Salt Trail camp and 19 April to 16 May for the confluence camp.

Objectives were (1) to document and observe reproduction in the study area and (2) to conduct mark-recapture studies for a population estimate and movement data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gear and sampling.** Upstream and confluence sites used hoop nets (1 m diameter, 2.5 m length), trammel nets (91.5 m x 2.5 m, 4 cm outer and 2.5 cm inner mesh), minnow seines and a larval seine. Nets were set for 1 to 29 days at about 20 locations, with 3.3 km to 15 km upstream from the confluence and at the confluence itself.

**Confluence dynamics.** The Little Colorado River and [[Colorado River]] mainstem interact strongly. When mainstem flows were high (for example about 15,000 cfs), the Little Colorado mouth backed up and trammel nets could fish only in the mainstem. When mainstem flows were reduced (about 3,000 cfs or less), nets fished in water below 0.5 m deep.

**Water temperature.** Little Colorado River temperatures ranged from about 16 to 23 °C in the study period, and the report notes that spawning temperatures were within the optimum range for the species.

**Associated fauna.** Non-native fish caught included [[Channel Catfish]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Striped Bass]]. Stomach analyses of several species were recorded but the detailed results are summarised only briefly in the source.

**Parasitism.** Anchorworm infestation of humpback chub was very low (less than 1%), but two chubs from the Salt Trail reach carried large numbers of tapeworms, which the author notes as the first reported occurrence of this parasite in this population.

## Key Findings

- **Catch composition (confluence nets, 1989):** 1,114 fish of 12 species were collected. Humpback chub made up 84.7% (about 947 fish) and native species 97% overall, with about 3.2% non-native fish.
- **Upstream nets (1989):** 1,113 fish of six species were taken; humpback chub made up 84.7% and native fish 97.7%.
- **Total catch:** Approximately 2,500 fish of 12 species were recorded for the whole 1989 season.
- **Sex ratio:** Males ranged from about 41.5% to 53.3% of sexed fish across weeks, and females from about 28% to 62% (with 50% sexed in the confluence sample).
- **Spawning:** Ripe fish were present, with ripe males and females recorded; larval fish appeared at the confluence during the season, but spawning was not directly observed. Larval fish were also present at the Salt Trail camp at the start of the study.
- **Length structure:** Confluence collections were dominated by fish 100 to 150 mm, while larger and older fish dominated later collections; the report infers a shift toward older year classes over 1987 to 1990.
- **Population estimate:** A Peterson-type (Chapman-style) estimate for the whole reach was 1,932 humpback chub (fish of all sizes included); confluence-only estimates were lower, about 1,000 (with ranges given in source). Estimates were made using the formula N = (M × C) / R.
- **Recapture rates:** Overall recapture of PIT-tagged fish at the confluence was 17%; the total collection recapture rate was about 27%. Most fish moved little, with a maximum upstream movement of 9.5 km recorded for one fish.
- **Growth:** Several recaptured fish showed growth over periods of 1 to 3 years, from about 13 mm to 73 mm.
- **Tagging:** 771 humpback chub were tagged in 1989; 393 with PIT tags and 137 with external carlin tags.
- **Thermal tolerance:** Not conducted because no fry were produced.
- **Recommendations:** (1) Continue monitoring this species and document spawning requirements and longevity; (2) Establish a temporary weir in an upstream area to better estimate population size and structure; (3) Analyse carp intestinal parasites and their impact on humpback chub; (4) Investigate Razorback Sucker identity and hybrids from the Grand Canyon region, given the species' federal listing.
