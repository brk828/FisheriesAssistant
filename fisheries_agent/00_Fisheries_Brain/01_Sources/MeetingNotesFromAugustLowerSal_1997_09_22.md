# Meeting Notes: Lower Salt River Winter-Time Releases (August 12, 1997)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document comprises meeting notes and correspondence from a multi-agency working group (Arizona Game and Fish Department, US Bureau of Reclamation, Salt River Project, Central Arizona Water Conservation District, Arizona Municipal Water Users Association, Tonto National Forest, US Fish and Wildlife Service, Trout Unlimited/Arizona Flycasters, and Arizona State University) convened to evaluate a proposal to supplement winter-time water releases from Stewart Mountain Dam to Granite Reef Dam on the [[Lower Salt River]]. The stated dual purpose was to (1) establish a nationally recognized recreational trout fishery and (2) assess/mitigate potential impacts to native fish populations ([[Roundtail Chub]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]]) and the threatened [[Bald Eagle]] (Blue Point Territory pair) that forage in this tailwater reach. The group agreed to pursue an experimental/evaluation approach: a 2-year baseline study followed by 2 years of experimental supplemented flows and trout stocking, split into an "agreements" subgroup (water exchange, legal/financial arrangements) and a "biology/experimental design" subgroup.

## Background & Description

No direct morphometric data are reported in this correspondence; the document is administrative/planning in nature. Species mentioned for study are identified only taxonomically:

- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (Gila robusta) — native cyprinid of concern in the reach.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]** (Catostomus insignis) — native catostomid; documented as a preferred prey item for foraging bald eagles during spawning season.
- **[[Desert Sucker]]** (Catostomus/Pantosteus clarkii) — native catostomid co-occurring with Sonora Sucker.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** and **[[Brown Trout]]** — nonnative salmonids proposed for winter stocking; noted by anglers as already established/expanding in the reach.

Yamaguchi's letter references size-based management targets for trout (not morphology per se, but proposed regulatory size classes):
- Proposed slot limits: 10"–14", scaling to 13"–18" or 15"–20" as population grows.
- Comparable quality-fishery precedent: 20" minimum-size, hard-artificial-lure-only restrictions (e.g., Rocky Ford Spring Creek, WA; San Juan River Quality Section, NM).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat context**: The study reach is a hydrologically-controlled tailwater below Stewart Mountain Dam, historically subject to reduced/variable winter flows. The [[Verde River]] below Bartlett Dam is cited as a comparative reach for baseline study.
- **Coexistence of native and nonnative fauna**: Reclamation highlighted that native fishes (Roundtail Chub, Sonora Sucker, Desert Sucker) persist despite "a considerable nonnative fish fauna" in this tailwater — an ecological puzzle the group sought to understand via research (food habit studies, habitat usage, behavioral observation).
- **Downstream dependency**: Native fish communities in [[Salt River Project]] canals downstream of Granite Reef Dam reportedly dominate the local ichthyofaunal assemblage and are thought to depend on emigration of individuals from the upstream Salt River reach — implicating this reach as a source population for downstream canal fish communities.
- **Predator-prey linkage — Bald Eagle and native suckers**: Hunt et al. (1992) documented the Blue Point Territory bald eagle pair using Bulldog Cliffs (immediately below Stewart Mountain Dam) and a ~5 km reach near Blue Point Bridge, with peak use February–April coinciding with sucker spawning. The radio-telemetered male eagle spent more time on the river than the reservoir during this period, foraging preferentially on **spawning native suckers**. This establishes a direct trophic link between native catostomid spawning phenology and eagle habitat use/productivity.
- **Human disturbance factor**: Abandonment of the Bulldog Cliffs eagle nest was hypothesized to relate to human disturbance; eagles successfully fledged young from an alternate site (Willow Springs Canyon, tributary to Saguaro Lake) in 1987–1988.
- **Potential competitive interactions**: Anglers/observers anticipated that increasing winter flows to benefit trout stocking could also increase nonnative trout abundance to the point of direct competition with native suckers and chub for water/habitat and shared food resources — described analogously as fish populations expanding "to the level of the resource," similar to traffic filling available road capacity.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed experimental design**: 2-year baseline study (1998 start anticipated) of native fish status — focusing on reproduction timing/location, survivorship, age and growth, and emigration rates past Granite Reef Dam — followed by a minimum 2-year post-baseline study assessing combined effects of (a) supplemented winter flows and (b) winter rainbow trout stocking, ideally isolating each variable's incremental impact where feasible.
- **Nonnative-native interaction studies**: Reclamation requested food habit, habitat usage, and behavioral studies to characterize interaction significance between native and nonnative fish faunas.
- **Bald Eagle regulatory concern**: A biological assessment and possible formal Section 7 consultation with the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] were deemed necessary before implementing any modified release program, given potential impacts to bald eagle foraging patterns, prey availability, and territory productivity. Radio-telemetry was recommended as the most effective data collection method.
- **Water flow debate**: AGFD's initial proposal specified 50–150 cfs winter flow; angling community representative (Yamaguchi) recommended raising the minimum to 100 cfs (i.e., "100 to 150 cfs") to ensure sufficient carrying capacity for both native species and a nationally competitive sportfishery, anticipating flow-limited population capacity for both native and nonnative fish.
- **Institutional/legal considerations**: Identified logistical barriers included ADWR permitting for water exchange (2–4 month lead time), CAP aqueduct capacity constraints limiting "payback" water delivery to shoulder seasons (spring/fall), the Arizona Water Banking Authority's legal capacity for such exchanges, and potential added costs to SRP for fish salvage during canal maintenance dry-ups.
- **Proposed fishery management framework** (Yamaguchi letter): Recommended a dual-zone management structure — a no-harvest catch-and-release zone (~1 mile) plus a limited-harvest, hard-artificial-lure-only zone (~3–5 miles) with a moving slot limit, modeled on precedents from the San Juan River (NM) and Rocky Ford Spring Creek (WA), intended to sustain both a self-propagating trophy fishery and broader angler access while minimizing harvest-driven population decline (estimated sustainable harvest ≤1%).
- **Governance structure established**: Two working subgroups formed — an **Agreements Group** (Reclamation, CAWCD, Maricopa County Flood Control District, AMWUA, AGFD) handling water exchange/finance instruments, and a **Biology/Experimental Design Group** (AGFD, Reclamation, SRP, FWS, Tonto National Forest, Trout Unlimited/Arizona Flycasters/ASU) handling the research and monitoring design, with [[W.L. Minckley]] (ASU) as an involved technical advisor.
