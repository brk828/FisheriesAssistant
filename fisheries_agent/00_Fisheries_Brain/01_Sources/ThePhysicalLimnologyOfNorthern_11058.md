## Executive Summary

This 1958 paper by Eville Gorham is a **physical limnology synthesis**, not primarily an ichthyological study, drawing on the 1897–1909 Bathymetrical Survey of the Scottish Freshwater Lochs (Murray & Pullar 1910). It statistically analyzes interrelations among lake **drainage area, lake area, length, mean breadth, mean depth, and maximum depth** across 399 Scottish lakes (262 rock basins, 137 glacial drift basins). The paper models water replacement time, seasonal thermal stratification as a function of lake size, and tests for a relationship between lake morphometry and organic content of profundal (bottom) muds. While the paper is not fish-centric, it explicitly references morphometry-productivity-fish yield linkages (via Hayes 1957) and provides the physical habitat framework — depth, thermal regime, water renewal rates, and sediment organic content — that underlies aquatic biological productivity, including fish populations, in these lochs. Notably, the paper reports that **no significant relationship** was found between lake dimensions and the organic content of profundal muds, and that plankton and macrovegetation data collected during the original survey were largely unpublished, though the collections are archived at the Royal Scottish Museum.

## Background & Description

*(Note: The paper's "morphology" concerns **lake basin morphometry**, not organismal fish morphology — reflecting the paper's focus on aquatic habitat structure.)*

- **Lake basin types**: Two categories distinguished by Peach and Horne — **rock basins** (n=262) and **glacial drift basins** (n=137, including 29 kettle holes formed from melting glacier ice blocks).
- **Rock basins** are on average **larger, narrower, and deeper** than drift basins:
  - Length/mean-breadth ratios in the largest rock basins exceed 20:1 (vs. ~3:1 in smallest), reflecting confinement in deep mountain valleys vs. plateau basins.
  - Maximum/mean depth ratio in rock basins declines from ~3.0 (shallow, V-shaped, less-eroded) to ~2.4 (deep, U-shaped, more glacially eroded) with increasing size — evidence of a **glacial erosional evolutionary sequence**.
- **Drift basins** show the opposite depth-ratio trend: max/mean depth ratio rises from ~2.2 (small) to ~2.8 (large), suggesting smaller drift lakes are more infilled by erosion/sedimentation.
- **Kettle holes**: shallow (median depth ~10 ft regardless of lake area), low drainage-area/lake-area ratio (~11–13), reflecting flat fluvio-glacial terrain.
- Overall average max/mean depth ratio: ~2.7 (rock basins) vs. ~2.4 (drift basins), indicating rock erosion by ice produces more strongly U-shaped cross-sections than drift erosion/sedimentation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This section synthesizes the **physical habitat parameters** relevant to biological/ichthyological productivity in these lochs, per the source's framing:

- **Photosynthetic zone limitation**: Using a cylindrical vs. conical basin model and Talling's (1957) [[Windermere]] photosynthesis-depth curve, the paper shows areal photosynthesis declines steeply as mean depth increases to ~80–100 ft, then more gradually — implying **shallow lakes support proportionally greater primary production per unit surface area**, a key driver of overall lake productivity and fish carrying capacity.
- **Rawson's (1953) net plankton standing crop data** from Canadian lakes follow a similar depth-dependent curve, supporting the hypothesis that **mean depth constrains time spent by primary producers in the photosynthetic zone**, linking directly to secondary (zooplankton/fish) productivity.
- **Water replacement time**: Calculated from rainfall (50 in) and evaporation (15 in) averages for Scotland; smaller lakes fully replace their water every few weeks, while the largest rock-basin lochs may take much longer — a parameter with direct implications for nutrient retention, plankton dynamics, and fish habitat stability.
- **Thermal stratification**: Seasonal surface and bottom water temperatures vary systematically with lake area (Figure 9), with implications for thermal habitat zonation relevant to fish species distribution (e.g., cold-water refugia in larger, deeper lochs).
- **Profundal mud organic content**: No significant correlation found with lake dimensions — implying benthic habitat quality (relevant to benthic fish food resources) is not simply predictable from lake size/depth alone.
- **Cross-reference to fish productivity literature**: The paper explicitly cites [[Hayes 1957]] (*J. Fish. Res. Bd. Can.*) on "variation in bottom fauna and fish yield in relation to trophic level and lake dimensions," situating this physical limnology work as foundational for understanding [[Fish Yield]] and [[Lake Morphometry|lake morphometric]] controls on productivity — an important conceptual link to [[Rawson 1955]]'s work on morphometry as a dominant factor in large-lake productivity.

## Key Findings

- **399 Scottish lochs analyzed** (262 rock basins, 137 drift basins), based on the 1897–1909 [[Bathymetrical Survey of the Scottish Freshwater Lochs]] (Murray & Pullar 1910).
- Strong log-linear correlations exist between:
  - Length and mean breadth (r = 0.75 rock, 0.69 drift)
  - Drainage area and lake area (r = 0.70 rock, 0.69 drift)
  - Mean depth and lake area (r = 0.68 rock; only 0.24 drift)
  - Maximum depth and mean depth (r = 0.95 rock, 0.92 drift) — among the strongest relationships found.
- **Rock basins are markedly deeper** than drift basins of equivalent area; drift-basin lochs are only ~one-quarter as deep as rock-basin lochs of similar surface area.
- Extrapolation to world lakes: Scottish rock-basin depth/area relationship closely matches large **Alpine and Scandinavian mountain lakes**; large North American lakes (draining to Hudson Bay/Gulf of St. Lawrence/Arctic) fall intermediate between Scottish rock and drift basin trends; remnants of glacial [[Lake Agassiz]] (Winnipeg, Winnipegosis, Manitoba, Lake of the Woods) plot below the drift-basin line due to heavy post-glacial sedimentation; [[Lake Balaton]] is anomalously shallow (max depth 36 ft) due to volcanic subsidence origin.
- **No relationship detected** between lake dimensions and organic content (ignition loss) of profundal muds — a notable negative finding relevant to benthic habitat/food-web studies.
- Water replacement time scales strongly with lake area: small lochs renew completely within weeks; large rock-basin lochs take substantially longer, with direct implications for nutrient cycling and plankton/fish habitat stability.
- Original survey plankton and macrovegetation data were **largely unpublished** but retained at the [[Royal Scottish Museum]], representing an untapped historical dataset for aquatic ecological (including fish) research.
- The paper serves as a **physical/morphometric baseline** explicitly intended to support future hydrobiological and biological (including fisheries) research on Scottish lochs.
