# Records of Atherinid Fishes at Inland Localities in Texas and Northern Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atherinidae

## Executive Summary

This reprint from the *Great Basin Naturalist* (Vol. XXV, 1965) by [[W. L. Minckley]] documents inland records of atherinid fishes (silversides) in Texas and northern Mexico. The central subject is the [[Tidewater Silverside]] (*Menidia beryllina*), recorded from several inland stream and reservoir localities, with the question of whether its presence reflects natural range extension or human-mediated introduction. The note also reports two Mexican [[Chirostoma]] species, based on collections from the [[Rio Conchos]] system, and discusses how fish movement by people, especially through bait and stocking water, may have shaped these distributions. Source text is OCR-degraded; figures are reported as legible.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Journal reprint (research note), with a literature-cited list of roughly 15 references.
- **Study focus:** Distribution and habitat of *Menidia beryllina* and *Chirostoma* spp. in inland waters of Texas and northern Mexico.
- **Field collections:** Seining on June 3–4, 1964, in the Rio Conchos, Chihuahua (near Las Varas, near Ciudad Delicias) and at Ciudad Camargo, with voucher specimens cited in the Texas State University collections (ASU).
- **Other sources:** Records from Texas Parks and Wildlife Department Federal Aid to Fisheries reports, personal communications and published surveys (e.g., [[Tilton and White]] 1964; [[Gunter]] 1945; [[Hubbs]] 1957).
- **Atherinids mentioned:** *Menidia beryllina*, the [[Mississippi Silverside]] (*Menidia audens*), and the Mexican silversides *Chirostoma sphyraena*, *C. estor* and *C. bartoni*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rio Conchos habitat (Las Varas):**
- Channel 50–75 ft wide, about 4 ft deep at the deepest point.
- Pools alternated with riffles over gravel; silt in quiet backwaters.
- No aquatic macrophytes; shoreline lined with trees and shrubs.
- Water milky in color and saline; discharge estimated at about 125 cubic feet per second.

**Ciudad Camargo habitat:**
- Large upstream pool, with small pools and riffles near the highway bridge.
- Water milky, malodorous, and apparently polluted with sewage.
- Bank vegetation of cattail (*Typha*), low grasses and small shrubs; submerged plants (*Cabomba*, *Ceratophyllum*, *Potamogeton*) in riffles.
- Discharge about 80–90 cubic feet per second (value partly illegible in OCR).

**Behavior:** At both sites, atherinids were concentrated below riffles in moderate current or in eddies. They occupied mid-water and appeared to avoid the banks.

**Introduction pathways discussed:**
- Accidental transport of *Menidia* eggs or young in bait containers and water used to carry larger marine fishes for stocking.
- Promiscuous spawning in *Menidia* makes eggs available for accidental collection.
- Downstream movement via the [[Pecos River]] and the [[Rio Grande]] into northern Mexico.
- Short-distance transfers to the [[Colorado River]] basin.
- Increasing fish movement in Mexico associated with reservoir construction.

## Key Findings

1. **Specimen counts:** 134 *Menidia beryllina* were obtained at Las Varas; the Camargo count is not legible in the OCR. Standard lengths of the Las Varas specimens ranged from about 35 mm to more than 60 mm.

2. **Texas records:** *M. beryllina* was documented from Rio Grande localities (between Eagle Pass and Big Bend National Park, and near La Grange), Pecos River drainage (Imperial Reservoir, Crane County; Red Bluff Reservoir, Reeves and Loving counties), and Lake Brownwood (Brown County) on the Colorado River. Records from Tilton and White (1964) and Gunter (1945) were also cited.

3. **Mississippi Silverside:** Reported from some Red River and Colorado River areas. The author did not collect it in his own work (1957–1961). Once introduced, it apparently underwent an extensive population expansion, which later declined.

4. **Range question:** The author argues that the upstream Rio Grande distribution of *M. beryllina* is more likely the result of human transport than natural dispersal, although natural extension cannot be fully ruled out.

5. **Mexican Chirostoma:** *Chirostoma sphyraena* is endemic to the Rio Lerma basin (Pacific watershed of south-central Mexico). A single misidentified *Chirostoma* specimen from the Rio Conchos area may also represent introduction from the Lerma basin.

6. **Chirostoma aquaculture:** Sol6rzano (1961, 1963) studied *C. bartoni* and *C. estor* at Lake Pátzcuaro, where they are fished commercially. Attempts to introduce *Chirostoma* species outside their native range have been made. Fish were also inadvertently moved in holding-tank water intended for other species.

7. **Stocked marine fishes:** Species obtained for stocking from Texas coastal waters, cited in the text, include [[Red Drum]], [[Black Drum]], [[Atlantic Croaker]] and [[Southern Flounder]] (OCR-degraded names, interpreted here).

8. **Conclusion:** Atherinid records in inland Texas and northern Mexico are likely a product of human-mediated introduction through bait containers, stocking water and fish movement, with the Pecos River and Rio Grande as probable dispersal corridors.
