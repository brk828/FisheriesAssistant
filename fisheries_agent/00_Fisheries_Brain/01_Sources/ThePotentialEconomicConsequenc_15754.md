# Economic Consequences of Designating Critical Habitat for the Rio Grande Silvery Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This ECONorthwest draft report (February 1996), prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] New Mexico Ecological Services Field Office, estimates the economic consequences of designating critical habitat for the endangered [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (RGSM). The Service listed the species on July 20, 1994, and proposed designating the main stream from Cochiti Dam to San Marcial, New Mexico, as critical habitat. Designation affects the economy through one mechanism: federal agencies may not fund, authorize or carry out actions that would destroy or adversely modify the habitat. In practice only one agency, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BuRec), is materially affected, through its river-maintenance program.

The authors conclude that the net national effect is probably close to zero, although the evidence suggests net benefits are more likely than net costs. The report is a draft, to be revised after public comment.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The RGSM once ranged throughout the Rio Grande but now occupies a 170-mile stretch of the Middle Rio Grande, about 5 percent of its historic range. It is the sole survivor of a guild of five cyprinids that shared habitat and reproductive strategies. It has no commercial or recreational fishery value.
- **Listing vs. designation:** Listing (1994) protects against jeopardy and "take" for all parties. Designation is narrower, binding only federal agencies, and is intended to support recovery. Congress directed that economic consequences be considered in designation but not in listing.
- **Recovery plan:** Initiated September 20, 1994. The draft was due summer 1996 and the final plan January 1997.
- **Study framework:** The report uses a six-step approach and a with-versus-without comparison, and it includes an assessment of the static (fixed-economy) and dynamic (adjusting-economy) effects.
- **Hydrology:** Average inflow to the Middle Rio Grande is about 1,060,000 acre-feet (af) per year (1895–1993, standard deviation 525,000 af). Of this, about 352,000 af is consumed by agriculture, municipal use, seepage and evaporation. About 708,000 af reaches [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]].
- **Infrastructure:** Major structures include [[Cochiti Dam]], [[Abiquiu Dam]], [[El Vado Dam]], the [[San Juan-Chama Project]], three diversion dams, and the inoperable [[Low-Flow Conveyance Channel]].
- **Institutions:** The [[Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District]] (MRGCD) irrigates roughly 54,000 acres per year out of 123,267 acres claimed. New Mexico does not recognize instream water rights.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The designation would protect the [[Bosque]], a cottonwood riparian forest described as one of the largest in the world, along with riverine habitat and water quality.
- Natural flows are highly variable. Low-flow years (below 536,000 af) occurred 11 times in the 1950–1993 record.
- The river is heavily modified: flood-control structures have eliminated former seasonal floods that supplied nutrients and moisture to the floodplain. The bosque depends on natural flows.
- The Pueblo of Isleta has pursued water-quality improvements below Albuquerque, which were upheld by the State and the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]].
- Downstream of the critical habitat, riparian amenities are less prevalent, water quality is lower, and use is more intensive.

## Key Findings

**Negative effects**
- BuRec river-maintenance costs are $3–5 million per year (bank stabilization, sediment removal, vegetation control, levee maintenance). The designation could curtail up to 40 percent of the program, roughly $1 million, although the authors consider the actual share probably smaller.
- The maximum water affected is estimated at 4,000 af, less than 0.5 percent of inflow and about 1 percent of consumptive use.
- Impacts would most likely appear as reduced irrigation diversions rather than reduced instream flow.
- The probability that the designation causes a water shortage is less than 20 percent.
- Salvage cost of water through river maintenance is at least $250 per af, so replacement by MRGCD is unlikely to be cost-effective.
- Marginal value of irrigation water for pasture is $19 per af per acre. Water from BuRec projects has a marginal value of zero. The net earnings impact of 4,000 af is $0–76,000.
- Employment effect is not measurable, with a possible initial loss of about 8 jobs in pasture irrigation, offset by adjustment. The net effect is near zero.
- Municipal shortages are considered unlikely because water rights can be bought and sold, MRGCD can be funded, and pricing can encourage conservation.

**Positive effects**
- Non-use value: New Mexico households are willing to pay $30 per year for five years for minimum instream flows. This implies $1.7 million per year and $8.5 million over five years statewide. The authors note these figures are aggregates that include other fish and wildlife benefits, and they do not support a per-af value.
- Quality of life: the designation is expected to reinforce bosque protection, visual aesthetics, recreation and tribal ceremonial access to the river.
- Subsidy reduction: the BuRec program subsidizes irrigation and flood protection, creating incentives for low-value use. Removing it may encourage reallocation toward higher-value uses.
- Amenity value: a 2.5 billion dollar annual value on amenities is estimated from a 15 percent amenity-related wage differential (1994 wage base over $16 billion).

**Context and conclusions**
- The evidence is insufficient to determine whether benefits exceed costs, but benefits are likely to outweigh costs nationally.
- The overall net effect is probably near zero because gains and losses tend to offset across groups.
- The designation has no effect on private property rights, since it binds only federal actions.
- Other federal agencies (including the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] and the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]]) reported no designation-specific impact. The BuRec was the only agency affected, and it reported that construction and river maintenance could be delayed, suspended, made costlier or cancelled.
