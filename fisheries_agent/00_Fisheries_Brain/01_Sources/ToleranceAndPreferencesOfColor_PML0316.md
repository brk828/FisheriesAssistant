# Tolerance and Preferences of Colorado River Endangered Fishes to Selected Habitat Parameters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit report (Report No. 5, December 1981, Utah State University, Logan) is a compilation of studies on the endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its core focus is laboratory measurement of the temperature and total dissolved solids (TDS) preferences, baseline blood chemistry, and swimming stamina of [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The document is a multi-part volume whose table of contents also lists a [[Physical-Chemical Habitat Study]], a [[Striped Bass]] movement study, a [[Fish Disease Survey]], and several hatchery culture reports. The text here covers the preference, tolerance, and swimming studies in depth. Its stated purpose is to give water development agencies habitat criteria so that projects can avoid harming protected fishes.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River drainage holds several endangered and threatened native fishes. Little was known about their habitat requirements when the study was conducted. The authors identified water temperature, velocity, and total dissolved solids as the factors most likely to be altered by development projects. The objectives were to:

1. Determine temperature and TDS preferences of juvenile and adult fishes.
2. Determine baseline hematological parameters for each species.
3. Determine swimming stamina at various velocities and temperatures.
4. Determine effects of swimming and transport stress on certain blood parameters.

Fish were obtained from the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and held at the [[Utah Water Research Laboratory]] (UWRL) in Logan, under controlled photoperiods (14L:10D) and at acclimation temperatures of 14, 20, and 26 °C. Adult squawfish were held at the [[Fisheries Experiment Station]] before transfer. Several disease outbreaks, including columnaris, were treated with [[Terramycin]] and nitrofurazone, and diseased fish were excluded from testing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature preference.** Final preferendum was defined following Fry (1947) as the temperature toward which a fish eventually gravitates. It was measured with an electronic [[Shuttle Box]] for razorback suckers and a horizontal [[Thermal Gradient Trough]] for the smaller species. Results were reported as modal temperatures.

**TDS preference.** A multi-compartment gradient device was used with saline inputs representing Colorado River water and oil shale processing effluent. Fish were allowed 24 hours to choose compartments.

**Swimming stamina.** Fish were tested in a [[Swimming Stamina Tunnel]] (Thomas et al. 1964). Fatigue was defined as the point at which a fish could no longer avoid a mild electric field (5–10 volts).

**Ecological implications.** The authors argue that reduced temperature and velocity tolerance, combined with cold reservoir tailwaters, would make these fishes avoid parts of the river. Their preferred temperatures are close to historical summer river temperatures. The authors link this to the disappearance of native fishes from the Green River below [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]]. They also note that squawfish, the most TDS-sensitive species, could be affected by evaporative salt increases in reservoirs and by oil shale development.

## Key Findings

**Temperature preference**
- Razorback sucker final preferendum: 23–24 °C, based on 20 and 26 °C acclimated fish. The geometric mean of individual modes across all four acclimation temperatures was 24.9 °C. Razorbacks acclimated to 8 °C had difficulty operating the shuttle box (only 4 of 20 fish succeeded).
- Humpback chub final preferendum: 24 °C.
- Bonytail chub final preferendum: 24.2 °C.
- Razorback, humpback, and bonytail chubs had nearly identical final preferenda (23–24.2 °C). Hybrid chub acute preferendum at 20 °C was 23.8 °C.
- Juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] final preferendum: 24.6 °C (plot method) or 25.5 °C (linear regression).
- Adult squawfish final preferendum: 25.4 °C (plot) or 25.3 °C (regression). Adults were not significantly different from juveniles in acute preferendum.
- Juvenile acute preferenda ranged from 21.9 to 27.6 °C. Adult acute preferenda ranged from 21.5 to 25.7 °C.
- Of the native Colorado fishes tested, acute preferred temperatures ranged from 18–29 °C (modes), consistent with historical summer temperatures in the basin.

**Egg temperature tolerance**
- Colorado squawfish: one lot developed only at 20 °C (50% normal hatch, 5-day incubation). Two other lots did not develop.
- Bonytail eggs: no hatching at 5, 10, 31, or 37 °C; 50–70% normal hatch at 14 °C; 40–100% at 20 °C; 70–90% at 26 °C.
- Humpback chub eggs: best hatch at 20 °C (100%, 3-day incubation) and 26 °C (90–100%).

**Total dissolved solids (TDS) preference**
- Colorado squawfish were the most mobile species in the gradient. They preferred the lowest TDS (modal 1.0–1.4 mmhos, about 0.6–1.1 mg/mL) and avoided TDS above 3.6 mmhos. Ninety-seven percent of 90 fish avoided that range.
- Bonytail chubs preferred 3.4–3.8 mmhos (4.1–4.7 mg/mL), with avoidance above approximately 5.1 mmhos.
- Humpback chubs preferred 1.3–3.0 mmhos in pooled treatment results. About 60% of 220 fish were in this range after 24 hours. No fish selected TDS above 9.4 mmhos.
- The authors predict that squawfish would be the species most affected by TDS increases from reservoir evaporation or oil shale effluent.

**Hematology (baseline)**
- Juvenile razorback suckers (n = 60; 15–30 cm) showed no sex or length differences. Hematocrit averaged 33.5%, hemoglobin 7.5 g/100 mL, and chloride 108 mEq/L. Glucose averaged 52.7 mg/100 mL.
- Juvenile squawfish (n = 31): hematocrit 38%, hemoglobin 7.5 g/100 mL, mean chloride 99.1 mEq/L.
- Juvenile humpback chub: hematocrit 30%, hemoglobin 7.2 g/100 mL. Thrombocytes were the most abundant white cell type (52.4%).
- Juvenile bonytail: hematocrit 34.5%, hemoglobin 7.1 g/100 mL.
- Hemoglobin and total protein measured by a refractometer were higher than spectrophotometric values. The authors judged the hand-held meter useful for trend monitoring.
- Transport stress: glucose rose from 54 to 144 mg/100 mL after a 15-hour haul. Chloride fell from 108 to 99 mEq/L, a change that was not statistically significant. No post-haul mortality occurred. The authors suggest salt could reduce osmoregulatory stress during transport.

**Swimming ability**
- Razorback suckers: could cruise for about 2 hours at 0.15–0.3 m/s. They could only sustain about 1 minute at 0.6 m/s. Larger fish performed significantly better (p = 0.05). Temperature did not significantly affect performance.
- Juvenile squawfish: sustained swimming for 1 hour or more at about 0.39 m/s. No fish swam more than a minute at 0.57 m/s. Performance was significantly better at 20 and 26 °C than at 14 °C.
- Adult squawfish: sustained swimming for about 1 hour at 0.9 m/s, roughly twice the stamina of juveniles. Temperature and length did not significantly affect time to fatigue.
- Juvenile bonytail: sustained swimming at 0.45 m/s (14 °C), 0.56 m/s (20 °C), and 0.63 m/s (26 °C). Performance was significantly affected by temperature.
- Humpback chub: about 2 hours at 0.32 m/s, but only a few minutes at 0.78 m/s. Ability was significantly and positively related to temperature.
- Applied guidance from the report: culvert passage distance is D = 60VT. The report gives 0.15 m/s as the maximum acceptable velocity for protecting small fish. It suggests that at 0.3 m/s at a powerplant intake, all species tested could avoid being overcome.

**Recommendations**
- Establish normal hematological profiles for each hatchery, since diet and temperature affect results.
- Use the refractometer for field and hatchery trend monitoring, and the spectrophotometer where accuracy is critical.
- Test swimming and preference responses in larger gradient troughs, since horizontal gradients are prone to positional effects.
