# Life History and Ecology of the Humpback Chub in the Little Colorado River and Colorado River, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Kaeding and Zimmerman]] (1983), published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, examines the life history and ecology of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), an endemic and federally listed cyprinid of the [[Colorado River]] system in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. Fieldwork in the [[Little Colorado River]] and adjacent Colorado River reaches (1980–1981) showed that the Little Colorado is the primary spawning and nursery area, while the regulated, cold mainstream Colorado below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] does not support recruitment. The authors conclude that the population persists largely because of reproduction in the Little Colorado River.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), a Colorado River cyprinid described from Grand Canyon specimens ([[Miller 1946]]).
- **Historic context:** The native fish community included several endemic species, a number of which are now formally listed as rare or endangered, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. None of the three was collected during this study.
- **Environmental change:** Closure of Glen Canyon Dam (1963) and Flaming Gorge Dam altered the thermal regime and sediment load of the Colorado River, eliminating reproduction for many native species.
- **Study area:** The Little Colorado River from its perennial source to Blue Spring (about 20 km), the Colorado River reach from about 3 km upstream to about 20 km downstream of the confluence, and the confluence itself (C 4).
- **Methods:** Sampling used minnow traps, seines (day and dark), trammel nets, and electrofishing; fish were aged from scales, sexed by external urogenital characteristics, and tagged for mark–recapture. Temperature and turbidity were recorded with thermographs and field instruments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Physicochemistry:** Conditions differed markedly between rivers. The Little Colorado was cooler in summer and warmer in winter than the Colorado, which remained cold and clear. Dissolved oxygen was near saturation except in some upstream areas. Conductivity and salinity were much higher in the Little Colorado than in the Colorado; turbidity was high in floods and during spring runoff.
- **Hydrology:** The Little Colorado was formerly perennial upstream to Grand Falls (about 120 km above the confluence) but has been seasonally dewatered since about 1900. Travertine dams between LC 1 and LC 2 may have blocked upstream fish movement.
- **Thermal limits:** Temperatures in the Colorado approached levels that preclude appreciable embryo and larval survival; [[Hamman 1982]] reported only 12% of fertilized eggs hatched at Colorado-like temperatures.
- **Community:** Fourteen fish species and one hybrid were recorded. Only humpback chub, speckled dace, bluehead sucker, and fathead minnow occurred in large numbers in the Little Colorado. Channel catfish showed bite-mark wounds on adult humpback chub, suggesting predation.
- **Diet:** Stomach contents were dominated numerically by immature Chironomidae and Simuliidae; Gammarus was also important in the Colorado samples.
- **Parasites and disease:** Thirteen bacterial species, six protozoans, and the fungus Saprolegnia were found at low incidence. The copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] was most prevalent in winter. Acute systemic [[Aeromonas hydrophila]] infection with abundant petechiae was observed in fish from the lower Little Colorado during spawning.

## Key Findings

- **Age and growth:** Scale annuli were useful for Little Colorado fish but unreliable for Colorado fish, because some fish formed an annulus late in their first year and others did not. Annual growth was largest in the first 3 years, reaching 250–300 mm total length.
- **Maturity:** Female sexual maturity began at about 250–300 mm, roughly 3 years of age in the Little Colorado. Of males collected in April 1981, 15 of 19 (79%) at 200–249 mm and 26 of 35 (74%) at 250–300 mm expressed milt; the smallest male with milt was 205 mm.
- **Spawning:** Gonad development was rapid from December to February–April, with spawning in April–May. Ovum diameter and gonadosomatic index patterns in the Colorado were similar to those in the Little Colorado, and one ripe male was found in the Colorado in May 1981.
- **Recruitment:** No humpback chub shorter than 140 mm were collected in the Colorado upstream of the confluence, even though mature fish were present. The authors conclude that the small fish found in the Colorado originated from spawning in the Little Colorado River.
- **Tagging:** Of 17 of 113 (15%) fish tagged in the Colorado and 12 of 242 (5%) tagged in the Little Colorado, recaptures were made. Recaptured fish moved up to 17.1 km. Nine of ten fish that moved more than 0.3 km upstream had been tagged in spawning seasons, suggesting upstream migration to spawning areas.
- **Catch statistics:** Age-0 catch rates were higher at night than during the day, and higher in turbid water during high-flow periods; seines likely underestimated small fish when the water was clear. Catch rates of age-0 fish were lower in 1981 than in 1980.
- **Meristics:** Dorsal/anal fin-ray counts of 10/10 were more common in the lower Little Colorado and confluence, suggesting possible introgression with [[Bonytail]] genes, following [[Holden and Stalnaker 1970]].
- **Conservation implications:** The authors argue that the species' persistence depends on the Little Colorado as a reproductive refuge. Humpback chub reproduction in the Colorado is improbable given temperature and water-level fluctuations, and the reach's value could be threatened by colonization of nonnative species or by changes in flow.
