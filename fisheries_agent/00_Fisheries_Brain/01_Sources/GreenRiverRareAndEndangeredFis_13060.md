# Green River Rare and Endangered Fish Studies, 1982-1985

#ichthyology #endangered-species #cyprinidae #catostomidae #riverine-ecology #flow-management

## Executive Summary

This final report synthesizes findings from the [[Colorado River Fishes Monitoring Project]] (1982-1985) and related studies conducted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Vernal Field Station in the [[Green River Basin]] from 1979-1986. The study focused primarily on three federally endangered species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Research employed radiotelemetry, standardized electrofishing surveys, larval drift sampling, and habitat assessment to document distribution, spawning behavior, migration patterns, and habitat requirements. Key findings include confirmation of two major spawning areas for Colorado squawfish (lower [[Yampa Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]]), documentation of extensive spawning migrations (up to 400 km), identification of critical nursery habitats, and evidence that abnormally high late-summer flows from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] negatively impact young-of-year survival. The razorback sucker population appears to lack successful recruitment despite confirmed spawning. The study provides foundational data for flow management recommendations and species recovery planning in the Upper Colorado River Basin.

## Background & Description

### Study Context and Authorization

The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (P.L. 93-205) mandated that Federal agencies consult with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service regarding potential effects of planned projects on listed species. Water resource development in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] prompted cooperative investigations by FWS, [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and the states of Colorado and Utah from 1979-1986. The [[Colorado River Fishes Project]] (CRFP, 1979-1981) initiated baseline studies, followed by the Colorado River Fishery Monitoring Program (CRFMP, 1982-1985) which expanded radiotelemetry work and flow-recruitment investigations.

### Study Area Geography

The [[Green River]] Subbasin comprises approximately 116,000 km² of the 284,000 km² Upper Colorado River Basin. The study area extended from Echo Park in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (River Mile 345) to the confluence with the [[Colorado River]] in [[Canyonlands National Park]] (RM 0), encompassing approximately 555 km of mainstem and major tributaries including the [[Yampa River]], [[White River]], and [[Duchesne River]]. The river flows through diverse geologic formations including [[Whirlpool Canyon]], [[Split Mountain Canyon]], [[Desolation Canyon]], [[Gray Canyon]], and [[Labyrinth Canyon]].

### Target Species

**Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): The largest minnow native to North America, historically reaching over 1.8 m in length. Fossils similar to existing native fishes trace to the middle Pliocene (approximately 8 million years ago), reflecting long evolutionary adaptation to the Colorado River system.

**Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): A large-bodied catostomid characterized by a prominent dorsal keel. Green River specimens ranged 426-608 mm TL, smaller than lower basin populations.

**Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): One of three *Gila* species in the basin, distinguished by pronounced nuchal hump and specialized morphology. Comprised approximately 28% of *Gila* spp. captured in Green River.

**Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*): Extremely rare; only one suspected specimen captured during the study period.

### Methodological Approach

The study employed multiple complementary methods:

1. **Spring Electrofishing Surveys** (April-June): Standardized catch-per-unit-effort (C/E) sampling across defined river strata using Coffelt VVP-15 equipment. A total of 419 Colorado squawfish captured 1980-1985.

2. **Radiotelemetry**: 114 Colorado squawfish and 10 razorback suckers implanted with transmitters (Smith-Root and AVM units, 40.660-40.700 MHz range). Surgical implantation following anesthesia with tricain methane sulfonate. Tracking conducted weekly during spawning season, with intensive diel sampling in 1981.

3. **Larval Sampling**: Systematic seine collections (0.8 mm mesh) in backwaters and shorelines to identify spawning sites and nursery areas. Drift net sampling (1 m diameter, 0.76 mm² mesh) conducted in 1982 to assess larval transport.

4. **Young-of-Year (YOY) Surveys**: Fall seining (September-October) with standardized 3×5 mm² mesh nets to assess recruitment success and nursery habitat use.

5. **Spawning Collections**: Trammel netting at suspected spawning areas during July-August to collect ripe adults and confirm reproductive activity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Hydrologic Regime and Dam Influence

[[Flaming Gorge Dam]], completed in 1963 at RM 407, fundamentally altered the Green River's natural flow and temperature regime. The dam operates for hydropower storage, reducing natural seasonal maximum flows while increasing late-season discharge above historic low flows. Original penstock intakes at 190 ft depth were retrofitted with a selective intake device allowing withdrawal from 20-190 ft depths, primarily to enhance the tailwater trout fishery. This results in cooler summer temperatures and warmer winter temperatures than natural conditions.

The [[Yampa River]] remains the largest free-flowing tributary, providing critical thermal and hydrologic amelioration to the regulated Green River below their confluence. Mean annual discharge of the Yampa is approximately 1.5 million acre-feet, with natural snowmelt-driven peaks in May-June (often exceeding 10,000 cfs) and low flows (100-500 cfs) August-March.

### Habitat Classification

The study employed standardized habitat and substrate classifications:

**Habitat types**: Main channel, backwater (shallow ephemeral embayments <3 ft deep, no measurable velocity), side channel, eddy (distinct whirlpool or counter-current), pool, riffle, run, shoreline, flooded bottom.

**Substrate types**: Silt, sand (<3 mm), gravel (3-76 mm), rubble/cobble (76-305 mm), boulder (>305 mm), bedrock.

### Community Structure and Non-Native Species

The native fish community has been substantially altered by water development and non-native species introductions. Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) are abundant in some reaches; two adult Colorado squawfish were collected with channel catfish lodged in their throats (Stratum C, 1982-1983), suggesting potential food limitation or inappropriate prey selection. Non-native species including black bullhead (*Ictalurus melas*) and green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) may suppress native fishes through predation on young, potentially contributing to recruitment failure in razorback sucker.

### Temperature Regime

Water temperature is critical to reproductive success. Spawning temperature data for Colorado squawfish:
- **Prespawning migration initiation**: 14-20°C (Yampa), 16-20°C (Green)
- **Spawning period**: Average 22°C (range 15-27.5°C)
- **Main channel maximum during spawning**: 25-27.5°C

Laboratory studies (Marsh 1985) found highest razorback sucker embryo survival at 20°C, with significantly lower hatch at 15°C and complete mortality at 5-10°C. Field measurements in 1983 showed a 10°C temperature difference between the Yampa and Green rivers during peak larval drift, potentially stressing larvae and contributing to poor recruitment.

Backwater nursery habitats averaged 22.8°C in fall 1984, compared to 19.5°C in adjacent mainstem, suggesting larvae actively seek warmer, more productive habitats.

## Key Findings

### Colorado Squawfish Distribution and Abundance

**Adult Distribution**: Spring electrofishing (1980-1985) captured 329 adults (>400 mm TL) and 90 juveniles (61-400 mm TL). Highest adult catch rates occurred in upper Green River strata:
- Stratum E: 1.10 fish/hour (average)
- Stratum F: 0.62 fish/hour
- Stratum G: 0.65 fish/hour
- Combined Green River average: 0.53 fish/hour

**Juvenile Distribution**: Juveniles concentrated in lower Green River:
- Stratum A: 0.74 fish/hour
- Stratum B: 0.18 fish/hour
- Overall juvenile average: 0.17 fish/hour

No juveniles were collected in the lower [[Duchesne River]]. Statistical analysis indicated that adult catch rates in Stratum A were not significantly different from Strata B, C, D, and F (Student's t, p>0.2), suggesting broader habitat use than initially apparent.

### Spawning Migrations and Homing Behavior

**Major Discovery**: Radiotelemetry documented extensive spawning migrations to two confirmed spawning areas:

1. **Lower Yampa Canyon** (RM 0-20): Confirmed 1981, verified annually through 1985
2. **Gray Canyon, Green River** (RM 145-160): Confirmed 1983

**Migration Patterns**: Of 123 fish tracked with adequate seasonal coverage:
- 40 classified as migratory (moved ≥32 km to spawning areas)
- 12 non-migratory (<32 km movement)
- 63% of seasonally tracked fish were highly mobile

**Migration Distances**: Ranged from 40-400 km (mean 138 km for Yampa spawners). Fish exhibited both upstream and downstream movements (positive and negative rheotaxis) to reach spawning sites.

**Homing Fidelity**: Multiple lines of evidence demonstrate homing behavior:
- Individual fish (e.g., #3057, #1642) returned to same spawning site in multiple years
- Recapture data: 37 of 101 tagged fish (37%) captured both during and outside spawning periods; 81% of these traveled >32 km
- Tag returns confirmed fish tagged in upper Yampa or Green rivers returned to lower Yampa spawning grounds in subsequent years

**Proposed Mechanism**: The [[Groundwater Seepage Hypothesis]] suggests olfactory imprinting on groundwater inputs at spawning sites. Both confirmed spawning areas receive input from spring-fed tributaries and sandstone/limestone seeps ([[Florence Creek]] in Gray Canyon, [[Warm Springs Creek]] in Yampa Canyon), providing potential chemical cues for orientation.

### Spawning Habitat and Behavior

**Habitat Requirements**: Spawning occurs in canyon reaches with interspersed deep pools/eddies and cobble bars. Two-phase spawning behavior identified:

1. **Resting-Staging Phase** (pools/large eddies):
   - Mean depth: 6.98 ft (2.13 m)
   - Mean velocity: 1.21 ft/s (0.37 m/s)
   - Function: Resting, feeding between spawning forays; male aggregation around females

2. **Deposition-Fertilization Phase** (cobble bars):
   - Mean depth: 3.09 ft (0.94 m)
   - Mean velocity: 1.58 ft/s (0.48 m/s)
   - Substrate: Gravel to boulder; average cobble volume 1,560 cm³ (Yampa site)

**Yampa River Spawning Reach**: Activity concentrated RM 15-18.8, characterized by large deep pools and eddies interspersed with runs and cobble bars. Physical habitat simulation (PHABSIM) modeling indicated optimal flows of 7,350 cfs (June), 1,600 cfs (July), 400 cfs (August) for the Cleopatra site (RM 16.5).

**Spawning Phenology**: 
- **Migration initiation**: Late May to mid-June (water temperature 14-20°C)
- **Optimal spawning period**: Approximately 3-4 weeks, typically late June through early August
- **Timing variation**: Earlier in low-water years (1981: June 29-July 8), later in high-flow years (1983-1984: July 20-August 10)

**Sex Ratio Bias**: Of 160 tuberculated fish captured on spawning grounds, 120 were ripe males and only 7 ripe females (17:1 ratio). Ripe males averaged 559 mm TL; ripe females averaged 710 mm TL (significantly larger, p<0.01). This bias may reflect differential mortality, unequal sex ratio at hatching, or differential capture vulnerability.

### Larval Ecology and Downstream Transport

**Spawning Site Confirmation**: Larval collections confirmed two major spawning areas and suggested seven additional suspected sites. Standardized seine sampling (1981, 1984, 1985) collected larvae throughout the Green River system.

**Downstream Drift**: Larvae are transported downstream from spawning sites during the 72-96 hour planktonic phase before becoming nektonic. Peak larval abundance in nursery areas occurred at the end of flood period, suggesting larvae utilize river transport for dispersal to productive downstream habitats.

**Nursery Area Identification**: Two major nursery areas identified in reaches with abundant ephemeral backwater habitat but no apparent spawning habitat:
- Upper nursery: RM 200-280 (Ouray-Sand Wash area)
- Lower nursery: RM 60-110 (Labyrinth Canyon area)

**Habitat Selection**: Larvae more abundant in shoreline seine collections than midchannel drift samples. Drift-captured larvae were predominantly protolarvae (young), while seined larvae were metalarvae (older), indicating active movement into warmer, more productive backwater habitats.

**Spawning Date Back-Calculation**: Using relationships developed by Haynes and Muth (1984), spawning dates estimated from larval size:
- 1981: June 19-July 12 (Yampa/upper Green); June 19-July 11 (lower Green)
- 1983: July 25-August 14 (Yampa/upper Green); August 14-24 (lower Green)
- 1984: July 19-August 16 (Yampa/upper Green); July 15-31 (lower Green)
- 1985: July 4-August 2 (Yampa/upper Green); June 21-July 10 (lower Green)

### Recruitment Success and Flow Relationships

**Year Class Strength Variation**: Fall YOY surveys (1979-1985) revealed dramatic inter-annual variation in recruitment:

| Year | Date | N Fish | C/E (fish/1000 m²) | Aug-Sept Mean Discharge (cfs) |
|------|------|--------|-------------------|-------------------------------|
| 1979 | 10/10 | 1,813 | 232.9 | 2,011 |
| 1980 | 10/7-8 | 1,576 | 270.6 | 1,922 |
| 1981 | 9/14-15 | 488 | 120.8 | 2,144 |
| 1982 | 10/5 | 641 | 121.6 | 2,983 |
| 1983 | 10/20-11/9 | 36 | 5.4 | 4,806 |
| 1984 | 10/9-28 | 27 | 7.6 | 4,525 |
| 1985 | 10/1-29 | 223 | 40.9 | 2,239 |

**Critical Finding**: Catch-per-effort negatively correlated with mean August-September discharge (r = -0.73, p = 0.06). Approximately 80% of variation in C/E correlated with mean daily discharge during the nursery period. High flows in late summer (1983-1984) resulted in near-complete recruitment failure.

**Growth Impacts**: Mean total length of larvae at end of growing season reduced by 27% in high-discharge years (1983-1984: 29-35 mm) relative to favorable flow years (1979-1980: 36-44 mm). Mean total length negatively correlated with August-September discharge (r = -0.87, p = 0.05).

**Mechanism**: High flows during August-September inundate and destabilize backwater nursery habitats. Measurements at three sites showed surface elevation changes of 1.79-2.12 ft between 800 cfs and 4,000 cfs releases from Flaming Gorge Dam. All backwater habitats measured at 800 cfs became flow-throughs at 4,000 cfs, eliminating nursery habitat.

**Temperature Effects**: Low temperatures may also stress larvae. In 1983 (poor recruitment year), Green River temperatures during peak larval drift were 10°C cooler than Yampa River. Laboratory studies (C. Berry, Utah State University) found larval Colorado squawfish stressed but not immediately killed by 10°C temperature decrease; stressed larvae more susceptible to predation and transport into unfavorable habitats.

**Optimal Conditions**: 1980 exhibited hydrographic conditions most beneficial to successful reproduction and recruitment, with moderate spring flows and lower late-summer discharge.

### Adult Habitat Use and Movement

**Microhabitat Preferences**: Adult Colorado squawfish most commonly found in eddies and runs along shorelines over sand and silt substrates. Visual observations indicated selection of sheltered microhabitats behind boulders and heavy use of eddy-run interfaces
