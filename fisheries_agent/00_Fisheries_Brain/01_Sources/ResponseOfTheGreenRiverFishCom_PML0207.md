# Response of the Green River Fish Community to Flow and Temperature Changes from Flaming Gorge Dam (1996 to 2004)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program Project 115, March 2006) by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] evaluates how changes in [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations affected physical habitat and the fish community of the regulated Green River from Browns Park to Island-Rainbow Park. The main comparison is between 1994 to 1996 and 2002 to 2004 sampling periods, placed within pre-dam and earlier post-dam historical context. The study used [[Electrofishing]], [[Seine Sampling]], [[Trammel Net Sampling]] and [[Drift Net Sampling]].

The authors conclude that the net effect of flow and temperature regimes on the native fish community was mixed. Native fish declined sharply in Lodore Canyon, while non-native fishes, particularly [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]] and small-bodied cyprinids, expanded. Results apply only to the low-flow, warm-summer conditions of drought years, since average and wet years were not sampled.

## Background & Description

Dam-related events affecting the upper Green River were identified as: dam installation (1962), penstock modification (1978), flow re-regulation (1992), and higher peak flows, lower base flows and drought since 1997. Historically the river had high spring peaks and cold, stable base flows. Post-dam, June mean maximum flow fell from 381 m³/s to 139 m³/s, and sediment transport decreased by 54%.

The study area ran from Swinging Bridge in Browns Park to the downstream end of Island-Rainbow Park. The [[Yampa River]] is a largely unregulated tributary joining the Green at Echo Park and serves as a source pool of native fishes and a source of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] larvae.

The study set 13 objectives, including determining whether shifts in large- and small-bodied fish distribution occurred, whether Colorado pikeminnow spawn upstream of the Yampa confluence, and characterizing a [[Humpback Chub]] population in [[Whirlpool Canyon]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and temperature regimes.** Flow variability was reduced, base flows were more stable, and summer temperatures in Lodore Canyon in 2002 to 2004 were more similar to pre-dam conditions. Summer mean maximum temperatures in the Green River were 17.0 to 18.7 °C, with daytime highs reaching 25 °C in July 2002. The Green River was within 5 °C of the Yampa River on all but four days in 2000 to 2004, and those four days occurred in August 2002. Such temperature matching reduces cold-shock risk for drifting pikeminnow larvae.

**Flow recommendations.** [[Muth et al. 2000]] proposed flow and temperature recommendations for five hydrologic conditions. Spring peaks range from about 130 m³/s (dry years) to more than 244 m³/s (wet years), and base flows range from 23 to 28 m³/s (dry) to 79 to 85 m³/s (wet). Summer temperatures of 18 to 20 °C were targeted in Lodore Canyon. The 2002 to 2004 conditions fell only within low and moderately low water-availability years, which occur about 30% of the time.

**Predation and invasion.** Red shiner predation on catostomid larvae and fathead minnow predation on sucker larvae were identified as likely mechanisms for reduced native recruitment. Turbidity and high-flow events displaced fish, including young smallmouth bass, downstream.

**Historical context.** Eleven native and six introduced fishes were recorded pre-impoundment (1959 to 1960). Native reproduction fell sharply after 1967, and penstock modification in 1978 restored reproduction for most native species.

## Key Findings

**Community composition (2002 to 2004, all gears combined)**
- Total catch: 209,466 specimens, comprising 9 native and 19 non-native species plus 7 hybrid combinations.
- Native fishes made up 10.3% of the catch, non-natives 89.3%, and hybrids 0.4%.
- Seine samples were 92.4% non-native; trammel nets captured about two-thirds native fish.
- Most common native fishes: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]].
- Most abundant non-natives: [[Red Shiner]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Redside Shiner]], [[White Sucker]], [[Common Carp]].

**Lodore Canyon trends (1994 to 1996 vs. 2002 to 2004)**
- Native fish percentage (electrofishing) fell from 63% to 39%.
- Native fish percentage (seine) fell from 69% to 14%.
- Catch rates (fish/h) decreased significantly for bluehead sucker (16.6 to 10.1), flannelmouth sucker (29.5 to 17.9), roundtail chub (0.3 to 0.1), speckled dace (1.4 to 0.3) and mountain whitefish (4.2 to 1.1).
- Mottled sculpin was the only native fish to increase in electrofishing catch.

**Non-native expansion**
- Channel catfish catch rate rose from 1.3 to 6.1 fish/h, a significant increase.
- Smallmouth bass rose from a single fish captured by electrofishing in 1994 to 1996 to a widespread population; reproduction was first documented in 2002 and expanded upstream through 2005.
- Red shiner expanded 925% and sand shiner 352% in Lodore Canyon, with a 243% rise in fathead minnow.
- Brown trout catch rate was similar (16.4 vs. 15.0 fish/h); northern pike CPUE was 0.2 fish/h in both periods.

**Colorado pikeminnow**
- 37 individuals were captured in 2002 to 2004 (mean 573 mm TL); captures were made throughout Lodore and Whirlpool canyons.
- Electrofishing catch rate was about 0.4 fish/h in 2002 to 2004, against 0.6 fish/h in 1994 to 1996 (decline not statistically significant).
- Drift net sampling detected no larvae in any year, but ripe male pikeminnow were found in Lodore Canyon in 2001 and 2003, suggesting possible spawning.

**Humpback chub, roundtail chub and bonytail (Whirlpool Canyon)**
- Eight humpback chub were captured in 2002 to 2004; no young were found.
- Estimated roundtail chub abundance in Whirlpool Canyon (autumn 2003, [[Mark Model]]) was 1,956 (95% profile likelihood interval 1,214 to 3,546).
- Hatchery bonytail were captured after 2002 and 2004 stocking; survival appeared short-term, and most fish stocked in June 2005 were in poor condition, with Lernea infections.

**Hybridization**
- Hybrid suckers were 4.3% of all fish in Lodore Canyon in 2002 to 2004 and increased overall since 1980.
- Putative [[Razorback Sucker]] × white sucker hybrids were found in drift nets in 2003 and 2004 and may be the first documented hybridization of these species.

**Sampling methods and statistics**
- Electrofishing catch rates were significantly higher in autumn (3.05 fish/h) than summer (1.72 fish/h) in 2002 to 2004.
- Turbidity did not significantly reduce electrofishing catch rates.
- Seine density was highest in Whirlpool Canyon (WH1 mean 4.14 fish/10 m²) and lowest in Lodore Canyon reach LD2 (0.71 fish/10 m²).

**Management recommendations**
- Continue annual monitoring of water temperature, flows and fish community.
- Add real-time temperature stations at Echo Park and above the Yampa confluence, or model temperature differences.
- Conduct electrofishing in summer and autumn; seine in summer and autumn; avoid spring sampling.
- Use trammel nets for chub monitoring in late summer or autumn.
- PIT-tag all chubs and continue removal of predaceous non-native fishes, especially smallmouth bass, channel catfish and northern pike.
- Investigate causes of low survival in stocked bonytail.

**Conclusions**
- Drought-period flows and temperatures moved the regulated reach toward pre-dam conditions, but that restoration favored non-native predators and competitors.
- Additional years under average or wet conditions are needed before the effects of the new Flaming Gorge recommendations can be fully assessed.
