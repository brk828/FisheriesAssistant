# Results of Planting Kokanee Salmon in Two Adirondack Mountain Lakes, New York

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 note in the *Progressive Fish-Culturist* by Dwight A. Webster and William Flick (Cornell University) reports the results of experimental plantings of [[Kokanee Salmon]] fry in two small New York lakes. In [[Black Pond]], the aim was to test kokanee as a forage fish for [[Landlocked Salmon]]. The test was judged inconclusive. Kokanee grew well but did not establish a self-sustaining population, and the landlocked salmon declined in condition. In [[Third Bisby Lake]], a single planting produced adults that spawned naturally in an inlet stream, and the authors planned further plantings to build a self-perpetuating population.

## Background & Description

Kokanee, or dwarf sockeye salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), were planted in several New York lakes to provide angling. They can also serve as forage for other salmonids in western North American lakes. All kokanee stock came from [[Flathead Lake]], Montana. Eyed eggs from three year-classes (1955, 1956, 1957) were hatched at the [[Cornell University]] Experimental Fish Hatchery.

- **1955 year-class:** planted as advanced fry shortly after they began feeding (May 4, 1955).
- **1956 and 1957 year-classes:** planted as 1-inch fingerlings in late May or early June.
- All fish were released in several "schools" in the open waters of the lakes.

The study used [[Trap Netting]], [[Gill Netting]], angling records, and a [[Rotenone Application]] to evaluate survival and growth.

### Black Pond

Black Pond lies on the [[Brandon Preserve]] in Franklin County. It covers 25 acres, has a maximum depth of 58 feet, and is thermally stratified, with a severe oxygen deficit developing by late summer. The watershed is limited, and the intermittent outlet goes underground a short distance from the discharge. Resident fishes were [[Landlocked Salmon]], brook trout, [[Lake Trout]], common sucker, [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and three minnow species. [[Alewife]] were introduced in spring 1958 to obtain preliminary data on their establishment as forage.

### Third Bisby Lake

Third Bisby Lake is one of a chain of four Bisby Lakes on the [[Adirondack League Club]], Herkimer County. It covers 44 acres, has a maximum depth of 40 feet, and is yellow-brown in color, with an oxygen deficit below 35 feet. The fish community included lake trout, a remnant population of frostfish, [[Smallmouth Bass]], and considerable numbers of suckers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Black Pond**
- The 1955 kokanee year-class was not fully mature by age II+, and some fish matured at age I+ and others at age II+. This divided age at maturity suggests variable growth and maturation under local conditions.
- Growth slowed sharply in the 1956 year-class. Adults of the 1955 year-class averaged about 13.1 inches, while a sample of 1956 fish averaged 10.1 inches and 0.56 pound.
- During rotenone treatment, dissolved oxygen fell from 4.0 ppm at 25 feet to 0.5 ppm at 30 feet. Surface water temperature was 57°F. Kokanee moved into an area of toxic water at the southwest end of the pond, and the authors attributed the concentration partly to this oxygen gradient.
- The authors suggested that kokanee's efficient use of plankton contributed to the decline in landlocked salmon condition. Predation on newly planted fry was probably substantial.

**Third Bisby Lake**
- Spawning kokanee were observed in the inlet stream. The stream bottom consisted mostly of large, well-consolidated rubble, which allowed only imperfect redds to be built. Many eggs lay loose on the bottom, indicating they had not lodged in shallow egg pits.
- No lake spawning occurred along the limited gravel shoreline.

## Key Findings

### Black Pond results

- **Kokanee recovery (rotenone, October 4, 1958):** Total recovered weight was 227 pounds, a minimum standing crop of about 9 pounds per acre. This was likely close to the maximum present, but deep-water fish could not be counted.
- **Recovered age groups:** 186 mature fish of the 1955 year-class and 252 fish of the 1956 year-class (age I+).
- **1957 trap netting (November 3–12):** 40 mature males and 9 green females of the 1955 year-class. The largest males measured 14.2 inches and 0.9 pound. Two juveniles (11.2 inches) were also taken, along with five juveniles of the 1956 year-class averaging 8.3 inches and 0.21 pound.
- **Gill netting (May 18, 1958):** 27 kokanee from the two year-classes were caught. Immature 1955 fish were 11.9 inches, compared with 13.5 inches for the fish that matured the previous autumn.
- **Failed year-class:** No recoveries of the 1957 year-class were made. The authors presumed the planting was a complete failure. The fry were in poor condition after transport.
- **Natural reproduction:** Seven fingerling kokanee from the 1958 year-class, naturally spawned in Black Pond, averaged 2.5 inches. Spawning sites were unknown.
- **Angling:** Only three kokanee were taken by angling, with negligible fishing pressure aimed at them. Intensive gill netting for a week before rotenone treatment caught just one kokanee, because the fish stayed off bottom and offshore.
- **Landlocked salmon:** 1,000 fry were planted in June 1953, followed by 250 fin-clipped 3-inch yearlings in June 1954. Of the 42 salmon angled in 1957, all but one came from the marked planting. The 28 measured averaged 17.3 inches and 1.2 pounds. Nine mature salmon from the 1957 trap net averaged 16.9 inches and 1.3 pounds. By 1958, the mean weight had dropped to 0.81 pound at about 17.2 inches, showing deteriorating condition.

### Third Bisby Lake results

- **Planting:** A single planting of 5,000 kokanee was made on May 4, 1955.
- **Angling:** No fish were taken until 1958, when two were caught on spinning lures and others were hooked. Fishing pressure was light and access difficult, so availability to anglers could not be fully tested.
- **Spawning run (autumn 1958):** No fish were seen on November 1. On November 21, four pairs were spawning in the inlet. Eleven kokanee taken by seine or gill net averaged 17.3 inches (range 16.8–18.0 inches) and 1.8 pounds. All females were gravid or nearly so, and some were partly spent.
- **Age:** A fish taken in July 1959 had four annuli, indicating that some kokanee reached age IV+ and presumably spawned in fall 1959.

### Management conclusions

- Kokanee size differences between the two lakes likely reflected different levels of competition and basic productivity.
- The Black Pond test did not clearly establish kokanee's value as forage for landlocked salmon. The authors suggested that much larger plantings with high survival might produce stunting, with adults of 6 to 8 inches.
- Maintaining kokanee as a forage species through artificial plantings was judged impractical. Spawning conditions in Black Pond could be improved, but were unlikely to sustain a dense stock.
- For Third Bisby Lake, the planned program was to improve the natural spawning area and add fry plantings to establish a self-perpetuating population.
- Several New York introductions succeeded, but stocks depended on annual plantings. A Connecticut lake population persisted for several years but became extinct once efficient angling methods were developed (cited as Webster, 1940, in the text).
