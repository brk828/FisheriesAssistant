# A Look at a Lake (Beckman, 1947)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #fisheries-survey

## Executive Summary

This 1947 popular-science article by William C. Beckman of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]], Fish and Fisheries Division, [[Michigan Department Of Conservation]], describes a lake inventory survey on Long Lake, Michigan. Through a fictional-style dialogue with a curious fisherman, the author explains the annual temperature and oxygen cycle of deep lakes, the reasons for [[Winterkill]] in shallow lakes, and how survey data guide stocking decisions. The article argues that targeted, data-based stocking is more useful than indiscriminate fish planting.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Long Lake, about 60 feet deep with an area of about 150 acres. A survey party was working on the lake when a fisherman stopped to ask about their chemical tests.
- **Institute role:** The Institute is the research branch of the Fish and Fisheries Division. Its goal is "better fishing for everyone." Its staff are technically trained specialists.
- **Research topics listed:** breeding habits, population studies, [[Trout]] investigations, feeding habits and food, fertilization to speed growth, age and growth rates, creel censuses, and parasite studies.
- **Source:** Reprinted from *Michigan Conservation*, Vol. XVI, No. 6, July 1947, pp. 6, 7, 12.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Annual Lake Cycle

- **Spring mixing:** After ice melts, surface water warms. Water is densest at 39°F, so it sinks and mixes the lake completely, usually in March or April. Temperature, oxygen, and carbon dioxide become uniform from top to bottom.
- **Summer stratification:** Three layers form by midsummer:
  - **Surface layer:** warm and nearly uniform, mixed by wind. Example: about 75°F at the surface and 73°F at 15 feet.
  - **[[Thermocline]] (middle layer):** temperature drops at least 0.5°F per foot of depth. Example: 73°F to 55°F over 20 feet.
  - **Bottom layer:** nearly uniform, about 50°F. Oxygen may or may not be present by midsummer.
- **Oxygen loss:** Oxygen is high in the surface layer but falls steeply in the middle layer. At 55 feet in Long Lake, the survey measured no oxygen at all. Fish must move upward to breathe.
- **Fall mixing:** Cooling surface water reaches 39°F and mixes the lake again. Water below 39°F floats and freezes, forming an ice cover.
- **Winter stratification:** Ice blocks wind mixing. Water becomes warmer with depth (about 39°F at the bottom, near freezing at the surface). Oxygen is consumed by fish, plants, and decay, and is replenished only near the surface by photosynthesis.

### Winterkill

- Oxygen may disappear completely or nearly so in **shallow lakes** rich in organic matter. Deeper lakes rarely winterkill because they hold more oxygenated water.
- Winterkill is not annual. It tends to occur in winters with heavy, long-lasting snow cover, which blocks sunlight needed by aquatic plants to produce oxygen.

### Habitat Suitability

- Temperature and oxygen profiles determine whether a lake suits **trout** (cold water plus adequate oxygen) or only **warm-water fishes**. Some lakes are cold enough for trout but have no deep oxygen, while others are oxygen-rich but too warm.

## Key Findings

### 1. Lake Inventory Methods

- **Mapping (winter):** A baseline is established across the widest part of the lake and marked with brush every 100 feet on the ice. Perpendicular measurements are taken to the shore. Depths are measured by ice soundings using a special device that also samples bottom mud.
- **Master maps:** A draftsman at the Institute's Ann Arbor office produces master maps. Bottom types are shown in color tints. Maps are sold to the public at printing and coloring cost.
- **Mapping coverage:** The Institute has mapped about **1,800 lakes**, out of **11,037 lakes** of all sizes shown on Michigan maps.

### 2. Sampling Methods

- **Gill and fyke nets:** Set at various depths and locations to catch larger fish. Lifted early in the morning and reset if needed. Catches are weighed, measured, scale-sampled, checked for parasites, and stomach-sampled.
- **Shore seining:** Estimates production and survival of young-of-the-year game fish, relative abundance of forage fish, and abundance of crayfish and tadpoles. Samples are preserved for laboratory identification.
- **Vegetation:** Plant samples are collected and preserved. Weed beds are sketched on maps.
- **Bottom fauna:** A small dredge samples mud from about **one square foot** per sample. Mud is sieved, and invertebrates are counted and measured for volume.
- **Chemistry:** Temperature, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and acidity are measured at several depths.

### 3. Laboratory Analysis

- Fish ages are determined from scales, and past growth is compared with state averages.
- Stomach contents are compared with bottom-sample food.
- Chemical and physical data are compared with the requirements of each fish type.
- A final report with recommendations (including stocking) is sent to the district fisheries supervisors.

### 4. Species Diversity

- There are **over 150 different kinds of fish** in Michigan waters. Some need careful identification, which is why samples are preserved.

### 5. Stocking Policy

- The Department has stopped **indiscriminate planting** of warm-water fish such as [[Bluegill]], not stocking altogether.
- A single female bluegill can lay about **17,000 eggs**. The average hatchery planting had been about **9,094 fingerlings**, much less than one female's output. Most bluegill-dominated lakes can sustain themselves.
- Stocking remains valuable in these cases:
  - Lakes that have winterkilled, to speed fish production.
  - Lakes lacking species such as [[Largemouth Bass]] or [[Northern Pike]].
- The Department's view is that stocking should be targeted rather than applied to many lakes that may already have too many undersized fish for their food supply.

### 6. Organization

- Field survey parties work in summer, and the laboratory is in Ann Arbor.
- Reports go from the Fish and Fisheries Division office in Lansing to district fisheries supervisors, who arrange stocking.
