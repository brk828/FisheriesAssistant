# Synchronism in the Rhythmic Activities of Animals

#animal-behavior #comparative-ethology #rhythmic-behavior

## Executive Summary

This 1916 article by Wallace Craig examines whether animals possess innate mechanisms for maintaining synchronous rhythms with external stimuli, analogous to human behaviors like marching in step. The author critically reviews reported cases of synchronous behavior in various taxa including fireflies, ants, crickets, and birds, concluding that most observations likely result from statistical illusion, environmental forcing, or lock-and-key reflexes rather than true rhythmic synchronism. Only the case of [[Snowy Tree Cricket]] chirping shows potential evidence of genuine synchronism, though even this remains questionable.

## Background & Description

The document distinguishes between human learned synchronism (keeping step while walking, dancing to music) and potential innate synchronous mechanisms in animals. Craig establishes two categories where animals legitimately synchronize with external rhythms:

1. **Slow environmental rhythms**: Seasonal or diurnal cycles where temperature and light changes have sufficient time to influence organisms
2. **Direct physical contact**: Cases like canaries on swing-perches or spiders on webs where bodily contact mediates synchronization

The author notes that most animal behavior conspicuously lacks rhythmic synchronism. Examples include horses trotting independently when driven abreast, circus "dancing" animals requiring trainer prompts, and birds never singing in unison despite possessing "wonderful musical powers."

Lock-and-key reflexes produce apparent synchronism (fighting cocks jumping simultaneously, [[Limicolae]] flocks turning together) but these actions are not rhythmical and represent a fundamentally different phenomenon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document examines synchronous behavior across multiple taxa and environments:

**[[Fireflies]] (Lampyridae)**: Multiple observers reported synchronous flashing, but Craig identifies critical methodological flaws. Dr. H.O. Bumpus observed approximately 60 fireflies over two acres with flash intervals of perhaps half a second, but noted asynchronous individuals were also present. Craig argues that with large numbers flashing at slightly different rates, "accidental synchronism" must occur frequently, requiring statistical analysis to distinguish from true synchronism.

**[[Ants]] (Formicidae)**: Cox reported ants knocking heads against leaves simultaneously when alarmed. Craig dismisses this as observer illusion, noting it would require ants to perceive time relations and anticipate the sound.

**[[American Bittern]]**: Professor W.B. Barrows reported observing a bittern swaying synchronously with wind-blown grass, but Craig questions whether the observer could accurately judge movement against a moving background.

**[[Snowy Tree Cricket]] (Oecanthus niveus)**: Professor A.F. Shull's 1907 observations provide the strongest evidence. Shull initially refuted Dolbear's 1897 claim that all crickets in a field chirp simultaneously, but documented cases where two individuals maintained synchronism. Shull's methodology avoided statistical fallacy (only two individuals) and included accurate timing measurements. However, Shull himself remained uncertain whether synchronism exceeded chance, noting that temperature strongly influences chirping rate, potentially explaining apparent synchronization.

## Key Findings

1. **Absence of conceptual synchronism**: Animals (except possibly [[Primates]]) lack awareness of relations between their actions and external rhythms, and cannot purposefully imitate rhythmic patterns

2. **Statistical illusion in mass observations**: Large groups of organisms acting at slightly different rates inevitably produce apparent synchronism through chance alone; observers are psychologically predisposed to perceive rhythm

3. **Cricket synchronism remains unresolved**: The [[Snowy Tree Cricket]] case represents the only well-documented potential example, but three alternative explanations remain viable:
   - Accidental coincidence
   - Temperature-mediated rate matching
   - Lock-and-key stimulation between individuals

4. **Methodological requirements**: Determining true synchronism requires statistical examination, repeated observations with accurate timing, and control for environmental variables

5. **Call for rigorous observation**: Craig concludes by requesting "complete and accurate observations" from naturalists, emphasizing the psychological importance of resolving whether animals possess innate synchronic mechanisms

The document represents an early critical examination of animal behavior methodology, highlighting the importance of distinguishing observer bias from genuine biological phenomena.
