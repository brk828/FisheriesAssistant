# Animal Candidate Review for Listing as Endangered or Threatened Species: Notice of Review (1991)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species-review

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register notice (published November 21, 1991, Vol. 56, No. 225) from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] is a Proposed Rule that presents a revised compilation of native U.S. vertebrate and invertebrate animal taxa being considered as candidates for possible addition to the [[Endangered and Threatened Wildlife List]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. It is a notice of review, not a listing proposal. Candidate taxa receive no substantive or procedural protection under the Act. The notice supersedes all earlier animal notices of review, requests new information on candidate taxa, and asks the public for comments. Fish make up a substantial share of the entries, alongside mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, mollusks, insects and crustaceans.

## Background & Description

The notice is compiled from evaluations by the Service's Fish and Wildlife Enhancement biologists in its Regional Offices and field stations. It was edited by Dr. George Drewry of the Division of Endangered Species and dated September 19, 1991. The table is arranged alphabetically by scientific name within major group headings. Each entry gives a status category, a status trend, the lead [[Service Region]], the family, the common name, and the historic range by postal code abbreviation.

**Status categories:**
- **PE / PT:** Taxa already proposed as endangered or threatened.
- **Category 1:** Sufficient substantial information exists to propose listing, but proposals are precluded by other listing activity.
- **Category 2:** Listing may be appropriate, but conclusive data on vulnerability and threat are lacking. Many taxa in this category may ultimately not warrant listing.
- **Category 3:** Taxa no longer under consideration, split into:
  - **3A:** Taxa possibly extinct or rediscovered (the notice states that most 3A extinctions occurred well before 1973).
  - **3B:** Names that are not distinct entities under the Act's definition of "species."
  - **3C:** Taxa found to be more abundant or widespread than previously thought, or not subject to any threat.

A second column, added for the first time, gives a status trend: Stable (S), Declining (D), Improving (I) or Unknown (U).

The notice also has an [[Organization of the Table]] section explaining that common names were given less weight because standardization is incomplete, and that group names in parentheses are used for many mollusks and insects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The notice links candidate status to environmental planning. Although candidate taxa have no legal protection, the Service encourages federal agencies to consider Category 1 and 2 taxa in environmental planning under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]]. The notice also states that status is only one part of a taxon's condition and can change quickly through natural and human-caused factors.

Aquatic and aquatic-associated taxa in the table include:
- **Freshwater fishes:** Desert and spring-dwelling species such as the [[Tecopa Pupfish]], [[Pecos Pupfish]], [[Devils River Minnow]], [[Oregon Chub]] and [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]], plus [[Lake Sturgeon]], [[Paddlefish]], madtoms, darters and chubs. Several salmonid entries include [[Atlantic Salmon]] populations in Maine and cutthroat and redband trout in the West.
- **Mussels:** Many [[Freshwater Mussels]] of the Southeast, such as the [[Cumberland Elktoe]], are candidates. Most are in Region 4.
- **Snails:** Numerous freshwater and cave snails, including [[Springsnails]] and [[Pleuroceridae]] (elimia and rocksnails).
- **Crustaceans:** Cave amphipods, isopods, crayfishes and fairy shrimps.
- **Aquatic insects:** Mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies, and water beetles.

Cave-adapted and spring-adapted species make up a large share of the invertebrate entries, as do endemic taxa of Hawaii and Puerto Rico.

## Key Findings

- **Purpose:** The notice presents the Service's current judgment on the possible vulnerability and status trends of native U.S. animal taxa and is intended to guide monitoring and future listing decisions.
- **Major changes from earlier notices (listed as eight items):**
  1. Addition and removal of candidate taxa.
  2. Changes to category assignments.
  3. Reassignment of some species that were previously proposed for listing.
  4. A new alphabetical arrangement by scientific name within group headings.
  5. Omission of taxa previously treated as non-candidates.
  6. Identification of the lead Region for each taxon.
  7. Additions and deletions to State historic range lists.
  8. A report of known status trends.
- **Scale of the list:** The notice lists about 1,840 taxa (the scanned count is partly garbled). Category 3 contains 334 taxa. Scanning errors make some subcategory and PE/PT totals hard to read.
- **Fish entries:** Freshwater fishes are numerous. Many are Category 2 or 3 taxa in the southwestern deserts (pupfishes, dace, Gila chub), the Southeast (darters, madtoms, shiners), and the Pacific Northwest (cutthroat and redband trout, Oregon chub).
- **Regional leads:** Seven Service Regions are named as lead offices. Region 1 covers the Pacific Northwest and California. Region 2 covers the Southwest, Region 3 the Midwest, Region 4 the Southeast, Region 5 the Northeast, Region 6 the Mountain West, and Region 7 Alaska.
- **Requests for information:** The Service asks for data on taxa that should change category, nominations of new taxa, recommendations for critical habitat, documentation of threats, taxonomic corrections, and status trend data. Comments are accepted on a continuing basis, and substantive changes will be published on a two-year cycle, with the next update anticipated in 1993.
- **Clarifications:** The notice corrects two common misreadings. Category 3A is not a list of recently extinct species, and Category 3C does not mean a species is especially vulnerable to extinction.
- **Contacts:** The notice names the Regional Endangered Species Coordinators and Dr. Janet E. Hohn, Deputy Chief, Division of Endangered Species, as the primary contacts.
