# The Nutrient Composition, Dynamics, and Ecological Significance of Drift Material in the Red Cedar River

#stream-ecology #nutrient-dynamics #aquatic-ecology #primary-production #limnology

## Executive Summary

This technical report presents a comprehensive year-long study (July 1958 - July 1959) of drift material in the [[Red Cedar River]], Michigan—a warm-water tributary of the [[Grand River]] system. Drift material was defined as living and non-living suspended matter removable by filtration through No. 20 mesh bolting cloth (0.076 mm aperture), including organic debris, invertebrates, filamentous algae, and coarse sediments, but excluding smaller particles like diatoms (sampled separately). The study quantified drift weight, phosphorus and nitrogen content, and their relationships to stream discharge, temperature, and seasonal patterns. Three sampling stations were established along the river's 49-mile course through agricultural lands receiving treated sewage and minor industrial wastes. The research demonstrated that drift material, though representing only 0.1% of total solids transported, constitutes a major contribution to energy flow and nutrient cycling in the stream ecosystem. Peak drift concentrations occurred during spring floods, with discharge being the dominant controlling factor.

## Background & Description

The [[Red Cedar River]] originates from Cedar Lake in Livingston County, Michigan, and flows approximately 49 miles through rolling agricultural lands before entering the Grand River in Lansing. The watershed drains 472 square miles with a gradient averaging 2.5 feet per mile. The river passes through or near Fowlerville, Webberville, Williamston, Okemos, and East Lansing, receiving treated sewage from suburban residences and minor industrial wastes. Despite these inputs, the stream showed no gross pollution indicators (sludge beds, odors, oxygen depletion) during the study period, though it received appreciable nutrient loads.

Three artificial impoundments exist on the river at Williamston (created 1840 for water power), Okemos, and East Lansing (recreational). The stream bed varies from mud and silt in upper regions to rocks and gravel in middle and lower portions, with some sandy stretches. Banks are well vegetated with gradual slopes minimizing erosion. Discharge varies from a few cubic feet per second during dry periods to over 5,000 cfs during serious floods, with minimum flow typically in fall and maximum in spring/early summer due to snowmelt and rain runoff from frozen ground.

**Station Characteristics:**
- **Station I**: 0.3 miles below Williamston dam; ~50 feet wide, 18 inches average depth; small rocks and gravel bottom; riffle area with swift current; dense beds of *Vallisneria americana* present after midsummer
- **Station II**: 3.3 miles below Station I, above Zimmer Road bridge; 60 feet wide, 36 inches average depth; large rocks and gravel with sand/silt; upstream of wide shallow pool with velocity reduction; scattered *V. americana* and *Sagittaria latifolia*
- **Station III**: 6.5 miles below Station II, below Dobie Road bridge; 70 feet wide, 18-48 inches depth; primarily sand bottom; low velocity; minimal benthic vegetation; near permanent thermograph location

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was motivated by limited understanding of drift material's role in stream biodynamics, particularly as food source and nutrient carrier. Previous work by [[Jones]] (1949) and [[Teal]] (1957) demonstrated organic debris importance for aquatic insects, with Teal showing imported organic material accounted for three-fourths of annual energy in a small spring. [[Odum]] (1959) theorized that organic matter import could equal photosynthetic production in some communities.

Drift material represents a critical interface between living and non-living ecosystem components, as described by [[Lindeman]] (1942). Non-living drift is consumed as organic detritus by animals, while gradual decay releases nutrients for plants. Living drift components are either consumed as food or die and decompose, returning nutrients through bacterial action. [[Ruttner]] (1953) identified three components of total phosphorus and nitrogen: (1) inorganic, (2) dissolved colloidal organic, and (3) particulate (bound in suspended particles—the drift material).

[[Brehmer]] (1958) previously theorized phosphorus transport in the Red Cedar River occurred through sloughed-off periphyton becoming sestonic, with high downstream soluble phosphorus from decomposition of senescent periphyton. The current study tested and expanded these concepts through direct measurement of drift composition and movement.

The Red Cedar River's relatively rich bottom fauna and periphyton communities provided biological context. Water temperatures ranged from 0°C in winter to warmer summer conditions. The stream's warm-water classification and agricultural watershed setting influenced both drift sources (terrestrial plant material, soil particles, aquatic vegetation) and nutrient loading patterns.

## Key Findings

**Sampling Methodology and Validation:**
- Developed pumping system using Briggs & Stratton 3/4 hp gasoline engine with Jabasco suction pump, filtering water through No. 20 mesh nylon bolting cloth (0.076 mm aperture)
- Pumped 50-foot hose to sampling point; filtered 3-12 minutes depending on conditions; mean 275 liters per drift sample
- Applied Straub "two-point" sediment sampling method at 0.2 and 0.8 depth when conditions permitted
- Statistical analysis of duplicate samples confirmed single samples were representative (two-tailed t-test, p>0.05)
- Total of 60 drift collections over annual period; more frequent sampling during floods

**Drift Weight and Distribution Patterns:**
- Monthly mean drift weight ranged from 13.82 mg m⁻³ (January) to 2,645.14 mg m⁻³ (April)
- Annual total: 265.0 metric tons of drift material (excluding diatoms) moved past Station II
- Drift concentration increased with depth; bed load samples consistently highest
- During floods (April 8), bed load concentration (2,695.8 mg m⁻³) was 2.3× greater than 0.5 depth (1,077.5 mg m⁻³)
- Station I (below Williamston dam) showed higher values than downstream stations during warm periods, attributed to reservoir as source of planktonic material and floating vegetation (*Lemna minor*)
- Winter shift: Station III showed highest concentrations in January due to reduced decomposition rates allowing drift to travel farther downstream

**Organic vs. Inorganic Components:**
- Percent ash-free dry weight (organic component) varied inversely with total drift weight
- Highest organic percentage during late fall/winter: 60.98% (December), 63.20% (January), 54.87% (February)
- Lowest organic percentage during spring floods: 19.40% (March), 16.80% (April)
- Increased organic percentage in cold water resulted from reduced bacterial decomposition
- Bed load samples after May 8 contained lower organic percentage than upper strata samples; lighter organic material remained suspended longer than sand/silt

**Phosphorus Dynamics:**
- Total annual phosphorus movement: 12,631.8 kg (12.6 metric tons)
- Drift phosphorus contributed 282.2 kg (2.2% of total phosphorus load)
- Water phosphorus: 5,358.3 kg dissolved, 12,349.7 kg total (including particulate <0.076 mm)
- Monthly drift phosphorus ranged from 34.17 μg m⁻³ (January) to 2,458.48 μg m⁻³ (April)
- Strong correlation between drift phosphorus and total drift weight: regression Y = 0.339 + 1.109X, r = 0.85 (p<0.01)
- Phosphorus content closely followed organic component pattern (Figure 7)
- 69% of annual phosphorus moved in 25% of year; 90% in 50% of year
- 89% of drift phosphorus moved in just 16% of year, demonstrating extreme discharge concentration
- Equivalent to 159 metric tons of superphosphate fertilizer (18% P₂O₅)

**Nitrogen Content:**
- Organic nitrogen in drift generally higher than phosphorus
- Cellular N:P ratio for drift material: 1.60 (compared to 1.04 for periphyton reported by Grzenda 1960)
- Total annual organic nitrogen in drift: 433.3 kg
- Available nitrogen (NH₃-NO₂-NO₃) annual total: 241,300 kg
- ~80% of nitrogen movement occurred during March-April floods
- Ratio of available nitrogen to dissolved phosphorus ~20:1; to total phosphorus ~10:1 during normal flow
- Nitrogen concentrations maintained longer during floods than phosphorus; attributed to organic material remaining suspended while heavier sand settled

**Discharge Effects (Primary Controlling Factor):**
- Discharge increases caused both higher drift concentrations and greater total transport (multiplicative effect)
- Peak drift concentrations occurred during rising stage, not at peak discharge—demonstrating flushing action
- Example: March 3 at 16.99 m³ sec⁻¹ showed 4,764.71 mg m⁻³ drift; March 12 at similar discharge (17.216 m³ sec⁻¹) showed only 326.89 mg m⁻³ after flushing
- Initial discharge increase swept accumulated materials; subsequent high flow transported less concentrated drift
- Fall rains (October-November) increased drift through input of fallen leaves and organic debris
- Early spring thaw (February) had minimal effect when water flowed over ice; effects pronounced after ice disappeared and ground thawed
- Turbidity more sensitive to slight discharge changes than drift material; turbidity primarily from silt/clay, more easily suspended than larger drift particles

**Seasonal Patterns:**
- Lowest drift: late fall through winter (ice cover, low discharge, reduced productivity, shortened photoperiod, low temperatures)
- Highest drift: spring floods (March-April) from increased discharge, runoff, warming temperatures
- Water temperature reached 0°C by December 2, 1958
- Ice covered stream all of January and most of February; production at minimum
- Reduced decomposition in cold water increased organic percentage but decreased total drift weight

**Biological Components:**

*Zooplankton and Bottom Fauna:*
- Zooplankton negligible; <10 copepods per sample (filtering ~300 liters)
- Copepods identified as *Cyclops* spp.; occasional backswimmers (Notonectidae)
- Stations I and II had more zooplankton than Station III due to proximity to Williamston reservoir
- Bottom fauna in <50% of samples; primarily midges, oligochaetes, rarely mayfly nymphs
- Mean 3.5 organisms per sample (3.0 copepods, 0.5 others)
- Annual drift: 3,636,550,900 organisms total (520,026,800 excluding copepods)
- Annual bottom fauna drift weight: 416 kg (using 0.8 mg per organism)
- Drift values comparable to north Sweden study by Müller (1954) with similar current velocities
- Drifting fauna readily available as food for carnivores; important for organism distribution

*Phytoplankton (Diatoms):*
- Diatoms collected on Millipore filters (0.45 μ pore size) from 100 ml water samples
- Examined at 970× with oil immersion; quantified using statistical methods
- Dominant genera: *Navicula*, *Synedra*, *Gomphonema*, *Cyclotella*, *Cymbella*, *Melosira*, *Cocconeis*
- All except *Cyclotella* have attachment methods; benthic origin confirmed
- Genera matched those in periphyton collected by Peters (1959) from Red Cedar River
- Seasonal succession evident: *Navicula*, *Synedra*, *Gomphonema* dominant late winter/early spring (February-April); *Cyclotella* peaked May-July
- Concentrations ranged 192-3,549 diatoms ml⁻¹ (February-July)
- Spring maximum attributed to warming temperature effects on production and increased velocity removing sessile forms
- Abrasive action of silt during floods evident in early March samples
- Annual diatom movement: 633.9 metric tons live weight (247.3 metric tons dry weight, 123.9 metric tons organic weight)
- Caloric value: 634.4 × 10⁶ calories (using 5.12 cal g⁻¹ organic weight)
- Diatoms considered basic food form; many insects and organisms rely on drifting diatoms

*Tripton:*
- Vast majority of drift material
- Included: sand granules, inorganic particles, leaf bits, roots, bark, seeds, plant material, senescent algal mat clumps, other organic matter
- Sources: terrestrial plants, aquatic sources, watershed soils, industrial/sewage outfalls

**Comparative Phosphorus Studies:**
- Annual phosphorus figures similar across studies when discharge considered:
  - July 1957-July 1958: 16,048.4 kg at 5.064 m³ sec⁻¹ (Grzenda 1960, Station III)
  - Feb 1958-Feb 1959: 9,763.0 kg at 2.786 m³ sec⁻¹ (Vannote 1961, Farm Lane)
  - July 1958-July 1959: 12,631.8 kg at 4.907 m³ sec⁻¹ (current study, Station II)

**Water Chemistry Relationships:**
- Total solids primarily dissolved materials; high correlation with specific conductivity (r = 0.94)
- Specific conductivity formula: Ys = 47.424 + 1.248X
- Discharge diluted soluble substances; conductivity and total solids declined during floods
- No positive relationship between drift material and conductivity or total solids
- Turbidity affected by discharge similarly to drift but more sensitive to slight flow changes
- Turbidity corrections made for intrinsic color by filtering and subtracting filtrate reading

**Phosphorus Analysis Methods:**
- Drift phosphorus determined on ash residue after dry ashing at 550°C for 30 minutes
- Wet ashing (perchloric acid) gives higher values by breaking rock particles; dry ashing measured readily available phosphorus only
- Tests with *Anabaena variabilis* culture confirmed no phosphorus loss on ignition
- Digestion with acidified ammonium molybdate; reduction with stannous chloride (blue color)
- Klett-Summerson colorimeter with red filter (640-700 mμ); 10-minute color development
- Water samples acidified with H₂SO₄ to prevent phosphorus loss to polyethylene container walls
- Three phosphorus fractions: total (100 ml water after digestion), dissolved (Millipore filtered), drift (ash residue)

**Energy Flow and Ecological Significance:**
- Drift material only 0.1% of total solids (61,100 metric tons annually) but major energy contributor
- Using 10% herbivore conversion efficiency: drift could support 52 metric tons herbivore biomass
- Using 5% carnivore conversion efficiency: could support 2.6 metric tons large carnivores (e.g., smallmouth bass)
- Drift continuously supplies downstream communities while being replenished from upstream
- Net-spinning Trichoptera and bristle-mouthed Diptera depend on filtering drift particles for food
- Some algae can utilize organic phosphorus directly (Chu 1946)
- Drift represents both import (food/nutrients into communities) and export (senescent material sloughing off)
- Supports Odum (1959) energy flow model where organic matter import can equal photosynthetic production
- Drift movement maintains nutrient levels in downstream waters (e.g., western Lake Erie per Curl 1959)

**Annual Material Budget (Station II, July 1958-July 1959):**
- Total solids: 61,100 metric tons
- Drift (excluding diatoms, fauna, P, N): 265.0 metric tons
- Diatoms: 247.3 metric tons (dry weight)
- Bottom fauna: 0.4 metric tons (live weight)
- Phosphorus: 7.3 metric tons particulate, 5.4 metric tons dissolved
- Nitrogen: 11.3 metric tons particulate, 241.7 metric tons dissolved
- CaCO₃: 37,200 metric tons (calculated from mean alkalinity 253 mg l⁻¹)
- Silt, clay, zooplankton, other dissolved: 23,121.6 metric tons

**Methodological Innovations:**
- Comparison of bottle-dip vs. filtered sampling showed no significant difference in phosphorus (one-tailed t-test, p>0.20)
- Interpretation: bottle samples adequate for microscopic particles (silt, clay, diatoms) but insufficient for larger, diffusely distributed drift
- Larger volume (several minutes pumping) necessary for representative drift collection
- Vertical sampling followed sediment distribution theory; plotted drift concentration similar to theoretical sediment pattern
- High surface concentrations during floods attributed to water turbulence causing violent mixing
- Sampling frequency critical during floods: daily during rise, every other day during recession

**Comparative Context:**
- Drift values comparable to sediment studies but much lower quantity than total sediments
- Lane and Koelzer (1943): particles <0.076 mm compose up to 97% of sediments in some cases
- Red Cedar drift excluded this fraction, preventing direct sediment comparisons
- Diatom concentrations (192-3,549 ml⁻¹) within range of other studies: Pennak (1943) found 9-2,575 ml⁻¹ in Colorado mountain stream; Robeck et al. (1954) found 50-67,000 ml⁻¹ in Columbia River
- Bottom fauna drift numerically similar to Müller (1954) north Sweden study (hundreds of thousands per month) with comparable current velocities (1.6-2.3 ft sec⁻¹ vs. 1.0-2.0 ft sec⁻¹)
