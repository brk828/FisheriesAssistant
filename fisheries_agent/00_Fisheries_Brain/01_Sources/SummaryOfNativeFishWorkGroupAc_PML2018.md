# Native Fish Work Group: Razorback Sucker Recovery in Lake Mohave, 1991–1994

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This note summarizes annual activity reports (1991–1994) of the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), a multi-agency ad hoc team formed by mutual consent in 1990 to rebuild the wild adult population of [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. The main method was on-site rearing and stocking of young fish derived from wild adults and from larvae captured at spawning grounds. The program produced a successful spawn and survival of young fish in several years, but its annual target of 5,000 fish was not met in 1991 or 1992. From 1993 onward the group shifted toward larval capture, laboratory rearing, and backwater stocking. The OCR source is partly degraded, so some figures are reconstructed from context and noted as such.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** [[Razorback Sucker]], a native catostomid whose wild adult population in Lake Mohave had declined. The NFWG aimed to replace this population with stock that was large in number and quality, including as much [[Genetic Diversity]] as possible.
- **Founding goal:** Establish at least 5,000 young fish per year, a target the group set when it was formed.
- **Primary method:** On-site rearing of fish in backwater habitats, using wild adults from the lake and larvae captured on spawning grounds. Stocking took place in [[Yuma Cove]], [[Davis Cove]], and later other backwaters.
- **Partner agencies:** The document refers to multiple cooperating agencies, including Arizona State University (ASU) and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], which received some fish. Each agency contributed staff, facilities, and funds according to its capabilities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Yuma Cove:** A cove on the lake that was isolated by rebuilding an earthen berm in 1991. The site was treated to remove [[Exotic Fish Removal|exotic fishes]] before stocking.
- **Davis Cove:** A cove at the southern end of the lake near [[Davis Dam]]. It was used for holding and growing fish that had reached a size where they could survive. Water level changes, storms, and erosion repeatedly disrupted the barrier and the cove's water.
- **Competition and predation:** Reports noted high densities of [[Dragonfly Larvae]] feeding on young fish in 1992, and [[Largemouth Bass]] stomachs were examined in 1993 for evidence of predation on stocked fish.
- **Ephemeral backwaters:** Several temporary backwaters, including [[Cottonwood Cove]], [[Willow Cove]], and [[Dandy Cove]], were used for stocking larvae in 1994. Their short-lived nature required annual planning and structural maintenance.
- **Water quality and weeds:** Ongoing studies covered water quality and [[Weed Control]] in the stocking areas.

## Key Findings

### 1991 Activities
- Yuma Cove was isolated by rebuilding a berm, and exotic fishes were removed by poisoning.
- Adult razorbacks were collected from the lake during February and stocked into Yuma Cove. They spawned, and larvae were captured.
- No young or adult razorbacks and no exotic fish were observed after May. Adults were removed through the summer and the cove was treated again. The report attributes the lack of recruitment to a cause that is illegible in the source.

### 1992 Activities
- Yuma Cove was restocked in February 1992 with **29 females and 60 adult** razorback suckers.
- The fish spawned and produced **316 young**: 167 to Davis Cove, 129 to the lake, and 20 to ASU. (These splits are reconstructed from the garbled source; the sum matches 316.)
- Between July and September, fish over 300 mm total length were removed from Yuma Cove and stocked in Davis Cove to grow further.
- In October, fish over 300 mm were captured at Yuma Cove, measured, tagged, and released into the lake.
- The report describes the year as successful but **short of the 5,000-fish goal**.

### 1993 Activities
- No additional stocking was planned for Davis Cove beyond fish reared there in 1993. Davis Cove was unstable because its barrier was continuously breached by water-level changes and storms, which made it impossible to keep fish inside.
- Fish reared in ephemeral backwaters were moved out of Davis Cove, and larvae were found in the stomachs of largemouth bass.
- The group concluded that fish reared in backwaters had survival rates that were the most promising among its methods. The report also states that it was surprising that fish survived at Yuma Cove.

### 1994 Work Plan and Progress
- The 1994 plan was to capture wild larvae from spawning grounds, rear them in the laboratory for two to four weeks, then stock them once they fed actively and developed strong swimming ability.
- Stocking records cover weeks from late January to early April 1994, with weekly totals of wild larvae and stocked fish (the table is largely illegible in the scan).
- Equipment was purchased for a rearing facility at Cottonwood Cove, which had electrical service and a permitted workshop. Larvae were aerated and held in tanks, and fish were recaptured and restocked into the lake.
- Planned work included monitoring growth and survival, [[Genetic Diversity]] studies, [[Weed Control]], and water quality.
- An environmental compliance package (EA, BA, and a permit) for the Davis Cove barrier was in progress. Plans also called for deepening and rebuilding the berms of two or three backwaters.
- A distribution table lists larval stocking across backwater sites as of 31 March 1994.

### Conclusion
The report concludes that the combined multi-agency program had been very successful within the area each agency contributed, and that the shared cost of accomplishing each task was reasonable.
