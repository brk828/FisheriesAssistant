#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 California Department of Fish and Game administrative report documents the introduction, growth, diet, and depth distribution of [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*) in [[El Capitan Reservoir]], San Diego County, California. Following an obscure 1874 introduction, a deliberate stocking program began in 1959 using eggs imported from Minnesota's Detroit River. Walleye successfully established a substantial population in El Capitan Reservoir, exhibiting exceptionally fast growth and early sexual maturity, attributed almost entirely to an abundant forage base of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*). Despite the biological success of the introduction, angler harvest was very poor, and no evidence of natural reproduction (spawning) was found. The study also used a novel vertical gill-net array to examine how [[Artificial Destratification]] of the reservoir affected walleye depth distribution, finding that destratification produced greater vertical dispersion of fish compared to stratified or naturally destratified conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Stizostedion vitreum* (Mitchill) — Walleye, family Percidae.
- **Size range sampled:** 12.6 to 22.2 inches fork length (FL).
- **Scale sampling sites:** Two sites compared — "above" the lateral line (anterior to dorsal fin origin) and "below" the lateral line (at the tip of the pectoral fin). The "below" site produced less variable scale size/shape and was considered more reliable for back-calculating growth.
- **Body length-scale length relationship:** Assumed linear (following Cleary 1949; Forney 1965; Carlander and Whitney 1961), though other studies (Carlander 1945; Eschmeyer 1950; Smith and Pycha 1961) reported a curvilinear relationship.
- **Body-scale correction factor:** Empirically derived as 1.5 inches (37.0 mm) FL, closely matching Smith and Pycha's (1961) 1.6 inches (40.6 mm) TL and Cleary's (1949) 1.4 inches (35.7 mm) SL, but differing substantially from Carlander and Whitney's (1961) 3.26 inches (82.8 mm) TL and Forney's (1965) 2.28 inches (57.9 mm) TL values.
- **Growth rate:** Exceptional — El Capitan walleye's mean growth exceeded all other North American populations compared (see [[Walleye Growth Comparison]] below). Two-year-old El Capitan fish were larger than eight-year-old walleye in Minnesota's Red Lakes and six-year-old fish in Oneida Lake, New York.
- **Sexual dimorphism in growth:** Males consistently exhibited the slower growth characteristic typical of walleye populations generally.
- **Anomaly:** One scale sample exhibited three clear annular marks suggesting a nonexistent 1961 year class; interpreted as containing a false annulus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** [[El Capitan Reservoir]] is described as a eutrophic, warmwater reservoir used for municipal water storage by the City of San Diego, located seven miles east of Lakeside at 553 feet elevation.
- **Thermal/oxygen regime:** An oxygen deficit typically develops in the hypolimnion from May to October, restricting fish to the epilimnion and upper thermocline (generally above 8 meters depth) during stratified summer conditions.
- **Trophic role:** Walleye functioned as an efficient, highly specialized piscivore. Stomach content analysis (75 stomachs, July 1964–September 1965) showed the diet was almost exclusively [[Threadfin Shad]] (93% by number), with unidentified fish (likely also shad) comprising 6.4%, and a single largemouth bass fingerling as the only other prey item. Contrary to management hopes, walleye did not substantially prey on centrarchid panfish or other game fish.
- **Depth distribution:**
  - In summer 1964 (pre-destratification), walleye were confined to the upper 8 meters due to hypolimnetic oxygen deficits.
  - In winter, depth distribution was not perfectly homogeneous despite fairly uniform physical/chemical water conditions.
  - Following artificial destratification (air injection, commenced June 10, 1965), walleye showed significantly greater vertical dispersion in summer 1965 compared to summer 1964 (Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, one-sided, significant difference at Station I).
  - Overall, 69.4% of all walleye captured in vertical gill nets were caught above 3.0 meters depth.
  - Diurnal vertical migration (deeper by day, shallower at night) is noted as a possible confound in comparing sampling periods, per [[Niemuth Churchill and Wirth 1959]].
- **Reproductive ecology:** Sexual maturity was reached unusually early — males at age 2, females at age 3 (observed February–March 1965). No spent fish were found; egg reabsorption was observed in ovaries, and no successful reproduction was detected via rotenone sampling, electrofishing, or seining from 1964–1966, despite mature fish being present. Streams feeding the reservoir are intermittent, likely precluding riverine spawning.

## Key Findings

- **Introduction history:** First California walleye introduction occurred in 1874 (16 fish from Vermont's Missisquoi River planted near Sacramento), with limited follow-up. A more deliberate second introduction began in 1959 into warmwater reservoirs south of the Tehachapi Mountains, explicitly avoiding northern drainages to protect salmonid resources.
- **Stocking source:** Eggs were obtained from the Minnesota Conservation Department (Detroit River broodstock) in exchange for California steelhead/rainbow trout eggs (1959, 1960, 1962, 1963); hatched initially in a temporary Santa Ana River hatchery, later at the Mojave River Hatchery.
- **Population establishment:** Stocking of fry and fingerlings successfully established a substantial, self-sustaining (numerically, not reproductively) walleye population in El Capitan Reservoir.
- **Growth performance:** Walleye growth in El Capitan exceeded all other North American populations reviewed (see comparison table), attributed to abundant threadfin shad forage, favorable year-round water temperatures (rarely below 50°F even in winter), and a long growing season — echoing findings by Stroud (1949) for Norris Reservoir, Tennessee.
- **Angler harvest was very poor:**
  - Opening day (Aug 1, 1963) creel census: 14 walleye averaging 14 inches FL.
  - Season total (1963–64, City of San Diego records): only 33 walleye reported caught among ~9,000 boat anglers (~0.0035 walleye/angler).
  - 1965 season: only 6 walleye observed despite interviewing >50% of estimated anglers.
  - 1966 season: only 8 walleye observed in creel.
  - Possible causes: extreme forage abundance reducing catchability, anglers not targeting walleye specifically, and high reservoir turbidity reducing lure visibility.
- **No evidence of natural spawning** was found despite reaching sexual maturity, likely due to lack of suitable (intermittent) stream habitat.
- **Management conclusion:** Despite demonstrating that walleye stocking can reliably establish a population and control threadfin shad, and that growth potential in such reservoirs is excellent, the poor angler harvest meant benefits were "too negligible to merit continuation of stocking" at El Capitan. Recommendations for future efforts included angler education, improved fishing techniques, and considering broodstock from the southern portion of the walleye's native range for potentially favorable genetic adaptation.
- **Methodological contribution:** The study employed a specially designed vertical gill-net array (see [[Miller and Perrin 1967]]) across three stations of varying depth to assess fish distribution relative to [[Artificial Destratification]], representing a novel application for reservoir fisheries research.
