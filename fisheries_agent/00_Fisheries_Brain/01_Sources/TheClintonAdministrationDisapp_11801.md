# Clinton Administration Environmental Policy Review (Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance Newsletter, Fall/Winter 1994)

#environmental-policy #public-lands #wetlands

## Executive Summary

This [[Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance]] (SUWA) newsletter, Vol. XI, No. 3 (Fall/Winter 1994), assesses the first [[Clinton Administration]] term's record on public lands and environmental policy. The authors, [[Cindy Shogan]] and [[Mike Matz]], conclude that the administration has been disappointing. They argue its promises on forests, wetlands, wilderness, and mining reform have largely gone unmet, and that its decisions are driven by political compromise rather than environmental merit. The document is advocacy and policy commentary, not a scientific study. It contains no field data, no sampling, and no fish or aquatic ecosystem research. Aquatic and wildlife references (salmon, spotted owl, wetland functions) appear only as policy context.

## Background & Description

SUWA's stated mission is the preservation of the wild lands at the heart of the [[Colorado Plateau]] and their management in a natural state. The organization works through research, public education, and advocacy for permanent protection under National Park and National Wilderness Preservation systems. It is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, a member of [[Community Shares Utah]], and publishes its newsletter four times a year. Its staff and board are based in [[Salt Lake City]], [[Moab]], and [[Washington D.C.]]

The newsletter reviews the [[Clinton Campaign]] platform, *Putting People First*. In 1992 Clinton pledged to:

- Preserve ancient forests for their scientific and ecological importance.
- Make the "no net loss" wetlands pledge a reality.
- Rededicate national park and wilderness agencies to a true conservation ethic.
- Designate the [[Arctic National Wildlife Refuge]] as wilderness and halt new offshore drilling.

The authors frame these as the standard against which the administration's first 18 months (described as "nearly two years" in the text) should be judged.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Old growth forests.** The [[Pacific Northwest Forest Plan]] debate pitted protection of the [[Northern Spotted Owl]] against timber jobs. Of 24 million acres of remaining forest, a scientific team produced eight options with varying risks to the owl. After Northwest politicians objected, a ninth option was created. The authors state that Option 9 would allow the destruction of more than 30% of remaining ancient forests in Washington, Oregon, and Northern California. They also say it threatens the salmon industry, permits too much logging under unproven practices, and does not allow forests enough time to recover. Lawsuits before [[Judge William Dwyer]] have halted new logging in federally managed ancient forest since 1991.

**Wetlands.** The administration's plan, "Protecting America's Wetlands: A Fair and Flexible Approach," was developed by the [[White House Interagency Wetlands Workgroup]]. The authors credit it with reversing the Bush exclusion of one percent of Alaska's wetlands from [[Section 404]] protection. They criticize its proposal to exempt 54 million acres of wetlands converted to agricultural use. They note that seasonally farmed wetlands provide flood protection, groundwater recharge, water filtration, and wildlife habitat, and argue they deserve full [[Clean Water Act]] protection.

**Wilderness and Utah.** The authors argue that the [[Utah Wilderness Bill]] (H.R. 1500) covers 5.7 million acres of wildlands that could be protected on an interim basis. They say the administration has been reluctant to act, in part because of "War on the West" rhetoric. The authors note that the [[Bureau of Land Management]] has been asked to review wilderness inventories in other Western states, but not in Utah.

**Arctic and Alaska.** The administration considered lifting the ban on exporting oil carried by the [[Trans-Alaska Pipeline]]. The authors argue this would increase development pressure on sensitive land and marine habitat in Alaska and California and raise spill risks. They describe the administration's proposed "twinning" of the Arctic Refuge with Canadian parks as cosmetic and short of wilderness designation.

## Key Findings

- **Broken promises:** The authors judge the administration's public lands record disappointing. They attribute this to "special interests and political backscratching" rather than environmental priorities.
- **Rejected revenue proposals:** Environmentalists proposed a 12 percent royalty on minerals from public lands, higher grazing fees, a BTU energy tax, and ending below-cost timber sales. The authors say the budget initially included these, but they were dropped after Western senators and governors objected. The BTU tax was traded for [[Senator Boren]]'s vote, according to Bob Woodward's *The Agenda*.
- **Forest plan:** Option 9 would permit logging of more than 30% of remaining ancient forests. A coalition of 12 environmental groups has filed a legal challenge.
- **Wetlands:** The plan's gains are outweighed by the exemption of 54 million acres of agricultural wetlands and the expansion of state and local control.
- **Utah wilderness:** The authors call interim protection for 5.7 million acres of Utah wildlands an easy decision. They say the administration has not acted.
- **Arctic Refuge:** The authors say the administration has been silent for twenty months on wilderness designation and now offers only a "cosmetic" alternative.
- **California Desert Protection Act:** If enacted, it would designate over nine million acres as parks, preserves, and wilderness. The authors contrast this with the [[Alaska Lands Act]], which protected 103 million acres, and say the Interior Department has been absent from the campaign.
- **Mining reform:** The authors call the chance to reform the [[General Mining Law of 1872]] "being squandered" after 122 years. They suggest a claims moratorium as pressure.
- **Coal report card:** The Citizens Coal Council gave the administration an "A" for rhetoric, a "D-" for protecting federal lands, and an "F" for protecting private lands under the [[Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act]].
- **Character assessment:** The authors argue that Clinton's record as Arkansas governor shows he backed away from environmental controversy after losing re-election, and that "As President, he hasn't changed."
