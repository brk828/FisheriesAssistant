# Monitoring of Native Fishes of the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon: Spring 2001 Trip Reports

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This trip report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) Arizona Fishery Resources Office documents two sampling trips to the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in spring 2001: 30 April–11 May and 4–15 June. The primary goal was a [[Mark-Recapture Experiment]] to estimate the abundance of [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) over 100 mm total length in the lower 14.2 km of the river. Crews at three camps used [[Hoopnet Sampling]] and, at the confluence, [[Trammel Net Sampling]] to capture and tag fish, record reproductive condition, sample predator stomachs, and compare the results with USFWS data from 1991–2000.

Humpback chub was the most abundant species on both trips (576 of 1154 fish in April/May; 1677 of 4157 in June). Non-native fish made up 16% of catches in April/May and 25% in June, compared with about 1% in 1991–1995 USFWS catches. Spawning activity was evident in April/May and had largely ended by June. Young-of-the-year (YOY) humpback chub were scarce in June 2001, which suggests weak 2001 recruitment, though the authors caution that June may be too early to detect YOY fish reliably.

## Background & Description

The report covers three native catostomid and cyprinid species of conservation interest in the [[Grand Canyon]] system: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*). Humpback chub is a federally listed large-bodied minnow, and the other two are native catostomids whose monitoring data help show trends across the community. Adult and juvenile size classes were tracked using total length, fork length, weight, sex, and sexual condition (ripe or spent; tuberculate or breeding colors).

Personnel included staff from the USFWS and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), plus volunteers and University of British Columbia researchers. Data were delivered to the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]] as two Microsoft Access files (May-2001-LCR.mdb and June_2001-LCR.mdb). The full mark-recapture analysis was to be reported separately.

**Sampling design**
- Three camps were set along the LCR, by distance above the confluence: Salt Camp (10.7 km), Coyote Canyon Camp (5.5 km), and Boulders Camp (2.0 km).
- Each camp fished about 45 hoopnets in April/May and 56–60 in June, checked every 24 hours. Nets were fished for 4 days per sub-reach.
- Hoopnets were 0.5–0.6 m diameter, 1.0 m long, with 6 mm mesh and a single 10 cm throat.
- Trammel nets (75 ft × 6 ft, 1 in or 1.5 in mesh) were fished near the confluence from an inflatable kayak to catch large humpback chub and flannelmouth sucker.
- All humpback chub over 100 mm and other native fish over 125 mm were PIT-tagged (see [[PIT Tagging]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and water quality**
- **April/May:** The river was near base flow but turbid (under 300 NTUs) at the start. Two minor [[Spate Events]], on 2 May and 5 May, raised turbidity to about 998 and then about 7,000 NTUs. Each spate raised the water level only about 13 cm at one measured point. Turbidity fell to 145 NTUs by 10 May. Water temperatures ranged from 18.0 to 22.2 °C (mean 20.0 °C). Continuous Hydrolab recording failed, so manual readings were used.
- **June:** The river ran at base flow and was clear blue. Turbidity ranged from 8 to 14 NTUs (mean 11.3). Water temperatures ranged from 16.7 to 22.9 °C (mean 21.3 °C). The steady conductivity decline (4464 to 3212 µS) was judged an equipment artifact, possibly from salt precipitating on the probe.

**Biotic context**
- Exotic species present in the LCR included carp, red shiner, fathead minnow, plains killifish, black and yellow bullheads, channel catfish, and rainbow trout.
- A single adult [[Green Sunfish]] (157 mm total length) was captured near Salt Camp in April/May. The report calls this the first known capture of this predatory exotic in the LCR proper, between the confluence and Blue Springs. The specimen was retained as a voucher.
- Parasite occurrence on humpback chub is discussed under [[Lernaea Parasite]] in the Key Findings.

## Key Findings

**1. Catch totals and species composition**
- April/May: 1154 fish captured. Humpback chub 576; other species included fathead minnow, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, red shiner, speckled dace, and channel catfish.
- June: 4157 fish captured. Humpback chub 1677 (about 40% of the catch); fathead minnows rose to 20% of the catch from 7% in April/May.
- Exotics made up 16% (April/May) and 25% (June) of the catch, versus about 1% in 1991–1995. Most of the rise came from [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]] increases.

**2. Humpback chub abundance and capture rates**
- Hoopnet catches of humpback chub differed by only 69 fish among camps in April/May (Salt 211, Coyote 198, Boulders 142) and by 85 fish in June (Salt 501, Coyote 571, Boulders 586).
- [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPUE) in hoopnets was 0.034–0.049 fish/hr in April/May, versus 0.092–0.113 fish/hr in June. The authors attribute the higher June rates to clear base-flow conditions.

**3. Size structure and movement**
- Most humpback chub fell in the 75–225 mm size range on both trips.
- Adults over 300 mm were far more common in April/May (165 in hoopnets) than in June (40), although June had more hoopnet effort. The authors conclude that post-spawning adults had moved back toward the mainstem by June.
- Salt Camp caught more humpback chub over 235 mm on both trips (88 vs. 71 vs. 48 in April/May; 89 vs. 14 vs. 7 in June). The authors suggest that many resident fish over 235 mm may be concentrated in the Salt reach.
- Salt Camp caught far fewer small humpback chub (50–125 mm): 19 in April/May versus 46 at Coyote and 27 at Boulders; 130 in June versus 253 and 303. The cause is unknown, though avoidance of larger chub or predation are possible explanations.
- Flannelmouth sucker and bluehead sucker showed the same pattern, with more adults captured in April/May than in June. Most adult flannelmouth sucker were taken in the Boulders reach and at the confluence.

**4. Recruitment**
- Only eleven humpback chub under 50 mm were captured in June 2001. Clear YOY spikes appeared in June 1993, June 1994 (not detailed), and June 2000. The authors therefore judge 2001 YOY production to be poor, though sampling may have been a month early. Fall 2001 sampling was recommended to confirm the cohort's strength.
- A 1992 cohort appears to have failed. The 75–125 mm peak in June 2001 suggests recruitment from 2000.
- The authors attribute large year-to-year variation in recruitment to environmental variation in the LCR watershed, which may cause environmental stochasticity in long-term population growth.
- Flannelmouth sucker peaks at about 120 mm and 225 mm suggest recruitment in 1999 and 2000. Bluehead sucker showed a peak near 70 mm. Very few bluehead over 75 mm were captured, raising concern about this species' status in the LCR.

**5. Reproductive condition**
- April/May: Spawning was active, with localized clusters. 77 ripe humpback chub were captured (six females), along with 38 males and 18 females showing tuberculation or spawning colors and six spent females. The spent females were all over 363 mm and were captured below 5 km, suggesting the spawning peak had passed.
- Twenty-six ripe flannelmouth sucker were captured, including three females at 4.2–4.9 km along Phragmites banks. Fifty-one ripe bluehead sucker were captured, including seven females, mostly at 4.1 km.
- June: Only thirteen ripe humpback chub were captured, all males. Eighty-four males and 37 females had spawning coloration. The authors suggest tuberculation marks an immediate sign of spawning, while coloration may indicate post-spawning condition.

**6. Predation by non-native fish**
- About 35 large non-native fish (black bullhead, yellow bullhead, channel catfish, carp, rainbow trout) were examined for stomach contents. Most stomachs were empty or held algae, insects, sticks, or bait.
- The report describes direct predation on humpback chub in several cases: a yellow bullhead (255 mm) with two chub (105 and 80 mm), a yellow bullhead (210 mm) with one chub, a black bullhead (185 mm) with one chub, and a 357 mm chub with a catfish bite mark. A rainbow trout (303 mm) contained a 95 mm speckled dace. The text summarizes these as three predation instances on humpback chub.
- Ictalurids were captured in greater numbers during turbid March/April conditions (57 fish, fewer nets) than in June (34 fish). The authors suggest these predators are more active in turbid water.

**7. Parasites**
- The external parasite [[Lernaea Parasite]] (*Lernaea cyprinacea*) occurred on 4.3% of humpback chub in April/May (mean 1.5 per fish) and 6.3% in June (mean 1.0 per fish).
- Earlier records were higher. November 2000 averaged 2.5 parasites per fish, and October 2000 averaged 1.7. None were recorded in April or June 2000. The authors consider the variation cyclical and within the normal range for the LCR.

**8. PIT tagging and logistics**
- April/May: 385 fish were PIT-tagged (250 humpback chub, 76 flannelmouth sucker, 59 bluehead sucker). 222 humpback chub were recaptures from earlier trips.
- June: 986 fish were tagged (836 humpback chub, 141 flannelmouth sucker, 9 bluehead sucker). 246 humpback chub were recaptures.
- Tags ran short in both trips, so some suckers were not tagged. The authors recommend about 700 tags for an April/May trip and about 1800 for a June trip (approximately 100 spare per camp).
- General guidance: winter trips may tag 100–400 fish, spring and summer trips often need over 1000 tags, and fall depends on flood events. Plan roughly 200–300 tags per camp for fall and winter trips and 400–500 per camp for spring and summer trips.
