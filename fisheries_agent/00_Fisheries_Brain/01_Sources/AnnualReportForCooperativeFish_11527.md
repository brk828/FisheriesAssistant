# Cooperative Fishery Units: Annual Report for 1967

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fishery-science

## Executive Summary

This Resource Publication (No. 62), prepared by Donald Chapman, Leader of the [[Idaho Cooperative Fishery Unit]] and issued by the United States Department of the Interior in June 1968, reports on the 1967 activities of the [[Cooperative Fishery Units]] program. The program is a nationwide partnership of the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], state fish and game agencies, and colleges and universities, authorized by [[Public Law 86-686]]. In 1967 the 23 units trained biologists, carried out graduate research on fishes and aquatic ecosystems, and provided extension and consulting services. The report is a compendium of unit-by-unit status notes, student thesis abstracts and a publications list, rather than a single research study.

## Background & Description

The units were established at university campuses in 23 states between 1961 and 1966, the earliest being the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit]] (December 1961) and the most recent the [[Washington Cooperative Fishery Unit]] (December 1966). Each unit is jointly supported by the Bureau, a host university and usually a state fish or game department. Coordinating committees, with representatives from the cooperating agencies, review graduate proposals and annual budgets. The Bureau supplies two biologists per unit, a Unit Leader and an Assistant Unit Leader. Universities provide space and facilities, and state agencies provide funds and often facilities for graduate research.

The stated purpose of the program, drawn from [[Public Law 86-686]] (September 2, 1960), is to facilitate cooperation among federal agencies, universities, states and private organizations in research and education on fish and wildlife. Federal participation is limited to assigning Department of the Interior technical personnel, supplying available equipment and paying incidental expenses.

Program objectives are, in order of importance, training fishery biologists, research on ecological and environmental problems and techniques, public education, continuing education for professionals, and special assignments. Training covers fishery management, ecology, population dynamics, taxonomy, fish culture, pathology, nutrition, limnology, oceanography, statistics and biometrics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Unit research addresses a broad range of aquatic systems and stressors. Stream and reservoir studies examine trout microhabitat, cover, water velocity and dam operations, for example on the [[Green River]] below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and on the [[Gunnison River]] in [[Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Monument]]. Pollution and contaminant work includes [[Pesticide Effects On Aquatic Organisms]], herbicide and insecticide tests on algae and fish, the rotenone and [[Silvex]] pond studies, and [[Acid Mine Drainage]] and [[Strip-Mine Reclamation]] studies in Ohio and Pennsylvania. Other work covers estuarine nursery marshes, marine sport fisheries, coral-reef and Hawaiian stream communities, and invertebrate food webs including [[Midges]], [[Zooplankton]] and [[Opae]] (atyid shrimp).

Environmental factors recurring in the reports include water temperature and discharge, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, pH, alkalinity, salinity and water level. Several studies link these variables to spawning, growth, survival and distribution.

## Key Findings

**Program output (1967)**

- 49 courses were taught by unit leaders and assistants, with an enrollment of 553 students.
- 256 graduate students were supervised in thesis work or special problems.
- 38 unit-supported graduate students received advanced degrees: 36 M.S. and 2 Ph.D.
- Of those 38, 11 took positions with state conservation agencies, 5 with universities, 4 with federal agencies, 4 with other employers, and 14 entered Ph.D. programs.
- 46 papers by unit personnel were published in 1967 (listed in Appendix B).
- The report expects degree completions to rise sharply in 1968–1970 as the first graduate cohorts finish.

**Selected research findings**

- **Furazolidone in trout ([[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** At 35 mg/kg per day for 20 days, the highest tissue residue was 0.482 ppm in brown trout on day 10. All species averaged below 0.075 ppm by 10 days after treatment. In a 40-day oral safety trial at 50 mg/kg per day, 2 of 43 mortalities were of undetermined cause and none were attributed to the drug. The highest accepted dietary dose ranged from 90 g/100 lb of food (brown trout) to 190 g/100 lb (rainbow and catchable cutthroat trout).
- **Black Canyon fishes ([[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Seven species were captured, and suckers dominated the fauna. Five habitat types were described. Pool and riffle percentages increased as water volume and velocity fell.
- **Midge emergence in an alpine lake:** At least 10 midge species occurred in emergence samples. Emergence counts fit a negative binomial distribution with k usually between 0.1 and 0.2, suggesting contagion.
- **Focal-point residency of stream trout:** Focal points covered less than 3% of each fish's range. On average 15% of each study section was used 95% of the time, and 94% of time was spent in shaded areas.
- **Flint River redeye bass ([[Georgia Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** 137 bass were examined. Fecundity ranged from 5,396 to 21,779 eggs per female. Maturation occurred in the third and fourth year, and spawning followed a late-May rise in water level.
- **Isolated reef, Hawaii ([[Hawaii Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Rotenone removal yielded nearly 5,000 fish of 77 species, a standing crop of 1,117 lb/acre. Repopulation was slow.
- **Mountain-stream alterations ([[Montana Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** About 23% (6 of 30 miles) of Little Prickley Pear Creek was altered. Altered mountain sections had no non-trout species, and trout were 78% less abundant there. Channel alterations caused an estimated loss of 4,700 trout (2,200 lb).
- **Sockeye and steelhead behavior ([[Idaho Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Over-wintering steelhead and salmon generally stayed in stream substrate while temperatures stayed below about 41°F. For juvenile steelhead, 60% of daily movements were under 3 m.
- **Smallmouth bass in the Snake River:** 76% of tagged bass recovered after at least seven days were in the pool where they were tagged. Of those that moved, 71% moved less than 1,200 m.
- **Strip-mine ponds ([[Ohio Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Only 12 of 170 impoundments could support fish, and only one held a substantial bluegill population. Zooplankton was almost entirely rotifers in low-pH ponds. Pyrite oxidation continued to lower runoff pH.
- **Rotenone and invertebrates ([[Pennsylvania Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Bottom samples declined by 52% for Trichoptera, 44% for Ephemeroptera, 97% for Plecoptera and 96% for Diptera. Drift increased for every organism studied.
- **Silvex in farm ponds ([[Louisiana Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Residues peaked at 24 hours after treatment and fell to negligible levels within one to three weeks. The highest value was 78 ppm in bluegill. Phytoplankton did not regain its normal structure within the three months observed.
- **Crawfish and catfish feed (Louisiana):** Pelleted crawfish waste (about 35% crude protein dry matter) gave mean weight gains of 0.345 lb versus 0.447 lb for the pelleted commercial ration. Raw waste dispersed rapidly and was largely unavailable to fish.
- **Green River below Flaming Gorge Dam ([[Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** 23,735 fish of 20 species (9 native, 11 exotic) were taken in 639 collections. Introduced trout largely replaced native fishes in the first 26 miles below the dam. Colorado squawfish and Colorado chub grew more slowly after dam closure than before.
- **Striped bass and American shad ([[Washington Cooperative Fishery Unit]] and [[Massachusetts Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Peak American shad runs on the Columbia River occurred between 63 and 65°F. Age data from 1,056 Massachusetts striped bass showed growth comparable to southern populations, with greatest annual growth in the second year.
- **Massachusetts farm ponds:** Of 12 small ponds studied, most were acidic and of low productivity and were marginal for both cold- and warm-water species.
- **Trout fitness and conditioning ([[Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** Exercised rainbow trout acquired the conditioned avoidance response faster than controls in 12 of 16 experiments.
- **Channel catfish in Arizona impoundments ([[Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit]]):** A sonic internal fish tag was developed with a service life of at least 100 days.

**Conclusions and plans**

The report concludes that the program is broadening its base, with new units such as Washington and Oregon still building programs. Future work is planned on strip-mine reclamation, hatchery trout fitness, stream-drift invertebrates, salmonid genetics and the sporadic fisheries of shallow eutrophic lakes. Most unit research is short and summarized, and readers are referred to unit personnel for full data.
