# Seasonal Changes in Harvest and Fishing Effort on a Section of the Des Moines River, Iowa

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #Ictaluridae #recreational-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This creel census study examined seasonal patterns in fishing pressure and harvest on a 7-mile section of the [[Des Moines River]] in [[Boone County]], Iowa, between July 1957 and September 1958. The study area, located between Fraser and Boone dams, was surveyed using a modified Latin square design with 2-hour sampling periods. The river provided approximately 85,000 man-hours of fishing annually (excluding night and winter fishing), with an average catch rate of less than one fish per two hours. [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Carp]] dominated the harvest across all seasons, together absorbing most angling pressure. Weekend fishing pressure consistently exceeded weekday pressure, with Sunday being the most heavily utilized day. The study documented clear seasonal and diurnal patterns in angler distribution and species composition.

## Background & Description

The [[Des Moines River]] is Iowa's largest river, originating in southwestern Minnesota and flowing 535 miles southeasterly into the [[Mississippi River]]. The river is geologically young (approximately 11,000 years old) with a stream bed consisting mainly of bedrock, gravel, and sand. In central Iowa, the stream gradient of 1.5 feet per mile ensures reasonably rapid flow. Approximately 93% of the drainage area is agricultural land.

The study area encompassed a 7-mile section in [[Boone County]] between the lowhead dam at Fraser and the waterworks dam at Boone. The [[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]] initiated this intensive creel census survey in summer 1957. Sampling employed a modified Latin square design subdivided into weekday (Monday-Friday) and weekend (Saturday-Sunday) schedules to ensure adequate coverage of heavily fished weekend days.

Fishing days were considered 14 hours long during spring and summer, and 12 hours during fall. Individual sampling periods lasted 2 hours. Fisherman counts were completed using a small flat-bottomed boat with 5-horsepower motor when water levels permitted navigation; otherwise, automobile surveys were conducted as much of the river is readily accessible by vehicle. Angling statistics were secured through personal contact with anglers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Des Moines River]] in central Iowa functions primarily as a [[Channel Catfish]] stream. Since central Iowa has relatively few large lakes, rivers in this section, especially the Des Moines River, absorb the bulk of fishing pressure. The river supports a diverse fish community including [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Walleye]] (*Sander vitreus*), various sucker species (Catostomidae), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), and bullheads (*Ameiurus* spp.).

Water conditions significantly influenced catch patterns. High water during July 1958 reduced [[Channel Catfish]] harvest during their normally productive period. Conversely, very low water in August 1958 caused larger fish to retreat to deeper holes, further reducing catch rates.

Most anglers (72%) lived within a 10-mile radius of the river, with only 3% traveling more than 75 miles (typically visiting relatives). This local angler base reflects the river's importance as a regional recreational resource.

## Key Findings

**Temporal Distribution of Fishing Pressure:**

Weekend fishing pressure exceeded weekday pressure across all seasons. Sunday was consistently the most heavily utilized day, with family groups combining picnicking with fishing. Friday experienced the lowest weekday pressure, likely due to its proximity to the weekend when anglers preferred leisurely trips.

Mean fisherman hours by day of week varied seasonally:
- Summer 1957: Monday (398), Tuesday (458), Wednesday (552), Thursday (429), Friday (405), Saturday (672), Sunday (967)
- Fall 1957: Monday (236), Tuesday (222), Wednesday (240), Thursday (320), Friday (64), Saturday (254), Sunday (526)
- Spring 1958: Monday (213), Tuesday (161), Wednesday (105), Thursday (255), Friday (186), Saturday (404), Sunday (850)
- Summer 1958: Monday (284), Tuesday (353), Wednesday (266), Thursday (231), Friday (263), Saturday (452), Sunday (592)

**Diurnal Patterns:**

Spring and summer seasons displayed two distinct peaks in fishing pressure:
1. Mid-afternoon (2-4 p.m.) coinciding with warmest temperatures, especially pronounced in spring when many anglers sought [[Carp]]
2. Late evening (6-8 p.m.) pronounced in summer schedules, when anglers fished after work and when [[Walleye]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were most productive

Fall fishing concentrated during mid and late afternoon (1-5 p.m.), the warmest part of the day. Late evening periods were lightly utilized due to early darkness and cool temperatures.

**Total Fishing Pressure:**

Estimated total angling pressure (excluding after-dark fishing):
- Summer 1957: 3,916 fisherman hours per week (559 per mile)
- Fall 1957: 1,714 fisherman hours per week (245 per mile)
- Spring 1958: 2,563 fisherman hours per week (366 per mile)
- Summer 1958: 2,440 fisherman hours per week (349 per mile)

Annual total: approximately 85,000 man-hours for the 7-mile section.

**Catch Rates by Angler Type:**

Boat anglers generally achieved the best success rates. Waders had the lowest catch per hour but often were competent fishermen targeting specific species and rejecting small fish. Shore anglers were most numerous, taking nearly every species and keeping many small fish.

Fish per fisherman hour by angler type and season:
- Summer 1957: Boat (0.52), Shore (0.44), Wading (0.35), Total (0.44)
- Fall 1957: Boat (0.46), Shore (0.29), Wading (0.19), Total (0.28)
- Spring 1958: Boat (0.34), Shore (0.28), Wading (0.53), Total (0.30)
- Summer 1958: Boat (0.48), Shore (0.39), Wading (0.23), Total (0.39)

Overall average: less than one fish per two hours of fishing.

**Species Composition:**

[[Channel Catfish]] and [[Carp]] dominated harvest across all seasons, together absorbing most angling pressure.

Percentage of total catch by species:
- **Channel Catfish**: Summer 1957 (47.5%), Fall 1957 (36.2%), Spring 1958 (37.9%), Summer 1958 (29.9%)
- **Carp**: Summer 1957 (41.2%), Fall 1957 (37.3%), Spring 1958 (35.1%), Summer 1958 (56.7%)
- **Walleye**: Summer 1957 (3.6%), Fall 1957 (16.4%), Spring 1958 (3.7%), Summer 1958 (3.8%)
- **Suckers**: Summer 1957 (0%), Fall 1957 (3.7%), Spring 1958 (16.4%), Summer 1958 (3.7%)
- **Smallmouth Bass**: Summer 1957 (1.7%), Fall 1957 (2.8%), Spring 1958 (2.4%), Summer 1958 (1.3%)
- **Flathead Catfish**: Summer 1957 (1.0%), Fall 1957 (2.0%), Spring 1958 (2.3%), Summer 1958 (0.9%)
- **Bullhead**: Summer 1957 (3.1%), Fall 1957 (0.8%), Spring 1958 (0.8%), Summer 1958 (3.3%)
- **Others**: Summer 1957 (1.9%), Fall 1957 (0.8%), Spring 1958 (1.1%), Summer 1958 (0.4%)

[[Channel Catfish]] were most important in Summer 1957 and Spring 1958. [[Carp]] dominated Summer 1958 harvest (56.7%). Reduced [[Channel Catfish]] catch in Summer 1958 was attributed to high water in July (normally peak harvest period) and very low water in August causing larger fish to retreat to deeper holes.

[[Walleye]] were most important in fall (16.4% of catch). Though ardently sought year-round by dedicated anglers, spring [[Walleye]] harvest was limited because the legal season began 6 weeks after census initiation. Suckers were abundant in spring (16.4%), with many used solely as cut bait for [[Channel Catfish]].

[[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] were relatively unimportant percentage-wise but each had a reasonably large following of patient, specialized fishermen.
