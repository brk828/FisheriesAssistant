# Evaluation of Flushing of a High-Selenium Backwater Channel in the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates whether a water control structure, opened on 5 December 1996, that routed Colorado River flow through the [[Walter Walker State Wildlife Area]] (WWSWA) backwater channel could reduce selenium and other inorganic elements in water, sediment, aquatic invertebrates, forage fish, and endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. The work ran from May 1995 to September 1998 and was carried out by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. Selenium fell sharply in most ecosystem components after the structure opened, but pikeminnow muscle-plug selenium did not show a matching decline. The authors conclude that flushing worked for the physical environment and forage base, while fish residues were governed by factors such as river flow, fish size, and dietary history.

## Background & Description

Selenium contamination in the upper and lower [[Colorado River Basin]] has been linked to population declines of federally listed endangered fishes, particularly the [[Razorback Sucker]]. Four endangered species occur in the upper basin: Colorado Pikeminnow, Razorback Sucker, [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. The [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] aims to restore self-sustaining populations while allowing continued water development. The [[Floodplain Habitat Restoration]] effort reconnects floodplain habitats, but site selection must avoid selenium problems.

Adult Colorado Pikeminnow are typically found in deep, fast waters and large tributary pools. Young fish under 50 mm favor quiet backwaters and shallow pools, feeding on cladocerans, copepods, and chironomid larvae. Osmundson and Burnham (1998) estimated 599 Colorado Pikeminnow in the upper river (254 adults in 0–? km and 344 adults and subadults in the lower 181 km), a population lower than historical accounts suggested.

The WWSWA backwater was a former gravel pit beside the river, separated by a dike. Spring runoff backed river water into the channel, and groundwater from the cobble aquifer, along with North Pond discharge, carried elevated selenium. The sampling stations were WW1 through WW9, with WW4 and WW8 redesignated WW4a/WW4b and WW8a/WW8b after the structure opened.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water quality at the site followed seasonal flow. Conductivity at the marsh station (WW4b) fell from 6160 µmhos/cm in 1995 to 2620 µmhos/cm in 1998 after the structure opened. Selenium in filtered water correlated positively with nitrate, calcium, hardness, magnesium, sulfate, and conductivity, which the authors link to irrigation-influenced groundwater.

Selenium moves through the food web from water and sediment into algae, plankton, invertebrates, and small forage fish (such as [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]]), then into piscivorous pikeminnow. Depuration is slow in larger fish, with half-lives of 19–30 days in young fish and over 100 days in adult razorback sucker muscle.

Regional context: [[Selenium Toxicity Thresholds]] reported in the paper include a 2 µg/g sediment toxicity threshold (Lemly, 1993a) and a whole-body fish threshold of 4 µg/g (Lemly, 1996). Uncontaminated aquatic systems typically have below 1 µg/L selenium (Maier and Knight, 1994).

## Key Findings

- **Water:** Filtered selenium at WWSWA was 21.0 µg/L in 1995, 23.5 µg/L in 1996, 2.1 µg/L in 1997, and 2.1 µg/L in 1998. (Note: the OCR-garbled 1997 value is reported in the paper's text as 2.1 µg/L.)
- **Sediment:** Selenium in sediment cores and traps was 8.5 µg/g in 1995, 4.8 µg/g in 1996 (as read from the scan), 4.8 µg/g in 1997, and 1.1 µg/g in 1998.
- **Aquatic invertebrates:** Selenium was 12.2 µg/g in 1996, 5.5 µg/g in 1997, and 4.9 µg/g in 1998.
- **Forage fish:** Selenium was 21.2 µg/g in 1996, 20.2 µg/g in 1997, and 8.6 µg/g in 1998, a 68% reduction and a fourfold drop from 1996 to 1998.
- **Colorado Pikeminnow muscle plugs:** Means were 9.5 µg/g (1995, n = 49), 9.5 µg/g (1996, n = 40), 9.0 µg/g (1997, n = 54), and 10.3 µg/g (1998, n = 3). No significant differences occurred among 1995–1998, but all were below the 16.1 µg/g mean from 1994 (Osmundson et al., 2000).
- **Pikeminnow–flow link:** Muscle-plug selenium was negatively correlated with May mean river flow (r = −0.87, p = 0.05) for 1994–1998 and decreased with increasing fish weight (r = −0.34, p < 0.0001, n = 157) and total length (r = −0.37, p < 0.0001, n = 162).
- **Recaptures:** Of 16 fish captured in 1994, all were recaptured in 1995–1997. Of 45 fish captured in 1995, 25 were recaptured. Of 35 fish captured in 1996, 26 were recaptured. Of 54 fish captured in 1997, 21 were recaptured.
- **Whole-body estimate:** Using a 1.667 conversion factor, the authors estimated whole-body pikeminnow selenium at 15.0–27.1 µg/g inside WWSWA, which is 5–9 times the NCBP 85th percentile.
- **Interpretation:** The authors conclude that flushing efficiently lowered selenium in water, sediment, and biota, and that pikeminnow residues were likely driven by high-flow years, fish size, and fidelity to home feeding ranges. They recommend continued operation and monitoring, since continued flushing would probably have reduced residues further.
