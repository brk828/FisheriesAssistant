## Executive Summary
This document is a 1993 annual administrative report submitted by William J. Matter (University of Arizona) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under [[Endangered Species Subpermit PRT-676811]], detailing research activities involving the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]]. The report is largely a compliance/status update rather than a primary data paper, covering two components: (1) a failed attempt at field collection using "mini-minnow traps" in [[Cienega Creek]], and (2) the loss of a captive laboratory population due to infrastructure failure. The report also references an associated M.S. thesis by Robert Forrest on Gila topminnow habitat use, submitted alongside this report.

## Background & Description
No morphological data, measurements, or physical descriptions of the [[Gila Topminnow]] are provided in this source. The document is administrative in nature and focuses on permit compliance, population management, and field/lab logistics rather than anatomical or taxonomic characterization.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range/habitat**: [[Cienega Creek]], a marshy desert stream system in Arizona, representative of the species' native [[Sonoran Desert Aquatic Habitat]].
- **Population source**: The captive laboratory population was founded from 25 wild-caught individuals collected from Cienega Creek in 1991, later expanding to several hundred fish through captive reproduction — indicating high fecundity/reproductive rate typical of live-bearing [[Poeciliidae]].
- **Habitat use research**: Referenced (but not detailed in this report) via Robert Forrest's M.S. thesis, which specifically examined [[Gila Topminnow Habitat Use]] through combined field and laboratory studies.
- **Intended experimental use**: The captive population was being maintained for laboratory preference trials testing topminnow responses to "selected environmental conditions," suggesting ongoing interest in the species' [[Habitat Preference Testing]] and microhabitat selection within marshy cienega environments.

## Key Findings
- **No field collections were made in 1993**: Planned trials using "mini-minnow traps" to capture Gila topminnow from marshy areas of Cienega Creek did not proceed due to lack of reliable field/student assistance.
- **Captive population established in 1991**: A laboratory population of several hundred Gila topminnow was successfully bred in captivity from 25 founder individuals collected from Cienega Creek, demonstrating viable captive propagation potential for this endangered species.
- **Total loss of captive population**: A summer storm damaged the above-ground holding pool at the Arizona Cooperative Wildlife and Fisheries Research Unit; failure of the vinyl liner resulted in complete water loss and death of all captive fish.
- **No specimens recovered**: Rapid decomposition and predation (by birds and other animals) prevented recovery of dead fish for preservation in the University collection, resulting in a total loss of research material from this population.
- **Research discontinuation**: Because the associated M.S. thesis research (by Robert Forrest) was already complete, the permit holder did not request authorization to re-establish a captive population, and formally notified USFWS that no Gila topminnow were currently being held under the subpermit.
- **Associated thesis**: The report references a completed M.S. thesis by [[Robert Forrest]] on [[Gila Topminnow Habitat Use]], which utilized both field and laboratory components enabled by this permitted research program.
