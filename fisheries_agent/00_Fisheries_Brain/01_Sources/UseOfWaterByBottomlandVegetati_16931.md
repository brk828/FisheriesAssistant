# Use of Water by Bottom-Land Vegetation in Lower Safford Valley, Arizona

#botany #hydrology #phreatophytes

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Water-Supply Paper (1950), prepared with the [[Defense Plant Corporation]] and based on 1943–44 fieldwork, quantifies the [[Use of Water]] consumed by nonbeneficial [[Phreatophytes]] along the [[Gila River]] in lower [[Safford Valley]]. It was undertaken because all surface water in the basin above Coolidge Dam had been appropriated, and a proposed plan to clear vegetation and save water for copper mining at [[Morenci Copper Mines]] was abandoned in November 1943. The study nevertheless produced a total water-use estimate of about 28,000 acre-feet per year for the 46-mile reach, computed by six largely independent methods that agreed within 20 percent of their mean.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Lower Safford Valley is an alluvial lowland 1–3 miles wide, covering about 38,000 acres (slightly under 60 square miles), underlain to an average depth of about 100 feet by silt, sand, and gravel deposited by the river.
- **Land use:** Farmed lands are irrigated from wells and the river; the bottom land is a belt of phreatophyte-covered vegetation subject to periodic flooding. Irrigation began in 1872; groundwater pumping for irrigation began about 1930. Pumpage was about 52,000 acre-feet in 1944.
- **Vegetation:** Plants are divided into xerophytes (e.g., [[Creosote Bush]], *Covillea tridentata*, which were excluded from the study) and [[Phreatophytes]], whose roots reach the [[Water Table]] or capillary fringe. Major bottom-land phreatophytes are [[Saltcedar]], [[Baccharis]], [[Cottonwood]], and [[Mesquite]]. Originally native cottonwood and willow dominated; saltcedar, introduced into the region in the early 20th century, spread rapidly and displaced most native plants. Saltcedar shoots grew 6–8 feet in about 12 months and reached an average of 21/4 feet in 3 months after cutting at the [[Fort Thomas Test Plot]].
- **Climate:** Mild and arid. Average annual rainfall at Thatcher was 9.50 inches (34-year record to 1937). The average frost-free period was 203 days.
- **Purpose:** To measure water consumed by "worthless" vegetation, the first attempt to measure this on a scale of economic significance, and to supply basic data for estimating water that might be saved by clearing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water sources for the groundwater reservoir:** Spring flow and upward seepage from older alluvial fill, underflow of the Gila River and washes, river recharge when the water table is below river stage, and infiltration from rainfall and irrigation. About one-half of diverted river water and one-fourth of pumped water reach the water table.
- **Water table:** Depth ranges from about 1 foot to 12 feet in the bottom land and from about 8 to 50 feet in farmed areas. The water table generally slopes toward the river and downstream, with gradients of 10–20 feet per mile in some reaches. Gila River gains from the reservoir in some reaches and loses in others (e.g., Glenbar to Fort Thomas, losing in June–August).
- **Water quality:** Surface waters contain about 250 to 6,000 parts per million (ppm) dissolved solids; ground waters range from about 200 to more than 10,000 ppm. Dilute waters are mostly sodium or calcium with bicarbonate; highly mineralized waters are mostly sodium and chloride. Artesian leakage along fault zones (e.g., near Fort Thomas and Eden, including [[Indian Hot Springs]]) raises local salinity and temperature.
- **Salt cycling by saltcedar:** Saltcedar exudes highly saline water (guttation). One sample had 41,000 ppm dissolved solids, about 20 times the local groundwater. Salt crystals accumulate on the soil and corrode exposed observation-well casings.
- **Concentration effects:** Baccharis used more water where groundwater was dilute. Extremely high salinity may kill some species, and saltcedar's use may decline at high salinity, though the data were not conclusive.

## Key Findings

**Total use of water (12 months ending September 30, 1944)**

- Total use by bottom-land vegetation in the Thatcher–Calva reach was about **28,000 acre-feet** on **9,303 acres**.
- About **23,000 acre-feet** came from the ground-water reservoir; the remainder came from precipitation (about 5,000 acre-feet in the summary; table 10 gives 5,390 acre-feet).
- More than **75 percent** of the ground-water draft was used by saltcedar, which covered about 50 percent of the gross area.
- The authors note 1944 was a subnormal year, so total use in other years may exceed 28,000 acre-feet.

**Methods (six, compared in [[Comparison of Results]])**

1. [[Tank Method]]: native plants grown in large tanks with a maintained water table at the [[Glenbar Experiment Station]].
2. [[Transpiration-Well Method]]: diurnal water-table fluctuations multiplied by the [[Coefficient of Drainage]] (specific yield).
3. [[Seepage-Run Method]]: river gains measured at about monthly intervals, with the growing-season minus nongrowing-season difference taken as use.
4. [[Inflow-Outflow Method]]: a water budget with use computed as the residual.
5. [[Chloride-Increase Method]]: use inferred from the increase in chloride concentration of groundwater crossing the vegetated area.
6. [[Slope-Seepage Method]]: groundwater inflow to the bottom land estimated from the water-table gradient, transmissibility, and reach length.

- Results by all methods fell within **plus 18.7 to minus 19.9 percent** of the average (Glenbar to Black Point reach). The authors describe this agreement as an indication of reasonable accuracy, though not laboratory-level precision.

**Use of water per species (100 percent volume density, feet per year, including 0.57 foot from precipitation)**

- [[Saltcedar]]: 7.2 feet
- [[Cottonwood]]: 6.0 feet
- [[Baccharis]]: 4.7 feet
- [[Mesquite]]: 3.3 feet

**Methodological contributions**

- The [[Volume-Density Method]] (a way to convert tank results to field density) was developed during the study and is considered applicable to other areas and phreatophyte types.
- Three other methods (water inventory, cut-twig, and ground-water rating curve) were proposed but could not produce quantitative results in this valley.

**Clearing and water salvage**

- Clearing could reduce consumption, but the water saved would be less than 28,000 acre-feet per year, because evaporation from bare soil would continue and any replacement grasses would also use water.
- Burning green saltcedar failed to kill it permanently; cutting also failed (regrowth within 3 months). Roots must be removed or destroyed for permanent clearing.
- Clearing would likely reduce flood hazard by increasing channel capacity, and it would increase downstream Gila River flow.
- Clearing would probably not waterlog the bottom land, and it would have no direct effect on groundwater quality beneath farmed lands. Salt concentration in the river would temporarily rise and then decline after a new equilibrium formed.

**Conclusions**

- The study found that data from one growing season are limited; the authors recommend at least two.
- No method developed here can be applied directly to other areas, but the volume-density approach and the methodological comparisons are offered for similar arid basins.
