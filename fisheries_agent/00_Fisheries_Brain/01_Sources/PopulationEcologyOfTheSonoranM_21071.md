# Population Ecology of the Sonoran Mud Turtle at Quitobaquito Springs

#herpetology #chelonian-biology #kinosternidae #conservation-biology #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 14-year study (1982-1995) documents the population ecology of the [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense longifemorale*) at [[Quitobaquito Springs]], [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona. The population represents an endemic subspecies, the [[Rio Sonoyta Mud Turtle]], restricted to Quitobaquito and nearby [[Rio Sonoyta]] in Mexico. The population declined from hundreds in the 1950s to approximately 143 by 1971 and ~100 by the early 1980s, reaching a minimum in the late 1980s. Primary causes include degradation of shallow-water juvenile habitat during early management actions (1961 pond renovation), resulting in reduced juvenile survivorship. Chemical analysis revealed low organochlorine pesticide levels and no evidence of heavy metal toxicity at concentrations likely to cause mortality. However, turtles showed remarkably low lipid storage (mean 2.81% wet weight), suggesting severe nutritional stress. Competition with the thriving [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) population for aquatic invertebrates appears to limit food availability. Habitat restoration in 1989-1990 (creation of shallow, vegetated spring channel) showed early positive results with increased juvenile survivorship and population recovery to ~130 individuals by 1993.

## Background & Description

The [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] is a strongly aquatic southwestern species occurring in perennial to semi-perennial waters. At [[Quitobaquito Springs]], the population represents *K. s. longifemorale*, a subspecies endemic to this hydrologically isolated system. The study site consists of a man-made pond, spring pools, and channel habitat maintained by the [[National Park Service]] as primary refuge for the Federally Endangered [[Desert Pupfish]].

**Morphology and Growth**: Females reach approximately 102 mm carapace length (CL) at age 7 years, the second-lowest growth rate among six Arizona study sites. Males mature at age 4 years (80-84 mm CL); females at just under 6 years (100 mm CL minimum). Growth is moderate in early life and slows abruptly at sexual maturity. Maximum observed age was at least 23 years.

**Habitat Use**: Juveniles strongly prefer shallow water (<1 m depth) with dense emergent vegetation, particularly [[Tule]] (*Scirpus americanus*) stands. Adults utilize all pond areas but show preference for structurally complex habitats. Trapping success for juveniles was 3.3-fold higher in the newly created spring channel (1989-1990) than in pond margins.

**Historical Context**: [[Quitobaquito Springs]] was inhabited and farmed for hundreds to thousands of years. Following National Park Service acquisition in 1957, cessation of grazing and irrigation led to choking vegetation growth. Management interventions in 1961 and 1969-1970 included pond draining and bulldozing, creating a too-deep, straight-walled pool with minimal shallow-water habitat critical for juvenile turtles. Many turtles were removed or given away as pets during these operations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic Ecosystem**: The oasis has no outlet and experiences high evapotranspiration rates in this arid environment (mean annual rainfall varies). Primary productivity is substantial, but aquatic invertebrate availability for turtles is chronically low. The natural vegetation climax appears to be a sedge meadow with little open water.

**Food Web Dynamics**: [[Sonoran Mud Turtles]] are omnivorous, preferring invertebrates and fish but feeding heavily on plants when animal prey is scarce. At Quitobaquito, dissections revealed large amounts of plant material, primarily young [[Tule]] shoots. Invertebrate abundance averaged 0.82 invertebrates/trap-day (range 0.17-1.63), significantly lower than other study populations. The [[Desert Pupfish]] population (mean 4,780 individuals, ~15,000/ha) likely competes intensively with turtles for aquatic invertebrates—a previously unreported competitive interaction between fish and turtles.

**Predation**: Numerous avian predators observed include belted kingfishers, great blue herons, black-crowned night herons, egrets, and mergansers. Wading birds frequently foraged in shallow water where juvenile turtles were most vulnerable. Hatchlings face high predation risk due to small size and soft shells.

**Water Quality**: Total dissolved solids ranged 688-1300 ppm. Arsenic levels were modestly elevated in water and sediment but below toxic thresholds. Chemical analysis of eight turtle carcasses detected low levels of p,p'-DDE (DDT metabolite) in four individuals (mean 0.0455 ppm dry weight), insufficient to support a pesticide toxicity hypothesis. Mean concentrations of boron, chromium, selenium, strontium, and zinc were significantly elevated compared to [[Spiny Softshell Turtles]] from the contaminated [[Gila River]], but direct toxicity could not be established.

## Key Findings

**Sampling Effort**: 74 days of trapping over 14 years yielded 1,099 hoop net trap-days and 470 modified minnow trap-days. Total of 560 turtle records including 273 recaptures of 211 marked individuals, plus 22 recovered carcasses.

**Survivorship Estimates**:
- Adult females: 85.5% annual survivorship (lower than most reported populations)
- Adult males: 90% annual survivorship
- Age 2 juveniles: 62% annual survivorship
- Ages 3-4 juveniles: 84% annual survivorship (transitional to adult rates)
- Egg/hatching success: 85-90% (nest predation negligible)
- First-year survivorship: 28% (from 108 estimated hatchlings to 30 age-2 individuals)

**Reproductive Parameters**:
- Clutch size: mean 4.0 eggs (n=3 clutches)
- Clutch frequency: 1.5 clutches/year/female (relatively low for species)
- Age at first reproduction: females just under 6 years; males 4 years
- Relative clutch mass: 0.069 (low compared to other populations)
- Egg mass: 4.9 g/egg (moderately high)

**Sex Ratio**: Adult sex ratio strongly male-biased at approximately 2:1 (69 males, 44 females observed). Differential survivorship (90% male vs. 85.5% female) accounts for 1.59:1 ratio; remaining 1.26:1 imbalance may reflect temperature-dependent sex determination. Period 1974-1978 showed particularly high male recruitment, possibly related to cooler incubation temperatures during a relatively cool, dry period.

**Population Dynamics**:
- 1950s: "hundreds" of individuals
- 1971: 143 individuals (Gehlbach estimate)
- Early 1980s: ~100 individuals
- Late 1980s: minimum ~85-90 individuals
- 1993: ~130 individuals (apparent recovery)

**Life Table Analysis**: Net replacement rate R₀ = 1.57 indicates population growing at 60% per generation (generation time = 12 years). Sensitivity analysis confirmed stable to growing population under current conditions. Reducing juvenile survivorship (ages 2-4) to 0.50 yields R₀ = 0.52, demonstrating critical importance of juvenile survival for population trajectory.

**Mortality Observations**: Twenty-two dead turtles recovered 1982-1995, an unusually high number for the small population. Most showed no signs of predation or injury. Peak mortality occurred in late 1989-early 1990 during severe drought and record heat. Four of five radio-tagged females died within 0-2 days after capture/handling episodes in 1993, possibly from stress combined with poor nutritional condition.

**Nutritional Status**: Lipid extraction from eight carcasses revealed critically low body fat: mean 2.81% wet weight (range 0.89-4.18%), lipid index 7.6%. These values approach those of experimentally starved turtles and are far below normal ranges for mud and musk turtles (typically 15-40% lipid index). One female with atretic follicles had the lowest lipid content (0.89%). Fat pads were small or completely collapsed; livers averaged only 0.32% of body mass.

**Recruitment Patterns**: Temporal fluctuation evident in age structure. Strong recruitment pulse in early-mid 1970s. Good recruitment during 1982-1985 (average/wet years) and 1989 (following good 1988 rains). Depressed recruitment in 1990 (drought depths). Bumper crop in 1991 (following strong summer 1990 and winter 1990-1991 rains). Recent recruitment correlated with rainfall during egg production periods.

**Age Structure Changes**: Proportion of 3-5 year olds increased significantly from 16.3% (1983-1985) to 42.0% (1992-1995), while 2-year-olds remained stable at ~24% (χ² = 14.58, p < 0.05). This indicates significantly improved juvenile survivorship in the 1990s following habitat restoration.

**Management Response**: Winter 1989-1990 habitat improvements included: (1) creation of 100+ meter semi-natural spring channel with shallow, vegetated water modeled after [[Tule Creek]]; (2) excavation of small shallow area in northeast pond corner; (3) installation of "nesting island." Early results show increased juvenile survivorship and capture rates 3.3-fold higher in channel habitat.

**Hypotheses for Population Decline**:
1. **Juvenile habitat loss** (strongly supported): 1961 pond renovation eliminated shallow vegetated habitat, reducing juvenile survivorship through increased predation vulnerability
2. **Sex ratio anomalies** (possible): Temperature-dependent sex determination may produce male-biased cohorts during cool periods; significance unclear
3. **Nutritional stress** (strongly supported): Chronically low food availability, evidenced by depleted lipids, slow growth, low clutch frequency, and plant-dominated diet
4. **Environmental toxins** (not supported): Low organochlorine levels and no convincing evidence of heavy metal toxicity at acutely toxic concentrations, though interactive effects with nutritional stress cannot be ruled out

**Competition Hypothesis**: Strong evidence suggests [[Desert Pupfish]] success (densities ~15,000/ha) depresses aquatic invertebrate availability for turtles. Experimental studies demonstrate pupfish significantly reduce invertebrate abundance. Growth rate across six Arizona populations correlates closely with invertebrate abundance (r = 0.952, Y = 77.1 + 16.8X). This represents a novel documentation of competition between native fish and turtle populations.

**Conservation Status**: The [[Rio Sonoyta]] population in Mexico faces threats from agricultural and urban development plus aquifer depletion for irrigation. International collaboration needed to assess and protect Mexican populations. Quitobaquito represents critical refuge for this endemic subspecies.
