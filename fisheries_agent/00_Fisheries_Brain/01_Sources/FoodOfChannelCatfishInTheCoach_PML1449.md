# Food of Channel Catfish in the Coachella Canal, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, published 1981 in the *Journal of the Arizona-Nevada Academy of Science*) examines the diet of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) in the [[Coachella Canal]], California. Fish were collected in November 1980 by rotenone sampling and their stomach contents were analyzed. The diet was dominated by aquatic insects, especially a hydropsychid caddisfly larva, *Smicridea* sp. The study also reports the unusually high use of an aquatic moth larva, *Parargyractis confusalis*, which had not previously been recorded in catfish diets. Threadfin Shad and Asiatic Clams were locally abundant in the canal but were not primary food items. The author concludes that channel catfish are omnivorous and opportunistic, and that lining earthen canals with concrete may favor the invertebrate prey on which catfish depend.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), a North American ictalurid widely valued as a sport fish.
- **Introduction history:** Introduced into the lower [[Colorado River]] in 1892–93. Common by the early 1940s and now established throughout the river system, including hundreds of kilometers of artificial waterways.
- **Prior diet studies:** The paper reviews earlier work (see [[Miller 1966]] and [[Carlander 1969]]) and summarizes that young catfish feed mainly on aquatic insects and benthic arthropods, while larger individuals become increasingly piscivorous.
- **Study area:** The earthen [[Coachella Canal]] in Imperial County, California. Three sampling stations were used:
  - **Station A:** Pool-like area and straight reach directly below a concrete check-drop structure.
  - **Station B:** Linear reach with no structures.
  - **Station C:** Double-barreled concrete box siphon.
- **Acknowledgments:** Support came from the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (Boulder City, Nevada) and the [[California Department Of Fish And Game]].

### Methods

- **Sample:** 153 channel catfish, preserved in 10% formalin, measured (total length) and weighed in the laboratory.
- **Stomach analysis:** Esophagus and stomach were separated from the intestine. Contents were identified and counted at 10× magnification, and the volume percentage of each food item was visually estimated.
- **Size groups:** Catfish were grouped by total length (<100, 100–149, 150–199, 200–299, and ≥300 mm) and analyzed by size class and station.
- **Intestines:** Contents of 20 fish (101–322 mm TL) were qualitatively examined for comparison with stomach contents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Canal lining effects:** The author notes that as earthen canals are increasingly lined with concrete to improve water transport efficiency, populations of invertebrates such as [[Hydropsychidae]] caddisflies and aquatic Lepidoptera may increase. Earlier work by [[Marsh And Steinmetz 1981]] is cited for this pattern. Because these insects are a primary food for catfish, the author suggests that such modifications may enhance the fisheries supported by artificial waterways.
- **Invertebrate habitat:** The abundance of caddisfly and moth larvae on concrete surfaces is considered the likely reason for their dominance in the catfish diet.
- **Prey comparisons:** Diet results most closely resemble those of [[Dill 1944]] for an irrigation canal in California. Other regional studies include [[Kimsey Et Al 1957]] (lower Colorado River, Palo Verde Lagoon) and [[Minckley 1981]] (lower Colorado River).
- **Threadfin Shad:** The shad is common in the canal, but catfish made little use of it. A similar pattern was reported at Palo Verde Lagoon.
- **Asiatic Clam:** Important as food for channel catfish in the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta and on the lower Colorado River, but in the Coachella Canal this clam was the most abundant benthic invertebrate on natural substrates, yet was rarely eaten.

## Key Findings

### Overall diet

- **Dominant food:** The hydropsychid caddisfly larva *Smicridea* sp. occurred in 99% of stomachs, averaged 181 individuals per stomach, and contributed 62.2% of total biomass. One 323 mm catfish contained 646 individuals. About 20,000 caddisflies were counted across all stomachs, but only 18 were pupae and none were adults.
- **Moth larvae:** *Parargyractis confusalis* (aquatic Lepidoptera larvae) occurred in almost 62% of stomachs and made up a substantial share of biomass. Their use was highest at Station A. This is the first record of this insect in channel catfish diets, according to the author.
- **Chironomid midges:** Present in more than 70% of stomachs but contributed only a fraction of a percent of biomass. Occurrence was about 90% at Station A, versus 33% at Station B and 20% at Station C.
- **Odonates:** Mostly damselflies of the genus *Hetaerina*, with a few gomphid dragonflies. Occurrence was about 64% overall (75% at A, ~33% at C), and odonates made up nearly 16% of biomass, largely through their importance at Station A.
- **Mayflies:** *Baetis* sp. nymphs were a minor component of the diet, contributing only about 10 mg overall.
- **Leptocerid caddisflies:** *Nectopsyche* sp. occurred in more than 26% of stomachs, but made up a small share of biomass except in larger fish at Station A.
- **Crayfish:** Procambarus clarkii occurred in only about 3% of stomachs but could make up a large share of individual meals.
- **Fish:** Fish made up only about 8% of the overall diet. Threadfin Shad remains were found in two catfish at Station A. In one of these, shad made up 77% of stomach contents.
- **Filamentous algae:** *Cladophora glomerata* occurred in about 10% of stomachs and made up nearly 17% of total biomass, mainly in fish that had eaten algae in quantity.
- **Asiatic Clam:** Remains were found in the intestine of one 256 mm catfish. The author reports that intestinal contents of 20 fish matched stomach contents in composition and proportion.

### Size and station patterns

- **Overall size:** Total length ranged from 75–415 mm (mean 161 mm). Mean weight was 46.66 g (range given in the source; the OCR text is partly garbled).
- **Station differences:** Fish were largest at Station A (mean 168 mm, 52.79 g), smallest at Station B (mean 105 mm, 13.20 g), and intermediate at Station C (mean 129 mm, 18.16 g). Diets were broadly similar across stations.
- **Feeding by size:** Caddisfly biomass in fish at Station A dropped in larger size classes. Odonate use rose approximately linearly with fish size, from about 13% in fish under 100 mm to 100% in fish of 200–299 mm.
- **Stomach fullness:** All but two stomachs contained some food, and most were full.

### Conclusions

- Channel catfish in the Coachella Canal are omnivorous and opportunistic. Their diet is dominated by caddisfly larvae, with moth larvae, odonates, and chironomids also important.
- The high use of aquatic moth larvae is unusual and had not been reported before.
- Threadfin Shad and Asiatic Clams are abundant in the canal but are not major food sources.
- Concrete lining of earthen canals may increase invertebrate populations that serve as catfish prey, possibly boosting canal fisheries.
