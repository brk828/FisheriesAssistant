# Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta) Status Survey of the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program Technical Report 186, January 2002, by Jeremy B. Voeltz) assesses the current status of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and the [[Headwater Chub]] (*Gila nigra*) in the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was funded by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under the [[Central Arizona Project]] Native Fish Conservation and Nonnative Aquatic Species Control and Management programs. The report compiles taxonomy, life history, historic and current distribution, and stream-by-stream status, and it is intended to inform whether future management of these species is needed.

Roundtail chub are estimated to occupy about 18% of their historic range in the lower basin, and headwater chub about 40%. Of 30 streams known to hold either species, 17 are rated Unstable-Threatened, 12 Stable-Threatened, and 1 Stable-Secure.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The roundtail chub is one of six or seven recognized *Gila* species in the Colorado River basin (Table 1 lists the species). The name *grahami* (Baird and Girard 1854), once applied to intermediate populations, was shown to be a synonym of *G. robusta* and therefore invalid. The earliest available replacement name, *Gila nigra* (Cope and Yarrow 1875), was recognized as a full species by Minckley and DeMarais (2000) and will be used in the 2002 American Fisheries Society checklist. Taxonomic history is complicated by hybrid-origin hypotheses (DeMarais 1986, 1992) and morphological overlap among *G. robusta*, *G. nigra*, and *G. intermedia* (see [[Gila Chub]]).

**Conservation history.** *G. robusta* was a candidate species from the late 1970s. The Desert Fishes Recovery Team recommended listing in several years (1989–1996), but no listing was initiated, and the species was dropped from candidate status in 1995–1996 when category 2 was abolished. The Pahranagat roundtail chub (*G. r. jordani*) is separately protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Description.** Roundtail chub typically measure 250–350 mm total length, occasionally 500–600 mm in large rivers. The body is thick and moderately streamlined, the dorsal fin is weakly falcate, and the fatty nuchal hump is rare. Dorsal fin rays usually number nine, lateral line scales usually exceed 80, and vertebrae number 43–49. Coloration is dusky to green dorsally, silvery below, with orange-red breeding coloration. The largest recorded specimen caught by hook and line measured 469.9 mm and weighed 1782.62 g (lower Salt River, March 1984). Headwater chub are similar but smaller, with morphometric counts intermediate between *G. robusta* and *G. intermedia*, and individuals are difficult to distinguish in the field.

**Life history.**
- *Diet:* Omnivorous. Roundtail chub in Aravaipa Creek ate a wide variety of food items, 6 of 21 (28.6%) unique to them. Headwater chub diets are dominated by aquatic insects, ostracods, algae, and plant material, with no fish remains in Fossil Creek samples.
- *Maturity and reproduction:* Reproductive activity usually begins at ages 2–5. Spawning occurs late spring to early summer, roughly February through June in the lower basin, at water temperatures near 18 °C (roundtail) and 22 °C (headwater). Females from the Verde River contained 7,267–26,903 eggs; one Fossil Creek female of 321 g held 36,959 eggs.
- *Age and growth:* Growth is about 50 mm per year until age 4, slowing afterward. In the upper Verde, otolith annuli were laid down around May, and age-readings were 100% consistent on known-age hatchery fish (Brouder et al. 2000).
- *Parasites:* Reported infections include the copepod *Lernaea*, yellow grub (*Clinostomum marginatum*), the Asian fish tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), and multiple trematodes and nematodes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Roundtail chub occupy cool to warm, mid-elevation rivers, favoring deep pools and eddies near cover such as boulders, undercut banks, and vegetation. Juveniles use shallower glides and runs with shoreline cover. Headwater chub occupy middle and headwater reaches of mid-sized streams, adults in deep nearshore pools next to riffles, and juveniles in shallow low-velocity margins. Seasonal movements of roundtail chub in Aravaipa Creek track thermal conditions, and most movements recorded in the upper Verde were under 100 m.

**Threats.** The report identifies several recurring pressures:
- Nonnative predators and competitors, notably [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], green sunfish, and crayfish (*Orconectes* and *Procambarus* species).
- Habitat alteration from groundwater pumping, diversions, reservoirs, and mining. Roundtail habitat is essentially eliminated when flows fall below about 0.3 m³/s (10 cfs).
- Livestock grazing, which erodes banks and removes riparian vegetation.
- Toxic effluents and sediments from mining.
- Development and growing water demand across the basin.

**Land and water context.** Many streams flow through Navajo, Fort Apache, San Carlos, and other reservations where AGFD was denied access, leaving status unknown. Monitoring of the Verde, Salt, and Gila basins relies on recent sampling by AGFD, Arizona State University, and the U.S. Forest Service. Management programs such as the [[Tres Rios Project]] aim to restore riparian habitat near the Gila–Salt confluence.

## Key Findings

**Distribution**
- Historic range (lower basin) estimated at about 5,000 km of stream: about 4,500 km for roundtail and 500 km for headwater chub.
- Current range is about 1,000 km (20% of historic range): 800 km for roundtail (18%) and 200 km for headwater chub (40%).
- About 3,300 km (66%) of historic range no longer holds chubs. About 700 km (14%) has unknown current status, mostly on reservation waters.
- Chubs are known to occur in at least 30 streams and considered extirpated from 13 (including the lower Colorado mainstem, Little Colorado River, Zuni River, Big Sandy River, lower Gila, San Pedro, San Francisco, and Bill Williams).

**Status ratings (30 occupied streams)**
- 17 Unstable-Threatened, 12 Stable-Threatened, 1 Stable-Secure (Deadman Creek, headwater chub).

**Selected stream findings**
- *East Clear Creek:* Stable-Threatened; roundtail common in 1999–2000 sampling, mainly intermittent reaches.
- *Boulder Creek:* Stable-Threatened; roundtail the most abundant species sampled in 1996 and abundant in 1999.
- *Francis Creek:* Stable-Threatened; roundtail 47% of catch, 80% juveniles, indicating reproduction.
- *Aravaipa Creek:* Stable-Threatened; one of Arizona's most intensively monitored streams; red shiner reinvasion a threat. A fish barrier was completed on lower Aravaipa in 2001.
- *Verde River:* Unstable-Threatened; common in upper reaches, but upper-river fixed-station data show decline; lower river population estimated above 6,000 fish by mark-recapture.
- *Salt River (upper):* roundtail very rare; flathead catfish, which may preclude reestablishment, were stocked in the early 1970s.
- *Tonto Creek:* Unstable-Threatened; headwater chub restricted to canyon reaches above Gun Creek.
- *Trout Creek:* Unstable-Threatened; no roundtail found in middle reaches in 2001, habitats occupied by nonnatives.
- *Dry Beaver Creek:* Extirpated (estimated); green sunfish abundant, no chubs found.
- *Horton and Christopher Creeks:* Extirpated; "bonytails" reported by Madsen (1935a) likely headwater chub, no museum specimens.
- *Rye Creek:* Extirpated; nonnative fish and crayfish abundant.

**Management recommendations**
1. Preserve the existing range of genetic variation among *Gila*, regardless of taxonomic status.
2. Recognize *Gila nigra* in AGFD Commission Order 40 and agency literature.
3. The current angling limit (1 chub/day, over 13 inches) probably has little negative effect on roundtail. Headwater chub rarely reach 13 inches, so they should share the same regulations because field identification is difficult.
4. Maintain habitat, secure water rights on AGFD properties, and limit grazing, OHV use, recreation impacts, and timber harvest in chub watersheds.
5. Conduct more thorough sampling, since current surveys are sparse and often limited to easily accessible reaches.
6. Identify translocation sites; for example, roundtail from Cherry Creek could be moved to Ash Creek.
7. Develop working relationships with the White Mountain Apache and San Carlos Apache tribes to determine the status of about 14% of historic range.
8. Ban live transport of crayfish, a regulation AGFD adopted to limit spread of nonnative crayfish.

**Knowledge gaps**
- Status of populations on reservation waters is unknown.
- No museum collections were found for the Middle Fork Gila River, although sampled and presumed *G. nigra*.
- Whether roundtail chub once occupied Silver Creek, a Little Colorado River tributary, is unresolved.
