# Introduction of Palaemonetes paludosus into the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Palaemonidae #crustacean #species-introduction #forage-enhancement

## Executive Summary

The [[California Department of Fish and Game]] introduced the freshwater shrimp [[Palaemonetes paludosus]] into the [[Lower Colorado River]] system beginning in 1958 to improve the food chain for small [[Largemouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus salmoides]]) and [[Channel Catfish]] ([[Ictalurus punctatus]]). Initial plantings of approximately 1,225 shrimp were made in isolated ponds near [[Lake Havasu]] and within [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]. By 1962, surveys confirmed limited establishment in several locations, with the largest population in Imperial Camp bait pond. The shrimp showed strong association with dense aquatic vegetation and protective cover. Evidence of predation by largemouth bass was documented, and additional transplants totaling 1,100 shrimp were conducted in 1963 to expand distribution.

## Background & Description

[[Palaemonetes paludosus]] naturally occurs in freshwater ponds, lakes, and streams of the coastal plain from Florida to New Jersey. The species is always associated with cover and is most abundant in dense beds of submerged aquatic plants. In Florida, it serves as an important food source for [[Black Crappie]] ([[Pomoxis nigromaculatus]]) and largemouth bass under 10 inches long.

Mature shrimp range in length from 23 to 53 mm. The species was selected for introduction after literature search and correspondence with biologists in the eastern United States. The [[Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission]] supplied the initial stock for this experimental introduction.

Studies in [[Lake Havasu]] and the River below [[Parker Dam]] had shown a lack of forage for small largemouth bass and channel catfish, prompting the search for a suitable forage organism to improve the food chain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The shrimp were planted in four isolated pond locations:

1. **Havasu Landing pond** (old U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service boat harbor): Approximately 225 shrimp planted August 27, 1958
2. **Taylor Lake pond** (east of Bear Canyon Bluff): Part of 1,000 shrimp planted November 1, 1958
3. **Picacho pond** (18 miles above Imperial Dam): Part of 1,000 shrimp planted November 1, 1958; has direct connection with river channel
4. **Imperial Camp bait pond** (Arizona side, one-quarter mile above Imperial Dam): Part of 1,000 shrimp planted November 1, 1958

The habitat is characterized by luxuriant growths of rooted aquatic vegetation. Principal emergent plants include cattails ([[Typha angustifolia]] and [[T. domingensis]]), marsh cane ([[Phragmites communis]]), and bullrushes ([[Scirpus californicus]], [[S. acutus]], and [[S. olneyi]]). During summer and fall, [[Sago Pondweed]] ([[Potamogeton pectinatus]]) and [[Spiny Naiad]] ([[Najas marina]]) grow profusely in deeper water.

Shrimp were most common near shore in sago pondweed and spiny naiad beds, but also associated with submerged tree limbs, brush, windblown cane, and cattails.

Water chemistry analysis from Imperial Camp bait pond showed: pH 8.1-8.3; total CaCO₃ 184 ppm; total chlorides 262 ppm; total hardness 310 ppm; and total solids 1,275 ppm. This differed from side lakes and ponds of the river system, which averaged: pH 8.2; total CaCO₃ 132 ppm; total chlorides 150 ppm; total hardness 357 ppm; and total solids 850 ppm.

## Key Findings

**1962 Survey Results** (cooperative surveys by California Department of Fish and Game and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]):

- **Havasu Landing pond**: Limited shrimp population despite abundant sago pondweed and spiny naiad growth. Fish predation (approximately 25 small sunfish—mostly [[Bluegill]] ([[Lepomis macrochirus]]) and some [[Green Sunfish]] ([[L. cyanellus]])—per square foot) apparently controlled expansion.

- **Taylor Lake pond**: Fair shrimp population found only in association with spiny naiad, although cattails were dominant plants.

- **Picacho pond**: Few or no shrimp found. Acceptable protective cover lacking. Connection with River and flushing action from heavy summer rains likely explains shrimp occurrence in the River.

- **Imperial Camp bait pond**: Largest shrimp population. Shrimp collected only near shore with dense overhanging brush, tree limbs, and cattails. Shrimp appeared to school and move between areas, complicating detection efforts.

- **River distribution**: No shrimp found from Needles downstream to Imperial National Wildlife Refuge, except in River and backwaters approximately 1 mile upstream from Imperial Dam.

**Reproductive Activity**: Female shrimp in bait pond carried eggs from April 8 through September 19, 1962. Small shrimp (10 mm long, measured from rostrum to tip of telson) collected July 12, 1962, indicated earlier hatching.

**Predation Evidence**: Two shrimp taken from stomach of 7.4-inch largemouth bass (March 2, 1962) and one shrimp from stomach of 4.0-inch bass (May 15, 1962), both from No Name No. 2 backwater lake approximately 1 mile above Imperial Dam.

**1963 Transplants**: Total of 1,100 shrimp (12-38 mm long) transplanted from Imperial Camp Bait Pond to eight new locations in January and March 1963, including sites at Branson's Resort, Marina Village, Lost Lake Camp, Palo Verde areas, and Lake Havasu. Approximately 5% of shrimp carried eggs in March; none in January.

**Collection Methods**: Small dip nets brushed quickly under vegetation proved most effective sampling technique, superior to small-mesh seines, plankton nets, and diving gear.
