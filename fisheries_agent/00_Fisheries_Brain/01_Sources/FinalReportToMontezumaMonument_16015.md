# Limnological Investigations of Montezuma Well, Arizona

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-macrophytes #invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1965 final report to [[Montezuma Castle National Monument]] presents a multi-year limnological study of [[Montezuma Well]], a spring-fed, artesian lake in a limestone depression in Yavapai County, Arizona. The work was funded by a [[National Science Foundation]] grant (NSF-13161) to [[Arizona State University]], with [[Gerald A. Cole]] as Principal Investigator. The study covers morphometry, temperature and heat budget, currents, specific conductance, light and transparency, water chemistry, dissolved oxygen, primary productivity, chlorophyll, the outflow irrigation ditch, sediments and pollen, radiocarbon dating, and the biota.

The Well is a closed-basin system: deep fissures supply water, which leaves through a subterranean swallet and an outer outlet. Its water is strongly acidic from dissolved [[Carbon Dioxide]], and the report attributes the absence of fish to this high free CO₂ despite adequate oxygen. The most notable finding is a radiocarbon anomaly: CO₂ in the inflow dates to about 25,000 years, which led to a re-evaluation of dating methods for the sediments.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The Well lies in the Plio-Pleistocene [[Verde Formation]] at approximately 34° 39′ N, 111° 45′ W, at an elevation of 1,112 m. Its rim stands 17 m to 22 m above the water level. Since 1947 it has been a detached portion of [[Montezuma Castle National Monument]], administered by the National Park Service.
- **Study period:** Work began in July 1960 with an inflatable raft. Sporadic visits continued after the main field period, which ran through the summer of 1962. [[William T. Barry]] and [[John G. Weise]] assisted. [[Richard Heviy]], [[Paul S. Martin]], and [[Paul E. Damon]] of the University of Arizona participated.
- **Origin debate:** The report lists several explanations: an extinct geyser, a meteorite crater, a volcanic crater, and cave collapse (the official Monument explanation). [[Lange (1957)]] proposed a deep salt-lens dissolution and collapse origin, which the authors consider to have merit because the surrounding topography is not typical karst and the Well is the only depression of its type in the Verde Valley. Comparisons were drawn with the [[Cenotes of Yucatan]] and [[Devil's Inkwell]] in New Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and mixing**
- Water enters through at least three deep fissures. Inflow was estimated at a minimum of 4,159 acre-feet/day, based on weir readings, with a peak reading implying 7,293 acre-feet/day. A separate figure of 1.5 million gallons/day is used in the heat and CO₂ calculations.
- Water leaves by the subterranean **swallet**, which reappears in a cave before emerging at the small **outer outlet** about 60 m away. Fluorescein dye showed a transit time of seven minutes. Blocking the swallet with sandbags lowered the outer-outlet flow by only about one-third, which implies undiscovered subsurface outlets.
- Because of the artesian regime, stratification and meromixis are not expected. About one-tenth of the total volume is exchanged every 24 hours.

**Temperature and heat budget**
- Whole-lake temperature varies seasonally. Highest temperatures occur in July and August, above 25 °C, with a surface high of 29.1 °C. Lowest mean temperatures occur in December and January, above 19 °C. A cold snap on 10–11 March 1952 dropped the mean to 19.46 °C.
- Deep-fissure waters are nearly constant. The west hole had a mean of 24.76 °C and an annual range of 0.73 °C. The east hole had a mean of 24.29 °C. Inflow temperatures fall between 74 °F and 76 °F.
- Heat budget: the warmest mean was 24.46 °C and the coldest was 19.09 °C (27 Dec 61). Heat content per unit area was 16,407 g cal/cm² (greatest) and 12,804 g cal/cm² (least), a difference of 3,603 g cal/cm². Inflow supplies more than 1,800 g cal/cm²/day.
- Evaporation averages 0.89 cm/day (winter 0.433 cm/day; summer at least 1 cm/day), with corresponding heat losses of 253 g cal/cm²/day (winter) and 521 g cal/cm²/day (summer).

**Currents**
- Fluorescein tracing and temperature changes show a counter-clockwise circulation pattern, with sinking currents at certain points. Eddy diffusivity estimates range from 0.203 to 0.267 g cm/sec, with an October nocturnal estimate of 0.099 g cm/sec. These values are described by the authors as open to question because of solar warming.

**Specific conductance and light**
- Specific conductance at 25 °C ranged seasonally from 1,122 to 1,394 µS/cm, with negligible vertical variation on any date.
- Turbidity was zero relative to distilled water at all depths.
- Secchi disc readings were taken with a standard 20 cm disc. The mean Secchi value was 3.03 m. The euphotic zone lower limit (1% of surface light) corresponded closely to the Secchi depth, and the mean Secchi depth was where about 80% of surface radiation was absorbed.

**Water chemistry (Table 3, mean values as given)**
- pH: 6.15 (night or overcast) to 6.9 (bright sunny days). Acidity is caused entirely by carbonic acid.
- Free CO₂: about 687 mg/l at pH 6.2 and alkalinity 560 mg/l; about 137 mg/l at pH 6.9. Doudoroff (1957) indicates that 100–200 ppm is rapidly fatal to moderately susceptible freshwater fishes.
- Dissolved oxygen: inflow at 6.25 mg/l, about 75% saturation. Seasonal means ranged from 5.9 mg/l in mid-September to 8.29 mg/l in February. Saturation approaching 400% was recorded in the surface of weed beds on summer days.
- Phosphate: vertical series mean 0.546 mg/l total and 0.229 mg/l orthophosphate. Swallet mean about 0.500 mg/l total and 0.3 mg/l orthophosphate. Outlet below the cave: 0.202 mg/l total and 0.075 mg/l orthophosphate (as given in source). Phosphate loss was about 253 kg/day.
- Nitrogen: NH₄-N 0.38 mg/l, NO₂-N 0.001 mg/l, NO₃-N 0.05 mg/l. The N:P ratio of about 2.5 is very low.
- Silica: 22 mg/l at the end of 1961, later values up to 28 mg/l; 25 mg/l is taken as typical.
- Fluoride: mean 0.25 mg/l. USGS 1940s–1950s figures were about twice this value.
- Iron: 0.038 mg/l (total, unfiltered). Manganese could not be detected.
- Total hardness, calcium, and magnesium were titrated (EDTA). Calcium ion was calculated by multiplying calcium hardness by 0.4.

**Comparison with Soda Springs and Beaver Creek**
- Beaver Creek (4 Nov 1960): residue 325 mg/l, total hardness 222 mg/l, alkalinity 300 mg/l, pH 7.9. The Well outlet had residue 638 mg/l, hardness 424 mg/l, and alkalinity 582 mg/l. There is little similarity between the two waters.
- Soda Springs (6 Jul 1962): inflow temperature 24.3 °C, conductance about 4,900 µS/cm (possibly a meter artifact), pH 6.4, alkalinity 48 mg/l (as CaCO₃), and an estimated free CO₂ of about 337 mg/l. Ca/Mg ratio was 2.77 at Soda Springs and about 2.9 in the Well.

**Irrigation ditch changes**
- Stations I–V were sampled along the outflow ditch. Station I was the swallet, Station II the outer outlet, and Station V the second weir (Parshall Flume No. 6 area). The total distance was 1.632 km, with a flow time of about 1 hour 20 minutes. Free CO₂ fell about ten-fold downstream (from 262 mg/l at Station I to 29 mg/l at Station V), and pH rose (6.6 to 9.35 in Table 4, Station IV).
- Dissolved oxygen increased from the outlet to Station V, rising by about 1.5 mg/l. Calcium, magnesium, and total alkalinity fell downstream as CaCO₃ precipitated.

**Primary productivity and chlorophyll**
- Annual gross photosynthesis was estimated at 2.55 g O₂/m²/year from diel oxygen data (1960–62), with a daily mean net photosynthesis of 0.431 mg O₂/m²/day and night respiration of 0.419 mg O₂/m²/day. The authors describe the Well as a near steady-state system.
- Mean chlorophyll: lake 17.6 mg/m³, outer outlet 16.8 mg/m³, swallet 12.2 mg/m³. Chlorophyll determinations were abandoned after November 1962 because the method extracted incompletely.
- Macrophyte standing crop in August 1962 exceeded 1,620 g dry weight/m² at 7 m depth. The authors consider this comparable to the highest aquatic macrophyte production known.

**Radiocarbon anomaly**
- CO₂ from the inflow contains about 5% modern carbon and an apparent age of about 25,000 years.
- Submerged *Potamogeton* was dated at approximately the same age. Partially emergent *Chara* gave an apparent age of about 17,000 years.
- Pollen from the core indicated post-Pleistocene (pine-dominated) sediments, but radiocarbon age was much greater, which the authors attribute to ancient CO₂ entering with the inflow.

**Biota**
- **Fish:** No fish are present. The authors link this to high free CO₂ and absence of fish-related factors.
- **Plants:** *Potamogeton illinoensis* (possibly a hybrid with *P. gramineus*, per E. C. Ogden), *P. pectinatus*, *P. natans* (ditch only), *Zannichellia palustris*, *Scirpus validus*, *Typha angustifolia*, and two *Chara* species (*C. vulgaris* and *C. contraria*, per A. T. Hotchkiss's identifications). The peat bed on the north shore is composed primarily of *Scirpus* remains.
- **Algae:** Kidd and Wade (1963) reported 19 species from the Well and 19 from Beaver Creek, with none in common. Red alga *Batrachospermum* occurs near the outer outlet, where free CO₂ is high.
- **Zooplankton:** Calanoid copepods and planktonic cladocerans are absent. Cyclopoid copepods, nauplii, and *Hyalella* (amphipod) are present. Estimated numbers in May 1961 included 796 *Hyalella* per m² (as given). Plankton is concentrated in the upper layers on dark days and sinks below the surface during bright sun.
- **Invertebrates:** Two damselflies (*Telebasis* and *Enallagma*), the belostomatid *Abedus brevicollis*, the water scorpion *Ranatra quadridentata*, dytiscid beetles, the veliid *Microvelia*, the beetle *Hydroscapha natans*, and chironomid-like and ceratopogonid larvae are recorded. Chironomid larvae are absent from soft sediments, which the authors call a faunistic puzzle.
- **Amphibians and reptiles:** The leopard frog (*Rana pipiens*) is present year-round. The canyon treefrog (*Hyla arenicolor*) occurs at the southwest margin. The Sonoran mud turtle (*Kinosternon sonoriense*) is common; the largest individual measured 24.1 cm, and one weighed 430 g. Females climb the basin slope to nest.
- **Birds and mammals:** Black phoebe, pied-billed grebe, and coots/ducks (baldpates especially) are recorded in fall and winter. A Canada goose and two canvasback ducks were noted. Muskrats are present; raccoons and skunks likely prey on turtle nests.

## Key Findings

- **Hydrology:** Inflow is 4,159 to 7,293 acre-feet/day, and about one-tenth of the lake volume is exchanged daily. The swallet-to-outer-outlet transit takes seven minutes.
- **Thermal regime:** The lake warms and cools with the seasons (summer above 25 °C, winter above 19 °C), but inflowing deep water stays near 24–25 °C. Heat budget per unit area ranges from 12,804 to 16,407 g cal/cm².
- **Circulation:** Counter-clockwise rotation with sinking currents at certain points, driven by entering water.
- **Acidity and CO₂:** Water is acidic (pH 6.15–6.9) due to carbonic acid. Free CO₂ can reach about 687 mg/l. The authors propose this as the reason no fish are present, despite adequate oxygen.
- **Outflow changes:** Free CO₂ falls about ten-fold down the irrigation ditch, pH rises, and calcium, magnesium, and alkalinity precipitate.
- **Productivity:** Annual gross photosynthesis of 2.55 g O₂/m²/year; net and respiration are nearly balanced, indicating a steady-state system.
- **Radiocarbon:** Inflowing CO₂ dates to about 25,000 years, and *Chara* about 17,000 years. This anomaly undermined the radiocarbon dating of the sediment core.
- **Sediments:** Deep sediments are fine-grained organic ooze; coarse sand fills the fissures. Pollen profiles suggest a Pleistocene pine-oak-juniper forest; the peat bed at the north shore is primarily *Scirpus*.
- **Biota:** The biota is dominated by aquatic plants, amphipods, damselflies, waterbugs, and the Sonoran mud turtle. Fish are absent.
- **Authors' conclusions:** The Well is a closed-basin, steady-state system that is described as a theoretical climax ecosystem; the peat deposit may represent an earlier successional stage.
