# Sonoran Topminnow and Desert Pupfish Status in Arizona, 1990–1991

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department special report, prepared by [[Matt Brown]] and [[Francisco J. Abarca]] under Project E5-2, Job 9 (Title VI of the Endangered Species Act), updates the status of [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in Arizona. It draws on field surveys from May 1990 to August 1991. Both species were federally listed as endangered (topminnow 1967; pupfish 1986) after habitat loss and predation by non-native fishes, especially [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), caused severe declines. The report concludes that natural and introduced Gila topminnow populations continue to decline, that introduced populations lack genetic diversity, and that delisting criteria for the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] have not been met.

## Background & Description

- **Taxa covered:** Sonoran topminnow (two subspecies: [[Gila Topminnow]], *P. o. occidentalis*, and [[Yaqui Topminnow]], *P. o. sonoriensis*) and desert pupfish (two forms: Lower Colorado River pupfish, *C. m. macularius*, and [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]], *C. m. eremus*).
- **Scope:** Only Arizona populations are included, although both species also occur in Mexico, New Mexico and California.
- **Key definitions:**
  - *Introduced* populations occur at sites within historic range that had no record of natural populations; *wild* populations are any not held at a site with a mailing address (captive populations at universities, zoos, hatcheries or backyards were not surveyed).
  - *Natural* populations are those never artificially mixed with other stocks. A site renovated (poisoned to eradicate fish) and restocked only with its own fish remains natural; restocking with another population makes it non-natural.
  - A site is *successful* if it contains fish of any population size. A population is *extirpated* only after absence on at least three survey occasions.
- **Site quality categories** (after [[Simons]] 1987): Category 1 (moderate–high numbers, persists without human support); Category 2 (persists only with human support); Category 3 (too few individuals or too little habitat to persist long); Category 4 (threatened by mosquitofish or other exotics).
- **Site numbering:** Sites were numbered by Simons (1987) and expanded by [[Bagley et al. 1991]]; Appendix A lists all surveyed populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats:** Habitat modification and destruction, and predation by mosquitofish, drove the declines. Other threats noted include flooding, drying, cattle impacts, cattail encroachment and siltation, pesticide contamination and fish kills (Quitobaquito), dredging, groundwater pumping in Mexico lowering spring flows, and deliberate or accidental introductions of exotic fish and bullfrogs.
- **Metapopulation dynamics:** Topminnows naturally undergo periodic extirpation followed by recolonization and explosive breeding. Stable drainage-level populations depend on many connected sites, and [[Habitat Fragmentation]] and isolation prevent recolonization.
- **Sampling methods:** Introduced populations were monitored January–May 1991. Natural populations were sampled in August 1990 and January 1991, with dipnets (1/8 inch mesh) and seines. Winter and spring sampling may detect fewer fish than summer sampling, so failed sites were resampled in July–August 1991.
- **Voucher specimens** were deposited in the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes.

## Key Findings

**Gila Topminnow: natural populations**
- All eight natural populations that were successful in 1989 persisted in 1991. Three (sites 1, 2 and 8) remained secure, while five (sites 4, 7, 9, 10 and 11) continued to be threatened by mosquitofish.
- Sharp Spring (site 4) was the most genetically heterogeneous population according to mtDNA analysis. The report recommends special management attention for it.

**Gila Topminnow: introduced populations**
- Gila topminnow have been introduced into 192 wild habitats and more than 105 captive sites, with over 343 introductions in total. 132 sites were sampled in 1990–1991.
- Only 19 wild introduced populations were successful in 1991, about 10% of the 192 introductions. Of these, four were Category 1, nine Category 2, four Category 3 and two Category 4.
- Thirteen wild introduced populations failed between 1989 and 1991, mostly from flooding. Thirteen more failed between 1985 and 1989, mostly from drying. Three failed Category 1 sites persisted for no more than ten years.
- No new Gila topminnow populations were established in 1990–1991.

**Gila Topminnow: genetics and management**
- Most introduction stock came from Monkey Springs, which lacks the genetic variation of other sites. Only one successful wild population derived from Sharp Spring stock, and Salt Creek is the only other wild population from non-Monkey Springs stock; it faces imminent extirpation from mosquitofish.
- Recommended site selection has three steps: identify candidate sites, evaluate them on site, and stock the highest-ranked ones.
- Recommended priorities: large complex sites; clusters of small nearby sites; isolated custodial refugia; and isolated sites whose earlier failure was caused by preventable disturbance.
- Upper reaches of the Babocomari, Arivaca and Sonoita creeks and the San Pedro River were identified for acquisition, renovation and stocking.

**Yaqui Topminnow**
- Fourteen wild populations were successful in 1991: twelve Category 2, one Category 3 and one Category 4.
- Only Tule Pond (site 142) holds a natural population, and it remains stable.
- The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] 1984 recovery plan's delisting criteria are not met. Federal ownership of the San Bernardino Ranch does not give complete control of aquatic habitat (e.g., vegetation burned at House Pond, January 1991). Mosquitofish persist in Black Draw, and groundwater pumping in Mexico undermines all sites. Most sites held topminnows for only 1–3 years; only Leslie Creek held them for more than ten.

**Desert Pupfish**
- The only natural Quitobaquito pupfish population is at Quitobaquito Springs (site 128) in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument. It appeared secure in 1991, with fish abundant in a new concrete canal.
- Lower Colorado River pupfish were eliminated from Arizona before the 1950s.
- Of five successful introduced desert pupfish populations in 1991, three contained Lower Colorado River pupfish. Two (Bog Hole and Finley Tank) contain fish of questionable genetic background, which prevents further introductions. Both are recommended for renovation.
- The desert pupfish recovery plan had not been finalized by the USFWS at the time of the report.

**Failure analysis (Table 5, 1985–1991)**
- Of 21 recorded failures, drying and flooding were the most frequent causes (the totals in the source table are 21 drying and 8 flooding, with 8 unknown and smaller counts for low oxygen, cattle, mosquitofish and dredging).

**Recommendations**
- Stock numerous sites with custodial populations as short-term refugia.
- Acquire and renovate large, complex habitats for long-term survival.
- Continuously restock individual sites until self-sustaining habitats are secured, and abandon the search for "perfect" sites.
