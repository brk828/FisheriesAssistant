# Possible Arizona Faulting from Mississippian Facies Analysis and Plate Tectonics: A Stratotectonic Approach

#geology #tectonics #mississippian-stratigraphy #conodonts #foraminifera

*Note: The source is a geological paper, not an ichthyological or aquatic-ecology study. It contains no fish or species data. The "Ecological & Environmental Context" section therefore covers the ancient marine depositional setting and the tectonic setting only.*

## Executive Summary

The document is the Arizona Geological Society Digest (Volume X, March 1976) and contains two papers. The main paper, by William J. Purves (Phillips Petroleum Company), proposes a "stratotectonic" method. It combines detailed, time-bound Mississippian paleogeography, petrographic microfacies, and conodont/foraminiferal age control with regional geophysical and structural data. The aim is to detect intraplate faulting in Arizona. The author concludes that the state is divided into northwest- and northeast-trending, laterally offset, fault-bound blocks, and that intraplate tectonic activity is present. A second paper, by George H. Davis and Eric G. Frost, examines the internal structure and emplacement mechanism of a small gravity-glide sheet in Saguaro National Monument (East), Tucson.

## Background & Description

**Stratotectonic approach (Purves).** The method treats the Mississippian System as the time-bound unit for regional examination. It relies on the premise that inductive reasoning from stratigraphic data can be productive in regional paleotectonic evaluation. The data set integrates:
- LANDSAT and ERTS photomosaic imagery (U.S. Geological Survey, 1972–1973)
- [[Geologic Map Of Arizona]] (Wilson, Moore, and Cooper, 1969)
- [[Aeromagnetic Map Of Arizona]] (Sauck and Sumner, 1970)
- Regional Bouguer gravity anomaly map (West, 1972), applied mainly in the Colorado Plateau province
- Seismic refraction study in central Arizona (Warren, 1969)
- Structural syntheses by Kelley (1955) and Mayo (1958)
- Radiometric ages of igneous activity (e.g., Damon and Mauger, 1966)

**Sampling and methods.** Twenty-four Mississippian reference sections were measured across Arizona. Samples of 2–3 kg were analyzed petrographically using [[Folk Classification]] (Folk, 1959, 1974). Thin sections were evaluated for conodont and foraminiferal content. Conodonts were extracted using the Collinson (1963) method. Published literature and AmStrat well logs were tied to these sections.

**Plate-tectonic framework.** Arizona lies in the southwestern part of the [[North American Plate]] (Dietz and Holden, 1970). The conventional assumption is that plates behave as rigid slabs floating on the [[Asthenosphere]] (Isacks, Oliver, and Sykes, 1968), with deformation confined to plate margins. Purves argues that the many documented thrusts, tear faults, gravity gliding, strike-slip faults, and folds in Arizona show the interior of the plate was not rigid. He calls for more attention to intraplate activity.

**Davis and Frost paper.** Deformational features in Paleozoic limestone and shale within Saguaro National Monument (East) were interpreted by McColly (1961) and Davis (1973, 1975) as products of gravity gliding. Large-scale (1:120) mapping revealed five folded imbricate plates. The authors attribute the deformation to [[Gravity Gliding]], using the multiple-décollement rheological model of Kehle (1970). Shale-rich layers with bedding-plane cleavage, overturned folds, recumbent rootless folds, boudinage, and slickensides mark the fault boundaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's paleoenvironmental reasoning concerns the Mississippian marine setting, not living ecosystems.

- **Clear-water epeiric sea.** The authors apply the [[Shaw-Irwin Clear-Water Sedimentation Model]] (Shaw, 1964; Irwin, 1965). This describes an extremely low-gradient sea with little or no clastic input. Similar microfacies would form over wide areas. Facies correlations could be predicted over hundreds of miles in low-energy nearshore and below-wave-base settings, but only over tens of miles in high-energy zones.
- **Clastic-free succession.** The Mississippian is largely free of clastics. The authors infer that no vertical tectonism or basement control was active during deposition, so isopach anomalies are attributed to post-depositional tectonism.
- **Biostratigraphy.** Megafossils are scarce, but conodont- and foraminifera-bearing units give stage-level resolution. This allows depositional thinning to be separated from post-Mississippian erosional thinning.
- **Regional tectonic setting.** The paper links plate motion to the opening of the Gulf of Mexico. It notes salt deposits in the restricted early Gulf, and Early Cretaceous reefs after shoreline stabilization (Bryant and others, 1969).

## Key Findings

**Purves (1976), Arizona stratotectonics**

1. **Block segmentation.** The stratotectonic map of the Mississippian System shows Arizona divided into laterally offset, fault-bound blocks bounded by northeast- and northwest-trending faults. The stratigraphic anomaly lines are tabulated as numbers 24 to 101, and each line is checked against aeromagnetic, ERTS, gravity, Colorado Plateau structure, and seismic refraction data (Table 1).

2. **Isopach variation.** Within blocks, Mississippian isopach variation generally does not exceed 50–100 feet. Across some anomaly lines the variation is 100 feet within a few miles (line 19), or 100–400 feet (lines 80–82).

3. **Estimated lateral offsets.**
   - Left-lateral strike-slip of about 25–40 miles, by matching isopach gradients (lines 8 and 20, and Laramide fault zone GC).
   - Right-lateral movement of about 80 miles postulated for the Laramide fault zone L area, based on microfacies matching.
   - Tertiary right-lateral strike-slip of about 15 miles along lines 41 and 54. The author relates this zone to the Las Vegas shear system in Nevada.

4. **Relative chronology of fractures.** Stratigraphic anomaly lines crosscut in the following order (oldest first):
   - West and northwest fractures: post-Mississippian to pre-Laramide, interpreted as Late Triassic to Early Cretaceous
   - Northeast fractures: Laramide
   - Northwest fractures: Tertiary

   The author states that published radiometric dates are consistent with this sequence.

5. **Inductive plate model.** The model proposes that North American plate separation from the Eurasian–African plates produced Late Triassic to Early Cretaceous strike-slip faulting, segmenting the plate into west-northwest belts. Later interference from the Cocos-Nasca spreading zone and the overriding East Pacific Rise is linked to the Laramide and Tertiary events. A plate motion chronology from [[Coney]] (1973) is cited: northwest 180–80 Ma, southwest 80–40 Ma, and northwest 40 Ma to present.

6. **Pre-Laramide event.** Anomaly line 82 abuts line 81, which indicates a separated and overprinted pre-Laramide event, interpreted as Late Triassic to middle Cretaceous. It lies near a Jurassic pluton. Similar pre-Laramide segments may exist near lines 83–93 in southeastern Arizona.

7. **Basin-and-range link.** The formation of basin-and-range physiography may be related to these pre-existing fracture patterns. Vertical tectonics may occur when lithospheric slabs jam.

8. **Caveats.** The author states that not all anomaly lines are assumed to be correct. The method is an attempt to simplify a complex structural history, and it is hoped the study generates new ideas rather than settles the question. The author also notes that the Colorado Plateau, long considered simple layer-cake stratigraphy, may have had more disruptive influences than assumed.

**Davis and Frost, Saguaro gravity-glide sheet**

- The rocks consist of five folded imbricate plates. Fold frequency and tightness increase downward within each plate.
- Folded fault boundaries coincide with shale-rich layers.
- Gravity gliding occurred by incremental movements, with folding and faulting occurring at the same time.
- Low-angle transport of brittle limestone layers was accomplished by shear flow in an underlying low-viscosity, shale-rich décollement zone.
