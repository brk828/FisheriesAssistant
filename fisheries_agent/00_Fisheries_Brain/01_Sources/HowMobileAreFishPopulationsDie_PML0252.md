# Diel Movement, Population Turnover, and Site Fidelity in Sonora and Desert Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study used [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags) and [[Stationary Antennas]] to track individual habitat use, [[Turnover Rate|population turnover]], and [[Displacement]] in two common southwestern North American suckers, the [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) and the [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarkii*), in the [[West Fork Gila River]] of [[New Mexico]]. Tagged fish frequently used the focal antenna reach, but only a subset was consistently present, and population size and turnover varied from day to day. Most individuals made extended departures (one or more days) from their home pools, yet many showed [[Site Fidelity]] to a particular habitat and returned throughout the study. The authors conclude that [[Diel Movement]] and short-term forays can produce high turnover and may cause individuals to be misclassified as sedentary.

## Background & Description

Movement regulates population dynamics, predator-prey interactions, community structure, and habitat use in stream ecosystems. The authors frame population-level movement as having two components: displacement (distance moved by individuals) and turnover (the proportion of individuals moving). Turnover matters because it shapes population size and structure within a habitat. Historically, stream fish were thought to be largely sedentary (see [[Restricted Movement Paradigm]]), but later work suggested that populations often mix sedentary and mobile individuals.

Both study species belong to [[Catostomidae]], the suckers, which are often the most abundant large-bodied native fishes in southwestern streams.

- **Sonora Sucker:** Feeds on invertebrates and detritus. It is abundant in deep, low-flow pools with fine substrates, often in aggregations of more than 30 individuals.
- **Desert Sucker:** An algivore that scrapes algae from hard substrates. Small individuals (about 50 mm standard length) feed continuously with crepuscular peaks.

Adults of both species are often found in the same reach. Juveniles are abundant in the West Fork Gila River for both species, but adult Sonora Suckers dominate the community (ratio of C. insignis to C. clarkii: 0.9:1 for juveniles, 5.8:1 for adults; unpublished data cited by the authors).

Tracking individual movement at population scale had been difficult. [[Radio Telemetry]] is limited in the number of tagged fish, and [[Electroshocking]] recapture is limited in sampling frequency. [[Acoustic Tags]] require water depths greater than about 3 m. Remote PIT detection allows low-cost, long-term monitoring of many individuals in small streams.

The study addressed three questions: (1) how individuals use habitat and move diurnally, (2) how turnover varies within a habitat over time, and (3) whether individuals show day-to-day fidelity to a habitat during summer. The authors also examined changes in behavior associated with hydrologic events ([[Spates]] and low flow) and between years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study location.** The upper [[Gila River]] is a tributary of the [[Colorado River]] originating in the Mogollon Mountains of western New Mexico. The study site at the [[Heartbar Wildlife Area]] lies near the confluence of the West and Middle forks, about 3 km downstream of the [[Gila Cliff Dwellings National Monument]]. Flow is dominated by snowmelt, with a summer [[Monsoon Season]] (July–September) contributing a substantial share of rainfall. Discharge was monitored with a gauge rod in 2009 and with Hobo water level loggers (recorded every 5 minutes) in 2010, with a rating curve used to estimate discharge.

**Habitat structure.** Large fish were captured only in discrete [[Refuge Habitat]]s: pools, root wads, and undercuts. Refuge sites were characterized by depths typically greater than 1 m, overhead cover (woody debris or undercut banks), and depositional sediment. The tagging reach was about 1.8 km long, and the stationary antenna reach was roughly 200–300 m. The 2009 high flows substantially changed stream morphology, so the stationary antenna site was relocated in 2010.

**Refuge–foraging trade-off.** Refuge habitats reduce predation risk but can limit feeding. The timing, frequency, and distance of moves between refuge and foraging habitats depend on [[Predation Risk]], which is often size-dependent. Shallow water appears to be avoided during daylight, possibly because of [[Predation Risk]] from piscivorous birds (e.g., herons, osprey).

**Hydrology.** Low flows limited movement into shallow habitats, and in dry years could prevent movement between refuges altogether. Spates produced downstream, lateral, and temporary displacements, and reduced water clarity extended fish activity into daylight hours.

## Key Findings

**Study design and sampling**

- 431 C. insignis and 119 C. clarkii (550 total) were PIT-tagged in the 1.8 km reach between 2008 and 2010 (May–July). Tagged fish were weighed, measured, and released at their capture location.
- Detection efficiency was above 80% for central antennas in 2009 and for most antennas in 2010, but was below 30% for antennas 4 and 5 in 2009 because of lower amperage on longer cable runs. Antenna 3 in 2010 reached 50%.
- Detections were recorded for 249 fish in 2009 and 102 fish in 2010 within the stationary antenna reaches.
- Based on hoop net recaptures in 2009, the authors estimate that 45–80% of fish present in the focal reach carried PIT tags.
- Stationary antennas collected 65 days of data in 2009 and 28 days in 2010.

**Diel movement and habitat use**

- Fish moved through antennas and into shallow water (<50 cm) mainly at night, with crepuscular peaks in activity. Only occasional daytime forays occurred, and visual surveys confirmed that most fish remained in pools under overhead cover.
- Daytime movement coincided with incident light. After the June 2009 spate, activity extended into daylight while water was turbid (zero visibility, rising to 0.5 m visibility within about two days).
- In 2010, use of the shallow side channel was related to discharge (F = 31.891, p < 0.0001). Fewer individuals used the side channel when average depth fell below 10 cm.

**Behavioral categories**

- In 2009, individuals fell into three main categories: occasional forays (typically one antenna), regular forays (multiple antennas), and transient-directed movement (through the whole reach with brief visits to the focal reach).
- In 2010, the same patterns appeared, plus individuals that used only the side channel.

**Site fidelity and residency**

- Individuals were classified as transient (<10 days) or frequent (>10 days) visitors to pool habitats.
- Approximately half of C. insignis and C. clarkii were transient in 2009, while most C. insignis were transient in 2010.
- Transient and frequent fish did not differ significantly in standard length for C. insignis (transient 271 ± 11 mm; frequent 257 ± 7 mm; p = 0.11).
- Frequent visitors in 2009 showed strong fidelity to one pool (Wilcoxon signed-rank test against a mean of 0.5, p < 0.0001). Of 52 frequent visitors in pool 1, 39 were originally tagged there. Of 54 frequent visitors in pool 2, 24 were originally tagged there.
- Nearly half of fish detected in pool 1 in both years were tagged there, and most fish observed in the upstream and downstream reaches were tagged in pools 1 and 2.
- Nine individuals were frequent visitors in both years, and several more were observed in both years.

**Home range**

- Home range was calculated as the maximum linear displacement between the two most distant detections for each individual and year.
- For C. insignis in 2009, reported means were 374 ± 56 m for frequent visitors and 195 ± 82 m for transient visitors (F = 17.92, p < 0.0001). For 2010, frequent visitors averaged 93 ± 166 m and transient visitors 411 ± 197 m (p = 0.05). C. clarkii in 2009 averaged 168 ± 134 m (frequent) and 819 ± 156 m (transient) (p = 0.005). Note that the Results text describes frequent visitors as having smaller home ranges, which conflicts with these reported means and with the Discussion; the values are reported here as printed.
- About half of transient C. insignis and frequent C. insignis had home ranges under 250 m. Some frequent visitors made forays to distant habitats, producing home ranges of 500–1500 m.

**Population size and turnover**

- Daily population size in 2009 was more variable in pool 1 (91 ± 10 individuals) than in pool 2 (33 ± 6 individuals; p = 0.002).
- Residents (fish present more than 25 days) were fewer in pool 1 than pool 2 and made up a larger share of the daily population in pool 2 (22% vs. 45%; p < 0.0001).
- Daily turnover ranged from 0% to more than 60% in both years and was higher in 2010 than in 2009.
- In 2010, turnover was positively correlated with change in population size (p = 0.004). In 2009 no such relationship was found.
- High turnover in pool 2 in 2009 was partly driven by fish moving from pool 1 into pool 2 (F = 16.23, p = 0.0004), but turnover in pool 1 was not related to movement from pool 2.
- Population composition was more similar over 2-day intervals than over weeks (p < 0.0001).

**Response to spates (2009)**

- Of 97 individuals with sufficient data, 29 made brief (<3 day) displacements out of the reach, 32 made sustained upstream displacements, 12 made sustained downstream displacements, and 24 showed no change in activity.
- Responses varied within species and among individuals. Some individuals made sustained moves to a new habitat, others returned to their home pool, and some did not respond at all.

**Interpretation and implications**

- High turnover may explain why many fish marked in mark–recapture studies are never recaptured. In this study, high turnover produced a wide range of displacement distances.
- Diel and short-term movements, combined with high site fidelity, may cause individuals to be misclassified as sedentary despite frequent movements into other habitats. The authors suggest that Sonora and desert suckers are more mobile than earlier studies (e.g., [[Bestgen Et Al 1987]]) indicated.
- The authors argue that population size fluctuations complicate using density to assess habitat quality, and that movement parameters such as immigration and loss may provide more stable metrics.
- [[Foraging Theory]] suggests that individuals should move to new habitat when the expected fitness gain outweighs the gain from staying. Foraging forays may temporarily strand fish away from preferred refuges, causing them to use the nearest available refuge and contributing to daily turnover.
- Management implications: fish movement across regulation boundaries may weaken effectiveness of restricted fishing areas, and flood responses vary within species, which complicates the use of controlled floods to suppress non-native fish.
