# Starvation and Food Availability in Larval Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report describes three laboratory experiments on the effects of food availability on mortality and growth in larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The work was motivated by the near absence of [[Recruitment]] to adult populations in [[Lake Mohave]], where wild-caught larvae were found with mostly empty stomachs at the time of hatching. The experiments show that larvae cannot survive long without food. Starvation became irreversible roughly between days 7 and 17 after hatching, and the critical period for starvation fell approximately between days 17 and 21. Survival and growth depended strongly on the density of food (*Artemia salina* nauplii) offered.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a [[Catostomidae|Catostomid]] fish of the [[Colorado River]] system. Early life stages are considered especially vulnerable during the switch from endogenous yolk reserves to exogenous feeding, and early mortality is thought to be a major limit on recruitment. In Lake Mohave, captured larval razorback sucker averaged 10.6 mm total length (TL). Their stomachs were mostly empty, and zooplankton density was low at the time of hatching ([[Langhorst and Marsh]], 1986). No recruitment to adult populations had been detected despite extensive sampling ([[Minckley]], 1989; [[Minckley and Marsh]], unpublished data).

Three experiments were conducted indoors at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] to test how food availability affects larval mortality and growth.

**Methods (summary):**
- Larvae were held in one-gallon glass jars with continuous flowing water, at a constant temperature (controlled by a water bath) and under uniform lighting. Jars were arranged in series rather than randomly.
- Wild-caught larvae from Lake Mohave were stocked at 3.0 individuals per jar.
- Food consisted of newly hatched [[Artemia Salina]] nauplii, hatched from commercial eggs and counted with a Sedgewick-Rafter cell.
- Fish were fed three times a day at set intervals.
- Mortality was analyzed with an equation adapted from [[O'Connell and Raymond]] (1970), and treatment differences were tested with the [[Mann-Whitney U Test]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Larvae depend first on yolk reserves, which were absorbed at about day 7 post-hatch in these experiments. After that point, larvae must find adequate prey. The [[Irreversible Starvation]] window in these experiments ran from about day 7 to day 17 post-hatch. Larvae that received food only after this window could not recover. The [[Critical Period]] for survival was reported as approximately 17 to 21 days after hatching.

Temperature is an important modifier. Earlier studies showed that temperature affects incubation length, hatching size, yolk sac absorption rate, and metabolic efficiency. As a result, the timing of the critical period may shift under field conditions. Lake Mohave is colder (about 11 to 15°C) during the larval period than the temperatures typically used in laboratory work, so field conditions may differ from the experiments.

Food availability in Lake Mohave was low. Zooplankton prey densities averaged about 1.5 items per liter ([[Langhorst and Marsh]], 1986). Field-caught larvae were not generally older than 19 days, and most had little or no food in their guts.

## Key Findings

**Experiment 1 (starvation, no food):**
- Mortality of unfed larvae increased to more than 50% during the experiment.
- Mortalities were concentrated between days 20 and 30 post-hatch. By day 30 all larvae had died.
- Larvae that starved died at a mean size of 9.6 ± 0.8 mm TL (Fig. 5; Appendix A).
- Survivors were not significantly different across treatments in mortality timing (p > 0.05).

**Experiment 2 (food delayed by 4-day intervals, starting day 5 to day 29):**
- Larvae given food starting on day 5 experienced no mortality.
- Mortality rose sharply with longer delays. The highest mortalities occurred when food was first offered at day 25 and day 29 (93.3% at day 29).
- Survivors that began feeding on day 5 were the largest (about 23 mm TL). The smallest survivors (about 15.8 mm TL) began feeding at day 29.
- Mortality did not differ significantly among starts at days 9, 13, and 17 (p > 0.05).

**Experiment 3 (food density, 5 to 1000 nauplii per liter):**
- Mortality at 5 and 10 nauplii per liter was high, about 90% at 5 per liter, but differences among these treatments were not significant.
- Larvae fed 50, 100, 500, or 1000 nauplii per liter had less than 20% mortality, with no significant differences among these treatments.
- The threshold separating high from low mortality lay between 10 and 50 nauplii per liter. At 50 per liter, fish received about 20 nauplii each at the beginning and end of the trial.
- Growth followed food density. Fish at 5 nauplii per liter reached about 14.9 ± 0.9 mm TL, whereas those at 500 and 1000 per liter reached about 25 mm TL.

**Conclusions:**
- Razorback sucker larvae are highly vulnerable to food shortage during the period between yolk absorption (about day 7) and about day 17 to 21 post-hatch.
- Food density in the range of roughly 10 to 50 nauplii per liter (about 1 to 20 nauplii per fish) appears to separate high from low survival. The author reports that 5 nauplii per fish was a satisfactory amount for survival during the critical period.
- Lake Mohave zooplankton densities (about 1.5 items per liter) are far below the levels associated with good survival, which may help explain the lack of recruitment.
- Because temperature changes development timing, the critical period is likely to differ in other thermal environments, and field predictions should account for this.
