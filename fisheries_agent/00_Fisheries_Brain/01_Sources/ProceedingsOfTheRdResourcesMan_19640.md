# Proceedings of the 3rd Resources Management Conference (Southwest Region, 1976)

#aquatic-ecology #riparian-ecology #resources-management #carrying-capacity

## Executive Summary

These proceedings from the National Park Service Southwest Region Superintendent's Conference (Fort Worth, April 29–30, 1976; coordinator Roland H. Wauer) bring together case studies on how recreational use affects river corridors, beaches and backcountry in NPS units. The focus is [[Carrying Capacity]] and the monitoring, data systems and management planning needed to manage visitor use. Most papers concern riparian plants, rodents, beach vegetation and water quality rather than fish. Fish appear mainly in the [[Colorado River Research Project]] inventory of contracts and in discussion of Ozark floater impacts. The main conclusions are that permit data can be mined for management insight, that impact is concentrated at a few easily accessed sites, that use has not yet reached damaging ecological thresholds in the measured parameters, and that management objectives must be defined before carrying capacity can be set.

## Background & Description

The conference covered several NPS river and coastal units:

- **[[Big Bend National Park]] (Rio Grande corridor):** Ditton, Schmidly, Boeer and Graefe (Texas A&M) assessed human impact on floodplain riparian communities as baseline data for carrying capacity.
- **[[Grand Canyon National Park]] (Colorado River):** Johnson and Martin described a multidisciplinary research program on the 279-mile river corridor, from Echo Cliffs to Grand Wash Cliffs.
- **[[Ozark National Scenic Riverways]]:** Marnell reviewed a river-use investigation on the Current River and Jacks Fork, covering about 140 river miles.
- **[[Buffalo National River]]:** Babcock (University of Arkansas) reported a multi-year ecosystem study focused on water quality and hydrology.
- **[[Padre Island National Seashore]]:** Behrens, Carangelo and Finkelstein (University of Texas) examined how vehicle and foot traffic affects backshore vegetation and beach volume.
- **[[Rocky Mountain National Park]]:** Butts traced the evolution of a backcountry permit and management program from 1965 to 1975.

Wauer's closing summary and the introductory systems-approach paper set out the planning framework used across the sessions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rio Grande riparian zone.** Floodplain vegetation occurs wherever periodic flooding allows, except where cliffs meet the channel. The study identified 64 major riparian sites. Of these, 26 are road-accessible, 18 via the River Road (designated backcountry campsites), and 8 via paved roads. Twelve rodent species occur in the riparian habitat; nine belong to Heteromyidae and Cricetidae. Livestock trespass from Mexico was reported as seasonal and tied to river water level.

**Colorado River.** Glen Canyon Dam changed the river from a silt-laden, variable flow (1,300 to 200,000 cfs historically) to a silt-free, more predictable flow (5,000 to 35,000 cfs). Feral burros were identified as a serious threat to beaches and vegetation, with the Museum of Northern Arizona documenting beach degradation and declines in plant and animal diversity. Aquatic studies, including fish surveys by Tulane University and Dr. Royal Suttkus, began later, in FY1975.

**Ozark rivers.** Intensive canoe floating raised concerns about crowding, gravel-bar damage and root exposure. Photographic surveillance and floater card surveys were used to monitor traffic.

**Buffalo River.** The river has low productivity but a diverse biological community. Fecal coliform levels fall within Arkansas standards for primary contact recreation. Carbonate bedrock (fractured limestone and dolomite) makes both groundwater and surface water highly vulnerable to pollution.

**Padre Island beaches.** Heavily trafficked beaches showed fewer plant species and lower plant density. Sediment dynamics also differed among sites.

**Rocky Mountain backcountry.** Physical impacts included trampled vegetation, trash, trail erosion and human waste in the first three to five miles of trail.

## Key Findings

### Rio Grande Corridor (Big Bend NP)

- **Use distribution (1975 backcountry permits):** 3,894 parties in total. Non-river road camping accounted for 2,318 parties (59.5%), River Road camping 849 (21.8%), and float trips 727 (18.7%). The Rio Grande attracted about 40% of total backcountry use.
- **Float trips:** 841 party-sections were recorded, with 7,405.5 man-days. The three canyon sections (Santa Elena, Mariscal and Boquillas) accounted for 69% of parties and 81% of man-days.
- **Site concentration:** Two River Road sites (Gravel Pit and Solis) received 40% of River Road man-days. Six sites together received 72%.
- **Impact clustering (64 sites, 15 variables):** Cluster analysis produced three groups: heavy impact 15 sites (23%), moderate 10 (16%), light 39 (61%). Of the heavily impacted sites, 11 (70%) are road-accessible.
- **Principal components:** Components I and II explained 56% of variance (37.44% and 17.92%). Human impact variables dominated component I. Livestock impact loaded weakly, indicating a uniform, low-level constant along the river.
- **Use-impact correlation:** Subjective impact and annual River Road use were positively correlated (r = 0.459, significant at p = 0.02–0.05).
- **Rodent response:** Rodents were trapped at 18 sites for 6 nights each (720 trap nights per site, using 120 Sherman traps). Correlations between rodent density and human or livestock impact were not significant, so impact has not reached a level that threatens rodent populations.
- **Conclusion:** Impacts represent change rather than damage. Management must define an acceptable level of change. Access is the main driver of impact distribution, so it can also be used to manage it.

### Colorado River (Grand Canyon NP)

- **Growth:** Visitors rose from fewer than 100 per year (1955) to 16,432 in 1972. The average annual increase from 1960 to 1972 was 59%.
- **Allotments:** Commercial passenger-day allotment was 105,000 in 1972, then reduced to 89,000 from 1973 (commercial use in 1972 was 88,135). Private use was frozen at 7,600 user-days after 7,611 were used in 1972.
- **Policy timeline:** Research was approved in 1970. A monitoring program was approved through FY1981. A River Management Plan was targeted for 2977 (draft and final EIS stages in 1976–77).
- **Budget:** More than $250,000 per year was allocated by FY1975. About $20,000 per year was spent on monitoring. A total of about $750,000 was planned for monitoring, with half for baseline data.
- **Research portfolio:** Appendix C lists 29 projects, covering periphyton, limnology, phytoplankton, bacteria, fishes, riparian ecology, burros, beach erosion, sociological carrying capacity and human waste decomposition. Fish projects included a preliminary survey ($2,500) and a status survey with emphasis on fishes ($15,437).
- **Management tools:** Regulations limited launch numbers, party sizes, motor horsepower and trip frequency. Upstream travel was prohibited. Sanitation rules required pack-in/pack-out and chemical toilets.

### Ozark National Scenic Riverways

- **Growth:** In 1971, 12,600 canoes were counted on the Upper Current River. A moratorium on new commercial outfitter permits was imposed that year, when commercial operators accounted for 90% of traffic. Canoe traffic doubled over five years to 36,100 in the most recent season reported.
- **Public use:** About 49,000 people floated the stretch between Memorial Day and Labor Day 1975.
- **Permit system:** NPS-permitted rental establishments still accommodated 80% of canoe use.
- **Approach:** Restraints aimed to preserve the status quo, not to set a formal carrying capacity. Manager Marnell argued that research cannot make carrying-capacity decisions alone, and that management objectives and public input must come first.
- **Methods:** Super-8 time-lapse surveillance and computerized floater card surveys yielded traffic volume, composition and distribution data. Graduate-student thesis projects, funded at $1,000–$5,000 each, provided much of the fieldwork.

### Buffalo National River

- **Study structure:** Year 1 was a reconnaissance with about eight benchmark water-quality stations. Year 2 added land-use mapping, vegetation analysis, a benthic macroinvertebrate inventory and resource capacity analysis. Year 3 emphasized geohydrology.
- **Water quality:** The river is cool, clear and low in productivity. Trace elements are generally low. Nutrients and minerals respond strongly to storm events. Hydrology dominates water quality.
- **Models to develop:** Three priority model types were identified. These are low-flow frequency (considered most important), instantaneous high-flow frequency and average annual flow. Recommended applied models cover dissolved oxygen, temperature and dissolved solids; algal growth and nutrient transport; and coliform, bacteria and sediment transport.
- **Vulnerability:** Carbonate geology makes the river highly susceptible to pollutant entry. Waste disposal must be carefully managed.
- **Timeline:** About two more years of study were projected before criteria standards could be set.

### Padre Island National Seashore (Beach Traffic)

- **Study sites:** NOTRAF (vehicles prohibited, light foot traffic), PEDTRAF (pedestrian traffic only, near a campground), VEHTRAF (unrestricted vehicles and pedestrians) and SHELL (shell beach, limited by four-wheel-drive access). The study ran 10 months (1975), with 12 transects in the two most intensive sites.
- **Vegetation:** Density at VEHTRAF was about half that at NOTRAF (quadrat ratio 0.50; point-frame ratio 0.54). Grasses declined relative to forbs with increasing traffic. Aerial litter was more than twice as abundant at NOTRAF (36% vs 16%).
- **Vegetation extent:** Vegetation extended 160 ft at NOTRAF and 110 ft at VEHTRAF in year one. By year two the average extents were nearly equal (209 and 196 ft), due to sparse *Tidestroemia* colonization.
- **Sediment:** Vegetated portions of all beaches gained volume (roughly 20–43 cubic ft per month). The whole beach lost volume at VEHTRAF (−28 cubic ft per month) and PEDTRAF. NOTRAF's whole beach grew steadily. SHELL was stable, with about 1 cubic ft per month of sedimentation.
- **Mechanism:** Traffic lifts sand into wind streams, destroys sand-trapping vegetation, and widens the traffic zone. The net result is sand moving landward and beach loss seaward.
- **Storm effect:** No storm surges occurred during the study, so storm resistance remains untested.

### Rocky Mountain National Park (Backcountry Management)

- **Growth:** Backcountry camper-days rose from under 10,000 per year around 1964 to more than 50,000 by 1975, a five-fold increase. A 1972 estimate gave about 200,000 visitor-days and 37,000 camper-days.
- **Site inventory:** In 1972–73 there were 181 designated sites in 51 areas, plus 7 group areas. Roughly 200 sites existed in total, about one per square mile.
- **Permit system:** A mandatory permit was introduced in 1972 with computerized summaries (BUS program). Sub-drainages were used as the geographic base. Party data were reported as visitor-days, party-days and stock-days.
- **Compliance:** Fazio's 1973 study found about 92% compliance. Most violators were locals who knew the system.
- **Sanitation:** Helicopter removal of human waste used 50-gallon air-lift vaults. The one-summer cost was $4,430 (about $85 per trip). This practice was later limited to two areas because vaults were misused for trash.
- **Education:** A slide-tape presentation was judged the most effective way to reach users, because newspaper outreach did not reach them. Bright colors and dogs were discouraged. Fire rings and wood use were restricted.
- **Growth in use:** Use rose 18% in 1975, and the permit system was overloaded, with eight staff handling about 50,000 registrations.

### Cross-Cutting Themes

- **[[Systems Approach]] (Wauer):** Management should define objectives first, then constraints, components, divisions and a feedback loop. The eight-step sequence runs: determine problem existence, define it, document it, choose a strategy, gather data, analyze and recommend, evaluate, then implement and monitor.
- **Data retention:** Raw permit records should be kept for usage evaluation. Vegetation transects should be permanently marked for long-term monitoring.
- **Critical habitat:** Area managers should prepare Critical Habitat Management Plans before endangered-species requirements become urgent.
- **Concessionaire cooperation:** Concessionaire support is critical to visitor capacity projects.
- **Flexibility:** Plans should adapt to new data, such as the cave radiation issue, and to regional population growth near parks like Rocky Mountain.
