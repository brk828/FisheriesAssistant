# Razorback Sucker Repatriation and Population Status in Lake Mohave (Draft Final Report, March 2004)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report, prepared by Carol A. Pacey and submitted by Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]] to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region), summarizes the [[Colorado River Fishes Database]] and assesses the status of wild adult and repatriated [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]] as of March 2004. The March 2004 wild adult population estimate is 966 fish (95% CI 580–1,711), a sharp decline from earlier estimates. Between 1992 and 1 March 2004, 91,565 PIT-tagged juveniles were stocked. Survival is strongly related to size at release: fish over 350 mm were recaptured at nearly four times the rate of fish under 300 mm. The authors recommend a minimum release length of 300 mm and describe unexplained low overall survival.

## Background & Description

**Program and governance.** Since 1992 ASU has served as the central repository for field data gathered by the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), a cooperative body including the Arizona Game and Fish Department, USGS Biological Resources Division, the Nevada Department of Wildlife, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the National Park Service. The NFWG produces and rears native lower Colorado River fishes, mainly razorback sucker. Larvae are collected annually from shoreline shallows of Lake Mohave and reared at off-site facilities, including [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Bubbling Ponds, Dexter NFH, and Boulder City ponds. Juveniles are then stocked into the lake or moved to predator-free backwaters.

**Fish marking.** Juveniles are tagged with 400 kHz [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags), measured, and stocked. Stocked fish are assumed to be safe from predation when they reach about 300 mm TL, based on [[Marsh and Brooks]] (1989), who identified catfish predation as a key threat.

**Survey design.** The NFWG revisits the same localities each March, which is the main spawning season, along with May and November sampling. Gears include trammel netting and electrofishing. Data are entered manually into Excel and then into an Access database. The database covers razorback sucker from Lakes Mead, Mohave, and Havasu and below Parker Dam, and it also holds bonytail and humpback chub data from other mark-recapture studies. Only Lake Mohave razorback sucker and bonytail data are actively managed.

**Key definitions.**
- *Capture*: first encounter. *Recapture*: subsequent encounter.
- *Short-term recapture*: recapture within the same sampling period.
- *Unique capture*: first capture for each PIT tag.
- *Paired data*: matching stocking and collection records for each PIT tag.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reproduction and survival.** The authors state that razorback sucker can reproduce in the wild, but larvae survive only in protected, predator-free environments. Survival after release is uncertain for more than 99% of stocked fish, especially those under 300 mm.

**Population decline.** The wild adult population has dropped from roughly 44K (as printed, likely OCR-affected) about a decade earlier to fewer than 1,000. The report's conclusions describe historical levels in the hundreds of thousands.

**Habitat and rearing sites.** Lakeside backwaters include Yuma, Princess, and Placer coves, along with Chemehueve, Davis, and Nine Mile coves. Off-site facilities include Boulder City Golf Course Ponds, Boulder City Wetlands Park, Bubbling Ponds, Dexter NFH, and Willow Beach NFH. Repatriated fish have been captured at Valhalla, Mojave Wash, and Tequila coves, and Yuma, Jeff Davis, and Gold coves.

**Other species.** The NFWG monitoring also records other native fishes, including [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), and many non-native species, though the report focuses on razorback sucker.

## Key Findings

### Wild adult population
- The March 2003 single-census estimate is 966 fish (95% CI 580–1,711), using the [[Chapman Modification of the Peterson Estimator]] (Ricker 1975).
- The 2002 estimate was 1,150 (95% CI 724–1,917).
- The 2003 estimate is significantly lower than the previously published 2,698 (Marsh et al. 2003).

### Stocking
- From 1992 to 1 March 2004, 91,724 juveniles were stocked, of which 91,565 were PIT-tagged. Fish with missing or duplicate tags were excluded from analysis.
- Off-site facilities produced 67,418 of these fish. Lakeside backwaters produced 24,147, with Yuma Cove the most productive at 11,148.
- The most successful stocking years were 1999 (16,010) and 2003 (16,843), together 36% of all stocked fish.
- Princess, Placer, and Yuma coves together received 40% of all stocked fish.
- The 12-year mean stocking TL was 303 mm (range 96–563 mm).

### March 2004 survey (15–19 March)
- 258 razorback suckers were captured: 48 (19%) untagged and 210 (81%) PIT-tagged.
- Sex and maturity: 111 female (43%), 123 male (48%), 9 juvenile (3%), and 15 indeterminate (6%).
- 30 short-term recaptures were excluded.
- Of the 180 remaining PIT-tagged fish, 118 (65%) were repatriates, 50 (28%) were wild adults, and 12 (7%) were not in the database.
- Among repatriates, 38 were female (mean TL 552 mm) and 65 were male (mean TL 525 mm). Females were generally larger.
- Mean stocking TL of the repatriates sampled was 333 mm.

### Repatriate captures and population
- 1,067 repatriates have been captured from 1992 to March 2004 (excluding 51 short-term and 208 recaptures). This is about 1.1% of PIT-tagged fish released, but the authors caution this does not reflect survival, since many fish had not reached maturity.
- Using March-only data, the repatriate population estimate is 1,366 fish (95% CI about 735–2,573; the lower bound is printed as "1735" and appears to be an OCR error).

### Size at release and survival
- 48% of released fish (43,762) were 300 mm or smaller; 40% (36,963) were 300–350 mm; and 12% (10,754) were over 350 mm.
- Collected fish by release size: 335 from fish ≤300 mm (0.76% of that group), 428 from fish 300–350 mm (about 1.2%), and 303 from fish over 350 mm (2.8%).
- Survival of fish released over 350 mm is about four times that of fish released under 300 mm.
- Marsh et al. (in press) found that increasing length from 300 to 350 mm would more than double post-stocking survival, and that first-year survival for a 350 mm fish is two and a half times that of a 300 mm fish.
- To address the fact that larger fish are more catchable, a four-year time delay was applied. Tables 13–14 present the delayed size-class distributions and survival estimates, but several values are illegible in the source.

### Tag loss and bias
- The authors estimate that 10–20% of apparently repatriated fish had no detectable PIT tag when captured. Tag loss has not yet been quantified.
- Possible sources of bias include inappropriate models, tag loss, delayed recruitment of repatriates to spawning aggregations, and differing catchability of repatriates and wild fish.

### Conclusions and recommendations
- The repatriation program has returned about 1,400 fish to the lake, which is far short of the 50,000-repatriate target.
- Repatriates should be reared to at least 300 mm TL before release, and larger sizes should be pursued if practical, even at the cost of fewer fish.
- Nine Willow Beach NFH fish released in February 2004 (mean 335 mm, range 330–335 mm; 31–43 days at large) were recaptured 16–25 river miles south of the release site, showing rapid dispersal.
- Release timing should be included in future management decisions, because capture probability differs between release months.
- The authors are "perplexed" by low overall survival, which they attribute either to unknown mortality factors or to estimation bias. Future work will test the models and quantify tag loss.
