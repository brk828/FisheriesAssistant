# Fish Culture in Pennsylvania

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-culture

## Executive Summary

This 1963 (First Revision) booklet from the [[Pennsylvania Fish Commission]] describes the Commission's fish cultural system: nine production stations plus one research station, the species they raise, and the methods used to hatch, rear, and stock fish in public waters. The core program is [[Trout]] production, with roughly 2 million trout reared annually. Warmwater programs focus on [[Muskellunge]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]]. Fish are also salvaged from sanctuaries and open waters such as [[Pymatuning Reservoir]] and [[Lake Erie]]. The document is primarily descriptive and educational rather than a research report.

## Background & Description

The booklet was prepared by the Conservation Education Division to inform visitors to the stations, support student reference needs in biology, history, and conservation, and explain the fishery program to anglers.

**Station network:** The Commission operates nine fish cultural stations, located in Centre, Crawford, Cumberland, Wayne, Bedford, Forest and Erie counties. A tenth facility, [[Benner Spring Fish Research Station]], conducts fish culture research and serves as headquarters for the Division of Research and Fish Management. Station summaries:

- [[Bellefonte Station]] (Centre County): three units (Pleasant Gap, est. 1903; Lower Spring Creek, est. 1933; Upper Spring Creek, est. 1936). Contains 159 holding tanks and troughs, 294 ponds and raceways (1,053,723 sq ft), and about 52 full-time employees.
- [[Reynoldsdale Station]] (Bedford County, est. 1928): trout and limited largemouth bass; 76 tanks/troughs, 127 ponds/raceways (144,200 sq ft), 15 employees.
- [[Pleasant Mount Station]] (Wayne County, est. 1904): trout and warmwater species; 122 tanks/troughs, 119 ponds/raceways (760,370 sq ft), 24 employees. Includes [[Hankins Pond]] (67 acres).
- [[Tionesta Station]] (Forest County, est. 1928): muskellunge rearing; 45 tanks, 61 ponds/raceways (175,086 sq ft), 7 employees.
- [[Linesville Station]] (Crawford County, est. 1939): warmwater only, mainly bass and muskellunge, plus collection from the Pymatuning sanctuary; 24 tanks, 58 ponds/raceways (869,271 sq ft), 15 employees. Supervised together with [[Walnut Creek Station]].
- [[Corry Station]] (Erie County, est. 1876, the oldest current Commission hatchery): trout and minnows; 110 tanks/troughs, 131 ponds/raceways (473,556 sq ft), 11 employees. Includes 18-acre Brown's Pond.
- [[Union City Station]] (Erie County, est. 1905): warmwater species, mainly muskellunge, northern pike and forage minnows; 85 tanks, 33 ponds (219,650 sq ft), 5 employees. Supervised with Corry.
- [[Huntsdale Station]] (Cumberland County, construction from 1932, full size by 1942): mainly trout, plus bluegill ponds; 70 tanks/troughs, 240 ponds/raceways (980,939 sq ft), 29 employees. Has a public aquarium.
- [[Walnut Creek Station]] (Erie County): operates the Lake Erie salvage work with the work boat *The Angler*, and repairs and tars all Commission nets.

**Scale of operations:** The Commission reared more than 2 million brook, brown and rainbow trout (6 to 30 inches) annually, stocked a few thousand lake trout, and raised steelhead and kokanee for experimental plantings. In 1962 it joined the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in a cooperative trout stocking program. Up to 74 tank trucks and other vehicles travel about one-half million miles per year, and trout are stocked in more than 4,500 miles of streams and 2,688 acres of lakes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water sources:** Most stations depend on springs. [[Reynoldsdale Station]] draws on a large limestone spring (1,200 to 1,400 gallons per minute), and [[Benner Spring Fish Research Station]] on a spring yielding 6,000 to 8,000 gallons per minute. [[Huntsdale Station]] uses eight major springs and a spring-fed mountain stream. The Pleasant Mount station's main source is Belmont Lake. Upper Spring Creek is fed entirely by Spring Creek. Tionesta draws on Tubbs Run.
- **Why springs matter:** Spring water is more constant in flow and temperature and less prone to pollution than stream water, a key reason stations are sited near springs.
- **Water quality management:** Water aerators are used at several stations to raise dissolved oxygen and remove harmful gases.
- **Natural spawning timing:** Trout are spawned in the fall. Bass and muskellunge spawn naturally in spring (bass at about 60°F, muskies earlier in spring), and pickerel spawn naturally in Hankins Pond.
- **Lake Erie and reservoir fisheries:** Trap netting on Lake Erie (late May to early fall) and at Pymatuning Reservoir (until early June) supplies adult broodstock and fish for stocking.
- **Native range notes:** Rainbow trout originally spawned in early spring in cold, fast streams of northern California, Washington and Oregon. Eastern chain pickerel naturally occur only in some Susquehanna and Delaware river basin waters.

## Key Findings

**Trout culture**
- Trout cycles start with egg taking in the fall; a fall-spawning rainbow strain developed through selective breeding allows all trout species to be reared in year classes with similar starting dates.
- Eggs are obtained by stripping ripe brood fish manually. Up to 4,000 breeder trout may be handled yearly.
- Eggs hatch in 36 to 55 days depending on temperature; 52°F is considered ideal. The [[Jar Hatching Method]], developed at Benner Spring, is now used at all trout stations.
- Many more eggs are taken than needed, because of infertility, natural mortality, fingerling plantings, cooperative sportsmen's projects, and hatchery losses from disease, pollution and lightning.
- Sac fry are yolk-sustained for about 15 days, then feed on a fine commercial dry diet.
- Trout are reared about 15 months, reaching 6 to 10 inches. About 84 percent of stocked trout are this size. About 16 percent of each year class is held over; two-year-olds measure 12 to 16 inches and three-year-olds 15 to 20 inches.
- Brood stock may be held up to 4 years, reaching up to 30 inches and 8 pounds, and produce 500 to 4,000 eggs per year.
- Stocking begins in March and runs for three to four months.

**Bass culture**
- Bass must spawn naturally. Males build nests (smallmouth on gravel in depths up to six feet; largemouth on plant roots, gravel or sand, usually shallower).
- Eggs hatch in 6 to 10 days; 65°F is optimal. Fry are removed to separate ponds to protect them from parental cannibalism.
- Young bass are fed daphnia for about five weeks, then ground marine fish, meat products and live minnows. Fingerlings (3 to 5 inches) are usually stocked in fall.
- Bass propagation occurs at Linesville, Bellefonte, Pleasant Mount and Reynoldsdale.

**Muskellunge culture**
- Eggs are stripped from adult muskies taken in trap nets, mainly at Pymatuning Reservoir. Eggs hatch in about 12 days at about 55°F.
- Fry are reared in indoor running-water tanks, which gave better growth and lower mortality than outdoor ponds. Large fingerlings are considered more effective than fry plantings.
- Muskies eat only live food, up to 10 minnows per day each. For the 1959 production of 13,650 fingerlings, more than 12½ million forage fish were required.
- Muskies reach about 30 inches and mature between years three and four.

**Northern pike and chain pickerel**
- Pike programs mirror muskellunge programs but produce fewer fish, using eggs from Lake Erie and Canadohta Lake, with rearing limited to Union City. Planting is restricted to drainages that already contain pike, since pike may compete with muskellunge.
- Chain pickerel spawn naturally in Hankins Pond (67 acres). They are collected by trap net, pond draining, or barbless hook-and-line through ice, and used only for specialized stocking within their natural range.

**Walleye**
- Earlier mass releases of eyed eggs and fry, mainly in Lake Erie, showed no improvement in catches, and the program was largely abandoned by the bordering states, Ontario and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- Today fry are reared to fingerlings at four stations and released in June and early July in selected waters to improve population balance and fishing.

**Pan fish and forage fish**
- Bluegills, catfish, suckers and minnows are reared to a limited extent. Minnows serve as forage for pike, muskellunge and bass programs; daphnia are also cultured.

**Wild collection and other sources**
- Fish are salvaged from closed sanctuaries, including Pymatuning (crappies, bluegills, walleyes, catfish, bass) and Lake Erie (bass, walleye and others), and are also purchased from commercial fishermen.
- About 200,000 brown bullheads and channel catfish were purchased from commercial fishermen on the [[Delaware River]].
- Young eels (elvers) from the lower [[Susquehanna River]] in Maryland are transferred annually to the Susquehanna watershed above the large dams.
- Collected fish augment populations in open rivers and ponds throughout the Commonwealth.
