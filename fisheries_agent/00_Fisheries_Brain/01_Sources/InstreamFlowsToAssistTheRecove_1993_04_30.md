# Instream Flows to Assist the Recovery of Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-ecology #Cyprinidae #instream-flow-methodology

## Executive Summary

This 1993 draft report, prepared for the [[Endangered Fishes Recovery Program]] Instream Flow Subcommittee, synthesizes ecological rationale for setting instream flow regimes to protect endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River System]] (notably the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], historically termed "squawfish," and the [[Humpback Chub]]). The author argues that river ecosystems are too complex ("N-dimensional," stochastic) for simple deterministic flow-habitat models, and instead advocates the [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]] (Ward and Stanford 1983) as a robust framework for understanding how flow regulation shifts the position of the [[Rhithron-Potamon Transition]] downstream, degrading potamon habitat critical to endangered big-river fishes. The report critically reviews the evolution and shortcomings of instream flow methodologies — from the simple "Montana Method" (Tennant 1975) to the widely-used [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) — concluding that IFIM is fundamentally unsuited to large, turbid, low-gradient river reaches like those occupied by Upper Colorado River endangered fishes, and that alternative approaches grounded in sediment transport and channel geomorphology may offer more defensible flow recommendations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System context**: Upper Colorado River system, including the Green and Yampa Rivers — large, complex, historically stochastic alluvial rivers now altered by deep-storage reservoir regulation.
- **Zonation concept**: The river continuum is divided into a **rhithron zone** (upper, higher-gradient, cooler, trout-favorable reaches) and a **potamon zone** (lower-gradient, warmer, slower reaches favorable to endangered fishes). Flow regulation from deep-storage reservoirs causes a *downstream discontinuity* — extending rhithron-like conditions further downstream and reducing potamon habitat extent, to the detriment of species like [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Humpback Chub]].
- **Critical habitat features**: Backwaters, eddies, and deep-water mainstem habitats are ecologically essential to adult and juvenile endangered fishes, particularly at higher flows — not the low-flow shallow habitats that instream flow models tend to emphasize.
- **Sampling bias**: Adult monitoring programs (USFWS 1987b) have historically emphasized shallow shoreline habitats sampled via electrofishing, yet field studies (Tyus and McAda 1984; McAda and Kaeding 1991) show these fishes routinely use deep, turbid water — a habitat type difficult to sample and thus underrepresented in habitat suitability data.
- **Comparative river systems**: Contrasted with smaller, more homogeneous, historically well-studied regulated streams in England and the U.S. (e.g., Binns and Eiserman 1979; Armitage 1989), where flow-productivity relationships are more statistically tractable due to lower environmental stochasticity.

## Morphological / Physical Habitat Characteristics

*(Note: This source addresses river/channel physical characteristics and habitat structure rather than fish anatomical morphology.)*

- **Channel dynamics**: Sediment transport, channel geomorphology, and channel slope jointly determine physical habitat availability and are proposed as more reliable predictors of biologically meaningful channel change than simplistic flow-threshold models.
- **Bankfull flow issues**: Determining a single "channel-forming" bankfull flow is problematic due to local morphological variation and sparse peak-flow hydraulic data.
- **Preferred methodology**: Empirical characterization of sediment gradation, channel geomorphology, and slope, allowing estimation of sediment movement dynamics as a function of peak flow event duration (cf. Andrews and Nelson 1989, applied to a large bar complex in the Green River).
- **IFIM's physical habitat model structure**:
  1. Transects divided into cells measuring/simulating depth, velocity, cover, and substratum — treated as independent variables (a contested assumption).
  2. Life-history stage occurrence (YOY, juvenile, adult, spawner) related to hydraulic variables to build **habitat suitability curves**.
  3. **Weighted Usable Area (WUA)** calculated per cell and summed across a reach and range of discharges, producing a continuous WUA-discharge function.

## Key Findings

1. **Serial Discontinuity Concept** (Ward and Stanford 1983) is presented as the most robust ecosystem-level integrative framework for predicting river response to flow regulation, superior to deterministic or single-variable models.
2. **Model generality is elusive**: Morhardt (1986) reviewed 72 studies attempting to derive general instream flow-productivity models; only one (Binns and Eiserman 1979) produced a statistically valid result, attributable to regional biophysical homogeneity rather than model generality.
3. **The Montana Method** (Tennant 1975) relates regulated flows to historical average flow regimes but fails to account for flow variability's relationship to channel geomorphology.
4. **IFIM critique**: Despite being an "industry standard" (Reiser et al. 1989), IFIM suffers from:
   - Misuse of suitability curves as probability functions (Patten 1979; Mathur et al. 1985; Moyle and Baltz 1985)
   - Unjustified assumption of independence among depth, velocity, and substratum variables
   - No demonstrated link between WUA and actual biomass/productivity (Mathur et al. 1985; Scott and Shrivell 1987; Bowlby and Roff 1986)
   - Failure to incorporate predation and density-dependent population processes (Moyle and Baltz 1985; Orth 1987; Stanford and Ward 1992)
   - Sensitivity to choice of hydraulic simulator (e.g., HEC-2) and use of non-site-specific suitability curves (Gore and Nestler 1988)
5. **IFIM failure in the Upper Colorado River**: Application by Rose and Hann (1989) produced WUA maxima for squawfish and humpback chub life stages at unrealistically low flows (exceeded nearly 100% of the time historically) — ecologically nonsensical given the fishes' known reliance on backwater/eddy habitats generated at higher flows. IFIM systematically underestimates WUA where low-velocity habitat is abundant (as in the potamon zone) and cannot model zero-flow habitats.
6. **IFIM is best suited to small, uniformly-flowing streams** (consistent with log-velocity profile and HEC-2 assumptions), not large, stochastic alluvial systems like the Upper Colorado.
7. **Recommended alternative approach**: Empirically derived sediment transport and channel geomorphology models (e.g., Andrews 1986; Lyons and Pucherelli 1992; Andrews and Nelson 1989) offer a more defensible basis for flow negotiations than incremental habitat modeling in large, complex rivers.
