# The Status of the Black Bass Fishery in Lake Mead and a Program Toward Restoration and Enhancement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 final report to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (contract 7-07-30-X0028), prepared by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGF) and the [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] (NDOW), examines the decline of the [[Largemouth Bass]] fishery in [[Lake Mead]] over a five-year study (1977–1982). The study tested whether the decline reflected reduced recruitment, how water-level management could improve the fishery, and whether artificial enhancement (cover, fertilization, stocking) could restore it toward 1959 levels. The authors conclude that reduced nutrient loading after [[Glen Canyon Dam]] construction, limited cover, and water-level fluctuation are the main drivers, and they recommend a sequence of four alternatives, with stocking as a last resort.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Lake Mead lies on the [[Colorado River]] about 483 km downstream of [[Hoover Dam]], spanning roughly 177 km with a shoreline of about 885 km, a maximum surface area of 660 km², and a volume of 35,064,000,000 m³ (hydraulic retention time 3.6 years). It consists of large basins (Boulder, Virgin, Temple, Gregg's) and narrow tributary arms, including the Overton Arm.
- **Climate:** Mohave Desert environment, mean annual precipitation 13.1 cm, with large seasonal temperature ranges.
- **Vegetation:** Sparse; emergent and submergent species include [[Tamarisk]], [[Pondweed]], [[Spiny Naiad]], and cattail, with terrestrial shrubs such as creosote and brittlebush.
- **Historical stocking:** Largemouth bass, bluegill, black crappie, green sunfish and channel catfish were stocked by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in the mid-1930s. [[Threadfin Shad]] were introduced in 1954–1956 as forage.
- **Study design:** Creel surveys at nine locations; nesting, survival and cover surveys at several coves in the Upper and Lower Basins; food-habit analysis; plankton and macroinvertebrate sampling; sediment cores; and stocking and introduction feasibility reviews.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water levels:** Filling of [[Lake Powell]] from 1963 reversed the seasonal storage pattern, so levels fell in spring instead of rising. Declining spring levels were linked to lower bass production (NDOW 1963–1976).
- **Nutrients and plankton:** Sediment cores (Prentki et al. 1981) show total phosphorus in the Upper Basin fell from almost 5000 MT/yr to under 400 MT/yr, and nitrogen from almost 8000 MT/yr to under 900 MT/yr. Limnetic zooplankton abundance decreased about tenfold from 1971 to 1979–80 (Wilde 1981).
- **Forage:** Adult bass shifted from a shad-insect diet to a centrarchid-crayfish-insect diet, probably because of reduced shad abundance linked to [[Striped Bass]] predation.
- **Cover:** Over 50% of surveyed areas lacked cover; pondweed and spiny naiad were the only relatively abundant aquatic plants.

## Key Findings

**Fishery decline and angling**
- Largemouth bass harvest averaged about 500,000 fish per year (1951–1969), comprising over 60% of the total harvest; since 1969 it has averaged about 200,000 per year.
- Largemouth bass fell from 42.5% of the catch in 1974 to 11.9% in 1981 (Table II); striped bass rose from 0.3% to 46.6% (1979).
- Angler success (bass per angler) rose from 1.14 in 1977 to 1.68 in 1981.
- Martin et al. (1980) valued the largemouth bass fishery at $9.2 million annually (figure as extracted; OCR uncertain). If success doubled, angling pressure would increase by 60%.
- Nevada anglers were the major users (62.7–75.6% of anglers, 1978–1981), followed by California.

**Survival and recruitment**
- Mean nesting success across five years was 48.7% (n = 413 nests; 201 successful). Annual success was 44.4%, 44.6%, 59.1%, 50.6% and 56.1% (1978–1982).
- Peak nesting occurred April 13 to May 10 at a mean nest temperature of 18.6 °C; mean nest duration was 10.5 days.
- Mean initial nest depth was 160.4 cm; most nesting was at 60–210 cm.
- Egg counts averaged 10,227 per nest (range 850–37,600); hatching success averaged 93.9%; swim-up survival averaged 37.8%.
- Skin diving was judged a good method for monitoring fry, but electrofishing favoured larger bass. Only 7.1% of tagged bass were recaptured by skin diving (October 1981).

**Water level and cover**
- Nests at depths of 0–150 cm were most vulnerable to wind and wave destruction; a drawdown of 6 cm/day caused about 88% of nests to be susceptible.
- Pondweed and spiny naiad provided most aquatic cover; inundated tamarisk and brittlebush were important cover for young bass.

**Enhancement experiments**
- Hay bales, plastic artificial kelp and tamarisk bundles were tested in a Virgin Basin cove (1980–1981). Hay bales produced sustained plankton increases; artificial kelp drew the most fingerling use in 1981.
- No bass fry were recorded with tamarisk bundles in 1980–1981.

**Stocking**
- Ruby Marsh bass returns ranged from 7.5% to 30.5% for 1975–1978 releases (Table XX).
- Hatchery-reared catchable bass returns were 0.6–72.4% depending on group; finger-size (5–15 cm) returns were very low (two fish from one group).
- Full restoration to 1959 levels was estimated to require about 0.5 million 30 cm bass annually, and an intensive hatchery of roughly 60 ha was estimated at about $2 million annually.

**Recommendations (in order of preference)**
1. **Alternative 1 – Cover and fertilization:** Seed shoreline zones with grasses and fertilizer, maintain stable levels from March to May, and run a pilot program in four coves.
2. **Alternative 2 – Fertilization:** Introduce nutrients at the [[Virgin River]] or Diamond Creek inflow, with monitoring; estimated fertilizer cost about $3 million annually for the Virgin Basin/Overton Arm.
3. **Alternative 3 – Artificial cover:** Install artificial structures in replicate coves and evaluate survival.
4. **Alternative 4 – Stocking:** Only after the others; AGF did not recommend introducing new black bass or forage species, while NDOW argued that [[Alabama Spotted Bass]] should not be ruled out.

**Potential introductions reviewed**
- Alabama spotted bass, smallmouth bass and Florida largemouth bass were assessed; the authors judged all to risk hybridization or displacement of the resident largemouth bass.
- Forage species reviewed included crayfish (Orconectes causeyi), grass shrimp, alewife, spottail and emerald shiners, Bonneville cisco and gizzard shad; none was recommended.
