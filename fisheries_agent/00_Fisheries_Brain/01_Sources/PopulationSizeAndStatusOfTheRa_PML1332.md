# Population Size and Status of the Razorback Sucker in the Green River Basin, Utah and Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study estimates the population of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Green River, Utah and Colorado, using capture-recapture data collected from 1980 to 1988 by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]]. The upper Green River population was estimated at 948 fish (95% confidence interval 758–1,138). Fish in the lower Green River were too rare (13 captured, 1 recaptured) for a reliable estimate. The authors argue that the upper Green River holds the largest surviving stock of the species in its native riverine habitat, but that the absence of recruitment and low adult density make the population increasingly vulnerable, and that new management strategies and possible federal listing are needed.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America. Once abundant and widely distributed, it is now threatened with extinction. In riverine habitats it persists only in the mainstream and lower parts of tributaries of the Green and upper Colorado rivers. Relict subpopulations remain in mainstream reservoirs of the lower Colorado River basin below Lee Ferry, Arizona; these are composed of very old individuals.

Previous reports noted the precarious status of the species in the upper basin, but no population size estimate had been published before this study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling area and methods.** Fish were collected mainly by [[Electrofishing]], with some captured by seine and trammel nets. Sampling covered Green River kilometers 35–555 (measured from the mouth), along with reaches of the Yampa and White rivers. Effort varied among reaches because of habitat, remoteness, and logistics. Each fish was tagged with a uniquely numbered Carlin dangler tag. Data from 1983 were excluded because flooding sharply reduced sampling effort and no razorback suckers were caught in the upper Green River.

**Model selection.** The program [[CAPTURE]] was used to compare eight population models. The model selected allowed capture probability to change over time but assumed no individual heterogeneity and no trap response. This choice was supported by the variable effort among years and the lack of a plausible biological reason for individual differences in capture probability.

**Barriers and closure.** Rapids in [[Gray Canyon]] and lower [[Desolation Canyon]] (km 211–282) and a diversion dam at km 206 appear to block fish movement between the upper and lower Green River. No razorback suckers were captured in these canyons. The authors judged that geographic closure holds for the upper population, since no fish have been captured above km 555 in nine years and tag returns and radio telemetry showed little movement.

**Recruitment.** Larval razorback suckers have been documented below suspected spawning sites in the upper Green River, and ripe adults have been captured at several spawning sites. However, no juveniles (20–400 mm) were captured despite extensive seining from 1979 to 1986, indicating a lack of recruitment.

**Threats.** The authors cite predation by introduced predators, such as [[Ictalurid Catfishes]], on young fish as a likely cause of recruitment failure. They also cite habitat alteration after the impoundment of [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] in 1962. Lack of recruitment is also seen as the cause of past population declines in the lower Colorado River reservoirs.

**Age.** Estimated ages for two Green River adults were over 19 years (otolith count) and 12 years (vertebral count). Vertebral counts tend to underestimate age, so the true ages may be higher, but remain several years younger than the 44-year-old fish reported from Lake Mohave.

## Key Findings

- **Upper Green River population:** 948 fish (SE 97; 95% CI 758–1,138), from 410 fish captured with 68 recaptures. The coefficient of variation was 10.2%, which the authors considered good precision.
- **Capture probability:** Yearly capture probability ranged from 0.02 to 0.09.
- **Lower Green River:** Only 13 fish captured, with 1 recapture, from 1980 to 1988. The population is considered relict and too small to estimate.
- **Catch per unit effort (CPUE):** Averaged 0.49 fish per hour in the upper Green River and 0.05 in the lower Green River.
- **Size range:** Captured adults ranged from 426 to 617 mm total length.
- **Tag retention:** Estimated at about 94.4%, based on the loss of only 4 tags. Tag loss was considered negligible.
- **Conservation status:** The razorback sucker does not receive protection under the Endangered Species Act. The authors call for a coordinated recovery plan and reconsideration of federal listing, warning that the population will decline without recruitment.
- **Research needs:** Increasing sampling effort in future years would raise capture probability and improve estimate reliability. Further research and management are needed to address low density, poor recruitment, and habitat alteration.
