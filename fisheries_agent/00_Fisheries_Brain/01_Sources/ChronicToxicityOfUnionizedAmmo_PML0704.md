# Chronic Toxicity of Un-ionized Ammonia to Endangered Colorado Pikeminnow and Razorback Sucker Larvae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests how sensitive two endangered fishes, the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), are to [[Un-ionized Ammonia]] (NH3). Both species are native to the Upper Colorado River, where [[Groundwater Contamination]] from the [[Moab Uranium Mill Tailings Pile]] releases ammonia into shallow backwaters that serve as critical habitat. Post-swim-up larvae were exposed for 28 days in [[Static Renewal Test]]s and compared with the standard surrogate [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*).

The three species were similar in sensitivity. Their 28-day LC50 values ranged from 0.54 to 0.76 mg/L NH3. Chronic values (ChVs) for growth and mortality ranged from 0.40 to 0.70 mg/L NH3. The authors conclude that existing ammonia criteria, if achieved by remediation, would protect these endangered fishes, even when the most conservative LC1 value is used as the basis.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The Colorado Pikeminnow and the Razorback Sucker are two of four endangered fishes whose critical habitat includes the un-impounded Upper Colorado River above [[Lake Powell]]. The other two are the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) must consider, under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]], whether any private, state, or federal activity could jeopardize remaining populations.

**Ammonia chemistry and toxicity.** Ammonia exists as un-ionized NH3 and ionized ammonium (NH4+). The balance depends on pH and temperature. Un-ionized ammonia is the more toxic form, and its share rises as pH increases above 8.0. In fish it can cause loss of equilibrium, gill hyperplasia, and mortality, because high external concentrations stop the outward diffusion of ammonia across the gill surface. Ammonia enters aquatic systems from natural sources (organic matter decay, animal waste) and human sources (sewage effluent, fertilizers, industrial processes).

**Contamination source.** The [[Moab Uranium Mill Tailings Pile]] sits beside an abandoned uranium-processing facility on the western bank of the [[Upper Colorado River]] near Moab, Utah. Groundwater beneath the pile contains total ammonia up to 1000 mg/L. This groundwater enters the river over a 500 m downstream distance. The U.S. Department of Energy (USDOE), with other agencies, is evaluating options including [[Capping]] tailings in place, tailings removal, and [[Groundwater Remediation]].

**Objectives.** The study had two aims: (1) to determine the relative sensitivity of post-swim-up larval Colorado Pikeminnow, Razorback Sucker, and Fathead Minnow to NH3; and (2) to determine whether existing water quality criteria, if met by remediation, would protect these endangered fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Backwater habitat.** Under seasonal low-water conditions, mainly from August to March, shallow backwaters form beside the tailings pile. Total ammonia in these backwaters ranges from about 1 mg/L to over 1000 mg/L, depending on exchange with the main river. Shallow backwaters are typically warmer than the main channel and contain abundant zooplankton. They therefore support fast larval and juvenile growth, which helps fish avoid predation. The same habitats may also pose a toxicological risk, so the authors consider them critical from regulatory, ecological, and toxicological viewpoints.

**Exceedance of criteria.** Un-ionized NH3 in backwaters frequently exceeds 1.0 mg/L. The abstract reports this as more than 10 times the Utah 30-day average chronic criterion of 0.07 mg/L NH3 (20 °C, pH 8.2). The Utah 30-day criterion for total ammonia is 1.26 mg/L at 20 °C and pH 8.2, but it varies with pH and temperature, so chronic criteria can be greatly exceeded in these backwaters.

**Criterion discrepancy.** The document gives the Utah un-ionized ammonia criterion as 0.07 mg/L NH3 in the abstract and table note, but as 0.02 mg/L NH3 in the Discussion, where it is described as applying when fish early life stages are present. Both values are based on a 5.92% un-ionized fraction at pH 8.2 and 20 °C. The reader should verify which value applies.

**Test water.** Fish and tests used CERC well water (20 °C, pH 8.2, alkalinity around 250 mg/L as CaCO3, hardness about 250 mg/L as CaCO3). Measured conditions averaged 19.9 ± 1.0 °C, dissolved oxygen 7.4 ± 0.8 mg/L, and pH about 8.24.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**
- Larvae of Fathead Minnow, Colorado Pikeminnow, and Razorback Sucker were exposed for 28 days to nominal total ammonia-N concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5, 10, and 15 mg/L, using [[Static Renewal Test]] methods.
- Each treatment had 30 fish in three replicate one-liter beakers with 10 fish per beaker.
- Test solutions were renewed daily (about 50% of water replaced). Ammonia was measured by [[Salicylate Nitroprusside Colorimetry]] with a detection limit of 0.01 mg/L.
- Control survival was 93–97%, above the 80% acceptability criterion of the [[USEPA Test Guidelines]].

**Chronic values (ChVs), calculated as the geometric mean of the NOEC and LOEC using [[Analysis of Variance]]**

| Species | ChV, mortality (mg/L NH3) | ChV, growth (mg/L NH3) |
|---|---|---|
| Fathead Minnow | 0.43 | 0.43 |
| Colorado Pikeminnow | 0.70 | 0.40 |
| Razorback Sucker | 0.67 | 0.67 |

- Mortality and growth ChVs were similar for the Fathead Minnow and Razorback Sucker.
- For the Colorado Pikeminnow, the growth ChV was lower than the mortality ChV.

**28-day lethal concentrations (LCx), estimated by [[Maximum Likelihood Regression]]**

| Species | LC50 | LC20 | LC1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fathead Minnow | 0.69 | 0.42 | 0.13 |
| Colorado Pikeminnow | 0.76 | 0.61 | 0.38 |
| Razorback Sucker | 0.54 | 0.38 | 0.25 |

- Units are mg/L NH3. The 95% confidence intervals of the LC50 values overlapped across species, so the three were judged similar in sensitivity.
- The Fathead Minnow LC1 was similar to the Razorback Sucker LC1 but lower than the Colorado Pikeminnow LC1.
- Razorback Sucker and Colorado Pikeminnow LC1 values were similar when their confidence intervals were compared.

**Time-dependent mortality**
- Fathead Minnow mortality at the highest concentration occurred early, between days 3 and 9, and then stopped.
- Razorback Sucker mortality was delayed until about day 11 and reached total mortality by day 28.
- Colorado Pikeminnow showed intermediate timing.

**Relative community sensitivity**
- Using the [[USEPA Ambient Water Quality Criteria]] (1999) species dataset, normalized to pH 8.0 and 25 °C, the USEPA EC20s for a seven-species community range from 0.07 to 0.48 mg/L NH3.
- Among those species, the Bluegill was most sensitive (EC20 0.07 mg/L NH3), and the Channel Catfish (EC20 0.48 mg/L NH3) and Green Sunfish (EC20 0.32 mg/L NH3) were among the least sensitive.
- The Fathead Minnow (EC20 0.17 mg/L NH3) is among the more sensitive species in that set.
- The study's LC20 data were adjusted downward by a factor of 2.5 for comparison.
- The adjusted EC20s were 0.17 (Fathead Minnow), 0.24 (Colorado Pikeminnow), and 0.15 mg/L NH3 (Razorback Sucker). These correspond to the 35th, 49th, and 31st percentiles of the theoretical community [[Species Sensitivity Distribution]].

**Comparison with previous literature**
- Mayes et al. (1986) reported a 28-day Fathead Minnow LC50 of 0.30 mg/L NH3, about 2.3 times more sensitive than this study's LC50 of 0.69 mg/L.
- The USEPA re-analysis of early life-stage data gave Fathead Minnow EC20s of 0.16 and 0.28 mg/L NH3, which the authors describe as 2.6 and 1.5 times more sensitive than their 28-day LC20 of 0.42 mg/L. The authors attribute the difference to fish age or inter-laboratory variation.
- This study's 96-hour LC50 for Fathead Minnow (0.87 mg/L NH3) is lower than the 96-hour values for juveniles (1.50 mg/L; Mayes et al. 1986) and adults (average 1.68 mg/L; Thurston et al. 1983). The authors attribute this to life-stage differences.

**Methodological observations**
- Mortality-based regression estimates match the ANOVA-based ChVs. The Fathead Minnow ChV corresponded to about 20% mortality, and the Razorback Sucker ChV to about 50%.
- The Colorado Pikeminnow growth ChV (0.40 mg/L) approximated its LC1 (0.38 mg/L), suggesting that ANOVA gave a reasonable estimate of low-level effects.
- The authors note that ANOVA-based NOEC and LOEC values depend on test design, and that regression of growth data is complicated by nonlinear and hormetic responses.

**Conclusions**
- The three endangered and surrogate species are similar in ammonia sensitivity and rank among the most sensitive fishes tested, using comparable endpoints.
- Existing chronic ammonia criteria, if met by remediation at the Moab site, would protect these endangered fishes, even when the conservative LC1 value is used.
- The findings support using standard surrogate species, such as the Fathead Minnow, for protecting endangered fishes. Related studies cited by the authors reached similar conclusions for other species, including [[Bull Trout]] and [[Lost River Sucker]].
- Shallow backwaters with ammonia above criteria remain a concern, so remediation should prioritize them.
