# Species Introduction in a Tropical Lake: Cichla ocellaris in Gatun Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents how a single introduced piscivore, [[Cichla ocellaris]] (Peacock Bass), reshaped the biotic community of [[Gatun Lake]] in the Panama Canal Zone. Cichla was first released in 1967 and spread through the lake as a wave of predation. Its arrival was followed by sharp declines in native fishes, a more than 90 percent drop in the key planktivore [[Melaniris chagresi]], shifts in zooplankton composition, and absences of tertiary consumers such as tarpon and terns. The authors also report, with explicit caution, an apparent rise in mosquito abundance and a shift toward [[Plasmodium falciparum]] malaria. A parallel case at [[Lake Atitlán]] in Guatemala, where [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] were introduced in 1958, is used for comparison. The paper argues that predicting the ecological consequences of introductions is currently not possible at a basic level, and it outlines testable predictions for Gatun Lake.

## Background & Description

**Introduction history.** Before 1958, Lake Atitlán sustained fisheries for small native fishes (probably *Poecilia sphenops* and *Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*) and a crab fishery (*Potamocarcinus guatemalensis*) that supplied protein and cash income for local Guatemalan Indian communities. In 1958, [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) were introduced. Largemouth Bass fed voraciously on native fishes. About 15 years later, the local fishes and crabs were largely gone.

**Cichla ocellaris.** This cichlid is native to the Amazon River and its tributaries in northern South America. It is bright yellow with black vertical bars and carries a conspicuous ocellus (eyespot), black with a gold ring, at the base of the caudal peduncle. It commonly reaches 2 kg and 50 cm total length. It was valued by anglers as a strong fighter and as food.

**Introduction to Gatun Lake.** In 1965, a local businessman, with Panamanian government cooperation, moved about 100 fingerlings from rearing tanks in Buga, Colombia, into a small impoundment formed by damming the Quebrada Ancha creek. During the rainy season the impoundment overflowed into the Rio Gatuncillo, a tributary at the northern end of the [[Chagres River]]. The authors infer that fish spawned there by late 1966, reaching the Chagres River by 1967. Gatun Lake, formed in the early 1900s by damming the Chagres River, has a surface area of 42,315 hectares. There were no effective controls or regulations on introductions in Panama or the Canal Zone.

**Study system.** The authors had studied the lake for several years before Cichla arrived, which allowed before-and-after comparisons. Their sampling sites were concentrated around [[Barro Colorado Island]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spread dynamics.** Cichla is strictly piscivorous and native to river habitats. In the Chagres River it increased rapidly on abundant small fishes. By early 1970 catches were reported near [[Gamboa]], where the river enters the lake. By June 1970 it had reached Frijoles. It appeared around Barro Colorado Island in spring 1971 and spread toward Gatun. At the time of writing, it was moving into the Trinidad (Southwest) Arm. The fish moved as a wave with a leading edge of subadults, so edge populations differed in dynamics from those at the rear. Two processes slowed spread: intensive human fishing, and strong territoriality during breeding, with parents guarding fry for about 60 days or more. This produced a reproductive lag in newly colonized habitat. The authors describe the fish community as far from equilibrium.

**Trophic structure.** The authors describe the lake food web as operating through [[Trophic Levels]], from primary producers (phytoplankton, filamentous algae) through primary consumers (zooplankton, insects) to secondary and tertiary consumers. Cichla, at times feeding at up to a fifth trophic level, mainly affects secondary consumers. Its effects cascade to other levels, a pattern described as a [[Trophic Cascade]].

**Mosquitoes and disease.** The Panama Canal Company's [[Mosquito Index]] was considered unsuitable for this study because the monitoring sites (Balboa and Cristóbal) use regular organophosphate control. Gamboa, with only partial control using local 5 percent DDT applications, provided usable records. Malaria had been a serious problem in the region, and the vectors include [[Anopheles]], [[Culex]], and [[Mansonia]] genera.

## Key Findings

**1. Native fish collapse.** Table 1 is based on field observations over six years around Barro Colorado Island. The authors report that Cichla effectively eliminated six of the eight previously common diurnal fish species and drastically reduced a seventh. Species listed include [[Astyanax ruberrimus]], [[Roeboides guatemalensis]], [[Gambusia nicaraguagensis]], and [[Poecilia mexicana]], each with reported decreases of 50 to 100 percent.

**2. Community comparison (1972).** Two paired collections used 30 m × 5 m nets at a non-Cichla site in the Trinidad Arm and a Cichla site at Barro Colorado Island cove. The Trinidad Arm community contained 14 fish species, 11 of them contributing significantly to biomass. The Barro Colorado Island site contained seven species, dominated by Cichla and [[Cichlasoma maculicauda]]. Of the 11 previously important species, Cichla had completely eliminated seven and reduced three. Cichlasoma apparently increased, possibly because Cichla removed predators of Cichlasoma fry. The authors also note that the small *Eleotris* increased under Cichla, while *Roeboides*, unexpectedly abundant at the Cichla site, may survive by hiding in dense vegetation.

**3. Melaniris decline.** [[Melaniris chagresi]] is a key planktivore, with three higher-level species feeding heavily on it. Adults concentrate along shore while feeding and breeding, which exposes them to Cichla, whose predation is confined to shore regions. Daytime counts of adults passing the Dock Sampling Station showed a greater than 90 percent reduction in 1971 and 1972 compared with the pre-Cichla period (January 1969 to February 1970). Nighttime net tows (1 to 6 per date, 100 m tracts) at the Buoy Sampling Station showed a marked decline in young, which the authors attribute to fewer adults rather than direct Cichla predation.

**4. Zooplankton shift.** Young Melaniris feed almost exclusively on zooplankton. The horned morph of [[Ceriodaphnia cornuta]] is less favored by Melaniris predation than the unhorned morph, which has a reproductive advantage in the absence of predation. The authors predicted that a Melaniris decline would reduce the percentage of horned Ceriodaphnia. Seasonal data from the Buoy Sampling Station are consistent with this, with the 1971 and 1972 values including the lowest recorded from that station.

**5. Tertiary consumers.** [[Atlantic Tarpon]] (*Tarpon atlanticus*) enter Gatun Lake through the Panama Canal locks and feed on Melaniris. Immature [[Black Tern]] (*Chlidonias niger*) flocks, often over 150 birds, follow the tarpon and take Melaniris leaping from the water. Tarpon and terns were seen almost daily around Barro Colorado Island from May to October, but were absent in summer 1971 and early summer 1972. During the same period they were common in non-Cichla areas. Kingfishers and herons also became less common in Cichla regions.

**6. Mosquito and malaria trends (interpreted cautiously).** At Gamboa, total mosquito numbers (Anopheles, Culex, Mansonia) were lowest in 1968, the pre-Cichla year. They rose dramatically in 1969, when Cichla was probably peaking in the region, and remained appreciably higher than 1968 in later years. Each 1968 monthly average, with one exception, was the lowest for that month across the four years of records. Until 1969, more than 95 percent of malaria victims in the Canal Zone carried *P. vivax*, and less than 5 percent carried *P. falciparum*. From 1969 onward, 60 percent or more carried *P. falciparum*. Outbreaks in 1971 (Las Cumbres, an artificial lake) and 1972 (Escobal, near the Trinidad Arm narrows; 330 cases, exclusively *P. falciparum*) occurred near Cichla-containing waters. The authors stress that rainfall was an alternative explanation, but Barro Colorado Island rainfall did not track mosquito peaks (2238 mm in 1968, 2194 mm in 1969, 3226 mm in 1970, 2162 mm in 1971). They conclude that another factor was probably important.

**7. Predictions.** Assuming no further introductions, the authors predicted that:
- (a) The predation pressure coefficient at any locale can be inferred from the secondary-consumer spectrum and relative abundances.
- (b) Because Melaniris influence is confined to within 100 m of shore, its removal will increase total zooplankton density and average body size of the largest species, with very small taxa such as rotifers declining.
- (c) Phytoplankton species composition and abundance will track the predation pressure coefficient. Cichla-influenced areas already show lower phytoplankton standing crops, despite no gross chemical differences in lake water.

**8. Overall conclusion.** The authors argue that predation, and predation alone, currently structures the Gatun Lake ecosystem from tertiary consumers down to primary producers, while the lake remains out of equilibrium. They expect this influence to subside as the lake approaches a new equilibrium, but note that the permanence of the changes cannot yet be evaluated. They emphasize that accurate ecological prediction for species introductions is not yet possible, even in [[Biological Control]] programs with more than seven decades of study.

**Relevance to protein security.** Fish introductions are widely used to supply protein in tropical regions. The authors argue that the Lake Atitlán case shows the ecological cost of such introductions can exceed the benefit, eliminating an accessible local protein source and the associated income.
