# Summary of FY 1999 Fish Surveys for a Long-Term Monitoring Plan in the Gila River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report by [[Paul C. Marsh]] summarizes fish sampling conducted in fiscal year 1999 (October 1, 1998 to September 30, 1999) by the Arizona Game and Fish Department, Arizona State University, and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] under a long-term monitoring plan for fish populations in selected waters of the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona. Sampling covered rivers, a set of irrigation canals, and several opportunistic sites between 29 September 1998 and 28 January 1999. Across all stations, 27 taxa were captured, of which 23 were non-native. The report emphasizes that native fishes were scarce or absent in the canals and that data comparability was limited by variable sampling effort, subjective area estimates, and inconsistent electrofishing technique. Comparisons with earlier years (FY 1995 and 1996) were deliberately excluded.

## Background & Description

The monitoring program followed standard operating procedures for rivers and streams and for canals described by [[Clarkson 1996]] (a–c). Stations were sampled in seven monitored waters:

- San Pedro River, downstream of the U.S.–Mexico border
- Gila River, between Coolidge Dam and the Ashurst-Hayden Diversion
- Salt River, between Stewart Mountain Dam and Granite Reef Diversion
- [[Central Arizona Project Canal]] (CAP), at selected pump plants
- [[Salt River Project Canals]] North (SRPn) and South (SRPs)
- Florence-Casa Grande (FCG) Canal

Additional non-standard sampling occurred on SRPn, SRPs, and the Consolidated and Tempe canals. Gear included backpack electrofishing, seines, and, on some canals, boat-mounted electrofishing. The SRP canals were divided by electrical fish barriers into "above barrier" and "below barrier" reaches.

Species were identified by four-letter codes (Table 2). Native taxa in the survey were [[Roundtail Chub]] (Gila robusta), [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]], and [[Desert Sucker]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Natives made up roughly 15% of the 27 taxa but only a minority of catch in most waters. The proportion of native fishes in total catch varied sharply by station:

- San Pedro River: 74.3% native (highest)
- Gila River: 22.4% native
- Salt River: 44.7% native
- CAP Canal: 0% native
- SRPn: 17.4% native above the barrier, 14.3% below
- SRPs: 24.8% native above the barrier, 27.1% below
- FCG Canal: 1.7% native above the barrier, 1.3% below

Non-native species dominated the canal systems. [[Tilapia]] (mostly young-of-year, undetermined species, including redbelly and blue tilapia) predominated above the electrical barriers on SRPn and SRPs. [[Bluegill]] dominated the CAP Canal. Red shiner was the most abundant species in the Gila River and FCG Canal, and largemouth bass and mosquitofish were prominent in the Salt River and San Pedro River respectively.

The report notes that canal catches were partly opportunistic and qualitative, so cross-station comparisons of abundance are limited. Canal operations, including scheduled drawdowns, strongly influenced sampling success.

## Key Findings

**Overall community**
- 27 taxa captured in total (including Lepomis hybrids): 8 in FCG, 12 each in San Pedro and CAP, 13 in Salt, 15 in Gila and SRPs, and 17 in SRPn.
- 4 native taxa (15% of total species); 23 non-native.
- Number of non-native taxa per station ranged from 6 (FCG) to 14 (SRPn).

**San Pedro River**
- 12 species; longfin dace was the only species present at all stations and the most abundant overall (64% of numbers).
- Natives comprised 74% of the catch overall.
- Mosquitofish was the second most abundant species (13%).

**Gila River**
- 15 species (including Lepomis hybrids); 3 native.
- Red shiner was the most abundant species overall (36% of catch).
- Native species made up 22.4% of total catch.

**Salt River**
- 13 species, 3 native, 10 non-native.
- Desert sucker was the most abundant species (35%); largemouth bass was second (17%).
- Native fishes comprised 44.7% of the catch.

**CAP Canal**
- 12 taxa, all non-native; none native.
- Bluegill was most abundant (53% of numbers), followed by carp (22%), hybrid sunfish (8%), redear sunfish (5%), and largemouth bass (4%).

**SRP North Canal**
- 17 taxa, including undetermined Tilapia and 3 natives.
- Tilapia (redbelly plus undetermined) and red shiner were the most abundant species (57% and 15% of numbers).
- Tilapias were overwhelmingly predominant above the electrical barrier but absent below.

**SRP South Canal**
- 16 taxa, including undetermined Tilapia and 3 natives.
- Native fishes comprised 25.9% of the total catch (Table 3 value as reported).
- Non-native tilapias were the most abundant fishes overall.

**Florence-Casa Grande Canal**
- 8 species, 2 native; red shiner was the predominant species overall (75% of catch).
- Native species comprised only 1.4% of numbers.

**SRP Consolidated and Tempe canals**
- Consolidated canal: 10 species including native desert sucker; mosquitofish was the most abundant.
- Tempe canal: 5 species, including native desert sucker and Sonora sucker, both considered abundant.

**Methodological limitations**
- Sampled area was estimated visually by individual field crews and was not verified; the authors state these data cannot be considered reliable without verification.
- Electrofishing technique and efficiency varied among users, limiting comparability among samples and stations.
- Native sucker abundances in SRPn are likely gross underestimates because collectors subsampled large aggregations.

**Recommendations**
- Provide intensive, hands-on training in protocols and equipment for all personnel, plus minor protocol adjustments.
- Prepare a sketch map for each station on every visit, recording the wetted margin, macrohabitat types, substrate, flow direction, and notable features.
- Obtain keys to locked gates along the SRP canal roadways and make them available to authorized users.
- Improve communication between canal operators (SRP, SCID) and monitoring staff so sampling can coincide with scheduled outages.
- Evaluate boat-mounted electrofishing as a standard technique for SRPn and SRPs under normal flow, as it may become necessary if scheduled drawdowns are discontinued.
