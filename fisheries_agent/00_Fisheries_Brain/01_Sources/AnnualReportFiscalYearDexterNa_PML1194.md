# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center: Annual Report, Fiscal Year 1991

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Fiscal Year 1991 annual report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center (Dexter, New Mexico) documents the culture, holding, spawning and distribution of imperiled freshwater fishes of the American Southwest. Dexter was designated a Technology Center in FY91, one of only five in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The station holds 17 species under the [[Endangered Species Act]], many of them endangered or threatened. The report covers spawning results, egg and fry distribution, the damage caused by two severe storms (March and June 1991), an emergency salvage of Lost River, Shortnose and Warner suckers from Klamath Basin, and a list of future infrastructure needs.

## Background & Description

- **Establishment:** Authorized under the [[Lea Act of 1931]] (as written in the source, "White Act of 1931") to meet demand for warmwater game fish. Land was acquired by the Federal Government on August 31, 1931 for $3,265.90, and the station covers 640 acres in Chaves County.
- **Mission:** Following the 1973 Endangered Species Act, the station adopted seven objectives: maintain a viable gene pool of imperiled fishes, develop culture techniques, study biological requirements, supply fish to authorized agencies, exchange expertise, publish findings, and run a public information program.
- **Species held:** The FY91 Table 1 lists 17 species from five families (Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Ictaluridae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae). Most are from New Mexico or states contiguous to Colorado, Texas, Chihuahua and Sonora. The report notes that no other place in the world holds such an array of these fishes at a single locality.
- **Facilities:** A holding house with sixteen 360-gallon concrete tanks, a hatching battery and laboratory; four 6' x 40' raceways; ten 3' x 10' fiberglass circular tanks; and 48 earthen ponds (22 surface acres total). Waste water is collected in two sumps isolated from other surface waters.
- **Setting:** Northern fringe of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]], elevation 3,500 feet, average rainfall about 12 inches, 180–200 growing days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water supply:** Culture water comes from shallow aquifer wells (about 150 feet deep) with a combined capacity of 1,800 gpm. Water is a constant 64°F, alkaline, with total hardness averaging 2,100 mg/L and total dissolved solids 3,500 mg/L. Rights include shallow water (1,295 acre-feet/annum) and an artesian right (318 acre-feet/annum). The artesian well (1,000 feet deep) failed during the year; it had collapsed at 150 feet.
- **Regional change:** The report notes that dams and diversions have altered or destroyed many Southwest freshwater ecosystems, and that the near disappearance of native fishes was largely unintentional.
- **Co-management:** The sump area is co-managed with the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish as a Wildlife Management Area, open to seasonal hunting for dove, pheasant and waterfowl.
- **Aquatic pest control:** Persistent [[Chara]] (macroalgae) in earthen ponds prompted tests of [[Grass Carp]] and the herbicide Norosac G.
- **Parasites and disease:** Anchor worm (*Lernaea* sp.), yellow grub (*Clinostomum marginatum*), and leeches were found on Klamath Basin suckers. Woundfin suffered an "Ich" outbreak, and Guzman Beautiful Shiner remained positive for Asian tapeworm.

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker (RBS)**
- Spawning of 12 F1 domestic females produced an egg-to-swimup survival of 15.0% (total swimup fry 54,367; mean 183/gram).
- Eggs and fry were distributed to Colorado State University, NFCRC Yankton, University of Arkansas, Page Springs SFH (AZ) and Dexter production ponds, and 247 adults went to the Arizona Game and Fish Department (824 pounds).
- Due to the grow-out requirement, the stocking size was raised to 15 inches for razorbacks (about 30 months) and 8 inches for squawfish (about 40 months). No fingerling distribution is planned for at least two years.
- A March 25 dust storm (winds over 65 mph) silted fry tanks, causing an acute-to-chronic die-off of the Colorado River upper-basin fry. Air temperature then fell to 19°F.
- A June 6 thunderstorm (winds over 100 mph) destroyed 12 of 19 fiberglass tanks, and a power outage lasted about 27 hours.

**Colorado Pikeminnow (Colorado Squawfish, CSF)**
- 20 F1 domestic fish spawned May 29, 1991 with carp pituitary: 2,209,355 eggs (mean 110,467 per female), 646,220 swimup fry (29.9% egg-to-swimup survival).
- Hail on June 6 killed over 200 one-year-old fish, and a later hailstorm killed 155 young.

**Bonytail Chub (BTC)**
- Wild-origin brood spawned April 23–24 for contaminant research produced 119,000 eggs, with 30,000 sent to Yankton, SD.
- 117,418 eggs were produced June 11 for an otolith growth study (Dr. Dean Hendrickson, Texas Memorial Museum); growth was greatly reduced at 50°F but similar at 70°F and 80°F.
- Total FY91 stocking was 74,315 (including 40,940 eggs at Lake Mohave); 300 adults in raceways were lost in the June storm. Broodstock: 471 domestic F1 and 8 Lake Mohave-origin fish.

**Woundfin**
- Of 50 injected females only eight released ova (about 800 eggs), yielding 345 swimup fry. The year-end population was 250 adults and 205 young-of-the-year.

**Chihuahua Chub (CCH)**
- 19 females spawned May 8 produced 128,152 eggs (mean 6,745 per female); 10,707 swimup fry were reared for reintroduction in southwestern New Mexico.

**Pahranagat Roundtail Chub (PRC)**
- 10 of 30 injected females ovulated (3,936 ova), producing 2,629 swimup fry. Two hundred fingerlings from the pond were tagged with PIT tags, and the remaining stock is 275 adults.

**Virgin River Roundtail Chub (VRC)**
- Pond spawning produced 50,000 or more recruits. 104 adults were moved for overwintering.

**Yaqui Beautiful Shiner and Guzman Beautiful Shiner**
- 911 Yaqui beautiful shiners were collected in October 1989 from Chihuahua; 791 were transported across the border. The species is no longer held at Dexter. The Guzman Beautiful Shiner was still positive for Asian tapeworm.

**Yaqui Catfish**
- Electrophoresis of 223 fish (33 hybrids, 11 channel catfish or backcrosses, 179 apparent Yaqui catfish). Morphometric and X-ray data were compared with 51 channel catfish. Spawning trials are planned for 1992.

**Desert Pupfish, Leon Springs Pupfish and Gila Topminnow**
- 372 desert pupfish went to Arizona Game and Fish Department and 35 to the New England Aquarium. Over 10,000 Gila topminnow were moved to an overwintering pond.

**Big Bend Gambusia**
- Outdoor overwintering was poor, but indoor aquaria fish survived. About 5,000 fish were transferred, and transfer of maintenance to Uvalde NFH is proposed.

**Klamath Basin Suckers (Emergency Salvage)**
- A USFWS "jeopardy opinion" under Section 7 directed the Bureau of Reclamation to collect Lost River, Shortnose and Warner suckers from the Clear Lake, Klamath Basin area. Collection yielded 85 Shortnose, 24 Lost River and 75 Warner suckers, with no transport mortalities. Parasites were removed manually.

**Facilities, Funding and Future Needs**
- Storm damage exceeded $30,000 for repairs, replacement and cleanup. Cyclical maintenance obligations totaled $1,313,000 in FY91 (wells/water lines $938,027; buildings $50,118; equipment $3,578 in the listed categories).
- Water rights are currently considered fairly secure, but junior shallow rights dating from after the mid-1940s could be at risk. About $300,000 was available for pond lining in FY92.
- Priority needs include a Genetic Plan for all hatchery operations (based on Dr. Tony Echelle's report) and staff with genetics expertise.

**Outreach and Visitors**
- About 2,500 people visited in FY91, including 33 official dignitaries and 8 guided groups. Staff contributed a co-authored chapter in *Battle Against Extinction* and a paper on Colorado squawfish reintroduction in the lower Colorado River basin.
