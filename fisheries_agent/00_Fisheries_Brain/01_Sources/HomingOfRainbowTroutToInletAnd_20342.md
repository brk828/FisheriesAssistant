# Homing of Rainbow Trout to Inlet and Outlet Spawning Streams at Loon Lake, British Columbia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This four-year field study (1953–1956) by [[Lindsey]], [[Northcote]] and [[Hartman]] tested whether [[Rainbow Trout]] in [[Loon Lake]] return to the stream of their own origin (inlet or outlet) to spawn. Trout were trapped and marked as young and adults in both streams. Homing at first spawning was about 94%, and the authors concluded that inlet and outlet spawners are not two genetically distinct types. Instead, their behaviour appears to be shaped largely by environmental conditions, with [[Homing Behaviour]] remaining strong but imperfect.

## Background & Description

Rainbow trout can spawn in lake inlets or outlets, and in Loon Lake both occur. The study addressed two competing explanations for opposite current responses: (1) genetically distinct "inlet spawners" and "outlet spawners," which could indicate [[Sympatric Speciation]]; or (2) a single genotype whose responses are set by environmental differences between streams. The question matters for speciation theory and for fishery managers deciding which lakes to stock and how to collect eggs.

The study took place at Loon Lake, about 8 miles long, with a maximum depth of 213 feet, at an elevation of 2820 feet. Rainbow trout were the only fish species present. The main inlet drains Upper Loon Lake, and the outlet flows toward a falls about 4 miles downstream that is impassable to fish. Studied sites included the [[Inlet Stream]], [[Outlet Stream]], [[Hihium Creek]] and [[Thunder Creek]].

Methods combined several approaches:
- Two-way [[Fish Traps]] in the inlet, outlet and tributaries, with over 40,000 spawners examined in total.
- Marking of young by [[Fin Clipping]] and of adults by aluminum strap tags and Petersen disc tags.
- Recovery of marks in traps and in [[Angler Recapture]] samples.
- [[Adult Transfer Experiments]] between streams.
- [[Morphological Analysis]] of meristic characters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history and timing.** Outlet fry emerge from late May or early June to early August. Several thousand fry enter the lake between July and September, and others overwinter in the stream and enter as fingerlings the following spring or summer. Inlet fry emerge from late June to late September and move downstream soon after, with almost all entering the lake in their first year. Inlet fry output approaches 100,000 fish annually.

**Spawning runs.** Spawners are mainly 4- and 5-year-old fish, with a few age-3 males. The outlet run begins in mid-March, peaks in middle to late April, and may extend into July. The inlet run is first seen in May, with large numbers in late June and early July. The two runs overlap only partly, and most outlet spawners have left the lake before the inlet run is well under way.

**Run size.** About 5,000 fish spawn in the outlet and about 22,000 in the inlet. Estimated inlet runs were 23,248 (1954) and 19,870 (1955). Average run sizes were 21,559 for the inlet and 5,284 for the outlet. Anglers' estimates suggested about 77,696 to 94,881 catchable trout in the lake in 1954.

**Environmental drivers.** The authors hypothesised that fish enter a spawning stream once outlet temperature rises above about 5°C, usually early in April. Predation on outlet fry and fingerlings, including by mink, kingfishers and yearling trout, was noted as a possible reason outlet survival may be slightly lower.

## Key Findings

**1. Strong homing at first spawning.**
- Simplest calculation: 902 of 922 clipped first spawners (97.8%) returned to their natal stream.
- Weighted by origin: 98.8% of 882 outlet-origin recoveries and 75.0% of 40 inlet-origin recoveries homed.
- Corrected for incomplete inlet sampling, using estimated runs for 1954 and 1955: 94.0% of outlet-origin fish and 87.6% of inlet-origin fish homed.
- Whole-population estimate: homing was **93.8%** effective (Table IV). The outlet-origin figure was 94.1% and the inlet-origin figure 93.7%.

**2. Straying and genetic exchange.** Roughly 6% of all spawners are estimated to be strays each year. Because the inlet run is much larger, about a quarter of the outlet run consists of inlet-hatched fish. The recovery of 20 marked outlet-to-inlet or inlet-to-outlet strays was called direct evidence of genetic interchange. The authors concluded this rules out two genetically distinct homing types.

**3. Repeat spawners home even more strongly.** Of 3,044 marked fish that survived spawning in the outlet, 263 were recovered spawning there again, and only one switched to the inlet. This gives a homing rate of **99.6%** for repeat outlet spawners. No marked spent inlet fish were recovered spawning later.

**4. Adult transfer experiments.**
- Results depended on timing and ripeness. After the May 4 transfer, when few females were ripe, 17 of 50 transferred outlet adults returned to the outlet. Only 7 of 46 transferred inlet adults returned to the inlet.
- After the May 12 transfer, when all females were ripe, only 4 of 48 transferred outlet adults returned to the outlet, and no transferred inlet adults returned to the inlet.
- Some transferred adults spawned in the new stream and then moved toward the lake, showing that behaviour is not fixed.

**5. Mixing within the lake.** Fin-clipped inlet spawners were recovered at Outlet Trap 1 (101 fish in one marking period). Tagged adults were taken across the lake, and tagged inlet fish were caught in about equal numbers in both halves of the lake. The authors described considerable mixing rather than discrete populations.

**6. Morphological differences (1953 adults).**

The differences may be environmental rather than genetic. Temperature during development can alter meristic counts in *Salmo*.

**7. Environmental basis of current response.** Two-way transfers of fry between the inlet and outlet, reported in a separate paper, showed that young fish behave according to the stream they are placed in. Direction of movement appears to be controlled mainly by temperature and light, although some innate difference was not ruled out.

**8. Interpretation.** Homing is strong but not perfect. The authors considered odour orientation and visual or spatial cues as possible mechanisms. Odour orientation was considered less likely because fish homed equally to the inlet and to the outlet, and sun orientation was excluded because transfers used covered cans.

**9. Management implications.** Inlet and outlet populations should be treated as semi-discrete. Removing all eggs from the outlet run would reduce it to about a quarter of its size over time, and removing all inlet eggs would nearly eliminate that run. Because outlet-bound offspring are concentrated where angling is heaviest, outlet depletion would have a disproportionate effect on the fishery. This applies to other heavily populated trout lakes with inlet and outlet spawning, which have been used for [[Hatchery Egg Collection]]. See [[Fisheries Management]].

**10. Sampling caveats.** The authors stressed that incomplete sampling can give misleading homing values. Several sources of unsampled spawning were discussed, including gravel upstream of Outlet Trap 1, Thunder Creek, and possible shoal spawning in the lake. Lake spawning was judged unlikely to change the main conclusions, based on the size of the catchable population relative to the spawning runs.

**11. Broader context.** Homing has been documented in other non-anadromous salmonids, including [[Kokanee]] in Kootenay Lake (less than 3% straying) and [[Brown Trout]] in a Scottish reservoir. The authors proposed that homing balances spawner numbers against each stream's reproductive capacity, while occasional straying allows colonisation of new areas. They also defined "reproductive homing" as the return of an animal to its place of origin before reproduction.
