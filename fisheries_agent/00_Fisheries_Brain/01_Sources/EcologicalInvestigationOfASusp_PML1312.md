# Habitat Features of a Suspected Colorado Squawfish Spawning Site on the Yampa River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study, published in the *Great Basin Naturalist* (Vol. 45, No. 1, January 1985) by Vincent A. Lamarra, Marianne C. Lamarra and John G. Carter, quantifies the physical and biological environment of a suspected [[Colorado Squawfish]] spawning site at Yampa River mile 16.5. Field sampling in July 1981 (24–26 July) combined [[Seine Sampling]], [[Macroinvertebrate Drift]] nets, benthic cores, [[Periphyton]] chlorophyll *a* measurements, and fish stomach analysis. The site was dominated by loose [[Cobble Substrate]] with interstitial voids containing little or no organics, adjacent to large sandy pools. The authors conclude that coarse, well-washed substrate, abundant food, and slow adjacent flow are likely critical for preferred reproductive habitat of Colorado squawfish. Larval fish were captured only below the cobble area, and fish feeding was highest at night.

## Background & Description

The study addresses the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large endemic cyprinid of the [[Green River]] system. Spawning adults had been observed at Yampa River mile 16.5, but the timing and habitat requirements of spawning were poorly documented. Logistical problems prevented sampling in early July, when spawning was observed, so the authors compared river conditions in late July with those reported for the suspected spawning period (5 July) and found them similar. A 1981 survey near Transect 2 estimated river width at 70 m compared with 54 m measured in this survey. Both periods occurred during post-runoff conditions, with a flow reduction of 180 cfs (480 cfs to 300 cfs) between them.

Study objectives were to:
- Identify the unique physical and biological features making river mile 16.5 attractive to spawning squawfish.
- Describe trophic interactions among fish, drift insects and larval fishes.
- Compare the structure and function of this reach with other [[Yampa River]] and [[White River]] ecosystems.

Sampling used two field seining sites at mile 16.5 and two at mile 18.0 (five transects in total: three cross-river transects at mile 16.5 and two at mile 18.0), with five seine haul locations (SH1–SH4 plus a combined SH2+SH3 site). Fish were identified, measured for total length, and stomachs were removed and preserved in 70% ethanol. Stomach fullness was measured to the nearest 0.01 ml. Drift and benthic samples passed through a 0.25 mm sieve. Interstitial sediment cores (4.6 cm diameter) were separated into five size fractions (A >14.7 mm; B 14.7–4.0 mm; C 4.0–0.5 mm; D 0.5–0.25 mm; E <0.25 mm). Chlorophyll *a* was extracted in 90% acetone and measured with a [[Turner Fluorometer]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The study reach lies between steep canyon walls, with cobble-covered islands and marshy shorelines with loose organic substrates. Transects at mile 16.5 and 18.0 ranged from riffles of various sizes to run-pool habitats; runs and pools were deeper with lower velocities, while riffle transects had higher average velocities. Transect depth and velocity data are summarized in Table 1 of the source.

**Biological community.** Benthic macroinvertebrates (Ephemeroptera: *Baetis*; Plecoptera; Trichoptera: Hydropsyche) were similar to those reported in earlier Yampa studies (Bailey and Alberti 1952; Carlson et al. 1979; Annear 1980), and macroinvertebrate density matched levels reported by Annear (1980). Periphyton and macroinvertebrate biomass varied strongly among transects, with the highest chlorophyll *a* averages at Transect 2 and the lowest at Transect 4. Macroinvertebrate biomass was significantly related to water velocity (P = 0.43; p = 0.01), an association the authors interpret as indirect, because the dominant benthic functional group was fine-particulate collectors or filter feeders (87% of total biomass).

**Fish community.** Species composition within Dinosaur National Monument appears to have changed since earlier surveys. Holden and Stalnaker (1975) reported bonytail, largemouth bass, bluegill, sunfish and walleye, but these were not found in 1981, while plains killifish and sand shiner were collected. Comparisons with the 1982 Colorado River Fishery Project data showed high similarity, with two exceptions: squawfish were absent from this survey, and a possible [[Humpback Chub]] was present. The study also found higher densities of [[Redside Shiner]] than in the 1982 collections.

**Native to nonnative relationships.** Across several Colorado River tributaries, the proportion of nonnative fishes increases near the Green River confluence. Lanigan and Berry (1979) attributed this to decreased habitat diversity, while Valdez and Clemmer (1982) suggested that native-dominated habitats are unfavorable to nonnatives. Work on the White River correlated endemic fishes with coarse riffle substrates. At the Yampa site, natives to nonnatives were in a ratio of roughly 2:1, consistent with the same substrate-based pattern.

## Key Findings

### Substrate and interstitial habitat
- Yampa River mile 16.5 was dominated by [[Cobble Substrate]] with interstitial voids containing little or no organics, associated with sand-substrate pools.
- Interstitial fine sediment fractions (<0.25 mm and 0.25–0.5 mm) made up only about 4% of the river sediments sampled.
- Periphyton, macroinvertebrates and fishes were at their lowest densities on sandy substrates and at their highest on substrates smaller than boulders (excluding sand).
- The authors argue that the armored cobble of the White River prevents interstitial voids from developing, which makes the mile 16.5 cobble habitat unique among observed sites.

### Larval fish and drift
- Larval fish (9.0–13.0 mm) were captured only in drift nets below the cobble area, between 2125 and 0100 hours, with a peak at 0100–0125 hours.
- Five larval fish species were identified: [[Speckled Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and carp. This suggests the cobble habitat was used for spawning or nursery purposes by species other than squawfish.
- No larval fish and only 500 macroinvertebrates per hour entered the area, whereas five larval fish per hour and over 2000 macroinvertebrates per hour left it during the same period.
- Macroinvertebrate drift peaked between evening and 0200 hours, showing strong nocturnal biological activity.

### Feeding
- Feeding was assessed using a comparison of [[Electivity Index]] values (Strauss 1979) for the four dominant fish species.
- Roundtail chub and speckled dace had richer diets relative to available food than redside and red shiners, which appeared to eat items in proportion to their availability.
- Red shiners had significantly greater stomach fullness (62.6 ± 4.5%) than all other species except sand shiners (49.4 ± 8.9%).
- Fish captured between 1950 and 2140 hours were significantly fuller (56.2 ± 3.6%) than those caught at other times (41.7 ± 2.4%), coinciding with maximum invertebrate drift.

### Species distribution
- [[Roundtail Chub]] were the dominant endemic fish and preferred cobble substrate.
- Redside and red shiners were most abundant over cobble substrates and preferred intermediate (2.5–7.5 cm) substrates.
- [[Sand Shiner]] was the only species that showed a preference for silt substrates.
- Endemic fishes avoided sand and gravel and selected intermediate to large substrates.
- All species avoided sand.
- Fish were observed moving from the sandy-pool area into the lower sections of riffles during the period of maximum feeding intensity, and the lowest fish density was observed at SH3.

### Conclusions
- Substrate type and associated interstitial sediment size were the dominant abiotic factors.
- Nocturnal hours were the most biologically active period.
- The mile 16.5 cobble area appeared to be heavily used by several larval fish species and by actively feeding adults.
- The combination of well-washed coarse cobble, abundant drift and benthic food, and adjacent slow, uniform laminar flow may be critical for selecting preferred [[Colorado Squawfish]] reproductive sites.
- The authors also note that Haynes and Muth (1982) observed squawfish at mile 16.5 on 14 August 1982, estimated to be 25 days old, indicating another spawning site above mile 16.5.
