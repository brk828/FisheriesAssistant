# Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus): Proposed Endangered Species Listing (1990)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the 1990 *Federal Register* proposed rule (Vol. 55, No. 99, May 22, 1990) by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) to list the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. It is accompanied by the species background, a summary of threats, a finding that [[Critical Habitat]] is not determinable or prudent, the reopened comment period notice, and state and agency correspondence. The Service concluded that natural recruitment has not been documented for about 30 years, adult numbers have declined, and the species occupies less than 25% of its historic range. The rule's proposed date for comments was 23 July 1990 (60 days), with public hearing requests due 45 days after publication.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by Abbott in 1861 from a single specimen from the Colorado River. Placed in *Catostomus* by LaRivers (1962), then assigned its own genus *Xyrauchen* by Eigenmann and Kirsch (1889). Formerly known as the "humpback sucker."
- **Morphology:** Adults are identified by an abrupt, sharp-edged dorsal ridge behind the head and a large, fleshy subterminal mouth. Adults are robust, often exceeding 3 kg (6 lb) and 600 mm (24 in). Fish under 150 mm lack the dorsal keel and are hard to tell apart from other sucker young.
- **Historical range:** Once abundant across about 5,635 km (3,500 mi) of the Colorado River basin, including Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming, and Baja California Norte and Sonora, Mexico.
- **Current distribution:** About 1,200 km (750 mi) of river in the upper basin and about 322 km (200 mi) of the lower Colorado River (impounded by Hoover, Davis, and Parker dams). Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining population. Lanigan and Tyus (1989) estimated 758 to 1,138 fish in the upper Green River.
- **Diet:** Benthic feeder and plankton consumer. Stomach analyses show benthic fauna, algae, chironomids, detritus, and mud. Larvae select *Bosmina* (Cladocera) and avoid Copepoda in Lake Mohave.
- **Age and growth:** Scale-based ageing underestimated age. McCarthy and Minckley (1987) estimated 24 to 44 years for Lake Mohave fish, with 89% hatched before impoundment (the source text gives 70 fish sampled).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and early life.** In the lower basin, spawning occurs from late January through April over gravel bars swept clean of silt, often in a communal group of several males with one female. In the upper basin, Tyus (1987a) recorded ripe fish from May 3 to June 15. Water temperatures during spawning were 11.5–18 °C in the lower basin and 10.5–18 °C in the Green River. Incubation time is inversely related to temperature (for example, about 9 days at 15 °C and 3.5 days at 25 °C per Marsh 1985). Poor hatching success occurs below 15 °C.

**Habitat use.** Adults move to spawning areas and are otherwise relatively sedentary. Radio-tracked fish in the Green River moved less than 5 km over winter. They selected slow runs, slack waters, and eddies, at depths of 0.6–1.4 m and velocities of 0.03–0.33 m/s. Larval fish in Lake Mohave stayed near shore after hatching, then disappeared or moved to depths greater than 15 m. Juvenile habitat is largely unknown.

**Temperature.** Bulkley and Pimentel (1983) reported a preferred range of 22–25 °C and avoidance of temperatures below 14.7 °C and above 27.4 °C. Cold tailwater reaches below Flaming Gorge (105 km; summer averages under 15 °C) and Glen Canyon Dam (384 km; summer temperatures rarely exceed 15 °C) rarely yield captures.

**Flow alteration.** Storage reduced spring runoff by 28% in the Green River and 37% in the Colorado River during May and June. Mean annual discharge at the Green–Colorado confluence fell 18%. Peaking operations at Flaming Gorge increased daily flow fluctuations by 400% at Jensen, Utah. Low, stable flows are needed for growth and survival of young fish.

**Predation and competition.** Predators include green sunfish, warmouth, channel catfish, flathead catfish, and threadfin shad. Walleye and northern pike have become prominent in the Green River. Channel catfish and largemouth bass predated juvenile fish stocked in isolated coves. Competition with red shiner, common carp, and channel catfish is hypothesized but poorly quantified. Tilapia, smallmouth bass, and grass carp have been introduced or legalized.

**Hybridization.** Early studies reported high hybrid ratios with flannelmouth sucker (16 hybrids to 73 razorbacks, and 40 to 53, respectively; 8 hybrids among 95 razorbacks). Recent electrophoretic work on Lake Mohave fish found less than 5% flannelmouth genes, which Buth et al. (1987) considered insignificant.

**Recruitment.** No young (under 150 mm) fish from natural reproduction were recovered from Lake Mohave, and no suckers beyond 15 mm total length were recorded in some studies. Marsh and Langhorst (1988) found that larvae survived longer in predator-free backwaters. Food availability may also limit recruitment, with food densities of about 10 organisms per liter needed within 10 days of yolk absorption.

**Water chemistry and quality.** Current state water quality standards are not strict for this species. The species is adapted to the Colorado River's turbid, warm, and variable conditions, which regulators do not explicitly protect.

**Federal and state management.** The species is protected under state laws in all basin states except Wyoming (where it is extirpated) and New Mexico (where no records existed). State regulations do not address habitat destruction or species introduction. The Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Implementation Program (Recovery Program) is a cooperative effort, not a regulatory mechanism, and covers only the upper basin (excluding the San Juan River).

## Key Findings

1. **Status:** Adult numbers show a downward trend, and natural recruitment has not been documented in about 30 years. The population is mostly old adults.
2. **Range:** The species occupies less than 25% of its original range.
3. **Threats (Factors A–E):**
   - **A. Habitat loss and modification:** Dams, water depletions, flow and temperature change, and impoundment. About 15 dams have been built on the lower Colorado and its tributaries (Gila and Salt rivers). Consultations under Section 7 covered over 100 projects in the upper basin. Major transbasin diversions include the Central Utah Project (165,000 acre-feet) and the Two Forks Project (45,000 acre-feet).
   - **B. Overutilization:** Commercial harvest historically occurred, but no current markets exist. Harvest is not considered a current threat.
   - **C. Disease and predation:** No evidence that disease is a significant factor. Predation on eggs and larvae is heavy, and predation is widely hypothesized as the main cause of recruitment failure.
   - **D. Inadequacy of regulatory mechanisms:** State laws protect fish from take but not from habitat loss or introduction of nonnative species. The Recovery Program is not a regulatory mechanism.
   - **E. Other natural or manmade factors:** Nonnative fish introduction (42 species established in the upper basin per Tyus et al. 1982; 37 in the lower basin per Minckley 1979), hybridization, and a pre-impoundment poisoning project on the Green River (considered short-term).
4. **Critical habitat:** Not determinable or prudent. Designation would provide no net benefit because federal agencies already know the habitat, and Section 7 protects it.
5. **Listing rationale:** Endangered (not threatened) status is appropriate because of the greatly reduced range, the lack of successful recruitment, the partitioning of the range by dams, habitat alteration, and nonnative species.
6. **Agency comments:** The U.S. Forest Service (July 23, 1990) concurred with listing and did not propose critical habitat for National Forest lands. The Arizona Game and Fish Department (August 27, 1990) objected to exclusion from lower-basin management and urged full partnership. The Dexter National Fish Hatchery staff (January 12, 1990) supported listing but found the draft poorly written and flagged errors in citations and data. The Utah State Office draft (December 14, 1989) and Albuquerque Regional Office memo (March 15, 1990) document the internal review process.
7. **Process:** The comment period was reopened from July 27 to August 27, 1990, with a public hearing on August 14, 1990, in Farmington, New Mexico.
