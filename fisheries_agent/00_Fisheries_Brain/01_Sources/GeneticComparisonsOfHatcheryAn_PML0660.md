# Genetic Variation in Hatchery and Natural Stocks of Three Small Endangered Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] to compare the genetic structure of captive and wild stocks of three federally listed endangered desert fishes maintained at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]: the [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], the [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]], and the [[Pecos Gambusia]]. The captive populations had been held for 6-8 years, which is estimated at 12 or more generations. Hatchery stocks were founded from small numbers of wild adults and grew rapidly in outdoor ponds. The authors found no significant loss of genetic variability overall, although some rare alleles appear to have been lost. The paper concludes with practical recommendations for captive management of small, spontaneously spawning species.

## Background & Description

The three species are small, spring-dwelling fishes with highly restricted ranges. All are [[Endangered Species Act]] listed. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service estimated that about 35 southwestern fish species may require artificial propagation to prevent extinction (Rinne et al. 1986). The Dexter hatchery is the most intensive program of its kind, maintaining 14 species at the time of writing and having held 22 species over the previous 15 years.

Effective gene resource management is central to captive programs for threatened organisms, yet little was known about how captive fish populations compare genetically with the wild populations they represent. Prior comparisons were largely limited to salmonids and to two southwestern species: the [[Desert Pupfish]] (Turner 1984) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (Buth et al. 1987).

Hatchery practice at Dexter falls into two categories:
- **Spontaneously spawning species** (the three studied here), in which large numbers of individuals reproduce in ponds without intervention.
- **Artificially spawned species**, induced by hormone injection and stripping, such as the razorback sucker and the [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Earlier surveys found no striking genetic change in these long-lived, large-bodied species.

Before this study, the spontaneously spawning stocks had not been assayed genetically. The authors noted that these fish could show relatively high rates of genetic change because of their short generation times. Small spring-dwelling desert fishes typically produce about one generation per year (Constantz 1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Founding of hatchery stocks**

- **[[Comanche Springs Pupfish]]:** Natural populations occur only in a complex of interconnected springs and irrigation canals near [[Balmorhea Texas]]. About 40 adults were transplanted in November 1974 from an irrigation canal carrying water from Griffin Spring. The captive population was extirpated in 1986 because the natural populations seemed secure.
- **[[Leon Springs Pupfish]]:** Natural populations are restricted to a short section of the [[Leon Creek]] drainage near Fort Stockton. In January 1976, 80 adults were transplanted from the upper watercourse of Leon Creek. This stock is still maintained.
- **[[Pecos Gambusia]]:** Occurs naturally in four widely separated spring systems in the [[Pecos River]] drainage of Texas and New Mexico, including the Leon Creek area. In November 1974, 30-40 adults were transplanted from the lower Leon Creek watercourse. The captive population was extirpated in 1981, again because the wild populations seemed secure.

**Hatchery conditions**

Founding groups had roughly equal numbers of males and females. Each was placed in a 0.1-0.2 hectare pond. All three species spawned spontaneously and exceeded 1,000 adults within one year of arrival. Population sizes above 500 adults were then maintained consistently. The captive populations received only annual transfers to newly prepared ponds.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- Samples were collected by seine from natural and hatchery populations between 12 and 18 August 1982. The hatchery Pecos gambusia sample was taken in November 1981, when that population was extirpated.
- Sampling covered all geographically defined subdivisions of each species, such as different springs or stream sections.
- Liver, epaxial muscle, and combined eye/brain tissues were assayed by horizontal [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]], examining 21-28 presumptive gene loci per species.
- Average heterozygosity per individual was computed by Nei's (1978) method using the BIOSYS-1 program. Loci were scored as polymorphic when the most common allele frequency was below 0.95.
- Deviations from [[Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium]] were tested with chi-square goodness-of-fit tests with Levene's (1949) correction.

**Genetic results**

- **No significant loss of overall variability:** For all three species, mean alleles per locus, proportion of polymorphic loci, and mean heterozygosity per individual were similar between hatchery samples and the source-site and all natural-site samples.
- **Adh-A heterozygote deficiency:** A persistent deficiency of heterozygotes at the [[Adh-A Locus]] appeared in seven wild Comanche Springs pupfish samples and in the hatchery sample (χ² = 15.72, P < 0.001). Inbreeding did not explain it, because other loci showed no heterozygote deficiency. The authors suggested a possible [[Wahlund Effect]], with the cause otherwise unknown. There was no evidence of a null allele.
- **Hardy-Weinberg conformity in hatchery stocks:** The hatchery samples of Leon Springs pupfish and Pecos gambusia showed no statistically significant deviations from Hardy-Weinberg expectations at any locus.
- **Allele frequency differences:** Hatchery and source-site allele frequencies differed significantly at Adh-A only for Comanche Springs pupfish (χ² = 7.2, P < 0.008) and Leon Springs pupfish (χ² = 9.3, P < 0.003). The authors considered these hard to interpret because of the anomalous Adh-A pattern.
- **Rare allele loss:** Some alleles missing from hatchery samples were present at low frequency in wild samples. Sampling error due to small sample sizes probably explains some absences, but the authors concluded that some [[Rare Alleles]] were lost in the hatchery populations. Two alleles present in hatchery samples were absent from the source site but found at other sites, which sampling error probably explains.

**Discussion and management recommendations**

- Captive populations founded from few individuals and grown quickly can lose rare alleles even when overall variability remains high. Small populations are also vulnerable to [[Genetic Drift]].
- The authors warned against complacency with small species that spawn spontaneously in hatcheries and appear to need little attention.
- Minimum requirements for captive stocks include:
  1. Initiating captive stocks with hundreds of individuals from natural populations.
  2. Monitoring the genetic status and population size of captive stocks.
  3. Where possible, periodically supplementing captive stocks with genetic material from natural populations.
- Hatchery populations should be managed to maintain [[Effective Population Size]] and to limit the loss of [[Genetic Variation]] over generations.
