# The Larger Animal Parasites of the Fresh-Water Fishes of Maine

#ichthyology #marine-biology #parasitology

## Executive Summary

This 1954 monograph by [[Marvin C. Meyer]] of the University of Maine, published by the Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game, surveys the larger animal parasites (metazoans) of Maine's freshwater fishes. It was written for anglers, camp owners and students as well as parasitologists. The study rests on field examinations of 478 fish from 25 species taken in summer and early fall 1952 from about 30 locations, supplemented by four graduate student reports and more than 200 contributed hosts, giving nearly one thousand fish in total. The work covers parasite biology and transmission, harmful effects on hosts, the (unproven) risk to humans, control options, and a systematic treatment of the groups encountered, with a key to adult forms.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Anglers and camp owners had sent many inquiries about parasitized fish to the [[Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game]]. The study aimed to identify the parasites present, their distribution and effects, and to explain them to a lay audience.
- **Definition:** A parasite is an animal that obtains its living at the expense of a usually larger animal of a different species (the [[Host]]). The author stresses that parasitism is very common and that virtually no wild fish is free of parasites.
- **Scope:** Only larger metazoans are treated. [[Protozoa]], bacteria, fungi and viruses are excluded except incidentally. The one-celled *Ichthyophthirius multifilis* ("ich") is mentioned briefly.
- **Nomenclature:** The text insists on [[Binomial Nomenclature]] (Linnaeus, 1758) because common names vary regionally. Example: "smallmouth bass" is also called black bass, Oswego bass, bronzeback and redeye bass.
- **Structure:** Part One covers general biology and recommendations. Part Two covers the groups encountered: Copepoda, Pelecypoda (glochidia), Hirudinea, Acanthocephala, Trematoda, Cestoda and Nematoda, plus a literature review, methods, glossary and index.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Acquisition:** Passive entry through the mouth is typical for Acanthocephala, digenetic trematodes, cestodes and nematodes. Active contact is typical for copepods, leeches, monogenetic trematodes and cercariae.
- **Feeding and age:** Young fish feed on crustaceans and so become exposed to larval worms. The parasite load therefore "reflects clearly the manner of life led by the host".
- **Habitat:** Weedy, mud-bottomed bays are rich in parasites because they support the necessary intermediate hosts (snails, copepods, amphipods). Heavy losses of white perch with infected livers occur during spawning in shallow lakes with low water and very warm days.
- **Balance of nature:** The author argues that the parasite-host relationship is usually balanced, so epidemics rarely cause lasting harm and hosts are never entirely free of parasites. Piscivorous birds (cormorants, herons, kingfishers, pelicans) are part of the life cycles of several parasites and are protected under the [[Migratory Birds Treaty]].
- **Food chains:** Snails, copepods and other crustaceans serve as intermediate hosts and as food for young fish. Herring, sardines and smelts feed on copepods throughout life.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and prevalence**
- 478 fish from 25 species were examined in 1952. Of these, 292 were game fish, 105 pan fish and 81 rough fish, from about 30 locations.
- The most-examined species were smallmouth bass (76), landlocked salmon (74), lake trout (46), eastern brook trout (38) and chain pickerel (35).
- The author explicitly declines to report percentage prevalence. Samples were non-random, hatchery fish were selected for parasites, and comparisons across studies are unreliable.

**Effects on hosts**
- Every parasite is considered harmful, even at low numbers.
- Gyrodactylus elegans (monogenetic) caused an epidemic in lake trout at the Craig Brook hatchery in 1896, reducing young fish from 39,000 to 10,000.
- Murphy (1942) reported heavy glochidia infestations (600 to 1,200 per fish) impairing gill circulation and killing rainbow trout fingerlings in California.
- The bass tapeworm (*Proteocephalus ambloplitis*) causes matted organs and can render fish sterile. Its larvae also make the flesh unpalatable.
- *Philonema agubernaculum* causes mesenteric adhesions that can cause sterility and "egg-bound" fish in landlocked salmon and eastern brook trout.
- Yellow grub (*Clinostomum marginatum*) and black spot are considered unsightly but not lethal. Cooking destroys them.

**Human health**
- The author found no evidence that Maine's native fish parasites infect humans. Cooking or adequate freezing kills larvae.
- The "broad" tapeworm (*Dibothriocephalus latus*) was not found in Maine. Its larval forms are not encysted in local fish, so the cysts seen here cannot be that species.
- The text compares the risk to raw oysters and notes that trichina worm infects about one in six Americans, a figure cited for context.

**Life cycles**
- Most digenetic trematodes need three hosts: bird to snail to fish, then back to bird. The yellow grub has eight developmental stages. A single infected snail may release up to half a million cercariae.
- Leptorhynchoides thecatus (thorny-headed worm) needs an amphipod (*Hyalella azteca*) as first intermediate host, and bass are the normal final hosts.
- The bass tapeworm needs a copepod, then a young bass as second intermediate host, and a larger bass as final host.
- Salmincola edwardsi (fish louse) is direct and needs no intermediate host.

**Control recommendations**
- Natural control has never been practical. Hatchery pools can be treated chemically or with dips, and food can be managed.
- Stocking should avoid moving the bass tapeworm, *Philonema* and other parasites into parasite-free waters. Stocking permits might require certification that fish are free of the larval stage of the bass tapeworm.
- Research on the *Philonema* life cycle is recommended before any control measures can be assessed.
- The Canadian *Triaenophorus crassus* control programme (R. B. Miller) is described as a cautionary example. Attempts to reduce pike, coracidia and ciscoes achieved only limited results. The author compares this with the long research needed to control malaria.

**Taxonomic inventory (summary)**
- Copepoda: 4 species, including *Salmincola edwardsi* and *Ergasilus* sp.
- Hirudinea: 3 species.
- Acanthocephala: 6 species.
- Trematoda: about 20 named species across the Monogenea and Digenea.
- Cestoda: about 17 named species.
- Nematoda: about 15 named genera or species.

**Conclusions**
- Parasites are a normal part of freshwater ecosystems, and their effects are a matter of degree.
- Better knowledge of life cycles is the basis for any future management.
