# Distribution, Migratory Behavior, and Habitat Use of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey open-file report (Open-File Report 98-252) documents a sonic telemetry study of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the Colorado River that holds most of the remaining wild population of the species. Ten adults and six juveniles fitted with sonic transmitters were tracked from November 1994 to July 1997 to describe habitat use and migration. Adults showed pre- and post-spawning movements, returned to the same spawning areas, and used distinct summer ranges. Juveniles were cover-oriented and moved widely right after release. The authors argue that predation by [[Nonnative Predators]] in shallow nursery habitat is the main barrier to recruitment, and they recommend further research and a revised stocking protocol.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a long-lived catostomid, with many individuals exceeding 35 years, and is believed to be near the end of its estimated lifespan. Most earlier research concerned adults in the relic Lake Mohave population and a small population in the Green River, Utah. Juvenile records were extremely rare; only six juveniles had been collected from Lake Mohave in the previous 20 years, and these may have been captive-reared stock.

The population declined by 60 percent over the decade preceding the study (Marsh 1995). The [[Native Fish Work Group]] was formed to stock 50,000 juveniles to replace the declining population.

**Study design:**
- Adults (n = 10 tracked): captured at Yuma and Tequila coves (RK 34 and 39) in November 1994; mean total length 609 mm (555–650 mm), mean weight 2.75 kg (1.75–4.21 kg).
- Juveniles (n = 25 stocked, 5 externally tagged): averaged 339 mm and 369 g; released in groups of four at several coves and points.
- Transmitters: 73 kHz sonic tags, battery life about 14 months, detection range about 1,000 m, with body temperature reported to ±0.25 °C.
- Surgery: implanted transmitters into the body cavity under [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222) anesthesia; surgery took roughly 7–12 minutes.
- Tracking: weekly boat surveys over 71 GPS waypoints ("listening stations") spaced 1,600 m apart, with two complete surveys per week for 18 months, both day and night.
- Spawning period was defined as November through April; non-spawning as May through October.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir conditions:** Water visibility exceeded 10 m. Temperatures ranged from 12 to about 30 °C, flows from none to 3 m/s, and depths could exceed 40 m. Upper reaches (near [[Willow Beach]]) had narrow temperature ranges because of cold hypolimnetic releases from [[Hoover Dam]]. Lower reaches stratified in summer.

**Key habitat features:**
- Spawning aggregations occurred over wave-washed alluvial terraces of sand and gravel and rubble shorelines.
- The cold/warm water interface between Chalk Cliff Lights (RK 69) and Fire Mountain Lights (RK 60), where Hoover releases mix with reservoir water, supported high algal production and concentrations of fish.
- Cottonwood Basin (Nevada shoreline) and Owl Point (RK 45) were heavily used areas.
- Juveniles used shallow, vegetated coves, flooded tamarisk, and submergent vegetation.

**Predators and competitors:** Juveniles overlap with [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Red Shiner]]. The authors describe predator populations as having expanded because of channel degradation, reservoir fish populations, and sport fishery management.

## Key Findings

**Adult movement and range**
- Adult daily movement averaged 527 m/day (range 442–785 m/day). Females averaged 582 m/day and males 445 m/day.
- One adult (#348) moved more than 17 km in fewer than 24 hours.
- Average linear range of the 10 adults was about 39 km (18–72 km). Females ranged farther (average 42 km, 29–72 km) than males (average 35 km, 15–51 km).
- During the spawning peak (January 31 to March 15), females were significantly more active than males (ANOVA F = 6.79, P = 0.0314).

**Spawning**
- Adults assembled in early December between RK 26 and RK 90, with aggregations often numbering in the hundreds.
- Tagged adults spawned from mid-January to March at multiple sites.
- Migrations up to 50 km before and during spawning were recorded.
- Pre-spawning staging started as early as November and continued until late March or April, suggesting the spawning season may last up to 6 months, longer than the 4–6 weeks reported for riverine populations.
- Males remained at spawning sites, while females joined them to spawn and then left, a pattern consistent with mixed paternity and genetic diversity.
- Nine of ten sonic-tagged adults used more than one spawning area, and the authors could not confirm that spawning occurred at every site.

**Post-spawning and summer use**
- Post-spawning movements were as frequent as pre-spawning migrations.
- About 60 percent of the fish migrated upreservoir after spawning to the mixing zone between RK 69 and RK 50.
- Some adults showed fidelity to particular summer areas, suggesting home ranges.

**Juveniles**
- Five young-of-year (YOY) juveniles with external 45-day transmitters moved more than 1,000 m/day in the first 10 days after release (range 1,193–3,750 m/day).
- After the second week, they moved along shorelines into protected coves.
- Of the internally implanted juveniles, only one (#2273) retained its transmitter and remained active for 20 months. In November 1995 it traveled at least 145 km, came within 3 km of [[Davis Dam]], and then moved upstream to within 8 km of Hoover Dam.
- Of 20 surgically implanted juveniles, only one remained active after 14 months.

**Habitat and depth**
- Fish were usually within 50 m of shore (fidelity significant, P < 0.001) and typically at depths of 3.1–16.8 m (based on 147 vertical temperature profiles, May–November 1995).
- Adults preferred 18–22 °C in summer and seldom occupied water above 25 °C. Body temperatures ranged from 12 to 29 °C overall.
- No correlation was found between depth and time of day.
- Adults seldom entered deep canyon habitat near Davis Dam.

**Transmitter retention**
- About 50 percent of adults remained active 14 months after release.
- Six adults remained active for up to 48 months.

**Management recommendations**
1. Study the migratory behavior of subadults and assess their risk of entrainment, passage, and stranding at diversions.
2. Examine predator/prey relationships in early life stages and identify habitat conditions that support recruitment.
3. Develop a lower-basin stocking protocol: stock fish larger than 40 cm during winter, when predators are less active.
4. Assess whether reservoir fluctuations produce shallow vegetated habitat that depresses predator densities.

**Stocking outcome:** Preliminary data indicated about 57 percent of stocked fish over 20 cm survived (Marsh 1997). The report notes that predation is the main factor limiting natural recruitment.

**Mueller et al. cover letter (July 14, 1998):** Accompanied the report and a second report on post-stocking dispersal of 55 juvenile razorbacks in Lake Mohave and Lake Powell. In that second study, most fish were detected in shallow, terminal cove and wash habitats, which made acoustic detection difficult.
