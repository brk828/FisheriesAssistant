# Decline of the Native Fish Fauna of the Sierra Nevada Foothills, Central California

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Moyle and Nichols (1974) documents a comprehensive survey of fish fauna in Sierra Nevada foothill streams draining into the San Joaquin Valley, California. Sampling at 167 localities (1969-1971) yielded 24 species (12 native, 12 introduced). Comparison with historical records (pre-1900, primarily [[Rutter 1908]]) reveals a dramatic contraction in the ranges of native species—particularly [[California Roach]], [[Hardhead]], [[Sacramento Squawfish]], and [[Sacramento Sucker]]—coincident with the expansion of introduced species and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Healthy native fish assemblages now persist only in a narrow, comparatively undisturbed middle-elevation band of foothill streams. The authors warn that isolation of remaining native populations by degraded lowland stream stretches (dominated by introduced fishes) increases vulnerability to local extinction, especially during drought, and argue that non-endangered native fish faunas still warrant conservation attention and inclusion in environmental impact assessments.

## Background & Description

This paper is primarily a distributional/ecological survey rather than a morphological study; morphological notes are limited to identification-relevant traits:

- **[[California Roach]]** (*Hesperoleucus symmetricus*): Small-bodied [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]]; originally described by C. Girard in 1854 from specimens at Fort Miller, San Joaquin River (now submerged under Millerton Lake).
- **[[Hardhead]]** (*Mylopharodon conocephalus*): Larger cyprinid; historically patchy distribution even prior to disturbance (Reeves, 1964).
- **[[Sacramento Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus grandis*): Large-bodied piscivorous cyprinid, widely distributed but now uncommon at low elevations.
- **[[Sacramento Sucker]]** (*Catostomus occidentalis*): Family [[Catostomidae]]; juveniles dominate foothill stream catches, suggesting these streams function as nursery habitat while adults inhabit larger rivers/reservoirs.
- **Burrowing taxa** — [[Lampetra|lampreys]] (*Lampetra* spp.), [[Sculpins|sculpins]] (*Cottus* spp.), and possibly [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) are noted to burrow into substrate, likely leading to undersampling by seine methods.
- **Chinook Salmon** (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*): Juveniles collected were 10.5–11 cm TL (April 1970, Mill Creek).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Sampling & Methods
- 6 mm mesh seines (1 m deep, 3–9 m length) used across 167 sites.
- Environmental variables recorded per site (see [[Moyle and Nichols 1973]]).
- Visual snorkel surveys validated relative seine catch abundances.

### Species Associations (four recognized habitat-based assemblages)
1. **[[Introduced Fishes Association]]** — Low-elevation, disturbed waters; variable composition typically including ≥2 [[Centrarchidae|centrarchid]] species, [[Mosquitofish]], and catfish/cyprinid species.
2. **[[Native Cyprinid-Catostomid Association]]** — Larger streams at intermediate altitudes; dominated by [[Sacramento Squawfish]], [[Hardhead]], and [[Sacramento Sucker]]; confined to narrow altitudinal band.
3. **[[California Roach Association]]** — Small intermediate-elevation tributaries; dominated numerically by [[California Roach]]; notably absent from upper San Joaquin and Fresno river systems where roach have disappeared.
4. **[[Rainbow Trout Association]]** — High-altitude streams; artificially maintained via stocking/poisoning programs even in lower-elevation streams that would otherwise support the Native Cyprinid-Catostomid Association.

### Competitive Interactions
- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (introduced) occupies habitat ecologically similar to [[California Roach]] and appears to have competitively displaced roach in the upper San Joaquin and Fresno River systems — a documented case of introduced-species replacement rather than pure habitat degradation, since roach persist even in disturbed habitats (e.g., sewage outfalls) in the absence of green sunfish.
- **[[Mosquitofish]]** (introduced for vector control) associated with human-disturbed stream sections alongside other introduced fishes.

### Anadromous/Migratory Species
- **[[Chinook Salmon]]**: Historically ran up Kings and San Joaquin Rivers into foothills in large numbers pre-[[Friant Dam]] (built 1946); now only ascend during exceptionally high runoff years.
- **[[Pacific Lamprey]]** (*Lampetra tridentata*): Anadromous; observed spawning below Friant Dam (1969); ammocoetes collected downstream near Fresno.

### Case Study: San Joaquin River at Friant (Temporal Fish Community Shift)
Comparative collections (1898 Rutter → 1934 Needham & Hansen → 1940s Dill → 1970 Moyle & Nichols) document:
- 1898: Transitional Valley-floor/foothill fauna, predominantly native.
- 1934: Introduced species established alongside natives.
- 1940s: Increased introduced species richness; natives still largely present.
- 1970 (post-[[Friant Dam]], completed 1942): Fauna immediately below dam dominated by planted [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Prickly Sculpin]], [[Threespine Stickleback]], and [[Mosquitofish]]; further downstream dominated by [[Carp]], centrarchids, and catfishes — near-total native species replacement.

## Key Findings

- **24 total species** collected (12 native / 12 introduced) across 167 sites in Fresno, Tulare, Madera, Mariposa, Tuolumne, Stanislaus, and Calaveras counties.
- **Range contraction in natives**: [[California Roach]], [[Hardhead]], [[Sacramento Squawfish]], and [[Sacramento Sucker]] show substantial pre-1900 vs. 1969-1970 range reductions, especially at lower elevations.
- **Range expansion in introduced species and Rainbow Trout**, driven by stocking programs and habitat alteration favoring warm-water tolerant or game species.
- **Six native species documented historically by Rutter (1908) were not recollected**: [[Sacramento Blackfish]], [[Splittail]], [[Thicktail Chub]] (now extinct/very rare), [[Tule Perch]], [[Sacramento Perch]] (all primarily lowland forms), and [[Speckled Dace]] (historically rare in this system).
- **Habitat compression**: Healthy native fish populations now restricted to a narrow middle-elevation band of relatively undisturbed foothill stream sections.
- **Isolation risk**: Native populations in different stream systems are now isolated from one another by degraded lowland reaches dominated by introduced fishes, increasing vulnerability to local extinction during drought with no recolonization pathway.
- **Human-driven habitat change** (impoundment, reduced streamflow, siltation) is identified as the primary driver differentiating Introduced Fishes Association habitat from Native Cyprinid-Catostomid Association habitat.
- **Broader Sacramento-San Joaquin context**: Native fish fauna of this drainage is 75% endemic ([[Miller 1958]]); Valley-floor native assemblages already largely replaced by introduced fishes, as evidenced in Indian midden fish-remain studies ([[Schulz and Simons 1973]]).
- **Conservation argument**: Non-endangered native fish assemblages still merit protection and should be incorporated into environmental impact assessments, as cumulative natural stressors (drought, high temperatures 30-35°C) combined with anthropogenic alteration can destroy unique native fish associations even without formal endangered status.
