# Ecological Value of Floodplain Habitats to Razorback Suckers in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report (Wydoski and Wick, 1998), prepared for the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Program]], reviews the ecological value of [[Floodplain Habitats]] for recovering the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and anticipates how endangered, native and nonnative fishes will respond to floodplain enhancement or restoration. It is intended as a reference for habitat projects under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]].

The authors conclude that razorback sucker recruitment in the Upper Basin is limited mainly by high mortality during the larval "[[Critical Period]]", driven by starvation in regulated, low-productivity river channels and backwaters and by predation and competition from [[Nonnative Fishes]]. Reconnecting floodplains is presented as the key habitat strategy, combined with streamflow management, nonnative fish control and captive propagation, all implemented concurrently under [[Adaptive Management]].

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker, along with the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]], is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The Colorado River system has a highly endemic fauna: 64% of native species are endemic (Miller 1961). Nonnative species now make up 76% (42 of 55 species) of the Upper Basin fish community (Tyus et al. 1982).

Floodplain habitats are described as the most productive zones in large river-floodplain systems, supporting the [[Flood Pulse Concept]] that links inundation to secondary production. The report reviews zooplankton and benthic invertebrate densities across habitats, larval feeding and starvation, predation and competition, spawning use of floodplains, and streamflow regulation effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and fragmentation:** Dams such as [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (1963) and the Aspinall Unit (1978) reduced historic spring peaks, enabling levee construction. Many floodplain areas are now disconnected, and the [[River Continuum Concept]] energy transfer has been disrupted.
- **Turbidity and productivity:** High sediment loads in Upper Basin rivers limit light penetration. Backwaters, wetlands and floodplain depressions are warmer and clearer, favouring phytoplankton and zooplankton production.
- **Floodplain inventory (Irving and Burdick 1995):** 135 sites in the Green River Subbasin (about 7,458 ha potential area, 132 Green River sites) and 158 sites in the Colorado River Subbasin (Colorado River 110 sites, about 4,948 ha; Gunnison River 48 sites, about 1,305 ha). Floodplain terraces dominated the Green River sites (75% of 99 classified).
- **Nonnative dominance:** Red shiners and fathead minnows made up 90.4% of 149,489 fish collected from primary backwaters between 1986 and 1994 during the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]].
- **Key sites:** [[Razorback Bar]] on the middle Green River (estimated 500 adults, with about half of captured adults marked in 1998, suggesting decline), [[Old Charley Wash]] floodplain wetland (147 ha), and [[Intersection Wash]] backwater.

## Key Findings

**Recruitment bottleneck**
- Razorback larvae 9–11 mm TL at swimup; most captured larvae were 11–12 mm TL with a mean age of 12–17 days. Only about 20% of larvae from the Green River exceeded 12 mm TL (largest 20 and 24 mm TL; oldest 34 days).
- In [[Lake Mohave]], starvation mortality was estimated at 23–78% (1992–1995), and remaining larvae were largely consumed by nonnative fishes, with no natural recruitment recorded.

**Food requirements**
- The critical period requires about 30–60 food organisms per fish per day (Papoulias and Minckley). Irreversible starvation occurred between 19 and 22–23 days in laboratory work at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Survival was 80–90% at ≥58 organisms per fish per day; best growth required ≥527 per fish per day over 50 days.
- In earthen ponds, survival over 8 weeks was 67.4–89.8% at 12.5–43.3 zooplankton per liter, with growth increasing with density.

**Habitat productivity**
- Mean cladoceran plus copepod densities (Table 1, Upper Basin): main channel up to 1.3/L, backwaters up to 13.1/L, floodplain habitats up to 81.5/L.
- Chironomid densities (Table 4): main channel up to 4,150/m², backwaters up to 31,125/m², floodplains up to 23,055/m².
- Zooplankton in main channels never reached critical-period levels; only Intersection Wash and Millard Canyon backwaters did.

**Predation and competition**
- Red shiners and fathead minnows dominate backwaters; fathead minnows attack catostomid larvae as a school and tear prey apart.
- Adult red shiners (36.1 mm TL) consumed all 100 razorback larvae in a cage experiment (Modde and Wick 1997).
- Nonnative fishes colonised a reconnected gravel-pit pond within four months and reproduced within eight months.

**Streamflow and floodplain inundation**
- Overbank flooding at the Ouray refuge required 481–575 m³/s (17,000–20,300 cfs); a 566 m³/s flow occurred 17 times in 47 years before, but 7 times in 29 years after Flaming Gorge Dam.
- Excavating higher terraces or breaching levees is proposed where flows cannot be restored.

**Growth and stocking**
- Razorbacks grew from 55 to 307 mm TL in [[Humphrey's Pond]] (1987) and from 44 to 255 mm TL at [[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]] (1993).
- Authors recommend rearing for two growing seasons and stocking fish of about 300 mm TL (~12 in) or larger. Experimental stocking of 100 mm fish has not succeeded; seven recaptures came from 1,068 larger fish (209–308 mm) stocked in 1996.

**Recommendations (summary)**
1. Continue acquisition and reconnection of floodplains; preserve functional floodplains.
2. Pursue levee removal and terrace excavation; use depression ponds for rearing.
3. Manage flows (timing and magnitude) for spawning at Razorback Bar and for inundation during the critical period.
4. Apply nonnative control mainly in reaches adjacent to reconnected floodplains; total control is considered impractical.
5. Integrate streamflow, habitat, nonnative control and captive propagation under adaptive management.
6. Recruitment every 5–10 years may sustain the long-lived species (up to 44 years or more), though annual recruitment is preferred.
