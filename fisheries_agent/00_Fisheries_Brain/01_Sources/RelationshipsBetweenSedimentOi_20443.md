# Oil Spill Effects on Benthic Macroinvertebrates in Plum Creek, Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1974 study by Hoehn, Stauffer, Masnik, and Hocutt (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University) assessed how an accidental [[Number 2 Fuel Oil]] spill in May 1973 affected [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] in Plum Creek, a small third-order stream in the [[Tennessee-Clinch River System]]. The authors correlated [[Hexane-Extractable Residues]] in sediments with macroinvertebrate abundance and [[Diversity Index]] values at downstream stations. They concluded that the spill acted mainly as a toxic agent, through water-soluble oil fractions, rather than by making sediments unsuitable for colonization. The paper also presents a sediment oil-analysis method offered as a candidate for future standardization.

## Background & Description

- **Event:** Several thousand liters of Number 2 fuel oil were released when a tank truck overturned, with the spill occurring from the tanker portion.
- **Initial response:** Within hours, straw floating booms were placed to contain oil, an absorbent was spread on the surface, and a straw dam was built downstream to capture oil that escaped upstream containment.
- **Immediate observations:** A few dead fish, crayfish, and caddisflies were seen after the accident, but no extensive biotic damage was initially detected.
- **Motivation:** The authors noted that chemical data on sediments are rarely collected in benthic pollution studies, and that no standard method existed for quantifying oil residues in benthic substrates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream setting:** Plum Creek is a small, third-order tributary within the Tennessee-Clinch River System in southwestern Virginia.
- **Sediments:** Pool sediments were finely divided and clay-rich. No scouring rains occurred between the spill and sediment collection.
- **Confounding source:** Station 1, the upstream control, had low macroinvertebrate numbers attributed to contamination from cow pasture drainage. Its hexane-extractable residue was higher than most stations, which the authors attributed to humus-derived organics rather than oil.
- **Downstream barrier effect:** The straw dam was never removed, and it became the locus of the highest sediment residue, with oil apparently settling behind it on water-soaked straw and suspended material.
- **Receiving waters:** Observations by the authors found no evidence of damage to the Clinch River.

## Key Findings

- **Study design:** Six stations were established three days after the spill (two upstream, four downstream; Station 1 served as the upstream control). Sampling occurred at first visit and at roughly 2, 6, and 18 weeks post-spill.
- **Sampling method:** Three one-minute D-frame kick samples were taken per site in pool and riffle areas. Organisms were preserved in 40% isopropanol, and taxa and specimen counts were recorded by habitat preference (surface, mid-water, bottom, sub-bottom).
- **Sediment analysis:** Thirty grams of sediment were extracted with hexane in a Soxhlet extractor for 48 hours. Residues were expressed as weight per gram of sediment, with the mean of five control extractions subtracted.
- **Main result (second sampling date):** Invertebrate numbers fell at Stations 3 and 4 while diversity remained essentially unchanged. The authors interpreted this as all taxa being affected roughly equally, not selectively.
- **Recovery trend:** Populations rose at Station 5, indicating recovery. At Station 6, below the straw dam, numbers dropped sharply again, coinciding with the highest hexane-extractable residue.
- **Long-term recovery:** By about 18 weeks, downstream sites were as populous as control stations.
- **Mechanism conclusion:** Toxicity of water-soluble oil components was judged the most likely cause. Sediment oil did not rise progressively downstream or with declining sub-bottom populations, as a sediment-unsuitability effect would predict.
- **Method caveat:** The hexane method was not fully developed. Distinguishing oil from humus-derived organics relied on qualitative traits (color, consistency, odor), and the authors called for further study of whether hexane is the best solvent.
- **Literature gap:** Earlier freshwater oil-spill studies (e.g., [[Bury 1972]]) did not measure oil concentrations in sediments or water, limiting comparison.
- **Conclusion:** The sediment method is proposed as a tool for immediate interpretation of biological data and for monitoring when benthic recolonization becomes possible, pending a standard protocol.
