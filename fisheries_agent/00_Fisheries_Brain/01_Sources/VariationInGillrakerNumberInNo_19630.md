# Variation in Gillraker Number in North American Kokanee, *Oncorhynchus nerka*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 study by Joseph S. Nelson (University of British Columbia, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, 25(2): 415–420) compares gillraker counts among freshwater [[Kokanee]] populations and their anadromous progenitor, the [[Sockeye Salmon]]. Gillrakers were counted on the first four gill arches of kokanee from 19 localities and sockeye from 3 localities. The study finds significant between-population variation in anterior gillraker number, stability across year-classes and decades, and notable differences between sympatric kokanee and sockeye in Takla Lake. The author concludes that the degree to which these differences reflect genetic divergence remains unknown.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** Kokanee are the freshwater form of *Oncorhynchus nerka*, thought to have been independently derived from sockeye salmon in most localities across their range (Ricker, 1940; Nelson, 1968).
- **Life forms:** Kokanee may coexist in sympatry with sockeye or occur in allopatry, where sockeye are absent.
- **Prior work:** [[Vernon 1957]] reported significant differences in vertebrae, scale rows and gillrakers among kokanee within [[Kootenay Lake]], but no detailed study had compared kokanee across different lakes.
- **Meristic characters:** Gillraker number is a meristic trait; numerous studies indicate both genetic and phenotypic bases for such characters (Barlow, 1961).

**Methods:**
- The first left gill arch was removed and gillrakers were counted under a binocular microscope, counting all projections in a near-lineal series.
- Second, third and fourth arches were counted in seven kokanee and three sockeye populations.
- Prior work (Nelson, MS, 1960) and the present data showed no obvious association of first-arch anterior gillraker number with specimen size, sex (over the size range examined), or latitude, longitude or altitude.
- Sockeye were collected in 1964; kokanee collections span several years.

## Key Findings

1. **Between-population variation:** Considerable variation in anterior gillraker number occurred on the first four arches among kokanee from different lakes, with many statistically significant differences. Populations in close proximity also differed, for example Christina vs. Moyie, Hustan vs. Bonanza, Horsefly vs. Shuswap, and Pinchi and Sand vs. Babine.
2. **Geographic trend in Takla region:** Except for posterior first-arch counts, there was a marked difference between the Takla and Francois populations and generally a decrease in gillraker number from Takla through Trembleur to Stuart.
3. **Sympatric vs. sockeye differences:** In all counts except posterior first-arch rakers, Takla Lake kokanee from Narrows Creek had higher gillraker numbers than sockeye from Gluske Creek. The difference in first-arch anterior means was 3.6, more than twice the greatest difference (1.65) reported by Vernon (1957) among three Kootenay Lake kokanee races.
4. **Stuart Lake:** Differences between kokanee and sockeye from the same river system were limited, with only posterior first- and second-arch counts differing significantly.
5. **Anderson Lake:** Kokanee had significantly fewer anterior first, anterior fourth, posterior first and posterior second arch gillrakers than sockeye.
6. **Temporal stability:** Gillraker numbers in seven year-classes of Nicola Lake kokanee differed only slightly, despite a marked increase in spawner size and decrease in spawner age over seven years (Lorz and Northcote, 1965). Unpublished data for Narrows Creek, Takla Lake (raker mean 38.2, range 36–43; 1940) agreed closely with 1962 collections.
7. **Sympatry and allopatry:** The first-arch means of 8 allopatric and 11 sympatric kokanee populations fell within the range of sockeye means reported by Fukuhara et al. (1962), Rounsefell (1962) and IPSFC unpublished data. No evidence indicated that allopatric kokanee differ more from sockeye than sympatric kokanee.
8. **Plasticity:** McCart and Anderson (1967) showed that kokanee reared in an artificial environment had significantly fewer gillrakers than their parents, indicating environmental influence.
9. **Open questions:** The degree to which gillraker differences reflect genetic divergence is unknown, and populations have not been reared in a common environment to test whether differences persist.
