# Introduction of the Opossum Shrimp (Mysis relicta Loven) into California and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mysidae #species-introduction #lake-trout-forage

## Executive Summary

Between 1963 and 1964, California and Nevada fisheries agencies introduced approximately 182,300 live [[Mysis relicta]] (opossum shrimp) into four western lakes: [[Lake Tahoe]], [[Lower Echo Lake]], [[Huntington Lake]], and [[Blue Lake]]. The primary objective was to establish a forage base for juvenile [[Lake Trout]] (Salvelinus namaycush) in [[Lake Tahoe]], where studies had revealed inadequate invertebrate prey. Source populations were collected from [[Upper Waterton Lake]] in Alberta, Canada, and [[Green Lake]] in Wisconsin. The introduction was supported by Dingell-Johnson Projects California F-21-R and Nevada F-15-R under Federal Aid to Fish Restoration funds.

## Background & Description

[[Mysis relicta]] is one of four freshwater North American species in the order Mysidacea. This crustacean inhabits cold, deep lakes in northeastern United States, Canada, and northern Europe, and also exists in certain eutrophic European lakes. Adults reach approximately 80 mm in length with a life span of approximately 2 years. Females carry about 40 eggs per clutch in a brood pouch during development. Young remain in the brood pouch for 1 to 3 months until reaching about 4 mm length. Sexual maturity is reached at 1 or 2 years of age.

Reproduction typically occurs from October through May, though in some lakes it may occur year-round. The species feeds on zooplankton, phytoplankton, and detritus. [[Mysis relicta]] exhibits strong negative phototaxis, remaining near the bottom during daylight hours and migrating to surface waters at sunset, descending again at dawn. Small mysids stay farther off the bottom than large individuals. Distance above bottom increases with depth (1.8 meters at depths less than 91 meters compared to 7 meters at 152 meters depth). The species has been found at all depths in [[Great Slave Lake]] (maximum 614 meters), including depths less than 10 meters where low light intensity prevails.

Maximum temperature tolerance is listed as 14°C, with minimum oxygen concentration of 4 cc per liter, though collections have been made in both warmer waters and lower dissolved oxygen conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Studies by Larkin (1948), Hacker (1956), and Rawson (1961) demonstrated that [[Mysis relicta]] is critically important in the diet of juvenile [[Lake Trout]] and various coregonids, which serve as chief forage fishes for adult [[Lake Trout]]. In one Wisconsin lake, when M. relicta was especially abundant, survival of young stocked [[Lake Trout]] increased threefold. 

[[Lake Tahoe]] lacked a similar invertebrate forage species, with juvenile [[Lake Trout]] diet consisting largely of cladocerans. The need for enhanced forage was identified through Dingell-Johnson Project studies (California F-21-R and Nevada F-15-R, "Lake Tahoe Fisheries Study"). Secondary introductions to [[Lower Echo Lake]], [[Huntington Lake]], and [[Blue Lake]] were made to increase establishment chances locally and test trout-forage potential in coldwater fluctuating reservoirs.

Distribution patterns described by Beeton (1960) for [[Lake Huron]] and [[Lake Michigan]] and by Larkin (1948) for [[Great Slave Lake]] informed collection and release strategies. Light penetration initiates and controls vertical migrations, though thermal conditions (absolute or gradient values) may cause modifications.

## Key Findings

**Collection and Transportation Methods:**
- Opossum shrimp were collected using a tow net measuring 5 × 1.5 × 12 feet attached to tubular steel frame with runners maintaining 6 inches off bottom
- Net constructed with 4 meshes per inch bobbinet for body and 16 meshes per inch for cod end
- Collections made at depths from 40 to 140 feet
- Optimal tow duration was 5 minutes; longer tows accumulated excessive mud causing suffocation
- Up to 5,000 opossum shrimp captured per tow

**Stocking Summary:**
- Total introduced: approximately 182,300 live individuals
- [[Lake Tahoe]]: ~165,300 (1963-1964) at depths 45-1,200 feet across multiple sites (Emerald Bay, Bijou, Upper Truckee River, Cascade Creek, Rubicon Point, Homewood, Dollar Point, Incline Creek, Logan Shoals, Cave Rock)
- [[Lower Echo Lake]]: 15,000 (1963)
- [[Blue Lake]]: 2,000 (1964)
- [[Huntington Lake]]: 20,000 from [[Green Lake]], Wisconsin (November 1963)

**Survival and Acclimation:**
- Initial survival tests showed mortality after 48 hours when transferred directly to [[Lake Tahoe]] water
- 85% survival achieved using 50:50 mixture of Waterton and Tahoe water for 30-minute tempering period before release
- One specimen survived 6 months in aquarium with [[Lake Tahoe]] water at 42°F under subdued light with aeration
- Holding densities up to 1,000 per gallon proved successful at Waterton Park Hatchery

**Transportation Results:**
- Air shipments from Cutbank, Montana to [[Lake Tahoe]]: 10-hour trips (5-6 hours air transit)
- Two container types tested: 5-gallon aluminum milk cans (3,000 shrimp, iced and aerated) and 5-gallon plastic cubitainers (1,000 shrimp, half-filled with water and ice, oxygen-replaced, sealed)
- Wisconsin shipment: 30-hour trip resulted in ~10% mortality
- Ground transport test (1964): 36-hour truck transport with varying densities
  - 1,000 per container: ~10% mortality
  - 2,000-2,500 per container: ~20% mortality
- Temperature maintenance critical: 39°F initial, maintained below 45°F with styrofoam lining and fiberglass insulation
- Mid-trip re-icing and re-oxygenation necessary for extended ground transport
