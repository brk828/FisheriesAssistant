# Site-Specific Aquatic Water Quality Criteria Guidelines

#aquatic-biology #water-quality #environmental-regulation

## Executive Summary

This EPA Project Summary (EPA-600/S3-84-099, December 1984) announces the [[ORD Guidelines]] for deriving [[Site-Specific Water Quality Criteria]] by modifying [[National Water Quality Criteria]]. The guidelines were produced by the Office of Research and Development (ORD) and authored by Anthony R. Carlson, William A. Brungs, Gary A. Chapman, and David J. Hansen. Their purpose is to link regulatory decisions for priority water bodies to each water body's capacity to receive wastewater discharges while maintaining acceptable water quality. The document describes three procedures for tailoring national criteria to local conditions, ranked by cost and resource demand. It is a summary of a fuller report, not a research study with new data.

## Background & Description

The document is a technical summary and publication notice. It covers the following:

- **Regulatory basis:** [[Clean Water Act Section 304]] directs EPA to publish and periodically review water quality criteria protecting public health and welfare, aquatic life, and recreation. Criteria draw on data from aquatic plants and animals across trophic levels and are used by states to set ambient water quality standards.
- **Purpose of modification:** National criteria serve as benchmarks for protecting the biological integrity of all water bodies, but they may need adjustment for specific sites. The new protocols account for site-specific variation in species composition, physical factors, and chemical water quality variables.
- **Enforceable outputs:** States can use site-specific and national criteria to develop water quality standards, mixing zone guidance, and water quality-based effluent limits. These also consider water body use, the environmental and analytical behavior of pollutants, the accuracy of extrapolating laboratory results to field conditions, and how well the tested species represent the protected water body.
- **Implementation steps:** Site selection, data collection and evaluation, biological and water quality sampling, toxicity testing, and generation of site-specific criteria.
- **Publication details:** The full report is available from the [[National Technical Information Service]] (Order No. PB 85-121 101 as printed in the OCR text; cost $8.50, subject to change). Contact is through the [[Environmental Research Laboratory]] in Duluth, MN.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guidelines address aquatic life broadly rather than any single taxon. Recommended toxicity test methods cover [[Fishes]], [[Aquatic Invertebrates]], and aquatic plants. The approach treats differences between water bodies as ecologically significant: species sensitivity to a chemical, the biological availability and toxicity of pollutants, and the physical and chemical characteristics of the receiving water all affect the appropriate criterion. The document does not report species lists, field sites, sample sizes, or measured water chemistry values.

## Key Findings

The document presents three procedures in the ORD Guidelines for developing site-specific criteria:

1. **[[Recalculation Procedure]]:** Adjusts for differences in the sensitivity of resident species to a chemical. It is the least labor-intensive and least costly option, and may require no additional toxicity tests.
2. **[[Indicator Species Procedure]]:** Uses resident or non-resident indicator species to account for differences in the biological availability and/or toxicity of a pollutant. These differences result from the physical conditions and chemical characteristics of the receiving water.
3. **[[Resident Species Procedure]]:** Accounts for differences in both species sensitivity and ambient water quality. It is the most resource-intensive and costly option, potentially requiring many acute and chronic toxicity tests with resident species in site water.

The guidelines also provide a recommended list of state-of-the-art toxicity test methods for aquatic organisms, including fishes, invertebrates, and plants. The main conclusion is that criteria tailored to local aquatic life and water uses, rather than applied uniformly, give states a consistent and cost-effective basis for water quality standards and discharge limits.
