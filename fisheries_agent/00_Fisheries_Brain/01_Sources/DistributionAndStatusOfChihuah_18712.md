# Distribution and Status of Chihuahua Chub (*Gila nigrescens*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Propst and Stefferud (New Mexico Department of Game and Fish; USDA Forest Service) report the results of surveys conducted from 1987 to 1991 on the [[Gila Nigrescens]], or [[Chihuahua Chub]], across its historic range in the [[Mimbres River]] (New Mexico, USA) and the [[Guzman Basin]] and [[Laguna Bustillos Basin]] (Chihuahua, Mexico). The species has declined sharply in range and abundance over the past century and now survives mainly in remote, relatively unmodified reaches. The authors identify high habitat specificity, combined with widespread habitat destruction, as the most likely primary cause of decline, with non-native fishes (particularly potential predators) as a major secondary factor. The authors conclude that the species' future is "bleak."

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila nigrescens* (Girard), a cyprinid native to the Mimbres River and the Guzman and Laguna Bustillos basins.
- **Size:** Most populations consisted of individuals under 100 mm standard length. Males were smaller than females (males 106–155 mm; females 173–285 mm, based on the few sexed specimens).
- **Taxonomy:** The name history is complex. [[Baird and Girard 1854]] described the Mimbres form as *Gila pulchella*, but that name was a *nomen nudum* and became a secondary homonym. [[Girard 1856]] described Rio Janos specimens as *Tigoma nigrescens*. Confusion arose when a Rio Grande chub was treated as *G. nigrescens*, until [[Miller and Hubbs 1962]] separated it as [[Gila Pandora]]. The Laguna Bustillos and Guzman forms may be distinguishable, and the Mimbres population may be separable as well, so taxonomic status requires clarification.
- **Historical records:** First collected in the Mimbres River in 1851, not found there again until 1975. In Chihuahua, first collected in the Casas Grandes drainage in 1854.
- **Legal status:** State Endangered, Group 1 ([[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]], 1976) and Federal Threatened ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], 1983).
- **Manuscript context:** The paper was drafted in 1993 and was to be submitted to the journal *SWAN* after review by W. L. Minckley.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling.** The Mimbres Drainage was sampled thoroughly from 1987 through 1991, excluding headwaters with only nonnative salmonids. Chihuahua field work took place in March 1990, focusing on sites with a good probability of finding the chub. Sites were selected from literature, museum records, local interviews and expert advice. Some historic sites were not sampled because they were dry, severely modified, or unlikely to support the species. Sampling used backpack [[Electrofishing]] (12- or 24-V DC) with dipnets, and seines (4.6 × 1.8 m, 0.5 cm mesh). Collection permits in Chihuahua did not allow retention of voucher specimens, and all fish were returned alive.

**Habitat associations.** Chihuahua chub was habitat specific and most often occupied [[Lateral Scour Pools]] where flow pressed against a bank or a partial obstruction such as a boulder, cliff or [[Root Mass Habitat]]. The scour habitat was typically confined to less than 60% of the wetted channel width. Typical scour pools were 4–7 m long, 1–2 m wide and about 1 m deep, with water velocity usually under 15 cm/s (immediately adjacent velocities were often higher, up to 260 cm/s, as reported). Substrate was commonly gravel. Secondary habitats were [[Corner Pools]] and [[Backwater Pools]], which often contained woody material. Larger individuals (over 100 mm) were almost exclusively found in lateral scour pools formed by root masses of uprooted and standing trees. Almost all macrohabitats with chubs had extensive cover from snags, organic debris or large tree roots.

**Associated species.** Native fishes in the study drainages include:
- [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*)
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
- [[Rio Grande Sucker]] (*Catostomus plebeius*)
- Mexican stoneroller (*Campostoma ornatum*)
- Undescribed *Cyprinodon* sp. (pupfish)

Non-native fishes recorded include common carp, western mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), black and brown bullheads, bluegill, largemouth bass, rock bass, green sunfish, channel catfish, rainbow and brown trout, and longfin dace. The authors note that the native fish fauna was depauperate, with three native species each in the Mimbres and Laguna Bustillos drainages and five to nine in the other drainages.

**Threats.** Recognized threats include:
- Irrigated agriculture and water diversion, which dewatered many reaches
- [[Habitat Modification]] (channelization, levees, removal of riparian trees)
- Livestock grazing and ranchette development
- Groundwater pumping and water-quality degradation
- Introduction of non-native fishes
- Overharvest for food. Local residents reported night harvesting with torches and spears, and the chub was described as a desirable food fish.

Parasites observed included *Lernaea* sp., *Ichthyophthirius* sp., leeches and fungal growths.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- Chihuahua chub was found at 28 of 40 stream sites with fish, one of nine spring sites, and neither of two lakes sampled in Chihuahua.
- It was moderately common (≥10% of specimens) at 12 stream sites, but fewer than 30 chubs were collected at six of these.
- In the Mimbres Drainage, the chub was regularly found only at Archuleta/Moreno Spring, where it was not common. Seventeen specimens were captured there on 17 September 1992, ranging from 28 to 237 mm, with all but three under 100 mm.
- At Rio del Carmen near Ricardo Flores Magón, 81 specimens were collected by W. L. Minckley in 1964 and 32 by Miller in 1979. The river was dry there in 1990, and the most downstream 1990 record was three fish near San Lorenzo.
- At Rio Piedras Verdes near Col. Juárez, moderate numbers (16–80 per collection) were preserved in 1964 and 1979, but only seven specimens (all under 60 mm) were found at three sites in 1990.

**Size structure and reproduction**
- Multiple size classes at several populations indicated successful recruitment.
- The authors infer that most reproduction was by fish under 130 mm or by a few over 150 mm.
- Ripe males and gravid females occurred in March at lower-elevation sites, and individuals under 20 mm occurred at higher sites. The spawning season appears extended, potentially from early spring through early autumn.

**Length–weight relationships**
- Laguna Bustillos populations were not significantly different from one another (F = 1.8735, p > 0.05). Combined: LnWT = 0.0440 + 0.02995SL (n = 38, r² = 0.9624).
- Guzman populations differed significantly overall (F = 6.7353, p < 0.01), but removing San Gerónimo eliminated the difference. Combined Guzman populations excluding San Gerónimo: LnWT = -0.23056 + 0.02945SL (n = 308, r² = 0.9333).
- The San Gerónimo population had the narrowest length range (53–110 mm), and its relationship was considered unrepresentative.

**Habitat and population drivers**
- Presence and abundance of pools associated with root masses of large trees best predicted chub occurrence and abundance.
- Chub were absent or rare in channelized or discontinuous-flow systems, such as the Mimbres River and Laguna Bustillos streams, where large trees were scarce.
- Chub were moderately common only in remote, relatively unmodified reaches of the Santa Clara, Santa María and Casas Grandes rivers, where mature cottonwoods were common.
- Chub were rare or absent where habitat was degraded and non-native fishes were common.

**Source-population hypothesis**
- Populations appear maintained by "core" populations in remote upstream reaches, with small downstream individuals probably derived from upstream sources.

**Conservation outlook**
- The authors state that the persistence of Chihuahua chub depends on remote reaches free from settlement and water development. These are uncommon and fragmented, representing a small fraction of the historic range.
- In the United States, the chub survives only because Archuleta/Moreno Spring is unmodified.
- Stream dewatering and habitat destruction may cause extirpation from the Laguna Bustillos Basin. In the Guzman Basin, the species may survive only in remote reaches of the Santa Clara, Santa María, Casas Grandes and Piedras Verdes rivers.
- Recommended research gaps: status of unsampled areas, basic biology and habitat needs, interactions with non-native fishes, and taxonomic clarification of isolated populations.
