# Literature Review of the Thermal Requirements and Tolerances of Organisms below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft literature review, prepared by Richard A. Lechleitner, compiles published thermal tolerance and requirement data for organisms in the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Its purpose is to assess how a possible shift to warmer outflows, through a [[Multiple Level Intake]] or [[Selective Withdrawal]] structure drawing epilimnetic water from [[Lake Powell]], could affect the aquatic food base, parasites and fishes of the [[Grand Canyon]] tailwater. The review covers an amphipod, aquatic insects, the Asian tapeworm, two primary producers (filamentous algae and diatoms), and a large set of native and nonnative fishes.

The central message is that the tailwater is currently cold, with water never exceeding about 15°C in the Glen Canyon reach and winter temperatures not falling below 7°C. Most of the thermal data come from studies on populations outside the Colorado Basin, so the author concludes that local laboratory and field testing is needed before responses can be predicted.

## Background & Description

The review is a draft (Draft #1) that synthesizes literature across several taxa rather than reporting new data. It draws on a large bibliography: about 82 fish references, 27 references for non-fish organisms, and several Habitat Suitability Index models. Its figures and tables summarize upper and lower lethal temperatures, optimum growth temperatures, final preferred temperatures, spawning and egg hatching temperatures, and egg survival for [[Native Fish]] and [[Nonnative Fish]] of the Colorado River.

Thermal tolerance terms used throughout include the [[Upper Lethal Temperature]], [[Lower Lethal Temperature]], [[Optimum Growth Temperature]], and [[Final Preferendum]] (final preferred temperature).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime below the dam.** Glen Canyon Dam releases water from the hypolimnion (a [[Hypolimnetic Release]]). This produces a cold, thermally stable [[Tailwater]] with little seasonal variation. The review argues that this stability disrupts thermal cues that many organisms need. Spring warming triggers hatching and emergence in some insects, and winter chilling near 0°C breaks diapause in others.

**Comparison with warm-release systems.** Fraley (1979), cited by the review, studied a cold-water river receiving warm water from a shallow reservoir. Summer means reached 19.1°C, about 3°C above upstream temperatures. Macroinvertebrate density rose, but diversity fell, and the community shifted toward warm-water-tolerant taxa.

**Proposed intake change.** A multiple level intake would likely warm summer releases and reduce winter cooling. The review notes that epilimnetic withdrawal would also change the chemistry of releases, including the supply of fine particulate organic matter and zooplankton that feed filter-feeding invertebrates.

**Tributaries as sources.** Grand Canyon tributaries hold a more diverse macroinvertebrate fauna than the mainstem. They could serve as sources of colonizing species if mainstem conditions became favorable.

## Key Findings

### 1. Gammarus lacustris (Amphipod)
- Smith (1973), working with a Minnesota population, reported a 96-hr TL50 of 26°C and a 30-day TL50 of 22–24°C for animals acclimated to 18°C.
- Survival after 30 days at 18°C was 95%. No organisms survived exposure to 27°C.
- Reproduction was optimal at 18°C, with reduced fecundity at 15, 21 and 24°C. Temperatures below 15°C were not tested.
- The author suggests the species could tolerate increases to at least 18°C and possibly 20°C.
- A viable population exists in Glen Canyon, where water never exceeds 15°C. The author argues this population may have a different thermal tolerance from the Minnesota population, so local study is needed.

### 2. Aquatic Insects and Macroinvertebrates
- The insect fauna below Glen Canyon Dam is impoverished and dominated by aquatic dipterans, which can make up as much as 85% of the standing crop of drifting invertebrates (Leibfried and Blinn 1988).
- Low summer temperatures may exclude species that cannot grow fast enough to compete. Slow immature growth may also reduce adult size and fecundity.
- Lack of winter chill may prevent completion of life cycles in species with winter diapause. Winter temperatures in the tailwater do not fall below 7°C.
- Life history and thermal tolerance data for Grand Canyon aquatic insects are lacking. The review recommends laboratory tests on the main species and multi-year experiments, possibly using naturally colonized communities exposed to epilimnetic water, the approach used by Wrenn et al. (1979) for Tennessee River communities.

### 3. Asian Tapeworm (Bothriocephalus acheilognathi)
- The parasite was found in [[Humpback Chub]] from Grand Canyon (Angradi et al. 1992). In 1990, 80% of larval humpback chub (13–35 mm total length) were infected. No tapeworms were found in 1989. The infection rate for the full population has not been determined.
- Infections have also been reported in woundfin, speckled dace, Virgin River roundtail chub, Virgin spinedace and red shiner in the Virgin River.
- The parasite's life cycle includes development within [[Cyclopoid Copepods]], some of which occur in the Colorado River in Grand Canyon.
- Granath and Esch (1983b) found that at 20°C, egg maturation required a minimum of 60 hr, with peak coracidia production of 35% at 96 hr. Growth and development of mature worms were stimulated above 25°C. The highest densities of immature worms occurred at 20°C, and temperatures below 20°C were not tested.
- The review concludes that warmer mainstem temperatures (>20°C) would likely increase parasite reproduction and that epilimnetic withdrawal could increase copepod populations. Together these could enhance the parasite population.

### 4. Cladophora glomerata (Filamentous Green Alga)
- Different races may have different thermal tolerances. The Glen Canyon race probably performs best below 15°C (Whitton 1970).
- In the Great Lakes, Cladophora became dominant above 15°C and thrived up to 25°C (Herbst 1969).
- In seven Ontario rivers, it dominated periphyton up to 18°C and had an upper thermal tolerance of 23.5°C (Wong et al. 1978).
- Lake Michigan populations showed a thermal optimum between 28 and 31°C (Lester et al. 1988).
- The author states that effects of warming on Glen and Grand canyon populations must be determined before predictions can be made.

### 5. Epiphytic Diatoms
- Blinn et al. (1989) found that diatom communities on Cladophora changed within two weeks of exposure to higher temperatures.
- At 18 and 21°C, smaller, low-profile species that grow close to the substrate replaced the larger, upright, light-shading species that dominate in cool tailwater conditions.
- The threshold temperature for this shift was between 12 and 18°C.
- A shift in diatom composition could affect grazing macroinvertebrates, since grazers prefer larger upright diatoms (Sumner and McIntire 1982).
- Warming to 19–20°C to boost humpback chub reproduction could therefore shift diatom composition substantially.

### 6. Native and Nonnative Fishes
- The review compiles thermal data in Figures 1–3 and Tables 1–3 for six native species (Gila cypha, Ptychocheilus lucius, Rhinichthys osculus, Xyrauchen texanus, Catostomus discobolus, C. latipinnis) and about a dozen nonnative species.
- **Humpback Chub**: optimum egg hatching is 19–20°C (Marsh 1985). Table 2 gives a spawning optimum of 19–20°C, an upper limit of 26°C and a lower limit of 10°C. Reported spawning occurs in Grand Canyon from April to June.
- **Razorback Sucker**: spawning optimum 20°C, upper limit 25°C, lower limit 15°C.
- **Bonytail Chub**: spawning optimum 20°C, upper limit 26°C, lower limit 14°C.
- **Striped Bass** (nonnative): egg hatching was highest at 18°C, with 87.4% hatching. Hatching fell below 50% at 24°C and 26°C.
- Reported thermal tolerances for many nonnative species, such as [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Walleye]], [[Common Carp]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]], exceed those of native Colorado fishes, which is relevant to a warmer tailwater.
- The review notes that these tables draw largely on populations from other systems, and the Colorado River data remain limited.

### 7. Research Gaps and Recommendations
- Degree of Asian tapeworm infection in Gila cypha and other native and nonnative fishes, and in cyclopoid copepods of Grand Canyon.
- Thermal tolerances of Grand Canyon aquatic insects, tested in the laboratory and over multiple years.
- Effects of warming on Cladophora races and diatom communities in both canyons.
- Local temperature tolerance of the Glen Canyon Gammarus population.
- The author concludes that the effects of a multiple level intake on the tailwater biota are difficult to predict without this additional testing.
