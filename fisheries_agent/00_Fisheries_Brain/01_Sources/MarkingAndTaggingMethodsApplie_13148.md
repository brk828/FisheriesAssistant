# A Review on Marking and Tagging Methods Applied to Eel (*Anguilla anguilla*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anguillidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Jan Nielsen (County of Vejle Environmental Office, Denmark) synthesizes published and personal-communication evidence on marking and tagging methods for the [[European Eel]] (*Anguilla anguilla*). Across methods, marking and tagging are reported to cause tag loss, infection, reduced growth or mortality, largely because of the eel's ecology. Most trials were conducted in situ, which makes firm conclusions on tag loss, growth and survival difficult. The author concludes that all tested methods affect eels negatively, that some are unfit for use, and that most should be tested further under controlled conditions, preferably on yellow eels rather than silver eels.

## Background & Description

Marking (e.g., dye immersion, tetracycline baths, fin removal) distinguishes groups of fish, often for a limited period until growth or regeneration removes the mark. Tagging (external or internal, numbered devices) identifies individual fish but is generally more harmful and suffers from tag loss. The review stresses that [[Yellow Eel]] and [[Silver Eel]] differ strongly in behaviour and physiology, and that study aims must be defined before choosing a method. Eels are also commercially valuable, so visible marks or wounds can lower market prices, a problem the paper does not treat further.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Eel ecology drives most of the reported failures. Yellow eels are sedentary and digging-active, so trailer tags and wounds interfere with burrowing and feeding. Silver eels migrate to the sea, so recaptures of tagged silver eels reflect escape from fisheries rather than tag retention. Environmental factors discussed include sunlight deactivating [[Tetracycline]] marks, the pH of unbuffered tetracycline solutions (2.5–3.5, lethal to most fish), and the chelation of tetracycline by magnesium and calcium in marine water, which calls for a 1–3% NaCl carrier. Ecological comparisons also draw on [[American Eel]] (*A. rostrata*) and Japanese eel (*A. japonica*) studies.

## Key Findings

**Marking methods (reviewed)**

- **Submersion in colour bath:** [[Bismarck Brown]] and [[Neutral Red]] were the only effective dyes among nine tested by Fries (1965). Bismarck brown marks were recognizable up to 6 days; Cantrelle reported 3–5 weeks for Bismarck brown and 3–7 days for Neutral red. Neutral red is reported toxic by some authors. Tzeng (1984) marked 4,225 *A. japonica* glass eels with FX fluorescent dye, with a 47% total recapture rate and no observed damage.
- **Tetracycline bath:** Dekker marked 1,000 glass eels with oxytetracycline (0.1 g/l); marks were clear after one week. Recommended for tank or pond studies and ageing eels of known age.
- **Radioactive bath:** Hansen & Fattah (1986) used radioactive europium; not recommended because of radiological risk and the danger of eating marked eels.
- **Injection:** Rubber latex (Sadler, Stott) gave positive results with recaptures up to about 18 months and near-zero mortality reported. Acrylic paint showed mixed results: marks retained for at least 4 years in Seymour's study, but pigment migration into kidney tissue was reported by Sadler (1981). Alcian blue marks persisted for up to 3 years (Wickström) and 5 years (Rasmussen, 1 recapture). Dekker (1986) marked 3,000 eels with oxytetracycline plus alcian blue, but only 4 of 6 recaptured eels with an alcian blue spot showed a tetracycline otolith mark.
- **Silver nitrate cauterization:** Results were ambiguous. Hansen (1982) marked 99 yellow eels (30–79 cm), recapturing 47 over 2.5 years with fast growth (up to 10–15 cm/yr). Rasmussen (pers. comm.) reported only 0.5% mean weight growth after one year. Harder et al. (1961) noted mortality mainly in the first weeks. Overall, the method is judged very harmful.
- **Cold branding (freezing):** Liquid nitrogen branding of *A. rostrata* (Sorensen et al., 1983) showed no mortality over 10 months. Rossi et al. (1986) branded 500 yellow eels individually and 1,170 with group marks, recording a 26.2% recapture rate, recognizable marks for at least 2 years, and possible growth reduction for at least five months.
- **Burning (hot metal):** Marks were indistinguishable after three months (Lee & Lasserre, 1979), so the method is not recommended.
- **Fin clipping:** Nielsen (1983) removed a pectoral fin from 2,764 yellow eels (25–50 cm), with 52 recaptured the following year, mainly due to ineffective fishing. Fin removal appeared harmless with no observed infection, but growth data are lacking.

**Tagging methods (reviewed)**

- **Jaw tags ("Marque Barettes"):** Berg (1986) tagged about 9,000 eels, with recaptures over 10 years. Annual mortality of tagged eels differed from untagged eels by only about 2%, so tagging mortality was judged effectively zero. Tagged eels grew at about 50% of untagged eel rates in early years. Berg's work suggests this is possibly the least harmful eel tag, provided it is fixed deep in the corner of the mouth. Nielsen (1983) tagged 500 yellow eels with the same tag type.
- **Internal tags:** Gundersen (1979) tagged 1,273 eels with streamer tags and 166 without. Aquarium trials showed 36% of streamer-tagged eels shed tags over six months, versus 15% shedding in the first month for tags without streamers. The author recommends internal tags without streamers where detection equipment is available, with a one-month holding period before release.
- **Gill tags:** Wickström (tank trial, 30 eels) recorded 70% mortality within two months; the single field trial gave a 9.1% recapture rate.
- **Floy anchor tags:** In Poland, 2,651 eels were tagged with 9.7% recapture (Leopold). In Ireland, 3,602 eels were tagged, with retention up to 4 years and an extremely low 1.2% recapture (Moriarty). Naismith found 58% tag loss after 6 months in a pond (84 eels). Vellestad et al. (1986) tagged 1,350 descending silver eels with a near-complete recapture rate (about 95% combined), showing Floy tags suit silver eel migration studies.
- **Plastic arrow tags:** Deelder & Tesch (1970) tagged 700 eels with 2.9% recapture, probably due to tag loss after 40 days.
- **Wire-fastened tags:** Bartel & Kosior (1974) tagged 9,276 eels with 9.3% recaptured, most in the first 2–3 months; the authors concluded the method was insufficient.
- **Synthetic thread tags:** Tesch and Deelder used thread-fastened plates on several thousand eels, with only 5–20% recaptured, mostly in 3–4 months. Tag loss was at least 15% within two months.
- **Carlin tags:** Silver eels showed satisfactory recapture (about 46%) in several thousand releases, while yellow eels tagged with Carlin tags showed stunted growth and weight loss (Rasmussen: 18% weight loss at one year). Westin & Nyman (1979) found 26% recapture for standard placement and 19% for placement around the vertebral column.
- **Modified Carlin tag (no link):** In aquaria, tag loss and mortality were 17% after 2 months and 22% after 6 months; sea recapture was 7–8%.
- **Thread-fastened double-attachment tags:** Wickström's tank trial showed 77.5% mortality (31 of 40 eels), contrasting with Pursiainen & Tulonen's 15 recaptures from 83 Finnish eels with no bleeding or infection.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Marking and tagging will continue, so the least harmful methods must be identified. Mortality and stunted growth should be assumed when interpreting data.
- Trailer tags are harmful to yellow eels; high recapture rates appear mainly in silver-eel studies. Firmly fixed tags, especially jaw tags, performed best.
- Testing should use controlled tank or pond experiments and monitor infection, tag loss, mortality, growth and mark duration, ideally including ageing of eels of known age. Silver eels should be excluded from testing.
- Methods recommended for further testing: submersion (dyes, tetracycline); alcian blue and rubber latex injection; silver nitrate cauterization; cold branding; fin removal; internal tags without streamers; jaw tags; Floy tags; Carlin tags with stainless steel wire or polyethylene thread; and modified Carlin tags without link.
- Ultrasonic and radio tags were excluded because they require special equipment and serve only special purposes.
