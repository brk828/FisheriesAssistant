# FAO Aquaculture Newsletter No. 20 (December 1998): Integrating Fisheries, Aquaculture and Water Resources

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

FAO Aquaculture Newsletter No. 20 (December 1998) is a multi-topic issue. It covers the policy framework for sustainable aquaculture, the integration of fisheries with agriculture, corrected statistics for Chinese aquaculture, water-resource allocation for inland fisheries, the risks and benefits of introduced aquatic species, and a series of FAO and partner projects and publications. The central message is that aquaculture is the fastest-growing food-producing sector and will have to supply future fish protein, but that its growth depends on an [[Enabling Framework for Aquaculture]], integrated resource planning, and the fair allocation of water among competing users.

## Background & Description

The issue is a periodic bulletin of the [[Inland Water Resources and Aquaculture Service]] of the FAO Fisheries Department, edited by Ziad H. Shehadeh. Its opening editorial, by Shehadeh, introduces the need for an enabling environment for aquaculture. It refers to the [[Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries]], to the FAO/World Bank draft strategy presented at the 1997 Summit of the Seas, and to an interregional assistance programme funded in part by the Netherlands and Norway.

The main contents are:

- **Integrating Fisheries and Agriculture to Enhance Fish Production and Food Security** (Willmann, Halwart and Barg): an abridged article from *The State of Food and Agriculture 1998*.
- **Aquaculture Production in China** (Rana, Perotti, Montanaro and Immink): a statistical correction for Chinese shellfish production.
- **Resource Use in Aquaculture and Inland Fisheries**: a summary of the EIFAC symposium on water for sustainable inland fisheries and aquaculture, held in Portugal in June 1998.
- **Impacts of Introductions on the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Aquatic Biodiversity** (Bartley and Casal), with a companion article on the [[Database on Introductions of Aquatic Species]] web site (Garibaldi and Bartley).
- **Projects and Other Activities** and **New FAO Publications**, covering the SARDev regional programme, the Conference on Aquaculture in the Third Millennium, collaborative studies with [[ICLARM]], and recent ALCOM publications.

Most of the issue is written for policy-makers, extension workers and fisheries planners. Figures and tables are referenced in the text but are not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Integrated farming and nutrient cycling.** Agricultural by-products such as manure and crop residues are used as fertilizer and feed. Integrated farming has a long history: the note cites Chinese fish polyculture combined with poultry, livestock and crops more than 2,400 years ago, and rice-shrimp rotation in the intertidal zones of South and Southeast Asia. Rice-fish systems can reduce pesticide use, because fish feed on pests and push the economic threshold for pest control higher. Carp have been used to control snails in Viet Nam.

**Biocides and eutrophication.** Harm to fisheries comes mainly from inappropriate biocide application, which can alter reproduction, metabolism, growth and population size, not only cause mortality. Nutrient runoff and sewage drive eutrophication. This creates anaerobic conditions and harmful algal blooms, which have caused substantial losses to coastal fisheries and aquaculture over the last two decades. Sewage treatment around [[Lake Constance]] significantly reduced that lake's nutrient loading.

**Hydrological change.** Dams, reservoirs, drainage and water abstraction have caused large declines in natural fish populations. In some European rivers, wild stocks of salmon, sturgeon and Allis shad no longer exist. Reservoirs can yield large fisheries gains, as at Lake Kariba. Rapid drawdowns can destroy spawning habitat, and water loss to evaporation must be weighed against fish production.

**Salinisation.** Shrimp culture has reduced agricultural yields where saline water seeped into adjacent fields. Excessive groundwater abstraction causes seawater intrusion into coastal aquifers.

**Introduced species.** Alien species can cause ecological effects (competition, hybridisation, predation, disease transmission, habitat alteration) and socio-economic effects (changes in fishing pressure, land use and access rights). Introductions can also be beneficial. The note cites tilapia production in Asia (>700,000 mt in 1996) versus Africa (39,245 mt), and Chilean salmonids, which support roughly 20% of the world's farmed salmon.

## Key Findings

### Enabling framework and policy

- Aquaculture policy has often been treated in isolation from other sectors and from its environmental, legal and social linkages. This has had negative consequences.
- Governments must create an "enabling environment" with fair access to resources, conflict-resolution mechanisms, and access to information, credit and markets.
- Balancing growth with ecosystem conservation requires attention to competition for resources, a shrinking public sector and globalised markets.
- The FAO strategy for implementing the Code of Conduct includes developing codes of responsible practice for aquaculture, with the applicability of approaches assessed particularly for small-scale farmers.

### Fisheries, agriculture and food security

- Reported global freshwater capture production is about 7.5 million tonnes, but actual catches may be as much as double the reported figures.
- In many Asian countries over one-half of animal protein intake comes from fish. In Africa the proportion is 17.5 percent.
- Inland fisheries landings relative to other production systems have declined over the past few decades, largely because of changes to aquatic habitats.
- Aquaculture grew at a compound rate of nearly 11% per year over the last decade. By 1996, production of cultured fish, molluscs, crustaceans and aquatic plants reached a record 34.12 million tonnes, valued at $46.5 billion. More than 85% came from developing countries.
- Annual aquaculture production is projected to exceed 40 million tonnes by 2010.
- Integrated rice-fish systems combined with vegetable or fruit crops grown on bunds gave a reported twelve-fold increase in economic benefits compared with traditional rice farming, according to the Indian Council of Agricultural Research.
- Net income on rice-fish farms was reported to be higher than on rice monoculture by margins of 7 to 65 percent.

### Chinese aquaculture statistics

- China dominates world aquaculture production. Its share rose from 37.7% (3.8 million tonnes) in 1984 to 67.8% (23.1 million tonnes) in 1996.
- FAO found that China had reported three molluscs (blood cockle, Japanese carpet shell, Pacific cupped oyster) and unclassified marine molluscs as shucked or shelled weight. These were converted to live-weight equivalents using factors of 1.35, 2.13, 6.11 and 2.13, respectively.
- Chinese shellfish production (live weight) rose from 347,000 tonnes in 1984 to 4.70 million tonnes in 1996. Earlier reporting underestimated it by 3.16 million tonnes in 1996.
- After adjustment, China's total reported aquaculture output rose from 7.5 to 8.2 million tonnes in 1990, and from 20.0 to 23.1 million tonnes in 1996.

### Water resources and inland fisheries (EIFAC symposium, 1998)

- The symposium was attended by 68 participants from 23 countries.
- Aquaculture must be accepted and legally recognised as a legitimate water user.
- Fisheries planners must take part in water allocation fora. Aquaculture should not be penalised by unrealistic effluent standards, and aquaculture operators must improve their discharges.
- Integrating aquaculture into irrigation systems is an option for improving water-use efficiency.
- Stocking new species in stressed systems may offer alternative fisheries, but risks must be weighed. Economic and social valuation of inland fisheries is a priority.
- The EU river basin management plans, due by December 1999, must reflect the needs of inland fisheries and aquaculture.
- By 2025 the world population is expected to reach about eight thousand million. Future fish protein growth must come from aquaculture and enhancement of wild stocks, since oceanic fisheries cannot supply substantial increases.

### Introductions of aquatic species

- The [[Database on Introductions of Aquatic Species]] (DIAS) contains about 3,150 introduction records. It was begun by Robin Welcomme in the early 1980s and was taken over by Devin Bartley in 1991.
- Using links to FAO statistics, introduced fishes were estimated to contribute about 17% of total fish production (17.6% in 1996).
- Aquaculture was the most often cited reason for introduction (38.7% of records). Government organisations were responsible for more introductions than any other group.
- Most reported ecological effects were negative, while reported socio-economic effects were more often beneficial.
- Limitations include reporting bias (successful or highly damaging introductions are more likely to be reported) and the fact that only first cross-border introductions are recorded.
- Chinese carp introduced into barrier lakes in coastal Mozambique illustrates the risk of introducing species without knowing the receiving ecosystem.
- Bartley and Casal argue that risk assessment must be paired with benefit assessment, and that national registries should record the receiving environment.

### Projects, meetings and publications

- **SARDev**: A regional programme on Sustainable Aquaculture for Rural Development was approved by the NACA Governing Council in December 1997. A planning workshop is scheduled for 29–31 March 1999 in Chiang Mai, Thailand.
- **Conference on Aquaculture in the Third Millennium**: To be held in Thailand, 20–25 February 2000, with an expected 500–600 participants.
- **ICLARM study**: A macro-analysis of production, accessibility and consumption patterns of farmed freshwater fish in Bangladesh, China, India, the Philippines and Thailand, running from 1 December 1998 to 31 December 1999.
- **Integrated agriculture-aquaculture kit**: A revision of a 1992 information kit, planned for publication by the end of 1999 or early 2000.
- **Rice-fish technical report**: Under preparation with FAO-RAP and ICLARM.
- **EIFAC symposium on fisheries and society**: To be held in Hungary in June 2000, with title abstracts due by 31 March 1999.
- **APFIC 26th Session** (Beijing, September 1998): Agreed that sustainability is the strategic issue for aquaculture and that small-scale rural aquaculture is an effective vehicle for rural development. It established three ad hoc working groups (food safety, capture fishery data collection, rural aquaculture).
- **Africa research network**: A mission to Ghana, Burkina Faso, Mali, Zambia and Zimbabwe (October–November 1997) found that resources are very limited except for infrastructure in Ghana and Zambia. Zambia is among the main aquaculture producers in Sub-Saharan Africa. Zimbabwe has more than 20,000 ha available for large-scale irrigation.
- **Domestication workshop**: A World Aquaculture Society workshop on domesticating aquaculture species, 28–29 April 1999, Sydney, limited to 24 participants.
