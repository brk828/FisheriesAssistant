# Walleye Population in Escanaba Lake, Vilas County, Wisconsin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #walleye

## Executive Summary

This study estimates the [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion v. vitreum*) population of [[Escanaba Lake]], a 291-acre lake in [[Vilas County]], Wisconsin, where no bag, size, or season limits applied from 1946 onward. Large catches from 1948 to 1950 prompted the [[Wisconsin Conservation Department]] to run a [[Mark-Recapture]] (tagging) experiment in spring 1951. Using the [[Petersen Method]], the population on May 11, 1951 was estimated at 9,102 walleyes (95% range 8,192 to 10,922). The 1951 exploitation rate was 20.5%. The authors attribute the population's persistence to [[Natural Reproduction]] of walleyes, since the lake had not been stocked with walleyes since 1942.

## Background & Description

Escanaba Lake lies in the Northern Highlands of Wisconsin, southeast of Boulder Junction, and is part of the [[Five Lakes Project]]. That project was designed to determine how regulations on size, bag, and season affect fish populations. Anglers on the lakes must hold a permit and report their catch. A checking station was staffed during summer at Escanaba Lake, and winter permits were returned to the Trout Lake Forestry Headquarters, about 4 miles away.

Key lake characteristics:
- Area: 291 acres; maximum depth: 36 feet.
- Phytoplankton: fairly abundant in summer.
- Methyl-orange alkalinity: 12 ppm; bound CO₂: 10.7 ppm.
- Summer stratification, with oxygen usually lost completely below the [[Thermocline]] by August.

The lake originally contained no walleyes. They were established by fry stocking between 1933 and 1937. The document reports a ten-year total of 5,060,378 fry, with annual counts ranging from roughly 94,000 to 1.6 million in the years shown. The lake was last stocked with any fish in 1945 and with walleyes in 1942.

Other fish species present include [[Yellow Perch]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Muskellunge]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Rock Bass]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Black Crappie]]. Escanaba Lake was originally a smallmouth bass lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sampling and harvest records show how walleye abundance changed over time:

- **1946 fyke netting:** 336 net-days yielded 1,010 lb of fish, including only 32 walleyes.
- **1947 spawning season:** [[Fyke Net]] catches totaled 97 walleyes. Age and length data for these fish are in the source's Table 2. Males were mostly age groups 1 to 6 and females were measured at 21 to 29.5 inches.
- **[[Creel Census]] (1946–1951):** Walleye catch was very poor until summer 1948. A strong year class from 1946 and another from 1947 then drove a large summer 1948 catch of 4,003 walleyes. In 1950, 5,716 walleyes were caught, and a 1949 year class joined the catch in 1950 and 1951.
- **Angler effort:** Summer 1950 had 35,166 man-hours at 0.22 fish per man-hour. Summer 1951 had 19,740 man-hours at 0.16 fish per man-hour.
- **Harvest from 1948 to spring 1951:** Anglers caught 12,379 walleyes from the strong year classes (per the text).

The author notes that the stocked fry did not produce abundant year classes. This is supported by comparing the stocking records with the 1947 age data and the 1946–1947 creel records. Natural reproduction, by contrast, produced large year classes in 1946, 1947, and 1949.

## Key Findings

**Population estimate (1951)**
- 984 adult walleyes were tagged from four 1-inch-mesh fyke nets set May 3–11, 1951. The text also gives 948 in one place, and the calculations use 983 tagged fish present on May 12 (one tagged fish was found dead and returned). This discrepancy is in the source and is unresolved here.
- Creel census (May 12–October 15, 1951): 202 tagged fish were recaptured from a total catch of 1,867 walleyes.
- Marked-to-unmarked ratio: 0.108, with [[Clopper-Pearson Confidence Limits]] of 0.09–0.12.
- Population estimate: 9,102 on May 11, 1951. The 95% range is 8,192 to 10,922.

**Exploitation**
- Exploitation rate: 0.205 (20.5%) of the tagged walleyes were caught from May 12 to October 15, 1951. The author assumes untagged fish were caught in the same proportion and concludes that the 1951 harvest was "not heavy."

**Sources of error**
- Tagged fish caught may be under-reported. Early in the season, no station attendant was present until May 19, and a department employee only checked the lake almost daily. Fishermen may also have missed tags, especially late at night. Under-reporting would make the population estimate too large.
- Handling and tagging mortality were assumed negligible, and tagged fish were not spread evenly around the lake, which the author justified by walleye movement after spawning.

**Tagging effects**
- Tags were No. 3 steel (1⅞ in.) for fish 10.5–24.0 in. and aluminum (2.0 in.) for fish over 24.0 in., attached in the upper jaw.
- Most recaptured fish showed no tag irritation. In 1952 spawning-run netting, 47 tagged fish were caught; 2 showed irritation and one 26-inch fish had lost its tag.
- Recaptured tagged fish grew an average of 0.33 inches over roughly a year (Table 4). The author concludes the tags likely slowed growth relative to untagged fish.

**Conclusions**
- Natural reproduction, not stocking, maintains the walleye population. Even with relaxed regulations, heavy angling, and many fish removed, the remaining stock was thought sufficient to produce future abundant year classes and continued angling opportunity.
