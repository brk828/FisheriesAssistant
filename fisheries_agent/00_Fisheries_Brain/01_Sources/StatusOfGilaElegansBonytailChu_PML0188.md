# Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*) Species Status Account

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a species status account for the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), a cyprinid fish listed as **Endangered**. It covers taxonomy, historical and present range, population decline, life history, research needs, and authorities. The species was once abundant in the main channels of the [[Colorado River Basin]] but has been virtually eliminated from its former range. Declines are attributed to dam construction, flow regulation, cold-water releases, and competition from introduced species. Surveys in the 1970s found only isolated individuals, and natural reproduction is considered doubtful. The account recommends surveys of remaining tributaries and possible artificial propagation.

## Background & Description

**Morphology.** The bonytail is a relatively large cyprinid, reaching about 16–18 inches, with a distinctively narrow "pencil-thin" caudal peduncle. Compared with [[Gila Robusta]] (roundtail chub), it differs in peduncle depth, having 10 dorsal and anal rays (versus 9 in *robusta*), and a more anterior dorsal fin. [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970) provide a full diagnosis.

**Taxonomic status.** For years, *G. elegans* and *G. robusta* were treated as subspecies under the mistaken belief that environment produced ecotypes. Work by [[Paul Holden]] (1968) and Holden and Stalnaker (1970) established both as valid species, and ecological studies by [[Vanicek and Kramer]] (1969) documented life-history differences. The taxonomic boundary between *G. elegans* and [[Gila Cypha]] ([[Humpback Chub]]) is unclear, probably due to recent introgressive hybridization triggered by environmental change. The rarity of both species has prevented further clarification.

**Range history.** Historically, the bonytail likely overlapped with the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen*) in the main channels of the Colorado and Green rivers and major tributaries, from Mexico to Wyoming.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Suitable habitat is poorly documented. The streamlined body suggests a swift-water specialist, yet all collections by Vanicek (1967) and Vanicek and Kramer (1969) came from pools and eddies away from strong currents, usually over silt or silt-boulder substrates. The species appears poorly adapted to the colder water released below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], and it has not been recorded above the [[Echo Park]] area.

**Threats.**
- Colorado River basin development and associated environmental change have nearly eliminated the species from its former range.
- Flow regulation and dams are linked to the decline in the lower basin.
- Cold-water releases reduce spawning success in the upper basin.
- Introduced species, including salmonids in cold water and centrarchids, cyprinids, and catfishes in warm water, likely suppress remaining populations.

**Food and feeding.** Vanicek and Kramer (1969) found that bonytails feed largely on terrestrial insects taken at the surface, along with plant debris and filamentous algae. None of the 35 large specimens examined had eaten fish. The document notes doubt that plant material provides nutrition unless the fish can digest cell walls.

**Ownership and occurrence.** Ownership varies. The most significant recent occurrence is in Echo Park, within [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (Holden and Stalnaker, 1973).

## Key Findings

**Historical abundance and decline**
- Early records describe the bonytail as abundant in large river channels in the 19th and early 20th centuries, before large dams were built.
- In the lower basin, the species was once among the most common fish of the Needles–Yuma area. Miller and Lowe (1964) list the last record from the [[Gila River]] as 1926.
- In [[Lake Mead]], it was abundant in 1940–41, with reports of large schools in 1941. By 1950 it was rarely seen (Wallis, 1951).
- In the upper basin, the species was relatively abundant in Echo Park in 1964–66 but was very rare by 1968–71 (Holden and Stalnaker, 1973).

**Present status (as of the document)**
- The species persists at very low abundance in the lower basin. Only one or two specimens per year have been reported from [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. A specimen was taken near the Willow Beach hatchery in 1971.
- Surveys by Holden (1972) and George Kidd (1972) in the lower Gunnison and Colorado rivers found no bonytail or humpback chub.
- In August 1969, Steve Seaman found bonytail relatively abundant in the [[Yampa River]] above Dinosaur National Monument.
- No bonytail or humpback chub had been taken from [[Lake Powell]] since 1970.
- Recent large chubs examined from the Gunnison and San Juan rivers were *G. robusta*, not *G. elegans*.

**Life history**
- **Maturity:** Sexual maturity is reached late. Ripe males were aged 5–8 years. The upper age for females is garbled in the scan, but the minimum was 5 years.
- **Spawning temperature:** Peak spawning in the Green River occurred from late June to early July at about 65°F. Vanicek (1967) found that both *robusta* and *elegans* spawn at the same time but in different places.
- **Reproductive failure:** No successful spawning was found above the Yampa confluence in 1964 and 1966, when cold releases from Flaming Gorge Dam kept temperatures well below 65°F. In 1965, young-of-the-year were collected at Lodore under warmer conditions, and yearlings were collected in 1966.
- **Lake spawning:** The only published observation of spawning is from [[Lake Mohave]] in May 1954 (Jones and Sumner, 1954). About 500 fish spawned over a gravel shelf, with 3–5 males courting each female. Eggs were broadcast without nest building, and one female carried about 10,000 eggs. Lake reproduction is considered unlikely to sustain populations.

**Research needs and recommendations**
- **Priority:** Survey the upper basin to locate any surviving viable populations. Paul Holden doubts success, given his extensive upper-basin surveys.
- **Survey sites:** The [[San Juan River]] should be checked, although [[Navajo Lake]] has probably eliminated big-river species there. The White River, Yampa River above Dinosaur, Dirty Devil River, and San Rafael River are also recommended.
- **Protection:** Any populations found should receive special protection from further environmental change.
- **Propagation:** Artificial propagation may maintain populations where the environment appears favorable but natural reproduction is lacking.
