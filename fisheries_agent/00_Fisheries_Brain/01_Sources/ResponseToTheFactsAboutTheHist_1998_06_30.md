## Executive Summary

This document is a scientific/policy rebuttal by Dr. James E. Deacon (UNLV) to a 1998 Washington County Water Conservancy District (WCWCD) report defending its operation of the Quail Creek Project on the Virgin River. The core scientific dispute concerns whether a stipulated minimum flow of **86 cubic feet per second (cfs)** — derived from Deacon and Hardy's habitat and recruitment research on the endangered **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) — was intended to protect biological needs of the species across a defined 12-mile river reach, or merely to satisfy downstream water rights at a single diversion point. Deacon argues, using the original Biological Opinion (BO), Environmental Assessment (EA), and his own unpublished analyses (Hardy and Deacon 1982; Deacon and Hardy 1984), that the flow requirement is explicitly biologically motivated and reach-wide, not point-specific, and that the Quail Creek Project's actual operation deviates from the regime originally analyzed — contributing to documented declines in woundfin and the broader **[[Virgin River Native Fish Community]]**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat definition (known woundfin habitat):** The critical river segment is explicitly identified as the ~12-mile reach from **LaVerkin Springs to Washington Fields Diversion** (comprising two ~6-mile sub-segments flanking the Quail Creek Diversion), described as "the uppermost portion of known woundfin habitat."
- **Excluded reach:** The reach from the Quail Creek Diversion upstream to LaVerkin Springs (through Timpoweap Canyon) was *not* classified as known woundfin habitat and was therefore excluded from mandatory bypass-flow protection, despite carrying "bypass" water potentially diverted to a hydroelectric plant.
- **Flow–recruitment relationship:** Deacon (1979) found mean spring flows **<100 cfs** correlated with poor reproductive success/recruitment. Deacon and Hardy (1984, cited in BO as "in press") refined this, showing juvenile woundfin recruitment (1977–1982) was most successful when mean monthly streamflow fell between **200–800 cfs** during the period of fry development and growth.
- **Weighted Usable Area (WUA) modeling:** Hardy and Deacon (1982) modeled habitat suitability across two reaches:
  - Upper 1.5-mile reach: Pah Tempe Springs to mouth of Ash Creek.
  - Lower 12.5-mile reach: mouth of Ash Creek to Washington Diversion.
  Maximum WUA for **juveniles** occurred at **90 cfs**; maximum WUA for **adults** occurred at **85 cfs** in the LaVerkin Spring–Washington Diversion reach — the empirical basis for the 86 cfs compromise figure.
- **Operational regime modeled:** Only water *withdrawals* at the Quail Creek Diversion were analyzed (bypass flows plus natural tributary inputs from Pah Tempe Springs, LaVerkin Creek, and Ash Creek); reservoir *releases* were explicitly excluded from the original impact analysis — a key point of contention regarding current Project operation.
- **Comparative case — [[Warner Valley Project]]:** Received a jeopardy opinion from USFWS because it failed to conform to an 80–100 cfs minimum flow standard below LaVerkin Springs, contrasting with Quail Creek's non-jeopardy status, contingent on adherence to the flow regime "as presented for analysis."
- **Drought interaction:** Reduced flow variability and reduced minimum flows attributable to Quail Creek Diversion operation, combined with LaVerkin/Ash Creek diversions, are argued to compound drought effects (e.g., 1989–1992 drought) more severely than earlier droughts (1974–1977) when Project-related flow alteration was absent.

## Key Findings

1. **Biological, not water-rights, basis for BO stipulation:** All eight references cited in the Biological Opinion's literature section pertain to native Virgin River fish biology (seven by Deacon/students, one by Miller & Hubbs); none address water rights, undermining WCWCD's claim that the 86 cfs figure was rights-driven.
2. **86 cfs as reach-wide biological minimum:** The 86 cfs (or natural flow, if lower) bypass requirement applies to the **entire 12-mile reach** from Pah Tempe Springs/LaVerkin Springs to Washington Fields Diversion — not solely to the point of diversion at Washington Fields — based on the structure of the original Hardy and Deacon (1982) habitat analysis.
3. **Historical derivation of 86 cfs:** The specific value emerged as a negotiated compromise between Deacon's earlier 80–100 cfs recommendation (from the Warner Valley Project assessment) and Hardy and Deacon's (1982) modeled optima of 85–90 cfs for adult/juvenile habitat, agreed upon with USFWS biologist Terry Hickman as scientifically defensible and low-controversy.
4. **Reservoir releases excluded from compliance calculus:** Because the original analysis never incorporated water *released* from Quail Creek Reservoir, WCWCD is not obligated under the EA to release stored water to maintain 86 cfs when natural flows fall below that threshold — though it may choose to do so to satisfy separate state water rights.
5. **Documented adverse effects on woundfin:** Peer-reviewed and non-peer-reviewed literature (Deacon 1988; Gregory & Deacon 1994; Holden & Zucker 1996; Williams & Deacon 1998) attribute part of the observed decline in woundfin population density over ~20 years to reduced flow variability and reduced minimum flows caused by Quail Creek Diversion and reach-wide diversions (LaVerkin, Ash Creek).
6. **Contested alternative hypothesis (drought-only):** Hardy et al. (1995) proposed that woundfin decline correlates primarily with drought/annual flow variability rather than Project operation; Deacon counters this was not peer-reviewed nor independently corroborated, and that differential population impacts between the 1974–1977 and 1989–1992 droughts are inconsistent with a drought-only explanation.
7. **Non-compliance conclusion:** The Quail Creek Project, as currently operated, is judged **not** consistent with the flow regime "presented for the analysis of the impacts of the reservoir operation on the woundfin and its habitat," constituting a violation of the operational conditions attached to the BLM right-of-way grant and underlying Biological Opinion.
