# Monitoring Study of Reintroduced Razorback Suckers in the Lower Colorado River (May 1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game monthly report, dated May 1987, covers a monitoring study of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) reintroduced into the [[Lower Colorado River]]. In May, about 600 juvenile (one-year-old) razorback suckers were collected from a pond in Blythe and stocked into a [[Backwater Habitat]] downstream of Parker, Arizona. Post-release sampling used [[Trammel Net]] sets, [[Minnow Trap]] sets, and a shoreline seine. Stomach examinations of resident predatory fish were also completed. Razorback suckers were captured only in the trammel nets, and no stocked fish were found in the stomachs of [[Channel Catfish]]. Two [[Largemouth Bass]] did contain one razorback each. Most significantly, [[Bluegill]] were found to be consuming larval fish, and the report suggests these were razorback sucker larvae.

## Background & Description

- **Study focus:** Monitoring movement and survival of juvenile razorback suckers after release, and identifying potential predators of the species.
- **Subject species:** Razorback sucker, a native large-river catostomid of the lower Colorado River. The document does not describe its morphology or taxonomy in detail.
- **Juvenile stock:** About 600 one-year-old fish. Mean total length was 156 mm (range 124–286 mm). Mean weight was 49 g (range roughly 20–1270 g, per OCR).
- **Source and marking:** Fish were trapped at the [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]]. The left pelvic fin was clipped for future identification using [[Fin Clipping]].
- **Transport and release:** Fish were transported to a training structure backwater downstream of Parker, Arizona (river mile 167). This backwater had received earlier razorback stocking. Transport survival was 99% (5 mortalities).
- **Sampling design:** Five trammel nets (44 m long, 1.5 m wide, 2½-inch stretch mesh) and six large minnow traps were set throughout the backwater before release. Nets were placed on the east bank, perpendicular to the shoreline, and did not fully span the backwater width. Minnow traps were set in deeper areas (1.5–2 m) near the midline.
- **Predator sampling:** Stomachs of resident fish were removed and examined under a dissecting microscope (7X–30X). Captured fish included [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Warmouth]], [[Common Carp]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and [[Green Sunfish]] (the latter appearing in the trammel net catch table).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The release backwater is a slow-water area near the shoreline. Roughly 250 m of shoreline was seined, and the bank is primarily sandy beach intermixed with bulrush (*Scirpus*) growth.
- **Post-release movement:** Net checks showed dispersal activity soon after release. One razorback was caught in Net 1 about two hours after stocking, and another was caught at 2200 hours. Captures at the 0100–0600 check and the 0400–0500 check suggested nocturnal movement along the shoreline. Most captures occurred in nets nearest the Colorado River confluence and near the shore portions of the net sets.
- **Predator community:** Resident predators included channel catfish, largemouth bass, bluegill, warmouth, and other sunfish. Forage species included threadfin shad and common carp.
- **Larval fish availability:** Razorback larvae were extremely abundant in the backwater one day after stocking. No larvae of any other fish species were encountered at that time, which supports the identification of ingested larvae as razorback sucker.
- **Diet composition:** Food items were reported as percent occurrence for each predator species. Bluegill diets contained a high share of organic matter, chironomid larvae, and other invertebrates. Full percentages are presented in the report's tables.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and survival**
- About 600 juvenile razorback suckers were stocked into the Parker backwater, with 99% survival during transport.
- Nets caught razorback suckers within hours of stocking, indicating rapid dispersal and nearshore movement.

**Capture results**
- [[Trammel Net]] sets captured razorback suckers on the night of stocking. No razorback suckers were captured in the 19 May netting.
- The [[Minnow Trap]] sets caught no fish of any species at any time.
- The 250 m shoreline seine caught no razorback suckers.
- Trammel net catch totals for all species were tabulated by net and time, and the report notes an absence of total catch at the 0400–0500 check.

**Predator stomach analysis**
- **Channel catfish (8 fish, 2 May sampling):** No razorback suckers were present in any stomach.
- **Largemouth bass (2 fish):** Both contained one razorback sucker each (approximately 170 mm total length and 110 mm standard length, per OCR).
- **19 May sampling:** 9 channel catfish and 4 largemouth bass were examined. None contained razorback suckers.
- **Larval fish in predator stomachs:** Larval fish were found in 10 of 428 stomachs examined. The report notes that species identification remains incomplete because larvae were digested rapidly, so the material is provisionally listed as "larval fish."
- **Bluegill as primary consumer:** Eight of the 10 larval-fish stomachs came from bluegill. Three of these bluegill were collected in the Parker Division backwater about one day after the initial larval planting.
- **Larval density:** Bluegill with larval fish averaged 4.75 larvae per stomach (range 1–18).
- **Other larval predators:** Warmouth and threadfin shad each contained larvae. A large bluegill (roughly 127 mm total length) contained 4 larvae, and a warmouth (roughly 127 mm total length) contained 2.

**Conclusions and next steps**
- Stocked razorback suckers are vulnerable to predation by largemouth bass, and larval razorback suckers are consumed by bluegill and other sunfish.
- Definite confirmation that the ingested larvae are razorback suckers is pending, and additional inspection is required.
- The report presents only preliminary results, and the document ends with a list of consultant hours (40 per week, covering May 4–29, 1987).
