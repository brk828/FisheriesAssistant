# Life History and Ecology of the Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta*) in the Verde River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Technical Guidance Bulletin (July 2000) reports a two-year study of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) in the upper [[Verde River]] (March 1998 to March 2000) and in [[West Clear Creek]] near [[Bullpen Campground]] (June 1998 to December 1999). The species is not federally listed but is a native Arizona sportfish that is nearly extirpated from sections of the Verde and Salt River drainages. The study covered species composition, habitat selection, population size, movement, age and growth, and reproduction. Roundtail chub were mostly found in pools, and the population in the upper Verde River was self-sustaining but apparently recruitment-limited. Recruitment appears to be linked to late-winter/early-spring floods, and nonnative predators and altered flow regimes are identified as the main threats. The bulletin ends with seven management recommendations.

## Background & Description

The study was funded under [[Federal Aid in Sportfish Restoration]] Project F-14-R and was conducted by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Research Branch. The stated objectives were to describe species composition, distribution and abundance; quantify habitat selection by life stage; estimate population size; quantify movement; determine age and growth; and describe reproductive capacity. Life-history information from both streams was then used to identify management and conservation options.

The roundtail chub is a large cyprinid. Fish in the upper Verde River ranged from 65 to 428 mm total length (TL), with a mean of 276 mm. Wild fish ranged from age-0 to age-7, and the largest individuals reached about 407 mm TL at age-7 (back-calculated, otolith method).

**Study sites:**
- **Upper Verde River:** The Verde River is one of the largest perennial rivers in the [[Gila River Basin]]. It originates at 1,325 m at Sullivan Lake, flows about 300 km southeast, and joins the [[Salt River]] at 402 m. The upper 72 km is among the few remaining unregulated desert Southwest river reaches. The drainage area is about 8,148 km², with a base flow of 0.57 m³/s at USGS gage #09503700 near Paulden. Floods above 400 m³/s occur roughly every 5 to 10 years. Five sampling sites were used, including [[Croll Property]], [[FR-638]], Perkinsville, and Sycamore Creek.
- **West Clear Creek:** This tributary of the Verde River heads on the Mogollon Plateau at 1,811 m and runs about 60 km through a deeply incised gorge. It drains about 262 km² and has a base flow of about 0.45 m³/s. Its high-gradient, cobble/boulder reaches have sheer cliff walls, and spring floods are more frequent because of snowmelt.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types:** The upper Verde River has cobble/boulder high-gradient riffles, low-gradient pebble/gravel riffles, and runs and glides over gravel or sand. West Clear Creek is mainly high-gradient with cobble/boulder substrate.

**Native versus nonnative fish:** In the upper Verde River, roundtail chub made up a large share of the catch alongside [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), desert sucker (*Catostomus clarkii*), and longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*). Native fishes dominated catches at all sites in both years. Nonnative species were most abundant at downstream sites. The study describes this native dominance as unusual compared with many Arizona streams. West Clear Creek was also mostly native-dominated.

**Predator density:** Nonnative potentially predatory fish were less dense in West Clear Creek (3.2 fish/1,000 m²) than in the upper Verde River (8.6 fish/m²).

**Flood-driven recruitment:** Age-0 roundtail chub appeared after floods and were absent after a year without a late-winter/early-spring flood. The authors link this pattern to recruitment success.

**Thermal and light refuge:** Pools provide shade in otherwise sunlit, shallow, open water. The authors note that *Gila* species tend to avoid direct sunlight, so pools may be important thermal refuges.

## Key Findings

### 1. Habitat selection
- **Upper Verde River:** Glides were the most available macro-habitat, but adults primarily used pools and avoided riffles and glides. Among the nine pool types, adults preferred backwater pools (boulder-formed), pocket water, and corner pools, and avoided lateral scour pools. Adults used depths of 21–50 cm and 51–100 cm most often, avoided depths under 20 cm, and selected velocities under 20 cm/s. Adults avoided shoreline and overhanging vegetation and selected instream boulders.
- **West Clear Creek:** Glides were the most available macro-habitat, but all three size classes primarily used pools. Pocket water (50.7%) and plunge pools (32.7%) were the dominant pool types. Juveniles and young-of-year (YOY) avoided lateral scour pools and selected pocket water. Juveniles selected instream boulders and avoided emergent vegetation, instream trees, and undercut banks. Of YOY, 79% were collected from interstices under boulders or in slack water behind them.

The Croll Property estimate was almost twice that at FR-638. CPUE did not differ significantly between the two sites. Nonnative predator percent composition and CPUE were higher at FR-638, which suggests that nonnative fish may affect that population more.

### 3. Movement (PIT-tagged fish > 200 mm TL)
- 240 fish were tagged at Croll Property and 121 at FR-638. Movement was recorded for 43 and 27 recaptured fish, respectively, giving 70 total, of which 21 were recaptured more than once.
- Mean net displacement was 263.9 m (range 0.0–5.7 km). After removing eight fish that moved more than 1 km, the mean was 96.8 m.
- 55.7% of fish moved less than 100 m, and net movements were split evenly upstream and downstream.
- Movement did not vary seasonally or with length.
- 83% of recaptured fish were from pools, the smallest macro-habitat by area.
- 18 fish showed site fidelity, recaptured where tagged, with a mean residence time of 325 ± 32 days.
- These movement distances were similar to those reported for [[Roundtail Chub]] in [[Aravaipa Creek]].

### 4. Age and growth
- 280 fish (50–415 mm TL) were sacrificed for [[Otolith Aging]]. Annuli form once a year, around May, and were validated by marginal increment analysis and by hatchery-reared known-age fish. Three independent readers agreed 100% of the time on known-age fish.
- In 1998, the majority were age-5 (44%) from the 1993 year-class, followed by age-3 (36%) from 1995. In 1999, age-6 (42%) and age-4 (28%) dominated. Both dominant year-classes followed late-winter/early-spring floods.
- Back-calculated length at age 1–7 (otoliths): 101, 182, 248, 301, 344, 379, 407 mm TL. Using PIT-tag data: 101, 176, 234, 277, 309, 333, 351 mm. The authors found the PIT-tag data more reliable for ages below age-4.
- Mean annual growth from PIT-tag recaptures: 33.7 mm (200–250 mm TL), 21.4 mm (251–300 mm), 17.8 mm (301–350 mm), and 10.3 mm (> 350 mm).
- Lengths at age were much larger than those reported for [[Roundtail Chub]] in the Gila River Basin in New Mexico, Fossil Creek, and the Green River, Utah. The authors attribute most of the difference to scale-based aging in earlier studies.
- West Clear Creek fish were much smaller than upper Verde River fish. The authors suggest this reflects habitat availability and the distance-upstream size gradient.

### 5. Reproductive capacity
- Sex data came from 207 fish, with a male-to-female ratio of 151:56 (about 3:1).
- Length-weight exponents were 3.161 (males), 3.151 (females), and 3.022 (both sexes), indicating isometric growth. Males and females grew at the same rate.
- Males averaged 277 mm TL. The smallest male with milt was 156 mm (age-2). Females averaged 329 mm TL, and the smallest mature female was 189 mm (age-2 or age-3).
- Gonadosomatic index peaked in April and dropped sharply in May. Larvae were collected April 24, 1998.
- Fecundity ranged from 7,267 to 26,903 eggs. The average reproducing female was age-5, 328 mm TL, and produced 13,948 eggs.
- Forty-two spawning observations were recorded. On May 16–17, 1999, spawning was documented in the Verde River about 20 m upstream of the Sycamore Creek confluence. Eggs were adhesive and ranged from 1.8 to 3.8 mm (mean 2.7 mm, n = 39). Spawning occurred over clean gravel at the riffle-to-glide transition, at 31.6 cm depth, 43.8 cm/s velocity, and 18.3 °C.
- [[Sonora Sucker]] spawned at the same location 3–4 weeks earlier. Roundtail chub spawned about 3 weeks after a small spate and suckers about 1 week after, which the authors suggest may be a common spawning cue for several southwestern native fishes.

### 6. Biological interactions
- **Predation:** Six nonnative predatory fish species were examined. Roundtail chub, and native fishes as a whole, made up a small share of their diets. Crayfish and aquatic invertebrates dominated by number and weight. Most native fish prey were 34–90 mm long, suggesting that predation may limit recruitment more than adult survival.
- **Smallmouth bass impact:** The estimated population of [[Smallmouth Bass]] > 100 mm at Croll Property was 183 (95% CI 153–229) in fall 1999. Many bass had consumed prey fish, and the authors project that at a weekly consumption rate of one roundtail chub per bass, the population could consume about 19,302 roundtail chub. This is a hypothetical scenario, not a measured outcome.
- **Parasites:** Of roundtail chub examined, 22% were infected with the parasitic copepod [[Lernaea Parasitic Copepod]], with the highest prevalence (76%) in fall 1999 and a maximum intensity of 19 per fish. Infection rates did not differ significantly among season or year.
- **Health:** The mean mHAI (Modified Health Assessment Index) was 9.4 in 1997, which the authors read as "good" condition. A lower mHAI indicates better health.
- **Asian fish tapeworm:** Infected fish were significantly smaller than uninfected fish. The authors suggest the [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]] may slow growth, increase susceptibility to infestation, and increase mortality, possibly affecting other native cyprinids.

### 7. Angler survey (May–June 2000)
- 105 anglers were interviewed. 32% of those who had heard of roundtail chub knew it was an Arizona sportfish.
- 80% of anglers who had heard of the species were unaware of the current fishing regulations.
- 53% had never caught a roundtail chub.
- Anglers who had caught the species described it as "fun to catch," said they liked "the way they fight," and called it "similar to trout but not as good."
- Among anglers who were unaware of the species, 43% said they were interested in fishing for it after learning about it but did not know how, where, or with what bait.

### 8. Management recommendations
The authors conclude that habitat alteration, nonnative predators, and regulation of stream discharge have the greatest potential to limit roundtail chub. They recommend:
1. Protect or restore habitats that provide distinct runs/glides, riffles, and pools for resource partitioning.
2. Prioritize sections with a natural hydrograph, especially those with late-winter/early-spring floods (about 20–50 m³/s) and an intact riparian corridor.
3. Avoid introducing or spreading undesirable exotic fishes.
4. Suppress nonnative fish and possibly crayfish densities to a minimum.
5. The current regulations (1 fish per day, minimum 13 inches / about 330 mm) and angling effort likely allow roundtail chub to reach maturity and spawn several times, so they are probably not having a severe negative impact.
6. Increase public awareness of the species' sportfish status and of how and where to fish for it.
7. Continue PIT-tag collection and monitoring to track population status and trend in the upper Verde River.
