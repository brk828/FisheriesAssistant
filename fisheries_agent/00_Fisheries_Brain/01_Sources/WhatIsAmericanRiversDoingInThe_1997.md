# What Is American Rivers Doing in the Southwest?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-ecology #endangered-species #tyrannidae #colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1997 advocacy memo from the Southwest Center for Biological Diversity, along with an accompanying newspaper letter, addressing institutional conflict within the **Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program ([[MSCP]])**. The source is **not a primary scientific study** of fish morphology or physiology; rather, it is a policy critique concerning the failure of the MSCP steering committee to protect the endangered **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]** population at **[[Lake Mead]]**, and a broader call to restore **native fisheries, wildlife, and habitat** on the **Lower Colorado River and Delta**. It documents institutional/regulatory conflict between conservation organizations (Southwest Center, Sierra Club, Defenders of Wildlife) and American Rivers over how aggressively to challenge water/power industry interests controlling river management.

## Background & Description

No morphological, anatomical, or physiological data on fish or other taxa are presented in this source. The document is administrative/political in nature and does not describe body plans, coloration, size classes, or diagnostic taxonomic features of any species. The only species named explicitly is the **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]**, a passerine bird (not a fish), for which no morphological description is given — only its conservation status and population trends are discussed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat system**: The **[[Lower Colorado River]]** and its **delta**, including the reservoir **[[Lake Mead]]**, are identified as the focal ecosystem. The river corridor supports riparian vegetation used by the flycatcher and unspecified **native fish populations**.
- **Anthropogenic alteration**: The river has undergone "decades of alteration" tied to water and power development, which the [[MSCP]] was nominally created to address by balancing endangered species/habitat needs against human water and power demands.
- **Reservoir management impacts**: Lake Mead water levels are directly implicated in flycatcher habitat viability — a biologist's Biological Opinion concluded that lake levels needed to be lowered to prevent extinction of the flycatcher population at that site, indicating hydrological management (reservoir stage) as a limiting ecological factor for riparian/aquatic habitat.
- **Water year context**: 1997 was designated a "surplus water year" on the Lower Colorado, with the memo asserting that releasing three million acre-feet would not create shortages in basin states — framing water release volume as an ecologically relevant management lever with contested socio-political interpretation.
- **Delta ecosystem**: The Colorado River Delta is named as a target for restoration of "native fisheries, wildlife and habitats," implying degradation of delta aquatic/fish communities from upstream flow alteration, though no specific fish taxa are enumerated.

## Key Findings

- The **[[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]]**, **[[Sierra Club]]**, and **[[Defenders of Wildlife]]** proposed a conservation plan to restore native fisheries and habitat on the Lower Colorado River and Delta; **American Rivers declined to participate**, instead focusing efforts within the industry-influenced **[[MSCP]]**.
- The MSCP charter explicitly prioritizes maintaining and maximizing current water and power development, constraining its capacity for substantive river management reform.
- The MSCP steering committee **rejected calls to review or save** the southwestern willow flycatcher population at Lake Mead.
- A U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service **Biological Opinion** (later overturned through internal agency reassignment) found that Lake Mead's water level needed to be lowered to prevent flycatcher extinction; the authoring biologist was removed from the case, and the opinion was subsequently altered at the regional office level, ultimately **authorizing destruction of the entire population**.
- The agency approved **"take" of 25% of the entire flycatcher subspecies**, including three complete populations in Arizona, despite acknowledging that "extinction is foreseeable" for the species.
- Two agency flycatcher biologists resigned in protest over the handling of the Lake Mead case.
- The Southwest Center pursued **two lawsuits and one appeal** to protect the flycatcher, supported by an amicus brief from Defenders of Wildlife and op-eds from the Sierra Club; American Rivers declined to take a position on this litigation.
- American Rivers also declined to co-sign a letter to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service seeking clarification on which entity would hold the MSCP **take permit** and that entity's existing take of endangered species.
- The memo frames this dispute as symptomatic of **fragmentation within the conservation coalition** on Colorado River management, driven by differing tolerance for compromise with water/power industry stakeholders within the MSCP process.
