# The Effect of Black Bullhead Catfish and Bluegill Removals on the Fish Population of a Small Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Alfred Houser]] and [[Bob Grinstead]] of the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] tested whether removing large amounts of [[Black Bullhead Catfish]] and [[Bluegill]] from a small lake would improve the remaining fish community. Fish were trapped, marked and recaptured from 1958 to 1960 at [[Ardmore Rod and Gun Club South Lake]]. Removal rates reached as high as 42 lb/acre for a single species and 50 lb/acre combined. The authors concluded that removals alone did not produce greatly improved fish populations. [[Redear Sunfish]] and [[Warmouth]] showed gains, while [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[White Crappie]] showed more detrimental than beneficial effects.

## Background & Description

The study site is a 17.24-acre impoundment built in 1896 for recreation. It holds 111.9 acre-feet at full pool and reaches a maximum depth of 18 ft. Cattails and bulrush fringe the shallows, and dense [[Aquatic Vegetation]] (Ceratophyllum demersum and Chara sp.) grows to about 8 ft. The water is clear and the bottom is smooth mud.

Earlier, in 1955, the lake was treated with [[Rotenone Treatment]] to reduce excessive [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Common Carp]]. The partial-kill treatment eradicated both species. Continued sampling after that treatment was reported by [[Jenkins 1955]] on gizzard shad effects in small Oklahoma lakes.

By summer 1958 large populations of black bullhead and bluegill had developed. Neither species was valued by club members, and few were harvested. The club therefore agreed to remove them in subsequent studies, which created a test of whether removing less desirable species would benefit more desirable ones.

**Methods**

- **Gear:** Double-funnel cylindrical nylon traps, 3 ft in diameter and 6 ft long, with five steel hoops and 3/4-inch square-mesh webbing. All captured fish were measured, weighed and fin-clipped before release.
- **Trapping effort:** Trapping periods lasted 9 to 25 days. Trap lifts ranged from 131 (summer 1958) to 655 (winter 1960).
- **Population estimation:** Released fish were estimated by the [[Schnabel Estimator]] (maximum likelihood, mark and recapture). Removed fish were estimated by the [[DeLury Method]], a linear regression based on declining catch per unit effort.
- **Randomization:** Trap locations were moved and fish were scattered at release. In the final estimate, a random station design used a 74-square grid of 100-ft cells and a table of random numbers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Population size, size structure, condition, and growth were all examined. Size classes followed [[Swingle Harvestable Size Criteria]] (1950), which relate to [[Balanced Fish Populations]]. The authors noted that sampling error from too few trapping days, gear selectivity and possible [[Homing Behavior]] limited precision, though the trends across the three-year series were considered reliable.

Population dynamics were shaped by the removals and by [[Natural Mortality]] and angling. Total lake numbers were nearly unchanged at the end of the study, but total weight remained about one-third below the starting value. Overall, the study suggests that fish community responses depend on water conditions as well as on the removal of unwanted species.

## Key Findings

**Population size and removals**

- The summer 1958 estimate was 55,965 fish, close to the winter 1960 estimate of 53,583.
- Total standing-crop weight was 6,379 lb in summer 1958 and 4,211 lb in winter 1960.
- The largest single-species removal was 42 lb/acre of black bullheads in 1958. The largest combined removal was 50 lb/acre in winter 1960.
- Removals reduced the total standing crop by as much as 19 percent.
- The authors reported that 3,827 black bullheads weighing 1,149 lb were removed before the last estimate. Up to 96 percent of the estimated population was taken in winter 1958.

**Harvestable-size fish (A-value)**

- The A-value, the percent of the population of harvestable size, was 41 in summer 1958 and rose to 63 by winter 1958.
- It fell sharply after the first removal, reached 55 in summer 1960, and declined to 35 in winter 1960.

**Species-level results**

- **Largemouth bass:** Estimates had wide confidence limits and the sample was small. There were no measurable gains in numbers or size. Growth rates decreased.
- **White crappie:** Numbers fluctuated widely. No reproduction was seen after 1958. Standing crop and growth declined, and the only benefit was a slightly improved condition index.
- **Redear sunfish:** This was the only species with important gains. Young-fish growth improved, reproduction was high in all three years, and the population increased sharply in both numbers and weight.
- **Warmouth:** Numbers increased, growth accelerated, condition improved and more desirable-size fish were produced. The population stayed too small to noticeably improve angling.
- **Bluegill:** The authors judged this species most seriously affected. Growth and condition declined, small fish increased and fewer desirable sizes remained. The winter 1960 estimate of 8,688 fish was almost identical to the post-removal summer count, but weight had fallen by 253 lb.
- **Black bullhead:** This species showed a remarkable ability to recover. Numbers in winter 1960 were nearly the same as in 1958 despite heavy removals. Growth declined for young fish, and fish older than three years had apparently been eliminated by 1960.

**Condition and growth**

- Largemouth bass and bluegill condition indexes decreased. Redear condition was unchanged except for a summer 1960 decrease attributed to spawning.
- White crappie, warmouth and black bullhead condition indexes increased, which the authors attributed to environmental improvement from the removals.
- Growth rates from scale analysis, based on collections made since 1955, declined for white crappie and bluegill. Redear older fish grew more slowly, while warmouth growth increased steadily after removals began.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- The removal of less desirable species by trapping did not immediately produce greatly improved populations of more desirable species.
- Other factors, including aquatic plant abundance and water fertility, probably have equally important effects on largemouth bass and white crappie production.
- The authors recommended additional measures to achieve favorable results: [[Aquatic Vegetation Control]], [[Pond Fertilization]] and [[Corrective Stocking]], alone or in combination.
