# Colorado River Endangered Fishes Critical Habitat: 1993 Draft Biological Support Document

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) draft document supports a proposed designation of [[Critical Habitat]] for four [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes listed as endangered: the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The document was prepared in September 1993 to inform Federal, State, Tribal, local agencies and the public, and it accompanies an economic analysis. It reviews each species' distribution, status and life history, then applies a set of [[Primary Constituent Elements]] and selection criteria to identify river reaches for protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The proposal was driven by litigation over the Service's failure to designate critical habitat concurrently with the razorback sucker's 1991 listing.

## Background & Description

**Legal and procedural history.** The Colorado squawfish and humpback chub were listed as endangered on March 11, 1967, and the bonytail on April 23, 1980, all without critical habitat. A 1978 squawfish proposal (623 miles) was withdrawn to comply with 1978 Act amendments. The razorback sucker was proposed for threatened status in 1978, withdrawn in 1980, petitioned for endangered listing in 1989, and listed without critical habitat on October 23, 1991. After a [[Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund]] suit, the court ruled on October 27, 1992 that the Service had violated the Act. The proposed rule was published January 29, 1993 (58 FR 6578).

**Basin setting.** The [[Colorado River Basin]] drains about 242,000 square miles in the United States and about 2,000 square miles in Mexico. The 1922 Colorado River Compact divides it into the [[Upper Basin]] (headwaters to Lee's Ferry, Arizona) and the [[Lower Basin]] (Lee's Ferry to the Mexican border).

**Fish fauna.** Carlson and Muth (1989) identified 36 native fishes, while the document's Table 1 lists 38 taxa. Of these, eight (21 percent) occur throughout the Basin, six (15 percent) in the Upper Basin, and 24 (63 percent) in the Lower Basin. Several species are extinct, including the Las Vegas dace, Pahranagat spinedace and Monkey Springs pupfish. Of the remaining native species, 44 percent are federally listed, 28 percent are federal candidates, and only 20 percent have not been identified as potentially needing protection. Over 70 percent appear on State special-status lists.

**Species profiles.**
- **Razorback Sucker:** Once among the most abundant mainstem fishes, historically occurring across about 3,500 miles of the Basin. Populations now consist mostly of old adults with little recruitment.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** The largest North American minnow, with historical weights exceeding 80 lb. Native populations are restricted to the Upper Basin, and the species is extirpated from the Lower Basin.
- **Humpback Chub:** Endemic to the Basin and restricted to remote, whitewater canyons. The largest population is in the Little Colorado River and Grand Canyon.
- **Bonytail Chub:** The rarest native fish in the Colorado River. It persists mainly as a small number of old adults (40 years or more) in [[Lake Mohave]] and other Lower Basin reservoirs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Floodplain dependence.** The document emphasizes that seasonally inundated floodplains, bottomlands, backwaters and oxbows supply nutrients, terrestrial food, warm low-velocity nursery habitat, and cover. Loss of floodplain inundation in the Missouri Basin was associated with a reduction of as much as 98 percent of fish biomass (Karr and Schlosser 1978). The native fishes evolved with a high-spring, low-winter flow regime, which dams and diversions have largely eliminated.

**Threats.** Declines are attributed to habitat loss from flow, temperature and sediment regulation; fragmentation by dams that blocks genetic exchange; introduced nonnative fishes that prey on or compete with natives; and other human disturbances. Relevant regional problems include:
- [[Glen Canyon Dam]] cold, sediment-free releases and fluctuating flows in Marble and Grand Canyons.
- [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] reduction in the Green River spring peak (from about 8,000 cfs to about 4,000 cfs at Greendale).
- [[Selenium Contamination]] in Green River return flows, implicated in razorback larval mortality.
- Oil spill of about 10,000 gallons into the Yampa River in June 1989, linked to reduced squawfish larval and young-of-year abundance.

**Barriers.** The Redlands Diversion Dam on the Gunnison River is described as a complete barrier to upstream fish passage. Government Highline Dam and Price-Stubb Dam on the Colorado River also block passage.

**Reservoir and hatchery roles.** Razorback suckers have survived for decades in reservoirs but show no recruitment there. Coves and bays in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Mead]] are considered analogs of riverine backwaters. Hatchery work at Willow Beach and Dexter National Fish Hatcheries has produced bonytail and razorback fry.

## Key Findings

- **Razorback Sucker critical habitat:** The Service proposed 15 reaches totaling 1,824 miles, about 52 percent of the species' historical range. Upper Basin reaches total 944 miles and Lower Basin reaches total 880 miles. The largest remaining riverine population is in the upper [[Green River]] (estimated 758 to 1,138 fish, Lanigan and Tyus 1989), and the largest overall population is in [[Lake Mohave]] (about 60,000 adults).
- **Recruitment failure:** No significant recruitment has been documented in recent years for razorback suckers. Juveniles have been extremely rare since the early 1950s, and bonytail recruitment is apparently nonexistent or very low.
- **Spawning timing and conditions:** Razorback spawning in the Upper Basin runs April through mid-June at 48 to 63°F. Lower Basin spawning runs January through April at 53 to 64°F. Colorado squawfish spawn June to mid-August at 64 to 77°F, peaking at 72 to 77°F. Humpback chub spawn soon after peak spring flows at about 68°F.
- **Squawfish migrations:** Adults may migrate up to 200 miles to reach spawning areas. One radio-tagged fish moved about 200 miles between April and September 1982.
- **Squawfish reintroductions:** More than 300,000 squawfish have been introduced in the Salt and Verde Rivers, Arizona, but no reproducing Lower Basin population has been confirmed.
- **Humpback chub populations:** More than 21,000 adult and juvenile humpback chub were collected in the Little Colorado and Colorado Rivers in Grand Canyon from 1987 onward. In Cataract Canyon, 108 fish (22 adults, 56 juveniles) were collected from 1985 to 1988. A spawning population persists in Yampa Canyon, with 32 fish in breeding condition captured from 1986 to 1988.
- **Bonytail counts:** 32 adults were collected from Lake Mohave from 1974 to 1987, with 16 more in 1988 and 1989. Vanicek and Kramer (1969) estimated growth at about 2, 4 and 6 inches over the first three years, with a 15-inch, 7-year-old maximum.
- **Hatchery performance (bonytail):** Hatching success was 35 percent at 59°F, 32 percent at 68°F, and 0.5 percent at 77°F. Fecundity in a 1983 trial ranged from 1,015 to 10,384 eggs per fish, averaging 4,677.
- **Regulatory framework:** Critical habitat under Section 3(5)(A) includes occupied and unoccupied areas essential to conservation, and it applies to Federal actions via Section 7 consultation. Designation does not create a management plan or population goals. Only areas in the 100-year floodplain containing the constituent elements qualify, and previously developed areas are excluded.
- **Lake Mohave significance:** The population provided primary brood stock for hatchery propagation. Recent cove-culture efforts released 153 fish from the 1992 year class, with five recaptured in March 1993.
- **Next steps:** Final designation follows review of public comments and the economic analysis. Management needs identified include fish passage at dams, flow reoperation (notably the [[Aspinall Unit]] on the Gunnison River), nonnative fish control, and protection of bottomlands and backwaters.
