# Ecological Studies of Aquatic Habitats in Death Valley National Monument, with Special Reference to Saratoga Springs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This annual progress report, compiled by [[James E Deacon]] with contributions from [[James F LaBounty]], [[Carol J James]] and others, documents a multi-year ecological program on isolated desert spring and marsh systems in [[Death Valley National Monument]]. The core study site is [[Saratoga Springs]], home to the endemic pupfish [[Cyprinodon nevadensis nevadensis]]. The report covers climate and water level changes, spring and marsh water chemistry, paleoecology (pollen coring), food utilization, population size and survivorship, temperature relations, community metabolism and primary production, and early work on [[Devils Hole]] and the pupfish [[Cyprinodon diabolis]]. Funding was provided by the [[National Science Foundation]] and the [[National Park Service]]. Most sections present preliminary data; several analyses are explicitly deferred to later reports.

## Background & Description

The Saratoga Springs system comprises a small, constant-temperature main spring pool (about 68 m² in the sampled pond) that drains into a shallow, highly variable marsh and an adjacent dry Big Lake basin. The pupfish *C. n. nevadensis* is described as a distinct subspecies, and the report notes several endemic invertebrates, including a snail, a beetle and a mosquito. Fish sampled for food analysis averaged 35.67 mm standard length and 2.22 g in weight, with males consistently larger than females.

[[Devils Hole]], in Ash Meadows, Nye County, Nevada, is the habitat of *C. diabolis*, a species about which little was previously known. Earlier work was limited to taxonomic studies and general observations by Wales (1920), Miller (1948) and La Rivers (1960).

Climate records show air temperatures from 27°F (20 December 1965) to 132°F (June–July 1966) at ground-level stations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spring versus marsh:** Spring water chemistry is nearly identical between the two spring sources and has changed little since first analysis in 1917. The marsh is at least seasonally far more concentrated and temperature-variable. The report characterizes the spring as extremely stable and the marsh as extremely variable.
- **Water level:** Big Lake and the Amargosa River channel declined steadily through spring and summer 1967. The river bed went dry on 29 July 1967 and the auger holes on 2 September 1967; both were still dry on 16 November 1967. Groundwater and river levels fall faster than marsh levels, consistent with the absence of direct springs recharging them.
- **Paleoecology:** Four pollen cores were collected from the marsh by [[Peter J Mehringer]] at the University of Arizona Geochronology Laboratory. The cores record about three vegetation changes, interspersed with three drier periods. A seed sample was submitted for radiocarbon dating.
- **Temperature regime:** The main spring pool stays at 25.5–29.5 °C. Marsh water and bottom muds fluctuate widely; the top 5 cm of silt moderates temperature, and fish were observed buried in silt during hot and cold periods.
- **Vegetation:** [[Ceratophyllum demersum]] dominates the spring pool, with [[Ruppia maritima]] providing some cover on the bottom.
- **Birds:** Birds were mostly associated with the marsh and riparian habitat, with few observed on salt flats or desert.

## Key Findings

**Food utilization (Leser and Deacon).** Analysis of 756 fish collected monthly (October 1965 to March 1967), of which 270 contained food, found invertebrates made up the bulk of the diet, contrary to an earlier herbivory hypothesis by La Rivers (1962). Frequency of occurrence was highest for algae, gastropods, amphipods, coleopterans, odonates and hemipterans. Vascular plant material appeared undigested and may have been ingested incidentally. Seasonal shifts were significant: amphipods dominated in spring, algae in spring and summer, gastropods in fall and winter, and coleopterans peaked in winter. Using a digestion-rate method adapted from [[Darnell and Meierotto]], feeding chronology showed two peaks: a stronger morning peak (starting about 0600, maximum near 0800–0900, declining by 1000) and a longer afternoon peak beginning near 1300 with maximum near 1500. Feeding periods matched periods of highest trap catch, supporting a bimodal diurnal activity pattern.

**Population size.** Mark-recapture estimates (Petersen and Schnabel methods) produced order-of-magnitude estimates, with the most reliable (Schnabel, 8–10 July 1966) accurate to roughly ±58 fish. Estimates were generally within about ±100 individuals. The population was low in February–April and high in September.

**Survivorship and growth.** Life tables derived from length-frequency data (using total length as a proxy for age, following [[Deevey]] 1947) indicated high early survival, a period of relatively steady mortality, and reduced late mortality. Growth averaged about 3 mm per month. The smallest fish captured in standard traps was 14 mm.

**Temperature tolerance.** Maximum tolerance was reached near 42–43 °C in observations; loss of equilibrium occurred below 8–10 °C, although some marsh fish swam weakly at 2.8 °C in January 1967. Trap catches declined at midday when water temperatures exceeded about 32 °C in September.

**Community metabolism and primary production.** Diurnal oxygen curves over a 53-hour period (November 1967) gave gross primary production of 1.42 mg/l DO/m²/day (0800 17 Nov to 0800 18 Nov) and 1.36 mg/l/day (1600 to 1600), with a 1.36 estimate from another interval. Light-dark bottle estimates showed plankton production was negligible, and [[Ceratophyllum demersum]] accounted for virtually all primary production. Fish made up most consumer biomass and respiration. Total ceratophyll biomass was estimated at 102,023 g wet weight (4,092 g dry), and benthic invertebrates at about 0.48 g/ft², or roughly 253.8 g for the pond. Calorimetry gave caloric values for Ceratophyllum, pupfish, dragonflies and other insects, with a total standing crop of about 21,630 cal in the pond.

**Devils Hole (James).** Begun 10 June 1967 as a master's thesis project. Standard minnow traps failed to capture *C. diabolis*; seines and dip nets proved successful. The population fluctuated from an estimated 790 or more to about 250 individuals. Spawning ceased in mid-September 1967; the last very young fish (5 mm) were seen 10–25 September. Adult males became pale in late October. Notes also recorded insects and bats falling onto the water and seasonal algae growth on the shelf.

**Other work.** [[Fenton Kay]] is studying the herpetofauna of Saratoga Springs (thesis due January 1969). [[James F LaBounty]] is analyzing the taxonomy and ecology of *Cyprinodon salinus* from Salt Creek and Pupfish Marsh (thesis due September 1968).
