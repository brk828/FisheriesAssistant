# Arizona Stream Water Quality, National Water Summary 1990-91

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-quality #hydrology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey state summary describes stream water quality in Arizona. Data come from 13 monitoring stations. Ten stations are used for conditions in water years 1987-89, and all 13 for trends. Irrigation return flow, mine drainage, sewage effluent, and urban runoff degrade water quality in parts of the State, while remote basins stay near natural conditions. Reservoir dilution, sediment trapping, and wet years in 1983-87 explain many of the observed trends. The document covers water chemistry only; no fish or invertebrate data are reported, apart from a mention of the trout fishery below Glen Canyon Dam.

## Background & Description

- **Water use:** About 87 percent of freshwater withdrawals irrigate nearly 1 million acres of crops. In 1985, surface water supplied about 52 percent (3,330 million gallons per day) of withdrawals; of that, about 92 percent went to agriculture and about 7 percent to public supply.
- **Climate:** Annual precipitation ranges from about 5 inches in the Basin and Range Lowlands to about 25 inches in the Central Highlands. More than 95 percent of precipitation evaporates or is transpired by plants.
- **Population:** 3.7 million in 1990, up about 35 percent since 1980, concentrated in Phoenix and Tucson. About 1.2 million people relied on surface water in 1985.
- **Monitoring:** Data are held in the USGS National Water Information System and the EPA's STORET. Data are reported by water year (October 1 to September 30). Analyses follow USGS-approved standard methods.
- **Constituents examined:** fecal coliform bacteria, dissolved sulfate, dissolved solids, dissolved nitrite plus nitrate, total phosphorus, dissolved selenium, and suspended sediment.
- **Trend method:** The seasonal Kendall test, with concentrations adjusted for streamflow where possible. Adjustment was skipped when more than 10 percent of samples fell below the reporting limit or when flow was substantially human-controlled.
- **Pollution designations:** In 1989 the State delineated 380 miles of streams where toxic substances were identified or probable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- [[Colorado River]]: flows from Lake Powell through the [[Grand Canyon]] to Lake Mead and forms Arizona's western border. Daily hydropower releases at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] cause discharge fluctuations of concern for the trout fishery below the dam. Beach and sandbar erosion in the Grand Canyon is a major concern.
- [[Gila River]]: drains most of southern Arizona. The reach from west of Phoenix to site 12 is effluent dominated, because sewage-treatment-plant effluent sustains perennial flow. Boron at site 12 commonly exceeds irrigation limits.
- [[Pinal Creek]]: a small Salt River tributary affected by copper mining near Globe.
- [[Salt River]]: receives saline spring inflow upstream of site 6 and is stored in a series of reservoirs east of Phoenix.
- [[Wet Bottom Creek]]: a Verde River tributary in a roadless wilderness area, underlain by relatively insoluble igneous and volcanic rocks and little affected by human activity.
- [[Verde River]]: mostly grazing land use, with only small irrigation diversions upstream of site 10.
- Table 1 lists the environmental significance of each constituent. Nutrients can cause algal blooms and excessive growth of aquatic plants. Selenium is toxic above trace levels. Suspended sediment can harm aquatic organisms and fill reservoirs. Dissolved solids and contaminants can harm aquatic organisms.

## Key Findings

**Conditions, water years 1987-89 (medians)**

| Site | Result |
|---|---|
| Site 1, Colorado River at Lees Ferry | Dissolved solids 492 mg/L (fourth lowest); suspended sediment 4 mg/L (second lowest, due to trapping in Lake Powell) |
| Site 13, Colorado River at the international boundary | Dissolved solids 730 mg/L, mainly from irrigation return flow below Parker Dam |
| Site 4, Gila River at Calva | Fecal coliform 51 col/100 mL, nitrite plus nitrate 0.69 mg/L, phosphorus 0.09 mg/L (all second highest); suspended sediment 307 mg/L (highest) |
| Site 12, Gila River at Gillespie Dam | Fecal coliform 490 col/100 mL, nitrite plus nitrate 8.7 mg/L, phosphorus 2.3 mg/L (all highest); sulfate 480 mg/L and dissolved solids 2,250 mg/L (second highest) |
| Site 5, Pinal Creek | Sulfate 2,100 mg/L and dissolved solids 3,380 mg/L (both highest), from copper mine drainage |
| Sites 6 and 7, Salt River | Dissolved solids 1,140 mg/L and 537 mg/L; the site 6 value reflects saline springs, and site 7 is diluted by snowmelt and tributary inflow |
| Site 9, Wet Bottom Creek | Sulfate 5.3 mg/L, dissolved solids 170 mg/L, phosphorus 0.01 mg/L; nitrite plus nitrate and selenium both below 1 mg/L and µg/L respectively as reported; all among the lowest |
| Site 10, Verde River | Dissolved solids 358 mg/L; nitrite plus nitrate under 0.1 mg/L |

Site 11 had slightly lower medians than site 10 for fecal coliform, sulfate, dissolved solids, and suspended sediment, probably from tributary dilution.

**Trends**
- **Fecal coliform:** decreased at site 13 and showed no trend at nine stations.
- **Sulfate:** increased at Pinal Creek (site 5), where acidic mine drainage in ground water arrives gradually; sulfate was about 60 percent of dissolved-solids load there in 1988. It decreased at sites 1, 2, 13, 4, 6, and 10, possibly reflecting reduced copper-smelter emissions.
- **Dissolved solids:** increased at site 7, probably from evaporative concentration in dry years. They decreased at site 4 and site 13, probably from dilution in the wet years 1983-87.
- **Nitrite plus nitrate:** increased at site 13 for an unknown cause; decreased at site 1, probably from dilution.
- **Total phosphorus (1982-89):** decreased at sites 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 11, with no trend at six other stations. Improved agricultural practices and less use of phosphate detergents are suggested for some sites.
- **Selenium:** decreased at site 1 (probable dilution) and at site 11 (unknown cause).
- **Suspended sediment:** decreased at sites 1, 7, and 11, mainly from sediment trapping in large upstream reservoirs; the decrease at site 5 has an unknown cause.

**Management**
- The Arizona Department of Environmental Quality (ADEQ), created by the Arizona Environmental Quality Act of 1986, regulates stream water quality and is updating surface-water standards to cover more pollutants, including toxic substances.
- The ADEQ files biennial reports under section 305(b) of the Clean Water Act.
- In 1973, seven States created the Colorado River Basin Salinity Forum, and in 1975 they adopted numerical dissolved-solids standards. The standards are maintained under the Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Act (authorized 1974, amended 1984).
