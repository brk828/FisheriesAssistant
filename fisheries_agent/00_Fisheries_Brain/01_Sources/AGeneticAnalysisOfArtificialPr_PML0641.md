# Desert Fishes Council 1994 Symposium: Endangered Catostomid and Cyprinid Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *Proceedings of the Desert Fishes Council*, 1994 Symposium Volume (published 1995), contains three abstracts on endangered freshwater fishes of western North America. The first is a genetic study of [[Razorback Sucker]] hatchery propagation at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The second describes germplasm storage and sperm cryopreservation for [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The third is an economic assessment of proposed [[Critical Habitat Designation]] for [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]] in the [[Klamath Basin]]. The excerpt also includes a short Spanish-language recommendation against using the few remaining wild fish of the Upper Colorado population in breeding programs.

## Background & Description

The fishes discussed are long-lived or range-restricted native species of the western United States, several of them listed as endangered. The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) are the focus of the two biological abstracts. Their genetic and reproductive work is connected to recovery programs in the [[Colorado River Basin]], where the document also mentions [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) as future candidates for the same methods. The [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*; printed as "tuxans" in the OCR text) and [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*) are the subject of the economic abstract.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Genetic management in hatcheries.** The genetic study is framed by the need for hatchery progeny to reflect the genetic structure of wild populations. Stock for the Dexter hatchery came from [[Lake Mohave]] in 1981 and 1982. The author recommends against using the few remaining wild fish of the Upper Colorado population in a breeding program. The stated reasons are reduced genetic variation caused by [[Genetic Drift]] and a possible increase in unwanted genes through hybridization with the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]].

**Germplasm preservation.** The cryopreservation work addresses genetic resources for species at risk of extinction. The authors developed field methods to collect, evaluate, refrigerate and cryopreserve sperm, a need that is especially pressing when wild stocks are small.

**Socioeconomic setting.** The economic abstract examines a region with habitat-dependent industries and local communities. It considers how riparian, stream and lake conditions relate to human welfare. The analysis method was built after the [[Endangered Species Committee]] hearings on the [[Northern Spotted Owl]].

## Key Findings

**1. Razorback Sucker genetic structure and hatchery progeny (Dexter NFH)**
- Method: 17 allozyme loci previously found polymorphic in razorbacks, plus mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) analysis. Two year classes were compared with the [[Lake Mohave]] population. See [[Allozyme Analysis]].
- Natural (Lake Mohave) population mean heterozygosity: **0.099**.
- Year class 1987 mean heterozygosity: **0.125**. Year class 1990: **0.080**.
- Neither year class differed significantly from Lake Mohave in heterozygosity.
- The 1990 year class had **greatly reduced survivorship** relative to earlier years and **significantly lower mtDNA haplotype diversity** (see [[Mitochondrial DNA Haplotype Diversity]]).
- The authors argue that multiple molecular data sets should be used when planning and monitoring [[Captive Breeding]] programs.

**2. Germplasm storage for Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub**
- Developed and tested methods for sperm collection, evaluation, refrigeration and [[Cryopreservation]].
- Cryopreserved sperm was thawed and used to fertilize eggs. [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] were **successfully produced** with cryopreserved sperm.
- Future work: refine methods and extend them to [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]]. The authors see the approach as useful for [[Germplasm Storage]] and genetic management of endangered fishes.

**3. Economic consequences of critical habitat for Klamath Basin suckers**
- Estimated economic effects of the proposed critical habitat designation by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] for the Lost River Sucker and Shortnose Sucker in the Klamath Basin of Oregon and California.
- Impacts were grouped into four categories: (1) habitat-degrading industries, (2) industries with spillover costs when habitat degrades, (3) groups who see the designation as improving local quality of life, and (4) groups who place intrinsic value on the suckers and their habitat.
- A **worst-case, static estimate** of costs was developed. The study also describes how the local economy would adjust and reduce adverse impacts.
- Long-run benefits to quality of life were judged especially important. Likely gains include better riparian and stream aesthetics, water-based recreation, reduced toxins, reduced peak flows with higher summer flows, and more wildlife.
- The evidence was judged insufficient to quantify all benefits and costs. The authors consider the **net effect on national economic welfare to be close to zero**. Under plausible assumptions, they say one could reasonably conclude that the designation would **increase** national economic welfare.

**4. Recommendation on the Upper Colorado population**
- Stated in the Spanish-language text: do not use the few remaining wild fish of the Upper Colorado population in a breeding program, because of reduced genetic variation from drift and possible hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]].
