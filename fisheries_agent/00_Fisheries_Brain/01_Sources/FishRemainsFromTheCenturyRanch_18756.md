# Fish Remains from the Century Ranch Site (LAn-227), Los Angeles County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #archaeoichthyology

## Executive Summary

This appendix, written by W. I. Follett for the 1962–1963 Annual Report of the [[Archaeological Survey]] (University of California, Los Angeles), identifies fish remains from the [[Century Ranch Site]] (LAn-227). The site lies in an inland valley of the [[Santa Monica Mountains]], about 64 miles by canyon bottom from the mouth of [[Malibu Creek]] at [[Santa Monica Bay]], and is tentatively dated to approximately 500–1300 A.D. The collection contains 2 teeth and 266 vertebrae; 176 specimens were identified at least to genus, representing about 17 taxa in 12 families. All identified fishes are marine. The author concludes that the pelagic and offshore species point to the use of boats by the site's inhabitants, while several inshore species could have been taken from shore.

## Background & Description

The remains were collected by Thomas Blackburn and Ernest Chandonet and are deposited at the Department of Anthropology-Sociology, UCLA, under Accession Number 351. The site sits on a small rise between Las Virgenes Creek and Stokes Creek, just above their junction. It is at an elevation of about 550 feet. The fish discussion is an appendix to a companion report by Blackburn and Chandonet on the site's archaeology.

Most specimens are incomplete or fragmentary. Only centra (vertebral bodies) of the elasmobranch vertebrae persisted, and identifications rest largely on vertebral morphology, with the author noting that many "precaudal" and "caudal" vertebrae are diagnostic only to position group. Vertebral diameter was used as a proxy for estimating the total length and weight of the original fish, using modern size records from [[Fish Literature]] sources such as Fitch (1958) and Walford (1937).

Taxa are grouped by family:
- **Sharks:** [[Mako Shark]] (Lamnidae), [[Leopard Shark]], [[Blue Shark]], and [[Soupfin Shark]] (Carcharhinidae), and [[Pacific Angel Shark]] (Squatinidae).
- **Rays and flatfishes:** [[Shovelnose Guitarfish]] (Rhinobatidae) and [[California Halibut]] (Bothidae).
- **Bony fishes:** [[Kelp Bass]] (Serranidae), [[Pacific Barracuda]] (Sphyraenidae), [[Yellowtail]] (Carangidae), [[Pacific Mackerel]], [[California Bonito]], [[Oceanic Skipjack]], and [[Albacore]] (Scombridae), [[White Seabass]] (Sciaenidae), [[California Sheephead]] (Labridae), and [[Rockfishes]] (Scorpaenidae).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish assemblage is entirely marine, reflecting a coastal-marine resource base. Species were grouped by habitat preference:

- **Pelagic:** Albacore, oceanic skipjack, and (commonly regarded as pelagic) mako and blue sharks.
- **Offshore:** Pacific barracuda, yellowtail, Pacific mackerel, California bonito, and white seabass. The author notes that under primitive conditions, when these fishes were more abundant, they may have approached shore more often than they do today.
- **Shallow-water and nearshore:** Leopard shark, soupfin shark, Pacific angel shark, shovelnose guitarfish, California halibut, kelp bass, and California sheephead.
- **Deep-water:** Larger southern California rockfishes, which live near the sea bottom in water too deep to fish from shore.

The site's position far up the canyon raises the question of freshwater-anadromous access. The absence of [[Steelhead]] remains is noted as surprising, since the species historically ascended streams of the region, including probably [[Malibu Creek]] in wet years.

## Key Findings

**Sample and identification**
- Total: 2 teeth and 266 vertebrae; 176 identified at least to genus; about 17 species in 12 families.
- Specimens are fragmentary; most identifications are tentative and several (e.g., the leopard, soupfin, smoothhound, and sharks with similar centra) are explicitly questionable.

**Taxa and material (vertebrae or centra unless noted)**
- [[Mako Shark]] (*Isurus oxyrinchus*): 2 teeth (1 complete, 27.7 mm extreme length, from a shark about 7 feet long) and 2 centra; identification of the centra is presumptive.
- [[Leopard Shark]] (*Triakis semifasciata*): 6 centra.
- [[Blue Shark]] (*Prionace glauca*): 1 centrum.
- [[Soupfin Shark]] (*Galeorhinus zygopterus*): 7 centra, questionably referred.
- [[Pacific Angel Shark]] (*Squatina californica*): 5 centra, one partly charred.
- [[Shovelnose Guitarfish]] (*Rhinobatos productus*): 15 centra.
- [[California Halibut]] (*Paralichthys californicus*): 11 vertebrae.
- [[Kelp Bass]] (*Paralabrax clathratus*): 5 vertebrae; identification questionable.
- [[Pacific Barracuda]] (*Sphyraena argentea*): 2 vertebrae.
- [[Yellowtail]] (*Seriola dorsalis*): 19 vertebrae, including one charred precaudal.
- [[Pacific Mackerel]] (*Scomber japonicus diego*): 2 vertebrae.
- [[California Bonito]] (*Sarda lineolata*): 18 vertebrae.
- [[Oceanic Skipjack]] (*Katsuwonus pelamis*): 2 vertebrae.
- [[Albacore]] (*Thunnus alalunga*): 41 vertebrae (30 incomplete, 11 fragments), the largest single sample of tunas.
- [[White Seabass]] (*Cynoscion nobilis*): 1 vertebra fragment.
- [[California Sheephead]] (*Pimelometopon pulchrum*): 5 vertebrae.
- [[Rockfishes]] (*Sebastodes* sp.): 29 vertebrae; several species occur regionally but are generally not separable by individual vertebrae.

**Size estimates from vertebral diameter**
- Largest-bodied estimate: a 6th precaudal albacore of 17 mm diameter represents a fish about 3 feet long.
- Yellowtail: a caudal of 19.5 mm represents a fish about 36 inches long and about 16 pounds.
- Pacific barracuda: an incomplete precaudal of 11.5 mm represents a fish about 36 inches long.
- California sheephead: an incomplete 2nd precaudal of 13 mm represents a fish about 27 inches long.

**Depth distribution**
- Most specimens come from the 6–12" through 30–36" levels; a few are recorded from deeper levels (36–42" and 48–51") in the summary table. Several vertebrae are charred.

**Interpretation of fishing methods**
- Pelagic taxa (albacore, skipjack) and deep-water rockfishes indicate boat use. Mako and blue sharks were probably also taken from boats.
- Five offshore species (barracuda, yellowtail, Pacific mackerel, bonito, white seabass) were probably taken mainly from boats but could occasionally have been taken from shore.
- Seven shallow-water taxa could have been taken from shore by hook and line, net, or spear, as well as from boats.
- Supporting evidence includes a 1769 diary entry by Miguel Constansó recording use of boats to catch "bonito" near the mouth of the Ventura River, and reports of yellowtail caught from piers at Avalon and Redondo Beach.

**Regional comparisons**
- Remains of California sheephead were also reported at the [[Chumash]] village site at Muwu, at San Nicolas Island sites, and at the [[Little Harbor Site]] on [[Santa Catalina Island]].

**Absence of freshwater-anadromous fish**
- No [[Steelhead]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) remains occur, despite the species' likely access to Malibu Creek under primitive conditions in years of heavy rainfall.

**Edibility**
- All identified fishes, including the sharks and guitarfish, are edible.
