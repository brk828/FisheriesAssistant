# Fishes of the Headwaters of Shavers Fork

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae #percidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary
This 1956 survey by Frank J. Schwartz documents the first complete inventory of aquatic fauna in the remote headwaters of [[Shavers Fork]], a high-elevation tributary of the [[Cheat River]] in Pocahontas/Randolph County, West Virginia. Reachable only by rail, foot, or a single highway crossing, this stream — reportedly the highest-elevation river flowing east of the Mississippi — supports a specialized cold-water fish assemblage of **5 families and 14 species**, a small subset of the 7 families/50 species known from the broader Cheat River watershed. The paper documents species distribution, spawning behavior, morphology, and habitat associations, and closes with a conservation appeal to preserve the area as a Wild River Area, given its ecological fragility and vulnerability to erosion and mineral extraction impacts on stream chemistry and trout stocking viability.

## Background & Description
Species-level distinguishing features documented in the source:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdneri iridius*): Rosy lateral stripe/band, adipose fin (fleshy lobe between dorsal and caudal fins), small black body spots, spotted tail. Includes a stocked "Golden Rainbow" strain (14–18 in, 1–2.5 lbs at stocking).
- **[[Brook Trout]]** (*Salvelinus fontinalis*): Rosy-orange venter, orange ventral fins edged in white/black, red/blue body spots, worm-like (vermiculated) dorsal markings. Native individuals typically ≤6 in, though a 17.2 in specimen was recorded in 1965.
- **[[Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta*): Brown body with larger black spots (vs. rainbow), reddish-yellow lateral spots, yellow venter; notably **no spots on the tail** (diagnostic vs. rainbow trout).
- **[[White Sucker]]** (*Catostomus c. commersoni*): Protrusible, papillose mouth; many small body scales; breeding males develop crimson/maroon sides and tubercles on caudal/anal fins.
- **[[Northern Hognose Sucker]]** (*Hypentelium nigricans*): Large concave squarish head, five black saddle blotches down the sides, golden/bronze body; breeding tubercles on pelvic, caudal, anal fins.
- **[[River Chub]]** (*Hybopsis micropogon*): Greenish-tan, jaw barbels, overhanging upper jaw, dusky lateral band; breeding males develop bulbous, tuberculate snout.
- **[[Creek Chub]]** (*Semotilus atromaculatus*): Barbeled, dark spot at anterior dorsal fin base, blackish lateral band; up to 8+ in; breeding males show large tubercles on snout, body, and pectoral fins.
- **[[Longnose Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys cataractae*): Black/grey, sleek, barbeled, elongated snout; 6–8 in; breeding males develop tubercles and orange/reddish fins.
- **[[Southern Blacknose Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys atratulus obtusus*): Olive-tan/gray dorsum, black lateral band, white/cream belly; breeding males show orange-maroon lateral band and fine head/body tubercles.
- **[[Stoneroller]]** (*Campostoma anomalum anomalum*): Bronze-copper body, horizontal black dorsal fin bar in males, cartilaginous lower jaw ridge; breeding males develop large horny tubercles.
- **[[Rosyface Shiner]]** (*Notropis rubellus*): Sleek, sharp-nosed, bluish above/silvery below; breeding males show rosy-red head/anterior body coloration with fine tubercles.
- **[[Sharpnose Darter]]** (*Percina oxyrhyncha*): Long-snouted, slender, perch-like, two dorsal fins, long pectoral fins; ~4–5 in; the rarest species recorded.
- **[[Fantail Darter]]** (*Etheostoma flabellare flabellare*): Small (~3 in), barred black/tan body; males possess small knobs on first dorsal fin spines; large pectoral fins used to prop against current.
- **[[Mottled Sculpin]]** (*Cottus bairdi*): Large head, two dorsal fins, large pectoral fins; males have orange-tipped spiny dorsal fin; up to 5 in.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Thermal regime**: Cold, spring-fed headwaters maintain year-round trout habitat; June water temperatures as low as 48°F; snow persists into mid-June; winter ice depth up to 2 ft with heavy anchor ice and spring ice jams near Cheat Bridge.
- **Longitudinal zonation**:
  - Extreme headwaters/smallest tributaries: dominated by native [[Brook Trout]], [[Mottled Sculpin]], [[Southern Blacknose Dace]], occasional darters.
  - Mid-reaches (widening stream): [[Creek Chub]], darters, and additional trout species appear.
  - Larger downstream reaches (Spruce to Cheat Bridge): full 14-species assemblage present, including [[River Chub]], [[Rosyface Shiner]], suckers, and [[Sharpnose Darter]].
- **Substrate/habitat associations**: Stream bottom dominated by boulders, stones, and gravel except at Spruce (sandy, calm pools). Riffle specialists ([[Longnose Dace]], [[Fantail Darter]], [[Mottled Sculpin]]) rely on swift, rocky riffle habitat; [[River Chub]] builds large stone-pile nests (up to 3–4 ft diameter) used communally by multiple species for spawning.
- **Sterile tributaries**: Some western tributaries (e.g., Black Run, Randolph County) are too nutrient-poor/pure to support even native brook trout, illustrating fine-scale habitat filtering.
- **Interspecific competition**: [[Creek Chub]] is described as a vigorous competitor with [[Brook Trout]] for food and space; if water warms, creek chub is expected to displace brook trout as dominant species — an early indicator of climate/thermal sensitivity in this system.
- **Predator-prey dynamics**: The hellbender salamander (*[[Cryptobranchus alleganiensis]]*) preys on small fish, crayfish, worms, and insects at Cheat Bridge. Minnows and darters, along with hognose suckers, compete for and consume river chub nest eggs.
- **Food web role**: Smaller cyprinids and cottids serve primarily as forage for larger game fish (trout) rather than direct human food value.
- **Stocking dynamics**: Hatchery-reared rainbow, brown, and brook trout are stocked by rail as far as Beaver Creek (9 mi south of Cheat Bridge); browns show notable holdover and some natural reproduction.

## Key Findings
- First complete aquatic survey of the Shavers Fork headwaters conducted in 1956 by Frank J. Schwartz; prior work (Goldsborough & Clark 1908; McGavitt & Davis 1935; E. Rainey 1940) was fragmentary.
- Documented **14 species across 5 families** (Salmonidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Percidae, Cottidae) in Shavers Fork south of Cheat Bridge, versus 50 species/7 families in the full Cheat River watershed.
- [[Sharpnose Darter]] is flagged as the rarest species in the entire Cheat River watershed, historically associated with the Greenbrier/New-Kanawha drainage but found to have colonized upstream via Second Fork.
- A "Golden Rainbow" trout strain was experimentally stocked beginning in the early 1960s (103 stocked April 1963; 1,300 more October 1963), but its long-term survival/biology in the wild remained unknown at time of writing.
- Native brook trout populations, historically abundant and occasionally exceeding a pound, persist mainly in extreme headwaters and smaller tributaries; a notably large 17.2-inch specimen was recorded in 1965.
- The paper links watershed integrity directly to fishery quality: deforestation/erosion would widen and warm the channel (reducing depth and shading), while mineral extraction would degrade water chemistry enough to end hatchery trout stocking for ~30 miles downstream of Cheat Bridge.
- Shavers Fork (headwaters to Bowden) was designated a **Wild River Area** under the West Virginia Statewide Recreation Plan, reflecting the conservation urgency raised in this study.
- Associated non-fish fauna noted: dragonflies, damselflies, stoneflies, the crayfish *[[Cambarus bartoni]]*, and the hellbender salamander *[[Cryptobranchus alleganiensis]]*.
