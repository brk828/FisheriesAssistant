## Executive Summary

This document is a **Recovery Plan** for the **Unarmored Threespine Stickleback** (*Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni*), a critically endangered freshwater subspecies endemic to Southern California. Historically distributed across the [[Los Angeles Basin]] (San Gabriel, Los Angeles, and Santa Ana River systems) and the upper [[Santa Clara River]] drainage, the subspecies has been decimated by habitat destruction (urbanization) and genetic swamping via intergradation with the introduced partially armored subspecies *G. a. microcephalus*. By the early 1970s, the **only known surviving population** existed in a precarious 10-mile stretch of the Santa Clara River within **Soledad Canyon**, Los Angeles County. The plan outlines a two-phase conservation strategy involving population surveys, habitat protection, prevention of subspecies intergradation, transplantation to new sites, and captive breeding/propagation research.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic Classification**: One of three recognized subspecies of *Gasterosteus aculeatus* on the U.S. West Coast:
  - *G. a. williamsoni* — **unarmored** (plateless) form
  - *G. a. aculeatus* — **fully armored** subspecies
  - *G. a. microcephalus* — **partially armored** subspecies
- **Lateral Plate Counts**: Degree of armor (bony lateral plates) serves as the primary diagnostic and conservation metric:
  - Populations are considered "pure" *williamsoni* when largely **plateless**.
  - A mean plate count **greater than 0.5 plates per side** in a population sample indicates likely **intergradation** with *microcephalus* (per Dr. Carl Hubbs' methodology).
  - Existing wild populations show varying degrees of intergradation, resulting in variable plate counts rather than a uniform plateless phenotype.
- **Original Description**: First described by Girard in 1854 as *Gasterosteus williamsoni*, from headwaters of the Santa Clara River near Vincent Station, Williamson's Pass area, Los Angeles County (~12 miles SW of Palmdale, CA).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native Range**: 
  - [[Los Angeles Basin]] — San Gabriel River, Los Angeles River, Santa Ana River systems (population extirpated since the 1940s)
  - Upper [[Santa Clara River]] drainage, Los Angeles County
  - Huasna River and Alamo Creek (Santa Maria River system, San Luis Obispo County) — believed native (Miller, 1969)
- **Introduced/Non-native Occurrence**: Mojave River (first noted 1940), likely introduced as bait fish; this population has since **lost taxonomic identity** through intergradation with later introductions of *microcephalus*.
- **Habitat Type**: Intermittent, seasonally flowing streams; Soledad Canyon section maintains flow even in driest periods, while the broad sandy wash below the canyon (extending to Saugus) is dry most of the year except during flood flows.
- **Co-occurring Native Fishes**: Chubs and suckers persist alongside stickleback in Soledad Canyon and demonstrate resilience to periodic flood-scouring events.
- **Natural Barrier Dynamics**: A natural geomorphic barrier between Soledad Canyon and the lower river (below Saugus) currently prevents upstream migration of *microcephalus*/intergrade populations, effectively isolating and protecting the pure *williamsoni* stock.
- **Population Dynamics**: Large population numbers observed in summer/fall; population dips in winter. Periodic large flood flows scour the canyon but populations rebound reliably — a historically consistent and non-threatening natural disturbance regime.

## Key Findings

1. **Sole Surviving Population**: As of the plan's writing (early 1970s), the only confirmed extant population of pure/near-pure *williamsoni* is restricted to a **10-mile stretch of Soledad Canyon** on the upper Santa Clara River.
2. **Primary Threats**:
   - **Habitat destruction** from urban/industrial expansion (historic cause of Los Angeles Basin extirpation).
   - **Genetic introgression** with *microcephalus*, largely introduced via **stocked catchable trout plantings**.
   - **Anthropogenic risk in Soledad Canyon**: proximity of railroad (chemical/oil spill risk), recreational development (campgrounds, swimming pools, trout ponds), and potential accidental transport of *microcephalus* from below Saugus "in a matter of minutes."
3. **Regional Case Studies of Intergradation**:
   - Santa Maria drainage (Alamo Creek) intergraded following 1940 introduction of *microcephalus*; exacerbated by inundation from **Twitchell Reservoir** construction on the Cuyama River.
   - Mojave River population fully intergraded, effectively lost as distinct *williamsoni* stock.
   - Lower Santa Clara River (below Saugus) populations confirmed as *microcephalus*/intergrades via 1971–1972 surveys.
4. **Legal Protection Status**: Listed under the federal **Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966** and the **California Endangered Species Act of 1970**; fully protected by law.
5. **Conservation Actions Undertaken (Pre-Plan)**:
   - Experimental transplants of *williamsoni* to **San Felipe Creek** (San Diego County) and **Piru Pond** (Angeles National Forest) — no signs of establishment success reported to date.
6. **Recovery Plan Structure**:
   - **Phase One**: Systematic ichthyological survey (contracted to a university ichthyology department) of the upper Santa Clara River and ~30–40 tributaries to determine population status, intergradation zones, and habitat suitability for future introductions (proposed for dry-season 1973, July–October).
   - **Phase Two**: Habitat protection via cooperation with landowners/agencies; establishment of an **endangered species management area**/ecological reserve in Soledad Canyon; physical barriers to prevent *microcephalus* infiltration; continued transplantation program; captive propagation studies (partnership with **California State Polytechnic University, Pomona**, and Dr. Jon Baskin) to test breeding viability and selective breeding for plateless (pure) strains.
7. **Governance Gap**: Only the **U.S. Forest Service (Angeles National Forest)** holds public streambed land in Soledad Canyon (~1/4–1/2 mile), controlling only a small fraction of the critical habitat; most land is under private/commercial control with mixed cooperation.
