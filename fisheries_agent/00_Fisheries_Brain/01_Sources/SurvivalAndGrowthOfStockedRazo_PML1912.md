# Survival and Growth of Stocked Razorback Sucker and Bonytail in Multiple Floodplain Wetlands of the Middle Green River Under Reset Conditions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (October 2005), funded by the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] and prepared by Tim Modde and G. Bruce Haines of the [[Colorado River Fish Project]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Vernal, UT), tests whether draining and "resetting" floodplain wetlands before spring river connection improves survival and growth of stocked larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) in the presence of non-native fishes. The study ran in 2003–2004 during drought conditions across [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Bureau of Land Management]] floodplains in the middle Green River.

Survival was highest for bonytail (6.3% in 2003; 1.3% in 2004) versus razorback sucker (0.1% and 0.4%). Razorback sucker grew faster on average (0.94 mm/d in 2003; 0.61 mm/d in 2004) than bonytail (0.53 and 0.50 mm/d). Reset floodplains held far fewer predatory non-native fishes than floodplains with residual populations. Razorback sucker outgrew predation risk from age-0 non-natives, while a portion of stocked bonytail remained vulnerable to the largest age-0 [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*) in late July and August. Statistical power was limited, and no significant relationship was found between survival and environmental variables.

## Background & Description

The authors frame the study around the decline of razorback sucker and bonytail in the Upper Colorado River Basin, attributed to failed recruitment. Early life-stage habitat use in the natural riverine environment is poorly documented. Floodplains are hypothesized to serve as nursery habitat, but [[Non-Native Fish Invasion]] has limited their value since large-scale development.

Earlier work set the stage:
- [[Modde et al. 2001]] found age-0 razorback sucker in Old Charley Wash in two successive high-flow years.
- [[Birchell and Christopherson 2002]] (Stirrup floodplain, residual non-native populations) detected no age-0 razorback sucker survival, though age-1 fish (~100 mm TL) showed greater than 10% survival.
- A pen experiment (Christopherson et al. 2004) showed age-0 larvae could survive an assigned non-native assemblage.

The present study tests whether the reset approach works at a larger geographic scale with self-colonizing non-native populations, and identifies floodplain features linked to higher survival and growth. Survival and growth were chosen as criteria because sites where fish persisted longest, even in drought, would offer the greatest margin of error for future reset applications.

Study floodplains were natural depressions with added dikes and water control structures on the refuge. They ranged from about 6 ha to 57 ha. The middle Green River study reach lies between river miles 305 and 249 (kilometers 494–403).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and connectivity.** In 2003, peak flows of 19,000 cfs allowed surface connection of all floodplains for at least 16 days. Water control structures admitted non-native fish at lower flows (about 5,000–9,000 cfs). In 2004, peak flow was 11,500 cfs, which was insufficient to overtop dikes. Old Charley Wash, Johnson Bottom, and Thunder Ranch received limited or no river recharge, and Leota-10 was supplemented by pumping from the river and Pelican Lake.

**Water quality.** Dissolved oxygen remained above 4.0 mg/L in most wetlands in June 2003, but declined sharply in Leota-10 (1.8 mg/L on July 23). Dead adult carp and bonytail were observed from late July onward. In 2004, dissolved oxygen fell below 1.0 mg/L in Thunder Ranch and Johnson Bottom by late July. pH ranged from 7.63 to 9.95 in 2003. Turbidity was generally low, with one reading of 150 NTU in Old Charley Wash. Temperatures briefly exceeded 30 °C in July in shallow sites.

**Zooplankton and vegetation.** Zooplankton densities in 2003 ranged from 0.6 to 11.7 individuals/L, peaking between June 2 and July 8 and declining to below 2/L by August 5. Peak submergent vegetation was observed on July 17, 2003. Submergent cover ranged from 1% in small floodplains to 51% in Johnson Bottom, and was positively related to floodplain area.

**Fish community succession.** Early-summer assemblages were dominated by riverine species (red shiner, sand shiner, carp). By late summer, lentic species (fathead minnow, green sunfish, black bullhead) became more abundant as age-0 fish spawned in the floodplain. Thunder Ranch, isolated from the river for about two decades, contained only green sunfish and three-spine stickleback plus stocked razorback sucker.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and survival**
- 2003 stocking: about 1,500 larval razorback sucker and 1,235 larval bonytail per ha across five sites (Table 1a); 2004: 6,562 razorback sucker and 1,654 bonytail per ha across four sites (Table 1b).
- 2003 July survival: bonytail 1.6–13.6% (highest in Johnson Bottom); razorback sucker 0.0–0.4%.
- 2004 survival (harvest estimates, July–August): razorback sucker 0.1–0.7% at refuge sites; bonytail below 0.1% (Old Charley Wash) to over 2.8% (Johnson Bottom).
- Depletion estimates of surviving age-0 razorback sucker in 2004 ranged from 22 (Leota-10) to 1,523 (Johnson Bottom).
- Bonytail reproduced in Above Brennan, and small individuals (<40 mm) in Leota-10 suggested local reproduction.

**Growth**
- 2003 growth: razorback sucker 0.94 mm/d; bonytail 0.53 mm/d.
- 2004 growth: razorback sucker 0.61 mm/d; bonytail 0.50 mm/d. Growth ranged 0.4–0.8 mm/d across species, and was highest in Leota-10.
- Razorback sucker condition factor (K) at the end of August in Leota-10 was 0.970 (age-0) and 1.432 (age-1, Johnson Bottom).

**Non-native fish biomass and predation**
- Mean non-native biomass: 25.9 kg/ha in 2003 and 8.2 kg/ha in 2004 (Table 6). Standing stock ranged 6.5–68.3 kg/ha.
- Residual (non-reset) floodplains held several orders of magnitude more small-bodied and large-bodied predators than reset floodplains (Figures 10a–b).
- Razorback sucker body depth exceeded the mouth gape of all age-0 non-native predators. Mouth-width analysis showed 16.7% of bonytail in Leota-10 were vulnerable to age-0 black bullhead, 3.4% to carp, and 0.2% to green sunfish in late August.

**Statistical relationships**
- No significant linear relationship was found between razorback sucker or bonytail survival and non-native biomass or submergent vegetation (Table 7).
- Bonytail survival correlated with submergent vegetation in both years and with non-native biomass in 2004 (simple correlation; R² = 0.79 and 0.41, Figures 9a–b). This was not statistically significant.
- Greatest growth and survival occurred in the largest, deepest floodplains with the most submergent vegetation (Johnson Bottom and Leota-10).
- No relationship was found between native fish survival and turbidity, dissolved oxygen, or pH.

**Dispersal considerations**
- A modeled estimate (Valdez and Nelson 2004) predicted 99% loss of drifting razorback sucker larvae within 58 river km of the spawning bar. Empirical catch rates in the Ouray and Jensen reaches (1993–1996) were not significantly different (paired t-test, P > 0.05), so the authors argue the modeled result conflicts with field data.

**Conclusions**
- Razorback sucker and bonytail larvae stocked into inundated floodplains survived non-native predators at rates consistent with sustainable riverine populations (Dey 1981; Welcomme 1985).
- Draining floodplains and initializing fish biomass before spring peak flows was an effective tool for increasing larval growth and survival, and would likely benefit naturally produced endangered fishes in the Green River subbasin.

**Recommendations**
1. Develop a plan to reset all large floodplains under recovery-program management authority.
2. Assign equal value to Jensen and Ouray floodplains for enhancement planning, based on larval survival and four years of drift data.
3. Explore middle Green River floodplains as a means to increase bonytail numbers.
4. Stock both larvae and adults into floodplains to raise survival and potential recruitment.
5. Conduct further study of how long juvenile and adult bonytail remain in floodplains and whether they reproduce there.

**Limitations.** Sample size was small, so statistical confidence in factors affecting growth and survival was limited. The Leota-10 breach in August 2004 likely reduced the population estimate, and depletion estimates were not always obtainable, which probably underestimated some populations.
