# Transitional Monitoring of Sand Bars along the Colorado River

#geomorphology #fluvial-ecology #sediment-dynamics #dam-operations #adaptive-management

## Executive Summary

This proposal, submitted 21 August 1995 by researchers from [[Northern Arizona University]], requests $201,816 to continue transitional monitoring of [[Colorado River]] sand bars and backwaters in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The project bridges ongoing research to a long-term monitoring program during the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase II period. Principal investigators Rod Parnell, Leland Dexter, Abe Springer, Matt Kaplinski, and Joseph E. Hazel, Jr. propose integrating sand bar surveys, daily photographic monitoring, and backwater geochemistry/hydrology studies to assess impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on sediment resources and aquatic habitat. The work addresses critical adaptive management needs during a period of changing flow regimes, including sustained medium-high flows (18,000-20,000 cfs) from June-October 1995 that deviate from historic Interim Flow patterns.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations directly influence fluvial sediment stability in [[Grand Canyon]] through hydraulic erosion and aggradation. Sand bars are recognized as foundational to ecosystem maintenance, providing critical habitat for endangered native fish species and supporting recreational resources. Historical studies since 1965 documented dam-induced changes through aerial photography and topographic surveys at approximately 20 sites. The [[GCES Phase I]] program (early 1980s) established classification systems for alluvial deposits and recirculation zones. [[GCES Phase II]] (1990-1991) conducted discrete research flows to determine impacts of specific discharge regimes on sand bar stability.

The proposal builds on established monitoring protocols developed during GCES Phase II, including rigorous topographic survey control at 33 study sites that overlap with historical survey locations dating to 1973. Study sites span from Glen Canyon through Grand Canyon, with surveys extending from the 5,000 ft³/s stage elevation to above 30,000 ft³/s contours. Previous monitoring documented that Interim Flows (implemented August 1991) significantly eroded sand bars, with deposition occurring below maximum Interim Flow stage while erosion dominated at and above that water level.

The backwater geochemistry component investigates nutrient processes and hydrologic regimes within sand bars as important links in understanding eddy system trophic structure. Eddy environments provide critical rearing habitat for endangered native species and potentially important sources of trophic material. Geochemical analysis of groundwater within reattachment bars indicated progressive nutrient storage in return current channel environments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The post-dam [[Colorado River]] ecosystem depends fundamentally on sand-sized sediment storage in channels and recirculation zones. Sand bars form the physical foundation structuring the fluvial ecosystem. [[Return current channels]] (RCCs) and associated backwaters create critical habitat for native fish species including endangered taxa. These eddy environments serve as important rearing areas and potential sources of trophic material for the broader aquatic food web.

Flow fluctuations from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] create dynamic groundwater conditions within sand bars, affecting nutrient delivery to surface waters. The flux of dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus from riparian environments to RCC surface waters and backwater habitats depends on subsurface hydrologic connections. Nutrient spiraling through beach-RCC-backwater systems represents a critical but poorly examined process in the post-dam ecosystem.

Summer 1995 flows of 18,000-20,000 cfs represented significant departures from historic Interim Flow patterns, potentially causing major changes in sediment storage patterns throughout the system. Winter 1993 also saw flow pattern changes due to [[Little Colorado River]] tributary flooding. Such flow variations can significantly alter sand bar morphology and volume on timescales from hours to years.

## Key Findings

**Primary Objectives:**

1. **Sand Bar Surveys**: Monitor topography and bathymetric surfaces of 33 [[Colorado River]] sand bars using standard ground survey techniques and methodologies developed during GCES Phase II. Compare topographic change to assess effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] flows. Test hypotheses that Interim Flow parameters have not changed sediment resource conditions and that sustained medium/high flows (18,000-20,000 cfs, June-October 1995) have not significantly affected sand bar morphologies and volumes.

2. **Daily Photographic Monitoring**: Continue daily photography at 20 sites with 100% overlap with ground-based survey sites. Rectify oblique images to planimetric models using ERDAS v.7.5 software to determine area changes. Test hypotheses that daily photography adequately captures short-term bar failure frequency and that rapid changes occur on timescales requiring daily monitoring beyond semi-annual surveys.

3. **Backwater/RCC Marsh Interrelationships**: Conduct coupled Bromide/Nitrate tracer study at mile 43.2L to determine interconnections between sand bar, RCC, and backwater environments. Test hypothesis that fluctuating flow conditions create minimal groundwater flux beneath RCCs, resulting in low delivery of dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus to surface waters. Determine field-scale denitrification rates essential for understanding nutrient spiraling.

4. **GIS Database Integration**: Update sediment-related portions of GCES/NPS GIS database at NAU Department of Geology. Integrate existing studies with transitional and long-term monitoring plans to ensure consistent data structure.

**Methodological Approach:**

- Bi-annual field surveys during low-discharge periods (spring/fall) on 15-20 day river trips
- Ground-based teams using Leitz Set4c and Set3c total stations with digital data collectors
- Point densities: 200-400 points for smaller sites (~2,000 m²), 750-1,000 points for larger sites (~10,000 m²)
- Bathymetric surveys using Hydrographic Survey Package (HSP) with shore-based total station and boat-mounted transducer
- Survey coverage from 5,000 ft³/s to above 30,000 ft³/s stage elevations
- Conservative (Bromide) and non-conservative (Nitrate) tracer study beginning January 1996
- 8-day intensive sampling period followed by biweekly to monthly sampling
- Tracer introduction along 20m line source dissolved in river water (maximum 100 ppm Br, 10 ppm NO₃)

**Budget and Timeline:**

- Total funding requested: $201,816 (includes 20% overhead)
- Direct costs: $168,180
- Personnel costs include survey crews, data analysts, research technicians, graduate students, and system administrator
- April 1996: 17-day river trip to survey 30 study sand bars
- January-September 1996: Monthly film changes for photographic monitoring
- January 1996: Begin tracer study with intensive sampling
- February-April 1996: Biweekly to monthly tracer sampling
- Deliverables include quarterly reports, draft technical reports (August 1995), and final reports (September 1995)

**Critical Support Requirements:**

- Logistical support from GCES program for river trips
- GCES survey division support for Hydrographic survey equipment
- Monthly film changes integrated with other trips
- Access to mile 43.2L site via river or Emminence Break for biweekly/monthly sampling

**Integration with Broader Monitoring:**

The proposal addresses multiple items in the transitional monitoring plan including sediment resource monitoring (III:C(1a), III:C(1c)), wetland and riparian resources (IV:A(2)), and eddy environment impacts (IV:E(3,4), IV:D(2)). Results will inform adaptive management decisions, dam-release flood planning, and predictions of GCD-EIS preferred alternative effects. The work ensures continuous comparative datasets during EIS review and Record of Decision development, providing baseline data for long-term monitoring programs.
