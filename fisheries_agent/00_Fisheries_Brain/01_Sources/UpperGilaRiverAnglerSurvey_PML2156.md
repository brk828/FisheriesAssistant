# Upper Gila River Angler Survey 1998

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Region V Fisheries Program, Report 98-5-1, February 1999) quantifies recreational angling on the [[Upper Gila River]] and three nearby [[Gila Valley]] lakes during April to October 1998. The study was prompted by a 1997 proposal to remove harvest and possession limits on flathead catfish, channel catfish, largemouth bass and smallmouth bass. Those limits were lifted on the Verde and Salt rivers but kept on the Gila River because of public resistance and a lack of data on angler use. A [[Stratified Two-Stage Roving Creel]] design was used.

Anglers spent an estimated 8,152 hours on the river (mostly for catfish) and 31,880 hours on the area lakes (mostly for centrarchids). About 89% of interviewed anglers said removing bag limits would increase fishing success or have no effect on it. The authors conclude that river fishing quality depends mainly on annual spring run-off and precipitation, and that bag-limit removal would not harm angling success or catfish numbers.

## Background & Description

The Gila River flows west from New Mexico across Arizona to the Colorado River near Yuma. [[Coolidge Dam]] (1928) created [[San Carlos Reservoir]] and blocks upstream fish migration. The reach from Coolidge Dam to the New Mexico border is designated critical habitat for the federally endangered [[Razorback Sucker]], a large, long-lived riverine species of the Colorado River drainage.

The study area is the Gila Valley in Graham County, from the San Carlos Apache Reservation boundary near Geronimo to the [[Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area]]. It covers about 40 miles of river with 12 angler access areas. Irrigation diversions and returns dominate the flow regime. Inflows from the San Francisco River, Bonita Creek and Eagle Creek add to flow. The three lakes are [[Roper Lake]] (32 surface acres), [[Dankworth Pond]] (5 surface acres) and [[Cluff Ranch Pond 3]] (15 surface acres). San Carlos Reservoir was excluded because it lies 50 miles away on the reservation, is mainly boat-fished, and differs from the river catfish fishery.

Study design followed the four-step planning process of Malvestuto (1991). Survey days were stratified by month, day type (weekday or weekend) and period (early a.m. or late p.m.). Roughly six weekday and four weekend surveys were run each month. Four opinion questions were added to the river interview. They covered prior interviews, live bait use, how often bag limits were filled, and opinions on removing limits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Angling quality follows spring run-off and precipitation. Channel catfish migrate upstream during high spring flows to spawn and feed, and move downstream to wintering pools or reservoirs as flows drop. Flathead catfish are more territorial and move only when necessary.
- **Shad and shiners:** Anglers linked good catfish fishing to the appearance of threadfin shad during spring run-off. The authors consider shad probably temporary members of the Upper Gila River assemblage rather than year-round residents. [[Red Shiner]] were found to be abundant in recent surveys (in press), and many anglers misidentified shiner schools below diversions as shad.
- **Live bait:** Non-native minnows used as bait can enter the fish assemblage when released or escaped, so bait use was tracked. Live bait is legal only on the Gila River within Graham County.
- **Stocked lakes:** Roper Lake and Cluff Ranch Pond #3 are also stocked with winter catchable [[Rainbow Trout]]. The lakes are mainly warmwater fisheries.
- **Access constraints:** Steep canyon walls limit access for rafters and off-road vehicles. Flows above the Pima Bridge to Solomon Bridge reach are unstable, and angler numbers there are low.

## Key Findings

**Upper Gila River effort and harvest**

- 76 river creels were conducted from April 15 to October 28, 1998, yielding 924 counts at 12 access areas (604 weekday, 320 weekend). Period counts were 455 early and 469 late.
- 216 anglers were encountered. Most access-area counts (842) found no anglers, and 96.9% of all counts (895) found 3 or fewer.
- Anglers concentrated downstream of the last irrigation diversions, from Ft. Thomas Bridge to the Curtis Canal Diversion, in April and May. Ft. Thomas Diversion was the most used site. Thatcher and Solomon bridges had no or almost no anglers.
- Angler counts differed significantly by location, month and period, but not by day type (Kruskal-Wallis tests, α = 0.05). Counts were highest in April and May, none were recorded in July, and late-period counts were higher than early-period counts.
- Estimated effort was 8,152 angler hours, about 2,467 angler trips, and roughly 2,527 fish harvested (0.31 fish/hour × 8,152 hours).
- Catch per unit effort (CPUE) was 0.53 fish/hour. This exceeds the 0.50 CPUE goal in the AGFD *Wildlife 2000* Strategic Plan.
- Average interviewed angler: 2.38 hours fished, 1.25 fish caught, 0.73 fish harvested, 19 miles traveled, and fishing success rated between fair and poor (mean 1.63). Success rating was weakly correlated with catch and harvest.
- Target species: 85% of anglers fished for "any catfish" or "catfish", 11.1% for "anything", 3.3% specifically for [[Channel Catfish]], and 0.7% for [[Flathead Catfish]].
- Species caught (N = 198): channel catfish 83%, green sunfish 6.1%, flathead catfish 5.5%, common carp 5.5%. Species harvested (N = 115): channel catfish 89.6%, flathead catfish 6.1%, carp 4.3%.
- Most channel catfish harvested were under 10 inches, and most of those were released. Harvested flathead catfish ranged from 11 to 32 inches.
- Bait: 73.7% of anglers used some form of live bait, and worms were the most common (88.4%). Only 7.8% used live minnows.
- Residence: 89% of river anglers were from Graham County, 6.4% from Cochise and 2.5% from Maricopa. Non-residents made up 1.9%.

**Bag-limit opinions (river)**

- 13.7% of anglers had been interviewed before and were excluded from the opinion analysis.
- Of 124 anglers asked how removing the 6-bass and 25-catfish bag limit would affect success, 85.5% said no effect, 3.2% said an increase and 11.3% said a decrease.
- Only 14.5% (about 15%) had filled a bag limit in the previous 15 years. No bag limits were observed being taken during the study.

**Area lakes**

- 215 angler surveys were completed: Cluff Pond #3 (N = 102), Dankworth Pond (N = 56) and Roper Lake (N = 57). Some Cluff Pond #3 data came from ongoing regional management work.
- Lake effort was 31,880 angler hours and about 8,855 angler trips. Lake CPUE was 0.42 fish/hour and HPUE was 0.10 fish/hour.
- Average lake angler: 1.8 hours fished, 0.75 fish caught, 0.17 fish harvested, 47 miles traveled, and success rated between poor and fair (mean 1.57).
- Graham County residents made up 70.7% of lake anglers. Non-residents made up 1.8%.
- Target species: 55.3% "anything", 20.6% [[Largemouth Bass]], 9.9% sunfish, 5.7% channel catfish, 5.0% rainbow trout, 3.5% black crappie.
- Species caught (N = 126): [[Bluegill]] 64.3%, largemouth bass 19.0%, black crappie 7.9%. Harvest was low (N = 29), mostly sunfish (69.0%) and largemouth bass (27.6%).
- Significant differences were found only for miles traveled (by month and location) and estimated trip length (by period).

**Comparison and economics**

- Lakes supported about 3.9 times the river's angler hours (31,880 vs. 8,152). Combined effort was 40,032 hours and 11,322 trips.
- The river supplied about 44% of harvested fish (2,527 of 5,715 estimated) from 20% of total hours.
- Using the $50/angler-day national average from USFWS (1996), the river fishery contributed about $123,350 and the lakes about $442,750. Because most river anglers are local, these figures probably overstate economic contribution.
- Winter rainbow trout creel surveys (Mitchell 1995) estimated 13,247 hours at Roper Lake and 13,757 hours at Cluff Pond #3 for 1993 to 1994.

**Predictors and conclusions**

- Catfish anglers estimated that only 2 to 3 years per decade provide quality river fishing. Quality is tied to spring run-off and shad or shiner presence, which are linked to migration rather than to a predatory effect.
- The authors conclude that removing bag and harvest limits on bass and catfish would not reduce fishing success or catfish numbers on the Upper Gila River.
- Restricting live bait-fish use would have little effect, since only 7.8% of anglers used minnows.

**Management recommendations**

- Future surveys should focus on April and May at the most-used locations.
- Catfish movement and assemblage studies should continue, especially in the Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area, where flows are higher and more permanent.
- Catfish assessment and enhancement should be carried out at the three lakes to support year-round quality fishing when river flows are poor.
- Results should be shared publicly, and public input should inform future decisions.
