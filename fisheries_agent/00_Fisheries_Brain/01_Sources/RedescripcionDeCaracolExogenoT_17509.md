# Redescription of the Exotic Snail Thiara (Melanoides) tuberculata (Müller, 1774) and Its Distribution in Mexico

#malacology #freshwater-biology #thiaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 taxonomic paper by [[Contreras-Arquieta]], [[Guajardo-Martínez]] and [[Contreras-Balderas]] redescribes the exotic freshwater prosobranch snail *Thiara (Melanoides) tuberculata* in Mexico and maps its national distribution. The species is thought to have reached Mexico around the 1960s, most likely through the [[Aquarium Trade]]. The authors report 63 new localities, bringing the known total to 65 across 12 states, with [[Veracruz]] holding the most records (42). The paper also gives a diagnostic shell, opercular and radular description for Mexican populations, a summary of the species' world range, and notes on its ecology, dispersal and taxonomic synonymy.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** The first Mexican record was made by [[Abbott 1973]] near Veracruz. In 1980, [[Jiménez, Guajardo and Torres]] reported the snail as *Oncomelania* sp. from Parras de la Fuente, Coahuila, and in 1985 [[Contreras-Balderas and Maeda-Martínez]] recorded it as *Tarebia* sp. from the same locality. The authors treat these misidentifications as synonyms of *T. tuberculata*. A 1981 report by [[Reddell]] of a large population of *Melanoides maculata* in the Cantil Blanco cave, Veracruz, is considered probably the same species.

**Type locality and synonymy.** The type locality is the Coromandel Coast, India, where Müller described it in 1774 as *Nerita tuberculata*. Other synonyms include *Melanoides fasciolata* Oliver, 1804, and the Mexican misidentifications listed above.

**Shell.** The shell is narrowly elongate-conic and imperforate, with 11 (11.82 ± 0.61) to 13 whorls that increase regularly. Its colour ranges from light to dark brown, often with irregular reddish-brown blotches that sometimes form longitudinal bars or zig-zags. The reticulate sculpture has fine spiral and axial ribs, and the oval aperture carries a brown columellar band in some populations. Shell length ranges from 23.72 to 45 mm. The largest specimen (45 mm, apex eroded by about 6 mm) came from the Puebla population. Some Veracruz and Nayarit populations have decollated shells, in which the embryonic whorl is lost.

**Operculum.** The operculum is paucispiral, dark brown to black, with an acentric nucleus carrying 2 to 2.5 whorls. It is about 1.59 ± 0.12 times as high as wide.

**Soft anatomy.** The species is parthenogenetic and ovoviviparous. The brood pouch holds 62 to 88 juvenile snails, 75% of them fully formed. Each pouch has 157 to 240 uniseriate triangular gill filaments (194 ± 28 mean) and a mantle collar with 12 digitiform papillae.

**Radula.** The radula is taenioglossate, with 69 to 121 rows of seven teeth each. The radular formula is R (4–6)-1-(4–6), L 1-1-(4–6), MI 10–11 and ME 10–12 cusps.

**Morphs.** Following [[Pointier (1989)]], the Mexican material best matches the Goiser morph, with numerous reddish-brown blotches and tubercles on the axial and spiral margins. Some individuals lack the columellar band and resemble the Falaise morph, and Falaise-type individuals also occur within Goiser populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and physicochemical tolerances.**
- In Veracruz, the snail occurs in 95% of the rivers sampled, at water temperatures of 17–23 °C and conductivity of 200–900 µmhos.
- In Durango, it lives in thermal waters at 33 °C and pH 7, at densities of 1500–2500 individuals per m².
- Its behaviour is described as burrowing in sand and mud by day, then emerging in the night and morning to climb substrates up to the air–water interface.

**Pollution sensitivity.** The species appears intolerant of polluted water. In Guadalupe, Nuevo León, it was found only in one spring that feeds the Río La Silla, which receives domestic and industrial discharge. Water there ranges from 15 to 26 °C.

**Parasitology.** [[Rodríguez-Vargas and Salgado-Maldonado (1991)]] reported the snail as an intermediate host of the trematode [[Centrocestus formosanus]] at the Manantial Las Estacas, Morelos.

**Dispersal.** The authors suggest that the [[Aquarium Trade]] is the main pathway, since aquarists discharge rinse water into drains that reach rivers. The operculum helps juveniles and adults survive transport. Waterbird transport on feathers or feet is also possible, and a single individual may found a new population. The mechanism is not confirmed, and the authors note that the isolated populations in Quintana Roo, Coahuila, Durango and Nayarit do not reveal a clear invasion route.

**Comparison with related species.** Compared with [[Thiara Granifera]], *T. tuberculata* has a taller, narrower shell, finer reticulation, no nodules, and a lower aperture-to-shell-height ratio. Compared with earlier descriptions, the Mexican populations have more whorls than [[Burch (1962)]] reported (9–12) and than the Dominican populations described by [[Gómez et al. (1986)]] (8–11). The Dominican material also has basal denticles on the rachidian tooth, which the Mexican material lacks.

**Current status.** The species is an established alien. It is an Asian introduction with high reproductive capacity, found in 65 localities across 23 hydrological basins in 12 Mexican states.

## Key Findings

- *Thiara (Melanoides) tuberculata* was first recorded in Mexico in 1973 and has spread widely since its introduction in the 1960s.
- The paper adds 63 new localities to the known records, bringing the total to 65 in 12 states. Veracruz holds 42 of these. The source's state tally sums to 64, one more than the stated 63, a discrepancy in the source itself.
- Earlier Mexican records under *Oncomelania* sp., *Tarebia* sp. and *Melanoides maculata* are reassigned to *T. tuberculata*.
- The species is parthenogenetic and ovoviviparous, with brood pouches of 62–88 juveniles and 69–121 radular rows.
- It tolerates warm and moderately conductive waters, including thermal springs at 33 °C, but seems to avoid heavily polluted water.
- It serves as an intermediate host of *Centrocestus formosanus*, which gives it relevance for parasitology.
- Aquarium trade is the most likely dispersal route, with bird transport also plausible.
- The authors conclude that the species is now part of Mexico's established introduced freshwater fauna and probably occurs in other, unsurveyed states.
