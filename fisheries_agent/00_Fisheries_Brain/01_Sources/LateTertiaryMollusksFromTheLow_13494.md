# Late Tertiary Mollusks from the Lower Colorado River Valley

#malacology #marine-biology #potamididae

## Executive Summary

This paleontological report by [[Dwight W. Taylor]] (Museum of Paleontology, University of Michigan, 1983) describes a new brackish-water snail, [[Batillaria Californica]], from the late Miocene [[Bouse Formation]] of the Colorado River Valley, Arizona and California. The Bouse Formation records a brackish to marine embayment that extended northward from the head of the [[Gulf of California]]. The new species is significant because its nearest living relative, [[Batillaria Minima]], lives in the Gulf of Mexico and Caribbean Sea.

The study also reports sparse freshwater mollusks from the calcareous facies of the [[Muddy Creek Formation]] in southern Nevada. These freshwater assemblages argue that the Muddy Creek deposits were not formed in brackish or marine water. The author therefore disputes earlier interpretations that the Muddy Creek was deposited at the head of the Bouse estuary.

## Background & Description

**Geological setting.** In the late Miocene, a brackish to marine embayment extended up the Colorado River valley from the head of the Gulf of California. The Bouse Formation was previously described by [[Metzger]] and colleagues (1973). Its benthic fauna is sparse and includes ostracodes, foraminifera, and marine, brackish-water and freshwater mollusks.

**New species: *Batillaria californica*.** The species belongs to the subgenus *Lampanella* in the family [[Potamididae]].
- **Diagnosis:** A small *Batillaria* lacking nodulose or strong spiral sculpture on the body whorl. It combines the flat-sided spire, oblique aperture channel, heavy shell and columellar callus of *B. minima* with the fine spiral sculpture of *B. mutata*.
- **Shell:** Small, slender, pyramidal, with 10-11 flat whorls. Spiral cords and threads override weak axial riblets.
- **Type measurements:** The holotype (USNM 305208) measures 6.2 mm long. Paratypes measure 7.4 mm, 6.2 mm and 6.9 mm. The largest specimen seen is 8.0+ mm.
- **Type locality:** USGS locality M3091, Imperial County, California, in roadside exposures of basal limestone of the Bouse Formation.

**Comparisons.**
- [[Batillaria Mutata]] of the Galápagos Islands is larger, has a thinner shell, more convex whorls, and nodulose sculpture below the suture.
- [[Batillaria Minima]] of the western Atlantic is larger, with flatter whorls, stronger riblets, and an anterior aperture channel more nearly normal to the columella.
- Two other American fossil species, *B. transecta* (Miocene, Tampa limestone, Florida) and *B. advena*, are considered doubtful or distinct. The validity of *B. transecta* is questioned because of variation in *B. minima*.

**Associated fauna.** The limestone yielded abundant Batillaria, characean algae, and small crabs. Marine mollusks (*?Barleeia*, *Diplodonta*, *Halodakra*), and barnacles were less common. The habitat is consistent with that of *B. minima*, which is "often found in abundance in shallow brackish water where it lives in the mud of the intertidal zone" (Bequaert, 1942).

**Other occurrences.** Material was examined from three further localities in Yuma County, Arizona (USGS M3089, M3090, and a 1965 Trigo Mountains locality), plus a lost collection and one in Imperial County, California (USGS 18414).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography.** In the Tertiary, there was no Isthmus of Panama. Marine mollusks ranged widely through the Caribbean and central eastern Pacific. Descendants survived unequally on either side of Middle America: Pacific-only species are "pacific" and Caribbean-only species are "caribphile" (Woodring, 1966). *B. californica* is the northernmost record of this former Tertiary Caribbean faunal province. Its relative *B. minima* is therefore a caribphile species. The three species of *Lampanella* are more closely related to each other than *B. mutata* is to them.

**Fossil and living *Batillaria* distribution.** The subgenus *Batillaria* s.s. ranges from southern Hokkaido, Japan, to the Mekong River, Viet Nam, including a Philippine locality. A second California fossil, *B. ocoyana* from the Miocene San Joaquin Valley, belongs to *Batillaria* s.s. Other brackish-water fossils show a similar pattern. Those from southern California are related to Gulf and Caribbean species, while those from further north are related to East Asian species (Table 2).

**Age and correlation.** The freshwater assemblages share one species, *Valvata* cf. *V. idahoensis*. This species is otherwise known only from the late Miocene [[Salt Lake Group]] of southeastern Idaho. It supports a late Miocene age and the correlation of the Bouse and Muddy Creek formations (McKee et al., 1967).

**Potassium-argon dating (as reported).**
- Basal Bouse tuff: minimum 3.02 ± 1.15 m.y. (Metzger, 1969); a non-devitrified analysis gave 8.1 ± 0.5 m.y.; four analyses of three samples gave 5.47 ± 0.20 m.y. (Damon et al., 1978).
- Fortification Hill basalt (below the calcareous facies): 10.6 ± 1.1, 10.9 ± 1.1, and 5.88 ± 0.18 m.y.
- Hualapai Limestone Member: 8.66 ± 2.2 m.y.

**Freshwater habitats.** Living relatives indicate perennial freshwater conditions:
- *"Fluminicola" avernalis* lives in warm springs at the head of the Muddy River, a tributary of the Virgin River. It indicates oxygenated, spring-fed fresh water.
- *Anodonta californiensis*, a freshwater mussel, inhabits perennial creeks, rivers and lakes.
- Living *Valvata* are restricted to perennial fresh water.

**Older and younger faunas.**
- *Older than Bouse:* A single collection from the northern Sacramento Mountains contains the snail *Bulimnea*. Middle Miocene (Barstovian) Barstow and Caliente formations and latest Miocene (Hemphillian) Bedrock Springs Formation also contain *Bulimnea*. The Argus Range has a Barstow-type fauna of 10 species. No surely late Miocene (Clarendonian) freshwater mollusks are known from southeastern California.
- *Younger than Bouse:* A Mohave County, Arizona, locality (Sacramento Wash, unit B of the older Colorado River alluvium) yielded four extant species: *Bakerilymnaea cubensis*, *Physa virgata*, *Vertigo ovata*, and a *Succinea* species. These are consistent with a Pliocene–Pleistocene age.

## Key Findings

- **New species described:** [[Batillaria Californica]] is a small, slender brackish-water snail with 10-11 whorls, found in the Bouse Formation. Its closest living relative is [[Batillaria Minima]] of the Gulf of Mexico and Caribbean.
- **Northernmost Caribbean-province element:** *B. californica* marks the northern edge of a former Tertiary Caribbean faunal province, before the Isthmus of Panama formed.
- **Bouse embayment supported:** The brackish to marine Bouse Formation records a late Miocene embayment from the head of the [[Gulf of California]] (a marine-influenced environment).
- **Muddy Creek fauna is freshwater:** All mollusks from the Muddy Creek Formation in Table 3 are strictly freshwater. They could not have lived in brackish, marine or hypersaline conditions. The author therefore rejects the hypothesis that the Muddy Creek Hualapai Limestone was deposited at the head of an ancestral Gulf of California.
- **Spring-fed carbonate:** The large volume of carbonate precipitates in the Muddy Creek Formation is attributed to spring discharge (Longwell, 1936). This is consistent with the presence of *"Fluminicola" avernalis*, a spring-dwelling snail.
- **Drainage implications:** The molluscan evidence suggests no ancestral Colorado River tributary entered the Bouse embayment. Only northern tributaries such as the Virgin and White rivers are indicated, if they existed. This differs from the interpretations of Lucchitta (1972, 1979), in which an ancestral Colorado did flow into the embayment.
- **Late Miocene age:** The sparse, non-overlapping freshwater faunas indicate that the Bouse and Muddy Creek assemblages are late to latest Miocene. A latest Miocene (Hemphillian, roughly 5.5–9 m.y.) age is consistent with selected potassium-argon dates. An older Clarendonian age can be supported by a different selection.
- **Southward drainage hypothesis:** A shared *Valvata* species linking Nevada and southeastern Idaho suggests a former southward drainage across the Bonneville basin into the lower Colorado River valley. This remains speculative.
- **Further work needed:** The author concludes that resolving the various interpretations will require more fossils and field studies.
