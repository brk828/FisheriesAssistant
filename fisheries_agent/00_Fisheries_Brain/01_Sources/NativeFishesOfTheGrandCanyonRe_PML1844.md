# Native Fishes of the Grand Canyon Region: An Obituary?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter from *Colorado River Ecology and Dam Management* (Proceedings of a Symposium, Santa Fe, New Mexico, May 24-25, 1990) by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) reviews the decline of the native fish fauna of the lower [[Colorado River]], including the [[Grand Canyon]] region. The author uses historical accounts to reconstruct a pristine fish community, document the collapse of native species beginning around 1900, and evaluate whether the fauna can still be saved. The central argument is that establishment of nonnative fishes, enabled by river regulation, was the ultimate cause of extirpation. The author concludes that the prognosis is poor and that current politics, not biology, stand in the way of recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] drains more than 600,000 km² of mountains, plateaus and basins. Historically it carried highly variable flows, with discharge at Yuma varying over five orders of magnitude (0.5 to 7,100 m³/s) in a 40-year pre-dam record. Sediment transport was similarly variable, with silt loads through Grand Canyon from 45.4 to 455 million metric tons per year between 1922 and 1935 (the year range as given in the text).

The native fauna was highly endemic. Evermann and Rutter (1895) listed 5 families, 18 genera and 32 indigenous species for the basin. Six of eight fishes native to [[Grand Canyon National Park]] are endemic. Key native species discussed include:

- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): a canyon specialist with a predorsal hump; the only reproducing population in the lower basin is in the Little Colorado River.
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*): a streamlined mid-water fish now the most endangered freshwater fish in North America, persisting mainly in captivity and in Lake Mohave.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): a giant minnow that once reached 1.8 m and 36 kg, now largely extirpated from the lower basin.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): the largest catostomid in the basin, with a keel and protrusible mouth; about 50,000 adults remain in Lake Mohave.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), which remained relatively common at the time of writing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pristine conditions.** Floods rarely harmed fishes, which moved to shore or onto floodplains. Clay-laden water could suffocate fishes, but this was rarely observed in western streams. Food was a probable seasonal limitation during turbid high flows, while low-water periods allowed algal and invertebrate production. Canyon reaches, fed by subterranean water from alluvial "reservoirs," provided cool, nutrient-rich refugia during drought.

**Habitat partitioning.** Adult squawfish occupied quiet shoreline places near cover; flannelmouth suckers stayed on the bottom in deep, slow water; bluehead suckers scraped algae; razorbacks fed near the bottom in open channels; humpback chubs concentrated in canyon eddies and whitewater pools. Trophic roles were distinct, with squawfish piscivorous as adults and razorbacks sieving plankton or detritus.

**Life history and reproduction.** Most species spawned on clean gravel or cobble in swift water, with timing varying by species. Squawfish showed strong site fidelity and migrated hundreds of kilometers upstream to spawn; young squawfish moved downstream to nursery areas. Humpback chubs in the Little Colorado River showed both upstream and downstream movements, with individuals recaptured up to 8 years after tagging.

**Dams and temperature.** Glen Canyon Dam releases hypolimnetic water at roughly 7.0 to 10°C, which rarely warms to 16°C by Lake Mead, more than 400 km downstream. Successful humpback chub reproduction requires 15°C or higher, and natural spawning most likely occurs between 19 and 22°C. Cold water therefore excluded native reproduction from Marble and Grand canyons, while trout came to dominate the cold tailwater fishery.

**Nonnative invasion.** Stocking began early (at least 20 nonnative species were planted in Utah before 1900), shifted to predatory sportfish, and later included tropical aquarium escapees. The basin's fauna rose from about 30 native species to 80 or more. Reservoirs favored lentic-adapted nonnative fishes such as threadfin shad, red shiners, green sunfish, and striped bass. Interactions with natives include predation on larvae, competition for space and food, and fin damage by mosquitofish.

## Key Findings

- **Historical decline:** Fish fauna collapse began just after 1900, progressing from downstream to upstream in the same sequence as river regulation.
- **Current status:** Of the native species listed for Grand Canyon National Park, humpback chub is the only one with a reproducing population there; bonytail, Colorado squawfish, and razorback sucker are extirpated or exceedingly rare. Speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker, and bluehead sucker remain relatively common.
- **Dams alone insufficient:** The author argues that blockage of spawning migrations and cold water are the only direct dam effects that clearly extirpate native species, and is not fully convinced even of these. He hypothesizes that nonnative fish introduction and enhancement, enabled by river alteration, forced natives to extinction.
- **Nonnative fishes dominate:** The author states that "no part of the river is natural so long as alien fishes predominate," and that controlling nonnative warmwater species is "the single most important requirement" for native fish survival.
- **Flooding as a partial benefit:** Canyon floods temporarily displace nonnative fishes, benefiting natives, but reservoirs and ponds upstream reinvade canyons soon after.
- **Razorback recruitment failure:** Lake Mohave razorbacks spawn intensely, larvae emerge, and no recruits have been found since 1967. Predation by nonnative fishes is considered the most likely cause of larval loss, after rotenone removal of predators allowed natural reproduction.
- **Warming as a tradeoff:** Warming Marble/Grand canyon waters could allow reintroduction of squawfish and bonytail but would displace the trout fishery and favor warmwater nonnatives. Modifying Glen Canyon Dam intakes is described as potentially opening "a Pandora's box."
- **Striped bass and smelt:** Striped bass fisheries collapsed in Lake Powell and Lake Mohave after overpopulation and food shortage. A proposal to stock rainbow smelt in Lake Powell is opposed, with the author predicting it would damage fishes already in jeopardy.
- **Policy and research critique:** Agency-driven, short-term, unpublished research dominates; the author calls for peer-reviewed open literature, external senior scientists, and renewed commitment to endangered species legislation.
- **Conclusion:** The prognosis is poor. The author states that "the biologists have done their jobs," that broodstocks and techniques exist, and that current politics stand in the way of a successful management program for the Grand Canyon region.
