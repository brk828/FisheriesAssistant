# Phylogenetic and Biogeographic Analysis of Western North American Springsnails (Pyrgulopsis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae #gastropoda #freshwater-ecology #phylogenetics #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This Scholarly Studies Program proposal (Winter 1999 review cycle) by Robert Hershler ([[NMNH]]) and Hsiu-Ping Liu ([[Southwest Missouri State University]]) seeks funding to conduct a comprehensive phylogenetic and biogeographic analysis of *[[Pyrgulopsis]]*, the largest genus of nonmarine snails in North America (123 species). The project aims to evaluate monophyly of the genus using mitochondrial DNA sequences (mtCOI and mtCOII genes, approximately 2500 bp total) combined with morphological characters, and to use the resulting phylogeny to analyze historical biogeography of western North America. The proposal represents a resubmission with modifications addressing previous panel concerns about cryptic species recognition, analytical methods, and sequencing protocols. The study will sample 68 taxa (62 described species plus 6 undescribed congeners) from 104 populations across the western United States and northern Mexico, representing the full range of morphological variation and geographic distribution within the genus.

## Background & Description

*[[Pyrgulopsis]]* belongs to the North American subfamily [[Nymphophilinae]] (family [[Hydrobiidae]]) and represents a major component of western aquatic biodiversity. Most species (94 of 113 western species) are endemic to single springs, spring systems, or local topographic basins, providing fine-scale endemism suitable for assessing historical area relationships in a geologically complex region. The genus is distributed across interior drainages of northeast Mexico, the [[Rio Grande]], [[Colorado River]], [[Great Basin]], [[Snake River]], [[Sacramento River]], and California coastal drainages. The fossil record suggests a minimally late Miocene age (10 Ma) for the genus based on *P. truckeensis* from the [[Truckee Formation]].

Previous morphology-based phylogenetic analysis (Hershler 1994) failed to establish monophyly and provided little resolution, with 1959 equally parsimonious trees (CI=0.24). Preliminary molecular data from seven congeners using approximately 600 bp of mtCOI gene yielded encouraging results: 582 bp aligned sequences with 125 parsimony-informative characters produced a single shortest tree (428 steps, CI=0.64) with high bootstrap support for most branches. Results were congruent with morphological evidence suggesting eastern species allocated to *Pyrgulopsis* are not closely related to western taxa, and indicated a direction of penial morphology evolution within the genus.

The genus exhibits remarkable morphological variation in penial structures, which provide potentially useful phylogenetic characters though questions of structural homology remain unresolved. Many congeners are strongly differentiated by autapomorphies. Several broadly distributed species require revision based on morphological variation and fixed-allele differences among populations from different drainages (e.g., *P. kolobensis*, *P. wongi*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

*[[Pyrgulopsis]]* springsnails are obligately aquatic with limited vagility, presumably incapable of dispersing across terrestrial barriers. They exhibit much greater local endemism than western fishes and salamanders, often persisting in poorly watered basins devoid of vertebrates. These tiny snails are typically concentrated near spring sources rather than uniformly distributed within drainage systems, contributing to their tendency for local differentiation. A few species inhabit lakes, rivers, and large streams. Some species are restricted to basin floor habitats while others also occupy montane slopes, suggesting possible upland headwater transfers across drainage divides.

Western aquatic habitats are being degraded at accelerated rates due to human activities. *Pyrgulopsis* species are particularly sensitive to perturbations because of their small-sized habitats and narrow distributions. Some congeners have become extinct in historic times and many others are at risk, with acquisition of material for study needing to occur while the fauna remains relatively intact. The [[Amargosa River]] basin, a major hotspot of aquatic endemism in the West, has been identified as a biotic and geologic composite based on previous work on the related genus *[[Tryonia]]*.

## Key Findings

**Review Committee Assessment (Winter 1999):**

The panel reconfirmed this could be an important study group and lauded the intended utilization of complementary morphological and molecular data sets. The 2.5 Kb mtDNA region represents an adjustment from the earlier version and should be sufficient. The number of taxa has been appropriately scaled to 68 (about half of perceived taxa). The weeding approach rather than focus on a subclade is reasonable given that no such unit can be recognized at this time.

**Concerns Raised:**

- Recognition of cryptic taxa is likely beyond reach as 200 samples from 133 populations is insufficient for population-genetic methods; this matter had not been adequately addressed
- Reviewers were critical about suggested methods of analysis; the statement about "no evidence of saturation" was incorrect
- The scaled-back biogeographic analysis created concern about perceived hesitation in utilizing analytical tools for assessing patterns of congruence between organismal and biogeographic topologies
- Sequencing protocol likely too optimistic: good-quality reads rarely exceed 700 bp, requiring 4 reactions for COII and 6 for COI (75% increase in total reactions)
- Estimated cost per reaction ($12) is about twice the common rate for core labs (e.g., University of Iowa, Cornell), though using such facilities may help keep expenses down

**Proposal Modifications:**

The investigators responded by: (1) removing the cryptic species component, submitting it separately to Department of Interior; (2) clarifying that saturation analysis showed no indication within the ingroup based on nucleotide substitution patterns; (3) incorporating multiple biogeographic analytical methods including [[Brooks Parsimony Analysis]], [[Component Analysis]], and COMPARE in MacClade; (4) increasing sequencing reactions as recommended; (5) justifying that Liu's lab sequencing cost ($10 per reaction) is actually lower than many other institutions ($17-$30 for non-affiliated faculty).

**Budget Request:** Year 1: $32,507; Year 2: $33,951; Total: $66,458 over two years

**Previous SSP Funding Success:** Hershler received three previous SSP awards (1988-1995, totaling $145,977) that resulted in multiple peer-reviewed publications on hydrobiid systematics and biogeography, including comprehensive treatments of *[[Tryonia]]* and Great Basin springsnails.
