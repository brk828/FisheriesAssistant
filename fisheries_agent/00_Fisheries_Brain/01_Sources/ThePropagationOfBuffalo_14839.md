#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary
This 1952 report from *The Progressive Fish-Culturist* documents two methods of propagating [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*, listed in text as *Megastomatobus cyprinella*) and [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) at the U.S. Fish-Cultural Station in Natchitoches, Louisiana, in 1951. The authors, Meddie C. Walker and Philip T. Frank, aimed to produce fingerling-sized buffalo for experimental use, noting a lack of recent literature on buffalo propagation. Two approaches were tested: (1) **artificial propagation** using hand-stripped gametes and hatching jars, and (2) **natural propagation** induced by simulating a river's spring flood pulse within a controlled pond, a method derived from [[Canfield 1922]].

## Background & Description
- **Sexual dimorphism at spawning**: Female buffalo have smooth skin, while ripe males develop small, rough dermal protuberances producing a "sandpaper" texture — a key field method for sex identification during spawning season.
- **Brood fish size**:
  - Smallmouth buffalo broodstock: ~8 lbs (three fish, artificial spawning trial); pond-stocked adults averaged 5 lbs.
  - Bigmouth buffalo broodstock: ~4 lbs (two fish).
- **Egg characteristics**: Ripe eggs are watery, free-flowing, and naturally adhesive. Water-hardened eggs approximately doubled in size compared to freshly stripped eggs.
- **Fry characteristics**: Newly hatched fry resembled mosquito larvae in size and were transparent.
- **Growth benchmarks** (artificial propagation cohort):
  - Day 0 (hatch): mosquito-larva sized, transparent.
  - ~5 weeks (May 21): 2 inches in length.
  - ~7 weeks (June 4): 3 inches long, 10 lbs per thousand fish.
- **Natural propagation cohort**: 15,000 No. 3 fingerlings recovered weighing 112.5 lbs total (June 4 drain).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Spawning trigger**: [[Buffalo Fish]] spawning is strongly correlated with rising water levels, mimicking natural spring river floods — heavy rainfall events preceded spawning activity in both the wild hoop-net fishery and the experimental pond.
- **Habitat association**: Wild spawning was observed by commercial fishermen in hoop nets following a heavy rain event (March 29, 1951), indicating buffalo aggregate and spawn in response to hydrological pulses in riverine systems.
- **Spawning substrate**: Rye grass was scattered in the experimental pond to provide spawning material, suggesting buffalo utilize submerged vegetation as an egg-adhesion substrate in natural settings.
- **Temperature range for spawning/incubation**: Water temperatures during the natural spawning event ranged from 56–62° F, with a notable temperature drop to 41° F during a dormant period, followed by resumed activity after warming and rainfall.
- **Interspecies co-occurrence**: Both bigmouth and smallmouth buffalo were found together in the same hoop-net catches and pond environment, requiring careful species segregation during hatchery operations to prevent hybridization.

## Key Findings
- **Artificial spawning protocol**: Hand-stripping of milt and eggs, followed by fertilization in a washbasin, was successful for both species. Corn starch (~2 oz per pint of eggs/milt mixture) was critical for de-adhering eggs during a required **1-hour stirring period** — shorter stirring (30 min) caused egg clumping and losses, particularly in smallmouth buffalo.
- **Incubation**: Eggs incubated in modified hatching jars over ~10 days at temperatures between 56–62° F. Fungal infestation appeared on Day 9 (April 7) and was treated with a saturated copper sulfate (CuSO₄) solution, believed to retard fungal growth.
- **Hatch timing**: Hatching began April 9 at 62° F water temperature, proceeding gradually over 2 days.
- **Fry handling**: Fry were held 2 days post-hatch to absorb yolk sacs before being transferred to rearing ponds, first to a 0.28-acre pond, then a 0.88-acre pond, following stocking density concerns (~200,000 fry in the small pond caused slowed growth).
- **Natural pond propagation success**: Raising pond water levels by 2 feet over 1 week (mimicking natural flooding) did not immediately induce spawning — fish required an additional natural rainfall trigger (~1 month later) before spawning activity was observed, despite the artificial water-level manipulation.
- **Cross-validation of methods**: Ripe eggs collected artificially from the river population were obtained the day after spawning was observed in the experimental pond, and hatchery-hatched schools of fry appeared in the pond shortly after wild fry hatched — suggesting synchronized spawning timing between wild and experimental populations under similar environmental cues.
- **Historical context**: This work references [[Canfield 1922]] as the original source for the water-level-manipulation method of inducing natural buffalo spawning, indicating continuity in propagation technique over three decades with limited intervening documentation.
- **Editorial note**: Co-author Philip T. Frank resigned from the Fish and Wildlife Service after this study to pursue a B.S. in fish culture at Alabama Polytechnic Institute.
