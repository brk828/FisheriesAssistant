# Copper Sulfate Effects on Microcystis and Zooplankton in Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by Johnie H. Crance (Alabama Department of Conservation), reprinted from *The Progressive Fish-Culturist*, tested whether low concentrations of [[Copper Sulfate]] (0.05 to 0.08 p.p.m.) could control scum-forming [[Microcystis]] blooms in farm ponds without harming [[Zooplankton]] or fish. Five earthen ponds at the Auburn University Farm Ponds Project were treated during 1957. Microcystis colony counts declined after most applications, zooplankton numbers generally rose within days, and no oxygen-deficiency fish kills were observed. The paper is based on part of a 1958 M.S. thesis in fisheries management.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Dense [[Microcystis]] blooms cause economic losses by producing odors and tastes in drinking water, clogging filters, deterring swimmers and anglers, and reducing sales of fishing permits. In Alabama, fish kills followed sudden die-offs of blooms that depleted dissolved oxygen.
- **Control method:** Copper sulfate is among the most widely used algicides. Earlier work (Steyn, 1944) reported that Microcystis flos-aquae can be eradicated with about 1 lb of copper sulfate per 100,000 gallons of water.
- **Toxicity context:** Doudoroff and Katz (1953) concluded that copper sulfate below 0.025 p.p.m. as Cu was not rapidly fatal to most common fishes. Fisher (1956) found Daphnia survived 36 hours in 5 p.p.m., and Chironomidae survived 7 days in 10 p.p.m. Daphnia were killed by 0.5 p.p.m. over 20 hours in lake water with a methyl orange alkalinity of 224 p.p.m. (Rose, 1954).
- **Rationale:** Hale (1950) proposed that killing algae could benefit fish by triggering a food chain of bacteria, protozoa, and then Rotifera and Crustacea.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study ponds**
- Five earthen ponds on the [[Farm Ponds Project]], Auburn University, Auburn, Alabama.
- Pond areas ranged from 2.5 to 25.5 acres; average depths 3.8 to 5.9 feet.
- Total hardness ranged from 32 to 46 p.p.m. as CaCO3.
- Established fish populations: [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*).
- Ponds were fertilized following Swingle and Smith (1947) recommendations.

**Treatment**
- Copper sulfate (CuSO4·5H2O, at least 98% pure) was applied at 0.7 to 0.9 lb per surface acre (0.05 to 0.08 p.p.m.) between July 27 and November 15, 1957.
- Treatments used cotton cloth bags submerged about 0.5 ft, which released the chemical over about 24 hours; wave action distributed it.
- Applications were made only when dense blooms were present and skies were not cloudy.

**Sampling and methods**
- Microcystis colonies counted from 1 mL surface subsamples in a Sedgwick-Rafter cell (10 random fields, multiplied by 100).
- Zooplankton concentrated with a Wisconsin plankton net (about 0.06 mm openings) and counted under a dissecting microscope.
- Chemical parameters measured: phosphorus, nitrate nitrogen, ammonia nitrogen, calcium, total hardness, carbon dioxide, and oxygen.

## Key Findings

1. **Microcystis control:** An appreciable decrease in Microcystis colonies generally followed applications of 0.05 to 0.08 p.p.m. copper sulfate. The longest controlled period was about 25 days (ponds 8 and 6).
2. **Inverse relation with zooplankton:** Microcystis abundance and zooplankton numbers were generally inversely related. Zooplankton usually increased within a few days of treatment.
3. **Dominant taxa:** Copepods predominated in all ponds except pond 8, where cladocerans and copepods were roughly equal. Rotifers, Chaoboridae larvae, and ostracods were also frequent; their counts are not reported in detail.
4. **Declining response:** Reductions were generally smaller after later treatments. Pond 14's final treatment (0.07 p.p.m., September 13) reduced the alga less than its first (0.05 p.p.m., August 4). The authors suggested possible resistance, while noting that seasonal temperature decline also contributed.
5. **Seasonal change:** Microcystis was greener in July and early August and yellow, smaller-celled, and muddy-looking from late August to November. Species differences were not confirmed.
6. **No fish kills:** No oxygen-deficiency fish kills followed treatment. The author noted that the risk appeared lower when about 0.75 lb per acre was used.
7. **No chemical correlation:** Microcystis abundance did not correlate with phosphorus, nitrate nitrogen, ammonia nitrogen, or calcium concentrations.
8. **Conclusion:** Low-dose copper sulfate can suppress Microcystis in farm ponds for several weeks with no detected harm to fish, while zooplankton numbers generally increase.
