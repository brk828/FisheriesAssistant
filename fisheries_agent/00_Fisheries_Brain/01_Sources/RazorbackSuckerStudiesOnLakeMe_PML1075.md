# Razorback Sucker Studies on Lake Mead, Nevada: 1996-1997 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report, prepared by [[BIO/WEST, Inc.]] for the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]] (SNWA), documents the first year (October 1996 to June 1997) of a multi-year study of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mead]]. Sampling by the [[Nevada Division of Wildlife]] (NDOW) in the 1990s suggested that a relatively young population existed in the lake, unlike other Lower Colorado River reservoirs where old adults predominated. The study aimed to determine population size, life history, habitat use and spawning locations using [[Trammel Netting]], [[Sonic Telemetry]] and [[Larval Fish Sampling]].

Two study sites were used: [[Las Vegas Bay]] (near [[Blackbird Point]]) and [[Echo Bay]]. From October 1996 to June 1997, 61 adult and nearly 5,000 larval razorback suckers were collected, and 21 adults were surgically implanted with sonic tags. Spawning occurred from mid-February to early May, peaking in March and April, with large larval numbers found only at Echo Bay. The revised population estimate was roughly 400 to 450 adults, most at Las Vegas Bay. Recaptured fish grew about 10 mm per year. Of the 21 tagged fish, only 7 remained active and consistently detectable through June 1997, raising concerns about tag expulsion. The study was to continue with the same general sampling program under SNWA funding.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid native to the Colorado River Basin. It was once widespread but has declined due to mainstem dam construction, altered flow and temperature regimes, habitat loss, and non-native species predation and competition. It is listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1991 final rule).

Earlier Lake Mead collections were sparse. NDOW captures from 1990 to 1996 (Table 1) included fish at Las Vegas Wash and Echo Bay, along with stocked subadults. A total of 26 fish were stocked into Las Vegas Bay in 1994 and 13 (reported as 14 in one passage) into Echo Bay in 1995, all PIT-tagged and derived from the Dexter National Fish Hatchery 1984 year-class reared at Floyd Lamb State Park.

Observations suggested Lake Mead fish were much younger than [[Lake Mohave]] fish, showing low incidence of blindness, lesions, tumors and scarring. One 366 mm total length (TL) near-adult was collected in 1994. The authors noted Lake Mead may be the first reservoir where a relatively young population persists more than 50 years after reservoir formation.

Two hypotheses explain recruitment: (1) fish moved into Lake Mead from the Grand Canyon after [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closure in 1963, when cold releases (7-11 °C post-dam versus pre-dam 9.5-29.5 °C) may have triggered downstream movement; and (2) some larvae spawned in the lake by the original population survived to adulthood and returned to natal spawning sites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mead water levels rose roughly 10 feet during the study (from about 1190 to 1200 feet above sea level), flooding salt cedar (*Tamarix* sp.) and creating riparian and wetland vegetation. Las Vegas Wash is a permanent inflow of 200 to 250 cubic feet per second carrying urban runoff, tertiary-treated wastewater and industrial effluent, making Las Vegas Bay a highly productive system. Blackbird Point spawning occurred at depths of 40 to 80 feet, much deeper than typical Lake Mohave spawning sites (1 to 15 feet). Echo Bay has no permanent inflow but is bordered by flooded salt cedar and wash mouths.

Echo Bay spawning took place in 10 to 20 feet of water near the back of the bay, in two small coves protected from wind, with emergent cover nearby. Winter lake temperature was a constant 13 °C, limiting the usefulness of temperature-sensing tags for depth determination.

Other species captured incidentally included [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], green sunfish, mosquitofish, threadfin shad and bluegill.

## Key Findings

**Population size**
- The initial [[Capture-Recapture]] estimate (program CAPTURE and Schnabel estimator, 1990-1996 data) was about 150 to 275 adults at Las Vegas Bay and 50 to 75 at Echo Bay; the Service adopted a conservative combined estimate of 200 adults, permitting tagging of up to 10 percent (20 fish).
- The revised estimate (adding 1996-1997 data) was about 300 to 400 at Las Vegas Bay and about 50 to 60 at Echo Bay, for a total of roughly 400 to 450 adults. Confidence intervals remained wide, so more mark-recapture data are needed.
- No overlap between the two groups was seen in sonic tracking or trammel netting, so separate estimates were made.

**Capture and catch**
- Trammel netting totaled 134 net nights (95 at Las Vegas Bay, 39 at Echo Bay). Catch rates were 0.29 fish/net night at Las Vegas Bay and 0.85 at Echo Bay.
- Of 61 adults caught (28 Las Vegas Bay, 33 Echo Bay), 24 were recaptures; 17 of the 33 Echo Bay captures were recaptured fish.
- Catch rates peaked in April at both sites.

**Spawning**
- Ripe males were collected from February; the first ripe female came from Blackbird Point on February 25. Spent fish were taken in March and May.
- Spawning ran from mid-February to early May, with peak activity in March and April.
- Echo Bay produced nearly 5,000 larvae overall, with 3,193 at Site 1 (CPM 11.01) and 1,273 at Site 2 (CPM 9.79) during March to May. Larvae were far fewer at Las Vegas Bay.
- Larval light traps performed roughly an order of magnitude worse than crappie lights and were dropped after initial tests.

**Movement and telemetry**
- Echo Bay fish were tracked as far as 15 miles north to Muddy Bay near the Muddy River mouth, with frequent use of Rogers Bay (about 3 miles north).
- Las Vegas Bay fish mostly stayed within 5 miles of their capture site and used the mouth of Las Vegas Wash.
- Fish from the two areas did not overlap in distribution. Movement appeared reduced compared to Lake Mohave, where tagged fish moved up to 35 miles.
- Among the 21 tagged adults (20 tags, one reused), only 7 were still active and consistently detected in June 1997 (Las Vegas Bay: 4; Echo Bay: 3). Six had stopped moving but were locatable, and several were not relocated.

**Growth**
- Recaptured wild fish grew an average of about 9.6 mm TL per year (range 1.1 to 22.4 mm), with a combined average of about 10 mm per year.
- This is about three times the growth reported for Lake Mohave or Upper Colorado River riverine populations, which the authors linked to possibly younger age structure.
- Stocked fish grew about twice as fast, with the fastest recorded at about 20 mm per year.

**Tag loss**
- The report attributes tag loss to possible tag expulsion (confirmed in one case: tag 2264 recovered by SCUBA lying partly buried in silt), tag malfunction, fish death, or signal masking by vegetation or structure.
- Expulsion rates of 30 to 50 percent were reported in a Wahweap hatchery pond study of Upper Basin fish, and the authors noted similar high loss rates in other recent telemetry studies.

**Juveniles**
- Juvenile sampling (minnow traps, seines, hoop and fyke nets, gill nets) produced no juvenile razorback suckers. A single 20 mm TL larva was captured by seine on May 21.

**Recommendations for Year Two**
- Continue monthly [[Trammel Netting]] in areas of concentration.
- Track sonic-tagged fish and implant 10 additional tags (31 total).
- Sample larvae at both sites during the spawning period.
- Continue juvenile sampling at both sites, with SNWA funding at a level similar to 1996-1997.
