## Executive Summary

This 1994 workshop presentation by Bruce D. Shupp (Chief, Bureau of Fisheries, NYS Dept. of Environmental Conservation) critiques the breakdown in cooperation ("collision") between federal fish and wildlife agencies (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service [FWS] and National Biological Survey [NBS]) and state fishery agencies during the early-to-mid 1990s. The author argues that a change in Department of Interior/FWS leadership led to hastily developed federal initiatives — notably the **Supplemental Environmental Impact Statement for Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration (SEIS)** and **"An Ecosystem Approach to Fish and Wildlife Conservation"** — that were rolled out without adequate consultation with state partners, who hold the primary legal mandate for managing most U.S. fish and wildlife resources. The paper calls for renewed dialogue ("LET'S TALK") between federal and state agencies to repair the partnership and align on shared [[Ecosystem Management]] goals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While no species-level ecology is discussed, the document situates its argument within the broader context of aquatic ecosystem governance:

- **Geographic/administrative scope:** Northeastern United States and [[Great Lakes Basin]], with the author's direct partnerships spanning FWS, NBS, other states, universities, and the public.
- **Conceptual "ecosystem approach":** The paper notes that state agencies had already been implementing an [[Ecosystem Approach]] to resource management for over 30 years prior to the federal government's 1990s promotion of the concept as novel — implying long-standing multi-species, habitat-level management practice at the state level (rather than single-species regulation alone).
- **Referenced ecological challenges:** The author acknowledges ongoing management challenges including restoring depleted [[Salmon]] stocks and controlling **nuisance exotic species**, both framed as central issues within the ecosystem-level management mandate shared between state and federal authorities.
- **Institutional "habitat":** Rather than a biological niche, the paper describes an administrative "niche" — the division of authority in which **states hold primary statutory responsibility** for managing most fish and wildlife resources, while federal agencies (FWS/NBS) provide funding, coordination, and broader ecosystem-zone frameworks (e.g., FWS Region 5's ecosystem "teams" in the Northeast).

## Key Findings

- The perceived "collision" between federal and state fisheries agencies stemmed from **federal exuberance and lack of consultation**, not deliberate sabotage — new Department of Interior/FWS leadership pursued new initiatives without first assessing existing state programs.
- Two federal documents — the **SEIS** and **"An Ecosystem Approach to Fish and Wildlife Conservation"** — are identified as flashpoints, criticized for being based on "perceptions, myths, misconceptions and lack of facts" regarding the existing national fish and wildlife management effort.
- The **ecosystem approach** promoted by FWS as an innovation was, according to the author, already standard practice among state resource agencies for over three decades.
- States are legally and constitutionally the primary managers of most fish and wildlife resources in the U.S., and the author insists this mandate must anchor any new federal-state partnership structure.
- A secondary "collision" arose from the federal government's slow response to extensive, detailed state critique of the SEIS and ecosystem approach documents.
- Positive developments cited include a **May 1994 state-federal partnership meeting in San Antonio**, hosted by the [[Southeastern Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies]], and the formation of **FWS Region 5 ecosystem "teams"** in the Northeast that nominally include states as "full partners" — though the author notes persistent inconsistency and poor coordination across FWS regions and the Washington office.
- The paper closes with a call to action: federal agencies should slow national-level planning and internal program evaluations (e.g., fish hatcheries, ecosystem management zones) until genuine dialogue with state and public partners is established — summarized in the recurring imperative **"LET'S TALK."**
- Broader framing: the past 50 years of U.S. fish and wildlife conservation are characterized as highly successful, and the author cautions against discarding proven state-led management strengths in pursuit of untested federal restructuring.
