# Trout or Salmon? Identification Guide for New York Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #field-identification #game-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1952 field guide addresses the practical challenges of identifying salmonid game fishes in [[New York State]] waters. The document provides diagnostic characters for six species: [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*), [[Lake Trout]] (*Cristivomer namaycush*), [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*), and [[Red Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*). The guide emphasizes that identification difficulties arise from both species diversity and extreme intraspecific variability, including developmental changes, diet-dependent coloration, and rapid color adaptation to background conditions. Practical identification focuses on specimens approximately seven inches in length, corresponding to legal size limits for most protected species.

## Background & Description

The guide covers six salmonid species inhabiting New York waters, acknowledging but deliberately avoiding subspecies controversies (e.g., whether landlocked salmon differ from sea-run [[Atlantic Salmon]], whether rainbows and steelheads are identical, or whether [[Kokanee]] differ genetically from sea-run red salmon).

**Key Anatomical Features:**

The [[Adipose Fin]] serves as a primary diagnostic character for the Salmonidae family, though it also occurs in bullheads, smelt, whitefish, and ciscoes. Within salmonids, adipose fin characteristics help distinguish species.

**Pectoral Fin Ray Counts:**
- [[Lake Trout]]: Usually 9 rays
- [[Brook Trout]]: 10 or 11 rays
- [[Brown Trout]]: 10 or 11 rays
- [[Atlantic Salmon]]/Landlocked: 10 or 11 rays
- [[Rainbow Trout]]: 14 to 16 rays
- [[Red Salmon]]: Data not specified

**Scale Counts (Lateral Line):**
- [[Lake Trout]]: More than 190 scales (very fine)
- All other species: Less than 145 scales (coarse)

**Coloration Patterns:**

[[Brook Trout]]: Whitish markings on darker background; red spots (sometimes absent in young and hatchery fish); fins usually have strong black and white edging; forked tail.

[[Brown Trout]]: Wavy dark markings; red spots often absent; adipose fin usually reddish; reddish tinge not present on many hatchery specimens.

[[Rainbow Trout]]: Red stripe often absent; tail heavily spotted; black spots or black edging on adipose fin.

[[Lake Trout]]: No black spots on body; tail forked.

[[Atlantic Salmon]]: Red spots only in young (parr stage); tail forked in young; pectoral fin usually blackish; dusky pectoral and adipose fins in silvery smolt stage; loses red spots above seven inches when transitioning to smolt coloration.

[[Red Salmon]]: Black markings often faint; being experimentally stocked in multiple waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document emphasizes extreme phenotypic plasticity in salmonids related to environmental conditions:

**Ontogenetic Variation:** Fish undergo dramatic changes from egg through fry, immature, and adult stages. [[Atlantic Salmon]] exhibit two distinct immature forms: parr (with red spots) and smolt (plain silver without spots).

**Diet-Dependent Coloration:** [[Brook Trout]] fed on certain foods fail to develop red spots. [[Brown Trout]] typically show red spots in streams but may lack them in lakes.

**Background Adaptation:** Salmonids possess the ability to change color almost instantly (light or dark) according to background conditions.

**Habitat-Specific Variation:** Three species ([[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]]) can develop bright silvery coloration with X-shaped black spots in lake populations, creating convergent phenotypes. In this silvery form, salmon show dusky pectoral and adipose fins, brown trout show yellowish pectoral fins with reddish tinge on adipose fin, and rainbow show black spots or black edging on adipose fin.

**Hatchery Effects:** Many recognition marks consistently found in wild trout are absent in hatchery-reared specimens. Recently planted brook trout may lack red spots and show reduced fin marking contrast due to pale background color. Normal coloration is regained over time after stocking.

## Key Findings

**Identification Strategy:** The guide advocates searching for points of difference rather than similarity, as multiple species can appear superficially similar under certain conditions.

**Legal Considerations:** Proper identification is essential for compliance with [[Conservation Laws]], particularly the seven-inch size limit for most protected salmonids. Anglers must distinguish legal-sized [[Brook Trout]] from undersized [[Lake Trout]], and avoid mistaking short salmon for legal-sized [[Brown Trout]].

**Diagnostic Approach:** The seven-inch size standard is optimal because: (1) it corresponds to legal size limits, (2) [[Atlantic Salmon]] typically transition from parr to smolt coloration at this size, and (3) silvery variations in lake populations of [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] may appear at this size.

**Practical Application:** The guide emphasizes that field identification requires knowledge of anatomical terminology (fins and other structures) to apply diagnostic characters effectively. The author, John R. Greeley, invokes Louis Agassiz's principle to "Study Nature, not books," acknowledging that written descriptions and illustrations provide only starting points for field identification of highly variable organisms.

**Conservation Value:** Beyond legal compliance and avoiding fines, enhanced identification skills increase angler enjoyment and contribute to responsible fisheries management through accurate reporting and selective harvest.
