# The Toxicity of Zinc Sulphate to Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #ecotoxicology #freshwater-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by R. Lloyd (Water Pollution Research Laboratory, Stevenage) investigates the acute toxicity of zinc sulphate to [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) under varying water chemistry and physical conditions. The central finding is that water hardness is the dominant factor modulating zinc toxicity: zinc sulphate is markedly less toxic in hard water than in soft water, with the dose-response relationship shifting from curvilinear (approaching a toxicity threshold) in hard water to linear in very soft water. The study also demonstrates that calcium speciation (bicarbonate vs. chloride) affects toxicity independent of total hardness, that temperature affects survival time but not threshold concentration, and that dissolved oxygen depletion increases zinc toxicity unless fish are pre-acclimatized. Critically, the paper provides histological and radiotracer evidence refuting the prevailing hypothesis that zinc kills fish via mucus coagulation on gills, instead implicating direct cytological damage to gill epithelium as the mechanism of death.

## Background & Description

While this is primarily a toxicological (not morphological) study, the following anatomical/physiological findings pertain to *Salmo gairdnerii*:

- **Gill epithelium**: Target tissue of zinc toxicity. Histological examination (Dr Gwyneth Parry) revealed:
  - Sublethal zinc (3 p.p.m.): no structural deviation from normal within 2 days.
  - Moderately high zinc (4.0–4.5 p.p.m.): swelling of gill lamellae prior to death.
  - Very high zinc (20 p.p.m.): cytological breakdown of gill epithelium within 2.5 hr.
  - No excessive mucus secretion was observed at any concentration, contradicting earlier "mucus coagulation/asphyxiation" models of heavy metal toxicity (Carpenter 1927; Ellis 1937; [[Jones 1938]]; Westfall 1945).
- **Zinc tissue distribution** (via ⁶⁵Zn radiotracer, two fish killed at 20 p.p.m. Zn):
  - Highest concentration in gills (63.2 and 59.6 µg Zn/g wet weight), but this represented only 17–35% of total body zinc — most zinc was distributed throughout remaining body tissues, liver, and precipitated mucus of the post-anal region.
  - Contrasts with intraperitoneal injection studies (Saiki & Mori 1955) where kidney accumulated the highest zinc concentration, suggesting gill uptake from waterborne exposure is a distinct toxicological pathway from systemic distribution.
- **Osmoregulation**: In very soft water (12 p.p.m. as CaCO₃) lacking supplemental NaCl, unfed rainbow trout showed a measurable drop in blood osmotic pressure (via freezing-point depression) within 96 hr, indicating osmoregulatory stress; addition of 15 p.p.m. NaCl eliminated this effect.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Species**: *Salmo gairdnerii* (Richardson) — [[Rainbow Trout]], a member of [[Salmonidae]], tested at ages 3–15 months, sourced from commercial trout farms supplied by both hard (280 p.p.m. CaCO₃) and soft (50 p.p.m. CaCO₃) water systems.
- **Habitat chemistry parameters manipulated**:
  - **Total hardness**: 320 p.p.m. (hard, borehole water), 50 p.p.m., and 12 p.p.m. as CaCO₃ (soft, artificially demineralized).
  - **pH**: ranged 6.5–7.8 across experiments; zinc solubility decreases at pH >8.0, becoming largely insoluble by pH 8.5 in hard water.
  - **Temperature**: 12–22°C range tested.
  - **Dissolved oxygen**: tested at 8.9, 6.2, 3.8 p.p.m. O₂ (non-acclimatized) and 9.45, 6.3, 3.65 p.p.m. O₂ (acclimatized), against a fully air-saturated baseline.
- **Relevance to freshwater pollution ecology**: This work is foundational to [[Water Quality Criteria]] development for heavy metal contamination in rivers receiving industrial effluent, directly informing regulatory thresholds for zinc discharge relative to receiving water hardness. It underscores that toxicity bioassays must report the mineral content of dilution water to be ecologically meaningful — a concern raised regarding earlier studies (e.g., [[Goodman 1951]]) where stock tank water itself contained ~1 p.p.m. zinc, potentially inducing unrecognized acclimatization in control populations.
- **Comparative species data**: Findings are contextualized against [[Cairns & Scheier 1957]]'s work on common bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*), which found zinc ~4x more toxic in soft (~50 p.p.m.) vs. hard (~200 p.p.m.) water over 96 hr — broadly consistent with the hardness-dependent curves established here for rainbow trout.

## Key Findings

1. **Water hardness is the primary modulator of zinc toxicity.** In hard water (320 p.p.m. CaCO₃), the log-concentration/log-survival relationship is curvilinear, approaching a threshold concentration (C₀ = 3.5 p.p.m. Zn) below which zinc is non-toxic. In very soft water (12 p.p.m. CaCO₃), the relationship is linear (Ostwald's equation: CT = K, n = 1.064, K = 1648 min), with no apparent threshold. At intermediate hardness (50 p.p.m.), the curve is intermediate/curvilinear (C₀ = 1.5 p.p.m. Zn, T₀ = 140 min).

2. **Threshold reaction time**: Under hard-water conditions, a threshold reaction time of ~160 minutes exists, below which increasing zinc concentration further does not reduce survival time. Toxic action is reversible for at least half of this threshold period — fish removed from 20 p.p.m. zinc solution at 40–80 min survived indefinitely, while those at 120+ min did not.

3. **Sublethal acclimatization confers resistance**: Fish pre-exposed to sublethal zinc concentrations (2.5–3.5 p.p.m.) for 14 days survived significantly longer (P<0.001) when subsequently challenged with 10 p.p.m. zinc (400–500 min) compared to non-acclimatized fish (290 min).

4. **Calcium speciation matters independent of total hardness**: Zinc was markedly less toxic in water where calcium was present as bicarbonate compared to water with an equivalent calcium concentration as chloride — refining and partially contrasting with [[Jones 1938]]'s findings on lead/zinc toxicity to sticklebacks.

5. **Precipitated zinc remains toxic**: Suspended (precipitated) zinc compounds formed at higher pH in hard water were approximately as toxic as an equivalent concentration of dissolved zinc, indicating that solubility state does not eliminate toxicity risk.

6. **Temperature effects survival time, not threshold**: A 10°C temperature rise (12→22°C) reduced median survival time by a factor of 2.35, consistent with general poison toxicology (typical factor 2–3), but the threshold concentration of zinc remained essentially unchanged across temperatures.

7. **Dissolved oxygen depletion increases toxicity, but acclimatization mitigates this**: Non-acclimatized fish showed increased zinc toxicity at lower DO; the ratio of zinc concentrations needed for equal toxicity (1000 min survival) between 100% and 40% air-saturation was 1:0.7. This effect was substantially reduced when fish were pre-acclimatized to the test oxygen level for 18 hr.

8. **Mechanism of death re-evaluated**: Contrary to earlier literature attributing heavy metal fish kills to mucus coagulation/asphyxiation on gills, this study found minimal mucus precipitation under most experimental conditions. Radiotracer (⁶⁵Zn) and gill-washing experiments confirmed zinc was incorporated into gill tissue itself (not merely adherent as external precipitate), and histology confirmed direct cytological breakdown of gill epithelium at high concentrations — supporting a mechanism of direct gill tissue damage rather than mucus-induced asphyxiation, paralleling findings by [[Schweiger 1957]] for nickel, mercury, and cadmium toxicity in carp.

9. **Practical implication for water quality assessment**: Toxicity data derived from distilled or very low-mineral-content water (where control fish die from osmotic stress alone) are of limited practical value; representative dilution water hardness must be specified and controlled when establishing safe discharge limits for zinc.
