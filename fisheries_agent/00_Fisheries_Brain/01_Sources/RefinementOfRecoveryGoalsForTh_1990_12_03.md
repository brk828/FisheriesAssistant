# Refinement of Recovery Goals for the Endangered Fishes of the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-conservation #cyprinidae #catostomidae #population-viability-analysis #colorado-river-basin

## Executive Summary

This 1990 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service draft memorandum outlines a plan to refine recovery goals for four rare/endangered fishes native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (historically "Colorado squawfish"), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The document does not describe fish morphology or natural history directly; rather, it proposes using **Population Viability Analysis (PVA)** to move beyond existing, largely qualitative recovery plan goals (based on professional judgement) toward quantitative, defensible targets for population size, number of populations, spatial distribution, and habitat area requirements (Maximum Area Requirements, MAR). The paper reviews demographic, environmental, catastrophic, and genetic sources of extinction risk, and proposes a workshop-driven process (February 1991) involving PVA authorities and species experts to identify data gaps and build quantitative recovery goals for the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Colorado River Basin]] mainstem and tributary specialists** — all four taxa are large-river endemics historically restricted to warm, turbid, free-flowing reaches of the Colorado River system.
- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]**: Target self-sustaining populations identified in the Green River Basin (Green River from its confluence with the Colorado River to Echo Park, lower 220 km of the [[Yampa River]], lower 240 km of the [[White River]]), the Colorado River from Palisade, Colorado to [[Lake Powell]], and the [[San Juan River]] from Lake Powell upstream to river kilometer 305.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Associated with canyon-bound reaches; candidate wild population locations include the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers in [[Grand Canyon]], the Black Rocks/Westwater Canyon area of the Colorado River, the Yampa and Green rivers in [[Dinosaur National Park]], Gray and Desolation Canyons on the Green River, and [[Cataract Canyon]] on the Colorado River.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: By 1990, populations were so depleted that the stated interim recovery objective was simply "prevent extinction," reflecting near-functional extirpation from the wild.
- **Metapopulation structure**: The paper frames these species' habitat use as patchily distributed across discrete river reaches separated by intervening unsuitable or lower-quality habitat — a classic [[Metapopulation]] structure — raising questions about whether recovery is better achieved via one large population or several interconnected subpopulations linked by [[Gene Flow]] corridors.
- **Threat drivers (systematic pressures)**: flow regulation (dams/diversions), water quality degradation, habitat alteration and fragmentation, and introduction of nonnative/exotic fishes — all directly tied to large-scale water development in the basin.
- **Stochastic risk factors**: demographic (survival, fecundity, sex ratio, disease), environmental (flow variability, food supply, biotic interactions), catastrophic (floods, droughts), and genetic (inbreeding, drift, founder effects) uncertainty.

## Key Findings

- **Existing Recovery Plan goals were qualitative**, based on professional judgement of Recovery Team experts, specifying only the *number* of populations needed (e.g., five viable wild + two refugia populations for [[Humpback Chub]]) without defined population sizes, genetic targets, or explicit habitat area requirements.
- **[[Population Viability Analysis]] (PVA)** was proposed as the primary tool to quantify: (1) total population size needed, (2) number of populations/subpopulations, (3) spatial distribution, and (4) Maximum Area Requirements (MAR) of habitat needed for persistence.
- **Metapopulation dynamics**: A metapopulation (N=10,000 distributed across subpopulations) may have higher persistence probability and greater genetic variance than a single large population of equal total size — provided subpopulations are connected by gene flow and occupy uncorrelated environments (i.e., a single catastrophe cannot strike all patches simultaneously). However, undersized subpopulations risk inbreeding depression and genetic drift, and local extinctions erode metapopulation-wide genetic variability.
- **Goodman's (1987) extinction model**: Formalizes expected time to extinction as a function of mean per capita growth rate (r), maximum population size (Nm), and variance in per capita growth rate (V). Surrogate parameters (e.g., optimal net reproductive rate as proxy for r, carrying capacity as proxy for Nm — both functions of body mass) can substitute when direct data are unavailable.
- **Demographic uncertainty** dominates extinction risk primarily in small populations (10s–100s of individuals); persistence time increases geometrically with population size.
- **Environmental uncertainty** causes persistence time to increase only linearly with population size — critically, there is **no minimum population size that guarantees long-term persistence** under environmental variability; very large or numerous populations are required absent reductions in variability or increases in growth rate.
- **Catastrophic uncertainty**: Persistence time increases linearly with the *log* of population size, implying diminishing returns — suggesting eventual extinction is inevitable for all species under sufficiently severe or frequent catastrophic regimes.
- **Genetic uncertainty**: Loss of heterozygosity via [[Genetic Drift]] and [[Inbreeding Depression]] becomes severe once populations bottleneck. A 1% decrease in genetic variance is associated with a 1–2% decrease in fitness; wildlife/animal breeding standards recommend keeping inbreeding below ~1% per generation. Even low rates of migration (as little as one migrant per generation) between subpopulations can substantially counteract drift and inbreeding effects, approximating panmictic behavior across a subdivided population.
- **Data requirements for PVA** (per Shaffer and Goodman, cited in text): annual survival and variance by life stage, reproduction rates, density-dependent effects on survival/reproduction, inter-population migration data, and year-to-year variability in vital rates. Where data are lacking, surrogate data from similar species/habitats or expert judgement may be substituted.
- **Process/timeline**: A PVA Workshop was scheduled for February 1991, bringing together the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], species experts, and PVA authorities, with a goal of finalized quantitative recovery goals by September 1991 if existing data proved sufficient; otherwise, additional study plans would be developed.
- **Precedent species for PVA application** cited include the Puerto Rican parrot, Florida panther, grizzly bear, Furbish's lousewort, Bay checkerspot butterfly, Northern spotted owl, California condor, black-footed ferret, and (via a habitat-driven variant) the [[Gila Topminnow]] and pupfish species — establishing PVA as a cross-taxa conservation planning tool being newly extended to Colorado River Basin fishes.
