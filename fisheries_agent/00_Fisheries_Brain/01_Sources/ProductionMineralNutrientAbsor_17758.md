# Production, Mineral Nutrient Absorption, and Biochemical Assimilation in Justicia americana and Alternanthera philoxeroides

#aquatic-botany #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-angiosperms

## Executive Summary

This study by Claude E. Boyd (Auburn University, 1969) quantifies seasonal production, mineral nutrient uptake, and biochemical assimilation in a [[Justicia Americana]] stand at [[Lake Ogletree]], Alabama, with comparative data from an [[Alternanthera Philoxeroides]] stand and four additional *J. americana* stands. The central finding is that [[Mobile Nutrients]] (nitrogen, sulfur, potassium) were absorbed mainly in early spring, before peak growth, and were then translocated to new tissue. The author proposes this strategy may help explain why shallow lakes dominated by vascular plants often contain little phytoplankton. Standing crop and plant chemistry also differed between lentic and lotic sites and tracked [[Water Hardness]].

## Background & Description

Vascular aquatic angiosperms often dominate shallow lakes, produce large standing crops, and cover extensive areas. Before this work, chemical composition and production had usually been measured separately, so nutrient stocks in lake vegetation were estimated from literature values. The study was designed to measure these processes together for individual stands.

Both focal species are emergent perennial herbs that often form extensive, nearly monospecific stands in shallow water of lakes and streams:

- **Justicia americana** ("water-willow"): dies back to the mud each winter, and regrowth apparently arises from rootstocks. Leaves are borne almost entirely on aerial stems, and submersed stems bear adventitious roots forming a thick mat.
- **Alternanthera philoxeroides** ("alligator weed"): studied in a wide, shallow drainage ditch at Maxwell Air Force Base, Montgomery, Alabama. Its root system was not extensive, and root production was not measured.

### Methods (summary)

- **Lake Ogletree:** a 77.7 ha impoundment 6 km SE of Auburn. Ten samples per date were harvested with a 1/4 m² frame on four dates in spring and summer 1968. The author estimated that at least 95% of total biomass was included.
- **Other J. americana stands:** Lake Seminole (Chattahoochee, Florida), Cahaba River (near Montevallo), Halawakee Creek, and Chewacla Creek, sampled in June with five samples each.
- **Chemistry:** Kjeldahl nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, cations, and trace metals were analyzed. Samples were dried at 60 °C for chemistry and 105 °C for dry matter. Trace-metal values may be contaminated by the Wiley mill, though the author judged comparisons between sites and dates valid.
- **Water:** pH, alkalinity, phosphorus, calcium, potassium, and metals were measured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Ogletree stand.** The stand covered 5.66 ha, or 7.8% of the lake surface. Stand width ranged from 2.6 to 18.3 m (mean 6.2 m). Lakeward extent was limited by depth in sheltered areas (to about 1.5 m) and by wave action in exposed areas (seldom deeper than 0.75 m). Plant density averaged 716/m² and did not differ with depth. Associated flora was sparse: [[Utricularia Biflora]], [[Spirogyra]], and [[Chara]] occurred in open areas, and about 1.1 ha of Chara lay beyond the lakeward margin on August 19.

**Water chemistry (Table 9).** Lake Ogletree, Lake Seminole, and Halawakee Creek were soft-water sites with similar phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium. The Cahaba River and Chewacla Creek had higher concentrations of these ions. Total alkalinity ranged from 20.5 ppm (Halawakee Creek) to 105.8 ppm (Chewacla Creek). Lake Ogletree was described as relatively shallow (mean depth around 4 m) and low in dissolved nutrients.

**Lentic versus lotic settings.** Lentic sites supported higher standing crops than streams, and the still-water Cahaba River stand exceeded the two swift-water streams. The author attributed low stream standing crops mainly to adverse physical conditions (stream velocity and a more consolidated, less organic hydrosoil), not to nutrient supply.

**Biogeochemical role.** The stand absorbed a large share of available nutrients, and the author concluded that *J. americana* was the major producer of organic material in Lake Ogletree.

## Key Findings

### 1. Production and standing crop (Lake Ogletree, *J. americana*)

- Growth was first observed on March 20.
- Maximum standing crop was **2458 g dry wt/m²** on July 10.
- Production rates were 18.9, 31.1, and 10.1 g/m²/day across the first three sampling intervals. The overall rate from March 20 to July 10 was **21.9 g/m²/day**.
- Standing crop declined at **3.7 g/m²/day** between July 10 and August 19.
- Aerial standing crop was much lower than whole-plant values (for example, 640 g/m² on July 10).
- Dry matter content of fresh plants rose from **11.3%** (May 16) to **18.7%** (August 19).

### 2. Mineral nutrients

- Percentage values for ash, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, potassium, and calcium declined with time. Nitrogen fell from 2.83% to 1.63%, and ash from 17.74% to 14.05%.
- More than 50% of the total absorption of ash and most macronutrients (except magnesium) occurred before the first sampling date.
- Nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur uptake was fastest in early spring (March 20 to May 16). Metal ion uptake peaked between May 16 and June 14.
- Between July 10 and August 19, most element standing crops fell faster than dry matter, which the author attributed partly to leaching from submersed tissue and rain-leached aerial shoots.
- Per gram of net production, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and potassium uptake was much higher in early spring than later. In the June 14 to July 10 interval, several nutrients showed net loss.

### 3. Mobile versus immobile nutrients

- Nitrogen, sulfur, and potassium (termed "mobile nutrients" after Meyer et al., 1960) were absorbed most heavily early in the season and were probably translocated to new growth later.
- The author proposes that early storage could limit phytoplankton growth and reduce nutrient competition during the growing season, and that this could explain the dominance of dense, near-monospecific angiosperm stands.

### 4. Organic constituents and energy

- Caloric content stayed nearly constant (about 3.76–3.79 kcal/g).
- True protein peaked at **174 g/m²** on July 14, but no net protein synthesis occurred after June 14.
- Total amino acids declined by **55%** over the study. Crude protein fell from 17.69% to 10.19%.
- Organic assimilation was greatest early and was followed by a point where metabolic use exceeded assimilation, even while dry matter still increased. The author attributes the fall in caloric figures to higher respiration.

### 5. Comparative stands (June data)

| Stand | June standing crop (g dry wt/m²) | Productivity (g/m²/day) |
|---|---|---|
| Lake Ogletree | 2195 ± 201 | 25.5 |
| Lake Seminole | 1634 ± 106 | 17.8 |
| Cahaba River | 802 ± 63 | 8.0 |
| Halawakee Creek | 409 ± 34 | 5.2 |
| Chewacla Creek | 322 ± 34 | 4.1 |

- Plant density, not chemical composition, mainly drove the standing-crop differences.
- Phosphorus and potassium were the most strongly concentrated elements, with concentration factors of about 40,000–47,500 for phosphorus.
- Calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus in plants tracked their concentrations in the water (the "hard water" stands had higher plant ash).
- Organic constituents and caloric values were remarkably similar across stands.
- Nitrogen/phosphorus ratios ranged from 12.9 to 25.8 across the five stands.

**Data note:** The text gives an ash range of 13.31% at Lake Seminole, but Table 8 lists 18.81% for that site. The source is internally inconsistent here.

### 6. *Alternanthera philoxeroides* stand

- Standing crops were **882** (May 8), **841** (June 4), and **798 g dry wt/m²** (July 10).
- Net productivity was 10.1, 17.0, and −1.2 g/m²/day.
- Peak dry matter was lower than at Lake Seminole and Lake Ogletree but higher than in the other three *J. americana* stands.
- Ash and macronutrient uptake followed a pattern similar to *J. americana*.
- Caloric content rose from 3.70 to 8.70 kcal/g across the study period.

### 7. Productivity comparisons

- Maximum *J. americana* productivity (37.1 g/m²/day) and *A. philoxeroides* productivity (17.0 g/m²/day) are listed for comparison with [[Typha Latifolia]] (52.6 g/m²/day), [[Pistia Stratiotes]] (15.3), [[Eichhornia Crassipes]] (14.6), [[Ceratophyllum Demersum]] (5.7), and [[Myriophyllum Verticillatum]] (2.8).
- The author suspected that biweekly sampling would have revealed *J. americana* rates near those of Typha.

### 8. Conclusions

- Early-spring nutrient storage by mobile nutrients, followed by use during rapid growth, is the key pattern. The author suggests it is a general strategy in some vascular aquatic plants, not one limited to these species.
- Both focal species are strong competitors that form near-monospecific stands. The author links this to mineral competition, and notes that both species became more invaded by other species in fertile environments.
- Lentic sites support greater standing crops of *J. americana*, while plant chemistry is mostly governed by water hardness.
