## Executive Summary

This OCR-degraded document is a fragment of a fisheries survey of the [[Colorado River]] system, with associated correspondence stamped as received by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Area Office in Phoenix in April and May 1981. The legible content covers three native species: [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Most pages are field data sheets and catch tables, and their numbers are largely illegible, so this note reports only the text that could be read reliably. The main message is that all three native species were scarce or declining, with the razorback sucker showing the lowest relative abundance of any native fish in the survey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado River Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**
- Historically present throughout the region, including near the confluence of [[Havasu Creek]] (river mile 533).
- Young fish eat insects and crustaceans; adults are piscivorous and were the major carnivore of the Colorado River system before impoundment, the introduction of exotic species, and other habitat modifications.
- A population study reported fish about 0.7 m long in their eleventh year of life (Seethaler 1978).
- Historical records describe individuals approaching 2 m in length and over 32 kg (Minckley 1973), indicating a long-lived species.
- In the Green River, spawning occurs from early July through mid-August (Vanicek and Kramer 1969). When the species was more widespread, spawning extended from spring to late summer (Miller and Lowe 1967).
- Status: persists in [[Lake Powell]], where anglers catch one or two a year (Seethaler 1978). It is considered virtually extinct in Arizona waters (Minckley 1973) and survives in limited numbers in the Green, Yampa, San Juan, Gunnison, and mainstream Colorado rivers.

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**
- Diet includes algae, dipteran larvae, and planktonic crustaceans (Jones and Sumner 1954; Minckley 1973).
- A slow-growing, long-lived species. Examined fish were 17–22 years old and up to one meter total length (W. L. Minckley, pers. comm.).
- Reproduction in impoundments occurs from late winter to early summer.
- Historical spawning migrations were reported in the Animas River (Jordan 1891) and in tributary streams (Chamberlain 1904). Recent spawning has been observed over gravel bars in the Yampa and Upper Colorado rivers (McAda 1977).
- A gravid female taken at the mouth of the [[Paria River]] in June 1978 indicates spawning use of that tributary within [[Grand Canyon]] (Minckley and Carothers 1980). Two ripe females were taken at the mouth of the [[Virgin River]] in 1979 (T. C. McCall, Arizona Game and Fish Department, pers. comm.).
- Hybridization with flannelmouth sucker has been reported from the Lee Ferry–Paria area.

**Flannelmouth Sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*)**
- Historic remains are known from [[Stanton's Cave]] (Euler 1978; Miller and Smith 1973).
- Collected from many localities in the study area and described as common throughout the reach.

## Key Findings

- **Razorback sucker was the rarest native fish in this survey.** Only one specimen was taken during the study, at the mouth of the Paria River. It had the lowest relative abundance of any native species collected.
- **Historical and recent inner-canyon records for razorback sucker are sparse.** The only earlier Grand Canyon record is one specimen from Bright Angel Creek in 1944, and later collections were restricted to the Lee Ferry–Paria area (1963).
- **Paria River and Virgin River mouths appear to be spawning sites.** Evidence includes a gravid female (June 1978) and two ripe females (1979).
- **Squawfish is largely extirpated from Arizona waters** but persists in Lake Powell and the Upper Colorado River system, with very low catch rates.
- **Both sucker species are long-lived and slow-growing,** which makes recovery slow and population declines hard to reverse.
- **Hybridization between razorback and flannelmouth suckers** is documented in the Lee Ferry–Paria area, a concern for genetic integrity of the native razorback sucker.
- **Data quality:** Much of the catch table content is illegible in the scan, so no abundance totals, sample sizes, or site-by-site results can be reliably reported.
