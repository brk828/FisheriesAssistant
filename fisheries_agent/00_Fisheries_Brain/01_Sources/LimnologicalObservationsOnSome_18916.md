# Limnological Observations on Streams of the New River Watershed Near Mountain Lake, Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1948 field survey, by Charles S. Shoup and colleagues from the [[Mountain Lake Biological Station]] (University of Virginia) and Vanderbilt University, characterised thermal, chemical and biological-productivity features of six tributary streams draining Salt Pond Mountain into the [[New River]] in the summer of 1947. The streams fall into two contrasting groups: soft-water mountain streams on the [[Clinch Sandstone]] (Little Stony Creek, Pond Drain, Hunter's Branch and the upper Big Stony Creek) and alkaline lowland streams on dolomite and limestone (Sinking Creek and Spruce Run Creek, with the lower Big Stony Creek transitional). The author concludes that bedrock solubility, through the [[Bicarbonate Buffer System]], largely controls water chemistry and food production, and that the alkaline lowland streams likely support more fish flesh per unit area.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Salt Pond Mountain, Giles County, rises above 4000 ft (highest transit station Bald Knob, 4363 ft), forming a divide between Atlantic and Mississippi drainages. All study streams are tributaries of the New River, which flows into the Kanawha (Ohio drainage).
- **Study streams (six chosen, plus sub-stations):**
  - *Sinking Creek*: originates from a limestone spring in Craig County and sinks underground before reaching the New River.
  - *Spruce Run Creek*: a small lowland stream from a limey spring near Newport, entering near Goodwin's Ferry (1662–2400 ft).
  - *Little Stony Creek*: a soft-water mountain stream falling from about 3800 ft to 1600 ft at Pembroke.
  - *Big Stony Creek*: a mountain stream in its upper reaches that gains buffers from limestone, quarries and farmland before reaching the New River at Norcross.
  - *Pond Drain*: drains [[Mountain Lake]] itself, an oligotrophic soft-water lake.
  - *Hunter's Branch*: a small spring-fed brook, typical of regional highland streams.
- **Prior work:** The fish distribution of several of these streams was studied by [[Burton and Odum (1945)]].
- **Geology:** Upland streams flow over insoluble Clinch Sandstone and shales; lowland streams cross soluble Copper Ridge (Cooper Ridge) Dolomite, Stones River Dolomite and Beekmantown formations (after Butts, 1933).
- **Methods:** Followed prior Tennessee protocols (Shoup 1940, 1944; Shoup, Peyton and Gentry 1941), using [[Theroux, Eldridge and Mallmann Methods]] for water analysis and the [[New Hampshire Biological Survey]] for stream grading. Bottom fauna were sampled with a [[Foerst Square-Foot Sampler]] at three positions per site.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature.** Mid-summer water temperatures were judged adequately low for game fish, except possibly in the lower Sinking Creek and lower Big Stony Creek. Rapid flow in Little Stony Creek and Spruce Run lowered readings. Pools, shelter, adequate food and freedom from predators (including humans) were identified as the controlling factors on fish abundance.

**Vegetation.** Aquatic vegetation was predominantly scant, consistent with mountain streams with scouring flow over sand and gravel. Spruce Run had scant vegetation with some algae; Little Stony Creek sites ranged from common to abundant.

**Cover.** Shade and overhang were scored on a 1–10 scale for left bank, right bank and stream middle. Forested mountain sites had high cover grades; lowland sites, passing through pasture, had low grades and were vulnerable to warming.

**Bottom substrate.** Substrates were broken bedrock, sand, gravel and rubble. The author cites the New Hampshire ranking of food productivity by substrate: silt, small rubble, large rubble, coarse gravel, fine gravel, bedrock, sand. Rubble and coarse gravel gave cover and attachment for many insect larvae.

**Water chemistry.** Upland streams were soft and acidic. Little Stony Creek, Pond Drain and Hunter's Branch had pH about 6.0–6.3 and low alkalinity, with free CO2 indicating carbonic acid dominance. A bog at 3174 ft in the limestone-free zone had free CO2 of 63.0 ppm, M.O. alkalinity of 8.0 ppm, oxygen of 4.0 ppm and pH 4.8 (aerated pH 5.2), attributed to CO2 and humic acids. Lowland streams reached pH 8.0 or above and total alkalinity above 100 ppm; Spruce Run had total alkalinity of 122.0–135.0 ppm and no free CO2 except in pools.

**Oxygen.** Unpolluted, well-aerated streams were considered reliably oxygenated, with levels tracking water temperature. The author cites [[Embody (1927)]], who recommended at least 4.29 ppm oxygen for brook trout waters.

## Key Findings

1. **Stream contrast by bedrock:** Upland and lowland streams differed sharply in chemistry and productivity, reflecting the solubility of their bedrock. Lowland alkaline streams (Sinking Creek, Spruce Run) showed higher productivity potential.
2. **Food production:** Field food-grade estimates favoured the lowland streams, mainly due to greater numbers of organisms rather than species distribution. Food grades followed a three-tier scale (Grade I: >2 cc or g with >50 organisms; Grade II: 1–2 cc with >50 organisms; Grade III: <1 cc and/or ≤50 organisms). Full station-by-station values are in Tables I–IV, which cover Sinking Creek, Spruce Run, Little Stony Creek, and lower Big Stony Creek, Pond Drain and Hunter's Branch.
3. **Predators and fish distribution:** Field notes recorded turtles, snakes (abundant at Spruce Run Station 7) and predatory insects. Burton and Odum (1945) found wide fish distribution in Sinking and Spruce Run creeks and a more restricted range in Little Stony Creek. The author proposes that the richer food base suggests greater fish-flesh production per unit area in the alkaline streams.
4. **Oxygen and temperature:** Oxygen and temperature were adequate in all streams surveyed, and no pollution was detected.
5. **Management implication:** Shade, pools and predator control, not only water chemistry, were identified as likely drivers of fish abundance. Riparian tree and shrub planting along pasture-bordered reaches was suggested to limit warming.
6. **Reference chemistry:** New River 1 mile below Pearisburg (July 31, 1947) recorded pH 8.0, methyl orange alkalinity 50.0 ppm, phenolphthalein alkalinity 2.0 ppm, dissolved oxygen 8.0 ppm and air temperature 89.0 °F, reflecting limestone input from quarries.
7. **Scope of conclusions:** The survey was preliminary to longer-term lake studies. Its conclusions are based on single-season, field-level sampling. The author's conclusion is that the study contributes to knowledge of biogeochemical relationships in fresh waters.
