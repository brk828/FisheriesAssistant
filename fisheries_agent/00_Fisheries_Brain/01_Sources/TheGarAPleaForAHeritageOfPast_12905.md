# Kentucky "Wappy Hunting Ground": The Gar, A Plea for a Heritage of Past

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lepisosteidae

## Executive Summary

This popular-science essay, written by [[Branley Allan Branson]] of Eastern Kentucky State University and published in a 1950s-era Kentucky sportsmen's publication, argues that [[Gar]] (family Lepisosteidae) and their relative the [[Bowfin]] are ancient, ecologically important fishes that are poorly understood and threatened by habitat loss and persecution. The author describes gar species identification, air-breathing physiology, reproduction and larval development, diet, and their role in controlling overpopulation of rough fishes and stunted game fishes. The essay closes with a call for conservation and understanding rather than eradication. The document also contains brief unrelated club news items.

## Background & Description

- **Evolutionary history:** The author describes gars as descendants of bony fishes that, roughly two hundred million years ago, moved from oceans into estuaries and freshwater, persisting in the Americas where they became dominant warm-water predators.
- **Species:** Six gar species exist (five in the United States, one in Cuba). The document treats them as follows:
  - [[Alligator Gar]] (*Lepisosteus spatula*): thick-set body, broad short snout, two rows of enlarged upper-jaw teeth; 8 to 10 feet long and over 200 pounds; found in large tributaries of the Mississippi, Kentucky, and Ohio rivers, west to northern Mexico, and occasionally at sea. A possible link to the Cuban gar is suggested.
  - [[Longnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus osseus*): up to about five feet and 30-odd pounds; snout roughly 20 times longer than broad; ranges from the Great Lakes (except Superior) and Vermont west to the Dakotas and south to Mexico.
  - [[Shortnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus platostomus*): slender, up to two feet; prefers large muddy Mississippi tributaries, west to east Texas.
  - [[Spotted Gar]] (*Lepisosteus oculatus*): thicker body, usually a few large spots on top of the head; common in clear weedy waters from lakes Michigan and Erie through the Mississippi Basin to Florida. The [[Florida Spotted Gar]] may be only a variant.
- **Bowfin:** Described as a first cousin of gars (*Amia calva*), with a similarly ancient fossil record.
- **Armor:** Gars have diagonal rows of enameled scales joined by peg-and-socket joints, reported to turn arrows and spears and, at an angle, 22-caliber slugs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Air breathing:** Gars retain a complex, nearly double lung with thousands of alveoli, connected to the mouth by a tube guarded by a muscular sheath called the glottis. Their gills are described as inefficient, so they surface in a rapid "bob-a-rebob" sequence of head bobs. Surfacing frequency correlates with water temperature, and gars surface less in winter.
- **Experimental observations:** Gars survived indefinitely in water without dissolved oxygen when they could reach the surface. Plugging the glottis with paraffin caused suffocation within hours, and preventing surfacing in warm water caused drowning.
- **Predators:** Adults have few natural enemies other than humans; the [[American Alligator]] is the only animal noted to feed on them.
- **Diet:** Strict carnivores, feeding mainly in morning hours. About two-thirds of diet is fish (perches, sunfishes, shads, minnows) and the remainder mostly crayfish. Studies cited show 6 to 18 percent of normal diet is food and game fish, with up to 52 percent game species in some small impoundments where rough fish were scarce.
- **Ecosystem role:** In waters altered by human activity that favor [[Rough Fish]] such as carp and buffalo, gars help control rough fish and reduce stunting of [[Black Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and related sunfishes.
- **Threats:** Draining of swamps, river pollution, and persecution, including throwing gars on banks and breaking their heads, were identified as pressures.

## Key Findings

- **Reproduction:** Spawning usually begins in mid-May and continues into June in shallow weedy waters. Females are courted by two to four smaller males. Eggs adhere to weeds and stones and hatch in about eight days. A single large female may produce 35,000 to 40,000 eggs in one season.
- **Eggs are toxic:** The author states that gar eggs are exceedingly poisonous and should never be eaten.
- **Larval development:** Larvae remain nearly motionless for seven to 12 days while absorbing yolk, attached by a cement gland, and resemble floating sticks. They then feed on mosquito larvae and other insects, switching to fishes and crayfish. Juveniles reach four to six inches by the end of the first summer. Sexual maturity takes three to five years.
- **Food value:** Contrary to the popular "rotten-fleshed" reputation, the author and associates found gar flesh tasty though dry. Residents of southeastern Oklahoma and adjacent Arkansas use gars as food.
- **Management:** Where population control is warranted in artificial impoundments, following study by qualified naturalists, fishing with nets, lures, spears, or arrows is recommended over wholesale killing.
- **Conservation message:** The author urges understanding and preservation of gars as part of America's aquatic heritage.
