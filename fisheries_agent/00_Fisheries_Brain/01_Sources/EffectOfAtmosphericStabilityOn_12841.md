# Effect of Atmospheric Stability on Water Temperature Predictions for a Thermally Loaded Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #thermal-ecology #stream-ecology #water-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This paper is **not** a fish-centric or taxon-centric study; rather, it is a physical/hydrological investigation of heat exchange and water temperature prediction in a thermally loaded stream reach affected by cooling-water discharge from a coal-fired power plant. The study is included here for its high relevance to [[Thermal Pollution]] and fish habitat thermal regimes, as accurate stream temperature prediction directly underlies management of [[Thermal Loading]] impacts on aquatic organisms. DeWalle (1976) tested two evaporative heat-exchange formulations — the Lake Hefner equation (Jobson, 1973) and a modified Shulyakovskiy equation incorporating free convection (Ryan et al., 1974) — to predict downstream water temperatures in a 5.45-km reach of the West Branch Susquehanna River, Pennsylvania, downstream of a 628 MW power plant. The Hefner equation substantially underestimated evaporative cooling under unstable atmospheric conditions (mean error −2.11°C), strongly correlated with an atmospheric stability index (R² = 46%). The modified Shulyakovskiy (Russian) equation, which explicitly accounts for free convection, reduced prediction error to 0.89°C and eliminated stability-related bias (R² < 1%). These findings have direct implications for accurately modeling thermal habitat conditions in power-plant-affected streams, which is a critical variable for fish thermal tolerance, distribution, and cooling-water regulatory compliance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While the paper does not directly study fish or other organisms, its subject matter — the **thermal habitat regime of a river receiving power-plant cooling water** — is foundational to the ecological niche characterization of thermally tolerant and thermally sensitive fish species in regulated/impacted rivers.

- **Habitat type**: Lotic (flowing freshwater), thermally loaded (heated) river reach immediately downstream of a once-through cooling power station.
- **Thermal regime**: Measured water temperatures ranged from ~28°C to over 40°C (initial temperatures 33.0–42.6°C across measurement periods), well within or exceeding thermal stress/lethal limits for many temperate freshwater fish species, underscoring the ecological relevance of accurate prediction models for [[Thermal Tolerance]] studies.
- **Seasonal context**: Data collected during summer low-flow conditions (1972–1973), when thermal loading and potential ecological thermal stress are typically at their maximum — a critical period for fish community thermal refugia and avoidance behavior.
- **Atmospheric-aquatic coupling**: The study demonstrates that atmospheric stability (buoyant/unstable air over heated water, driving free convection) significantly alters evaporative cooling rates, which in turn controls the spatial and temporal extent of thermal plumes affecting downstream habitat suitability for [[Fish Thermal Refugia]].
- **Relevance to aquatic ecosystem management**: Accurate downstream temperature prediction is essential for regulatory thermal discharge limits designed to protect fish populations from lethal or sub-lethal thermal stress, making this modeling work indirectly but importantly tied to [[Thermal Loading]] impact assessments in aquatic ecology.

## Key Findings

- **Energy budget model**: A steady-state, one-dimensional energy budget equation (Runge-Kutta 4th-order numerical integration) was used to predict water temperature along a thermally loaded stream reach over 131 five- or six-hour periods.
- **Hefner equation performance**: Using Jobson's Lake Hefner evaporation equation, downstream temperature prediction errors (measured minus predicted) averaged **−2.11 ± 0.36°C**, indicating systematic underestimation of evaporative cooling.
- **Stability correlation (Hefner)**: Prediction errors were significantly correlated with an atmospheric stability ratio (SR) (R² = 46%), with the largest errors (~−4°C) occurring under highly unstable atmospheric conditions (SR ≈ −60°C s² m⁻²).
- **Modified Shulyakovskiy (Russian) equation performance**: Incorporating a free-convection term reduced the average prediction error to **0.89 ± 0.25°C** and effectively eliminated the influence of atmospheric stability (R² < 1%).
- **Diurnal pattern**: Atmospheric instability over the heated stream was greatest at night (low wind, dominant free convection) and lowest during the day (forced convection dominant) — a diurnal stability cycle more pronounced than that observed at the unheated Lake Hefner reference site.
- **Wind direction correction**: Accounting for the relative velocity of air versus stream flow direction (vector correction) further reduced prediction errors using the Russian equation (from 0.29 ± 0.46°C to −0.28 ± 0.44°C), suggesting the corrected model may slightly underestimate evaporative heat loss.
- **Management implication**: The Lake Hefner evaporation equation, originally derived for unheated, neutrally stable natural water bodies, is not suitable for thermally loaded systems; models used for thermal discharge compliance and fish habitat thermal prediction should incorporate free-convection terms (e.g., modified Shulyakovskiy) and wind-relative-velocity corrections.
- **Broader context**: Findings corroborate prior work (Weeks et al. 1971; Ryan et al. 1974) showing that evaporation equations lacking free-convection terms systematically underestimate cooling in artificially heated water bodies, a recurring issue relevant to accurate simulation of [[Thermal Loading]] effects on riverine fish habitats.
