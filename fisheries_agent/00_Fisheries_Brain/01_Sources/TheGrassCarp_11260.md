# The Grass Carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*)

## Executive Summary
This 1971 California Department of Fish and Game administrative report evaluates a proposal to introduce the [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), also known as the [[White Amur]], into California waters for aquatic weed control. The report synthesizes life history data from global literature and assesses ecological risk. Despite well-documented efficacy in controlling submerged and floating aquatic vegetation, the author (Pelzman) recommends **against introduction**, citing high likelihood of escape into game fish waters, potential establishment of wild populations in California's larger rivers, salinity tolerance suggesting possible establishment in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], and probable direct/indirect competition with native and sport [[Game Fish|game fishes]]. The species was recommended to remain on California's prohibited species list, comparable in concern to the previously introduced [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*).

## Background & Description
- **Body form**: Elongated body with relatively large head and mouth; one of the largest members of family [[Cyprinidae]].
- **Distinguishing feature**: Lacks barbels, unlike common carp.
- **Coloration**: Greenish-brown dorsally; silvery on sides and undersurface.
- **Maximum size**: Length to 4 ft; weight to 100 lb.
- **Digestive tract**: Extremely short relative to body size for a primarily herbivorous fish — atypical of most herbivorous fish morphology.
- **Flesh quality**: Excellent flavor and texture but contains numerous floating rib bones; valued as a food fish in East Asia.

Growth rate is reported as **2–3 times faster** than [[Common Carp]] (Greenfield, 1970). Sexual maturity is reached between **5–9 years** (Cross, 1959), though Lin (1935) found mature individuals in the West River, China, as young as 3–4 years old.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Physiological Tolerances
- **Temperature**: Survives 32°F to over 90°F.
- **Salinity**: Tolerates up to 10‰ (parts per thousand).
- **Dissolved oxygen**: Tolerates concentrations as low as 0.5 ppm.

### Reproductive Ecology
- **Spawning season**: April to mid-August, at water temperatures above 68°F (Nikolskii) or 79–86°F (Lin).
- **Spawning triggers**: Requires a current of 2–5 ft/sec and a rapid rise in river level (>4 ft within 12 hours), typically following heavy rainfall; spawns in the center of large rivers, often below rapids.
- **Fecundity**: A 16-lb female may carry ~100,000 eggs (not all spawned simultaneously).
- **Egg/larval development**: Semipelagic eggs; hatching occurs 24–34 hours post-fertilization. High turbidity during spawning may reduce predation on eggs.
- **Pond reproduction**: Does not typically reproduce naturally in ponds outside native range (rare exceptions in Japan and Taiwan); requires induced spawning via pituitary extract injections in aquaculture settings.

### Diet and Feeding Behavior
- **Larvae/juveniles**: Feed on zooplankton and phytoplankton; diet shifts toward vegetation before reaching 2 inches in length (transition may never be fully complete).
- **Adults**: Omnivorous, but plant material dominates diet; documented consuming grass, leaves, aquatic plants, small fish, earthworms, silkworm pupae, mussel flesh, beef, insects, and even decayed cloth/shoes (Lin, 1935).
- **Digestive inefficiency**: ~50% of ingested food passes through undigested; full gut transit occurs in ~8 hours at 82–86°F. This creates a secondary trophic subsidy — feces reported to promote plankton growth (Stroganov, 1963) — but is also cited as a potential eutrophication concern.
- **Consumption rate**: Under favorable conditions, can consume more than its own body weight in plant material daily.

### Role as Biological Control Agent
Multiple field trials demonstrated efficacy in aquatic weed control:
- Avault (1965): 12–16 in fish (~685/acre equivalent) achieved complete control of 8 plant species within 2–3 weeks, later eliminating tougher species like [[Water Hyacinth]] and [[Eurasian Milfoil]].
- Sills (1970): 2,000 fish stocked in a 20-acre pond eliminated all rooted vegetation within six weeks.
- Stott & Orr (1970): ~1,499 fish consumed 13.4 tons of waterweed and 8.4 tons of parrotfeather over ~5 months.
- Stocking density strongly affects outcome — low densities (100–250 small fingerlings/acre; Sills 1970) failed to control established growth, while ~12 fish/acre succeeded in other trials (Kuronuma & Nakamura, 1957).

### Disease Susceptibility
- Documented infections/infestations include *[[Ichthyophthirius multifiliis]]*, *Lernaea cyprinacea*, *Achromobacter* sp., *Pseudomonas* sp., and *Aeromonas punctata* — with notable mortality events in transplanted stocks (Arkansas, Hungary, London).

## Key Findings
- Grass carp is a highly effective, fast-growing, environmentally tolerant [[Aquatic Weed Control|biological control agent]] for submerged and floating vegetation, but tolerance ranges (temperature, salinity, low oxygen) make it a strong candidate for establishing feral populations outside its native range.
- The species does **not** reliably reproduce in still pond systems but *can* reproduce in flowing rivers meeting specific flow/thermal/turbidity conditions — a profile matched by many of California's larger rivers, raising establishment risk.
- Because young grass carp rely on zooplankton and invertebrates, they would likely compete directly with juvenile native [[Game Fish|game fishes]] for food resources.
- Root/rhizome uprooting behavior during feeding could increase water turbidity, potentially degrading habitat for sight-feeding predatory game fish, paralleling documented negative impacts of [[Common Carp]].
- Salinity tolerance (up to 10‰) raises specific concern for potential establishment and ecological disruption in brackish transitional zones such as the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]].
- The report's central recommendation: grass carp should **not** be introduced into California and should remain on the state's prohibited species list, with exceptions only for public aquarium display under Title 14 CAC Section 671(e)9(c).
- This report is an early example of a formal **invasive species risk assessment** applied to a proposed biological control organism, weighing agricultural/utility benefits (weed control) against broader ecosystem risk.
