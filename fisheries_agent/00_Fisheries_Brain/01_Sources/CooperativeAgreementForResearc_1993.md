# Cooperative Agreement for Research and Laboratory Services — Larval Fish Laboratory (FY93)

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ColoradoRiverBasin #ConservationBiology

## Executive Summary
This FY93 annual report documents laboratory sample-processing services performed by the [[Colorado State University Larval Fish Laboratory]] under a cooperative agreement with the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] as part of the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]]. The work involved sorting, identifying, and archiving fish larvae and young-of-year (YOY) specimens collected via seine and drift-net methods across the [[Colorado River]], [[Green River]], and [[Gunnison River]] systems. Data support three ongoing monitoring efforts: the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP), flow-effects studies, and Aspinall Unit studies. Across all datasets, nonnative cyprinids ([[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Sand Shiner]]) dominated fish assemblages (often >90% of catch), while endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and native [[Gila Species]] were detected in low numbers, providing critical baseline data for endangered fish recovery efforts.

## Background & Description
Specimen size data were reported primarily as total length (TL) ranges used for species identification and life-stage classification (larvae vs. YOY):

- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (referred to historically as "Colorado squawfish"):
  - Green River (Fall 1992): 3 individuals, 34–45 mm TL
  - Utah reach, Colorado River (Fall 1992): 26 specimens, 19.6–48.0 mm TL
  - Colorado River (Spring 1993): 15 individuals (size not specified, all captured ≤ river mile 32.2)
  - Colorado River drift-net (Aspinall, Summer 1993): 45 individuals, 7.5–10.2 mm TL (larval stage)
- **[[Creek Chub]]**: single specimen, Green River, 41–50 mm TL
- **General size distribution (Gunnison River seine, 1992)**: TL ranged from <10 mm to >90 mm; majority (82%) fell between 21–40 mm (54% at 21–30 mm; 28% at 31–40 mm), indicating predominance of larval/early YOY fish in these samples.
- Some cyprinid and Gila specimens could not be identified to species due to physical damage or deterioration, a recurring limitation in field-preserved larval samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The report characterizes fish community composition across multiple river reaches, highlighting the dominance of nonnative species and the sparse but ecologically critical presence of native and endangered fishes.

**Nonnative-dominated assemblages:**
- [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], and [[Sand Shiner]] were consistently the most abundant taxa across nearly all sample sets (56–99.9% of individual collections).
- [[Common Carp]] was the dominant nonnative species (~13%) in Aspinall drift-net collections, an unusual reversal from typical cyprinid dominance.

**Native species strongholds:**
- The Aspinall drift-net collections (Colorado & Gunnison rivers, summer 1993) showed markedly different community structure: 86% native fish, dominated by [[Gila Species]] (~57%), [[Speckled Dace]] (~13%), and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (~12%) — suggesting these drift-net methods/locations sample habitat more favorable to native recruitment than standard seine methods.
- [[Gila Species]] (assumed largely [[Roundtail Chub]]) were more frequently collected in Colorado reach sites than Utah reach sites of the Colorado River.

**Endangered species distribution:**
- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] larvae/YOY were detected in low densities in the Utah reach of the Colorado River, the Green River, and lower Colorado River reaches (≤ river mile 32.2), but were absent from upstream Colorado reaches and entirely absent from Gunnison River seine collections in 1992.
- No [[Razorback Sucker]] were identified in any Gunnison River seine collections in this reporting period.

**Habitat/reach patterns:**
- Utah reach of the Colorado River exhibited even higher nonnative dominance (99.8%) than the Colorado reach (98.1%).
- Species composition and relative abundance shifted seasonally (fall vs. spring) and spatially (upstream vs. downstream reaches), reflecting differing spawning and drift timing among native and nonnative taxa.

## Key Findings
- **ISMP Fall 1992 (Colorado River)**: 101 samples, 60,316 fish, 16 species + 1 hybrid sucker; ~98–99.8% nonnative. Only 26 [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] individuals found, exclusively in the Utah reach.
- **ISMP Fall 1992 (Green River)**: 12 samples, 4,824 fish, 10 species; only 0.3% native, including 3 [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
- **ISMP Spring 1993 (Colorado River)**: 46 samples, 21,012 fish, 7 species; 99.9% nonnative. Natives limited to 15 [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and 2 [[Roundtail Chub]].
- **Flow-Effects Study (Summer 1992)**: 256 samples received; ~30% processed to date. Priority given to summarizing [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Gila Species]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] data.
- **Aspinall Drift-Net (Summer 1993)**: 243 samples (108 Colorado River, 135 Gunnison River), 5,236 fish, 12 species; 86% native — a notable departure from seine-based nonnative dominance patterns. 45 [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] larvae detected in Colorado River samples.
- **Seine Collections 1992 (Colorado River)**: 79 collections, 71,440 fish, ≥14 species; no endangered species detected; natives (<2% of catch) dominated by [[Gila Species]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]].
- **Seine Collections 1992 (Gunnison River)**: 36 collections, 22,275 fish, 12 species + hybrid catostomids; 89.8% nonnative; no [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] or [[Razorback Sucker]] detected.
- **Programmatic status**: All FY93 goals for sample processing and identification under the cooperative agreement were reported as met; budget fully expended ($86,800) with no variance.
- **Data limitations**: Damage/deterioration of some cyprinid and Gila specimens limited species-level identification confidence in several collections.
