# Effects of Woodcutting on a Pinyon-Juniper Community Near Ash Fork, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pinyon-juniper-woodland

## Executive Summary

This 1981 Arizona State University Department of Zoology environmental assessment, prepared by the Wildlife Management 412 class, evaluates how four land-use alternatives would affect a 32 ha pinyon-juniper woodland about 4.5 km southwest of [[Ash Fork]], Yavapai County. The alternatives were (1) no action, (2) removal of dead and down timber, (3) removal of standing dead and dead and down timber, and (4) [[Clear Cutting]]. Nine ecosystem components were studied: [[Climate]], [[Soils]], [[Vegetation]], amphibians and reptiles, birds (nongame, game and raptors) and mammals. The authors concluded that clear cutting would have the greatest negative effect on the ecosystem, while the other alternatives produced mixed positive and negative outcomes.

The document is a field assessment, not a fish study. No fish, aquatic invertebrates or water chemistry are reported; the study site had no surface water.

## Background & Description

The site is a hilly, rocky [[Pinyon-Juniper Woodland]] at 5200-5300 ft (about 1585-1615 m) elevation, in the Cathedral Caves Quadrangle. It was recently subdivided into two 16 ha parcels, owned in part by Dr. W. L. Minckley and Dr. John Rinne, who contracted the assessment. The stand was unusual for Arizona because it showed no evidence of woodcutting or tree control for grazing.

The woodland covers about 30 million acres in Arizona and New Mexico (Springfield 1976), more than three times the area of Arizona's commercial forests. Fuelwood cutting was being considered as an alternative to grazing-based control, but its effects were poorly documented.

The assessment was carried out by 17 biologists over 112 man-days between 14 March and 12 April 1981. Sampling covered about 3.5% of the study area in roughly 92 man-hours.

Canopy composition was 65% [[Utah Juniper]] (*Juniperus osteosperma*) and 35% [[Pinyon Pine]] (*Pinus edulis*). Vegetation survey data indicated an estimated 9-11 cords/acre of wood.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** Weather data came from Ash Fork, 18 km northeast at 1623 m. Records from 1921-1980 (56 years) give a mean annual temperature of 12.5 °C, with a July high of 23.5 °C and a January low of 2.3 °C. Mean annual precipitation is 331.6 mm, bimodal with summer peaks (July-August monsoon thunderstorms) and a smaller winter peak. Annual totals ranged from 608 mm (1978) to 170.5 mm (1950). May and June are the driest months. The climate is semi-arid.

**Soils.** The soil is a [[Purner Soil Series]], a loamy, mixed mesic mollisol derived from limestone. It is shallow (rooting depth under 50 cm), with moderate permeability, low available water capacity and moderate erosion hazard. Soil pH was about 7.9 in both treated and untreated areas. High calcium carbonate and free lime reflect the limestone parent material. Chained (juniper-removed) and unchained plots differed mainly in electrical conductivity, sulfate and nitrate. Manganese and iron were unusually high.

**Vegetation.** The stand is a climax, old-age pinyon-juniper woodland with good reproduction. Forty-three plant taxa were recorded (Table IV-1). Shrubs were led by [[Cliffrose]] (*Cowania mexicana*), with about 111 shrubs/acre. Snags averaged 15/acre. Ground cover was low (about 17% vegetated), and overstory cover was about 21%. Cattle and feral burros graze the area, favoring increases in snakeweed (*Gutierrezia sarothrae*).

**Herpetofauna.** One amphibian (tiger salamander, *Ambystoma tigrinum*) and three lizards were found, with no snakes. The [[Eastern Fence Lizard]] (*Sceloporus undulatus*) was the most common, with 51 observations; most were on or near down wood. The study was done in early spring, when maximum temperatures averaged 17 °C, so activity was probably low. Of 17 reptile and 3 amphibian species with ranges covering the site, only 4 were found.

**Birds.** About 76 bird species from 6 orders and 22 families breed in pinyon-juniper woodlands regionally. About 28 nongame and game species were recorded on site. Abundance ratings used a scale from Abundant (10+ sightings) to Rare (1 sighting). Raptors were counted using direct observation for diurnal species and tape-recorded calls for owls at four stations.

**Mammals.** Of 40 expected mammal species, 23 were present and 17 were not. The most abundant small nongame mammals were [[Pinyon Mouse]] (*Peromyscus truei*) and white-throated woodrat (*Neotoma albigula*). Coyote (*Canis latrans*) was the most common carnivore. Black-tailed jackrabbit and western cottontail were the most common game mammals. Total trap success was 4.7% (56 captures from 1202 trap-nights). Mule deer pellet groups (64) outnumbered cow droppings (26) on 80 plots.

## Key Findings

- **Climate:** The area is semi-arid, with a mean annual precipitation of 331.6 mm and summer-dominant rainfall (Ash Fork records 1921-1980).
- **Soils:** Limestone-derived Purner soils are high in CaCO3, which buffers nutrients and limits their plant availability. Removing the juniper overstory increased infiltration and leaching.
- **Vegetation:** Juniper removal was predicted to increase snakeweed, erosion and bare ground, and to raise cliffrose and herbage yields. Clear cutting would produce "dramatic" vegetation changes.
- **Herpetofauna:** Removing dead and down wood was predicted to seriously harm the herpetofauna. Clear cutting was predicted to reduce the amphibian populations, which depend on temporary water, and to favor open-habitat lizards and snakes.
- **Birds:** Approximately 27% of birds were predicted to be adversely affected by removing dead and down wood, 43% by removing all dead wood, and about 70% by clear cutting. Clear cutting was judged the most damaging to birds, while about 14% of species would benefit from the shrub invasion.
- **Raptors:** Seventeen raptor species were expected; 10 diurnal and 4 nocturnal species were recorded on site (with ravens included). Clear cutting would remove nest sites and perches for most raptors but could benefit kestrels, golden eagles and turkey vultures by opening the habitat.
- **Mammals:** Removing dead and down wood would affect about 26% of species. Removing all dead wood would affect 39%. Clear cutting would affect all species, with major declines expected for Pinyon Mouse and woodrats.
- **Overall conclusion:** Clear cutting had the greatest negative effect on the ecosystem. The authors recommended additional randomized, replicated plots and mitigation measures, including piling slash, seeding native grasses and providing a water source.
