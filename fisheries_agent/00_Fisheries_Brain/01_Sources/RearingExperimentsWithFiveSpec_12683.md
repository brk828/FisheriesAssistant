# Rearing Experiments with Five Species of Australian Freshwater Fishes: I. Inducement to Spawning

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percichthyidae #Plotosidae #Theraponidae #Eleotridae #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by J. S. Lake, conducted at the [[Inland Fisheries Research Station]] near Narrandera, New South Wales, tests what triggers spawning in five endemic Australian freshwater fishes and compares them with the introduced [[European Perch]]. Controlled pond experiments, run from September 1960 to May 1966, indicate that specific water temperatures combined with floods or rising water levels act as the main spawning triggers, and that the stimulus is likely linked to water entering dry soil. The author proposes that inundated dry ground releases a chemical cue, possibly related to [[Petrichor]], that fish detect by smell.

## Background & Description

The paper's species, drawn from the [[Murray-Darling River System]] (27 fish species recorded in New South Wales, eight of them introduced), are:

- [[Golden Perch]] (*Plectroplites ambiguus*), a native commercial species with pelagic eggs.
- [[Freshwater Catfish]] (*Tandanus tandanus*), a native commercial species that breeds regularly in farm dams and builds gravel nests.
- [[Murray Cod]] (*Maccullochella macquariensis*), a large native species with eggs laid in hollow logs or pipes.
- [[Silver Perch]] (*Bidyanus bidyanus*), a native commercial species with pelagic eggs.
- [[Western Carp Gudgeon]] (*Carassiops klunzingeri*), a small species (3–5 cm) with eggs attached to vegetation.
- [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*), introduced to the system in 1888 and studied for comparison.

Prior work on these fishes was largely taxonomic or anecdotal. Earlier studies covered diet, larval development and artificial feeding, and [[Langtry Survey]] data (1949–1950) covered abundance, length–weight relations and gonad development. Fishermen had long believed the larger fishes spawn during floods. The study uses fish held in clay-lined ponds, including "flood ponds" of about 0.1 ha with a deeper section (1.2–2.4 m) and a shallow flood plain that could be drained and re-flooded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Murray-Darling system fluctuates widely. Rainfall is irregular and river gradients are low, so long reaches shrink to waterholes or dry out during drought. Floods from highland snow and rain or monsoonal rain can cover hundreds of miles of floodplain. Lake notes that floods drive plankton and insect blooms that support the survival of young, and cites comparable findings for [[Wild Ducks]] (Frith 1959), [[Puntius]] species in Indonesia, [[Major Carps]] in India and Pakistan, and [[Speckled Dace]] in Arizona (John 1963).

Water quality in the field ranged from pH 7.1–7.8, total alkalinity 63–90 ppm as CaCO3, conductivity 220–400 µmhos at 20°C, and Secchi disk readings of 12–240 cm. None of these had a noticeable effect on gonad development or spawning. Spawning has also occurred in the Darling River at a conductivity of 3000 µmhos at 20°C. Lake notes that [[Sodium]] and chloride are the dominant ions in Australian inland waters (Williams 1966).

Shallow water is also significant. Lake notes that the spawning habit of *C. klunzingeri* in very shallow water makes it vulnerable to sudden drops in water level caused by irrigation demand.

## Key Findings

**Spawning triggers by species**

- *Golden Perch* and *Silver Perch*: spawn above 23°C when water is added and the level rises. Silver Perch spawned at 23.3°C and Golden Perch at 23.6°C (the lowest temperature recorded for spawning). Both produce pelagic eggs.
- *Freshwater Catfish*: spawns at 24°C, builds circular gravel nests (0.7–1.2 m diameter in larger ponds), and does not need a water-level rise. Eggs are demersal and pass between gravel, and one pair uses one nest. Nests are abandoned when exposed by lowering the water level.
- *Murray Cod*: spawns at 20°C when water is added at the first attainment of that temperature, provided suitable attachment objects are present. Temperatures above 21°C caused oocyte death and resorption. Eggs were attached to the inside of fibro-cement pipes, and the author suggests hollow logs are used in rivers.
- *Western Carp Gudgeon*: spawns at 22.5°C, apparently driven by temperature rather than flooding. Eggs are attached to grass and twigs, and males guard the eggs.
- *European Perch*: spawns at 11.5°C in early spring (typically late August at Narrandera). Adding water sped spawning, and all fish in a flood pond spawned when the flood plain was inundated.

**Key results for *Plectroplites ambiguus* (Golden Perch)**

- Development and spawning occurred in both clear and turbid water. Total dissolved solids always exceeded 140 ppm.
- Spawning did not occur unless water was added, even when temperatures were above 23.6°C. Water that was added and allowed to flow out at the same time did not induce spawning.
- Spawning usually occurred at night, most often 3–5 hours after sunset.
- Fecundity was about 500,000 eggs per individual. Only some eggs were shed in many fish, and complete shedding was seen mainly in large females over 3 kg.
- Fish held below 23.6°C could delay spawning for months and still be induced. Fish held above that temperature for several weeks suffered oocyte breakdown.

**Key results for *Bidyanus bidyanus* (Silver Perch)**

- Spawning was first detected unexpectedly in December 1960 when larvae were collected in plankton nets. It was suspected to occur when ponds were topped up after evaporation.
- A rise of only 15 cm in water level was sufficient. Water temperature had to exceed 23.3°C to a depth of 90 cm.
- Spawning usually occurred late in the afternoon, and all fish shed most of their ova at one spawning.

**Key results for *Tandanus tandanus* (Freshwater Catfish)**

- Nest-building began about 1–2 weeks before spawning, after water reached about 24–25°C. Spawning did not occur below 24°C.
- Gravel was preferred to mud, but nests were built in mud, sand and gravel. Gravel up to 5 cm in diameter was used.
- Five females of 1.5–2 kg (T.L. c. 47–55 cm) each contained 18,000–26,000 ova.

**Key results for *Maccullochella macquariensis* (Murray Cod)**

- In 1963 seven fish (four males, three females) were placed in a 0.6 ha flood pond. Filling began on October 17 and the pond was full on October 21 at about 20.5°C. Eggs were found on a pipe removed on October 25, and spawning probably occurred on October 22.
- Spawning repeated in 1964 when temperatures reached 20°C in mid-November, with Secchi readings never below 100 cm.
- Three farm dams near Narrandera also showed spawning after rain at about 20°C, one with only a slight rise in water level.
- Fecundity was 20,000–30,000 ova in fish of about 3.3 kg, and about 200,000 in fish of 20–22 kg.

**Key results for *Carassiops klunzingeri* (Western Carp Gudgeon)**

- Observed spawning on November 5, 1963, at 22.5°C, 14 days after Murray Cod spawned in the same pond.
- Ovaries held 1,000–2,000 ova. Eggs were 0.40–0.51 mm in diameter and were found from 5 to 25 cm depth.

**Key results for *Perca fluviatilis* (European Perch)**

- Spawning occurred at night in all eight ponds used, on twigs in 1961 and on rooted plants at 120–180 cm depth in later years.
- Sexual activity appeared to depend more on fixed annual factors, particularly temperature, than on floods, although floods may still aid survival.

**Proposed mechanism**

- Lake concludes that the stimulus is probably a factor released by inundating dry ground, possibly the volatile compound [[Petrichor]] (about 30 mg/m² yield from silicate rocks; Bear and Thomas 1964). Lake calls this hypothesis, which he plans to test further, more acceptable than [[Swingle's Repressive Factor Hypothesis]] (1953), which he regards as weakly supported by the evidence.

**Limitations**

- Sample sizes were small: for example, seven fish for Murray Cod in the key 1963 trial and a handful of individuals for several species. Lake himself notes that the petrichor mechanism is untested.
