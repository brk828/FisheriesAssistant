# Age and Growth of June Sucker (*Chasmistes liorus*) from Otolith Annuli

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This short communication by [[Mark Belk]] (Brigham Young University) reports the first age and growth data for the federally endangered [[June Sucker]], *Chasmistes liorus*, an endemic catostomid of [[Utah Lake]]. Age, length-at-age and age-at-death were estimated from presumptive annuli on lapilli otoliths of 10 fish that died, possibly from spawning-related stress. The study supplies baseline growth data for managers working on [[Species Recovery]] and for evaluating captive-reared and reintroduced juveniles. Results show a three-stage growth pattern, apparent maturity by about age 5 to 10, and long adult life, which the author links to recruitment limitation and the threat posed by [[White Bass]] predation.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Chasmistes liorus* (June sucker), endemic to Utah Lake, Utah County, Utah (Miller and Smith 1981). Listed as endangered; the wild population may number fewer than 500 individuals (C. Keleher, pers. comm.).
- **Threat:** The population suffers from poor recruitment to adulthood, attributed to predation on juveniles by the abundant introduced white bass (*Morone chrysops*) (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1995).
- **Conservation practice:** Adults are captured as they migrate up the Provo River to spawn. Gametes are stripped and fertilized, and juveniles are reared in captivity until large enough to avoid white bass predation, then released into Utah Lake, except for brood stock.
- **Study rationale:** Adult size was well documented, but no growth data existed. Understanding natural growth is needed to judge captive-reared juvenile success and expected recruitment timing.
- **Sample:** Ten fish died in 1992 (7) and 1994 (3) from unknown causes. Standard length, total length, mass and sex were recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Relation to other catostomids:** Age and growth patterns resemble those of other large-bodied western suckers, including *Chasmistes cujus* and *Xyrauchen texanus* (Scoppettone 1988; Scoppettone and Vinyard 1991).
- **Life-history strategy:** Delayed maturity and long adult life are interpreted as adaptations to uncertain recruitment driven by environmental fluctuation (Scoppettone and Vinyard 1991). These traits have let June suckers persist despite recent, strongly limited recruitment caused by human disturbance.
- **Population concern:** The author notes the aging adult population could become extinct without improved recruitment.
- **Sex-related growth:** U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1995) reported larger reproducing females than males in 1980 and 1991. Within the first ten years, growth trajectories did not differ significantly between sexes.

## Key Findings

- **Methods:** Lapilli were embedded in epoxy resin, sectioned in the frontal plane, and annuli were counted under a dissecting microscope (24X) with image capture and MOCHA image analysis software. Length-at-age was back-calculated with a modified Fraser-Lee method (Campana 1990).
- **Age range:** Fish were 10 to 40 years old at death. Two were 10 years old in 1992, and the remainder were more than 25 years old.
- **Three-stage growth:**
  - Rapid growth for the first 3–5 years; fish averaged 69% of mean total length at death by age 5.
  - Intermediate growth until about age 8–10.
  - Reduced growth after age 10.
- **Length milestone:** 85% of mean total length at death was reached by age 10 (Table 1).
- **Sex differences:** No significant differences between sexes in the first ten years (all P > 0.1). All females were larger than males at death, which the author attributes to faster female growth after sexual maturation.
- **Reproductive status:** All individuals were reproductively active at death.
- **Maturity:** Growth slowed after about age 5, and because all fish were reproductively active, the author suggests maturity may occur as early as age 5 and no later than age 10. The smallest reproductive fish recorded in 1980 (440 mm males, 490 mm females total length) would likely have been 6–10 years old.

- **Implications:** The data provide baseline comparisons for reintroduced and hatchery-reared June suckers and support recovery planning for [[Recovery Plan]] goals, including the need to improve recruitment.
- **Limitations:** The author acknowledges that ten fish is a small sample, though it may represent about 2% of the remaining population, a larger proportion than most age and growth studies sample.
