# Survival, Growth and Movement of Esox masquinongy Transplanted from Nogies Creek Sanctuary to Public Fishing Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esox-masquinongy

## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by Barry S. Muir and John G. Sweet, University of Toronto, tracked adult and juvenile [[Esox Masquinongy]] (maskinonge, lunge) that were tagged, netted in autumn from [[Nogies Creek Sanctuary]], and transplanted to the public fishing waters of [[Pigeon Lake]] and [[Sturgeon Lake]] in the [[Trent Canal System]], Ontario. The study covers plantings from 1952 to 1958 (excluding 1956). Of 1533 tagged fish transplanted, 227 were recaptured by anglers by the close of the 1960 season. Recapture was concentrated in the first season after planting, and fish younger than age-group V contributed little to the fishery. Estimated exploitation of adult-sized fish during the first fishing season ranged from 0.19 (a minimum) to 0.47 (probably high), with the authors judging the true value to be about 0.35 or greater. Transplanted fish grew faster in the larger lakes than in the sanctuary and moved considerable distances, many in river channels.

## Background & Description

**Species and management concern.** Ontario conservationists had long been concerned about the apparent decline of maskinonge. Artificial propagation was the main response. In 1960, about four million fry and fifty thousand fingerlings were reared at the provincial hatchery at Deer Lake and planted across the province. The authors note that maskinonge do not normally mature until the fifth or sixth year, so raising fish to a larger size before planting was considered worthwhile. Earlier fry-planting work by Elson (1940) found a minimum first-year survival of 0.08%.

**Transplant program.** From 1952, juvenile and adult maskinonge were netted each autumn at [[Nogies Creek Sanctuary]] and moved to Pigeon and Sturgeon lakes to test their value for restocking public waters. Hatchery fingerlings planted into the sanctuary showed survival to age III of roughly 3% to 10% (unpublished data). Fish were tagged with a numbered yellow plastic disc attached to the preopercular bone with nylon monofilament. Catches were held in retainer nets, usually moved the same day in tanks (about 115 imperial gallons), and transported in late afternoon trips of under thirty minutes, so no icing or aeration was needed.

**Angler participation.** Anglers were asked to return tags, including those from sublegal fish, along with capture details. An end-of-season lucky draw was used to publicize the study. The authors believed angler cooperation was good, although some tags were not returned.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nogies Creek Sanctuary.** The sanctuary is a five-mile river stretch ending in an artificial lake of about 100 acres. The river is 20 to 30 yards wide and 4 to 12 feet deep, with a July flow of about 90 cubic feet per second (Crossman, 1956). Dead trees and dense aquatic vegetation cover the drowned land. A concrete dam at the lower end and a ruined stone dam at the upper end limit immigration and emigration; only three tags were recovered from fish that apparently escaped over the dam.

**Pigeon and Sturgeon lakes.** Both lakes are fairly eutrophic and contain large, shallow, weedy areas. Residents reported that both had lost many weed beds over the previous two decades, with Sturgeon Lake changing more. Pigeon Lake appeared to support larger populations of local game species, though no figures were available. Water flow follows the long axis of both lakes, with an estimated 1200 to 1500 cubic feet per second through Bobcaygeon in July and August and about 4500 cubic feet per second during spring flooding.

## Key Findings

**1. Recapture rates**
- 841 tagged fish were planted into Pigeon Lake (124 recaptured, 14.7%) and 692 into Sturgeon Lake (103 recaptured, 14.9%).
- Of the 103 recaptures from Sturgeon Lake, 32 (31.1%) had moved into Pigeon Lake through the locks or over the dam. The authors therefore treated the two lakes as one system.
- Recapture by year of planting ranged from 11.0% (1952) to 23.3% (1953). By age at planting, it ranged from 5.4% (age-group II) to 33.3% (age-group IX).
- Of the 227 recaptures, 196 occurred in the first season after planting (n+1). No tags were returned after the fourth year.

**2. Recovery of juvenile versus adult fish**
- Recapture increased with age, with an apparent discontinuity between age-groups IV and V (about 10% to 20%).
- For age V and older, planting-year returns ranged from 15.5% to 31.6%. These fish were presumably of legal size the following season.
- Of 757 age V-and-older releases, 142 were recaptured in n+1 and 17 in n+2.

**3. Exploitation and mortality (age V and older)**
- The first estimate of exploitation was 142/757 = 0.188 (19%), a minimum that ignores transplant mortality and tag loss.
- Using the [[Catch Curve Method]] (Ricker, 1958), total apparent mortality between n+1 and n+2 was estimated at 0.88. This was treated as a maximum, since tag loss increases with time in the sanctuary.
- The instantaneous disappearance rate was 2.12, split into an instantaneous fishing mortality of p = 0.75 and other losses (including natural mortality, tag loss, and tagging effects) of q' = 1.37. These correspond to conditional rates of 0.53 for fishing and 0.75 for other causes.
- Exploitation during the fishing season was estimated at u = 0.47: of the 303 tagged fish still tagged at season opening, 142 (47%) were caught. The authors considered this probably high and concluded the true value was "at least mid-way" between 0.19 and 0.47, of the order of 0.35 or greater.

**4. Growth**
- Anglers reported 121 lengths in n+1 and 19 in n+2 for age-groups IV to VIII at planting.
- Average one-year increments in n+1 were approximately 1.5 to 1.7 inches for age-groups IV and V, falling to about 0.9 to 1.0 inches for age-groups VIII and IX (the OCR-garbled value for age VI has been read as 1.1).
- Growth in the second year (n+2) appeared greater and more normal than in the first, but the sample of 19 fish was too small for firm conclusions.
- Tagged fish grew faster in the public lakes than the tagged fish returned to the sanctuary in 1957, though still slower than untagged sanctuary fish. The authors noted that the growth estimates are underestimates because recaptures occurred before the end of the growing season.

**5. Movement**
- Of 144 n+1 recaptures with locatable points, 55 (39%) were less than half a mile from release or in an "across" direction, 44 moved downstream and 45 moved upstream.
- Of the 44 downstream movers, 26 (59%) passed through the locks or over the dam from Sturgeon to Pigeon Lake, and most were caught in the river below the dam.
- A total of 98 of the 144 fish were recaptured in rivers or at river mouths, indicating a strong preference for moving water. Tagged fish were also caught in the Bobcaygeon River out of proportion to untagged fish.
- Average distance moved rose with age from 1.8 miles (age III) to 2.7 miles (age VII), excluding two fish that moved 23 miles downstream each.

**6. Spawning and contribution to the stock**
- A single tagged fish was filmed during 1959 underwater spawning observations in Pigeon Lake. It is unknown whether it spawned, but the authors judged it probable that transplanted fish contribute to the spawning stock.

**7. Management implications and conclusions**
- Fish younger than age-group V showed low returns, suggesting that transplanting sub-adults is not warranted unless tag loss is very high.
- Of adult-sized fish, 21.5% of tags were reported. Allowing for unreported and lost tags, utilization might reach 30%. The authors described this as inefficient compared with allowing anglers direct access to the sanctuary, but argued that opening the sanctuary would quickly lead to over-exploitation of concentrated juveniles. Transplanting spreads the additional catch across the whole angling population.
- Planned work was to estimate tag loss, tagged-fish mortality, and the share of the total catch supplied by transplanted fish.
