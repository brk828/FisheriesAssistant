# Molecular Variation and Evolutionarily Significant Units in the Endangered Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #poeciliidae #endangered-species #population-genetics

## Executive Summary

This study examines genetic variation in the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis) across four remaining natural populations in Arizona using microsatellite loci, and integrates these findings with previous allozyme, mtDNA, and MHC data. The research addresses the critical question of defining evolutionarily significant units (ESUs) for conservation management. [[Monkey Spring]] emerged as the most genetically differentiated population, with the highest proportion of private alleles (0.620 per locus). All four watersheds showed significant genetic differentiation and are physically isolated by 101-580 km of stream channel. The authors recommend managing and conserving all four watersheds separately as distinct ESUs, revising earlier management decisions that prioritized [[Sharp Spring]] stock exclusively for reintroductions.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] was historically one of the most abundant fishes in the [[Gila River]] drainage but is now federally and state-listed as endangered, existing naturally in only four isolated Arizona watersheds. Major factors in their decline include loss and fragmentation of shallow-water habitat and establishment of the non-native [[Central Mosquito Fish]] (Gambusia affinis). The species has become a model organism for endangered species research, with extensive genetic investigation using multiple marker types.

Previous management decisions were heavily influenced by genetic data. After studies by Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) and Quattro & Vrijenhoek (1989) indicated [[Sharp Spring]] fish had more allozyme variation and higher fitness than [[Monkey Spring]] fish, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service stopped using Monkey Spring fish for reintroductions and used only Sharp Spring stock. This study reevaluates those recommendations using more variable genetic markers.

The research examined 40 individuals from each of four populations: [[Bylas Spring]], [[Cienega Creek]], [[Monkey Spring]], and [[Sharp Spring]]. Fish were sampled in 1995 from stocks established from 20 wild-caught females per site in 1994. Ten microsatellite loci were identified, five of which proved polymorphic: Pooc-6-10, Pooc-C15, Pooc-LL53, Pooc-G49, and Pooc-O056. Three are simple dinucleotide repeats; two contain interrupted sequences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The four populations occupy dramatically different habitats with distinct physical and biological characteristics. [[Bylas Spring]] represents the only remaining mainstem [[Gila River]] stock, isolated by at least 580 km from other populations. It has very small habitat size (few liters per minute flow supporting at most a few hundred adults), variable temperature, and has experienced recurring bottlenecks. The population went extinct at the site in 1990 and was reestablished from a refugium.

[[Monkey Spring]] appears isolated from a tributary of [[Sonoita Creek]] by a natural 10-meter high travertine dam formed approximately 10,000 years before present, suggesting little or no immigration for more than 20,000 generations. It maintains constant warm spring temperature and year-round reproduction. This site historically supported a now-extinct pupfish species (Cyprinodon) and a morphologically distinct form of [[Gila Chub]] (Gila intermedia). Monkey Spring topminnows show relatively low fecundity in nature and approximately 50% longer male development time under laboratory conditions compared to other sites.

[[Cienega Creek]] and [[Sharp Spring]] have large population sizes and historically substantial connectedness with other sites. Sharp Spring represents the upper [[Santa Cruz River]] and may have had very large historical population size based on museum collections. The populations are separated by 250 km (Cienega-Monkey) and 101 km (Monkey-Sharp), with extensive dry reaches between sites except during floods.

Neither [[Cienega Creek]] nor [[Monkey Spring]] has been infested with [[Central Mosquito Fish]], while [[Bylas Spring]] and [[Sharp Spring]] have been impacted. Mosquito fish presence introduces competitors, predators, and potentially non-native parasites and pathogens, creating substantially different selection pressures.

## Key Findings

**Microsatellite Variation Within Populations:**
- [[Bylas Spring]] showed the lowest genetic variation, polymorphic for only one locus (Pooc-G49) with expected heterozygosity He = 0.075
- [[Sharp Spring]] had highest average expected heterozygosity (He = 0.281) and most alleles averaged over loci (3.8), primarily due to 12 different alleles at Pooc-C15
- [[Cienega Creek]] showed He = 0.263
- [[Monkey Spring]] showed intermediate variation (He = 0.202)
- All locus-population combinations showed observed heterozygosity not significantly different from Hardy-Weinberg expectations
- No evidence of interlocus associations: average correlation coefficient for individual homozygosity = 0.049 (range -0.125 to 0.200, all non-significant)

**Genetic Differentiation Among Populations:**
- Each population possessed at least one private allele
- [[Monkey Spring]] had highest proportion of private alleles (0.620 per locus on average)
- [[Sharp Spring]], [[Bylas Spring]], and [[Cienega Creek]] had average private allele frequencies of 0.247, 0.150, and 0.140 respectively
- When Sharp, Bylas, and Cienega combined, they have 0.599 alleles per locus per population that Monkey Spring lacks
- Genetic distance (Nei 1978) smallest for Cienega Creek-Sharp Spring comparison, largest for Bylas Spring-Monkey Spring
- FST and RST values highly statistically significant for all population pairs (permutation test)
- UPGMA phylogenetic tree shows [[Monkey Spring]] most divergent; [[Cienega Creek]] and [[Sharp Spring]] cluster together with [[Bylas Spring]] closely related

**Comparison Across Marker Types:**
- Microsatellite data generally consistent with allozyme, mtDNA, and MHC data, allowing for sampling error
- [[Bylas Spring]] consistently shows lowest variation across all marker types
- [[Sharp Spring]] remains most genetically variable overall, but [[Cienega Creek]] and [[Monkey Spring]] only slightly lower
- Substantial genetic differentiation for all population pairs across microsatellite loci
- mtDNA (Quattro et al. 1996) shows far less variation than microsatellites or MHC, possibly due to low resolution of six-cutter restriction endonucleases
- Allozyme data (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985) showed Bylas and Monkey Springs shared alleles at all 25 loci examined (genetic distance = 0), contrasting sharply with microsatellite and MHC results
- No association between MHC and microsatellite variation within individuals: mean correlation coefficient = -0.018 (range -0.15 to 0.16, none significant)
- Pattern of variation at microsatellite locus Pooc-LL53 nearly identical to MHC locus, likely reflecting similar population history rather than linkage

**Population History and Non-Selective Factors:**
- [[Bylas Spring]] low variation attributed to small effective population size, recurring bottlenecks, elevated location suggesting few founders and unlikely immigration
- [[Monkey Spring]] isolation for ~10,000 years (>20,000 generations) explains high proportion of private alleles despite substantial population size
- [[Sharp Spring]] and [[Cienega Creek]] large variation reflects historically large populations and substantial connectedness
- Bottleneck-generated genetic distance may account for substantial differentiation among Sharp Spring, Bylas Spring, and Cienega Creek
- Recent extreme physical isolation prevents mitigation of bottleneck effects by gene flow

**ESU Recommendations:**
- Molecular genetic studies strongly support [[Monkey Spring]] as separate ESU (most genetically divergent by multiple measures)
- Other three sites qualify as management units: populations with divergent allele frequencies connected by low gene flow levels making them functionally independent
- Physical isolation extreme: 580 km separating Bylas from others, 250 km between Cienega and Monkey, 101 km between Monkey and Sharp, with extensive dry reaches
- Non-genetic attributes support conservation significance: [[Bylas Spring]] only remaining mainstem Gila River stock; Monkey Spring historically supported unique endemic vertebrates; differential mosquito fish presence creates different selection pressures
- Life history differences: male development time ~50% longer in Monkey Spring; Monkey Spring shows low fecundity in nature and year-round reproduction vs. seasonal in others
- **Primary recommendation: all four watersheds should be managed and conserved separately**, revising previous management policy of using only Sharp Spring stock for reintroductions
