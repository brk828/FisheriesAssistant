# Post-Perturbation Genetic Changes in Populations of Endangered Virgin River Chubs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how a management poisoning event affected the genetics of the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*), an endemic, endangered cyprinid of the Virgin River in Utah, Arizona and Nevada. A 34-kilometer reach was treated with [[Rotenone]] to eradicate non-native [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*). A detoxification failure caused lethal concentrations to pass through an additional 50 km of stream. Using [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] at three polymorphic loci, the authors compared pre- and post-poison chub samples and hatchery stocks. Pre-poison fish formed a single panmictic population. Post-poison fish from [[Littlefield]] showed large allele frequency shifts and heterozygote excesses consistent with a severe [[Genetic Bottleneck]], with the altered pattern persisting about 2.5 years after treatment. The authors conclude that genetic data are essential for assessing the impact of catastrophes on endangered species, provided baseline data exist.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** The Virgin River chub was formerly *Gila robusta seminuda* and is known only from the Virgin River system. It is an obligate mainstream fish, ecologically restricted to deeper channel parts.
- **Fish fauna:** The Virgin River has five other native species: [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*, endangered), Virgin spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*, candidate for listing), flannelmouth sucker, desert sucker and speckled dace. Thirteen non-native species have been introduced to the system.
- **Invasive threat:** The red shiner, native to the Rio Grande/Mississippi drainages, was introduced to the Colorado River basin in the 1950s as bait and forage fish. It reached the lower Virgin River by the 1960s and was implicated in native fish declines across the region.
- **Management context:** Piscicides have long been used to remove unwanted fishes, followed by reintroduction or natural recolonization. The authors frame the poisoning as a major biotic disturbance comparable to drought, volcanism or chemical spills.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Barriers and dispersal:** A high-gradient canyon blocked upstream red shiner spread for about two decades. Red shiners passed the gorge in 1984 and reached the river below Washington Fields Diversion, Utah.
- **Treatment sequence:**
  - September 1988: a 10 km reach below Washington Fields Diversion was poisoned without detoxicant; natural oxidation and low discharge prevented a downstream kill.
  - October 1988: the lower 24 km were poisoned, but detoxification failed through human error. Dead fish appeared more than 30 km (Littlefield, Arizona) and 50 km (Mesquite, Nevada) downstream.
  - Late October 1988: the full 34 km reach was re-poisoned with downstream barriers in place; bioassays indicated successful detoxification.
- **Flood event:** In January 1989, a failed dam on Quail Creek Reservoir produced a flood about twice any previously recorded. The authors judged its direct effect on chubs to be minor and implausible as the cause of the genetic changes.
- **Recolonization barriers:** Littlefield lies more than 60 stream-km downstream of potential colonizers. A dry 1989 and irrigation diversions left a dry reach through the Virgin River Gorge, and 1990 and winter-spring 1991 remained relatively dry.

## Key Findings

**Sampling design**
- Pre-poison samples: Littlefield, Arizona (1987, N = 17, mixed year-classes) and Washington Fields Diversion, Utah (young-of-1988, N = 50).
- Post-poison wild samples: Washington Fields Diversion (young-of-1989, N = 16); Littlefield (young-of-1989, N = 32); Littlefield adults collected in 1991 (N = 21, two-year-olds of the 1989 year-class).
- Hatchery samples from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]: artificially stripped gametes (N = 50) and volunteer pond spawning (N = 50).
- Markers: Cbp-1 (nonspecific calcium-binding protein), Ck-A (creatine kinase) and Pgm-A (phosphoglucomutase), analyzed by starch gel electrophoresis.

**Pre-poison baseline**
- No significant Hardy-Weinberg deviations or between-sample allele frequency differences. Earlier data (Rosenfeld and Wilkinson 1989) agreed, so there was no evidence of spatial or temporal genetic divergence.

**Post-poison changes**
- Washington Fields Diversion: allele frequencies resembled the original condition, but there was an excess of heterozygotes at Cbp-1. The authors attribute this to immigration from the unpoisoned reach upstream.
- Littlefield: large allele frequency shifts at Cbp-1 and Pgm-A, and a significant heterozygote excess at Ck-A. The two post-poison Littlefield samples (1989 and 1991) did not differ significantly (G = 0.42, p > 0.50) and were pooled. They were of the same year-class and parentage.
- The 1991 Littlefield adults were genetically indistinguishable from 1989 juveniles about 2.5 years after poisoning, indicating that substantial recolonization from upstream had not yet occurred.

**Mechanism**
- The authors rejected differential reproduction or migration and selection as explanations, and identified a severe reduction in the number of spawning adults (a genetic bottleneck) as the most plausible cause.
- A Ck-A model showed that the observed genotypes (no aa homozygotes among 53 post-poison fish, with an a allele frequency of 0.43) match expectations from only about three spawning fish. Expected versus observed genotype counts were 45 vs 39.75 and 8 vs 13.25 (χ² = 3.09, P > 0.05).

**Hatchery stocks**
- Both Dexter NFH stocks differed from the source population. Artificially spawned fish showed excess heterozygotes at Cbp-1 and a significant allele frequency shift at Ck-A. Volunteer-spawned fish showed excess heterozygotes at Ck-A but allele frequencies similar to the source stock.
- The rare Pgm-A allele (about 3%) was absent from both hatchery samples (P = 0.048 by binomial probability).
- The artificial brood was produced by six females and eighteen males, giving an effective population size of at most eighteen. The authors estimated that 15% of alleles at frequency 0.05 would be lost.

**Management conclusions**
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service decided not to augment the Virgin River population with Dexter stocks. The authors recommend that any future restocking use a large effective population size of brood fish.
- Two options were proposed: (1) continued demographic and genetic monitoring of Littlefield chubs against upstream fish, with the risk of inbreeding depression (monitoring fluctuating asymmetry as an indicator); or (2) supplemental hatchery stocking, which would reduce inbreeding and increase genetic variability but could reduce effective population size through differential reproduction of wild and captive parents.
- The paper argues that piscicide use should account for nontarget native species, that incomplete kills can cause genetic changes in survivors, and that allozyme surveys of imperiled species are a prudent baseline and monitoring tool.
