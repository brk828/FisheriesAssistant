# Arizona Fish Guide: Sport Fish Species Accounts and Regulations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae #percidae #esocidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a popular Arizona angling handbook, *The Arizona Fish Book* by Bob Hinsch with illustrations by Mary Hinsch (copyright 1982), covering the major sport fish found in the state's reservoirs, lakes, rivers and streams. Each species account typically gives identification features, spawning behavior, feeding habits, distribution within Arizona, recommended baits and lures, and state or world records. The book closes with the 1982 Arizona fishing rules and regulations (open season, licence fees, daily bag and possession limits) and advertisements. The species set includes native and introduced fishes, several of which the guide notes are imported or stocked.

## Background & Description

The guide is organized as one account per species. Covered taxa include:

- **Black basses:** [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*).
- **Temperate basses and relatives:** [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*), and [[Yellow Bass]] (*Morone mississippiensis*).
- **Trout and char:** [[Arizona Native Trout]] (*Salmo apache*, with *Salmo gilae* noted), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), and [[Arctic Grayling]] (*Thymallus arcticus*).
- **Panfish:** [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lucania cyanellus* as written in the source).
- **Other species:** [[Tilapia]] (*Oreochromis mossambicus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Walleye]] (*Sander vitreus*), [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*).

The source names some taxa inconsistently (for example the bluegill is labelled with a different genus in the printed species list), so binomials above follow the species headings where legible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Introductions and stocking:** Many Arizona sport fish are non-native. Striped bass were first stocked in [[Lake Havasu]] and later in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]] (mid-1970s). White bass were introduced to [[Lake Pleasant]], described as a "closed" system. Yellow bass were stocked in Roosevelt Lake in 1932. Walleye were first stocked in the mid-1960s. Threadfin shad, a key forage species, was introduced into Arizona bass lakes in the early 1950s.
- **Native trout decline:** The guide states that Gila trout disappeared from Arizona, and Apache trout nearly disappeared through hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]]. Natural barriers in small mountain streams preserved pure populations. Apache trout are listed as threatened, and recovery programs involve Arizona Game and Fish and the White Mountain Apache Tribe.
- **Biogeography:** The author links Arizona trout to Sierra Madre (Mexico) strains and to a brackish sea that covered southern Arizona about five million years ago.
- **Forage relationships:** Threadfin shad is the dominant forage fish for largemouth bass, striped bass, white bass, yellow bass, walleye, and crappie. Crayfish, insects, and minnows are important for other species.
- **Habitat limits:** Arizona's desert climate limits walleye distribution. Carp can live in all Arizona waters below 6,000 feet, and the author notes they are nearly impossible to eradicate from small waters. Tilapia are restricted by water temperature to warm canals and ponds in the southern part of the state.
- **Indicator concept:** The grayling is described as an indicator of clean, cold high-country water.

## Key Findings

- **Popularity:** The largemouth bass is called Arizona's favorite fish, and more anglers spend more time and money on it than on any other species.
- **Spawning patterns:** Largemouth bass spawn from April into late summer, when water reaches about 65 degrees. Males guard eggs until hatching. Eggs number 200 to 5,000 per pound of body weight.
- **Striped bass feeding:** A study of 1,000 stomachs from Lake Powell showed shad and only a few crappies as prey; the author concludes that predation by stripers is not a serious threat to bass populations.
- **Record fish:** Records are listed for many species. Examples include a 59-pound striped bass from the Colorado River south of Bullhead City (March 1977), a 21-pound, 5-ounce rainbow trout from the Willow Beach area (September 1955), and a 65-pound flathead catfish from San Carlos Lake (the largest on the Arizona record list).
- **Native trout status:** Apache trout are found nowhere except Arizona and are on the threatened list, with restocking planned in historic habitat first.
- **Walleye and pike:** Walleye and northern pike became established in Arizona after introduction; pike are concentrated at Lake Mary, south of Flagstaff.
- **Regulations (1982 season):** The open season ran January 1 through December 31, 1982. Limits included 10 smallmouth bass in the aggregate (minimum 16 inches for some waters), 5 striped bass, 6 walleye, 6 northern pike, 25 catfish in the aggregate, and 10 trout in the aggregate, with special rules for the Colorado River, Lee's Ferry, Becker Lake, and some urban parks. Licence fees were listed by class and residency (for example, a general fishing licence at $6.90 for residents and $18.50 for non-residents as printed).
- **Management tension:** The book notes a long-running conflict between native game fish interests and introduced species, particularly carp and stocked trout, and the conservation status of Arizona's native trout.
