# Memorandum on Arizona Native Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #conservation #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #catostomidae #arizona-native-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1987 internal memorandum from [[The Arizona Nature Conservancy]] outlines the precarious status of Arizona's native freshwater fish fauna and details the organization's conservation strategy. Of the 31 pre-settlement native fish species, one is extinct, four are extirpated from Arizona, and roughly half are listed or proposed for listing as Threatened or Endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The memo attributes these declines primarily to the introduction of exotic species (now exceeding 100 resident species in Arizona) and the loss of perennial stream habitat. The document catalogs TANC's three-tiered preserve strategy: (1) preserves under direct TANC ownership, (2) preserves transferred to other management entities, and (3) potential preserves under investigation, each supporting specific imperiled native fish taxa.

## Background & Description

- **Historical baseline**: Arizona's pre-settlement freshwater fish fauna comprised 31 native species.
- **Current fauna**: Due to introductions of exotic species, over 100 fish species now reside in Arizona waters, many of which are predatory upon or competitive with native species.
- **Species status**:
  - 1 species extinct
  - 4 species extirpated from Arizona
  - ~50% of remaining native species listed or proposed for listing as Threatened or Endangered
- **Case example — [[Woundfin]]**: Survives only in a small portion of the [[Virgin River]]; population size and range are critically limited, making it vulnerable to extirpation from a single catastrophic event.
- **Case example — [[Desert Pupfish]]**: Mexican populations in the [[Colorado River]] delta were reportedly destroyed within a three-year period, precipitated by the introduction of [[Tilapia]] via 1983 floodwaters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary threats identified**:
  1. Introduction of exotic/non-native fish species (predation and competition)
  2. Loss of perennial stream habitat and stable stream flows
- **Conservation rationale**: The memo identifies habitat preservation and maintenance of stable stream flows as the single most important factor in protecting remaining native fish populations.
- **Land tenure opportunity**: Historical land ownership patterns mean most Arizona perennial streams are privately held, creating realistic opportunities for conservation acquisition — a key strategic lever exploited by [[The Arizona Nature Conservancy]].
- **Habitat types referenced**: Perennial streams, cienegas (wetland/marsh habitats), and springs — critical refugia for range-restricted native fish in an arid landscape.
- **Interbasin threats**: The Tilapia introduction into the Colorado River delta illustrates how flood events can transport exotic species across previously isolated aquatic systems, accelerating native fish decline.

### Summary Observations
- TANC's tripartite strategy (retain/transfer/investigate) reflects a pragmatic conservation model leveraging both direct land management and partnerships with federal agencies and NGOs.
- Multiple preserve sites protect overlapping species (e.g., [[Gila Topminnow]] appears at five separate sites), indicating a deliberate strategy of distributing populations across multiple protected localities to reduce extinction risk from localized catastrophic events.
- The Yaqui River drainage species suite ([[Yaqui Topminnow]], [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Sucker]], [[Yaqui Catfish]]) represents a distinct biogeographic assemblage protected via two linked preserves ([[San Bernardino Ranch]] and [[Leslie Creek]]).
- The [[Hassayampa River]] preserve uniquely serves as an active reintroduction and nursery site in cooperation with a federal agency, distinguishing it from passive habitat-protection preserves.
