# Razorback Sucker Movements in Lake Mohave and Baited-Trap Bias in Owens Tui Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document holds two abstracts from the Proceedings of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (Vol. XXXII, 2000 Symposium). The first is a telemetry study of wild adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen texanus]]*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, which tracked fish by [[Sonic Telemetry]] from 1994 through July 1997. The second is a classroom-based experiment on the [[Owens Tui Chub]] (*[[Gila bicolor snyderi]]*) in [[McNally Ditch]], a distributary of the [[Owens River]] in Inyo County, California, testing whether bait types changed trap catches. The first study is the more substantial, with spawning movements, depth use and thermal use as its main results.

## Background & Description

**Razorback Sucker study (Mueller, Marsh, Knowles et al.)**
- Authors and affiliations: U.S. Geological Survey; Arizona State University; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service; [[Bureau of Reclamation]].
- Subject: wild adult razorback suckers in Lake Mohave, a reservoir on the Colorado River.
- Method: sonic telemetry, with tracking from 1994 through July 1997.
- Sample: 10 adult fish (the Spanish abstract gives "10"; the English OCR is unclear).

**Owens Tui Chub study (Hartshorn and Tobey, Bishop Union High School)**
- Setting: McNally Ditch, about 3 miles north of the Laws Museum, Inyo County, California.
- Design: a high school biology class tested baits to find a more effective trapping method for the native Owens tui chub.
- Motivation: trapping of this native fish had been debated because of possible injury, so a less harmful and more effective method was sought.
- Baits tried, reportedly ranging "from chocolate to cat food," were compared against traps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Mohave razorback suckers**
- Movement rates ranged from 0.00 to 17.35 km per day, and the mean distribution figure was 39 km. The abstract compares these with riverine populations, which the authors describe as having movements of this kind.
- All study fish returned to spawning areas used in previous years, and they also visited other spawning areas.
- Mature females moved substantial distances between spawning sites during the reproductive period, at roughly 1 to 28 km per month (the scan of this range is partly unclear).
- Fish were most active after spawning and moved most in the months after reproduction. The Spanish abstract notes they moved toward areas known for high algal production.
- Fish were typically within 50 m of shore (p < 0.001). Depths ranged from 0.2 to more than 30 m.
- Adults were detected across the full available thermal gradient (about 2 to 30 °C). Summer body temperatures typically fell between 18 and 22 °C.
- Within-reservoir movements showed no correlation with depth or time of day.
- The authors suggest that fish may use vertical position to select specific temperatures during reservoir stratification.

**Owens tui chub trapping**
- Traps with baits differed significantly from the comparison condition (p < 0.01). The scan obscures which condition attracted more fish, so the direction of this result should be checked against the original.
- The authors concluded that the fish are attracted to the odor of the bait less than they expected. They suggest the chub's relatively small brain and nose may limit olfactory-based attraction.

## Key Findings

1. **Lake Mohave razorback suckers are highly mobile.** Movement rates of 0.00–17.35 km/day and a mean of 39 km were recorded. These are described as comparable to riverine populations.
2. **Spawning site fidelity with exploration.** All fish returned to prior spawning sites and also used other spawning areas.
3. **Post-spawning activity peaks.** Movement was greatest after spawning, and females moved between sites during the spawning season.
4. **Shoreline association.** Fish were usually within 50 m of shore (p < 0.001), in water from 0.2 to over 30 m deep.
5. **Thermal tolerance and selection.** Fish occupied the full available range (about 2–30 °C) but summer body temperatures were typically 18–22 °C. Vertical movement may be used to select temperatures during stratification.
6. **Baited traps are biased for Owens tui chub.** Bait treatments produced a significant difference in trap catch (p < 0.01). The authors question whether olfactory cues attract this fish as strongly as assumed.
