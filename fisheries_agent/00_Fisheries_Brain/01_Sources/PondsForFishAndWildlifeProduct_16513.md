# Ponds for Fish and Wildlife Production

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Missouri Conservation Commission bulletin (reprinted 1953) is a practical guide to building and managing [[Farm Pond]]s for three purposes: farm water supply, wildlife habitat, and fish production. Part I covers [[Multi-Purpose Pond]]s, including site selection, dam construction, watershed protection, clearing muddy water, plantings, and pond sealing. Part II covers [[Fish Pond]]s, including depth and watershed ratios, aquatic plant control, water fertilization, and stocking and harvest. The document is a management manual, not a research study, and it gives no sample sizes or experimental data. Its central message is that sound construction, watershed care, and balanced stocking of [[Largemouth Black Bass]] and [[Bluegill Sunfish]] are required for good results, and that shortcuts reliably fail.

## Background & Description

The bulletin notes that a farm pond is first a device for storing surface water. A wave of pond construction across Missouri's uplands followed the drought of the 1930s. The pond movement was promoted by the State College of Agriculture, the [[Missouri Conservation Commission]], the Soil Conservation Service, and the Production and Marketing Administration. The Commission's standard specifications called for a minimum depth of 8 feet and a minimum surface area of a quarter-acre. During the 1952 drought, ponds built to these specifications kept an ample water supply, while many poorly built ponds dried up or became too shallow for livestock and fish.

The document presents a "pond world" in which water, soil, plants, and animals interact. It describes the Commission's aims of supporting sportsmen, relieving pressure on public hunting and fishing places, and improving relations between farmers and sportsmen through cooperative pond development. It states that a well-built farm pond is "nine-tenths" of a multi-purpose pond, and that the remaining work adds fish production, wildlife habitat, recreation, and possible irrigation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site and watershed.** Suitable soil must contain enough clay to hold water. The document recommends a gentle slope and a watershed of about five acres per surface acre of water in [[Multi-Purpose Pond]]s. Watersheds should be in good sod pasture or unburned woodland to keep water clear. Springs are poor sources for fish ponds because their cold water limits growth and can intensify vegetation problems. Creek-bed ponds are considered a poor risk because of large watersheds, erosion, silt, and pollution.

**Silt and turbidity.** The bulletin warns that a single rain can deposit a layer six to twelve inches deep in a pond below eroding gullies. Muddy water blocks sunlight, reducing the growth of microscopic plants and animals that form the base of the fish food chain. Turbidity can be caused by the soil type, by [[Bottom-Feeding Fish]] such as carp, buffalo, and bullheads, or by [[Muskrat]]s and [[Crayfish]] that dig in banks and stir sediment.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Dense submerged or emergent plants give small forage fish too much escape cover. The larger predators then cannot control them, and the result is stunted fish. Plants recommended for multi-purpose ponds are restricted to the shoreline, where they control wave erosion and provide cover for [[Bullfrog]]s and food for waterfowl.

**Water quality and oxygen.** Decaying vegetation used to clear water can consume oxygen. Excess material in hot weather can cause fish suffocation, and it can also produce odors and dark coloration.

## Key Findings

**Construction and safety**
- Dam fill is built in six-inch well-packed layers. Best clay goes in the core, second-best on the water side, and poorest on the downstream side. A [[Core Cutoff Trench]] must be dug down to clay subsoil.
- The [[Spillway]] is the most common point of dam failure when it is poorly built or freeboard is too low. Spillways should be wide, located on solid undisturbed ground, and level from side to side. In fish ponds, water should never flow over the spillway deeper than three inches.
- Freeboard should be at least 3 feet above the spillway floor after settling.
- Spillways on ponds with the lowest watershed ratio should be at least 10 feet wide, and width should increase as the watershed-to-surface ratio rises.
- Deepening pond edges by 1.5 to 2 feet improves fishing and helps control aquatic weeds.
- Test holes should be dug before construction, because digging through clay into sand or gravel can cause leaks.

**Size and depth**
- Multi-purpose ponds: from about a quarter-acre to two or three acres, with maximum depth of 8 to 15 feet.
- Fish ponds: depth of 12 to 16 feet in the deepest part, and ponds of one acre or larger are generally better.
- Fish ponds: watershed not to exceed 8 acres of drainage per surface acre of water.

**Watershed and basin treatment**
- Raw areas, including the basin, should be limed, fertilized, mulched, and seeded as soon as construction ends. Soil tests through the County Agricultural Extension Agent determine the rates.
- Manure and complete fertilizer should not be applied to areas that will be covered by water, because nitrogen stimulates algae. Only [[Superphosphate]] and mulch are applied there.
- Mulch is spread about 5 inches deep, with 20 per cent superphosphate added at 50 pounds per ton.
- Nurse-crop seeding rates: rye or wheat at 100 pounds per acre (late summer or fall), oats at 75 pounds per acre (spring or early summer), and Sudan grass at 25 pounds per acre (hot, dry mid-summer).
- Shoreline [[Wild Millet]] is sown in a five-foot swath. Two or three pounds of seed are enough for an average one-acre pond.

**Clearing muddy water**
- [[Gypsum Clearing]] uses 12 to 15 pounds per 1,000 cubic feet of water. It costs more than vegetation treatment and increases water hardness, but it avoids the odors that vegetation can cause.
- The volume of a pond is calculated as surface area in square feet times one-third of the greatest depth in feet.
- Vegetation clearing uses dried material spread in an even layer in water no deeper than three feet. The amount should not exceed the mowed area of a good stand equal in size to the pond's water area.
- Bottom-feeding fish must be removed and the pond restocked with the correct species. Crayfish holes can be treated with chloride of lime (four ounces per three quarts of water), and muskrats must be removed.

**Fencing and plantings**
- [[Multiflora Rose]] is recommended as a permanent livestock fence that also provides quail and songbird cover. Sericea and Korean lespedeza sown as a border add wildlife value and help control erosion.
- Fence setbacks: at least 30 feet from the shoreline, at least 10 feet from the downstream toe of the dam, and at least 100 feet from the water's edge where most drainage enters.
- The Commission sells a [[Wildlife Bundle]] of 1,050 trees and shrubs, including enough multiflora rose for the fence. Orders are placed through the County Extension Agent between November 1 and January 30.

**Aquatic plant control (Part II, fish ponds)**
- Only grass and millet seedings are recommended around fish ponds. Submerged plants are especially harmful because they shelter small fish and cause stunting.
- Young invaders such as cattails, willows, and water primrose can be controlled by uprooting them every two weeks.
- [[Ammate]]: one pound of crystals (80 per cent ammonium sulfamate) plus two tablespoons of detergent powder per gallon of water. One gallon covers 100 square feet on cattails to 300 square feet on water primrose. Ammate is best applied to new growth in late spring.
- [[Esteron 245]]: one fluid ounce plus two heaping tablespoons of detergent per gallon of water for arrowhead, water primrose, water willow, and mud plantain. For bulrushes and cattails, one fluid ounce per gallon of fuel or diesel oil is used. Cattails are best treated in early summer when they reach about four feet. One gallon covers 300 to 500 square feet.
- [[Oktone]]: two fluid ounces per gallon of fuel or diesel oil for duckweed. Mats should be removed with a minnow seine before spraying.
- [[Copper Sulphate]]: not more than 3 pounds per acre-foot (about 1 part per million) for filamentous algae and Chara. Repeated light applications are preferred over a single heavy one. Only half of a heavily infested pond should be treated at once to avoid oxygen depletion.

**Water fertilization**
- Fertilization is recommended only for ponds that will be heavily fished, because it can increase weeds and stunting and may be costly per pound of fish harvested.
- Fertilizer: 4-12-4 mixture, applied at 80 pounds per acre in applications about two weeks apart from late April, up to 1,000 pounds per acre per year.
- Fertilizer should only be applied in water one to four feet deep.
- Monitoring: if a hand held elbow-deep cannot be seen, another 80-pound application is needed. The test is repeated at least every two weeks.

**Sealing leaks**
- Seepage in new ponds is normal until the soil saturates. Continued loss beyond use and evaporation indicates a leak.
- [[Bentonite Sealing]] swells to about 15 times its volume. Three methods are described: mixed blanket, pure blanket, and sprinkle. Sprinkling uses KWK Volclay pellets and requires a known leak location. Sealing may take about a week to show full effect.
- Screened cinders can seal leaks caused by burrowing, because they discourage further digging.

**Stocking and fish management**
- Bluegill and largemouth black bass are the two species currently recommended. Bass are essential as predators that keep bluegill populations in balance.
- Planting ratio: ten bluegill to one bass by number of fingerlings (one to three inches). Recommended stocking for fertile ponds: 1,000 bluegill and 100 bass fingerlings per acre.
- The critical factor is weight. Four to five pounds of bluegill are needed as forage to produce one pound of bass.
- Bluegill usually spawn at one year of age and grow large enough for sport in their second year. Bass usually reach spawning maturity at two years of age.
- Overcrowded sunfish can drive bass from nests and eat eggs. Bass then fail to reproduce, and restocking will not help until surplus sunfish are removed.
- Harvest: at least 10 times as many bluegill as bass should be removed each year. Stunted fish should be targeted for increased fishing.
- Green sunfish, bullheads, and crappies should not be stocked with bass and bluegill. Green sunfish compete with bluegill. Bullheads make water turbid. Crappies compete with adult bass and prey on small bass.
- Extreme stunting may require complete draining, or renovation with [[Rotenone Renovation]] under Commission supervision.

**Stocking program and legal framework**
- Under Missouri law, wildlife including fish belongs to the public. A farmer may take wildlife on his own farm without a permit, but public use of stocked ponds is governed by the [[Pond Stocking Agreement]]. That agreement requires the landowner to manage fishing, avoid further restocking, and consult the Commission before releasing any fish.
- Initial stocking applications must be inspected and endorsed by the Conservation Agent. Applications received before April 1 receive bass in spring and bluegill in fall. Applications between April 1 and August 1 receive both species for fall delivery. Later applications wait until the following year.
- Ponds must meet multi-purpose specifications before they are eligible for stocking, and special fish-pond management is recommended for the highest yields of large fish.
