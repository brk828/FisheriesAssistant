# A Landscape Approach for Detecting and Evaluating Change in a Semi-Arid Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #landscape-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study develops a landscape-scale change detection method to quantify vegetation and land cover change in the Upper [[San Pedro River]] basin, a transboundary semi-arid watershed spanning southeast Arizona and northeast Sonora, Mexico. Using digital land cover maps derived from [[North American Landscape Characterization]] (NALC) [[Landsat MSS]] imagery from 1973, 1986 and 1992, the authors compute composition and pattern metrics. Grassland and desertscrub declined in extent and became more fragmented, while [[Mesquite Woodland]] expanded in area, patch number and connectivity. Urban cover also grew substantially. The authors frame these shifts as important for watershed hydrology and ecosystem vulnerability, and they note that the study was not designed to establish cause and effect.

Note: the source document is a research article, not fish or aquatic-community research. Its subject is terrestrial land cover in a riparian-influenced watershed.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses twentieth-century compositional change in dominant American West vegetation, where perennial grasses have been replaced by more xerophytic mesquite woodland and desertscrub. Proposed drivers include [[Anglo-American Settlement]] land use, historical climate change, [[Livestock Overgrazing]] and [[Fire Suppression]]. Prior evidence relied on [[Repeat Photography]] and historic land survey descriptions, which the authors consider limited for area-wide quantification.

The study watershed covers approximately 7,600 km² (5,800 km² in Arizona; 1,800 km² in Sonora). Elevation ranges from 900 to 2,940 m and annual rainfall from 300 to 750 mm. The basin is a transition zone between the [[Sonoran Desert]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. The authors define vulnerability as a desired state in which community-type diversity, productivity and resistance to disturbance are maintained.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Land cover classes (10):** forest, oak woodland, mesquite woodland, grassland, desertscrub, riparian, agriculture, urban, water and barren.
- **Riparian corridor:** dominated by cottonwood and Goodding willow; the authors note its importance for hydrology and protective designation after 1986 acquisition.
- **Hydrological implications:** the authors state that energy and water balance characteristics differ significantly among cover types, so shifts in cover alter basin hydrology.
- **Degradation processes:** the authors identify two principal processes in western rangelands: loss of vegetative cover (decline in biomass and diversity, with exotic annuals or woody xerophytes replacing native cover) and accelerated water and wind erosion.
- **Land use:** a single open-pit copper mining district near Cananea, Sonora, and urban centers including Sierra Vista, Arizona.

## Key Findings

**Methods and accuracy**
- Imagery: [[Landsat MSS]] scenes (path/row 35/38 and 35/39) from three pre-monsoon dates: 5 June 1973, 10 June 1986 and 2 June 1992; 60 m pixel resolution.
- Classification: unsupervised ISODATA producing 60 spectral classes, grouped into 21 intermediate classes and then 10 land cover categories, with ancillary topographic maps and field GPS visits.
- Accuracy assessment: 457 reference sample points from [[Digital Orthophoto Quadrangles]] yielded an overall accuracy of approximately 74.8% (± 3.979), with user's accuracy above 60% for all classes except urban and barren. Urban user's accuracy was 44.00% and barren 55.00%, attributed to difficulty detecting low-density rural residences at 60 m resolution.
- Analysis was performed in ARC/INFO, with landscape metrics computed in a separate statistical package.

**Land cover change, 1973–1992**
- [[Grassland]]: decreased steadily; over 50,000 ha lost. About 46,025 ha were lost in 1973–1986 and 5,450 ha in 1986–1992.
- [[Desertscrub]]: over 59,000 ha lost, with about 89% of the loss occurring in 1973–1986.
- [[Mesquite Woodland]]: increased from 2.75% of the landscape in 1973 to 14% by 1992. Extent rose about five-fold between 1973 and 1986 (from 20,812 to 107,334 ha). More than 85,000 ha were gained overall.
- [[Urban Expansion]]: increased from 3,254 ha (1973) to 12,271 ha (1992), a 277% relative increase. The largest surge occurred in 1973–1986.
- [[Riparian Corridor]]: declined from 8,674 ha to 6,245 ha (1973–1986), then recovered after protective acquisition of 23,490 ha of corridor in 1986.
- Forest and oak woodland changed little.

**Fragmentation and connectivity**
- Grassland and desertscrub patch numbers increased while average patch sizes declined: desertscrub from 6.18 to 4.52 ha and grassland from 11.34 to 6.05 ha.
- Mesquite patches increased in number, average size and connectivity, coalescing into large closed-canopy stands.
- Urban largest patch grew from 4,141 to 4,761 ha (as reported), while average urban patch size and connectivity decreased, likely reflecting outlying rural urbanization.

**Conversion sources**
- 88,508 ha, or 97.7%, of mesquite gain came from desertscrub and grassland combined.
- 8,873 ha, or 94.5%, of urban gain came from the same two classes.

**Conclusions**
- Extensive, highly connected grassland and desertscrub are the most vulnerable ecosystems to fragmentation and loss from mesquite encroachment.
- Landscape pattern indicators provide a cost-effective, large-area method for evaluating watershed condition, complementing fine-scale ground surveys.
- The authors explicitly state that the study does not determine cause and effect, though rapid urbanization is identified as a major recent factor.
