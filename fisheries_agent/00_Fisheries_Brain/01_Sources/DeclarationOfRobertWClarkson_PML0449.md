# Declaration of Robert W. Clarkson

#ichthyology #marine-biology #red-shiner

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of court papers from the consolidated case *Southwest Center for Biological Diversity v. Babbitt* (D. Ariz., Civ. 97-474 and 97-1479), together with a sworn declaration by fishery biologist Robert W. Clarkson of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR), Phoenix Area Office, dated 10 October 2000. The central question is whether the arrival of the non-native [[Red Shiner]] in [[Aravaipa Creek]] is attributable to BOR's operation of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), and whether that arrival amounts to a prohibited "take" of two listed fishes, [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

Clarkson's declaration argues that the red shiner's presence in Aravaipa Creek is better explained by a long, slow, mostly natural spread from the Gila River and San Pedro River than by a CAP-linked incursion, and that the data "dispute" the 1994 [[Biological Opinion]]'s finding that the probability of CAP causation was "high, if not conclusive." The court disagreed in part. It granted summary judgment to the Southwest Center on the take issue, finding that the BOR violated Section 9 of the ESA, and denied the federal defendants' cross-motion on that point.

## Background & Description

**Species profile (as described in the document):**
- The red shiner is described as an aggressive colonizing species whose size-related swimming ability was judged to limit upstream movement to distances of less than dozens of miles per year.
- It is a non-native fish in the Gila River basin. The document does not give its scientific name, native range, or life-history traits.
- Listed native species of concern are spikedace and loach minnow in Aravaipa Creek. The razorback sucker is named as critical habitat affected in the Verde, Salt and Gila rivers. No biology for these species is given.

**Key actors and documents:**
- **Clarkson declaration (10 Oct 2000):** Sworn statement of a BOR fishery biologist responsible for monitoring the Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives (RPA) in the 1994 Biological Opinion on CAP water transport and delivery.
- **Court order (filed 26 Sep 2000):** Grants in part and denies in part the Southwest Center's Motion for Summary Judgment and the federal defendants' Cross-Motion. Motions were heard on 27 July 2000. The order refers back to a 30 September 1999 order.
- **Biological Opinion:** Issued 15 April 1994 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (the OCR text reads "1984," which is almost certainly a scan error for 1994).
- **Parties:** Southwest Center for Biological Diversity (plaintiff); Central Arizona Water Conservation District (plaintiff in the consolidated action); Bruce Babbitt, John Rogers and Eluid Martinez (federal defendants, in official capacities).

**Legal framing:** The court applied the ESA's take prohibition and rejected the traditional preliminary-injunction balancing test for ESA cases, noting that Congress removed courts' traditional equitable discretion in ESA injunction proceedings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Red shiner timeline in the watershed:**

- **Gila River (1976–1999):** First captured near the San Pedro confluence in 1976 (USBR 1976). Under standardized BOR monitoring from 1991, it was consistently one of the most abundant species between Ashurst-Hayden Diversion Dam and Coolidge Dam. Catches: 800 (1991), 809 (1992), 995 (1993), 387 (1995), 183 (1996, single station), 368 (1997), 921 (1998, six stations), 1,296 (1999). The declaration argues these numbers imply establishment well before 1991.
- **Gila River above Safford (1977–78):** 129 red shiners were taken, making up 22% of all fishes collected (Minckley and Clarkson 1979).
- **San Carlos Reservoir origin:** The species was likely introduced to [[San Carlos Reservoir]] in 1972 as bait fish and then moved upstream. Small fishes pass downstream through [[Coolidge Dam]], so the declaration considers it "almost certain" that reservoir fish colonized the Gila River by the 1970s.
- **Lower San Pedro River (1988–2000):** Noted in 1988 but no museum specimens before 1990. BOR counts at 2–3 stations were: 2 (1991), 9 (1992), 0 (1993), 10 (1994), 99 (1996), 67 (1997), 60 (1998), 52 (1999), 0 (2000). The 1995 count is not legible in the OCR text.
- **Aravaipa Creek (1990–2000):** First recorded in September 1990 (one specimen, ASU). An October 1990 stream-wide survey took 27 fish, including the "5 out of hundreds of seine hauls" from an AGFD memo. Not seen again until October 1993 (one specimen). Next captured in October 1997 (19 fish). Subsequent stream-wide surveys ranged from 4 to 45 fish per event (for example, 45 in October 1999 by AGFD; 4 in April 2000 by ASU).
- **CAP canals (1990–2000):** First recorded at the Bouse Hills pumping plant in 1990 (numbers not reported). Later catches include 1 fish from the middle CAP and 26 from the lower CAP (1995), and 205 from the upper CAP plus 3 from the lower CAP (1999). The 2000 sample, upper CAP only, captured 58.

**Barrier and water-delivery history:**
- [[Pima Lateral Canal]] (PLC) electrical barrier: built in 1989 and operating before the first CAP water went down the Pima Lateral Feeder Canal (PLFC) in April 1990. Shut down in May 1992 after the [[China Wash]] barrier on the [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]] (FCGC) came online in May 1990.
- Outages: the PLC barrier had no reported failures before 1997. In 1997 it was offline due to a backup-generator fuel interruption. Failures to operate during power outages occurred on 28 August 1998 (1 hour), 4 September 1998 (1 hour 30 minutes) and 23 September 1998 (1 hour 20 minutes). No later failures were reported through the date of the declaration.
- CAP deliveries to the FCGC began in April 1990, with gaps from 1991 to 1997. Deliveries have occurred in various months since, including May 2000 onward.

**Connectivity:** The estimated path from the PLFC to Aravaipa Creek (down the PLFC, up the PLC, through the FCGC and Gila River, then down the San Pedro River) is about 66 stream miles. Clarkson argues that the low red shiner abundance in the CAP and the long path together make CAP-mediated transport unlikely.

**Biological Opinion on invasion risk:** The BO notes that the CAP "provides a 'highway' for the movement of non-native species within the Gila River basin," and that, historically, Gila basin rivers were connected by surface flow before dams and diversions fragmented them. It predicts that over the 100-year life of the CAP, the probability is "high that one or more non-native fish species will use the CAP as an avenue to colonize habitats now occupied by listed fish species."

## Key Findings

1. **Clarkson's opinion (Declaration, para. 13):** The data "dispute" a finding that the probability of CAP causation was "high, if not conclusive." He concludes that red shiner's long-term presence in the Gila and San Pedro systems, the rarity of the species in the CAP, and the long travel path all argue against a CAP-linked incursion.

2. **Population fluctuation is expected:** Clarkson notes that stream fish populations vary with flood and drought, competition, predation, sampling effort and sampler bias, so the Aravaipa Creek numbers should be read with caution.

3. **Court finding on pre-existence:** The defendants argued that red shiners were already in the San Pedro and Aravaipa systems by the 1970s, so the species' current presence could not be attributed to the 1990 PLFC project. The court noted that the BO itself recognized that "a few" non-native fish already occupied areas of concern.

4. **Court finding on attribution:** Based on the BO's conclusions and predictions, the court found the probability that the PLFC project caused the jump from "5 fish out of hundreds of seine hauls" to an "incursion" to be "high, if not conclusive." The court also cited the BO's statement that the CAP "provides enhanced opportunities for such non-natives to colonize, recolonize, or augment existing populations."

5. **Defendants' citation problem:** The court found that the defendants cited a study listed in the BO's literature section for the claim that red shiner was "one of the most abundant fish species" of Gila basin mid- to low-elevation streams. The defendants did not quote the study, and the BO did not appear to make that claim. The court considered the phrase "most abundant," without context, to be vague.

6. **Ruling on take:** The court found that a take of spikedace and loach minnow in Aravaipa Creek had occurred, and that the BOR therefore violated Section 9 of the ESA. It granted the Southwest Center's motion for summary judgment on this issue and denied the federal defendants' motion.

7. **Predicted impacts:** The BO predicts that non-native species using the CAP will adversely affect listed habitat for spikedace and loach minnow in Aravaipa Creek, the upper Gila River, the Upper Verde River, and the Blue and San Francisco rivers, and critical habitat for razorback sucker in the Verde, Salt and Gila rivers.

8. **Remaining data gaps:** Several annual counts are missing or illegible in the OCR text, including Gila River 1994 and San Pedro River 1995. The document does not give the scientific names, native ranges or ecology of the fish species beyond what is stated above.
