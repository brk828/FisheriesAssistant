# Red Shiner Incursion into Aravaipa Creek: Expert Declaration in Central Arizona Project Litigation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a sworn declaration (dated October 2000) by fisheries biologist [[Robert W. Clarkson]] of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR), filed in consolidated federal litigation over the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The declarant addresses whether the nonnative [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) is moving into [[Aravaipa Creek]] through CAP water deliveries, and whether this threatens native fishes protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The text also includes the court's order on cross-motions for summary judgment, which the OCR text indicates was resolved in favor of the plaintiffs, with the court finding that BOR had not complied with the terms of a [[Biological Opinion]].

## Background & Description

**Species profile.** The red shiner is a small-bodied (about 2–3 inches), short-lived member of the family Cyprinidae. It is native to the western portion of the Mississippi basin and tolerant of harsh conditions, including high turbidity and high water temperatures. The declarant notes it does well in degraded conditions, which explains its success in Arizona. Although the species is aggressive and colonizing, the declarant considers it unlikely that individual fish could travel long distances on their own. Upstream migration of roughly 14 miles from the Pinal Feeder Canal outlet to Aravaipa Creek would therefore take several years under favorable conditions, not a single movement.

**Litigation context.** The case began as a consolidated challenge by the [[Central Arizona Water Conservation District]] (CAWCD) and others to a 1994 [[Biological Opinion]] issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on the transportation and delivery of CAP water. The plaintiffs argued that the BOR had failed to implement the reasonable and prudent alternatives required by that opinion. Key federal defendants include the Department of the Interior and the Bureau of Reclamation. The filing also refers to the [[Center for Biological Diversity]] as a plaintiff.

**Declarant's qualifications.** Clarkson describes himself as a fisheries biologist with the BOR's Phoenix Area Office and states that his duties include monitoring and related work on BOR projects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pathway for introduction.** Red shiners were first documented in the Gila system in the Gila River, which was connected to CAP infrastructure through the Pinal Feeder Canal and the [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]]. The CAP began delivering water through the Florence-Casa Grande Canal in April 1990. Delivery records show intermittent deliveries through 1990–1991, with a gap until 1997 and further deliveries from September 1997 onward.

**Historical presence.** Red shiners were captured in the [[Gila River]] above and below [[Coolidge Dam]] during 1977–1978 sampling, where they made up a reported share of the total catch. The species was apparently introduced into [[San Carlos Reservoir]] before 1990 and then moved downstream into the Gila River. Clarkson concludes that red shiners in the San Carlos reservoir were able to colonize the Gila River downstream as early as the 1970s.

**Native fishes at risk.** Aravaipa Creek supports native fishes, including [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], both listed under the Endangered Species Act. Relevant concerns include competition and predation by nonnative species, which the declarant describes as possibly reducing or eliminating native populations.

**Sampling variability.** Clarkson cautions that catch numbers are highly variable and depend on flow conditions (such as floods and drought), sampling effort, and sampler bias. Counts therefore do not show population trends on their own.

## Key Findings

- **First capture in Aravaipa Creek.** The first recorded capture of a red shiner in Aravaipa Creek occurred in September 1990, when a single specimen was taken during sampling of the lower creek by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD). A standardized survey in October 1990 captured 27 individuals (OCR reading), a count the declarant notes was one of several hundred native fish sampled.
- **Gila River trends.** Red shiner captures on the lower [[Gila River]] and [[Lower San Pedro River]] were reported across multiple years from 1977 to 2000, with numbers varying widely. Counts on the lower San Pedro declined from 99 in 1996 and 60 in 1997 to 52 in 1999 and zero in 2000.
- **Dispersal and timing.** Clarkson estimates that a red shiner could reach Aravaipa Creek from the Pinal Feeder Canal over several years, and that the species is unlikely to move that distance rapidly on its own. He finds a more immediate pathway through CAP water deliveries.
- **Opinion on incursion probability.** In his opinion, the combination of CAP water deliveries, the species' colonizing ability, and the existing presence of red shiners in the Gila basin makes it highly probable that the CAP project is the source of red shiners entering Aravaipa Creek. He describes this probability as "high, if not certain."
- **Predicted impacts.** Clarkson states that introduction of red shiners would likely lead to competition and predation effects on native species, with the potential for further spread into other native habitats. He notes that the Bureau's own analysis recognized these risks.
- **Court's conclusion.** The court's order, as reproduced in the OCR text, grants the plaintiffs' motion for summary judgment and denies the federal defendants' cross-motion. It finds that the Project is likely to adversely affect listed fishes and that BOR failed to comply with the Biological Opinion's requirements, which the court treats as a violation of the Endangered Species Act.
