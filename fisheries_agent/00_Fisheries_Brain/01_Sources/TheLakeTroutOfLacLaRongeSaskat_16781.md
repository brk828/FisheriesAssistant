# The Lake Trout of Lac la Ronge, Saskatchewan (1948–1960 Study)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada* paper (D. S. Rawson, University of Saskatchewan) reports a ten-year study (1948–1959, with creel data to 1960) of [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) in [[Lac la Ronge]], a large, moderately productive lake on the southern edge of the [[Precambrian Shield]]. Methods combined [[Gill Netting]], [[Tagging and Recovery]], spawn-taking, scale-based [[Age and Growth]] analysis, stomach analysis, and a full-time [[Creel Census]]. The study concludes that the trout population is thriving and that the lake could support an angling catch at least double the 1960 level of about 51,000 lb per year.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), a highly regarded game fish and, after [[Whitefish]], the second most valuable commercial species in the lake.
- **Distribution:** Widespread across northern North America; in Saskatchewan it occurs mainly north of latitude 55°. Lac la Ronge lies near the southern edge of its typical range. The author finds no close relative in Siberia and notes that the species shows little justification for subspecies, apart from the [[Siscowet]] of Lake Superior.
- **Morphology and colour:** Fish are blue-grey to blue-black, with light spots covering 3–7 scales and red-tinted paired and anal fins. Head length averages one-fifth of fork length, compared with one-quarter in nearby MacKay Lake.
- **Flesh colour:** Of 337 trout sampled by anglers (June–September 1960), 83% were pale (creamy white or pale yellow) and 17% bright (yellow-orange or bright orange). A colour-standard scale was built from [[Munsell Colour System]] matches.
- **Study lake:** [[Lac la Ronge]] covers about 550 sq mi (1425 km²) in the original text, while the Summary gives 500 sq mi. Its main basin is mesotrophic and [[Hunter Bay]] (about 50 sq mi) is oligotrophic.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and temperature.** Trout move with water temperature. Spawning occurs at 10–11°C, and trout avoid water warmer than 10–12°C. In July and August, when thermal stratification sets in, they concentrate below 20 m. Only 1.35% of 519 trout were caught in water warmer than 11°C. Ferguson (1958) gave 12°C as the upper preferred limit for the species.

**Lake structure.** About 85% of the main lake is shallower than 20 m, while nearly half of Hunter Bay is deeper than 20 m. Trout therefore concentrate in deep "holes" in the main lake during summer.

**Diet and competition.** Ciscoes, whitefish and ninespine sticklebacks dominate adult diets. [[Burbot]] is the main competitor in the main lake, where it outnumbers trout. [[Walleye]] and [[Northern Pike]] also feed on trout prey.

**Parasites.** Tapeworms (*Eubothrium* and *Proteocephalus*) occur in the gut. Larval *Triaenophorus crassus* cysts occur in the flesh. Nematodes (*Cystidicola stigmatura* in swim bladders) and the external copepod *Salmincola siscowet* were also recorded.

**Fishery context.** Commercial netting took a large share of trout until management shifted effort toward [[Whitefish]]. Angling grew rapidly after a road opened in 1947.

## Key Findings

**Life history and spawning**
- Spawning occurs in the first week of October on shallow rocky reefs (1–3 m), at about 10–11°C, with the run beginning around 25 September.
- Sexual maturity ranges from 5 to 8–9 years. Almost all fish are mature at 8. Length is a closer predictor of maturity than age.
- Among 228 females examined, 8% were non-spawners, with undersized eggs (1.6–2.5 mm). About 10% of mature trout are estimated to skip spawning in any given year.
- The sex ratio of the spawning run averaged 1 female : 0.7 male.
- Egg diameter grew from 2.8 mm in late June to 6.1 mm at spawning.

**Growth**
- A 10-year-old trout averages 26 in (66 cm) fork length and 8 lb (3.6 kg).
- Growth was identical for both sexes. Growth in the main lake was faster for the first 5 years. Trout in [[Hunter Bay]] then grew faster and caught up by about age 12–13.
- Main-lake fish were slightly larger in 1958 than in 1948–49, with the increase about 3% at each age.
- Trout over 15 years old are 25–50% heavier than Great Slave Lake trout of the same length.
- Only 33 trout weighing 30–41 lb were recorded between 1949 and 1960. The largest scale-aged fish was 40.5 in (101 cm), weighing 34.5 lb, at about 23 years.

**Diet**
- Of 511 stomachs, 257 were empty. Ciscoes, whitefish and other fish made up 90% of adult trout food. Invertebrates (mainly *Mysis relicta*) were important for young trout, especially in their third and fourth years.
- Estimated volume shares for large trout: ciscoes about 50%, whitefish about 15%, other fish about 30%, and invertebrates under 5%.

**Distribution and movement**
- Trout were widespread from ice-out in mid-May to late June. They then moved below 20 m for July–August and returned to shallow water in mid-September.
- Of 429 trout tagged with Petersen tags, 60 (14%) were recovered. Distances were up to 22 miles (35 km), with an average of 8.8 miles for 29 trout.
- About one-twelfth of recaptures crossed between the main lake and Hunter Bay, suggesting moderate exchange. About one-third of the population may wander.
- The data suggest "homing" to spawning grounds: 25 of 278 spawn-tagged trout returned to the same reef in later years.

**Population and catch**
- Standard gill-net catches showed no change in average trout length (about 23.1 in across periods) or year-class composition over the decade.
- Trout catch per set increased from 1.9 to 4.4 in the main lake and from 4.6 to 7.9 in Hunter Bay (1948–49 vs. 1958–59). Authors suggest availability to nets increased.
- Piscivores made up 8% of the catch by number and 25% by weight in the main lake, and 12% and 36% in Hunter Bay. Trout alone made up 11.5% of catch weight in the main lake and 26% in Hunter Bay.

**Fishery management and harvest**
- Commercial catch averaged about 250,000 lb per year (1950–1960), with trout held to 8,600 lb per year (3.5%), down from about 113,500 lb per year in the earlier 29-year period.
- Anglers took about 43,000 lb of trout per year (1950–1960), averaging 5,800 fish. Average catch per angler was about 36 lb in 1956–1959 and rose to 52.6 lb in 1960.
- Restrictions in 1951–52 (daily limits and a 25 lb cap) reduced the catch per angler. The 15-inch size limit was removed in 1958.
- The author concludes that Lac la Ronge could support at least twice the 1960 angling harvest of trout, which would be about 51,000 lb per year.

**Recommendations and conclusions**
- The trout population is judged to be thriving. Current angling regulations are considered satisfactory for conservation.
- Whitefish fishing can be managed for commercial yield with little incidental trout catch, particularly in summer.
- Burbot competition in the main lake may limit trout production, and the author flags it as an area for future attention.
