# Potential for Hybridization Among Gila Spp. Following Bonytail Reintroduction in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado River Recovery Program annual project report (FY99) describes a study of the risk that native [[Gila Species]] will hybridize following the planned reintroduction of [[Bonytail]] in the Upper Colorado River Basin. The principal investigator is J. Michael Hudson of the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife Resources]]. The study assesses hybrid viability by comparing laboratory-produced hybrid crosses with pure crosses. The project was delayed and, as of this report, had 10% of FY99 work completed and no funds expended. Its first year is now FY2000, and it is scheduled to run through FY2005.

## Background & Description

The reintroduction plan for bonytail in the Upper Colorado River identified a potential impact from hybridization with [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]. The plan called for evaluating this risk through two approaches:

1. Genetic monitoring of existing Gila populations in the basin, to determine whether hybridization was occurring.
2. Artificial crosses to test the viability of laboratory-produced hybrid crosses relative to laboratory-produced pure crosses.

The working hypothesis is that hybridization is common in fishes, particularly among species that do not provide parental care, fertilize eggs externally, and have limited habitat relative to what was historically available. Hybrids between Gila species have been observed in the Colorado River. The report states that if hybrids (F1 and F2 generations and backcrosses) have significantly lower relative fitness than any pure Gila cross, hybridization among Gila is unlikely to increase significantly after pure Gila are reintroduced.

The overall goal is to determine the potential risk of Gila hybridization following bonytail reintroduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study concerns native Colorado River fishes that are the subject of a federal recovery effort. The main ecological concern is genetic: hybridization could dilute the genetic integrity of the native Gila species. The report identifies two conditions that favor hybridization in fishes: external fertilization and limited available habitat. Historically, these species had access to a larger range than they do now.

The project's timeline was also shaped by operational constraints:

- **Disease control:** The project was held up by a disease control protocol being established with the Utah Department of Agriculture for interstate transfer of fish.
- **Facility construction:** Construction delays limited the available space for holding fish.
- **Holding of adults:** Adult humpback chub, roundtail chub, and bonytail are being held at the facility until the hatchery building, funded through CIP-2 funds, is complete.

## Key Findings

**Objectives**
- Quantify the viability of laboratory-produced hybrid Gila crosses relative to laboratory-produced pure Gila crosses.

**Working hypothesis**
- If hybrid crosses have significantly lower relative fitness than any pure Gila cross, hybridization among Gila is unlikely to increase significantly following reintroduction of pure Gila.
