# Copepoda Parasitic on Australian Fishes, XI. Impexus hamondi New Genus, New Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Lernaeoceridae #parasitic-copepod #taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper describes *Impexus hamondi*, a new genus and species of parasitic copepod from the family [[Lernaeoceridae]], collected from the visceral cavity of [[Parvicrepis parvipinnis]] at [[Long Reef]], [[Collaroy]], [[New South Wales]], [[Australia]]. The specimen represents only the third record of lernaeocerid copepods from Australian waters. Despite being based on a single, damaged specimen, the parasite exhibits distinctive morphological features warranting establishment of a new genus. The author provides a comprehensive key to all known genera of [[Lernaeoceridae]] based on body shape, holdfast structure, and egg-sac morphology.

## Background & Description

The family [[Lernaeoceridae]] comprises parasitic copepods commonly found on fishes worldwide but conspicuously scarce in Australian waters. Prior to this description, only two lernaeocerid species had been recorded from Australia: *Lernaeenicus hemirhamphi* from the eye of *Hemirhamphus intermedius* in [[St. Vincent Gulf]], [[South Australia]], and *Lernaeolophus sultanus* from the lower lip of *Tetraodon* sp. off [[Lord Howe Island]].

The holotype specimen (NMC 15315, deposited in the [[National Museum of Natural Sciences]], [[Ottawa]]) was collected by Dr. Richard Hamond on 25 August 1968 from the low water mark in the surf zone at [[Long Reef]]. The parasite was attached to the left flank of a small host fish (19.4 mm long), with the anterior end of its trunk partially covered by the host's pelvic fin. Dissection proved extremely difficult due to deep embedding in the visceral cavity, resulting in severing of the trunk from the anterior part at the body wall penetration point.

### Morphological Description

**Cephalothorax**: The distinctive feature is three pairs of ovoid tubercles. Four tubercles of similar size are arranged at the same level: one pair dorsolateral and one pair ventrolateral. A pair of thoracopods is located immediately distal to the ventrolateral tubercles. A short, cylindrical, necklike tube connects these tubercles with the third (anterior) pair. Anterior to these tubercles, the cephalothorax breaks into numerous irregularly branching processes forming a formidable holdfast, covered and bound by host connective tissue capsule. The mouth and buccal appendages are present on the ventral surface at the level of the anterior tubercles, densely surrounded by holdfast branches.

**Neck and Trunk Connection**: The four posterior tubercles and trunk are linked by a short cylindrical neck, approximately as long as the neck linking anterior and posterior tubercles but half its diameter. The neck position is subterminal, oriented at nearly right angles to the trunk's long axis.

**Trunk**: Oval in dorsal aspect with convex dorsum and almost flat ventral surface. The posterior extremity is rounded and protrudes slightly beyond egg-sac attachment points. No caudal furca observed. No processes or outgrowths present on trunk.

**Egg-sacs**: Tightly coiled, with seven coils in the examined specimen. Left sac coils clockwise, right sac anticlockwise.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The host species [[Parvicrepis parvipinnis]] (Waite, 1906) is a small fish inhabiting the surf zone at low water mark. The parasite's deep embedding in the visceral cavity with trunk protruding from the body wall represents a highly invasive parasitic strategy. The small size of the host (19.4 mm) relative to the parasite dimensions (cephalothorax and trunk each 1.50 mm) indicates substantial parasitic impact.

The scarcity of lernaeocerid records from Australian waters contrasts sharply with their common occurrence in other world oceans, suggesting either genuine biogeographic rarity or undersampling of this parasitic group in the region.

## Key Findings

### Taxonomic Diagnosis

Generic affinity of lernaeocerid species is determined by three primary characters: (1) body shape (sigmoid versus cylindrical trunk), (2) presence or absence of profusely branching holdfast, and (3) egg-sac nature. Three egg-sac types exist in [[Lernaeoceridae]]: simple long straight sacs (e.g., *Lernaeenicus*), sacs looped irregularly around central axial rod (e.g., *Lernaeocera*), and coiled sacs (e.g., *Haemobaphes*).

### Generic Distinction

*Impexus* is distinguished from all other lernaeocerid genera by the combination of:
- Body ovoid with flat ventral and convex dorsal surfaces
- Neck at an angle with trunk, subterminal position
- Spirally coiled egg-sacs
- Profusely branching holdfast constituting the most anterior body part (preoral segments)

The genus most closely resembles [[Phrixocephalus]] and [[Creopelates]]. *Impexus* differs from *Phrixocephalus* in that the holdfast constitutes the most anterior body part (preoral segments) rather than being posterior to cephalic lobes (postoral segments). It differs from *Creopelates* in holdfast distribution: *Creopelates* has short bushy outgrowths around the neck, while *Impexus* has irregular branching processes at the anterior cephalothorax.

### Taxonomic Key

The author provides a comprehensive dichotomous key to 15 genera of [[Lernaeoceridae]] (excluding *Thanatodectes*, known only from a decapitated specimen). Key diagnostic features include:
- Body shape (sigmoid vs. non-sigmoid)
- Egg-sac morphology (coiled, axial rod, or straight)
- Presence/absence of brushlike outgrowths
- Neck characteristics (length, position, ornamentation)
- Thoracopod structure (biramous vs. uniramous)
- Cephalothorax morphology

### Taxonomic Complication

*Cardiodectes rotundicaudatus* (Izawa, 1970) represents the only known exception to the rule that members of the same lernaeocerid genus always have the same egg-sac type. This species possesses coiled egg-sacs (loose coils with large diameter) unlike other *Cardiodectes* species. *Impexus hamondi* is distinguished from all *Cardiodectes* species by trunk shape (ovoid vs. always cylindrical) and neck position (subterminal vs. terminal).

### Methodological Challenges

Establishment of the new taxon on a single damaged specimen was undertaken due to clear morphological distinctiveness from all known genera. The small size (host only 19.4 mm) and fragility made description of appendages impossible. Diagnosis was based entirely on distinctive gross morphology. The profusely branching holdfast, bound by host connective tissue capsule, proved virtually impossible to dissect without destruction.
