# The Superficial Waters of the Lake Kinneret Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #hydrology #freshwater-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This chapter details the surface hydrology feeding [[Lake Kinneret]] (Sea of Galilee), focusing on the [[Jordan River]] system, secondary wadis, and freshwater/thermal springs of the surrounding watershed. While not a fish-specific study, the data presented—river discharge regimes, spring locations, and comparative water chemistry—establishes the critical abiotic context (flow variability, ionic composition, thermal inputs) that governs habitat conditions for aquatic life in the lake and its tributaries. Key hydrological engineering (the 1957 Hula drainage) and long-term discharge records (1959–1975) are presented alongside a catalog of ~30 springs contributing to the lake's water budget and chemistry.

## Background & Description

The **[[Jordan River]]** is the principal inlet to [[Lake Kinneret]], formed by the confluence of three headwater rivers:
- **Snir (Hatsbani River)**
- **Dan River**
- **Hermon River (Banias River)**

Historically, the Jordan River discharged into the swampy **[[Lake Hula]]** basin before reaching Kinneret. In **1957**, artificial drainage of the Hula plain was completed, and the river was channelized into western and eastern canals that merge into a single canal terminating at the **Pardes Huri gauging station**.

Secondary surface inflows include:
- **[[Wadi Amud]]** and **[[Wadi Tsalmon]]** — drain the western watershed; completely dry in summer (ephemeral/intermittent flow regime).
- **[[Meshushim River]]** — drains the central Golan Heights (eastern watershed); average yield generally <1 m³/sec, but capable of extreme flash-flood peaks.

An extensive network of **freshwater and thermal springs** (Table 12; 30 springs cataloged) supplements lake inflow, arising from tectonic disruption of water-bearing strata in the region. Spring elevations range from -192 m (Nun spring) to +790 m (Hushnie spring), with discharges spanning from ~1 to 300 L/sec and chloride concentrations ranging from 18 mg/l up to 532 mg/l (Magela spring), reflecting the geologically diverse, fault-influenced hydrology of the Jordan Rift Valley.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** The Jordan River exhibits a **flood-type regime** with low base flow (a few m³/sec) and highly variable monthly and interannual discharge. This variability (documented 1959–1975) has direct implications for downstream habitat stability, nutrient/sediment loading, and lake water-level fluctuations affecting littoral and inflow-dependent fish communities.
- **Extreme discharge events:** Maximum recorded Jordan River discharge was **214 m³/sec (23 January 1969)**; minimum was **0.80 m³/sec (10 July 1973)** — an over 250-fold range, indicative of a flashy Mediterranean-climate hydrograph with strong winter/spring peaks (February–April) and summer lows (July–September).
- **Water chemistry gradients:** Comparative ionic composition data (Fig. 34) show distinct chemical signatures among the **Kinneret lake water, Jordan River water, and Hot Springs water**, with differing dominance patterns across Ca, Mg, K, Na, Cl, SO₄, and HCO₃. Hot/saline springs (e.g., Sahina, Magela, Balzam — all with high chloride, 72–532 mg/l) likely contribute significant salinity and mineral loading to the lake, a factor relevant to osmoregulatory conditions for resident fish fauna.
- **Thermal/spring inputs:** Springs at negative elevations (e.g., Ravid, Nun, Sahina, Magela, Balzam, all below -100 m) suggest deep groundwater or hydrothermal sources, potentially creating localized thermal refugia or chemically distinct microhabitats near the lake shoreline.
- **Anthropogenic modification:** The 1957 Hula drainage project fundamentally altered the natural flow path and wetland filtering function previously provided by Lake Hula, with likely downstream consequences for nutrient delivery, turbidity, and habitat connectivity into Lake Kinneret — an important consideration for any assessment of the lake's fish and invertebrate communities.

## Key Findings

- **Jordan River average yields** (Pardes Huri station, 1959/60–1970/71): ranged from **7.9 m³/sec (August)** to **29.6 m³/sec (February)** (Table 11).
- **Absolute extremes:** Max discharge 214 m³/sec (Jan 1969); Min discharge 0.80 m³/sec (Jul 1973).
- **Meshushim River:** average yield <1 m³/sec; recorded peak of **115 m³/sec (4 April 1971)**, illustrating extreme flash-flood potential in the eastern Golan watershed.
- **Spring inventory (Table 12):** 30 springs cataloged with elevation, discharge, and chloride concentration; discharge ranges from ~1 L/sec to **300 L/sec (Sahina spring)**; chloride ranges from **18 mg/l (Mantsura)** to **532 mg/l (Magela)**.
- **Water chemistry (Fig. 34):** Kinneret, Jordan River, and Hot Springs waters show distinguishable ionic signatures across Ca, Mg, K, Na, Cl, SO₄, HCO₃ — underscoring the mixed hydrochemical inputs shaping lake water quality.
- **Historical hydrological engineering:** The 1957 drainage of the Hula plain rerouted the Jordan River through constructed canals, permanently altering the natural inflow pathway to [[Lake Kinneret]].
