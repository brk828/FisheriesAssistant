# Pesticide Residues in Fish and Aquatic Organisms of Agricultural Drains

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecotoxicology

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game administrative report examines pesticide contamination of fish and other aquatic organisms in agricultural drains of the [[Imperial Valley]], [[Coachella Valley]], and [[Palo Verde Valley]]. The study combined two approaches: residue analysis of organisms collected from 14 sites (February 15 to March 17) and [[Live Car Bioassay]] experiments that exposed caged fish to irrigation runoff. Organochlorine residues ([[DDT]] and its breakdown products DDD and DDE, plus [[Dieldrin]]) were found in tissue, and several samples exceeded federal and state limits. Bioassays showed toxic runoff from [[Kelthane]] and [[Atrazine]] use, inconclusive results for [[Lannate]], and no detectable residues from [[Phosdrin]]. The author concludes that past and present pesticide management is largely inadequate for protecting non-target aquatic life and recommends better practices, especially for kelthane and atrazine.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared by the Department of Fish and Game (Region 5, Environmental Services Branch) and funded by the Colorado River Regional Water Quality Control Board through an interagency agreement. It follows an earlier DFG survey (Herngesell, 1975) that identified the drains as possible habitat for increased biological productivity and a valuable sport fishery if managed properly.

The three irrigation districts together operate more than 1,500 miles of open drains (the figure is partly garbled in the scan). Agricultural runoff, aerial drift, and surface runoff were tentatively identified as the main sources of pesticides entering these drains. U.S. Geological Survey monitoring had already found pesticide concentrations above the EPA Maximum Acceptable Toxicant Concentrations in many drains, with parathion, malathion, and diazinon especially common.

The study had two parts:

1. **Residue occurrence:** Aquatic organisms were collected by electrofishing, gill netting, seining, hook and line, and hand collection. Samples were analyzed for DDD, DDE, DDT, and dieldrin by the California Department of Food and Agriculture. Individual fish of a species were pooled when small, and sites were checked against Imperial County Agricultural Commissioner application records.
2. **Live car bioassays:** Cages made of 3/4-inch wire mesh over wood frames held about 10 fish each (average weight about 1 lb, from a DFG warmwater hatchery with no prior pesticide exposure). Three cages were placed per experiment: one in the headwater canal, one above the tailwater effluent, and one below it. Cages were removed after tailwater runoff ceased or after 96 hours, and the fish were tested for toxicity daily. Livers from 10 fish per group were composited, giving four composite samples per experiment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report notes that drain biota are affected by several pressures:

- **Pesticide persistence:** Organochlorines are highly persistent and bioconcentrate. Residues can remain in fish long after use has stopped, and drains can keep releasing them through delayed flushing from groundwater or from contaminated sediment.
- **Sediment interaction:** Mechanical dredging stirs sediment and resuspends toxicants applied years earlier. Pesticides bind strongly to detritus, so the largest reservoirs of residue are sediment and detritus.
- **Feeding ecology:** Residue levels depended on feeding habits. Carp, which are detritivores, had the highest levels, while tilapia, which feed mainly on aquatic plants, had the lowest.
- **Community shifts:** Repeated toxic or sublethal runoff can shift fish communities. Species with large populations and high reproductive rates, such as mosquitofish and mollies, dominated drains with fluctuating toxicity. These species can recolonize quickly after a fish kill, which may explain the scarcity of game fish in parts of some drains.
- **Invertebrates:** Macroinvertebrate populations were reported as sharply reduced by pesticides, though they recovered through immigration and rapid reproduction.

The report also summarizes a related literature review on Sonoran Desert streams. Flooding is described as a natural barrier against the invasion of non-native fish, which are considered a major threat to native desert species. Flood regimes are altered by impoundment and water diversion, and the resulting changes in flow, turbidity, and temperature harm native fishes and their habitat. The authors argue that variable-rate discharges must be considered in any management plan. Shading and turbidity during floods also affect primary production, and longfin dace may have life-cycle triggers linked to flooding.

## Key Findings

**Residue occurrence (organisms from 14 sites in three valleys)**

- Measurable amounts of pesticide residue were found in all tissue samples collected in agricultural supply and drain waters.
- The presence of organochlorines in fish tissue indicates that some non-persistent pesticides may also enter the physiological system, though chronic effects in fish are not yet well understood.
- EPA standards for the protection of fish predators were exceeded in six fish samples from the Coachella and Imperial valleys. For DDT (including DDE), the standard was 5.0 ppm. Its limit for dieldrin and other cyclodiene insecticides was 0.1 ppm, and total DDT in one carp sample was reported over five times the acceptable level.
- DDT levels in some fish muscle samples exceeded the level considered safe for human consumption. The Department of Health, Food and Drug Administration recommends that fish not be eaten when total DDT exceeds 5.0 ppm. One carp sample from Avenue 24 Drain (Coachella Valley) and the Alamo River exceeded this level.
- Dieldrin was detected above the standard in one channel catfish from the Trifolium area and in one group of mollies from the Holtville Drain (Imperial Valley). The author warns that these values could indicate a hazard to predators such as bass and birds.
- Since none of the organisms collected were targets of organochlorine pesticides, the author concludes that past management and application practices were inadequate.
- Residues were found in the Palo Verde Main Canal and Palo Verde Outfall Drain, but they were lower overall than at other sites, although organisms at all sites contained significant levels.

**Live car bioassays**

- **[[Kelthane]]:** The results provide conclusive evidence that kelthane-contaminated irrigation runoff was toxic. Fish in cages exposed to runoff from kelthane-treated fields had residues of 0.053 and 0.16 ppm in liver. All experimental fish died within the exposure period. Dead mollies from the drain contained 0.66 ppm kelthane. Management practices for kelthane are judged inadequate.
- **[[Atrazine]]:** Atrazine was applied at 1.5 (units garbled in scan, likely lb/acre) to a field of silo corn. Fish exposed to return flows had liver residues of 0.010 ppm (detection limit 0.001 ppm). Three fish died and others were severely stressed. The author notes that more deaths were likely had the test run the full 96 hours. Management practices for atrazine appear inadequate.
- **[[Lannate]]:** Results were inconclusive. Hatchery control fish already contained lannate, indicating contamination before testing, and the source was not identified (possibly supply water, aerial drift, or contaminated food).
- **[[Phosdrin]]:** No residues were detected in fish from four field applications on alfalfa, corn, and melons, and no fish showed toxic reactions. Current practices appear adequate, and further phosdrin bioassays are unnecessary.

**Recommendations and conclusions**

- Management procedures for some toxicants are inadequate for protecting non-target organisms. Pesticides can kill fish outright or cause sublethal effects. Sublethal exposure can still reduce fish populations by inhibiting spawning, increasing fry mortality, and reducing growth and fecundity.
- Pesticide management practices for kelthane and atrazine need improvement. Practices for phosdrin appear adequate.
- Special precautions and management techniques are needed for mechanical dredging in canals and drains.
- The bioassay method is a valid way to test the suitability of pesticide management and should be expanded and continued in all three valleys.
- Only some of the 15 planned bioassay experiments could be completed. Limitations included finances, manpower, and the use of single field conditions for kelthane and atrazine. Caged fish also probably underestimate residue uptake by resident fish, since resident fish are exposed through food and sediment as well as water. Interactions with other pesticides in the drains could not be measured.
