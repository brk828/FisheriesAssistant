# Declaration of John Hamill - Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #endangered-species #habitat-management

## Executive Summary

This legal declaration by John Hamill, Director of the Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], describes a comprehensive cooperative effort initiated in January 1988 to recover four endangered fish species, including the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The program involves federal agencies, state governments (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), and environmental organizations, with a budget of approximately $60 million over 15 years. The declaration was submitted in Civil Action No. 92-F-884 (Colorado Wildlife Federation v. John Turner) and argues that critical habitat designation is not necessary for recovery efforts to succeed, as the Implementation Program already provides detailed action plans for species recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a member of the family [[Catostomidae]] native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. In 1988, the species was a candidate for listing under the Endangered Species Act but was included in the Implementation Program to manage it proactively and prevent the need for formal protection. The species exhibits severely declining numbers of adult fish and lacks natural reproduction, making it the highest priority for artificial propagation efforts.

The Implementation Program encompasses the upper [[Colorado River]] basin, excluding the [[San Juan River]]. Recovery plans have been developed for [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]] as required by Section 4 of the Endangered Species Act. A recovery plan for the [[Razorback Sucker]] was under development at the time of this declaration, with recovery actions being identified by fisheries biologists knowledgeable in native fishes based on best available data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Flooded Bottomlands]] are considered critical habitat for [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery because they provide abundant food (zooplankton) and protection from predators and competitors. The species historically used the 15-Mile Reach of the [[Colorado River]] above the confluence of the [[Gunnison River]]. Natural flow regimes, particularly in the [[Yampa River]], are considered critical to survival and recovery of razorbacks and other endangered fishes in the [[Green River]] basin.

Nonnative fishes pose significant threats through competition and predation. The program addresses this through refined reservoir operations designed to create unfavorable conditions for nonnative fish survival and reproduction while improving habitat for endangered species.

## Key Findings

**Program Structure and Funding**: The Implementation Program contains five principal elements: (1) habitat management (provision of instream flow); (2) habitat development and maintenance; (3) stocking of native fishes; (4) nonnative species and sport fishing management; and (5) research, monitoring, and data management. For fiscal year 1993, 12 projects totaling $695,400 related to [[Razorback Sucker]] were included in the work plan.

**Habitat Management Activities**: [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] (Green River, Utah and Wyoming) is being re-operated with higher spring water releases to increase flooding frequency of bottomland habitats. The dams of the [[Aspinall Unit]] (Gunnison River) are being re-operated to provide research flows and higher spring flows. The [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] is filing for a 581 cubic feet per second instream flow right in the 15-Mile Reach. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] is releasing 20,000 acre-feet of water from [[Ruedi Reservoir]], Colorado, to enhance flows in the 15-Mile Reach. A proposal to acquire the Juniper-Cross Mountain water rights on the [[Yampa River]] for instream flows is under evaluation.

**Habitat Development**: The program is pursuing restoration of fish passage to the [[Gunnison River]] and upper mainstem [[Colorado River]] near Grand Junction, Colorado, by constructing fish ladders on diversion dams built in the early 1900s. These measures will provide razorbacks with unrestricted passage to over 100 miles of historical habitat. A project near Jensen, Utah, is restoring access to flooded bottomlands on the [[Green River]]. Flooded bottomlands will be inventoried throughout the basin in 1993 and 1994, with acquisition, restoration, and management potentially beginning in 1994.

**Artificial Propagation Program**: The [[Razorback Sucker]] is the highest priority species for artificial propagation due to severely declining adult numbers and lack of natural reproduction. Objectives include producing sufficient hatchery-reared razorbacks to support research and recovery activities and conserving genetic diversity present in wild populations. Genetic surveys are underway to determine genetic composition of wild razorbacks, with analysis completion expected in 1994. Captive or refuge populations have been established for wild razorbacks from the upper [[Colorado River]], upper [[Green River]], and [[Lake Powell]]. An imprinting and chemoreception study is being conducted to determine how razorbacks locate spawning areas. A habitat suitability assessment is being conducted in unoccupied reaches of the [[Colorado River]] and [[Gunnison River]] to determine desirability of reintroduction.

**Research and Monitoring**: Studies are underway to determine habitat and flow needs of razorbacks in the Green, Colorado, and Gunnison Rivers. A long-term monitoring program has been established to assess status and trends in razorback populations and channel change and sedimentation that may affect razorback habitats. An interagency data management program has been established.

**Nonnative Species Management**: Federal reservoir operations are being refined to improve habitat conditions for endangered fish while providing unfavorable conditions for nonnative fish survival and reproduction. An information and education effort has been developed and implemented to minimize mortality by angling.
