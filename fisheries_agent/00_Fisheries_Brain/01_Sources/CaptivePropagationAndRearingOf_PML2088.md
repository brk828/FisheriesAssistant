# Captive Propagation and Rearing of Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) — Final Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 2001–2003 final report, prepared by [[Heather L. Parmeter]] and [[Steven P. Platania]] for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Phoenix Area Office) under Federal Grants 01-FG-32-0030 and 03-FG-32-0020, aimed to develop laboratory propagation techniques for [[Spikedace]] and to advise on adapting them to hatchery production. Wild adults were collected from the [[Gila River]], New Mexico, treated for parasites and disease, and used in spawning trials. Age 2 fish were the principal cohort.

Key outcomes were: a health management protocol for [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] (white spot disease) and a transport protocol, both judged essential for future propagation; at least 30 documented spawning events from February through July; failure of hormone injections to induce spawning; and spawning under environmental manipulation (photoperiod and temperature), although control aquaria spawned as readily, so the effect remains inconclusive. Laboratory-spawned progeny reached about 30–45 mm SL roughly one year after hatching.

## Background & Description

Spikedace belong to the Plagopterini ("spine-fins"), a tribe of six species in three genera endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and adjacent drainages in Utah, Nevada, Arizona and New Mexico. *Meda fulgida* is a laterally compressed, obligate stream cyprinid reaching about 70 mm total length. Its scales are reduced and deeply embedded, and the eyes are relatively large. Breeding males develop yellow-to-brassy coloration at the fin bases and on the flanks, and both sexes are tuberculate during the breeding season.

Historically widespread in the Gila River system of southwestern New Mexico and central and southeastern Arizona, spikedace now persist in only a few reaches, including the Verde River, Eagle Creek and Aravaipa Creek in Arizona, and in the East and West Forks of the Gila River in New Mexico. The species is listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and is a species of special concern in Arizona. Declines are attributed to habitat loss, altered flow regimes and interactions with [[Nonnative Aquatic Species]]. Captive propagation and repatriation were pursued as one management strategy. Prior to this work, no reliable rearing or propagation techniques existed for the species.

The report also reviews the closely related [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] and [[Woundfin]], whose captive reproduction records informed the approach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Adults frequent shallow riffles and runs of moderate depth. Larvae and juveniles are common in quiet, shallow water with aquatic vegetation.
- **Diet:** Adults feed mainly on ephemeropteran (mayfly) nymphs. Juveniles prey largely on dipterans.
- **Reproductive timing:** Mid-spring to mid-summer, depending on runoff magnitude, duration, temperature and velocity. Gravid females move from pools to riffles, and paired spawning is rare.
- **Life span:** Reported to age 4, though age 1 and age 2 fish dominated sampled populations.
- **Hydrology:** The 2001–2003 period was a severe regional drought. Mean spring discharge (1 March–31 May) at the Redrocks gauge was 229 cfs in 2001, 141 cfs in 2003 and 52 cfs in 2002. Maximum mean daily spring flows were 415 cfs (2001), 327 cfs (2003) and 74 cfs (2002).
- **Collection site:** Gila River, Grant County, New Mexico, within the Gila National Forest. Water temperature, pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen and nitrogen compounds were recorded at each collection (Table 2).

## Key Findings

**Collection and transport**
- Six wild collections were made from 2001 to 2003, yielding almost 1,000 spikedace, of which about 625 were retained.
- No mortality occurred during transport from the field to holding facilities.
- Transport used polyethylene bags with river water, sodium chloride (0.5% ppt), a water conditioner and oxygen, carried in ice chests. The temperature was brought to 16–18 °C.

**Parasite and disease control**
- Wild spikedace were highly susceptible to *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*. In November 2001, 54% (n=106) of Population III was lost, mostly between 6 and 9 November 2001.
- A treatment protocol combining salt, formalin and water changes was developed. It targets free-swimming theronts, since encysted trophonts are unaffected by chemical treatment. Acriflavine was tested as an alternative.

**Spawning and hormone trials**
- At least 30 spawning events were documented, with reproductive activity from February through July.
- About 10 hormone-injection attempts were made, using carp pituitary extract (CPE), salmon pituitary extract, LH-RH analogue and human chorionic gonadotrophin. None produced clear spawning. One CPE-injected aquarium (n=12) spawned, but uninjected fish in another tank spawned at about the same time, so the result is not conclusive.
- Environmental manipulation (temperature, 14-h photoperiod, feeding, substrate and density) produced spawning in four of seven aquaria in 2002, including the control. In 2003, spawning occurred in 12 aquaria across four populations. No consistent environmental driver was identified.
- Gravel substrate was not preferred over cobble or plant structure, and "substrate was not a necessary component" was concluded from spawning observations.

**Reproductive biology**
- Spikedace are communal, broadcast spawners with small (about 2 mm), demersal, adhesive eggs.
- Multiple spawning events by individual fish are likely.
- Eggs hatched in about seven days at 19 °C. Hatchlings were about 5 mm TL with a small yolk sac. The swim bladder developed and exogenous feeding began about two to three days post-hatching.
- Stripping of gametes was ineffective, and eggs were sticky and quickly fungused.
- Males rarely expressed milt. Reliable sex determination relied on pectoral fin shape (broad and fan-shaped in males versus slender and pinnate in females).

**Rearing and growth**
- Larvae were initially fed BioKyowa A-250 fry food. Its loss after the 2001 BSE-related import restrictions left no fully effective substitute.
- In a density experiment, 227 larvae from March 2002 were reared at 16–63 individuals per aquarium. Mean SL after five months was highest at the lowest density (40.7 mm) and lowest at the highest density (32.8 mm).
- A 2003 feeding trial compared artificial rotifer with freeze-dried cyclops. Survival was 0% and 25% on rotifer, and 25% and 45% on cyclops, a difference the authors describe as tenuous given the small pilot sample.
- Progeny of the 2002 cohort (about 14 months old) did not spawn, and none showed secondary sexual characteristics.

**Recommendations for hatchery production**
- Treat wild brood stock for ectoparasites on arrival.
- Use discrete aquaria or recirculating systems with carbon, mechanical, UV and biological filtration.
- Maintain water temperature between 18 and 22 °C, dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L, and pH between 7.5 and 9.0. Keep ammonia below 0.20 mg/L, nitrite below 0.50 mg/L and nitrate below 10 mg/L.
- Replicate natural photoperiod shifts, and provide gravel (<64 mm) or gravel-lined trays that allow adults to be removed after spawning to limit egg cannibalism.
- Feed adults a varied diet and larvae commercial fry foods four to five times daily.
- Captive propagation cannot offset habitat loss, altered flows or predator and competitor introductions. It should supplement, not replace, recovery actions.

**Study limitations**
- The authors acknowledge that the hormone and environmental trials lacked statistical validity, since the controls spawned as readily as the test aquaria.
- The relatively low fecundity and short life span of spikedace make hatchery production labour-intensive and low-yield compared with species such as [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].
