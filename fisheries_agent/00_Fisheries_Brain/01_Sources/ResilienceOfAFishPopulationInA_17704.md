# Resilience of a Fish Population in a Stream in Pennsylvania

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how a mixed fish community in [[Six Mile Run]], a small stream in Warren County, Pennsylvania, responded to a [[Rotenone]] treatment intended to clear the stream for stocking [[Brook Trout]] only. The toxicant reduced the population severely, but did not kill all fish. Survivors and migrants rapidly repopulated the stream, [[Brown Trout]] remained dominant, and a stocked brook trout planting largely failed to persist. The authors conclude that fish populations in suitable environments recover quickly, and that thorough treatment precautions are needed if eradication is the goal.

## Background & Description

In the large nonglaciated areas of the eastern United States, natural lakes are scarce, so stream fish management is central to recreational angling. Trout management in small to moderate streams has traditionally relied on stocking, with occasional habitat work such as low dams, deflectors, and fish shelters. Chemical toxicants were more recently used to remove unwanted species before stocking.

The study's objective was to eliminate a mixed fish population from Six Mile Run and restock it with brook trout alone. The stream had a history of good brook trout fishing, but brown trout and several nongame species had dominated in recent years. The original goal of killing all fish was not achieved, but the authors recorded the rapid recovery of the population and the poor competitive performance of the planted brook trout.

Six Mile Run is a soft headwater tributary of the [[Tionesta River]], draining a small part of the western slope of the [[Allegheny Plateau]]. It is only a few miles long and about 30 feet wide at its mouth. Flow ranges from a few cubic feet per second in dry summers to hundreds during spring runoff. The bottom consists of rounded stones, gravel, and sand, with sparse higher aquatic plants. Turbidity is usually low and the water only slightly colored.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Prior toxicant work informed the treatment design:

- [[Berry and Larkin]] (1954) tested [[Fish-Tox]], a commercial product of unknown rotenone content, in British Columbia streams. Water temperature, vegetation, and organic detritus all affected toxicity, and about 5 ppm was often needed for a reasonable kill.
- [[Lennon and Parker]] (1959) surveyed stream flow, species present, and the concentration and exposure time needed for each species, then devised a detailed application procedure. Killing more resistant species such as carp required a 6-hour exposure to 1 ppm of [[Pro-noxfish]].
- Earlier work by [[Larimore]] and colleagues (1959) and Thompson (1944) documented the recuperative ability of stream fish after disturbance, a phenomenon the authors note is still underestimated by fish managers.

## Key Findings

**First treatment (fall 1957)**
- A five-person crew applied rotenone across six sections of stream, targeting a concentration above 1 ppm over the entire 3.5 miles treated. Backwaters, isolated pools, spring seepages, and small tributaries were also treated.
- Concentrations probably exceeded 5 ppm briefly near application sites, but exposure at those levels was very short.
- Fish began dying within an hour, and all fish were collected on the first day. The absence of living fish afterward was not treated as proof of complete kill.
- The concentration and exposure time were below the levels shown to be needed for complete kill. The authors judged that extending the kill beyond the stream mouth to ensure full eradication was inadvisable for public-relations reasons.
- Sixteen species were identified at the first treatment. More than 90 percent of the population consisted of brown trout, white sucker, hog sucker, creek chub, pearl dace, blacknose dace, and mottled sculpin.

**Stocking and survival**
- On September 6, 1957, 858 brook trout (4 to 8 inches) were planted. On October 7, 1957, 3,000 additional brook trout (3 to 6 inches) were stocked, for a total of 3,858 fingerlings. These were not marked.
- In March 1958, 800 fin-clipped brook trout (6 to 8 inches) and 100 fin-clipped [[Rainbow Trout]] (7 to 9 inches) were stocked.
- A [[Fish Barrier and Trap]] installed near the mouth caught 13 brook trout on March 11, the planting day. No further downstream movement was observed through March 29, 1958.
- When the stream was treated again in October 1958, only 1 marked rainbow trout and no marked brook trout were recovered. Many marked fish were probably taken by anglers, but no creel census was made.
- Of the 3,858 fall 1957 fingerlings, only 83 (2.2 percent) were present in October 1958. Many were large enough to be caught by anglers.

**Second treatment (October 1958)**
- Brown trout were not planted after the 1957 treatment, yet 210 were collected in October 1958. Some were mature (10 to 13 inches), but most were young-of-the-year, indicating spawning after the first treatment.
- 23 species were collected, including all 16 from the first treatment. Some of the additional species may have been overlooked in 1957 because of their small size and scarcity.
- Brook trout were more numerous, many as survivors of the fingerling stocking. Stoneroller minnows increased greatly, and mottled sculpin declined. Overall composition changed little, and brown trout remained the dominant game fish.

**Conclusions**
- Rotenone is an effective collecting method for large numbers and varieties of fish, but rapid repopulation of large stream sections occurs within a short period.
- Eradication may be possible only with very thorough treatment, following the precautions outlined by [[Lennon and Parker]] (1959).
- Stocked brook trout failed to compete successfully with the rapidly rebuilding wild population, and reliance on stocking alone did not restore a brook-trout-only fishery.
