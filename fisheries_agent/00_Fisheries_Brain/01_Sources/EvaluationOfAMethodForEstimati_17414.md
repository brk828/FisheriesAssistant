# Evaluation of a Method for Estimating Food Consumption Rates of Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Robert F. Carline]] and [[James D. Hall]] (Department of Fisheries and Wildlife, Oregon State University, 1973, *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada* 30: 623-629) tests whether food consumption rates of fish in nature can be estimated from [[Laboratory Growth Data]]. Juvenile [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) were studied in three similar experiments, comparing fish held individually in restricted [[Aquarium]] units with fish in a [[Experimental Stream Tank]] that displayed typical territorial and aggressive behavior. Both groups showed similar [[Growth Efficiency]] across a wide range of rations. The authors conclude that laboratory food and growth data may provide reasonably accurate estimates of food consumption of coho salmon in nature.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Food consumption is a key parameter in [[Fish Population Growth Models]] but is difficult to measure in the field.
- **Proposed method:** [[Davis and Warren (1968)]] suggested estimating field consumption by extrapolating from laboratory curves relating food consumption to growth rate. This assumes laboratory fish have similar activity energy requirements, or at least similar growth efficiencies, to fish in nature.
- **Study species:** Coho salmon were chosen because their biology and social behavior were well documented in earlier experimental streams ([[Chapman 1962]]; [[Mason 1969]]).
- **Aim:** To bridge the gap between laboratory and field approaches by testing the method with a stream system that retained natural behaviors while feeding was precisely controlled.

**Methods summary:**
- Stream tank: glass-walled, divided into two circulating channels by a partition, each with two riffles and two pools. Water velocity was 7 cm/s on riffles and under 3 cm/s in pools. Each half confined fish to 4.5 m² and water exchange was 8% of tank volume per hour.
- Aquaria: clear plexiglass, 18 × 23 × 25 cm, supplied with stream water at 60 mL/min.
- Food: common housefly (*Musca* sp.) larvae delivered in known amounts, with fish fed usually twice daily.
- Fish were starved 48 h before each experiment, individually marked by cold branding, and measured and weighed at start and end. Final dry weight was obtained after oven drying at 65 °C for 5 days.
- Gross efficiency = change in weight / total food consumed; net efficiency = change in weight / (food consumed minus maintenance ration). Relations were fitted by least-squares regression, with curvilinear models used where they significantly reduced residual sums of squares.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Territoriality and hierarchy:** In the stream, a dominant fish defended the upstream half of each channel, while subordinates formed a hierarchy with varying aggressiveness. Subordinates defended positions only in limited areas.
- **Feeding behavior:** Dominant fish consumed much of the food. Subordinates consumed less at restricted rations than at unrestricted rations. Dominants often waited for drifting larvae rather than foraging on riffles.
- **Aquarium fish:** Mostly inactive, remaining near the bottom except during feeding. Activity differed markedly from stream fish.
- **Diel and drift context:** [[Mason 1969]] reported that juvenile coho density and aggression on riffles peak at dusk and dawn, linked to invertebrate drift. Because fish here were fed twice daily, their natural diel pattern may have been altered. Night observations showed fish in pools and inactive.
- **Velocity and habitat:** Coho tend to avoid strong currents and favor back eddies and pools, which reduces swimming energy costs ([[Mundie 1969]]; [[Ruggles 1966]]). In contrast, juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) favor faster currents, where swimming may be a significant energy cost ([[Everest and Chapman 1972]]).
- **Comparison with other salmonids:** Juvenile chinook and steelhead trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) tend to be inactive at night, so active night feeding may be specific to juvenile coho ([[Edmundson et al. 1968]]).

## Key Findings

**Experiment I (stream vs aquarium, n = 11 fed and 3 starved aquarium fish; stream group of about 11 fish):**
- Stream and aquarium fish had statistically indistinguishable food–growth relations at the 5% level, so a single curve was fitted to pooled data (R = 0.93, significant at 1%).
- Gross efficiency rose from the maintenance ration to a maximum near 35 mg/g/day of food consumption, then declined. Net efficiency declined continuously.
- Starved fish lost weight at 4 to 24 mg/g/day, with more variability than fed fish.

**Experiment II (smaller fry, five fish per pool):**
- Stream and aquarium fish again did not differ significantly. Cover in aquaria did not affect growth efficiency, although aquarium fish did not appear to use it.
- Maximum consumption was higher than in Experiment I. Growth efficiency declined sharply beyond about 45 mg/g/day.

**Experiment III (added overhead cover, darkened aquarium bottoms):**
- Growth efficiencies of stream and aquarium fish remained similar, though variability was greater than in Experiment II.
- Maximum consumption was higher, probably due to warmer water. Growth gains levelled off beyond 80 mg/g/day, indicating a sharp decline in gross efficiency.

**Aggression and growth efficiency:**
- Stepwise multiple regression showed that aggression rate did not significantly reduce residual sums of squares in any experiment. The authors concluded that the aggression levels observed had no measurable effect on food utilization for growth.

**Conclusions and implications:**
- Gross growth efficiency rose from zero at maintenance to a peak at intermediate rations, then fell in all three experiments. This pattern agrees with [[Warren and Davis 1967]], [[Rafail 1968]], and [[Brett et al. 1969]].
- The authors suggest that wild coho rarely reach high laboratory growth rates, so their feeding levels likely lie on the ascending part of the efficiency curve. The decline at high rations may therefore be ecologically minor.
- Laboratory data can yield reliable estimates of food consumption for juvenile coho in nature. Future work should better simulate stream conditions, especially a natural feeding regime.
