# Distribution of Midwestern Fishes on Multivariate Environmental Gradients, with Emphasis on *Notropis lutrensis*

## Executive Summary
This 1985 study by William J. Matthews uses Principal Components Analysis (PCA) of 19 physical and biotic habitat variables across 101 stream localities in eight U.S. states to characterize the environmental distribution of [[Notropis Lutrensis]] (red shiner) and seven other widespread midwestern fish taxa. Collections were made over a single 35-day span in 1978 to minimize seasonal and temporal confounds. The analysis found that *N. lutrensis* is most abundant in turbid, muddy, species-poor streams with unstable banks, and least abundant in clear, gravel/rubble/bedrock streams with diverse fish assemblages — corroborating classic subjective habitat descriptions from state fish faunas (Cross 1967; Pflieger 1975; Miller and Robison 1973; Smith 1979). The paper also introduces a four-category classification scheme ("rare-specialist," "rare-generalist," "common-specialist," "common-generalist") to describe species' joint patterns of frequency and environmental breadth, positioning *N. lutrensis* as the clearest "common-generalist" among the eight taxa studied.

## Background & Description
- **Focal species**: [[Notropis Lutrensis]] (red shiner), a small, widespread cyprinid native to the midwestern United States and northern Mexico, noted for tolerance of physicochemical stress and for reaching peak abundance in physically harsh streams (Matthews and Maness, 1979).
- **Comparator taxa** (all Cyprinidae except Fundulus, Cyprinodontidae), chosen because they range across most of the same geographic area:
  - [[Campostoma Anomalum]]
  - [[Notropis Stramineus]]
  - [[Pimephales Promelas]]
  - [[Pimephales Vigilax]]
  - [[Fundulus Zebrinus]] (=kansae)
  - [[Phenacobius Mirabilis]]
  - [[Hybognathus Spp.]] (pooled *H. placitus*, *H. argyritis*, *H. hankinsoni* due to taxonomic ambiguity; Burr, 1980)
- **Study design**: 101 stream localities across eight states (central/southwestern U.S.), sampled 29 May–1 July 1978 by seining (4.6 m seine, 4.8 mm bar mesh) for 30–45 min per site across all available habitat types in a 100–200 m reach. Up to ~150 individuals per species preserved per locality. Ozark uplands (Arkansas/Missouri) excluded because *N. lutrensis* does not occur there.
- **Variables analyzed**: 15 local physical characters + 4 biotic characters (describing the non-*N. lutrensis* fish assemblage), detailed in Appendix A (e.g., turbidity, substrate type, algae, macrophytes, width, depth, current speed, backwaters, riffles, shade, shore stability, species richness/abundance of other fishes and cyprinids). Fluctuating physicochemical variables (temperature, oxygen, pH) were deliberately excluded.
- **Analytical method**: PCA (NT-SYS software) on a correlation matrix of range-standardized (0–1) variables. Loadings >|0.40| considered ecologically meaningful (following Felley, 1984). Despite explaining less total variance, PC I and PC III were selected for two-dimensional scatterplots because they offered the greatest ecological interpretability (following Gauch, 1982; Bock et al., 1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **PC I** (22.1% of variance) was the primary axis of ecological interpretation, loaded by 13 of 19 variables. High PC I values (right side of plots) = many fish/cyprinid species, clear water, attached algae, gravel/rubble/bedrock substrate, riffles present, stable shorelines. Low PC I values (left side) = few other fish species, high turbidity, mud substrate, unstable banks.
- **PC II** (12%) added little interpretive value beyond stream width.
- **PC III** (11.8%) loaded on four additional variables: high values = deep, fast current; low values = shallow, slow current, backwaters, sand substrate.
- Combined, PC I and PC III accounted for 33.9% of total variance and were used to construct the primary ordination space (Figs. 2–4) against which species presence/absence and abundance were mapped.
- **Environmental gradient summary**:
  - Right side of ordination space = "many species, many cyprinids, clear, gravel-rubble-bedrock, riffles, stable shore, algae, shaded."
  - Left side = "few species, muddy, turbid, unstable shore."
  - Top = deep, fast current; Bottom = shallow, slow current, backwaters, sand.

## Key Findings

### Abundance patterns of *Notropis lutrensis*
- Present in almost all sampled environments (78 of 101 localities), but abundance ("abundant" ≥100 individuals; "present but not abundant" 1–99; "absent" = 0) differed significantly across PCA-defined quadrants (G-test, df=6, G=17.962, p<0.01).
- Most abundant in **Quadrants II, III, IV** (23–46% of localities) vs. only 4% in Quadrant I.
- Absence was more common in the right half of ordination space (Quadrants I & II: 39% and 36% absent) vs. left half (Quadrants III & IV: 12% and 8% absent).
- Difference in abundance driven mainly by the **PC I (left-right) axis**, not PC III (top-bottom) — pooling upper/lower halves showed no significant independence (G=0.814, p>0.5).
- **Ecological conclusion**: *N. lutrensis* thrives in turbid, muddy, species-poor streams with unstable banks; scarce/absent in clear, gravel/rubble/bedrock streams rich in other cyprinids/fishes. Abundance not strongly tied to sand presence or stream width; occurred in both deep-swift and shallow-slow conditions.

### Species classification framework
A fourfold model was proposed based on frequency of occurrence (rare vs. common) crossed with environmental breadth (specialist vs. generalist):

| Taxon | N (localities) | Multivariate Area (A) | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Notropis Lutrensis]] | 78 | 741 | Common-generalist |
| [[Campostoma Anomalum]] | 38 | 536 | Common-generalist (limited in turbid environments) |
| [[Notropis Stramineus]] | 31 | 448 | Common-generalist (concentrated in Quadrant III, 65%) |
| [[Pimephales Promelas]] | 25 | 329 | Common-specialist (80% occurrence in one quadrant) |
| [[Pimephales Vigilax]] | 19 | 407 | Rare-generalist |
| [[Fundulus Zebrinus]] | 15 | 339 | Rare-generalist (67% in Quadrant III) |
| [[Phenacobius Mirabilis]] | 7 | 193 | Rare-generalist |
| [[Hybognathus Spp.]] | 7 | 32 | Rare-specialist (entirely within one quadrant) |

- **Multivariate area (A)** = number of grid squares of PC I–III space enclosing all localities where a taxon occurred (overlay method on scatterplots), quantifying environmental breadth.
- [[Hybognathus Spp.]] is the clearest **rare-specialist**: rarest taxon, smallest environmental space, confined to one quadrant (sand bottoms, shallow water).
- [[Phenacobius Mirabilis]] is equally rare but occupies a much larger, more diverse environmental space across all four quadrants — a **rare-generalist**.
- [[Notropis Lutrensis]] is the clearest **common-generalist**: most frequently collected (2× any other taxon), largest multivariate space, present across all four quadrants.

### Percent similarity among taxa (Table 3, Kohn & Riggs 1982 index)
- *N. lutrensis* showed moderate-to-high similarity to all other taxa (49–94%), consistent with its generalist status.
- Taxa concentrated in Quadrant III ([[Notropis Stramineus]], [[Pimephales Promelas]], [[Hybognathus Spp.]], [[Fundulus Zebrinus]]) showed mutually high similarity (65–87%).
- Lowest similarities (<0.5) occurred between [[Campostoma Anomalum]] and five other taxa, and between [[Hybognathus Spp.]] and [[Phenacobius Mirabilis]] — reflecting *C. anomalum*'s avoidance of large turbid mainstem rivers where the other species commonly occur.
- Mean pairwise similarity across all 28 species pairs: 65% (SD=19%).

### Agreement with classic autecological descriptions
The multivariate results corroborated long-standing, subjective habitat descriptions in standard regional ichthyology references (Cross, 1967 [Kansas]; Pflieger, 1975 [Missouri]; Miller and Robison, 1973 [Oklahoma]; Smith, 1979 [Illinois]):
- **N. lutrensis**: turbid, few-species streams — matches Cross (1967), Cross and Collins (1975), Pflieger (1975), Miller and Robison (1973), Smith (1979).
- **N. stramineus**: sandy substrate affinity confirmed, though found across a broader range of turbidity/clarity than references suggested.
- **C. anomalum**: strongly associated with moderate/high-gradient, clear, gravel-rubble-bedrock riffle habitats — matched Cross and Pflieger closely.
- **F. zebrinus**: shallow water, few co-occurring species — consistent with Cross (1967) and Echelle et al. (1972).
- **P. mirabilis**: tolerant/generalist across many habitat types, matching Cross's description of small numbers occurring almost everywhere.
- **Hybognathus spp.**: restricted to shallow, sandy prairie river habitats — matches Cross and Pflieger.
- **P. vigilax**: mainly backwaters/pools with sand-silt bottoms and turbidity tolerance, though also found over rubble-gravel without backwaters at some sites.
- **P. promelas**: muddy/sandy backwater pools with few other species, though not as ubiquitous in Kansas as Cross (1967) suggested.

### Discrepancies with prior single-watershed/microhabitat studies
- [[Matthews and Hill (1979a)]] found in the South Canadian River that turbidity had negligible effect on red shiner microhabitat selection and that the species avoided turbulent flow — apparently contradicting the present broader-scale finding that turbidity is a key correlate of high abundance.
- Rose and Echelle (1981), studying a single Texas watershed, found *N. lutrensis* positively associated with riffle habitat and total species number — opposite to the present cross-regional pattern.
- **Interpretation**: These apparent contradictions largely reflect differences in **spatial scale** — local microhabitat selection within a heterogeneous single reach can differ substantially from the broader geographic pattern of which streams support high red shiner abundance. The paper cautions against extrapolating single-site or single-watershed habitat studies to predict species distribution across a full geographic range, and vice versa.
- Matthews and Hill (1980) additionally noted that *N. lutrensis* shows wide habitat use during benign conditions but narrows to specialized habitat during environmental stress, complicating single-snapshot habitat characterizations.

### Broader implications
- Agreement between rigorous multivariate analysis and traditional expert-based autecological descriptions provides **mutual validation** for both approaches.
- The success of this method for well-studied species suggests multivariate ordination techniques (PCA) are a viable tool for characterizing habitat use of poorly known fishes or in undersampled regions (e.g., much of the tropics).
- Findings reinforce [[Cross (1967)]]'s original observation that *N. lutrensis* is most abundant where few other fish species are present — supported here both by biotic-variable loadings on PC I and by direct abundance-quadrant analysis.
