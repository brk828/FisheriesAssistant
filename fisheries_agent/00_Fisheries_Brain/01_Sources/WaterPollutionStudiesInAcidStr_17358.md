# Water Pollution Studies in Acid Strip-Mine Lakes: Changes in Water Quality and Community Structure Associated with Aging

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This report describes an ongoing limnological study of five strip-mine lakes in Callaway County, Missouri, with monitoring running continuously from January 1962 to 1965. The authors examine how acid mine drainage shapes water chemistry and aquatic biota, and how these lakes change as acidity declines with age. They propose an orderly, predictable three-stage succession (Youthful, Early Recovery, Late Recovery) in which acidity, dissolved solids, and thermal stratification fall while biological diversity and productivity rise. The most acid lakes are chemically similar to acid underground mine discharges. The rate of succession, however, is not predictable and may last 30 to 45 years where waste coal or spoil banks persist.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Five strip-mine lakes, labelled A1, A2, A3, B and D. Lakes A1, A2 and A3 are acidic ("Red" and "Blue" lakes); Lakes B and D are near-alkaline.
- **Regional setting:** The lakes lie in the [[Western Interior Coal Province]], where about 65,000 acres have been strip-mined across five states. The [[Bevier Coal Formation]] is a highly volatile bituminous coal with 3 to 5 percent sulfur, which may contain pyrite and marcasite.
- **Research history:** Studies began in 1940 ([[Heaton Study]]), were extended in 1950 ([[Crawford Study]]), and have been continuous since January 1962.
- **Stated objectives:**
  1. Identify effects of acid drainage on water quality and biota.
  2. Characterize changes in water quality and community structure as acid pollution declines (successional change with aging).
  3. Compare water quality in the most acid lakes with acid discharge from underground mines.
- **Methods:** Routine monthly field and laboratory observations, following [[Standard Methods for Water Analysis]], with more complete seasonal analyses by the University of Missouri Spectrographic Laboratory. Methods included colorimetry (Cu, Fe), flame spectrophotometry (Ca, Ba, Na, K, Sr), atomic absorption (Zn, Mg), potentiometry (Cl), gravimetry (Al, sulfate), and hot phenolphthalein titration for acidity.
- **Funding and setting:** Supported in part by Public Health Service Research Grant WP-00379 and Fellowship Award 1-FL-WP-22892, conducted on the [[Cedar Creek Land Use Area]] under the U.S. Forest Service, and contributed by the [[Missouri Cooperative Fishery Unit]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake A1 ("Red Lake")**
- pH 2.0–2.9; acidity 819–6680 ppm; area 0.3 acre; maximum depth 6 ft; watershed 1.5 acres with extensive waste coal beds.
- Deep red-amber color, attributed to iron compounds (ferric sulfate and iron hydroxides/oxides).
- Total iron up to 2730 ppm, almost entirely in solution; sulfate 777–7600 ppm.
- Strong inverse thermal stratification from December through March, with bottom water 4–8 °C warmer than surface water.
- Chemically similar to underground mine drainage. Iron is lost from samples within 4 hours unless analyzed promptly.
- Biota is severely restricted: three aquatic insect taxa, plus the green algae *Chlamydomonas* and *Euglena mutabilis*, and iron bacteria.

**Lakes A2 and A3 ("Blue Lakes")**
- pH 2.8–4.1; acidity 30–683 ppm. Water is blue or blue-green and highly transparent, because clay flocculates and dissolved solids are reduced. Total iron does not exceed 50 ppm.
- Median acidity: 3749 ppm (A1), 656 ppm (A2), 59 ppm (A3). Lake A2 is shallow and usually homothermous. Lake A3 is sheltered by high spoil banks, yet shows active mixing (surface–bottom differences usually under 3 °C).
- Lake A3 is often bright green from blooms of *Closterium* or *Chlorella*. Fish cannot survive in either lake.

**Lakes B and D ("Near-alkaline")**
- pH near neutral; acidity zero; methyl orange alkalinity 27–184 ppm. Iron is only ferric and never exceeds 0.5 ppm.
- Lake B is highly transparent, with pondweed (*Potamogeton diversifolius*) in early summer, replaced by stonewort (*Chara sejuncta*) in fall. Blue-green algal blooms develop in late fall.
- Lake D drains a 95-acre watershed of eroding pasture. Silt has built a delta that split the lake into two basins, and the turbidity suppresses rooted plants.

## Key Findings

1. **Acid drainage chemistry:** The most acid lake (A1) closely matches underground mine drainage from [[Temple and Koehler Analyses]], [[Barnes et al Analyses]], and [[Carpenter and Herndon Analyses]], although the latter reported higher acidity, sulfate, total iron and total solids.

2. **Successional stages:** The authors describe three recognizable stages:
   - **Youthful** (Lake A1): pH 2.0–2.9, deep red, marked thermal and chemical stratification, few surviving species.
   - **Early Recovery** (Lakes A2 and A3): pH 2.8–4.1 rising toward 3–5, deep blue, increasing biotic variety, usually homothermous.
   - **Late Recovery** (Lakes B and D): pH 6.3–7.9, zero acidity, low dissolved solids similar to Midwestern farm ponds and reservoirs, usually summer-stratified, highly diverse flora and fauna.

3. **Declining acidity:** Historical data over a 24-year period from lakes A3, B and D show progressive declines in acidity and dissolved substances, with increasing biota. Local residents reported that Lake A2 was formerly red.

4. **Biotic diversity:** Total taxa (excluding bacteria and protozoa) increase from 5 (Youthful) to 52 (Early Recovery) to 158 (Late Recovery). Lakes B and D contain 11 fish species, 41 net plankton taxa, 49 nannoplankton taxa, 8 macrophyte species and 49 bottom-fauna taxa. Lake A3 has 3 macrophyte species, 12 net plankton taxa, 15 nannoplankton taxa and 22 bottom-fauna taxa.

5. **Productivity:** Gross photosynthesis is greater in Late Recovery Lake D (range 0.0–10.4 g O₂/m²/day, mean 4.8) than in Early Recovery Lake A3 (range 0.0–7.6, mean 2.5). The depressed curve in A3 is attributed to acid limitation of algae and macrophytes. Late Recovery lakes resemble mid-western farm pond communities metabolically, though the authors note the data are not strictly comparable seasonally.

6. **Rate of succession:** Direction is predictable, but rate is not. Youthful and Early Recovery stages may persist 30–45 years where waste coal piles restrict watersheds (A1, A3) or extensive spoil banks cover large watersheds (A2). The authors made no attempt to accelerate recovery. Earth cover has been proposed to reduce acid formation, and inflow from alkaline farmland into Lake D may have hastened its succession.

7. **Summary conclusions:**
   - The most acid lakes are chemically similar to acid underground mine discharges.
   - Progressive aging brings a steady decline in acidity.
   - This decline is accompanied by lower dissolved solids, altered thermal stratification, greater biotic diversity, and greater potential for total productivity.
