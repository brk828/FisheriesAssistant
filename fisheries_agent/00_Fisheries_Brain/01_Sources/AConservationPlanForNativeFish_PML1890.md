# A Conservation Plan for Native Fishes of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This BioScience article (March 2003, Vol. 53 No. 3) by [[Minckley]], Marsh, Deacon, Dowling, Hedrick, Matthews and Mueller proposes a science-based management plan for the four federally endangered "big-river" fishes of the lower [[Colorado River]]: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The authors argue that current recovery goals are grossly inadequate and that the species must be maintained as large, genetically diverse, panmictic populations. The central proposal is a network of predator-free, excavated off-channel habitats that serve as breeding and rearing sources for repatriation into the main channel. The article is a conservation recommendation rather than a primary research study, although it draws on mitochondrial DNA analyses, population census data and mark-recapture results.

## Background & Description

The original lower Colorado River fish fauna comprised 13 species, including 10 freshwater species. Three largely marine taxa (machete, striped mullet and spotted sleeper) and [[Woundfin]] were extirpated before 1900, and [[Roundtail Chub]] and Colorado Squawfish have also been lost. The four big-river fishes are the focus of the plan. They were once widespread and abundant but are now federally listed as endangered (USFWS 2002a–d).

The river is one of the most controlled in the world, supplying water and power to tens of millions of people. Historical species-level endemism for the Colorado River fauna is approximately 75%. Dam construction, cold tailwaters, habitat degradation, desiccation and nonnative species have driven the decline.

Over the past century most of the native fauna has been replaced by nonnative species, particularly below [[Hoover Dam]]. Table 1 lists roughly 30 native and nonnative taxa; the most consequential nonnatives include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Common Carp]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]] and [[African Cichlids]]. Most nonnatives are ecological generalists that are predatory or omnivorous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The decline is attributed mainly to predation and competition by nonnative species rather than to flow regulation alone. The authors note that native species reproduce successfully in predator-free habitats but rarely in waters shared with nonnatives, which prey on larvae and juveniles.

Management has been shaped by the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP, 1987, roughly $60 million over 15 years) and "reasonable and prudent alternatives" to jeopardy opinions. The authors criticize the RIP as focused on bureaucratic procedure, dependent on water-development funding, and largely unpublished and unreviewed in its site-specific analyses. The lower basin was de-emphasized relative to the upper basin, even though it held important humpback chub and razorback sucker populations and the last wild bonytail.

Population status reported in the article:
- **Colorado Squawfish:** last wild fish caught in 1975 in the lower river.
- **Bonytail:** persist only as a few wild fish in [[Lake Mohave]] and perhaps [[Lake Havasu]], augmented by hatchery stock.
- **Humpback Chub:** one viable population in the Little Colorado River–Grand Canyon complex; the population was about 10,000 adults into the early 1990s but has declined substantially. The main stem holds perhaps 10% as many fish, and cold water from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] precludes main-stem reproduction.
- **Razorback Sucker:** the wild adult population in Lake Mohave fell from an estimated 73,500 (1980–1993) to 18,248 (1992–1999). Annual estimates declined from 40,093 in 1992 to 9,086 in 1999. The population is dominated by large, old adults, with no evidence of recruitment. Repatriated fish made up about 12% of the population by 1999, and the total was estimated at about 12,000 adults.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery goals are inadequate.** The authors judge the USFWS population goals of 700 to 5,800 individuals per species or reach as far too low. They recommend a target of stabilization (level III) defined by retained genetic variability rather than by numbers.
- **Effective population size.** Using the 500 effective-population threshold for maintaining genetic variation, the authors estimate a census population of roughly 5,000 adults per generation for an N_e of 500. With an N_e of 1,000, about 10,000 adults would be required, under the assumption that N_e is about 10% of census size.
- **Genetic evidence.** Mitochondrial DNA analysis (Garrigan et al. 2002) estimated long-term female effective population sizes (table 3) of 97,500 for humpback chub (Little Colorado), 89,500 for bonytail (Lake Mohave, declining) and 669,000 for razorback sucker (expanding). The authors conclude that these species historically existed in large numbers, but also that the fixed sample sizes and low bonytail diversity indicate a need for caution.
- **Hatchery limitations.** Brood stocks have been founded from as few as 5 males and 5 females, which the authors call "utterly inadequate." Hatchery culture has reduced genetic variability in bonytail and razorback sucker. Hatcheries are judged a poor choice for maintaining diversity.
- **Reproductive potential.** A single female razorback sucker carries about 85,000 ova, so a small number of females could in principle replace the Lake Mohave population, but the resulting progeny would be siblings with dangerously low variation.
- **Off-channel habitat design.** The plan calls for excavated habitats (each about 1 to 2 hectares, roughly 2.5 to 5 acres), with about ten complexes, excluded from nonnatives by size-graded gravel screens at inlets and outlets. A prototype was built at [[Beal Lake]] on the Havasu National Wildlife Refuge. Habitats should be spaced along the river, sit below the water table and be linked by gravity flow.
- **Production model.** A model of 50 females per hectare, 10 progeny per female and 250 off-channel habitats predicts about 125,000 juveniles per year. In an example with 5% first-year survival, the authors estimate about 60,000 adults stabilizing in about 10 years.
- **Survival drives success.** Repatriating large subadults (over 35 cm TL) is far more productive than releasing small fish. Razorback sucker first-year survival of about 5% rises to about 80% in later years. A rise in first-year survival from 5% to 15% gives about a 40% increase in population.
- **Harvest and turnover.** Fish should be harvested by seining, traps, weirs, electrofishing or nets during cooler seasons. Replacing 33% of breeding female razorback suckers annually would turn over the parental pool each decade.
- **Nonnative control.** Options include increased angling pressure on nonnatives and renovation of contaminated habitats, which the authors expect to be necessary.
- **Conclusion.** Current programs are fragmented and lack an overall plan, and genetic and demographic conditions are "inarguably worse" than when the species were listed. The authors state that proactive decisions and funding are needed, and that "what may well be missing is the political will to carry it out."
