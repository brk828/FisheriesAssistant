# Implications of Streamflow Variability and Predictability for Lotic Community Structure

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[N. LeRoy Poff]] and [[J. V. Ward]] analyzes long-term daily discharge records from 78 unimpounded streams across the continental United States to build a quantitative framework for streamflow variability and predictability. Eleven summary statistics, grouped into overall flow variability, flood regime, and intermittency, were clustered with a nonhierarchical (K-means) method into nine stream types. The authors propose a hierarchical conceptual model in which intermittency is the first-order control on a stream's flow template, followed by flood frequency, flood predictability, and overall flow predictability. The discussion of biological implications is explicitly speculative, given the scarcity of biological data matched to the flow records.

## Background & Description

Stream ecologists have long debated whether community structure is governed by abiotic disturbance or biotic interactions, and streamflow is widely treated as the key environmental gradient. Prior comparative studies usually examined only one or two flow measures, often using monthly rather than daily data. This study uses mean daily discharge to characterize flow variability, its temporal predictability, and the timing, frequency, and duration of floods and intermittent conditions.

Key methodological elements:

- **Study sites:** 78 gauged streams chosen to represent major geomorphologic, physiographic, and [[Ecoregions of the United States]]. Streams that were impounded, channelized, or substantially diverted were excluded. Record lengths ranged from 17 to 81 years.
- **Data transformation:** Flows were natural-log transformed and "modularized" by dividing by each stream's grand mean, yielding a dimensionless flow index.
- **Flood definition:** The threshold flood was the 2-year return-interval flow (50% exceedance on lognormal probability paper), treated as a proxy for [[Bankfull Discharge]] and as a significant disturbance because it moves substratum.
- **Predictability:** Calculated with [[Colwell's Predictability Index]], which partitions predictability into constancy (C) and contingency (M).
- **Basin descriptors:** AREA, ANNQ, Q/AREA and LNANNQ were recorded as setting variables but excluded from clustering.

Table 1 defines the 15 variables in four categories: basin descriptors, overall flow variability, flood regime, and intermittency.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame flow as an integrating physical attribute that controls habitat volume, current velocity, channel geomorphology, and substratum stability. Floods and low-flow or zero-flow intermittency are treated as the two principal disturbances structuring stream communities, consistent with the [[Disturbance Concept]] of Sousa (1984) as applied to streams.

Expected biological responses, summarized in Table 4 across intermittency, flood frequency, flood predictability and flow predictability, include:

- **Intermittent streams:** Diapause or aestivation in invertebrates, desiccation avoidance, increased dispersal capability, and high physiological tolerance in fishes. Biotic interactions are expected to matter little under such harsh conditions.
- **High flood frequency:** Small body size, fusiform shapes favored in fishes, behavioral avoidance of floods, and low recruitment with uneven age structure.
- **High flood predictability:** Flood-timed reproduction and emergence, and a stronger role for biotic interactions in the flood-free season. Examples cited include [[Pacific Salmonids]] whose fry are present during flood-free summers and cyprinids whose life histories track flood timing in the desert southwest.
- **Predictable, stable flow:** Greater development of biotic interactions and more predictable successional trajectories.

## Key Findings

- **Nine stream clusters** were identified: harsh intermittent, intermittent flashy, intermittent runoff, perennial flashy, perennial runoff, snowmelt, snow + rain, winter rain, and mesic groundwater. The authors chose nine clusters because this number optimized group separation and interpretability, not by a formal statistical criterion.
- **Geographic affiliation:** Harsh intermittent streams were concentrated in the arid to semi-arid Southwest, with one exception added a posteriori (Mauvis Coulee, North Dakota). Mesic groundwater streams were mostly eastern, and snowmelt and snow + rain streams were largely western montane systems.
- **Basin size is not the driver:** A MANOVA showed that basin descriptors separated clusters (Wilks' Λ = 0.42, p < 0.001), but this was attributed mainly to Q/AREA (F = 3.94, p < 0.001) and to the correlation structure of the variables. The authors conclude that the four basin variables could not satisfactorily reproduce the nine clusters.
- **Hierarchical conceptual model:** Hydrologic variables are ranked as intermittency, flood frequency, flood predictability, and overall flow predictability/variability. For perennial streams, flood predictability must also be considered, and overall flow predictability matters only within certain combinations of flood frequency and predictability.
- **Abiotic versus biotic control:** Highly variable or unpredictable flow regimes are predicted to favor abiotic control, while predictable, benign flows favor biotic interactions such as competition and predation. The authors note that most systems fall between these extremes.
- **Example systems:**
  - *Sagehen Creek (California):* Winter floods reduced egg survival and adult densities of native brook trout, allowing nonnative rainbow trout fry to compete successfully in late spring.
  - *Sycamore Creek (Arizona):* Insects adapted to frequent floods through accelerated development and rapid recolonization by oviposition.
  - *Brier Creek (Oklahoma):* Floods are the main structuring agent, but competition and predation also contribute during periods of flow constancy.
- **Limitations:** The authors state that the biological implications are largely speculative because of the "virtual absence of empirical data" matched to long-term flow records. They call for multi-year biological sampling and long-term datasets linked to reliable flow records.
- **Conclusion:** Any stream can be placed in the 11-dimensional flow space, summarized in a four-dimensional space, or displayed in a continuous three-dimensional flow space (flood frequency, flood predictability, and overall flow predictability). A stream's position in this space offers an a priori basis for expectations about its biotic attributes, all else being equal.
