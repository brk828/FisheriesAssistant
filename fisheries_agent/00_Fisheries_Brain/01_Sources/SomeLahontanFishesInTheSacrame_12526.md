## Executive Summary
This 1950 report documents an unusual biogeographic occurrence: the presence of four fish species indigenous to the **Lahontan Basin** — a historically isolated endorheic basin — found living within the **Sacramento River drainage** in the Sierra Nevada of California. The discovery was made incidental to a rotenone (chemical) treatment of [[Miller Lake]] (Placer County) and [[Richardson Lake]] (El Dorado County) intended to eradicate "rough fish" prior to restocking with rainbow and eastern brook trout. The author, J. B. Kimsey, proposes two competing hypotheses for the species' presence: recent introduction by bait fishermen, versus a natural, ancient transfer via a landslide-induced stream capture connecting the Lake Tahoe (Lahontan) drainage to the Sacramento drainage.

## Background & Description
The source text is a faunal/biogeographic note and does not provide detailed morphometric or meristic data. Species identified are documented only taxonomically, by subspecies designation, and by relative abundance:

- **[[Siphateles bicolor obesus]]** (Tui Chub) — Cyprinid; described as reaching nuisance-level overpopulation, suggesting strong reproductive/colonizing capacity in still-water (lake) habitat.
- **[[Richardsonius egregius]]** (Lahontan Redside) — Cyprinid; present in smaller, less conspicuous numbers.
- **[[Rhinichthys osculus robustus]]** (Speckled Dace, robust subspecies) — Cyprinid; present in smaller, less conspicuous numbers.
- **[[Catostomus tahoensis]]** (Tahoe Sucker) — Catostomid; dominant species in Miller Lake population alongside a few very large brown trout.

No length, weight, coloration, or fin-ray count data are reported.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat types**: Two subalpine lakes (Miller Lake, Richardson Lake), an associated headwater pond, and connecting streams (Miller Creek, draining to the [[Rubicon River]]; McKinney Creek, draining to [[Lake Tahoe]]).
- **Community composition**:
  - **Richardson Lake**: dominated by *S. b. obesus*, with substantial populations of the other three species.
  - **Miller Lake**: dominated by *C. tahoensis*, plus a few large brown trout (*Salmo trutta*, introduced game species), with the other three Lahontan natives also present.
- **Drainage relationships**: Miller Lake currently drains into Miller Creek → Rubicon River (Sacramento drainage), rather than into Lake Tahoe (Lahontan Basin) via McKinney Creek, despite geographic proximity and only a slight grade separation between the two systems.
- **Refugia**: Despite chemical eradication treatment of the two lakes, populations of all four species persisted in a small pond at the head of Miller Creek and in Miller Creek itself, acting as a source for potential re-invasion — prompting construction of barrier dams at lake outlets.
- **Interaction with management**: The lakes were being managed as put-and-take salmonid fisheries (rainbow and eastern brook trout), with the Lahontan cyprinids/catostomids treated as undesirable "rough fish" competitors.

## Key Findings
1. **Mass introduction event**: Four Lahontan Basin-native fish species (*[[Siphateles bicolor obesus]]*, *[[Richardsonius egregius]]*, *[[Rhinichthys osculus robustus]]*, *[[Catostomus tahoensis]]*) were found co-occurring in the Sacramento River drainage, representing a trans-basin faunal transfer.
2. **Likely vector — bait fishermen**: The overpopulation of *S. b. obesus* appeared recent and rapidly became a nuisance, strongly suggesting anthropogenic introduction via bait bucket transfer.
3. **Alternative hypothesis — natural stream capture**: Geomorphological evidence (slight grade separation between Miller Lake and McKinney Creek, terrain resembling an old landslide) combined with a historical record (Rutter, 1907/1908, *Bull. U.S. Bur. Fish.* XXVII, pp. 103–152) of *C. tahoensis* in a stream in "Miller Pass" suggests Miller Lake may have been naturally diverted from the Lake Tahoe drainage into the Sacramento drainage by a landslide, predating bait-fisherman activity.
4. **Differential species origin plausibility**: The author argues *S. b. obesus* was "almost certainly" introduced by bait fishermen (recent, nuisance-level), whereas *R. egregius* and *R. o. robustus*, being less conspicuous, could plausibly have been present naturally since before 1907 — though Rutter's failure to report them makes this uncertain.
5. **Eradication incomplete**: Chemical treatment of Miller Lake and Richardson Lake in August 1949 did not eliminate the species from the watershed, as refugial populations persisted in upstream pond/creek habitat, necessitating barrier dam construction to prevent recolonization.
6. **Presence of large brown trout**: Miller Lake also supported a few very large brown trout, indicating an additional historical or intentional salmonid introduction predating the treatment.
