# Colorado River Endangered Fish and the Flaming Gorge Flow Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 magazine feature (article by Chris Madson, photography by Lance Beeny) examines how dams on the [[Colorado River]] system changed desert rivers and their fish, and how managers now try to protect endangered native species while maintaining sport fisheries. It focuses on [[Wyoming]] waters, especially the [[Green River]], the [[Little Snake River]], and [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], and describes a [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] plan to mimic the natural spring hydrograph below Flaming Gorge Dam.

## Background & Description

The article opens by framing the pristine Colorado as a set of contradictions: warm, silt-laden, flood-prone rivers whose native fish were adapted to harsh, variable conditions. Before the dams, these rivers swung between floods and low flows, carried heavy sediment, and held water temperatures that were extreme by eastern standards.

After the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] was empowered to remake the Colorado drainage, dams evened out flows, trapped silt, and stabilized water temperature. Native willows and cottonwoods, along with the introduced [[Saltcedar]], colonized the mud flats and stabilized the banks. Managers also introduced non-native sportfish, including [[Channel Catfish]], northern pike, bass, rainbow trout, lake trout and [[Kokanee Salmon]], which could survive the altered river.

The change came at a cost. Several native species, including the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], were listed as federally endangered. Managers in several states now face the task of protecting these fish while supporting demand for sport fishing, including in cold tailwaters below the dams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Squawfish record on the Little Snake:** In 1990, a Colorado squawfish turned up during fish sampling on the Little Snake River, the first Wyoming record since the 1960s. The author regards it as a single, unusually migratory fish during a good water year, since no others were found in later years.
- **Flaming Gorge as the key reservoir:** [[Fontenelle Reservoir]] stores only a fraction of the water released from Flaming Gorge, so Wyoming's endangered-fish plan applies chiefly to Flaming Gorge. The reservoir supports a trophy lake trout fishery, brown trout, a thriving kokanee population, and smallmouth bass and channel catfish. The tailwater below the Utah dam is regarded by many anglers as among the best trout water in the West.
- **Spawning habitat:** Kokanee spawn in the fall in redds in sand or gravel at depths as shallow as three feet or as deep as about ten feet. Eggs hatch in midwinter, but the young remain in the gravel until spring. Reservoir drawdown before fry emerge in May could cause losses.
- **Winter conditions:** Recent winter releases have been fairly high, breaking up ice and scouring the channel downstream. Native fish are adapted to low winter flows, while many introduced species do not tolerate them well.

## Key Findings

1. **Hydrograph mimicry plan:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] negotiated a plan to restore natural high spring flows below Flaming Gorge. Experimental releases of about 4,500 cubic feet per second are planned for spring over the next three years, starting the year the article was written, and are expected to work with natural flows in the [[Yampa River]].
2. **Spawning and rearing benefits:** Spring releases are expected to flood backwaters and eddies, providing traditional spring foraging areas and spawning sites for the squawfish, humpback chub and razorback sucker.
3. **Release method:** The plan calls for drawing the "warmest available water" from near the reservoir surface rather than from the bottom.
4. **Summer and fall tapering:** After the spring peak, flows will be reduced slowly to less than half the peak flow and held there through fall.
5. **Stable winter flows:** The plan calls for fairly low, constant winter flows that maintain ice cover, giving native fish a winter advantage over introduced species.
6. **Water supply constraint:** Timing the release is relatively easy; the challenge is having enough water to release. In the recent past, reservoir levels fell as much as ten feet in winter when water was held back for storage. In a normal water year, there is no fluctuation; in drought years, the author cites a rough estimate of about one year in five when drawdown could harm kokanee.
7. **Review of stocking:** Wyoming Game and Fish Department policy now requires review by the [[Wyoming Game and Fish Department]] and federal biologists before stocking non-native fish, and the state has moved to eliminate channel catfish stocking in the Little Snake River, which is a key endangered-fish habitat.
8. **Future negotiations:** Further talks aim at changing late summer, fall and winter flows between Fontenelle and Flaming Gorge. The changes are expected to be modest but could improve conditions for endangered fish downstream.
9. **Management concerns:** Biologists are concerned about predation by non-native species such as channel catfish, smallmouth bass and largemouth bass; competition from fathead minnows for plankton and predation on juvenile squawfish; and hybridization of razorback suckers with other suckers.
10. **Hard trade-offs:** Managers recognize that the reservoir now supports a sport fishery that people value. The article concludes that future decisions will involve difficult trade-offs between native species and popular non-native sportfish, and asks whether the native fish will survive long enough for managers to restore them.
