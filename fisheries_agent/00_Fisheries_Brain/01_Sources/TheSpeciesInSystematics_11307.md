# The Species in Systematics

#ichthyology #marine-biology #species-concept

## Executive Summary

This theoretical essay by M. J. Scoble, drawn from a Transvaal Museum monograph on species and speciation, asks whether species are real units of evolution and whether a "biology of species" can exist independently of a "biology of populations." It examines the [[Biological Species Concept]] (BSC), the [[Recognition Concept]], and the ontological status of species as individuals versus classes. The core argument is that the BSC applies only to actually interbreeding (nondimensional) populations, leaving the multidimensional species of taxonomy without a categorical genetic basis, and that a homeostatic view may explain species, including uniparental ones, without relying on gene flow.

The document contains no empirical data, sample sizes, or organism-level observations. It is not aquatic or fish-specific; the species discussed are general taxonomic and evolutionary units, with antelope horns and butterflies cited only as examples.

## Background & Description

The essay opens by noting the extensive literature on the species concept and frames basic questions: whether biologists share a single meaning of "species," whether common criteria exist for the species category, and whether species are objectively definable. Systematists are presented as having a stronger stake in these questions than population biologists, because taxonomic species are meant to describe units of evolution rather than convenient groupings.

Scoble distinguishes between species and higher taxa on ontological grounds. Following [[Ghiselin]] (1974) and [[Hull]] (1976), species are argued to be individuals because they lack an "essence," whereas higher taxa are classes defined by shared properties. Individuals are born and die: for species, birth corresponds to origin and death to extinction. Caplan (1981) raised concern that treating species as individuals might revive typological species, a concern answered by Kitts (1983) and Splitter (1982), who describe species as historical entities or "natural kinds."

The essay also traces the historical framing of the species category. [[Linnaeus]] treated the genus as an abstract idea, a view Cain (1957) links to Aristotelian essentialism and to the creationist thinking of his era. Mayr's (1942) biospecies definition, "groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations which are reproductively isolated from other such groups," is the central reference point.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The essay addresses species as units of evolution and as entities shaped by mate recognition and ecological niche, rather than by any particular habitat. Mayr's (1982) later definition shifts emphasis toward ecology: a species is "a reproductive community of populations... that occupies a specific niche in nature." No specific ecosystem, water body, or environmental dataset is discussed.

Paterson's (1978) view holds that speciation is largely an incidental effect of the breakdown of the specific-mate recognition system (SMRS) in allopatry, rather than reinforcement by natural selection in sympatry. The SMRS is treated as kept under strong stabilizing selection, and speciation as a fairly rare event, consistent with punctuated equilibria.

## Key Findings

- **Biological species concept limits:** Splitter (1982) concludes the BSC applies to interbreeding populations but is incompatible with polytypic (multidimensional) species, so allopatric or allochronic populations cannot be shown conspecific on reproductive grounds.
- **Potential interbreeding lacks a categorical basis:** Using a salt-solubility analogy, the essay argues that potential interbreeding cannot be grounded in genetic theory, so the BSC lacks a categorical basis for untested populations.
- **Gene pool concept is insufficient:** The notion of a shared gene pool "has no sense independently of the original terms of the BSC" (Splitter, 1982: 171).
- **Nondimensional species as individuals:** The nondimensional species is defined by actual interbreeding and requires no genetic essence, so species are treated as individuals in this sense.
- **Recognition concept:** Paterson's (1978) recognition concept defines species by shared SMRS and offers a way to compare mate-recognition traits (e.g., courtship sequences, antelope horns per Vrba, 1980) in allopatric populations, though it provides no independent genetic explanation.
- **Homeostatic species model:** Species are viewed as homeostatically maintained entities, with uniparental "species" potentially explained by genetic equilibrium rather than gene flow. Scoble notes this remains a "comfortable explanation" but the genetic mechanism is "elusive."
- **Agamospecies:** Uniparental species may still be units of evolution even if they are not ontologically individuals, which the author treats as a stressed, cautious conclusion.
- **Core open question:** Whether any genetic explanation can ground the conspecificity of populations that do not exchange genes remains unresolved; the author concludes the multidimensional species has not been successfully defined in reductionist genetic terms.
