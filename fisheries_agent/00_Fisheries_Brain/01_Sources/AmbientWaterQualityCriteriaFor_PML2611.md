# Ambient Aquatic Life Water Quality Criteria for Arsenic

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) document, produced by the [[Environmental Research Laboratory]] in Duluth, Minnesota and Narragansett, Rhode Island, revises the national aquatic life criteria for [[Arsenic]] under Section 304(a) of the [[Clean Water Act]]. It compiles acute and chronic toxicity, toxicity to aquatic plants, bioaccumulation, and other data for inorganic arsenic(III), inorganic arsenic(V), and the organic compounds [[Monosodium Methanearsonate]] (MSMA) and disodium methylarsonate (DSMA). The document is mostly a scientific assessment with a regulatory summary. Fish are only part of the evidence; invertebrates and algae supply much of the data.

The main finding is that inorganic arsenic(III) is the best-characterized form. Freshwater criteria are a four-day average of 190 µg/L (not to be exceeded more than once every three years) and a one-hour average of 360 µg/L. Saltwater criteria are about 36 µg/L (four-day) and 69 µg/L (one-hour). The authors recommend measuring [[Acid-Soluble Arsenic]] rather than total recoverable arsenic, but no approved method for it existed at the time, so total recoverable arsenic was used for the criteria.

## Background & Description

- **Legal basis:** Section 304(a)(1) of the Clean Water Act requires EPA to publish criteria reflecting the best scientific knowledge of effects of pollutants on aquatic life. These criteria replace earlier EPA aquatic life criteria for arsenic (1976, 1980). Once adopted by states as water quality standards under Section 303(c), they become enforceable.
- **Chemistry:** Arsenic occurs in all living organisms. Arsenic(III) reacts with sulfhydryl groups of proteins, and enzyme inhibition is probably its main toxic mode. Arsenic(V) uncouples oxidative phosphorylation. Inorganic arsenic(III) oxidizes slowly to arsenic(V) at neutral pH and more rapidly in strongly acidic or alkaline solutions.
- **Methods:** Total recoverable arsenic cannot distinguish arsenic(III) from arsenic(V). Acid-soluble arsenic (filtered after acidification to pH 1.5–2.0) was judged the best practical measure, but EPA had not yet approved a method for it.
- **Methodology:** The criteria follow the [[Guidelines For Deriving Numerical National Water Quality Criteria]] (Stephan et al., 1985). Final Acute Values and Final Chronic Values were derived from species mean data, and acute-chronic ratios were used to link them. The literature search covered data through May 1984. Many studies were excluded for reasons such as unacceptable control survival, unclear test concentrations, or non-North American species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Speciation:** The toxicity of the forms of arsenic varies by species. In general, arsenic(III) was more toxic than arsenic(V) to fish such as the rainbow trout and fathead minnow, while arsenic(V) was more toxic to some invertebrates such as the saltwater amphipod.
- **Environmental modifiers:** Water temperature increased the toxicity of arsenic(V) to green sunfish. Food availability reduced the toxicity of arsenic(III) to Daphnia by about threefold. Phosphate decreased the toxicity of arsenic(V) to a marine red macroalga but did not change arsenic(III) toxicity.
- **Bioaccumulation:** Arsenic is not strongly bioconcentrated in fish. The highest bioconcentration factor (BCF) was 17 for inorganic arsenic(III) in a snail, and a BCF of 350 was reported for the eastern oyster after 112 days. Fish tissue has a low bioconcentration factor and a short half-life, so residues are unlikely to threaten predators.
- **Environmental fate:** Arsenic's effects depend on pH, redox potential (Eh), organic content, suspended solids, and sediment. Buffering capacity in the carbonate system is higher at pH 1.5–2.0 than at pH 8–9, so small pH differences in the acid range should not substantially change results.

## Key Findings

**Acute toxicity to aquatic animals**

- **Inorganic arsenic(III), freshwater:** Sixteen freshwater species were tested. Species Mean Acute Values (SMAVs) ranged from about 812 µg/L (a cladoceran) to about 971,000 µg/L (a midge). The Final Acute Value (FAV) is **718.2 µg/L**. The fathead minnow and bluegill were among the more sensitive fish.
- **Inorganic arsenic(III), saltwater:** Twelve saltwater species were tested, with values from 232 µg/L to 161,030 µg/L. The saltwater FAV is **137.1 µg/L**, about one-half of the lowest Species Mean Acute Value. The Pacific oyster and Dungeness crab zoeae were among the more sensitive species.
- **Inorganic arsenic(V):** Six species in five genera were tested, with values from 850 µg/L (a cladoceran) to 491,000 µg/L (mosquitofish). Not enough data were available to calculate freshwater or saltwater FAVs for arsenic(V).
- **MSMA:** Eight freshwater species were tested, with values ranging from about 31,243 µg/L to 1,403,000 µg/L (values as reported in the OCR text). Bluegill was least sensitive to MSMA among those tested. The fathead minnow was about 12 times more sensitive to MSMA than to sodium arsenate. Not enough data were available to derive a FAV.

**Chronic toxicity to aquatic animals**

- **Fathead minnow, arsenic(III):** A life-cycle test gave a chronic value of **914.1 µg/L**, based on chronic limits of 633.0 and 1,320 µg/L.
- **Freshwater Final Chronic Value (FCV):** Based on the acute-chronic ratio (geometric mean 3.803), the freshwater FCV for arsenic(III) is **188.9 µg/L**.
- **Saltwater chronic data:** A 35-day mysid life-cycle test showed no significant effects at 631 µg/L, while reproduction and survival were affected at a higher concentration. This yielded a chronic value of 895.2 µg/L, which is far above the saltwater FCV (about 36 µg/L), so the saltwater FCV relies on the acute-chronic ratio approach.
- **Arsenic(V):** A fathead minnow early life-stage test gave a chronic value of 891.6 µg/L (lower and upper limits 530 and 1,500 µg/L). Daphnia showed similar sensitivity to both arsenic forms.
- **MSMA and other organic forms:** No chronic tests were available.

**Toxicity to aquatic plants**

- Effects on algae and plants from arsenic(III) occurred at concentrations as low as about 2,320 µg/L for some species, and to over 59,000 µg/L for *Selenastrum capricornutum*.
- The most sensitive freshwater alga, *Selenastrum capricornutum*, had a 14-day EC50 of **48 µg/L** for arsenic(V). Diatom growth was reduced at 20 and 22 µg/L, and photosynthesis at 19 µg/L.
- Arsenic(III) and arsenic(V) were about equally toxic to the saltwater alga *Champia parvula*.

**Bioaccumulation**

- Freshwater bioconcentration tests with arsenic(III), arsenic(V), and organic arsenic compounds gave a highest BCF of 17 (snail).
- A saltwater BCF of 350 was obtained for the eastern oyster after 112 days.
- No Final Residue Value could be determined because no maximum permissible tissue concentration was available.

**Other findings**

- Early life-stage tests were the most sensitive indicator. Values for embryos and larvae of the toad (40 µg/L) were about 4.5 times lower than the freshwater FCV.
- Daphnia, Gammarus, and other invertebrates were grouped as sensitive taxa in the criteria derivation.

**Criteria recommendations**

- **Freshwater:** A four-day average of **190 µg/L** and a one-hour average of **360 µg/L** for arsenic(III), each not to be exceeded more than once every three years on average. The criteria might be too high if locally important species (e.g., [[Skeletonema Costatum]]) are very sensitive.
- **Saltwater:** A four-day average of **36 µg/L** and a one-hour average of **69 µg/L**, on the same frequency basis.
- **Insufficient data:** Criteria for arsenic(V) and organic arsenic compounds could not be derived. Arsenic(V) is acutely toxic to freshwater animals at concentrations as low as 2,319 µg/L (saltwater values are similar).
- **Design flows:** EPA recommends 1Q5 or 1Q10 as the design flow for CMC (acute) and 7Q5 or 7Q10 for CCC (chronic) in steady-state models.

**Recommendation on measurement**

EPA recommends using acid-soluble arsenic in future criteria but applied total recoverable arsenic until an approved method was available. Acid-soluble measurements may be more scientifically sound and require less filtering and digestion than total recoverable methods. Total recoverable measurement may overestimate or underestimate risk, depending on the arsenic species present.
