# Ecological Notes on Parasites of Fishes from Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the parasites of seven native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a relatively undisturbed Arizona stream with almost no exotic fishes. Ten parasite species were recorded: six protozoans, one digenean, one cestode and two nematodes. The work focuses on host specificity, site specificity, and seasonal changes in infection levels. Key results include the apparent maintenance of [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] by a pool-preferring sucker, strong organ and host specificity among [[Myxobolus]] species, age-related resistance to myxosporidians, two annual reproductive cycles in [[Isoglaridacris Bulbocirrus]], and [[Pantosteus clarki]] identified as the primary host of [[Rhabdochona Decaturensis]].

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek is one of the few Arizona streams with seven native fishes and essentially no exotic species. Five minnows (*Agosia chrysogaster*, *Gila robusta*, *Meda fulgida*, *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Tiaroga cobitis*) and two suckers (*Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*) are present. Earlier natural history work, especially by Minckley (1981), provided the background for this parasitological study. The main objective was to analyze host-parasite relationships in terms of host specificity and temporal variation in infection levels.

**Methods (summary):**
- A preliminary sampling trip in February 1980 was used to refine techniques. Only reliable data from it were used.
- Fish were collected by [[Seining]] (4–5 m seines, 6 mm mesh) and by 110 V AC or DC [[Electrofishing]]. Stunned fish were captured with the seines.
- Collecting trips from July 1980 to July 1981 were timed to seasonal water levels and temperatures (Siebert 1980). Mean temperatures were 23.93 ± 2.02 °C in July 1980 (high summer flows), 16.64 ± 0.96 °C in fall, and 13.10 ± 2.17 °C in winter.
- Fish were examined for parasites. Ectoparasites were damaged in handling and were not included.
- Myxosporidian cysts were crushed and examined on slides. Other parasites were fixed, stained and identified by standard parasitological methods.
- Prevalence, intensity and incidence were analyzed with Pearson's product-moment correlations, chi-square tests and contingency tables. The significance level was α = 0.05.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Parasite community.** The 10 species belong to six genera. Appendix 1 lists each fish with its parasites, the number of fish examined, prevalence, and site of infection. Key points:
- *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* (ciliate) occurred in all seven fish species.
- *Myxobolus* spp. were found only in suckers and one minnow. *M. catostomi* and *M. discrepans* were in *C. insignis*, *M. nodularis* was in *C. insignis* (body musculature), *M. oblongus* was in *P. clarki* (pectoral and pelvic fins), and *M. macrocapsularis* was in *A. chrysogaster* (gills).
- *Ornithodiplostomum ptychocheilus* (metacercariae, mesenteries) was found in *G. robusta*.
- *Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus* (cestode, intestine) occurred in *G. robusta*, *C. insignis* and *P. clarki*.
- *Rhabdochona decaturensis* (nematode, intestine) occurred in five hosts, and *Dacnitoides* sp. was recorded in *G. robusta*.

**Host ecology.** Pool-preferring habits and the slow water in pools may increase exposure of pool-dwelling suckers to infective spores. Dead fish also accumulate in pools. *P. clarki* is herbivorous, while *C. insignis* and *G. robusta* are largely carnivorous (Minckley 1973; Schreiber and Minckley 1981). Diet appears to affect infection: the carnivorous diet of *G. robusta* may allow cestode establishment, and the herbivorous diet of *P. clarki* may reduce ingestion of intermediate hosts.

**Seasonal and environmental factors.** Floods may remove invertebrate intermediate hosts (such as mayflies) and may cause fish losses. Winter temperatures and floods were associated with some low-infection phases, but other cycles showed no clear link to temperature or flow.

## Key Findings

**1. Host and site specificity**
- Myxosporidia were highly host- and site-specific. Each species was tied to a particular host and organ. Two unrelated hosts can share a myxosporidian, but the infection occurs in the same organ.
- *C. insignis* and *P. clarki* had no myxosporidians in common, which the author interpreted as evidence for physiological differences. This supports retaining *Pantosteus* at generic rank (Minckley 1973) rather than treating it as a subgenus of *Catostomus* (Smith 1966).

**2. Age-related resistance**
- Myxosporidian incidence in *C. insignis* rose with fish size to a maximum and then declined. Larger fish showed resistance, which the author attributed to acquired immunity or age-related tissue changes.

**3. Temporal dynamics of myxosporidians**
- Over one year, *M. catostomi* was low when *M. discrepans* was high, and vice versa. The author concluded that the parasites were temporally adapted to their niches independently, not competing directly, because the same fish were often infected with both.
- *M. nodularis* had two periods of low incidence, in autumn (September–October) and early summer (May–June). The cause remained speculative. Temperature did not explain the pattern because incidence was high in both December and July.
- Migration did not explain the lows: incidence was high in winter, when fish left the canyon. Differential mortality of young fish after floods was proposed but untested.

**4. Life cycle of *Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus***
- The cestode showed a clear specificity for catostomids (*C. insignis* and *P. clarki*), with *G. robusta* as the only cyprinid infected by adults.
- Worms were absent in September–October and May–June. This indicated two recruitment phases per year, each followed by maturation and loss of worms, in both *C. insignis* and *G. robusta*.
- With *C. insignis*, infection intensity correlated positively with fish size (r = 0.5477, as printed). The author suggested that larger fish eat more and so ingest more intermediate hosts.

**5. *Rhabdochona decaturensis* and primary host status**
- Infections in *C. insignis* and *P. clarki* were followed for a year. Each host had four alternating phases of high and low intensity, which indicated two generations per year (spring and summer).
- Infection in *C. insignis* lagged behind *P. clarki*, so the author suggested the cycle reflects parasite-intrinsic processes and host physiology rather than external factors.
- Intensity did not differ significantly between the two suckers, but incidence was higher in *P. clarki*. The author identified *P. clarki* as the primary host for *R. decaturensis* in Aravaipa Creek. Infection levels correlated with fish size in both *C. insignis* (r = 0.2129) and *P. clarki* (a larger r, printed garbled as "O.SSS").
- Other infections in *A. chrysogaster*, *M. fulgida* and *G. robusta* were rare and probably accidental.

**6. Flow-rate analysis of host roles (Table 3)**
Using the flow-rate model of Holmes et al. (1977), the author estimated transmission among hosts:

| Measure | *C. insignis* | *P. clarki* | Other minnows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incidence (%) | 58.01 | 74.12 | 0.1 |
| Mean intensity | 8.6 | 7.24 | 1.3 |
| Relative fish population size H (%) | 10.9 | 16.3 | 72.8 |
| Proportion of gravid worms (%) | 32.98 | 33.9 | 20.0 |
| Flow rate from host R | 0.3767 | 0.6229 | 0.0004 |
| Flow rate to host R′ | 0.3831 | 0.6162 | 0.0007 |

*P. clarki* had the highest flow of worms through it, which supports its role as the primary host. The author also noted that *C. insignis*, with its carnivorous diet, would be expected to have higher abundance. Low abundance there implies low establishment success or high worm mortality.

**7. Myxosporidian sample sizes**
Captions for Figures 1 and 2 give the counts of infected *C. insignis* as 88 fish with *M. discrepans*, 22 with *M. catostomi*, and 100 or 109 with *M. nodularis* (the two captions disagree). The sample numbers should be checked against the original.

**8. Scope and conclusions**
- The author concluded that parasite populations and host ecology are closely linked, and that regulatory mechanisms in the primary host may control a multi-host parasite system.
- Cycles of recruitment, maturation and mortality were seen in several helminths, and the author noted that the cycles here appear to be generated by two phases of mortality.
- The work was based on a dissertation (Mpoame 1981) and was published in the *Journal of the Arizona-Nevada Academy of Science* (vol. 17, 1982).
