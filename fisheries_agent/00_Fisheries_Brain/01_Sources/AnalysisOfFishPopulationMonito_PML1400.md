# Analysis of Fish Population Monitoring Data for Selected Waters of the Gila River Basin, Arizona, 1995–1999

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] by Paul C. Marsh and Brian R. Kesner, analyzes five years (1995–1999) of standardized fish monitoring from natural streams and canals in the [[Gila River Basin]] of central Arizona. Monitoring was required under a [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] biological opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), which identified four federally listed native fishes at risk. The authors conclude that the monitoring design can detect only large-scale changes, that the [[ANCOVA]] trend models fit poorly, and that hypothesis-driven analysis and multivariate methods are needed to improve future trend detection.

## Background & Description

The CAP canal system delivers water from [[Lake Havasu]] to the Gila River basin and could spread nonindigenous fishes and other biota. A 1994 biological opinion determined that the project would jeopardize four federally listed native fishes: [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), and adversely affect critical habitat for the first three. A long-term monitoring plan (Clarkson 1996) was then implemented in 1995, covering six watercourses: the CAP aqueduct, Salt River Project (SRP) canals, Florence-Casa Grande (FCG) Canal, Salt River, Gila River, and perennial reaches of the San Pedro River.

Earlier assessments (Wilson 1996; Abarca and Allison 2000; Allison 2000) concluded that only large-scale changes in community structure would be detectable, because many species are rare and catch data are highly variable. Allison (2000) found that trends could be adequately described for only two of 17 species, [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Red Shiner]].

Methods used [[Backpack Electrofishing]] as the standard stream gear, supplemented by seining, trammel and hoop nets, and boat electrofishing. The canals were sampled mainly by boat electrofishing, entanglement and entrapment gear, and angling. Data were analyzed with ANCOVA following Allison (2000), with log+1 transformation, Durbin-Watson tests for autocorrelation, and Levene's test for homogeneity of variance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish communities in the arid Southwest are subject to stochastic variation and high disturbance. The authors note that this variability has long confounded sample-based analysis (Meffe and Minckley 1987; Velasco 1997), and their results matched this pattern. Natural streams held a mix of native fishes and introduced species from Africa, Asia, and the eastern and northwestern United States. Canals were dominated by introduced fishes, including [[Tilapia]] and carp. Barriers were used to limit the upstream spread of nonindigenous fish in the canals: an electrical fish barrier separated the upper and lower reaches of the FCG and SRP canals. The CAP canal has no electrical barrier.

Age 0 fish were excluded from analysis because recruitment varies strongly between years, qualitative records were common, and some collections did not separate age 0 from older fish.

## Key Findings

- **Data base:** 89 station samples from 1995 to 1999 were usable for stream analysis. Canal data were analyzed by graphical inspection only, because sample sizes were small and variation was extreme.
- **Stream fauna:** 24 fish taxa were recorded across the three natural streams (11 on the San Pedro, 17 on the Gila, 17 on the Salt). Five taxa are native to the Gila basin: *Agosia chrysogaster*, *Gila robusta*, *Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*, and a sucker hybrid. *Agosia chrysogaster* was the most abundant overall, followed by *Cyprinella lutrensis* and *Gambusia affinis*.
- **Significant trends:** *Agosia chrysogaster* and *Pantosteus clarki* declined significantly from 1995 to 1999, consistently across reaches and rivers. *Gambusia affinis* showed no consistent trend, and *Cyprinella lutrensis* trended differently among reaches. All other species showed no significant trend.
- **Model fit:** ANCOVA was an "ill fit" with numerous assumption violations. Durbin-Watson tests flagged significant autocorrelation for *Dorosoma petenense*, *Ameiurus melas*, and *Pantosteus clarki*. Levene's test showed heterogeneous variances for most species. For *Pantosteus clarki*, a significant year effect explained only 11% of variation.
- **Analytical artifacts:** The log+1 transformation can reverse trend direction when zeros are common. For *Cyprinella lutrensis* in the Gila River, raw counts increased while log+1 values decreased. Relative abundance removed some artifacts but violated normality and homoscedasticity.
- **Multivariate approach:** Non-metric multidimensional scaling (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity on log+1 abundances, 13 species) showed that between-station variation within some reaches was nearly as large as between-reach variation. The authors concluded that stations are weak sampling units and that habitat or environmental variables should be incorporated.
- **Canal patterns:** No consistent monotone temporal trend in catch or proportion of catch was identified for any species in any canal reach. The CAP canal had 15 species, with [[Bluegill]] the most abundant. The FCG canal had 12 species, with *Cyprinella lutrensis* the most abundant. The SRP Arizona canal had 25 species, with undetermined Tilapia the most abundant. The SRP South canal had 25 species, with undetermined Tilapia again the most abundant.
- **New records:** No new records for the Gila basin were documented, but several species were new to individual CAP monitoring stations, including [[Flathead Catfish]] (San Pedro River), [[White Bass]] (CAP canal), and [[Sailfin Molly]] (Salt River). One record of a black bullhead was re-identified as yellow bullhead (*Ameiurus natalis*).
- **Recommendations:** (1) Collect at least one sample per reach and station every year, to avoid zero or missing data. (2) Use at least one gear type suited to both large and small fish at each station, because gear selection creates size-based bias.
- **Conclusion:** Standardized monitoring provides a baseline for detecting future change, but the authors caution that changes may be difficult to attribute to CAP. They also state that demonstrating change is a prerequisite for management responses by resource agencies.
