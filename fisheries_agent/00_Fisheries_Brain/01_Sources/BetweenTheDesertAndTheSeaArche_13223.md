# Between the Desert and the Sea: Archaeological Research at Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument

#archaeology #cultural-ecology #desert-ecosystems #hohokam-culture #paleoindian

## Executive Summary

Archaeological survey conducted by the [[NPS Western Archaeological and Conservation Center]] at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] (ORPI) between 1989 and 1993 documented 12,000 years of continuous human occupation. The survey covered 8,000 acres (2% of the monument) and recorded 199 archaeological sites, substantially altering understanding of southwestern Arizona archaeology. The research demonstrates how humans adapted to environmental changes in the desert ecosystem and their effects on water resources, vegetation, and soil systems. Sites span from [[Paleoindian Period]] (ca. 10,000-8500 B.C.) through historic [[Hia C'ed O'odham]] occupation, with particular emphasis on [[Hohokam Culture]] reservoir-based agriculture and its long-term environmental impacts.

## Background & Description

The cultural component of ORPI ecosystems was largely unknown prior to this systematic survey. The stratified sample survey methodology integrated archaeological data collection with natural resource observations, documenting locations of tortoise carapaces, elephant trees, night-blooming cereus, and crested organ pipe cacti alongside cultural materials.

**Chronological Periods Documented:**

- **[[Paleoindian Period]]** (ca. 10,000-8500 B.C.): [[Clovis Tradition]] and [[San Dieguito Tradition]]. Single Clovis projectile point recovered from [[Growler Valley]]. Extinct cienega identified in [[Quitobaquito Basin]] surrounded by Paleoindian tools, suggesting potential mammoth remains (similar to finds at [[Quitovac]], Sonora, Mexico, 35 miles southeast).

- **[[Southwestern Archaic]]** (8500 B.C.-A.D. 300): Post-Pleistocene hunting-gathering adaptation with seasonally mobile groups exploiting wild plants and small game. Sites consist of chipped stone tools, manufacturing debris, roasting features, and sleeping circles. Archaic artifacts embedded in terrace deposits along [[Alamo Wash]] confirm mid-Holocene stabilization dates.

- **[[Prehistoric Ceramic Period]]** (A.D. 300-1450): Dominated by [[Hohokam Culture]] in the [[Papagueria]] peripheral region. Colonial period (A.D. 775-975) marked by red-on-buff ceramics along trails to [[Sea of Cortez]]. Sedentary period (A.D. 975-1150) saw expansion with shell manufacturing sites. Classic period (A.D. 1150-1400) showed radical reorganization with [[Tucson Basin]] emerging as second core area.

- **[[Protohistoric Period]]** (A.D. 1450-1700): Sites with ceramics similar to Tucson basin and San Pedro area, associated with [[O'odham Groups]].

- **Historic Period** (post-A.D. 1700): [[Hia C'ed O'odham]] villages, cemeteries, and shrines; Mikal Levy's store; Andrew Dorsey's 1860s canals and orchards at [[Quitobaquito]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The research addresses critical questions about the [[Quitobaquito Ecosystem]]:

- Is the present pond a remnant of a much larger cienega?
- Were there shifts in spring locations with concomitant changes in cienega locations?
- Were changes related to groundwater aquifer shifts, climatic changes, or human use?

**Water Resources:** [[Hohokam Reservoirs]] at ORPI were wells dug to intercept shallow groundwater tables. In areas where permanent water supplies were almost nonexistent, reservoirs provided stable water sources enabling year-round occupation and centralized authority over labor forces.

**Environmental Impacts:** Greatest degree of erosion occurs in areas of large Hohokam villages. Long-term use resulted in soil compaction; abandonment of water control systems permitted large-scale sheetwash erosion. Study of prehistoric water-control technology is applicable to addressing current erosion problems.

**Paleoecology:** Paleoindian and Early Archaic site locations serve as predictive tools for identifying areas where springs may have once existed. The environment during these periods was substantially different from present conditions.

## Key Findings

**Survey Results:**
- 8,000 acres surveyed (2% of monument)
- 199 archaeological sites recorded
- 12,000 years of continuous human occupation documented

**Hohokam Settlement Patterns:**
- Earliest ORPI use during Colonial period (A.D. 775-975)
- Majority of sites date to Classic period (A.D. 1150-1400), indicating continued expansion in western Papagueria
- Large reservoir-based village identified in [[Valley of the Ajo]] surrounded by smaller villages
- Agriculture enabled by water collection from large watershed catchments and canal allocation requiring organized labor
- Shift from Phoenix basin to Tucson basin exchange networks indicated by ceramic assemblages
- Unnamed Red ware ceramic may have been manufactured locally
- [[Salado Culture]] polychrome ceramics indicate occupation into late Classic period (post-A.D. 1300)

**Cultural Continuity:**
- Trail through [[Bates Mountains]] used since Early Archaic (ca. 8500 B.C.) as main travel corridor between desert and Sea of Cortez
- Currently used by hikers and researchers

**Historic Period Discoveries:**
- Three Hia C'ed O'odham cemeteries recorded
- Previously unknown practice of cremation burial among O'odham documented
- First baseline information on Hia C'ed O'odham material culture of late 1800s
- European-derived goods (Levi buttons, mining crucibles, glass, cans) provide opportunity to examine cultures in contact

**Interdisciplinary Integration:**
- Archaeological database complements natural environment data
- Discourse between disciplines facilitated by holistic ecosystem approach
- Survey recorded both archaeological and natural resource data
- Proposed interdisciplinary project for test excavations in extinct cienega involving archaeologists, geomorphologists, hydrologists, paleobotanists, palynologists, and paleontologists

**Environmental Management Implications:**
- Understanding past helps manage fragile Quitobaquito ecosystem
- Study of paleohydrology applicable to current groundwater aquifer pumping problems
- Prehistoric water-control technology study may slow or stop continued erosion
