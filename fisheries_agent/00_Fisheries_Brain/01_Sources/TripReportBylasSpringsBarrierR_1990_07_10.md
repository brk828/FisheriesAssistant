# Trip Report: Bylas Springs Barrier Repair

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #freshwater-conservation #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This trip report documents a maintenance and repair action conducted on July 9, 1990, to address a leaking fish barrier at Bos/Medicine Springs, part of the [[Bylas Springs]] complex on the San Carlos Indian Reservation, Arizona. The barrier system was constructed to protect native [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) populations from upstream invasion by non-native [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Repair work included application of bentonite clay to seal underground leaks, removal of a salt cedar (*Tamarix* sp.) whose roots were compromising barrier integrity, and enlargement of spillway notches to reduce head pressure and clogging risk. No formal fish sampling was conducted, though a "large number" of poeciliid fish were observed in the impoundment, with no black males (a mosquitofish-typical trait) noted.

## Background & Description

- **Site complex**: Bylas Springs consists of three discrete native habitats for [[Gila Topminnow]]:
  - **Bos/Medicine Springs** complex
  - **Middle Spring**
  - **Salt Creek**
- **Species of concern**: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), a federally listed subspecies native to the Gila River Basin, endemic to isolated spring and stream habitats in Arizona and Sonora, Mexico.
- **Invasive threat**: [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), a congener known to competitively exclude and hybridize-displace native topminnow populations through aggressive behavior and reproductive interference.
- **Management history**:
  - Concrete barriers were constructed historically at all three sites to prevent upstream movement of mosquitofish.
  - In 1982, chemical fish eradication (piscicide treatment) and subsequent restocking of topminnow were conducted at Bos/Medicine Springs and Salt Creek.
  - The original barriers failed due to inadequate design — flow bypassed the structures, allowing mosquitofish reinvasion.
  - In 1989, rock-filled gabion wings were added to the Bos/Medicine Springs and Salt Creek barriers to rechannel flow over (rather than around) the structures.
  - Following gabion installation, new leakage developed at Bos/Medicine Springs beneath the concrete on the east side and beneath the spillway (between the vertical concrete wall and the concrete apron).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Population status at time of report**:
  - [[Gila Topminnow]] at **Middle Spring** were **extirpated in 1989**, likely due to extremely low spring flow (a hydrological/climatic stressor rather than direct invasive competition).
  - **Bos/Medicine Springs** and **Salt Creek** were contaminated with [[Western Mosquitofish]], indicating barrier failure had already allowed reinvasion prior to this repair effort.
- **Habitat vulnerability**: The case illustrates the fragility of isolated desert spring refugia for the [[Gila Topminnow]], where both invasive species incursion and natural flow variability (drought-driven low flow) threaten population persistence.
- **Vegetation/infrastructure interaction**: A salt cedar (*Tamarix* sp.), an invasive riparian tree, was found growing against the downstream face of the Bos/Medicine Springs barrier. Its root system was identified as both a structural threat to the dam and a probable conduit for underground water leakage — highlighting the intersection of invasive plant management with aquatic habitat barrier maintenance.
- **Barrier design lessons**: The original narrow V-shaped spillway notches were prone to clogging and inadequate flow control; enlarging them to U-shaped notches with a centered low point was intended to reduce head pressure and clogging risk, addressing a recurring engineering failure point in these small-scale conservation barriers.

## Key Findings

- **Repair actions taken at Bos/Medicine Springs** (July 9, 1990):
  1. Impounded area was pumped out to allow access to the barrier face.
  2. **1,200 lbs of bentonite clay** (Volclay SG-40) applied along the upstream dam face, mixed into mud in a 1–2 ft wide strip across the entire structure width, to seal seepage.
  3. Salt cedar tree removed from the downstream east face of the barrier; stump treated with **Tordon RTU herbicide** to prevent resprouting.
  4. Spillway notches on both the Bos/Medicine Springs and Salt Creek barriers were re-cut from narrow V-shapes to U-shapes with a centered low point.
- **Immediate outcome**: Leakage beneath the removed tree ceased by project completion. Leakage between the apron and barrier slowed substantially, though minor residual water replenishment in the downstream puddled area was still observed.
- **Fish observations** (non-systematic, incidental):
  - No formal fish sampling was performed.
  - A large number of poeciliid fish (unspecified species-level identification) were observed in the Bos/Medicine Springs impoundment and on the concrete apron.
  - **No black males were observed** — a detail likely referencing the melanistic male phenotype sometimes seen in [[Western Mosquitofish]], suggesting the observers were attempting a rough field assessment of whether mosquitofish were present versus topminnow.
- **Site not assessed**: Middle Spring was not visited due to time constraints, leaving its topminnow extirpation status from 1989 unconfirmed as still current.
- **Personnel/agencies involved**: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Ecological Services, Phoenix; San Carlos Indian Reservation), Arizona Game and Fish Department, with report circulated to Arizona State University researchers ([[Paul Marsh]], [[W.L. Minckley]]) and San Carlos Recreation and Wildlife, indicating this was part of a broader, multi-agency native fish conservation program for the [[Gila Topminnow]].
