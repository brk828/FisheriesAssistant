> **Note:** This source does not concern fish. It is a limnological study of two hypersaline desert ponds (Red Pond and Green Pond) at Kiatuthlanna, Arizona, describing water chemistry, halophytic flora, and an invertebrate-dominated faunal community centered on the brine shrimp [[Artemia salina]]. The note below is structured to reflect the source's actual focus on aquatic chemistry and invertebrate/algal biota rather than fish.

## Executive Summary

This paper is the second installment of a limnological appraisal of two adjacent (~0.4 km apart) salt ponds — **Red Pond** and **Green Pond** — at [[Kiatuthlanna]], a Zuni archaeological/mythological site in Apache County, Arizona. Building on physical data from Part I (Cole & Whiteside 1965), the authors present detailed **water chemistry** (Table I) collected in March, June, and August 1964, and describe the **halophytic vegetation** and **faunal assemblage** of each pond. Red Pond is markedly more saline (22%) than Green Pond (11%) and falls into Clarke's (1924) **chloro-carbonate** category, approaching but not reaching the extreme chloride category of true "soda"-type or chloride brines. Green Pond is relatively richer in carbonate, sitting on the boundary between carbonate-chloride and chloro-carbonate types. Both ponds are thermally stratified (dichothermic) with anaerobic, H₂S-rich hypolimnia in summer. The dominant macroscopic animal in both ponds is the brine shrimp **[[Artemia salina]]**, present in extraordinary densities and exhibiting an intensely red hemoglobin-rich coloration indicative of low dissolved oxygen. Other biota include brine flies (**Ephydra hians**), biting midges (**Culicoides variipennis sonorensis**), tabanid larvae, odonates, and incidental vertebrate mortalities (toads, a salamander, a coot). The green alga **Ctenocladus circinnatus** in Green Pond represents a notable, possibly third, North American record.

## Background & Description

While no fish are present, the paper details morphological and physiological traits of the key invertebrate and algal taxa:

- **[[Artemia salina]] (Brine Shrimp)**
  - Phyllopod crustacean (Anostraca/Branchiopoda).
  - Individuals from Kiatuthlanna exhibit an **intense red coloration**, attributed to high **hemoglobin** concentration (cf. Gilchrist 1954), a physiological adaptation to low dissolved oxygen in the anaerobic/hypoxic hypolimnetic and surface waters.
  - Green Pond's population appeared visually "redder" than Red Pond's when viewed en masse from the basin rim — the shrimp imparted a red hue to the water surface.
  - Distinguished from the pale, hemoglobin-poor Mono Lake, California population described historically by Mark Twain (Clemens 1872) as "white feathery" worms.
  - Physiological salt tolerance research (Croghan 1958; Martin & Wilbur 1921) indicates sensitivity to potassium and bicarbonate toxicity; survival requires **Na/K ratio > 10**.

- **Ephydra hians (Brine Fly)**
  - Pupae attach at right angles to submerged sticks and strands of *Distichlis*.
  - Larvae occupy moist pond margins and shallows.

- **Culicoides variipennis sonorensis (Ceratopogonid Midge)**
  - Larvae common in both ponds; adults are biting and considered a hardy species tolerant of polluted/saline waters.

- **Ctenocladus circinnatus (Green Alga)**
  - Formed macroscopic masses in Green Pond shallows, likely aggregated by wave action.
  - Represents a possible third North American occurrence record (after California and Saskatchewan).

- **Halophytic Flora**
  - *Scirpus validus* (Green Pond) — tall (~2 m), robust, gracefully curved, non-halophytic bulrush.
  - *Scirpus olneyi* (Red Pond) — dense, salt-tolerant bulrush; dominant along the west shore.
  - *Scirpus americanus* — abrupt replacement of *S. olneyi* along southwest shore of Red Pond.
  - *Juncus balticus* and *Distichlis spicata* (=*D. stricta*) form zonation bands correlating with salinity gradients, following patterns described by Bolen (1964) for Utah salt marshes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Two closed-basin, hypersaline desert ponds exhibiting **dichothermy** (thermal stratification) in spring/early summer, transitioning to circulation later in summer; deep waters are **anaerobic and sulfidic (H₂S)**, with sapropelic sediments devoid of macroscopic fauna.
- **Salinity range**: Red Pond ~22%, Green Pond ~11% at peak summer concentration; TDS values reaching >220,000 mg/L in Red Pond by August.
- **Chemical classification**: Waters progress along a concentration sequence from a dilute "caved-in well" source (Harrell & Eckel 1939) → Green Pond (carbonate-chloride boundary) → Red Pond (chloro-carbonate, approaching but not reaching extreme chloride brines like [[La Sal Del Rey]]).
- **Phosphate dynamics**: Extremely high orthophosphate values (240–250 mg/L in Red Pond), likely linked to **die-off and decomposition of Artemia populations** between June and August, releasing phosphorus and soluble iron into the water column — an invertebrate-mediated nutrient cycling process.
- **Faunal community structure**: A simplified, stress-tolerant assemblage typical of athalassohaline (non-marine-origin) hypersaline systems:
  - **Primary/dominant consumer**: *Artemia salina* — only known Arizona locality for this species (Koehn & Cole 1964).
  - **Associated insects**: *Ephydra hians* (brine fly), *Culicoides variipennis sonorensis* (biting midge), unidentified tabanid larvae.
  - **Transient/incidental fauna**: dragonflies and damselflies (non-breeding visitors, likely from nearby freshwater troughs/tanks), and occasional vertebrate casualties (*Bufo* sp. toads, *Ambystoma tigrinum* salamander, *Fulica americana* coot) found dead, presumably unable to tolerate the hypersaline conditions.
  - **No fish are reported or implied to occur** in either pond, consistent with the extreme salinity and periodic anoxia rendering the system unsuitable for fish.
- **Comparative hypersaline systems**: The Kiatuthlanna ponds are compared geochemically (via triangular anion plots) to other *Artemia*-bearing hypersaline waters worldwide, including [[Mono Lake]] (California), [[Great Salt Lake]] (Utah), Sambhar Salt Lake (India), Hot Lake (Washington), Tso Kar (Tibet), and Rezajeh-See (Iran) — situating Kiatuthlanna among a rare group of **carbonate-rich ("soda-type") brine shrimp habitats**, most similar chemically to Mono Lake.

## Key Findings

1. **No fish taxa are present or discussed** in this source; the pond ecosystems are dominated by brine shrimp, brine flies, and biting midges — organisms adapted to extreme salinity and periodic anoxia.
2. Both ponds are **chemically distinct hypersaline environments**: Red Pond (chloro-carbonate, higher salinity, higher Ca/Mg ratio) versus Green Pond (carbonate-chloride boundary, lower salinity, near-zero Ca/Mg).
3. A clear **evaporative concentration sequence** is documented from a dilute historical well sample (1934) through Green Pond to Red Pond, with a marked relative decline in sulfate (likely due to precipitation of Na₂SO₄ and CaSO₄ upon reaching solubility limits) and a shift toward chloride dominance.
4. **[[Artemia salina]]** populations show striking density and health differences between ponds and sampling periods: Green Pond's August population was thriving (imparting a red hue visible from a distance), while Red Pond showed signs of a population crash (many pale corpses) despite ongoing copulation in both ponds.
5. The intense **red hemoglobin pigmentation** of Kiatuthlanna's *Artemia* is interpreted as evidence of chronic low dissolved oxygen stress in these ponds, distinguishing them from paler populations elsewhere (e.g., Mono Lake).
6. Decomposing *Artemia* die-offs are proposed as a plausible mechanism driving **sharp seasonal increases in orthophosphate and soluble iron** concentrations observed between March/June and August samples — an invertebrate population serving as an active biogeochemical nutrient source.
7. Geochemically, Kiatuthlanna's waters (along with Mono Lake) are unusual among known *Artemia* habitats in having **higher carbonate content**, approaching "soda-type" lake chemistry — a condition Macan (1963) noted is generally inhospitable to Artemia elsewhere, making Kiatuthlanna ecologically distinctive.
8. Vegetation zonation (bulrush species *Scirpus validus*, *S. olneyi*, *S. americanus*; *Juncus balticus*; *Distichlis spicata*) tracks salinity gradients consistent with the tolerance hierarchy established by Bolen (1964) for Utah salt marshes.
