# Denver Wildlife Research Center: Balancing Wildlife and Human Interests (1968)

#wildlife-management #vertebrate-pest-control #ornithology #mammalogy

## Executive Summary

This 1968 Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife publication (Resource Publication 46) describes the work of the [[Denver Wildlife Research Center]], one of 24 federal wildlife research centers. The document is a program overview rather than a research paper. It covers control of nuisance birds and mammals, humane [[Coyote Control]], waterfowl disease, [[Safer Pesticides]] research, international pest-control aid, and wildlife management on public lands. No fish, aquatic invertebrates, plankton, or water chemistry data are presented; the aquatic-relevant content is limited to waterfowl botulism and rodent/pesticide effects on the wider food chain. No quantitative study results are reported beyond historical and general figures.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The publication frames wildlife management as a balance between conserving wildlife and meeting the needs of a growing human population. It states that the goal is "practical and humane ways of balancing the population ledger so that all species—including man—can live in harmony."
- **History:** Federal wildlife damage research began in 1912, when the Bureau of Biological Survey started a [[Ground Squirrel]] control program on national forests. A laboratory was founded at Albuquerque, New Mexico, in 1920, moved to Denver in 1922, and renamed the Denver Wildlife Research Center in 1960.
- **Staffing:** The Center employs over 90 professional and 30 support staff.
- **Location:** Building BY 16, Denver Federal Center, Denver, Colorado 80225.
- **Parent agency:** The [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], within the [[U.S. Department of the Interior]] (created 1849).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Human-wildlife conflict:** Animals such as starlings, grackles, blackbirds, coyotes, bobcats, bears, rodents, and nutria damage croplands, feedlots, livestock, trees, and water impoundments. Game species such as deer, elk, and antelope can also damage crops and orchards.
- **Food-chain effects:** Persistent pesticides can build up in the environment and enter milk, eggs, fish, and other foods consumed by humans and wildlife. Research aims to identify chemicals that are lethal to specific pests but harmless to other organisms.
- **Waterfowl disease:** [[Avian Botulism]] is triggered by a bacterial toxin. Outbreaks are linked to soil, food, water, and other environmental conditions, which the Center is studying.
- **Public lands:** Watershed, timber, and forage lands provide habitat for elk, deer, coyotes, squirrels, rabbits, prairie dogs, game birds, and songbirds. Management depends on knowing species' life histories and habitat needs.

## Key Findings

**1. Nuisance bird and mammal control**
- Birds such as starlings, grackles, and blackbirds cause economic losses and health problems. Control is targeted to each species' vulnerabilities, studied through physiology, feeding, reproduction, and movement.
- Researchers test lethal chemicals, trapping innovations, reproductive control, habitat manipulation, frightening devices, barriers, and repellents.
- For mammals, the work includes selective lethal baits (designed to be taken only by a target species) and screening of industry-submitted chemicals as repellents or selective agents.

**2. Rodents as pests**
- The publication states that rats have caused more deaths and damage to mankind than any other wild mammal and continue to spread disease and cause agricultural losses "totaling billions of dollars."
- A Hawaiian project is studying three rat species that damage sugar cane and other crops.
- A cable-protection project seeks repellents to stop burrowing rodents such as pocket gophers from gnawing underground communication and power lines.

**3. Humane coyote management**
- The Center is testing reproductive inhibitors in baits. Females that eat them become temporarily infertile, which can lower populations without killing animals. The publication says feasibility is still being determined.

**4. Golden eagle controversy**
- Livestock interests want eagles controlled because of predation on sheep and goats. Conservationists argue the species is declining and needs stronger protection.
- Studies use census data, migration patterns, and food habits. The work is done with the [[National Audubon Society]] and livestock associations.

**5. Safer pesticides**
- Insecticides protect crops but can harm fish, birds, mammals, and humans. The Center is identifying chemicals that target specific pests with minimal effects on other life.

**6. International aid**
- Rodents in some regions destroy more than 50 percent of rice and other food crops. Weaver finches in Africa threaten cereal crops. Vampire bat rabies kills "hundreds of thousands of cattle" each year in Central and South America.
- A 1967 agreement between the [[Agency for International Development]] and the Department of the Interior launched international research, headquartered at Denver, which employs U.S. and foreign scientists.

**7. Waterfowl botulism**
- In 1932 a single outbreak in the Bear River Delta, Utah, killed about 250,000 ducks. The disease has been reported from Canada to Mexico.
- Antitoxin can treat affected birds, but the publication states the greater need is prevention.

**8. Public lands research**
- Field stations in nine states (Arizona, Colorado, Minnesota, Montana, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Texas, Utah, and Oregon) support work on herbicide effects on forest and range wildlife, the role of rodents and porcupines in tree regeneration, and big game food needs.
- Turkey population studies in Arizona are intended to inform management in other Western states. Work on game birds, including grouse, turkey, and waterfowl, seeks to integrate vegetation and bird management.
