# Parker II Channel Modification Project: Draft Environmental Assessment and Agency Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This note covers a 1990 draft Environmental Assessment (EA) prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] for the Parker II Channel Modification Project on the Lower Colorado River, together with a cover letter and an agency review letter from Paul F. Marsh (Associate Research Professor, Arizona State University Center for Environmental Studies). The project proposes to stabilize eroding banklines, control river width with permeable training structures and jetties, and reduce sediment delivery to downstream users. Three alternatives were evaluated: Alternative A (Stabilize in Place, the agency-preferred option), Alternative B (Channel Realignment using dry cuts), and Alternative C (Stabilize as Required, i.e., no-action continuation). The review letter challenges the fish-community data used in the assessment, the omission of [[Razorback Sucker]] (a proposed federally endangered species), and the absence of discussion of proposed [[Colorado Squawfish]] reintroduction.

## Background & Description

The Parker II Division runs roughly 29 river miles. Bankline erosion contributes an estimated 135,000 to 615,000 cubic yards of sediment per year, with property losses of about 4 to 20 acres per year and erosion rates of 2 to 68 feet per year. Sediment removal downstream costs an estimated $236,000 to $1,000,000 per year at $1.75 per cubic yard. The project is authorized under the [[Colorado River Front Work and Levee System]] and was accompanied by a Clean Water Act Section 402/404 permitting process.

Fish sampling by [[Hiebert and Grabowski]] (1987) recorded 17 introduced fish species across eight habitat types (riprap, two cutbank types, cattail-bulrush, riparian structure, sand beach, mixed habitat, and the Poston Wastewater outflow). The dominant species were bluegill and common carp. Sampling covered 1983 to 1985.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation:** Mapping in 1986 recorded 18,753 acres of riparian vegetation, including 7,533 acres of salt cedar and 5,237 acres of screwbean mesquite. Salt cedar and arrowweed increased substantially since 1981.
- **Wildlife:** The flood plain supports about 200 bird species, 13 small mammal species, 14 large mammal species, and 32 reptile and amphibian species.
- **Special status species:** Twenty-seven special status species occur in the general area. Informal consultation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] identified the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and [[Bald Eagle]] as federally listed species potentially affected. The Razorback Sucker was considered possibly extirpated from the project area.
- **Water quality:** The river was well mixed; side channels and slack waters did not differ significantly from the main channel. The Poston Wasteway differed. Protected uses under Arizona standards include full-body contact, aquatic and wildlife uses, and domestic and agricultural supply.
- **Hydrology:** Expected annual average flows are about 17,000 cfs, roughly 3,000 cfs above pre-Lake Powell levels, which the document expects to sustain erosion in targeted areas.
- **Historical and cultural resources:** The flood plain has a long record of use, from Archaic-period camps through the Patayan culture (A.D. 700 to about 1540) and later European contact beginning in 1540. Many floodplain sites are no longer relocatable because of river processes and human activity.
- **Recreation:** Boating, fishing, swimming, hiking, and riverside residential use are increasing; the area is developing as a major recreational zone.

## Key Findings

**Fish community and habitat effects (from the Hiebert and Grabowski data):**

- Converting cutbank to riprap is expected to increase bluegill (+19%), carp (+58%), and smallmouth bass (+38%), keep largemouth bass similar, and decrease green sunfish (-50%) and channel catfish (-100%). Flathead catfish CPUE was 0.05 for both habitats.
- Deep backwater riparian habitat produced much higher overall CPUE than other habitats. Bluegill averaged 13.09 per 100 m; largemouth bass (+597%), bluegill (+347%), and carp (+369%) were projected to rise relative to cutbank.
- Using the combined projection (Table 11), the estimated expected catch by electrofishing rises from about 195 to 325 fish per 100 m of bankline across the affected reaches, with bluegill increasing by about 109 percent.
- Cattail-bulrush habitat is expected to serve as a nursery area for fish and crayfish.

**Alternative comparison:**

- Alternative A: about 79 acres of vegetation removed; 18 acres of revegetation planned; about 50 acres of new marsh/backwater habitat expected. Overall fishery effects are expected to be beneficial if backwaters are maintained.
- Alternative B: about 329 acres of vegetation removed; 83 acres of revegetation; about 190 acres of new marsh/backwater habitat. Dry cuts require archaeological test trenching and monitoring.
- Alternative C: impacts unknown at present and deferred to case-by-case compliance documents. Marsh and sandbar habitat would gradually decline.

**Reviewer concerns (Marsh, 1990):**

- Fish data collected in 1983 to 1985 may not reflect current conditions; the reviewer urged the Bureau to re-survey fish in the project area.
- The Razorback Sucker, under federal listing review, was not adequately evaluated, and the reviewer provided recent literature and offered contacts at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Arizona offices.
- The 1987 proposal to reintroduce Colorado Squawfish as an experimental non-essential population was not discussed in the assessment, though that species would receive protection under the Endangered Species Act.

**Document-stated status of the Razorback Sucker:** The assessment excluded it from consideration based on unsuccessful reintroduction efforts by the California Department of Fish and Game in 1987 and 1988 (Langhorst, 1989).

**Mosquitoes:** Backwaters deeper than two feet and open to flow are not predicted to increase mosquito populations.

**Permits and agreements:** Clean Water Act Sections 402 and 404 permits, an Arizona Department of Environmental Quality water quality certification, and a right-of-way agreement with the Colorado River Indian Tribes are required.
