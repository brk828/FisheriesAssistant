> **Note:** This source is an entomological (not ichthyological) study of Australian stonefly (Plecoptera) nymphs and their freshwater stream ecosystem. Framing below preserves the paper's actual taxonomic and ecological focus.

## Executive Summary

This 1964 preliminary report by H. B. N. Hynes documents the nymphal biology and taxonomy of Plecoptera (stoneflies) collected from Australian freshwater streams, primarily via twelve monthly samples from **[[Monbulk Creek]]** near Melbourne, Victoria, supplemented by casual collections from Victoria and Tasmania. The study is notable for being conducted almost entirely on **immature (nymphal) life stages**, as adults proved extremely difficult to collect or rear — a recurring problem attributed to the crypticity of Australian stoneflies in a dry climate. Key taxa discussed include **[[Eustheniidae]]** (likely *Eustheniopsis*), **[[Spaniocerca]]** (Notonemourinae), several **[[Gripopterygidae]]** (including *Leptoperla* and *Dinotoperla*), and rare **[[Penturoperlidae]]** nymphs whose morphology suggests a phylogenetic link to the Japanese family **[[Scopuridae]]**. The paper emphasizes life-history timing (voltinism), gill morphology and ontogeny, feeding ecology, and cross-continental morphological convergences among stonefly nymphs.

## Background & Description

### Eustheniidae (probable *Eustheniopsis* sp.)
- Nymphs possess **six lateral abdominal gill filaments**, present even in the smallest instars (1–2 mm), a diagnostic feature of the subfamily Eustheniinae — unlike Nemouridae/Perlidae, gill filament number does **not increase with growth**.
- Gill structure changes ontogenetically:
  - Small/young nymphs: long, simple, laterally extended filaments, pale green.
  - Half-grown nymphs: gills become **constricted at intervals** ("string of beads" appearance).
  - Large nymphs (>15 mm): gills turn **under the abdomen** and colour shifts to **purple**.
- Largest specimens recorded: 28–30 mm, nearing emergence with visible wing pad enation.
- Noted strong general resemblance to **[[Perlodidae]]** nymphs; author speculates that tergal processes in adult *Isoperla digitata* (Kawai 1963) could be phylogenetic remnants of Eustheniid-type gills.

### Spaniocerca (Notonemourinae)
- Likely monospecific in Monbulk Creek, tentatively identified as **S. tillyardi**.
- Life-history data derived from size-frequency distributions across 159 specimens.

### Gripopterygidae
- Commonest species (possibly **[[Leptoperla]]**) — 138 specimens sampled monthly; species boundaries within the sample uncertain (may include >1 species).
- A distinct, rarer Gripopterygid (possibly **[[Dinotoperla]]**) shows a novel morphological adaptation: **broad, flattened body with expanded femora**, structurally reminiscent of Ecdyonuridae (mayflies) or the Tricorythid mayfly genus *Dicercomyzon* — described as a previously unreported modification type within Plecoptera.

### Penturoperlidae
- Only 12 specimens collected, ranging **3.5–16.5 mm**, several in poor condition.
- Possibly the nymph of **[[Eunotoperla]]** (per Illies 1960b), supported by comparison with known adult wing length (25–30 mm, Tillyard 1924).
- Retains **archaic features**: unpaired filum terminalis, paraprocts drawn into beaded filaments.
- Closely resembles South American **[[Klapopteryx]]** and, strikingly, Japanese **[[Scopura]]** nymphs in ornate notal structure.
- Comparative dissection of *Scopura* nymphs (courtesy Dr. Kawai) revealed previously overlooked structures (missed by Uéno 1929): a filum terminalis and small paraproctal filaments — supporting a proposed evolutionary link:
  > "the Scopuridae are a sort of missing link between Penturoperlidae and Gripopterygidae."
- Tasmanian material includes 7-tailed Penturoperlid nymphs with an extra beaded filament flanking the filum terminalis and a long tapered tergal spine — interpreted as intermediate morphology supporting the Penturoperlidae–Scopuridae–Gripopterygidae lineage hypothesis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: [[Monbulk Creek]] — small, swift, sandy-bottomed stream with heavy vegetable debris load; temperature range ~9°C (winter, July) to ~18°C (summer, February).
- **Co-occurring fauna**: dominated by Gammaridae (Crustacea) and Leptophlebiidae (mayflies); also includes Baetidae, Siphlonuridae, Aeschnidae (dragonflies), Corixidae, Hydropsychidae, Leptoceridae, Psephenidae, Dryopidae, Helmidae, Hydrophilidae, Limoniinae, Orthocladiinae, Tanypodinae, Rhagionidae, and Sphaeriidae (bivalves).
- **Trophic role**: Eustheniid nymphs are **entirely carnivorous** — gut analysis of 10 specimens (7–20 mm) revealed Leptophlebiidae (7 of 10), chironomid larvae (4), plus a gammarid, tubificid, and lumbriculid. Described as likely the **major invertebrate predator** in the stream, ecologically parallel to New Zealand's *Stenoperla prasina*.
- **Life-history/voltinism patterns**:
  - **Eustheniidae**: multi-year life cycle (>1 year); nymphs hatch winter/spring, reach ~9 mm after one year, ~20 mm by the following winter, emerge mid-summer — consistent with long-lived carnivorous Eustheniidae (cf. Helson 1934 on *Stenoperla prasina*, 2–3 year cycle), contrasting with shorter-lived carnivorous Perlodidae.
  - **Spaniocerca**: hatch in spring (possibly earlier, evading capture as very small nymphs over winter), grow through summer, emerge in autumn (March–April) — **opposite seasonal pattern** to Northern Hemisphere winter-growing Capniidae. Compared to South American Notonemourinae (Illies 1961), which show broad, overlapping adult emergence periods across many months.
  - **Gripopterygidae (Leptoperla-type)**: absent March–June; cast skins/ripe nymphs found August, September, December, February — suggesting either a **prolonged emergence period** or possibly **two generations per year**, which would be unusual/unique among temperate stoneflies if confirmed.

## Key Findings

1. **Nymph-based taxonomy under constraint**: Nearly all identifications remain tentative because adults could not be collected or reared, despite experienced collecting efforts — suggesting Australian stoneflies may be unusually cryptic as adults in arid climates.
2. **Ontogenetic gill transformation in Eustheniidae**: Gill filament number is fixed from early instars, but gill shape, position, and pigmentation change dramatically with growth — a useful diagnostic and life-history marker.
3. **Extended, multi-year development in Eustheniidae** nymphs, contrasting with the shorter life cycles typical of ecologically similar carnivorous Perlodidae.
4. **Reversed seasonal life-history in Spaniocerca** relative to Northern Hemisphere Capniidae — spring hatching/autumn emergence rather than winter growth.
5. **Possible bivoltinism** suggested in Gripopterygidae (*Leptoperla*-type), which would be unprecedented among known temperate stoneflies.
6. **Novel morphological convergence**: A Gripopterygid (possibly *Dinotoperla*) shows flattened body/expanded femora convergent with mayfly (Ecdyonuridae, Tricorythidae) body plans — a previously unrecorded adaptation type in Plecoptera.
7. **Phylogenetic bridge hypothesis**: Comparative morphology of Penturoperlidae, Scopuridae, and Gripopterygidae abdominal gill/filament structures supports Illies' concept of Penturoperlidae as archaic, with **Scopuridae as an intermediate lineage** linking retractile gill filament clusters to the pointed tergal spine typical of Gripopterygid nymphs. Tasmanian 7-tailed Penturoperlid specimens provide additional transitional evidence.
8. **Faunal composition of Monbulk Creek** provides a broader baseline record of co-occurring freshwater macroinvertebrate families in a southeastern Australian upland stream, useful for comparative freshwater ecology studies (see [[Freshwater Malacostraca]], [[Gammaridae]], [[Leptophlebiidae]]).
