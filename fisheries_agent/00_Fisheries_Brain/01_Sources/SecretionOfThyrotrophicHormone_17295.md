# Secretion of Thyrotrophic Hormone by Pituitary Transplants in a Teleost Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 *Nature* research letter tests whether a teleost pituitary can keep producing [[Thyrotrophic Hormone]] (TSH) once it is cut off from the [[Hypothalamus]]. The authors transplanted pituitaries into the tail musculature of [[Mollienesia Formosa]], an all-female viviparous fish, then removed the host pituitary by [[Hypophysectomy]]. Grafted-hypophysectomized fish had more active [[Thyroid Gland]] tissue than hypophysectomized controls, and in six fish the thyroid was more strongly stimulated than in any intact control. The authors conclude that the teleost pituitary can secrete considerable TSH without its hypothalamic connection, and that teleost hypothalamo-hypophysial control of TSH may differ substantially from mammals.

## Background & Description

**Concept under test.** In mammals, normal TSH secretion depends on hypothalamic stimulation transmitted through the pituitary stalk, although low basal output may persist without it. In amphibians, stalk transection or heterotopic pituitary transplantation reduced or stopped TSH output in some studies, but not in all. Teleost fishes had not been studied in this respect before.

**Study organism.** *M. formosa* is an all-female species that reproduces gynogenetically, so the descendants of one female form a clone with identical genotypes. Tissue transplants within a clone are accepted and survive permanently. Clones were maintained in fresh water through matings with males of *M. sphenops* and [[Limia Vittata]].

**Thyroid anatomy.** As in most teleosts, the thyroid is not encapsulated. It consists of follicles scattered in the gular region beside the inferior jugular veins and ventral aorta, embedded in loose connective tissue. A few follicles were also found between the heart and the oesophagus in some fish.

**Osmoregulatory link.** Preliminary work showed that removing the pituitary abolishes the ability of *M. formosa* to live in fresh water, similar to [[Mollienesia Latipinna]]. For this reason, all fish were moved to dilute sea water (12 ppt) before surgery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The experiments were run under controlled laboratory conditions rather than in the wild:

- **Salinity:** Dilute sea water (12 ppt), chosen because hypophysectomized fish cannot survive in fresh water.
- **Temperature and light:** 25 °C (±1 °C) with 9 h of illumination per day.
- **Diet:** Fed twice daily with iodine-enriched Aronson's mixture, plus fresh-frozen *Artemia* three times a week.
- **Duration:** Four weeks of post-operative maintenance, with some grafted fish kept for seven months.

The study does not examine field populations, natural habitat, or community ecology.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**
- Mature fish received a pituitary graft from a donor of the same clone 3–4 weeks before hypophysectomy. Non-grafted fish from the same clones served as controls.
- Group sizes: 13 grafted-hypophysectomized fish, 17 hypophysectomized controls, and intact controls (the text states 14; Table 1 lists 4, most likely an OCR error for 14).
- Thyroids were examined histologically using Masson, PAS, and trichrome stains. Grafts were checked in serial sections, and heads of hypophysectomized fish were sectioned to confirm that no pituitary tissue remained.

**Thyroid histology (Table 1)**
- The transplanted pituitary kept the thyroid in a more active state than the hypophysectomized controls.
- In six grafted-hypophysectomized fish, the thyroid was more strongly stimulated than in any intact control, with marked hyperplasia. Epithelium and follicles filled most of the thyroid area and invaded the bases of the gill arches.
- Reported mean cell heights (as legible from the scan): lowest cell height about 2.7 ± 0.3 µm in intact fish, 1.6 ± 0.2 µm in hypophysectomized fish, and 2.9 ± 0.7 µm in grafted fish. Greatest cell height was about 6.3 ± 0.6 µm (intact), 2.8 ± 0.2 µm (hypophysectomized), and 7.3 ± 1.0 µm (grafted).

**Pituitary graft findings**
- Thyrotrophs were identified as PAS-positive and AF-positive cells in the proximal pars distalis (meso-adenohypophysis).
- All eight grafts examined so far were healthy and well vascularized, with large numbers of thyrotrophs showing signs of high secretory activity.
- Grafts also contained acidophils of the rostral pars distalis, corticotrophs, inactive gonadotrophs, very few putative somatotrophs, and cells of the pars intermedia.
- In two grafted-hypophysectomized fish kept for seven months, grafts still contained many active thyrotrophs, and thyroids were markedly hyperplastic. The authors take this as evidence that function can persist for a long time.

**Evidence for physiological (not artefactual) activity**
- The grafted-hypophysectomized fish grew slightly, which suggests that TH and some growth hormone were present.
- Their skin stayed silvery, like the intact controls. The hypophysectomized fish had dull skin, likely from guanine loss. In *M. latipinna*, thyroxine restores silvery skin. This implies that blood TH levels in the grafted fish were at least as high as in the intact controls.
- The authors also report that thiourea treatment of intact *M. latipinna* produced a thyroidal response consistent with negative feedback between TH and TSH, as in mammals.

**Interpretation**
- TSH can be secreted at a higher rate by the transplanted pituitary than by the gland in its normal position.
- The authors suggest the hypothalamus normally inhibits TSH secretion. Two explanations are offered for the continued elevated TSH output despite normal or supra-normal TH levels:
  1. TH acts on the hypothalamus to reinforce its inhibitory influence on TSH.
  2. TH acts directly on the thyrotrophs, and the hypothalamus adjusts the threshold at which TH suppresses TSH output.
- No direct measurements of blood TH were made.

**Conclusions**
- The teleost pituitary can retain the capacity to secrete large amounts of TSH after separation from the hypothalamus.
- The hypothalamo-hypophysial relationship for TSH in teleosts may differ considerably from that in mammals.
