# Corrected Distributional Records for Texas Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #catostomidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

Clark Hubbs (University of Texas, 1954, *Texas Journal of Science* 6(3)) re-evaluates published distributional records for Texas freshwater fishes. Much of the earlier literature, largely pre-1900 and perpetuated by [[Fowler 1945]], [[Baughman 1950]] and [[Knapp 1953]], contains misidentifications and incorrect localities. Using previously unpublished specimen data from several museum collections, the author removes or downgrades many records, redefines species ranges, and resolves several nomenclatural problems. The most notable corrections concern the Brownsville and Galveston localities, which the author shows are almost certainly erroneous.

## Background & Description

The paper is a critical checklist revision rather than a field study. It draws on specimens held at the [[United States National Museum]], the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]], the [[Texas Natural History Collection]], the Texas A&M collection and the Philadelphia Academy of Natural Sciences. Many corrections rest on examination of preserved material and on reports from named collectors, with some records cited as personal communications.

The author discusses roughly 90 nominal taxa across the following groups:
- Lampreys, salmonids and suckers (Catostomidae)
- Minnows and shiners (Cyprinidae, especially the [[Notropis Lutrensis Species Group]])
- Bullheads and catfishes (Ictaluridae)
- Killifishes, silversides and livebearers
- Temperate basses, sunfishes and black basses (*[[Micropterus]]*)
- Darters (Percidae, including the [[Etheostoma]] complex)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several records are explained by habitat and water chemistry:
- **Pecos River:** Diversion for irrigation raised temperature, reduced flow and increased salinity, probably unfavourable to montane fishes such as cutthroat trout. Salinity in the lower Pecos likely explains the atypical *Lucania parva* specimens.
- **Edwards Plateau springs:** The author notes that [[Edwards Plateau]] fishes such as *Etheostoma lepidum* and *E. spectabile* depend on dissolved chemicals from limestone. Aquarium observations showed these are necessary for survival.
- **Coastal and brackish waters:** Galveston Island brackish water is unsuitable for stream species such as *Campostoma anomalum* and *Etheostoma spectabile*.
- **Rio Grande at Brownsville:** Modern December 1953 collections found only *Hybognathus placita* and *Carpiodes carpio* of the 19 species previously attributed to the locality. Common species there include [[Dorosoma cepedianum]], [[Gambusia affinis]], [[Menidia beryllina]] and [[Mugil cephalus]].
- **Introductions:** Several western populations, including *Ameiurus melas*, trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) in McKittrick Canyon, and smallmouth bass in Texas hatcheries and Caddo Lake, are attributed to stocking. The tench (*Tinca tinca*) introduction was unsuccessful.

## Key Findings

**Records removed or judged improbable**
- *Ichthyomyzon gagei* range extended to the San Jacinto and Trinity systems (Dendy and Scott 1953).
- *Hiodon tergisus* (mooneye) occurrence in Texas is doubtful.
- No authenticated native trout survive in Texas; *Salmo gairdneri* was introduced to McKittrick Canyon, Hudspeth County.
- *Catostomus commersoni* is not expected in southern Texas. Its Brownsville record is virtually certain to be erroneous.
- *Phenacobius mirabilis*, *Campostoma anomalum*, *Etheostoma spectabile*, *Hysterocarpus traski* and *Cymatogaster aggregata* were all judged not to occur at Brownsville or Galveston.
- *Micropterus coosae* is not known west of Alabama, so East Texas records are based on a sight record.
- The mudsucker-type *Etheostoma microperca* does not occur in Texas, and Fowler's record probably refers to *E. gracile*.

**Range redefinitions**
- *Moxostoma congestum* (gray redhorse) is known from four Brazos River localities: Little Brazos River (Brazos County), South Bosque River (McLennan County), Bee Creek (Bosque County) and Ioni Creek (Palo Pinto County).
- *Notropis lepidus* (Edwards shiner) is proposed as a new common name. It is restricted to streams of the Edwards Plateau, with the author's new list of localities.
- *Notropis brazosensis* is recorded from the San Bernard and Colorado rivers, and from the Red River in Oklahoma.
- *Notropis roseus* (central weed shiner) has seven Texas Natural History Collection records south and east of the Edwards Plateau.
- *Micropterus treculi* (Texas spotted bass) is treated as a distinct species, based on sympatric collections with *M. punctulatus* in the Guadalupe River.
- *Hadropterus shumardi* (river darter) is most abundant in gravel riffles of coastal plain rivers, with multiple Guadalupe, San Marcos and Sabine records.
- *Etheostoma asprigene* (mud darter) inhabits larger east Texas streams and is not known west of the Neches drainage.
- *Etheostoma grahami* (Rio Grande darter) is absent from the Nueces system, where it is replaced by *E. lepidum*.

**Nomenclatural decisions**
- *Notropis notatus* and *N. texanus* are placed in the synonymy of *N. venustus* (blacktail shiner), with *N. venustus* retained as the valid name for stability.
- *Notropis lutrensis* (red shiner) is distinguished from *N. lepidus* and *N. proserpinus* by a key based on head tubercles, mouth position, and body depth.
- Two morphologic forms of *N. lutrensis* are recognised in the United States: the plains form *N. l. lutrensis* and the coastal form *N. l. suavis*.

**Conclusions**
- Of 19 species attributed to Brownsville, the author judges 7 impossible there, 4 probable absent, and 7 possible.
- Several other taxa, including *Gila nigrescens* and *Lepomis cyanellus*, remain "possible" or "probable" pending additional data.
- The author concludes that many historic "Texas" and "Brownsville" records require verification by new collections and examination of surviving specimens.
