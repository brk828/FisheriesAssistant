# Influence of Hybridization on the Evolution of the Pantosteus clarki (Catostomidae) Complex

#ichthyology #catostomidae #freshwater-fish #evolutionary-biology #hybridization

## Executive Summary

This dissertation proposal by Carol L. Secor (Arizona State University, 1998) outlines a comprehensive study to test whether introgressive hybridization with [[Pantosteus discobolus]] (bluehead sucker) has shaped the genetic and morphological patterns observed in the [[Pantosteus clarki]] (desert sucker) complex. The [[Desert Sucker]] exhibits considerable morphological variability across its range in tributaries of the lower [[Colorado River]] (Gila, Bill Williams, and Virgin river basins), with allozyme and morphological patterns suggesting past hybridization. The proposed research will employ multiple character sets—allozymes, mtDNA sequences (cytochrome b), and morphological traits—to detect hybridization signatures, estimate timing of gene transfer, and describe the dynamics of introgression across the species' range. Preliminary mtDNA data already indicate hybridization in the [[Virgin River]] (10-15% of P. clarki carry P. discobolus haplotypes) and at the confluence of the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers.

## Background & Description

The genus [[Pantosteus]] (family [[Catostomidae]]) comprises six recognized species distributed throughout western North America. [[Pantosteus clarki]] inhabits desert and mountain streams in the Gila, Bill Williams, and Virgin river basins—all tributaries of the Colorado River below Grand Canyon. Smith (1966) documented complex morphological variation within P. clarki, with local populations exhibiting highly variable morphologies. The Virgin River system contains two morphotypes: a mainstream form with thinner caudal peduncle and larger fins versus a tributary form. Populations from the pluvial White River resemble classical P. clarki from the Gila River system, while Meadow Valley Wash populations are intermediate. This variation led Minckley (1973) to hypothesize that P. clarki represents a species complex.

[[Pantosteus discobolus]] occurs in the upper and middle Colorado River and parts of the Snake and Bonneville basins. Although currently allopatric with P. clarki, historical climatic and geological changes may have allowed contact and hybridization. Koehn (1967) found that Virgin River populations of P. clarki exhibited serum transferrin patterns similar to P. discobolus, while Verde River samples showed unique patterns similar to [[Catostomus]] species. At the esterase-1 locus, P. discobolus is fixed for a slower-migrating allele while southern P. clarki populations are fixed for a faster-migrating allele; Virgin River populations show higher frequencies of the slower allele, exhibiting a clinal pattern indicative of hybridization.

Three morphological characters show diagnostic or semi-diagnostic patterns: (1) number of predorsal scales (partially diagnostic; Virgin River P. clarki populations overlap with P. discobolus ranges of 44-45+ scales, while other P. clarki populations have means <45); (2) caudal peduncle depth (P. discobolus: 5.4-9.1% standard length; P. clarki: 7.7-10.0%); and (3) number of lateral line scales (P. discobolus averages higher counts than P. clarki, with Gila River populations having fewest scales).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Desert fishes of southwestern United States and northern Mexico are ideal for studying introgressive hybridization because cyclical climatic forces have created repeated periods of isolation, contact, and opportunity for hybridization between closely related taxa (Hubbs and Miller 1948, Minckley et al. 1986, Axelrod 1979). Environmental instability associated with Pleistocene climatic cycles has influenced species distributions, allowing currently allopatric species to hybridize in the past.

Hybridization is common among catostomids, and [[Pantosteus]] species regularly interbreed with local [[Catostomus]] species (Hubbs et al. 1943, Smith 1966). Only two pairs of the six Pantosteus species are currently sympatric, with known hybridization occurring only between P. discobolus and [[Pantosteus platyrhynchus]]. Introduced P. clarki have hybridized with native P. platyrhynchus in the Sevier River drainage to form a hybrid swarm (Koehn 1967). P. discobolus in Little Colorado River tributaries is thought to be influenced by introgressive hybridization with [[Pantosteus plebeius]] from adjacent Rio Grande tributaries (Smith 1966, Smith et al. 1983, Crabtree and Buth 1987).

Southwestern aquatic systems are increasingly threatened by human activities and habitat modification (Miller 1961). The Virgin River was poisoned in 1988 to eradicate non-indigenous species, potentially impacting morphologically unique P. clarki populations irreparably.

## Key Findings

**Research Objectives:**
The study will test the hypothesis that genetic and morphological patterns in P. clarki result from introgressive hybridization with P. discobolus. If introgression has played an important role, populations impacted by hybridization will exhibit a mosaic distribution of features across character sets, while individuals distant from potential hybridization areas will show correlation of character states.

**Experimental Design:**

*Sampling:* At least 20 individuals will be collected from each locality spanning the distributional limits of the P. clarki complex and geographically adjacent species. Samples will be frozen on dry ice and processed for muscle, liver, and brain extracts.

*Morphological Analysis:* Three key characters (predorsal scales, caudal peduncle depth, lateral line scales) will be measured. Principal components analysis will be calibrated using reference populations outside hybridization zones, then applied to suspected hybrid populations to calculate hybrid indices and identify specimens as P. clarki, hybrid, or P. discobolus.

*Allozyme Analysis:* Initial surveys will focus on 15+ loci previously found polymorphic in this complex or other catostomids. Crabtree and Buth (1987) identified one diagnostic locus (Dldh-B) and three nearly fixed loci (Est-1, Est-2, Pnp-2) between the species. Hybrid indices will be calculated as the number of P. discobolus alleles present in each individual. Tests for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium and linkage disequilibrium will be conducted.

*MtDNA Analysis:* Two fragments of the cytochrome b gene (309 and 270 bp) will be surveyed using single-strand conformational polymorphism (SSCP) and direct sequencing. All SSCP variants will be sequenced. The entire cytochrome b gene will be sequenced from a subset of individuals representing all six Pantosteus species for larger-scale phylogenetic analysis.

*Phylogenetic Analysis:* Historical relationships among mtDNA haplotypes and allozymes will be analyzed using PAUP 4.0. Bootstrap analysis will assess relationship strength. Congruence tests will determine if mtDNA and allozyme trees match.

*Multicharacter Analysis:* Correlations between allozyme hybrid index and morphological principal components scores will be calculated. Allele frequencies, hybrid indices, and morphological histograms will be mapped to identify clinal patterns. Cytonuclear disequilibrium will be calculated to detect non-random associations between allozyme genotype and mtDNA haplotype.

**Preliminary Results:**
Preliminary cytochrome b sequence data indicate that P. plebeius, P. discobolus, and P. clarki are distinct taxa, allowing detection of introgression. Little Colorado River Pantosteus is distinct from other taxa, consistent with suggested complexity. Hybridization is evident in Virgin River mainstem samples (10-15% of P. clarki exhibit P. discobolus mtDNA haplotypes) and at the Little Colorado/Colorado River confluence (2 of 5 P. discobolus individuals carried P. clarki haplotypes).

**Expected Outcomes:**
If hybridization has occurred, phylogenetic analysis may show haplotypes from one species occurring in individuals of the other, with P. clarki and P. discobolus not forming monophyletic groups. Trees built with different character sets may conflict. Geographic patterns in hybrid indices will indicate introgression extent, with northern Virgin River populations expected to show higher hybrid indices due to proximity to P. discobolus. Values of Hardy-Weinberg and linkage disequilibrium varying across the hybridization zone would indicate selection for specific alleles or allelic combinations. Ancient hybridization may show decoupling of genetic and morphological characters.

**Significance:**
This multicharacter approach will detect hybridization and describe the extent, dynamics, and nature of introgression within the P. clarki complex. The study will provide empirical data valuable for modeling how evolutionary forces have shaped present diversity. Data will identify differentiated populations meriting conservation efforts and provide an index of diversity within populations for management purposes.
