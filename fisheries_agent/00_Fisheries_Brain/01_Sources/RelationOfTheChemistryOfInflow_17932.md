# Relation of Inflow Water Chemistry to Organic Productivity in Small Arizona Fishing Impoundments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 University of Arizona Master of Science thesis by Andrew Joseph Kemmerer, prepared in the [[Wildlife Management]] program and supervised by William J. McConnell, tests whether the chemistry of water flowing *into* small fishing impoundments predicts their biological productivity. Inflow waters were sampled from streams tributary to 12 lakes in northern and southern [[Arizona]]. Productivity was assessed through [[Gross Primary Productivity]] measurements at three lakes and through [[Fingerling Trout Growth]] rates at others. The author concludes that alkalinity (as calcium carbonate), total phosphate and nitrate favor higher productivity, that watershed geology and slope control inflow chemistry, and that inflow chemistry likely dominates over basin morphometry.

## Background & Description

The thesis opens with a review of productivity research, beginning with [[Thienemann]] (1927), who linked productivity to lake basin morphometry. Later workers related productivity to single nutrients such as phosphorus (Deevey, 1940) or to total dissolved solids as a salinity index (Rawson, 1951; Northcote and Larkin, 1956). The author argues that fish yield is an imperfect productivity index and that gross primary productivity, a measure of photosynthesis at the base of the food chain, is preferable, although predicting fish yield from it remains unresolved (McConnell, 1963).

Methods centred on sampling inflows rather than standing lake water, because phytoplankton remove dissolved ions from lake water within days (Nelson and Edmondson, 1955). Samples were collected in amber glass bottles, with checks for ion exchange and no significant change found over three days. Surface (storm) water and ground (post-storm) water were distinguished. Samples were analysed for temperature, pH, total dissolved salts, phenolphthalein and methyl orange alkalinity, iron, nitrate, sulfate, chloride, silica and phosphate fractions, with spectrographic analysis of cations on selected samples. Watershed and lake morphometry, including mean depth and [[Shoreline Development Factor]], were taken from maps and Arizona Game and Fish Department records.

Study areas were split into southern lakes, which receive mostly summer convectional rain, and northern lakes, which receive mostly winter frontal snow and spring melt. Most inflows are intermittent, so they do not develop extensive aquatic floras that would strip nutrients from the water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study lakes.** Twelve impoundments were studied, ranging from about 3,400 ft to 8,975 ft in elevation. Lake areas ranged from roughly 7 acres (Rose Canyon Lake) to 570 acres (Big Lake). Lake characteristics included:

- **Pena Blanca Lake:** 49 acres, mean depth 22 ft, watershed about 15 sq mi, stocked with [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Threadfin Shad]]; later invaded by black bullheads, green sunfish and mosquitofish.
- **Parker Canyon Lake:** 182 acres at full pool, mean depth 33 ft, watershed about 9 sq mi, three intermittent inflows (Parker, Merrit and Collins Canyon Creeks).
- **Rose Canyon Lake:** 7.33 acres, watershed about 4 sq mi, granite geology, heavily fished, invaded by [[Green Sunfish]].
- **Lago del Oro:** 260 acres, watershed about 47 sq mi, mixed geology.
- **Lynx Creek Reservoir:** 55.2 acres, schist and granite watershed, sulfate-rich inflow.
- **Blue Ridge Reservoir:** 325 acres at spillway height, 157 ft maximum depth, shore line development factor 8.4, sedimentary geology.
- **Knoll, Bear Canyon and Woods Canyon Lakes:** small Mogollon Rim impoundments on Coconino sandstone and Kaibab limestone, with very low dissolved solids.
- **Show Low and Rainbow Lakes:** Mogollon Rim lakes, basalt and sedimentary watersheds, subject to water pumping and fluctuation.
- **Big Lake:** 570 acres, basalt watershed, a history of winter fish kills from rooted aquatic plants, a dam raised 10 ft in the 1950s.

**Geology and inflow chemistry.** Watersheds of sedimentary rock, especially limestone, produced water with higher dissolved salts. Igneous and metamorphic watersheds produced lower salinity, with exceptions. Steep watersheds yielded very dilute water whatever the geology.

**Atmospheric and organic inputs.** Dust from alkaline desert floors and aerosols trapped by ponderosa pine foliage were proposed as sources of ions and trace elements. Leaching of organic debris was proposed as a nitrate source.

## Key Findings

- **Chemistry predicts productivity.** Increasing alkalinity (as CaCO₃), total phosphate and nitrate favoured higher lake productivity. Concentrations of calcium carbonate below 30 ppm and total phosphate below 0.23 ppm were associated with poor production. Lakes were generally productive when alkalinity exceeded 85 ppm and total phosphate exceeded 0.36 ppm. A nitrate concentration above 0.05 ppm seemed to allow higher productivity, though the optimum was hard to define.
- **Ranking outcomes (chemistry only):** Rainbow, Pena Blanca, Lynx Creek, Parker Canyon and Big Lakes were rated "good." Show Low Lake was rated "fair." Rose Canyon, Knoll and Woods Canyon Lakes were rated "poor."
- **Morphometry is secondary.** Mean depth and shore line development showed no clear relationship to productivity alone. Combined with chemistry, they gave a clearer relationship. Several lakes with favourable morphometry (Woods Canyon, Knoll, Rose Canyon) still had poor productivity. Every lake with favourable chemistry rated at least "fair."
- **Geology controls inflow chemistry.** At Parker Canyon Lake, the two sedimentary watersheds (Parker and Merrit Canyons) gave alkalinity above 100 ppm, while the andesite-dominated Collins Canyon gave much lower alkalinity but higher sulfate.
- **Surface and ground water differ.** Nitrate, orthophosphate and iron were higher in surface water, while calcium bicarbonate, sulfate, chloride and silica were higher in ground water. Surface water was turbid and yellow-brown, while ground water was clear.
- **Primary productivity peaks after rains.** The highest photosynthesis in Pena Blanca, Parker Canyon and Rose Canyon Lakes occurred at the end of the summer rainy season, as turbidity fell.
- **Growth evidence.** Fingerling rainbow trout grew about 0.2 to 0.9 inches per month (about 0.6 inches per month at Lynx Creek and 0.6 inches per month at Parker Canyon). Green sunfish at Rose Canyon Lake (n = 20, collected 19 August 1964) averaged 9.49 cm total length after roughly two growing seasons, well below the 14 to 17 cm typical after three summers (Carlander, 1950), suggesting low productivity.
- **Predictions for unfinished impoundments.** Blue Ridge Reservoir and Lago del Oro were predicted to have "fair to good" fish production. Bear Canyon Lake was predicted to have "poor" production because of very low inflow chemistry, despite favourable shape.
- **Sulfate hazard.** The high sulfate in Lynx Creek Reservoir inflow (about 155 to 170 ppm) had not yet harmed productivity, but reduction to hydrogen sulfide can cause fish kills; a small pond on the drainage was reported to have died from this cause.
- **Trace elements.** Concentrations of other nutrients and trace elements varied with no appreciable effect, suggesting that minor and trace elements enter lakes mainly via airborne dust and aerosols.
- **Methodological caveats.** Productivity ratings were approximate, the sample sizes were small (often 3 to 17 samples per stream), gross primary productivity could not yet be converted into fish yield, and equal weighting of factors was arbitrary, though it gave the best correlation.

**Principal conclusions:**
1. Alkalinity, total phosphate and nitrate favour productivity, modified by basin morphometry.
2. Watershed geology and steepness are the dominant controls on inflow chemistry.
3. Similar trace-element composition across inflows suggests that trace nutrients arrive via atmospheric dust and aerosols.
