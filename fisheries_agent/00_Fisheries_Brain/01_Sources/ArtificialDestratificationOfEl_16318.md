# Artificial Destratification of El Capitan Reservoir by Aeration (Part I: Effects on Chemical and Physical Parameters)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game bulletin (Fish Bulletin 141, 1968, by Arlo W. Fast) is the first in a series on the artificial destratification of [[El Capitan Reservoir]], San Diego County. It reviews how [[Compressed Air Lake Aeration]] (destratification) and [[Hypolimnion Aeration]] alter the thermal, oxygen and chemical regime of a eutrophic, warm monomictic reservoir, and assesses the consequences for the fishery, water quality and economics. Aeration in 1965 and 1966 eliminated most thermal stratification, distributed oxygen to all depths and lowered iron, manganese and hydrogen sulfide, at a yearly operating cost of about $2,926. Warmer bottom temperatures, however, could preclude a year-round coldwater fishery. Subsequent parts were to address zoobenthos, phytoplankton and fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Stratification cycle:** Most lower-elevation California lakes show one annual cycle of stratification, from about March to November. The cycle is classed as "warm monomictic" (Hutchinson, 1957). Three zones form: the [[Epilimnion]], the [[Metalimnion]] (thermocline, defined as a change of at least 1.0°C per 1.0 m) and the [[Hypolimnion]]. Eutrophic hypolimnia often become anoxic, with hydrogen sulfide, iron and manganese accumulating.
- **Reservoir:** El Capitan Dam impounds the intermittently flowing San Diego River about 40 km east of San Diego. Dam construction ran from 1932 to 1934 at a cost of $5.8 million. The dam is 66.7 m high, and the reservoir has steep, decomposed-granite sides with a reduced [[Littoral Zone]]. Estimated original capacity was 1.44 × 10⁸ m³, reduced to 1.39 × 10⁸ m³ by 1956 (a 3.5% loss).
- **Fishery:** The reservoir opened to fishing in 1955 and is dominated by warmwater species: [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), green sunfish, brown bullhead, [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and white crappie (periodically abundant, declining since 1961). [[Walleye]] were introduced in 1962–63. More than 300,000 largemouth bass were stocked between 1956 and 1963.
- **Watershed:** About 51,800 ha, including the upstream Cuyamaca Reservoir watershed. Imported Colorado River water has been stored since 1958.

### Destratification Techniques Reviewed

| Technique | Key points from the text |
|---|---|
| [[Diffuse Air Injection]] | Judged the most efficient method for large reservoirs; average efficiency about 0.42% versus 0.14% for mechanical pumping (Bernhardt, 1967). Used at El Capitan and Lake Wohlford. |
| [[Aero-Hydraulic Cannons]] | Polyethylene tubes, five or more per lake, fed by a small compressor (5 hp sufficient for 1.48 × 10⁶ m³). |
| [[Floating Aerator]] | Perforated pipe on a float; mobile and usable for chemical dispersal. Indian Brook Reservoir destratified in 6 days. |
| Blower-type | Babson Reservoir, about 470 cfm; judged less efficient than other methods. |
| Plastic hose grid | Commercial system for sewage ponds; never tested in a large stratified lake. |
| Diffuse air tubes | Vertical tubes that draw hypolimnion water up; more efficient for small air volumes. |
| Mechanical pumping | Less efficient than air injection in cited studies (Hooper et al., 1953; Schmitz, 1958; Symons et al., 1967). |
| [[Bernhardt Hypolimnion Aerator]] | Aerates without destratifying; Wahnbach Reservoir, about 37 × 10⁶ m³. |
| [[Speece Hypolimnion Aerator]] | Two-pipe, low-pressure air re-use design; untested at the time of writing. |

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**El Capitan system and operation.** A LeRoi 50-S-2 compressor (50 hp, 480 V) delivered air through about 92 m of 3.96 cm PVC pipe on the bottom. The last 31 m was suspended by 13 styrofoam floats and perforated with 90 holes (3.175 mm) in clusters, with spacing varying along the pipe. The system was in a section closed to recreation. At 20 m of water over the release point, system pressure was about 50 psi, of which about 30 psi was hydrostatic. Aeration ran continuously on a 24-hour schedule.

**Operating periods.**
- 1965: June 10–21, paused June 21–July 1, then July 1–October 4.
- 1966: March 19–October 17, uninterrupted.
- 1964 and 1967 served as control years.

**Costs.** Installation cost about $6,010. Estimated annual cost, with 10-year amortization at 6% ($825), power (about $1,674), service charges ($177) and maintenance ($250), was about $2,926.

**Primary production and sediments.** Rooted aquatics are scarce because water levels are unstable, so phytoplankton (bluegreen algae, diatoms and green algae; over 150 genera recorded 1960–66) dominates primary production. Deep-basin bottoms are covered with organically rich colloidal silt and sludge, while shallow bottoms are coarse sand and gravel.

**Climate and water quality.** Surface temperatures ranged from 1.1°C to 28.3°C. Air and surface water temperatures were strongly correlated (r = .906, n = 521). Most rain falls December through March, so the reservoir receives most runoff during isothermal periods. Ionic concentrations were negatively correlated with water volume: calcium, bicarbonate, sulfate and total hardness all showed Spearman rho values near −0.85 to −0.92 for 1935–1964 data.

## Key Findings

**Temperature and heat budget**
- Normal stratified surface temperatures reached minima of about 11.5°C in January and maxima of about 25.5°C in late July or early August. Bottom temperatures ranged from about 11.0°C to 13.5°C.
- During destratified years, the summer bottom temperature nearly equaled the surface temperature, an average increase of about 10.5°C over the normal yearly maximum bottom temperature. Highs of 23.0°C (1965) and 24.5°C (1966) were observed.
- Average maximum water temperature was about 22.5°C in stratified years, versus 24.5°C in the two destratified years, about 2.0°C higher.
- The highest heat content was 25,116.0 cal/m³ above 0°C (1966), the second highest 23,900.5 cal/m³ (1965).
- Destratification did not change winter temperatures.

**Stability**
- Stability falls to zero when a lake is isothermal. Aeration sharply reduced stability in 1965 and 1966 (1966 values lower than 1965, attributed to the longer destratification period).
- Stability is strongly tied to water volume, so the author judged current efficiency calculations (Symons and Robeck, 1966) "not refined enough" for valid comparisons.

**Oxygen**
- Before aeration, an oxygen deficit covered depths from about 9 m to the bottom (June 1964).
- After destratification, oxygen was distributed to all depths, though surface concentrations were lower in autumn, which the author attributed to accelerated oxidation.

**Water chemistry**
- Iron and manganese at 7 and 17 m exceeded the U.S. Public Health Service (1962) combined limit of 0.3 mg/l in August 1964 (1.46 mg/l iron at 17 m). Concentrations in August 1965 and 1966 were below 0.3 mg/l at all depths.
- Hydrogen sulfide odor was absent in 1965 and 1966 and reappeared in 1967 after prolonged stagnation.

**Fishery implications**
- Destratification increases habitable volume. Channel catfish and threadfin shad extended their distributions throughout the lake, and walleye moved deeper but not as deep as catfish or shad.
- Benthic invertebrates (zoobenthos) and zooplankton spread to deeper zones; the author expected benefits to benthic feeders such as catfish, bullheads and carp.
- Increased bottom temperatures may preclude a year-round coldwater fishery (most coldwater fishes are stressed above 24.5°C; temperatures above 21°C are generally undesirable).
- Hypolimnion aeration is proposed as a way to maintain two-story lakes supporting both warmwater and coldwater fisheries without raising hypolimnion temperatures.

**Economics**
- Chlorine demand at Lake Wohlford fell from about 12 mg/l to about 7 mg/l, saving about $6,000 a year in chlorine.
- At El Capitan, estimated evaporation reduction was 6.5% in 1966 (about 2.36 × 10⁶ m³, or about 192 acre-feet, worth about $9,600). This exceeded the system's roughly $3,000 yearly operating cost.
- A compensation area of 138 ha for El Capitan is the surface area whose evaporation savings would just cover operating costs (based on a 4% reduction).

**Management conclusions**
- The most appropriate method depends on local limnology, the relative importance of changes, and the fishery management goals. Destratification may conflict with coldwater fisheries.
- Aeration was judged financially profitable and beneficial across all measured aspects in reservoirs aerated to date.
