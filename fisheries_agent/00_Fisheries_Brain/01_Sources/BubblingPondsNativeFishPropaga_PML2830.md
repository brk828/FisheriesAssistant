# Native Fish Propagation and Research Facility Status Update: Bubbling Ponds Hatchery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This status update describes a new propagation and grow-out facility for endangered [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] at [[Bubbling Ponds Fish Hatchery]] near Cornville, Arizona. Construction was completed in June 2007. [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] funded the facility through a cooperative agreement under a [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] 2001 Biological Opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]]. The facility keeps backup populations to guard against catastrophic loss of rare [[Gila River Basin]] fishes and produces fish for reintroduction and repatriation. Its main advantage is a pathogen-free [[Artesian Well]] supplying about 90 gpm at a constant 20°C.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and mandate.** The facility responds to a federal obligation to fund projects that benefit native Gila River fishes. Its stated goal is to prevent catastrophic loss of rare populations of spikedace and loach minnow by maintaining backup populations and propagating fish for reintroduction and repatriation.

**Water source.** A well installed in 2003 supplies the existing [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] research wet laboratory and the new facility. It yields about 90 gpm of artesian water at a constant 20°C. The water is neutral in pH, has no high nitrogen saturation, and is pathogen-free, which made it suitable for aquaculture and the reason the open-air facility was sited here. The new building lies about 40 feet east of the existing wet laboratory.

**Two-building system.** The program has two separate structures, each with a distinct role:

- **Research wet lab (built 2000):** a temperature-controlled [[Quarantine Facility]] with nine 500-gallon circular fiberglass tanks, each operating as an isolated recirculating system. Its wastewater drains to a septic tank with no connection to other water supplies. Newly acquired fish are treated here for diseases and parasites before moving outdoors.
- **Production and grow-out facility (completed June 2007):** an open-air structure, 30 × 60 ft, with an 11-ft metal roof on a 3-ft concrete wall. Chain-link fencing runs from the wall to the roof to exclude avian and terrestrial predators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tank design.** The production building holds 24 circular fiberglass tanks (6 ft diameter, 1,900 L), each plumbed to artesian water at 2.3 gpm when all tanks run. Water enters through a 4-inch supply loop that equalizes pressure, with twelve 2-inch lines feeding pairs of tanks. A 12-inch layer of pea gravel provides drainage and covers the supply and drain lines. Each tank drains individually into a center trough through a 6-inch drop, which prevents backflow and contamination. The trough drains to a pond linked to the main hatchery drainage system.

**Habitat simulation.** Spawning is encouraged by:

- manipulating photoperiod with an automatic-timer fluorescent lighting system;
- adding cobble and sand substrates;
- placing large boulders and woody debris to replicate natural pools and riffles.

**Water depth.** Spikedace tanks run at about 20 inches of depth. Loach minnow tanks run shallower, at roughly 8–10 inches, which makes fish easier to observe for health checks. Densities are kept below 300 fish per tank, which the authors state encourages spawning and reduces bacterial problems.

**Aeration.** A [[Regenerative Blower]] feeds a centralized aeration system with two air stones per tank. It supplies backup aeration when flow is stopped for harvest or when densities are high.

**Holding structures.** External standpipes control water level. Internal standpipes retain fish during harvest. Drainage screens made of two layers of slotted well pipe with sand between them let water drain while holding young fish.

## Key Findings

**Husbandry and feeding.**

- Adult spikedace and loach minnow are fed Tetramin flake and freeze-dried bloodworms.
- Larvae receive live [[Artemia Nauplii]] for the first two weeks after hatching, then microencapsulated diets.
- A powdered razorback sucker diet from the Bozeman Technology Center (distributed by Nelson and Sons, Inc.) also appears effective for larval spikedace and loach minnow. The razorback sucker is not a facility species.
- Feeding is ad libitum twice daily via vibratory auto-feeders set for 7:00 am and 5:00 pm. Gap size adjusts feed type and quantity.

**Propagation protocol.**

- Captive stocks are supplemented annually with adult fish from original source populations.
- A subset of each population is moved to a separate tank at low density, which appears to promote spawning and larval survival.
- Adults and larvae are separated to prevent cannibalism.
- Two tanks are needed to hold each metapopulation as refuge; three to four tanks are needed for each population being propagated. More offspring require more tank space.
- Juveniles may tolerate densities above 300 per tank, but effects on growth and survival are unknown.

**Capacity.**

- Refuge-only maintenance: up to 12 separate populations (minimum two tanks each).
- Propagation and repatriation: up to 6 populations (three to four tanks each).
- Tanks can be switched between species as needed.
- Co-culturing both species in one tank could increase population capacity but has not been tested.
- A larger outdoor holding tank for grow-out of small fish is an option for expansion.
- The Table 1 inventory of fish on station is not legible in the source and is not summarized here.

**Conclusions.** The facility supplies much-needed pathogen-free space and water for refuge populations of threatened and endangered Gila Basin fishes. It can reduce the risk of catastrophic wild losses and supply fish for future repatriation. The authors state that the propagation program must be paired with efforts to stop or reverse the causes of wild native fish declines.
