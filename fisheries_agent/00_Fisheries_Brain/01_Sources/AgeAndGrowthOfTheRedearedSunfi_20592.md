# Age and Growth of the Red-Eared Sunfish in Reelfoot Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report, the second in a series on the life history of fish in [[Reelfoot Lake]], determines the age and growth rate of the [[Red-Eared Sunfish]] (*Eupomotis microlophus*, Gunther). Age was estimated from the scales of 563 fish collected during the summers of 1937 and 1938 using a modified [[Polariscope]] developed by the author. Fish reached the [[Legal Length]] of 6 inches during their third summer of life, and [[Sexual Maturity]] was reached in the second summer. Age groups 4 and 5 made up 91 per cent of the sample. Growth rates of males and females were nearly identical. The author states the study was intended to provide a basis for better [[Game Laws]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Red-Eared Sunfish]], *Eupomotis microlophus* (Gunther), a member of the family [[Centrarchidae]].
- **Study site:** [[Reelfoot Lake]], Tennessee. The study was published by the [[Reelfoot Lake Biological Station]] (Contribution No. 26) of the Tennessee Academy of Science, Nashville, in January 1939 (vol. 3, pp. 61-71).
- **Author:** Robert J. Schoffman, C.S.V., Fenwick High School, Oak Park, Illinois.
- **Motivation:** Conservationists, sportsmen, and commercial fishermen requested specific information on age and growth in this lake to support better game laws.
- **Sample collection:** Fish were taken from commercial fish docks at the Spillway (south shore), Samburg (east shore), and Walnut Log (northeast end), plus fish supplied by sportsmen at Walnut Log Lodge. Commercial gear included wire nets, barrel nets, and trammel nets. Collections were described as representative of daily catches during the summers of 1937 and 1938.
- **Validation basis:** The author notes that the scale method for age determination in Centrarchidae had been validated by earlier researchers, including [[Barnes And Anson]] (1923), Potter (1925), Wright (1929), Bolen (1924), Hiel (1931), Tester (1932), Hubbs and Hubbs (1931, 1933), Hubbs and Cooper (1935), and [[Schoffman]] (1938).

### Methods: Scale Analysis

- The standard scale method was not used. Instead, a modification of [[Schoffman]]'s (1938) method was applied, using **unprepared scales** mounted between two ordinary microscope slides, hinged at the bottom with adhesive tape.
- The [[Polariscope]] uses two polaroids (polarizer and analyzer) instead of a Nicol prism analyzer and dark glass polarizer. Magnification reaches up to 75X, using a 9X or 12X ocular.
- Reported advantages: low cost of construction; equally good results with sunlight or artificial light; magnification or reduction; portability through full disassembly; optional projection onto ground glass; and greater contrast between annuli and the rest of the scale.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning season:** Begins in May and continues until September. Females were observed in spawning preparation or actively spawning, and egg masses formed in the ovaries from mid-summer to late summer.
- **Growing season:** Averages 215 days, with an annual mean temperature of 58.3° F.
- **Hatch timing effects:** Fish hatched early had a better start than those hatched later. Late-hatched fish reached only a size sufficient to survive their first winter, which the author argues gives them an unequal advantage in later growth.
- **Sampling period:** The investigation ran from June 12 to August 20.
- **Sex-ratio bias:** Males were caught more readily because they were feeding and could be taken by both nets and hook and line. The author states the observed ratio does not represent the actual population.
- **Reference data:** Local weather information was drawn from the U.S. Weather Bureau summary at Cairo, Illinois (Barron, 1935).

## Key Findings

### Sample and Age Structure

- **Sample size:** 563 red-eared sunfish were aged.
- **Age group 4:** 69 per cent of all fish. Males made up 68.34 per cent of all males caught and 76 per cent of fish in this group; females made up 70 per cent of all females and 24 per cent of fish in this group.
- **Age group 5:** 22 per cent of all fish. Males made up 21 per cent of all males and 74 per cent of fish in this group; females made up 24 per cent of all females and 26 per cent of fish in this group.
- **Age groups 4 and 5 combined:** 91 per cent of all fish.
- **Sex composition (age groups 2 to 6):** Males 77 per cent and females 23 per cent (Section V gives males 76 per cent and females 23 per cent).
- **Table 1 sample counts by summer of life:** Total age 2 = 2; age 3 = 31; age 4 = 389; age 5 = 124; age 6 = 17 (sums to 563).

### Length and Weight

- **Legal length:** 6 inches. Age group 4 fish averaged about 1 inch over legal length. The average fish reached legal length during its third summer of life.
- **Average total length by summer of life (Table 1):**
  - Summer 2: 4.33 inches, 1.25 ounces
  - Summer 3: 6.01 inches (Section VI and text give 6.90 inches and 4.36 ounces for the third summer; the table lists 6.01 inches, 2.25 to 3.00 oz range)
  - Summer 4: 6.90 inches
  - Summer 5: 7.45 inches
  - Summer 6: 8.14 inches
- **Largest fish:** A male of age group 6, measuring 8.85 inches.
- **Heaviest fish:** Males of age groups 5 and 6, weighing 8 ounces (Table 1). The Summary states the oldest and heaviest specimen weighed "614 ounces" (as printed; likely an OCR error for 6¼ ounces, which should be verified against the original).

### Growth Rate

- **Growth in the first two summers (females):** 4.33 inches total. Assuming constant growth, 2.16 inches per summer.
- **Growth in the first three summers (males):** 6.03 inches total. Assuming constant growth, 2.01 inches per summer for the first two summers.
- **Annual length increments:**

| Interval | Females (in/yr) | Males (in/yr) |
|---|---|---|
| Summer 2 to 3 | 1.67 | not stated |
| Summer 3 to 4 | 1.01 | 0.83 |
| Summer 4 to 5 | 0.42 (marked decline) | 0.94 (slight decrease) |
| Summer 5 to 6 | 0.7 (slight increase) | 0.68 (slight drop) |

- Female growth exceeded male growth by 0.25 inch in the fourth summer. Male growth exceeded female growth by 0.25 inch in the fifth summer.

### Weight Growth

- **Females, first two summers:** 1.25 ounces total. Constant-increase assumption gives 0.62 ounce for the first summer.
- **Males, first three summers:** 2.55 ounces total. Constant-increase assumption gives 0.82 ounce per summer for the first two summers.
- **Weight increments:** Females increased 1.50 ounces per summer from summer 2 to 3, decreased to 1.25 ounces from summer 3 to 4, and stayed constant from summer 4 to 5. The female increment then decreased slightly to 1.10 ounces (summer 5 to 6). Males increased to 2.25 ounces per summer (summer 3 to 4), dropped to 0.60 ounce (summer 4 to 5), and rose slightly to 1 ounce (summer 5 to 6).
- Both sexes reached nearly the same weight in the sixth summer.
- Greatest weight per length: males during the fourth summer; females during the fifth summer.
- Early in life, length increased faster than weight. Later, weight increased faster in proportion to length.

### Length-Weight Relationship (Figure 4)

- Male increase was constant until about 6.8 inches, then decreased slightly until 8.15 inches.
- Female increase was constant until 7 inches, then increased slightly until 8.13 inches.
- The marked male-over-female weight-length difference near 5 ounces and 7 inches was attributed to younger females that had already spawned and were preparing to spawn again.

### Reproduction

- **Sexual maturity:** Reached during the second summer of life.
- **Spawning frequency:** Females could spawn two or three times before reaching legal size, depending on when in the third summer they were caught.
- **Post-maturity yearly increase:** About 1 inch in length and 1 ounce in weight.

### Summary Conclusions (as stated by the author)

1. Age was determined for 563 red-eared sunfish using a new polariscope.
2. The average fish reached legal length during its third summer, and this group made up 69 per cent of the sample.
3. Over one-half of the fish were caught soon after reaching legal length.
4. The oldest specimen was in its sixth summer; it was also the heaviest specimen. The longest specimen (8.85 inches) was also in its sixth summer.
5. Sexual maturity was reached during the second summer.
6. Practically no difference in growth rate was found between the sexes.
