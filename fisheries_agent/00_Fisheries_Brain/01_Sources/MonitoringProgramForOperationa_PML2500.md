# Monitoring Program for Operational Use of Triploid Grass Carp in the Coachella Canal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (December 1990), prepared by the [[Coachella Valley Water District]] (CVWD), evaluates the operational use of [[Triploid Grass Carp]] stocked to control aquatic vegetation in the unlined 58 km reach of the [[Coachella Canal]], California. Nearly 15,000 triploid grass carp were stocked in late 1985; most aquatic vegetation was eliminated within six months. About 2,500 fish remained in fall 1988. Because prestocking data were limited for most variables, all conclusions are tentative.

Findings indicate that [[Channel Catfish]] were largely unaffected or possibly benefited, while [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] abundance and condition may have declined, plausibly through loss of vegetation-associated habitat. Results for [[Common Carp]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] were inconsistent or insufficient, and creel survey results were contradictory.

## Background & Description

- **Authority and agreement:** The [[California Fish and Game Commission]] approved an Environmental Impact Report (Beaty et al. 1985) on May 17, 1985. A Memorandum of Understanding was signed August 30, 1985, requiring a monitoring program; its format was agreed on October 17, 1985.
- **Objectives:** Control aquatic plants to reduce costs for CVWD, verify grass carp effectiveness, and determine impacts on resident fishes and the fishery.
- **Setting:** The Coachella Valley is home to over 125,000 permanent residents (over 200,000 in winter). The 122-mile canal, with 1,300 cfs capacity, diverts water from the [[Colorado River]] via the [[All-American Canal]]. It has three sections: a concrete-lined upstream section (77 km), the unlined 58 km study reach divided into 24 reaches by 25 inverted siphons (numbered 7 to 32), and a concrete-lined downstream section ending at [[Lake Cahuilla]].
- **Stocking:** On October 23 and November 5, 1985, CVWD received 18,023 triploid grass carp from J. M. Malone and Son Enterprises (Lonoke, Arkansas), averaging 322 mm total length and 357 g. Of these, 15,871 were stocked into the unlined canal; the remainder went to the concrete section.
- **Study design:** Vegetation was sampled at least three times yearly by SCUBA biomass transects in six reaches; fish were sampled by [[Electrofishing]] in summer (June–July) and winter (December–January) in standardized passes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic vegetation.** Species present included curlyleaf pondweed, slender naiad, filamentous algae, [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), and [[Eurasian Watermilfoil]] (*Myriophyllum spicatum*). Sago and watermilfoil alternately formed near-continuous mats. Mechanical removal in 1983 was followed by regrowth to problem densities by summer 1985. A drawdown in October 1985 exposed sago above Siphon 24 for three days, and reach 21 density fell from 181 to 50 g dry weight/m² before stocking. Sago was eliminated by May 1986; watermilfoil persisted until September 1986, consistent with its known chemical defenses (Ostrofsky and Zettler 1986; Planas et al. 1991) that make it relatively unpalatable to grass carp. No rooted vegetation was found after September 1986.

**Grass carp diet and condition.** After 1986 the triploids ate almost entirely terrestrial vegetation (principally [[Bermuda Grass]]), with sediment, trace terrestrial and aquatic insects, and no fish parts found in 204 examined stomachs. Condition fell sharply after stocking, then recovered by 1989.

**Habitat changes.** Loss of macrophyte beds also removed quiet "backwater" zones between beds and shorelines, which are used by spawning bluegill and likely largemouth bass.

**Invertebrates.** [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula*) appeared in catfish stomachs in summer 1987, the first summer without vegetation, and became dominant before declining.

## Key Findings

**Grass carp population and survival**
- Electrofishing CPE showed no clear decline in grass carp abundance, largely because larger fish are more catchable and the population was essentially a single cohort.
- A Reach 21 mark-recapture estimate (summer 1988) gave 2,932 fish for the whole unlined canal; a November 1988 drawdown count gave 1,965 fish. The adopted average was about 2,449 (reported as 2,440 in the loss analysis).
- Loss of 84.6% of stocked fish was attributed to downstream movement through gates (mostly soon after stocking), angler removal (considered minor), and mortality (including poor nutrition after May 1986). A constant loss rate of 46.4% per year was estimated.
- Triploids grew slowly, partly because of crowded rearing (1984 year-class), and partly because of vegetation loss. Fish were larger downstream, probably because the Siphon 31 gate maintains a reservoir with accessible bank vegetation.

**Channel catfish**
- No change in electrofishing CPE since stocking. Rising conductivity (1986–1988) should have lowered CPE, so the stable catch may indicate a growing population, though alternative explanations (cessation of CDFG stocking, reduced competition) were proposed.
- Age-I fish length increased significantly by ANOVA (not by regression) after stocking; a shift toward fish and Corbicula in the winter diet was noted, and condition of mid-sized and possibly large fish improved.

**Largemouth bass**
- Electrofishing CPE declined markedly from 1986 to 1988 and remained significantly negative through 1990, with a slight rebound and near-nonsignificance overall. Conductivity likely contributed, but grass carp probably did too.
- Size-class composition was unchanged, which argues against reproductive failure; aquatic invertebrate consumption fell sharply, while fish rose in diet importance, supporting the vegetation-as-refuge hypothesis.
- Mid-sized bass condition declined in ANOVA, although the overall regression slope was positive.

**Bluegill and common carp**
- Bluegill CPE declined significantly (negative regression), with the same interpretation as bass; post-stocking condition of mid-sized bluegill was positive but based on very small samples.
- Common carp CPE did not change; juveniles have never been found, so the population depends on continual immigration. Condition cycled, with inconsistent statistical results.

**Flathead catfish**
- No evidence of CPE change; summer condition of very large fish declined by ANOVA, but the pre-stocking estimate rested on five fish from one year (1983).

**Creel survey (Chapter 4)**
- Channel catfish made up over 80% of the creel catch; bass were rarely caught. Angler numbers and daily harvest were correlated; weekday and weekend CPE were not.
- Weekday CPE declined while weekend CPE rose, suggesting improved fishing for casual anglers and reduced success for serious anglers (hypothetical). No significant seasonal or yearly effects were found on harvest or angler number.
- Fishing in the canal is illegal (posted no trespassing); CDFG stocked channel catfish until June 1986 (total of about 193,531 fish stocked 1974–1985, per Table 10 summary).

**Conclusions**
- Vegetation removal could likely have been achieved at a stocking rate below the 55 fish/acre used. The [[Imperial Irrigation District]] stocked 57 fish/acre with smaller fish (averaging 193 mm) and virtually eliminated [[Hydrilla]] by July.
- Mechanical removal (chaining with bulldozers) and herbicides cause serious side effects beyond vegetation loss; grass carp have little direct impact. The main constraint on grass carp use, fear of uncontrolled reproduction, is addressed by triploid sterility (Van Eenennaam et al. 1990).
- Overall, triploid grass carp appear to have had a neutral to positive effect on channel catfish, and possibly a negative effect on largemouth bass and bluegill, which the canal had always provided only marginal habitat for.
