## Executive Summary

This note synthesizes a set of regulatory and scientific correspondence (1998–1999) surrounding the relicensing of the Childs-Irving Hydroelectric Facility on Fossil Creek, Arizona, and its implications for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The core scientific dispute centers on whether Fossil Creek/Fossil Springs can or does support razorback sucker following an accidental 1988 stocking of ~10,000 fingerlings, and whether increased minimum flows (as part of relicensing) would restore habitat relevant to species recovery. A key analytical tool discussed is the Grogan & Boreman (1998) extirpation probability model, which EnviroNet Inc. applied to conclude a 100% probability of extirpation at Fossil Springs — a conclusion contested on methodological grounds by the [[U.S. Forest Service]] and the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]]. The U.S. Forest Service (Tonto National Forest) and independent ichthyologists (Dean Hendrickson, Paul Marsh) argue that Fossil Creek, especially under restored full flows and travertine-dam-driven pool formation, holds meaningful potential as recovery/refugia habitat for the Verde River razorback sucker population, even if it cannot sustain a fully self-reproducing population on its own.

## Background & Description

The source documents contain minimal direct morphological data, as they are regulatory/policy reviews rather than a primary taxonomic study. Notable habitat-linked biological facts include:
- **Species**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), family Catostomidae — a large-bodied, long-lived freshwater sucker native to the Colorado River Basin, notable for a distinctive dorsal keel (not described in detail in this text but inferred from taxon identity).
- **Life stage growth evidence**: Fingerlings stocked in 1988 (~10,000 total, split between Fossil Springs above Irving Dam and Fossil Creek below Irving Dam) were documented growing to **subadult size** by 1990, indicating viable growth potential in spring-influenced habitat.
- **Longevity**: Referenced as a "long-lived fish," supporting the argument that occasional/episodic recruitment events could sustain multi-cohort presence in the [[Verde River]] over time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Fossil Creek is a **travertine-influenced** stream system; current (reduced-flow) conditions are dominated by shallow run habitat over bedrock, unsuitable for razorback sucker. Under restored/increased base flows, channel morphology is expected to shift toward **travertine dam formations creating complex, deep pool habitats** — structurally similar to the spring habitat where the 1988 stocked fish survived and grew.
- **Habitat limitation**: At Fossil Springs, suitable deep-pool habitat is confined to less than a few hundred feet within a ~1/4 mile reach of perennial water — an extremely limited habitat footprint relative to species needs.
- **Population context**: Razorback sucker persistence in Fossil Creek must be considered relative to the broader [[Verde River]] drainage and the [[Colorado River Basin]] recovery program, rather than as an isolated, self-sustaining population target.
- **Connectivity**: Downstream movement from Fossil Springs to the Verde River is normally obstructed by miles of flume, two powerplants, and Stehr Lake; however, **flood events** could allow fish to bypass diversions and enter the Verde River directly, potentially contributing episodic recruitment pulses.
- **Comparative analog**: The Little Colorado River, another travertine stream with similar discharge, hosts naturally occurring razorback sucker populations, supporting expert opinion (Hendrickson) that Fossil Creek could analogously support survival and growth to adulthood under full flow and non-native species removal.
- **Threats/co-occurring stressors**: Non-native fish species (exotics) in Fossil Creek are cited as a factor suppressing razorback sucker survival; removal of exotics is recommended as a component of habitat renovation.
- **Regional recovery network**: Comparable examples of razorback sucker recolonizing lower Colorado River tributaries are cited, including the [[Bill Williams River]] and [[Las Vegas Wash]].

## Key Findings

1. **Stocking history**: In 1988, ~10,000 razorback sucker fingerlings were accidentally introduced to Fossil Creek, split between Fossil Springs (above Irving Dam) and Fossil Creek proper (below Irving Dam).
2. **Survival evidence**: A 1990 multi-day trammel net and snorkeling survey detected **13 razorback suckers** at Fossil Springs, confirming growth from fingerling to subadult stage — a survival rate proportionally higher than that observed in large-scale Salt and Verde River stocking efforts, and comparable to or exceeding recent natural recruitment at [[Lake Mohave]].
3. **Absence of detection ≠ extirpation**: Subsequent surveys failed to detect razorback sucker at the springs, but the U.S. Forest Service argues absence of detection does not equal proof of absence, citing the general difficulty of sampling rare individuals in deep, complex habitats, and historical examples of species reappearing after decades of non-detection.
4. **Extirpation probability model controversy**: EnviroNet Inc. applied the [[Grogan & Boreman 1998 Extirpation Model]] (from *North American Journal of Fisheries Management*, 18:522–529) to estimate a **100% probability of extirpation** of razorback sucker at Fossil Springs. This was disputed by the Southwest Center for Biological Diversity on multiple grounds:
   - Use of an inflated sample size (10,015, combining both stocking sites) rather than the more defensible ~5,000 fish stocked specifically above Irving Dam.
   - Improper inclusion of the initial stocking event as an "incidental observation," violating model assumptions.
   - Mixing of early life stage and adult observations, contrary to model protocol.
   - Misuse of the model as a definitive determination rather than an "early warning" screening tool, contrary to the original authors' intent.
5. **Habitat restoration potential**: Under increased/full minimum flows, Fossil Creek's channel morphology is expected to transition from shallow bedrock runs to deep, travertine-dammed pools — structurally suitable for razorback sucker growth and survival, mirroring conditions at Fossil Springs.
6. **Critical habitat designation history**: The U.S. Forest Service recommended Fossil Creek for critical habitat designation based on documented fingerling survival and restoration potential; the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service ultimately excluded it because not all primary constituent elements for a viable population were present, though the final rule acknowledged that non-designated areas can still hold conservation and recovery value.
7. **Expert consensus (Hendrickson, Marsh)**: Independent ichthyologists concluded that adult razorback sucker are documented to survive across a wide range of natural and artificial habitats (streams, rivers, ponds, reservoirs), and that there is "no apparent reason" adults cannot survive in Fossil Creek, particularly with restoration of full historical flows and removal of non-native competitors/predators.
8. **Refugia value**: The draft recovery plan for razorback sucker identifies natural refugia as a recovery tool, especially given excess hatchery production; Fossil Creek is proposed as a superior, more ecologically integrated alternative to artificial grow-out ponds.
9. **Management disagreement**: The Forest Service disagreed with EnviroNet/APS's conclusion of "no effect" on razorback sucker from the hydroelectric relicensing, asserting that the correct framing should assess Fossil Creek's contribution to **regional recovery** in the Verde River system rather than isolated self-sustainability within Fossil Creek alone.
