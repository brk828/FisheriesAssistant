# Strawberry Reservoir Fishery Restoration: The 1990 Rotenone Treatment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This public information document describes the history, decline, and planned restoration of the [[Strawberry Reservoir]] trout fishery in [[Strawberry Valley]], Utah. Nongame fish, mainly [[Chubs]] and [[Suckers]], came to dominate the reservoir and its tributaries and displaced [[Cutthroat Trout]]. The [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] planned a 1990 chemical treatment with [[Rotenone]] to remove nearly all fish, followed by restocking with trout and restoration of [[Tributary Spawning Habitat]]. The project cost $3.5 million and was planned to produce a high-quality trout fishery by 1991–1993.

## Background & Description

**Reservoir and fishery history.** [[Strawberry Reservoir]] was built with a planned capacity of 283,000 acre-feet. Trout were first stocked after construction. The record shows a cycle of heavy fishing, fish removal, and restocking:

- A state record cutthroat of 26.5 lbs was caught.
- Fish traps were used to collect trout eggs, and a new fish trap was later built.
- One million trout were stocked in one period, and 1,500,000 cutthroat and rainbow trout were planned for restocking after the 1990 treatment.
- Anglers were recorded keeping 10,000 fish (18,000 anglers), 105,000 fish (61,500 anglers), 303,000 fish (155,000 anglers), and 460,000 fish (140,000 anglers) across different periods.
- Catch rates fell from one trout kept per five hours of fishing to one per three hours.
- Around 700 tons of nongame fish were commercially removed in an early period.
- Fishing camps were built and later removed. A marina and concession area were constructed.
- [[Soldier Creek Dam]] was constructed, and the combined [[Strawberry Reservoir]] and Soldier Creek system was later managed as one unit. Projected water level was 560,000 acre-feet, down from a recorded 650,000 acre-feet.

**Cause of decline.** The document identifies three main factors: fish introductions, habitat loss, and water level changes. In the 1940s, anglers introduced nongame fish, probably via minnow buckets used as [[Minnow Bait]]. These species multiplied, and trout populations declined over the next 20 years. Nongame fish were chemically removed in 1961, which improved trout fishing. They reappeared in the early 1970s, and trout fishing began a slow decline.

**Current status.** The text reports that 95 percent of fish in Strawberry are chubs and suckers. The accompanying chart labels give chubs 92 percent, suckers 3 percent, and [[Cutthroat Trout]] 5 percent. Tributary habitat has also been degraded by past land management, so 95 percent of young fish produced in the tributaries are chubs and suckers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal stratification and oxygen.** In August, the reservoir stratifies into two layers. There is not enough oxygen for fish below about 30 feet. Treating the top 30 feet and the tributaries was expected to remove about 99 percent of chubs and suckers. This is the basis of the [[Chemical Fish Removal]] strategy, which exploits [[Thermal Stratification]] to focus on the layer where most fish live.

**Rotenone chemistry.** The document describes rotenone as a plant-derived compound from tropical roots. It blocks oxygen uptake in fish cells. At application rates used for fish removal, the document reports very low toxicity to birds and mammals, and livestock can safely drink the water. Breakdown depends on temperature, light, oxygen, and alkalinity. At 70°F, rotenone neutralizes in five to seven days. An oxidizing chemical may be added to accelerate breakdown. The document states that rotenone has no lasting harmful effects on the environment and refers to a separate report, Publication No. 87-14, for details.

**Dead fish and nutrient recycling.** Dead fish will sink or wash ashore and decay. The document says cleanup is not feasible because of the quantity involved. It states that decomposed nutrients will be recycled as food for small aquatic plants and animals, which will in turn feed the trout stocked several weeks later.

**Habitat restoration.** Restoring spawning habitat in the tributaries is described as a high priority. The document estimates that more than 10 million trout could be produced naturally if the streams are restored. Without restoration, chubs and suckers will continue to dominate tributary reproduction.

**Human health.** Members of the public are not allowed to collect dead fish. The fish should not be eaten, because rotenone is not registered by the [[U.S. Food and Drug Administration]] for fish destined for human consumption.

## Key Findings

**Project organization and funding**

- The [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] conducted the treatment and is responsible for the chemical treatment of the reservoir and all streams in Strawberry Valley.
- The [[U.S. Forest Service]] administers public lands and recreation facilities. The Central Utah Water Conservancy District administers reservoir waters.
- The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] administers federal funding. Three-quarters of the $3.5 million cost comes from the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]], funded by federal excise taxes on fishing tackle, sporting equipment, boats, and fuel. One-quarter comes from Utah angler license fees.

**Treatment operations**

- About 900,000 pounds of powdered rotenone were mixed into slurry in ready-mix concrete trucks.
- Twelve barges distributed the slurry in treatment zones over five days.
- Liquid rotenone was applied to all inflowing streams before, during, and after the lake treatment. Oxidizer was applied as needed to outflowing waters.
- Some 240 personnel worked on the six-week treatment process.

**Restoration plan**

- Remove chubs and suckers by chemical treatment of the reservoir's top 30 feet and tributaries, targeting about 99 percent removal.
- Restock with [[Bear Lake Cutthroat Trout]], [[Kokanee Salmon]], and [[Rainbow Trout]] in the fall. Anglers were expected to catch a sizeable fish by the following May.
- Restore tributary spawning habitat so trout can reproduce naturally and reduce long-term stocking costs.

**Schedule (1990–1991)**

- Fish traps are set and training begins in July.
- Chemical application runs from mid-August. A second stream treatment lasts 18 days.
- Trout are restocked in the reservoir in the fall, and fishing reopens in January 1991.

**Public access and regulations**

- The reservoir and adjacent land were closed to the public from August 17 to August 31, 1990, under a [[U.S. Forest Service]] special order issued under 36 CFR 261.50 and 261.53.
- Violations are punishable by fines of up to $500 and/or up to six months' imprisonment (16 USC 551). Federal, state, and local officers and permitted persons are exempt.
- Fishing on the reservoir was allowed with a 16-trout limit through August 16, 1990. Tributaries were open from May 26 through August 3 with a 4-trout limit. The reservoir was closed to fishing from August 17 through December 31, 1990, under the [[Fishing Regulations]] amendment of March 1990.
- The public could watch the operation from designated safe observation points. The [[Strawberry Visitor Center]] served as the public information headquarters, with daily video updates.

**Projected outcome**

- 1991: frying-pan-sized trout.
- 1992: good fishing for trout over 14 inches.
- 1993: an excellent fishery for families and experienced anglers alike.
