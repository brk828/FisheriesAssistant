# Changes in Growth Rates of Fishes Following Reduction in Population Densities by Winterkill

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Centrarchidae #Percidae #population-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1948 study by William C. Beckman (Michigan Department of Conservation) examined four southern Michigan lakes—**Batteese Lake**, **Goose Lake**, **Duck Lake**, and **Green Lake**—following a severe winterkill event in winter 1944–1945. The research tested the hypothesis (previously established by [[Beckman 1941]] and [[Beckman 1943]] on [[Rock Bass]]) that drastic reduction in fish population density via mortality events leads to increased growth rates in survivors due to reduced intraspecific competition. Scale samples collected immediately post-kill and in subsequent years (1946–1948) were analyzed using calendar-year growth increment methods ([[Hile 1941]] methodology). Results confirmed a short-term growth acceleration in nearly all species/lake combinations, but this benefit was **not sustained beyond 1–2 years** in most cases, raising serious questions about the practical utility of population reduction (e.g., via chemical treatment) as a long-term fisheries management technique for relieving **stunting**.

## Background & Description

This paper is a population-growth study rather than a morphological description; "morphological" data present are limited to **growth metrics** derived from scale annuli (total length increments in millimeters, back-calculated by calendar year).

- **Growth measurement method**: Direct-proportion calculations from scale annuli (body-scale relationship data unavailable for species involved).
- **[[Lee's Phenomenon]]**: Present but judged too slight to significantly bias averages.
- **Key growth metric**: Annual percentage deviation of growth from a pre-kill baseline mean (1938–1944, adjusted to 0.0%).
- **Stunting noted**: Batteese Lake bluegill population was classified as **stunted** prior to winterkill (growth well below state average per [[Beckman 1949]] state-wide standards). Bluegill in the other three lakes grew at approximately the state average pre-kill.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Shallow, vegetated, eutrophic lakes prone to periodic winterkill (oxygen depletion under ice).
  - Batteese Lake: max depth 15 ft
  - Goose Lake: max depth 10 ft
  - Duck Lake: max depth 9 ft
  - Green Lake: max depth 13 ft
- All lakes had abundant rooted vegetation over most of the bottom.
- **Winterkill history**: Prior severe kill in winter 1935–1936; next severe kill winter 1944–1945. Minor subsequent kills: Goose Lake (1946–1947), Duck Lake (1947–1948).
- **Differential mortality by species group**: Game fish (e.g., [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Black Bass]]) suffered proportionally higher mortality than "rough fish" (chubsuckers, bullheads, golden shiners), consistent with findings from [[Bennett 1948]] on Gale Lake, Illinois.
- **Post-kill competitive dynamics**: Rough fish species (e.g., lake chub suckers) rebounded disproportionately, potentially outcompeting game fish for food resources and contributing to the decline of growth-rate gains after the initial post-kill year. Chub sucker gill-net catches in Green Lake rose from 285 (May 1945) to 584 (May 1947).
- **Predator-prey implications**: Loss of largemouth bass (a key predator) in some lakes (e.g., near-total elimination in Green Lake) may allow uncontrolled bluegill population growth, reinforcing stunting cycles unless predator restocking occurs.

### Species-Specific Density Reduction & Growth Response
- **[[Bluegill]] (Batteese Lake)**: 165,000 removed (1,650/acre). Growth increased +35.6% above pre-kill mean in 1945 but remained below state average; declined nearly to baseline by 1946.
- **[[Bluegill]] (Green Lake)**: Only 16,852 removed (215/acre) — apparently eliminated the *entire* local population (no bluegill caught for 2 years). Species reestablished via stocking of 50 adults; now maintains average growth.
- **[[Largemouth Black Bass]] (Green Lake)**: 1,512 removed (19/acre) apparently eliminated the species from the lake; required restocking.
- **[[Bluegill]] (Goose Lake)**: 41,922 removed (98/acre) → slight growth improvement; uniquely, growth *rose further* in 1946 (19.5% → 29.0%) rather than declining as in other lakes/species.
- **[[Largemouth Black Bass]] (Goose Lake)**: 13,922 removed (33/acre); growth did **not** improve beyond pre-mortality levels — the sole exception in the study where reduction did not benefit growth.
- **[[Pumpkinseed]] (Green Lake)**: Largest immediate post-kill growth spike observed (+79.2% in 1945), followed by a sharp decline to +15.5% (1946), then partial recovery to +39.1% (1947).
- **[[Yellow Perch]] (Duck Lake)**: Second-largest spike (+61.3% in 1945), with the sharpest subsequent decline — growth fell *below* pre-mortality levels in both 1946 and 1947.
- **[[Yellow Perch]] (Green Lake)**: Milder initial increase (+35.0% in 1945), gradual decline (32.7% in 1946, 9.8% in 1947).

### General Patterns
1. **Universality of short-term effect**: All species/lake combinations except Goose Lake largemouth bass showed a growth increase following the winterkill.
2. **Non-durability of benefit**: Enhanced growth typically persisted only 1 year; by year 2–3 growth rates regressed toward or below pre-kill baselines.
3. **Age-class effect**: Growth improvements were most pronounced in younger age groups; older fish (with naturally smaller annual increments) showed less obvious benefit.
4. **Differential species mortality**: Rough fish (chubsuckers, bullheads, golden shiners) survived winterkill at higher rates than game fish, later dominating some lake communities and likely suppressing sustained game-fish growth gains via food competition.
5. **Management implications**: 
   - Simple population reduction (partial poisoning/kill) is likely **insufficient as a stunting-remedy technique** given the short duration of benefits.
   - Selective removal of stunted species (rather than blanket population reduction) may be more effective.
   - Restocking predatory species (e.g., [[Largemouth Black Bass]]) after prey reduction is proposed as a promising avenue to sustain desirable growth and control future stunted cohorts.
   - Accurate population estimation methods are identified as a critical unmet need for effective fisheries management.
