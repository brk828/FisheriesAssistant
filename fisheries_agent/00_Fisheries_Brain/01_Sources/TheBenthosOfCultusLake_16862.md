# The Benthos of Cultus Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports quantitative and qualitative data on the bottom fauna (benthos) of [[Cultus Lake]], a deep, oligotrophic lake in southern British Columbia. The study used dredgings collected largely in 1932 as part of a limnological investigation of factors controlling [[Sockeye Salmon]] production. The profundal (deep-water) benthos averaged about 530 mg/m² dry weight from January to May and 720 mg/m² from June to September. [[Chironomidae]] (midge larvae) and [[Oligochaeta]] (worms) made up almost all of this biomass in roughly equal proportions. Samples showed near-random spatial distribution, except that oligochaetes were strongly clustered in winter. Midges are an important food for trout and other fishes, whereas sockeye consume little benthos and use the bottom fauna mainly as a reserve food source.

## Background & Description

The 1932 dredgings were originally intended to clarify the ecology of sockeye salmon in Cultus Lake. Because sockeye rarely ate bottom organisms, the work was halted before comprehensive sampling, especially in shallow water. The author judged the data worth publishing for comparison with other lakes.

**Lake characteristics:**
- Area: 626 hectares; maximum depth 42 m; mean depth about 32 m.
- Steep shores; more than four-fifths of the bottom lies in the profundal zone (below the 20 m contour).
- Classified as oligotrophic based on hypolimnial oxygen. Only about one-third of hypolimnial oxygen was consumed during the long summer and early autumn stagnation period.

**Methods:**
- Quantitative samples were taken with an [[Ekman Dredge]] sampling 500 cm².
- Sediment was washed through wire screen and bolting silk; the finest mesh was about 15 threads per cm.
- Most effort focused on the profundal zone because of its large area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat zones**

- **Shallow littoral (to about 3 m):** Exposed to wave action with little rooted vegetation. Bottom types range from bedrock and boulders to stony shores, rubble, and sand. Stony shores, the most common type, support bulrushes (*Scirpus*), horsetails (*Equisetum*), a few cat-tails (*Typha*), and dragonfly nymphs (including *Macromia rickeri*). Snails (*Physa*, *Lymnaea*) are also present. No systematic collection was done here.
- **Deep littoral (to about 14 m):** Dense stands of rooted submersed plants, except over massive rock. Bottom changes from gravel to sand and then to mud with depth. Vegetation includes *Chara*, several [[Pondweed]] species (*Potamogeton*), *Ceratophyllum demersum*, and *Myriophyllum*. The blue-green alga *Nostoc* was abundant in winter at 10–20 m.
- **Sublittoral (14–20 m):** Little ecological distinction from the littoral above.
- **Profundal (below 20 m):** Grey ooze containing carbonized fir needles and plant material. Macroscopic fauna is limited in species.

**Notable benthic taxa (deep littoral and sublittoral)**
The fauna was identified only to coarse taxonomic levels. Notable groups include:
- Oligochaeta (regularly present)
- *Hyalella azteca* (abundant to about 15 m) and Ostracoda (abundant)
- Chironominae and Tanypinae larvae (common throughout)
- Trichoptera larvae from several families
- Snails (*Physa*, *Valvata*, *Helisoma*, and others) and small clams (*Pisidium*, *Musculium*)

**Profundal fauna**
- Dominated by Chironomidae, especially a large red *Chironomus*, plus smaller Chironominae and Tanypinae.
- Oligochaetes, probably *Tubifex*, were represented by a single species.
- *Hyalella* and *Pisidium* occurred occasionally.
- Ostracoda were abundant but not counted.
- *Hexagenia* nymphs may occur sparsely but none were found.

**Comparison with other British Columbia lakes**

Paul Lake, Okanagan Lake, and Shuswap Lake were compared with Cultus Lake. Cultus, Okanagan, and Shuswap were alike in having only midges and worms in significant numbers, at roughly three midges per worm. Paul Lake differed: it had fewer worms but many [[Fingernail Clams]] (Sphaeriidae) and [[Gammarus]], along with some planarians. Its total fauna was numerically smaller than Cultus but larger in weight because of the big amphipods.

## Key Findings

### Abundance and biomass

- Profundal dry weight averaged **530 mg/m²** (January–May) and **720 mg/m²** (June–September), equivalent to 4.7 and 6.4 lb/acre.
- Average wet weight (converted using factors from Cultus materials): **4,300 mg/m²** (Jan–May) and **6,130 mg/m²** (June–Sept).
- Dry weight split for January–May: Chironomidae 208 mg/m², Oligochaeta 320 mg/m², other organisms 2 mg/m². For June–September: Chironomidae 410 mg/m², Oligochaeta 306 mg/m², other organisms 1 mg/m².
- Oligochaetes made up about half the standing crop. The author noted they probably did not make up half the annual production.
- Dry-to-wet conversion factors were 6.6 (large chironomids), 9.2 (medium and small chironomids), and 8.7 (oligochaetes).

### Seasonal patterns (Table I)

- Total Chironomidae per m² rose from **240** (January) to **940** (September).
- By early autumn, midges were about twice as numerous as in the preceding spring.
- Large *Chironomus* larvae declined from about **70/m²** in late January to about **20/m²** in late March, because adults emerge December–March. They recovered to about **160/m²** in summer.
- Oligochaete numbers showed no significant seasonal change. Egg capsules were found in August and September.

### Spatial distribution (Table II)

- Samples were close to random, with insignificant or barely significant departures for most groups.
- Oligochaetes were clumped in winter. The χ² total was **73.4** against a critical value of **31.4** (20 degrees of freedom, 5% level). Winter variance was seven to nine times the mean, where random distribution would give equal values.
- Samples were judged representative of the northern half of the profundal zone; the southern half was physically similar.

### Sampling effort

- **27 dredgings** were made in profundal ooze in seven series, January–September 1932, at depths of roughly 30–40 m (the source text reads "80 to 40", which is likely a scan error).

- Biomass per unit area decreased as lake size increased.
- Paul Lake had a deeper, cooler profundal zone than Cultus.

### Fish utilization

- **[[Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarkii*) and **[[Coho Salmon]]** (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) eat large numbers of midge pupae emerging in spring.
- **[[Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*) take benthos, especially snails and inshore insects, but their main food is fish.
- **Chub** (*Mylocheilus caurinus*) and **[[Mountain Whitefish]]** (*Prosopium williamsonii*) eat bottom organisms from shallow areas but are uncommon.
- **[[Sucker]]** (*Catostomus macrocheilus*) is numerous and eats bottom organisms along with mud.
- **Sculpins** (*Cottus asper*, *Cottus aleuticus*) eat benthos and fish. Both are prey for **[[Dolly Varden]]** (*Salvelinus malma*), which converts benthic production into fish biomass.
- **[[Sockeye Salmon]]** (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) fingerlings eat practically no benthos. Adult residual sockeye occasionally ate emerging midges. The bottom fauna mainly serves as a "buffer" food reserve for predatory fish when sockeye are scarce.

### Conclusions

- Oligochaetes are a large part of profundal biomass but are poorly used by fish, except possibly by suckers.
- Chironomid larvae and pupae are the main benthic food link to salmonids in Cultus Lake.
- Shallow-water benthos was not studied quantitatively, so comparisons with the whole regional series are incomplete.
