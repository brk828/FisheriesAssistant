# Neurological Basis of Salmon Migration: A Hypothesis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This March 1983 proposal from the [[University of Alaska]] requests **$32,723** from the [[Alaska Sea Grant College Program]] for a one-year study (proposed start January 1, 1984) of whether mature [[Pacific Salmon]] use the [[Geomagnetic Field]] to navigate from mid-ocean back to their natal stream. The authors argue that an [[Olfactory Imprinting]] mechanism cannot alone explain ocean homing, and propose that a magnetic imprint established during early life, acting as long-term memory, could do so. The work is framed as a conditioning study in the laboratory, with Sea Grant funding limited to objectives 1 and 2, and with objectives 3 and 4 to be requested from the [[National Science Foundation]].

## Background & Description

**Project team.** Co-principal investigators L. M. Cheek and T. Nishiyama ([[Institute of Marine Science]]), H. Behrisch ([[Institute of Arctic Biology]]), and D. Stone ([[Geophysical Institute]]). The CVs describe Behrisch as a zoochemist working on temperature and enzyme regulation, Nishiyama as a fisheries oceanographer with extensive [[Bristol Bay]] sockeye and walleye pollock work, Cheek as a laboratory technician with a chemistry background, and Stone as a geophysicist specialising in paleomagnetism and geomagnetism.

**Central problem.** Mature salmon make trans-oceanic migrations and return to the same natal stream, sometimes from thousands of miles away. The proposal states that this is "one of nature's more intriguing phenomena."

**Hypothesis.** "Do salmon use the earth's geomagnetic field to orient their spawning migration to specific geographical areas from mid-ocean?"

**Rationale against olfactory imprinting alone.** The abstract gives three reasons:
1. Fish converge on natal stream mouths from a number of different headings.
2. Fish migrate directly through one or several oceanic current systems.
3. Imprint compounds (volatile organics) would decompose and be highly diluted in ocean water.

**Evidence the authors cite.**
- Electrophysiological responses to home-stream water (Dizon et al., 1973).
- Morpholine-imprinted salmon fry that returned as adults (Hasler et al., 1978).
- Magnetic orientation in European robins, cave salamanders, homing pigeons, bees, and salmon fry (Quinn, 1980).
- Magnetite reported in monarch butterflies, dolphins, green sea turtles, tuna, and bacteria.

**Proposed mechanisms.** The authors compare salmon with [[Ampullae of Lorenzini|elasmobranch electroreceptors]] and [[Lateral Line System]] function, and consider both ferromagnetic-particle torsion and electromagnetic induction as possible detectors. They also propose that the smolt transition (fresh to salt water) may be a critical window, because hormones such as cortisol and thyroid hormones shape neural development and receptivity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Migration pattern.** Pacific salmon fry follow coastal currents, particularly the [[Alaskan Coastal Current]], then enter the [[Alaska Gyre]]. Depending on species, they may circle one to several times before spawning migration. Bristol Bay sockeye follow the Alaska Gyre and the Western Bering Sea Gyre. Five-year-old fish, after about three years at sea, begin the spawning run north of the Aleutians.

**Stock mixing.** Tagging shows that stocks from both sides of the North Pacific can be caught in a single high-seas net set. The authors note that Larkin (1975) considers high-seas gill-netting potentially disastrous for declining stocks for this reason.

**Pink salmon recaptures.** Fish tagged at three points in 1959 (Royce et al., 1968) show that:
- Returning fish converge on a given area from different headings.
- Fish caught in one place may take widely divergent bearings to reach their spawning grounds.
- Migration continues rather directly through the Bering Sea Gyre. Fish bound for the Kamchatka Peninsula may cross as many as three oceanic currents.

**Geomagnetic conditions.** Magnetic storms exceeding 1300 gamma horizontal intensity and lasting more than 4 days are "not uncommon" at high latitudes (USGS 1979). The authors note that overcast conditions limit celestial cues in this region, so magnetic cues may be especially important.

**Field values cited.** Using the Figure 2 guide, the proposal lists these geomagnetic values (V = vertical intensity, H = horizontal intensity, inclination in degrees from horizontal):

| Site | V | H | Inclination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kamchatka Peninsula (pink salmon) | 47000 | 21000 | 66°N |
| Cape Olyutorskiy | 50000 | 18500 | 70°N |
| Unimak Island (control) | 46000 | 20500 | 66°N |

The authors note that the horizontal intensity difference between the two sites falls within the range of geomagnetic disturbances, which leaves open whether a separate longitude mechanism exists.

**Conservation and food security.** The proposal links salmon biology to food supply and says degradation of streams and overexploitation have reduced runs in several North American rivers. It suggests that artificially imprinting populations could help re-establish runs in [[Under-Exploited Streams]].

## Key Findings

**Specific objectives (Grant Year 1984)**
1. **Learning and biochemistry:** Condition salmon fry to a magnetic vector and monitor learning by labelled RNA or protein synthesis in specific neural structures (Sea Grant funded).
2. **Celestial vs. magnetic cues:** Determine whether orientation to light cues is modified by the magnetic vector, and at what light intensity the magnetic cue predominates (Sea Grant funded).
3. **Critical period:** Test whether maximum RNA and protein synthesis accompanies conditioning during the critical period, and whether that period coincides with the fresh-to-salt-water transition (NSF).
4. **Detection mechanism:** Determine whether detection is via torsional behaviour of ferromagnetic particles or electromagnetic induction (NSF).

**Methods, Objective 1.** Fry are conditioned under low light in a [[Hasler Conditioning Chamber]] inside a [[Helmholtz Coil]] (2 m). Fish are rewarded with food, or given a brief shock for an escape response. The field direction changes each session so that the reward chamber is always different. Tritiated uracil or lysine is injected before the final training session, and RNA and protein are then extracted from dissected brain regions.

**Methods, Objective 2.** Flood lamps serve as an artificial sun. The magnetic vector is set 135° from the sun's azimuth, conditioned to 70°, then shifted to 215°. A change in conditioned orientation would indicate use of both cues. Lamp intensity is dimmed in steps.

**Methods, Objective 3.** Sockeye smolts from the [[Trail Lakes]] system are trapped at lake exits, at two river locations, in the estuary, and in [[Kachemak Bay]]. Gill Na-K-ATPase, thyroid hormone, and corticosteroids are measured. Control fish are held in a null magnetic field. A laboratory study exposes smolts to increasing salinity, thiourea, cortisol, and thyroid hormones.

**Methods, Objective 4.** Neural circuits are to be mapped using [[2-Deoxyglucose Labelling]], horseradish peroxidase tracing, and fluorescent antibodies. Experiments are modelled on the kitten visual-cortex work of Singer et al. (1981).

**Anticipated applications.** If successful, the work could predict return success, identify the critical imprinting window, and support artificial imprinting of salmon to return to a chosen area "either as a breeding population or as a marketable product."
