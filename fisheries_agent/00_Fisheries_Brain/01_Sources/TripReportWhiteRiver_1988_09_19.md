# Trip Report: White River (Loach Minnow Survey)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #loach-minnow #Tiaroga-cobitis #ESA-Section7

## Executive Summary
This trip report documents a September 19–21, 1988 field survey of the White River system (North Fork, mainstem, and Diamond Creek) on the White Mountain Apache Reservation, Arizona. The survey was conducted in conjunction with a Section 7 consultation site visit for the proposed [[Miner Flat Dam]] and Reservoir project, led by the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA). The primary scientific objective was to assess the current distribution and status of the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) in the project-affected reach of the [[White River]], given prior documented occurrences at the [[East Fork White River]] confluence and downstream reaches. Electrofishing and seining across ten sites revealed that loach minnow were extremely rare in the sampled reaches, with only a single individual captured, raising concerns about population status and the potential impacts of impoundment, cold-water hypolimnetic releases, and increased exotic predator introductions associated with the proposed dam.

## Background & Description
Limited morphological data are reported; the focus of this survey was distributional/occurrence-based rather than morphometric. Species identified in the field were preliminarily distinguished based on external characters, with voucher specimens of [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] preserved for formal identification confirmation at Arizona State University.

- **Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)**: Small-bodied cyprinid associated with swift riffle habitat; captured specimen taken from a riffle with large rubble substrate, swift current, 6 inches to 1 foot water depth.
- **Desert Sucker (*Pantosteus clarki*)**: Common catostomid encountered at nearly every site sampled.
- **Sonora Sucker (*Catostomus insignis*)**: Co-occurring catostomid, generally less abundant than desert sucker.
- **Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*)**: Most numerically abundant species across all sites; used as an indicator taxon and voucher species due to taxonomic similarity/confusion potential with loach minnow.
- **Smallmouth Bass (*Micropterus dolomieui*)**: Single small individual captured in a deep, slow-moving side channel — noted as an introduced predatory species of concern.
- **Rainbow Trout (*Salmo gairdneri*)**: Present in North Fork sites near Alchesay Hatchery, likely of hatchery origin/influence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The [[White River]] system supports a native fish assemblage centered on riffle-dependent cyprinids and catostomids, occupying swift, shallow, rubble/gravel substrate habitats typical of montane southwestern streams.

- **Habitat conditions**: Flows were low and clear during sampling; riparian vegetation (extensive willow and cottonwood) was in good condition along both the North Fork and mainstem.
- **Substrate preference**: Loach minnow habitat is characterized by large rubble substrate in swift current at shallow depths (6 in–1 ft), though some riffle habitats were noted as "small and embedded" (fine sediment-clogged), reducing suitability.
- **Water temperature**: Ranged from 57–60°F across sites, consistent among North Fork, mainstem, and Diamond Creek locations, though sampled during light-to-heavy rain events.
- **Geomorphic barrier hypothesis**: A ~2.5-mile-long, ~100-foot-deep vertical-walled canyon extending from near the Whiteriver sewage disposal ponds downstream to near the mouth of the [[East Fork White River]] is proposed as a potential dispersal barrier limiting upstream loach minnow movement into the North Fork. Deep pools between sheer cliffs make this canyon largely inaccessible to backpack electrofishing gear.
- **Anthropogenic disturbance**: A gravel mining operation on the North Fork (near Site 7) had dredged approximately 1/4–1/2 mile of riverbed and diverted the main channel against the canyon wall, degrading habitat structure in that reach.
- **Project impact concerns**: The proposed [[Miner Flat Dam]] would impound a narrow, deep reservoir within a 200-foot deep vertical-walled canyon. Bottom-release water is expected to be cold, and warming downstream would likely be slow due to canyon shading and heavy riparian cover — a thermal regime alteration with potential consequences for native fish, including loach minnow, downstream.
- **Exotic species risk**: While the East Fork itself would not be directly inundated, increased recreational/reservoir access is expected to elevate introductions and abundance of exotic predatory fish (e.g., [[Smallmouth Bass]]), indirectly threatening native fish assemblages in the East Fork and downstream reaches.

## Key Findings
1. **Loach minnow extremely rare in survey area**: Of ten sites sampled across the North Fork, mainstem White River, and Diamond Creek, only **one loach minnow** was captured (Site 2, mainstem near Canyon Day, ~2.6 river miles below the East Fork/North Fork confluence).
2. **No loach minnow detected at the East Fork/North Fork confluence** (Site 1), despite this being a location of prior documented collections.
3. **No loach minnow found in North Fork proper** at any of the six North Fork sites sampled, despite apparently suitable riffle habitat at several locations.
4. **Historical population context**: The largest known loach minnow population occurs in the [[East Fork White River]] (documented by Propst et al. 1985 and Elliott, February 1988), which was not resampled during this trip due to time constraints, despite not being directly inundated by the Miner Flat project.
5. **Potential barrier canyon identified**: A deep, vertical-walled canyon near the town of Whiteriver may represent a natural dispersal barrier explaining the apparent absence of loach minnow from the North Fork — warranting further investigation (e.g., canyon rim survey for riffle habitat).
6. **Habitat degradation from gravel mining**: An active gravel operation has altered ~0.25–0.5 miles of North Fork channel morphology, a potential contributing factor to reduced habitat suitability in that reach.
7. **Species assemblage consistency**: [[Speckled Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Sonora Sucker]] were the dominant and most widely distributed species across nearly all sites, indicating a broadly intact native fish community outside of loach minnow scarcity.
8. **Dam design concerns**: The proposed dam's deep, narrow reservoir configuration and bottom-release design raise concerns for downstream thermal regime alteration that could further stress native, warmer-water-adapted fish species including loach minnow.
9. **Voucher specimens preserved**: Speckled dace and the single loach minnow specimen were preserved and submitted to Arizona State University for taxonomic confirmation and permanent collection deposition.
