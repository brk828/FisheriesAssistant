# A Program for Maintaining the Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 paper by [[Gordon Mueller]] (National Biological Service) describes a field-level management program to sustain the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, which holds the largest remaining population of the species. The reservoir population is aging, with fish believed to be more than 40 years old, while young fish rarely survive to adulthood, mainly because of predation by [[Nonnative Fish Species]]. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), a cooperative body of seven state and federal agencies, responded with a quick-response program: collect naturally spawned larvae, rear them in predator-free [[Rearing Areas]] to about 25 cm, and release at least 10,000 young adults with [[PIT Tagging]] while monitoring survival. The author frames this as a first step in a long-term management commitment rather than a full recovery program.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a long-lived, endemic catostomid of the Colorado River system. Its range has been reduced to less than 25% of its former extent, and nearly all fish collected over the previous two decades were adults. The Lake Mohave population declined 60% (59,500 to 23,300) over five years (Marsh 1994). Reservoir reproduction occurs, but few larvae survive to reach adulthood, and young longer than 25 mm TL are rarely found.

Recovery efforts began in 1976 with the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] and the 1979 [[Colorado River Fishery Project]], later expanded in 1987 into an upper basin recovery program covering the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The 10-year remaining cost of the 15-year program was estimated at US$84-134 million. A cooperative [[Stocking Program]] in Arizona streams (about 12 million small suckers, 1980-1989) did not establish self-sustaining populations, and the species was listed as Endangered in 1990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary threat:** Predation by nonnative fishes is considered the single most important factor limiting recruitment in Lake Mohave, although it was not quantified. Over 40 nonnative fish species have been introduced into the Colorado River basin. [[Channel Catfish]] were shown to eat stocked small suckers.
- **Piscivores in Lake Mohave:** [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]].
- **Habitat:** Habitat degradation in the lower basin is extensive, but reservoirs have also inundated seasonal floodplain nurseries and stabilized aquatic habitats. Razorback suckers historically spawned on rising spring runoff and dispersed larvae into productive floodplain nurseries before nonnative predators became established.
- **Study reservoir:** Lake Mohave is a regulatory reservoir formed by [[Davis Dam]], with a 5-m vertical fluctuation zone and a surface area of 11,400 ha.
- **Genetics:** Mitochondrial DNA diversity in the Lake Mohave population is high relative to upstream relict populations, consistent with direct descent from a large pre-impoundment population (Dowling and Minckley 1993).
- **Rearing sites:** Nine ephemeral backwaters (0.1-0.8 ha) were developed. Davis Cove (1.2 ha, maximum depth 4 m) was closed by a 10-mm bar-mesh net and flotation boom, later replaced by an earthen berm in 1994. [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] lies 16 km below [[Hoover Dam]].

## Key Findings

- **Program design:** Larvae are collected from the reservoir and stocked into isolated backwaters, grown to 25 cm (a size believed to evade most predators), tagged, and released. Fish under 25 cm are moved to a permanent cove. The minimum target was 10,000 fish over five years.
- **Propagation methods tested:** (1) stocking backwaters with adults (ratio of one female to two males); (2) stripping gametes from reservoir spawners (about 250,000 eggs from 24 females and about 60 males in 1993, with 200,000 placed in Yuma Cove); (3) light-trap collection of phototactic larvae; (4) hatchery supply from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The NFWG ranked natural larval collection first in its preferred order of methods.
- **1992 genetic result:** Although 28 females were stocked, 15 sampled juveniles derived from only 5 females, and 8 came from a single female.
- **Larval collection:** Over 11,000 larvae were collected in the 3-month 1994 effort (January to late March) and reared to 2 cm before stocking into nine backwaters at 4,000-10,000 larvae/ha.
- **Backwater results (Table 1):** Six of nine backwaters produced young. Survival ranged from 12% to 76% in backwaters, and the 1994 harvest was about 2,200 young suckers. The 1993 Yuma Cove stocking of laboratory-reared fry (2.6 cm) gave 91.5% survival (386 juveniles from 420). Fry at 1.1-1.5 cm had 5.2-12.5% survival, and fish larger than 2.5 cm had 80%.
- **Davis Cove:** Electrofishing and gill netting removed over 600 kg of nonnative fish before stocking. Predation by Lepomis and juvenile largemouth bass on smaller hatchery fish (average 6.8 cm) was observed. Subsequent sampling found only 2% of hatchery fish and over 50% of larger cove-reared fish.
- **Recaptures:** Five of 153 cove-reared fish released in autumn 1992 were recaptured in spring 1993, all males, up to 34 km from release points. Ten 28-52.9 cm fish were recaptured in 1994, with a 1993 male reaching 50-55 cm in about 2 years. A total of 640 fish had been stocked by 1993, with 15 returned; 2,880 fish had been released by the time of writing.
- **Spawning-season stocking:** In January 1991, 100 adults (33 females, 67 males) were stocked into Yuma Cove, and no young survived. In January 1992, 88 adults (28 females, 60 males) produced 296 juveniles.
- **Policy conclusion:** Full recovery was judged unrealistic because it would require habitat restoration or changes to dam operations and the removal of valuable nonnative sport fisheries. Active management was instead adopted as a "practical safeguard" while recovery is developed. The author argues that the "hands-off" approach is failing for the razorback sucker and the bonytail, which is now considered extirpated from the upper basin, and that long-term management commitments are necessary.
