# Our Living Resources: Cui-ui, Bonytail, and Razorback Sucker in the Interior West

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This item is a short article from the *Our Living Resources* series, prepared by the [[National Biological Service]] in 1995 for the Interior West and written by Paul C. Marsh. It covers the status, decline, and recovery prospects of three large-bodied freshwater fishes of western North America: the [[Cui-ui]] of Pyramid Lake, Nevada, and the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The text is partly garbled by OCR, so the summary relies on the legible passages. The article presents the cui-ui as the most recovery-ready of the three, with a discussed population-dynamics model for recovery planning. The razorback sucker and bonytail are described as having very limited natural reproduction, with recovery depending heavily on hatchery stocking and management of remaining populations.

## Background & Description

- **Cui-ui (*Chasmistes cujus*)**, family Catostomidae: a sucker endemic to Pyramid Lake, Nevada, and its tributaries. It is noted for its unique characteristics and for being a shared feature with few other upstream fishes.
- **Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**, family Catostomidae: a sucker of the Colorado River system. Reproduction is described as rare, and wild populations are aging.
- **Bonytail (*Gila elegans*)**, family Cyprinidae: a minnow found only in the Colorado River Basin. It is described as extinct or nearly so in the wild, with remaining fish mostly from hatchery origin.
- The article also refers to a Colorado River minnow whose name is partly illegible in the scan.

The article notes that the razorback sucker and bonytail were listed as endangered, and that the cui-ui is classified under the federal endangered species process as well. The text describes the Colorado River Basin as a region where the native fish fauna has been strongly transformed by dams, diversion, and changes in river flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The three fishes share a history of decline linked to river regulation. The article lists the following causes:

- **Dams and altered flow regimes** that transformed river systems and reduced native habitat.
- **Water diversion and reservoir construction**, including changes in river temperature and flow.
- **Introduced non-native predators and competitors**, which the text says prey on young fish and compete with the natives.
- **Chemical contaminants and parasites**, which are mentioned as additional stressors.
- **Habitat loss** in the Green River and other tributaries, where spawning and nursery areas were reduced.

Reproduction is a central concern. The article states that razorback suckers rarely produce young that survive to adulthood in the wild, so populations are dominated by older individuals. Because the adults are long-lived but not replacing themselves, the population is described as at risk of collapse within a limited time frame.

## Key Findings

1. **Cui-ui outlook is comparatively positive.** The article describes the cui-ui as having excellent potential for recovery. Its population is reported to be rebounding, and the species is supported by a planned allocation of water from Pyramid Lake and the Truckee River that the text says is intended to provide for reproduction.

2. **A population-dynamics model supports recovery planning.** The article describes a population-dynamics study used for cui-ui recovery. The model was calibrated with data already generated over the study period, and it is intended to test management options before they are applied.

3. **Razorback sucker and bonytail are in severe decline.** Both species are described as rarely reproducing successfully in the wild. The article reports that remaining suckers are mostly old individuals, and that bonytail have been found only rarely in the wild.

4. **Recovery depends on hatchery programs and remnant population management.** The text describes hatchery propagation, including fish stocked from captive-reared populations, such as those released into Lake Mohave to supplement the wild population. It also notes that stocking must be balanced against the need to protect remaining wild fish, and that habitat changes are being pursued to support native fish.

5. **Scientific sampling provides limited numbers.** The OCR text contains two sets of values, "mean age = 15, sample size = 126" and "mean age = 11, sample size = 94," but the species or population to which they apply cannot be reliably determined from the scan. They should be verified against the original publication before use.

6. **Recovery debates and management tradeoffs remain.** The article notes that recovery approaches are debated, and that stocking for recreational species and management practices that reduce native populations create a complex ecological puzzle. The authors call for continued research and coordinated management across agencies.
