# Pollen and Archaeology at Wetherill Mesa

#palynology #paleoecology #archaeology

## Executive Summary

This 1965 paper by Paul S. Martin and William Byers reports fossil pollen analysis of nine stratigraphic profiles from prehistoric deposits at [[Wetherill Mesa]], [[Mesa Verde National Park]], Colorado. The study aimed to clarify ecological change during the occupation and abandonment of the Pueblo cliff and mesa-top settlements about 700 years ago. The paper finds that [[Corn Pollen]] (Zea) and a caper-family type ([[Cleome Serrulata]]) mark occupation layers, that pollen content varies with deposit type, and that a late rise in pine and juniper pollen follows abandonment. The authors conclude that this rise could reflect either [[Secondary Plant Succession]] or climatic change, so the pollen record cannot settle why the Mesa Verde population left.

The document contains no information on fish, aquatic invertebrates, plankton, or water chemistry. Its only aquatic-adjacent elements are [[Check-Dams]] and alluvial sediment, which are treated as agricultural terraces rather than aquatic habitat.

## Background & Description

The paper is a Wetherill Mesa Archeological Project contribution, with the Geochronology Laboratories at the University of Arizona. Pollen analysis began in 1960 under James Schoenwetter and continued in 1961–63 under William Byers. The National Park Service supported the work because pollen analysis had proved useful for cultural and climatic questions in the Old World.

The authors frame the central question as why agricultural civilization failed at Mesa Verde about 700 years ago. Arid Southwestern archaeological deposits generally preserve rich pollen records, which the authors treat as a potential source of ecological evidence for occupation and abandonment.

**Methods**

- **Samples:** Nine profiles collected by field archaeologists, all within 3 km of each other on Wetherill Mesa.
  - Five from [[Mug House]] (Site 1229), a cliff dwelling: a trash slope, two kiva fills (Kiva D and Kiva G), a work area, and a trash profile on an upper ledge.
  - Two from farming terraces (Sites 1444 and 1539).
  - Two from light drift trash of Basketmaker and Pueblo age (Site 1644, test trench 3, verticals A and B).
- **Temporal coverage:** About the last 1000 years, including at least 200 years of prehistoric occupation.
- **Sampling interval:** Mostly 6–12 cm (0.2–0.4 ft).
- **Extraction:** A standard acid–base series using HCl, 50% HF, KOH, and sodium hypochlorite bleach for organic removal, followed by panning. Samples were mounted in glycerine and stained with basic fuchsin.
- **Counting:** A 200-grain pollen sum per level, with a separate low-power scan of about 1000 grains for Zea. Grass pollen larger than 80 microns was tallied as Zea.
- **Preservation:** Better in Mug House trash and fill than in check-dam alluvium, but poorer than in human coprolite or lake mud.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation today and in the past**

- The present vegetation of Wetherill Mesa is pinyon-juniper woodland, with Mormon tea and sagebrush as important wind-pollinated shrubs.
- During occupation, pollen of the dominant pinyon and juniper was reduced. The pollen record during Pueblo times is dominated by cheno-ams (Chenopodiaceae plus Amaranthus), grasses, and Compositae, interpreted as weedy species on disturbed ground near the pueblos and terraces.
- Spruce and Douglas-fir pollen were scarce or absent. The authors attribute the rare spruce (Picea) grains, both prehistoric and modern, to long-distance wind transport from the San Juan, Chuska, and other high mountains.

**Agricultural landscape**

- [[Check-Dams]] built of stone walls held soil behind them across drainage channels. The authors consider these to be farming terraces watered by summer runoff, although no plant macrofossils have been found in them.
- Maize-bean-squash agriculture is considered basic to the Pueblo economy. In this record, maize was well represented, squash pollen was present but scarce, and bean pollen (Phaseolus) was absent.
- Squash and bean are insect-pollinated and produce few, sticky pollen grains, so their pollen is unlikely to be abundant in sediments.

**Deposit types and formation**

- Kiva fill accumulated rapidly as occupational debris and roof fall, then more slowly as water-laid sediment. Economic-plant pollen in kiva fill most likely came from rubble and refuse deposited after abandonment, not from the period of use.
- The trash slope is considered the most reliable record because it spans a longer time.

**Abandonment and climate**

- The main stratigraphic event is a rise in juniper and then pine pollen after abandonment, about 700 years ago (13th century). Oak did not increase.
- The authors offer three interpretations:
  1. Climatic change that eliminated the conditions for Puebloan agriculture and favored woodland expansion.
  2. Widespread [[Secondary Plant Succession]] after the mesa tops were abandoned, with trees expanding into formerly disturbed or cultivated land.
  3. Very local succession on or near the check-dams and pueblos only, with no widespread change in woodland.
- Alternatives 2 and 3 do not require a major regional climate change. The authors conclude that the pollen data alone cannot discriminate among these explanations.

## Key Findings

**1. Corn pollen signals cultivation at the check-dams**

- In the terrace alluvium, nine corn grains were found among 12,700 counted grains, a frequency of 0.07%.
- Modern soil from a Chapin Mesa plot planted in Indian corn since 1919 gave 0.22% Zea in 24,000 grains and 0.14% in 13,000 grains.
- Corn in soil from an irrigated hybrid corn field at Kansas Settlement, southern Arizona, reached 1.09% of a 21,500-grain sample.
- The authors conclude that soil behind the check-dams most likely served as corn plots, with the terrace Zea frequency comparable to modern cultivated soils.

**2. Cleome-type pollen is unexpectedly abundant**

- The Cleome-type pollen grain (tricolpate or tricolporate, reticulate, prolate, 16–19 microns) most closely resembles Cleome, although the authors note possible confusion with genera such as Wislizenia and Polanisia.
- At Mug House, 1045 grains were recorded among 10,000 identifications.
- In the farming terraces, 35 Cleome-type grains were seen in 3597 determinations (about 1%).
- The authors suggest the caper family played a larger economic role than macrofossils indicate. Ethnobotanical records describe Hopi, Zuni, and Navajo use of Cleome as greens, and Schoenwetter found a maximum in Cleome pollen just before abandonment at Long House.

**3. Maize is abundant in Mug House, mainly in one layer**

- Byers recorded 1129 Zea grains in 10,000 pollen determinations at Mug House.
- Most of this came from layer 3 of the work area, which contained a mass of corn tassels.

**4. Pollen content depends on deposit type**

- Within Mug House, the trash slope, kiva, and work area profiles differ greatly, especially in economic pollen.
- Profiles from the same deposit type (adjacent kivas or trash profiles) agree much more closely.

**5. Site 1644 is poorly preserved**

- Of 20 levels extracted from the Basketmaker III and Pueblo I–II drift trash, only 11 could be counted.
- Economic-plant pollen was slightly more abundant than at the terraces but much less common than within Mug House.

**6. Abandonment signal is clear but its cause is not**

- All profiles containing postoccupation sediment show a sharp rise in pine and juniper pollen.
- The authors state that the stratigraphic signal is clear, but the explanation remains "entangled in a web of acceptable hypotheses."
- Comparable tree-pollen maxima at A.D. 600, 900–1100, and about 1400 in Arizona (Hevly 1964), and in northern New Mexico (Schoenwetter and Eddy 1964), were noted. The authors suggest the Wetherill Mesa signal may be cultural rather than climatic, because cheno-am and economic-plant curves reach maxima together.

**7. Minor taxa and contamination**

- Squash pollen (two grains) occurred in fill containing a jar of Cucurbita seeds. The authors consider the association possibly fortuitous.
- Cretaceous pollen (0.4%) in the last two profiles was interpreted as rebedded from the Mesa Verde Formation.

**Overall conclusion:** The pollen record shows that corn and Cleome were grown or tolerated around the check-dams and Mug House during occupation, and that abandonment was followed by a juniper-then-pine rise. The authors conclude that the record does not resolve whether climatic change caused the abandonment.
