# Woundfin Population Changes in the Virgin River, 1976–1999

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from BIO-WEST, Inc. (Holden, Golden and Zucker, April 2001) analyzes a 24-year monitoring database for the [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), an endangered cyprinid of the [[Virgin River]] system in Utah, Arizona and Nevada. The study was commissioned to answer why native fishes declined and to establish whether the database could support statistically valid conclusions. The authors found that seine-unit catch data (natural log of woundfin + 1) were reliable, that woundfin abundance declined significantly at all four primary stations, and that low flows, [[Red Shiner]] invasion, [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] operations, a 1989 dike breach, and rotenone treatments all contributed, with the relative weight varying by station. The report concludes that no healthy woundfin populations remain in the Virgin River.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), listed as Endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). Once abundant over more than 100 river miles, it was reduced to about 30 miles.
- **Other native species:** [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*), listed as Endangered, and [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*), proposed for listing because of habitat loss and fragmentation.
- **Non-native species:** [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), which spread upstream and became abundant around [[Beaver Dam Wash]] from about 1985–1987. [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) was analyzed as a companion species.
- **Monitoring history:** Fish sampling by the Virgin River Fishes Recovery Team since 1976, usually in spring (April or May) and fall (September). Data were consolidated into a centralized database by Dr. Thom Hardy, Utah State University.
- **Study partners:** Funded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] with assistance from the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]]; additional funding from the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] for the 1999 update.
- **Study stations (river miles):** [[Ash Creek]] (RM 94.75–95.32), [[Hurricane Bridge]] (RM 84.19–89.19), [[Twin Bridges]] (RM 71.52) and [[Beaver Dam Wash]] (RM 34.95).
- **Key methodological questions** (raised by the Nevada Division of Wildlife):
  1. Why did woundfin decline at most stations over 20 years?
  2. What was the effect of prolonged drought?
  3. What was the effect of Quail Creek Reservoir (completed 1985)?
  4. What was the effect of the 1989 Quail Creek Dam breach?
  5. What caused the red shiner increase?
  6. What was the effect of the 1988 chemical treatment in the Utah section?

**Prior analyses reviewed:** Deacon and Hardy (1984) linked spring flow to recruitment (flows under 130 cfs and over 1,000 cfs reduced reproduction); Deacon (1988) linked Quail Creek flow changes and water quality to declines; Gregory and Deacon (1994) attributed declines to reservoir diversions, red shiner invasion and the 1988 poisoning; Lentsch and Bradwich (no date) linked red shiner expansion to the absence of flash floods above 2,000 cfs in 1985–1986; Hardy et al. (1989, 1995) and Hardy (1991, 1994) used PHABSIM habitat modelling and found no strong flow effects on habitat.

**1995 Las Vegas workshop:** Convened at UNLV on July 10–12, 1995, the meeting ended in debate over the database's reliability rather than consensus on causes. It led to the present study's focus on data reliability and presentation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design and data limitations.** Seine data were collected by different investigators using inconsistent protocols. Passes were not always taken to depletion, so precise population estimates were not possible. Seining targeted sandy run habitat, and the total area sampled varied widely. For these reasons the authors judged density per area unreliable and used the count of woundfin per seine unit instead.

**Habitat and microhabitat.** Runs held significantly more woundfin than riffles at Ash Creek and Hurricane Bridge (p < 0.0001). Pools differed by station. Runs had the lowest coefficient of variation (123.8), compared with pools (137.97) and riffles (174.9). Beaver Dam Wash was later found to be dominated by cobble substrate (Holden and Abate 1999), unlike the sand-run habitat typical of woundfin.

**Flow regime.** Discharge differed significantly among seasons, with the order from lowest to highest being summer, fall, winter, then spring. Upstream [[Pah Tempe Hot Springs]] flow increased after sinkholes formed in 1985. The [[Quail Creek Dam]] breach on January 1, 1989 produced peak flows of over 60,000 cfs (reported as 66,000 cfs at Hurricane gage). Drought periods were identified in 1970–71, 1974–77, 1989–91, 1994, and 1996–97.

**Hydrologic alteration (IHA analysis).** Post-1985 spring and summer flows were substantially lower at Hurricane Bridge and Littlefield. Average spring flows were more than 30% lower at Hurricane. The upstream reference gage at Virgin, Utah, showed a similar pattern.

**Water temperature.** Water temperature had only weak positive relationships with woundfin abundance (R² ≤ 0.16), and most values were estimated rather than measured.

**Other species.** Virgin spinedace increased at Hurricane Bridge and Ash Creek in later years, which the authors interpreted as possible evidence of a more tributary-like habitat. Speckled dace declined after 1985 at Twin Bridges and Beaver Dam Wash.

## Key Findings

**Methods and data reliability**
- First-pass counts and seine-unit totals were strongly correlated across all 16 station-season combinations (mean R² = 0.919; range 0.851–0.976; slopes near 1.0, mean 1.126). Seine-unit data were slightly less variable and were used for most analyses.
- Coefficients of variation were significantly higher for first-pass data (p = 0.022), but the difference was small (about 2.0 percentage points).
- Spring and fall were the most consistently sampled seasons and were used for trend analyses. The overall mean seine-unit catch was 1.011 in spring and 1.208 in fall.
- Natural-log transformation (ln(count + 1)) normalized the data and reduced the influence of extreme values.
- Hardy et al. (1989) suggested that at least 25 seine units were needed to detect population changes.

**Population trends**
- Woundfin abundance differed significantly between years at all stations (all ANOVA p < 0.001).
- Regressions of woundfin on year were highly significant negative relationships at all stations (p ≤ 0.01).
- At Ash Creek and Beaver Dam Wash, year explained more than 47% and more than 69% of the variation, respectively.
- At Hurricane Bridge and Twin Bridges, year explained over 53% (spring) and over 62% (spring) of the variation, but less than 36% of the fall variation.
- Woundfin catch was significantly higher in 1976–1985 than in 1986–1999 at Ash Creek, Hurricane Bridge, Twin Bridges and Beaver Dam Wash (p < 0.001).
- Year-class strength (fall average length and percentage of juveniles) showed no significant trend over 1977–1999.

**Flow effects**
- Hurricane Bridge woundfin were significantly and positively related to yearly minimum and mean discharge (p < 0.05).
- Summer 50% exceedence flows above 150 cfs were associated with significantly higher woundfin abundance than other flow levels (except 51–75 cfs). Years below 50 cfs had the lowest abundance (p < 0.001).
- Fall woundfin were significantly higher in years with a 50% exceedence flow above 100 cfs (p < 0.001). The authors concluded that flows below 100 cfs are poor for woundfin, and below 50 cfs especially so.
- Drought years had significantly lower woundfin abundance at all stations (p < 0.013). Hurricane Bridge was the only station showing a significant response to high spring runoff (p = 0.011).
- Drought years had significantly higher woundfin than non-drought years at Ash Creek and Beaver Dam Wash in early drought years. These results are attributed to prior declines at those stations.

**Red shiner and interactions**
- Beaver Dam Wash and Twin Bridges had significantly higher red shiner abundance after 1985 (p < 0.001).
- Woundfin and red shiner were strongly negatively correlated at Beaver Dam Wash (fall R² = 0.643, p < 0.001; spring R² = 0.578, p < 0.001). The relationship was weaker at Twin Bridges (spring R² = 0.27; fall R² = 0.18).
- Woundfin at Beaver Dam Wash were extirpated by fall 1988, following a rotenone treatment in October 1988.
- Red shiner were absent from Ash Creek and Hurricane Bridge, so declines there cannot be attributed to this species.

**Quail Creek and events**
- Woundfin abundance at Ash Creek and Hurricane Bridge declined significantly after 1985, even without red shiner (p < 0.001).
- The 1989 dike breach was followed by a short-term drop at Hurricane Bridge, but abundance recovered by fall 1989.
- Hurricane Bridge estimates fell from over 13,000 woundfin in the late 1970s and early 1980s to about 1,600 in 1998.
- Ash Creek had no woundfin captured from fall 1996 until April 2000.

**Conclusions**
- Multiple stressors acted at each station: red shiner (Beaver Dam Wash, Twin Bridges), low flows and drought (Ash Creek, Hurricane Bridge), Quail Creek operations, and rotenone treatments.
- Hurricane Bridge, with the most consistent numbers, shows the value of an adequate, stable flow regime for woundfin recovery.
- The authors state that no healthy woundfin populations remain in the Virgin River or in any section unaffected by human disturbance.
