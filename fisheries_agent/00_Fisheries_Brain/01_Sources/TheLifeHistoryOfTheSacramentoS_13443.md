# Sacramento Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus grandis*): Life History, Ecology, and Age and Growth

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950 reprint from *California Fish and Game* by [[Taft]] and [[Murphy]] summarizes the life history of the [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*), one of the largest North American cyprinids. The paper covers its distribution, ecological competition with [[Trout]] and [[Steelhead]], food habits, spawning, and age and growth. Growth was estimated from scales, using a logarithmic scale-to-length method. The study relies mainly on fish from [[Coyote Creek]] and the [[Sacramento River]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *P. grandis* is one of four large western carps in the genus. [[Ptychocheilus Lucius]] of the [[Colorado River]] reaches the greatest size. Jordan and Evermann (1896–98) reported a maximum weight of 80 pounds for *P. lucius*.
- **Size of related species:** *P. grandis*, *P. umpquae* (Umpqua River, Oregon), and *P. oregonensis* ([[Columbia River Squawfish]] system) have reported maximum weights of about 15 pounds and lengths of three feet.
- **Distribution:** Snyder (1908, 1913) separated these species and defined their ranges in Oregon and California. *P. grandis* occurs in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Drainage]], the [[Russian River]], and the Pajaro River system.
- **Common names:** Sacramento pike, chappaul, hardhead, and whitefish (unofficial).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and competition**
- The species occupies lowland and middle-elevation streams, roughly the Upper Sonoran and Transition life zones. It lives with [[Largemouth Black Bass]] and catfish at lower elevations and with trout at middle elevations.
- Before exotic species were introduced, it was the co-dominant carnivore in the lowlands, alongside the [[Sacramento Perch]]. It prefers relatively warm streams with ample pools. Adults use lowland lakes and sloughs, but spawning requires running water.
- Habitat loss has reduced its numbers. The loss of low-lying lakes and overflow basins in the [[Central Valley]] contributed to this decline.
- [[Shasta Dam]] releases cold water that has displaced the squawfish from many miles of the Sacramento River below the dam. Trout are now dominant there.
- At [[Clear Lake]], Lake County, the species was once very abundant and supported a sport fishery. It is now practically extinct there, likely due to predation by largemouth black bass and other introduced species and to degraded spawning and nursery streams.
- Many small California streams have warmed through lower summer flows and reduced streamside cover, caused by erosion, diversion, flooding, and fire. Squawfish and other rough fish have displaced trout in many of these stretches.

**Migration**
- Observations at [[Sulphur Creek]], Sonoma County, from 1947 to 1949 showed large numbers of squawfish moving from the Russian River into the creek in March and April. Yearlings and non-spawned adults both took part. Fish lingered through the summer and returned to the Russian River in the fall.
- Not all individuals migrate. A tagged 22.5-inch male was recaptured in the same Sulphur Creek pool on March 9, 1947; February 26, 1948; May 13, 1948; September 21, 1948; and September 2, 1949.
- Because squawfish migrate upstream, the authors suggest that a barrier allowing trout and steelhead to pass could reduce squawfish numbers. Several biologists have proposed barriers, and at least one experimental barrier was expected.

**Food habits**
- A sample of 36 stomachs (1.3–7.0 inches fork length) was taken by seining on the [[Russian River]] near Cloverdale on September 2, 1949. The fish fed on aquatic insects, with small fish eating tiny larvae and large fish eating large insects.
- Two angled fish (10.5 and 12.5 inches) from Sulphur Creek had empty stomachs. They would only take a small minnow as a lure. Large squawfish have also been seen capturing small fish.
- [[Chapman and Quistorff]] (1938) examined 95 *P. oregonensis* stomachs (7.3–21.5 inches); only 37 contained food. Nine of these (24 percent) held unidentifiable fish remains, and the rest held aquatic insects.
- [[Ricker]] (1941) found that squawfish preyed heavily on young [[Sockeye Salmon]] at [[Cultus Lake]], British Columbia. Young red salmon up to one year old were the main food for squawfish over 4.5 inches long, except from May to September.

**Predation and the Cultus Lake campaign**
- Squawfish were netted more often than any other predator in the Cultus Lake study. A systematic removal campaign (Foerster and Ricker, 1941) reduced their numbers in 1938 to about one-tenth of the 1935 level.
- This increased survival of planted sockeye fry to downstream migrants by 3.1 times and of eggs from naturally spawning females by 4.4 times.
- The authors conclude that both squawfish species compete with trout for food and prey on trout young. They also compete for space, since a stream filled with squawfish offers little room for trout.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- *P. grandis* is confined to the Sacramento-San Joaquin basin, the Russian River, and the Pajaro River system, plus several small adjoining streams.
- The species is abundant over most of its range, but it has declined with loss of lowland lakes and warming of streams.

**Spawning**
- Spawning occurs in April and May, mainly in running water. Eggs are adhesive and attach to rocks or gravel.
- One observed spawning run (8–10 inch fish, Upper Blue Lake tributary, April 16, 1947) deposited large numbers of eggs on the underside of a stone at a riffle.

**Growth and age**
- Ages were determined from scales. An annulus (year mark) forms in March, and the pattern held across several hundred fish. Only five of over 900 specimens could not be aged.
- Maximum age was five years for [[Coyote Creek]] fish and nine years for Sacramento River fish.
- The largest Coyote Creek fish measured 298 mm (standard length) and was five years old. Taft's measurements were restricted to specimens up to 383 mm standard length. The reported correlation between standard and fork length was r = 1.000.
- Scales first appear at about 20 mm fish length. Growth is rapid early in the first year, and scales grow proportionally faster than fish during much of that year.
- Growth in Coyote Creek is rapid from May to early July, slows from July to November (when the stream is nearly dry), and resumes with flow in November.
- Two groups of Coyote Creek fish completing their first year (69 fish on March 7, 1927; 53 fish on February 20, 1928) gave back-calculated first-year lengths. Group 1 had a mean of 56.22 mm, median 53.25 mm, and mode 50.0 mm. Group 2 had a mean of 52.12 mm and median 50.0 mm.

**Regional growth comparison**
- Sacramento River fish were about 10 mm longer than Coyote Creek fish at the end of year one, nearly 30 mm longer at year two, and about 20 mm longer at years three through five.
- Sacramento fish grew faster in the first three years. Growth rates were practically equal in years four and five.

**Methods**
- A scale-to-length regression, plotted on a logarithmic scale, allows back-calculation of fish length at each annulus. The method assumes a scale that is large at one time stays large throughout life. A test on 21 scales from a four-year-old fish supported this assumption, with minor exceptions.

**Management implications**
- Removing squawfish could increase downstream steelhead migrant production in streams such as Sulphur Creek by several times.
- The authors recommend barriers to upstream migration as a way to reduce squawfish numbers.
