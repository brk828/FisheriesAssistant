# Biotic Factors in Running Water

#stream-ecology #macroinvertebrates #planarian-flatworms #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This symposium paper by [[T. T. Macan]] (Freshwater Biological Association, Ambleside, 1962) argues that the composition of stream communities is often shaped by living components of the environment, not only by current. Macan uses an Arctic stream (Illies, 1956), a long-term study of [[Ford Wood Beck]] (1950–1961), and comparisons with the [[Afon Hirnant]] and a calcareous stream to illustrate this. The central case is a marked faunal shift in Ford Wood Beck, which the author links to organic enrichment from a village septic tank and to predation by the carnivorous flatworm [[Polycelis Felina]]. The paper is a conceptual and descriptive survey rather than a formal statistical study.

## Background & Description

Macan opens by warning against overemphasising a single factor. Half the symposium contributions had titles beginning "The influence of current on...", and he aims to restore balance by highlighting biotic factors. Earlier, [[Huet]] (speculative) proposed that greater water drag explained scarcity of [[Gammarus Pulex]] in rivers, while Macan and Mackereth suggested predation by [[Cottus]] fish (absent from small streams) as an alternative. Macan also cites [[Mackereth]]'s work showing that [[Perla Bipunctata]] guts contained more [[Simulium Venustum]] than random feeding would predict. He suggests such a patch could reflect predation on the stream edges rather than current selection.

**Arctic stream (Illies 1956, Lapland).** Table 1 contrasts collections taken 15 m and 200 m below a lake outflow. Net-spinning Trichoptera (e.g., Hydropsyche, 27 vs 17 per 1,000 cm²) and filter-feeding Simulium dominate near the outflow, where lake plankton and detritus are abundant. Further downstream, typical stony-stream species such as [[Heptagenia]] and [[Baetis]] become numerous, although current velocity is unchanged. Macan interprets this as a community structured by food supply and biotic interaction.

**Ford Wood Beck.** This stream is just over 1 km long and nowhere more than about 2 m wide. Its valley section has a gradient of about 2%, its valley sides about 8%, and flow is about 50 cm/s in the valley and about 100 cm/s on the sides. Nearly all of the catchment is pasture. Four collecting stations are sampled, plus regular stations in the tributaries [[Outgate Beck]] and [[Sykeside Beck]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Survey methods.** Most collections used a coarse net, with results expressed as numbers caught in five minutes. Emergence traps were used to identify Trichoptera and Chironomidae, which cannot be identified in the aquatic stage. Monthly sampling ran from 1950 to 1952. An emergence trap was installed at station 1 in 1952. From 1956 onward, collecting was annual in March, with occasional late-June collections for summer species. No collections were made in 1957, but the series has been unbroken since.

**Human influence.** Mains water reached the village of Outgate in late 1951. By the end of 1951, seven houses were connected, and by the end of 1952, all fourteen were. Four of these houses had previously used wells. Waste water went to septic tanks, one of which overflowed into the main stream about 100 m above where it resurfaces. Water used at the farm rose from 34 to 134 thousand gallons over the study period, according to Table 2 (values as recorded).

**Physical stability.** Macan argues that the physical environment of Ford Wood Beck did not change, so biological interactions are the most plausible cause of the observed changes. He rejects climate as the driver because the affected species have very different life histories, including winter growers, summer species and bivoltine species.

**Stream differences.** Macan explains several distributional patterns:
- [[Planaria Alpina]] is a cold-water species, restricted by temperature and by competition with Polycelis felina. Its winter range extends only slightly.
- [[Wormaldia Occipitalis]] is much commoner at Outgate, probably because of lower temperatures, and it cannot persist in the drying Sykeside Beck because it takes two years to complete its aquatic stage.
- [[Leuctra Nigra]] and [[Diplectrona Felix]] are most abundant at station 4, which has a moderate current, low run-off and a silty substratum.
- [[Perla Bipunctata]] occurs only at stations 1–3. It takes three years to mature, so it cannot recolonise the drying Sykeside Beck. [[Perlodes Mortoni]], which completes development in one year, occurs throughout and is commonest in Sykeside.

## Key Findings

**1. Septic-tank enrichment and the Ford Wood Beck shift.**
- Mains water connection to Outgate led to overloading of septic tanks, which Macan considers the most important change. This probably increased the amount of organic matter, especially living organic matter, flowing downstream.
- Macan concludes this was beneficial to most animals. Polycelis felina increased most, from less than one per station in early years, to more than one thousand per five-minute collection in 1959 (compared with two or three per station previously), with a drop in 1960 and a rise again in 1961.

**2. Predation by flatworms.** Macan proposes that Polycelis felina caused the decline of several species. His laboratory observations in a white dish showed flatworms attacking nymphs that had become impeded by mucus, though the trigger for attack was not identified. He notes that the affected species are mainly nymphs living on stone surfaces (e.g., [[Ecdyonurus Torrentis]], [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]], [[Baetis Rhodani]]) and stoneflies living under stones, while [[Baetis Pumilus]] was less affected.

**3. Population trends.**
- [[Ecdyonurus Torrentis]] disappeared after 1959 and has not been recorded since.
- [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]] was the most abundant species in 1953 but declined from about 1956, with none taken in 1960.
- [[Baetis Rhodani]] declined from being among the most abundant species.
- [[Habrophlebia Fusca]] rose from being extremely scarce to among the commonest species, mainly in Outgate Beck.
- [[Ephemerella Ignita]] has spread upstream.
- [[Brachyptera Risi]] was scarce early on, then increased substantially at Sykeside in 1960 and remained higher in 1961. Macan could not explain this in the unaltered reach.
- Two species were lost completely, and no new species appeared. Macan considers the change in proportions substantial even though species richness hardly changed.

**4. Refugia and recolonisation.** Rhithrogena semicolorata has not been eliminated, probably because of a reservoir population in the Sykeside tributary, where flatworms are scarce. Macan infers that Rhithrogena and Baetis rhodani drift downstream before emergence, based on the abundance of nymphs in Outgate Beck compared with few adults.

**5. Comparison with the Afon Hirnant.** [[Hynes]]'s survey of the Afon Hirnant (rising at 580 m, about 10 km long) reveals a similar fauna. Macan identifies eight species differences:
- Three are attributed to slower flow in parts of the Hirnant (for example, [[Siphlonurus Lacustris]]).
- Four relate to temperature, competition, or both (for example, [[Diura Bicaudata]], which is excluded from lower reaches by Perlodes mortoni).
- One is attributed, speculatively, to chemistry: the complete absence of [[Gammarus Pulex]] from Welsh streams.

**6. Flow and colonisation.** Macan notes that faster flow carries more food past a fixed point, and [[Ambühl]] demonstrated the relation between flow and oxygen supply. Torleya major nymphs are swept away in floods and reappear after a year or two. Macan suggests that proximity to a source population is the key ecological factor determining whether such a species persists.

**7. Conclusion.** Macan concludes that biotic interactions, including food supply, predation and competition, can produce marked faunal change in a stream whose physical environment has not altered. He calls for greater attention to biological factors alongside physical ones.
