# Chemical Characteristics of a Desert Stream in Flash Flood

#freshwater-ecology #hydrology #water-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This 1978 study by [[Stuart G. Fisher]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University) documents changes in water chemistry and discharge during a single flash flood on [[Sycamore Creek]], a desert tributary of the [[Verde River]] in central Arizona. Suspended particulates rose from 0.3 g/l to a maximum of 55.2 g/l, while total dissolved solids declined through dilution. Nitrate and phosphate generally increased, following the pattern of suspended material, which the authors attribute to leaching of particulates. The authors conclude that peak flow events dominate sediment and nutrient transport, and that water-resource managers estimating watershed outputs or reservoir inputs in the Southwest must monitor these events closely.

## Background & Description

**Flash floods in desert drainages.** [[Flash Flood]] events are common in intermontane deserts of the American Southwest. They are most intense on watersheds with high relief, extensive exposed bedrock, sparse vegetation and shallow soils, particularly when [[Cyclonic Storm]] systems produce localized, intense rainfall. Hoyt & Langbein (1955) noted that flash floods seldom produce peak discharges above 25 per cent of normal flood peaks for US streams as a whole. Nonetheless, fast-flowing torrents contribute disproportionately to the filling of [[Reservoir Sedimentation|reservoirs]] in arid lands, and carry large loads of dissolved and particulate material downstream.

**Human impacts.** Many accounts describe flash floods devastating human settlements, often built on alluvial fans and debris cones, for example in Willard and parts of Salt Lake City (Utah) and Los Angeles (California). The authors state that no prior data existed on transport characteristics of a desert stream in flash flood.

**Study site.** Sycamore Creek lies about 32 km northeast of Phoenix. Key characteristics:

- Channel length: about 65 km
- Watershed area: 505 km²
- Elevation range: 427 to 2164 m
- Gradient: about 23 m/km
- Upper 85 per cent of the drainage: rugged igneous and metamorphic terrain with shallow soils
- Vegetation: desert scrub at lower elevations, with juniper, oak, pinyon pine and ponderosa pine at the highest elevations
- Sampling point: 552 m elevation, 32 km above the confluence with the Verde River

**Climate.** Annual precipitation ranges from about 30 cm (510 m) to 51 cm (1040 m at [[Sunflower]]). Mean pan evaporation at [[Bartlett Reservoir]] is 313 cm/yr, about six times precipitation, so streamflow is near zero for much of the year.

**Methods.** The flood was sampled on 26 September 1976, during an intense cyclonic disturbance centred on the catchment. Samples were taken before, during and for two days after the flood. Discharge was taken at 15-minute intervals from a [[Stream Gauging|recording gauge]] operated by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] (Sycamore Creek near Fort McDowell). Analyses included:

- Conductivity (25 °C)
- [[Nitrate]], [[Phosphate]] and iron (colorimetry)
- Colour (optical comparator)
- Suspended sediment (gravimetric) and organic fraction (loss on ignition at 550 °C)
- [[Total Alkalinity]] (titration)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical disturbance and riparian response.** The leading edge of the flood, roughly one metre high, passed the sampling site at about 1150 h. Its estimated velocity was 2 m/s, consistent with travel time over about 11 km in 1.33 h (2.3 m/s). Large organic debris, from particles under 1 cm to cacti and tree trunks over 2 m long, was transported, mostly in the first 15 minutes. The authors note that flood debris deposited on banks more than a metre above mean high water can mislead attempts to reconstruct past flood levels.

- [[Seep Willow]] (*Baccharis glutinosa*) was physically agitated but showed no significant damage after the water subsided. Its beds trapped fine organic debris during recession.
- Other riparian species that retained debris included *Platanus wrightii*, *Populus fremonti*, *Salix goodingii* and *Prosopis juliflora*.
- [[Bermuda Grass]] (*Cynodon dactylon*) on banks and bars was stable and changed little.
- Boulders over 0.3 m in diameter were not moved and served as focal points for redeposition of substrate.

The authors link these observations to the concept of a "perpetual succession" of [[Riparian Vegetation]] (Campbell & Green, 1968). They suggest that substrate, channel form and upstream stores of reproductive plant material sustain a disclimactic vegetation type along erosive watercourses in southwestern deserts.

**Regional precipitation.** Rainfall was highly localized. Stations within 50 km recorded 2.5 cm or more of daily rain for a day or more before the flood. Stations to the north and east recorded 1.37 to 3.18 cm during the 24 h including the flood, while stations to the south and west recorded less than 0.13 cm. Adjacent normally dry washes, such as Pine Creek and Mesquite Wash, stayed dry, which the authors take as evidence of the local nature of cyclonic storms.

**Ecosystem role of flash floods.** From the desert stream perspective, flash floods are short-lived catastrophic events that can decimate biota and reset successional sequences. From a watershed perspective, they move large volumes of material from the surface into downstream systems, especially [[Reservoir]] impoundments. Minckley (1969) reported that water leaving large desert reservoirs resembles flood-stage inflow water, so reservoirs integrate stream inputs.

## Key Findings

**1. Discharge and hydrograph**

- Discharge rose from 0.23 m³/s to 40.8 m³/s as the initial wave passed.
- Two discharge peaks were recorded: 45.6 m³/s at 1210 h and 53.3 m³/s at 1315 h. The authors attribute these to staggered tributary inputs from different sub-catchments.
- Recession was gradual: 1.05 m³/s after 24 h and 0.08 m³/s after 48 h. The authors describe this as atypical for desert flash floods, which often last only a few hours.

**2. Suspended sediment**

- Suspended solids rose from 0.3 g/l to 47.6 g/l at the leading edge and peaked at 55.2 g/l, about 200-fold, before the first discharge peak.
- After the initial peak, concentrations fell to 27.7 g/l, rose to nearly 40 g/l during the second pulse, then declined to 2.6 g/l at 24 h and to trace levels by 48 h.
- The organic fraction stayed nearly constant at 9 to 13 per cent (mean 11 per cent) of particulate transport.
- The authors propose that the early peak reflects entrainment of loose channel material, while the later, lagging rise reflects finer sediment from sheetflow and re-sorted deposits.

**3. Dissolved constituents**

- Specific conductance fell from 395 µmhos/cm to 172 µmhos/cm, consistent with dilution. Pre-flood levels returned by 48 h.
- Total alkalinity fell from 3.07 to 1.28 mEq/l and recovered by 48 h. Neither conductivity nor alkalinity responded to the second pulse.
- Nitrate and phosphate declined briefly as the front passed, then rose to two peaks, generally increasing by a factor of 2 to 4 over base-flow levels.
- Dissolved iron and colour roughly tripled during flooding, with peaks occurring during recession of each major pulse.

**4. Mechanisms**

- Dilution explains the fall in total dissolved solids. The authors cite comparable patterns in other streams (e.g. Hem, 1969; Rinne, 1975).
- Nitrate, phosphate and iron behaved differently. The authors suggest leaching of particulates is the main source, and that high particulate loads may shift phosphate equilibrium from particles to water (Taylor & Kunishi, 1971).

**5. Mass transport and flood frequency**

- Median daily discharge (1961–70) was 0.07 m³/s. Discharge exceeded 2.83 m³/s only 2.9 per cent of the time.
- Flows below the median accounted for only 1.8 per cent of total water yield. About 70 per cent of water was produced at flows above 2.83 m³/s.
- Seven days in ten years, with mean discharge above 28.3 m³/s, produced 28.3 per cent of total water output.
- Flows below 10 times the median contributed only 7 per cent of runoff. The authors conclude that monitoring should focus on peak events.
- Dissolved transport was under 0.1 per cent of particulate mass at peak discharge, though peak flows still dominate dissolved material delivery over time.

**6. Broader implications**

- Bormann et al. (1969) found a single autumn storm accounted for 54 per cent of particulate output from a New England watershed over two years.
- McConnell (1968) found Pena Blanca Lake, Arizona, received most of its organic input during rare flash floods. Rinne (1975) reported similar dominance for [[Roosevelt Lake]].
- Managers estimating watershed outputs or reservoir inputs in the Southwest must therefore prioritise monitoring of infrequent, high-discharge events.
