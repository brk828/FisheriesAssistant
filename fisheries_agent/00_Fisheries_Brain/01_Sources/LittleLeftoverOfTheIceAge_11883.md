# Little Leftover of the Ice Age: The Desert Pupfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This popular-science article describes the [[Desert Pupfish]], a one-inch killifish that survives as a relict of the last [[Ice Age]] in isolated desert springs of the [[Death Valley]] drainage. The author argues that the fish is a scientifically important example of rapid [[Divergent Evolution]] and a conservation priority. Its populations face destruction from water pumping, land development, pesticides and introduced predators. The text reports that two pupfish species have already been lost and five others listed as endangered. The article also traces the early scientific attention paid to the fish and the first proposals, in the 1940s, to protect the habitat at [[Devils Hole]]. The document ends mid-sentence, so its conclusions are incomplete.

## Background & Description

The article opens with a public debate over whether a very small fish deserves protection. It presents the pupfish as one of the nation's great biological treasures and a relic of the Ice Age, with populations surviving for about 20,000 years.

- **Distribution:** Some pupfish live in [[Owens Valley]], California, and others in Death Valley's [[Salt Creek]]. Most are found in about two dozen hot and cold springs in [[Ash Meadows]], across the Nevada line.
- **Physical description:** The fish belongs to the killifish family and measures no longer than a little finger. Females are slim and mottled brown. Males have iridescent blue and purple coloring and defend territories of about eighteen inches, which gives the fish its nickname.
- **Historical discovery:** A [[U.S. Biological Survey]] team first reported the species in 1891. Its reports of blue, gem-like springs, twenty to thirty feet deep, were initially doubted.
- **Devils Hole:** A scientist found a small population of about 200 pupfish in a ten by forty-foot black crevice. The pool lies fifty feet below the surface, and the fish survive at 108 degrees Fahrenheit by feeding on algae and breeding on a narrow ledge. These fish, *Cyprinodon diabolis*, are described as the most divergent of the desert pupfish and therefore the most prized.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article emphasizes the extreme conditions the fish tolerate and the threats they face.

**Physiological tolerances (as reported):**
- One species tolerates water about six times as salty as sea water.
- Another lives in springs reaching 112 degrees.
- Some tolerate high [[Borate]] concentrations.
- Others endure temperatures ranging from nearly freezing to 110 degrees.

**Evolutionary significance:** The author states that this adaptability within a short evolutionary period makes the pupfish one of the world's best examples of [[Divergent Evolution]], surpassing [[Darwin's Galapagos Finches]] in this respect. Scientists suggest that the fish's adaptive biology might help humans adapt to their own changing world.

**Threats identified:**
- **Water extraction:** The article calls present pumping from springs and [[Aquifers]] the most destructive factor, and warns that the falling water table may eliminate all remaining habitat.
- **Agricultural diversion:** Water is taken for farming, and surface water is diverted for irrigation.
- **Habitat alteration:** Bulldozing has damaged tree-shaded springs. Pools have been drained, and streams channelized.
- **Chemical pollution:** Pesticides and other farm chemicals have entered habitats.
- **Introduced species:** Transplanted [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and exotic tropical fish have further reduced pupfish populations.

**Conservation status (as reported):** Two species have been lost and five others listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Key Findings

- **Relict status:** The pupfish has survived in isolated habitats for about 20,000 years since the Ice Age. The article notes that the region was once an integrated system of lakes and rivers.
- **Extreme tolerance:** Populations withstand salinity six times that of sea water, springs of 112 degrees, high borate levels and temperatures from nearly freezing to 110 degrees.
- **Evolutionary value:** The article presents the species as a leading example of rapid divergent evolution.
- **Imminent threat:** Pumping from springs and aquifers is identified as the greatest danger. [[Phil Pister]], a California Fish and Game biologist, warns that without a strong commitment to overcome the problems, "the pupfish is finished."
- **Early protection efforts:** In the 1940s, [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Robert Rush Miller]] proposed making Devils Hole part of [[Death Valley National Monument]], established in 1932. The National Park Service initially opposed the proposal, because protecting an endangered species was not then considered sufficient grounds for a park. Preservation of unusual geological formations was accepted as grounds.
- **Research history:** Hubbs had studied the pupfish since 1915, and Miller was described as the leading authority on the species.
