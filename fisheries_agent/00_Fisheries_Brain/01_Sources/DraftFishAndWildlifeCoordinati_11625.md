# Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act Assessment: Central Arizona Project Diversions on the Verde and East Verde Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act report, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (December 14, 1988), assesses impacts of proposed [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water exchanges and direct diversions on the Verde and East Verde Rivers in Yavapai and Gila Counties, Arizona. It evaluates only the effects of water removal on aquatic and riparian resources; pipelines, pumping plants and existing water rights are excluded.

Eight CAP allottees hold a combined 17,343 acre-feet per year (about 23.9 cfs if diverted continuously). The report concludes that the diversions, as originally proposed, would cause significant adverse effects on native fishes and riparian vegetation, particularly in the upper Verde River and the East Verde River. It recommends minimum instream flows, maximum diversion rates, long-term monitoring and general mitigation. The report was prepared with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] concurrence.

## Background & Description

The study area covers about 165 miles of high-quality aquatic and riparian habitat, including extensive mature cottonwood stands. The [[Verde River]] flows south off the [[Mogollon Rim]] for roughly 125 miles undammed before reaching [[Horseshoe Reservoir]]. The [[East Verde River]] enters from the east about 25 miles upstream, with a perennial length of about 40 miles. Its flow is supplemented by intermittent trans-basin imports from Blue Ridge Reservoir under a [[Phelps Dodge Water Exchange]] with the [[Salt River Project]].

The CAP was authorized under the Colorado River Basin Project Act. In March 1983, the Secretary of the Interior allocated CAP water to 120 Arizona users; eight of them planned to exchange allocations and divert from these rivers. Only three submitted detailed plans:

- City of Prescott and Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community: combined French-drain system on the Verde River, with pipeline to the city.
- Town of Payson: French drain on the East Verde River, diverting a constant 1.7 cfs until 2010, then 3.6 cfs (1,231 and 2,606 acre-feet per year).
- Camp Verde Indian Community and Tonto Apache Indian Community: diversion patterns modeled by Reclamation, not detailed plans.

The study used the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) with [[Physical Habitat Simulation System]] modeling, supported by the [[Great Plains Method]] for flow recommendations. The evaluation covered nine fish species selected by a team of [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[U.S. Forest Service]] personnel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation and riparian zone.** Upland vegetation shifts from grassland near the headwaters to juniper woodland, chaparral and desertscrub downstream. The headwater riparian zone is dominated by seep willow. Mature [[Fremont Cottonwood]] stands are concentrated near [[Dead Horse Ranch State Park]], which holds probably the largest contiguous mature cottonwood stand on the Verde River. The report estimates that about 95 percent of Arizona's historic riparian habitat has been lost, and that the remaining cottonwood-willow and mesquite riparian vegetation covers 101,793 acres (0.15 percent of the state).

**Channel morphology.** The upper Verde flows through a volcanic canyon, shifts between sandy meadows and cobble canyons, and widens into a broad, meandering floodplain in the Verde Valley. The East Verde headwaters are high-gradient, boulder-filled channels.

**Human uses.** Grazing, abandoned mines, irrigation diversions, and growing recreation (angling, rafting, ORV use) affect the system. About 52,900 angler-days were recorded on the East Verde River in 1986.

**Fish community.** The upper Verde still supports a strong native community: longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker, roundtail chub, speckled dace and spikedace. Introduced species including [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Carp]] and channel catfish increase downstream; spikedace are essentially absent below Sycamore Creek. Flathead catfish are replacing channel catfish. The East Verde headwaters are a put-and-take [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery.

**Special status species.** [[Spikedace]] is federally threatened, with critical habitat proposed from Sullivan Lake to below Sycamore Creek. [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Peregrine Falcon]] are federally endangered. Four bald eagle nesting territories are known above Horseshoe Dam. [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] have been reintroduced. A southwestern [[River Otter]] population was reintroduced from Louisiana otters (1983–1985) and has established itself.

**Wildlife.** Riparian habitats support raptors (common black-hawk, zone-tailed hawk, Cooper's hawk), riparian songbirds (yellow-billed cuckoo, Bell's vireo), and amphibians and reptiles (lowland leopard frog, Mexican garter snake). Fish and amphibians can make up more than 78 percent of the common black-hawk diet.

## Key Findings

**Flow reductions**
- The diversions would reduce flow by about one-half in Reaches 1 and 2 (upper Verde) and about two-thirds on the East Verde during periods without trans-basin imports.
- At Tangle Creek, the proposed diversions would remove about 4.4 percent of average annual flow. Proportionate removal ranges from 0.8 to 42.1 percent of mean median monthly flow depending on the gage.
- Flood flows (bankfull or greater) would not be altered in volume or timing (Table 6). Peak-flow removal percentages are below 0.5 percent at all four gages.

**Biological impacts**
- Reaches 1–3 (upper Verde): significant adverse impacts to the rare native fish community and to spikedace. Roundtail chub habitat is essentially eliminated below 19 cfs. Diversions would increase red shiner habitat.
- Reaches 4–5 (Verde Valley): short-term acute effects are minor, but cumulative long-term effects could damage riparian and aquatic communities.
- Reaches 6–7: relatively insignificant flow change, though habitat for adult fish in Reach 6 declines as flow drops.
- Reaches 8–10 (East Verde): heavy impacts on the trout fishery and native fish, with extended dewatering and possible mortality from temperature and oxygen stress.
- Riparian vegetation: the primary long-term effect would be narrowing of the riparian zone, mainly in Reaches 4 and 5, through lateral loss of bank storage water.

**Recommended minimum instream flows (cfs)**
- Reaches 1–2 (Paulden gage): 24 year-round; no withdrawals from these reaches.
- Reach 3 (Clarkdale gage): 60 (single minimum flow).
- Reaches 4–5 (reinitiated gage below Camp Verde): 13.
- Reach 6 (Camp Verde gage): 40 (June) to 200 (January), monthly.
- Reach 7 (Tangle Creek gage): 100 (June–July) to 255 (January), monthly.
- Reach 8 (Highway 87): 8 (February) to 20 (May), monthly.
- Reaches 9–10 (Childs gage): 10 to 28, monthly.

**Maximum diversion recommendations**
- Maximum instantaneous rates are set for each entity: City of Prescott and Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community at 15 cfs. Rates for the remaining entities (Cottonwood Water Company, Camp Verde Indian Reservation, Camp Verde Water Company, Tonto Apache Indian Reservation, E&R Water Company, City of Payson) are listed in Table 9.

**Monitoring**
- Riparian vegetation: aerial photography, permanent photo points, bank-storage wells and vegetation plots, on a biennial to five-yearly schedule.
- Aquatic: at least one permanent fish station per reach (Reaches 1–10), sampled annually at first, then every two years and eventually every five.
- Hydraulic: permanent stream profile transects, monitored annually during construction and biennially for seven years after.
- A four-agency team reviews monitoring data and assigns responsibility for impacts. Reclamation bears the cost of mitigation for impacts it is found to cause.

**General mitigation measures**
- Avoid or minimize direct damage to riparian and aquatic systems during construction.
- Purchase water rights for instream flow and riparian lands or conservation easements.
- Develop a multi-agency watershed and riparian habitat improvement plan.
- Improve habitat and consider additional trout stocking in the East Verde headwaters.
- Quantify mitigation once withdrawal plans are final.

**Conclusions**
- The study area holds unique, scarce and irreplaceable resources, so in-kind replacement is not considered feasible.
- Simultaneous withdrawal of all CAP allocations would exceed available stream flow; the state adjudication process must address this before withdrawals are permitted.
- Indirect effects from population growth and increased recreation are expected to continue regardless of the project.
