# Evaluation of Fall-Run Chinook Salmon Yearling Releases

#ichthyology #marine-biology #oncorhynchus

## Executive Summary

This report, a second evaluation by the California Department of Fish and Game, tests whether rearing [[Fall-Run Chinook Salmon]] to yearlings at [[Feather River Hatchery]] and releasing them can raise adult returns over [[Fingerling Release]] practice. Marked yearlings from three brood years (1967, 1969, 1970) were released at two sites: the hatchery and [[Rio Vista]] on the [[Sacramento River]]. Returns were measured across ocean commercial and sport fisheries, the Sacramento-San Joaquin spawning populations, and hatchery recoveries, then compared with fingerling returns and assessed through a [[Benefit/Cost Ratio]].

Yearling releases returned substantially more salmon than fingerling releases and were economically justified, with an overall unweighted benefit/cost ratio of 18.0:1. The 1970 brood year was a notable exception with poor returns.

## Background & Description

Earlier work (Warner, Fry, and Culver 1961) found that yearling rearing built strong hatchery and spawning returns for a given stream, though ocean commercial returns were negligible because many fish matured early at smaller size and reached the 66-cm commercial size limit late. The Department therefore began a second evaluation in 1969, using [[Feather River]] stock. It also tested whether releasing yearlings in the lower Sacramento River, rather than at the hatchery, would further increase returns, as it does for fingerlings.

**Study Design**
- **Rearing:** Fingerlings from the 1967, 1969, and 1970 brood years were reared at Feather River Hatchery, Butte County, for about one year.
- **Splitting:** Yearlings from each brood year were divided into two nearly equal lots. One lot was released at the hatchery and the other at Rio Vista (Bryant's Marina, Sacramento River).
- **Excluded group:** A brood year (printed as 1958 in the OCR text) was excluded because of heavy hatchery losses to [[Infectious Hematopoietic Necrosis]], known locally as Sacramento River Chinook Disease.
- **Marking:** The 1967 brood was marked by two fin clips. The 1969 and 1970 broods received two fin clips plus color-coded [[Coded-Wire Tag]] nose tags. The 1969 brood was the first salmon marked with nose tags in California.
- **Release totals:** About 266,790 marked salmon were released (136,545 at the hatchery and 130,245 at Rio Vista). Average sizes ranged from 38 g (12/lb) to 76 g (6/lb).
- **Marking interruption:** Fin-clipping of the 1969 brood was halted by [[Parr-Smolt Transformation]] symptoms and resumed in January 1969.

**Site Geography**
The hatchery to Rio Vista driving distance was roughly 200 km (about 3 hours by planting truck), and the stream distance via primary channels was about 225 km.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishery Sampling Methods**
- **Ocean commercial:** Landings were estimated using a [[Hartley-Ross Ratio]] estimator applied to port sampling data across California, Oregon, and Washington. Pacific Coast state reporting systems and the Regional Mark Processing Center supplied the totals.
- **Ocean sport:** California used party-boat and skiff creel surveys, Oregon used punch-card reporting with coast creel counts, and Washington used punch-card and Coast Guard counts with fisher reports.
- **Freshwater:** Hatchery counts were treated as accurate because every fish was examined for marks. Spawning ground counts were treated as minimums, since carcass deterioration limited recovery of marked fish. The [[American River]] stock survey did not record marks, so its marked count was estimated from the proportion of marked [[Feather River]] fish entering [[Nimbus Hatchery]].
- **Statistics:** Site differences were tested with a [[Chi-Square Test]].

**Economic Methods**
Ocean commercial value was the estimated catch multiplied by the price paid by buyers. Sport value was based on angler expenditure per fish caught, drawn from a California Department of Fish and Game survey (1953 base, adjusted through 1974 using Consumer Price Index changes). Fish production costs covered egg taking, rearing, and capital investment, but not marking costs.

## Key Findings

**1. Ocean Commercial Returns**
- Returns ranged from 0.5% to 5.1%, with an unweighted mean of 3.5% across brood years.
- The 1967 and 1969 broods contributed most, with over 90% landed in California. About 66% were caught as 3-year-olds and 34% as 4-year-olds.
- The 1970 brood contributed only 543 fish (0.5%). Only 15% were caught as 3-year-olds, while 85% were caught as 4-year-olds.
- Hatchery-released yearlings returned about 4.0% versus 3.0% for Rio Vista releases (roughly 1.3 times higher).

**2. Ocean Sport Returns**
- Returns ranged from 0.3% to 5.0%, with an unweighted mean of 2.6%.
- The 1967 and 1969 broods contributed an estimated 4,691 fish (3.2%). The 1970 brood contributed 402 fish (0.3%).
- Eighty-seven percent of sport-caught salmon were 3-year-olds and 12% were 4-year-olds.
- Hatchery releases returned 3.1% versus 2.1% for Rio Vista releases (about 1.5 times higher).

**3. Freshwater Returns (Sacramento-San Joaquin System)**
- Returns ranged from 0.0% to 2.0%, with an unweighted mean of 1.4%.
- Rio Vista releases returned more adults (1.5%) than hatchery releases (1.2%).
- About 90% of hatchery-release returns were recovered in the Feather River. Only about 22% of Rio Vista-release returns were recovered there; most went to the [[American River]].
- Thirty-six percent of freshwater returns were 2-year-olds, and about 62% were 3- and 4-year-olds.

**4. Total Returns**
- Total percent returns ranged from 1.4% to 13.2%, with an overall mean of 7.5%.
- Total returns were 5.1% for Rio Vista releases and 7.1% for hatchery releases.

**5. Comparison with Fingerling Releases**
- Fingerling releases (3.5 million marked fish, averaging 5 g, from 1961 to 1971 at Coleman and Nimbus hatcheries) returned 1.0% at Rio Vista and 0.5% at the hatchery.
- Yearling fishery returns were 5.1 times higher for Rio Vista releases and 14.2 times higher for hatchery releases.
- Yearlings also contributed substantially more to spawning stocks, roughly 8.8 times more for Rio Vista releases.
- The hatchery-versus-Rio Vista difference was statistically significant (reported as p < 0.01).

**6. Yearling Production Costs**
- Rearing costs ranged from $0.77/kg for the 1969 brood to $3.57/kg for the 1967 brood, mainly due to differences in mortality during rearing.
- Average cost was about 6.1 cents per fish. Each marked yearling cost about 10 cents on average because marked fish were larger (fewer per kilogram).
- The marked group cost $27,453 to produce.

**7. Value of Marked Salmon**
- **Commercial:** 7,936 marked salmon weighing 39,228.5 kg were landed during the five seasons (1971 to 1974), worth an estimated $67,390.78. Of this, $27,591.31 came from Rio Vista releases.
- **Sport:** 5,101 marked fish were caught between 1969 and 1974, worth an estimated $160,314. Rio Vista releases contributed 2,092 fish ($65,823), and hatchery releases contributed 3,009 fish ($94,491).

**8. Benefit/Cost Ratio**
- The ratio ranged from 1.8:1 to 53.1:1, with an overall unweighted mean of 18.0:1.
- Hauling costs could not be separated from production costs, so the ratio is slightly low for Rio Vista releases and slightly high for hatchery releases. The hatchery advantage is therefore understated.

**9. Conclusions and Management Recommendations**
- Yearling releases produced impressive total returns, even accounting for the weak 1970 brood year, whose poor contribution was not explained by hatchery or river conditions.
- Releasing yearlings at the hatchery was the more favorable option for returns and was recommended for building runs in a particular stream without importing eggs.
- Rio Vista releases were not justified on returns alone. They may still be useful to reduce straying into the Feather River, provided sufficient salmon return to the Feather River Hatchery to sustain a self-sustaining program.
- Concern was raised that yearlings released at Rio Vista might prey on naturally produced fingerlings. Estimates suggested a potential loss of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 wild salmon in the fishery, but the yearlings stayed in the upper river and had no detectable effect on the wild salmon population.
