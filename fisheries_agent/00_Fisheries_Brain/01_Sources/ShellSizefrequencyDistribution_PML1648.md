# Shell Size-Frequency Distributions of Corbicula Manilensis in a Clam-Fouled Steam Condenser

#malacology #freshwater-biology #corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This short paper by [[Robert F McMahon]] (Department of Biology, University of Texas at Arlington) examines the introduced [[Asiatic Freshwater Clam]], *Corbicula manilensis*, which fouled the condenser tubes of a clam-fouled steam condenser at an electric generating plant. Shells were collected from three sections of the Number Two condenser (intake cold-water box, condenser tubes, and warm-water outlet box) and their size-frequency distributions were compared. Shells lodged in the condenser tubes were significantly smaller than those in the water boxes, indicating that fouling is driven by small clams that pass through the intake and lodge in tubes whose inside diameter closely matches their shell height. The paper proposes that condenser tubes with an inside diameter of 29.0 mm or more could prevent this fouling.

## Background & Description

*Corbicula manilensis* is an introduced Asiatic freshwater clam first recorded in the Columbia River, Washington. It has since spread across a large portion of freshwater drainage systems in the southern United States, where it has become a major pest. Recently it has been reported to foul raw-water systems, including steam condensers.

The study focuses on one [[Steam Condenser]] of an electric generating unit at the [[Handley Power Plant]], which uses [[Lake Arlington]] water as coolant. The Number Two condenser was fouled repeatedly, whereas the Number One condenser had never been fouled by this species. Specimens were collected from the intake (cold-water box, CWB), the condenser tubes (CT), and the outlet (warm-water box, WWB). Living specimens and empty shells were measured for shell length (SL), height (SH) and width (SW) using calipers, and the size distributions were compared with [[Students T-Test]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Settlement and transport:** Veligers do not settle inside condenser tubes because water velocities there (described as far too high) prevent it. Clams must therefore be carried into the tubes already grown, lodged at constrictions or bends.
- **Source populations:** Dense populations occur in the shallow shore areas of Lake Arlington and in the intake canal. Clams can move out of bottom silt and be carried by currents and waves. Clams also accumulate behind the [[Traveling Screens]] of the plant intake.
- **Screen selectivity:** Screen mesh of about 12.7 mm excludes clams large enough to foul the condensers, but smaller clams and spat pass through. In slower embayment or tunnel water, these small clams can grow to a size that lodges in condenser tubes within two to six months.
- **Water quality triggers:** Decline in lake water quality (high summer temperatures, low oxygen, or [[Chlorination]] and other disturbances) may cause resident clams behind the screens to release from the bottom and enter the intake current.
- **Control methods:** Earlier authors reported that adult clams are not susceptible to shock chlorination, and recommended constant chlorination of intake water. The author notes that present EPA regulations generally prohibit the discharge of chlorinated raw intake water, and that shock chlorination may actually promote fouling by stressing resident populations.

## Key Findings

- **Size segregation:** Shell size distributions differed significantly among the three collection sites (P < 0.05) in all three dimensions measured (SL, SH, SW).
- **Intake (CWB):** Sample of 72 shells had a mean SL of 21.1 mm (s.d. ±6.19 mm, range 5.7–33.9 mm) and a mean SH of 19.98 mm.
- **Condenser tubes (CT):** Shells removed directly from the tubes were the smallest group in the condenser: mean SH of 19.95 mm (s.d. ±4.74 mm, range 8.2–27.8 mm); mean SL of about 9.4–10.4 mm (range 10.6–32.0 mm as reported); mean SW of 14.0 mm (range 6.1–18.9 mm).
- **Warm-water box (WWB):** Sample of 100 shells had a mean SL of 16.22 mm (range 4.6–28.1 mm), a mean SH of 14.x mm, and a mean SW of 9.89 mm (range 2.7–13.6 mm).
- **Soft tissue:** Approximately 20–80% of shells from each section contained intact soft parts. No evidence of byssal attachment to tube walls or other condenser surfaces was found, contrary to earlier suggestions.
- **Tube diameter threshold:** Condenser tubes of the fouled Number Two unit have an inside diameter of 19.7 mm, which allows clams of SH near 19.7 mm to lodge. The Number One unit, which has never fouled, has an inside diameter of 29.0 mm; a tube this size would only stop clams with SH greater than about 22 mm, a small fraction of the Lake Arlington population.
- **Mechanism:** Fast water velocities in the Number One tubes appear to abrade and break up lodged shells, preventing accumulation. Slower flow in the Number Two tubes allows a steady buildup of lodged shells that eventually reduces condenser efficiency.
- **Largest specimen:** A 50 mm shell length was the largest specimen collected in over a year of bi-weekly sampling at Lake Arlington (Aldridge, 1976).
- **Recommendation:** Designing steam condensers for future electrical and industrial facilities with tube inside diameters greater than 29.0 mm may eliminate fouling by *C. manilensis*. The author notes the paper is a preliminary study limited to one condenser.
