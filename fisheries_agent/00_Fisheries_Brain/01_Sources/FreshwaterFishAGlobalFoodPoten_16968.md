# Freshwater Fish: A Global Food Potential

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Shelby D Gerking]] argues that freshwater fish can meaningfully supplement the world's supply of dietary protein, with the greatest near-term potential in intensively managed [[Pond Culture]]. The author holds that rivers and lakes could roughly double their present yield, but that overexploitation, [[Eutrophication]], [[Acidification]] and pollution by domestic and industrial wastes limit that gain. Marine fisheries are judged to have an ultimate capacity of about twice the present level, and the author concludes that artificial ponds, not natural waters, are the most reliable route to expanded production. The paper also stresses that expansion is constrained by human factors: a shortage of trained personnel, cultural preference for other proteins, and high production costs.

## Background & Description

The paper begins from the premise that world population will keep growing exponentially, increasing demand for dietary protein from terrestrial and aquatic sources. Aquatic food is divided into marine and freshwater components, and the author notes that freshwater yield could grow proportionally more than marine yield.

**Context of marine fisheries.** Marine catches expanded rapidly after World War II, but many stocks once thought dependable have declined under intensified exploitation. Menhaden, herring and sardines are cited, along with Atlantic and Pacific salmon, cod, some flatfish and tuna populations. The Indian Ocean and southern-hemisphere oceans were not yet yielding to capacity.

**Freshwater base.** Based on 1974 FAO statistics, freshwater fish contributed 14.8% (10.2 million metric tonnes) of the global aquatic harvest of 68.8 million metric tonnes. The author argues that this small base means percentage gains can be large.

**Key species.** Catch records for freshwater species are poor, so the most important species is a matter of judgment. The author's "big five" are salmon, trout, [[Tilapia]], [[Milkfish]] and [[Common Carp]]. Restricted to strictly freshwater forms, the "big three" are trout, tilapia and carp. Salmon, shad, eels, mullet and milkfish were treated as questionable freshwater species because they are anadromous, catadromous or brackish.

- **Trout:** Several coldwater species originally from the Northern Hemisphere, including [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Rainbow trout is the most favored culture species.
- **Tilapia:** Originally African, now important in Southeast Asia and adapted to warmer parts of the Western world.
- **Carp:** Ubiquitous, with common carp leading. [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idellus*) is being trialed in pond culture in Europe and the United States.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Freshwater habitats.** Some species occur in every freshwater type, from small streams to large lakes, but yields differ. Large rivers are poor producers because floods, turbidity, shifting bottoms and changing channels make harvests unpredictable. Dams, channelization and pollution have reduced many great rivers to low commercial catches.

**Natural lakes and reservoirs.** Large natural lakes yield significant fish but suffer severe population fluctuations. Reservoirs show a boom-and-bust pattern: nutrients released on flooding raise fish production for a few years, then bind into vegetation and sediments, lowering output. The limnology of large reservoirs is said to be insufficiently explored.

**Man-made threats.**
- *Overexploitation* can shrink average individual size until fish are no longer of interest to anglers. Salmon, trout and whitefish have been kept stable only through strict regulation.
- *Eutrophication* first boosts harvests, then alters water chemistry. Herring and whitefish in deep clear lakes may disappear as bacterial oxidation of organic matter from algal blooms depletes deep-water oxygen. Nutrient sources include domestic and industrial wastes and intensively cultivated land.
- *Acidification* from NOx and SOx pollution, precipitating as nitric and sulfuric acid, has excluded trout and salmon from low-mineral, poorly buffered lakes and streams. Effects are documented in southern Sweden, Norway, eastern Canadian lakes and the Adirondack lakes of the northeastern United States. Coldwater species are more sensitive than warmwater species.
- *Rivers:* [[Atlantic Salmon]] have been excluded from parts of the [[Rhine River]] by pollution and dams; [[Pacific Salmon]] runs on the [[Columbia River]] are a fraction of former stocks. The author states there is hardly an unpolluted stream left in the world.

**Commercial and catch data.** The [[Mississippi River]] record shows commercial catch in bordering states fell from 33,240,000 kg (1950) to 24,138,000 kg (1972), a decrease of 27%.

## Key Findings

**1. Pond culture offers the greatest potential.** Intensively managed ponds can produce as much protein per unit area as land planted in primary grains (Table 1). Holt's estimate is a 15-fold increase in pond fish production over 25 years, if prioritized (Figure 1). The author argues that ponds are preferable to natural lakes, reservoirs or rivers for expansion because they permit precise control of stocking, feeding and harvest.

**2. Protein yield comparison (kg/ha/yr, Table 1).** Grain protein: oats 230, barley 194, rice 360, corn 385. Fish protein: carp under heavy fertilization 159; channel catfish under heavy supplementary feeding 260; carp under heavy supplementary feeding 750. Values are indicative only, since they come from different areas and are not ideal maxima.

**3. Carp production scales with management intensity (Table 4, Japan, kg/ha/yr).** Low-fertility irrigation ponds 76; medium-fertility 180; high-fertility 490; very heavy feeding 5,000; floating cages 4,000. The source also lists running-water/stream and closed recirculating system values (printed as 400 000–2.000 000 and 4000 000), which the author considers unrealistic for practical operations.

**4. Rainbow trout culture is exceptional.** In large isothermal springs with rapid waste removal, production can reach nearly 150,000 kg/ha/yr. Such conditions are described as rare.

**5. Tilapia fertilization response (Table 5, polyculture with grass carp and *Puntius javanicus*, Malacca).** Using unfertilized control set at 100 kg/ha/yr, phosphate (P₂O₅) at 20 kg/ha gave 592 kg/ha/yr; 40 kg/ha gave 773; 60 kg/ha gave 1,059.

**6. Feasible growth estimates.** Marine fisheries could reach about twice present levels. Natural lakes, reservoirs and streams may also reach about twice present yields. Greenwood estimates untapped US freshwater stocks at about five times the current commercial catch.

**7. Scientific knowledge is adequate, but human capacity is limiting.** Pond design, disease control, spawning, thermal and oxygen requirements are reasonably understood, though carp and trout are better studied than tilapia. Few experts can direct large programs, and regions such as Central and South America lack organized fish culture. The author calls for training combined with research, either centered where expertise exists or through sending experts abroad.

**8. Social and economic barriers.** Domestic animal protein is often preferred to fish, and cattle and sheep carry status. Introduced species such as the carp face resistance; the carp's reputation as a competitor in the United States is unlikely to be erased. Freshwater fish production costs are higher than they should be, and better cost accounting and engineering could lower prices.

**9. Remedies and outlook.** Regulated fisheries have sustained catches, and pollution control and cleanup efforts have produced results, including fish returning to the lower Thames. Restoring pre-pollution conditions is considered unrealistic. The author recommends rational trade-offs informed by scientific investigation and intergovernmental regulation of pollution and fisheries.

**Author.** [[Shelby D Gerking]] is Professor of Zoology at Arizona State University and former Director of the Indiana Aquatic Research Unit. His research covers fish populations, production, protein metabolism and temperature tolerance, and his forthcoming book is *Ecology of Freshwater Fish Production*.
