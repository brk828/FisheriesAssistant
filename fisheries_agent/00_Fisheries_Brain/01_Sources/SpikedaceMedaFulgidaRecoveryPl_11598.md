# Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's September 1991 [[Recovery Plan]] for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a threatened [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] fish, addresses a species extirpated from most of its historic range in the [[Gila River Basin]]. Remaining populations occur in the upper Gila River in New Mexico, and in [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]], and the upper [[Verde River]] in Arizona, and all are under threat. Major threats include dams, water diversion, watershed deterioration, groundwater pumping, channelization, and introduction of non-native predatory and competitive fishes, notably the [[Red Shiner]].

The recovery objective is protection of existing populations, restoration of populations in portions of historic habitat, and eventual delisting if possible. Delisting criteria cannot be quantified until further research is completed. The plan lists nine actions needed and estimates a minimum total cost of $115,000.00 per year over a minimum 20-year recovery period (1989 dollars), excluding land or water acquisition. Recovery cannot occur in less than 20 years.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and morphology:** A small, sleek, slender, laterally compressed minnow of the family Cyprinidae, described as a monotypic genus. Scales are reduced to small deeply embedded plates; two spinose dorsal-fin rays; seven dorsal rays; usually nine anal rays; pharyngeal teeth formula 1,4-4,1; barbels absent.
- **Coloration:** Bright silvery sides with vertically elongated black specks; olive-gray to brownish back; white underside. Breeding males become golden or brassy.
- **Listing status:** Listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended (proposed FWS 1985; listed FWS 1986). Arizona lists it as threatened; New Mexico lists it as group 2 endangered under the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act. Neither state listing otherwise protects the species or its habitat.
- **Critical habitat:** Proposed in 1985, with designation deferred until 18 June 1987; the proposal remains in force but provides limited protection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and abundance**
- *Historical:* Widespread in moderate-sized, intermediate-elevation streams of the Gila River system upstream of Phoenix, including the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers, the Agua Fria, San Pedro, and San Francisco systems, and the East, Middle, and West forks of the Gila in New Mexico.
- *Present:* Arizona populations occur only in Aravaipa Creek (Graham and Pinal Counties), Eagle Creek (Graham and Greenlee Counties), and the upper Verde River (Yavapai County). In New Mexico, restricted to the mainstem Gila River and its East, Middle, and West forks. The Eagle Creek population, described as "quite small" by FWS (1986), was later found to be more substantial.
- Transplants from Aravaipa Creek to Sonoita Creek (1968) and 7-Springs Wash (1970) were extirpated.

**Habitat and life history**
- Occupies flowing waters usually less than one meter deep; adults aggregate in shear zones, gravel-sand bars, and quiet eddies at riffle edges. Juveniles favor quiet pool margins over soft, fine-grained bottoms.
- **Reproduction:** Spring breeding (April–June), triggered by discharge and temperature. Adhesive, demersal eggs; fecundity 90 to 250 ova; ovum diameter near 1.5 mm; no territoriality.
- **Growth:** Young reach 35–40 mm SL by November; about 40 mm SL at end of year one; 50–63 mm SL at end of year two. Maximum size near 65 mm SL (Aravaipa Creek) and 68 mm SL (upper Gila). Longevity typically one to two years.
- **Diet:** Carnivorous, feeding mainly on drifting aquatic and terrestrial insects (larval [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Trichoptera]] (hydropsychids), and [[Chironomidae]]); prey typically 2–5 mm long.

**Co-occurring fishes:** Native: [[Loach Minnow]], speckled dace, Sonora sucker, desert sucker, longfin dace, and possibly roundtail chub. Non-native: [[Red Shiner]], channel catfish, flathead catfish, largemouth and smallmouth bass, green sunfish, and introduced trouts at higher elevations.

**Reasons for decline**
- Habitat destruction (dewatering, impoundments, altered flow regimes, channelization, bank stabilization) and non-native fishes have acted independently and in concert.
- Watershed impacts from livestock grazing, mining, agriculture, and timber harvest increase sedimentation and contaminant loads. A wastewater spill at the Cananea Mine, Sonora, killed all fishes across a 100-km downstream reach (Eberhardt 1981).
- Spikedace do not persist in reservoirs.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objectives and priorities**
1. **Protect existing populations:** Identify extent and protection level of populations; prioritize by need; designate critical habitat; enforce Sections 7 and 9 of the ESA; ensure perennial flows with natural hydrographs; curtail non-native fish introductions, including discouraging seining and live bait; evaluate fish barriers (e.g., on Aravaipa Creek against red shiner); acquire key lands and water rights.
2. **Monitor populations:** Standard locations, techniques, and a computerized database; bi-annual (spring and autumn) population estimates; genetic baseline surveys.
3. **Investigate non-native fish interactions:** Direct (predation, displacement) and indirect mechanisms, via field experiments and laboratory studies.
4. **Quantify habitat needs:** Substrate, velocity and depth, temperature, water chemistry (pH, turbidity, alkalinity, dissolved oxygen), combined effects, and watershed size and flood regime.
5. **Restore depleted habitats** and **reintroduce** spikedace to historic range. The San Pedro River system, Arizona, is identified as the most amenable reintroduction site (type locality, with Aravaipa Creek as a donor stock). The San Francisco River and Mescal Creek are also proposed for evaluation.
6. **Define self-sustaining population criteria** covering abundance, age structure, reproduction, recruitment, minimum stock size, and environmental variables.
7. **Captive propagation:** Not currently deemed necessary, but recommended as a contingency and for reintroduction supply.
8. **Information and education:** Public, media, national, and Mexican outreach; open professional information exchange and peer-reviewed publication.

**Red shiner interaction**
- Red shiner now occurs at all places formerly occupied by spikedace, except the San Francisco River above Frisco Hot Springs.
- In the upper Gila, investigators concluded the most likely mode was invasion of vacant niches left by extirpated spikedace (Propst et al. 1986; Bestgen and Propst 1986).
- In the upper Verde, the two species appear to coexist in a stable region of sympatry.
- Marsh et al. (1989) found apparent displacement of spikedace by red shiner based on habitat-use shifts in the field and laboratory.
- The plan notes that the concurrent decline of spikedace and expansion of red shiner is "powerful circumstantial evidence" of displacement.

**Critical habitat recommendations (Appendix A)**
- Aravaipa Creek: perennial portion, approximately 24 km.
- Verde River: approximately 57 km, from 0.8 km below the Sycamore Creek confluence to Sullivan Lake. Sycamore Creek (1.5 km) is not expected to be included in a final rule.
- Gila River (New Mexico): three sections totaling approximately 73 km.
- Eagle Creek: an additional approximately 38 km reach, from the Phelps Dodge Corporation diversion dam to the mouth of Sheep Wash, recommended for designation.

**Implementation schedule (Section III)**
- Priority 1 actions prevent extinction in the foreseeable future; Priority 2 maintain current status; Priority 3 provide for full recovery.
- Responsible agencies include FWS, AZG&F, NMG&F, USFS, BLM, BR, and the Desert Fishes Recovery Team.
