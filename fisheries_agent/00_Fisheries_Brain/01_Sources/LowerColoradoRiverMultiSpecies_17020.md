## Executive Summary

This draft issue paper, prepared for the Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program (MSCP) Steering Committee, addresses the management of non-native aquatic species as a key driver of decline in three endangered native fishes: the [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Bonytail Chub]], and the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (functionally extirpated from the plan area). The core biological problem identified is not a failure of adult spawning — razorback suckers and bonytail continue to reproduce, even in captivity or semi-natural ponds — but a near-total failure of larval and juvenile recruitment in the wild, attributed primarily to predation by an extensive assemblage of non-native and native species. The paper outlines a four-part conservation strategy (population augmentation, native fish refugia, habitat enhancement, and non-native species management) and presents nine non-exclusive management options (A–I) for mitigating the "predation-bottleneck," ranging from no action to active predator suppression, habitat manipulation, selective fish passage, research into biocontrol, and repatriation of the pikeminnow as a native apex predator.

## Background & Description

The paper is a management/policy document rather than a morphological study, but it references key life-history and size-related traits relevant to conservation design:

- **Razorback Sucker**: Recruitment-limited species capable of producing large numbers of larvae in the wild (>100,000 larvae collected in [[Lake Mohave]]; young-of-year documented in [[Lake Mead]]).
- **Bonytail Chub**: Successfully spawns in captivity and semi-natural settings (e.g., High Levee Pond, buckets, golf course ponds, hatcheries), indicating no inherent reproductive barrier — the bottleneck is post-hatching survival.
- **Colorado Pikeminnow**: Historically the apex piscivore of the Colorado River system; currently extirpated from the Lower Colorado River (LCR) plan area.
- **Predation-vulnerable size threshold**: Native fish reared in captivity are held in predator-free environments until reaching approximately **10–12 inches**, a size considered sufficient to preclude most predation — a key parameter for the "grow-out" management option.
- All three target species are described as **long-lived and highly fecund**, an evolutionary adaptation to a historically unstable river environment where successful recruitment did not need to occur every year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Lower Colorado River (LCR) ecosystem described in this paper has been extensively altered from its historical hydrology and geomorphology, with cascading effects on native fish niches:

- **Historical niche conditions**: Native fish evolved in a system with heavy sediment loads and high turbidity, which likely provided visual cover from predators during vulnerable early life stages. Dam regulation and bank stabilization have since reduced turbidity and sediment transport, altering predator-prey dynamics.
- **Current predator guild**: A broad and taxonomically diverse assemblage preys differentially on native fish life stages, including:
  - Invertebrates: crayfish, odonate (dragonfly) larvae
  - Amphibians: bullfrogs
  - Non-game fish: mosquitofish, shiners
  - Game fish: sunfish, carp, channel catfish, bass
  - Non-native species vastly outnumber natives in species richness, individual abundance, and biomass.
- **Habitat interdependencies**: The paper stresses that predator management cannot succeed in isolation from physical habitat and hydrologic restoration (e.g., reconnection of backwaters, marsh/riparian restoration) — habitat structure, turbidity, and flow regime jointly determine predation risk.
- **Refugial role**: Isolated ponds and off-channel refugia are proposed to serve dual functions — production of predator-naive juveniles for repatriation and maintenance of a genetic reservoir against catastrophic loss of wild or captive stocks.
- **Trophic restoration concept**: Reintroduction of the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] is framed as a potential mechanism to reestablish historic native-dominated trophic structure and suppress non-native fish populations through top-down predation.

## Key Findings

- **Recruitment, not spawning, is the primary bottleneck**: Razorback sucker and bonytail reproduce successfully under both wild and captive conditions, but juvenile survival in the open river is essentially zero, with predation on early life stages identified as the most significant limiting factor.
- **Four essential conservation strategy elements** were agreed upon by the Native Fish/Non-Native Aquatic Species Management Issues Group: population augmentation, native fish refugia, habitat enhancement, and non-native species management — all considered necessary and interdependent.
- **Nine management options (A–I)** were identified to address non-native species interactions, including:
  - (A) No action beyond augmentation/refugia/habitat work, treating the LCR primarily as a genetic refugium rather than a self-sustaining population site.
  - (B) Halting further non-native introductions, including restricting non-native baitfish use.
  - (C) Small-scale habitat manipulation (e.g., artificially increasing turbidity or localized flow) to reduce visual predation.
  - (D) Active non-native population suppression (e.g., carp/catfish removal), noted as having limited success historically, with risk of competitive release (e.g., crayfish population expansion).
  - (E) Selective fish passage structures to trap/exclude undesirable species during upstream movement.
  - (F) Long-term research into species-specific biocontrol (poisons, genetic/birth-control methods); explicitly flagged as unsuitable as a sole strategy due to political and technical uncertainty.
  - (G) Repatriation of the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] to restore historic trophic structure, potentially framed as an "experimental, non-essential" population to maintain angling opportunities.
  - (H) "Prey swamping" — mass augmentation to overwhelm predator consumption capacity.
  - (I) Grow-out of juveniles to larger, less predation-vulnerable sizes (10–12 inches) before river release.
- **Emerging threat**: [[Giant Salvinia]] (an invasive aquatic plant) is flagged as a potential catastrophic ecosystem threat to the LCR, prompting a multi-agency monitoring and control task force.
- **Socio-political constraint**: State game and fish agencies and the angling community require "no net loss" of angling opportunity, shaping options toward native-species substitution (e.g., promoting bonytail/pikeminnow as replacement game species) rather than outright non-native eradication.
- **Adaptive management** is identified as the essential operating framework underlying all proposed options, given substantial biological and cost uncertainties.
- The paper explicitly states that **cost estimates for the various options could not yet be determined** at the time of writing (January 2000).
