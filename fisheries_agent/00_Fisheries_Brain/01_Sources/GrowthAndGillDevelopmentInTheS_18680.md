# Growth and Gill Development in the Small-Mouthed Black Bass, *Micropterus dolomieu* Lacépède

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus

## Executive Summary

This 1931 contribution from the [[Franz Theodore Stone Laboratory]] (Ohio State University, Contribution No. 4) by John W. Price quantifies gill surface area across the full life history of the [[Small-Mouthed Black Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*). Using 127 preserved specimens from [[Lake Erie]], ranging from about 0.92 cm newly hatched larvae to 37 cm adults, the study measures gill filament number, filament dimensions, and the folded (lamellated) and smooth surfaces of each filament. It then combines these into a whole-gill surface formula. The author reports that total gill surface scales with body length to about the 2.37 power, that young fish have far more respiratory surface per gram of body weight than adults, and that weight scales with the cube of length. The paper is framed as the first continuous, species-wide analysis of gill growth in a fish.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a basic respiratory question: gill surface is where gas exchange occurs, so its area is probably an index of respiratory capacity at different body sizes. Earlier gill-surface measurements were few and general. The largest previous sample of one species cited was four fish (Piitter, 1909, on *Scorpaena*). Price argued that relationships between gill size and body measures could only be established reliably with a much larger continuous size series.

**Material.** The 127 specimens came from two sources:
- Fish of 1 to 7 cm, collected by Price in summer 1927. The smallest (about 1 cm, just hatched) were swimming at the surface in schools.
- Fish of 7 to 37 cm, supplied by Wilbur Tidd from State of Ohio Division of Conservation trap nets near North Bass and Middle Bass islands in October 1927.

All fish were killed and preserved in 10% formalin. Collection was restricted to one general lake area to keep fish of the same race under similar conditions.

**Methods.**
- *External measures:* total length (upper jaw tip to caudal fin tip), body depth and width at the anterior dorsal fin insertion, and weight after removal of stomach contents where practical.
- *Gill measures:* filaments were counted on one side of each arch and doubled. Each arch was measured at four locations, with 96 observations per gill set. Filament length, width, and depth were recorded.
- *Surface model:* each filament was treated as a four-faced wedge: two smooth rectangular lateral faces and two triangular faces bearing transverse secondary lamellae. Lamellae were treated as corrugated folds whose height and number per millimeter set the folded area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Respiratory role.** Gill filaments bear the secondary lamellae, where the blood is separated from the water by a single layer of flattened epithelial cells. Price regarded the folded surfaces as the true respiratory surface, following earlier authors such as Reiss (1881) and Piitter (1909).
- **Size-related metabolism.** The author links the high surface-to-weight ratio of young fish to their higher metabolic rate. This is an interpretation, not a directly measured result.
- **Comparative context.** The paper compares body-shape and weight-length relationships with other species, including the Weakfish (*Cynoscion regalis*), the California Sardine (*Sardinops caerulea*), the Lake Herring (*Coregonus artedi*), and the Northern Pike. Price says weight-length relations had not previously been published for *M. dolomieu*.
- **Environmental caveats.** Price noted that the constant in the weight relation may shift with season and condition. He also checked whether unusual variation in filament number related to nourishment, parasitism, or injury, but found no definite cause.
- **Sample caveat.** The 22 to 30 cm size class fell consistently below the fitted folded-area curve. The author attributed this to possible differences among fish from slightly different localities.

## Key Findings

1. **Filament number.** The smallest specimen (0.92 cm) had 192 filaments. Fish near 27 to 37 cm had about 2200. The count rises steeply in early growth, slows around 6 to 8 cm, and levels off at about 2200 by roughly 27 to 30 cm. Price concluded that later gill growth must come from filament and fold enlargement rather than new filaments.
2. **Arch differences.** The first anterior arch carries the most filaments. Counts decline with each more posterior arch, and the first arch has about 20% more than the most posterior one. The posterior arch's filaments are smallest, have the fewest in number, and lack lamellae in the smallest fish.
3. **Filament dimensions.** Filament dimensions grow linearly with total length:
   - Length: L = 0.05 + 0.191 × total length
   - Depth: 0.05 + 0.037 × total length
   - Width: 0.10 + 0.0109 × total length

   For every 10 cm of body length, filament length grows about 1.9 mm, depth about 0.37 mm, and width about 0.1 mm. Price found that filaments grow proportionally in all directions and keep the same shape throughout life.
4. **Lamellae.** Lamellae per millimeter decline with body length (n = 30.2 − 0.3344L). Lamellar height increases with body length (b = 0.042 + 0.00365L). Their product rises to a maximum and then levels off, so the folds of large fish are taller and more tightly packed.
5. **Folded surface.** Folded area per filament follows A = 0.035 L^2.18. Price reports that it rises slightly faster than the square of length.
6. **Smooth surface.** Smooth lateral area per filament follows A = 0.0059 L^2, showing simple square-law growth.
7. **Folds dominate.** The folded surface is roughly ten times the smooth surface. For a 36 cm bass, a single average filament has about 80 mm² of folded surface and about 8 mm² of smooth surface.
8. **Whole-gill surface.** Total gill surface follows G = 0.3636 L^2.37 (in cm²). Surface ranges from 2.44 cm² in a 2.2 cm fish to about 2000 cm² in a 37 cm fish. The correlation between gill surface and total length is r = 0.9541 ± 0.005373.
9. **Body form.** Depth and width increase linearly with length. Slopes are 0.274 for depth and 0.1593 for width (per cm of length), so depth grows about twice as fast as width. The author concludes that overall body form stays constant through life.
10. **Weight-length.** Weight scales with the cube of total length (W = 0.0169 L³ in grams). The correlation is r = 0.913 ± 0.009915. The paper also gives 0.918 for the same coefficient in the weight section, which is a internal inconsistency.
11. **Weight-gill relation.** Gill surface scales with weight as G = 3.65 W^0.785 (in cm²). Price also fitted a least-squares curve, √G = 0.07 + 0.012W + 0.0011W^(1/3)-type form, that fits the data more closely across the range.
12. **Surface-to-volume analogy.** Price compares the gill-weight relation with the geometry of a sphere, where √(surface) ÷ ∛(volume) is constant. He obtains a constant K = 2.486 for this series. He cites Piitter's value of 2.21 for *Scorpaena* and notes that a larger series is needed to confirm the hypothesis.
13. **Surface per gram.** Small bass have about 7.4 cm² of gill surface per gram of body weight. Bass of 30 to 36 cm weighing 600 to 850 g have only slightly more than 2.2 cm² per gram. The author attributes the higher young-fish ratio to their greater metabolic rate.
14. **Summary of claims.** Price describes the work as the first to measure gill surfaces and correlate them with body measures across an entire species' life history. The author states that the 127-fish sample is far larger than any previously analyzed series.
