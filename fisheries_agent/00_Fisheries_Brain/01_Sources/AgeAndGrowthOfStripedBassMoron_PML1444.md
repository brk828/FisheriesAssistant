# Age and Growth of Striped Bass in the Thames River Estuary, Connecticut

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report by [[Paul C. Marsh]] estimates the age and growth of [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) in the [[Thames River Estuary]], Connecticut. Fish were collected in 1972 and 1973 and aged from scales. Back-calculated fork lengths at the end of the first six years of life are reported as 17.2, 23.1, 31.8, 38.1, 46.7 and 54.3 cm, with some digits uncertain in the OCR scan. The author concludes that growth in New England striped bass has changed little over about 40 years and that these data provide a current baseline for detecting future change as coastal areas are developed.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The striped bass is a widely distributed anadromous marine fish that supports major sport fisheries on both coasts of North America. Its recreational and economic importance has generated a large life-history literature.
- **Prior New England studies:** The most recent age and growth work in the region cited by the author includes [[Merriman 1941]], [[Fitzpatrick and Cookson 1958]] and [[Massachusetts]] data, and Hassler and Beaulieu (1967, cited as "Magnin and Beaulieu" in the bibliography, in the [[St. Lawrence River]] region).
- **Study rationale:** The author states that current growth data, combined with earlier studies, allow evaluation of potential changes in bass populations as coastal areas become increasingly developed.
- **Author affiliation:** Paul C. Marsh, University of Connecticut, Department of Natural Resources Conservation, Storrs. Present address: University of Minnesota, Department of Entomology, Fisheries, and Wildlife, St. Paul. The work is Scientific Contribution No. 591 of the [[Storrs Agricultural Experiment Station]] and was part of a project under the Anadromous Fish Act, with support from the [[Connecticut Department of Environmental Protection]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system.** The [[Thames River Estuary]] forms where the [[Quinebaug-Shetucket River]] and the [[Yantic River]] join at Norwich, eastern Connecticut. It flows about 26 km south to [[Long Island Sound]] at New London.

- A salt wedge lies 1 to 7 m below the surface in the upper 5 km of the estuary, except during high spring discharge.
- Salinities are generally above 10 ppm below 3 m (as printed in the source).
- The estuary supports resident freshwater species and transient marine fishes.
- Striped bass occur in the estuary year-round, with peak numbers in spring.

**Sampling conditions.** Fish were taken from October 1972 through November 1973 in the upper 5 km using nylon gill nets, seines, and hook and line.

**Management concerns.** The author notes that coastal development for power generation, industry, recreation, navigation, and offshore resource use may alter coastal water environments. Rivers and estuaries used for spawning, nursery, and feeding areas may also be degraded by these activities. Monitoring current population status is therefore needed to identify and respond to potential impacts.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- Total fork length was measured to the nearest 1 mm.
- Scales were removed following [[Merriman 1941]] and impressions were made on cellulose acetate discs with a Michigan-type roller press, then enlarged and read with a microprojector.
- Distances from focus to each annulus and to the anterior margin were measured for the two clearest scales in each sample. Data were pooled with those of [[Whitworth et al. 1975]] to increase sample size.
- Fork length at annulus formation was back-calculated with a Lee-type proportional formula, using a scale-formation length (k) of 1.1 cm.

**Growth results**

- Back-calculated fork lengths at the end of the first six years of life are reported as 17.2, 23.1, 31.8, 38.1, 46.7 and 54.3 cm (OCR-uncertain digits in the scan).
- Maximum growth occurred in the first year, reaching 12.5 cm.
- Annual increments in each of the following five years ranged from 8.2 to 10.6 cm.
- Mean fork length at capture exceeded back-calculated lengths at all ages. The author attributes this to fish being collected late in the growing season but before annulus formation.
- Growth in the Thames River fish was similar to published values from [[Merriman 1941]] (New England), [[Pearson 1938]] and [[Mansueti 1961]] (Maryland), and [[Bason 1971]] (Delaware).

**Regional comparisons**

- Growth in Thames River and other New England bass was greater than that of striped bass in Quebec, which the author attributes to colder water and a shorter growing season. Quebec fish are also an indigenous population rather than part of the coastal migratory stock.
- Bass in the [[Roanoke River]], North Carolina (Trent and Hassler 1968), were also an indigenous population but had greater lengths at the end of each year than New England fish. They were exposed to warmer water and had a longer growing season.
- The author concludes that coastal stocks of New England and the Chesapeake Bay region have relatively uniform growth in length.

**Conclusions**

- Growth of New England striped bass has changed little in about 40 years.
- The data serve as a baseline for comparing future growth and assessing the impacts of coastal development on striped bass populations.
