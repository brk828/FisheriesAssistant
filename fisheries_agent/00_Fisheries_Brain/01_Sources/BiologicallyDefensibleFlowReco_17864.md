# Biologically Defensible Flow Recommendations for Colorado Squawfish Habitat in the 15-Mile Reach

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) final report, prepared by [[Lyon R. Kaeding]] and [[Douglas B. Osmundson]] (May 1989), proposes preliminary flow recommendations for the [[15-Mile Reach]] of the upper [[Colorado River]]. The reach is occupied habitat for the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the very rare [[Razorback Sucker]]. The main recommendation is a 700–1200 ft³/sec flow "window" for July, August and September, with a 600 ft³/sec lower limit in dry years. The flows are intended to keep adult habitat near maximum and to warm the river enough to advance spawning and improve first-year growth. The authors state that flows alone will not recover the species; they must be paired with monitoring, control of introduced fishes and possibly experimental stocking.

## Background & Description

The 15-mile reach runs from the Grand Valley Diversion (RM 185.1) to the [[Gunnison River]] confluence (RM 171.0). It is about 14.2 miles long, measured after the confluence shifted following the 1983–1984 floods. The report is the final flow document of a series under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (USFWS 1987). Its appendices are:

- **Appendix A** (Osmundson and Kaeding, 1988): radio-telemetry, electrofishing, larval and young-of-the-year (YOY) surveys, and habitat use in the Grand Valley.
- **Appendix B** (Kaeding and Osmundson, 1988): the hypothesis that slow growth combined with high early-life mortality drives the decline of squawfish in the upper basin.
- **Appendix C** (Hann and Rose, 1989): [[PHABSIM]] habitat-versus-discharge simulations.
- **Appendix D** (water-storage problems) and **Appendix E** (reviewers) are referenced but not reproduced in this text.

Both species have lost about 75% of their range since the turn of the century. Dams and diversions have altered flow and temperature regimes. Only the squawfish is federally listed as endangered, although the razorback is considered rarer and may be near extirpation in nature (Tyus 1987).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** Adult squawfish use runs most often, and this habitat is common in the reach. Pools are the main winter habitat, and many fish sit under ice in January. In spring (May–June) squawfish and razorbacks concentrate in flooded [[Gravel Pits]] and large [[Backwaters]]. Summer use shifts toward deeper water even as flows and depths reach their seasonal low.

**Temperature.** Upper-basin temperatures are well below the species' physiological optimum of 25 °C. In laboratory work, growth at 15, 20 and 30 °C was 18%, 54% and 51% of optimum, and growth stops below about 13 °C (Kaeding and Osmundson, Appendix B). Cool water delays spawning until mid-summer. Age-0 fish therefore enter winter small, and their overwinter survival is probably low.

**Flow and temperature.** Across 1959–81 mean-monthly data at Cameo, July flow and temperature were strongly negatively related (r = –0.93). Each 1,500 ft³/sec reduction in flow raised temperature by about 1 °C.

**Introduced fishes.** Predatory and competing non-natives include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]] and [[Sand Shiner]]. Native young (roundtail chub, bluehead sucker, speckled dace) declined from 1986 to 1988, while three common introduced species increased sharply.

**Other pressures.** Agricultural pesticides may flush into the river and concentrate at low flows. [[Angling Mortality]] from catfish fishing is a possible threat, because squawfish are vulnerable to bait and lures and the reach lies close to an urban area. [[Water Clarity]] may limit shallow-water use by squawfish, and clear water increases their exposure to sight-feeding predators.

**Razorback sucker.** Of 57 razorbacks captured in the Grand Valley during 1979–88, 50 (88%) came from flooded gravel-pit ponds. No young or juvenile razorbacks have been collected in the Grand Valley, which suggests a bottleneck in spawning or early survival.

## Key Findings

**Flow recommendation (July–September)**
- Target a flow window of **700–1200 ft³/sec**, measured at the PHABSIM site near Palisade (RM 181.4).
- In dry years (80% exceedance of total July–September discharge), **600 ft³/sec** is the acceptable lower limit, giving 92–94% of maximum habitat.
- Objective 1 is to keep aggregate run, pool and riffle habitat at **95% or more** of maximum. Table 1 places the peak at 900–1100 ft³/sec. The 95% range is 712–1177 ft³/sec (Set A) and 675–1177 ft³/sec (Set B).

**Habitat modeling (Appendix C)**
- Radio-telemetry data from 1986–88 were used to build habitat suitability curves. Summer depths were 0.5–8.0 ft and velocities 0–5.6 ft/sec, mostly over cobble.
- Two curve sets gave nearly identical results. Set B was preferred because it corrects for the sampling bias toward shallow, slow water.
- At 700 ft³/sec, habitat rises about 45% over the actual flow. Flows are about 44% lower than present, on average, during July–September.

**Temperature benefit**
- A 700 ft³/sec July flow was modeled to warm the reach by an average of **2.41 °C** in the first half of July and **1.51 °C** in the second half. The model was later recalibrated, which reduced the estimate from earlier drafts.
- Warmer July water is expected to advance spawning, lengthen the first growing season and produce larger age-0 fish. The Green River comparison (r = 0.95; R² = 0.91) supports this link between temperature suitability and age-0 size.

**Feasibility**
- Under current upstream operations, the July minimum was met in all but 3 of 31 years (1952–82). The August minimum was met in 45% of years and the September minimum in 16%.
- Delivering **10,000 acre-feet** per year during July–September would raise the years meeting minimums from 5 (16%) to 14 (45%).
- Historic flows are often too high, and very high July flows can delay the warming the authors seek.

**Natural flow comparison**
- Water development has *reduced* July–September flows relative to estimated natural flows. Hann and Rose attribute the resulting habitat gain to these lower summer flows.

**Spawning and reproduction**
- Radio-tagged squawfish concentrated in the reach during spring runoff, and some adults spawned there in some years. Larval squawfish were absent from the 15-mile reach in 1986–88.
- Estimated spawning dates were 26 July–5 August in 1986 and about 4 July in 1987 and 1988.
- YOY mean catch in the 18-mile reach fell from 4.02 per 100 m² (1986) to 2.75 (1987) and 0.12 (1988).
- Reproductive success may be rare and tied to infrequent years, and downstream drift of larvae is a possible explanation for the lack of larvae in the reach.

**Razorback sucker**
- The razorback was not modeled because of limited habitat data. The authors expect its needs to be met if squawfish flows are provided.

**Hypotheses and monitoring**
- Primary hypothesis: flow-driven warming produces earlier spawning, faster first-year growth and greater recruitment to the adult population.
- A short-term stocking program is planned to start in 1990, using marked fish to separate wild and experimental contributions. It would also test whether physical habitat limits the adult population.
- Monitoring is needed for flows, habitats (including backwaters) and fish responses.

**Other management**
- Store water on the **descending limb** of the hydrograph, which raises water-rights administration issues (Appendix D).
- Extra water could come from upstream reservoirs, purchase of water rights or conservation on irrigated lands.
- Angling and pesticide mitigation are recommended, including a public education program and surveys of pesticide levels.
- Winter (October–April) and May–June flow recommendations are still being developed. The winter study will address river icing and backwater habitat, and will be started in 1990.

**Conclusion**
- The authors state that flows are "only one, albeit major, step" in recovery. The 15-mile reach is seen as part of a larger Grand Valley system, and its flows need not match those of other reaches, such as the [[Yampa River]] where the goal is to maintain current conditions.
