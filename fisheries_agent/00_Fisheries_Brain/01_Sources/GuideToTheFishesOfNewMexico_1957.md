# Guide to the Fishes of New Mexico — Cyprinid Shiners (Notropis spp.)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-fish #Notropis

## Executive Summary
This excerpt from *Guide to the Fishes of New Mexico* (Koster, 1957) provides comparative taxonomic descriptions of several small cyprinid minnows in the genus *Notropis* native to New Mexico's river systems. Species covered include the [[Pecos Shiner]], [[Red Shiner]] (also called Redhorse Shiner), [[Guzman Shiner]], [[Proserpine Shiner]], and [[Sand Shiner]]. The text emphasizes fine-scale morphological distinctions (fin ray counts, scale counts, eye size, mouth position, pigmentation patterns) used to differentiate visually similar species, along with basic ecological notes on habitat, diet, spawning, and economic use as bait/forage fish.

## Background & Description

### [[Pecos Shiner]] (Notropis sp.)
- Adults typically less than 2.5 inches long at maturity.
- Caudal peduncle depth (measured from snout tip) reaches to middle of pupil (not just to iris or front of eye).
- Caudal peduncle depth roughly equal to inter-orbital distance (distinguishing it from [[Bluntnose Shiner]], which has thinner peduncle).
- Less streamlined and less silvery than [[Bluntnose Shiner]].
- Coloration: silvery with a broad, obscure band of small punctulations along the sides.

### [[Red Shiner]] (Notropis lutrensis)
- Distinguishing traits: black blotch between halves of lower jaw; oblique, nearly terminal mouth that fails to reach front of eye.
- Dorsal fin appears quadrangular — last ray more than half as long as first ray (unusual among local minnows).
- Lateral line scale count: ~35–40.
- Base coloration: silvery to steely olive fading to white ventrally; crosshatched pattern from dark-outlined scale pockets on upper sides.
- **Breeding males**: steely blue back/sides, red/orange tinge on fins, lower sides, and head; dark purple bar behind pectoral fin; pearl organs (nuptial tubercles) develop on head and body.
- Maximum adult length: ~4 inches.

### [[Guzman Shiner]] (Notropis formosus)
- Nearly identical to [[Red Shiner]]; distinguished by 8 anal fin rays (vs. 9 in Red Shiner).
- Smaller scales than Red Shiner.
- Tip of lower jaw and base of anal fin blackish (vs. whitish in Red Shiner).
- Found in Palomas Valley.

### [[Proserpine Shiner]] (Notropis proserpinus)
- Also has 8 anal fin rays (like Guzman Shiner), distinguishing it from Red Shiner's typical 9.
- More blunt snout; mouth lower and more horizontal.
- Scale pockets more intensely outlined than Red Shiner.
- Found in Rio Grande and Pecos valleys.

### [[Sand Shiner]] (Notropis deliciosus)
- Distinguished from [[Arkansas River Shiner]] by larger eye and shorter anal fin.
- Eye length approximately equal to snout length.
- Anal fin almost invariably with 7 rays.
- Mouth large; tip of upper jaw nearly level with lower margin of pupil; rear of upper jaw about opposite front of eye.
- Body moderately compressed.
- Lateral line scale count: 32–35, scales crowded anterior to dorsal fin.
- Scale pockets outlined in a rounded, irregular pattern (unlike Red Shiner's crosshatch pattern).
- Coloration: silvery olive generally; yellowish above/whitish below in clear water.
- Lateral line accentuated by pigment spots, often obscured by dusky lateral stripe.
- Indistinct black spot at caudal fin base; 2 additional spots under dorsal fin (one at each end).
- Maximum length: ~3 inches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **[[Pecos Shiner]]**: Schooling species inhabiting main channels of the Pecos River; spawns in summer.
- **[[Red Shiner]]**: Found in main rivers, canals, and creeks at lower elevations east of the Continental Divide; omnivorous diet (algae, insects, crustaceans); spawns spring–early summer; used as small bait/forage fish.
- **[[Guzman Shiner]]**: Restricted to Palomas Valley drainage.
- **[[Proserpine Shiner]]**: Inhabits Rio Grande and Pecos valley drainages.
- **[[Sand Shiner]]**: Found in rivers and larger creeks of the Arkansas and Pecos drainages; forms large schools that feed in shallow water; omnivorous; spawns in summer; valued as bait and forage minnow. Regional morphological variants exist between Arkansas and Pecos drainage populations.

## Key Findings
- Multiple *Notropis* species in New Mexico show strong morphological convergence (silvery coloration, dusky lateral bands), requiring fine anatomical characters (anal fin ray counts, eye-to-snout ratios, scale pocket pigmentation patterns) for reliable field identification.
- Anal fin ray count is a key diagnostic character separating [[Red Shiner]] (9 rays) from the closely related [[Guzman Shiner]] and [[Proserpine Shiner]] (8 rays each).
- Breeding dichromatism is pronounced in male [[Red Shiner]]s, involving color change, pearl organ (tubercle) development, and pattern intensification — a common reproductive trait among cyprinids.
- Habitat partitioning among similar shiner species appears linked to distinct river drainages (Pecos, Rio Grande, Palomas Valley, Arkansas), suggesting geographic isolation as a driver of speciation/subspeciation within *Notropis*.
- Several of these minnows serve important ecological and economic roles as forage and bait fish within New Mexico's aquatic systems.
