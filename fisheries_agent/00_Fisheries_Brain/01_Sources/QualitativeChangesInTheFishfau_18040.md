# Qualitative Changes in the Fish Fauna of the Upper Neosho River System, 1952–1967

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares fish collections made in the upper [[Neosho River]] System of Kansas in 1952 and 1967. The authors report a rapid deterioration of the fish fauna over the 15-year interval. Losses were greatest in the mainstream of the [[Cottonwood River]] and Neosho River, where numerous fish kills occurred in 1966 and 1967. Most of these kills were attributed to pollution from [[Cattle Feedlot]] wastes draining into streams. Several species declined sharply or vanished, including the [[Neosho Madtom]], and a few small surface-dwelling and introduced species became more widespread. The authors conclude that enforcement of 1967 state anti-pollution legislation could reduce pollution and fish kills.

## Background & Description

The streams of the upper Neosho System arise in the [[Flint Hills]] of Kansas. This region supported [[Tallgrass Prairie]] (bluestem) in early historic times, and much of it remains unplowed because of thin, flinty soils. The Flint Hills are now the largest remnant of the bluestem vegetation type and are regarded as among the finest grazing lands in the world.

Land use shifted over time. Until recently, the area was used for seasonal grazing by cattle brought in each spring and sold each autumn. In the 1960s, year-round cattle maintenance grew, and many thousands of cattle were concentrated in feedlots. Records from the Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission show twelve fish kills in the Cottonwood-Neosho mainstream between Florence and Neosho Rapids from March 15, 1966, through June 19, 1967. Reporting officers linked eleven of these kills to feedlot drainage. The State Legislature enacted laws in 1967 to reduce feedlot pollution.

Field work consisted of seine collections. In 1952, two persons sampled [[Cedar Creek]] and the [[South Fork Cottonwood River]] in August using seines 6 to 25 feet long with 1-inch or 1/4-inch mesh (bar measure). Collections from the Neosho River that year were made in July by five persons, with 1 to 2 hours spent at each station and usually one seine in operation. In 1967, groups of three to five persons sampled the same or similar sites over 0.5 to 1.5 hours per site, often with two seines in use at once. The 1967 sampling ran from June through August, a longer period than the single week each in July and August used in 1952, so the authors consider the 1967 samples the more thorough set. The [[John Redmond Reservoir]] (filled 1965) strongly affected the Neosho at Hartford, so that site was dropped in 1967, along with one upstream station. Four new mainstream stations replaced them. All collections were made in daylight.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pollution and fish kills.** Fish kills were the main documented stressor. The authors note that organic pollution depletes subsurface dissolved oxygen as wastes decay. Many declining species require clear, well-oxygenated water, and some also need clean gravel for spawning.

**Impoundment.** John Redmond Reservoir changed downstream conditions. Stations below it held more fishes than those above: about 10 species and 100 fishes on average above the reservoir in 1967, compared with 15 species at Burlington and 22 species at Neosho Falls below it. The reservoir also received introduced sport and forage fishes, and it appears to have favored [[Gizzard Shad]] and silversides.

**Tributary refuges.** The South Fork and Cedar Creek kept large, diverse faunas in 1967, while the mainstream lost most of its diversity. Tributaries in the western headwaters also retained species. Collections from [[Doyle Creek]] yielded 8 species, and three [[Cedar Creek]] collections yielded 11, 16 and 19 species. Records from six western headwater localities, combined with one Cottonwood site, indicate at least 33 species persist in that area.

**Drought.** A drought from 1952 to 1956 depleted [[Gravel Chub]] populations in the Neosho System. The species later recovered.

**Arkansas Basin significance.** The [[Topeka Shiner]] persists in only two streams in the entire Arkansas River System, the South Fork and Cedar Creek. Pre-1900 records show it was once more widespread in the basin.

## Key Findings

**Overall diversity and abundance**
- Richness at the 1967 sites was about as high as in 1952 overall (Table 1). Mainstream averages fell from 25 species per locality in 1952 (range 20–29) to slightly under 10 species above the reservoir in 1967.
- Fish abundance was substantially lower in 1967 than in 1952.
- Tributaries (South Fork, Cedar Creek) retained large, diverse faunas in 1967.

**Species that disappeared**
- Six species recorded repeatedly in 1952 were absent from all 17 sites sampled by the authors in 1967: [[Gravel Chub]] (*Hybopsis x-punctata*), [[Hornyhead Chub]] (*H. biguttata*), [[Rosyface Shiner]] (*Notropis rubellus*), [[Blue Sucker]] (*Cycleptus elongatus*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus natalis*) and the [[Neosho Madtom]] (*Noturus* sp., undescribed).
- Dowlin's collections later found the yellow bullhead. The summary therefore states that five species were absent from all 24 localities surveyed by the authors and Dowlin.
- The Neosho madtom is endemic to the upper Neosho Basin, and its survival is considered endangered.

**Species that declined**
- Fifteen additional species were much less frequent in 1967. The summary places the total at "at least 20" species declining in abundance.
- Notable declines include [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*), which vanished from the mainstream; [[Suckermouth Minnow]] (*Phenacobius mirabilis*), reduced from all mainstream sites to three of seven, with 231 specimens from one 1952 Neosho Rapids series; [[Duskystripe Shiner]] (*N. pilsbryi*), absent from the Neosho, Cottonwood and Cedar Creek in 1967; [[Stonecat]] (*Noturus flavus*); and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), taken at less than half as many localities as in 1952.
- Several darters declined: [[Logperch]] (*Percina caprodes*) and [[Orangethroat Darter]] (*Etheostoma spectabile*) became absent from the mainstream, and [[Slenderhead Darter]] (*P. phoxocephala*) was restricted to a few sites.
- The [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*), common in 1952, was found at only one mainstream place in 1967.

**Species that increased**
- Eight species were more widespread in 1967. Gizzard shad, channel catfish, largemouth bass, two crappies and walleye occur mainly as large, vagile species in John Redmond Reservoir, and most are introduced or spread after reservoir construction. None were abundant in 1967.
- Surface-dwelling species ([[Blackstripe Topminnow]], *Fundulus notatus*; [[Western Mosquitofish]], *Gambusia affinis*; [[Brook Silverside]], *Labidesthes sicculus*) became more common. Mosquitofish, introduced to Kansas, was first published from the state in 1954. The authors suggest surface habit reduces exposure to oxygen depletion and that competition eased after minnow declines.
- Largemouth bass (five of seven South Fork stations in 1967, versus none in 1952) probably reflect earlier farm-pond stocking. Walleye represented emigrants from John Redmond Reservoir, where it was introduced in 1965.

**Probable replacements**
- The [[Redfin Shiner]] (*N. umbratilis*) became more abundant in the South Fork, probably replacing *N. rubellus* and, to a lesser degree, *N. pilsbryi*.
- The [[Red Shiner]] (*N. lutrensis*) outnumbered the [[Bluntface Shiner]] (*N. camurus*) at all 1967 stations, in contrast to 1952 South Fork results.
- The [[Mimic Shiner]] (*N. volucellus*) increased in the South Fork, possibly replacing [[Sand Shiner]] (*N. stramineus*).
- [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*) replaced yellow bullhead in the South Fork, and [[Largemouth Bass]] may be partly replacing [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*) there.

**Conservation significance**
- The Topeka shiner's incidence did not change markedly between 1952 and 1967, but the South Fork and Cedar Creek are the only Arkansas Basin streams where it persists.
- The authors conclude that strict enforcement of the 1967 state law would reduce pollution and fish kills, which they regard as the main drivers of the decline.
