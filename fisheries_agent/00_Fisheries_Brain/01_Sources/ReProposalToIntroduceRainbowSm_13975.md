# Opposition to Rainbow Smelt Introduction in Lake Powell

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-policy #Osmeridae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal letter dated January 3, 1990, from [[Grand Canyon National Park]] Superintendent John H. Davis to Wayne Gustaveson of the [[Utah Division of Wildlife]], expressing opposition to a proposal to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]]. The introduction was intended to provide additional forage for [[Striped Bass]], but the National Park Service raised concerns about potential threats to endangered species, particularly the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The letter argues that insufficient research supports the proposal's conclusions, that the NEPA process was not followed, and that [[Grand Canyon National Park]] should be formally involved in all proceedings.

## Background & Description

The [[Utah Division of Wildlife]] proposed introducing [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]] to enhance forage availability for [[Striped Bass]]. The proposal acknowledged that smelt would likely spread downstream through [[Grand Canyon National Park]] into [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]], causing widespread ecological changes. The proposal concluded that "rare and endangered endemic fishes would have minimal interaction with smelt and would not face significant competition for food and space."

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) are endangered or threatened species present in [[Grand Canyon National Park]] and the [[Colorado River]] system. The history of fishery management in [[Lake Powell]] includes numerous non-native fish introductions that resulted in mixed success or decline in resident species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Very little was known about the habitat needs of chubs and suckers in the [[Colorado River]] system at the time of this correspondence. No research existed on interactions between these native species and [[Rainbow Smelt]], even under laboratory conditions. Almost all past research on smelt had been conducted in northern lakes and streams, primarily the [[Great Lakes]], making predictions about smelt behavior in [[Lake Powell]] difficult.

Little research information was available regarding the ecology of [[Lake Powell]] itself. The [[Desert Fishes Council]], composed of prominent fisheries and aquatic biologists, opposed the proposal, reflecting broader scientific concern that non-native fish introductions have been a major factor in the decline of native fishes.

The proposal had potential to impact not only [[Lake Powell]] but also downstream ecosystems including [[Grand Canyon National Park]], [[Lake Mead]], and [[Lake Mohave]].

## Key Findings

- [[Grand Canyon National Park]] formally opposed the [[Rainbow Smelt]] introduction on grounds that it could seriously threaten endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and threatened [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

- The literature cited in the proposal did not provide sufficient information to support conclusions that endangered endemic fishes would have minimal interaction with smelt or face insignificant competition for food and space

- Many fishery biologists believed that introduction of non-native fishes has been a major factor in the decline of native fishes in many situations

- The [[Desert Fishes Council]] also opposed the proposal for conservation reasons

- The proposal contained many assumptions and general statements based on unjustified extrapolations from a very small research base

- Research on smelt ecology was primarily from northern lakes and the [[Great Lakes]], limiting applicability to [[Lake Powell]] conditions

- Very little research information existed regarding [[Lake Powell]] ecology itself

- Previous non-native fish introductions in [[Lake Powell]] resulted in mixed success or decline in resident species

- The proposal did not follow the NEPA process; an Environmental Impact Statement with formal Section 7 Consultation under the 1973 Endangered Species Act was recommended

- [[Grand Canyon National Park]] had not been formally invited to proceedings despite potential adverse impacts on park resources

- The Park Service requested formal involvement in all further proceedings regarding this matter
