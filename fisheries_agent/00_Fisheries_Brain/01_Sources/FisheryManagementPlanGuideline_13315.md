# Fishery Management Plan Guidelines

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fishery-management #conservation-planning

## Executive Summary

This document provides comprehensive guidelines for developing fishery management plans for various management units including military installations, Indian reservations, and federal lands managed by agencies such as the National Park Service (NPS), Department of Defense (DOD), and U.S. Forest Service (USFS). The guidelines outline a standardized framework for creating management plans that clearly present schemes for accomplishing conservation goals and managing fishery resources. The document emphasizes that management plans should articulate what needs to be done rather than detailed implementation procedures, which are reserved for separate work plans. It includes detailed checklists for inventory and survey components covering physical, physio-chemical, and biological characteristics of both lake and stream environments.

## Background & Description

The guidelines establish that a fishery management plan is a document presenting a scheme for accomplishing goals established for conserving fishery resources and managing fisheries of a specific management unit. The document distinguishes between management plans (which tell what needs to be done) and work plans (which detail how implementation should occur). The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's role is to determine the goals of resource owners or managers and develop technically sound plans to accomplish those goals while encouraging adoption of conservation-oriented goals that avoid waste, harm, or loss of resources.

The guidelines specify that plans should address basic descriptive elements of the fishery management area including size, geology and soils, topography, climate, vegetative types, water uses, land use (historical and present), and human demographics. Plans should provide general descriptions of stream kilometers, lake hectares, species of commercial importance, predominant sport fisheries, and [[Threatened and Endangered Species]]. The status of the aquatic environment, threats to habitat, and feasibility of habitat improvement must be included.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guidelines provide extensive checklists for assessing both lake and stream environments across multiple ecological dimensions:

**Physical Characteristics** include surface area, depth measurements, water level fluctuation, inlets and outlets, bottom types, water transparency, velocity, gradient, flow volume, channel stability, and riparian vegetation. For streams specifically, parameters such as pool-riffle ratio, sinuosity, and barriers are emphasized.

**Physio-Chemical Characteristics** encompass water temperature profiles, oxygen profiles, pH, alkalinity, specific conductance, anions, cations, heavy metals, hardness, and various forms of pollution including herbicides, pesticides, chemical, thermal, and silt pollution.

**Biological Characteristics** include habitat quality indices, in-stream flow needs, aquatic vegetation, plankton, benthos, spawning areas, and comprehensive fish population data (species, population estimates, relative abundance, size ranges, weight information, condition factors, age and growth, population structure, age at maturity, sex ratios). The guidelines also address fish diseases, parasites, and stocking records. Interacting wildlife including amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals should be documented.

**Limiting Factors** are categorized as physical-chemical (temperature, nutrients, spawning areas, pools, riffles, ice, gradient, flow fluctuation, erosion, turbidity, barriers), biological (undesirable fish species or sizes, aquatic vegetation, diseases-parasites, bank cover), pollution factors (nitrogen supersaturation, mining, thermal, organic-sewage, siltation, irrigation, pesticides, metals), and man-caused factors (flow fluctuations, excessive fish harvest, barriers, water removals, channel alterations, bank encroachment).

## Key Findings

The guidelines establish a standardized structure for fishery management plans consisting of thirteen required sections:

1. **Title Page**: Title, installation or reservation, date prepared, and authors
2. **Summary Statement**: Executive summary providing quick overview, able to stand alone
3. **Introduction**: Purpose, preparing agencies, intended recipients, and relation of facility use to fishery resource
4. **Brief Description of Fishery Management Area**: Minimum eight elements from size to human demographics
5. **Description of Fishery Resources**: General description with detailed data in appendices
6. **Management History**: Brief description and evaluation of past activities including stocking, habitat improvement, reclamation, regulations, and population manipulations
7. **Potential for Management**: Assessment of existing fisheries and potential for developing new fisheries
8. **Goals**: General statements of intent and direction that are timeless; may include sub-goals
9. **Major Factors Influencing Achievement of Goals**: Biological or non-biological factors positively or negatively influencing objectives
10. **Strategies**: Methods or directives providing techniques for achieving goals using step-down approach
11. **Evaluation and Update**: Annual review by representatives of land manager/owner and cooperating wildlife agencies
12. **Distribution**: List of agencies receiving initial distribution
13. **Concurrences**: Agreement signatures from FWS area manager, FWS regional director, land manager, and cooperators

The appendix may contain maps, agreements, data (stocking records, management history, survey data, historical documents), species listings, cost estimates, bibliography, plans for individual waters, and other pertinent information.

**Management Recommendations** outlined in the checklist include survey needs, pollution abatement, flow stabilization, irrigation improvement, bank cover establishment, bank stabilization and fencing, watershed restoration, barrier removal, beaver stocking, fish species changes, spawning area improvement, channel improvement, access improvements, recreational use controls, esthetic improvements, regulation enforcement, fish stocking, fishing regulation changes, commercial management changes, aquatic vegetation control, fertilization, motor boat restrictions, reservoir drawdown, endangered species protection, research studies, establishment of minimum management criteria, and development prospects for new impoundments or stream improvement structures.

The Service's primary mandate is conservation, and personnel are encouraged to promote goals that ensure preservation of species and population diversity to maintain future management options. The management plan must reflect both the desires of owners or managers and the conservation mandate of the Service.
