# Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge: Draft Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (the Service) proposes to acquire approximately 1360 acres of upland, riparian, and aquatic habitat in [[Leslie Canyon]], Swisshelm Mountains, Cochise County, Arizona, and add it to the [[National Wildlife Refuge System]] as Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge. The stated purpose is protection of populations of two endangered fishes, the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] and the [[Yaqui Chub]], along with unique ash-willow-cottonwood riparian habitat. Three alternatives were evaluated: no action, fee acquisition of 1240 acres, and fee acquisition plus a habitat protective easement on about 120 additional acres. The third alternative is the preferred option because the easement buffers the refuge against destructive periodic floods.

## Background & Description

The proposal is a draft environmental assessment prepared by the Albuquerque, New Mexico office of the Service in April 1988. Acquisition would be authorized under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended, with possible funding from the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund]] (Land and Water Conservation Fund Act of 1965, as amended).

The refuge would be formed around [[Leslie Creek]], which flows northeast to southwest through the canyon and supplies the lotic habitat for both endangered fishes. According to the document, Leslie Creek is one of only two remaining U.S. localities for the Yaqui chub and one of three nationally known to support the Sonoran topminnow.

The three alternatives are:
1. No action.
2. Fee acquisition of 1240 acres of upland, riparian, and aquatic habitat.
3. Fee acquisition of 1240 acres plus a habitat protective easement on approximately 120 additional acres (preferred).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate:** Typical of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] region (Brown 1982). Summer temperatures above 100 °F are not uncommon, and winter temperatures below 20 °F but usually above 0 °F occur frequently. Annual precipitation at Douglas, about 15 miles south, averaged nearly 13 inches from 1974 to 1981.

**Soils:** Four major soil associations (White House-Tubac-Forest, Kimbrough-Cave, Luzena-Faraway, and Tortugas) are present, mostly shallow, cobbly, or gravelly soils on steep slopes, with some deep fine-textured soils on nearly level sites.

**Topography:** Elevation ranges from about 5500 ft at the northern end to about 4600 ft on the canyon floor, a relief of roughly 900 ft.

**Hydrology:** Leslie Creek drains about 79 square miles, formed by ephemeral drainages from the southern Chiricahua Mountains, and joins Whitewater Draw about 15 miles northwest of Douglas. Flow is highly erratic:
- In 1977, there were 310 days with flow below 0.05 cubic feet per second (cfs).
- A peak of 563 cfs was recorded on August 8, 1977.
- Flow exceeded 1.0 cfs on only 11 days from October 1983 to September 1984.
- A maximum discharge of 4600 cfs was recorded on July 21, 1984.

**Vegetation:** Chihuahuan Desert scrub dominated by shrubs, with grama, three-awn, and dropseed grasses and many cacti. The Leslie Creek floodplain upstream of the canyon is nearly pure [[Alkali Sacaton]] (*Sporobolus airoides*). A dense riparian woodland of alder, willow, ash, cottonwood, and scattered walnut extends about half a mile through the canyon.

**Wildlife:** No quantitative wildlife inventory exists. The Arizona Game and Fish Department (1972) listed about 70 bird species likely in riparian habitat and at least 20 more in upland areas. Rare species noted as possible residents include the black-bellied tree duck, black hawk, thick-billed kingbird, tropical kingbird, and swamp sparrow. About 15 mammal species may occur, including mule deer, javelina, badger, bobcat, and coyote.

**Land use:** The area is currently owned by [[The Nature Conservancy]]. Access is restricted, though a county road passes through, and some trespass and local use has occurred. Livestock grazing and development pressure are the main threats if the area stays in current ownership.

## Key Findings

- **Need for protection:** Leslie Creek is a critical stronghold for two endangered fishes, and the document identifies unauthorized introduction of exotic fishes as a major risk to resident populations.
- **No action rejected:** Without acquisition, the area could be subject to uncontrolled development or livestock grazing, so the no-action alternative was not preferred.
- **Fee acquisition alone is insufficient:** Acquiring 1240 acres would give legal protection but not adequate long-term protection from destructive floods.
- **Preferred alternative:** Fee acquisition of 1240 acres plus a roughly 120-acre habitat protective easement upstream of the fee boundary. The easement would prevent development and any use that destroys the sacaton grassland, which disperses high flows, reduces velocities, and absorbs flood energy such as the 1984 event.
- **Consequence of grassland loss:** Scouring and sediment deposition from floods could destroy fish habitat and adjacent riparian habitat.
- **Coordination:** The Arizona Game and Fish Department, local experts, academia, and citizen groups were consulted. The Arizona Congressional Delegation provided the necessary appropriation, and The Nature Conservancy led development of the proposal.
- **Data limitations:** Wildlife data are qualitative, and the hydrological record is limited to selected years (1977, 1981–1984).
