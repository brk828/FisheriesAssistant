# Reclassification of the Gila Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Interior) proposes to reclassify the [[Gila Topminnow]], a subspecies endemic to the [[Gila River]] drainage of New Mexico and Arizona, from **endangered** to **threatened** status under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The species was originally listed as endangered on March 11, 1967 (32 FR 4001). The Service's 1986 review of current status information found that the species' populations had met recovery criteria in its [[Recovery Plan]] (at least three years old and established in its historic range), and it concludes the species is no longer in danger of extinction in the foreseeable future. The document is a Federal Register proposed rule that solicits public comment and sets out the regulatory amendment to 50 CFR 17.11(h).

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** *Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*, a livebearing topminnow (family Poeciliidae) and subspecies of the Gila topminnow.
- **Native range:** The Gila River drainage from about 4,500 feet elevation down to the river mouth near Yuma, Arizona, and possibly the Lower [[Colorado River]].
- **Additional reported records:** Salt River, Verde River, San Francisco River (New Mexico), a few sites in the San Pedro system, numerous records from the [[Santa Cruz River]] system, and likely the San Simon drainage.
- **Recovery status at 1984:** When the recovery plan was completed (March 15, 1984), only nine isolated localities were known to have extant natural populations; all but one were in the Santa Cruz River system (e.g., Redrock Canyon, Cottonwood Spring, Monkey Spring, Sonoita Creek, Cienega Creek, Sheehy Spring, Sharp Spring, upper Santa Cruz River, and Salt Creek, a Gila River tributary). Several reclaimed and restocked populations also existed.
- **Regulatory history:** Listed as endangered March 11, 1967; incorporated into the endangered species list under the 1973 Act. No critical habitat was designated at listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** Historically, perennial rivers with stable channels, extensive lagoons, marshes, backwaters, springs and cienegas formed the core habitat. Habitat loss followed channel incision, lowered water tables, flooding of intermittent sandy washes, and loss of springs and marshes, confining the fish to smaller streams, springs and headwaters.

**Food habits.** The topminnow mainly browses on detritus and vegetation, whereas the [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) feeds mainly on invertebrates and small fish.

**Threat factors (Section 4(a)(1) analysis):**

| Factor | Summary |
|---|---|
| A. Habitat destruction | Decline since the late 1800s from aquatic habitat loss due to human activity; river systems retain only a small fraction of pre-1860 aquatic habitat. |
| B. Overutilization | Small, controlled take for scientific or educational purposes through federal and state permitting; commercial and sport take prohibited. |
| C. Disease and predation | Predation and harassment by introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (introduced to Arizona in 1926) is the principal cause of decline. |
| D. Regulatory inadequacy | Arizona lists the species as a Group 3 species, which provides no habitat protection. |
| E. Other factors | Private land ownership limits protection; public multiple-use lands face grazing, irrigation, timber, recreation and road impacts; illegal mosquitofish stockings are easier on public lands. |

**Mosquitofish impacts.** Documented extirpations were rapid: at Arivaca Creek, Arizona, topminnows introduced in 1936 were abundant by 1957 but were extirpated in under two years after mosquitofish arrived. At artesian springs and canals near Safford, topminnows (abundant in 1962) were gone by 1966 after mosquitofish were introduced in 1963. Minckley et al. (1977) report that between 1950 and May 1977 mosquitofish completely replaced the Gila topminnow at 15 localities.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed action:** Reclassify the Gila topminnow from endangered to threatened; the Service considers this consistent with the Act and conservation of the species.
- **Justification:** Recovery criteria in the species' recovery plan have been met, and the species is judged not to be in danger of extinction in the foreseeable future under continued careful management.
- **Effects of rule:** Protections under Sections 7 and 9 are essentially the same for threatened and endangered species; reclassification gives the Service the option of promulgating a special rule, which is not available for endangered species.
- **Reversibility:** The action is reversible; the species could be re-upgraded to endangered if management, habitat or other threats change.
- **Critical habitat:** None designated; the Service finds no net conservation benefit from designation at this time because existing protections remain.
- **Regulatory compliance:** An Environmental Assessment under NEPA is not required (notice published in the Federal Register, October 25, 1983, 48 FR 49244).
- **Public input:** Comments are sought on biological data, threats, commercial trade, environmental impacts of the rule, and alternatives. Public hearing requests must be filed within 45 days of publication; comments are due within 50 days.
- **Contacts:** Proposed rule prepared by Sue Rutman and Gerald Burton, Endangered Species Biologists, Albuquerque, New Mexico (505/766-3972).
