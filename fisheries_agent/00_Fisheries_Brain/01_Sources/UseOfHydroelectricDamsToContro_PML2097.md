# Use of Hydroelectric Dams to Control Evaporation and Salinity in the Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the Symposium on the Aquatic Resources Management of the Colorado River Ecosystem (Las Vegas, Nevada, November 1981), argues that the operation of [[Hoover Dam]] and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] can be altered to reduce evaporative water loss and dissolved salt concentrations in [[Lake Mead]]. Cold-water releases from Glen Canyon Dam, drawn from the hypolimnion of [[Lake Powell]], have already reduced advective heat inputs and evaporation. The author proposes that operating Hoover Dam from a surface rather than deep-water discharge would further reduce evaporation, with a combined reduction of about 217,000 acre-feet/yr at 1977–78 lake levels. The paper also identifies trade-offs for downstream trout habitat, nutrient budgets, and fisheries, and concludes that engineering and limnological studies are still needed.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The main-stem reservoirs of the Colorado River ([[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]]) store up to 53,459,400 acre-feet (56 × 10⁹ m³) at maximum capacity, yet local shortages persist and salt concentrations are approaching levels that could severely affect municipal and agricultural use.
- **Lake Mead:** Formed in 1935 by Hoover Dam; extends 114 miles from the mouth of Grand Canyon to Black Canyon; surface area of 163,000 acres (660 km²) and volume of 29,141,245 acre-feet at maximum operating level of 1,229 ft. [[Boulder Canyon]] divides it into the Upper and Lower Basins. Hoover Dam intake gates sit at 1,045 ft and 895 ft elevations; the lower gates have been used since 1954.
- **Inflow:** Colorado River inflow comprises about 99% of Lake Mead's inflow (remainder from the Virgin and Muddy Rivers, and [[Las Vegas Wash]] secondary-treated sewage effluent).
- **Data sources:** USGS Water-Supply Papers, Water-Resources Data reports for Nevada and Arizona, and Lake Mead Limnological Research Center data (October 1977–September 1978 used as the modeling baseline).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal and flow regime change:** Glen Canyon Dam (completed 1963 with Lake Powell formation; described elsewhere as 1967 for the river inflow regulation) eliminated the historic spring discharge pulse. River temperatures rose nearly 1 °C in late fall and winter and fell about 10 °C during the rest of the year. Discharge temperatures average about 8 °C; summer river temperatures reach 10–11 °C at Grand Canyon.
- **Advective energy:** Spring and early-summer advection historically added 300–400 cal/cm²/day to Lake Mead, nearly half of solar input. Post-dam advection contributes minimal heat (net gain of 9.04 cal/cm²/day in 1977–78).
- **Evaporation trends:** Pre-Glen Canyon annual evaporation averaged 85.2 inches/yr; during 1965–1970 it fell to about 74 inches/yr. The 1975–76 spikes were attributed to discontinuation of advection-based adjustments (October 1974) and a coefficient change in February 1976, not to river discharge changes.
- **Temperature gradients:** Surface temperatures in summer reach 27–30 °C versus 11–12 °C in the hypolimnion. Upper Basin waters are 1–2 °C colder than the Lower Basin, and the Colorado River density current extends into Virgin Basin mainly during late summer.
- **Downstream biota:** Black Canyon supports a popular cold-water [[Trout Fishery]]. Hoover Dam discharges are now nearly constant at 12–13 °C, linked to declines in aquatic insect populations that trout depend on.
- **Fertility and fish:** Reduced reservoir fertility after Glen Canyon Dam construction is linked to decline of the [[Largemouth Bass]] population in Lake Mead; a surface discharge could sustain greater fertility.

## Key Findings

- **Evaporation reduction from cold-water releases:** Glen Canyon cold-water discharges reduced Lake Mead evaporation by at least 911,000 acre-feet/yr (1.2 × 10⁸ m³), consistent with mid-1950s predictions of about 1,000,000 acre-feet/yr (the text states "100,000" in one place; the 911,000 figure is the computed estimate).
- **Surface discharge at Hoover Dam:** Estimated net advective heat difference of −38.59 cal/cm²/day between surface and deep discharge; equivalent to an evaporation decrease of about 10.5 inches/yr and an annual water saving of about 1,189,000 acre-feet (1.47 × 10⁸ m³) at 1977–78 lake elevations (per paper's estimate). Earlier work by Harbeck et al. (1952–55 data) estimated 720,000 acre-feet/yr (8.9 × 10⁷ m³).
- **Combined strategy:** Deep discharge at Glen Canyon plus surface discharge at Hoover Dam could reduce water loss by 217,000 acre-feet/yr (2.53 × 10⁸ m³) at 1977–78 levels.
- **Salinity effects:** Dissolved solids at Hoover Dam intake averaged 676 mg/L in water year 1978. Evaporation reduction of 120,000 acre-feet (surface discharge alone) would lower Lake Mead salinity by about 9 mg/L; 213,000 acre-feet (combined strategy) by about 16 mg/L, within five years, comparable to the Las Vegas Wash (8 mg/L) and Grand Valley (19 mg/L) salinity control projects.
- **Engineering constraints:** Hoover Dam's upper gates (1,045 ft) would still draw hypolimnetic water at 1978 levels (1,186 ft); intake modifications at higher elevations would be required, with feasibility studies still needed.
- **Ecological trade-offs:**
  - Warm-water releases may harm the cold-water trout fishery, but seasonal temperature cycles could boost aquatic insect production, supported by stocking warm-water-tolerant rainbow trout.
  - Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu (each about one-third of Lake Mead's surface area) would likely still see reduced net water loss and salinity, even if evaporation rose there.
  - Total nitrogen retention would increase by 66% and total phosphorus by 60% with a surface discharge, potentially benefiting the Largemouth Bass population.
- **Water budget discrepancy:** Measured Lake Mead storage exceeded water-budget estimates by about 220,000 acre-feet/yr (2.71 × 10⁸ m³) during 1960–1970, possibly reflecting overestimated evaporation.
- **Recommendations:** Further limnological studies in Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, and Lake Havasu; engineering studies on intake modifications; and assessment of pump-storage units (Spring Canyon and Rifle Range sites) with a diffuser to cool surface waters.
