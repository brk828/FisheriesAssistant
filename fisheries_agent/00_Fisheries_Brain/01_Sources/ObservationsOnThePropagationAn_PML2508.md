# Propagation and Rearing of Two Endangered Fish Species in a Hatchery Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ptychocheilus #xyrauchen

## Executive Summary

This preliminary hatchery report by [[Donald P. Toney]], Hatchery Manager at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], documents the holding, spawning, and early rearing of two endangered fishes: the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. Because the species had been under observation only briefly, the author treats each separately. Squawfish broodstock survived poorly, suffered a severe [[Ichthyophthirius Infestation]] and fungal and myxosporean complications, and produced eggs and hatching fry in July 1974. Razorback sucker eggs were collected from wild adults at Lake Mohave, hatched successfully in trays, and produced over one thousand fry that were moved to an outdoor raceway.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was listed on the United States List of Endangered Fauna in May 1974. The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was listed as endangered by the states of California and Nevada. Both species were held at Willow Beach, Arizona, at the time of writing.

The report describes the following activities:

- **Squawfish broodstock:** Eight fish were captured by netting and shocking in the [[Yampa River]], Colorado, in late July 1973 and transferred to Willow Beach on July 31, 1973.
- **Razorback broodstock:** Adults were collected near Cottonwood Cove, [[Lake Mohave]], in March 1974 during spawning. Thirty-two adults were moved to the [[Arizona State Hatchery]] at Page Springs on May 7, 1974, and eight were sacrificed for disease inspection.

The author notes that the report is limited because the observation period was short.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Holding facilities (squawfish):** Two concrete raceways, each 100 ft long, 8 ft wide, and 30 in deep, were connected to allow recirculation, which was needed to reach spawning temperatures. Water was pumped at about 200 gallons per minute between raceways. River water was added through valves to control temperature and replace evaporative losses. About the first 30 ft of one raceway held a gravel bed (water depth 3 to 12 inches) used as a spawning area.

**Water temperature:** Recirculation kept temperatures in the high 60s to mid 70s °F until mid-October 1973. River water at 55°F was then used until May 1974. Temperatures were raised to about 69°F by late May and ranged from 70 to 76°F in June.

**Hatchery rearing facilities:** Razorback eggs and early fry were kept in a concrete tank 3 ft wide by 15 ft long, filled with 55°F river water to a depth of 2 ft. A fine-screened tray 3 ft square sat below the surface, aerated by a stone at each end.

**Outdoor raceway (razorback fry):** An outdoor raceway, 8 ft wide by 100 ft long and 30 in deep, held zooplankton that was abundant, mainly Cladocera and blackfly larvae.

**Diet:** Squawfish broodstock would not accept formulated feed or pellets and would not swallow them. They preferred crippled trout, which were fed whole or with the heads removed. Razorback fry took finely chopped beef liver.

## Key Findings

### Colorado Squawfish

**Broodstock and survival**
- Of the eight squawfish received, seven arrived in good condition.
- The largest fish, a 5.5 lb female taken by shocking, showed impaired swimming, probably from spinal injury, and died four days later. Otolith readings gave an age of 16 years.
- On January 10, 1974, the seven fish weighed 22.8 lb in total. On May 30, 1974, they weighed 20.6 lb.
- The report gives a total of 14.1 lb and a gain of 1.4 lb over the nine-month period, though the scanned figures are partly garbled.
- About 90 lb of trout were fed over the period. The author describes feed conversion as "not too good."

**Growth and morphometrics**
- Length-weight data for the Willow Beach fish closely matched a relationship from [[Vanicek and Kramer 1966]].
- Calculated lengths for ten- and older-year-old fish in that study were 570 and 500 mm.
- The two measured Willow Beach fish were 531 and 594 mm long and weighed 1388 and 1900 g.
- Scale samples were not taken, so no direct age determination was made for the living fish.

**Health and parasites**
- The initial examination on August 1, 1973, by Biologist Rosenlund found [[Ichthyophthirius]], [[Lernaea]], and [[Trichodina]] or related organisms (the scan is partly unclear).
- The fish were treated with a potassium permanganate bath (1/1000) during early handling, with little effect.
- A heavy infestation appeared in late February 1974. Moving fish to an indoor tank with faster flow worsened their condition, and fungal lesions developed. Parasite density was estimated at about 100 per square inch.
- Moving the fish back outdoors and warming the water failed to stop the infestation. One fish died after handling, and examination showed numerous [[Myxosoma]] spores in the liver and kidney.
- **Treatment that worked:** Formalin (25 mg/L) and [[Malachite Green]] (0.05 mg/L) were added every other day for three weeks, beginning April 16, 1974. Within two days the fish began to recover, and no parasites were found after three weeks.

**Spawning and reproduction**
- Hand stripping on August 14, 1973, failed. Two mature males and possibly one or more females were found.
- On August 21, 1973, three possible females were injected with [[Chorionic Gonadotropin]] at 900 units per pound. Nothing followed, and the males stopped producing sperm.
- Each female was checked for external sex characteristics. Genital papilla length was 7/16 in on three probable females. On males it was 5/16 in on one and 1/2 in on another. The author notes this relationship appears opposite to that reported for [[Columbia Squawfish]] by [[Jeppson 1957]].
- Tubercles covered the bodies of the males, especially the head and pectoral fins.
- On June 24, 1974 (date partly garbled), about 70 very small eggs were obtained from female No. 987 after hormone injection. Males were very ripe.
- Eggs were found in the gravel one week after injection, probably laid about June 16. The eggs were adhesive, about 2.0 mm in diameter, and scattered over an area roughly 4 by 8 ft in 5 to 12 inches of water at about 72°F.
- Several thousand eggs were collected from the gravel by hand-stirring and net collection. Egg mortality was high in a floating net in a raceway, probably from suffocation. Eggs on the hatchery tray developed normally.
- Eggs from the spawning area showed well-pigmented eyes on July 4, 1974, and began hatching that evening. Hatchery-tray eggs hatched the next morning.
- Hatching fry measured about 6.2 mm.

### Razorback Sucker

**Broodstock**
- A joint Federal-State recovery team collected adults near Cottonwood Cove, Lake Mohave, on March 21, 1974. Average water temperature was around 55°F. Some eggs were stripped at the cove, and more were obtained after the adults were moved to Willow Beach.
- The number of eggs per female was not determined.

**Egg care and hatch**
- The egg count was not measured accurately. The author's rough estimate is 5,000 to 10,000 eggs.
- Eggs measured 2.1 to 3.2 mm in diameter.
- Two incubation methods were tested. In jar incubation with constant water flow, mortality was high from the start, possibly from low temperature or excess agitation, and all eggs were dead by day eight.
- On the screened tray in the tank, mortality was very low. Eggs hatched on days five and six, with an estimated hatch rate of 15 percent. Water temperature was 58°F on day one and ranged from 50 to 63°F afterward.

**Fry care and growth**
- At hatching, fry were 5 to 7 mm long and nearly unpigmented. Their eyes became pigmented about six days after hatching, and they began swimming to the surface.
- Fry were fed finely chopped beef liver four or five times daily for two weeks. After three weeks in the hatchery, including the egg stage, 1,259 fry were hand-counted and moved to the outdoor raceway on April 17, 1974.
- Fry fed heavily on zooplankton and grew to about 35 mm by the end of May. By June 30, the average length was about 60 to 70 mm, with some individuals over three inches (about 76 mm).
- Size variation became large, and a representative sample was hard to obtain without capturing many fingerlings, given their small size and heavy algae growth.

### Future Plans and Status

- A contract was let with [[Arizona State University]], Tempe, for a life-history study of the razorback under the direction of [[W. L. Hinckley]].
- Distribution plans for razorback fry were incomplete. The report says some fry would be held at the hatchery as long as possible, and others might be released to the wild.
- The future of the squawfish program was undetermined, since the broodstock was the only one in captivity and reproduction was not yet secured. Rearing its fry and building additional broodstock were under consideration.
- An update dated July 1974 reported squawfish fry swimming to the surface in hatchery tanks and raceways A-1, A-10, and B-1. Fry were too numerous to count accurately. The recirculation pump for two raceways was turned off to prevent fry from being pulled into the pump.
