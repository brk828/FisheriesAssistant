# Geology and Water Resources of the Gila and San Carlos Valleys in the San Carlos Indian Reservation, Arizona

#hydrology #groundwater #irrigation #geology #water-resources

## Executive Summary

This 1919 report by A. T. Schwennesen investigates ground-water conditions in the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] to determine the feasibility of developing irrigation water supplies through wells. The study was prompted by chronic water shortages affecting Indian farmers in the [[Gila Valley]] and [[San Carlos Valley]]. The investigation included geologic mapping, classification of arable lands, water quality analyses, and assessment of both shallow pumping and deep artesian prospects. The work revealed approximately 6,435 acres of arable land above the proposed reservoir site, with favorable artesian structure in parts of the [[Gila Basin]] but significant water quality concerns in shallow wells.

## Background & Description

The [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] encompasses two distinct physiographic regions: a northern/eastern plateau area characterized by mountains and lava plateaus, and a southern basin area containing the [[Gila Basin]] and [[San Carlos Basin]]. The basin area is traversed by [[Gila River]] (flowing westward) and [[San Carlos River]] (flowing southward to join the Gila at San Carlos).

The geologic framework consists of three principal Quaternary units overlying pre-Quaternary igneous and sedimentary rocks: (1) the [[Gila Conglomerate]], an ancient alluvial deposit up to 1,000 feet thick composed of sand, gravel, and boulders; (2) lake beds consisting of well-stratified sandstones, tuffs, marly clays, and limestones deposited in standing water, reaching estimated thicknesses of 600-800 feet; and (3) Recent alluvium forming the modern river valleys. Basalt flows of recent age cap portions of the sedimentary sequence.

The Quaternary history involved: (1) initial aggradation depositing the Gila Conglomerate; (2) erosion of alluvial slopes; (3) submergence by a lake with deposition of fine-grained sediments and concurrent volcanism; (4) lake disappearance and valley excavation; and (5) partial refilling with Recent alluvium and terrace formation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river valleys support distinct vegetation zones reflecting soil, topography, and water availability. Arable terraces above flood level support [[Mesquite]] and cat-claw as dominant native vegetation, with [[Cottonwood]] trees also common. Flood plains harbor thickets of cottonwoods, willows, and water-loving shrubs. Higher gravelly tracts near valley margins are characterized by [[Creosote Brush]] and cacti.

Surface water supplies from [[Gila River]] and [[San Carlos River]] are highly variable, with severe low flows during May-July when irrigation demand peaks. Stream-gaging records from 1899-1905 document extreme drought conditions, with the Gila sometimes ceasing to flow entirely. The 1914 records show San Carlos River had no flow during April-May, while Gila River averaged only 18 acre-feet per day in late May-June below irrigation diversions.

The existing irrigation system in 1913 served only 1,428 acres (421 acres Bylas district, 469 acres San Carlos district, 538 acres Peridot district) at an annual maintenance cost of $1,908 ($1.34 per acre). The system relied on temporary brush and earth diversion dams that washed out repeatedly, requiring constant reconstruction and resulting in significant water losses through seepage.

## Key Findings

**Land Classification:** The investigation identified 4,595 acres of arable land in [[Gila Valley]] above the proposed reservoir site (only 10% irrigated in 1913) and 1,840 acres in [[San Carlos Valley]] (30% irrigated in 1913). Total arable land: 6,435 acres.

**Shallow Ground Water:** Wells in Recent alluvium typically reach water at 15-20 feet depth on low terraces, with deeper water tables (32-45 feet) near valley margins. The alluvium appears capable of yielding sufficient water for irrigation if pumped from wells located near river channels, where recharge from stream seepage is greatest.

**Water Quality Concerns:** Chemical analyses revealed severe quality problems in [[Gila Valley]] shallow wells. Samples 9, 12, 13, and 14 contained 1,330-2,412 ppm total dissolved solids with prohibitively high chlorine content (white alkali). Sample 1 contained excessive sodium carbonate (black alkali). These waters were deemed "practically worthless for irrigation under ordinary conditions." In contrast, water from the San Carlos pumping plant showed moderate mineralization (fair quality), comparable to Gila River water successfully used for irrigation. The report recommended deferring final judgment until field experiments could assess actual crop effects.

**Artesian Prospects:** Geologic structure favorable to artesian conditions exists along the north side of [[Gila Basin]] for 15 miles from the east reservation boundary and along the south side for 20 miles below Bylas. In [[San Carlos Basin]], favorable structure occurs only along the west side for 5 miles north from the box canyon (within proposed reservoir). The [[Gila Conglomerate]] serves as the aquifer, with lake beds providing artesian confinement and pre-Quaternary rocks forming the basin floor. Small springs issuing near bluff bases suggest minor leakage but not major artesian cover failure.

**Drilling Requirements:** To test artesian potential requires penetrating Recent alluvium and lake beds, estimated at 700 feet average thickness but potentially 1,000-2,000 feet in some locations. The most favorable test well location was identified on Dewey Flat or 1 mile east of Dewey Flat on the south side of Gila Basin west of Bylas.

**Artesian Water Quality:** No direct data available, but water in [[Gila Conglomerate]] is predicted to be "good enough to be used for irrigation" based on recharge source (rainfall and mountain runoff) and formation lithology showing limited soluble minerals. However, buried saline beds along valley axes remain a possibility.

**Recommendations:** (1) Conduct pumping experiments at Bylas farm and San Carlos Valley to evaluate shallow ground water effects on crops under actual conditions; (2) drill artesian test well on south side of Gila Basin near Dewey Flat, prepared to reach 2,000 feet depth; (3) use shallow ground water to supplement surface supplies on existing irrigated tracts and extend irrigation to higher arable lands, to the extent water quality permits; (4) recognize that pumping costs will exceed current surface irrigation costs ($1.34/acre) but may be justified by assured dry-season supply and increased yields.

**Geologic Significance:** The lake bed deposits provide evidence of ancient standing water bodies in the [[Gila Valley]], with correlative deposits observed in [[San Simon Valley]], [[San Pedro Valley]], and possibly [[Sulphur Spring Valley]] and [[San Bernardino Valley]]. These related deposits have important bearing on late Quaternary geologic history and regional water resources of southeastern Arizona.
