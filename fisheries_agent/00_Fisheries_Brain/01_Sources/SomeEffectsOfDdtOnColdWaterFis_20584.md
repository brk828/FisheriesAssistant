# DDT Toxicity to Cold-Water Fish and Fish-Food Organisms in Montana; Cutthroat Trout Life History in the Flathead River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two 1963 papers from the *Proceedings of the Montana Academy of Sciences*. The main paper, by [[Norman D Schoenthal]], reports laboratory bio-assays and a field test stream study on the effects of the insecticide [[DDT]] on cold-water fishes and their food organisms. DDT was sprayed aerially over Montana forests to control the spruce budworm, and fish kills followed. The bio-assays used roughly 6,000 fish over the summers of 1957–1959. Mortality depended on fish source, size, and water conditions, while sublethal exposure through contaminated insects had a measurable effect. A field test in [[Trail Creek]] showed that DDT nearly eliminated the aquatic insect fauna and reduced trout populations downstream. The second paper, by [[Howard E Johnson]], is only partially present in the source. It contains the introduction and study-area description for a life-history study of [[Cutthroat Trout]] in the [[Flathead River]] drainage.

## Background & Description

**DDT and forest spraying.** DDT was applied from 1952 at one pound per acre to control the [[Spruce Budworm]] in Montana. Large areas of [[Yellowstone National Park]] and the [[Gallatin National Forest]] were sprayed in 1955. A heavy fish death on the upper Yellowstone River in early winter 1956 prompted a cooperative study by the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[United States Forest Service]], the [[Montana Fish and Game Department]], and the Montana Agricultural Experiment Station. Earlier qualitative and long-range studies cited include work in Idaho, Wyoming, Pennsylvania, Maine, British Columbia, and New Brunswick.

**Study species.** Wild fish were collected by electric shocker from unsprayed streams in western Montana. Hatchery fish came from the Anaconda and Arlee hatcheries. The species were:
- Cutthroat trout (*Salmo clarki*)
- Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*)
- Brown trout (*Salmo trutta*)
- Brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*)
- Mountain whitefish (*Prosopium williamsoni*)
- Longnose sucker (*Catostomus catostomus*) and largescale sucker (*C. macrocheilus*)

**Experimental facilities.** Bio-assays were run at the [[Ovando State Fish Hatchery]]. Testing used seven fiberglass units (210 liters each) set within redwood troughs and recirculated by centrifugal pumps. Redwood absorbed DDT at high concentrations and leached it at low concentrations, so it was replaced by fiberglass. Two earthen ponds (about 2,000 and 250 square feet) held pre- and post-test fish. The spring water supply had the following ranges: temperature 40–53 °F, turbidity 0–35 ppm, pH 7.6–8.2, methyl orange alkalinity 180–280 ppm, and total dissolved solids 275–330 ppm.

**Methods.** Bio-assays followed a modified [[Doudoroff Bio-Assay Method]]. Fish had a 24-hour pre-test hold. They were then exposed for 48 hours in recirculated water dosed with DDT dissolved in [[Number Two Fuel Oil]] and a hydrocarbon solvent. Post-test mortality was tracked for 30 days in 1957–1958 and six months in 1959. Statistics used [[Analysis of Variance]] with F-tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry effects.** Temperature, turbidity, and alkalinity all changed DDT toxicity in wild rainbow trout exposed to 1 ppm. Mean mortality was 2.1 at low temperature and 4.1 at high temperature. It was 4.7 at low turbidity and 1.5 at high turbidity, and 4.3 at low alkalinity and 1.8 at high alkalinity. Berck (1953) was cited for DDT adsorption onto sediment, which may explain the turbidity effect.

**DDT persistence in water.** Only a small fraction of added DDT stayed in solution. At 4 ppm added, 0.24 ppm was measured after 48 hours. At 5 ppm, 0.33 ppm was measured, and at 6 ppm, 0.6 ppm. In units dosed at 3 ppm, concentration fell from 0.56 ppm at one hour to 0.2 ppm at 48 hours, a 64% decrease.

**Test stream.** [[Trail Creek]] is a tributary of the Yellowstone River in Park County. The 8-mile study reach had no tributaries, an elevation of about 5,380 ft, and a gradient of 35 ft per mile. It lies in the transition between the upper Sonoran and Canadian life zones, has a severe winter, and floods each spring. Gravel was the dominant bottom type. Algae was the only important aquatic plant.

**Aquatic insects.** Immature aquatic insects made up 99.9% of bottom-organism volume. Orders present included Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Plecoptera, Coleoptera, and Diptera. Insects were the major trout food, and their loss altered the food web.

**Johnson's study area.** The upper [[Flathead River]] drainage in Flathead County and Glacier National Park has three major forks (North, South, and Middle) that rise on the western slopes of the [[Continental Divide]] at 8,000–10,000 ft. The drainage has steep, densely forested slopes. The main river flows 50 miles south through an agricultural valley to [[Flathead Lake]]. The area is one of the few remaining cutthroat trout fisheries in Montana.

## Key Findings

**DDT bio-assays (Schoenthal)**
1. Across roughly 6,000 cold-water fish tested in 1957–1959, rainbow trout mortality changed little between 0.5 and 10 ppm DDT.
2. Hatchery rainbow trout under 3.1 inches died at 100% at all concentrations. Mortality fell with size, to 66% for fish over 5.1 inches. Wild rainbow trout under three inches had 24% mortality. Hatchery trout showed about 50–75% higher mortality than wild trout.
3. Hatchery trout died 4–12 hours before wild trout of the same size. Larger fish survived longer, with first mortality at 10–18 hours for the smallest group and 18–22 hours before the largest.
4. Delayed mortality was high. Hatchery rainbow trout tested at 2 and 3 ppm showed 50% and 53% delayed mortality over 30 days, against 6% in controls.
5. Mortality rose with stress. Fish exposed to unfavorable conditions, held under constant swimming, or subjected to a sudden temperature drop of more than 4 °F died at higher rates. Disease was more common in treated fish, mainly furunculosis and fungal infections.
6. Feeding on DDT-killed aquatic insects raised mortality by 13–22%. For non-starved trout, mortality ranged from 17.6% to 35.2%. For starved trout it ranged from 39.8% to 83.6%, against control rates of 4.5% and 22.5%.
7. Exposure to algae containing DDT (6.8 ppm measured in vegetation) did not significantly increase mortality. Fish were exposed to 2 ppm DDT-treated algae for 48 hours and held for 200 days.
8. At 1 ppm for 48 hours, longnose sucker mortality was 94%. Rainbow trout had about 8% and other salmonids 0–2%. At six months, sucker mortality was 98%, and mountain whitefish, rainbow, and cutthroat trout ranged from 28% to 38%.
9. Tissue DDT was 4.0–19 ppm in dead trout and 5.5–23.7 ppm in survivors, so residues did not clearly separate survivors from victims.
10. Fuel oil at 9.9 ml and solvent at 0.6–3.3 ml did not cause significant mortality.

**Test stream results (Schoenthal)**
11. The aquatic insect population fell 99% within six days of application. One day after treatment, mounds of dead insects were found up to nine miles below the application point.
12. Trout populations in the first mile showed no significant change one year later. Below that point, cutthroat and brown trout fell 73% at the 1.0- and 2.0-mile stations.
13. Trout diet shifted from immature aquatic insects to adult insects and algae. Algae made up 49% of stomach volume at treated stations in autumn 1958.
14. Immature insect populations 0.75 miles or more downstream needed 11 months to regain 15% of pre-treatment numbers. Full recovery took about 17–18 months.
15. Repopulation came from both downstream drift and egg deposition. Diptera and Ephemeroptera returned first, followed by Trichoptera, Plecoptera, and Coleoptera.
16. Five dead trout were found in the first two weeks, and seven dead brown trout (11.0–12.1 inches) in October, a period when brown trout were spawning.
