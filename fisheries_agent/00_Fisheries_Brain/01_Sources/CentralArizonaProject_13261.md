# Central Arizona Project: Man's Quest For Water

#freshwater-ecology #water-resources #central-arizona-project

## Executive Summary

This document is a [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] public-information brochure describing the planned [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), a large water-importation scheme authorized by the [[Colorado River Basin Project Act]] (Public Law 90-537, September 30, 1968) but not yet constructed. The project would divert [[Colorado River]] water from [[Lake Havasu]] and convey it through pumping plants, aqueducts, and dams to agricultural and urban areas in Maricopa, Pinal, and Pima Counties, Arizona, and to parts of western New Mexico. The stated purpose is to reduce groundwater overdraft of more than two million acre-feet per year, which is causing water-level declines of 8 to 10 feet per year and land subsidence. The brochure gives a construction schedule (completion by 1980 if funds are appropriated on time) and a cost estimate of $832,180,000. It is a planning and promotional document, not a research study.

## Background & Description

**Historical and policy context**
- The opening passage notes that the [[Hohokam]] Indians established an extensive irrigation civilization in central Arizona before the time of Christ.
- Central Arizona's natural water supply has been out of balance with total demand for over three decades. The agricultural economy has "flourished and declined in direct relationship to the adequacy of water resources."
- The document asserts that imported water is needed "by the end of this century, or before" to "stave off water bankruptcy."

**Project components**
- **[[Havasu Diversion]] and [[Granite Reef Aqueduct]]:** An intake and high-lift pumping plant on the south shoreline of the Bill Williams River arm of Lake Havasu, about 2.5 miles upstream from Parker Dam. It lifts water over 800 feet into the 6.5-mile [[Buckskin Mountains Tunnel]]. The concrete-lined aqueduct runs about 190 miles to Orme Dam, with a maximum capacity of 3,000 cubic feet per second (cfs), four relift pumping stations (Bouse Hills, Little Harquahala Mountains, Belmont Mountains, and near the Hassayampa River), and a total pump lift of about 1,200 feet.
- **[[Orme Dam]] and Reservoir:** An earthfill multipurpose structure about 25 miles northeast of Phoenix, near the confluence of the Salt and Verde Rivers. It would provide terminal regulatory capacity, flood control, sediment control, conservation storage, and recreation.
- **[[Salt-Gila Aqueduct]]:** About 97 miles long, with a capacity of 1,800 cfs over its first 63 miles and 750 cfs over the last 34 miles. Two additional pumping plants lift water about 460 feet through the Picacho Mountains.
- **[[Buttes Dam]] and Reservoir:** On the Gila River in Pinal County, about four miles upstream from the Ashurst-Hayden Diversion Dam. It would improve regulation of San Carlos Project irrigation releases.
- **[[Tucson Aqueduct]] (Colorado Source):** A 20-mile concrete pipe aqueduct from near Marana, Arizona, with a capacity of 150 cfs and two pumping plants lifting water over 450 feet.
- **[[Charleston Dam]] and Reservoir:** On the San Pedro River in Cochise County, about 65 miles southeast of Tucson, primarily for municipal and industrial supply to Tucson.
- **Tucson Aqueduct (San Pedro Source):** A 70-mile concrete pipeline carrying about 12,000 acre-feet annually from Charleston Reservoir to Tucson, with a total pumplift of nearly 330 feet.
- **[[Hooker Dam]] and Reservoir:** On the Gila River in Grant County, New Mexico ("or a suitable alternative"). It would let New Mexico users increase consumptive use through exchange agreements with Arizona downstream users, mainly for domestic and mining-oriented industrial purposes.

**Power, distribution, and construction**
- **Power:** About 560 megawatts of capacity is required. Proposed thermal plants at Page and Four Corners would be fueled by coal from the Black Mesa and other deposits on the Hopi and Navajo Indian Reservations.
- **Distribution systems:** Congress authorized an additional $100 million for distribution and drainage systems to non-Indian lands, through the Distribution Systems Loan Act of 1955 (Public Law 84-130), the Small Reclamation Projects Act (Public Law 84-984), or direct Bureau of Reclamation design and supervision.
- **Construction:** About 10 years are required before water delivery. Materials include about 3.5 million cubic yards of concrete, 5 million barrels of cement, 240 million pounds of reinforcing and miscellaneous steel, and about 140 million cubic yards of earth movement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses aquatic and riparian resources only at a general level. It does not report any fish, invertebrate, plankton, or water-chemistry data.

- **Fish and wildlife:** Fish and wildlife conservation is listed among the project's benefits. Specific mentions include the reservoir recreation and fish and wildlife benefits of Buttes Dam and Hooker Dam, and fish hatcheries and wildlife refuge programs included in project costs.
- **[[Water Salvage]]:** Measures include utilization of [[Yuma Project]] return flow and [[Phreatophyte Control]] (clearing) along the lower Colorado River. Estimated yield is up to 320,000 acre-feet annually if current plans are fully implemented. The phreatophyte program would be coordinated with outdoor recreation and wildlife habitat and refuge programs on the lower Colorado River.
- **Groundwater:** Current annual overdraft exceeds two million acre-feet. Groundwater levels decline an average of 8 to 10 feet per year, with serious land subsidence in many areas.
- **Flow regulation and sediment:** Dams on the Salt, Gila, and San Pedro Rivers are intended to provide flood control, sediment retention, and regulated river flows.
- **Other:** The document mentions "Indian lands" and federally owned lands adjacent to Orme Reservoir, with recreation to follow an approved coordinated master plan.

## Key Findings

- **Water deficit:** Central Arizona has experienced a long-term supply-demand imbalance, sustained by groundwater overpumping. Annual overdraft exceeds 2 million acre-feet.
- **Groundwater decline:** Water levels fall an average of 8 to 10 feet per year, with land subsidence reported in many areas.
- **Authorization and status:** The CAP was authorized under Public Law 90-537 (September 30, 1968) but had not been constructed at the time of writing.
- **Beneficiaries:** Supplemental agricultural water for Maricopa, Pinal, and Pima Counties; municipal and industrial water for the Phoenix and Tucson metropolitan areas; and benefits to other water-deficient areas of Arizona and western New Mexico through water exchange.
- **Engineering scale:** The Granite Reef Aqueduct (about 190 miles, 3,000 cfs maximum, about 1,200 feet of total pump lift) and the Salt-Gila Aqueduct (about 97 miles) are the principal conveyances.
- **Schedule:** About 10 years of construction are needed, with completion targeted for 1980 if funding is appropriated on schedule.
- **Cost:** Estimated construction cost is $832,180,000. About 87 percent of construction costs would be repaid by direct beneficiaries. Flood protection and water salvage costs are nonreimbursable, as are Indian distribution system costs beyond repayment capacity.
- **Power requirement:** About 560 megawatts of capacity is needed, to be supplied by proposed thermal plants at Page and Four Corners.
- **Water salvage:** Up to 320,000 acre-feet annually could be recovered from Yuma Project return flows and phreatophyte clearing.
- **Stated benefits beyond water supply:** Flood control, outdoor recreation, fish and wildlife conservation, and sediment retention. These are described qualitatively; no quantitative fish or habitat data are given.
- **Data gaps:** The document contains no fish species, aquatic biota, water-quality, or habitat-survey data, and no environmental impact assessment results.
