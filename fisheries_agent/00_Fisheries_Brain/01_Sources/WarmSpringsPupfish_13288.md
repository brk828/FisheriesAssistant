# Warm Springs Pupfish Recovery Plan (Draft 1976)

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinodontidae #endangered-species #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This draft recovery plan for the [[Warm Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*) was prepared by an interagency recovery team in April 1976. The subspecies is one of six described forms of *C. nevadensis*, with two already extinct. *C. n. pectoralis* has the most localized distribution and smallest populations of the remaining subspecies, restricted to approximately seven spring systems within less than 1 km² near [[School Spring]] in [[Ash Meadows]], Nye County, Nevada. The plan outlines comprehensive recovery actions including land withdrawal, water rights protection, habitat management, population monitoring, vegetation control, and public education. Critical threats include groundwater pumping, emergent vegetation overgrowth in protective enclosures, and competition from exotic species like [[Mosquito Fish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).

## Background & Description

The [[Warm Springs Pupfish]] was originally described from Lovell's (School) Spring in 1948 by Miller. Subsequent surveys revealed populations in six additional spring flows within 1 km of [[School Spring]]: [[Indian Spring]], [[Marsh Spring]], [[Mexican Spring]] (extirpated 1973), and two [[Scruggs Springs]] locations. The subspecies is the smallest form of *C. nevadensis* and distinguished by high pectoral ray counts (predominantly 17) and strong tendency toward pelvic fin reduction or loss.

The species was declared endangered by the Federal Government in 1970 (Federal Register Vol. 35, No. 199) and by Nevada under General Regulation No. 1. All known populations occur in warm springs with relatively constant temperatures, supporting year-round reproduction from late January through October. Spring pool populations are extremely small, never exceeding 500 individuals maximum, with [[School Spring]] censuses ranging 136-231 fish (1972-1974) and [[Indian Spring]] showing 39-164 fish with seasonal doubling from winter minimum to late summer maximum.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Ash Meadows]] spring ecosystem represents a fragile desert aquatic habitat threatened by groundwater mining. The entire distribution of *C. n. pectoralis* occupies less than one square mile. Spring source pools constitute critical habitat where reproduction occurs; downstream habitats are marginal with little reproduction. Water temperatures in constant-temperature springs support extended spawning seasons (10 months), while fluctuating-temperature habitats show 3-4 month winter reproductive cessation.

Intraspecific competition for spawning territories and possibly food represents the primary natural limiting factor. Shallow shoreline habitat where reproductive territories occur is particularly vulnerable to water level decline. The [[Mexican Spring]] population extirpation in 1973 demonstrated unexpected consequences of protection: livestock exclosure led to increased emergent vegetation evapotranspiration, completely drying the smallest (350 liter) habitat during summer.

Parasitism rates are surprisingly high (~25% cestode metacercaria in liver). Avian predators including [[Belted Kingfisher]] (*Megaceryle alcyon*), introduced [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*), crayfish, and Odonata nymphs contribute to mortality, though bullfrog gut content analysis (n=7) revealed no pupfish remains. Exotic [[Platyfish]] (*Poecilia* sp.) were successfully eliminated from [[School Spring]].

## Key Findings

**Primary Recovery Objectives:**
1. Stop subspecies decline and maintain populations at secure levels
2. Maintain and improve quality/quantity of occupied habitats
3. Remove subspecies from endangered list

**Critical Threats Identified:**
1. Groundwater pumping lowering spring water levels, eliminating shallow reproductive habitat
2. Dense emergent vegetation growth in protective enclosures reducing available habitat through evapotranspiration
3. Competition from exotic fishes, particularly [[Mosquito Fish]] present in lower outlet streams

**Population Status (1971-1974):**
- [[School Spring]]: 136-231 individuals (November counts)
- [[Indian Spring]]: 39-164 individuals (seasonal variation)
- [[Marsh Spring]]: 32-80 individuals in spring pool; 2,000-3,000 in man-made reservoir (ephemeral habitat)
- [[Mexican Spring]]: maximum 47 individuals before 1973 extirpation

**Recovery Actions Outlined:**
1. **Land Security**: Public land withdrawal and private land acquisition within one mile of spring sources (approximately 2,240 acres in T.17S., R.50E. and T.18S., R.50E., M.D.B.&M.)
2. **Water Rights**: Procure existing rights and obtain certificates from State Engineer
3. **Habitat Management**: Remove exotic species, install barriers, control vegetation, document and supplement water flow
4. **Species Management**: Determine population quantity and taxonomic quality, conduct transplants as needed
5. **Research Priorities**: Investigate fish-vegetation-water relationships (FY 1976, $3,000); determine effects of emergent vegetation on spring ecosystems
6. **Public Education**: Site posting, maintain observation facility at [[School Spring]], low-key education program
7. **Enforcement**: Obtain and execute proper authority

**Critical Habitat Designation**: Seven spring systems and outflows within approximately one square mile near [[School Spring]], [[Ash Meadows]], containing space for territorial behavior, nutritional requirements, breeding sites, shelter, and water of proper temperature and chemical composition.

**Interagency Coordination**: Recovery team includes U.S. Bureau of Land Management (lead), Nevada Department of Fish and Game, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and University of Nevada Las Vegas. Estimated costs for priority actions range from $100-$3,000 per action item, with land acquisition costs requiring appraisal.
