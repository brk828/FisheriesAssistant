# Extent of Acid Mine Pollution in the United States Affecting Fish and Wildlife

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1964 [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] circular, prepared by Edward C. Kinney of the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], compiles State-reported data on waters in the United States where [[Acid Mine Drainage]] degrades fish and wildlife habitat. The work followed a series of 1963 inter-bureau Department of the Interior meetings. Data came from State Fish and Game Departments and pollution control agencies covering all 50 States.

The survey found 5,890 miles of streams and 14,967 acres of impoundments with potential fish and wildlife habitat if acid pollution were sufficiently reduced. About 97 percent of the stream pollution and 93 percent of the impoundment pollution came from [[Coal Mining]]. The report argues that abatement would provide sport and commercial fishing benefits, particularly in States with little public water area.

## Background & Description

The Bureau requested that each State report the following for affected waters:

- Watershed and water body name
- Type of coal or mineral and type of mining (surface, drift, shaft, slope)
- Stream length, average width and average flow, or lake surface acreage
- pH and total acidity (ppm as calcium carbonate equivalent)
- Potential for fish and wildlife if acid is reduced (High, Good, Moderate, None)

Twenty States reported acid mine pollution as a problem and 30 reported none. The 30 "no problem" States include Alabama, Alaska, Florida, Georgia, Michigan, New York and Texas. Several States reported incomplete data, and States with serious problems supplied more detail than the report could print.

Figures in the report document the problem, including a 1961 fish kill on the North Branch of the [[Susquehanna River]] near Sunbury, Pennsylvania. That kill was estimated at over one million fish, including 15,000 walleyes and 14,000 legal-size bass. Other figures show acid discharges of pH 2.8 to 4.0 from [[West Virginia]] mines and a restored native trout stream in West Virginia now at pH about 2.5.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes the chemistry and mining sources behind the impacts:

- **Acidity sources:** Most pollution came from bituminous coal (surface, drift, shaft and slope mines), gob piles and spoil. Copper, zinc, lead, barite, bauxite, phosphate and bog iron mining also contributed in some States.
- **Acidity levels:** Reported minimum pH values in polluted reaches ranged from about 2.0 to 6.0. Some streams had total acidity as high as 400 ppm.
- **Buffering:** Lightly buffered streams in the Tygart, Cheat and North Branch Potomac systems were especially vulnerable. In such waters, iron and phosphorus combine into insoluble iron phosphate, reducing productivity. This was noted for [[Black Hills]] streams in South Dakota.
- **Habitat change:** Some strip-mine ponds were alkaline and useful for wildlife, and the report notes that more aquatic habitat was created than destroyed in parts of Kansas. Acidic streams in Ohio, Tennessee and Pennsylvania were reported to be devoid of fish or nearly lifeless.
- **Reclamation:** Reclamation methods such as burying acid-producing material and restoring spoil contour were shown in figures. A former trout fishery in West Virginia was restored under a late-1930s WPA abatement program, but mining later resumed.

Pollution was concentrated in the [[Ohio River]] basin. Pennsylvania's Ohio River drainage affects were from bituminous mining, while its Susquehanna and Delaware basins were affected by anthracite mining.

## Key Findings

**Statewide extent**

- Total potential habitat: 5,890 miles of streams and 14,967 acres of impoundments.
- Pennsylvania: about 2,906 miles of streams and 10,100 acres of reservoirs, including the Conemaugh River Reservoir (6,820 acres) and Loyalhanna Reservoir (3,280 acres). Its streams include the Ohio River system (1,674 miles), Delaware system (201 miles) and Susquehanna system (1,031 miles).
- West Virginia: 1,150 miles of streams and 3,533 acres of impoundments, with total annual acid load over 10,000 tons. The state receives about 360 million gallons of acid mine water daily, per the report's figure caption.
- Kentucky: about 580 miles of streams, with no detailed pH data.
- Ohio: 392 miles of streams and 192 acres of lakes. About 114 miles have no fish potential even if acid is reduced. The Table 1 summary lists 278 miles for Ohio, a discrepancy in the source.
- Missouri: about 210 miles in 57 streams, mostly from surface coal mining, causing "serious fishery management problems."
- Illinois: about 222 miles of streams and 80 acres of backwater, plus 844 nonacid strip-mine ponds totaling 6,115 acres (Weber, 1962). Of about one-third of strip-mine waters surveyed, 14.2 percent had pH below 6.0.
- Montana: 48 miles of streams. In 1960 fish were killed over about 150 miles of the Clark Fork River.
- Smaller contributions were reported from California (54 stream miles and 1,000 acres of Shasta Lake, from copper and zinc mining), Maryland (83 miles, with 58 streams below pH 5.8 in 1962), Arkansas, Tennessee (over 125 miles of fish habitat destroyed), Indiana (58 miles of the Patoka River, barren of fish), Virginia (10 miles of the North Anna River) and others.

**Fishery and economic significance**

- Abatement would make at least 5,890 miles of streams and 15,000 acres of impoundments available for sport fishing. Commercial fishing on major waters such as the Ohio River would also improve.
- About 90 percent of polluted waters lie in States bordering the Ohio River. Illinois, Indiana, Kentucky, Ohio, Pennsylvania and West Virginia have 17.6 percent of the nation's licensed sport fishermen but only 5 percent of the water area.
- In 15 of the 20 affected States, inland public water per person is below the national average of 0.1947 acres.
- The six States with the heaviest pollution loads (Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Kentucky, Ohio, Illinois and Missouri) had about 4,646,000 fishermen. Their fishing license sales fell by 317,094 annual licenses between 1951-53 and 1961-63, unlike the national trend.
- Abatement would support angling for an additional 93,392 persons in those six States, or 1,718,412 extra fishing days at the national average of 18.4 days per year. Value to those States was estimated at $9,923,835, using average daily sport fishing spending of $5.775.
- Nationwide, restoration was estimated to provide about 2,000,000 recreation days annually, worth more than $11,500,000 in angler expenditures.
- The Outdoor Recreation Resources Review Commission (1962) projected sport fishermen would increase by 150 percent by 2000, and recommended pollution reduction as a key response.

**Conclusion**

The circular concludes that correcting acid mine pollution would restore fishing waters in areas with few fishing opportunities and high angler density, and would aid economically depressed regions. Its final position is that restoration of these historically important fisheries is especially urgent in the affected Ohio River States.
