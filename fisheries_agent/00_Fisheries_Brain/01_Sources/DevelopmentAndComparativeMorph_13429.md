# Development and Comparative Morphology of the Gonopodium of Goodeid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Goodeidae #freshwater-biology #cyprinodontiformes

## Executive Summary

This study by Turner, Mendoza, and Reifel examines the development and comparative anatomy of the **gonopodium** — a modified male anal fin used for internal fertilization — across representative species of the family [[Goodeidae]]. All goodeids are viviparous cyprinodont fishes restricted to the Mexican Plateau. The paper documents the ontogeny of anal fin ray differentiation in *[[Characodon eiseni]]* (likely belonging to *Xenotoca*) from birth to sexual maturity, and compares gonopodial structure across four subfamilies (Ataeniobiinae, Characodontinae, Goodeinae, Girardinichthyinae). The authors conclude that gonopodial specialization — involving suppression of ray bifurcation, growth retardation, and recurvature of rays 2–7 — arose independently and to varying degrees across lineages, under combined genetic and androgenic hormonal control. The work also proposes a zoogeographic model for the diversification of Goodeidae across the Mexican Plateau based on gonopodial morphology.

## Background & Description

- The **Goodeidae** are one of four families of cyprinodont fishes (along with [[Poeciliidae]] and [[Jenynsiidae]]) that have independently evolved viviparity or ovoviviparity, requiring internal fertilization via a modified anal fin (gonopodium) in males.
- Unlike the **Poeciliidae**, in which rays 3, 4, and 5 are elongated and specialized with hooks, spines, and fusions, **Goodeidae** show a distinct pattern: **rays 2 to 7** of the male anal fin are shortened relative to other rays, and in many species these rays remain **unbranched (unbifurcated)**, rather than elongated as in poeciliids.
- The study is based on:
  - Field-collected specimens from Mexico (fixed in formalin), supplemented by material from the Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan.
  - A breeding colony of *[[Characodon eiseni]]* (taxonomically uncertain; likely *Xenotoca*) maintained at Grinnell College, used for the ontogenetic series.
  - Fin rays were dissected, stained with alizarin, cleared in glycerine, and drawn via camera lucida.
- Study goals:
  1. Compare skeletal structure of the gonopodium across Goodeidae species.
  2. Document morphogenesis of the gonopodium in a single model species.
  3. Establish groundwork for future endocrinological studies of androgen-driven gonopodial development.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution**: Goodeidae are endemic to freshwater systems of the **Mexican Plateau** and adjacent lowland streams — a geographically confined and evolutionarily distinct radiation.
- **Zoogeographic hypothesis** (extending [[Turner 1946]]):
  - The subfamily **[[Girardinichthyinae]]** likely originated in lowland areas southwest of the plateau near **Colima**, with subsequent migration up/down the coast and onto the plateau.
  - *Ilyodon furcidens* and *Balsadichthys xantusi* retain the most **primitive gonopodial condition** (bifurcated rays 2–7, minimal shortening) and represent a non-migratory ancestral lowland stock; the two hybridize freely, supporting close relatedness.
  - *Xenotaenia* retained the primitive condition while migrating northward along the coast.
  - *Balsadichthys whitei* represents a transitional/intermediate specialization that migrated up the **Balsas River** toward Cuautla.
  - *Allodontichthys* represents a fully specialized lowland lineage (complete suppression of bifurcation), part of which migrated onto the plateau.
  - ***Ataeniobius toweri*** — restricted to the **Rio Verde** (Panuco River tributary) — shows the **most primitive gonopodial structure** in the family, consistent with earlier morphological evidence (absence of trophotaenia, presence of fin fold; Hubbs and Turner 1939) suggesting it is an early offshoot of the goodeid stock. Proximity of *Xenoophorus* (also primitive) in the Panuco system suggests this region as a possible secondary point of entry to the plateau.
  - ***Characodon lateralis*** is geographically anomalous: isolated in small lakes and headwaters of the **Rio Mesquital** near Durango, yet possesses one of the **most specialized gonopodia** in the family — suggesting extinction or isolation of intermediate ancestral forms, possibly in relict springs.
  - No consistent relationship exists between gonopodial specialization and geographic range size across the plateau interior (e.g., *Chapalichthys* is unspecialized but range-restricted; *Alloophorus* is unspecialized but widespread; *Skiffia lermae* is highly specialized but range-restricted; *Neotoca* is highly specialized yet widely distributed).
- **Endocrine context**: Gonopodial differentiation is hypothesized to be mediated by **endogenous androgenic hormones** secreted by the developing testis, analogous to well-established mechanisms in [[Poeciliidae]] (*Xiphophorus*, *Platypoecilus*, *Gambusia*, *Lebistes*, *Mollienisia*) and [[Jenynsiidae]] (*Jenynsia lineata*), though direct experimental confirmation in Goodeidae was not yet available at time of writing.

## Key Findings

### Morphogenesis of the Anal Fin in *Characodon eiseni*
- **At birth**: 15 rays present (rarely 16); ray 1 and the last ray are shorter than others; **no sexual dimorphism** evident.
- **60 days post-birth**: No difference in ray *length* between sexes, but a key divergence in **bifurcation**:
  - Males: all rays single/unbranched.
  - Females: rays 3–14 bifurcated (branches with 1–3 segments).
- **78 days post-birth**: In males, bifurcation suppression becomes localized to **rays 2–7**; rays 8–13 become bifurcated and follow female-like development thereafter. Rays 2, 3, 4 show slight elongation retardation; rays 5–7 elongate more than 2–4 but less than female counterparts.
- **98 days post-birth**: Retardation of the **2–7 ray complex** becomes pronounced in males; remainder of fin continues to elongate/bifurcate as in females.
- **Maturity (male ~55 mm SL)**: Rays of the 2–7 (extending to include ray 8) complex are roughly equal in length, more than double their 60-day length, but still much shorter than rays 9–14 — indicating **partial, not total,** growth retardation.
- **Females**: Show progressive ray elongation, terminal segment addition, and secondary/tertiary branching with age; rays 4–7 become longest; fin margin rounded; progressive **ankylosis (fusion)** of basal ray segments, most rapid in rays 6 and 7.
- **Secondary sexual traits**: Males show greater ray crowding, thinner/less-fused rays in the 2–7 complex (retaining flexibility), and more pronounced **recurvature** compared to females.

### Comparative Structure Across Subfamilies (Table I synthesis)
Three major axes of male specialization identified:
1. **Prevention of bifurcation** in rays 2–7.
2. **Retardation of growth** in rays 2–7 (extending to ray 8 with age).
3. **Recurvature** of rays 2–7.
Minor secondary traits: ray diameter changes, ray crowding, and basal segment ankylosis.

- **Ataeniobiinae** (*[[Ataeniobius toweri]]*): Most primitive — only ray 2 unbifurcated; considered basal to the family.
- **Characodontinae** (*[[Characodon lateralis]]*): Most specialized — **no rays in the 2–7 complex bifurcated**; strong recurvature and crowding.
- **Goodeinae** — divided by trophotaeniae type:
  - *Rosette-type trophotaeniae* group (*Neoophorus diazi*, *Goodea luitpoldii*, *G. atripinnis*, *G. gracilis*, *Allotoca dugesii*): rays 3–7 bifurcated in most species except *Allotoca dugesii* (fully suppressed, considered most specialized in this group).
  - *Ribbon-type trophotaeniae* group (*Xenoophorus exsul*, *Alloophorus robustus*, *Chapalichthys encaustus*, *Xenotoca variata*, *Zoogoneticus cuitzeoensis*, *Characodon eiseni*): a gradient from fully bifurcated (*Xenoophorus*, *Alloophorus* — primitive) through partial (*Chapalichthys*, rays 4–6 branched) to fully suppressed (*Xenotoca*, *Zoogoneticus*, *C. eiseni*).
- **[[Girardinichthyinae]]**: Two distinct groups:
  - Fully suppressed bifurcation: *Allodontichthys*, *Skiffia*, *Neotoca*, *Girardinichthys*, *Lermichthys*.
  - Fully bifurcated (primitive): *Balsadichthys xantusi*, *Xenotaenia resolanae*.
  - Intermediate: *Balsadichthys whitei* (bifurcation only in rays 6–7).

### Species-Level Highlights
- **Least specialized** (in both bifurcation and retardation): *Ataeniobius toweri*, *Neoophorus diazi*, *Alloophorus robustus*.
- **Most specialized** (in both traits): *Characodon lateralis*, *Neotoca bilineata*, *Lermichthys multiradiatus*.
- **Mixed specialization**: *Goodea luitpoldii* — growth retarded but bifurcation not prevented.
- **Extreme elongation without bifurcation**: *Skiffia lermae*, *Xenotoca variata*, *Characodon eiseni* — rays continue to elongate/segment (doubling length, adding up to 7 new segments) without branching.
- **Recurvature**: Most extreme in *Neotoca*, *Characodon lateralis*, and *Skiffia lermae*; generally accompanied by reduced ray diameter, except where single enlarged rays (e.g., ray 2 in *Skiffia lermae*) serve as muscle attachment points.

### Methodological Contribution
- Established a standardized comparative reference point — the **bifurcation point of ray 8** — allowing cross-species measurement of retardation/elongation in the 2–7 ray complex relative to a fixed developmental landmark.
- Identified **two distinct hormonally-responsive developmental fields**:
  1. **"Prevention of bifurcation" field** — acts earliest in development (in *C. eiseni*, affecting rays 3–7 simultaneously, no anteroposterior gradient).
  2. **"Retardation of growth" field** — acts later (from ~78 days in *C. eiseni*), initially affecting rays 2–4 most strongly, with a differential susceptibility gradient across rays 2–8.

### Endocrinological Interpretation
- Although direct hormone-manipulation experiments in Goodeidae were not yet conducted at time of publication, the authors infer — by analogy with [[Poeciliidae]] and [[Jenynsiidae]] — that **endogenous androgenic hormones** from the developing testis drive gonopodial differentiation, acting on genetically determined "target areas" (bifurcation and retardation fields) within the anal fin.
- Species-level morphological differences are attributed to a combination of:
  - Genetic differences in the sensitivity/extent of target areas.
  - Variation in androgen concentration or potency during development.
