# Studies on the Evolution of Box Turtles (Genus Terrapene)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #emydidae #terrapene

## Executive Summary

This monograph by [[William W. Milstead]] (Bulletin of the Florida State Museum, Biological Sciences, Vol. 14, No. 1, 1969) reconstructs the evolution of the North American box turtle genus [[Terrapene]] using osteological, shell and colour characters from at least 2,050 adult specimens grouped into 87 samples. The author divides the genus into a [[Carolina Group]] and an [[Ornata Group]], interprets subspecies as "almost species" units linked by gene flow, and proposes a Pleistocene history driven by sea-level and climate shifts. Several hypotheses (e.g., the origin of [[Terrapene Carolina Bauri]] and the ancestry of [[Terrapene Coahuila]]) are explicitly labelled speculative. The paper also tests and rejects a convergence-versus-relationship question between Terrapene and the Asian box turtle genus [[Cuora]].

## Background & Description

The study began at a 1959 meeting between Milstead, [[Walter Auffenberg]] and [[Donald Tinkle]], comparing Texas and Florida fossils. The initial phase examined extreme subspecies populations; the focus was on plastral osteology because plastral elements are more often preserved as fossils and are less variable than carapacial ones. Specimens over 99 mm carapace length were used to limit ontogenetic bias. Milstead visited most of the collections listed in the acknowledgments, and the work was supported by the [[National Science Foundation]] and a Guggenheim Fellowship.

Terrapene is diagnosed as a predominantly terrestrial, omnivorous emydine genus with a plastral hinge, a frontal entering the orbit and a large caroticopharyngeal foramen. It is distributed east of the cordilleras, with T. nelsoni the only western species. The 16 diagnostic characters are grouped by ease of use:

- Characters 1-3 (single specimens): postorbital bar, inner-toe mobility, highest point of carapace.
- Characters 4-11 (single specimens or small series): first central scute angle, plastral margin, first marginal shape, carapace elongation and vaulting, axillary scale, and related traits.
- Characters 12-16 (good series required): interhumeral and interfemoral seam lengths, toe number, plastral hinge position, and keel prominence.

Key diagnostic traits include the postorbital bar (present in the Carolina Group, absent in the Ornata Group) and the first central scute angle (steep, 50° or more, in the Carolina Group; low, 45° or less, in the Ornata Group).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Carolina Group** is forest-inhabiting. [[Terrapene Carolina Carolina]] occupies northeastern deciduous forests of the Carolinian biotic province and is presumed to be the most geographically stable member through the Pleistocene. [[Terrapene Carolina Major]] occupies palmetto-pine forests and coastal marshes on the northern Gulf coast.
- **Ornata Group** members are savannah and grassland inhabitants. [[Terrapene Ornata Ornata]] lives on the Great Plains, [[Terrapene Ornata Luteola]] in arid southwestern grasslands, and [[Terrapene Nelsoni]] in oak-savannah foothills of the Sierra Madre Occidental.
- **Terrapene coahuila** is the only aquatic or semi-aquatic member, known only from springs near Cuatro Cienegas, Coahuila. The author notes the habitat is very small, threatened by climate and agriculture, and in need of conservation plans.
- Pleistocene dynamics: high sea levels fragmented Florida into islands, and glacial low stands reconnected coastal plains. Gulf "circumferential corridor" movements, humid and arid phases, and the [[Rocky Mountain Corridor]] shaped range shifts and isolation.
- Fire-marked colour patterns in major, yucatana and mexicana are linked by the author to fire-prone habitats, though this is treated as uncertain.

## Key Findings

1. **Morphological variation:** No single character identifies a box turtle series. Multiple characters are needed, and each subspecies can be distinguished by one or more plastral ratios.
2. **Species concept:** The author defines species as reproductively distinct groups, and treats isolation in space or time alone as insufficient for species recognition. He argues subspecies are "almost species" connected by gene flow.
3. **Taxonomic proposal:** The genus is split into two species groups. The Carolina Group contains [[Terrapene Carolina]] (with seven subspecies including the extinct *T. c. putnami*) and [[Terrapene Coahuila]]. The Ornata Group contains [[Terrapene Ornata]] (three subspecies, including the extinct *T. o. longinsulae*) and [[Terrapene Nelsoni]] (two subspecies: *T. n. nelsoni* and *T. n. klauberi*).
4. **Stratigraphic record:** The oldest known Terrapene fossils are middle Pliocene. *T. o. longinsulae* (USNM 5983, Long Island, Kansas) is the oldest known representative of the genus. *T. c. putnami* is the earliest Carolina Group record, from middle Pliocene Florida deposits.
5. **Origin:** The Ornata Group is hypothesised to have evolved from the Carolina Group, with a common ancestor of medium size (130-150 mm carapace length), relatively flat, round, with a rounded plastral margin and four hind toes.
6. **Convergence with Cuora:** Terrapene and the Asian genus [[Cuora]] share a similar phenotype, but examination of 86 Cuora and Terrapene skeletons showed the axial skeletal traits to be stable and distinct. The author concludes the resemblance is convergent, and that the hinged plastron arose independently in several terrestrial turtle groups.
7. **Relationships with other emydines:** Terrapene and [[Emys]] appear to share a common ancestor that split from [[Clemmys]]. The resemblance between Terrapene and [[Emydoidea]] is considered convergent.
8. **Subspecies and intergrades:** Intergrades are designated with "x" (horizontal) and "x*" (vertical). Examples include *T. c. putnami x triunguis* and the proposed *T. c. bauri x major* populations. The author rejects tetranomial names except for two suggested cases.
9. **Body size and climate:** Northern populations of [[Terrapene Carolina Triunguis]] and *T. c. carolina* are larger, consistent with Bergmann's rule. A captive pen experiment (1962-63 and 1965-66, University of Missouri-Kansas City) suggested larger Florida turtles survived winters and smaller local turtles recovered first on warm days. The 1962-63 test was inconclusive because turtles stayed underground in leaf litter.
10. **Fossil sites:** The Ingleside (50-80 thousand years B.P.) and Friesenhahn Cave (10-14 thousand years B.P.) assemblages from Texas were compared. Friesenhahn's shape and plastral ratios closely match modern triunguis, with differences judged below the taxonomic level. Ingleside turtles were identified as *T. c. putnami x triunguis*.
11. **Coahuila origin:** Milstead proposes that *T. coahuila* descended from *T. c. putnami x triunguis* and became semi-aquatic after isolation in the Cuatro Cienegas bolson, though he notes it is a clumsy swimmer and has buoyancy problems. He argues its flat shell is a secondary adaptation.
12. **Sample scale:** A total of at least 2,050 adult specimens were examined in 87 samples. Several hundred others were discarded for failing sampling criteria.
