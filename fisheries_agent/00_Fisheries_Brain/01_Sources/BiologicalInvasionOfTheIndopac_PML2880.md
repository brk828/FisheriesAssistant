# Indo-Pacific Lionfish Invasion of the Atlantic Coast of North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scorpaenidae

## Executive Summary

This paper documents the occurrence of the [[Indo-Pacific Lionfish]] (*Pterois volitans*) along the Atlantic coast of North America and argues that it represents the first apparently successful introduction of a marine fish from the western Pacific to Atlantic coastal waters of the United States. Adults were collected or observed off North Carolina, Georgia and Florida, and juveniles were collected on Long Island, New York. Lionfish were also reported around Bermuda. The authors conclude that the species is a human-mediated introduction, most likely through aquarium releases, and that adults are surviving along the southeastern US continental shelf. Because the species' biology is poorly documented, the authors say predicting its ecological effects is difficult.

## Background & Description

**Biological invasions.** A biological invasion involves the arrival, survival, reproduction and dispersal of a species in an ecosystem where it did not previously occur ([[Carlton 1989]]). Invasions can result from natural range extensions or human-mediated introductions. Introductions of nonindigenous marine species have become more frequent, raising concern about their impacts on marine ecosystems ([[Cohen and Carlton 1998]]; [[Ruiz et al. 2000]]).

**Marine fish introductions are unusual.** The paper highlights several contrasts with other marine introductions:
- Marine fish introductions are rare. Of more than 200 established exotic species in the [[San Francisco Bay]] watershed, only 6 are marine or estuarine fishes. Of more than 550 fish introductions reported in the United States, fewer than 30 were marine fishes introduced to marine environments ([[Fuller et al. 1999]]).
- Most marine fish introductions result from purposeful releases for fishery development or aquarium release, rather than from [[Ballast Water]] transport.
- Success (reproduction and dispersal) of most marine fish introductions is often well documented, unlike many other aquatic introductions.

**Species description and identification.** The subfamily Pteroinae (Scorpaenidae) contains 5 genera, distinguished by features such as unbranched fin rays, dorsal-fin length, and the presence or absence of a fin spot. *P. volitans* and the closely related [[Pterois Miles]] are the two Indo-Pacific species of *Pterois* that are difficult to separate. They differ in dorsal and anal fin-ray counts, pectoral-fin length and dorsal-fin spot size. All lionfish specimens collected in this study were identified as *P. volitans*. Other photographed fish were assumed to be *P. volitans*, although some could have been *P. miles*.

**Native range.** *P. volitans* occurs from southern Japan south to the east coast of Australia (including Lord Howe Island), throughout Indonesia, Micronesia and Polynesia. *P. miles* occurs in the Indian Ocean and the Red Sea. Both species are associated with rock and coral substrate from the surface to 50 m, and many records come from harbor areas. Both species have venomous dorsal, anal and pelvic spines and are popular in the aquarium trade.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional setting.** The southeastern US continental shelf (Cape Hatteras to southern Florida) has relatively warm winter minimum water temperatures, while the northeastern shelf (Gulf of Maine) is cold-temperate. The southeastern shelf is undergoing change: reef fish communities are becoming more tropical and many fish species are overfished ([[Parker and Dixon]]). Native fish communities on the northeastern shelf are cold-temperate, and the northeast coast is considered too cold to support established lionfish populations.

**Temperature and survival.** Temperature is considered an important factor for lionfish survival, reproduction and dispersal. The authors report that observations over 2 years suggest individuals have survived winter temperatures along the southeastern US coast. Winter temperatures on the North Carolina shelf are somewhat lower than those off Lord Howe Island, which the authors treat as a comparable native-range sample. Bermuda, Lord Howe Island and coastal stations were used as temperature references. The authors suggest that temperatures on the North Carolina shelf may be near the southern limit of the species' range in the Northern Hemisphere.

**Larval transport and dispersal.** Larval duration is reported as 25 to 40 days, with settlement at about 10 to 12 mm. Larval transport pathways from the western Atlantic shelf and the Gulf Stream could allow lionfish to reach the southeastern and northeastern US coast. Larval development rates were compared with other scorpaenids, which develop at about 0.3 mm per day.

**Predation and diet.** Lionfish use a modified ambush style of predation. Their venomous spines and the apparent lack of predators are considered important for establishment. Few predators of lionfish are known from their native range, and southeastern US predators have no experience with venomous lionfish. Lionfish diet, dietary preferences and potential competitors in the introduced range are unknown.

**Potential ecosystem effects.** Introduced species can have deleterious or minimal effects on invaded ecosystems. Lionfish in the native range prey on a wide variety of fishes, shrimps and crabs. The authors note that lionfish could affect important reef fishes in the southeastern United States, but the impacts on prey populations and on native fishes are not known. Effects of introduced [[Marine Fish]] have been poorly studied, and the authors compare possible consequences with [[Great Lakes Invasions]], where [[Round Goby]] and [[Ruffe]] contributed to declines of native fishes.

## Key Findings

**Observations and collections**
- Nineteen substantiated observations and one specimen collection were made on the North Carolina continental shelf over one year, across multiple sites. The paper's text reads "8 different locations", which is partly unclear from OCR. Multiple lionfish were seen on two occasions. All North Carolina fish observed were adults, and the specimen collected was a female.
- An adult male was collected off Georgia in summer 2001, approximately 500 km southwest of the North Carolina sites. Two more were observed there.
- An adult was collected off St. Augustine, Florida. Multiple lionfish were also observed at the time of collection.
- Two juveniles were collected on wood pilings along the south coast of Long Island, New York, in summer 2001, about 800 km north-northeast of the North Carolina sites.
- In Bermuda, a juvenile was collected in late summer 2000 from a tide pool at Devonshire Bay. It was kept in a private aquarium until April 2001, when it was displayed at the Agricultural Exhibition. Photographs were taken of several Bermuda fish.
- Five specimens were examined: adults from North Carolina and Florida and two juveniles from New York were positively identified as *P. volitans*.

**Establishment and survival**
- The number and spatial distribution of observations, including adults seen repeatedly in North Carolina (2000 and 2001) and Georgia (2001 and 2002), suggest that adults are surviving along the southeastern US coast.
- The authors conclude that lionfish are likely established along the southeastern United States and that Bermuda may also support survival.

**Introduction pathway**
- The most likely pathway is aquarium release. The species is a popular aquarium fish, and an aquarium release in Florida in 1992 is cited as one example.
- Ballast water is a less likely pathway. Although 2 individual scorpaenids were reported from ballast water in other studies, no successful scorpaenid introductions from ballast water have been documented.
- Natural range expansion via the Panama Canal or through Atlantic currents is considered unlikely, because of the species' biogeographic distance from the Atlantic and the time required for transport.

**Management and future work**
- The authors recommend documenting the spatial extent of the introduction, including in the native range, and measuring bottom water temperatures over a large area during 2001 to 2002 and beyond.
- Genetic analyses of collected specimens, population-size estimates, reproductive state and dispersal estimates are recommended to predict future spread.
- Work on the diet, prey impacts and ecological interactions of lionfish is needed to assess effects on native reef ecosystems, which the authors consider potentially serious but not yet quantified.

**Conclusion**
The occurrence of *Pterois volitans* on the Atlantic coast of North America represents a human-mediated introduction that appears to have established. Predicting future ecological effects is difficult because of limited biological data, changing southeastern shelf communities, and the species' venomous defences and lack of predators.
