# Preliminary Fish Fauna Survey of Mill Creek, Riley County, Kansas (1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #stream-survey

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report, prepared by Tom Eady, Ward Faulkner, Jerry Kerbs, and Art Metcalf of the Fisheries Management Class at Kansas State College, documents a preliminary seine survey of [[Mill Creek]] in Riley County, Kansas, carried out between March 21 and early April 1956. The survey sampled six stations along the creek, plus a supplementary seining of the mouth of the creek in the [[Big Blue River]]. Fish were collected with a minnow seine. The creek's lower reaches were dry or nearly dry and produced no animal life, while upstream stations yielded about twelve fish species. The authors also assessed erosion and siltation and offered management recommendations, including [[Deflectors]], terracing, strip cropping, and willow planting.

## Background & Description

**Study objective and methods.** The survey was a class project of the [[Fisheries Management]] program at Kansas State College. Stations were chosen to represent the range of habitats along the stream, and the authors also considered possible stream improvement through management techniques. All specimens were collected with a [[Minnow Seine]].

**Location and access.** Mill Creek lies about 8 to 4 miles north of Manhattan, Kansas, reached via Kansas State Highway 13 and a gravel road west from Stockdale. The creek empties into the Blue River about one mile southeast of the Manhattan city limits. Automobile access ends about seven miles upstream at a fork of two small streams.

**Physical setting.** The creek originates in the [[Flint Hills]] at roughly 1,250 feet above sea level and is about eight miles long from origin to mouth. The valley floor is fertile loam, mostly planted to small grains with some row crops. The creek follows the south side of the valley, against hills covered with trees and grass. Elevation drop is 190 feet in the small tributary streams and 80 feet from the valley to the Blue River confluence.

**Channel conditions.** The stream bed varies among silt eroded from farmland, loam, clay, loose sand, rock, and bedrock. Pools range from about 10 to 50 feet long, 0.5 to 4 feet deep, and 5 to 30 feet wide, with a few deeper pools up to 4 feet deep, 50 to 100 feet long, and 8 to 10 feet wide. Siltation increases downstream and is severe at the mouth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat gradient.** The six stations form a sequence from the floodplain near the mouth to the rolling upland near the headwaters:

- **Stations 1 and 2 (floodplain, lower creek):** Station 1 at the mouth was dry, with a mud-bottomed pool, decaying leaves, and no flow. It yielded no fish or other animals. Station 2 was a rock-bottomed pool under a beaver dam, with 4 to 8 inches of mud over rock.
- **Stations 3 and 4 (transition to hills):** These stations had gravel or rock bottoms, abundant shade from elm, ash, and sycamore, algae, and some flowing water. Station 3 included a crayfish and a few insects such as *Notonecta*.
- **Station 5 (hillside foot):** A series of five muddy, deep pools with root snags and drop-offs, bordered by woody vegetation. Runoff from a hog and cattle lot entered above the station.
- **Station 6 (upland headwater):** A brisk, spring-fed reach with clear pools, a gravel or rock bottom, and crayfish, the most abundant of any station, plus a tadpole.

**Environmental threats.** The authors documented widespread erosion from the uplands, bank cutting into cultivated fields, and sediment filling the channel. Pools were shallow enough that they would likely winter-kill, and siltation threatened to fill any dam.

**Blue River comparison.** Supplementary seining at the creek mouth in the Blue River sampled three areas: a strong-current bank (Area A), a riffle (Area B), and a still backwater (Area C). Area C was the most productive.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna of Mill Creek.** About 12 fish species were recorded across the stations. The most frequently listed species were:

- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*): the most widespread species, present at every station except station 3 in the report's reading. The authors noted it was common to both Mill Creek and the Blue River, which they interpreted as evidence of broad environmental tolerance.
- [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus*): found at most stations, with a decline from station 3 upstream.
- [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*): rare except at station 6.
- [[Central Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*): increasingly common from station 3 upward.
- [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Pimephales notatus*): not common.
- Sunfishes [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] (*Lepomis humilis*) and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*): the orangespotted sunfish seemed more characteristic of downstream sections.
- Darters [[Johnny Darter]] (*Boleosoma nigrum*) and [[Orangethroat Darter]] (*Poecilichthys spectabilis*): sporadic, apparently linked to particular pool conditions, and both increased at station 6.
- [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*): few specimens, which the authors attributed partly to the sampling gear.
- [[River Carpsucker]] (*Carpiodes carpio*): a single specimen at station 2, though common in the Blue River.
- [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*): recorded in the Mill Creek collections.

**Blue River species.** Common species there included *Carpiodes carpio*, *Noturus flavus*, *Pimephales promelas*, *Notropis lutrensis*, *N. deliciosus*, *Phenacobius mirabilis*, and *Lepomis humilis*. Only *P. promelas* was common to both streams.

**Station 1.** No fish, insects, crustaceans, mollusks, or aquatic plants were found after two seines in the lower pool and five in the upper pool.

**Distribution patterns.** The authors stated that the scope of the survey did not allow firm conclusions, but noted tendencies: *C. carpio* appeared limited to the creek mouth, while *P. promelas* was absent only at station 3, a clear, shallow, rock-bottomed reach.

**Recommendations.**

- Build terraces on sloping and hilly land to reduce soil erosion from the watershed.
- Use strip cropping in the valley and on slopes to keep fertile soil on the land.
- Install [[Deflectors]] as the most practical stream improvement, placed on the north bank to divert current from eroding banks and to scour deeper pools.
- Plant willows along the banks to reduce erosion.
- Do not stock fish in the current shallow pools, because they would winter-kill and siltation would soon fill any dam.

**Conclusion.** The authors concluded that the preliminary survey identified the fish fauna and its distribution, and that erosion and siltation were the principal problems. Deflectors, terracing, and bank vegetation were proposed as the main management tools.
