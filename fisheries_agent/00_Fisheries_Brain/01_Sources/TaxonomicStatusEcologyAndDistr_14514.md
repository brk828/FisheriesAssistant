# Taxonomic Status, Ecology, and Distribution of the Headwater Catfish, Ictalurus lupus

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #ictaluridae #catfish #taxonomy #conservation

## Executive Summary

This final report (Contract No. 14-16-98@82-81-219) by [[Julian M. Humphries]] and [[Robert Rush Miller]] from the [[Museum of Zoology]], [[University of Michigan]], establishes the taxonomic validity of [[Ictalurus lupus]] (headwater catfish) as a distinct species from the closely related [[Ictalurus punctatus]] (channel catfish). Using multivariate morphometric and meristic analyses, the authors document the extensive historical and current distribution of I. lupus across the [[Rio Grande Basin]], [[Pecos River System]], and multiple Mexican river systems. The study identifies diagnostic characters including vertebral counts (44-48 vs. 49-58 in punctatus), anal fin-ray counts (22-27 vs. 26-31), and morphometric ratios. Field surveys in 1982 located healthy populations primarily in the Pecos River system, though habitat degradation from reservoir construction, low-water bridges, and irrigation diversion poses ongoing threats. Evidence of hybridization with I. punctatus was detected in the [[Devils River]] and lower [[Pecos River]]. The report recommends against listing I. lupus as endangered or threatened at present, but emphasizes the need to prevent stocking of I. punctatus into lupus habitat and to monitor populations, particularly the distinctive [[Rio Conchos]] population.

## Background & Description

[[Ictalurus lupus]] was originally described by [[Charles Girard]] in 1859 from the U.S.-Mexican Boundary region. Girard's description was cursory, noting primarily a low anal-ray count, and subsequent literature provided little additional diagnostic information. Early workers including Günther (1864), Jordan (1877), Jordan and Gilbert (1883), and Evermann and Kendall (1894) listed lupus but examined no new material. [[Meek]] (1904) first documented lupus from the [[Rio San Fernando]] and [[Soto la Marina]] systems in Mexico. Some researchers (Hubbs 1976, Robinson 1959) did not recognize lupus as distinct, considering it an ecological variant of [[Ictalurus punctatus]].

The only substantive morphological work prior to this study was [[Lundberg]]'s (1979) unpublished dissertation, which unfortunately relied on skeletal material from [[Cuatro Cienegas]], a population that may not be typical of the species.

**Diagnostic Characters**: Table 2 summarizes key differences between I. lupus and I. punctatus:
- **Anal fin-rays**: 22-27 (modally 24-25) in lupus vs. 26-31 (modally 28-29) in punctatus
- **Vertebrae**: 44-48 (modally 46-47) in lupus vs. 48-56 (modally 49-58) in punctatus
- **Pectoral spine length**: Generally <20% SL in lupus vs. >20% SL in punctatus (specimens 160-259 mm SL)
- **Cleithral process**: Short with rugose, shallow grooves in lupus vs. long with pronounced longitudinal grooves in punctatus
- **Supraoccipital spine**: Falls short of supraneural in lupus vs. fully meeting supraneural in punctatus

The morphometric system employed 30+ distance measurements providing complete areal coverage of body form, departing from traditional [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1947) standards to better capture shape variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Preferences**: Contrary to expectations based on the common name "headwater catfish," I. lupus occupies diverse habitats including:
- Swift-flowing riffle habitats in spring-fed tributaries (e.g., headwaters of [[Rio San Juan]])
- Main channel of the [[Rio Grande]] (sympatric with I. punctatus)
- Spring systems (e.g., [[San Solomon Springs]] in [[Toyah Creek]], [[Delaware Creek]], [[Balmorhea State Park]])

In the upper Rio San Juan, lupus were collected during daytime occupying niches similar to madtoms (genus [[Noturus]]) in eastern North America.

**Threats to Habitat**:
1. **Reservoir construction**: Creates favorable habitat for I. punctatus; [[Amistad Reservoir]] inundated lower [[Devils River]]
2. **Low-water bridges**: Concrete berms with limited drainage pipes create upstream pools and migration barriers for fishes
3. **Irrigation diversion**: Reduces available flowing water
4. **Stocking of I. punctatus**: Introduces competitor/hybridization threat into lupus habitat

**Hybridization**: Evidence of introgression between I. lupus and I. punctatus was detected in:
- [[Devils River]] (specimens E, F, H showed intermediate or punctatus-like characteristics)
- Lower [[Pecos River]] near [[Independence Creek]] (specimens A-D)
- Confirmed independently by electrophoretic studies (Kelsch et al., Yates et al., 1982)

**Geographic Variation**: [[Principal Components Analysis]] revealed significant phenetic differences in populations from:
- [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]]: Morphometrically similar to I. punctatus but meristically similar to other lupus populations
- Upper [[Rio Conchos]]: Most distinctive lupus population with shortest pectoral spine and pectoral-to-orbit distance; may warrant separate taxonomic status

Vertebral counts show geographic pattern (Table 1, Figure 8): upstream Rio Grande basin populations have lower counts (mean 45.3-45.9) while lower tributaries and coastal drainages have higher counts (mean 46.4-46.9).

## Key Findings

**1. Taxonomic Validity Confirmed**: [[Ictalurus lupus]] is a valid taxon distinguished from [[Ictalurus punctatus]] by multiple meristic, morphometric, and osteological characters. Multivariate analyses (PCA) using morphometric and meristic data produced nearly complete discrimination between species when problematic populations (Cuatro Cienegas, Upper Conchos) were included in analyses.

**2. Distribution More Extensive Than Previously Reported** (Figure 9):
- **United States**: All but extreme headwaters of [[Pecos River]]; [[Devils River]]; scattered populations in [[Rio Grande]] and tributaries; rare in [[Guadalupe River]], [[San Marcos River]], and [[Nueces River]] (most previous reports not well founded)
- **Mexico**: [[Rio Conchos]], [[Rio Salado]], [[Rio San Juan]], [[Rio San Fernando]], [[Soto la Marina]] river systems
- Blank areas on distribution map (e.g., lower Conchos) likely contain populations but have not been adequately surveyed

**3. Population Status Assessment** (1982 field expedition):
- **Healthy populations located**: [[San Solomon Springs]]/[[Toyah Creek]] (thousands of individuals), [[Delaware Creek]] (Texas and New Mexico), [[Black River]] (New Mexico)
- **San Antonio and Nueces drainages**: No lupus located; species apparently always rare in these systems
- **Mexico populations**: More stable than U.S. populations; not currently susceptible to extirpation
- **Access issues**: Political conflicts between landowners and state/federal officials prevented sampling on private land along Devils River

**4. Intraspecific Variation**:
- **Cuatro Cienegas population**: Morphometrically distinctive (similar to punctatus) but meristically typical of lupus; isolated endemic basin
- **Upper Rio Conchos population**: Most distinctive lupus population; consistently different on morphometric and meristic analyses; may represent distinct taxon requiring further study
- **Vertebral count variation**: Geographic cline from upstream (lower counts) to downstream/coastal drainages (higher counts); range 44-48 vertebrae

**5. Hybridization Evidence**:
- Morphological evidence of introgression in [[Devils River]] and lower [[Pecos River]]
- Electrophoretic studies (Kelsch et al., Yates et al., 1982) independently confirmed genetic intergradation in Devils River and upper Pecos
- Likely source: [[Amistad Reservoir]] stocking of I. punctatus

**6. Conservation Recommendations**:
- **Do NOT list as endangered or threatened** at present; distribution and population levels sufficient to prevent imminent extinction
- **Critical management action**: Avoid transplanting I. punctatus into lupus habitat
- **Notify state fish and game departments** of lupus existence and distribution
- **Monitor Devils River population** undergoing introgression
- **Modify low-water bridge construction** to reduce pooling and migration barriers
- **Further study needed**: Upper Conchos population to determine taxonomic status and range
- **Refuge populations identified**: Several local populations offer refuge should range be further diminished

**7. Material Examined**: 166+ specimens from 17 drainage units examined from collections at 10 institutions (UMMZ, USNM, TNHC, TU, FMNH, ASU, TAM, UANL, UNM). Sample sizes ranged from 1-119 specimens per locality.

**8. Methods**: Multivariate morphometric analysis using 30+ distance measurements providing complete areal coverage; three meristic characters (gill rakers, anal fin-rays, vertebrae); [[Principal Components Analysis]] of covariance matrix (morphometrics) and correlation matrix (meristics); sequential analyses removing problematic populations to assess inter- and intraspecific variation.
