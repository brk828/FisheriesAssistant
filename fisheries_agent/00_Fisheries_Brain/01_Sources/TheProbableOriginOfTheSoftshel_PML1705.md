# Probable Origin of the Soft-Shelled Turtle in the Colorado River Basin, and King Snake Field Notes

#herpetology #testudines #squamata #colubridae

## Executive Summary

This 1946 *Copeia* herpetology note contains two short reports. The first argues that the soft-shelled turtle [[Amyda Emoryi]] reached the [[Colorado River]] basin through human introduction in the upper [[Gila River]] of New Mexico around the turn of the twentieth century, rather than being native. The second is a set of field notes on the [[Lampropeltis Getulus Getulus]] (king snake) from West Virginia and adjacent areas, covering specimen counts, ventral scale data, and an observed predation event.

## Background & Description

**Soft-shelled turtle (*Amyda emoryi*).** The species was already known from the lower Colorado and Gila rivers of Nevada, Arizona, California and Baja California. Most earlier authors suspected introduction into the Colorado basin. The type locality lies in the Rio Grande, and the species had been recorded near [[Mesilla Dam]], Dona Ana County, New Mexico, about 115 miles from the upper Gila River.

**King snake (*Lampropeltis getulus getulus*).** The note lists 23 specimens from several counties and compares scale characteristics across regions. One specimen had a divided anal plate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Introduction pathway:** The author links the turtle's presence in the Gila River to a stocked dyke pond on Duck Creek, a Gila tributary near Buckhorn, New Mexico. The pond was reportedly stocked with catfish and soft-shelled turtles by a local rancher, [[Tom Lyons]], whose Gila Farm and Lyons Cattle Company operated along the river. His widow did not recall turtles being planted, so the claim could not be verified.
- **Flood connection:** [[Frank M Chamberlain]] suggested the turtle may have entered the Gila River when the pond flooded over at the same time as the catfish.
- **Introduced fauna:** Catfish (*Ameiurus* and *Ictalurus*) had already been introduced into the Colorado basin. Chamberlain's notes also record a catfish, possibly *Ameiurus melas*, collected in the Colorado River at Yuma, Arizona.
- **Historical absence:** No records of the turtle appear in early expedition and survey collections from the 1850s to 1900s, including Pacific Railroad Surveys, the U.S. and Mexican Boundary Survey, and U.S. Fish Commission and Bureau of Fisheries surveys. The author regards this absence as supporting introduction.
- **Current status:** The species is now common in the lower Colorado River below [[Boulder Dam]] and has been increasing there in recent years.
- **Mexican records:** [[Roger Conant]] found no records of the turtle from Pacific-slope streams in Mexico, where it might be expected if native. The author notes that Mexican river fauna is poorly known.

King snake habitat was mostly meadows, clover fields and forest edges, with some sites near streams. Most records came from farmland, hay or wheat fields, and meadows, with a few from forest.

## Key Findings

1. **Turtle origin:** The author concludes that *Amyda emoryi* was most likely introduced into the Colorado River basin via the upper Gila River, supporting Dill's earlier hypothesis.
2. **Key evidence:** Chamberlain's field notes (April 14–15, 1944, near Duncan, Greenlee County, Arizona) record a soft-shelled turtle that was not preserved, so its identity cannot be confirmed with certainty.
3. **Chamberlain's comment:** The turtle was described as "not rare" in the area.
4. **Introduction support:** The pond story, the absence of historical records, and the species' recent increase together fit a typical introduction pattern.
5. **King snake records:** The note tallies 23 specimens across several counties and gives detailed data for 11 West Virginia specimens, including ventral scale counts and a divided anal plate in one individual.
6. **Ventral variation:** Ventral counts were tabulated per specimen. The author notes a trend toward fewer ventrals in some regional samples.
7. **Predation observed:** In July 1943, near Moorefield, a king snake was seen attacking and swallowing a water snake (*Natrix*). It swallowed the prey whole after several attempts.
8. **Captive feeding:** Three captive king snakes were fed on lizards (*Sceloporus*) and small snakes (*Storeria*), per the notes (partly garbled in the scan).
