# Preliminary Habitat Assessment: Establishing a Native Fish Refugium at Butler Lake, Imperial National Wildlife Refuge, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fish-refugium

## Executive Summary

[[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region) assessed [[Butler Lake]], a 43-acre (17.2 ha) isolated floodplain lake on the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] (INWR), as a potential native fish refugium. In its current state the lake supports few fish or aquatic birds because it is in an advanced state of [[Hypereutrophy]]. Alterations to the [[Lower Colorado River]] flow regime have eliminated the periodic floods that historically scoured out salts and organic sediments and reset the [[Eutrophication]] process in floodplain lakes.

To comply with the 1997 Biological Opinion on Lower Colorado River operations, Reclamation must create or restore 300–600 acres of isolated protected habitat for [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]]. Butler Lake is one candidate under the [[Endangered Species Act]] mitigation program.

Seven restoration methods were evaluated. [[Bioremediation]] was selected as the proposed approach, with [[Aeration]] to be considered later. The report is a scientific habitat assessment, not a cost-benefit analysis.

## Background & Description

Butler Lake lies about 160 m east of the Colorado River in the Sonoran Desert. It has a mean depth of about 3 ft (0.9 m) and no known surface connection to the river or any other water body. It is classed as a [[Seepage Lake]].

The lake was named for John Butler, an early pioneer of fishing in the Martinez Lake area. An oral account from his son describes a productive sportfishery from the late 1930s to at least the early 1960s, including bass, catfish, crappie, and bluegill. Research on piscivorous birds indicated that bluegill and [[Mosquitofish]] were still present as late as 1994.

The assessment's stated purpose was to characterize eutrophication, evaluate habitat suitability for native fish, and select a restoration alternative for a potentially self-sustaining protected habitat. Work ran from January 1, 2004 onward. Participating agencies included Reclamation, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, INWR, the Arizona Fisheries Resource Office, the Water Resources Branch, and the Ecological Services Office, under the Lower Colorado River Backwaters Committee.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrology:** The area is arid, with an average annual air temperature of 73.5 °F (22.9 °C), 3.55 in (9 cm) of precipitation, and open-water evaporation of about 87 in (2.2 m) per year. Because the lake is a seepage lake, it has reduced [[Selenium]] levels and reduced non-native fish colonization, but it also has high summer temperatures, low summer [[Dissolved Oxygen]], and salt concentration through evaporation.

**Access:** Vehicle access is limited to two observation points. Emergent vegetation along the shoreline deters anglers and also makes biological sampling difficult.

**Trophic state:** Eutrophication is a natural succession from oligotrophic to eutrophic conditions. In Butler Lake, the natural flood-driven reset has been lost. Shallow lakes are highly productive because the littoral zone covers almost the whole basin.

**Comparison site:** [[McAllister Lake]], about 1 mile (1.6 km) south, is a similar seepage floodplain lake that has already undergone restoration. Butler Lake appeared better connected to the Colorado River than McAllister, with lower salinity and smaller temperature differences.

## Key Findings

**Bathymetry (May 24–28, 2004)**
- Area: 43 acres (17.2 ha). Mean depth: about 3 ft (0.9 m). Maximum depth: 5.8 ft (1.8 m).
- Most of the lake is 3–4 ft (0.9–1.2 m) deep. Two areas at the southern end exceed 5 ft (1.5 m).
- Volume: 142 acre-feet (175,337 m³). Perimeter: 9,206 ft (2,806 m). Shoreline development index: 1.90.
- 298 depth measurements were logged, and 44 substrate samples were taken.

**Substrate:** The bottom is almost entirely homogeneous silt/clay covered by thick organic debris with a strong hydrogen sulfide odor. The only exception is one rock outcrop. Cover is mostly cattails, with dead trees and little or no submerged vegetation.

**Water quality (Hydrolab profiles, January–September 2004)**
- Temperature: 11.22–29.99 °C (n=8), generally tracking the river and occasionally differing by 1–2 °C.
- pH: 7.5–8.94 (n=8), slightly higher than the river.
- Dissolved oxygen: 0.21–21.5 mg/L (n=8). Extreme vertical stratification appeared in May, and the water was virtually anoxic from June through August.
- Specific conductivity: minimum 4,950 µS/cm (n=9), rising slightly in summer.

**Chemistry (sampled May 2004)**
- Selenium and arsenic were below detection limits (<2.00 µg/L).
- Copper was at or below 1.0 µg/L. Iron was 12.5 and 13.8 µg/L.
- Sulfate was 1,123 and 1,248 ppm. Orthophosphate was 0.132 and 0.123 ppm.
- The inorganic N:P ratio averaged 42:1, indicating that [[Phosphorus]] most likely limits algal growth. The authors caution that this is based on a single sampling date.

**Algae and trophic state**
- The phytoplankton community is dominated by [[Cyanobacteria]], such as *Arthrospira*, *Anabaenopsis*, and *Oscillatoria*.
- Chlorophyll a was roughly 106–128 (units as reported). Secchi depths in September were 0.08 and 0.1 m.
- Both chlorophyll and total phosphorus place the lake in the hypereutrophic category of the [[Trophic State Index]].

**Fish survey**
- Five net-nights of experimental gill nets and six trap-nights of minnow traps yielded one mosquitofish (about 20 mm).
- Mosquitofish were seen along the shoreline during a fall 2002 visit.
- The survey was limited in scope and may not represent all species present.

**Birds**
- Avian use is far lower than at McAllister Lake in both numbers and species.
- [[American Coot]] was the most common species at Butler Lake. [[Ruddy Duck]] was most common at McAllister.

**Habitat ratings**
- [[Bradford and Vlach Rating]] (razorback sucker): spawning habitat "good" (15) and backwater habitat "good" (15). The spawning rating drops to "fair" during hotter summer months.
- [[Holden et al Rating]]: fish habitat "good" (17) and wildlife habitat "fair" (15).
- The authors note these systems are scoping tools and do not replace detailed analysis.

**Interpretation**
- Cyanobacteria dominance, anoxia, and low water clarity suppress submerged plants, the food web, and waterfowl use.
- The authors judge the lake could support a self-sustaining native fish population if restored to oligotrophic or mesotrophic conditions.
- Average summer temperatures approach or may exceed the razorback sucker avoidance range (27–31.6 °C), though they are within 1–2 °C of the river.
- Specific conductivity is about five times that of the Colorado River. It is similar to [[Ducks Unlimited Ponds]] where native fish have been introduced.

**Restoration alternatives (seven methods analyzed)**
- [[Dredging]]: effective for removing organic detritus, but costly, with difficult disposal and regulatory requirements. [[Beal Lake]] post-restoration surveys showed a large multi-species fish population.
- [[Pumping]]: useful for salinity reduction and supplemental nutrient export. Pumps are cheaper per acre than dredging.
- [[Alum]] (chemical manipulation): binds phosphorus for several years at about $300–700 per surface acre. It does not address organic sediments.
- Copper: effective against cyanobacteria but risks toxicity to fish and wildlife over time.
- [[Barley Straw]]: effective for 30–90 days but labor intensive and not feasible over 100 acres. Liquid extract costs about $20 per one-acre, four-foot pond.
- [[Aeration]]: encourages green algae and aids decomposition but has costs and risk of fish kills if it stops.
- [[Hydrological Engineering]]: connecting the lake to the river could flush nutrients but raises selenium and non-native fish risks. [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] is cited as a successful example.
- [[Bioremediation]] (proposed): microbial products degrade organic sludge. Radcliff (2004) claims a shift from hypereutrophic to oligotrophic in several months. Aerial application by helicopter is estimated at $3,000–5,000, with the helicopter cost under $2,000.

**Proposed plan and conclusions**
- Contract a professional bioremediation firm, using requests for proposals, a detailed work plan for stakeholder review, and a written interagency agreement.
- Defer any aeration decision until data show it is needed.
- The report notes that the 2005 completion deadline for the Biological Opinion is a key constraint.
- Bioremediation evidence is largely anecdotal. Only one response was received on the product from the native fish listserv.
- Expect eutrophication to continue after restoration, so a limited fishery life or ongoing management is likely.
