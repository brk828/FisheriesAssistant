# Food Habits of the Yellowstone Whitefish (*Prosopium williamsoni cismontanus*)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This 1950 study by Martin Laakso quantitatively analyzed the food habits of 385 [[Yellowstone Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni cismontanus*) and 52 trout (rainbow and brown) collected over one year (Sept. 1947–1948) from the [[Yellowstone River]] and [[Gallatin River]] in Montana. The study found that caddisfly larvae ([[Trichoptera]]) and stonefly nymphs ([[Plecoptera]]) made up roughly equal, dominant portions of the whitefish diet, followed by midges ([[Chironomidae]]), with mayflies ([[Ephemeroptera]]) contributing the least volume. Caddisflies and Dipterans together accounted for 87% of all organisms consumed by number. Diet composition differed between the two rivers: Yellowstone fish relied heavily on immature caddis- and stoneflies (>80% of volume), while Gallatin fish consumed a more balanced mix including fish eggs, Diptera, stoneflies, caddis, and mayflies. No significant dietary differences were found between whitefish size classes, though larger fish (>16 in) held more than twice the food volume of smaller fish (<14 in). The paper also documents dietary overlap and potential competition with sympatric trout species, particularly during winter months.

## Background & Description
This paper is a food-habits study rather than a morphological description, but it includes relevant size/length data used to categorize specimens:

- **Minimum angling size**: No fish smaller than 7 inches was captured via angling methods, introducing a sampling bias against smaller individuals and non-feeding fish.
- **Fingerling whitefish**: Two size classes examined — average 1¼ inches (June 22 sample) and average 2 inches (July 21 sample) total length, both seined from shallow shoreline habitat.
- **Adult size classes** (Yellowstone River, January collection of 60 fish) were divided into three groups:
  - Smallest group: under 14 inches total length
  - Largest group: over 16 inches total length
  - Largest group held **>2x** the average stomach food volume compared to smallest group.
- **Trout comparators**: Rainbow trout specimens ranged 7½–18 inches TL; brown trout specimens ranged 9–15 inches TL.
- No morphometric or meristic data (fin counts, scale counts, coloration) are provided in this source — the focus is exclusively on diet and stomach content analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: [[Prosopium Williamsoni Cismontanus]] inhabits cold headwater streams of the Missouri River drainage. Sampling occurred in:
  - A 65-mile Yellowstone River section (Corwin to 15 mi NE of Livingston), flowing north through a narrow valley between the Absaroka and Gallatin ranges; more stable water levels, peak spring flood in June, shore ice in winter but main channel remains open.
  - A 25-mile Gallatin River section (Williams Bridge to Manhattan bridge), flowing north along the western edge of Gallatin Valley; summer flow substantially reduced by irrigation withdrawal in the lower two-thirds.
  - Limited sampling near Trident (Missouri River, below Madison/Jefferson confluence) and the Gallatin River mouth.

- **Sympatric species / competitors**: Whitefish co-occur with [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonnii*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Longnose Dace]] (*Rhinichthys cataractae*), [[Burbot]] (*Lota lota*), and freshwater sculpin (*Cottus* sp.). Potential additional sympatric species include [[Flathead Chub]] (*Platygobio gracilis*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), and [[Stonecat]] (*Noturus flavus*).

- **Trophic role**: Primarily an insectivorous drift/benthic feeder consuming immature aquatic insects (caddisflies, stoneflies, midges, mayflies) plus occasional fish eggs and terrestrial invertebrates (ants, leafhoppers, earthworms, millipedes). Unlike rainbow trout, whitefish showed **no algae consumption** and generally avoided surface-drift terrestrial prey items commonly taken by trout.

- **Feeding behavior/physiology**: Whitefish fed actively and maintained full stomachs even in the coldest winter months, in contrast to trout, which reportedly feed less actively in cold water (per Needham 1930). A comparison of 124 whitefish vs. 13 trout stomachs (Dec–Feb) showed whitefish contained over **5x** as much food volume as trout — suggesting either higher feeding activity or slower digestion/gut clearance in cold conditions.

- **Competition dynamics**: Dietary overlap with trout was most pronounced in winter (similar prey composition), though [[Leonard 1941]] suggested food may not be limiting for trout in winter due to slow digestion. Competition was inferred to intensify in summer due to reduced food availability from spring runoff scouring ([[Muttkowski 1929]]) and synchronized insect emergence, with both whitefish and trout supplementing diets with surface-drift terrestrial/adult insect prey.

## Key Findings

### Overall Diet Composition (Yellowstone River)
- 81.1% of food by weight = immature caddisflies + stoneflies combined (~equal parts each)
- Diptera (mostly midges) ≈ 7–10% of volume
- Hydropsychidae (caddis) = ~25% of average diet, most numerous/frequent taxon
- Ephemeroptera (mayflies) = <2% of total volume, <4% of total number
- Caddisflies + Diptera larvae together = 87% of total number of organisms consumed (across full dataset)

### Seasonal Patterns — Yellowstone River

| Season | Dominant Food Item | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fall | Midges (32.2% volume); Brachycentrus (24.2%) | Highest unidentifiable material (74.2%) |
| Winter | Perlodidae, Pteronarcella badia stoneflies | Bibiocephala common (1/3 of stomachs); adult midges seen on shore ice |
| Spring | Psychomyiidae caddis; stonefly nymphs abundant | Rhyacophila present |
| Summer | Acroneuria, Pteronarcys (large, 2-yr nymphs) more prominent as other taxa emerge | |

### Seasonal Patterns — Gallatin River
- Fall: Fish eggs = largest single dietary item (nearly all Nov. 16–23 stomachs contained eggs, including 2 brown trout eggs in one whitefish)
- Winter: Midges dominant (55.6% of volume), present in every fish sampled — much higher contribution than Yellowstone River
- Spring/Summer: Greater use of surface food; Pteronarcys large volume in June/July; mayflies more prominent (avg. 62 nymphs/fish in a 14-fish July sample; one stomach held 130 mayfly subimagoes/adults)
- Overall mayfly contribution: ~11% of total volume (vs. only ~2% in Yellowstone River)

### River Comparison
- **Yellowstone River**: stonefly nymphs + caddis larvae = >80% of total food volume — more specialized, "typical" torrent-fauna diet
- **Gallatin River**: more balanced/representative use of caddis, fish eggs, Diptera, stoneflies, and mayflies

### Fingerling Diet
- Dominated by midge larvae (plus notable adult chironomid numbers), Baetis nymphs, immature stoneflies, and caddis larvae
- No significant dietary difference between the two fingerling size classes (1¼ in vs. 2 in)

### Size-Class Comparison (Adults)
- No major qualitative dietary differences across whitefish size classes
- Larger fish (>16 in) showed slightly higher proportion of stonefly nymphs
- Smaller fish (<14 in) showed slightly higher proportion of caddis larvae
- Largest size class averaged >2x food volume per stomach vs. smallest class

### Whitefish vs. Trout Comparison
- Brown trout and whitefish from the same site/date (Manhattan Bridge, Nov. 23) showed similar proportions of caddis, stonefly, and whitefish egg consumption (eggs most abundant in both)
- Nov. 29: stoneflies = 39% of brown trout stomach content vs. 15.5% for whitefish (same time/place)
- Rainbow trout stomachs contained algae (22.6% of volume, present in 36% of specimens) — **never found in whitefish**
- Rainbow trout used more surface/terrestrial prey (adult mayflies, midges, leafhoppers, beetles, ants) not utilized by whitefish at the same times
- An 18-inch rainbow trout contained 8 Pteronarcys nymphs — this stonefly species was never recorded in whitefish diet from the same area/time
- Winter comparison (124 whitefish vs. 13 trout, Dec–Feb): whitefish contained >5x food volume per stomach vs. trout, suggesting differing winter feeding activity/digestion rates

### Methodological Notes
- Angling bias excluded fish <7 inches and possibly non-feeding or bait-averse individuals
- Case-bearing caddis (Brachycentrus) volume correction factor established: uncased larvae volume = 48% of cased larvae volume (based on ~680 larvae across 17 stomachs)
- Gravel presence in stomachs indicated ingestion of case-building Glossosoma larvae/pupae
- Unidentifiable material ranged widely by season: Yellowstone (22.6–74.2%), Gallatin (24.5–83.0%)
