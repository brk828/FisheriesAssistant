# Harvest, Mortality, and Movement of Selected Warmwater Fishes in Folsom Lake, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

A disk-dangler tagging study conducted at [[Folsom Lake]], California, from April 1962 estimated annual fishing (harvest) and natural mortality rates for six warmwater game fishes. Tagged ictalurids ([[White Catfish]] and [[Brown Bullhead]]) showed low annual harvest and high survival, while centrarchids ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]]) showed high harvest and low survival. Centrarchids moved considerably less than ictalurids. The study's purpose was to determine whether anglers were efficiently harvesting existing fish populations. The author notes that several estimates are probably inaccurate, mainly because of changes in exploitation and natural mortality during the two-year period and because of nonresponse in tag returns.

## Background & Description

**Study objective:** To determine fishing and natural mortality rates of the more important game fishes in [[Folsom Lake]], and whether anglers were efficiently harvesting existing populations. The work was part of Dingell-Johnson Project California F-18-R, "Experimental Management of Warmwater Reservoirs," and was part of a broader investigation of the Folsom Lake fishery.

**Study site:** [[Folsom Lake]] is a 10,000-surface-acre fluctuating reservoir.

**Species tagged:**
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*M. dolomieui*)
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- [[Red-ear Sunfish]] (*L. microlophus*)
- [[White Catfish]] (*Ictalurus catus*)
- [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*; the source reads "J. nebulosus," corrected here)

**Tagging method:**
- Fish were marked with [[Disk-Dangler Tag]]s (modified Atkins tags), a method previously shown to be efficient for largemouth bass, striped bass, channel catfish, and white catfish.
- Tags were cellulose nitrate disks 0.040 inch thick, either 1 cm or 4 inch in diameter. Smaller tags went on centrarchids and larger tags on ictalurids.
- Tags were attached with tantalum wire 0.020 inch in diameter, midway between the first dorsal fin and the lateral line.
- Reward tags ($5 reward) were placed on 50% of the tagged largemouth bass, bluegill, and white catfish. The remaining 50% of these species, and all other species, carried nonreward tags.

**Capture methods:** Seines, electrofishing, Oneida traps, and fyke nets (Table 1). Most centrarchids were captured by night electrofishing; catfishes were caught mainly in entrapment gear.

**Tagging period:** Centrarchids were tagged April 26 through June 20, 1962. Ictalurids were tagged April 26 through August 24, 1962. Tagging was discontinuous, with some two-week gaps.

**Mortality methods:** Harvest and mortality followed [[Ricker Method]] (Ricker, 1958). First-year returns covered 0 to 365 days after tagging; second-year returns covered 366 to 730 days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Movement patterns:**
- Centrarchids (largemouth bass, bluegill) moved little, while ictalurids (white catfish, brown bullhead) dispersed much farther across the reservoir.
- Largemouth bass and smallmouth bass showed short movements, with no evidence of long-distance dispersal.
- The author attributes the difference in movement to species behavior, not to fishing pressure.

**Downstream passage:** Two white catfish were recovered from [[Lake Natoma]], downstream of [[Folsom Lake]], "after presumably passing through the dam."

**Night fishing:** Night fishing was identified as a potential way to increase harvest of catfishes. During experimental setline fishing at Folsom Lake in summer 1964, night sets outfished day sets for bullhead by 3.3:1. At Quabbin Reservoir, Massachusetts, night fishermen were 8.5% of total fishermen but caught 61.2% of the brown bullhead harvest (McCaig, 1963).

**Size-related vulnerability and nonresponse:** Differential tag return by size may reflect differential nonresponse by anglers rather than differential vulnerability of fish (see Key Findings).

**Regional comparisons:** Survival and mortality estimates were compared with other Californian waters (Clear Lake, Sutherland Reservoir), and with Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) reservoirs and Gladstone Lake, Minnesota.

## Key Findings

### Largemouth Bass
- Sample: 9.0 to 21.0 inches fork length, mean 11.8 inches (Table 2). Largemouth bass over 9.0 inches were tagged.
- Reward tag returns: 119 (51.7%); 111 in the first year and 8 in the second. Returns were considered essentially complete.
- Movement: mean 0.7 mile from tagging point to recapture; longest journey 6.0 miles; 63% of tagged bass were caught less than one mile from the tagging point.
- Timing: 51 (43%) of reward tags returned within 15 days; 89 (75%) within 60 days.
- Initial first-year exploitation rate: 0.48; survival between years: 0.07. Both were judged probably inaccurate.
- Aberrant group: 64 fish held in live cars for two days, tagged, and released on a Saturday morning in a heavily fished area. Anglers caught 44 (69%) in the first year, well above other groups (chi-square = 16.0 vs. 3.8 critical value). This group was removed.
- Adjusted first-year exploitation rate: 0.40 (considered the most useful estimate).
- Adjusted first-year survival: 0.11 (considered unreasonably low; probable cause is changing exploitation and/or natural mortality over two years).
- Summary: annual apparent mortality 0.89; exploitation rate 0.40; natural mortality 0.49 (Table 3).
- Supporting observation: 71% of angler-caught bass were under 14 inches long (von Geldern, MS, 1962).
- Chi-square test: no significant difference in return rates among one-inch length groups (chi-square = 7.86; critical value 14.07). All bass over 9 inches appeared equally vulnerable.
- Comparison: exploitation compared favorably with Sutherland Reservoir and two TVA lakes; survival was lower than in other reported lakes (Table 3).

### Smallmouth Bass
- Sample: 9.8 to 13.8 inches, mean 11.0 inches. Twenty-three fish tagged with nonreward tags.
- Returns: 13 in the first year, none in the second; considered complete.
- Movement: 7 fish with accurate recapture localities moved a mean of 0.7 mile.
- Timing: of 11 fish with accurate capture dates, 5 were caught within 45 days. Earliest recapture: 6 days; latest: 346 days.
- Mortality rates were not estimated because of small sample size.
- Summary: first-year exploitation rate 0.57, which the author describes as limited data.

### White Catfish
- Sample: 8.0 to 17.5 inches, mean 10.8 inches.
- Reward tag returns: 65 (30%); 42 in the first year and 23 in the second. More returns were expected.
- Movement: mean 2.3 miles; maximum 10.4 miles. Only 7 catfish were caught less than 0.5 mile from tagging point; 10 recaptures were more than 5.0 miles away.
- Timing: first catch 20 days after tagging; 8 returns in the first 90 days.
- Survival: 0.55; mortality: 0.45; exploitation: 0.19. These were close to values from other Californian waters (Table 4).
- Interpretation: high survival with low fishing and natural mortality suggests white catfish are underexploited in most Californian waters. Harvest could be increased by opening more areas to night fishing.
- No systematic errors described by Ricker (1958) were evident. Catfish distributed randomly with no differential vulnerability between years.
- Chi-square test: no significant difference between size at tagging and size of returned fish (chi-square = 3.04; critical value 14.07).
- Nonreward returns above and below 10.0 inches differed significantly (chi-square = 7.56; critical value 3.84). The author suggests earlier reports of higher vulnerability for larger fish (Pelgen and McCammon, 1955; McCammon and Seeley, 1961) may reflect reporting differences.
- A mean nonresponse factor of 0.46 from this species was later applied to brown bullhead returns.

### Brown Bullhead
- Sample: 8.0 to 14.8 inches, mean 11.6 inches (FL).
- Returns: 41 nonreward tags; 27 in the first year and 14 in the second. Returns are not considered complete.
- Movement: mean 1.7 miles; longest 16.2 miles. Only 15% were recovered within 1.0 mile of release.
- Timing: first return after 34 days; only three returns within 60 days.
- Adjusted estimates (corrected with the 0.46 white catfish nonresponse factor): survival 0.52, harvest (exploitation) 0.14, annual mortality 0.48.
- Comparison: McCammon and Seeley (1961) reported 0.76 survival and 0.07 harvest at Clear Lake (Table 5). Note: the Table 5 Folsom entries are partially garbled in the source scan; the values above are taken from the text summary.
- Chi-square test: tags from fish 11.0 inches or longer returned at a significantly higher rate than from smaller fish (chi-square = 4.80; critical value 3.84). The author suggests this may reflect variation in nonresponse rather than differential vulnerability.

### Bluegill
- Sample: 6.0 to 9.5 inches, mean 7.4 inches.
- Returns: 33 reward tags; 30 in the first year and 3 in the second.
- Movement: mean 1.1 miles (range 0 to 3.4 miles), based on 27 tags with sufficient data. 41% of returns came from the tagging point.
- Time at large: 4 to 404 days. 11 tags returned within 60 days.
- Estimates (limited data): survival 0.10; mortality 0.90; harvest 0.37; natural mortality 0.53.
- Reliability: the author says these estimates are doubtful. Tagged fish were over 6.0 inches and four to six years old (Tharratt, 1966), so most were expected to die within two years. Lack of tagging at the start of the second year prevents assessing accuracy.
- Statistical comparison by size was not attempted because of too few returns.

### Red-ear Sunfish
- Sample: 6.0 to 11.0 inches, mean 8.0 inches.
- Returns: three nonreward tags, all in the first year.
- Adjusted first-year exploitation rate: 0.28, corrected for an 83% nonresponse rate calculated for bluegill. Slightly lower than for bluegill.
- Returns were too few to estimate mortality parameters.

### Overall Management Conclusions
- Centrarchids (largemouth bass, bluegill) showed high harvest and low survival, and limited movement.
- Ictalurids (white catfish, brown bullhead) showed low harvest and high survival, and wider dispersal.
- Catfish populations appear poorly harvested in Californian waters. Opening more areas to night fishing is recommended as a way to raise catfish harvest.
- Tag return rates are sensitive to nonresponse, so size-based vulnerability conclusions require caution.
