# Primary Production and Periodicity of Phytoplankton

#phytoplankton #limnology #aquatic-ecology #asterionella #diatoms

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by J. W. G. Lund (Ambleside, England) at the Verhandlungen of the Internationale Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie (Stuttgart, February 1964), argues that phytoplankton ecology should shift from measuring *how much* primary production occurs to explaining *why* production is high or low. Drawing mainly on long-term observations from [[Windermere]], [[Esthwaite Water]] and other [[English Lake District]] lakes, Lund emphasises the importance of basic data (taxonomy, dry weights, carbon and silica content), the role of silica and phosphate in regulating spring diatom maxima, and the paradox that many species coexist in summer. The paper closes with reflections on research organisation and the value of individual scientific creativity.

## Background & Description

- **Author and context:** J. W. G. Lund, a leading freshwater phycologist, delivering a keynote-style address. It honours Professor Baldi and reviews progress since the introduction of the [[C14 Method]] (Steemann Nielsen technique) nearly a decade earlier, while recognising continued use of the classic oxygen method.
- **Study area:** The English Lake District, a compact area of under 2000 km² with lakes radiating from central mountains; a circle of 25 km radius encloses them all. Lakes are soft-water, with about 0.35–0.75 m.e./l total ions and dry residues of about 20–40 mg/l, and share similar climate and soils.
- **Basic data framework:** Lund stresses that algal taxonomy is inconsistent, so the authority followed must always be stated. He proposes expressing algal dry weights in *Chlorella* units, using *Chlorella pyrenoidosa* (about 4 µg dry weight per million cells) as the base.
- **Scope:** The paper is an informal, deliberately speculative review, with Lund acknowledging that it contains many platitudes and unresolved questions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Factors controlling production.** Lund lists light (intensity, duration, spectral quality), temperature (including growth limits), wind and mixing, major ions (which mainly determine flora type), minor ions such as nitrate, phosphate and silicate (which determine abundance), trace elements, vitamins, chelators and unidentified yellow substances.

**Biotic interactions.** Algae excrete extracellular substances that may poison, inhibit or stimulate other organisms. Grazing by zooplankton and parasitism by fungi are both important, although fungi are the predominant parasites.

**Seasonal regime in Windermere.**
- *Winter:* growth is limited by low light and temperature; nutrients accumulate from inflows and bottom-deposit decomposition.
- *Spring:* a diatom maximum occurs, dominated by fast-growing [[Asterionella Formosa]], and typically ends when silica falls to about 0.5 mg/l.
- *Summer–autumn:* nutrients are scarce, crops are irregular and follow rainfall-driven inflows, and many more species contribute to production.

**Vertical and spatial structure.** Lund describes the epilimnion as a "kaleidoscope": highly structured, with patchy distributions of algae, nutrients and zooplankton swarms, and with thermocline turbulence altering diatom and nutrient distributions.

**Broader relevance.** Lund argues that most glacial lakes in temperate and arctic regions likely share chemical (nutrient) limitation in late spring or early summer. Warmer-climate lakes such as [[Lake Victoria]] and [[Lake Nyasa]] are expected to show longer periods of nutrient limitation, although periodicity there is partly linked to water movement.

## Key Findings

1. **Silica controls the spring diatom maximum.** The spring peak of *Asterionella* correlates with silica falling to roughly 0.5 mg/l. This correlation held in the southern basin of Windermere for 18 consecutive years and was disturbed only once in the 16 years shown in Fig. 4 (1948). The 1948 exception coincided with drought, and in 1961 and 1962 the relationship was also not seen. Lund notes that the silica threshold also depends on phosphate, illumination and temperature.

2. **Phosphate is a likely general limiting nutrient.** In summer, phosphate phosphorus in Windermere falls below 1 µg/l, sometimes below 0.1 µg/l, and may limit the phytoplankton as a whole once the diatom maximum passes. Lund states that the nutrient(s) involved are not yet fully identified.

3. **Growth rate explains spring dominance.** In culture, *Asterionella* grows faster than [[Tabellaria Flocculosa]] and nearly as fast as *Fragilaria crotonensis*. *Fragilaria* numbers decline sharply from November to February/March, but the cause is unknown. Both survive darkness for at least three months without growing.

4. **Summer diversity paradox.** More species contribute significantly to primary production in the summer–autumn period than in winter–spring, the reverse of what competitive exclusion would predict (cited as [[Hutchinson Paradox of the Plankton]]). Lund suggests this arises from the constantly changing, patchy environment.

5. **Cold-water stenotherms are absent in freshwater phytoplankton.** All 25 Windermere species cultivated grew fastest at 18–25 °C, when nutrients were available and light was not excessive. Lund expects cold-water specialists, if any, to occur in the sea.

6. **Inhibition hypothesis not supported.** Experiments with water from dense *Oscillatoria agardhii* v. *isothrix* crops (up to 120,000 threads/ml in Fig. 7) did not inhibit *Asterionella*; growth was, if anything, stimulated. Lund warns against overreliance on inhibition explanations.

7. **Total carbon in spring crops is roughly constant.** In Esthwaite Water over many years, the total carbon incorporated into the spring crop is similar even when the relative abundance of species varies, suggesting an upper limit to production set by nutrients.

8. **Basic biomass relationships.**
   - About 1 million dried *Chlorella pyrenoidosa* cells weigh about 4 µg.
   - Desmids weigh about 500–2500 *Chlorella* units; *Peridinium cinctum* weighs about 9000.
   - Carbon is about half the ash-free dry weight in planktonic algae; well-silicified diatoms have carbon-to-dry-weight ratios of about 1:5, and other algae about 3:7.
   - Silica makes up 40–50% of the dry weight of well-silicified diatoms.
   - Diatoms contain about half as much nitrogen as green algae.

9. **Slow-growing desmids persist.** *Xanthidium subhastiferum* grows so slowly that its rate at 1 m equals that of *Asterionella* at 6 m, yet it has been recorded in Windermere since 1903 and every year since 1941. Lund suggests that a low nutrient demand per unit time may compensate. Lund has never observed a zygote of the plankton desmids in 18 years.

10. **Research recommendations.** Lund calls for long-term periodicity studies (which show whether a year is "normal"), basic data on algal composition and dry weight, standardised taxonomic authority citation, computer-aided analysis, and large-scale investigations. He stresses that individual, creative scientists, not teams, have historically driven fundamental advances.
