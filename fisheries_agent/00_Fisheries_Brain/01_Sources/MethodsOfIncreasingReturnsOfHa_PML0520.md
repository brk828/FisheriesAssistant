# Methods of Increasing Returns of Hatchery Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Idaho Fish and Game Department report, written by Paul Cuplin, summarizes roughly ten years of studies on how to increase the harvest of hatchery-raised trout by anglers. The work compares release timing, fish size, water body type, and fish conditioning for [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Kamloops Trout]]. The main conclusions are that spring releases of catchable-size fish in lakes produce the best returns, that fall releases perform poorly, that stream plantings disappear quickly and need frequent replenishment, and that trophy-size Kamloops programs were discontinued because of cost. An exercise-conditioning experiment was still in progress, so no conclusions were drawn from it.

## Background & Description

The Idaho Fish and Game Department studied the harvest of hatchery fish over a ten-year period, with increasing the creel return to anglers as the primary goal. The department reports that hatchery transport procedures work well and that fish arrive at release sites in good physical condition. Harvest is influenced by fish size, release timing, species, habitat type, and fishing pressure.

Planting catchable rainbow trout (about 8 to 12 inches) in lakes and reservoirs is controversial among some biologists. Planting is usually reserved for situations where fingerling stocking has failed and the water body is in a popular recreation area. The reported cost of producing and planting one pound of trout in Idaho was 58 cents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study focuses on stocked fish in managed recreational waters rather than natural populations. Fishing pressure is highest in the first six weeks of the season, so fish released in spring are exposed to the greatest angling effort. Stream-stocked fish often disappear from the water within three to four weeks, which the author links to raceway rearing that does not prepare fish for life in flowing water. The author argues that these fish are better suited to lake or reservoir environments.

Stamina and survival after release were also examined, based on the idea that exercise might toughen hatchery trout for the wild.

## Key Findings

### Release Timing in Lakes (Kelso and Spirit Lakes)

- A study by Jeppson evaluated catchable rainbow trout released monthly during the season in [[Kelso Lake]] (70-acre) and [[Spirit Lake]] (1,300-acre).
- The percent return declined with each later monthly release.
- The following year, fish released in April, the month with the greatest return, produced a return nearly equal to the previous April planting.

### Season-Wide Lake Returns

- Spring releases ranged from 16 to 100 percent return, with half above 50 percent and half below.
- Summer releases ranged from 5 to 54 percent.
- Fall releases ranged from 1 to 24 percent, and generally gave poor results.
- These figures do not account for the number of fish released, lake size, or fishing pressure.

### Trophy Kamloops Program (Pend Oreille Lake)

- [[Pend Oreille Lake]] in northern Idaho is known for trophy Kamloops. Eggs from northern Idaho were reared at [[Hagerman Fish Hatchery]] and planted to increase the number of trophy-size fish.
- Returns from 12- and 14-inch fish were each about 20 percent.
- Three-year-old 16-inch fish returned 38 percent.
- 10- to 12-inch Kamloops returned less than 3 percent.
- Five years after the program began in 1960, no trophy fish had been recovered from the catchable-size Kamloops planted. The program was discontinued because of the cost of rearing large fish.

### Stream Stocking

- The return pattern in streams resembles that in lakes, but total returns from stream plantings are lower than those from lake plantings in the waters studied.
- Fish planted too far ahead of the season opening performed poorly.
- Returns were recorded from 29 stream releases, with [[Stream Stocking]] requiring more frequent replenishment than [[Lake Stocking]] to sustain angler success.
- Second-season returns ranged from 0.3 to 3 percent. The highest second-season return (3 percent) came from a stream that had a 57 percent first-season return.

### Exercise Conditioning Experiment

- Researchers tested whether controlled exercise could improve trout stamina.
- Each test used three groups: unexercised fish, fish exercised 15 minutes daily for five days, and fish exercised 15 minutes daily for ten days.
- Exercise was done by lowering raceway water levels so fish had to swim vigorously for 15 minutes. This method avoided handling fish and allowed large numbers to be processed at once.
- The author states that it was too early to draw conclusions, and [[Creel Census]] data were to be evaluated the following fall. The author hoped for at least a small increase in harvest of exercised fish.
