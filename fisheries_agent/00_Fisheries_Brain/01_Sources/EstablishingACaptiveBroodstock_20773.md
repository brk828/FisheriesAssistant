# Establishing a Captive Broodstock for Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This genetics study evaluates the captive broodstock of the federally endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and sets out a protocol for building a [[Future Captive Broodstock]]. The 1981 F1 progeny produced from wild parents taken from [[Lake Mohave]] have a very small [[Effective Number of Founders]]: about 8.5 by hatchery production records and as low as about 3.5 by [[Allozyme Analysis]] and supporting [[Mitochondrial DNA]] data. The authors argue that the remaining F1 fish, and any wild fish captured in future, should be combined into a new broodstock with a balanced, random parental design, and that wild-caught fish should be incorporated while F1 parents remain available. The approach is presented as a model for other endangered fishes that may persist mainly in captivity.

## Background & Description

*Gila elegans* is a federally listed endangered cyprinid and one of the most critically imperiled North American freshwater fishes. Its decline is linked to habitat destruction and alteration from water development and to introduced non-native fishes. The only known wild survivors are an unknown number of large, old adults in Lake Mohave on the lower [[Colorado River]], plus scattered individuals in the upper Colorado River basin. Little recruitment has occurred since the closure of [[Davis Dam]] in 1954 and the subsequent establishment of non-native sport fishes.

In 1981, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service produced F1 progeny at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] from wild adults captured in Lake Mohave. A sample of about 2,300 of these fish was retained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], where approximately 350 still existed in late 1997. The paper argues that genetic variation in endangered species must be preserved as fully as possible, citing extinction risk linked to inbreeding and loss of variation (e.g., [[Inbreeding Depression]]).

**Study scope:** Because no wild parental fish were sampled, genetic data from F1 and F2 descendants are the only evidence available for reconstructing parental contributions. The authors combined previously published allozyme data, new allozyme and mtDNA data, and simulations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses population genetics rather than field ecology, but it provides context on the species' status and history:

- **Wild population:** Recruitment has been negligible in Lake Mohave since 1954. The remaining wild fish are large and old.
- **Hatchery rearing:** F1 fish were reared in ponds at Dexter. The later F2 "volunteer" progeny were produced in holding ponds where adults were observed preying on smaller bonytail.
- **Stocking history:** Hatchery progeny were stocked into Lake Mohave from 1981 to 1996. Introduced progeny can therefore be confused with truly wild fish, complicating identification.
- **Selection in captivity:** Earlier work on fishes and other animals shows that captive conditions can select for traits that reduce post-release fitness. This risk is greater when several hatchery generations are produced and parents contribute unequally.

## Key Findings

### 1. Parental contribution and founder number (1981 F1 cohort)

- Five adult males and six wild females were used in 1981. One female produced no eggs, so five pair matings contributed progeny.
- Egg numbers from the five productive females ranged from 5,850 to 37,700 (mean 25,090). Fertilization ranged from 0.91 to 0.98 (mean 0.95).
- Fertilized-egg proportions (p_i) ranged from 0.049 to 0.290 (mean 0.2). These are the "high" estimate, the most even distribution of contributions from the matings.
- Under the high estimate, N_ef = 8.48, or 84.8% of the 10 actual founders. This is the maximum for this cohort.
- Under the "low" estimate from genetic data, N_ef = 3.48. The authors infer that only three mating pairs contributed, and that most progeny came from one of them.

### 2. Allozyme evidence

- F1 sample: N = 30 (three polymorphic loci). F2 sample: N = 24 (six loci in an earlier study). Lake Mohave sample: N = 15 (one locus, Pgm-A).
- Six three-locus genotypes were found in the F1 sample. Genotype 1 occurred in 22 of 30 fish, which suggests most came from a single mating.
- At locus Pgm-A, the F1 sample showed a large, significant excess of heterozygotes, with observed heterozygosity of 0.833 versus expected 0.499.
- Under binomial probabilities, one such mating out of five was 3.02 times more likely than two or more. The authors therefore infer a single dominant mating.
- A "low" estimate of mating contributions (p1 = 0.733, p2 = p3 = 0.1335) was derived from these data.

### 3. Mitochondrial DNA evidence

- All 20 F1 fish analyzed had mtDNA haplotype a. Of the 16 Lake Mohave fish, 7 (0.438) had haplotype a, 5 (0.312) had haplotype b, and 4 (0.25) had haplotype c.
- Haplotypes b and c are not present in the original captive broodstock. They indicate genetic variation that survives in the wild population but is missing from hatchery fish.
- Simulations showed that a monomorphic sample of 20 was 4.4 times more likely under the "low" estimate (0.101) than under the "high" estimate (0.023). The data therefore favor the low estimate.

### 4. Effective founders in a future broodstock

- Simulations (5,000 replicates per combination) show that using a finite set of F1 parents reduces variation only modestly, provided enough pairs are used.
- With 30 pairs (60 fish), retention is 89.3% (high) and 97.1% (low) of the maximum possible. With 150 pairs, retention reaches 97.5% and 99.4%.
- The authors recommend about 30 pairs as adequate and state that more pairs add little unless annual progeny appear unrepresentative.

### 5. Value of incorporating wild fish

- Adding one wild fish raises N_ef from about 8.5 to 9.5 (11.8% gain) under the high estimate, or from about 3.5 to 4.5 (28.7% gain) under the low estimate.
- A wild fish's representation should lie between 1/(8.5 + N_w) and 1/(3.5 + N_w), where N_w is the number of wild fish captured.
- Mating-design examples (Table 7): with 30 F1 matings and one wild fish, a high-estimate design needs about nine F1 × wild matings, or 0.23 of progeny from those matings. Under the low estimate, the required share is 0.64 (one wild fish) and 0.74 (two wild fish).
- Ideally, wild fish should be incorporated while F1 parents are still available.

### 6. Recommendations for broodstock management

- **Random parent selection:** Choose parents at random, independent of phenotype, unless a genetic or congenital problem is identified. This limits selection for captivity.
- **Equalizing family sizes:** Previous work suggests that equalizing family sizes may delay adaptation to captivity, so the authors recommend this where possible.
- **Certifying wild fish:** A suspected wild fish could be mated with five F1 fish of the original broodstock, sacrificed, and aged by [[Otolith Analysis]]. Its progeny would then join the future broodstock at the proportion calculated above.
- **Using mtDNA as a marker:** A fish with haplotype b or c could be treated as carrying variation absent from the original broodstock.
- **Recaptured hatchery fish:** Recaptured F2 fish that survived in Lake Mohave may be preferable to hatchery-reared fish, because they have survived predators and natural conditions. Each contributes about half as many genes as an F1 parent.
- **Retention target:** A 90% retention of genetic variation, as suggested for other captive endangered species (Soulé et al., 1986), could serve as a goal, though it applies over multiple generations.

### 7. Wild population status and stocking records

- The authors describe three possible categories of fish in Lake Mohave: truly wild fish hatched before 1981 stocking, F1 fish released in 1981, and F2 fish released after 1981.
- Marsh (1997) estimated that "not less than 10% to perhaps as many as two-thirds" of a sample of 17 fish captured between 1989 and 1994 were wild.
- Stocking totals were 41,517 F1 fish (all marked with [[Oxytetracycline Marking]]) and 161,371 F2 fish (only 8.6% marked). Table 8 reports a total of 202,688, which does not match the sum of the two figures (202,888), so the discrepancy should be checked against the original records.

### 8. Effective population size comparisons

- The "high" estimate is a demographic measure (N_ef = 8.5). The "low" estimate is a genetic one (N_ef = 3.5). The low ratio of 3.5/10 = 0.35 is close to the 0.38 reported by Vucetich et al. (1997) for stable populations. Frankham (1995) reported an average effective-to-census ratio of about 0.10.
- A [[Pudovkin Heterozygote-Excess Method]] estimate from Pgm-A gave 1.05 effective breeders. The authors consider this implausibly low, since the minimum possible value is 2, but say it confirms that very few parents contributed.

### 9. Conclusion

The F1 broodstock at Dexter holds only a fraction of the variation present in the wild population, probably equivalent to about 3.5 to 8.5 wild fish. The authors conclude that the future broodstock should combine the remaining F1 fish with newly captured wild fish. Their approach aims to retain as much of the wild population's genetic diversity as possible while the species recovers.
