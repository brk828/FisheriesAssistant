# Underwater Observations of Largemouth Bass Spawning and Survival in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report, by Cal Allan and James Romero, presents diver-based underwater observations of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) reproduction and survival in [[Lake Mead]], a multiple-purpose reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]] in Nevada. The work was a cooperative effort of the [[Nevada Department of Fish and Game]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and the [[National Park Service]]. Data were gathered in four study coves across the [[Boulder Basin]] and [[Virgin Basin]] during the 1973 and 1974 spawning seasons, which differed sharply in lake level and weather.

The study identifies water-level fluctuation, wind and wave exposure, temperature, cover, nest invaders, predation, food availability and the male guardian's behavior as the main factors controlling nesting and fry survival. The authors conclude that nest success depended on stable water levels, sheltered sites, warm spring temperatures and the presence of a guarding male.

## Background & Description

Lake Mead was created in 1935 by [[Hoover Dam]], lies about 30 miles (48.3 km) east of Las Vegas, and has a maximum depth of 500 feet (152.5 m), a shoreline of 847 miles (1363.5 km), and a surface area of 157,900 acres. It supplies flood control, municipal water, power, irrigation and recreation. Largemouth bass were stocked soon after impoundment, and the lake gained national recognition as a prime bass fishery, supported by [[Threadfin Shad]] as forage.

Bass production declined after the construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1964, which created [[Lake Powell]] and reduced Lake Mead's water levels during the early filling years. Proposed causes included reservoir aging, angling pressure, weather and a high catch of spawning bass. Earlier studies on this fishery are cited (Moffett 1943; Jones and Sumner 1954; Deacon 1971; Hoffman and Jones 1973). Diving technology from the late 1960s made direct underwater study possible.

Study objectives were to evaluate seven factors: water fluctuation, wind and wave action, water quality, cover (terrestrial and aquatic), air and water temperature, predation (invertebrate and vertebrate), and human activity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Four coves were chosen, two in each basin. Boulder Basin is more eutrophic than Virgin Basin according to earlier water-quality work. One cover type had gradual slopes, wind exposure and vegetation above and below the water. The other was protected, steep and nearly bare of terrestrial vegetation. Transects averaged 1,200 feet (549 m) and were covered by two or three diver biologists.

**Methods.** Nests were marked with numbered fluorescent flags. At discovery, exposure (compass), depth, slope (inclinometer), substrate and temperature were recorded. Daily records covered water temperature, transparency, dissolved oxygen, pH, guardian presence, egg development, fungus, hatch, and vertebrate and invertebrate predators. Recording thermographs at 10 ft (3.05 m) logged 24-hour temperatures. Fry swarms and fingerlings were counted by divers. Stomach samples came from speared fish preserved in 10% formalin. Plankton was pumped and filtered through a silk bucket.

**Nest substrate.** Typical nests were composed of 2.5% rubble, 31.1% coarse gravel, 41.5% fine gravel, 16.4% sand, 7.4% silt and 0.2% organic debris. Nests were not found on pure sand or silt.

**Weather and water levels.** 1973 was cool and wet with calm, stable water during peak nesting, and water levels rose. 1974 was typical and windy, with receding levels and large temperature swings. The contrast between years was used as a natural comparison.

## Key Findings

**Nest site selection and physical conditions**
- Nest gradients ranged from 3 to 52 degrees. Steep, loose slopes contributed to failures, and bank erosion in 1974 buried or suffocated several nests.
- Nests exposed to open-water wind and wave action were unproductive. Surges reached 10 ft (3.05 m) in depth during moderate storms, and these nests produced no fry.
- Eighty nests were measured in four coves. Mean depth was 10.8 ft (3.3 m) in 1973 (range 4.8–24.5 ft) and 6.1 ft (1.9 m) in 1974 (range 3.7–10.0 ft). Nests were usually placed at the edges of submerged tamarisk, large rocks or ledges, and rarely in dense growth.
- In 1974, debris and algae accumulated on receding shorelines. In 1973, rising water kept substrate clean.

**Temperature and development**
- Bass spawned at temperatures as low as 54°F (12.2°C), below the commonly cited 60°F (15.6°C) threshold. Peak spawning in both years occurred between 60°F (15.5°C) and 65°F (18.3°C), in the last week of April and first week of May.
- Development time shortened as temperature rose. Eggs took 5.4 days at 55–60°F and 3.1 days at 65–75°F. Sac-fry took 7.2 days at 55–65°F and 6.0 days at 70–75°F (1973 data).
- A sudden drop of 4–6°F during windy, cold weather caused erratic spawning and nest abandonment, especially in the mid-60s °F.

**Nest invaders and predators**
- Fungus (*Saprolegnia*) was common, but its effect was inconsistent. Some heavily infested nests produced good fry, while others in 1974 held only dead sac-fry. Saprolegnia's optimum temperature is near the minimum spawning temperature.
- Brown hydra (*Pelmatohydra oligactis*) was common but was not considered a major cause of nest failure.
- Common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) were observed destroying nests by sucking up eggs. Many unobserved nest losses were attributed to carp.
- Water quality (pH, transparency, dissolved oxygen) showed no clear relationship with nesting success.

**Parental behavior**
- The male guards the nest and is essential for fry production. Guards threaten divers by flexing their gill covers. Late-season males were less aggressive, possibly due to temperature.
- Courtship involved a male display in which he rolled on his side, which drew the female back to the nest. Spawning followed with paired vibrations.
- Individual fry swarms often merged, and one guardian sometimes protected up to three swarms.

**Cover and survival**
- Cover value depends on water level management. Aquatic plants (pondweed, cattail, spiny naiad) established in 1972 after stable levels, but rapid flooding in 1973 drowned them. Flooded green terrestrial vegetation supported high 1973 production.
- Tamarisk lost much of its value after one year of flooding, reducing escape cover for fry and fingerlings. Cover also supports food organisms.

**Predation and fish community**
- Predation on young bass follows size: 1–2 inch (2.5–5 cm) fry ate 0.25–0.75 inch fry, and 3-inch (7.5 cm) fingerlings ate 1-inch fry. Young yearlings were not expected to suppress the 1973 year class as much as feared.
- Green sunfish and bluegill were abundant from May and likely increased fry and fingerling losses. Carp left coves by late May or early June. Crappie and channel catfish were present in small numbers.
- Stomach samples (adult bass n=49, yearling bass n=3, crappie n=9, carp n=4, 1974) were dominated by threadfin shad and crayfish.
- Predation was considered normal attrition, with surplus fry removed in strong years.

**Food availability**
- In 1973, zooplankton formed 50–100% of sampled volume, with Cladocera at 70–80% of that. In 1974, plankton volume was much lower, at 15–20% zooplankton, and copepods made up 70–30% of the zooplankton (reported as stated). Low 1974 plankton coincided with the most damaging fry loss.

**Migration and census**
- By late May through July, many fingerlings moved to 25–60 ft (7.6–13.3 m), so cove census captured only part of the young-of-year population. Residual cove counts were considered a reliable index of production.

**Late spawning and angler effects**
- Late nesting was confirmed in late June 1973 in both basins and may produce small year classes in poor years.
- In a trial in Indian Canyon during 1974, 50 active nests and eight fry balls were marked. Seven anglers fished the area, and five male bass (10%) were removed. Peak angling appeared to precede peak nesting, when bass are most available to anglers.

**Conclusions**
- Stable water levels, sheltered nest sites, sustained warmth and a present male guardian were key to bass reproduction and survival.
- Water-level management is central to balancing aquatic and terrestrial cover.
