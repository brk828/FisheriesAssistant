# Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program: 24th Annual Researchers Meeting Abstracts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of abstracts from the 24th Annual Researchers Meeting of the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], held January 15–16, 2003, in Grand Junction, Colorado. The abstracts cover recovery of endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] across the Colorado River basin. Topics include a self-sustaining pond population at [[Cibola High Levee Pond]], protected habitat creation on the [[Lower Colorado River]] floodplain, [[Selenium Contamination]] remediation at [[Stewart Lake]], floodplain stocking experiments on the [[Green River]], acclimation effects on stocked subadults, larval sampling in the [[Gunnison River]], and fish community surveys of tributaries to [[Grand Valley]] critical habitat.

## Background & Description

The recovery program addresses fishes listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Its main tools are hatchery stocking, habitat renovation and protection, and monitoring of wild recruitment. Several abstracts note that predation by introduced (nonnative) fishes on early life stages limits juvenile recruitment to the spawning stock. The document is a meeting proceedings compilation and contains no single research design. Its sections come from several agencies, including the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], and the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predation pressure:** Nonnative fishes and predatory species are repeatedly identified as a barrier to razorback sucker recruitment. Floodplain depressions that dry and refill reset nonnative fish populations to zero, which the Green River study used to test larval survival.
- **Water quality:** Selenium exceeded aquatic life standards in all sampled tributary sites. Concentrations ranged from 11.2 µg/L (Oct. 2001) to 56.0 µg/L (Mar. 2002). Waters were described as highly conductive, alkaline systems.
- **Hydrology:** Seasonal flows drive habitat availability. Extremely low flows in 2002 eliminated suitable light-trap sites on the Gunnison River.
- **Pond dynamics:** Constructed ponds with renovated substrates can support self-sustaining native populations, but egg predation by juvenile bonytail may limit razorback sucker recruitment.

## Key Findings

**1. Cibola High Levee Pond (Blythe, CA; 2.4 ha)**
- The community is reported as 99.85% native.
- Population estimates: about 7,800 bonytail (95% CL 5,955–11,630) and about 1,100 razorback sucker (844–1,190). Thousands of bonytail under 10 cm were not included.
- Carrying capacity is 4,350 fish/ha (635 kg/ha).
- Bonytail spawned on rip-rap, and fry used beaver-den tunnel entrances. Large schools of small (<15 cm) bonytail were observed excavating spawning gravel and consuming exposed eggs, which may be more harmful to razorback sucker, since its gametes are deposited among more accessible gravels.

**2. Native Fish Protected Habitats (Lower Colorado River)**
- The Bureau of Reclamation is developing 600 acres of protected habitat as mitigation. Two waterbodies totaling 210 acres are designated as NFPHs.
- Beal Lake (Havasu NWR) was renovated in December 2001 and stocked with 10,000 fingerling razorbacks in January 2002. No razorbacks were captured in the November monitoring, but more than 1,300 fish of five other species were collected.
- The Farm Ponds (Imperial NWR) were renovated in October 2002 and again in December. Four fish of two species were collected in the November evaluation.
- The success benchmark is recruitment of naturally spawned fish to maturity.

**3. Stewart Lake Selenium Remediation (Utah)**
- Remediation is adaptive and includes draining the lake, diverting irrigation return-flow drains, constructing new inlet and outlet structures, tilling sediments, and adding calcium oxide (lime).
- Planned activities include removing selenium-contaminated seeps on the north end and further sediment treatment. The long-term goal is a functioning wetland with nesting, nursery and foraging habitat for endangered fish.

**4. Floodplain Larval Survival (Green River, UT)**
- Larvae were stocked into a dry floodplain depression that was refilled by pumping river water, with nonnative fish present in partitioned sections.
- Larvae survived and grew to lengths of up to 115 mm by mid-summer, indicating that large numbers may overwhelm nonnative predation.

**5. Acclimated vs. Non-acclimated Subadults (Green River)**
- About 2,000 subadults (>250 mm) stocked directly from the Ouray National Fish Hatchery (1997–2001) were compared with about 1,100 fish that were reared in off-channel wetlands and entered the river voluntarily.
- A three-pass electrofishing survey from Split Mountain Canyon (rkm 352) to the confluence (rkm 0) recaptured 74 acclimated fish versus 29 hatchery-stocked fish.
- Downstream distribution was roughly equal between groups, so survival rather than dispersal appears to differ. The authors suggest acclimation is an important element of reintroduction.

**6. Larval Razorback Sucker Sampling (Gunnison River, 2002)**
- Wild razorback suckers were last captured in 1981 (three near Delta). Stocking began in April 1994. As of November 2001, a total of 18,400 fish had been stocked, about 95% of them at Delta (rm 57).
- Sampling ran May 2 to June 20 2002. Seine surveys were conducted 268 times, with 168 successful, and eight light-trap nights were run (six at Whitewater, two near Delta).
- Eight razorback larvae were identified (five certain, three tentative). Seven were collected in the lower reaches, and all eight were collected between May 21 and June 6.

**7. Washes and Tributaries Surveys (Grand Valley, 2001–2002)**
- Surveys covered five washes and creeks in October 2001, March 2002 and October 2002. A total of 2,968 fish was collected.
- Native species made up 47% of catch (1,383 fish). Flannelmouth sucker was 66.5% of natives, roundtail chub 14.8%, speckled dace 9.7%, and bluehead sucker 9%.
- Nine nonnative species made up 53% of the catch. Red shiner was 63% of 1,585 nonnatives, and fathead minnow was reported as 17.5%.
- Several age classes of nonnative species suggested year-round resident populations. Size distributions suggested native species are reproducing.
- Selenium exceeded state aquatic life standards at all sites.
