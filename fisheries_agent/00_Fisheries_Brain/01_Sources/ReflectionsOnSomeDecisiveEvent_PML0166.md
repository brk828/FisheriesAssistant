# Reflections on Some Decisive Events in the Early Life of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-ontogeny

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1984 essay by [[Eugene K. Balon]] (University of Guelph) argues for a saltatory model of fish ontogeny, in which development proceeds through discrete steady states separated by rapid transitions called thresholds, rather than through gradual change. The paper refines the [[Theory of Saltatory Ontogeny]], defines the hierarchical life-history model (periods, phases, steps), and critiques common assumptions about when ontogeny begins, what a larva is, and how early-life stages should be identified. It is a conceptual and theoretical paper; it reports no new original sample data.

## Background & Description

The essay is reprinted from *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 113, No. 2, March 1984), copyright American Fisheries Society. The text is partly degraded by OCR, and some passages and figure labels are unreadable; the summary below relies on the legible content.

Balon traces the saltatory concept to [[Vasnetsov]] (1953), who applied it to fish ontogeny, and notes its earlier use in plants. He distinguishes saltatory development from simply variable developmental rates or insect-type instar sequences. In his view, structures forming a system (an organ system) align their development so they become functional together, initiating a new vital function such as photoresponse, change of substratum, or nutrition at an accelerated rate.

**Key definitions:**
- **Threshold:** a rapid transition from one steady state of organ–organ or organism–environment interaction to another.
- **Period:** the longest interval of ontogeny. Balon describes four or five: embryo, larva (often missing), juvenile, adult, and senescence.
- **Phase:** a shorter interval within a period, marked by a developmental block.
- **Step:** the shortest saltatory interval.
- **Stage:** an instantaneous state of ontogeny, not a synonym for interval.
- **Activation:** the instantaneous event that starts ontogeny in the ovum; Balon argues it is more general than fertilization, citing gynogenesis as evidence.

Balon's example in Figure 1 is the Danubian cyprinid [[Danube Bream]] (*Abramis ballerus*), whose ontogeny is documented in Balon (1959) as three consecutive steps. The embryo-to-larva transition is presented as one of the most decisive thresholds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Balon argues that thresholds concern both environmental integration and purely endogenous developmental change. He links early-life evolution to environment in several ways:

- **Planktonic habitat:** the plankton is described as a "soup" rich in food particles and space for dispersal, which may justify retaining vulnerable larvae in most marine fishes.
- **Larval retention:** many fishes lack a larval stage. Their embryos develop directly into juveniles with adult structures, sometimes passing through an intermediate "alevin" state with mixed feeding (Balon 1980).
- **Reproductive evolution:** survival of young is enhanced by larger endogenous food supply and parental care. The sequence runs from scattering eggs, to guarding a clutch, to carrying the clutch on or in the parent's body. Brood care reduces predation and buffers water-level fluctuations (cited from Moser 1981).
- **Egg and embryo tolerance:** experiments on [[Chinook Salmon]] eggs (Alderdice et al.) are cited for temperature–incubation relationships and for effects of low dissolved oxygen on salmon eggs.

Balon also notes that populations of the same species may differ more in early-ontogeny characters than in adult phenotypes, so developmental studies from one area cannot represent the whole species range.

## Key Findings

1. **Ontogeny is saltatory, not gradual.** Balon states that assuming a single correlation between variables of consecutive stages, or that ontogeny is gradual, "can no longer be justified."
2. **Hatching is not a key developmental event.** Hatching is a process rather than an instantaneous event, and the larval period can be long. Balon argues that hatching is rarely the decisive event for ultimate survival value; the transition to exogenous feeding is more important.
3. **Larvae are not universal in fishes.** The belief that all fishes have larvae is described as a bias from marine studies. Many fishes develop directly into juveniles.
4. **Metamorphosis is a remodelling event.** A larger endogenous food supply allows some fishes to produce definitive organs directly, avoiding metamorphosis.
5. **Identification keys need developmental foundations.** Keys for eggs, embryos, larvae and juveniles are best built after detailed ontogenetic studies. Misunderstanding of early stages often comes from "larval taxonomy" approaches.
6. **Methodological caution.** Balon warns that developmental data from one geographic area cannot represent a whole species, and that threshold recognition affects how experiments are designed, data interpreted, and conclusions drawn.
