# Gila Trout Recovery and the Impact of Livestock Grazing in the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This magazine feature (Trout, Winter 1995) examines the endangered [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) and the recovery program run by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[U.S. Forest Service]] and the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]]. The Gila trout has been listed as endangered since 1966. The author argues that livestock grazing, especially on the [[Diamond Bar Allotment]], is the principal obstacle to recovery, degrading riparian habitat, silting spawning gravel and fragmenting populations. The piece also describes political resistance from ranchers and local "Wise Use" groups, and the agencies' reluctance to confront it. It frames the recovery effort as stalled, with the species at risk of extinction rather than near downlisting.

## Background & Description

The Gila trout is a native, desert-adapted salmonid of the Gila River drainage. The article reports that it tolerates water temperatures up to 80 degrees Fahrenheit, which is unusual for a trout. Its appearance includes gold flanks, fine spotting on the head and back, quartz-colored fin margins, yellow slashes below the lower jaw and pink bands on the sides of most adults. It is not a cutthroat or rainbow trout. The species was first described in the scientific literature 45 years before the article was written. Historically it hybridized with fish in the Gila River, and it was called "speckled trout" in the 19th century. Its habitat has been reduced to a few small headwater populations, and the recovery goal is to replicate the original five strains in five secure streams. Four strains have been replicated so far.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article describes several threats to Gila trout habitat:

- **Livestock grazing:** Cattle trample riparian banks and flood plains. Stripped vegetation lets runoff cut "headcuts" that lower water tables and dry streams.
- **Sedimentation and water quality:** Overgrazing upstream degrades water quality and silts spawning gravel. Algae and bacteria dominate standing water such as [[Wall Lake]].
- **Stock tanks:** Proposed earthen tanks in wilderness springs and wet meadows would increase grazing and alter habitat that feeds Gila trout streams.
- **Hybridization and non-native trout:** Non-native salmonids have contaminated native streams. Rainbow-Gila hybrids now dominate parts of [[Black Canyon Creek]].
- **Catastrophic events:** Heavy silt-laden runoff in August 1988 eliminated 90 percent of the McKnight Creek population. A later fire and flood affected Main Diamond Creek.
- **Predation and accidents:** Herons and a snake killed roughly 300 of 556 fish evacuated to federal hatcheries after the 1988 event.

The author also notes that beaver activity and a one-acre beaver pond on Diamond Creek occur in the same degraded flood plain.

## Key Findings

- **Population decline:** Gila trout populations have been reduced to a few small headwater populations. Gila Watch director Susan Shock states there are "about one tenth as many Gila trout alive today as there were in 1986." The most stable population, perhaps as many as 9,000 fish, was lost in the 1988–1989 disturbances.
- **Stalled downlisting:** In 1987 the recovery team was close to proposing downlisting from endangered to threatened. After the 1988 losses, the team reassessed the strategy. Downlisting would make legal angling possible.
- **Grazing overstock:** A 1983 four-year range study recommended cutting the Diamond Bar Allotment from 1,188 to 833 head. The Forest Service later reversed this under pressure from the bank that held the permit. The draft preferred alternative in the environmental impact study would reduce the allotment to 600–800 head and authorize 20 wilderness stock tanks, down from 33 previously approved.
- **Grazing trespass:** Permittee Kit Laney has continued to graze trespass cattle on Main Diamond Creek, which the Forest Service acquired and which is off-limits to grazing. Gila Watch filed suit over this on November 15.
- **Knowledge gap:** The Gila Trout Recovery Plan states that studies specifically on livestock effects on Gila trout have not been done. The author argues that other trout studies make the effects clear.
- **Restoration blocked:** Restoration of Mineral and Willow creeks was delayed in 1994 after opposition over antimycin, a toxicant used to remove competing non-native trout. The Gila Fish and Gun Club favors hatcheries over wild, self-sustaining populations.
- **Political opposition:** Local groups such as People for the West, the Catron County Commission and Wise Use ranchers have opposed the Endangered Species Act and federal land management. The article describes them as using intimidating rhetoric.
- **Recovery leadership:** Dr. David Propst, who leads the three-man recovery team, acknowledged that the agencies "haven't moved as aggressively as we should have." Propst's 1992 *Southwestern Naturalist* paper argued that past strategies needed re-evaluation.
- **Habitat recovery timeline:** Propst estimates that if cattle were removed from Black Canyon Creek, habitat recovery would take about five years before pure Gila trout could be reintroduced.
- **Citizen action:** The article lists the Forest Service district ranger and Chief of the Forest Service as contacts for public comment on grazing reduction.
