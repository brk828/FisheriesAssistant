# Colorado River Commission of Nevada Comments on Lower Colorado River Biological Opinion

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species #ESA-section7

## Executive Summary

This document comprises formal comments submitted by the [[Colorado River Commission]] of Nevada to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] regarding the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Biological Opinion]] on Lower Colorado River Operations and Maintenance (April 30, 1997). The Commission generally supports conservation efforts but raises substantial legal, scientific, and procedural concerns about the Opinion's jeopardy determinations, baseline definitions, and reasonable and prudent alternatives (RPAs). Key issues include: (1) inappropriate retrospective application of [[Endangered Species Act]] requirements to pre-existing conditions; (2) inadequate definition of proposed federal actions; (3) potential interference with the collaborative [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCRMSCP); (4) scientific uncertainties in species status assessments; and (5) economic impacts of habitat protection measures. The comments address three endangered fish species ([[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]]), the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], and [[Flat-tailed Horned Lizard]].

## Background & Description

The [[Biological Opinion]] evaluates effects of [[Bureau of Reclamation]] operations and maintenance activities on listed species throughout the [[Lower Colorado River]] from [[Lake Mead]] to the [[Southerly International Boundary]] with Mexico. The action area encompasses approximately 3,500 miles of historic habitat for some species, now reduced to 344-1,824 miles depending on species. The Opinion concluded that proposed operations would jeopardize the continued existence of [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*).

The [[Colorado River Commission]] participated in the [[LCRMSCP]] Steering Committee since 1993, representing Nevada water and power interests. The Commission arranged funding from Nevada stakeholders for development of a long-term, consensus-based multi-species conservation program intended to provide regulatory certainty while protecting listed species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Lower Colorado River]] ecosystem has been fundamentally altered by dam construction, water diversions, and non-native species introductions beginning in the 1800s. Major dams include [[Hoover Dam]], [[Davis Dam]], and [[Parker Dam]], which converted free-flowing riverine habitat to reservoir systems. Water temperatures below dams are too cold for native fish larvae, though adults survive. The river historically supported extensive populations of large-river native fishes but experienced dramatic declines coinciding with introduction of [[Common Carp]] and catfish before major dam construction.

[[Razorback Sucker]] populations in [[Lake Mohave]] represent a senescent cohort with no natural recruitment since the 1950s, though adults continue spawning. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] documented that predation by non-native fishes is the dominant factor preventing recruitment, not physical habitat conditions per se. Over 8,000 razorback suckers have been reintroduced to Lake Mohave through collaborative efforts.

[[Bonytail Chub]] has been eliminated from at least 85% of historic range. Critical habitat designation encompasses 312 miles (14% of historic habitat), with 36% occurring in the action area. The species' decline correlates with both dam construction and non-native fish establishment.

Riparian habitat for [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] has been both created and destroyed by dam operations. Historic photographs show virtually no riparian vegetation in [[Grand Canyon]] and downstream areas before dam construction. Current habitats include both permanent sites in river corridors and temporary sites in reservoir deltas subject to inundation. The Opinion documented approximately 454 WIFL territories, though significant unsurveyed habitat remains. [[Bureau of Reclamation]] surveys in 1996 found flycatchers at 15 of 27 sites (55.6%), contrasting with 10% occupancy rates cited for rangewide surveys.

## Key Findings

**Legal and Procedural Issues:**

1. The Opinion's "jeopardy threshold" concept inappropriately sets environmental baseline before past Bureau actions rather than at time of consultation, requiring restoration to pre-dam conditions not mandated by [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7.

2. The proposed action lacks sufficient specificity to permit fair analysis of effects or establish clear standards for re-initiation of consultation. Describing actions as a "program" rather than discrete actions merges past and future operations inappropriately.

3. Some Secretarial actions are not discretionary because they are dictated by federal statutes and contracts (water delivery schedules, power revenue agreements), yet the Opinion treats them as subject to modification.

4. The Opinion effectively implements the shelved [[Lower Colorado River Basin Management Plan]] without public participation, undermining the collaborative [[LCRMSCP]] process.

5. Required timelines (completion by January 1, 1999; implementation by May 15, 2001) may conflict with MSCP development schedule and predetermined solutions before scientific evaluation.

**Scientific Concerns - Razorback Sucker:**

1. Historic range figures are inconsistent (3,500 miles vs. 2,300 miles cited in different sections). Final critical habitat designation is 1,724 miles (49% of historic range), not 1,824 miles as stated.

2. Current status in Lower Basin is improving due to Native Fish Work Group efforts. The senescent population will likely be replaced within two years, providing time for long-term solutions.

3. Fecundity varies substantially between Upper Basin (46,740 eggs/female) and Lower Basin populations (100,800 eggs/female in [[Lake Mohave]]), but this distinction is not clearly presented.

4. Stocking programs perpetuate genetic diversity and provide sufficient numbers to evaluate recovery methods, contradicting Opinion's statement that "essential conservation efforts continue to be impaired or precluded."

5. Wild fry from Lake Mohave represent largest available genetic diversity for reintroduction below [[Parker Dam]].

**Scientific Concerns - Bonytail Chub:**

1. Historic range estimates are contradictory (3,500 miles on p.38 vs. 2,300 miles on p.100). The 3,500-mile figure actually refers to razorback sucker, not bonytail.

2. Critical habitat is 312 miles (14% of historic range), not 344 miles (15%) as stated; 36% occurs in action area, not 32%.

3. Proposed reintroduction to [[Lake Mead]] raises concerns about hybridization with [[Humpback Chub]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. Genetics study funded by [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] is incomplete; requiring action before results are available is premature and may harm both species.

4. Cause-and-effect reasons for bonytail decline remain insufficiently understood. Requiring extraordinary efforts before scientific understanding is established may take decades and waste resources if approaches prove ineffective.

5. "Experimental, non-essential" designation is more appropriate than full protection until cause-and-effect relationships are established.

**Scientific Concerns - Southwestern Willow Flycatcher:**

1. Opinion relies heavily on Whitfield and Strong (1995) California study without independent evaluation. Alternative explanations for observed differences in fledgling survival exist but are not considered (e.g., fledglings from late nests may be predisposed to nest in alternative sites as adults).

2. Characterizing birds absent in year two as "extirpated" lacks documentation. The Opinion acknowledges "it is not known whether these events represent mortality of flycatchers, changes in habitat quality or simply a tendency to move inherent to this species," yet presumes extirpation in every case.

3. Adjacent habitat to [[Lake Mead]] delta is characterized as "marginal" without supporting data, contradicting field observations by numerous biologists on May 6, 1997. [[Bureau of Reclamation]] consultant suspected 8-12 additional territories in [[Grand Canyon National Park]], with more likely on [[Hualapai Tribe]] lands.

4. Recreation is identified as major threat without specific documentation of spatial and temporal relationships between recreational activities and occupied habitat. Database of specific impacts should be provided.

5. Population estimate of 454 territories is incomplete. Unsurveyed portions of Lake Mead inflow area alone may contain 23-32 additional territories. Significantly more survey work is needed.

6. Treefall characterized as "widespread" lacks factual basis from control area comparisons. Treefall was common phenomenon in historic Colorado River due to extreme flooding (Grinnell 1914). Beaver activity may contribute to treefall but is not evaluated.

7. High selenium levels and effects on reproduction are stated without supporting Lower Colorado River data or references.

**Habitat and Management Issues:**

1. Terms "protection" and "preservation" are inadequately defined. Strict interpretation could remove all floodplain areas from operating range for Reclamation facilities, with enormous implications for Nevada's water supply allocation.

2. Reasonable and prudent alternative requires implementing management strategies regardless of assessment findings in step #2, which is unreasonable.

3. Daily water level fluctuations for hydropower are not demonstrated to impact endangered fish spawning or nursery habitat. No Lower Colorado River study supports need for reduction in fluctuations, which would severely impact hydropower generation.

4. Cowbird trapping standards are inconsistent: required for Reclamation lands regardless of parasitism rate, but only when exceeding 10% on other agency lands. Based on Sferra et al. 1997 data, only 6 of 20 sites exceed 10% parasitism. Consistent standard should be established.

5. Restoring Yuma Clapper Rail habitat within one year is not technically feasible; suitable habitat cannot develop in such brief timeframe.

6. Ecological restoration through artificial floods may conflict with goal of protecting specific habitats. Upper Colorado River studies show flooded bottomlands produce significant non-native fish, potentially detrimental to endangered species.

**Additional Species Comments:**

1. [[Common Carp]]: Bevans et al. (1996) documented endocrine system disruption, not adverse effects on reproductive organs as Opinion states. Relationship to razorback sucker remains undetermined.

2. [[Flat-tailed Horned Lizard]]: Preservation plan appears sound but questions remain about ORV exclusion methods (fencing vs. signage) and potential effects of agricultural chemical overspray on ant populations and lizard health.

3. [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]: Apparent conflict between statements that dams created "ideal" stable marsh conditions (p.68) and that habitat trends are downward and not "secure" (p.70) requires clarification.

**Recommendations:**

1. Economic impacts of implementing reasonable and prudent measures should be evaluated, particularly for floodplain species where absolute protection could prevent flooding or inundation.

2. Careful editing needed to correct citation omissions, inconsistent data, and unsupported conclusions.

3. Modifications to biological opinion should be discussed in open public forum with active public involvement.

4. Use of surrogate species to measure take of bonytail and razorback sucker requires additional criteria, as different behavior patterns affect entrainment susceptibility.

5. Assessment of need for reducing water level fluctuations should precede any requirement for reduction, given severe impacts to hydropower generation.
