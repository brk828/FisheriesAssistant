# Gila Box Geothermal Resources Leasing Proposal: Draft Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This draft Environmental Assessment (EA), prepared by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) Safford District Office, evaluates a proposal to offer staged geothermal steam leases across the Gila Box Assessment Area in Graham and Greenlee Counties, Arizona. The proposed action (Alternative A) covers 181,307.81 total acres, including 161,533.5 acres of Federal mineral estate, with nine lease applications filed. The No Action alternative (Alternative B) would prohibit geothermal leasing.

Scoping narrowed the original total-lease concept to leasing with [[No Surface Occupancy]] provisions for land within about one-half mile of perennial streams. The EA concludes that Alternative A, after mitigation, will have minor adverse impacts on soils, vegetation and wildlife, and is the preferred alternative. The document is a summary of a broad ecosystem assessment; it contains no fish sampling data, and the only aquatic species it names are the [[Woundfin]] minnow and associated riparian wildlife.

## Background & Description

**Legal basis and purpose.** The [[Geothermal Steam Act of 1970]] authorizes the Secretary of the Interior to lease geothermal steam and associated resources on administered lands. The stated purpose is to reduce dependence on imported oil, with possible economic benefits from electrical power, space heating, aquaculture and heat processing.

**Stage leasing.** Development proceeds through phases: Phase I (Casual Use and limited exploratory work), Phase II (Deep Exploratory), Phase III (Development) and Phase IV (Production). This EA covers only Casual Use and drilling of temperature gradient holes. Each later stage requires a new site-specific assessment, and the [[United States Geological Survey]] (USGS) is lead agency for post-lease operations.

**Governing rules.** Operations are governed by four [[Geothermal Resource Operational Orders]] (GRO Orders 1 to 4, issued January to February 1975 and August 1975) plus later GRO Orders 6 and 7 for pipelines and production metering (Federal Register, December 7, 1976). Standard lease terms and a Notice of Intent form are appended.

**Soils.** Six soil associations were mapped by the Soil Conservation Service, with erosion susceptibility tabulated by slope class. Natural erosion is slight except at open-pit mine and tailings areas, where the document reports composition of roughly 45% open pit mine, 25% unprocessed overburden, 25% chemically treated mine tailings and 5% slag dumps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Surface water.** The principal perennial courses are the Gila and San Francisco Rivers, and Bonita and Eagle Creeks, with many ephemeral or intermittent streams. Gila Basin streamflow has a winter runoff period (November to April) comprising 68% of mean annual rainfall (Minckley, 1979), and summer peaks from convective rainfall.

**Groundwater.** Alluvial fill is the main aquifer. Wells beside channels have water 5 to 6 m deep; tableland wells exceed 43 m. The Gillard Hot Springs system discharges an estimated 1,500 liters per minute along a 45 m seep line on the north bank of the Gila River. The Bonita Creek watershed, mostly outside the area, supplies 80% to 100% of drinking water for the City of Safford.

**Water quality.** Gila River water is generally good but varies with flow: turbidity rises at high flow, while pH, sodium, chloride and total dissolved solids peak at low flow. Groundwater mineralization changes by as much as 100% annually.

**Vegetation.** Riparian flood plains support mesquite and broadleaf species, including *Prosopis juliflora*, *Platanus wrightii*, *Juglans major*, *Populus fremontii*, *Baccharis glutinosa* and *Cynodon dactylon*. Aquatic vegetation occurs at springs, perennial streams and stockwater reservoirs. Minckley (1979) reported 191 algal taxa in the Gila River Complex.

**Wildlife.** The area supports about 154 bird, 58 mammal, 64 reptile and amphibian species, plus many invertebrates. Riparian habitat is the most sensitive and most diverse, with 275 avian individuals per 40 hectares averaged in 1978. Sensitive species include bald eagle, black hawk, osprey, great egret, snowy egret, coatimundi and Gila monster. Terrestrial habitat types, ranked by faunal diversity, are mesquite, pinyon-juniper uplands, terraces and creosote-ocotillo uplands.

**Human values.** Coronado Mountain has been proposed as a [[Research Natural Area]]. Three wilderness inventory units (AZ-4-20, AZ-4-22/23/24 and AZ-4-74) were reviewed; Wilderness Study Areas of 13,470 acres (Gila Box) and 17,422 acres (Turtle Mountain) were established November 15, 1980. Cultural inventories cover 75 surveys, with 55 prehistoric and 15 historic sites recorded, and 10 archaeological and 4 historic sites qualify for the [[National Register of Historic Places]]. The Kearney Campsite and Trail is listed and sits at the confluence of Bonita Creek and the Gila River.

## Key Findings

- **Preferred alternative:** Alternative A, with [[No Surface Occupancy]] near perennial streams. Expected adverse impacts after mitigation are minor.
- **No Action:** Alternative B has no adverse environmental impact but does not meet the purpose and need.
- **Principal concern:** Most significant impacts are tied to perennial streams and riparian zones, as determined in scoping. Riparian damage may take years to recover or never recover, which the EA rates as a moderate adverse impact.
- **Aquatic and protected species:** The Corps of Engineers proposed Camelsback Dam; the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed reintroducing the [[Woundfin]], a federally listed endangered minnow. The area also hosts endangered bald eagle and transient peregrine falcon.
- **Drilling hazards:** Holes are usually under 15 m, up to 610 m with approval. Unlined mud pits can leak toxic fluids into shallow aquifers, and uncased holes can mix aquifers of differing quality.
- **Mitigation:** Drill sites should be at least 300 feet from ephemeral or intermittent streams and springs, and 500 feet from stock ponds. Sites should avoid floodplains. Aircraft should fly at least 500 feet above terrain during the raptor breeding season (February 1 to June 15).
- **Wilderness and cultural protection:** Wilderness values are protected by the [[Interim Management Policy]] and a Wilderness Protection Stipulation. A [[Cultural Resources Cooperative Agreement]] between BLM and USGS (1978) sets procedures for protecting archaeological sites.
- **Irreversible losses:** Some soil, vegetation along road alignments, and non-renewable cultural resources will be permanently lost.
- **Short-term vs. long-term:** Impacts from temperature gradient holes should be minimal and short-term, with long-term resource productivity not appreciably affected.
