# Nitrogen Dynamics During Succession in a Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 *Ecology* study by [[Nancy B. Grimm]] (Arizona State University) quantifies nitrogen (N) cycling in Sycamore Creek, a lowland [[Sonoran Desert]] stream, using seven 24-hour ("diel") input-output budgets collected at different stages of recovery after [[Flash Flooding]] in 1981–1983. The study tests the [[Vitousek and Reiners Model]], which predicts that ecosystem N retention rises during early-to-middle succession and falls late in succession as biomass approaches steady state. Retention data broadly supported the prediction, though the late-stage decline could not be fully tested within the 38-day sequence. Fish are a minor component of the system (the most abundant fish, *Agosia chrysogaster*, contributed a small share of N storage), so the note is primarily about the stream ecosystem rather than ichthyology.

## Background & Description

The study site is Sycamore Creek, which has continuous flow in winter but breaks into six perennial segments during dry parts of the year. Diel budgets (Diels I–VII) were conducted at two sites dominated by coarse sand and fine gravel substrates. Summer [[Flash Flood]] events from localized thunderstorms scour substrates and remove much of the biota, especially algae and small macroinvertebrates, but recede within hours.

Desert streams were chosen because:
- Nutrient transformations are exaggerated by high temperature, high biotic standing crops, rapid turnover, and a high surface-area-to-volume ratio.
- Longitudinal N decline is pronounced, so net retention by a stream segment is readily measured.
- N is potentially limiting to primary production, and N:P ratios are low.

The fish component of interest is [[Agosia Chrysogaster]] (Cyprinidae), the most abundant fish in the creek. Its wet-mass standing crop ranged from 7 to 17 g/m² and averaged 13 g/m² (n = 4), converted to dry mass using a DM:wet mass ratio of 0.2 and used in all budget calculations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nitrogen storage:** Total N storage in the desert stream was 3–9 g/m², lower than in forest streams in Oregon (12 g/m²) and Quebec (22 g/m²). Up to 93% of N was in algae and autochthonous detritus, compared with >99% in allochthonous detritus in forest streams. Consumers held up to 14% of N.
- **Phosphorus and N limitation:** Soluble reactive phosphorus was high (42–79 µg/L) and dissolved inorganic N was variable (36–154 µg/L), giving atomic DIN:SRP ratios below 8 on all dates, consistent with widespread N limitation in the region (see [[Nitrogen Limitation]]).
- **Primary producers:** Periphyton standing crops were highest when floods had not occurred for months. [[Cladophora Glomerata]] progressively replaced early diatoms at site 1, while bluegreen mats (*Anabaena* and *Oscillatoria*), diatoms and flocculent detritus dominated at site 2.
- **Macroinvertebrates:** Standing crops ranged from 84,000 to over 100,000 individuals/m² and from 0.4 to 7.5 g/m² dry mass. Collector-gatherers made up over 80% of numbers and biomass. The mayfly *Leptohyphes* became dominant (82% of numbers, 90% of biomass) by day 27 at site 1.
- **Metabolism:** Gross primary production ranged from 2.74 to 12.25 g O₂·m⁻²·d⁻¹ and ecosystem respiration from 3.58 to 6.51 g O₂·m⁻²·d⁻¹. Surface sediments were autotrophic on most days.
- **Denitrification and hyporheic storage:** Denitrification was not measured, and the hyporheic zone was not sampled. The author estimated deep-sediment N storage could be about 14 g/m², roughly twice surface benthic storage, making it potentially significant.

## Key Findings

1. **Inputs and retention:** Inputs of N exceeded outputs on most study dates. Retention reached as high as 400 mg·m⁻²·d⁻¹, and outputs exceeded inputs on only one date (Diel IV). Retention was mainly inorganic N, presumed due to autotrophic assimilation.
2. **Transport composition:** Dissolved N dominated inputs (16–58% of total input was DIN and 37–69% DON), while particulate N in transport was 9–15% of inputs and was mostly autochthonous.
3. **Algal uptake:** Mean nitrate-N assimilation rates by periphyton (7.77 mg·m⁻²·h⁻¹, n = 157) were sufficient to account for whole-system nitrate retention in the diel studies.
4. **Test of the model:** Nitrogen retention followed the predicted pattern of increase from early to middle succession and decline in late succession across the seven diel studies. Inorganic N retention in the 38-day sequence rose from 3% (day 7) to 8% (day 11) and 12% (day 27) of inputs, with no late-stage decline observed. The author notes that longer sequences would be needed to test the late-stage decline and whether net biomass accrual also declines.
5. **Particulate export:** Particulate N was retained early in succession but lost later (Diels III, IV and VII), mainly as algal sloughing. Losses were small relative to DIN retention (9% and 60% of DIN retention for Diels III and VII).
6. **Consumers:** Consumers, including invertebrates (1–11%) and fish (3–8%), were significant N storage compartments, higher than in comparable forest streams. Fish are relatively large storage compartments in aquatic systems, and their long-term storage may be their most significant nutrient-cycling effect.
7. **Mass balance comparison:** Sycamore Creek was compared with [[Watershed 10]] (Oregon), [[Beaver Creek]] (Quebec), and [[Bear Brook]] (New Hampshire) in a four-stream table. Forest streams stored large amounts of woody debris (59–80% of N stored).
8. **Emergence loss:** Aquatic insect emergence removed N at rates that were high on an areal basis, though it accounted for only about 1% of total input.
