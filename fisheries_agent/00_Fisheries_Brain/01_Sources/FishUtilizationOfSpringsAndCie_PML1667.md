# Fish Utilization of Springs and Cienegas in the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Background & Description

**Desert springs** show a wide range of physical, chemical and biotic characteristics:
- Flow ranges from tiny seeps with barely perceptible flow to large vents producing hundreds to thousands of liters per second.
- Temperatures range from near ambient to lethally hot.
- Some springs carry toxic levels of dissolved carbon dioxide or high ionic concentrations that exclude fish. Others are relatively benign and support more diverse faunas.
- Features common to most desert springs are constant environmental conditions, geographic isolation, and endemism of fishes and other organisms.

**Cienegas** are marshy wetlands found at any elevation in North America. The most important to this discussion lie at low (<1000 m) to moderate (~2000 m) elevations along stream headwaters or associated with springs, at either outflow points or along spring runs. They are typically:
- Fed by springs, and so are relatively stable in their hydrology.
- Variable in size, from shallow habitat patches at points of slack flow along streams or springs to large, deeper wetlands covering many hectares. Larger cienegas with pool formations typically support fishes.
- Often exposed to harsh water-quality conditions and sometimes subject to drying.

Many springs and cienegas have vertical or sloping walls and subsurface structure that creates isolated habitats, and some have been altered by people.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Isolation and endemism:** Desert spring and cienegas fishes are often restricted to a single spring, spring complex, or a few adjacent wetlands. Many are endemic subspecies or species, and a large, diverse mollusk and invertebrate fauna occurs alongside them.
- **Human impact:** Water diversion, damming, groundwater pumping, and introduced predators such as large, predatory fishes have drastically altered many desert wetlands. These pressures have eliminated or reduced many native populations.
- **Historical refugia:** Desert wetlands once served as refugia for native fishes, but the author notes that their value has been greatly reduced by human-caused habitat loss.
- **Regional extent:** The author states that springs and cienegas make up only a small percentage of surface water in the arid Southwest, yet support a disproportionately large share of the regional ichthyofauna. The exact percentage is illegible in the scan.

## Key Findings

1. **High dependence on wetlands:** A large portion of the regional fish fauna depends directly on desert springs and cienegas for survival. Some species are found only in these habitats.
2. **Documented losses:** Habitat alteration and elimination have already caused irreversible losses of a high percentage of native fishes in the arid Southwest.
3. **Case example, Sonora Topminnow:** The *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (Sonora Topminnow) was described in 1941. It was once one of the most common fishes in the southern part of the Colorado River delta and occurred widely in Sonora. It has since declined sharply and is now at risk, linked to wetland alteration and introduced predators.
4. **Case example, Cuatro Ciénegas:** This intermontane basin in the Chihuahuan Desert contains numerous spring-head habitats and cienegas with high rates of endemism. Many of its fishes and other aquatic animals are restricted to a few springs or spring outflows. The scan does not legibly report exact species counts.
5. **Localized distribution:** The author indicates that fish persist in only about 13 localities within a particular region, most of them springs or cienegas. Only two isolated streams are mentioned as naturally occurring river habitats in that area.
6. **Future threat:** Native wetland fishes are at continuing risk from increasing human population, water use, and proposed changes. The author expects further losses unless conservation of desert aquatic systems is improved.

**Conclusion:** Conservation of desert springs and cienegas is essential to preserving the unique fishes and other aquatic life of the arid Southwest.
