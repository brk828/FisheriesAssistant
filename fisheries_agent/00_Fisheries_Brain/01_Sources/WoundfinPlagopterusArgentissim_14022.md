# Woundfin (Plagopterus argentissimus) - Life History and Status Report

#ichthyology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #plagopterini #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes published and unpublished information on [[Plagopterus argentissimus]] (woundfin), a highly specialized, endemic cyprinid minnow currently restricted to the [[Virgin River]] system in Utah, Arizona, and Nevada. The woundfin is the most silvery of all American minnows and exhibits extreme morphological specializations for life in swift, shallow, sand-bottomed desert streams. Once distributed from the Salt-Verde river confluence near Tempe, Arizona, through the Gila River to Yuma and upstream to the Virgin River system, the species has experienced dramatic range contraction. The report identifies critical knowledge gaps necessary for species conservation and includes speculation based on studies of related genera ([[Meda]] and [[Lepidomeda]]) within the tribe Plagopterini.

## Background & Description

[[Plagopterus argentissimus]] represents the most specialized member of the cyprinid tribe Plagopterini, subfamily Leuciscinae. The species exhibits a distinctive flattened head and belly with an anteriorly-depressed, streamlined torpedo-shaped body characteristic of fishes inhabiting swift, shallow, sand-bottomed streams. This morphology is convergent with species like [[Hybopsis gracilis]] and some western [[Rhinichthys]] dace.

Key morphological features include: expansive falcate fins, lip barbels, reduced eyes, extensive chemoreceptor sensory buds on the gular region and leading pectoral fin-rays, and near-scaleless body with small bone plates in leathery skin. Unique to Plagopterini are modified fin-rays: enlarged, elongated spinose rays in the dorsal fin (the second fitting into a groove in the first), thickened spinelike branched pelvic rays, and spinelike development near pectoral fin-ray bases. Mature males possess greatly thickened, dilated, and denticulate anterior pectoral fin-rays.

Meristic characters (from Miller and Hubbs, 1960): dorsal rays 8-10 (most commonly 8); anal rays 9-11 (most commonly 10); pelvic rays 7 per fin; pharyngeal teeth typically 1,5-4,1; vertebrae 39-41. The species rarely exceeds 75 mm standard length, though occasional females exceed 100 mm.

The taxonomic history is relatively straightforward. Cope (1874) originally described the species and erected the subfamily Plagopterinae. Jordan et al. (1930) elevated the group to family status (Medidae), though this was not widely followed. Hubbs (1955) established the current tribal ranking (Plagopterini), which is generally accepted. The tribe comprises three genera: two monotypic ([[Meda]] and [[Plagopterus]]) and [[Lepidomeda]] with multiple species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Characteristics**: [[Plagopterus]] reaches peak abundance in the mainstream [[Virgin River]], preferring areas of mobile sand bottom where substrate shifts slowly downstream under almost-laminar currents. Depths range from 5-35 cm, with disturbed fish moving to deeper areas. Unlike other plagopterines that inhabit pools ([[Lepidomeda]]) or eddying currents ([[Meda]]), woundfin occupy shifting sand habitats. They rarely ascend tributary streams far past the Virgin River floodplain.

Associated invertebrates are sparse: burrowing dragonfly naiads (Gomphidae), rare chironomid dipterans, and where solid substrates occur, simuliid dipterans, hydropsychid trichopterans, and baetid mayfly nymphs. Larval and post-larval woundfin inhabit backwaters and shorelines in shallow water, moving to deeper, swifter areas with growth.

**Physical-Chemical Environment**: The species tolerates extreme environmental variability documented by U.S. Geological Survey records. Temperature ranges from 1.1°C to 33.4°C. Discharge varies dramatically (minimum 7 cubic feet/minute to maximum 44,174 cubic feet/minute in September). Suspended sediments range from 40 mg/L to 180,000 mg/L. Total dissolved solids vary from 524 to 4,250 mg/L. Conductivity ranges from 900 to 4,490 mmhos/cm at 25°C. pH ranges from 6.9 to 8.4.

Bottom sediments mobilize during flooding to depths of many meters. Turbidity may change by factors exceeding 1,000 in minutes. Thermal features can shift from >30°C to <15°C within minutes during cyclonic storms. Ionic composition varies inversely with discharge.

**Fish Associations**: Currently, 8-9 non-native and 8 native fish species occur in the Virgin River system. [[Plagopterus]] is intimately associated with only 4 native species and one introduced species. Native associates include: [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (flannelmouth sucker), [[Pantosteus]] sp. A, [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (speckled dace - mainstream form), and [[Gila robusta seminuda]] (Virgin River roundtail chub). The introduced [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner) co-occurs with woundfin in lower reaches.

Woundfin typically occur alone over shifting sand bottoms, sometimes accompanied by young flannelmouth suckers. Speckled dace occupy lateral habitats near spring inflows and tributary mouths. [[Pantosteus]] sp. A prefers swifter waters with solid substrates. Flannelmouth suckers inhabit deeper, slower waters behind boulders. [[Gila robusta seminuda]] occupies deepest pools with eddying current. [[Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis]] (Virgin River spinedace) is restricted to spring inflows and creek mouths.

**Distribution Changes**: Original range (late 1800s) extended from the Salt-Verde confluence at Tempe, Arizona, to the Gila River mouth at Yuma, likely including mainstream Colorado River near Yuma, upstream to the Virgin River system. Current range (early 1970s) is restricted to La Verkin Springs on mainstream Virgin River and lower portions of La Verkin and Ash creeks in Utah, downstream to Bunkerville, Nevada. A single specimen was taken from middle [[Moapa River]], Clark County, Nevada in the late 1960s.

Transplantation attempts were made to four localities: [[Hassayampa River]] (Arizona - reproduction occurred 1971, population destroyed by flooding 1972-3), [[Verde River]] (Arizona - 1971, none taken since), [[Sycamore Creek]] (Agua Fria drainage, Arizona - few survived 1972-3 flooding, two gravid specimens preserved August 1973), and [[Paria River]] (Arizona-Utah - to be surveyed September-October 1973).

## Key Findings

**Life History Gaps**: Table 1 presents a comprehensive outline of life history topics rated on a scale of 1 (quantitative data) to 5 (no data). Major knowledge gaps (rating 4-5) exist for: developmental period, hatching, larval development, behavior, migrations, schooling, territorality, learning, instantaneous populations, mortality, recruitment, diseases, parasites, competitors, predators, embryology, anatomy, adaptability, and physiology. Only taxonomy, present range, habitats, and morphology have adequate data (ratings 1-2).

**Feeding Ecology**: No specific food habits studies exist for [[Plagopterus]]. Based on related plagopterines and morphology, woundfin likely feed primarily on organic materials moving along sandy bottoms, with occasional surface feeding on insects. The powerful pharyngeal arches, short intestinal tract, and morphological similarity to [[Hybopsis gracilis]] suggest carnivorous feeding. Aquarium observations show initial bottom feeding with preference for lowermost water column, later learning to accept drifting food. The species demonstrates at least partial reliance on visual cues but likely depends heavily on chemoreceptors in turbid conditions.

Related species data: [[Lepidomeda vittata]] feeds on adult aquatic and terrestrial insects from surface and water column, plus aquatic insect larvae and filamentous algae. [[Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis]] utilizes mostly aquatic insect larvae, shifting to algae during scarcity. [[Meda fulgida]] depends heavily on aquatic insect larvae with some adult insects, minimal algae.

**Reproduction**: Spawning timing and behavior remain unobserved for [[Plagopterus]], though young appear in summer collections. Based on other plagopterines, reproduction likely involves: spawning in early summer continuing sporadically through autumn; older, larger females spawning first with possible second complement later; younger females producing single annual complement; sexual maturity in second summer; maximum lifespan not exceeding 4 years with few individuals living beyond 2 years.

Reproductive cues may involve stream discharge variations. If ova are deposited on/within bottom materials (as in other plagopterines), spawning would be restricted to low discharge, clear water periods to avoid abrasive action of moving substrate or burial by sand. If ova float or attach to vegetation, spawning may occur during higher water.

Fecundity data from related species: [[Lepidomeda vittata]] produces 650-5,000 ova depending on size; [[Meda fulgida]] spawns 100-300 ova per complement. Breeding behavior in related species involves males patrolling shallow areas in moderate currents, females entering and being attended by multiple males, brief chase followed by egg release on bottom, adults floating downstream, then resuming activities. Ova are adhesive, adhering to substrate until hatching. Young move to stream margins upon hatching, feeding on ostracods, tiny aquatic insects, detritus, and pollen grains.

**Movement Patterns**: Data suggest unusual mobility for plagopterines. [[Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis]] documented movements exceeding 1 kilometer in short periods (Rinne, 1971). Large river segments sometimes devoid of spinedace, other times abundant, indicating major population movements. Similar patterns observed for [[Lepidomeda vittata]] and in Virgin River mainstream collections of [[Plagopterus]].

Systematic Virgin River collecting indicates unexplained disappearance of larger [[Plagopterus]] individuals in late summer, suggesting population segment movements. Weak schooling tendencies observed in wild and aquarium populations. Relative abundance can become exceedingly high: up to 200 individuals in single 8-meter seine haul, often the only species taken. Abundant populations encountered near Mesquite, Nevada; Beaver Dam Creek mouth, Arizona; and La Verkin-Ash creeks confluence, Utah. Largest populations sampled in early summer prior to spawning during spring runoff. Relative abundance lowest in low water periods before new year-class recruitment, possibly related to natural mortality or unknown adult movements.

**Interspecific Interactions**: [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner) severely interacts with [[Meda fulgida]], essentially replacing it in large parts of Gila River basin. In Virgin River system, red shiner is very rare near Littlefield, Arizona (where [[Plagopterus]] is common) but abundant downstream near Bunkerville, Nevada (where woundfin are rare/absent). This reciprocity may indicate competitive interaction or response to unknown physical/chemical gradients.

Predators include: piscivorous birds ([[Megaceryle alcyon]] - kingfisher, [[Ardea herodius]] and other herons) especially during low flow/clear water; [[Gila robusta seminuda]] on all life stages; [[Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis]] on larvae and smaller subadults; introduced [[Micropterus salmoides]] (largemouth bass) and [[Chaenobryttus cyanellus]] (green sunfish) on larvae and young; rare [[Ictalurus punctatus]] (channel catfish) potentially on all life stages.

**Population Dynamics**: Plagopterine fishes generally receive strong reproductive cues from or have reproductive success strongly influenced by stream discharge variations. [[Lepidomeda vittata]] undergoes remarkable abundance and distribution changes as function of discharge increases/decreases, shifting from "endangered" status in drought years to "abundant within restricted range" in high runoff years. One-year-old fish bear primary reproductive burden due to relatively short lifespan.

**Conservation Status**: The species is scarcely studied, endemic, and highly specialized. Major knowledge gaps must be filled for species maintenance. Transplantation efforts require greater care - a verified collection of [[Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis]] near Paria River mouth demonstrates risk that transplanted "rare" or "endangered" species could undergo population explosion and force other organisms toward extinction.

**Environmental Tolerances**: The osmoregulatory capabilities of desert fishes like [[Plagopterus]] must be remarkable given extreme ionic composition variations inversely correlated with discharge. Physical tolerance capabilities during flash floods are equally impressive. Light penetration varies from 100% reaching bottom to none during spates. Prolonged flooding maintains high suspended sediments for extended periods.
