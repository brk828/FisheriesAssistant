# Toxicity of Carbaryl and Malathion to Two Federally Endangered Fishes, as Estimated by Regression and ANOVA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 study by [[Beyers]], [[Kittle]] and [[Carlson]] (Colorado State University) estimates the toxicity of the grasshopper insecticides [[Carbaryl]], [[Sevin-4-Oil]] (a 49% carbaryl formulation) and [[Malathion]] to two federally endangered minnows of the Colorado River Basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). Tests comprised 4-day renewal-acute lethality tests and 32-day early life-stage (ELS) tests. Effect concentrations were derived both by hypothesis testing (ANOVA) and by a [[Linear-Plateau Regression]] threshold model. The authors found that the regression method gave consistently lower effect concentrations, but that the two approaches led to broadly similar conclusions.

## Background & Description

- **Insecticide use and concern:** Carbaryl and malathion control grasshoppers on rangeland and cropland in the western US. Both inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE), disrupting the central nervous system. Despite no-spray buffers, drift and runoff can deliver pesticide to aquatic habitats.
- **Study species:** Both species are large minnows that historically occurred throughout the Colorado River Basin. Populations declined after reservoir construction and other basin management. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listed Colorado Squawfish as endangered in 1967 and Bonytail in 1980.
- **Vulnerable life stage:** Young fish (June to October, first several months) occupy shallow, low-velocity, near-shore habitat with low water exchange, where pesticides may persist. Grasshopper control and the early life stages (<30 days old) therefore overlap.
- **Knowledge gap:** Prior toxicity data for carbaryl and malathion (summarized by [[Mayer and Ellersieck]]) came from species not closely related to these native fishes.
- **Objectives:** (a) estimate 4-day median lethal concentrations (LC50); (b) estimate effect concentrations for survival and growth; (c) compare hypothesis-testing and regression estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Test water:** Dilution water from a well on the Colorado State University campus, aerated and heated to 22 ± 1 °C. Typical ranges were dissolved oxygen 6.1–7.0 mg/L, pH 7.9–8.2, alkalinity 237–259 mg/L as CaCO3, hardness 334–378 mg/L as CaCO3, and specific conductance 720–790 µS/cm. Renewal-acute tests with Colorado Squawfish used differently aged water (pH 8.5–8.6, alkalinity 104–110 mg/L).
- **Hydrolysis:** Carbaryl and malathion hydrolyze rapidly at pH above 7. Concentrations declined by 58% (carbaryl) and 37% (malathion) over 24 h in renewal tests. Breakdown products may be more toxic than parent compounds, so the net effect of rapid hydrolysis is uncertain.
- **Exposure systems:** Renewal-acute tests used 1-L glass beakers renewed every 24 h. ELS tests used a continuous-flow mini-diluter (0.5 dilution factor, 0.055 L/min per replicate aquarium).
- **Acetone carrier:** Acetone never exceeded 0.5 mL/L in any exposure concentration.

## Key Findings

**4-day renewal-acute median lethal concentrations (95% CI):**

| Toxicant | Colorado Squawfish (mg/L) | Bonytail (mg/L) |
|---|---|---|
| Carbaryl | 1.31 (1.23–1.40) | 2.02 (1.79–2.25) |
| Sevin-4-Oil | 3.18 (2.87–3.52) | 3.31 (3.06–3.55) |
| Malathion | 9.14 (8.35–10.0) | 15.3 (14.4–16.4) |

- Sevin-4-Oil was approximately half as toxic as technical carbaryl. Carbaryl:Sevin ratios of 0.40 (squawfish) and 1.24 (bonytail) fell within normal experimental variation, so no synergistic or antagonistic formulation effects were detected.
- Malathion was approximately seven times less toxic than carbaryl.

**32-day ELS effect concentrations (µg/L):**

| Toxicant | Species | NOEC (growth) | Threshold (linear-plateau) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbaryl | Colorado Squawfish | 445 | 364 |
| Malathion | Colorado Squawfish | 1,680 | 455 |
| Carbaryl | Bonytail | 650 | 217 |
| Malathion | Bonytail | 990 | 521 |

- Growth was as sensitive as or more sensitive than survival in most comparisons. The exception was malathion in Colorado Squawfish, where growth was least sensitive.
- Survival NOECs were identical under the angular and logistic transformations.
- Linear-plateau regression explained a significant share of total variation (p = 0.001) in all cases.

**Comparison with other species:**
- Both endangered fishes were about as sensitive to carbaryl as cutthroat, rainbow and brook trout (two to ten times more sensitive than fathead minnow, channel catfish and bluegill for carbaryl, per the acute data).
- For malathion they were one to two orders of magnitude less sensitive than salmonids and bluegill, and roughly as sensitive as fathead minnow and channel catfish.
- In ELS comparisons, carbaryl effect levels overlapped those of fathead minnow (NOEC 210 µg/L, LOEC 680 µg/L). Malathion effect levels were about five to eight times higher than fathead minnow values (NOEC 200 µg/L, LOEC 580 µg/L).
- The authors concluded the endangered species fall near the tolerant end of sensitivity and are roughly equivalent to fathead minnow.

**Statistical method comparison:**
- Linear-plateau thresholds were consistently lower than hypothesis-testing NOECs. Six of eight thresholds were within a factor of two of the NOECs, the rest within a factor of four. In two cases the upper confidence limit of the threshold contained the NOEC.
- The authors attributed the discrepancy mainly to the design: only two replicates per concentration limited statistical power.

**Limitations noted by the authors:**
- Renewal-acute tests were confounded by pH, life stage and body size.
- The 32-day ELS exposure was short compared with 9- and 10-month fathead minnow studies, and reproductive effects were not studied, so chronic toxicity may be underestimated.
- Tested fish were protolarvae to juveniles; embryos were not present during exposure.

**Conclusion:** The authors recommend linear-plateau regression as a parsimonious, threshold-consistent alternative to hypothesis testing for fish concentration-response data. The findings support protecting the vulnerable early life stages of these endangered fishes from pesticide exposure.
