# Life-History of the Harpacticoid Copepod Canthocamptus staphylinus (Jurine)

#copepodology #harpacticoida #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This short paper by W. J. P. [[Smyly]] (Freshwater Biological Association) reports a roughly 19-month field and laboratory study of the life cycle of the freshwater harpacticoid copepod [[Canthocamptus Staphylinus]] in [[Scale Tarn]], a moorland pond near [[Windermere]]. The species shows one generation per year, a dormant encysted-adult phase in summer, and a free-swimming phase from autumn to late spring. Encystment appears to occur only once per individual, and only in unmated, sexually immature adults.

## Background & Description

*Canthocamptus staphylinus* is a small freshwater harpacticoid copepod. Earlier workers, including [[Lauterborn and Wolf]] (1909), [[Kessler]] (1913) and [[Gurney]] (1933), reported that it is abundant in winter but scarce or absent in summer. Gurney noted that adults are numerous and reproductively active in winter and early spring, but that from April onwards adults, though still numerous, are not sexually mature. [[Wolf]] (1905) observed that these sexually immature adults withdraw into the bottom mud and encyst.

Little quantitative work on the life cycle had been published. Smyly therefore sampled the population over about one and a half years using a metal tube sampler described in his earlier work (Smyly, 1957). The sampling approach is central to the study's quantitative basis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Scale Tarn, a moorland pond in the English Lake District. The population is free-swimming in winter and encysted in the bottom mud during summer.
- **Temperature effects:** In laboratory cultures kept at 0–2 °C, adults and copepodids developed slowly. Females kept at 18–20 °C lived actively for about three months, which suggests that temperature strongly governs development and survival.
- **Encystment cues:** [[Donner]] (1928) induced encystment in free-swimming animals caught in May by keeping them in stoppered bottles outdoors, where water temperature fluctuated naturally. Animals kept at a near-constant 16 °C did not encyst. Smyly concluded that warm temperature alone does not trigger encystment.
- **Sampling note:** Nauplii pass through the 100-mesh gauze used to filter excess water, so they are present in samples but absent from the seasonal abundance figure.

## Key Findings

**Annual cycle and phases**
- The annual cycle has two distinct phases:
  - A **dormant phase** of encysted adults, June to August.
  - A **free-swimming phase** with all life stages present, September to May.
- The population contains only one generation per year.

**Seasonal sequence**
- Newly emerged adults are initially all adult, with males and females equally numerous. No copepodids appear until late November.
- A breeding phase of about one month follows, during which the population grows rapidly to a maximum of about 3000 m⁻² (printed as "3000 m?" in the source).
- Numbers then fall, especially of males. Smyly attributes this decline mainly to mortality, since males have short lives under laboratory conditions.
- The October–November population is mostly adult females. Females carrying spermatophores only are most numerous, followed by females with both egg-sacs and spermatophores, with unmated females relatively scarce.
- Copepodids first appear in late November, increase until early March (when they briefly equal adult females in number), and then decline.
- Adult females and males increase again in spring as the copepodids mature. Spring females outnumber males and are virtually all unmated.
- By the end of May the free-swimming population has almost disappeared.

**Life span**
- Field data indicate that adult females live about **12 to 15 months** and adult males about **6 to 8 months**.
- The Summary gives slightly different figures, about **eight months for males and fifteen months for females**.
- Laboratory cultures at 0–2 °C showed adult females surviving **five months or more**, while adult males survived only **a few weeks**.

**Mating and reproduction**
- Each adult female carries at most one spermatophore, which indicates a single mating. The spermatophore changes from orange to black with age and is usually retained throughout life.
- Isolated mated females laid successive egg batches at intervals of **three to four weeks**. Egg-sacs were retained for about **two weeks**, and an average of **three sacs** was laid per female.
- Copepodids reared from these eggs took **three months** to become adult at 0–2 °C.

**Encystment**
- Encystment is probably a one-time event in each individual's life. Some mature females that lost a spermatophore might encyst a second time, but this is considered less likely.
- No adult females dissected from cysts carried spermatophores or egg-sacs, so encysted adults appear to be those that have not mated.
- Adult females with spermatophores may occasionally be free-swimming during the dormant period, as Donner also observed.
- Attempts to reproduce encystment in the lab with adults reared from eggs were unsuccessful: these adults died within a few weeks without encysting.

**Overall conclusion**
- The species has one generation per year, a summer dormant phase of encysted adults, and a winter–spring free-swimming phase.
- Encystment appears to be restricted to unmated, sexually immature adults and is likely to occur only once per lifetime.
- Adults emerging from cysts in autumn breed for about a month. Males then decline, the population becomes mainly adult females until copepodids appear in November, and the new generation matures the following April.
