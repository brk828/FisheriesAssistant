# Native Fishes at Buenos Aires NWR and ASU Research Park: Artificial Habitats for Imperiled Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

Paul E. Marsh (Center for Environmental Studies and Department of Zoology, Arizona State University) reports on stocking three imperiled Colorado River fishes into fishless artificial ponds at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (Buenos Aires NWR) and [[Arizona State University Research Park]] (ASU Research Park) in 1986–1987. The goal was to test whether these ponds could serve as grow-out habitat for native fishes before they are released into open waters. Survival and growth of [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were excellent at both sites, with razorbacks reaching 436 mm total length (TL) by autumn 1987. The author concludes that the Buenos Aires ponds are clearly suitable for rearing fish to a substantial size. Non-native fishes at ASU did not visibly affect native growth, but [[Colorado Squawfish]] were rarely captured there, most likely because of gear selectivity.

## Background & Description

The three species are endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of southwestern North America and are listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and several states. Their decline is attributed to habitat alteration and destruction, and to interactions with non-native fishes. The document describes the bonytail as perhaps the most critically endangered fish in the United States. Its recovery plan calls for all wild individuals to be collected and held in refugia. Razorback suckers remain abundant in [[Lake Mohave]] but do not recruit there, despite annual production of large numbers of larvae. Colorado squawfish have been extirpated from the lower basin for decades but still occupy parts of the upper basin.

Earlier work reviewed in the paper showed that:
- Stocked razorback suckers generally dispersed downstream, usually at night.
- Predation by [[Non-Native Ictalurid Catfish]] was a potentially significant source of mortality for juveniles.
- Long-term recapture rates were low.

The author therefore recommended rearing razorbacks in captivity for about two years before planting, so they would be too large for most predators.

Hatchery production depended on the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico. Grow-out space there and at the [[Page Springs State Fish Hatchery]] was limited, so the authors sought additional fishless ponds at Buenos Aires NWR and ASU Research Park.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Buenos Aires NWR.** The refuge covers grassland and rocky desert mountains in southern Arizona, drained mostly by the Santa Cruz River watershed. Aquatic habitat consists of artificial stock ponds called "tanks," which depend on rainfall. The tanks vary in size (roughly 1 to 315 ha) and in vegetation, algae and invertebrate communities. Preliminary surveys in December 1985 and April 1986 found only non-native fishes, including [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Mosquitofish]] and black bullhead. Amphibians included the [[Bullfrog]] and leopard frogs. Native fishes were absent from all sampled tanks, and some tanks had no fish at all.

**ASU Research Park.** The park in Tempe has three lakes (North, South and Middle), built in the early 1980s and filled in May 1985. Large pipes connect the lakes, and they are continuously aerated. The park has no outlets and receives make-up water from the [[Salt River Project]] canal system. The lakes were initially fishless, but non-native fishes arrived quickly:
- About 1,000 [[Redbelly Tilapia]] were stocked on 30 May 1986 to control nuisance algae.
- Tilapia reproduced successfully, and young-of-year were abundant by August.
- [[Red Shiner]] were found in North Lake, probably invading from the canals.
- [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]] and mosquitofish were found in small numbers.
- Four tilapia taxa were identified in October 1986: redbelly, blue, mozambique and a hybrid. Redbelly tilapia were the dominant species in later samples.

## Key Findings

**Stocking (1986).**
- Razorback suckers: about 3,000 juveniles (mean 116 mm TL) were planted in Morman Lake on 3 November 1986. Razorback fry (about 10 mm) were stocked into Morman, Rock and Tequila tanks on 31 March 1986.
- Bonytail: juveniles (mean 76 mm TL) were planted into Ring's, Bis Bertha and Road Camp tanks in November 1986.
- ASU Research Park: 11,048 Colorado squawfish juveniles (about 76 mm TL) were planted on 5 October 1986. In November 1986, 877 bonytail (76 mm) and razorback suckers (about 116 mm) were added. About half of each species went into Middle Lake, with the rest split between North and South lakes.

**Buenos Aires NWR results.**
- **January–February 1987** (about 3 months after stocking): 16 razorbacks (mean 166 mm TL, range 106–212 mm; mean weight 65 g) were taken from Morman Lake, in good condition. Bonytail were found in every tank into which they had been planted. Overall bonytail averaged about 130 mm TL (range 61–178 mm).
- **Late February 1987** (about 4 months): 13 razorbacks (mean 171 mm TL) were taken from Morman Lake.
- **15–16 August 1987**: Morman Lake yielded 84 razorbacks (mean 97 mm TL, 9 g). All were from the second stocking (31 March), and none from the November 1986 planting was recaptured. In trammel nets on 31 August–1 September, 7 razorbacks (168–311 mm TL, mean about 2?? mm) were caught. This confirmed survival and substantial growth.
- **Road Camp Tank**: 114 bonytail were caught in one seine haul on 1 August 1987 (116–?? mm). Fish showed little growth after the initial sampling, and bonytail were not found in the next seining. The author suggests a cause such as stress or mortality from deoxygenation, with predation also possible.
- **October 1987**: Bonytail in Road Camp Tank averaged about 188 mm TL, nearly a full year after planting. Razorbacks in Rock and Tequila tanks were robust and in excellent condition.

**ASU Research Park results.**
- **16 January 1987**: Many tilapia were dead or dying. Some had been beheaded, probably by a predator such as a [[Double-Crested Cormorant]] or another piscivore.
- **March 1987**: Hundreds of dead tilapia were found along the shorelines. Three decomposing razorbacks were recovered from South Lake.
- **6–8 April 1987**: Trammel netting caught mostly non-native fishes, along with razorback suckers, which were in good condition and were the most abundant native species.
- **8–9 April 1987**: Seine collections were dominated by tilapia and included razorbacks, bonytail and Colorado squawfish. Colorado squawfish were too few to assess quantitatively.
- **8–9 September 1987**: Trammel nets yielded 11 species. Largemouth bass were common in the catch, and black crappie and other species were also present. Razorback suckers were the most abundant native fish, with a mean TL of 169 mm (range 30.7–416 mm, n = 60).
- Bonytail (n = 9) averaged 191 mm TL (range 130 to about 370 mm) and were free of external parasites and in good health.

**Status of Colorado Squawfish.** No individuals were captured in the 1987 trammel nets, which the author attributes to gear selectivity. Fish too small for trammel nets to hold may be missed, while larger squawfish may escape seines. Electrofishing or other methods may be needed to find them.

**Survival and growth.** Growth of both bonytail and razorback suckers in artificial habitats met or exceeded that of wild populations in Lake Mohave and of hatchery stocks at the Dexter, Page Springs and Niland state hatcheries. The fish were free of anchorworm, a parasite that had been a problem at some facilities.

**Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Tanks are an effective grow-out system for imperiled fishes at Buenos Aires NWR.
- Fish reached substantial size in less than a year, which should improve survival after release into natural habitats.
- Additional refuge waters should be surveyed, and some may need reclamation to remove non-native species. Native fishes should be stocked as soon as they are available.
- Mature fish could be used to test the effects of exotic species on native reproduction and recruitment under controlled conditions, an experiment that is difficult to run in the wild.

**Public awareness.** Media coverage of the project generated numerous phone calls from the public. Interpretive signs at ASU Research Park describe the three fishes and educate daily visitors, and the author argues that public support is essential to endangered-species recovery programs.
