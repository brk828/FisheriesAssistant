# Delta Notes: Facts and Speculation on Effects of Colorado River Flows on Mexican Delta Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Penaeidae #Sciaenidae #Cichlidae #Moronidae #Centrarchidae #ColoradoRiverDelta

## Executive Summary

This 1985 field correspondence from Weisman to Minckley documents observations and interviews conducted in the [[Colorado River Delta]] region (El Golfo de Santa Clara, the levee system north to Riíto, and Laguna Salada) concerning a sharp decline in Gulf shrimp catches and the concurrent emergence of a freshwater sport/subsistence fishery driven by introduced species. The author hypothesizes that increased freshwater flows from the Colorado River created expanded but highly vulnerable shrimp spawning habitat, which is being exploited both by illegal human harvesting and by newly established populations of introduced predatory fish ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Tilapia]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Striped Bass]]). The note blends firsthand fisher testimony, cooperative economic data, and ecological speculation, representing an early record of the interaction between upstream water management, introduced species, and the collapse of the [[Totoaba]] and shrimp fisheries in the northern [[Gulf Of California]].

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** El Golfo de Santa Clara and the Colorado River Delta levee system (Riíto to the washed-out Highway 1 bridge near León), Mexicali Valley drainage canals, and Laguna Salada — recently reconnected to the sea due to elevated river flows.
- **Human fishery structure:**
  - **Venustiano Carranza Cooperative** — the only cooperative licensed for shrimp (52 members, 10 boats); historically harvested ~150 metric tons/year, now tracking closer to 60 tons.
  - **Santa Clara Cooperative** (30 members) and **Escamera Cooperative** (70 members) — limited to scale fish (panga-based), targeting shark, [[Sierra Mackerel]], and mullet, since more diverse scale species (flounder, pompano, cabrillo, totuava) are scarce at El Golfo compared to Punta Borrascoso ~60 km south.
  - An estimated **60 non-cooperative "free fishermen"** at El Golfo, plus growing numbers along the levee (at least 6 additional cooperatives of 25–40 members and many independents), engaging heavily in illicit shrimping.
- **Totuava (Totoaba macdonaldi):** Subject to an 8-year fishing ban at time of writing, yet actively poached; anecdote of truckloads of 15–80 kg totuava leaving El Golfo daily during a good year five years prior.
- **New freshwater fishery:** Emerged in the flooded delta zones following increased Colorado River flow, employing an estimated 300+ people (including displaced farmers). Target species and approximate prices:
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] — $180 pesos/kg (~$0.81 USD/kg), the primary economic driver.
  - [[Mullet]] — $100 pesos/kg (~$0.45 USD/kg)
  - [[Channel Catfish]] — $100 pesos/kg, market only recently developing.
  - [[Tilapia]] — considered a nuisance/"plague" species; good-tasting but no established market via Mexicali buyers; routinely removed dead or alive from gill nets to make room for target species. Most individuals observed were small (under 1 lb).
  - [[Striped Bass]] — increasingly observed, including egg-carrying females near the León bridge; a striped bass fishing tournament was noted in the Alamo Canal (Mexicali, San Felipe Highway KM 15).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrological changes:** Elevated Colorado River flows created extensive new flooded/estuarine habitat (documented from levee KM 97 south to KM 41), with significant standing water beginning around KM 86, connecting to cattail marshes and the Santa Clara Slough.
- **Irrigation drainage inputs:** Between levee KM 56–80, three sets of pumps (75–125 hp each) discharge agricultural drainage water across the dike into the delta; oxygenated outflow appears to concentrate [[Tilapia]] and attract bird predation.
- **Declining water levels / hydrological instability:** Multiple fishermen reported a sandbar breach downstream within the prior two months, accelerating drainage of floodwaters seaward and threatening the newly formed fishing grounds — raising urgent local concern about future U.S. water release schedules.
- **Laguna Salada reconnection:** Reportedly reconnected to the sea, becoming an important (and heavily exploited) shrimp habitat; fishermen described hanging nets across San Felipe Highway culverts to intercept shrimp en masse.
- **Introduced species as ecological disruptors:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Tilapia]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Striped Bass]] — all originally established in the U.S. portion of the Colorado River — are hypothesized to be significant predators on shrimp in the new brackish/freshwater spawning habitat, compounding pressure from human overharvesting.
- **Illegal harvest pressure:** 3/4" mesh nets (functionally useless for gillnetting scale fish but effective for small shrimp) were observed in active use; Mexicali buyers reportedly paid up to $1,000 pesos/kg for shrimp, incentivizing widespread poaching.
- **Corruption/enforcement failure:** Pesca officials from Mexicali allegedly accepted bribes (~$10,000 pesos / $45 USD) from fishermen caught with out-of-season shrimp, or confiscated nets and sold/consumed the catch themselves — undermining the intended shrimp spawning refuge status of the delta.
- **International black market context:** Excelsior (Mexico City newspaper) reportedly documented ~24 million pounds of Mexican shrimp (~1/3 of total national catch) illegally exported to the U.S. in the prior year, suggesting systemic incentive structures beyond the local delta.

## Key Findings

1. **Sharp shrimp catch decline:** Venustiano Carranza cooperative catch dropped from ~150 metric tons/year to ~60 tons; individual outings yielded as little as 4–5 kg over 4–5 hours of fishing. Pesca officials confirmed a general catch decline of at least 30% at El Golfo, San Felipe, and Puerto Peñasco.
2. **Season length concerns:** Some biologists cited in the report suggested shrimp spawning may begin by late April, meaning the officially sanctioned season (Oct 15–May 31) may overlap with and disrupt reproduction.
3. **Dual-edged hydrological effect (author's hypothesis):**
   - Increased freshwater flow expanded estuarine shrimp breeding habitat beyond historical limits, potentially boosting production.
   - However, this same habitat is highly accessible to both illegal human harvesters and newly established introduced fish predators, offsetting or reversing the benefit.
4. **Selective harvesting concern:** Higher prices paid by Mexicali buyers for larger shrimp may be selectively removing spawning females from the population — flagged as a hypothesis requiring confirmation (suggested follow-up in August).
5. **Rise of a freshwater/introduced-species fishery:** A previously nonexistent freshwater fishing economy (~300 people) has emerged in the flooded delta, centered on introduced [[Largemouth Bass]], with [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Striped Bass]] as growing secondary markets and [[Tilapia]] treated as an unmarketable bycatch nuisance.
6. **Water management dependency:** The stability of both the shrimp fishery and the new freshwater fishery is described as directly contingent on U.S. upstream water release policy, with local fishermen expressing anxiety over unpredictable flow reductions and sandbar-driven drainage changes.
7. **Governance/enforcement failure:** Documented bribery and confiscation abuse by Pesca officials, combined with insufficient enforcement capacity, are identified as major proximate drivers of continued illegal shrimp and totuava harvesting despite formal protections.
8. **Research/monitoring opportunity:** The Carranza cooperative offered a decommissioned boat and trawling access for scientific sampling of Gulf species composition; a Pesca-arranged shrimper trip and upriver poisoning/sampling permissions (via Mexicali officials) were also proposed, with April suggested as an optimal time due to increased aquatic activity.
