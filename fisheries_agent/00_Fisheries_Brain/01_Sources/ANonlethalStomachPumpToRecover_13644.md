# Non-Lethal Stomach Pump to Recover Stomach Contents of Humpback Chub: A Pilot Study

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #conservation-methods #feeding-ecology

## Executive Summary

This pilot study proposal evaluates the feasibility of using non-lethal stomach pumping techniques to assess food habits of [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Grand Canyon]]. The study aims to determine if stomach flushing can be performed without damaging the pharyngeal mill and to assess the efficiency of content recovery. The methodology would test two humpback chub captured from the LCR Reach of the [[Colorado River]] between January 7-10, 1990, using a modified Seaburg-design stomach pump. Following pumping, fish would be euthanized and dissected to evaluate technique effectiveness and potential tissue damage. Ten [[Rainbow Trout]] would also be sampled for comparative efficiency data.

## Background & Description

[[Humpback Chub]] is an endangered cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River system. Understanding food habits is critical for characterizing life history and ecology in the Grand Canyon, particularly in relation to [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. Previous research by Leibfried (1988) demonstrated that [[Rainbow Trout]] below Glen Canyon Dam consume large quantities of *Cladophera* algae, deriving nutrition from lipid-rich epiphytic diatoms. Whether humpback chub exhibit similar feeding strategies is unknown but important, as *Cladophera* production is closely linked to stream flow and dam operations.

Traditional stomach content analysis requires lethal sampling, which is problematic for populations with low abundance. Non-lethal stomach pumping has proven effective across multiple fish families including salmonids, centrarchids, ictalurids, percids, and esocids. Meehan and Miller (1978) found no significant survival differences between pumped and control juvenile salmonids after 30 days. Gengerke et al. (1973) reported pumping 864 stomachs of nine species with zero mortality. The technique has been successfully used with [[Roundtail Chub]] in the upper Colorado River basin.

Recovery efficiency is high: Meehan and Miller (1978) recovered 93% of prey items by number and 85% by weight from 155 salmonids. Hyslop (1980) concluded stomach pumping to be the most generally applicable method for removing stomach contents from live fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses critical questions about how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations affect food availability and timing for humpback chub. Sampling during flow changes would help determine whether behavioral changes (increased movement) result from greater food availability or habitat alterations. The relationship between dam operations, *Cladophera* production, and chub feeding ecology represents a key management concern for this endangered species.

Feeding is likely crepuscular in humpback chub, requiring nets to be set during early evening or morning periods. Minimizing net set time is essential to reduce digestion and regurgitation by entangled fish. The LCR Reach of the mainstem Colorado River serves as the sampling location, representing important humpback chub habitat.

## Key Findings

**Proposed Methodology:**
- Stomach pump design based on Gengerke's modification of the Seaburg design (Gengerke 1973, Seaburg 1957)
- Clear flexible tubing inserted into buccal cavity; water stream flushes stomach contents through outlet tube into collecting jar
- Interchangeable nozzles accommodate various fish sizes
- Hand-held rubber pump allows precise water flow control

**Pilot Study Protocol:**
- Sample two humpback chub from LCR Reach using gillnets (January 7-10, 1990)
- Mild anesthesia with MS-222 before pumping
- Preserve and label all flushed prey items
- Record handling stress observations and operation time
- Following pumping, euthanize fish with MS-222
- Extract stomach and gut through small ventral incision
- Freeze specimens in dry ice, transport via Tanner Trail
- Laboratory examination for pharyngeal teeth and esophageal damage
- Measure volume of preserved prey items and residual stomach contents
- Calculate efficiency as percentage (by volume) of recovered food items
- Photograph all aspects of pilot study

**Comparative Analysis:**
- Sample, pump, and dissect 10 rainbow trout in field
- Compare efficiency results to Meehan and Miller (1978) salmonid data

**Coordination:**
- Specimen disposition coordinated with Dr. Wayne Starnes, Holt Williamson, US Fish and Wildlife Service (Regions 2 and 6), Arizona Fish and Game, and National Park Service
- Detailed stomach content analysis protocol to be developed if pilot test successful and approved by ACT (Adaptive Management Team)
