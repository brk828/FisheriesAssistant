# Aquatic Habitats and Fishes of the Lower Colorado River: Havasu Division

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Clupeidae #benthos

## Executive Summary
This appendix describes the aquatic habitats, fishes, benthos, plants and algae, pigments and water chemistry of the Havasu Division of the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Surveys ran mainly 1974–76. Most of the division is [[Lake Havasu]], impounded by [[Parker Dam]]. Only the stretch between Parker Dam and [[Headgate Rock Dam]] flows.

Twelve fish species were caught or observed, and seven more are recorded for the division. [[Threadfin Shad]] dominated seine catches. [[Carp]] dominated gill-net catches. Benthos in the lake was sparse (about 2–9 kg/ha) except in the [[Bill Williams River]] Arm (20.6 kg/ha). Lake Havasu showed no thermal stratification in the sampled periods (June 1974 and winter 1974–75) and appeared polymictic through summer 1974. [[Moovayla Pond]], an isolated saline habitat, dried out in summer 1975 after a 1974 fish kill.

## Background & Description
- **Parker Dam (RM 510.2)** was built in the late 1930s as a desilting basin for domestic water diverted to California and to regulate irrigation discharge. Maximum discharge is about 1,075 m³/s, and the minimum daily flow over 24 hours is 42 m³/s.
- **Headgate Rock Dam** is a gravity-release irrigation diversion serving Colorado River Indian tribal lands. The flowing reach between the two dams is heavily developed for recreation.
- **Lake Havasu** covers about 8,000 surface ha at full pool. Water level rarely fluctuates more than 2 m, and depth is scarcely more than 18 m. The reservoir is subject to severe wind action, especially in summer cyclonic storms.
- **Substrates:** sand predominates upstream, and deep deltaic silts fill the Bill Williams River Arm near Parker, Arizona. Below Parker Dam the bottom includes varying boulder and gravel.
- **Banks and backwaters:** banks are modified by inundation or recreation. True backwaters are a minute proportion of the surface area. Habitats studied in detail were:
  - Moovayla Cove, 1–5 m deep with silt bottom and open to the river.
  - Moovayla Pond, a saline pool ringed by salt flats that dried in summer 1975.
  - Shallow sloughs (<1.5 m) with deep, malodorous bottoms, narrowly connected to the river through semi-aquatic plants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Fish community.** The sport fishery comprises [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]]. Smaller species are threadfin shad, [[Red Shiner]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Redear Sunfish]] and [[Bluegill]], plus abundant carp. Table H-I lists 19 kinds from the division. The seven not taken in 1974–76 include [[White Sturgeon]], [[Rainbow Trout]], goldfish, [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], fathead minnow and [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Benthos.** Chironomid dipterans, oligochaete worms and [[Corbicula]] (Asiatic clam) make up the benthos. Standing crop was highest near nutrient inflow (the Bill Williams delta). In the flowing reach, Corbicula dominated.

**Plants.**
- Attached vegetation in the lake was rare, limited to coontail, pondweeds and *Chara* in protected bays.
- Cattail beds are extensive on the Bill Williams delta, where cattail advanced more than 100 m per year in places.
- Below Parker Dam, willow, cottonwood, introduced tamarisk and introduced Bermuda grass line the reach.
- Sago pondweed, coontail, hornwort and *Chara* form dense beds in flowing water, extending more than 3.5 m into the current. Discharge changes detach drifting masses.
- Isolated, high-dissolved-solids waters support only halophytic spiny naiad and widgeon grass.

**Zooplankton.** Plankton was dense in Moovayla Pond, with a calanoid copepod abundant alongside ostracods. The adjacent slough held *Daphnia magna* (up to 4.0 mm), *Cyclops bicuspidatus*, *C. vernalis*, ostracods and *Asplanchna*.

## Key Findings
### Fishes
- **Seines (lake):** threadfin shad was the top species, at an estimated 7,429 individuals and 11.1 kg/ha. Young bluegill ranked second by number, followed by red shiner, mosquitofish, juvenile largemouth bass and young black crappie. The total was 26.9 kg and 10,000 fish/ha. Catches varied from about 500 to over 30,000 fish/ha.
- **Seines (river):** six species were taken, in decreasing abundance: red shiner, threadfin shad, bluegill, green sunfish, largemouth bass and redear sunfish. No quantitative data resulted.
- **Hoop nets** were ineffective in Lake Havasu.
- **Horizontal gill nets** averaged about 1.46 fish (about 2.71 kg) per daytime set and 0.55 fish (0.49 kg) per night set. Catch order was carp, channel catfish, largemouth bass, then threadfin shad. Daytime catches exceeded night catches, unusual for Colorado River netting. The source suggests deep-water netting may reduce light effects on movement.
- **Trammel nets** had much higher catch per unit effort. A daytime set averaged about 9.3 fish (nearly 7.0 kg) and a night set 11.0 fish (9.84 kg). Catch order was carp, largemouth bass, channel catfish, striped bass, then yellow bullhead.
- **Vertical gill nets** caught only a few threadfin shad, consistent with Bersell's results in central Arizona impoundments, so the method was discontinued.
- **Parker–Headgate reach:** sampling failed because of vandalism and public interference. Observations indicate heavy fishing pressure on sunfishes, largemouth bass, channel catfish and carp. Schools of threadfin shad and red shiner were seen in the Parker Dam tailrace, in contrast to few small fishes below [[Davis Dam]] in the Mohave Valley Division.
- **Moovayla Pond and sloughs:**
  - A 1972 gill-net sample was dominated by carp.
  - Sampling stopped because of that carp dominance.
  - Two spring 1975 slough gill nets caught 2 carp and 1 channel catfish.
  - No fish were present in the pond in 1975 after a summer 1974 fish kill.

- Chironomid distribution tracked substrate type more closely than oligochaete distribution did. Nutrient input or plankton may control the pattern rather than substrate alone.
- Comparison data from [[Roosevelt Lake]], Arizona (Rinne): 2.85–8.26 kg/ha and 210–999 organisms/m². Smaller downstream reservoirs ranged from none to 11.11 kg/ha and up to 1,471/m². These are comparable to Havasu values.
- Moovayla Pond had unusually high benthos densities, probably reflecting a lack of vertebrate predators. Sloughs held a similar fauna but at far lower biomass.

### Pigments
- Below Parker Dam in June 1974, chlorophyll *a* ranged from <0.1 to >4.5 mg/m³, with 20 of 29 samples at order-of-magnitude II.
- At Headgate Rock Dam, chlorophyll *a* ranged from not detected to 2.1 mg/m³. Eleven of 31 samples had none detected, and 5 were at magnitude II.
- Winter 1974–75 profiles showed no vertical stratification of pigments. Concentrations were slightly higher up-lake and in the Bill Williams Arm, consistent with Portz's nutrient-input explanation.
- Moovayla Pond samples showed chlorophyll *a* at magnitudes III–IV and "diatoms" at IV, from an unusual hypersaline assemblage as the pond dried.

### Physical-chemical conditions
- **June 1974 (30-hour series below Parker Dam, RM 510.3):**
  - Temperature varied erratically about a mean of 22.6 °C, and dissolved oxygen ranged from 7.4 to 11.0 mg/L.
  - pH ranged from about 7.3 to 8.0, turbidity from about 13 to 38 JTU, and PO₄-P from 0.02 to 0.29 mg/L.
  - There was no stratification, attributed to wind mixing. Data from Headgate Rock Dam three days later and again in July indicated polymictic conditions through the summer.
- **July 3–5, 1974 at Headgate Rock Dam:** dissolved oxygen and pH declined over the period. Possible causes are organic loading from intense recreation, hypolimnetic input or boat turbulence.
- **Winter 1976 (RM 513.8):** temperature 10.8–11.0 °C, conductance averaging 896 µmhos/cm, no turbidity, PO₄-P averaging 0.04 mg/L, and NO₃-N 0.12–0.16 mg/L (avg 0.14). On the Arizona side, PO₄-P was almost 10 times higher (20 samples), possibly from recreational development.
- **Winter 1974–75 lake profiles (to 18 m):**
  - Temperature was 10.8–11.5 °C and dissolved oxygen 10.3–12.8 mg/L.
  - NO₃-N was 0.12–0.16 mg/L.
  - There was no stratification, and bays did not differ from the main body.
- **Marshy habitats** were warmer and had lower dissolved oxygen (about 6.4 mg/L upward), pH 7.9–8.6, and turbidity from 14 to over 500 JTU (higher when fishes were active).
- **Moovayla Pond:** in April 1975 it was drying, with temperatures of about 25–26 °C, dissolved oxygen of 8.2–12.0 mg/L (dense algae), pH 8.6–8.9, turbidity of 245–310 JTU, and conductance of 11,000–12,000 µmhos/cm. Conductance reached 15,600 µmhos/cm just before desiccation in May 1975, by which time most fishes were dead.
