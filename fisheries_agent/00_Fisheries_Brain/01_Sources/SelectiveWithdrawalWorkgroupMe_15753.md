# Selective Withdrawal Investigations Workgroup Meeting Summary - Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #dam-operations #thermal-management

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation document summarizes an April 6, 1993 Selective Withdrawal Investigations Workgroup meeting held in Phoenix, Arizona. The multi-disciplinary workgroup evaluated the feasibility and potential impacts of installing selective withdrawal facilities at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to modify downstream water temperatures. The primary objective is to support maintenance and recovery of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) by establishing a second spawning population in the [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]] by warming release temperatures during the May-September spawning and rearing season. The workgroup identified critical questions regarding thermal requirements of native and non-native fish, achievable release temperatures, downstream warming potential, engineering feasibility, and ecosystem-wide impacts. A comprehensive research and analysis program was proposed covering [[Lake Powell]], the Colorado River below the dam, [[Lake Mead]], and hydroelectric power generation impacts.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] currently withdraws water from the hypolimnion (bottom layer) of [[Lake Powell]], resulting in cold, stable downstream temperatures year-round. This thermal regime differs dramatically from pre-dam conditions and is considered unsuitable for native fish reproduction and early life stage survival. The dam essentially "resets" the river to a headwater-like environment, with downstream conditions reflecting reservoir water quality rather than natural riverine conditions.

The proposed selective withdrawal facility would allow operators to draw warmer water from higher elevations in the stratified reservoir during May through September. Target release temperatures were initially recommended as: May 11°C (52°F), June-September 15°C (59°F). Combined with downstream instream warming of approximately 1°C per 35 miles (2°F per 35 miles), river temperatures at the confluence with the [[Little Colorado River]] (River Mile 77) could reach 17°C (63°F), and at [[Diamond Creek]] (River Mile 242) could reach 24°C (72°F). These temperatures fall within the estimated suitable spawning range of 16-22°C (61-72°F) for [[Humpback Chub]].

The workgroup noted that selective withdrawal operations might be required only one in five years, not annually, potentially reducing impacts. The facility would require separate NEPA compliance, potentially tiered off the Glen Canyon EIS then in development.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] ecosystem below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has been fundamentally altered by dam operations. The river between the dam and [[Lees Ferry]] is relatively productive despite low nutrient concentrations, considered the "powerhouse" providing energy for the balance of the river to [[Lake Mead]]. The food web includes:

1. **[[Cladophora]]** (filamentous green alga) as the foundation
2. **Diatoms** (single-celled algae) living on Cladophora filaments as high-energy food
3. **[[Gammarus lacustris]]** (amphipod) feeding on diatoms among Cladophora
4. **Chironomid and fly larvae** comprising significant portions of [[Humpback Chub]] diet

As turbidity increases downstream from tributary inputs, [[Cladophora]] is replaced by **[[Oscillatoria]]** (blue-green alga), which is more turbid-tolerant but less productive and does not provide the same habitat structure or diatom attachment surface.

Native fish evolved under historical Colorado River temperature ranges that were warmer and more variable than current post-dam conditions. The [[Little Colorado River]] confluence supports the only known reproducing population of [[Humpback Chub]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. Establishing a mainstem spawning population would provide critical redundancy for this endangered species.

Non-native fish species present in the system prefer warmer temperatures and lower, steadier flows than most native fish. Cooler temperatures in upper river reaches may function as a thermal barrier preventing upstream migration of non-native predators and competitors from [[Lake Mead]] or lower reaches. Warming operations could potentially remove this barrier, increasing predator-prey interactions detrimental to native fish.

## Key Findings

**Critical Questions Identified:**

The workgroup organized investigations around five elemental questions:

1. **What are the thermal requirements and tolerances of the fish?**
   - Historical lower Colorado River temperature ranges needed definition
   - Thermal requirements and tolerances of [[Humpback Chub]], other native/endangered fish, and non-native fish required refinement by life stage
   - Arizona Game and Fish Department recently completed laboratory studies on temperature tolerance of early life stages of [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]
   - Field studies using appropriate surrogate fish recommended for information applicable to [[Humpback Chub]]

2. **What release temperatures may be obtained from Glen Canyon Dam with selective withdrawal?**
   - Volumetric analysis using reservoir temperature profiles and area/elevation curves could provide preliminary estimates
   - Digitizing lake bathymetry would refine volume estimates
   - Reservoir temperature modeling would provide most accurate predictions
   - Model inventory recommended to identify appropriate models, data requirements, capabilities, and limitations
   - 1985 simulations demonstrated releases may not reach pre-dam temperatures but warmer releases were possible (May-October simulation period)

3. **How much downstream warming may be realized?**
   - Instream temperature modeling needed to predict temperatures along river corridor
   - Stream water quality model inventory recommended
   - Results would identify locations with suitable temperatures for native fish and potential competition areas with non-native fish

4. **Is it possible to retrofit Glen Canyon Dam with an effective selective withdrawal structure?**
   - Three-step hydraulic design process recommended: numerical modeling, physical modeling, and value engineering
   - Value engineering particularly important for complex structures with site-specific considerations
   - Site-specific analyses essential; direct application of designs from other sites carries risks
   - Reservoir temperature modeling results feed into numerical hydraulic modeling
   - Physical scale model would test numerical model results

5. **If we build it, will they spawn? (What are potential impacts?)**
   - Impacts identified across four areas: Lake Powell, Colorado River below dam, Lake Mead, and hydroelectric power generation

**Potential Impacts to Lake Powell:**

- **Heat Budget**: Lower reservoir temperatures may reduce evaporation, affecting water budget and temperature regime; impacts reservoir circulation, productivity, and fisheries
- **Nutrient Budget**: Removing water with lower nutrient content leaves higher concentrations in reservoir; may raise productivity but could lead to eutrophic conditions and depressed dissolved oxygen in vulnerable areas (coves, inlets)
- **Dissolved Oxygen**: Removing higher-DO water from epilimnion/metalimnion may lower concentrations, affecting productivity and fisheries; however, if withdrawal plume intercepts low-DO region (known to develop in metalimnion, as low as 2 mg/L), removal could increase reservoir DO
- **Total Dissolved Solids**: Removing lower-TDS water leaves higher concentrations, potentially affecting Lake Powell as drinking water source and for irrigation/salinity control
- **Threadfin Shad**: Lower lethal temperature of 45°F; more frequent occurrence of temperatures ≤45°F may reduce population, impacting sport fish that depend on them as food source
- **Productivity**: Cooler temperatures may reduce productivity rates and affect species composition and distribution
- **Spatial Variability**: Moving withdrawal current higher may entrain warm, nutrient-rich, sediment-laden spring inflow, affecting inflow movement, nutrient distribution, sediment distribution, epilimnion development, and heat distribution
- **Reservoir Circulation**: Changes in thermal regime may affect circulation and productivity

**Potential Impacts to Colorado River Below Glen Canyon Dam:**

- **Water Quality Changes**: Moving withdrawal from hypolimnion to surface layers would increase temperatures (objective), lower nutrient concentrations, generally increase dissolved oxygen, and lower TDS concentrations
- **Increased River Temperatures**: May impact primary and secondary productivity
- **Decreased Nutrients**: River already considered low in nutrients under hypolimnetic withdrawal; considerable algal growth between dam and Lees Ferry attributed to water clarity allowing deeper sunlight penetration and Cladophora's efficient nutrient use
- **Dissolved Oxygen**: Generally expected to increase with epilimnion/metalimnion releases, but could decrease if withdrawal plume intercepts low-DO metalimnion region (where bacteria decompose organic material from spring inflow sediments)
- **Biological Components**: Temperature and water quality changes may affect:
  - [[Cladophora]], [[Oscillatoria]], diatoms, and [[Gammarus]] (thermal tolerances unknown for lower Colorado River; temperature may influence algal dominance along with turbidity)
  - Non-native fish upstream migration and feeding rates
  - Fish parasites and nematodes (tapeworms in [[Humpback Chub]], trout nematodes, Lernae)
  - Aquatic insects (thermal requirements, tolerance, potential species introductions)
  - Predator-prey interactions

**Potential Impacts to Lake Mead:**

- Water released from [[Lake Powell]] plus tributary inflow comprises most inflow to [[Lake Mead]]
- Post-dam inflow is clearer, colder, lower in nutrients than pre-dam conditions
- Diminished nutrient input has limited [[Lake Mead]] productivity, reducing fisheries
- Warmer temperatures or reduced nutrient content may alter aquatic food base species composition and abundance, potentially impacting productivity and fisheries

**Potential Impacts to Hydroelectric Power Generation:**

- Selective withdrawal structure may impact efficiency
- Magnitude influenced by particular design
- Hydraulic modeling and value engineering needed to assess

**Monitoring and Research Recommendations:**

- **Non-native Fish**: Monitor thermal barrier function and upstream movement; monitor flow manipulation as management tool; results would benefit Adaptive Management Program
- **Stream Temperature Modeling**: Provide temperature estimates along river corridor suggesting suitable native fish locations and potential non-native competition areas
- **Water Quality Monitoring**: Temperature, dissolved oxygen, nutrients, TDS in reservoir and river
- **Organism Response Monitoring**: Changes in temperature, DO, nutrient concentrations
- **Literature Reviews**: Thermal requirements and tolerances, energetics, thermal shock
- **Laboratory Studies**: Refine temperature estimates for spawning, egg/fry/larvae survival, growth, energetics influences
- **Field Studies**: Using surrogate fish for information applicable to [[Humpback Chub]]
- **Factors Influencing Cladophora-Oscillatoria Dominance**: Research needed

**Program Status and Concerns:**

- Cooperating Agencies accepted Workgroup recommendation for further study
- Bureau of Reclamation supported investigations
- Upper Colorado Regional Office planning process commenced mid-summer 1993
- Draft study plan incorporated only selected components of Workgroup proposal, limiting study area to Glen Canyon Dam to Lake Mead
- Concern expressed that elements removed from original programs (Lake Powell Water Quality Monitoring and selective withdrawal investigations) lacked fundamental linkages between Colorado River system components
- [[Lake Powell]] water quality data provides basis for comparison of effects due to operational changes for any water quality analysis or research
- Meeting held to resolve differences; key points emerged regarding multi-disciplinary workgroup composition, justification/authority for studies (salinity control, Draft GCEIS commitments, Grand Canyon Protection Act), and linkages between water quality parameters and ecosystem components
- Issue discussed at January 20, 1994 Cooperating Agency Meeting

**Priority and Uncertainty:**

- Workgroup prioritized questions and impacts based on stated objective
- Top priority: life requirements of [[Humpback Chub]] and overall system health below dam
- Hydroelectric power generation impacts also considered very important
- Selective withdrawal operations acknowledged as having uncertainty and impacts
- Proposed analysis and research meant to minimize uncertainty and identify/estimate magnitude of potential impacts
- Significant concern exists regarding increased predator-prey interactions between non-native and native fish
- Thorough investigations stressed before adding selective withdrawal capabilities and implementing operations
- Organism response to environmental changes depends partly on how well life requirements are being met; stressed organisms may be unable to adjust to any change regardless of magnitude
- Even with proposed research, unknowns will likely remain that can only be determined after implementation
- Adaptive Management, long-term monitoring, and research included in seven of nine alternatives in Draft GCEIS
