# Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River: 15-Mile Reach Studies and Artificial Spawning Investigations (Second Annual Progress Report, 1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Colorado-Squawfish

## Executive Summary

This second annual progress report from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) Colorado River Fishery Project covers two linked studies of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in the Grand Valley, Colorado. The work was funded by the [[U.S. Bureau Of Reclamation]] under the Green Mountain and Ruedi Reservoir water sale conservation measures. The first study examined how the [[15-Mile Reach]] of the [[Colorado River]] is used for abundance, movement, spawning, and habitat. The second tested artificial spawning and pond grow-out methods. The report is a preliminary account of data through December 1987. The final report was scheduled for January 1989.

## Background & Description

The two species are endangered fishes of the upper Colorado River basin. The 15-mile reach is expected to lose flow as a result of the water sale, and the report notes concern that reduced flows could degrade habitat. Additional releases from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] may be needed to offset this.

Study methods included:

- **[[Electrofishing]]** to estimate adult relative abundance as catch per hour of shocking.
- **[[Radiotelemetry]]** of adults longer than 550 mm TL, using surgically implanted transmitters with estimated battery lives of 150-245 days (smallest) to 547-940 days (largest).
- **Larval seining** of backwaters from mid July through August, using 0.5-mm-mesh hand seines.
- **Young-of-the-year (YOY) seining** with 3-mm-mesh beach seines in two backwaters per 5-mile reach.
- **Microhabitat measurements** of depth, velocity, substrate, temperature, and water clarity at tagged-fish locations.

Spawning dates were back-calculated from larval length using age-length equations from Haynes and Muth (1985). A four-day embryo incubation period was assumed, based on Hamman (1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** In spring, flooded [[Gravel-Pit Ponds]] were used heavily by squawfish. From July to November, pools and runs were more common. In winter, adults used low-velocity backwaters and river pools that were often ice-covered. Depths of 2.0-5.9 ft were used most often overall. Use of deep water (>6 ft) rose in August and September, partly because of plunge pools below the [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] and [[Price Stub Dam]].

**Temperature.** The authors describe a behavioral thermal regulation pattern. Squawfish sometimes chose habitats up to 10.5 °C warmer than the main channel, which is close to the species' physiological optimum of about 25 °C. Spawning-minimum temperatures of 20 °C occurred in the 15-mile reach only in mid summer, which may shorten the first growing season.

**Water clarity.** Depth use was positively related to Secchi visibility, though weakly (r = 0.389; P < 0.01). Summer thunderstorm spates increase silt load and reduce clarity. Clear summer water may increase the vulnerability of young fish to sight-feeding predators.

**Pond ecology.** Grow-out ponds contained [[Fathead Minnow]] forage, common carp, and in some cases largemouth bass and channel catfish. Predation, cannibalism, forage limitation, low dissolved oxygen, and the [[Asian Tapeworm]] parasite were all considered as possible mortality factors.

## Key Findings

### Adult abundance and movement
- In spring 1986 and 1987, squawfish catch per hour in the 15-mile reach (CO-15) was about 3.8 times that of the adjacent downstream 18-mile reach. Razorback catch was about 3.6 times higher.
- Combined-year catch rates were 0.271 squawfish/h (CO-15) versus 0.114 (CO-18). Razorback rates were 0.083 and 0.029.
- Adults concentrated near RM 174.4-175.5 in spring and at the Redlands Diversion plunge pool in summer.
- Radio-tagged squawfish showed a range of behavior. Some stayed within 2.6 miles of release, some moved up to 45 miles downstream, and some made repeated trips to dam plunge pools.
- Razorbacks moved from the 15-mile reach to deeper pools or downstream sites. Their movements were consistent with possible spawning migrations.

### Spawning
- **1986:** Larvae were estimated to have spawned 26 July to 5 August, in the 18-mile reach and lower Gunnison River. None were found in the 15-mile reach.
- **1987:** One larva was estimated to have spawned on 4 July. The authors assumed a spawning period of 24 June to 14 July.
- The only year larvae were collected from the 15-mile reach (1982) was also the only year they were absent from the lower 18-mile reach. The authors suggest squawfish may spawn at varying sites depending on yearly conditions.
- Razorback suckers spawn in May and June. Ripe fish were captured in a gravel-pit pond on 3 and 11 June 1986. Larval razorbacks could not yet be identified.

### Larval and YOY catches
- Mean 1986 larval catch in the 18-mile reach was 4.02/100 m² (SD = 7.87), with mean total length 24.7 mm (SD = 3.14).
- Mean 1987 YOY catch in the 18-mile reach was 2.75/100 m² (SD = 9.34), with mean total length 27.0 mm (SD = 5.9).
- The authors concluded that 1987 was not a particularly good reproductive year for squawfish in the Grand Valley.

### Artificial spawning
- Captive squawfish failed to spawn in 1986 and 1987, both in ponds and at [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]]. Handling stress, lack of feeding, and fungal parasitism were major problems. Four fish were lost at Hotchkiss.
- Future spawning will take place at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], using up to ten adult squawfish captured in 1988.

### Grow-out pond results
- **[[Fish Chalet Pond]]:** About 7,000 squawfish were stocked in late August 1986. Growth was poor because forage was scarce, and condition declined from .7366 to .5499. A mark-recapture estimate in September 1987 found 107 survivors (CI 80-135), or 1.5% of those stocked.
- **[[West Pond]]:** Growth was faster. At least 34.9% of the stocked fish were alive after 10 months. A school of 2,440 fish was estimated in one shallow cove.
- **[[13th Hole Pond]]:** 234 survivors of an airline-delayed shipment were stocked in 1986. Catch fell from 63 to 4 fish per three-haul effort. Four to five fish survived into 1987, growing from 180 mm to 318 mm.
- **[[Humphrey Pond]]:** About 50,000 squawfish fry were stocked on 11 June 1987. Growth slowed after August, with condition falling, and Asian tapeworm was found by September. Razorbacks grew from 54 mm to 306.5 mm (mean length, mid June to mid November). Their condition peaked at 1.4011 in mid October.
- Squawfish growth in ponds was reported as about twice that in rivers or hatcheries (Osmundson 1987).

### Recommendations and plans
- Capture up to ten adult squawfish and all razorbacks for the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock program, with an augmentation plan due in summer 1988.
- Rotenone-treat all three ponds with infected fish in spring 1988 to prevent spread of [[Asian Tapeworm]].
- Manage ponds for fathead minnow production for at least one year before stocking squawfish.
- Rear squawfish to 200-250 mm TL before river stocking to reduce predation risk.
- Continue pond culture research, focusing on causes of low survival and stocking density.
