# Cage Culture of Tilapia aurea

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This short research report from the [[Fisheries Division]] of the [[Agricultural Experiment Station]] at [[Auburn University]] describes the second year of work on growing [[Tilapia Aurea]] in cages suspended in ponds. Tilapia have a long-standing problem of overpopulation in ponds. In cages, *T. aurea* did not reproduce, while at the same stocking rates in ponds it reproduced heavily. The author proposes two likely reasons for the lack of reproduction in cages and presents cage culture as a way to control breeding in ponds used for intensive production. Preliminary trials suggest the species grows well, converts feed efficiently, survives well, resists disease, and accepts commercial floating pellets. The first phase of production trials was scheduled for completion in November 1969. The text was published in the [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] *Fishculture Bulletin* (1969, 2(1):6).

## Background & Description

*Tilapia aurea* is a tilapia species that has been studied for aquaculture. The report notes that tilapia in general tend to overpopulate when cultured in ponds, which lowers the yield of marketable fish. The species is a [[Mouth Brooding]] fish: the female carries the eggs and young in her mouth, which provides parental care. The report does not give a full morphological description, a taxonomic classification, or a precise geographic range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study compares two culture systems:

- **Pond culture:** At stocking rates of 7,000 to 15,000 fish per hectare, *T. aurea* reproduced heavily, reaching populations of 250,000 to 346,000 per hectare.
- **Cage culture:** Fish held in [[Cage Culture]] systems suspended in ponds did not reproduce.

The author suggests two explanations for the lack of reproduction in cages:

1. **Altered spawning behavior:** Spent females were collected, which suggests spawning occurred. However, the author believes reproductive behavior was changed so that eggs were not fertilized.
2. **Loss of parental care:** Under cage conditions, eggs fall through the bottom of the cage, which prevents the female from mouth brooding. The author argues that even if some eggs were fertilized, the lack of parental care would stop normal development.

Because reproduction is prevented in cages, the report presents cage culture as a method for controlling reproduction in ponds used for intensive production of marketable fish.

## Key Findings

- **Reproductive control:** *T. aurea* did not reproduce in cages. In ponds at the same stocking rates, it reproduced to densities of 250,000 to 346,000 per hectare, showing the value of cages for preventing overpopulation.
- **Probable causes:** The author proposes (a) altered reproductive behavior that prevents fertilization and (b) loss of mouth-brooding parental care because eggs fall through the cage bottom.
- **Growth and efficiency:** Trials indicate fast growth and high efficiency in converting food into fish flesh.
- **Survival and health:** The species showed a high survival rate, including under occasional unfavorable environmental conditions, and apparent strong resistance to diseases and parasites.
- **Feeding:** *T. aurea* readily accepts artificial feed in the form of commercial dry floating [[Pelleted Feed]].
- **Potential:** The author concludes that the species has excellent potential for cage culture.
- **Project timeline:** The first phase of cage production experiments was to be completed by November 1969. The report does not present quantitative growth, feed conversion, or survival data.
