# National Fish Hatchery Review Panel Report 1994

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #conservation-policy #hatchery-operations

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive review of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's National Fish Hatchery (NFH) program was conducted by an independent panel convened by the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]] at the request of the Service Director. The panel assessed 78 federal hatcheries across 35 states that produce 220 million fish of 69 species annually with a 1993 budget of $54.1 million. The review concluded that fundamental changes are needed in the direction and management of the NFH program to support [[Ecosystem Management]] rather than simply maximizing fish production. The continuing decline of aquatic habitats and fisheries resources—with nearly 400 taxa requiring special protection and 40 species/subspecies extinct since 1900—mandates a shift from production-focused operations toward habitat restoration, protection of native biodiversity, and support for threatened and endangered species recovery.

## Background & Description

The federal fish hatchery system began in 1871 at [[Bucksport]] Maine and 1872 at [[McCloud River]] California. Three hatcheries established between 1879-1889 remain operational: [[Neosho NFH]] (Missouri), [[Leadville NFH]] (Colorado), and [[Craigbrook NFH]] (Maine). Major expansion occurred following the White Act (1930), Coordination Act (1934), and Mitchell Act (1934) during 1934-1960. The system peaked at 96 facilities before declining to the current 78 hatcheries, plus 9 Fish Disease Control Centers and 6 Fish Technology Centers.

Of the 78 NFHs, 52 produce coldwater species, 13 produce warmwater species, and 13 produce both. Thirty-eight hatcheries are involved in mitigation for federally-funded development projects. The NFH program represents approximately 80% of the Fishery Resources (FR) program's budget and personnel, leaving limited resources for habitat restoration and ecosystem management initiatives. The panel identified this proportion as excessive given critical habitat needs.

The review panel included 16 members representing universities, state agencies, federal agencies ([[National Biological Survey]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], [[U.S. Forest Service]]), tribal commissions, conservation organizations, and commercial aquaculture. The panel conducted interviews with state directors, researchers, field biologists, and visited all FWS Regional Offices during three meetings in 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The decline of North American fishes stems from five broad categories: (1) competition for water, (2) habitat alteration, (3) pollution, (4) introduction of exotic species, and (5) commercial exploitation. The panel found that habitat alteration and destruction are the major causes of decline, not inadequate hatchery production. Historical assumptions that hatchery production could compensate for habitat losses have proven incorrect.

Hatcheries themselves have contributed to problems through: (1) production of non-native species and hybrids that compete with or predate native species, (2) saturation of waters with hatchery fish attracting intensive fisheries that overharvest wild fish, (3) domestication reducing fitness for natural conditions, and (4) genetic pollution when hatchery fish interbreed with wild populations. The domestication process begins immediately when fish enter hatchery conditions, reducing capacity for growth and survival in nature.

In the [[Pacific Northwest]], despite record smolt releases, [[Pacific Salmon]] runs continue declining. Multiple factors contribute: physical injury/mortality from hydropower dams and turbines, ecosystem changes from riverine to reservoir systems favoring predatory species ([[Northern Pikeminnow]], [[Walleye]]), warming from clear-cut logging and livestock grazing, and siltation of spawning beds. The [[Snake River Sockeye Salmon]] population at [[Redfish Lake]] was designated an Evolutionarily Significant Unit (ESU) under the [[Endangered Species Act]], with only a single male returning in 1993.

The panel emphasized that artificial propagation should not substitute for habitat restoration. Given hospitable environments, hatchery fish can survive and establish reproducing populations, but stocking without addressing underlying habitat problems perpetuates decline. Genetic management technologies exist but have been inconsistently applied. Field data on genetic variability magnitude and distribution are often lacking.

## Key Findings

**Primary Mission Redirection**: The panel reached consensus that the primary mission of federal fish hatcheries must shift from numerical production to supporting ecosystem management and habitat restoration. Hatcheries should function as support to ecosystem managers, producing only species, stocks, strains, and numbers specifically identified in ecosystem management plans.

**Mitigation Program Failures**: Thirty-eight hatcheries operate under mitigation mandates (statutory or de facto), but many fail to achieve original goals. In the Pacific Northwest, runs continue declining despite record hatchery production because dams block access to spawning areas and alter riverine ecosystems. The panel recommends reopening mitigation agreements to negotiate reduced production, increased habitat restoration funding, and reimbursement for operating costs where pass-through funding was not provided. A 1991 audit identified approximately $1.5 million in unreimbursed costs for just two projects.

**Threatened and Endangered Species**: The panel found widespread support for using federal hatcheries to prevent extirpation of endangered species, but only as a last resort after rigorous population viability analysis. Captive propagation must be managed by recovery teams in concert with the National Fisheries Resource Program. Programs should terminate if self-sustaining populations cannot be restored within 3-5 generations or if habitat cannot be restored, with responsibility transferred to aquaria for zoo-like maintenance. Maintaining genetic heterozygosity is critical, requiring consultation with competent geneticists throughout propagation efforts.

**Recreational Fishing Role**: The majority of the panel concluded that providing hatchery fish for enhanced recreational fishing is not a federal responsibility since states manage recreational fishing and receive associated revenues. Federal involvement should focus on habitat protection, biodiversity maintenance, and increased access to federal lands. The panel recommends that states assume costs of producing fish for recreational purposes, with federal provision limited to Congressional mitigation mandates or tribal/treaty obligations. A minority report dissented, arguing federal responsibility should continue for waters on federal lands or projects built with federal funds.

**Facility Assessment and Divestiture**: Not all 78 NFHs will be needed under ecosystem management. The panel recommends regional ecosystem management teams evaluate each hatchery using criteria including: consistency with ecosystem management goals, mandated obligations, species production capability, cost-effectiveness, adaptability for threatened/endangered species, potential for habitat restoration center use, physical condition, and ecosystem compatibility. Facilities failing these tests should be considered for consolidation, reprogramming, leasing, or divestiture. Approximately $270 million would be needed to restore all facilities to full capability, but this investment is not justified given changing needs.

**Genetic and Ecological Safeguards**: Before stocking any hatchery fish, comprehensive assessments must address: availability of suitable habitat, adequate food base, appropriate spawning sites, and potential impacts on native biodiversity. Resource managers should use native populations as broodstock to maintain genetic diversity and prevent genetic swamping of wild stocks. No non-native strains or species should be stocked in areas containing unique species, threatened stocks, or endangered species. Population geneticists should be available to assist management teams in each region.

**Partnership and Communication**: The panel found that federal policy changes often occur without consultation with state partners, violating long-standing partnerships. States promoted tourism and invested in infrastructure expecting continued federal fish provision. Implementation of any changes must be time-phased with adequate lead time (potentially up to 10 years for some states) for political groundwork, budget development, and facility construction. Cost-sharing during transitions is essential.

**Fish Health Services**: The Service's decision to restrict Fish Disease Control Center services to federal hatcheries only (except virology) may be premature. The panel recommends temporarily postponing this decision until state and private capabilities are in place or USDA services become available. Private aquaculture should receive greater access to disease-free stocks in the federal broodstock registry to control pathogen spread to native populations.

**Budget and Program Structure**: The current budget structure focuses on hatchery operations rather than ecosystem needs. The panel recommends revising the budget format to focus on priority ecosystem management projects, with hatchery operations becoming part of regional budgets. This would provide greater latitude for setting priorities, assuming new initiatives, and redistributing funds. A multi-year (minimum 5-year) national fisheries management plan should be developed through negotiation with federal agencies, states, and tribes.

**Public Education**: Hatcheries attract thousands of visitors annually but fail to educate them about ecosystem management roles. Public perception remains that hatcheries exist solely to produce large numbers of fish. New outreach approaches must emphasize hatcheries' role as vital support to aquatic ecosystem stewardship, explaining resource problems, Service programs, and how specific species/strains support ecosystem management goals.

**Tribal and Treaty Obligations**: The Service must fulfill treaty and tribal responsibilities as mandated, but on a government-to-government basis through negotiated cooperative agreements and fisheries management plans. International treaty obligations should not dictate domestic policy. Harvest regimens must prevent over-exploitation and protect wild stocks. Four hatcheries formerly funded by the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]] have been operated from Service appropriations since 1988 without reimbursement.

**Private Aquaculture Relationship**: Commercial producers focus on maximum production using domesticated strains lacking genetic diversity, making their fish unsuitable for ecosystem management or threatened/endangered species work. However, the Service should evaluate leasing excess federal hatcheries to private operators. The panel recommends transferring lead federal responsibility for assisting private aquaculture to [[USDA]], with an inter-agency group ensuring aquaculture activities consider impacts of feral populations and exotic imports on native species.

**Evaluation and Monitoring**: After-stocking evaluations of how well hatchery fish achieve program goals (returns, harvest, survival to spawning, impacts on endemic species) are largely absent. Ecosystem management teams should conduct rigorous evaluations with hatchery personnel involvement. The marriage of high-quality fish production and refined field information is necessary for ecosystem management success.
