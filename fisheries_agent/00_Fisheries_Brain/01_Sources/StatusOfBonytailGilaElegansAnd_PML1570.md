# Status of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #colorado-river-fishes

## Executive Summary

Lake Mohave, an artificial reservoir on the [[Colorado River]] created by closure of [[Davis Dam]] in the early 1950s, holds the largest known populations of two endemic, federally endangered fishes: [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Using collections from the past decade, the authors give a status update for both species. Razorback sucker abundance appears stable, with catch rates and mark-recapture estimates showing no detectable decline. However, the population shows no evidence of recruitment beyond the larval stage, and the authors warn that it will likely be lost by the end of the decade unless the problem is remedied.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Lake Mohave]] is an artificial impoundment on the Colorado River mainstream, formed in the early 1950s when Davis Dam closed (Allan and Roden 1978).
- **Species status:** Both species belong to the imperiled "large-river" fish fauna of the Colorado River basin and are federally listed as endangered (USFWS 1983, 1991). Bonytail is described as arguably the most critically imperiled western fish species, but it is not extinct and is doing comparatively well in the lake.
- **Relative abundance:** Razorback sucker can be captured reliably at almost any time of year, and hundreds of adults can be handled in a few days during the winter-early spring spawning season. Bonytail is much less abundant, but there is a high probability of encountering it in spring (March to May).
- **Background sources:** The [[Bonytail Recovery Plan]] (USFWS 1990), Minckley (1983), and Minckley et al. (1991) provide earlier background. This paper updates status using collections over the past decade and was published in the [[Desert Fishes Council]] proceedings (Volumes XXII and XXIII, 1990 and 1991 symposia).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Monitoring methods:** Abundance was assessed two ways. [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPE) from standardized [[Trammel Net]] sampling indicates trends and is especially useful for long-term datasets. [[Mark-Recapture]] estimates provide absolute abundance but respond slowly to real change.
- **Community changes:** CPE for razorback sucker has tracked changes in other species in the lake, including an increase in [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) and a decrease in [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*).
- **Recruitment threats:** Proposed explanations for the lack of razorback sucker recruitment, and for the poor status of other native Colorado River fishes, include predation by non-native fishes, nutritional constraints, and drift out of the system (Minckley et al. 1991).

## Key Findings

**Razorback sucker abundance**
- Comparisons of data from 1974–1981 (Minckley 1983) with collections from 1981 to the present show no detectable difference in individual or population characteristics between the two periods.
- CPE varies seasonally (lower in summer) but has stayed near 2.5 fish per 100 m² of netting per 24 hours. Consistent trammel-net use in similar habitat over 18 years shows no evidence of change.
- Mark-recapture data: approximately 4,400 adults have been marked since 1981 (most since 1988; the figure is partly garbled in the source). Multiple census techniques estimate about 60,000 fish in the reservoir, and annual estimates over the past 5 years are indistinguishable from one another.
- The authors do not consider mark-recapture useful for short-term assessment because significant delays can occur before a real decline is detected. They recommend CPE as the standard status indicator.

**Size, condition and health**
- Average total length of adults increased from the 1960s into the early 1980s. Since then it has remained unchanged, with males averaging about 55 cm and females about 62 cm TL, based on nearly 10,000 fish measured.
- Weight data and records of anomalies, injury, disease, parasites, and eye condition (blindness, cataracts, bulging) show no detectable change over the past decade.

**Reproduction and recruitment**
- Spawning activity has been documented repeatedly and observed annually since the 1970s. Timing of peak activity varies with weather and lake conditions, but overall intensity has not changed in 20 years.
- Eggs are fertilized, embryos hatch and grow to swim-up, and larvae are abundant for a time in spring (Marsh and Langhorst 1988).
- Apart from 4 juveniles captured in 1987 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department, there is no evidence of recruitment beyond the larval stage. Adults in the lake are large and old (McCarthy and Minckley 1987), and no fish appear to occupy the stages between post-larval and adult life. The authors say this has not changed over two decades.

**Conclusion and warning**
- Razorback sucker abundance is stable, but without recruitment the authors expect that, unless remedied, this population will likely be lost by the end of the decade.
