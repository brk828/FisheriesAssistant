# Geographic Variations in Egg Complement of *Etheostoma lepidum*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #Etheostomatinae #freshwater-ecology #darters

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by Hubbs and Delco examines geographic variation in egg complement (fecundity) among five Texas populations of the [[Greenthroat Darter]] (*Etheostoma lepidum*), a small percid inhabiting spring-fed streams of the [[Edwards Plateau]]. Building on prior work showing that *E. lepidum* exhibits geographic variation in color pattern, meristic characters, and sperm-egg compatibility, the authors demonstrate that egg complement (number of eggs per female of a given size) also varies geographically, and that this variation is essentially an inverse proxy for egg size, since the body cavity is a limited, distensible space. The paper links these fecundity differences to two ecological drivers: population density (via food availability, per [[Svärdson's Egg Size Hypothesis]]) and the presence/absence of sympatric congeners (particularly [[Etheostoma spectabile]]), which affects intraspecific vs. interspecific competition among larvae.

## Background & Description

- **Egg complement vs. body size**: Number of eggs increases roughly **linearly** with female standard length (25–60 mm range studied) across all five populations — no logarithmic relationship was detected (contrasting with the logarithmic pattern reported for *Percina caprodes*).
- **Egg size as inverse of egg number**: Because the female body cavity has a fixed distensible capacity when ripe, egg number per female of a given size is inversely proportional to individual egg diameter. Thus, differences in egg complement are interpreted as proxies for **egg size variation**, not just fecundity per se.
- **Population size rankings (eggs per given female size), largest to smallest complement (i.e., smallest to largest egg size)**:
  1. **West Frio River** — most eggs per size class (smallest eggs), despite being allopatric to related species.
  2. **South Concho River** — intermediate-high.
  3. **San Saba River** — most eggs among the three "allopatric" comparison set discussed later; largest egg complement of that subset.
  4. **Guadalupe River** — smallest egg complement among sympatric/mixed populations (sample size caveat noted).
  5. **East Nueces River** — fewest eggs per given size (largest eggs), notably lower than all other four populations, most apparent at well-sampled size classes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: *E. lepidum* occupies spring-fed streams and rivers of the Edwards Plateau, Texas, including the South Concho River, San Saba River, Guadalupe River, West Frio River/Kent Creek, and East Nueces River systems.
- **Sympatry/allopatry with congeners**:
  - **East Nueces River**: *E. lepidum* is the **only darter** present (fully allopatric) — associated with **large egg size**.
  - **West Frio River**: also allopatric to related species, but has a **small egg size** — an anomaly used to test whether sympatry or surface-tension/egg-size effects better explain sperm-selection phenomena described in [[Hubbs 1960 Sperm Selection Study]].
  - **San Saba River**: **sympatric** with two other darter species — associated with **small egg size** in a sparse population.
  - **South Concho River**: no other darters at the collection locality (though present ~4 miles away) — allopatric-like, denser population, intermediate-large egg size.
  - **Guadalupe River**: mixed — some sublocalities sympatric, others allopatric with related species; data pooled across sublocalities, reducing confidence.
- **Niche partitioning with *Etheostoma spectabile***: In sympatry, *E. lepidum* and *E. spectabile* occupy **different microhabitat niches**, reducing interspecific larval competition relative to intraspecific competition. In allopatry, *E. lepidum* alone occupies both habitats occupied by the two species combined, resulting in denser single-species populations.
- **Reproductive ecology**: Spawns multiple times per season; spawning frequency is controlled by water temperature, and egg number per spawning event is chiefly controlled by female body size (per [[Hubbs and Strawn 1957a]]).

## Key Findings

1. **Geographic variation in egg complement is the rule**, consistent with Hubbs (1958)'s broader hypothesis for darters with adequate sampling — corroborating patterns previously found in *Etheostoma spectabile* and *Percina caprodes*.
2. **Egg complement scales linearly (not logarithmically) with female size** across all five populations sampled, differing from the logarithmic pattern seen in *Percina caprodes*.
3. **Population density correlates positively with egg size**: Denser populations (Nueces, South Concho) tend to have larger eggs/fewer eggs per size class, supporting the [[Svärdson-Lack Egg Size Hypothesis]] that food-limited, dense populations select for larger eggs (better individual offspring survival) rather than higher egg numbers.
4. **Sympatry with congeners correlates with smaller egg size / higher egg number**: Sparse, sympatric populations (e.g., San Saba) exhibit smaller eggs and higher fecundity, potentially because reduced interspecific competition among differently-niched larvae favors producing more numerous, smaller offspring.
5. **The Frio River population is an informative exception**: it is allopatric (like Nueces) yet has small eggs (like sympatric populations), providing a natural test case to disentangle whether **sympatry per se** or **surface-tension effects related to egg size** drive the sperm-selection phenomena documented in [[Hubbs 1960]].
6. **Methodological validation**: Median (rather than mean) egg counts from stripped females were used to minimize bias from incomplete stripping, natural partial spawning, poor female condition, or egg-bound double complements. Cross-validation between stripped, naturally laid, and ovary-counted eggs supported the reliability of stripping-based egg counts.
7. **Human/anthropogenic note**: The Guadalupe River population was heavily collected for a prior life-history study ([[Hubbs and Strawn 1957a]]), potentially altering population density and confounding density-based interpretations for that system.
