# Disposition of Captive-Reared Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document comprises a draft and a final Environmental Assessment (EA) prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Denver, Colorado; Michael Stempel and Richard Wydoski), dated March 7, 1995 (draft) and June 8, 1995 (final). It evaluates options for disposing of surplus captive-reared endangered fishes from the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]]. Four alternatives were considered: No Action, Discontinue Propagation, Stock Surplus Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin, and Implement Disposition Guidelines. The preferred alternative is to implement the Guidelines for Disposition of Captive-Reared Endangered Fish (Appendix A). The EA found no significant adverse impacts, so no Environmental Impact Statement was required, and a Finding of No Significant Impact was to be issued.

## Background & Description

The document covers four endangered large-river fishes of the upper [[Colorado River]] system: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). All were listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended.

Captive broodstocks are developed from pedigreed family lots, using a target of 25 paired matings to retain genetic diversity and keep inbreeding low. Captive-reared fish serve four purposes:
- Refugia: insurance against extirpation of wild stocks.
- Research and development: experimental studies on life-stage requirements, behavior, habitat use, and fish passage.
- Stocking: augmentation and restoration.
- Information and education: aquarium displays, with fewer than 100 fish per species in total.

Propagation often yields more fish than needed because attrition rates are unknown, so surplus fish consume scarce resources such as personnel, facilities, and funds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water development, dams, channelization, and the introduction of non-native fishes have altered the historic flow regime, water temperatures, and backwater and floodplain habitats. Dams also blocked natural migrations. Non-native fishes are suspected competitors and predators of the natives. The primary limiting factor is poor survival in early life stages, which causes inadequate recruitment for self-sustaining wild populations.

The affected environment covers critical habitat in the upper basin, including the Green River, Yampa River, White River, Colorado River, and Gunnison River reaches. It also includes two permanent propagation facilities, the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] (Utah) and the [[Grand Valley Fish Facility]] at Grand Junction (Colorado, Fish Technology Center context as stated). Two further sites were under feasibility study: a municipal raw-water site near Craig, Colorado, on the Yampa River, and a site in a dry wash of Lake Powell, Utah.

The Colorado River and its tributaries flow through 2,317 km (1,440 miles) of arid land and supply water to over 30 million people. The document notes the river is among the most heavily allocated in the world. The [[Recovery Program]] was initiated October 1, 1988, with a 15-year goal, and its five elements are in-stream flows, habitat development, native fish stocking, management of non-native species, and research and monitoring.

## Key Findings

- **No Action** was rejected because surplus fish occupy propagation space needed for priority programs and drain resources.
- **Discontinuing propagation** was dismissed because it would increase the potential for extinction. Captive broodstocks are an integral "insurance policy."
- **Stocking surplus fish in the wild** was not considered acceptable as an indiscriminate practice, since it could cause genetic swamping and compromise the genetic integrity of wild stocks. Stocking plans must be approved by the program, covered by a Federal Fish and Wildlife Permit, and, if they could affect wild stocks, subjected to a formal Section 7 consultation under the Endangered Species Act.
- **Implementing the Disposition Guidelines** is the preferred alternative. Surplus fish are disposed of in seven priority steps:
  1. Program needs (genetic/broodstock development, approved laboratory experiments, planned augmentation or restoration stocking).
  2. Other research studies not identified in annual plans.
  3. Agencies in the Lower Colorado River Basin for research and augmentation.
  4. Information, education, and public display.
  5. Maintenance for possible future use, if space allows and cost is not prohibitive.
  6. Museum or reference collections.
  7. Euthanasia by accepted protocol (Nickum 1988), with required permits.
- Priorities 6 and 7 require Regional Director approval. Documentation of priorities 1 through 5 must come before priorities 6 and 7 are pursued.
- The Guidelines were reviewed by approximately 350 persons and agencies, with a 30-day public comment period. Comments were incorporated into the final document.
- The EA concluded that no significant adverse environmental impacts were identified and that an Environmental Impact Statement was not needed.
