## Executive Summary
This document is not a primary scientific study but an internal **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Fisheries memo** dated December 21, 1993, discussing **NAFTA-related Fisheries Stewardship funding** for Fiscal Years 1994–1995. It outlines funding priorities, target watersheds, and programmatic focus areas (inventory, training, aquaculture) aimed at conserving imperiled aquatic species in the U.S.–Mexico border region. No direct morphological, taxonomic, or population data on specific fish species are presented; instead, the memo addresses **conservation program structure, budget allocation, and habitat/watershed targeting** relevant to [[Threatened and Endangered Fish Species]] in the Southwest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
While no specific species accounts are given, the memo identifies key **watersheds and habitats** targeted for aquatic conservation work under the NAFTA Fisheries program, implying these are priority ecological zones for imperiled aquatic species:

- [[Pacific Wetlands]] (in cooperation with Region 1)
- [[Lower Colorado River]] and [[Gila River]]
- [[Rio Yaqui]]
- [[Rio de la Concepcion]]
- [[Guzman Basin]]
- [[Rio Grande]]
  - Below [[Falcon Dam]]
  - [[Elephant Butte]] to [[Big Bend]]
- [[Pecos River]]

These systems represent critical habitat corridors for **native desert fish communities**, many of which include federally listed [[Threatened and Endangered Species|threatened, endangered, and candidate species]]. The memo emphasizes:
- **Aquatic community inventory** — including habitat analysis, water quality assessment, hydrology, genetic inventory, and disease inventory.
- **Training programs** — collaborative efforts with Mexican universities focused on **endangered fish culture** techniques.
- **Aquaculture substitution** — evaluating aquaculture as an alternative to commercial activities that negatively impact imperiled aquatic species.

This reflects a broader ecological management approach centered on **binational watershed stewardship** rather than single-species ecology.

## Key Findings
- The **NAFTA Fisheries team** (per Langley, meeting at Buenos Aires NWR, Dec 17, 1993) established **FY1995 funding priorities** in the following order:
  1. [[Threatened and Endangered Species]] and candidate species
  2. [[Biodiversity]] conservation
  3. [[Migratory Species]]
  4. Public [[Outreach]] and education

- The **President's FY1995 budget** allocated **$1.35 million** in Fisheries funding to support NAFTA-related conservation work.
- Field stations (including **Region 1**) must submit **competitive proposals** for funding; proposals due to the NAFTA team leader by **January 20, 1994** (a compressed timeline noted as a logistical challenge).
- Fisheries program focus areas identified by the author (Fjetland/Langley) include:
  - **Inventory** of aquatic communities, habitats, hydrology, genetics, and disease.
  - **Training** partnerships with Mexican universities for endangered fish culture.
  - **Aquaculture** as a conservation-compatible alternative to harmful commercial practices.
- A parallel administrative effort (Stine) compiled **1993 program accomplishments** and drafted **FY1994–1995 planning documents**, tied to Stewardship funding and the anticipated NAFTA budget.
- A **Southwest Regional Directory ("A User's Guide")** was being developed by Art Neddleman (Division of Refuges) to catalog Fisheries field station information, with design assistance available via supervisor Frank Bryce.

**Overall significance**: This memo illustrates **binational conservation planning infrastructure** (via NAFTA) supporting freshwater fish conservation in the U.S. Southwest and northern Mexico, emphasizing systemic watershed-level stewardship over individual species research. It serves as a valuable historical policy artifact for understanding funding mechanisms behind programs protecting species such as those found in the [[Gila River]] and [[Rio Grande]] basins.
