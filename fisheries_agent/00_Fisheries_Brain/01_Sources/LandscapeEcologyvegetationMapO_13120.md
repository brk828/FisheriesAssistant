# Landscape Ecology-Vegetation Map of Tlaloc and Pelado Volcanoes, Mexico

#vegetation-ecology #landscape-ecology #lagomorpha

## Executive Summary

This ITC Journal paper (1992-3) by Alejandro Velazquez describes a detailed [[Landscape Ecology-Vegetation Map]] at 1:75,000 scale for the Tlaloc and Pelado volcanoes in central Mexico. The map was built to support a [[Habitat Suitability Survey]] for the endangered endemic [[Volcano Rabbit]] (*Romerolagus diazi*), whose habitat is being fragmented. The study is terrestrial. It contains no aquatic or fish data.

The map identifies 13 natural and three cultural plant communities, grouped into 22 mapping units within eight main landscapes. The work follows the [[ITC Approach]] of mapping plant communities and environmental factors as integrated entities, using [[Aerial Photo Interpretation]] together with field sampling by the [[Braun-Blanquet Method]].

## Background & Description

Vegetation has long been mapped for climatic, soil, forestry and biogeographic purposes. In Mexico, earlier maps relied on physiognomy or on floristic types, such as those by [[Miranda and Hernandez-X]] and [[Rzedowski]]. Detailed quantitative mapping of plant communities, combining floristic and physiognomic traits, had not been done. The author argues this is needed to evaluate fauna habitats and plan sustainable resource management.

The [[Volcano Rabbit]] is endemic and endangered. Its habitat was fragmented on Tlaloc and Pelado, which are classed as "special protected areas". No ecologically sound evaluation had been carried out, so vegetation mapping was urgent. The author cites comparable work on the [[African Elephant]] in Tsavo, the [[African Buffalo]] in Lake Manyara, and the [[Javan Rhinoceros]] in Ujung Kulon. These studies show that wild herbivores select habitat by forage, cover, water and accessibility.

The stated purpose is to document current spatial vegetation patterns as baseline information for habitat-use studies and for conservation and management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area**
- The mapped area lies between 19°02' and 19°13'N and 98°56'30" and 99°16'W, mostly within Tlalpan and Milpa Alta (Federal District).
- Elevation ranges from 2500 to 3660 m asl. The area covers about 680 km².
- Lithology is dominated by basalts and andesites on Holocene lava flows. Pyroclastic materials occur in hollows and cones.

**Soils**
- Lithosols are dominant. Regosols occur on the main cones, Andosols and Phaeozems on cones, footslopes and flat areas.

**Climate**
- Two climatic zones follow the Köppen system modified by [[Garcia]]:
  - "C(w2)(w)big" at about 2800–3450 m: temperate, mild, subhumid, mean annual temperature 5–18°C.
  - "C(w2)(w)cig" above 3450 m: temperate, cool, subhumid, mean annual temperature 5–12°C.
- Dry season: November–May. Rainy season: June–October.
- Mean annual rainfall is about 1000 mm/year (10-year record, 1980–90, El Guarda station). Humidity increases from east to west.

**Biogeography and biodiversity**
- The area is a transition zone between the Neotropical and Nearctic regions, with a mix of endemic and northern and southern taxa.
- Vertebrates of note include the [[Pocket Gopher]] (*Pappogeomys merriami*), the [[Wild Hen]] (*Dendrortyx macroura*), *Sceloporus* lizards, and the volcano rabbit.

**Human pressure**
- The main threat is the growth of Mexico City onto the volcano footslopes. This brings agricultural encroachment, weekend tourism, logging, resin extraction and livestock grazing.
- Burning, grazing and soil extraction were recorded across the area.

## Key Findings

**Survey design and data**
- Eighty-five panchromatic black-and-white aerial photographs at 1:20,000 (February 1986) were used. Minimum mappable area was 1 ha.
- Fieldwork ran from September 1988 to January 1989. A total of 177 sample units were surveyed using a stratified random design.
- Sample-unit sizes: 625 m² for forest, 200 m² for shrubland, 125 m² for grassland.
- Classification used TWINSPAN with outlier exclusion. Of the 177 units, 159 remained in the analysis.
- The final analysis included 331 vascular plant species, 161 genera and 61 families.

**Vegetation classification**
- 13 natural and 3 cultural plant communities were identified and grouped into seven plant community groups (similar to alliances).
- Six physiognomic structural types were defined on grass and tree cover:
  - Open grassland (OG): under 15 cm, tree cover under 20%.
  - Dense grassland (DG): 15–50 cm, dense bunchgrass, mainly on cones and crater bottoms.
  - Wooded grassland (WG): 50–150 cm, tree cover 20–60%.
  - Woodland (W): the most widespread type, tree cover 20–60%, grass under 50%.
  - Forest (F): tree cover 60–80%, shrub layer most prominent.
  - Dense forest (DF): trees over 30 m, tree cover above 80%.

**The map**
- 22 map units grouped into eight main landscapes: plateaus, crater bottoms, undulating valleys, cones, escarpments, Holocene lava flows, accumulation plains and footslopes.
- Seven units contain a single plant community.
- The footslopes are the most widespread terrain and show the greatest mosaic of communities.

**Distribution patterns**
- *Pinus hartwegii* communities occur at high elevations but also near the volcano bases, at about 2850 m.
- Holocene lava flows act as "ecological corridors" along the full elevation gradient, linking high and low sites and enabling species dispersal.
- *Furcraea bedinghausii* mega-rosette communities occur mainly on Pelado, with plants up to about 6 m tall.
- Pure, relatively well-conserved fir (*Abies religiosa*) forest occurs on escarpments in the upper parts of Tlaloc and Pelado.

**Main drivers of plant community distribution (in order of importance)**
1. Climate: greater humidity on western slopes and cooler temperatures at high elevations.
2. Soil: depth and drainage, which strongly affect grassland types.
3. Elevation: closely linked to temperature.
4. Human activity: conversion of natural communities to field crops.

**Implications and recommendations**
- The map is a baseline for studying volcano rabbit habitat use, ecological amplitude and habitat preference, and for monitoring.
- The author recommends further quantitative work, such as [[Detrended Correspondence Analysis]] and [[Canonical Correspondence Analysis]], to test environment–community relationships.
- The paper concludes that a holistic, biotic-plus-abiotic approach offers an ecologically sound basis for habitat conservation of both plant and animal species.
