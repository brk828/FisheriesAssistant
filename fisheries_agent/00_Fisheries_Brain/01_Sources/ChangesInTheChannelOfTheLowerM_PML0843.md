# Changes in the Channel of the Lower Missouri River and Effects on Fish and Wildlife

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 Missouri Department of Conservation report (Aquatic Series No. 11, by John T. Funk and John W. Robinson) documents how [[Channelization]] of the lower [[Missouri River]], from Rulo, Nebraska to the mouth, reduced river length, water surface area, islands, and side channels between 1879 and 1972. The authors use Corps of Engineers maps comparing 1879 and 1954 channels, plus 1972 hydrographic surveys, to quantify losses. They then reconstruct commercial fishery history from 1894 to 1970 and review the decline of wildlife dependent on bottomland habitat. Their central conclusion is that habitat simplification, especially the loss of islands, chutes and backwaters, coincided with a less diverse fish community dominated by a few tolerant species.

## Background & Description

The report opens with contrasts between the river explorers described (including [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]], 1804) and the channel they would find today. Federal improvement of the channel began in 1884 under the [[Missouri River Commission]] and continued after 1902 under the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]]. The study area was limited to the Kansas City District reach from Rulo to the mouth, roughly 514.8 river miles in 1954, as the Omaha District reach was excluded.

Channel-control structures are described in detail:

- **Revetments**: woven willow or lumber mattresses ballasted with rock to stabilize banks.
- **Pile dikes**: clumps of piles that deflect current and cause silt to accumulate behind them, building land.
- **Rock dikes**: broken stone dikes, which are less permeable, create eddies and faster sediment accretion. Most pile dikes were later filled with rock.

Key development phases were:

- Pre-1885: little beyond poorly coordinated snag removal.
- 1885 to 1900: systematic, intensive snag removal and structure building, later lost to lack of maintenance.
- 1912 to 1933: navigation channel development, extended to Sioux City, Iowa, in 1933.
- 1945: authorization of a 9-ft by 300-ft channel, with channel stability as the main objective by 1967 (98% complete).
- Around 1960: replacement of pile dikes with rock dikes began.

Six upstream mainstem dams (Gavins Point, Fort Randall, Big Bend, Oahe, Garrison and Fort Peck) reduced winter flows and sediment load in the lower river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Channel area loss (1879 to 1972):**

- River length shortened by 45.6 miles, from 544 miles in 1879 to 514.8 miles in 1954, and to Rulo at mile 498.4 in 1972.
- Total water surface area fell from 121,739 acres (1879) to 71,151 acres (1954), a loss of 50,588 acres. Another 10,244 acres were lost from 1954 to 1972, giving a total loss of 60,832 acres (about 50%).
- Unconnected islands declined from 161 (24,419 acres) in 1879 to 18 (419 acres) in 1954, a 98% reduction in area.
- Total bank-to-bank area fell from 250,252 to 183,857 acres (27%), with much of the lost land annexed by riparian owners.

**Reach-level changes:** Water surface losses by reach ranged from about 24% to 59% by 1954, and total losses by 1972 ranged from about 27% to 67%, with Map 5 (Cote Sans Dessein to Sandy Hook) and Map 6 (Sandy Hook to Boonville) among the most altered.

**Habitat roles:** Islands, chutes and sloughs provided nursery and feeding areas, sheltered fishing sites and nesting and den sites for [[Catfish]] (snags were used as nest sites) and waterfowl. Bottomland forest and backwaters sustained [[Raccoon]], [[Deer]], [[Wood Duck]] and furbearers such as [[Otter]], [[Beaver]], [[Muskrat]] and [[Mink]].

## Key Findings

**Fishery history**

- Early commercial catches were dominated by catfish (30% in 1894, 24% in 1899) and sturgeon (lake sturgeon 2% in 1894, 0.3% in 1899; shovelnose 17% in 1899). Paddlefish were 8% in 1894 and 8% in 1899.
- [[Common Carp]] was introduced in the 1880s through a Missouri Fish Commission hatchery at Forest Park, St. Louis. Carp made up 12.1% of the 1894 catch and dominated after about 1908 (58%). The authors note carp likely displaced native buffaloes and carpsuckers.
- Sturgeon and paddlefish declined sharply. Paddlefish fell to 0.4% by 1931. The authors suggest overfishing was a major cause, with habitat change not ruled out.
- Commercial fishing was licensed from 1940. Catch reports show fishermen falling from 772 (1945) to 404 (1970), with the harvest varying from about 500,000 pounds in 1945 to about 106,000 pounds in 1965.
- Carp, buffaloes and catfishes made up 90 to 95% of the catch in the 1945 to 1970 period. Channel catfish increased in proportion after rock dikes became common, while [[Flathead Catfish]] declined.
- The authors conclude the fish community became dominated by a few species adapted to swift, turbid conditions, and that "the circumstantial evidence of a direct relationship between decreased diversity in the habitat and decreased diversity in the fish population is very strong."
- Reduced turbidity from upstream dams may have allowed [[Skipjack Herring]] and [[White Bass]] to spread, and the mimic and spotfin shiners were newly recorded.
- The species list (after Pflieger, 1975) includes about 60 species, with several added in recent collections.

**Wildlife history**

- Early travelers reported abundant game. Lewis and Clark's party killed 73 deer between May 27 and July 17, 1804.
- [[Carolina Parakeet]] disappeared by the mid-1880s, probably due to loss of sycamore and hollow-tree habitat.
- The [[Trumpeter Swan]] was virtually wiped out in the Missouri valley by 1900.
- Bison, elk and bear were virtually extinct along the river well before 1900.
- Deer were abundant in the river counties into mid-century; the river-margin deer herd is now among the largest and most productive in Missouri, and the loss of forest on former islands removed valuable habitat.
- Furbearers: otter is now very rare, beaver recovered after 1920s protection, muskrat and mink declined with loss of backwaters, and raccoon populations recovered after the 1940s.

**Conclusions**

The authors see the river's changes as a clear loss of fish and wildlife habitat, and they emphasize that the Corps' stated design intent, a stabilized single channel, removed the diversity of islands, chutes and backwaters on which the historic fish and wildlife depended.
