# The Life History of the Slough Darter (*Etheostoma gracile*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey Biological Notes paper (No. 58, 1967) by Marvin E. Braasch and Philip W. Smith presents the first detailed life-history study of the [[Slough Darter]] (*Etheostoma gracile*), the westernmost member of the subgenus *Hololepis*. Fieldwork was conducted in [[Dismal Creek]], Fayette County, Illinois, from February to December 1964. The study covers reproductive cycles, spawning timing and behavior, egg and larval development, growth, population age structure, food habits, associated species, competition, predation and parasitism. The authors report that spawning occurs later than previously recorded, in late May, and that the species is an evolutionarily advanced, highly specialized darter with no parental care.

## Background & Description

- **Subgenus context:** The eight species and subspecies of *Hololepis* typically inhabit swamps, sloughs, and low-gradient streams of the Coastal Plain and [[Mississippi River]] valley. Earlier ecological inferences came from [[Hubbs and Cannon (1935)]] and [[Collette (1962)]].
- **Morphology:** Adults are small, normally 35–50 mm total length. The lateral line has 13–27 pored scales and lies within three to five scale rows of the base of the spinous dorsal fin. Adult males show green blotches or vertical bands and, in breeding season, a blue-black spinous dorsal fin with red dots. Males have a smaller genital pore than anal pore; females have a larger genital pore.
- **Juvenile identification:** Young are recognized by a distinctly reddish eye, three small caudal spots, and an upward flexure of the lateral-line groove.
- **Taxonomy:** Described by [[Girard]] (1859) as *Boleosoma gracile*. *Poecilichthys butlerianus* and *P. palustris* are junior synonyms. Much past confusion arose from misidentification with *E. exile* (allopatric) and *E. fusiforme* (parapatric).
- **Distribution:** From southern Illinois through western Kentucky and Tennessee, the Missouri bootheel, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, central Texas, eastern Oklahoma, and southeastern Kansas. Generally abundant in southern Illinois.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** A roughly 34-mile stretch (as stated in the source) of [[Dismal Creek]] near the Route 37 bridge, flowing through dissected clay hills on Illinoian glacial till in the [[Wabash River]] watershed. Spring pools reached up to 20 feet wide and occasionally over 6 feet deep; fall pools were 10–15 feet wide and 2–3 feet deep. During drought the creek becomes discontinuous and pools stagnate. Pollution from grazing farm animals was considerable. Water temperature in shade closely tracked air temperature, deviating no more than 2 °F through spring.

**Habitat:** Slough darters in Dismal Creek occurred almost exclusively in mud-bottomed pools. In winter, individuals overwintered in deep sand-bottomed pools when shallower pools froze.

**Associated species:** Predominant species included [[Redfin Shiner]], [[Blackstripe Topminnow]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Creek Chubsucker]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Striped Shiner]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Pirate Perch]], [[White Sucker]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Grass Pickerel]] (12 listed). The slough darter was rivaled in abundance by [[Bluntnose Darter]]; other darters present were [[Blackside Darter]], [[Johnny Darter]], and [[Orangethroat Darter]].

**Environmental events:** In fall 1964 Dismal Creek was reduced to stagnant pools; a heavy fish kill followed the first December freeze, eliminating most slough darters. Dissolved oxygen was not measured.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- The study area was visited 23 times between February 14 and December 12, 1964, with sampling at 2-week to 4-week intervals depending on season. Collections were made with a minnow seine.
- Approximately 400 slough darters were examined, including supplemental collections from nearby creeks.

**Reproduction**
- **Males:** Breeding coloration begins to develop by mid-February and peaks by late April. Four to six large [[Nuptial Tubercles]] form on the chin by late April, with smaller ones on pelvic and anal fins. Color likely signals sex and territoriality. Males reach sexual readiness earlier than females and are sexually mature by the spring after hatching.
- **Females:** Egg differentiation begins in early November; significant growth occurs from March. Eggs were about 0.85 mm at spawning. More than 2,500 eggs are produced per ovary per season, but only about 20 percent reach maturity and only a few are laid. Ovaries change from white to yellow to yellow-orange. The genital papilla enlarges markedly from December to April (in 2-year-old females, from 0.9 × 1.1 mm in mid-February to 1.5 × 2.0 mm by late March).
- **Courtship:** Largely tactile. The male pursues the female, vibrates pectoral fins along her sides, and rubs his chin tubercles over her head and snout. The female is passive and deposits single eggs on objects such as leaf petioles, arranged neatly. The male fertilizes each egg as it is laid. Females laid 30–50 eggs in the laboratory and spawned only once; no parental care was observed.
- **Spawning time:** Late May in Dismal Creek (observed May 24–30 in aquarium fish; wild eggs possibly as early as May 15). This is later than [[Forbes and Richardson (1920)]] (April 28), [[Hubbs and Cannon (1935)]] (late March–April), and [[Collette (1962)]] (mid-March in Texas). The spawning season lasts only 1 or 2 days.

**Development**
- Hatching took 5 days at 73 °F, faster than other darters studied (e.g., 5.5–8.0 days in *E. nigrum*; 18 days in *E. blennioides*).
- Hatchlings were 2.8 mm total length. Pored lateral-line scales first appear at about 15 mm and about 1 week of age. Squamation is complete by about 3 weeks; green pigment and red dorsal dots appear by about 1.5 months, when fish are miniature adults.
- Growth is sigmoid, fastest soon after hatching. The young are sexually mature at the end of their first year. Little sex-related size difference was found.

**Population**
- Adults concentrated in deep pools in winter and became scarce in late spring. Only two adults were taken after July 1; postspawning samples were mostly young-of-the-year.
- Of 109 prespawning adults, 2-year-old fish (1962 year-class) made up 57 percent; 2- and 3-year-old fish together made up 94 percent. Only four were 1 year old, suggesting partial failure of the 1963 spawning. The sex ratio was approximately 1:1.
- The authors conclude physical habitat is the principal limiting factor and that habitat change likely controls population levels.

**Food habits**
- Adults fed mainly on midge larvae (Chironomidae) and microcrustaceans (copepods, cladocerans) in winter, with mayfly larvae dominating in May (56 percent by volume).
- Feeding was greatest in spring, intermediate in winter, and lowest in summer and fall. Food choice depends mainly on prey size and availability.
- Juveniles feed on midge larvae and microcrustaceans within about 2 weeks of hatching.

**Interactions**
- Interspecific food competition appeared unimportant; only [[Bluntnose Darter]] competed directly year-round. Darters feed on the bottom, whereas most minnows, topminnows, and sunfishes feed higher in the water column.
- Predation appeared low in Dismal Creek; no darters were found in sunfish stomachs there. Warmouth from another stream did contain darters. Esox niger and Micropterus salmoides are noted predators of the related *E. fusiforme*.
- Approximately 90 percent of slough darters in Dismal Creek were infected by a small fluke, *Crepidostomum ?isostomum*, with no apparent harm. Nematode infection was under 1 percent.

**Phylogenetic position**
- The authors judge the reproductive cycle evolutionarily advanced, showing greater specialization than *E. exile*. Similarities to *E. microperca* (subgenus *Microperca*) lead the authors to note a close relationship between the two groups, consistent with [[Collette (1965)]].
