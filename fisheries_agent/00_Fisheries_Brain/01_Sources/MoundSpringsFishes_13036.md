# Fishes of South Australia's North East Deserts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *Natural History of the North East Deserts* (Royal Society of South Australia, 1990) gathers chapters on the region's hydrology and aquatic systems, with its main fish content in Glover's chapter "Fishes." The region's fauna is diverse despite its aridity: 23 species in 11 families are recorded from the Lake Eyre drainage portion of the north east. The chapter reviews species diversity and endemism, fish ecology and dispersal, adaptations to harsh conditions, historical and current fisheries, and threats from bores, cattle, exotic fishes, tourism and illegal netting. Companion chapters on [[Mound Springs]] (Boyd) and [[Coongie Lakes]] (Reid & Puckridge) describe the springs and lake systems that sustain these fish populations.

## Background & Description

The north east deserts contain four main aquatic habitat types: river systems, artesian springs, artesian bores (and their drains), and dams and reservoirs. Water chemistry in these habitats, particularly temperature, salinity and dissolved oxygen, is highly variable. Glover's (1982) ecological review of central Australian fishes is based mainly on north east species, and the region is one of the best-studied parts of arid Australia for fish.

**Diversity and taxonomy**
- Twenty-three species in 11 families are recorded, including most species known from the [[Lake Eyre Drainage Division]].
- The fauna mixes predominantly tropical, temperate and widespread forms.
- The best-represented families are Plotosidae (eel-tail catfishes), Atherinidae (hardyheads), Teraponidae (grunters/perches) and Eleotridae (gudgeons). Most other families are represented by a single species.
- Several eel-tail catfishes are difficult to separate morphologically, and some species await description. Electrophoretic comparisons suggest that cryptic species may exist in central drainages.

**Widespread species**
The most widely distributed and commonly encountered fishes are:
- [[Bony Bream]] (*Nematalosa erebi*), maximum length 47 cm
- [[Lake Eyre Hardyhead]] (*Craterocephalus eyresii*), maximum length 10 cm
- [[Spangled Perch]] (*Leiopotherapon unicolor*), maximum length 25 cm
- [[Desert Goby]] (*Chlamydogobius eremius*), maximum length 6 cm
- [[Eastern Gambusia]] (*Gambusia holbrooki*), an exotic, maximum length 6 cm

[[Yellowbelly]] (*Macquaria ambigua*), maximum length 76 cm, is the largest common species. The [[Australian Smelt]] (*Retropinna semoni*, maximum length 10 cm) is also illustrated among the common fishes.

**Endemic species**
Several fishes are highly restricted in range but locally abundant:
- [[Dalhousie Catfish]] (*Neosilurus* sp. nov. 1), known only from [[Dalhousie Springs]]
- [[Dalhousie Hardyhead]] (*Craterocephalus dalhousiensis*), confined to Dalhousie Springs
- Dalhousie goby (*Chlamydogobius* sp. nov.), confined to Dalhousie Springs
- [[Flinders Ranges Gudgeon]] (*Mogurnda* sp. nov.), endemic to the north east

Endemic taxa at Dalhousie Springs and Elizabeth Springs (Queensland) suggest long isolation of these populations.

**Exotic species**
- Eastern gambusia and [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) are introduced. Goldfish occur only in the Coongie Lakes, where they are not abundant.
- A record of redfin perch (*Perca fluviatilis*) in the Cooper Creek drainage is considered erroneous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and flooding**
Across the broader region, flooding is the most important factor shaping the life histories of aquatic organisms. Glover (1982) and others conclude that floodwaters are the major, if not exclusive, dispersal mechanism for fishes in central Australia. Lake Eyre, when it fills, may be the essential transfer point for fish moving between major drainage basins.

**Dispersal**
- Aerial dispersal (via thermals or birds) probably occurs only very rarely, and evidence for it in central Australia is scarce and unconfirmed.
- Establishment of new populations by aerial transport would require an uncommon set of circumstances.

**Physiological tolerance and adaptation**
Most north east fishes are tolerant of extreme salinity, temperature and oxygen levels. The successful species (bony bream, Eastern gambusia, Lake Eyre hardyhead, spangled perch and desert goby) are widely dispersed and occur in most habitat types. The desert goby shows a suite of adaptations, including:
- Thermal refuges (congregating in cooler shallows or stream-bed silt, or partly withdrawing from water to cool by evaporation)
- Respiratory refuges (aerial respiration, or using oxygen-rich water near photosynthetic algal mats)
- Cryptic behaviour (strong preference for plant cover, rapid colour change, and feeding non-visually)
- A broad diet, rapid growth and fast population establishment

**Survival in dry conditions**
- No true fish in central Australia is known to survive aestivation in completely dry mud. The desert goby, and possibly spangled perch, can survive short periods buried in wet or moist mud.
- Spangled perch appear quickly in newly filled dams after heavy rain. This is most likely due to fish entering flash floods, though some may arrive by aerial dispersal.

**Mass mortalities**
Mass fish deaths in Lake Eyre have been documented in several studies. Causes are thought to include high salinity, deoxygenation, rapid temperature drops, algal toxins and gill clogging by algae.

**Artesian springs and bores**
- Fish in artesian bore drains are not believed to have been ejected from bores with the artesian water. Netting trials at Oodnadatta and Beresford railway station found no fish ejected from bore overflows.
- Bores have contributed to the degradation of some springs by draining artesian water, but they also provide beneficial supplementary habitat for fish.

**Mound springs context**
Boyd's chapter on [[Mound Springs]] describes the springs as the final stages of water movement through the [[Great Artesian Basin]]. Key points relevant to fish habitat include:
- Spring water temperatures are mostly 20–35°C, with extremes of 14°C and 46°C recorded.
- Holmes et al. (1981) measured discharge from 15 springs, with a median of 69 m³/day (range 1.7–337 m³/day).
- The largest Dalhousie springs reach about 14 ML/day (162 L/s), but most flow at less than 10 L/s.
- Several studies estimate that spring discharge is a small fraction of the basin's total water. Habermehl (1980, 1986a) suggests further extraction will have little, and generally only local, effects on outflow.
- Potential threats include reduced water levels from large-scale extraction, such as for the [[Roxby Downs Olympic Dam Project]].

**Coongie Lakes context**
Reid and Puckridge describe the [[Coongie Lakes]] as a series of impermanent, shallow lakes fed by the North West Branch of [[Cooper Creek]]. Flooding passes sequentially through Tirrawarra Swamp, Kudriemitchie Channel and the connected lakes. The lakes are listed on the Register of the National Estate, and a larger region is included in the RAMSAR Convention as a wetland of international importance for waterbirds.

**Fisheries and human use**
- Aboriginal people historically ate fish. Fish were especially important in drought periods, when "small fish" from artesian springs were pounded into a paste. These were probably mainly desert goby.
- Few north east fishes reach sizes suitable for modern fisheries, but both recreational and commercial fisheries operate on a significant scale.
- The recreational fishery is popular but fluctuating, targeting mainly yellowbelly, with bony bream and spangled perch also sought. It is concentrated in large waterholes on Cooper Creek between Innamincka and Coongie Lakes, and in the Diamantina River drainage south of Birdsville. Gill nets, which are illegal in the north east, are used along with hook and line.
- The commercial fishery is described as lucrative but essentially illegal because of the netting method. Catches of yellowbelly are reportedly trucked to the Melbourne market.

## Key Findings

- **High diversity in a harsh region:** 23 fish species in 11 families are recorded, including widespread, endemic and exotic components.
- **Widespread generalists dominate:** Bony bream, Lake Eyre hardyhead, spangled perch, desert goby and Eastern gambusia occur in most habitat types and are the most successful species.
- **Endemism is concentrated at Dalhousie Springs:** Dalhousie catfish, Dalhousie hardyhead and the Dalhousie goby are confined there, along with the Flinders Ranges gudgeon, which is endemic to the north east.
- **Cryptic diversity may be underestimated:** Taxonomic work is ongoing, with some eel-tail catfishes and possibly more cryptic species awaiting description.
- **Flooding drives the system:** Floodwaters are the main dispersal mechanism, and Lake Eyre is a key transfer point between basins.
- **Adaptations enable survival:** Tolerance to extreme conditions, thermal and respiratory refuges, cryptic behaviour and broad diets allow fish to persist in ephemeral and artesian habitats.
- **Mass mortalities are a natural risk:** Salinity, deoxygenation, temperature shocks, algal toxins and gill clogging cause fish kills in Lake Eyre.
- **Human impacts are significant:**
  - Uncontrolled bore flows and free-ranging cattle have degraded many artesian springs, and some springs have ceased flowing.
  - Exotic Eastern gambusia has displaced native fish in an artesian habitat in the Lake Frome basin, and may eliminate smaller native species.
  - Tourism damages waterside vegetation, snags and banks, and increases fishing pressure.
  - Illegal netting threatens larger fish, including across the Lake Eyre drainage in Queensland.
- **Regulation has begun:** Capping or regulating uncontrolled bores started in 1978, and fencing has protected some springs. Fencing, monitoring and provision of camping and interpretive facilities at Dalhousie Springs are recommended.
- **Conservation priorities:** Endemic species with restricted ranges are vulnerable to extinction from natural or other disasters. The chapter calls for regulation of human activities in ecologically sensitive areas and a monitoring programme capable of detecting adverse environmental change.
