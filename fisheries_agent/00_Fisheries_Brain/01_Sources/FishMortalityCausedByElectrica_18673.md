# Fish Mortality Caused by Electrical Shockers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 study by [[Vircit S. Pratt]] of the [[Idaho Cooperative Wildlife Research Unit]] measured mortality caused by [[Electrical Fish Shockers]] on three trout species. Experiments were run in hatchery raceways in Michigan in 1950 using 110-volt alternating current (A.C.) and 230-volt direct current (D.C.) units. Overall mortality was 6.4 percent, with A.C. causing significantly higher death rates than D.C. The author concludes that both unit types are acceptable for [[Stream Fish Population Studies]] because mortality under field conditions is expected to be negligible.

## Background & Description

Electrical shockers had become a widely used tool in [[Fish Management]], particularly for stream-fish population work. Earlier research focused mainly on improving catching efficiency, with little attention to mortality. Shocking-induced death is relevant to [[Mark-and-Recovery Method]] estimates: unrecorded deaths, including delayed deaths occurring days after apparent recovery, bias population estimates upward.

Prior reports varied widely. [[Hauck (1949)]] reported a 26 percent kill of large rainbow trout (average 3.7 lb) in an Idaho irrigation canal using a 495-watt, 110-volt A.C. generator and hand-held electrodes. [[Shetter (1947)]] found mortality generally below 1 percent in smaller Michigan stream trout. Some workers linked mortality to fish size, and others reported species-specific susceptibility (Smith and Elson, 1950; Larkin, 1950).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The experiment was conducted in raceways at the Almena sub-station of the Wolf Lake Hatchery. Water conditions were reported as within the range needed for effective shocking:

- Resistivity: approximately 3,250 ohms per cubic centimeter at 25°C
- pH: 8.0
- Methyl-orange alkalinity: 140 ppm as calcium carbonate (May 31 and June 19)

The author notes that raceway conditions differ from streams. Raceways hold high fish densities near the electrodes, and fish that were paralyzed but unseen could remain in the field. In streams, operators move electrodes quickly and fish concentrations are lower, so mortality is expected to be lower.

## Key Findings

- **Test subjects:** 450 legal-sized (7 inches or more total length) fish were used: [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Mean total lengths were 9.9 in (brook), 7.7 in (brown), and 7.5 in (rainbow).
- **Design:** Fifty fish per species were assigned to A.C. exposure, 50 per species to D.C. exposure, and 50 per species to controls that were handled but not shocked. Fish were held 1 foot from the electrode for 15 seconds, and the three shocking dates were May 31, June 19, and July 6, 1950.
- **Exclusions:** Of the fish, 24 trout were unaccounted for, likely removed by poachers driven off the property on June 12, with possible animal predation. These were excluded from the analysis.
- **Overall mortality:** Of the corrected 282 fish, 18 died (6.4 percent). Immediate mortality was 12 fish (4.3 percent), and delayed mortality was 6 fish (2.1 percent).
- **Current type:** A.C. killed 15 of 135 trout (11.1 percent), and D.C. killed 3 of 147 (2.0 percent). The difference was highly significant by [[Chi-Square Test]] (χ² = 61.7).
- **Handling vs. shock:** No control fish died, so the author attributes deaths to electrical shock rather than handling.
- **Size and species:** Total lengths ranged from 6.5 to 13.6 inches. Deaths occurred across small and large fish, and no clear species susceptibility was found.
- **Immediate deaths:** These fish escaped the seine nets, were lost in turbid water, and lay under the electrodes for several minutes beyond the prescribed exposure period.
- **Conclusion:** Both unit types are considered acceptable for stream population studies. The author expects negligible mortality in streams with comparable physico-chemical conditions, and the units should remain valuable for estimating stream-fish populations.
