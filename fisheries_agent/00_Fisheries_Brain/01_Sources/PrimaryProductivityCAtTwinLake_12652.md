# Primary Productivity (C¹⁴) at Twin Lakes Colorado: 1973-81 Study Results

#limnology #primary-productivity #phytoplankton #carbon-fixation #alpine-lakes #reservoir-ecology #pumped-storage

## Executive Summary

This report presents results from C¹⁴ primary productivity studies conducted at [[Twin Lakes]], [[Colorado]], from 1973-81, prior to operation of the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]]. The study characterizes baseline primary production rates in these high-mountain lakes (2802 m elevation) to enable assessment of pumped-storage impacts. Primary production was measured using C¹⁴ radioisotope tracer methods at monthly intervals. The upper lake averaged 11.3 g C/(m²·a) annual production, while the lower lake averaged 25.8 g C/(m²·a), both falling within oligotrophic to ultra-oligotrophic ranges. Production patterns were strongly influenced by annual runoff volume, with the two lakes displaying inverse productivity relationships: high runoff years reduced upper lake production (due to flushing and turbidity) but enhanced lower lake production (via nutrient transport). Light availability, nutrient concentrations (particularly phosphorus limitation), algal biomass, and water temperature also affected production rates. The study establishes critical pre-operational baseline data for this cooperative effort involving the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

[[Twin Lakes]] are located at the eastern foot of the [[Sawatch Range]] in the upper [[Arkansas River Valley]] of central [[Colorado]] at 2802 m elevation. The lakes originated from moranic damming of [[Lake Creek]] and were hydraulically modified beginning in the early 1900s for irrigation storage. The upper lake has a maximum surface area of 263.4 ha and depth of 28 m; the lower lake covers 736 ha with 27 m maximum depth. Total system capacity before the new dam was 154 × 10⁶ m³.

The [[Lake Creek]] drainage basin covers approximately 238 km². Mean annual inflow (1971-81) was 140.13 × 10⁶ m³, with 90% occurring May-August and peak flows in June (mean 24.2 m³/s). Annual inflow ranged from 70.19 to 186.28 × 10⁶ m³ depending on winter snow conditions. The [[Twin Lakes Tunnel]] augments flows with water diverted from the Western Slope under the [[Continental Divide]].

The lakes are second-class dimictic, circulating twice yearly with bottom temperatures >4°C during summer stratification. Average ice-off occurs May 6; ice-on December 6. Maximum ice thickness ranged 610-762 mm. Maximum surface temperatures average ~17°C (lower lake) and ~15°C (upper lake) in late July to mid-August. The upper lake functions as a settling basin for sediment-laden inflow.

Water chemistry characterizes the lakes as relatively soft, dilute calcium bicarbonate systems. Mean conductivity was 71 µS/cm (lower) and 75 µS/cm (upper); TDS averaged 68 mg/L. Principal ions include bicarbonate (26 mg/L), sulfate (14 mg/L), calcium (11 mg/L), and magnesium (1.9 mg/L). pH ranges from neutral to slightly basic (6.6-8.3). Light extinction coefficients averaged 0.51 m⁻¹ (upper) and 0.40 m⁻¹ (lower) for 1972-79.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The phytoplankton community is dominated by diatoms ([[Synedra]], [[Asterionella]]) and chrysophytes ([[Dinobryon]]), species characteristic of phosphorus-limited oligotrophic waters. Average phytoplankton densities exceeded 13,000 individuals/L during late summer stratification but dropped below 100/L in the upper lake during late winter/early spring.

Zooplankton is dominated by the introduced [[Mysis relicta]] (opposum shrimp), introduced in 1958 as forage for lake trout. This establishment eliminated pelagic [[Daphnia]] species, though smaller cladocerans ([[Bosmina]]) occur occasionally. Other zooplankton include copepods ([[Cyclops]], [[Diaptomus]]) and rotifers ([[Keratella]], [[Polyarthra]], [[Kellicotia]]). Average summer abundance in the lower lake exceeded 120 organisms/L; the upper lake often had <1 organism/L during spring runoff.

The benthos comprises chironomids, oligochaetes, and sphaeriid clams at densities of 0 to >3000/m² for each group. The fishery includes naturally reproducing [[lake trout]] and stocked [[rainbow trout]] (both introduced early 20th century), plus [[brown trout]], [[brook trout]], [[longnose sucker]], [[white sucker]], and dace. Native [[yellow-finned cutthroat trout]] and [[Colorado cutthroat trout]] are extirpated.

Sediments consist of unconsolidated glacial rock flour with accumulated heavy metals (iron, manganese, zinc, copper, lead) from naturally exposed mineral deposits in the watershed. The upper lake contains additional allochthonous woody debris from erosion following flow augmentation. During severe winters with deep snow cover, reducing conditions (E_h <300 mV) can develop in the hypolimnion, releasing toxic metal concentrations that cause biological "crashes," especially in the upper lake with its higher oxygen demand from organic debris.

## Key Findings

**Primary Production Rates:**
- Upper lake: 11.3 g C/(m²·a) average annual production (range across years shown in raw data)
- Lower lake: 25.8 g C/(m²·a) average annual production
- Ice-free season means: 43.7 mg C/(m²·d) upper, 80.0 mg C/(m²·d) lower
- Ice-covered season means: 33.6 mg C/(m²·d) upper, 63.9 mg C/(m²·d) lower
- Maximum single rate measured: 21,696 µg C/(m³·h) in lower lake, May 16, 1979
- Maximum spring mean: 1418.8 µg C/(m³·h) lower lake, May 16, 1979
- Maximum fall mean: 1328.2 µg C/(m³·h) lower lake, October 5, 1978
- ~30% of total annual production occurs during winter months in both lakes
- In dry years, ~40% of upper lake production occurs in winter

**Trophic Classification:**
- Upper lake: ultra-oligotrophic (ice-free season average 43.7 mg C/(m²·d), annual 15.9 g C/(m²·a))
- Lower lake: oligotrophic (ice-free season average 80.0 mg C/(m²·d), annual 29.4 g C/(m²·a))
- Comparison values: ultra-oligotrophic <50, oligotrophic 50-300, mesotrophic 250-1000, eutrophic 600-8000 mg C/(m²·d)

**Runoff Volume Effects (Primary Factor):**
- Strong inverse relationship between lakes: r = -0.91 between annual production rates
- Upper lake: production varies inversely with runoff (r = -0.58)
  - High runoff years: increased flushing, turbidity, nutrient loss → reduced production
  - Low runoff years: nutrient retention, reduced turbidity, warmer water → increased production
  - Upper lake acts as settling basin, buffering lower lake from direct runoff effects
- Lower lake: production varies directly with runoff (r = +0.81)
  - High runoff years: nutrient input from upper lake → increased production (allochthonous cycle)
  - Low runoff years: nutrient-limited, dependent on internal cycling (autochthonous cycle)
- Pattern: either lake (but not both simultaneously) experiences increased production depending on runoff

**Light Limitation:**
- Two critical periods: (1) winter snow/ice cover severely limits light; (2) spring runoff turbidity
- When ice is clear with light snow, significant winter production occurs (especially upper lake)
- Heavy ice/snow cover into April can trigger biological "crash" via toxic metal release from sediments
- Maximum ambient light flux: ~44 g-cal/(cm²·h) in mid-July (5-day average, 1980)
- Light extinction increases during June runoff peak, especially in upper lake
- Upper lake clarity directly proportional to runoff volume (r = 0.88 with inflow during ice-free season)
- Phytoplankton blooms within thermocline (June-August) reduce transmissivity 4-25%

**Nutrient Dynamics:**
- Phosphorus is the limiting nutrient
- Total phosphorus: mean 1.54 µg/L (upper), 1.34 µg/L (lower); 55% of samples at detection limit (1 µg/L)
- Orthophosphate: only 2 of 240 samples (through 1979) had detectable amounts (≥1 µg/L)
- C:N:P ratio in water (1890:55:1) vs. aquatic plants (40:7:1) indicates severe phosphorus shortage
- Total organic nitrogen: 65.6 µg/L (upper), 57.9 µg/L (lower) - adequate, 87% above detection limit
- Silica (4.85-5.29 mg/L) and total organic carbon (1.73-2.05 mg/L) not limiting
- Nutrients trapped by upper lake in low runoff years; flushed to lower lake in high runoff years
- Dominant phytoplankton ([[Dinobryon]], [[Asterionella]], [[Synedra]]) are species adapted to low phosphorus (<20 µg/L)

**Algal Biomass:**
- Chlorophyll a correlation with carbon fixation: r = 0.56 (lower), r = 0.67 (upper) for 1977-80
- Phytoplankton density correlation with carbon fixation: 5-year r = 0.31 (lower), r = 0.60 (upper)
- Strong correlations in 1979-80 (r = 0.71-0.79) after sampling/counting standardization
- Poor 1981 correlations may indicate: (1) discrete depth sampling missed algal concentrations, or (2) significant contribution from nanoplankton (<80 µm) retained by filters but not counted
- Upper lake: maximum density after fall turnover in high runoff years; spring/summer peaks in low runoff years
- Lower lake: fall peaks common; late spring/midsummer peaks in high runoff years; late winter/spring peaks in low runoff years

**Seasonal Patterns:**
- Production generally lowest during winter ice cover
- Upper lake: June decline during runoff; steady through November
- Lower lake: peaks after spring/fall turnover; midsummer decline during strong stratification
- Maximum production rates at 1-3 m depth; near-zero below euphotic zone in summer
- Fall turnover (mid-October) redistributes nutrients, causing production increase
- December-January: low rates; February: slight rise in upper lake when snow not deep

**Water Temperature:**
- Not a critical limiting factor when considered alone
- May have subtle interactive effects with light, nutrients, season
- Temperature suboptimal conditions may increase nutrient requirements per cell
- Possible effect: chlorophyll a increases while carbon fixation remains constant as temperature decreases

**Methodological Notes:**
- C¹⁴ technique modified: discontinued glacial acetic acid (caused 25-30% underestimation pre-1978); no filter rinsing (≥200 mL filtered prevents unfixed tracer retention)
- Sampling standardized 1977: five depths (1, 3, 5, 9, 15 m); 4-h minimum incubation; 300-mL BOD bottles
- Quality control: relative efficiency determination, bicarbonate alkalinity titration
- Chemiluminescence from tissue solubilizer-fluor reaction requires 3-4 day equilibration before counting

**Management Implications:**
- Baseline data established for pre-operational conditions (before late 1981 pumped-storage operation)
- Results applicable to other Rocky Mountain montane lakes/reservoirs for fishery management
- Limnological data indicates wide variability in potential to support fisheries
- More efficient stocking plans can be developed using this characterization
- Study demonstrates importance of runoff volume in regulating productivity of connected lake systems
