# Anotopterus Pharao: A Widespread, Probably Antitropical Bathypelagic Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anotopteridae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 *Bulletin of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography* paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], [[Giles W. Mead]], and [[Norman J. Wilimovsky]] reports new North Pacific records of the highly modified bathypelagic fish [[Anotopterus Pharao]], previously known only from the northeastern Atlantic and sporadic Antarctic and Davis Strait material. The authors conclude that the species has a probably antitropical distribution, largely confined to temperate and subpolar seas. They also argue that the four nominal Anotopterus species described by [[Orvar Nybelin]] (1946) are a single species, and they reaffirm that the family belongs in the suborder [[Iniomi]], closest to the [[Alepisauridae]].

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** The species was first recorded by [[Robert Collett]] (1896) as *Plagyodus* sp., based on a partly digested specimen from an [[Albacore]] stomach. [[Eric Zugmayer]] (1911) gave the definitive description and name, using a young specimen netted off the Strait of Gibraltar. Nybelin (1946) later revised the genus and described *A. antarcticus* and *A. arcticus*, while [[Regan]] (1913) had earlier named *Eugnathosaurus vorax* from a skull dredged off Coats Land, Antarctica.

**Morphology (as described).** The authors note the following features:
- A dorsal fin is absent, and the adipose fin is present.
- Pelvic fins are abdominal, with 9 to 11 soft rays and no spine, and lie flat against the body.
- Premaxillary teeth increase from about 30 at 112.5 mm to nearly 100 at 632 mm standard length.
- Mandibular dentition includes a fixed canine and depressible canines that become fixed with age.
- Pectoral fins are low, horizontal, and closely placed, with 14 rays per side in five of six examined specimens.
- Branchiostegal rays number 5 + 2 on each side.
- Pacific adults are dusky to nearly black dorsally, fading to yellowish white ventrally.
- Pacific adults have sharp, high basicaudal dermal keels.
- Fine epidermal pores cover the body, a feature shared only with the Alepisauridae.

**Specimens examined.** The authors list the following material:
- A 112.5 mm young fish from [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]] Station NH 25 (Northern Holiday expedition, 15 September 1951), taken with an Isaacs-Kidd midwater trawl.
- An 861 mm adult from Kamchatka, taken in a salmon drift-net (Tokyo University collection, Cat. No. 38999).
- A 640 mm adult from off Hokkaido (23 September 1951), taken in a dip-net under a light with a [[Pacific Saury]] in its stomach.
- A head from a British Columbia albacore stomach (USNM 112544).
- A 299 mm specimen regurgitated by an albacore off central California (Stanford University Natural History Museum No. 17760).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and temperature.** The genus has been recorded from subpolar and temperate parts of the Atlantic and Pacific and has not been reported from the tropics. It was absent from the bathypelagic fish list collected off Bermuda by [[William Beebe]] (1937) and from mid-water trawling by the Scripps Institution between California and Peru. The authors propose that it has an antitropical distribution, a term they prefer to "bipolar" (Hubbs 1950, 1952). When plotted against surface isotherms, the records suggest the range is bounded by monthly mean surface temperatures of:
- 18 °C in February (Northern Hemisphere) and August (Southern Hemisphere)
- 24 °C in August (north) and February (south)

**Size and latitude.** Known juveniles and small adults tend to occur in warmer waters, while the largest adults come from colder waters near the poles. Of seven known adults (632 to 861 mm), all come from the Antarctic, Davis Strait, or cold waters off Hokkaido and Kamchatka. Adults have been taken in surface nets only in the cold northwestern Pacific. The authors state that more data are needed before this size-latitude correlation can be considered confirmed.

**Feeding and associations.** Specimens have been found in the stomachs of [[Albacore]] (*Germo alalunga*), [[Lancetfish]] (*Alepisaurus ferox*), a halibut, and a sperm whale-type predator (the stomach of a whale in the Weddell Sea). The Hokkaido adult had eaten a saury (*Cololabis saira*). Regurgitation and stomach records dominate the data, which likely biases the sample toward predators' prey.

**Capture methods and bias.** The authors note that the small size of most reported specimens reflects collection circumstances as well as size-latitude patterns. Two specimens were taken in a net type that larger fish commonly escape, one on a long-line, several from predator stomachs, and others in surface drift-nets.

## Key Findings

1. **Antitropical distribution.** The species is probably antitropical, confined largely to temperate and subpolar seas, and its range matches surface temperature limits of 18 °C (winter) and 24 °C (summer).
2. **Single species.** The authors find "no convincing grounds" for recognizing more than one species within *Anotopterus*. They place *Plagyodus* sp. Collett (1896), *Eugnathosaurus vorax* Regan (1913), *A. antarcticus* Nybelin (1946), and *A. arcticus* Nybelin (1946) in the synonymy of *A. pharao*.
3. **Size differences are artifacts.** Size differences used to separate Nybelin's species are attributed to latitude, collection method, and age, not to species differences.
4. **Dentition is variable.** Tooth counts and forms in the jaws and palate vary with age, individual, and preservation, and show no clear specific distinctions. The pelvic ray count of "4" given by earlier authors is considered unlikely.
5. **Pacific range extension.** The new records extend the known range to five widely separated North Pacific localities, including British Columbia, Kamchatka, Hokkaido, central California, and the Scripps station.
6. **Family placement.** The family [[Anotopteridae]] belongs in the Iniomi, probably in the suborder Alepisauroidei, and is closest to the Alepisauridae (and related to the Paralepidae). Earlier alignments with the Gempylidae, Trichiuridae, or Lepidopodidae are rejected as superficial convergence, as are placements in the Isospondyli and the Clupeiformes.
7. **Rejection of "abnormal larvae" hypothesis.** The authors reject [[Maurice Roule]]'s view that Anotopterus is a teratological or abnormal larval form of scombroid fishes.
8. **Morphological notes.** Features described include the dermal keels, the dorsal mid-line rod, the lateral-line and cephalic pore series, and the coloration of fins and body.

The authors conclude that the fish's most intimate relationship is with *Alepisaurus*, and that its size-latitude pattern merits further study.
