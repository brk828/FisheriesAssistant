# Recommendations for Establishment and Maintenance of a Captive Broodstock for Bonytail Chub

## Executive Summary
This report by Philip W. Hedrick (Arizona State University), submitted to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, evaluates the genetic status of the captive broodstock of the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), a critically endangered cyprinid whose only known wild population persists as aging, non-reproducing adults in [[Lake Mohave]] (formed by [[Davis Dam]], closed 1954). Because natural recruitment has effectively ceased due to habitat alteration and non-native fish introductions, an F₁ cohort (~350 surviving individuals) was produced in 1981 at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] from wild-caught broodstock and is now held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (DNFHTC). Using allozyme and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) data combined with hatchery fecundity records, Hedrick reconstructs the effective number of founders contributing to this F₁ generation and provides quantitative genetic-management guidelines for establishing and maintaining a genetically representative "future captive broodstock," including incorporation of any additional wild-caught fish.

## Background & Description
*Note: This report is a population-genetics/conservation-management study and does not describe external morphology, coloration, or meristic traits of the Bonytail Chub. Genetic (allozyme and mtDNA) characterization is summarized below in lieu of morphological data.*

- **Allozyme loci examined**: sAat-A, Gpi-B, Pgm-A (common to both F₁ and F₂ samples), plus Cat-1, Est-2, Iddh-A (F₂ only).
- **F₁ sample** (n = 30, DeMarais unpublished, from DNFHTC): 3 polymorphic loci examined; six distinct three-locus genotypes detected.
- **F₂ sample** (n = 24, 1985 year class; Minckley et al. 1989): 6 polymorphic loci examined.
- **Pgm-A locus**: significant heterozygote excess in F₁ sample (P < 0.001), deviating from Hardy-Weinberg expectations; a third allele ("c") present in F₂ but absent in F₁.
- **mtDNA haplotypes**: Two haplotypes identified (a and b) among wild/mixed-history fish; all 30 sampled F₁ individuals were monomorphic for haplotype **a**, while 16 additional carcasses (including wild-caught fish) showed 10 with haplotype a and 6 with haplotype b (frequency of a ≈ 0.625 in this broader sample).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Historically a mainstem Colorado River fish adapted to large, turbid, free-flowing desert river conditions; now confined to reservoir habitat in [[Lake Mohave]] following river regulation.
- **Population status**: Wild population consists solely of an unknown number of large, old adults with little to no recruitment since Davis Dam closure (1954).
- **Threats**: Habitat destruction/alteration from water development projects and introduction of non-native sport fishes in Lake Mohave, which likely predate on or outcompete juvenile bonytail, preventing recruitment.
- **Reproductive ecology (captive)**: In 1981, 6 wild-caught adult females (one non-reproductive) were pair-mated with 5 wild-caught adult males at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery; fecundity ranged from 5,850–37,700 eggs/female (mean 25,090), with fertilization rates of 0.91–0.98 (mean 0.95).
- **Captive rearing concern**: Potential for unintentional selection for "captive-adapted" traits across hatchery generations (citing [[Frankham Et Al 1986]]; [[Ryman And Utter 1987]]), which could reduce fitness of hatchery-origin fish if reintroduced to the wild.
- **Uncertain wild/hatchery origin**: Difficulty distinguishing genuinely wild-caught fish from unmarked F₁ or F₂ fish released into Lake Mohave; Marsh (1997) otolith aging of 17 fish estimated 10–67% of recently collected "bonytail" were truly wild.

## Key Findings

### Genetic Reconstruction of Original Broodstock
- Reproduction data (Table 1) yield a **"high" estimate** of parental contribution (assuming equal genetic retention from all 5 successful pair matings), giving an **effective number of founders (Nₑf) = 8.48** — 84.8% of the maximum possible (10, from 5 pairs).
- Allozyme genotype analysis (Table 3) suggests a **"low" estimate**: most (22/30) F₁ individuals derived from a single dominant mating, with only 2–3 total matings contributing significantly, yielding **Nₑf = 3.48**.
- mtDNA monomorphism (all 30 F₁ fish = haplotype a) is statistically more consistent with the "low" estimate (fewer, more unequal matings) than the "high" estimate, based on 1,000-sample computer simulations (Table 4).
- **Conclusion**: The true effective number of founders for the original 1981 broodstock likely lies between **3.48 and 8.48**, far below the actual 10 wild parents used.

### Effective Founders in the Future Broodstock
- Using computer simulation (Table 5), effective number of founders **asymptotes quickly** with increasing numbers of F₁ parents used to found the next generation.
- With **30 pairs (60 individuals)** of F₁ parents: retains 89.3% ("high") to 97.1% ("low") of maximum possible genetic variation.
- Increasing to 150 pairs only marginally improves retention (97.5%–99.4%), indicating diminishing returns beyond ~30–60 pairs.

### Value of Additional Wild-Caught Fish
- Each additional wild fish incorporated substantially increases effective founder number proportionally more than additional F₁ fish would (e.g., +1 wild fish: 8.48→9.58 under "high" estimate; 3.48→4.48 under "low" estimate).
- Recommended representation guideline: each wild-caught founder's genetic contribution should fall between **1/(8.48 + Nw)** and **1/(3.48 + Nw)**.
- W × W matings (two wild fish) should generally be avoided (to prevent "wasting" two wild gamete sources in one cross) unless more than five wild fish are available.
- Table 6 provides concrete mating-scheme examples: incorporating a single wild fish into a program of 30 F₁×F₁ matings requires roughly 9 F₁×Wild matings ("high" estimate) or up to 35 such matings ("low" estimate) to achieve equivalent genetic contribution.

### General Recommendations for Broodstock Management
1. Use **30–60 pairs** of F₁ parents (spread across multiple years) to found the future broodstock — captures most retrievable genetic variation from DNFHTC stock.
2. Prioritize incorporation of **as many additional wild fish as possible**; if ≥10 wild fish are captured, base the broodstock primarily on them and cap F₁-derived contribution at ≤25%.
3. Maintain **complete pedigree records**; avoid full-sib and half-sib matings to minimize inbreeding; use **long generation intervals (≥10 years)** to slow loss of genetic diversity per unit time.
4. Maintain the broodstock at **≥2 separate facilities** for redundancy against disease, disaster, or accident.
5. Develop **highly variable genetic markers** (microsatellites, MHC loci) to verify wild origin of captured fish and identify close relatives.
6. Conduct **experimental sibling crosses** to quantify inbreeding depression in growth rate, fecundity, and survival, informing future fitness predictions.

### Broader Implications
- Random, phenotype-independent selection of broodstock parents is essential to avoid inadvertent "selection for captivity."
- Distinguishing true wild fish from hatchery-origin fish released into Lake Mohave remains a persistent challenge, complicating both wild population assessment and broodstock management decisions.
- This work exemplifies the application of **effective population size theory** ([[Hedrick 1985]]) and **founder representation analysis** ([[Ballou And Lacy 1995]]) to endangered species captive breeding programs.
