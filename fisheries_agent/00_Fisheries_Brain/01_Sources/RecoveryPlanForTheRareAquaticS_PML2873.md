# Recovery Plan for the Rare Aquatic Species of the Muddy River Ecosystem

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This First Revision of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) recovery plan, prepared by Selena I. Wilson and originally approved February 14, 1983, addresses one federally listed fish, the [[Moapa Dace]] (*Moapa coriacea*, endangered), and seven species of special concern in the Muddy River ecosystem of Clark County, Nevada. The plan (published 1995) sets reclassification and delisting criteria, a five-part action list, a cost schedule, and a summary of public comments.

The Moapa dace currently occupies about 9.5 km (5.9 mi) of habitat across five thermal spring systems and the upper Muddy River. Its main threats are habitat alteration, water loss, impoundments, and introduced nonnative fishes and parasites, especially [[Shortfin Molly]] and [[Mosquitofish]]. The plan's strategy centers on protecting habitat through landowner agreements, removing nonnative fishes, restoring habitat, monitoring populations, researching parasites, and conducting public outreach.

## Background & Description

The Muddy (Moapa) River rises from thermal springs at 30–32 °C (86–89.6 °F) in the Warm Springs area. Historically it flowed 48.4 km (30 mi) to the [[Virgin River]], but since the [[Hoover Dam]] was completed in 1935, its lower 8 km (5 mi) have been flooded by [[Lake Mead]]. Remaining riverine habitat has an average gradient of 1.5 m/km. Total spring discharge is about 1.3 m³/s (45.9 cfs). Nearly 75 percent of the actual inflow is lost to diversions, evaporation, and transpiration before reaching Lake Mead.

The Moapa dace was first collected in 1938 and described by Hubbs and Miller (1948). It is olive-yellow above with a white belly, a black spot at the tail base, and small embedded scales. Maximum length is about 120 mm FL, and the oldest recorded specimen was over 4 years old. It is most closely related to the speckled dace (*Rhinichthys*) and longfin dace (*Agosia*) within the [[Cyprinidae]] of the Colorado River Basin. The species was federally listed as endangered in 1967 and received recovery priority 1.

The plan covers seven other species of special concern: the [[Virgin River Chub]], [[Moapa Speckled Dace]], [[Moapa White River Springfish]], [[Moapa Pebblesnail]], [[Grated Tryonia]], [[Moapa Warm Spring Riffle Beetle]], and [[Amargosa Naucorid]]. Two further endemic species, a naucorid (*Usingerina moapensis*) and a water strider (*Rhagovelia becki*), are noted with unknown status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and life history.** Larval dace occur in slack water in upper tributaries. Juveniles use tributaries with increasing flow velocity. Adults use both tributaries and the main stem, with larger adults favoring faster water of 0.8–0.9 m/s. Aquatic plants are abundant in spring pools but nearly absent from swift adult habitat. Nonnative palms (*Washingtonia* and *Phoenix*) add structure but alter stream hydraulics.

**Water quality.** Temperature ranges 11–32 °C (67–89.6 °F). Dissolved oxygen is 3.4–8.4 mg/L, total dissolved solids 606–867 mg/L, and pH 7.1–7.9. Turbidity rises with distance from springs and reached 39.7 NTU during heavy agricultural runoff.

**Reproduction.** Moapa dace spawn year-round, with peak activity in spring. Sexual maturity is reached at one year, at about 38–45 mm FL. Fecundity ranges from 60 eggs (45 mm FL) to 772 eggs (90 mm FL). Successful reproduction has only been documented at 30–32 °C, so mature adults must migrate from the Muddy River into thermal tributaries to spawn. Mortality is estimated at 68 percent in the first year and 65 percent in the second.

**Diet.** Moapa dace are omnivorous. Stomach contents include insects, worms, scuds, algae, and sand. Adults feed mainly on drift and also forage from the substrate, and feed around the clock with peaks at dawn and dusk.

**Threats and land use.** Spring systems were enlarged, lined with concrete, chlorinated, and cleared of vegetation for recreation. Water rights have been diverted for municipal use. The [[Reid-Gardner Station]] diversion dam (built 1988–89) and the USGS Cipoletti weir both block upstream movement and reduce habitat. A June 1994 fire nearly eliminated dace from the Refuge spring system.

## Key Findings

- **Population trend.** An estimated 500–1,000 individuals were reported in 1969. By 1977, only a few hundred remained. The 1983–87 surveys by the [[Northwest Fisheries Research Center]] estimated 2,600–2,800 adults. A 1994 resurvey counted 3,041 adults, about 500 fewer than would have been expected without the fire.
- **Reclassification criteria (to threatened).** Existing instream flows and historical habitat must be protected in three of five spring systems and the upper Muddy River. At least 2,500 adults must be present across the spring systems and upper river. The population must include three or more age classes, with reproduction documented in three springs.
- **Delisting criteria.** At least 6,000 adults must persist for 5 consecutive years. Seventy-five percent of historical habitat must support spawning, nursery, cover, or foraging. Nonnative fishes and parasites must no longer adversely affect long-term survival.
- **Timeline.** Reclassification to threatened is estimated for 2000 and delisting for 2009.
- **Priority actions.** (1) Protect instream flows and habitat through landowner conservation agreements. (2) Conduct restoration and management, including nonnative fish removal and palm removal. (3) Monitor population annually by snorkeling in winter. (4) Research parasites and population health. (5) Provide public education.
- **Nonnative fishes.** Shortfin mollies and mosquitofish are established in nearly all occupied habitat. Their continued presence may preclude delisting. Blue tilapia pose a rising threat, and their introduction appears imminent.
- **Cost.** The total estimated cost for recovery from 1996 to 2001 is $1.6 million plus TBD, of which the 1996 funding need is $51,000 plus TBD.
- **Other species.** The [[Virgin River Chub]] population in the main-stem Muddy River between the Refuge stream and Interstate 15 was estimated at 20,593 fish. Moapa speckled dace totaled 706 captured in 1994. The Moapa White River springfish population was estimated at nearly 25,000 in 1984. The status of the snails, riffle beetle, and naucorid is largely unknown. Listing of these species is considered unnecessary if the plan is implemented.
- **Public response.** The draft plan drew 215 response letters. The USFWS rejected claims of ESA, NEPA, and Executive Order violations, stating that recovery plans are planning documents only.
