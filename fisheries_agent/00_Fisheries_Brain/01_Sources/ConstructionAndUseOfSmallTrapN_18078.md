# Construction and Use of Small Trap Nets

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-gear

## Executive Summary

This 1950 methods paper by [[Walter R Crowe]] of the Michigan Department of Conservation describes the construction, setting, and use of a small [[Trap Net]] adapted for population studies in [[Inland Lake]]s. It was developed from [[Submarine Trap Net]] designs used in the [[Great Lakes]] region and was modified to reduce the bulk and weight that made earlier commercial gear hard to transport. The author reports the final unit weighs about 100 pounds and costs roughly $200 to build. The net is described as less selective than gill nets, effective for warm-water species including black bass, and durable for 8 to 10 years if maintained. The paper is a practical field manual rather than a research study, so it reports no sample sizes or catch statistics.

## Background & Description

Fishery workers on inland lakes need adequate collecting gear, and no single net is efficient for all fish sizes and habitats. The [[Michigan Institute for Fisheries Research]] had earlier used heavy commercial nets built for the Great Lakes. These nets caught large numbers of fish but were difficult to handle and transport, and they needed a heavy boat and a large truck. A second commercial fisherman used lighter nets with a single pot and a back bridle attached to all four corners, which removed the need for a spreader stick. The author then set out to shrink the gear further so that lighter floats, leads, webbing, rope, and anchors could be used.

The first small trap net, built in 1947, had a 2-foot-deep, 2-foot-wide, 6-foot-long pot and a 200-foot leader 2 feet deep. It performed reasonably well but was less productive than the larger traps. The revised design uses a 100-foot leader that tapers from 4 feet to a 2-foot mouth, with a small netting umbrella across the wing opening. Five further nets were built with 3- and 4-foot depths and 100-foot leaders.

The paper gives detailed construction specifications, including:
- **Twine:** 12-thread seine twine in the pot (the part most worn by the gunwale) and 9-thread elsewhere.
- **Lines:** 6-thread Manila float line, 9-thread Manila lead line, and an 84-thread maitre cord coil for spare line.
- **Floats and weights:** aluminum gill-net floats (4.5 × 1.5 in) and 5-ounce split gill-net leads.
- **Anchors and bridles:** a head anchor line of at least 100 feet, side anchor lines of 50 to 75 feet, and bridle ends fastened around thimbles that act as pulleys.
- **Preservative:** tar treatment, which the author found satisfactory and which commercial fishermen prefer for its black color.

The paper also recommends a stable work boat, a 15-foot-6-inch hull with a 24-inch freeboard and a 56-inch beam, with hardwood gunwales, square bow and stern for outboard mounting, and a single forward seat. Essential equipment includes spare line, a sounding lead, extra buoys, a bobbin of 12-thread twine for prompt repairs, a sharp knife, and a long-handled dip net.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats fish capture in terms of selectivity and handling. Trap nets are described as generally less selective than gill nets at a given depth or habitat, and they capture most warm-water species readily. Unwanted fish can be released unharmed and removed more quickly than from gill nets. Trap nets also catch [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] in midsummer, a function the author says gill nets cannot reliably perform. Their seine-twine construction makes them hard to tear, snag, or entangle.

Cold-water species are a weak point. Trap nets were not very reliable for [[Trout]], [[Whitefish]], and [[Herring]] except under special circumstances, although they occasionally caught trout well.

Physical limits shape where the gear can be used. The nets generally cannot work efficiently below 35 feet of water. They need a firm bottom with a gradual slope so the net stays taut, because anchors do not hold in soft mud. The author suggests a 6-inch board tied between anchor points or longer anchor lines as workarounds. Once mastered, the nets can be set in rough water and even under ice.

## Key Findings

- The small trap net is a practical supplement to other sampling gear for inland lake population studies, combining the catch efficiency of large commercial nets with much lower weight and bulk.
- Total weight of each unit, including anchors and anchor lines, is about 100 pounds. The load can be split into two or three packages for handling.
- Construction cost is roughly $200 for the illustrated net, with labor about 55% and materials about 45% of cost. Larger nets cost little more in labor, so they are more economical per unit of catch capacity.
- Operating depth is limited to about 35 feet. Taut setting on a firm, gradually sloping bottom is necessary for good fishing.
- Trap nets reliably capture warm-water fish and black bass in summer, but they are not dependable for cold-water species.
- Nets are durable for 8 to 10 years if maintained. Recommended care includes dipping in preservative at least every other year, prompt patching, and periodic washing and drying.
- The author recommends setting nets so they are taut, since a tight net "fishes better than a loose one."
- The 13-step setting and lifting procedure requires a crew of two (an oarsman and a stern operator) and is simple once learned.
