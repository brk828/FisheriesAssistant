# Distribution, Variation, and Ecology of Lepidomeda vittata (Little Colorado Spinedace)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This *Copeia* paper (No. 1, March 1963) by [[Robert Rush Miller]] documents the distribution, meristic and morphometric variation, sexual dimorphism, larval pigmentation, and ecology of the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), a cyprinid endemic to eastern Arizona. The species had been exterminated across its known historical range between 1939 and 1960. A breeding population was rediscovered in [[East Clear Creek]] in July 1961, making the [[Clear Creek]] drainage the probable sole remaining refuge. The paper concludes that the species' survival there may depend largely on management by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

## Background & Description

The Little Colorado spinedace belongs to the tribe [[Plagopterini]], a group of spiny-rayed cyprinids that is gradually disappearing as its habitat is altered by humans. Miller describes it as a compact, distinctive member of the group.

**Historical range:** The species is known only from the Little Colorado River basin. It may also have occurred in suitable waters of western New Mexico, south of Gallup, that drain into the [[Zuni River]], though it no longer lives there. Only four early records exist (1873 to 1939), but the species was probably abundant in the upper Little Colorado River and several cool tributaries, including Silver Creek, Showlow Creek, Chevelon Creek, and Clear Creek.

**Morphology and meristics (Clear Creek material):**
- Anal rays: average 7.98
- Pectoral rays: average 16.88
- Pelvic rays: average 7.03
- Caudal rays: average 19.00
- Lateral-line scales: mostly above 90
- Gill rakers: average 7.93
- Vertebrae: average 41.73
- Mandibular pores: average 5.60
- Preopercular pores: average 7.60
- Total preopercular-mandibular pores: average 13.20 (a somewhat higher count than Illick's 1956 listing)
- Main-row teeth: 4-4, with teeth often missing in the specimens examined

**Morphometrics (32 specimens, 17 males and 15 females, 53 to 69.5 mm standard length):**
- Depressed dorsal fin length goes into predorsal length 1.9 to 2.35 times, a broader range than Miller and Hubbs (1960) reported.
- Head width is 1.45 to 1.6 times in head length.

**Sexual dimorphism:** Dimorphism is weak. The pectoral fin is noticeably longer in males. In males, pectoral length goes into head length 1.0 to 1.1 times (56.5 to 69.0 mm SL); in females, 1.2 to 1.3 times (53.2 to 69.5 mm). In males the pectoral fin tip consistently extends past the pelvic insertion, while in females it usually falls short. Ripe males have intense reddish-orange pectoral and pelvic axils, whereas females are watery reddish-orange or yellowish. Sexing by fin length was confirmed by gonad examination.

**Size:** The largest specimen was a 107 mm standard-length female. A 95 mm female and a 75 mm male (the largest male) were skeletonized.

**Coloration:** The sides below the dark vertical lines shine like polished silver, the venter is white, and the back is olivaceous to bluish or lead grey. Fine black puncticulations give a pepper-like effect. A conspicuous cream-colored spot appears at the dorsal fin origin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Decline:** Between 1939 and 1960 the species was lost from most or all of its known range. Extensive collecting by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and Miller's family from 28 August to 5 September 1960 failed to find it in the Little Colorado River or major tributaries where it had been common in 1938 to 1939. Contributing factors identified include:
- Pollution
- Repeated use of fish poisons ([[Toxaphene]] and [[Rotenone]]) to remove carp and other non-game fish
- Introductions of exotic species, including salmonids
- Altered stream flow

**Barrier effects:** The species likely did not maintain permanent populations in the Little Colorado River below [[Grand Falls]], about 35 miles northwest of Flagstaff. The river there was probably unsuitable, and the falls (roughly 150 feet of vertical drop) marked the upstream limit of the predatory [[Colorado Squawfish]].

**Rediscovery:** A single adult was seined from a stagnating pool in the dry channel of Clear Creek on 25 August 1960, the first record from that stream and county and the first capture since 1939. On 15 to 16 July 1961, the Miller and Hubbs families and Teruya Uyeno found a sizable breeding population in East Clear Creek, which was studied again on 13 August 1962.

**Sampling sites:** Stations ranged from marginal permanent but stagnant pools on Clear Creek (about 7 miles west of Chevelon Butte) to permanently flowing sections of East Clear Creek near the "96" Crossing and 5.5 miles below Jones Crossing, at elevations of 6,000 to 6,600 feet. Water temperatures in July and August ranged from 58°F to 78°F, while air temperatures ranged from 62.5°F in early morning to 83°F in late afternoon. Substrates included sand, gravel, rocks, boulders, silt, and bedrock. Water depths were 4 to 6+ feet, with capture depth up to 4 feet.

**Habitat quality:** The marginal lower stations were greenish-brown with algal blooms. The breeding site was clear with locally dense green algae, and its channel averaged 50 feet wide. East Clear Creek runs through a canyon with 300 to 400 foot walls, shaded by alders, willows, and yellow pine. [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] were present at all three upper stations. Clear Creek was one of the few gamefish streams in the drainage that had not been chemically treated.

**Preferred microhabitat:** The species occupies clear, flowing, relatively open pools 1 to 3 feet deep that are cold enough for trout but avoided by them. It favors pools with large boulders for refuge, and in shallow water it likely uses undercut or overhung banks. It was absent from large, well-shaded, sand-bottomed pools dominated almost exclusively by trout.

**Behavior:** Apart from trout, the spinedace was the most active fish observed. It swims 4 to 6 inches below the surface, explores the whole pool, and strikes at floating debris much like a trout. It does not normally school, but it gathers loosely when alarmed. Young suckers (*Catostomus* and/or *Pantosteus*) avoided adults as they swam past. On 16 July 1961, Clark Hubbs observed apparent courtship during a two-hour forenoon watch and a half-hour midafternoon watch, with males nibbling females near the vent. No spawning was seen. Spawning likely occurred earlier, perhaps in May, and again in July, as shown by young fish and courtship.

**Associated species:**
- Fishes: *Gila robusta* (scarce), *Rhinichthys osculus* (abundant), *Catostomus latipinnis* and *Pantosteus delphinus* (common); exotic *Salmo gairdneri* and *Salmo trutta*
- Herps: *Hyla arenicola*, *Rana pipiens*, *Bufo punctatus*, and *Thamnamophis elegans vagrans* (common)

**Conservation outlook:** The paper states that the species is now probably restricted to the Clear Creek drainage, and that its persistence there may depend heavily on Arizona Game and Fish Department activities. Its future depends on protecting its refugia from chemical treatment and other disturbances.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** *L. vittata* was extirpated from most or all of its known range by 1960. It was rediscovered in Clear Creek (1960) and East Clear Creek (1961), and these drainages now appear to hold its last known populations.
- **Early records:** Only four records exist from 1873 to 1939, though the species was once abundant in the upper Little Colorado River and several tributaries.
- **Causes of decline:** Pollution, fish poisoning with toxaphene and rotenone, exotic species introductions, and changed stream flow.
- **Taxonomic confirmation:** Clear Creek material supports Miller and Hubbs' (1960) diagnosis (4-4 main-row teeth, 8 anal rays, usually more than 90 lateral-line scales, and typically 41 or 42 vertebrae). New counts for head pores and gill rakers are reported for the first time.
- **Variation:** Measurements (32 specimens, 53 to 69.5 mm SL) closely match earlier data, though the range of depressed dorsal fin length is wider.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Weak overall; males are identified by longer pectoral fins that extend past the pelvic insertion and by reddish-orange fin axils during breeding.
- **Relationships:** Larval and pigment characters, especially the shared pigment spots and dorsal melanophore pattern, support Miller and Hubbs' view that *Lepidomeda* is closely related to [[Gila]] and that the Plagopterini may have evolved from a Gila-like ancestor. More larval material is needed to confirm this.
- **Ecology:** The species is trout-like in habitat use, preferring cool, clear, boulder-strewn pools. Spawning appears to occur in spring and summer.
- **Management implication:** The Clear Creek drainage may be the species' last refuge, and its survival likely depends on Arizona Game and Fish Department protection, including avoidance of chemical treatment of streams.
