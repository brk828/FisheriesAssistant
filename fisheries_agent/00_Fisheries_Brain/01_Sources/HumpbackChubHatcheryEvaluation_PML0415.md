# Evaluation of Captive Refuge Facilities for Humpback Chub (Gila cypha)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) report, prepared by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] Research Branch in May 2005, evaluates hatcheries, aquaria and established facilities across the United States as potential captive refuge sites for [[Humpback Chub]] (*[[Gila Cypha]]*) from the [[Little Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. It is a site-suitability assessment, not an endorsement of broodstock production or reintroduction. The AGFD states it does not endorse any captive or controlled propagation of Grand Canyon humpback chub at this time.

Fourteen candidate facilities were scored on a checklist and questionnaire, and about nine further facilities were rejected at the outset. The best-ranked sites were [[Mora National Fish Hatchery]] and the [[Hualapai Tribe Peach Springs Facility]].

## Background & Description

The [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Van Haverbeke and Simmonds 2004) evaluated three options for protecting or enhancing the wild population: (1) a captive broodstock program, (2) captive grow-out of wild-caught age-0 fish for release, and (3) augmentation through translocation. Translocation within the Little Colorado River above a natural barrier is the only option acted upon so far. All options carry some genetic risk, including drift, inbreeding and artificial selection in hatcheries, which can reduce effective population size.

Because a captive refuge population is the option least likely to harm the wild population, the AGFD proposed to capture 100 age-0 fish from the Little Colorado River in summer 2005. The fish would go to a facility that has shown it can manage refuge populations and agrees not to release incidental F1 progeny into the wild. Catastrophic loss of the wild population would reopen discussion of broodstock use, and a detailed broodstock plan and compliance with the [[Usfws Policy On Controlled Propagation]] would then be required.

Each facility was scored against a checklist of criteria covering ownership, topography, existing infrastructure, water source, pathogen control, containment, staffing and congeners (see [[Refuge Facility Scoring]]). Minimum eligibility criteria included:

- Land and water rights secure for 50+ years
- Capacity for at least 100 adult humpback chub in multiple ponds, tanks or raceways
- No location in a floodplain unless within native range
- Security from public, predators and escape
- Quarantine capability and pathogen-free or treated water
- No congeners held on site
- Staff on site or on duty 24 hours a day
- A legally binding agreement with the AGFD and USFWS

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water quality limits used as the benchmark (from Fiper et al. 1982) were:

| Parameter | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 45–80 °F (7–27 °C) |
| Dissolved oxygen | ≥ 5 ppm |
| Dissolved nitrogen | ≤ 100% saturation |
| pH | 6.5–9.0 |

The main ecological risks were:

- **[[Hybridization]]:** Escaped fish could hybridize with congeners such as [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], Gila topminnow, desert pupfish, spikedace, loach minnow and Little Colorado River spinedace.
- **[[Pathogen Transfer]]:** Source water from rivers, canals or reuse loops can carry pathogens, and many sites lacked any control.
- **Flood and escape:** Sites in floodplains, such as [[Wahweap Hatchery]] (within the 25-year floodplain of Wahweap Creek) and the [[Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]], risk escape.
- **Predation and climate:** Outdoor ponds are exposed to birds, mammals and temperature extremes.

## Key Findings

**Ranked facilities (as discussed in the report, best first):**

1. **[[Mora National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), score 133.** Indoor recirculating system, currently disease-free, with circular tanks (10 ft diameter × 5 ft deep), 10 × 50 ft fiberglass raceways, a quarantine facility, 24-hour sensor monitoring, an alarm system and on-site staff. It houses only Gila trout and rainbow trout, with no chub congeners. The main constraint is funding for labor, food, utilities and chemicals. The cost of housing at least 100 adults must be negotiated with USFWS Region 2.
2. **[[Hualapai Tribe Peach Springs Facility]] (Arizona), score 128.** Flow-through or reuse operation with well water, pathogen-free source, and ponds lined with 20-mil polyethylene. It is outdoors and vulnerable to predators, though bird netting is planned. It is still under construction, has no quarantine or climate-controlled storage, and no staff live on site. The operators expressed interest in cost sharing.
3. **[[Bubbling Ponds Refuge Facility]] (Arizona), score not clearly legible in scan.** A new AGFD facility funded through the Central Arizona Project fund transfer. It uses a new 80 gpm artesian well, mainly flow-through in 24 500-gallon tanks, and holds threatened and endangered Gila River basin species. Concerns include escape into Oak Creek and the Verde River, where roundtail chub occur, and no alarm system.
4. **[[University Of Arizona]] (Tucson), score 108.** Small reuse system with well water. Pathogen treatment (UV or chemical) would need extra funding. Water quality is questionable, and congeners are present or expected.
5. **[[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona), score in the 100s, exact value not legible in scan.** Pumps directly from the Colorado River, so the source water is a pathogen risk. Bonytail chub are on site. Humpback chub are already held indoors here, and if the group is genetically suitable it could serve as an existing refuge. The report recommends adding about 5% wild adults annually, which would replace all adults every 20 years, less than the species' lifespan.
6. **[[Wahweap Hatchery]] (Utah), score in the 100s, exact value not legible in scan.** Within a 25-year floodplain, above Glen Canyon Dam, with escape possible even without a 25-year flood. Its 35 ponds (24 lined, 0.4 acre surface area, ~5 ft deep) are under review.
7. **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), score in the 100s, exact value not legible in scan.** Holds 17 threatened or endangered species, including congeners (bonytail, Chihuahua chub, roundtail chub, Virgin River chub). It has 72 outdoor ponds, but no space is available. It also holds experimental humpback chub × bonytail hybrids, so the report judges it probably unwise.
8. **[[Oway National Wildlife Refuge]] (Utah), score 91 (per scan).** Shallow, unreliable wells, interconnected drains, unscreened effluent, and no staff for emergencies. Probably not a good choice, especially for Grand Canyon fish.
9. **[[Silver Creek Hatchery]] (Arizona), score 88 (per scan).** Used mainly for trout, with one pond for Little Colorado River spinedace. The main barrier is lack of space, and the water source is not pathogen-free. No humpback chub records exist from the upper Little Colorado River.
10. **[[Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona), score 79.** Source water is from the White River, which is in the Salt River drainage. Hybridization with other Gila species is likely and escape is probably unavoidable. The White Mountain Apache Tribe owns the land and water rights and is unlikely to sign the required agreement. Rejected.
11. **[[Sipes Wildlife Area]] (Arizona), score 66.** Ponds fed by runoff are unpredictable and occasionally dry. They are not built for fish culture, and escape during flooding is possible. Rejected.
12. **[[Achii Hanyo]] (Arizona), score 61.** Remote, poorly drained, unsecured, with no pathogen control, no maintenance plan, one operator with under half a year of experience, extreme temperature swings, and dissolved oxygen as low as 3.0 ppm. Known pathogens include Columnaris, Ich, Costia and Lernaea. Bonytail are already on site. Rejected.

**Other rejected candidates:** Glen Canyon Dam (no space, poor access, cold water), Monterey Bay Aquarium (saltwater only), Shedd Aquarium (renovating), Tennessee Aquarium, National Aquarium (no room), Aquarium of Niagara Falls (unresponsive), a New Mexico silvery minnow facility (capacity 35–40 adults) and a New Mexico hatchery (ponds and raceways, escape likely).

**Recommendations:**

- Capture 100 age-0 fish from the Little Colorado River in summer 2005 and hold at the best available site(s), preferably Mora and Hualapai.
- Add at least 5% wild fish annually to increase genetic diversity and allow full replacement of the captive population about every 20 years.
- Establish more than one refuge population to protect against the loss of any single site.
- Require all facilities to sign a legal agreement that prohibits propagation, bars release of F1 progeny into the wild, and requires preservation and documentation of dead fish.
- Genetically monitor any upstream translocation within the Little Colorado River to avoid affecting the downstream wild population.
