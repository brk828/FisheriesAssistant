# Consequences of Life at the Upstream Limits of Range on the Endangered Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ptychocheilus

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1986 draft manuscript by Lynn R. Kaeding (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Colorado River Fishery Project) and Douglas B. Osmundson (Utah Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit). A cover letter dated 17 April 1986 to Dr. Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) asks for his review before submission to *NAJFM*. The authors argue that the slow growth of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in upper-basin rivers is a consequence of the short growing season at the species' upstream range limit. Using population simulations, they show that slow growth magnifies the damaging effect of early-life mortality, and that the spawning timing of the species appears adapted to former lower-basin temperature regimes rather than present upper-basin conditions.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was once widespread and abundant in the large rivers and major tributaries of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It is described as the principal native piscivore of the basin. The species now occurs only in upstream portions of its former range and is threatened with extirpation. Earlier studies (Seethaler 1978; Holden and Nick 1982; Tyus et al. 1982) documented this decline.

The authors separate two sets of causes:

- **Causes of widespread extirpation:** water-resource development, introduction of non-native fishes, and poor land-use practices (Miller 1961; Minckley and Deacon 1968; Minckley 1973).
- **Causes of decline in remaining habitat:** less clear. This is the central question of the paper.

The paper examines how a high-latitude, high-elevation range limit produces a short growing season and slow growth, and how that affects population dynamics and recovery management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Growth compared with other piscivores.** Earlier workers reported slow growth of Colorado Squawfish in the upper basin without remarking on it (Vanicek and Kramer 1969; Seethaler 1978). Behnke and Benson (1983) suggested limited food availability. The authors reject this, because prey is abundant in upper-basin rivers. Upper-basin growth is also slow compared with [[Northern Pike]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].

**Pond evidence.** Osmundson (1986) stocked five-month-old Colorado Squawfish (50–75 mm long) in a pond with abundant age-0 [[Common Carp]] near Grand Junction, Colorado. One year later, fish averaged 226 mm TL and the largest was 304 mm. Osmundson attributed this to abundant food and a long growing season with temperatures near optimal for growth. Rapid growth like this had never before been reported for the species.

**Growing season and temperature.** Black and Bulkley (1985) found, from least-squares analysis of their Table 1 data, that no growth occurs below about 14 °C. Using 14 °C as the start of the growing season:

| River reach | Growing season (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Present-day upper Colorado | Less than five months |
| Historic middle basin | About six months |
| Historic lower basin | About eight months |

USGS records showed that upper-Colorado temperatures were not reduced by upstream water development (Bob Green, USFWS, unpublished data). Growth potential in former downstream habitats was therefore judged to be high.

**Spawning.** Spawning begins at 20–22 °C, normally in July or August in the upper basin (Harman 1981; Tyus and Karp 1984; Haynes et al. 1984). Fertilized eggs hatch 4–5 days later. In the historic lower river, spawning temperatures were reached in early May, giving young fish most of the long growing season for first-year growth. The authors conclude that spawning timing fits lower-basin conditions better than upper-basin ones.

**Management and temperature effects.** Marsh (1985) showed that survival of Colorado Squawfish embryos is appreciably reduced by low water temperatures such as those below mainstream dams. Yet dam operation has not reduced temperatures in most Colorado and Green River reaches still inhabited by the species, and fish remain rare in these reaches. Other factors must therefore be limiting the population. Moderated discharge regimes are judged unlikely to have directly affected the species, which evolved under highly variable flows.

## Key Findings

**1. Slow growth reflects the upstream range limit.** The authors conclude that slow growth in upper-basin rivers results from the short growing season, not from food limitation. The pond result (226 mm average, 304 mm maximum after one year) shows the species' growth potential when conditions are favorable.

**2. Slow growth delays maturity.** Maturity depends more on size than age. Upper-basin fish begin maturing at about 428 mm TL (6 years; Seethaler 1978). Slow-growing fish therefore spend more years exposed to predation and other mortality, and fewer reach maturity.

**3. Simulations: growth rate magnifies early-life mortality.** The authors simulated "slow" and "fast" populations under annual early-life mortality rates of 80, 90, 95 and 99%, with 20% mortality in later length classes. Maturity was set at 410 mm TL, at 6 years for "slow" fish and 3 years for "fast" fish. Key results:
- Fewer fish reached maturity as early-life mortality increased, and growth rate had a pronounced effect on survival.
- Increased early-life mortality reduced the "slow" population far more than the "fast" one.
- Population growth potential was markedly greater for the "fast" population, especially at 99% early-life mortality (Figure 4).
- Table 1 reports the percentage difference in mature-female production between populations, reaching 1200% at 99% mortality.

**4. Present-day population growth is far below simulated potential.** Because the upper-basin population is declining, its net reproductive rate (R₀) is below 1. This is a far worse condition than even the simulated "slow" population at 99% early-life mortality (Table 2).

**5. Table 2 growth calculations.** For the "slow" population, R₀ = 2.5, generation length G = 8 years, r = 0.115, and finite rate of increase λ ≈ 1.12. For the "fast" population, R₀ = 52, G = 6.5 years, r = 0.608, and λ = 1.837.

**6. Spawning is adapted to former lower-basin temperatures.** The authors argue that spawning timing is a reproductive strategy suited to former lower-basin habitats rather than present upper-basin conditions.

**7. Management implications.**
- Early-life mortality is likely to have increased with technological development. Its causes need to be identified and reduced where possible.
- Non-native piscivores have "important negative effects" on young Colorado Squawfish. Because eliminating introduced fishes is impracticable in a river system this large, the authors recommend preventing further introductions.
- Dam and reservoir operations could be used to lengthen the growing season and advance spawning, but this must be cautious, because it could also benefit undesirable species. Water development that reduces available temperatures should be avoided.
- Hatchery-based recovery should look for faster-growing phenotypes that may already exist in the genome. Selective breeding should maintain heterozygosity (Meffe 1986).

**8. Study limits.** The authors state there are no definitive data on survival of Colorado Squawfish in the upper basin. The simulations are therefore illustrative rather than predictive.
