#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This study by Rinne examines the microhabitat utilization of the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small, endemic cyprinid of the Gila River basin, across two hydrologically distinct desert stream systems: [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Arizona) and several larger streams in the Upper [[Gila River Basin]] (New Mexico). The species is confirmed as an **obligate riffle dweller**, consistently selecting shallow water (<20 cm) with moderate current velocity (30-40 cm/sec) over pebble-cobble substrate. Critically, the specific physical habitat parameters (depth, velocity, substrate size) shift significantly with stream size, indicating that generalized habitat requirement models for this threatened species are inappropriate. Life-stage segregation was also observed, with adults occupying deeper, faster water than juveniles, while larvae remained in shallow marginal habitats.

## Background & Description
* **Size:** A diminutive species, typically <80 mm Total Length (TL); adults can range up to 60-70 mm.
* **Life Stages Classification (Propst et al. 1984):**
    * Larvae: <28 mm TL
    * Juvenile: 28-37 mm TL
    * Adult: >38 mm TL
* **Body Plan:** Fusiform, dense, and compressed body shape.
* **Fins:** Large pectoral fins.
* **Adaptive Significance:** The dense, compressed body combined with large pectoral fins is highly adaptive for swift water, allowing the fish to maintain position via "exaggerated body movements in short bursts" between substrate "pockets" (per Minckley 1973).
* **Sexual Dimorphism:** Confirmed sexually dimorphic (Minckley 1965).
* **Ecological Analog:** Occupies a niche similar to riffle-dwelling darters (genus *Etheostoma*) of the Eastern United States.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The [[Loach Minnow]] is a specialized riffle-obligate species whose niche is defined by the interaction of current velocity and substrate roughness rather than substrate size alone.

* **Primary Habitat:** Shallow, swift riffles in low-gradient, hydrologically stable streams (e.g., [[Aravaipa Creek]]) as well as larger, higher-gradient mainstem rivers (e.g., [[Gila River]], [[Tularosa River]]).
* **Behavioral Adaptation to Current:** The species does not swim continuously in the current but rather occupies "pockets" or lee-side refuges behind substrate that block or reduce flow, sometimes to zero velocity. This suggests **relative bottom roughness** may be a better predictor of habitat suitability than absolute substrate particle size.
* **Stream-Size Dependent Habitat Shift:**
    * In smaller streams (e.g., Aravaipa Creek), the species occupies gravel-pebble substrate (Index 2.0–3.0).
    * In larger streams (Upper Gila Basin), it shifts to pebble-cobble substrate (Index 3.0–4.0), correlating with a general increase in modal particle size and stream velocity.
    * The [[San Francisco River]], being morphologically and hydrologically similar in size to Aravaipa Creek, showed the most similar habitat utilization patterns (lowest substrate index in the Gila basin comparisons).
* **Life-Stage Segregation:**
    * **Adults:** Occupy significantly deeper and faster water than juveniles in most seasons.
    * **Juveniles:** Occupy shallower, slower water.
    * **Larvae (<28 mm):** Remain in shallow, slow-velocity margins, likely over sand-gravel substrate, even during periods of high population density.
* **Population Dynamics:** During periods of extremely high population density (Aug/Sept 1983), the species was forced into sub-optimal habitat, causing occupied depth/velocity/substrate parameters to become statistically indistinguishable from areas where the species was normally absent.
* **Disturbance Response:** Following a 100-year flood event (>2,000 m³/sec, October 1983), the species shifted to significantly deeper water and larger substrate particle sizes compared to pre-flood conditions.

## Key Findings
1. **Obligate Riffle Specialist:** *Tiaroga cobitis* consistently selects shallower and faster water than is generally available in the stream reach (statistically significant in most season/site comparisons).
2. **Substrate Preference:** The species preferentially selects for larger substrate particle sizes relative to areas where it is absent, except during high-density summer periods.
3. **Habitat Scales with Stream Size:** Loach minnow occupied significantly larger substrate, and generally deeper water, in the larger streams of the Upper Gila Basin compared to the smaller Aravaipa Creek. This correlates with the relationship between adult body size (~40-50 mm) and the pebble/cobble substrate transition zone (40-80 mm).
4. **Regression Analysis Results:** Stepwise regression showed that physical habitat variables (depth, velocity, substrate) explained very little variance in loach minnow abundance (0-5% in most cases, up to 24-55% in only 3 of 13 comparisons). **Depth** was consistently the most significant predictor when significance was found; **velocity** contributed the least explanatory power (no contribution in 10 of 13 comparisons).
5. **Methodological Sensitivity:** Comparison with [[Turner and Tafanelli 1983]] revealed that velocity measurement technique (point measurement at 0.6 depth vs. transect measurement at 5 cm off bottom) significantly impacts reported velocity preferences, with near-bottom measurements yielding lower velocity values consistent with the species' known behavior of using substrate refugia.
6. **Conservation Implication:** The species' preference for shallow, low-velocity margins should *not* be interpreted as tolerance for dewatering or damming. Such modifications would force populations into deep pool habitats, leading to unsuccessful spawning and increased predation risk from non-native pool-dwelling species, potentially causing local extirpation.
7. **Conservation Status:** Listed as threatened in Arizona (1982) and endangered in New Mexico (1979); proposed for Federal threatened species status by USFWS (Williams and Sada, 1985).
