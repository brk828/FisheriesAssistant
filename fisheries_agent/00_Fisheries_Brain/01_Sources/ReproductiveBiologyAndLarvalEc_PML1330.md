# Reproductive Biology and Larval Ecology of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined the reproductive biology and early larval ecology of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, during 1983 and 1984. Spawning occurred in brief pulses in January 1983 and February 1984. Many adults had atrophied reproductive organs or aberrant gametes. Larvae were abundant from February 1984 until they disappeared in mid-April. The adult population was estimated at 25,000 to 75,000 individuals. Recruitment failure appears to be linked to the disappearance of larvae, so determining the fate of larval razorback sucker is the main focus of ongoing work.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid fish whose reproductive status in Lake Mohave was assessed through field collections, dissection, and tag-return data. Research was conducted by [[Arizona State University]] researchers and by the [[University of Nevada, Las Vegas]], which provided tag-return data from 1982 to 1983. Work on reproductive biology was intensified in 1983 and 1984, and detailed analyses of larval ecology began in 1984.

Key biological observations include:
- Adults included a high proportion of fish with atrophied reproductive organs or aberrant gametes. Diminutive or deformed ova were especially common.
- Females sacrificed for fecundity studies often had abnormal gonads and atretic or deformed ova.
- Spawning did not cause detectable mortality, but females sustained substantial abrasion damage during the spawning act.
- The population consists of fish 30 years of age or older, and some appear to be reaching [[Reproductive Senescence]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and larval habitat**
- Larvae emerged from spawning gravels in late January 1984 and continued to emerge through April.
- Larvae were concentrated in shallow water near spawning areas. They were also found along shorelines away from spawning sites and in mid-lake areas where depths exceeded 10 m.
- At night, larvae were [[Positive Phototaxis|positively phototactic]] and were readily captured. In daylight they did not enter benthic substrates. Instead, they were distributed among emergent and submergent vegetation along shorelines.
- Larval capture appeared to be continuous, with cohorts produced at frequent intervals.

**Larval size and development**
- Mean total length (TL) remained near 11.0 mm between February and April, with no evidence of growth over that period.
- Larvae disappeared at lengths greater than about 11.0 mm TL.
- The largest larva was reported as 15 mm in the abstract and as 18 mm in the main text. The OCR of these values is inconsistent, so the correct figure should be verified against the original.

**Larval diet and predation**
- Larval guts were dominated by [[Microcrustacean Zooplankton]] (Cladocera and Copepoda). Other food items were a minor component of the net zooplankton available.
- Larvae from the [[Salt River]], Arizona, in contrast, consumed larval chironomid midges.
- Stomach analyses of several hundred predatory fishes (including [[Common Carp]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and [[Green Sunfish]]) found no significant consumption of razorback eggs or larvae.

## Key Findings

1. **Reproductive impairment:** Many adults had atrophied gonads or aberrant gametes, and females experienced abrasion damage during spawning.
2. **Spawning timing:** Spawning peaked in brief windows in January 1983 and February 1984, continuing into April.
3. **Population size:** The adult population was estimated at 25,000 to 75,000 individuals, based on [[Tag-Return Population Estimate|tag returns]] from 1982 to 1983. The authors describe this as considerably lower than previous estimates.
4. **Age structure and senescence:** The population is composed largely of fish aged 30 years or older, and some appear to be near reproductive senility.
5. **Recruitment failure:** Little or no recruitment into the adult stock was detected. The stock may be approaching unproductive senility.
6. **Larval fate as the key question:** Larvae were abundant in February 1984 but disappeared by mid-April. The disappearance of larvae is presumed to explain recruitment failure, making [[Larval Fate]] the primary focus of ongoing studies.
7. **Predation not implicated:** Stomach analyses of several hundred fishes from six species provided no evidence that predators significantly consumed razorback eggs or larvae.
8. **Continuous production of young:** Larvae were produced in daily or periodic cohorts rather than in a single pulse.
9. **Diet contrast:** Lake Mohave larvae fed mainly on microcrustacean zooplankton, whereas Salt River larvae fed on chironomid midges.
