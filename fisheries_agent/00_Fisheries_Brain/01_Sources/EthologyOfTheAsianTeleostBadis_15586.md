# Ethology of the Asian Teleost *Badis badis*: Motivation and Signal Value of Colour Patterns

#ichthyology #marine-biology #badis-badis

## Executive Summary

This ethological study by [[George W Barlow]] examines the colour patterns of the small Asian teleost [[Badis Badis]] (Hamilton-Buchanan). The author orders the variation in body colouration into typical manifestations, links these to overt behaviour, and interprets their motivational basis and signal value. Colour patterns are treated as a graded, readable indicator of internal states, chiefly [[Fright Behaviour]], [[Aggressive Behaviour]] and [[Sexual Behaviour]]. The author argues that the patterns form a reciprocal fright–aggression system, that the [[Black Pattern]] is a poor visual signal, and that [[Approaching Female]] coloration derives from fright patterns but serves as an effective communication device.

## Background & Description

*Badis badis* is described as a small teleost, with most adults measured at 20–40 mm standard length. It is widely cited in aquarist literature as an extreme example of colour diversity. Teleosts are presented as masters of colour change, and the author notes that colour pattern often indicates motivation when other overt behaviour is absent. Prior work on colour and motivation is limited; the author cites [[Baerends Et Al 1955]] on the male guppy ([[Lebistes Reticulatus]]) as a notable exception.

The author proposes to replace "intensity" with "manifestation" or "threshold" when describing behaviour, because intensity has inconsistent meanings. The framework builds on [[Typical Intensity]] (Morris, 1957).

### Materials and Methods

- **Animals:** Adults of 20–40 mm standard length, mostly laboratory-bred from a Munich aquarium stock.
- **Housing:** Small aquaria of 30, 60 and 120 litres, densely planted, with fine gravel substrate.
- **Water conditions:** Hardness DH 4–6, pH 6–7, temperature 26–28 °C, circulated through a filter reservoir.
- **Lighting:** 25-watt fluorescent tubes.
- **Observation:** Mainly direct observation, supplemented by [[Strobe-Light Photography]].

### Markings

Three marking elements are recognised:

- **Bars:** Jagged vertical rows of brown to black scales. Single bars number about twelve when the background is uniform. Paired bars (five to six) are either [[Hollow Bars]] (paler centre) or [[Solid Bars]] (dusky centre). Red bars form the background when black bars are absent, with shade set by erythrophore concentration.
- **Ocelli:** Dark spots. The [[Pectoral Ocellus]] lies above the pectoral fin base and appears greenish-iridescent when set against the paler body. The [[Dorsal Ocellus]] is a large black blotch at the first dark bar, partly on the fin. The [[Caudal Ocellus]] is at the caudal fin origin and varies between bar-like and ovoid forms.
- **Eye-slash:** An oblique black line through the eye, extending through the mouth rictus onto the chin. A second eye-slash, from the orbit's central margin to the chin, is sometimes present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author treats the colour system as smooth but with characteristic "stopping places", the typical manifestations. Behavioural contexts are grouped into fright, aggressive, sexual and parental behaviour. Several environmental factors modify expression:

- **Background matching:** Starting colour and substrate affect the pattern. Fish on light substrates can display Pale or light tones; the author suggests the bottom colour may be unsuitable for the Approaching Female pattern.
- **Burrow context:** Burrow walls influence body darkness. Dusky Red males are darker in dark burrows and lighter in light ones. Black is more pronounced in dark burrows.
- **Territory:** Ocelli expression appears linked to the "without territory" state, which the author develops with input from [[Wolfgang Wickler]].
- **Predator model:** A [[Predator Model Apparatus]] (a model predaceous fish) is used to provoke fright responses.

Comparative notes include a rapid colour change in the filefish *Stephanolepis cirrhifer* (one to two seconds), and the fright pattern in *Etroplus* cichlids in under one second (personal observation). Morris (1958) is cited on colour inertia in the ten-spined stickleback.

## Key Findings

1. **Typical colour patterns.** *Badis badis* shows "at least eleven more or less distinct colour patterns." The author lists ten named categories, with Paired Bars divided into Hollow and Solid phases: Ocellated, Red Bars, Pale, Eye-Slash, Single Bars, Hollow Bars, Solid Bars, Dusky Red, Black, Spawning Female and Approaching Female.

2. **Colour change lags behaviour.** In one observed encounter, a subordinate male turned Dusky Red, was charged, fled, and only then cycled through Solid Bars, Hollow Bars, Single Bars and Red Bars. Colour is therefore of limited use for tracking rapid changes, but is valuable during longer encounters and when overt behaviour is absent.

3. **Fright gradient.** Fright patterns form a hypothetical threshold series, from greatest to least fright: Pale, Single Bars, Eye-slash, Pectoral Ocellus (sometimes Hollow Bars). Sub-adults show these when fleeing a predator model. Adults show the same arrangement, with Single Bars less frequent in mature males.

4. **Juvenile response.** Small juveniles (about 10 mm) under pursuit did not lighten. Their brownish bars became black, a reversal of the normal fright response.

5. **Aggression as darkening.** In incomplete fights, Hollow and Solid Bars are displayed for up to ten minutes. In complete fights, males oscillate between Solid and Hollow Bars and become Black during carousel fighting. The apparent winner is usually darker. The defeated fish becomes lighter and ends in the Ocellated pattern, and the winner's colours fade to Red Bars afterwards.

6. **Reciprocal fright–aggression model.** The author proposes that Paired Bars combine fright and aggression. Black bars, eye-slash and ocellus reflect fright and provide protection. Paired bars, red background and blue fins reflect aggression and increase conspicuousness. Darker patterns are read as lower fright and higher aggression, though the author notes the argument could be reversed.

7. **Ripe female fights.** Fights between ripe females are partly inhibited, and their bars are interrupted (Fig. 3).

8. **Ocelli are partly independent.** Dorsal and caudal ocelli are at least partly independent of the fright system. A male loses them when confronted with a net or predator model. Ocelli appear with nearly every pattern except Black and Approaching Female. The author proposes that "without territory" (and "weakly attached" or "intention to leave") explains their occurrence, and that the Ocellated pattern may function as an appeasing agent.

9. **Male sexual pattern.** Black is the male's sexual pattern. The author argues it is poorly adapted as a visual signal, since a black male in a dark burrow lacks contrast. He suggests an olfactory signal may have replaced the optic releaser. Black marks the highest levels of both sexual and aggressive motivation.

10. **Female approach pattern.** Approaching Female is light, with thin interrupted red bars and conspicuous amber eyes. It is associated with fright patterns, which the author judges to be a secondary motivation, since highly sexually motivated females approach with less hesitation but are not darker. The author argues the pattern derives from fright patterns, because dark or barred patterns would be read by males as a rival's signal.

11. **Spawning Female.** This pattern is darker and carries all three ocelli and both eye-slashes. The author links it to lower fright and possibly to the "without territory" state. Forselius (1957) is cited for the proposal that ocelli act as detractors, diverting biting away from the eyes.

12. **Paternal pattern.** The parental male is Dusky Red, becoming darker or Black during fast fanning and shuddering. Dorsal and/or caudal ocelli appear when wandering from the clutch, and a blue caudal-peduncle patch appears when he is more attached. The author judges this pattern to be driven mainly by internal states specific to paternal behaviour, with aggressive and sexual components. Its blue patch and lack of dark bars with persistent eye-slash are unique to it.

13. **Cross-taxon generality.** The dark-male, light-female sexual colour relationship is described as widespread among teleosts and possibly evolved more than once. The author suggests it derives from the fright–aggression system.
