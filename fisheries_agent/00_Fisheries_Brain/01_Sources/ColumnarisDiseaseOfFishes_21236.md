# Columnaris Disease of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-pathology #bacterial-disease #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This fishery leaflet provides a comprehensive overview of columnaris disease, a significant bacterial infection affecting freshwater fishes. The disease, caused by slime bacteria (*Chondrococcus columnaris* or *Cytophaga columnaris*), manifests as grayish-white lesions resembling fungal infections and can progress to systemic infection. The document details identification methods, causative agents, transmission modes, and control measures including chemical treatments and antibiotics. Originally issued in July 1958 and rerun in February 1966 by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], this leaflet serves as a practical guide for fish culturists managing disease outbreaks in both warmwater and coldwater species.

## Background & Description

Columnaris disease attacks many species of freshwater fishes and is also known as "Cotton Wool" or "Mouth Fungus" disease in aquarium fishes. The disease presents with grayish-white spots or areas on the head, gills, fins, dorsal and lateral sides of fish. These lesions superficially resemble fungus infections but gradually change to shallow ulcerations, and fins may become frayed. Necrotic areas may appear on gills, and in later stages the infection becomes systemic with causative bacteria demonstrable in internal organs.

The causative agent is a Gram-negative, long, thin, flexible bacterium belonging to slime bacteria ([[Myxobacteriales]]). The bacteria arrange characteristically in columns on fragments of infected tissues. On special media they produce microcysts (*Chondrococcus columnaris*) or may have none (*Cytophaga columnaris*). Two forms exist: cold-water forms infecting [[Salmonid Fishes]] and warm-water forms attacking warmwater and aquarium fishes.

Diagnosis is best made by scraping material from lesions and examining in living state in a drop of water. The presence of slowly oscillating long thin bacteria which congregate in columns while examined is important for diagnosis. Special media are needed for isolation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The source and reservoir of infection are not definitively established but most likely include soil, water containing organic matter, and infected fish. Transmission probably occurs through water or from fish to fish. The incubation period is not known, though the disease may take an explosive course in very hot and dry weather.

Scaleless warmwater fish are particularly susceptible. High water temperature decreases fish resistance to this disease. The disease is common during summer months, with excessively hot summer temperatures favorable for outbreaks in warmwater fishes. The cold-water form may be present in [[Salmon]] and [[Trout]] hatcheries year-round. The disease is best known in America.

## Key Findings

**Susceptibility**: Disease has been described from many species of freshwater fishes, with scaleless warmwater fish particularly susceptible. High water temperature decreases resistance.

**Control Methods for External Infections**:
- Dips in copper sulphate 1:2,000 for 1-2 minutes
- Prophylactic dips in malachite green 1:15,000 for 10-30 seconds
- Pyridylmercuric acetate (PMA) 2 p.p.m. in 1 hour prolonged treatment
- In warmwater ponds: copper sulphate at 1 p.p.m.
- In aquarium fishes: Aureomycin (chlortetracycline) at 10-20 p.p.m. or chloramphenicol (Chloromycetin) 5-10 p.p.m.

**Control Methods for Systemic Infections**:
- Sulfamerazine or sulfadiazine at 10-12 grams per 100 pounds of salmonid fishes per day administered with food
- Treatment effect only slightly beneficial
- Antibiotics not yet tried but likely to be beneficial

**Historical Research Findings**:
- Fish and Rucker (1945) provided first accurate description of disease in salmonid fishes, noting that control with external disinfectants was not possible when infection became systemic
- Johnson (1951) reported that sulfamerazine gave temporary control of systemic *Chondrococcus columnaris* infection in [[Steelhead Trout]], while PMA had no effect
- Johnson and Brice (1952, 1953) documented systemic infections in several species of [[Pacific Salmon]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], with sulfamerazine providing temporary and partial control of mortalities
- Garnjobst (1945) provided first excellent description of the organism in pure culture
- Ordal and Rucker (1944) provided excellent description of *Chondrococcus columnaris*, its life cycle and pathogenicity
