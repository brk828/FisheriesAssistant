# Humpback Chub Monitoring on the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon (1990 Season)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report documents the fourth year of monitoring the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), Grand Canyon, Arizona. It was prepared by C.O. Minckley of the [[Northern Arizona University]] Biology Department for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD). Field work ran from about 16 April to 16 May 1990 (the cover page date is OCR-garbled; the text consistently refers to 1990 data). The study had two goals: (1) to document and observe reproduction in this federally endangered species, and (2) to conduct mark-recapture and movement studies to estimate population size and movement. Sampling used [[Hoop Net]]s, [[Trammel Net]]s, seines, and angling across two camps. Humpback chub made up the largest share of the catch at both sampling areas, and the report found evidence of spawning in early April and a population dominated by young fish at the confluence. The author also raised concerns about a predatory [[Striped Bass]] population and about [[Carp]] impacts on early life stages.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is an endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. Its Little Colorado River population is the focus of this report. Earlier work by Minckley and colleagues, along with [[Kaeding and Zimmerman]] (1983), is cited as the baseline for this monitoring effort.

Sampling was carried out using standardized net sites from the confluence upstream, as in previous years:

- **Hoop nets:** 1 m diameter, 2.5 m long, some with 6 m wings.
- **Trammel nets:** 91.5 m × 2.5 m, with a 4 cm outer wall and a 2.5 cm inner wall.
- **Seines:** a 6.1 m × 1.9 m × 3 mm mesh minnow seine and a 1 m × 1 m fine-mesh larval seine.
- **Angling** for predatory species.

Nets were set at 15 confluence sites and 11 upper (Salt Trail Canyon) sites. Fish were measured to the nearest mm (total length) and weighed to the nearest gram. Sex was determined from gamete expression and the appearance of the urogenital papilla. The author cautions that sexing is not foolproof and that data from unripe fish reflect population trends only.

Tagging used two methods:

- **[[Carlin Tagging]]:** external tags inserted below the dorsal fin (dark blue in 1990; earlier colors in 1987–1989).
- **[[Pit Tagging]]:** [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] tags injected into the coelomic cavity. These had been tested on salmonids (about 99% tag retention and survival, per Prentice et al. 1985) and on upper-basin Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker and roundtail chub.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The sampling reach ran from the [[Confluence]] of the LCR and the Colorado River upstream about 15 km, including the [[Salt Trail Canyon]] camp (about 10 km upstream). Confluence nets sat in a tidal zone where mainstream flows can back up into the LCR. Water depth there changed with mainstream discharge (for example, the net was exposed at 30,000 cfs or less).

**Temperature and spawning cues.** LCR water temperature at the start of the study (about 16–17°C) fell within the range considered optimal for spawning (Hamman 1962; Marsh 1985). The author suggests spawning was triggered by warming water, rising day length and spring flooding. Spring-fed flows from the LCR's travertine complex were proposed to be cooler and to provide better conditions for upstream-migrating fish than mainstream Colorado River water.

**Species composition.** Native fish made up most of the catch. Species were:

- **Native:** humpback chub, [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Razorback Sucker]] (rare).
- **Non-native:** [[Channel Catfish]], [[Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Striped Bass]].

**Striped bass.** Striped bass were stocked into [[Lake Mead]] (1969) and [[Lake Powell]] (1974) and are now established in both reservoirs. Anglers and river runners reported striped bass below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and in the Grand Canyon. The author expects the population to become large enough to reduce introduced and native fish. Predation is expected to be limited by food supply.

**Parasites.** Anchorworm infestation was much less than 1% of humpback chub. Two fish from the Salt Trail camp had large numbers of tapeworms, the first reported occurrence of tapeworms in this population.

## Key Findings

**Sampling results**

- **Total catch:** 2,628 fish of twelve species were collected (Table 2). Humpback chub made up 62% of the total (N = 1,630). Native fish made up about 97% (N = 2,546). Non-native fish made up about 3%. Razorback suckers were a trace.
- **Confluence nets:** 1,414 fish of twelve species. Humpback chub numbered 931 (reported as 52.5%). Native fish were 96.7%. Three razorback suckers were taken. Catch per net-day ranged from 0 to 12.2 fish, and catch per hour from 0 to 0.5 fish.
- **Salt Trail nets:** 1,211 fish of seven species. Humpback chub numbered 691 (57.7%). Native fish were 95.6%. Catch per hour ranged from 0.2 to 0.5 fish.
- **Sex ratio:** Overall, 67% of sexed humpback chub at Salt Trail were male and 33% were female. Males made up 50% of sexed confluence fish.

**Reproduction**

- Spawning was not observed directly, but the presence of larval fish and of young 1990 fish indicates that spawning began in April, before research started.
- Ripe males outnumbered ripe females at both sites. Ripe fish were 15% of males and 2% of females at the confluence. Ripe males at Salt Trail were 26% and ripe females 0.5%.
- Fish under 20 mm were seen throughout the river at the start of the study.

**Population structure**

- Confluence catches were dominated by fish of 50–150 mm. The largest numbers over 250 mm occurred in the first two weeks.
- Length data suggest annual cohorts: fish under 100 mm are the current year's spawn, up to 150 mm are second-year fish, and up to 220 mm are third-year fish. The author notes these lengths vary between years.
- Comparisons across 1987–1990 show shifting year-class structure. In 1989, fish of 100–150 mm dominated Salt Trail catches. By 1990, the upstream population was larger and older. At the confluence, younger year classes were most abundant in 1990.

**Movement and tagging**

- Few tagged fish moved far. At the confluence, 35% of recaptured humpback chub moved downstream, 24% upstream, and 41% did not move.
- Upper-reach recaptures were mostly stationary: 66% did not move, 11% moved downstream, and 3% moved upstream.
- Thirty-six PIT-tagged fish from the confluence were recaptured (about 14%). Recapture data are summarized in Tables 6, 7, 11, 12 and 13. The author notes that some fish grew between capture and recapture, with growth ranging from roughly 6 to 66 mm in tagged fish.
- Mark-recapture population estimates (Lincoln-Petersen method, Ricker 1975) were made for the total, Salt Trail and confluence collections. The numerical results are not legible in the source text.

**Otolith and temperature experiment**

- Fifteen humpback chub (90–120 mm TL) were moved between the LCR and the Colorado River from 27 April to 5 May 1990 to test whether temperature shocks leave growth checks on otoliths. Five fish were held in the Colorado mainstream and ten in the LCR at the start. Otoliths had not been examined at the time of writing.

**Other species**

- **Razorback sucker:** Three were taken at the confluence between 20 and 26 April 1990. Two were male. Two were recaptured within 24 hours, and one was found dead after about 17 hours. The author suggests all Grand Canyon razorback and hybrid specimens be re-examined against upper-basin populations, since razorback sucker is proposed for federal endangered listing.
- **Hybridization:** Razorback–flannelmouth sucker hybrids have been reported from the Grand Canyon region.
- **Striped bass:** One juvenile (350 mm TL, 454 g) was taken at the LCR mouth.
- **Stomach contents:** Examined in channel catfish, striped bass, rainbow trout and brown trout. Most rainbow trout were empty. Channel catfish contained fish remains and invertebrates. Sample sizes were small.
- **Carp:** Carp were far more abundant than the catch data suggest. The author thinks they may be damaging early life stages of native fish.

**Recommendations**

1. Continue monitoring to determine the yearly status of humpback chub.
2. Begin analysis of carp intestinal tracts to determine their impact on early life stages.
3. Re-examine Grand Canyon razorback sucker and hybrid specimens.
4. Consider moving humpback chub from the LCR to a refugium to protect the population from striped bass predation, a proposal the author has advanced previously.
