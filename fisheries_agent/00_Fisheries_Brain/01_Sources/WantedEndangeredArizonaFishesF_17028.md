# Endangered Arizona Fishes for Future Generations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endemic-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1987 *ASU Research* feature describes the decline of three endemic Arizona fishes: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. All three are listed as endangered at the federal and state levels. Arizona State University researchers [[W.L. Minckley]] and [[Paul Marsh]] are participating in a recovery effort that aims to bolster populations and study the species' life cycles. The article attributes the decline mainly to habitat disruption from [[Dam Construction]] and to predation by [[Non-Native Species]] introduced for sportfishing and bait. It also reports that 16 of Arizona's 25 native fish species are endangered. The researchers identify habitat protection and public awareness as the long-term solutions. The article also describes a new experimental population established at a research site near the ASU campus.

## Background & Description

- **Historical abundance:** About 100 years earlier, the three species were so plentiful that settlers in the present-day Phoenix area sometimes used pitchforks to harvest them from drained irrigation canals.
- **Range:** Before settlement, the fishes ranged from Wyoming south to Mexico in the [[Colorado River]], and east to the New Mexico border in the [[Gila River]].
- **Recent decline:** Populations fell to dangerously low levels over roughly the past 40 years. The article says they were "found in abundance as recently as the 1940s."
- **Knowledge gaps:** The species became rare before scientists could study them closely, so little is known about their biology or the causes of their decline.
- **Regional context:** The researchers describe more than 30 fish species endemic to Arizona and the Southwest, many of which are threatened. The article notes that these fishes have unique adaptations to some of the harshest aquatic environments in the world.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline:**

1. **Habitat disruption from dams.** Dams back rivers into lakes and ponds upstream and can reduce downstream flow to a trickle. Marsh describes these changes as creating habitats "far different from those in which these fishes lived for millions of years." Natural flood and drought cycles are now controlled, and river flow is set by electric power and irrigation demands.
2. **Predation by non-native fishes.** More than 70 non-native species have been brought into Arizona waters as sport and baitfish. Largemouth bass, channel catfish, and several trout species are cited as examples of newer arrivals.
3. **Research gaps.** Marsh states that more research is needed to pinpoint the factors responsible for the decline, and that several theories exist.

**Conservation framing:** The researchers compare the loss to the decline of the bison in the last century. Minckley stresses that this is "not a nickel-and-dime change," meaning the species are moving from abundant to critically rare rather than from rare to rarer. Marsh notes that "once a species disappears, it is lost forever," and calls the fishes "legitimate national resources."

**Funding and partners:** The effort is funded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], with cooperation from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. The ASU researchers are part of a larger program to re-establish many endangered fish species in the wild.

## Key Findings

1. **Three endangered species:** The Colorado squawfish, bonytail chub, and razorback sucker are listed as endangered at the federal and state levels.
2. **Critically low bonytail numbers:** Minckley found only 24 bonytail specimens at Lake Mohave since 1976 and estimates only a few hundred remain.
3. **Razorback sucker in one reservoir:** The razorback sucker is reported only from Lake Mohave, with an estimated population of perhaps 50,000.
4. **Squawfish distribution:** Many thousands of Colorado squawfish are thought to remain, but only within the Colorado River.
5. **Endemic fish crisis:** More than 30 endemic Arizona and Southwestern fish species face the threat of extinction, and 16 of Arizona's 25 native species are endangered.
6. **Main threats:** Dam-related habitat disruption and predation by more than 70 introduced non-native species are cited as the chief causes of decline.
7. **Recovery approach:** The ASU team is working to establish populations to study the species' life cycles and to learn why the fishes disappear in the wild.
8. **New research population:** Under the project, the scientists stocked three interconnected lakes on the [[ASU Research Park]] grounds with 1,000 Colorado squawfish and an equal number of razorback suckers and bonytail chubs. The source says the largest lake is about 20 feet deep. The surface area is given as eight acres for the largest lake and eight acres total for all three, so the figure is ambiguous. The site is about five miles east of the ASU campus, which reduces the travel time from the 500-mile round trip to Lake Mohave. The source says the stocked lakes also support other species, including an algae-eating species stocked by park officials, panfish, bluegill, bass, and minnows that entered through a connecting Salt River Project canal.
9. **Angler protection:** Fishing is allowed at the lakes, but the rare fish are not yet large enough to be caught. Posters ask anglers who hook a rare fish to return it to the water and to call the ASU Center for Environmental Studies or the Arizona Game and Fish Department.
10. **Long-term solution:** Both researchers agree that [[Habitat Protection]] is the long-term answer, and that public awareness and accurate education for scientists and the public are essential to achieving it.
