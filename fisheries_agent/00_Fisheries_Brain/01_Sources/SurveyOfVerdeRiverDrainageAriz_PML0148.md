# Survey of Verde River Drainage, Arizona, for Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*), 2000–2002

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report, prepared by contractor Brian Bagley for the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Arizona Ecological Services Field Office), documents a multi-year fisheries survey of tributaries of the [[Verde River]] in central Arizona. The survey targeted [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a federally listed threatened cyprinid. Field work ran from August 2000 through 2002 and sampled 133 locations on 44 streams. Loach minnow was not captured at any site. The survey did, however, yield a new population of [[Gila Chub]] in [[Wet Bottom Creek]], confirmed several native and reintroduced populations, and documented heavy dominance of non-native fishes in many reaches. The file also contains the associated contract scope, budget, interagency correspondence, and peer-style editorial reviews of the draft.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** Loach minnow is endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of New Mexico, Arizona and Sonora, Mexico. It was federally listed as threatened, and its future was considered in jeopardy from habitat alteration and from competition and predation by non-native fishes. The species was known from about a dozen remaining populations (USFWS 1991). Its last recorded Verde River collection cited in the report was in 1938 (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology).

**Morphology (after Minckley 1973 and USFWS 1991).** A small stream-dwelling fish reaching standard lengths of about 68 mm, with an elongated, slightly compressed, ventrally flattened body. The mouth is small, terminal and highly oblique. The body is olivaceous, heavily blotched with darker pigment, with whitish spots at the base of the dorsal and caudal fins. Breeding males are darker and show bright red-orange coloration at the bases of the pelvic, pectoral and caudal fins and on the mouth.

**Preferred habitat.** Shallow, turbulent riffles in small to moderately large creeks and rivers with gravel-to-cobble substrates (Minckley 1973; Propst and Bestgen 1991; USFWS 1991).

**Study area.** The Verde River watershed covers approximately 16,900 km². Its headwaters rise at nearly 2,135 m (7,000 ft), and the river flows more than 301 km (187 mi) before merging with the [[Salt River]].

**Objective.** Conduct a fisheries survey of Verde River tributaries looking specifically for loach minnow, while documenting all co-occurring native and non-native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community composition.** The report lists 23 fish species captured in total: 8 native and 15 non-native. Key natives include [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], Sonora and desert suckers, [[Gila Topminnow]] (reintroduced), and reintroduced [[Gila Trout]]. Historically recorded non-natives include [[Green Sunfish]], red shiner, fathead minnow, mosquitofish and several catfishes and black basses. The report notes that 28 non-native fish species have been recorded in the Verde basin (Minckley 1973).

**Threats.** The author and reviewers emphasize that the impacts of humans and non-native species on Arizona's native fishes are well documented. Specific local stressors include:
- Fish poisoning of Beaver Creek in 1962 (Bassett 1962), followed by its domination by non-native species.
- Habitat loss in Verde tributaries near Chino and Beaver Creek (Hendrickson and Minckley 1984; Weedman 1996).
- Frequent intermittency and drying of streams, especially in the upper tributaries.
- Heavy cattle use and human disturbance in several riparian zones.
- Flood scour and the 1990 Dude Fire, which eliminated non-native trout from Dude Creek.

**Riparian significance.** The report describes the Verde system as encompassing a significant portion of Arizona's remaining riparian habitat and as a cornerstone for native fish survival.

**Taxonomic note.** Reviewers recommend recognizing the recent description of the headwater chub (*Gila nigra*), which raises the Verde basin native fish count to 12 species (the draft stated 11).

## Key Findings

**Survey effort and outcome**
- 133 sampling locations on 44 streams in the Verde River drainage.
- 23 fish species captured (8 native, 15 non-native).
- **No loach minnow were encountered** at any site. The author notes this species is difficult to encounter and can easily go undetected. Historically it was recorded from only three Verde drainage locations (Table 2 of the report), so it was likely never abundant there.
- Sampling employed [[Backpack Electrofishing]] (shocker), dip nets, [[Seine Sampling]] (block/kick seines) and gill nets. Specimens were identified, counted by young-of-year and older age classes, and released, except for voucher specimens deposited at the [[Arizona State University Museum]].

**Native fish highlights**
- **New Gila chub population:** Discovered in lower Wet Bottom Creek, where chubs were present but uncommon. Five chubs were taken to ASU for genetic analysis. Previously known from 26 locations in the Verde basin (Weedman et al. 1996). Continued presence was also confirmed at Walker Creek, Spring Creek and [[Williamson Valley Wash]].
- **Roundtail chub** were collected from Deadman Creek, South Fork Deadman Creek, Weber Spring and West Clear Creek.
- **Longfin dace** was the most commonly occurring native fish (29 locations); speckled dace was found at 20 locations.
- Sonora sucker and desert sucker were locally abundant at several sites.

**Reintroduction outcomes**
- **Gila trout** were reintroduced to Dude Creek after the 1990 Dude Fire eliminated non-native trout. In 2000 only a small part of the reach was occupied. Captured fish ranged from 4 to 10 inches.
- **Gila topminnow**, stocked in 1982, continued to maintain large numbers at Walnut Spring and Lime Creek.
- **Lime Creek** had natural barriers, few non-natives and substantial permanent water above FS road 24. The 2001 correspondence describes it as a relatively "easy" site for topminnow and native reintroduction, with a waterfall near Little Cougar Canyon and a remote, federally owned setting.

**Non-native fish dominance**
- [[Green Sunfish]] was the most commonly occurring non-native (28 locations), most abundant in lower-elevation tributaries.
- Brown and rainbow trout were common at higher elevations; they were stocked from as early as 1942 and still receive seasonal stocking by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]].
- Other frequent non-natives included red shiner, fathead minnow, yellow bullhead, mosquitofish and smallmouth bass. Less frequent species included common carp, flathead and channel catfish, black bullhead, largemouth bass, bluegill and rockbass.
- The author cautions that deeper-water species were likely undersampled because the survey focused on riffles.

**Dry and fishless sites**
- Many sites were dry, intermittent or fishless, including Grindstone Wash and Bear Canyon, which field crews could not locate with permanent water.

**Recommendations**
- Continued monitoring and surveys of additional reaches may reveal loach minnow or new populations.
- Protect portions of the Verde drainage from human and non-native impacts.
- Reviewers requested a large-scale sampling-location map, stream names or numbering tied to the appendix site descriptions, and recommendations for fishless sites suitable for native reestablishment.

**Project administration and correspondence**
- **Contract:** FY1999 RPA 3 Task 17 funding transfer under the Central Arizona Project (CAP), contract 22410-0-M525. The estimated total project cost was $7,500 over 24 months. Priority is linked to Loach Minnow Recovery Plan task 1.1 (identify the extent of existing populations and the protection each receives).
- **Scope:** Surveys by experienced crews using block seines and/or backpack electrofishing, with vouchers (up to 5 per species per site) and up to 10 loach minnow sub-samples for frozen genetic material if found. A sketch map was later required only for sites where loach minnow were found.
- **Reporting:** Interim progress reports at 6, 12 and 18 months; Draft Final Report at 24 months, with 45 days' Service review.
- **Reviews (September 2002):** Paul Barrett (USFWS), Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) and Robert Clarkson (Bureau of Reclamation) found the report thorough and well documented, with mainly editorial corrections. Corrections included reference dates (51 FR 39468, listed 1986; Gila-related citations corrected to 1997), "Pylodictis" spelling, species count updates and acknowledgment of permitting authorities and CAP funding.
- **Field logistics:** Monthly updates in 2001 noted monsoon delays and an August 2001 status note that the Williamson Valley Wash chub population persisted despite gravel filling its known pool in 1993.
