# Fishes of Arizona: Part 2 Check List

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This check list, by [[Robert Rush Miller]] and [[Charles H. Lowe]], is the first comprehensive inventory of the fishes known to occur in Arizona waters. It covers both native and introduced species, compiled from a review of the literature and museum records. The authors report **68 species**, of which **27 are native**, **37 are introduced**, and **4 are hypothetical aliens** that may become established. The list documents a period of rapid change between roughly 1950 and 1960, when habitat alteration and introductions of exotic fishes reduced native fish populations. Several native species are now close to extinction, and the authors expect further introductions.

## Background & Description

The check list follows the common-name conventions of the American Fisheries Society's *A List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada* (second edition). Its scope is the state's freshwater fauna, with the "lower Colorado River" defined as the reach below Lake Mead.

Documentation of Arizona fishes spans more than a century of work:

- **Nineteenth-century surveys:** Collections by the U.S. and Mexican Boundary Survey were reported by [[Baird and Girard]] and [[Girard]]. [[Cope and Yarrow]] (1875) and [[Evermann and Rutter]] (1895) summarized the fish fauna of the Colorado River basin.
- **Early twentieth-century work:** [[Gilbert and Scofield]] (1898) described fishes of the Gila and lower Colorado systems, and [[Snyder]] (1915) reported collections from Gulf of California tributaries.
- **Mid-century contributions:** Work by [[Hubbs]], [[Miller]], [[Lowe]], and others, including [[Miller and Hubbs]] (1960) on spiny-rayed cyprinids, provided most of the taxonomic and distributional data used here.

Archaeological fish remains from the lower Colorado basin were also examined (Miller 1955).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors describe a sharp decline in native aquatic habitats:

- **Habitat loss:** Channelization and elimination of surface flows altered aquatic habitats in the 1950s and 1960s.
- **Exotic species:** Introductions have increased rapidly. Before 1950, the state had more native than introduced species (reported as 25 versus 17; the source text is partly garbled here). By 1960, the estimated fauna included a much larger share of alien forms (Lowe 1960).
- **Replacement of natives:** The authors link exotic species to replacement and reduction of native fish populations.
- **Hybridization:** Hybridization between introduced and native taxa is a concern. [[Rainbow Trout]] hybridizes with both [[Cutthroat Trout]] and the native [[Gila Trout]], and [[Gila Sucker]] hybridizes with [[Sonora Sucker]].

No indigenous species is known to have been eliminated from the state. However, the authors state that several native fishes are "nearing extinction":

- [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), restricted to a few localities
- [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*)
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*)

Other native species, including [[Bonytail]], [[Woundfin]], and [[Spikedace]], are either extinct in parts of their former range or very limited. Several were used as baitfish, a practice that has also contributed to the spread of introduced species.

## Key Findings

**1. Species inventory**
- Total of 68 species known from Arizona waters:
  - 27 native
  - 37 introduced
  - 4 hypothetical aliens that could become permanent

**2. Native fauna in decline**
- The Gila River and Colorado River systems hold most of the native diversity.
- The Colorado squawfish, once up to nearly 100 pounds and about 6 feet long, has nearly disappeared from Arizona. A few adults have been caught recently in the lower Colorado River.
- The bonytail is extinct in the Gila River drainage and scarce in the Colorado River.
- The woundfin is extinct in the lower Gila River basin.
- The Gila topminnow survives in a few localities and was once abundant in southern Arizona.

**3. Major introduced groups**
- **Salmonids:** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Arctic Grayling]], and a dwarfed landlocked [[Sockeye Salmon]] form stocked in Ashurst Lake in 1959.
- **Cyprinids:** [[Common Carp]] (first noted in the Colorado River at Yuma in 1890), [[Golden Shiner]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Utah Chub]].
- **Catfishes:** [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Brown Bullhead]], and [[Flathead Catfish]].
- **Centrarchids:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Redear Sunfish]], and [[Warmouth]].
- **Other:** [[Striped Bass]] (planted in the lower Colorado River), [[Threadfin Shad]] (introduced to Lake Havasu in 1954 and 1955), and [[Sailfin Molly]] (in Salt River canals near Phoenix).

**4. Documented introduction histories**
- The Gila trout was once found in several headwater streams, including Blue River and Oak Creek. Its disappearance from most of its range prompted restoration efforts by the Arizona Game and Fish Department through artificial propagation and restocking.
- [[Red Shiner]] was established in the Little Colorado River in 1953.
- The [[Fathead Minnow]] was first collected in Arizona in 1952 at Lee's Ferry, in the Paria River.
- The [[Bluegill]] was established in Mormon Lake in 1934 and Salt River in 1937.

**5. Taxonomic and status uncertainties**
- Some taxa are under study, including the relationship between [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Gila Chub]], and the status of the bluehead sucker.
- Several hypothetical species, such as the [[White Bass]] and a hybrid sunfish, may or may not persist.

**6. Future outlook**
- The authors expect further introductions, including species already in the state's waters and others yet to be planted.
- They note that the loss of native fishes is unfortunate because these species have considerable scientific and aesthetic interest.
