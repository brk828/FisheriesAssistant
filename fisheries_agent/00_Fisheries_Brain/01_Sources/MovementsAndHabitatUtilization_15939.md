# Movements and Habitat Utilization of Reintroduced Razorback Suckers and Colorado Squawfish in the Verde River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 Arizona Game and Fish Department special report documents two years of radiotelemetry monitoring of hatchery-raised, adult-sized [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] released into the upper [[Verde River]], a tributary of the [[Gila River Basin]]. Twenty-four razorbacks and 15 squawfish were implanted with radiotransmitters and tracked by [[Aerial Telemetry]] and [[Ground Telemetry]]. Both species selected slow currents, moderate depths, and sand substrates, and used pools heavily. Movements were modest, with most fish staying within about 5 km of release, though a few travelled tens of kilometres. The authors judged the success of the reintroduction "equivocal." Heavy summer infestations by the exotic copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] were identified as a possible additional obstacle, and no tagged fish was confirmed to survive a complete summer.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** Both species are native "big river" fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] and were listed as endangered (squawfish in 1967; razorback in 1991). Squawfish were extirpated from much of their former range, and razorbacks persisted mainly as senescent reservoir populations.
- **Reintroduction history:** A razorback reintroduction into historic Gila Basin habitat began in 1981 under a 10-year Memorandum of Understanding between the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]]. Squawfish reintroductions began in 1985 under an "experimental, non-essential" designation. By the end of 1990, more than 600,000 squawfish and 11,800,000 razorbacks had been stocked into the Verde, Salt, and Gila drainages. About 90% of stocking events involved fish averaging 165 mm total length or less. Earlier efforts were largely limited by predation from introduced fishes, as described in [[Predation By Non-Native Fishes]].
- **Shift in approach:** Recent stocking used larger fish, which are more resistant to predators and could be fitted with radiotransmitters.
- **Study area:** All releases were made at RK 195 near Perkinsville. The Verde River is 286 km long and drains about 16,896 km². Base flow is about 25 cfs at Verde Ranch and about 90 cfs at the Clarkdale gauge (RK 173). Native fishes include longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker, roundtail chub, speckled dace, and the threatened spikedace. Introduced species include red shiner, mosquitofish, several catfishes, carp, and black bass (smallmouth and largemouth) and green sunfish.

**Methods overview**

- **Transmitters:** Made by Custom Telemetry & Consulting, Inc., with 3 V lithium batteries and operating at 40.5–40.8 MHz. Squawfish tags weighed 17 g (about 60 mm long), and razorback tags weighed 12 g (45 mm long).
- **Surgery:** Fish were anaesthetized with [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] and implanted using the [[Lateral Incision Method]], which replaced the earlier midline method because healing was inconsistent. Fish received oxytetracycline and [[Chloramine T]] treatment, and sutures were removed after 3–4 weeks.
- **Tracking:** Aerial flights were made weekly at about 150 m altitude. Ground monitoring recorded hourly locations over 24 or 48 consecutive hours each week, using triangulation from the shoreline.
- **Habitat sampling:** Current velocity, depth, and substrate were measured at random grid cells for habitat availability and at fish locations for habitat use.
- **Statistics:** Selection was tested with [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]] comparisons and [[Bonferroni Confidence Intervals]] (98.75% intervals, with alpha = 0.1 and k = 8 classes). Selection strength was expressed with [[Jacobs D Index]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and hydrology**

- Water temperature ranged from 3 to 27 °C, following a seasonal pattern.
- Conductivity ranged from 100 to 800 µS, with the largest drops during winter floods.
- pH ranged from 7.34 (February 1992) to 8.84 (December 1992), within the normal range for Arizona streams.
- The lowest dissolved oxygen readings occurred in July 1992, during extreme summer drought.

**Habitat availability in the study reach**

- Major habitat types were 59% run, 32% pool, and 9% riffle.
- The modal depth class was 20–39 cm (43%), and 81% of available depths were under 60 cm.
- Sand was the most common substrate (32%), followed by cobble (23%) and boulder (16%).
- Velocities under 0.1 m/s were most common, and 75% of velocities were under 0.4 m/s.

**Thermal and disease context:** Verde summer temperatures (17–25 °C) are near optimal for completing the life cycle of Lernaea cyprinacea, and the slow-flowing pool habitats used by both species may favour dispersal of its free-swimming stages.

## Key Findings

### Habitat selection

- **Velocity:** Both species used currents under 0.1 m/s more than available, and avoided 0.20–0.29 m/s.
- **Depth:** Both species avoided depths under 40 cm and selected 60–119 cm.
- **Substrate:** Both species selected sand and avoided cobble and boulder. Razorbacks also selected gravel and detritus.
- **Habitat type:** Neither species used riffles. Pools were used by 71% of squawfish locations and 61% of razorback locations.
- **Within-habitat patterns:** In pools, squawfish used slower and deeper water than available. Razorbacks in pools used silt more often (65% used vs. 44% available).

### Movement

| Metric | Colorado Squawfish | Razorback Sucker |
|---|---|---|
| Mean absolute aerial movement | 166 m/day | 204 m/day |
| Net aerial movement | 20 m/day upstream | 60 m/day downstream |
| Zero-movement aerial observations | 28% | 46% |
| Mean absolute ground movement | 17.1 m/hr | 14.5 m/hr |

- Most fish stayed within about 5 km of release. Two squawfish moved more than 30 km downstream, and one moved more than 45 km upstream. Two razorbacks moved more than 60 km downstream.
- Both species moved significantly farther at night (squawfish 19.5 vs. 14.6 m/hr by day; razorbacks 18.2 vs. 10.5 m/hr, P<0.001).
- Upstream and downstream hourly movements did not differ significantly for either species.
- Ground location accuracy was good. For 24 trials, the 95% confidence intervals of error were 2.73–4.27 m longitudinally and 2.69–4.17 m laterally, and errors did not differ among observers.

### Survival and tag retention

- Radio contact lasted 20–224 days for squawfish and 6–266 days for razorbacks.
- Final contact for 19 of 24 razorbacks and 10 of 15 squawfish occurred between June and September.
- Not a single implanted fish was confirmed to survive a complete summer. Tag expulsion was considered rare in these species, so mortality, probably linked to Lernaea infestation, was the leading explanation.
- Many recaptured razorbacks and some squawfish carried several dozen Lernaea each.

### Conclusions and recommendations

- The authors judged the reintroduction "equivocal." Stocking adult-sized fish appeared to reduce predation mortality and extend post-stocking survival compared with earlier small-fish releases, and the upper Verde provides habitat that can sustain large individuals at least seasonally.
- The main recommendations were to:
  1. Study the incidence and effects of Lernaea on native fishes in the Verde and elsewhere.
  2. Continue stocking adult-sized fish in the Gila Basin, with a well-defined and funded monitoring program.
  3. Use fish that have been in the wild for at least a year in behavioural studies, to avoid hatchery-pond habits.
  4. Consider further radiotelemetry studies in other Gila Basin rivers, and resurvey the study reach after the 1993 winter flood.
- The authors noted that the observed movement and habitat use in the Verde differed from upper-basin rivers. Shallower depths and slower currents were attributed partly to reduced deep-water availability and possibly to hatchery rearing, though other causes were not ruled out.
