# Use of Genetic Characters in Conservation Biology

#mammalogy #canidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This commentary responds to [[Wayne and Jenks 1991]], which proposed a hybrid origin for the [[Red Wolf]] from [[Grey Wolf]] and [[Coyote]] parentage. The authors argue that the debate has confused two issues: (1) how genetic characters should be used to identify species and hybrids, and (2) the role of hybridization in evolution. They contend that molecular data supplement, rather than replace, morphological and other evidence, and that neither divergence levels nor a lack of divergence reliably determine specific status. They conclude that the [[Endangered Species Act]] protects distinctive forms regardless of origin, and that introgressed taxa should not be denied protection for lacking "genetic purity."

## Background & Description

The commentary is a letter-style response in the journal *Conservation Biology* (March 1992) by Thomas E. Dowling, Bruce D. DeMarais, W. L. Minckley, Michael E. Douglas (Arizona State University, Department of Zoology) and Paul C. Marsh (Center for Environmental Studies, Arizona State University). It places the red wolf controversy within wider debates on [[Species Concept]] and conservation policy under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA).

The authors note that the red wolf debate has drawn several responses in the same literature, including [[Gittleman and Pimm 1991]], [[O'Brien and Mayr 1991]] and [[Rennie 1991]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mitochondrial DNA evidence:** [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) data show that coyotes and red wolves have hybridized. The authors reject the claim that grey wolves contributed to the red wolf's origin.
- **Historical hybridization:** Analysis of mtDNA from six pelts collected between 1905 and 1930 indicated that red wolves and coyotes hybridized even then. The authors state that these specimens are too few and too recent to determine whether human habitat modification increased [[Introgression]].
- **Hybridization in nature:** Grey wolf and coyote hybridization in the northern United States and Canada does not produce red wolf phenotypes, according to the original authors, who suggested that "unique environmental and genetic conditions" are required.
- **Conservation stakes:** The authors argue that the answer to the origin and frequency of hybridization affects management strategies for both species.

## Key Findings

1. **Molecular data are one set of characters.** They test hypotheses of divergence and phylogeny but do not supersede other evidence such as morphology.
2. **Absence of diagnostic markers does not imply conspecificity.** Genetic data typically cover only a small fraction of the genome, so failure to find divergence only means the [[Null Hypothesis]] of no divergence cannot be rejected. Speciation may result from very few genetic changes, so genetic divergence levels are not valid measures of specific status.
3. **Red wolves are a historical species.** Available morphological, protein and mtDNA evidence suggests that the red wolf is a distinct, close relative of the grey wolf that has hybridized with coyotes. Shared mtDNA would suggest recent common ancestry rather than hybrid origin.
4. **Hybridization has evolutionary value.** It is important in polyploid plant speciation and may rapidly introduce genetic variation, helping species adapt to changing environments (citing [[Lewontin and Birch 1966]], [[Grant 1985]] and [[DeMarais et al. 1992]]). It is not always important or always harmful, so each case requires careful evaluation.
5. **Protection should not depend on "purity."** The ESA was designed to protect distinctive forms (not only species) at risk of extinction, regardless of how they originated. Denying protection to introgressed taxa "defeats the purpose" of conservation efforts.
6. **Neutral markers are weak evidence of impact.** [[Genetic Markers]] are often selectively neutral and decoupled from the phenotypic traits under selection, so they may spread readily among taxa and reveal little about introgression's effects on fitness.
7. **Errors have irreversible consequences in conservation.** Scientific misunderstandings usually get corrected by further research, but condemning distinct historical entities because of perceived impurity or low divergence could cause the extinction of nonrenewable resources.
