# Effectiveness of Light Traps for Capture and Retention of Larval and Early Juvenile Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[National Park Service]] Cooperative Parks Study Unit by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University (Snyder and Meismer, 30 September 1997), evaluates whether floating, low-intensity [[Quatrefoil Light Trap]] designs can capture and retain larvae and early juveniles of endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes. Laboratory experiments tested [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae and juveniles and, more briefly, [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail]] larvae. Captures were modest (typically 13 to 51% in clear water), but retention of larvae was high, and the traps were judged useful for field monitoring of razorback sucker reproduction.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a federally endangered catostomid of the Colorado River Basin, with recruitment that has been very limited for decades. Predation, particularly by introduced fishes, is suspected as the main cause of larval mortality. Light traps have long been used to collect positively phototactic larvae and early juveniles of marine and freshwater fishes, and they are most useful for night sampling in habitats with little or no current.

The light trap evaluated is the commercially produced Southern Concepts quatrefoil trap: 32 cm tall, 24 cm in diameter, with four clear plexiglass lobes, a styrofoam float, a central spiral-scored light rod, a lens-focused lamp, and a voltage-limiting circuit that delivers about 2 lux at the trap perimeter and runs about 40 h on two alkaline D-cells. Entry slits are available at 2, 4, or 6 mm, and the catch basin has screened ports so live larvae can be held. The manufacturer's trap sells for over $300 under the name Edlite.

Three alternative designs were compared: the high-intensity Mueller Trap (a non-commercial quatrefoil with 10-mm slits, lit by a 7-W lamp), and the prototype Jennings Trap (a floating trefoil with 6 to 8 mm slits). Light intensity was measured with a Science and Mechanics Darkroom Model A-3 photo meter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Larval fish are strongly affected by light, water turbidity, time of day, and trap design. Laboratory tests used larvae reared at 18 °C in 1.2-m green fiberglass tanks (45 cm water depth) under light-excluding black tents. Turbidity was produced with bentonite clay at 20 to 30 and 50 to 75 FTU. The report also cites field work on Lake Mohave, the Green River, and the Tallapoosa River, and discusses how moonlight, zooplankton and macroinvertebrate swarms, odonate nymphs, and water clarity can change catches. Light intensity and phototactic behaviour also vary between species and developmental stages.

## Key Findings

**Capture (clear water, 1-, 4-, and 8-h sets, 50 larvae or 25 juveniles per trial, triplicate):**
- Razorback sucker larvae: mean capture percentages (MCPs) of 13 to 44%. Protolarvae rose from 18% (1 h) to 44% (8 h). Postflexion mesolarvae rose between 1 and 4 h. The 1-h MCP for flexion mesolarvae was 27%.
- Early juvenile razorback sucker: MCP of 47 to 51%, with no difference between 1- and 4-h sets.
- Colorado squawfish larvae: MCPs of 3 to 16%. Bonytail larvae: 5 to 36%, similar to razorback sucker for comparable stages.
- With trap lights off, razorback sucker capture was only 0 to 4%, so light is critical.

**Retention (mean retention percentages, MRPs):**
- Razorback sucker protolarvae: 81 to 93%. Postflexion mesolarvae: 97 to 99%.
- Early juveniles: 65 to 73%, notably lower.
- Colorado squawfish: 95 to 96% for protolarvae and 98 to 99% for postflexion mesolarvae. Bonytail was not tested for retention.
- With lights off, protolarvae retention fell from 81% to 16%.

**Dusk, dawn, and daylight:**
- Dusk capture was lower than at night but not significantly so (13% vs 18% for protolarvae; 5% vs 13% for postflexion mesolarvae).
- Dawn retention fell to 69% for protolarvae, but postflexion mesolarvae stayed at 99%.
- Recommendation: retrieve traps before dawn, and set them before nightfall only with the expectation of pre-dark captures.

**Turbidity:**
- In 50 to 75 FTU water, razorback sucker larval MCPs were 2.6 to 2.8 times greater than in clear water (flexion mesolarvae 27% to 71%; postflexion mesolarvae 13% to 36%).
- Early juvenile MCP fell from 51% to 15%.

**Light intensity, slit width, and design:**
- A 150-fold increase in intensity (Mueller Trap) did not significantly change postflexion mesolarvae MCP (10% vs 13 to 15%).
- For early juveniles, the high-intensity Jennings configuration gave MCP 8%, versus 19% at low intensity (not significantly different).
- A 4-mm slit trap did not significantly change postflexion mesolarvae capture (11% vs 13%), with retention of 93% vs 99%.
- A 2-mm slit trap caught no early juveniles (27 to 35 mm TL), because they could not enter it. Maximum fish length passing 2-mm slits was estimated to be below 27 mm.
- The Jennings Trefoil was much less effective for early juveniles than the Southern Concepts Trap (8 to 19% vs 51%).

**Field application and conclusions:**
- Traps were deployed in 1993 and 1994 in the middle and lower Green River and the Colorado River inflow to Lake Powell.
- 1993: about 62,000 larval and early juvenile fish were taken in 253 light-trap collections, including 350 razorback sucker larvae. 1994: 1,293 razorback sucker larvae were taken in 284 collections.
- In still backwaters, light trapping was at least as effective as daytime seining and more effective than drift netting.
- The authors conclude the traps are at least moderately effective in clear water for razorback sucker and bonytail larvae, and better for early juveniles. Results are qualitative rather than quantitative. The traps are judged useful as a complementary monitoring tool, and the rear-and-release program may need [[Night-Time Seining]] alongside them.
- Frequent emptying is advised to avoid anoxia and predation, and light sources should last the whole sampling period.
