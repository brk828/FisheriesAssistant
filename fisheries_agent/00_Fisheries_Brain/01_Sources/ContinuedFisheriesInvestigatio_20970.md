# Continued Fisheries Investigation of Tenkiller Reservoir, Oklahoma, During Its First Year of Impoundment (1953)

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #reservoir-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This Oklahoma Fisheries Research Laboratory report (Report No. 33, December 1953) documents the first post-impoundment fish survey of [[Tenkiller Reservoir]] in northeastern Oklahoma. It compares 1953 collections with pre-impoundment work on the [[Illinois River]] (1952) to track early changes in fish populations, growth and angling. Its central conclusions are that native brood stock was sufficient to populate the new lake, that growth rates of most game and pan fishes rose sharply after impoundment, and that hatchery stocking and length and bag limits were unnecessary for most species. The report also flags [[Carp]] as the species most in need of control and assesses whether [[Rainbow Trout]] could survive in the tailwater below the dam.

## Background & Description

Pre-impoundment surveys were conducted in summer 1952 in the Illinois River and its tributaries, largely within the proposed reservoir boundaries. Post-impoundment sampling ran from June 15 to August 1, 1953, with an additional sample on October 3–4, 1953. Its objectives were to:

1. Observe early impoundment effects on the growth and populations of river fishes.
2. Document the early conditions that precede the usual post-impoundment decline in growth and angling.
3. Determine the relative abundance of sport and commercial fishes, adults and young-of-the-year.
4. Assess the suitability of the river below [[Tenkiller Dam]] for trout.

[[Tenkiller Dam]] is an earthfill embankment on the Illinois River about 13 miles upstream from the Arkansas River confluence. At normal pool (elevation 630 ft), the reservoir is 34 miles long, has about 100 miles of shoreline and covers 12,500 acres. Impoundment began July 1, 1952. Most collections were made in the upper third of the lake, from Caney Creek Arm downstream to Pettit Bay, because fish were more abundant there and habitats were diverse.

Collection methods included [[Gill Netting]] (linen and nylon, five mesh sizes), seines, [[Rotenone Sampling]] and hook and line. Rotenone worked well in 1952 but was largely unsuccessful in 1953, when fish were spread over a much larger area. Ages were determined from scales, or from spines in catfishes. Lengths are total lengths in inches, calculated with a nomograph.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reservoir showed strong vertical stratification. A [[Thermocline]] lay at 25–40 ft at the dam, and dissolved oxygen was negligible below these depths. Water was clear (visibility about three feet), with predominantly gravel shorelines and many clear spring-fed tributaries. Duckweed and filamentous algae formed floating mats in a few coves. Average surface temperature was 84.5 °F through the third week of July and rose above 90 °F in the final week.

Water level rose from elevation 627 ft on June 15 to 628 ft, where it held through October 3, 1953. The lake was still filling, so the basin was flooding oak, hickory and sycamore habitat.

Water released from near the reservoir bottom had low oxygen. Surface-to-bottom dissolved oxygen fell from about 8.1 ppm to 0.0 ppm, with a deficiency (below 1.5 ppm) below 35 ft. Releases of up to 50 cfs through the outlet tunnel were aerated, rising to about 10.3 ppm 150 yards downstream. By December 12, 1953, with the reservoir uniformly oxygenated (6.0 ppm) and the fall overturn under way, releases from the hydroelectric penstock lacked this aeration. Downstream oxygen recovered only 2.4 ppm over seven miles.

## Key Findings

**Fish community and abundance (1953)**
- A total of 6,578 game, pan and coarse fishes of 31 species was collected (7 game, 8 pan, 16 coarse), compared with 3,134 fish in 1952 river samples.
- Game fishes made up 50.2% of the 1953 catch, coarse fishes 35.9%, and pan fishes 13.9%. Forage minnows and darters were preserved separately and excluded from age and growth analyses.
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (young-of-the-year in tremendous schools) probably outnumbered all other fishes in the reservoir. [[Golden Redhorse]] and carp were the next most abundant coarse species.
- [[Bluegill]] was the most numerous pan fish, and it made up 52% of pan fish sampled.
- Of the black basses, [[Smallmouth Bass]] was most numerous, but most of those fish were young-of-the-year.

**Black basses**
- Stocking: 75,000 largemouth fingerlings were planted between November 1952 and January 1953. Of 780 [[Largemouth Bass]] collected in summer 1953, 770 were native young-of-the-year, and only 8 might have come from the stocked stock.
- Of 1,584 smallmouth bass taken above the dam in July–August 1953, 1,543 came from the 1953 spring hatch.
- Calculated growth: largemouth bass ~1.75 mm/day, [[Spotted Bass]] ~1.45 mm/day, smallmouth bass ~1.30 mm/day. Largemouth bass were expected to reach the legal length of 10 inches by late October 1953, about five months after hatching.
- Young-of-the-year largemouth bass averaged 8.7 inches on October 3, 1953. Growth exceeded that of all other new Oklahoma impoundments (Table VI).
- Growth increases were attributed to inundated land, nutrients and food, and low fish density relative to resources. Growth rates were expected to begin declining around the third year.

**Crappies**
- Adult [[White Crappie]] were numerous, but young-of-the-year were scarce in seine samples. Young-of-the-year averaged 5.0 inches on October 3, 1953, versus 5.5 inches on October 17, 1952. Yearlings averaged 11.0 inches.
- [[Black Crappie]] young-of-the-year (n = 35) were seined, compared with 14 white crappie. Regression-based projection gave about 8.0 inches by October, above the species average reported by Carlander (1950).
- The authors predicted that black crappie would make up no more than 15% of the crappie population. They made up 16% of crappies sampled.

**Channel catfish and white bass**
- [[Channel Catfish]] totals were nearly equal (324 in 1953 vs. 322 in 1952). No young-of-the-year were taken. Growth rates were higher in the reservoir than below the dam and in other Oklahoma impoundments.
- Channel catfish reached the 10-inch legal length in their second summer, so the size limit was judged to serve no useful purpose.
- [[White Bass]] appeared above the dam for the first time in 1953, most likely from transplants by local anglers (13 young-of-the-year averaging 5.9 inches; 2 yearlings averaging 11.1 inches).

**Pan fishes and bullheads**
- [[Redear Sunfish]] growth was rapid, averaging 5.7 inches by October 1953 (earlier collections had not reached this length until their third year).
- [[Rock Bass]] was rare, with only 10 specimens collected in the lake.
- [[Black Bullhead]] showed two distinct growth patterns for fish of the same age. Yellow bullheads declined and were expected to contribute little to the sport catch.

**Carp**
- Carp increased from 19 fish in 1952 to 437 in 1953, 372 of which were young-of-the-year. Their rank among coarse species rose from 12th in 1952 to 2nd in 1953.
- Young-of-the-year grew about 2.51 mm/day and averaged 14.5 inches in October; the largest measured 16.7 inches. The authors described this as surpassing any known growth of the species in Oklahoma.
- The report judged carp the only coarse species abundant enough to warrant control soon.

**Trout below the dam**
- A total of 5,000 [[Rainbow Trout]] was stocked in winter 1952–1953. Water temperatures from 53.5 to 77 °F and dissolved oxygen of at least 8.6 ppm suggested suitability for the seven-mile stretch to Gore, Oklahoma.
- The authors concluded that dissolved oxygen, not temperature or minimum flow, might limit trout survival below the dam. Natural reproduction was considered unlikely.

**Management recommendations**
- No fish of any species should be stocked.
- Commercial carp control should begin immediately.
- Length limits on all fishes and bag limits on all except black basses should be removed, in order to encourage maximum harvest.
- Continuous study was recommended. A Dingell-Johnson federal aid project began in September 1953 under Henri J. Crawley, including trapping, creel and commercial census and tagging.

**Predictions tested in 1953**
- Of 18 predictions made from the 1952 survey, the report judged growth of basses, bluegill growth, the spread of white bass and the abundance of gizzard shad consistent with or exceeding expectations. Rock bass were rare, as predicted. Carpsucker and yellow bullhead trends were broadly consistent. Carp increased more than expected.
