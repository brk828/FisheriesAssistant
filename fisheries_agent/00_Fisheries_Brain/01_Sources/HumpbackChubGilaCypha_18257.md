# Humpback Chub (Gila cypha)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is an endangered cyprinid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], first described by Miller in 1946. At the time of this report, no population estimate was possible, reflecting the extreme rarity and difficulty of studying this species. Its decline is attributed primarily to anthropogenic habitat alteration from dam construction and subsequent hybridization with congeneric species, representing a case of genetic swamping threatening species integrity. Conservation recommendations centered on dam removal and habitat protection, though these ranged from aspirational (maximum) to more feasible (minimum) measures.

## Background & Description
- Named for its distinctive humped back (nuchal hump), an adaptation associated with life in turbulent, high-velocity river currents.
- Taxonomically related to and capable of hybridizing with other *Gila* species, including [[Gila Robusta Robusta]] (Roundtail Chub) and [[Gila Elegans]] (Bonytail Chub).
- Precise taxonomic boundaries between *G. cypha* and related *Gila* taxa were noted as unresolved at time of writing, with calls for continued taxonomic study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Sporadically distributed in the [[Grand Canyon]], [[Lake Powell]], the Colorado River mainstream above Lake Powell, and the [[Lower Yampa River]].
- **Historical distribution**: Unknown, complicating baseline assessments of range contraction.
- Endemic to swift-water canyon reaches of the [[Colorado River]] system, suggesting specialized adaptation to lotic (flowing water), high-turbidity environments.
- Coexists (and increasingly hybridizes) with other native *Gila* species in altered post-dam habitats.

## Key Findings
1. **No viable population estimate** could be determined at the time of this report, underscoring severe data deficiency for this species.
2. **Primary threat**: Habitat alteration due to dam construction on the mainstem Colorado River and major tributaries, which fundamentally changed the lotic conditions this species depends upon.
3. **Secondary threat**: Competition with introduced (non-native) fish species.
4. **Critical threat - Genetic swamping**: Closure of the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] triggered massive hybridization between *G. cypha*, *[[Gila Robusta Robusta]]*, and *[[Gila Elegans]]*, likely a major driver of species decline (Minckley and Rinne, 1970).
5. **Protective measures taken (pre-1974)**: Limited to preliminary taxonomic and life-history studies only — no direct habitat protections had yet been implemented.
6. **Maximum recommended measures (1974-1978)** included aggressive dam removal timeline:
   - [[Glen Canyon Dam]] removal by 1974
   - [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] removal by 1974
   - [[Boulder Dam]] removal by 1976
   - [[Davis Dam]] removal by 1976
   - [[Parker Dam]] removal by 1978
   - Moratorium on new dam construction, especially on the Lower Yampa
   - Continued taxonomic, population, habitat, and life-history research
   - Full federal and state legal protection
7. **Minimum recommended measures (1974-1978)** scaled back to:
   - Removal of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] only
   - Moratorium on new dam construction
   - Continued life-history research
   - Full federal and state legal protection
8. The stark contrast between "maximum" and "minimum" management scenarios highlights the tension between ecological restoration ideals and political/economic feasibility in large-scale river management.
