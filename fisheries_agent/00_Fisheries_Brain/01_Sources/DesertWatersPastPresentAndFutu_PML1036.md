# Desert Waters: Past, Present, and Future

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This issue of *The Nature Conservancy News* (Vol. 34, No. 5, September/October 1984) focuses on the aquatic ecosystems of North America's deserts. Its lead feature by [[Dean A. Hendrickson]] and Dennis M. Kubly argues that desert springs, pools, playas, and rivers are rare, geologically ancient, and highly endemic, yet are disappearing under groundwater pumping, dams, and land alteration. The issue also reports on the Conservancy's protection of [[Ash Meadows]] (Nevada) and [[Thousand Palms Oasis]] (California), a hydrology analysis of Ash Meadows, and a set of new preserve acquisitions across the United States. The president's letter frames the work as part of the [[National Wetlands Conservation Project]], a five-year effort funded in part by the [[Richard King Mellon Foundation]].

## Background & Description

The issue is a periodical rather than a research report. Its core content is four feature articles:

- **"Desert Waters: Past, Present, and Future"** (Hendrickson and Kubly): a geological and human history of desert aquatic systems.
- **"Bright Future for a Desert Refugium"** (Anne Schwartz): the story of the Ash Meadows acquisition.
- **"Coachella Valley's Thousand Palms"** (James W. Cornett): the palm oasis, its fauna, and the endangered fringe-toed lizard.
- **"The Hydrology of Ash Meadows"** (William W. Dudley Jr., U.S. Geological Survey): a groundwater analysis in the Ecology Forum.

The remaining content includes the President's Letter, New Projects (about 20 preserve acquisitions across roughly 12 states), Oak Leaf and Stewardship Awards, new life members and corporate associates, the Goodhill Foundation grant announcement, a planned-giving column, field course listings, and a national office directory. These are administrative and promotional material and are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Desert adaptation and aquatic refuges.** Desert animals such as the [[Desert Tortoise]] and the kangaroo rat survive drought through water storage or recycling. For many species, however, survival depends on the few sites where groundwater reaches the surface. According to the President's Letter, about 74 percent of federally listed threatened or endangered fish species survive in desert springs and pools. Of 31 fishes listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] in 1981, 23 are desert species, and the desert aquatic systems they depend on make up a very small share of the country's total aquatic resources.

**Geological history.** Western North American fauna has been evolving in isolation from eastern fauna since about 100 million years ago, when a seaway covered the western continent. Basin-and-range topography began forming 30 to 15 million years ago. Uplift of the Sierra Nevada and coastal ranges 10 to less than 5 million years ago blocked Pacific moisture and created regional deserts. Pleistocene [[Pleistocene Ice Ages]] wetter periods filled hundreds of interior valleys with lakes. The largest, [[Lake Manly]], was about 90 miles long and over 600 feet deep. As the climate dried, many lakes became playas, and aquatic habitats shrank to isolated "islands" in a sea of desert.

**Playas and closed-basin lakes.** Playas are abundant in the Great Basin and Mexican plateau regions. The Llano Estacado alone has between 20,000 and 30,000 dry lakes. Runoff from thunderstorms refills them briefly, hatching drought-resistant eggs of fairy shrimp, clam shrimp, tadpole shrimp, and water fleas. Playas also support large numbers of migrating waterfowl and shorebirds. Perennial closed-basin lakes such as [[Mono Lake]] and Great Salt Lake have salinities two to five times that of seawater, which excludes aquatic vertebrates. Their brine shrimp and brine flies nonetheless feed large gull populations. Only a few natural desert lakes, including [[Pyramid Lake]] in Nevada, still support fishes. The cui-ui sucker persists there alone.

**Springs as isolated refugia.** Desert springs are stable because they are fed by large groundwater reservoirs that are little affected by precipitation changes. Their isolation also prevents genetic exchange, which has produced many endemic forms. The springs of Death Valley, Ash Meadows, and the [[Cuatro Cienegas]] basin in Coahuila, Mexico, have distinctive faunas. Cuatro Cienegas contains marshes, mostly thermal springs, constant streams, saline playa lakes, and spring pools. More than half of its native species are found nowhere else, and it has the most spectacular array of endemic snails in the Western Hemisphere.

**Human alteration.** Native peoples altered desert waters long before European contact. The Hohokam built canal systems near what is now Phoenix, Arizona, from about 300 B.C. to 1450 A.D., possibly using nearly all of the Gila River's summer flow. Later irrigation and livestock grazing expanded these effects. From about 1860 onward, watershed alteration, stream diversion, and overgrazing accelerated. An 1892 drought in Arizona, combined with overgrazing, led to the "arroyo cutting" that turned many cienegas and perennial streams into deep, dry gullies. Modern threats include:

- Massive reservoirs on the [[Colorado River]], which has been confined, regulated, and diked, and which for decades did not reach the Gulf of California.
- Eight major dams on the [[Gila River]], along with exotic species, land management, and groundwater pumping.
- Pumping of aquifers that has dried or reduced large springs in the Mojave and Chihuahuan Deserts.
- Development pressure on playas, including off-road vehicle damage and proposals for military, hazardous waste, and nuclear waste sites.

The authors conclude that economic pressure, such as reluctance of eastern taxpayers to fund western water projects, may protect desert aquatic systems as much as water scarcity itself.

## Key Findings

**Ash Meadows, Nevada.** The Conservancy bought 12,613 acres of desert wetland in the Ash Meadows area and transferred most of it to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as a wildlife refuge. The oasis covers about 40,000 acres and gets less than three inches of rain in an average year. Key findings include:

- It has the highest number of endemic species for its size in the continental United States, including nearly two dozen plant and animal species found nowhere else. Seven endemic plants are proposed for federal listing, and four endemic fish species are classified as endangered.
- The area holds about 200 plant species. Its endemic [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) has a single population that fluctuates between 150 and 700 individuals in a limestone cavern pool about 50 feet below the desert floor.
- Ash Meadows has been compared to the Galápagos Islands as a natural evolutionary laboratory. Scientists studying its fish believe their rapid evolution supports punctuated equilibrium.
- Most of its water is "fossil" water, believed to have entered the groundwater system thousands of years ago.
- Development threatened the oasis. Preferred Equities planned a resort called Cal-Vada Lakes, for which the county approved subdivision of 20,000 houses. Land exchange and conservation easement options failed.
- The Conservancy bought the land from Preferred Equities for $5.5 million, with a $1 million loan at 5 percent interest. The Conservancy recovers the cost through fundraising and reinvestment. The $1 million Mellon contribution came through the National Wetlands Conservation Project.

**Devil's Hole and pumping.** Groundwater pumping by Spring Meadows, Inc. in the late 1960s and early 1970s dangerously lowered the water level in Devil's Hole. A 1976 Supreme Court ruling established a minimum allowable water level there, but it did not cover other pupfish, springflows, or pumping away from Ash Meadows. Dudley's USGS study found that:

- Pumping of some wells sharply reduced flow at springs more than a mile away, while pumping at other wells lowered the water level in Devil's Hole more than two miles away.
- The eight major Ash Meadows springs release more than 9,000 gallons per minute. Their chemistry is uniform and matches water from regional aquifer wells. Water temperatures range from 90 to 94°F in the southern and central springs and from 82 to 84°F in the northern springs.
- Ash Meadows' endemic flora and fauna depend on a regional carbonate aquifer that extends about 100 miles north and 60 miles east. Pumping anywhere in that system could eventually lower regional water levels and reduce springflow.

**Thousand Palms Oasis, California.** The Conservancy has acquired Thousand Palms Oasis and surrounding desert, which are now part of the 2,083-acre [[Coachella Valley Preserve]]. Notable findings include:

- The [[Desert Fan Palm]] (*Washingtonia filifera*) is the only native palm in the western United States and reaches up to 70 feet. The Southwest has less than 1,000 acres of palm groves.
- The oasis has 549 trees in three groves. Thousand Palms Grove grew from 116 trees in 1965 to 328 trees. Simone Grove grew from 185 trees in 1965 to 192. Powell Palms, which had 52 trees, lost nearly half after a 1979 flood.
- The oasis supports more than 100 bird species, 25 of which nest there. It is also the only known site of the western harvest mouse among the Colorado Desert oases studied.
- Fire may benefit palms. Adult trees rarely die from fire and produce nearly twice as many fruits after burning.
- The [[Coachella Valley Fringe-Toed Lizard]] (*Uma inornata*) lives only in these dunes. Its specialized adaptations include fringed toes for traction and sand-excluding nostrils, eyelids, and ear flaps. The federal government listed it as threatened in 1980 and California followed in 1981. The Conservancy's goal is a projected 13,000-acre assemblage.

**Other projects.** The Conservancy's National Wetlands Conservation Project, begun in early 1983, aims to protect wetlands worth at least $50 million. The Mellon grant of $15 million is the largest ever made by a private foundation for conservation. Among other recent projects:

- The [[Desert Tortoise Preserve]] in Kern County now covers 1,540 acres and supports about 200 tortoises per square mile.
- The Fern Ridge Preserve in Iowa protects habitat for a snail once thought extinct, with about 25 acres of known habitat.
- Several other preserves protect rare wetland, prairie, and cave habitats in Maryland, Missouri, Minnesota, New York, and elsewhere.

**Program funding.** The Goodhill Foundation issued a final challenge grant to extend the [[National Critical Areas Conservation Program]]. Its first phase, begun in 1980 with a $30-million goal, protected 48 nationally critical sites covering 192,000 acres. The second phase requires the Conservancy to raise $15 million to match the new grant three to one.
