## Executive Summary

This 1957 methods paper by Charles J. Chance (Tennessee Valley Authority) addresses standardized protocols for conducting **fish population surveys** ("standing crop and its composition") in large multipurpose reservoirs, using the **[[Rotenone Sampling Method]]** as the primary case study. The paper does not describe a single species but rather a sampling methodology applied across multiple TVA reservoirs (Wheeler, Norris, South Holston, Watauga, Cherokee, Fort Loudoun, Watts Bar, Douglas). It traces the historical development of rotenone sampling in the Tennessee Valley from 1939–1957, details field procedures (site selection, toxicant application, fish recovery, and data processing), and critically evaluates the limitations of rotenone as a quantitative standing-crop estimator. The author identifies three major sources of error — incomplete fish recovery, fish movement in/out of sample plots, and inadequate sample numbers — and proposes several experimental solutions, including SCUBA observation and mechanical barriers.

## Background & Description

This paper is methodological rather than morphological; no anatomical descriptions of individual species are provided. However, relevant biometric/processing notes include:

- Fish were sorted by species, measured to the **nearest inch** (length), and weighed to the **nearest 0.1 lb** on dairy scales.
- Smaller or numerically dominant species (e.g., young-of-the-year **[[Gizzard Shad]]** and **[[Mississippi Threadfin Shad]]**) were weighed to **0.01 lb** using more sensitive "Exact Weight Speed Production Scales."
- Fish selected for scale (age/growth) samples were measured to **0.1 inch** and weighed to **0.01 lb**.
- Young-of-year shad, due to extreme abundance, were not individually counted but estimated via **subsampling** (e.g., number/size class extrapolated from a 5-lb subsample within a 150-lb total catch).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat context**: TVA reservoirs range enormously in size (600–158,000 acres), encompassing coves, embayments, backwaters, and open/deep-water zones — each posing distinct sampling challenges.
- **Depth constraints**: Deep-water cove sites (45–75 ft) were excluded from sampling as they fall outside the effective depth range of the rotenone method.
- **Thermal/oxygen stratification**: Vertical profiles of dissolved oxygen (via **[[Winkler Method|Alsterberg (Azide) Modification]]**) and temperature (Foxboro electrical resistance thermometer) were used to detect oxygen-depleted density currents or thermoclines that could bias sampling results.
- **Seasonal habitat dynamics**: TVA flood-control operations cause dramatic seasonal reservoir shrinkage — e.g., **[[Norris Reservoir]]** area drops from 34,200 acres (spillway elevation, 1,020 ft) to 12,300 acres (950 ft, mid-October), a ~67% reduction — raising unresolved questions about how water-level fluctuation affects standing-crop estimates and comparability across time.
- **Differential species vulnerability**: Gizzard shad were most frequently observed in "drift kills" moving outside treated sample boundaries, suggesting species-specific mobility/behavior under rotenone stress.
- **Sampling zone placement**: Stations were deliberately located near the main reservoir body (not enclosed backwaters) to allow rotenone dilution via currents, minimizing kill outside the intended sample area.

## Key Findings

- **Historical development**: Rotenone (derris powder) sampling in the Tennessee Valley began in 1939 ([[Tarzwell]] 1940, 1.78-acre pond, Lauderdale County, AL); expanded to reservoir backwaters in 1941 (Wheeler Reservoir, 12 acres total); and to embayments (Norris Reservoir, 1.4 acres, [[Eschmeyer]] 1943).
- **Toxicant standardization**: TVA shifted from derris powder to **5% emulsifiable rotenone** in 1952 (applied at 0.5–1.0 ppm) for greater versatility and ease of handling.
- **Toxicant reliability critically affects yield**: A synergized rotenone formulation used experimentally in 1957 yielded only **42 lbs/acre** versus **240 lbs/acre** with standard 5% emulsifiable rotenone in the same reservoir — underscoring the importance of consistent toxicant formulation.
- **Application technique**: Rotenone-water mixture dispersed via a 5,000-gal/hr Homelite pump through 90 ft of plastic hose (last 40 ft perforated with ⅜-inch holes at 18-inch intervals); differential dosing (heavier on the "open"/lake side) creates a staggered kill rate to simplify recovery logistics.
- **Recovery protocol**: Two-day sampling — Day 1 for bulk recovery, Day 2 for cleanup; recovery performed via dip net (boat-mounted scoops for small fish remain experimental/unsatisfactory).
- **Measurement innovation**: Introduction of **stadia surveying** (1957) replaced visual "guesstimation" of sample area, correcting for previous errors of **25–100%** in area estimates — critical for converting raw catch data into valid per-acre density figures.
- **Yield variability across reservoirs (1957 data)**: Total fish recovered per acre ranged widely, from **38.0 lbs/acre** (Watauga) to **831.0 lbs/acre** (Wheeler, 1-acre sample), reflecting high natural variance and methodological sensitivity.
- **Three core limitations of rotenone as a quantitative tool**:
  1. **Incomplete recovery** of killed fish (supported by mark-recapture literature).
  2. **Fish movement** into/out of sample plots during treatment.
  3. **Insufficient number of samples** per water body, generally due to funding/time constraints rather than method design.
- **Proposed methodological improvements**: Use of SCUBA to directly observe fish behavior at treatment-zone boundaries; weighted cheesecloth squares to estimate unrecovered sunken fish; mechanical barriers to prevent fish movement across sample boundaries.
- **Open questions raised**: Optimal geometry (square vs. rectangular, shoreline-parallel vs. perpendicular) for open-water sample plots remains unresolved; relationship between reservoir seasonal area fluctuation and standing-crop estimate validity is unclear, and may not align with [[Carlander (1955)]]'s finding of no significant correlation between standing crop and water body area.
- **Practical conclusion**: Field biologists operating under routine management constraints likely lack the resources for rigorous quantitative standing-crop studies; such precision-focused work may be better suited to academic or dedicated research institutions.
