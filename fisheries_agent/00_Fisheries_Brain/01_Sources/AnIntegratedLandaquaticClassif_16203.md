# An Integrated Land-Aquatic Classification System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Lotspeich and Platts (1982, *North American Journal of Fisheries Management* 2:138-149) propose a simple, hierarchical land classification that integrates riverine and terrestrial systems into one ecological unit. The authors argue that accurate resource inventories require classification, and that existing schemes fail to link [[Terrestrial Ecosystems]] with [[Aquatic Ecosystems]]. Their framework classifies land by causes (geology and climate, the "geoclimatic" factors) rather than by effects or present uses, and uses the [[First-Order Watershed]] as the basic ecosystem unit. The system is organized into hierarchical levels from [[Domain]] to [[Land Site]], with the [[Land-Type Association]] identified as the level most useful for stream habitat mapping.

## Background & Description

**Policy context.** The paper traces the rising importance of resource inventories in U.S. planning, citing the [[Multiple Use and Sustained Yield Act]] (1960), the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (1969), the Resource Planning Act (1974), and Section 208 of the [[Clean Water Act]] (1972), which extended water-quality concern to all waters and watersheds, not only navigable ones. A 1979 Office of Management and Budget commission involving the USGS, Soil Conservation Service, Forest Service, Fish and Wildlife Service, and Bureau of Land Management sought a land inventory with a classification system. An earlier Department of Commerce land classification (1941), based on economic factors, had little acceptance.

**Design principles.** The authors list eight principles: classification by causes; avoidance of economic, political, and social bias; the first-order watershed as the basic ecosystem, mapped at 1:60,000 to 1:20,000; arrangement by natural attributes rather than uses; a common, easily understood vocabulary; hierarchy; mappability; and expression of interactive controlling factors.

**Key definitions.**
- *Ecosystem*: Tansley's (1935) definition, a system integrating all living and nonliving factors.
- *Natural landscape*: one in which the relationship between biological communities and the physical environment is the focus of inquiry (Hills 1976). Cultural landscapes, by contrast, focus on human communities.
- *Nonpoint source*: diffuse water-quality degradation from erosion, nutrients and pesticides, and acid rain.
- *First-order stream*: "a stream with sufficient continuous flow to support an aquatic biota at all seasons" (Lotspeich 1980).
- *Climate levels*: macroclimate, mesoclimate, and microclimate; microclimate was excluded from the classifying process.

**Literature evaluated.** The authors review Bailey's ecoregion system, the ECOSYM and ECOCLASS approaches (Davis and Henderson 1976), Wertz and Arnold (1972), Brown and Godfrey's genetic landform system (11 orders), and Hunt's geology-based soil and landform work. Their critique is that none of these integrated terrestrial and aquatic systems successfully, mainly because none used the watershed, with water as the controlling element, as the unit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water as integrating agent.** The authors argue that water is the active ecosystem agent, so streams and lakes are treated as products of the watersheds they drain. Biology is treated as a dependent variable, not a cause, which is why biophysical and biogeoclimatic approaches were rejected.

**Tesserae.** Vegetation and associated soil are combined as dependent units (tesserae, after Hills 1974) because four factors (climate, geology, time, topography) shape both. Soil is described as surficial geologic material emphasizing texture, the most important soil property controlling soil-plant relations.

**Time scales.** Geologic structures are measured in millions of years, most soils are no older than the Holocene (about 11,000 years), and plant associations change over tens to hundreds of years.

**Worked examples.**
- *Rogue River, Oregon*: [[Steelhead]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) spawn in small tributaries that are dry in summer, migrating upstream in wet weather, with progeny moving to larger streams before dry weather begins (Everest 1973). This reflects the wet-winter, dry-summer climate.
- *Idaho batholith*: Deeply weathered coarse granitic rock, shaped by glaciation and water, has an overriding influence on watershed form and aquatic communities (Platts 1974, 1979).
- *Caribou-Poker Creeks Research Watershed, Alaska* (Lotspeich and Slaughter 1981): A 10-km² watershed was tested using the [[Equivalent-Latitude Concept]]. South-facing slopes had no frozen ground, while valley bottoms and northern slopes did. The mapped permafrost on the topographic map closely matched the equivalent-latitude contour predictions.

**Fish–land linkage.** The authors contend that fish populations respond both to surrounding lands and to basin hydrology, and that existing fishery inventories describe only one resource and cannot show integration with other uses.

## Key Findings

1. **Classification by causes works better than by effects.** Geology and climate (geoclimatic factors) are treated as ultimate causes. Soils, vegetation, and fauna are treated as interacting dependent variables.

2. **The first-order watershed is the basic ecosystem unit.** It provides clear boundaries, is small enough for systematic study, is the unit cell of the drainage system (Strahler 1964), and is mappable at 1:60,000 to 1:20,000. Stream habitats are identifiable at this scale, letting fishery biologists manage the total land unit, not only flowing water.

3. **The hierarchy has seven proposed levels, from broadest to finest:**
   - *Domain* (about 2,000 km wide; map scale 1:7,500,000): broad arid-to-humid transition (ecotone).
   - *Province* (about 100,000 km²): large physiographic units with homogeneous geology. Based on Bailey's system, but vegetation-based boundaries were judged a violation of the causal principle.
   - *Section* (about 10,000 km²; 1:500,000): a single macroclimatic zone influencing whole landforms. The authors note no usable climate classification yet exists at this level.
   - *Region* (about 1,000 km²; 1:250,000): lithology and structure visible in drainage patterns; useful for reconnaissance.
   - *Land-Type Association* (1–10 km wide; 1:60,000–1:20,000): the level where first-order streams and stream habitats are identified. The authors consider it the most important level for detailed work.
   - *Land Type* (about 10 km²): the building block for land-use evaluation and the most useful level for stream classification. Stream habitats are homogeneous within a type.
   - *Land-Type Phase* and *Land Site*: needed for project and site management.

4. **Equivalent-latitude mapping offers a practical tool.** It equates potential incoming radiation with slope aspect and gradient and was validated in Alaska. The authors suggest it is applicable at scales of 1:60,000 and below, especially where rainfall is uniform.

5. **Terrain intelligence methods are a low-cost option.** Military terrain folios from World War II, prepared by interdisciplinary teams using topographic and geologic maps with minimal field checking, are proposed as a model for inventory and classification.

6. **Management implications.** A land-water classification would let managers predict and reduce stresses on fisheries, extrapolate research results, and transfer fishery management experience among similar areas. The authors recommend field testing of the hypothesis at about 1:24,000 scale, sampling several sites to capture geomorphic, climatic, and riverine variation.

7. **Gap identified.** The authors state that "an adequate land and stream classification system is not yet available," and that the system presented is a proposal requiring field validation.
