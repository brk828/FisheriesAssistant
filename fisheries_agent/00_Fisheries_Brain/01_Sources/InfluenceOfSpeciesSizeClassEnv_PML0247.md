# Introduced Fish Predation on Native Fishes in the Verde River System, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit report (Bonar, Leslie and Velez, January 2004) examines how introduced fishes prey on native fishes in the [[Verde River]], a perennial desert river in the [[Gila River Basin]]. Fieldwork ran monthly from March 2002 to January 2003 at 12 sites across four river sections. The study combined [[Diet Analysis]] of nonnative predators, a [[Wisconsin Bioenergetics Model]] to estimate consumption rates, and removal-based [[Density and Standing Crop]] estimates to calculate total predation impact by species, size class, habitat and season.

The central conclusion is that [[Largemouth Bass]] is the most significant predator of native fishes, with the greatest impact below [[Bartlett Dam]] in Section IV. Smallmouth bass has a localised impact in Section I. The report recommends targeting control at age 1 and 2+ largemouth bass in Section IV and smallmouth bass in Section I, while noting that control must protect the valuable sport fishery.

## Background & Description

Nonnative fishes were introduced across the Western United States for sport, food and biological control, mainly in the early and late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Introduced species now support most western sportfisheries; recreational fishing was worth over 6 billion dollars to 11 Western states in 2001. Introduced fish have also contributed to native fish declines alongside water diversion and habitat loss, and many western natives are listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

The Verde River flows about 300 km from Sullivan Lake to its confluence with the Salt River. Its watershed covers 17,212 km². Historically it supported up to 10 native fish species, five of them federally listed: razorback sucker, Colorado pikeminnow, loach minnow, spikedace and Gila topminnow. The study divided the river into four sections based on human impact:

- **Section I:** Sullivan Dam to Clarkdale; largely free of anthropogenic impacts.
- **Section II:** Clarkdale to Beasley Flats (49 km); most developed, with irrigation diversions and groundwater pumping.
- **Section III:** Beasley Flats to Horseshoe Dam (90 km); designated "Wild and Scenic" in 1984.
- **Section IV:** Below Bartlett Dam to the Salt River (41 km); regulated flows and the largest water volume.

The reach between Horseshoe and Bartlett Dams was not sampled.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Native fish declines in the desert Southwest are attributed to dams, habitat alteration, loss of riparian vegetation and nonnative species. Mechanisms include competition, predation, hybridisation, disease transfer and habitat change. Over 60 species were introduced to Arizona between 1900 and 1970. Within the Verde, native fishes also face competition from [[Red Shiner]] and [[Mosquitofish]], which are numerous but mainly herbivorous or insectivorous.

Population declines were attributed mainly to predation by largemouth bass and other piscivores. Small native species (<80 mm) are especially vulnerable because they fall within the gape width of many predators. Native desert fishes also lack evolved anti-predator defences against introduced predators. Longfin dace and Sonora and desert suckers occupy riffles, while larval [[Razorback Sucker]] use slow pools and backwaters that predators also use.

Seasons were defined as spring (March to May), summer (June to September) and winter (October to January), based on growth rates and water temperature. Native spawning peaks in spring and summer, overlapping with the highest predation.

## Key Findings

**Species richness and distribution**
- Over 30,700 fish were collected: 6 native and 13 nonnative species (19 total, 10 found throughout the river).
- Desert sucker, Sonora sucker and roundtail chub were found throughout the river. [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were only found in Sections II and III, where they were being repatriated. Longfin dace were found only in Section IV.
- Section IV had the most unstocked species (15), followed by Section III (13), Section II (13) and Section I (11).

**Diet of nonnative fishes (Chapter 1)**
- Tilapia, common carp, red shiner, mosquitofish and threadfin shad were primarily herbivores or insectivores (diet <0.5% fish).
- Bluegill, rainbow trout and green sunfish were primarily insectivores with less than 4% fish in their diet.
- Largemouth bass, flathead catfish, channel catfish, smallmouth bass and yellow bullhead had the highest proportions of fish in their diets.
- Largemouth bass had the highest fish proportion overall (16.7%, SE 1.05), including 8.3% native fish (SE 0.80), four times that of any other piscivore.
- Native fish appeared in the diets of largemouth bass, flathead catfish, channel catfish and yellow bullhead only below Bartlett Dam, and in smallmouth bass only in the headwaters.

**Consumption rates (Chapter 2)**
- Largemouth bass had the highest daily ration of fish (5.2 mg/g greater than any other species) and of native fish (2.9 mg/g greater).
- Daily rations were highest in Section IV, where largemouth bass consumption was 12.1 mg/g higher than other species.
- Rainbow trout had the second-highest daily ration of fish, though only 32 were sampled. Its fish diet comprised 3.83% of diet by weight and no native fish were eaten. Stocking of 5,031 rainbow trout in 2002 was estimated to be a potential predation source.
- Daily rations were lowest in winter and highest for all piscivores in spring.

**Density and standing crop (Chapter 3)**
- Nonnative fishes were about 2.6 times denser (95% CI 2.2 to 3.1) than natives per 100 m² and had about 2.8 times the standing crop (95% CI 2.0 to 4.0).
- Native fishes were densest in Sections I and IV; nonnative fishes were densest in Section I.
- Total fish standing crop was similar to other temperate and tropical rivers worldwide.
- Longfin dace declined from about 1,400 individuals in 1994 to 7 recorded in 1999–2003.

**Predation impact (Chapter 4)**
- Predation on native fishes was observed only in Sections I and IV.
- Largemouth bass in pools and runs in Section IV consumed the most native fish: 582.3 mg/100 m²/day in pools (SE 111.7) and 238.7 mg/100 m²/day in runs (SE 52.6).
- Age 1 and 2+ largemouth bass consumed more total prey fish than age 0 fish.
- Smallmouth bass was the only predator consuming native fish in Section I.
- Total predation was highest in summer, when growth and temperature peak.

**Management implications**
- Target control at age 1 and 2+ largemouth bass in Section IV, and at smallmouth bass in Section I.
- Rainbow trout stocking overlaps native spawning; the report recommends reviewing stocking dates and locations, and considers stocking into [[Bartlett Reservoir]], where native densities are lowest, a conservation option.
- Mechanical removal and intensive angling are options, with bounties considered for large catfish and largemouth bass. Adaptive management is suggested, starting with small-section trials.
- Sport fishing generates substantial revenue for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], so control must be balanced against sportfish value.
