# Ecology and Management of Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, a book chapter by Harold M. Tyus, reviews the life cycle, status, and management options for the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest minnow native to North America. The species has been extirpated from most of the lower [[Colorado River]] basin and survives in the upper basin, with its largest population in the [[Green River]] sub-basin. The chapter argues that recovery depends on flow-based habitat management grounded in the species' complex life cycle, cautions against poorly planned habitat "improvements," and calls for a long-term conservation ethic applied at the ecosystem scale.

## Background & Description

The Colorado Squawfish reaches total lengths of 1.8 m and weights of 36 kg (Miller 1961). Local names included "salmon," "white salmon," and "Colorado River salmon," reflecting its size, body shape, springtime coloration, and migratory habits. Native peoples and early settlers used it as food.

Knowledge of the species was historically taxonomic and distributional. Life-history study began in earnest in the 1950s, when studies tied to [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] (under section 8 of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] Act) started. The [[Endangered Species Act]] listed the species as endangered in 1967 and extended protection in 1974.

Interest in the species grew during the 1970s, when environmental concern and water development spurred research on imperiled fishes. In the late 1970s the [[Colorado River Fish Project]] (CRFP), an interagency cooperative effort involving three field research stations, was formed to focus research in the upper basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life cycle and adaptations.** The species co-evolved with a depauperate native fish fauna and is a generalist adapted to large seasonal flow fluctuations, low food bases, and changing riverine conditions. It survived climatic shifts from pluvial to arid conditions by using long-distance migration. Adults are predaceous and long-lived; Seethaler (1978) aged scales to 11 years, while otolith and vertebrae work suggested ages of seven to 30 years for wild Green River adults.

**Spawning and migration.** Adults make extensive migrations between winter and summer habitats. Two major spawning migrations have been documented: to Yampa Canyon (Yampa River, river km 0-32) and to Gray Canyon (Green River, river km 292-256). Adults show fidelity to specific spawning reaches, and the authors suggest an olfactory homing mechanism. Spawning occurs in riffles and shallow runs, with resting-staging areas in pools and eddies nearby.

**Early life stages.** Larvae hatch in 3.5 to 5.0 days and drift downstream. Young-of-year fish occupy shallow, alongshore ephemeral backwaters formed as water levels recede in late summer, which are critical nursery habitat. Juveniles (about 50 to 400 mm TL) move upstream later to repopulate upper reaches, and subadults and adults occupy shorelines.

**Threats.** Losses are attributed mainly to conversion of riverine habitat to impoundments, replacement of warmwater habitat by cold tailwaters, and migration barriers. Other suspected factors include non-native fishes, cumulative water depletion, and pre-impoundment poisoning (a 1962 rotenone treatment of the reservoir area). Predation and competition by introduced species such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Northern Pike]] are discussed, with mixed evidence.

**Hydrology and temperature.** Recruitment in the Green River was linked to late-summer discharge. Recruitment was high in 1979 and 1980 (August-September discharge 44.8 to 53.2 m³/s) and low in 1983 and 1984 (84.9 to 118.9 m³/s). Cold water during larval drift contributed to losses in 1983 but did not explain the 1984 loss.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** The species is extirpated from most of the lower basin. About 254 percent of its former range remains occupied (as stated in the Conclusions), and the largest viable population is in the Green River.
- **Abundance (Green River):** Approximations of the adult population ranged from 3,875 to 17,252, with a grand average of about 9,000 adults. Accuracy is unknown.
- **Abundance (upper Colorado mainstem):** Perhaps fewer than 1,000 adults remain; abundance is roughly an order of magnitude lower than in the Green River.
- **Catch rates (1979-1981, CRFP data):** Green River averaged about 0.23 to 0.28 fish/km for young, juveniles, and adults combined by reach (reported as 22, 22, and 22 per year by life stage in the summarized data), roughly ten times the upper Colorado mainstem values.
- **Age and maturity:** Ripe males averaged 55.5 cm TL and ripe females 55.4 cm TL on spawning grounds (1981-1988 data: 14 ripe females, 194 ripe males). Growth of 58 tagged recaptured adults averaged 1.2 mm per year (as reported).
- **Diet:** Adults are primarily piscivorous but also take crickets, mice, birds, and earthworms. Age-0 fish eat mainly invertebrates.
- **Management priorities:** Under the ESA, the first priority is removing threats to extinction; downlisting and delisting are secondary goals. Habitat management hinges on determining instream flow needs and recognizing that recovery requires protecting existing populations.
- **Flow recommendations:** The Green River flow program uses releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] to maintain backwater nursery habitat. Flow-habitat models (IFIM/PHABSIM) are considered experimental; empirical data were prioritized over model outputs.
- **Stocking:** Stocking is only useful within comprehensive programs in which life requirements are understood. Hatcheries should be treated as temporary genetic refuges, not recovery solutions.
- **Conservation ethic:** The author argues for a long-term conservation ethic and ecosystem-scale management, not "preservation" alone, and notes that other large migratory fishes worldwide face similar declines.
- **Funding context:** USBR had contributed about $5.5 million to upper basin recovery efforts, roughly 0.05 percent of hydroelectric revenue from mainstem dams over the same period (as stated).
