# Fossil Creek Flow Restoration Agreement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-habitat

## Executive Summary

A historic 1999 agreement between [[Arizona Public Service]] (APS) and an environmental partnership led by [[American Rivers]] will close and decommission the Childs-Irving hydropower plants on [[Fossil Creek]], which have diverted over 95% of the creek's flow since 1908. Under the agreement, full flows are to return to the creek bed by December 31, 2004, with restoration work possibly taking another five years. APS accepts responsibility for decommissioning and restoration costs. The article presents the agreement as a means to renew native fish habitat, riparian vegetation, and [[Travertine Formations]] across 14 miles of stream, while noting unresolved questions about the [[Diversion Dam]] and long-term management of the watershed.

## Background & Description

**Agreement and partners.** The agreement was released November 17, 1999, ending 14 months of negotiations between APS and an environmental partnership. Partners named include American Rivers (lead), the Arizona Riparian Council, Friends of Arizona Rivers, the Northern Arizona Audubon Society, the Sierra Club Grand Canyon Chapter, and [[The Nature Conservancy]] Arizona Chapter. The [[Center for Biological Diversity]] also signed. The [[Yavapai-Apache Nation]] is a formal intervenor alongside American Rivers and the Center for Biological Diversity.

**Setting.** The Fossil Creek watershed lies along the [[Verde River]] between Strawberry and Camp Verde. It is mostly national forest land, surrounded by the [[Mazatzal Wilderness]] and [[Fossil Springs Wilderness]]. The creek originates from a cluster of springs that supply up to 50% of the Verde River's water during low-flow periods. The project lies on portions of the [[Coconino National Forest]] and [[Tonto National Forest]].

**Hydropower operation.** Water at the diversion dam is channeled through a wood, steel, and concrete flume to four turbines in two power plants. Output totals 4.2 megawatts, a small share of APS's annual generation. None of the water is consumed; it re-enters the Verde River about three miles upstream of the Fossil Creek confluence.

**History.** The Arizona Power Company, a predecessor of APS, built Childs-Irving in the early 1900s, largely by hand with men and mules, to power mining near Jerome and nearby settlements. By 1920 mining was declining, and the plants supplied Phoenix. During a mid-1920s drought, the Salt River Valley relied heavily on Childs-Irving power. The project was added to the [[National Register of Historic Places]] in 1991 and received American Society of Mechanical Engineers awards for its gravity-fed design. Historical societies in Yavapai and Gila counties are investigating protection of the Childs powerplant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Current condition:** The upper portion of the creek, about 1/2 mile above the diversion dam, has a healthy native fish community and diverse native plants. The 14 miles downstream are dramatically affected by reduced flows.
- **Expected recovery:** Restored flows and rebuilt travertine formations are predicted to recreate historic waterfalls about 15 feet high within ten years, comparable to those at [[Havasu Creek]], as recorded by explorers around 1900.
- **Species:** The article refers generally to native fish, riparian ecosystems, and "many threatened and endangered species." No species are named.
- **Recreation pressure:** Vegetation in the Fossil Springs Wilderness is heavily impacted by recreational use. The article states the [[USDA Forest Service]] is not meeting its own management objectives, and visitation is expected to rise sharply once flows return.
- **Nonnative fish:** The article raises whether the diversion dam sits on a bedrock ledge that naturally blocks upstream migration by [[Nonnative Fish]].

## Key Findings

- **Flow restoration:** Full flows are to return by December 31, 2004, reversing diversion in place since 1908.
- **Restoration scope:** Work may include removing some or all structures, stabilizing disturbed areas, and restoring riparian communities over roughly five additional years. See [[Hydropower Decommissioning]].
- **Cost:** APS estimates removing all project works at nearly $19 million, including over $6 million for the diversion dam alone. APS accepts responsibility for these costs.
- **Diversion dam dispute:** APS wants to keep the diversion dam. The environmental community is waiting on ongoing research to answer two questions: (1) whether the dam sits on a natural bedrock barrier to nonnative fish, and (2) how removal would change the upstream and downstream reaches.
- **Deadline:** A signed final agreement was due by July 1, 2000, to be negotiated among APS, the Forest Service, intervenors, and federal and state agencies. It will address which structures remain, liability, restoration measures, and license arrangements.
- **Regulatory process:** The [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] (FERC) has jurisdiction over non-federal hydropower dams on federal lands. APS's renewal application for a 30-year operating license has been pending since 1991.
- **Energy significance:** The plants generate 4.2 megawatts, under one percent of APS's annual output, and consume no water.
- **Management gap:** The article calls for a long-term management plan between the two national forests to address recreational impacts and protect sensitive habitats.
- **Expected outcome:** Restoration is expected to create prime riparian and aquatic habitat for threatened and endangered species and to restore the travertine waterfalls and pools.
