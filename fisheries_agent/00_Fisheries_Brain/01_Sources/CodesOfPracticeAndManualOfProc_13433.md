# Codes of Practice and Manual of Procedures for Considering Introductions and Transfers of Marine and Freshwater Organisms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #biosecurity

## Executive Summary

This FAO-published manual (EIFAC Occasional Paper No. 23, edited by Gary E. Turner, Rome, 1988) consolidates international guidance on the introduction and transfer of non-indigenous marine and freshwater organisms. It was prepared jointly by the [[International Council for the Exploration of the Sea]] (ICES) Working Group and the [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] (EIFAC) Working Party. It provides a national governance framework, a "universal protocol" covering [[Ecology]], [[Genetics]], [[Inspection and Certification]], [[Quarantine]] and [[Pathology]], and specific protocols for commercial species, research-only imports, [[Salmonids]], [[Molluscs]] and [[Eels]]. Appendices supply a [[Review and Decision Model]], ICES and EIFAC submission procedures, and health certificate templates.

## Background & Description

The document responds to rapidly increasing introductions and transfers linked to aquaculture and fishery enhancement. Concerns about [[Exotic Species]] stem from ecological disruption, disease spread and genetic dilution. The ICES Code of Practice was adopted in 1973 and revised in 1979 (ICES Cooperative Research Report No. 130, 1984). EIFAC endorsed a parallel inland Code at its fourteenth session in Bordeaux (27 May to 3 June 1987). The manual is also published as ICES Document No. F:35A and runs to 44 pages.

The document distinguishes:
- **New introductions**: non-indigenous species not previously established in the receiving area.
- **Current commercial practice**: species moved in large quantities for aquaculture, held in tanks or outdoor systems with access to open waters.
- **Research imports**: organisms held by research institutions, with or without open-water stocking.

A glossary (Appendix V) defines key terms such as [[Broodstock]], [[Gynogenesis]], [[Introgression]] and [[Transferred Species]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors state that no group of biologists, ecologists or geneticists can predict with certainty the outcome of an introduction. An organism well known in its native habitat may behave differently in a new one, and once established a species may be difficult or impossible to eradicate. Escape is treated as an inevitable outcome of most applications, so enclosed systems are assessed with the same risk framework as wild release.

The ecology section requires review of native and receiving-site physical and chemical conditions, diet, reproduction, competition, predation, migration, disease history, potential fisheries pressure and control options. It recommends phased introductions with controlled trial plantings and long-term monitoring against pre-release baseline data.

The genetics section warns that manipulation for aquaculture can narrow the genetic base and remove disease-resistance traits. It cites [[Ricker 1972]] and [[Utter 1981]] on salmonid homing and population structure. It recommends sterile or non-breeding stock where possible, protected reserves, and evaluation of genetically modified organisms (polyploidy, gynogenesis, gene transfer) before release.

Quarantine and pathology guidance is strongly conservative: pathogens may be carried without visible signs, so quarantine may need to last for life, with testing across generations (F0, F1, F2). Intake water should be sterilized or disinfected, and effluents treated before discharge.

## Key Findings

**National framework (Section 2)**
- Countries should adopt legislation and a national committee or working group to review, recommend on and monitor introductions. Regional committees may mirror local environmental conditions.
- Where one country's introduction may affect another, applications go to ICES (marine and anadromous species) or EIFAC (freshwater species) for risk analysis.

**Universal protocol for new introductions (Section 3)**
- Ecology: seven required assessment steps, from physical-chemical comparison through long-term monitoring.
- Genetics: five stages of risk analysis and staged, small-scale introductions so that releases can be halted quickly.
- Inspection and certification: country-level lists of eligible species and parasites, exporter certificates, and qualified inspectors. Proponents must supply sufficient numbers for testing.
- Quarantine: 16 detailed requirements covering disinfection, water treatment, waste disposal, staff and equipment hygiene, and containment design.
- Pathology: import permits must list prescribed diseases. Sampling is designed to give 95% probability of detecting an infected carrier at an assumed incidence. Imports from certified disease-free sources over two years are preferred.

**Commercial species (Section 4)**
- Inspection on arrival, sterilization of packing material and water, periodic pathogen sampling at holding sites, and secure containment with monitoring of range expansion.

**Research imports (Section 5)**
- Open-water use requires the same procedures as Section 3. Strict laboratory confinement needs no feasibility analysis, but effluents connected to open waters must be sterilized.

**Species-specific appendices (Appendix I)**
- *Salmonids*: a proscribed list including VHS, IHN, IPN, BKD (*Renibacterium salmoninarum*), furunculosis (*Aeromonas salmonicida*), enteric redmouth (*Yersinia ruckeri*), whirling disease (*Myxosoma cerebralis*) and PKD. Testing occurs twice yearly over two years, with specified sample sizes (for example, 90 O+ fish for virus examination in early summer and 150 O+ fish in early winter). Ova may be disinfected with iodophor at 100 ppm active iodine for 10 minutes. SPF farms are classified into Categories I and II, with wild-sourced stock subject to life-long quarantine.
- *Molluscs*: a list of notifiable diseases (iridovirosis, marteiliosis, bonamiosis, haplosporidiosis, *Perkinsus* sp.). Sites with these diseases in the previous four years are excluded. Inspection is four times per year. A sample size of 100 is recommended, detecting disease at about 1% prevalence with 95% confidence.
- *Eels*: an inventory of transfers and a list of proscribed agents. Elvers are quarantined for six weeks, with a 14-day test-fish exposure using juvenile salmonids. The document notes that a French study found viruses in 10 of 23 elver samples, and it therefore considers the risk "very high" for elvers from such areas.

**Review and Decision Model (Appendix II)**
- Based on Kohler and Stanley (1984), with five review levels and decision boxes. Experts score questions on an "opinionnaire" scale. Proposals are approved, rejected or passed to more detailed review, including a literature-based species synopsis and targeted research.

**Procedures and templates (Appendices III, IV)**
- ICES route: proposer, delegate, ICES General Secretary, Working Group, then back to ICES. Annual reports are required after approval.
- EIFAC route: proposer, National Correspondent, Secretariat, Working Group, task force if needed, Sub-Commission, then plenary endorsement.
- Appendix IV provides templates for a Certificate of Health and an Exporter's Declaration.
