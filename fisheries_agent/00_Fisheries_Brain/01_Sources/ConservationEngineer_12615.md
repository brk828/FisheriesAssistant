# Wildlife Views, September-October 1970: Arizona Game & Fish Issue

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This bi-monthly issue of [[Wildlife Views]], published by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], is a mixed-content magazine rather than a single research report. Its most substantive scientific material is the third installment of the "Native Arizona Fishes" series by [[John N. Rinne]] and [[W. L. Minckley]], which reviews the six *Gila* chub species of Arizona and documents range contractions and hybridization. Other articles cover the conservation engineer's role in wildlife protection (by [[Bob Koons]]), stinging insects, placer gold prospecting, an alphabet-abbreviation quiz, and short notes on desert agriculture, air pollution, and reader correspondence.

## Background & Description

The issue (Vol. 17, No. 5) is funded by hunting and fishing license sales and a federal excise tax on ammunition, firearms and fishing tackle, not state tax money. Subscriptions were free to Arizona addresses and $1 for out-of-state readers, with non-resident license numbers accepted in lieu of payment.

The chub article describes the "chub" name as applied to thick-bodied, dark-colored minnows of the family Cyprinidae. In Arizona the term mainly refers to the genus *Gila*, which makes up almost half of the state's native minnows. Six *Gila* species occur in the state: two mostly Mexican species, three widely ranging species, and one swift-water form.

Species described include:

- **[[Yaqui Chub]]** (*Gila purpurea*): enters the US only in the extreme headwaters of the Rio Yaqui on the San Bernardino Ranch, east of Douglas, Cochise County. Rarely exceeds 6–7 inches in Arizona. Now restricted to a single spring, where it lives with Sonora topminnows (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*). A pre-1900 record from Morse Canyon remains unverified.
- **[[Sonora Chub]]** (*Gila ditaenia*): known to science only since 1945; lives in Sycamore (Bear) Canyon west of Nogales, Santa Cruz County. Fine-scaled, dark olive, with a whitish belly and two lateral bands.
- **[[Gila Chub]]** (*Gila intermedia*): restricted to the [[Gila River]] system of southern Arizona, most abundant in pools of small creeks, swampy areas and artificial ponds. Absent from many former localities; reddens on fin bases and jowls during spring spawning.
- **[[Colorado River Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*): the most widespread and abundant Arizona chub, with three subspecies. Grows to about 20 inches and over 1.5 pounds. Local names include "Verde trout," "River trout" and "Gila trout."
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*): streamlined, with a depressed skull grading to a smooth dorsal hump. Formerly occurred throughout the larger rivers of the lower Colorado basin; now restricted to the Colorado mainstream.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): described as one of North America's most unusual fishes. Has an abrupt dorsal hump, a shorter caudal peduncle, an inferior horizontal mouth, and often greatly reduced scalation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Chubs are principally carnivorous as adults, eating aquatic invertebrates and sometimes other fishes. Smaller individuals feed mainly on algae. Adults may compete with game species, but the authors note this has not been specifically studied in Arizona.

The authors report that changes to southern Arizona's aquatic habitats over the past century have harmed the Gila chub. Swift-water species depend on the turbulent, silt-laden Colorado mainstream. The authors report evidence of "massive hybridization" among *G. elegans*, *G. cypha* and *G. robusta* near [[Glen Canyon Dam]] at Page, Arizona, over the preceding few years, and ask that specimens from that area be preserved or frozen for university analysis.

The chub article also notes that anglers take larger chubs, and that the fish provide sport in the Phoenix canal system. Chubs were eaten by Arizona Indians in the past, though their bones make them unpopular today. The authors argue that their greatest value is scientific: their relationships may reveal prehistoric drainage connections in the now-arid Southwest.

Other ecological content in the issue includes:

- **Engineering and conservation:** [[Bob Koons]], a civil engineer with the Department, argues that highway construction is a serious and accelerating wildlife threat, and that population growth will push wildlife habitat aside for industry, housing and agriculture. He describes the [[Association of Conservation Engineers]] as a bridge between engineers and wildlife professionals.
- **Stinging insects:** Part IV covers [[Southern Fire Ant]], [[Harvester Ant]], [[Velvet Ant]] and [[Honey Bee]] stings. Recommended first aid includes bathing stings in household bluing, ice, or wet mud for bee stings, and scraping out (not pulling) the honeybee stinger.
- **Placer mining:** [[Gold Panning]] and dry placer methods are described, including the claim that Rich Hill near Yarnell yielded $40,000 from a single acre in early days.
- **Desert agriculture:** a University of Arizona team led by Carl N. Hodges is developing a system that uses generator waste heat to desalinate seawater for greenhouse vegetables, with a pilot facility at Puerto Peñasco, Sonora.
- **Air pollution:** the issue cites more than 100 million tons of pollutants emitted by automobiles each year.

## Key Findings

- The *Gila* genus includes six Arizona species, with the Yaqui chub and Sonora chub restricted to the southern edge of the state and the Gila chub, Colorado River chub, bonytail and humpback chub in the Gila and Colorado systems.
- The Yaqui chub survives in a single spring; the Sonora chub is known from one canyon; the Gila chub has disappeared from many former localities.
- The bonytail has been reduced to the Colorado mainstream, and hybridization among three swift-water *Gila* forms is documented near Glen Canyon Dam.
- Chubs are carnivorous adults, feeding on invertebrates and occasionally fish, while juveniles eat algae.
- The authors emphasize the scientific value of chubs for reconstructing prehistoric Southwest drainages.
- Bob Koons argues that conservation engineers should be involved early in highway route selection and that engineering and ecology professions need greater cooperation.
- The issue's other content includes stinging insect first aid, gold prospecting guidance, a 30-item abbreviation quiz with answers on page 27, a National Wildlife Federation resolution opposing channel dredging on the Columbia River, and a reader letter noting a 66-pound flathead catfish record in Oklahoma compared with Arizona's 65-pound record from 1951.
