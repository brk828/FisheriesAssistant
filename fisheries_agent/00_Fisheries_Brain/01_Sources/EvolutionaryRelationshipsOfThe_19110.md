# Evolutionary Relationships of the Minnow Tribe Plagopterini (Teleostei: Cyprinidae) from Cytochrome b Sequences

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #Plagopterini #molecular-systematics #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This molecular phylogenetic study examined the evolutionary relationships of the tribe [[Plagopterini]], a group of spiny-rayed cyprinid fishes endemic to southwestern [[North America]]. Using [[Cytochrome b]] gene sequences, the research confirmed that plagopterins ([[Lepidomeda]], [[Meda]], and [[Plagopterus]]) form a unique monophyletic lineage most closely affiliated with eastern North American creek chubs ([[Couesius]], [[Margariscus]], [[Semotilus]]). The study revealed that [[Gila copei]] is nested within [[Lepidomeda]], rendering the genus paraphyletic and requiring taxonomic revision. Molecular clock estimates indicate plagopterins are an ancient lineage (at least 24 million years old), with speciation events correlated to formation of the [[Colorado Plateau]] and adjacent [[Basin-and-Range Province]].

## Background & Description

The tribe [[Plagopterini]] represents one of the most clearly defined groups of North American cyprinids, characterized by ossification of the first two rays of dorsal and pelvic fins into spine-like structures. As originally defined by Miller and Hubbs (1960), the group consists of six species in three genera: a polytypic [[Lepidomeda]] (four species, one extinct and one with two subspecies), monotypic [[Meda]], and monotypic [[Plagopterus]], all endemic to the middle and lower [[Colorado River]] drainage.

Previous morphological analyses by Coburn and Cavender (1992) placed plagopterins within the Gila clade of western minnows, most closely related to [[Agosia]], [[Moapa]], and [[Rhinichthys]]. However, molecular studies using 12S ribosomal DNA by Simons and Mayden (1997) suggested closer affinity to the "creek chub clade" rather than to members of genus [[Gila]]. The relationship of [[Gila copei]] (originally placed in monotypic genus [[Snyderichthys]]) to plagopterins has been particularly controversial, with morphological, allozyme, and mtDNA studies suggesting close affiliation.

The study examined representatives of all extant plagopterin taxa plus multiple populations of "[[Gila copei]]" from the [[Bonneville Basin]] and [[Snake River]] drainage. Outgroup taxa included representatives of the three major North American cyprinid clades (chub, shiner, and western), with [[Abramis brama]] and [[Notemigonus crysoleucas]] as leuciscin outgroups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Plagopterin fishes occupy freshwater habitats across a geologically complex region of southwestern [[North America]]. Their current distributions are highly restricted, with some taxa found in only single localities (e.g., [[Lepidomeda albivallis]], L. mollispinis pratensis) or single drainages. The species inhabit various river systems including:

- [[Lepidomeda mollispinis]] subspecies in the [[Virgin River]] drainage
- [[Lepidomeda albivallis]] in the pluvial [[White River]] (Pleistocene-Holocene tributary to Virgin River)
- [[Lepidomeda vittata]] in the [[Little Colorado River]] basin
- [[Lepidomeda copei]] in upper [[Snake River]] and [[Bear River]]
- [[Meda fulgida]] populations in [[Verde River]] and [[Gila River]]
- [[Plagopterus argentissimus]] in [[Virgin River]] drainage

The geographic distribution and phylogenetic relationships of these fishes reflect the complex hydrographic history of the region, including formation of the [[Colorado Plateau]], extensional faulting creating the [[Basin-and-Range Province]], and Pleistocene lake systems like [[Lake Bonneville]].

## Key Findings

**Phylogenetic Relationships:**

1. **Monophyly of Plagopterini**: The tribe [[Plagopterini]] (including "[[Gila copei]]") forms a strongly supported monophyletic group (76-85% bootstrap support across analyses).

2. **Sister Group Relationships**: Plagopterins appear most closely affiliated with creek chubs ([[Couesius]], [[Margariscus]], [[Semotilus]]) rather than with western minnows of genus [[Gila]]. However, these relationships were not strongly supported by bootstrap analysis (<50% in most analyses).

3. **Major Cyprinid Clades**: The western clade (including [[Moapa]]) was strongly supported (96-100% bootstrap). [[Agosia]] and [[Rhinichthys]], previously considered western taxa, were most closely affiliated with chubs and shiners.

**Internal Plagopterin Relationships:**

4. **Southern Clade**: [[Meda]] and [[Plagopterus]] form a well-supported sister group (99% bootstrap), representing the southern lineage.

5. **Northern Clade**: All [[Lepidomeda]] and "[[Gila copei]]" form a northern lineage (96% bootstrap) with four distinct groups:
   - L. mollispinis subspecies and L. albivallis form an unresolved trichotomy (100% bootstrap)
   - Snake River "G." copei is sister to the L. mollispinis group (99% bootstrap)
   - L. vittata is sister to this combined clade (61% bootstrap)
   - Bonneville basin "G." copei is the most basal lineage

6. **Paraphyly of Gila copei**: Snake River and Bonneville basin populations of "[[Gila copei]]" are not sister taxa. Snake River populations nest within [[Lepidomeda]], rendering both genera paraphyletic under current taxonomy.

**Taxonomic Implications:**

7. **Required Taxonomic Changes**: "[[Gila copei]]" should be transferred to genus [[Lepidomeda]]. Two species should be recognized:
   - L. copei of upper [[Snake River]] and [[Bear River]] (type locality "tributary of Bear River at Evanston, Wyoming")
   - An undescribed Lepidomeda sp. from the [[Bonneville Basin]]

**Divergence Times and Biogeography:**

8. **Ancient Lineage**: Plagopterins are an ancient lineage, with the [[Meda]] + [[Plagopterus]] and [[Lepidomeda]] clades diverging 24-41 million years ago (Ma), corresponding with initial formation of the southern [[Colorado Plateau]] margin (22-25 Ma) and beginning of extensional faulting forming the [[Basin-and-Range Province]] (>20 Ma).

9. **Meda-Plagopterus Split**: Divergence of [[Meda]] and [[Plagopterus]] occurred 28-35 Ma, possibly when [[Gila River]] and [[Colorado River]] paleorivers were isolated or in closed basins south of the Plateau.

10. **Meda Population Structure**: [[Verde River]] and upper [[Gila River]] populations of [[Meda fulgida]] diverged 1.2-3.3 Ma, attributed to habitat isolation or endorheic drainage basins.

11. **Northern Lepidomeda Divergences**:
    - Bonneville Lepidomeda sp. diverged from common ancestor 11.2-12.4 Ma
    - L. vittata diverged 8.5-9.7 Ma
    - Both likely related to [[Colorado Plateau]] uplift and [[Basin-and-Range]] extension

12. **Bonneville Basin Complexity**: Within Bonneville Lepidomeda sp., southern (Sevier sub-basin) and northern populations diverged 0.7-0.8 Ma, reflecting complex basin history.

13. **Virgin River Trichotomy**: L. m. mollispinis, L. m. pratensis, and L. albivallis form an unresolved trichotomy with divergence times of 0.0-1.4 Ma.

14. **Snake-Bear River Connection**: L. copei from middle [[Snake River]] and [[Bear River]] diverged 0.1-0.4 Ma, before Bear River's most recent connection to [[Bonneville Basin]] (~34,000 years ago).

**Biogeographic Hypotheses:**

15. **North-South Drainage Connection**: The sister relationship (1.6-8.0 Ma) between Snake River L. copei and Virgin River [[Lepidomeda]] taxa suggests a hypothesized south-flowing drainage connection east of the [[Bonneville Basin]], following structural alignments of basin-and-range topography. This connection would have existed before the upper [[Snake River]] formed its connection to the [[Columbia River]] system (~5.0-6.0 Ma).

16. **Vicariance and Tectonism**: Temporal relationships demonstrate the importance of tectonism and reliction in speciation of western fishes. More recently diverged forms (L. m. mollispinis, L. m. pratensis, L. albivallis) likely resulted from disruption of water connections through recent aridification of the region.

**Methodological Notes:**

Sample sizes: Single individuals represented most taxa due to previous studies showing limited intraspecific divergence. Multiple populations sampled for [[Meda fulgida]] (Verde and Gila rivers) and "[[Gila copei]]" (Bonneville basin and Snake-Bear River drainage). Cytochrome b sequences obtained through PCR amplification and dideoxy or cycle sequencing. Phylogenetic analyses included parsimony (unweighted and weighted 2:1 transversions:transitions) and neighbor-joining with gamma-corrected Tamura-Nei distances (alpha=0.3). Bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates assessed node support. Divergence rate: 0.5% per million years for cytochrome b in North American cyprinids.
