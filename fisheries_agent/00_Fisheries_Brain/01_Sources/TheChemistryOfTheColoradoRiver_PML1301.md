# Chemistry of Colorado River Tributaries in the Marble-Grand Canyon System

#freshwater-chemistry #limnology #hydrology #grand-canyon

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Dennis M. Kubly]] and [[Gerald A. Cole]] (presented at the First Conference on Scientific Research in the National Parks, New Orleans, 1976) characterizes the major ionic chemistry of tributaries entering the [[Colorado River]] between [[Lee's Ferry]] and [[Lake Mead]], in the [[Grand Canyon]] region of Arizona. The authors group the tributaries by water type, relate each type to the geology of its source aquifer, and assess how much each stream affects the chemistry and discharge of the mainstem. Sampling covers 1975–76. The main conclusion is that most tributaries are chemically dilute and dilute the river, while a few saline or highly mineralized sources, especially [[Blue Spring]] and [[Pumpkin Spring]], are the exceptions.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The [[Colorado River]] basin has a drainage area of roughly 243,000 units (as given in the scan). The river flows through the Marble and Grand Canyons, dropping about 7.9 feet per mile over the roughly 280-mile reach from Lee's Ferry to Lake Mead, where [[Lake Powell]] now lies upstream.
- **Regulation:** Lee's Ferry is a post-dam site. The authors note that water chemistry there is shaped by the dam, so the pre-dam river is not directly comparable.
- **Mainstem chemistry:** The river is a calcium-bicarbonate type, with sulfate and chloride forming minor parts of the ionic load. The paper states that the mainstem is not a single fixed composition, because tributary inflows change its chemistry along the reach.
- **Tributary types:** Tributaries are classified by water chemistry (see [[Ionic Composition]]) and by the bedrock of their source aquifers:
  - [[Dilute Dolomitic Waters]]
  - [[Impure Dolomitic Waters]]
  - [[Sodium Bicarbonate Waters]]
  - [[Sulfate Waters]]
  - [[Saline Sodium Chloride Waters]]
- **Stream types:** The authors distinguish two stream types. Long, low-gradient streams carry heavy sediment loads and boulders in floods. Short, steep streams, formed by headward erosion, carry clear water but move large boulders to their mouths during floods, which produces the rapids common in this section.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Discharge.** Between Lee's Ferry and Lake Mead, the combined average flow from tributaries probably does not exceed 750 cfs, which is less than 10% of the mean modern discharge of the river at Lee's Ferry. Table 1 lists discharge for about 18 tributaries, with gaps in the records. Some values are based on sporadic [[U.S. Geological Survey]] records. The authors warn that streams of the first type (long and sediment-laden) show a high degree of variability in flow compared with the second type.

**Groundwater sources.** Most tributaries are fed by the [[Karst Groundwater]] system of the [[Kaibab Plateau]] and the [[Paleozoic]] limestones and sandstones of the canyon walls. Key aquifers are the [[Redwall Limestone]], the [[Muav Limestone]], and the [[Coconino Sandstone]] (with the [[Supai Formation]] above it). Bedrock lithology largely determines each stream's chemistry.

**Chemical dilution.** Most tributaries are dilute relative to the mainstem, so they tend to ameliorate the salinity contributed by other sources. [[Total Dissolved Solids]] (TDS) of most influent streams are lower than the mainstem average, as shown in Figure 2. Exceptions are the saline springs, which raise TDS downstream.

**Turbidity.** The Little Colorado River introduces heavy turbidity that the authors say persists into Lake Mead. It reduces light transmittance and is not noticeably alleviated by the clear tributaries downstream.

**Sediment.** High flood flows from the Paria and other sediment-laden streams deliver suspended sediment that mixes into the Colorado more than five miles downstream.

## Key Findings

### Dilute Dolomitic Waters

- At least eight tributaries are calcium-magnesium-bicarbonate streams that emerge from [[Karst Groundwater]] systems north and east of the mainstem (emerging between about Mile 32 and Mile 96.2).
- Included are Vasey's Paradise, Redwall Moss Springs, Clear Creek, Bright Angel Creek, Shinumo Creek, Stone Creek, Tapeats Creek, and Deer Creek.
- Vasey's Paradise and Redwall Moss Springs are unusual because they emerge from the Redwall Limestone that forms the canyon walls.
- These waters are the most dilute in the [[Marble-Grand Canyon]] system. They respond rapidly to high precipitation and snowmelt, which suggests a link between concentration and residence time. Hunton (1974) noted, however, that a given water parcel may take years to travel through the aquifer because of storage in the large Paleozoic aquifers.
- They have the highest Ca/Na, CO3/Cl, and CO3/SO4 ratios and the lowest monovalent-to-divalent cation ratios in the system. Their silica content is at the bottom of the list.

### Impure Dolomitic Waters

- Havasu Creek, Lava Warm Spring, Three Springs, Spencer Creek, and Emory Falls enter from the south rim. They are higher in dissolved constituents than dilute dolomitic waters, with magnesium exceeding calcium and more sulfate, chloride, and sodium.
- [[Havasu Creek]] is one of the largest water sources on the south rim. It arises from seeps called Havasu Spring, about 10–12 miles up a narrow canyon in the Supai Formation.
- At the source, the Ca/Mg ratio is about 1.59 (by Cole's use of Hem's data), and collected source water had a ratio slightly above 2.0. At the creek mouth the average Ca/Mg ratio falls to 0.65, because significant calcite precipitates out. The water remains supersaturated with CaCO3 at the mouth.
- The CO3 ratio also falls from 6.8 at the spring to 4.2 at the junction with the Colorado. Havasu Creek has a high silica content (about 14.5 mg/L), which may come from the Supai sandstone.
- [[Spencer Creek]] drains part of the Muav Limestone. [[Lava Warm Spring]] arises from the upper Muav near Prospect Valley, just above the mainstem level.

### Sodium Bicarbonate Waters

- [[Diamond Creek]] and [[Travertine Falls]] are rare in Arizona. Their sodium-bicarbonate character is unexpected, because the other waters from the Muav are calcium-dominated.
- Diamond Spring, the main source of Diamond Creek, is actually an impure dolomite type. The authors attribute the shift (Ca/Mg falling from 1.44 to 0.53) to calcium precipitation after CO2 loss, warming, evaporation, possible inflow of saline Tapeats Sandstone water, and ion exchange in alluvium.
- Diamond Creek carries the highest SiO2 concentration in the system. Both streams show phenolphthalein alkalinity and are strongly supersaturated with calcite.

### Sulfate Waters

- Major sulfate contributors include the [[Paria River]], [[Kanab Creek]], and [[Elves Chasm]]. The Paria and Kanab are the two largest drainage systems in the region.
- The Paria and Kanab arise on the high plateaus of southern Utah. Their sulfate derives from gypsiferous Jurassic and Triassic sediments, which erosion has largely stripped from parts of the Kaibab Plateau.
- Kanab Creek is a gypsum water contaminated by magnesium. Elves Chasm is an anomaly. Its Paleozoic limestone source would predict dolomitic chemistry, not sulfate.
- The Paria is unusual: it is more concentrated during floods than at low discharge, because dilute springs near its confluence dilute it at low flow. It ranks first in the system for nitrogen and is very high in phosphorus. Its silica is tied for third place with Elves Chasm and Kanab Creek (about 11 mg/L). Only Havasu and Diamond creeks carry more silica.

### Saline Sodium Chloride Waters

- [[Pumpkin Spring]] is a warm spring (about 32°C) near the river's edge at Mile 213. Its discharge is small, so its effect on the mainstem is negligible. Travertine and evaporite deposits form a yellow-orange boil-shaped structure that gives the spring its name, Pumpkin Bowl. Its water is acidic from dissolved CO2 and carries a large carbonate load.
- [[Blue Spring]] delivers a relatively constant and considerable flow. It is the largest point-source contributor of dissolved solids in the region (Blackman et al. 1973).
- Blue Spring is a series of springs and seeps emerging from the Redwall Limestone in the Little Colorado River canyon, 3–13 miles above the confluence. Cooley (1963) estimated that it discharges a share of the groundwater of the 28,000-square-mile Black Mesa hydrologic basin (the exact percentage is garbled in the scan).
- The salinity comes mainly from the Supai Formation, which contains salt beds and acts as the source of high dissolved solids in the overlying Coconino Sandstone and in the Blue Spring discharge.
- At the source, the water is dominated by sodium and chloride, with significant calcium and carbonate. After emergence, CO2 release drives CaCO3 precipitation, shifting the water toward higher sodium and chloride.
- Dilution by the [[Little Colorado River]] lowers concentrations while keeping the relative composition similar. Measurements made in April 1975, with the Little Colorado at about 950 cfs, document this effect.

### Mainstem Water Chemistry

- Figure 4 shows a rise in total dissolved solids and anions in the Colorado below the Little Colorado confluence (about Mile 0.5, per the paper). The increase then diminishes downstream as dilute influent streams enter.

### Overall Conclusions

- Most tributaries are chemically dilute relative to the mainstem and reduce its overall salinity.
- Water chemistry tracks source-aquifer lithology, so tributary types map onto the geology of the canyon walls and the plateaus.
- Major exceptions are saline springs such as Blue Spring and Pumpkin Spring, which dominate local dissolved-solids loads.
- Sediment and turbidity from the Little Colorado and Paria are major physical effects on the mainstem that persist well downstream.
