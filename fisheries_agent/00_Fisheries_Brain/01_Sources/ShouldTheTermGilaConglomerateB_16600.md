# Should the Term "Gila Conglomerate" Be Abandoned?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #not-applicable

*Note: This document is a geological stratigraphy paper and contains no fish or aquatic-biota data; the taxonomic tags above are placeholders required by the note format.*

## Executive Summary

L. A. Heindl examines whether the name "Gila Conglomerate," applied since 1875 to alluvial valley fill across the upper [[Gila River]] basin of Arizona and New Mexico, remains a valid formational name. Heindl concludes that the deposits assigned to it are not a single homogeneous unit. They were laid down in separate structural basins under different conditions, and the paleontological evidence does not support correlation between them. The author therefore recommends that the term be abandoned as a formational name. In its place, the author proposes formal names for individual basin units, with the retention of "Gila" as a group name (the Gila Group) for the more or less contemporaneous uppermost set of deposits.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The study area lies in the [[Basin and Range Province]], characterised by mountain ranges standing above wide, sloping alluvial plains. Valley fill is composed of conglomerate, sandstone, mudstone, claystone, diatomite and volcanic deposits, interrupted by volcanism, deformation and erosion.
- **Original definition:** [[Gilbert]] (1875) first applied the term to alluvial deposits in four structural basins along the upper Gila River, which he treated as type localities. These are (1) above the mouth of Gilita Creek, (2) along the San Francisco River, (3) along Eagle (Prieto) Creek, and (4) near the mouth of Bonita (Bonito) Creek.
- **Extent of usage:** Since 1875 the name has been extended on the basis of textural similarity, lithologic similarity and, in some places, apparent physical continuity. It is currently assigned to deposits in at least six major structural and topographic alignments covering more than 30,000 square miles.
- **Excluded deposits:** Younger alluvium (terrace, flood-plain and channel deposits of incised streams), deposits younger than the highest erosion surface on the basin fill, and older pre-basin-fill deposits linked to older volcanics or in angular unconformity with the fill.
- **Age:** Gilbert considered the deposits Quaternary. Heindl's review finds late Pliocene to early Pleistocene ages for parts of the [[Bonita Creek-Safford Area]] units, and a general Pliocene to early Pleistocene age as probable for three other uppermost units. Older units are tentatively assigned to middle Tertiary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This paper is a regional stratigraphic and sedimentological study rather than an ecological one. The relevant environmental context is the depositional setting:

- Deposits form in interior or episodically integrated drainage basins and range from alluvial to paludal and lacustrine environments, including playa, lake and diatomaceous beds.
- Composition generally reflects adjacent bedrock sources, such as the [[Mogollon Mountains]], the Maple Peak volcanic ridge and the Peloncillo Mountains.
- The deposits are described as an important source of ground water, which is one of the economic reasons for re-examining their nomenclature.
- No aquatic fauna, flora or water-quality data are reported. Fossil evidence is limited to a few vertebrate and floral references, and no fish material is discussed.

## Key Findings

1. **Gila Conglomerate is not a single unit.** The four type areas contain deposits from more than one structural environment. Units in the four basins are not demonstrably continuous through the mountain ranges.
2. **Basin-by-basin separation:**
   - *Gilita Creek-Black Mountain area (NM):* Tuffaceous conglomerate (about 300 ft), felsitic conglomerate (up to 500 ft cliffs), rhyolite tuff (about 100 ft), and basaltic conglomerate (up to about 700 ft). The tuffaceous and felsitic units are lithologically dissimilar in places.
   - *Upper San Francisco River area (NM):* A single dissected alluvial fill, about 700 ft thick along the Mogollon Mountains, divided into zones and interpreted as playa, flood-plain and lake deposits in several areas.
   - *Eagle Creek-Duncan area (AZ):* Basaltic conglomerate (about 100 ft), rhyolitic conglomerate (about 1,000 ft), an alluvial unit (about 1,000 ft), and tilted tuffaceous beds (about 500 ft) with angular unconformity beneath the alluvial unit.
   - *Bonita Creek-Safford area (AZ):* Lower volcanic conglomerate and upper granite and basalt conglomerate, separated by an erosional surface with at least 200 ft of relief, plus a younger gneissic conglomerate (about 700 ft) on the west side of Safford Valley.
3. **Two sets of units.** The *uppermost set* (derived from adjacent mountains, deposited in present basins) includes the basaltic conglomerate of Gilita Creek-Black Mountain, the dissected alluvial fill, the Eagle Creek alluvial unit, and the gneissic, granite-and-basalt and volcanic conglomerates of Bonita Creek-Safford. The *lowermost set* (containing rock types not derivable from existing highlands, with structural explanation required) includes the tuffaceous and felsitic conglomerates and the basaltic, rhyolitic and tilted tuffaceous units. Units of the lowermost set are considered pre-Pliocene, possibly middle Tertiary.
4. **Recommendation: abandon the term as a formation name.** Because no one unit can be singled out as the "Gila Conglomerate," the name is considered inadequate for the type areas and, by extension, elsewhere.
5. **Proposed nomenclature:**
   - Give alluvial deposits formational status within each individual basin, using prevailing practice. The same formation name should not be applied across basins without validating evidence.
   - Designate units of similar lithology as members. For example, the dissected alluvial fill could be split into seven lateral units.
   - Use descriptive lithologic names until formal naming is warranted, because alluvial units can change rapidly.
   - Raise "Gila" to group status (the **Gila Group**) for the uppermost set and similar contemporaneous deposits, excluding the lowermost set and later Quaternary gravels. This follows a preliminary redefinition of "group" that permits horizontal, non-successive relationships.
6. **Caveat on the group proposal.** The author acknowledges that a horizontal group may cause some confusion, but argues it expresses general similarity and contemporaneity. A similar elevation of the Gila name was later applied near Mammoth, Arizona (Heindl, 1963), according to an editorial note.
7. **Bibliography.** The paper closes with an extensive selected bibliography of roughly 300 references on Arizona and New Mexico geology, stratigraphy and paleontology. These are not reproduced here. Key cited sources include [[Gilbert]] (1875), Fries (1940), Van Horn (1957) and Knechtel (1938).
