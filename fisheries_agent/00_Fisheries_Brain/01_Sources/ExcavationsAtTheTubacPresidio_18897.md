# Excavations at the Tubac Presidio

#historical-archaeology #zooarchaeology #tubac-presidio

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State Museum report (Archaeological Series No. 85, 1975) by Lynette O. Shenk and George A. Teague, with an appendix by James M. Hewitt, documents excavations at the [[Tubac Presidio]] in southern Arizona. Fieldwork ran from October 1974 to late January or February 1975 (about 16 weeks) and was confined to the headquarters building of the Spanish fort, which housed the commandant's residence and military headquarters. The report covers architecture, stratigraphy, and artifact assemblages (indigenous, Spanish colonial, Mexican, American and English), and tests hypotheses about acculturation and communication networks.

The document is a terrestrial historical-archaeology study. It contains no fish, aquatic-invertebrate, plankton or water-chemistry data. Its only marine link is a set of shell beads and non-artifactual sea shells. Fish remains are listed in the table of contents as part of the faunal appendix, but their counts are not present in the text supplied.

## Background & Description

- **Site:** Tubac lies on the west bank of the [[Santa Cruz River]] at about 3,000 ft elevation. The Tumacacori Mountains (about 5,000 ft) lie to the west and the Santa Rita Mountains (over 9,000 ft) to the east.
- **Presidio:** The Royal Fort of Saint Ignatius was founded in 1752 under Captain Juan Tomás de Belderrain. It was renamed the Royal Fort of Saint Rafael when the [[Pima Indian Company]] arrived in 1787. The headquarters building was U-shaped, with a west wing of about 10 rooms and a north wing, linked by a zaguán (passageway).
- **Occupation phases:** Piman rancheria and mission ranch (1730s–1751); Spanish presidio (1752–1776); Pima Company (1787–1821); Mexican garrison (1821–1854); U.S. Cavalry (1862–1865); Sonora Exploring and Mining Company (1855–1861); town school and state park (1900s).
- **Analytical periods:** Early (1750–1800), Middle (1800–1850) and Late (1850–1900).
- **Method:** A metric grid was laid out, with two-metre-square search units and meter-wide trenches. Deposits were excavated in natural levels (subdivided if over 20 cm thick), screened through 1/4-inch mesh, and no recovered artifacts were discarded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** The valley had cottonwoods along the river banks, mesquite on the plain, and natural grass pasture on the hill slopes. Early observers described it as well-watered, irrigable land suited to ranching.
- **Resources:** Apart from obsidian (source unknown), stone used for tools occurred within about 4 km (2.5 miles), in terrace gravels. Granite dominated grinding tools, and red chert dominated flake tools.
- **Constraints:** Timber was hauled from mountains several miles east, so abandoned buildings were stripped for roofing and adobe. Metal scrap was recycled, and heavy materials such as iron were hard to transport by pack train.
- **Marine material:** Polished shell beads represent marine species from the Gulf of California and Pacific Coast, including *Olivella dama*, *Conus* sp., *Vermetus* sp., *Spondylus* sp. and *Haliotis* sp. The authors suggest they moved along native trade routes.

## Key Findings

**Historical and architectural**
- The presidio was occupied at intervals, was often abandoned, and was largely dismantled for building materials. Population figures include 411 in 1754–1757, about 150 around 1774, about 400 by 1840, under 100 in 1851, and abandonment again by 1855.
- Garrison size is given as 50 men in one passage and 51 in another. Walls were adobe on cobble footings, with typically 60 × 30 × 10 cm bricks. Estimated construction labour was about 450 man-days.
- Excavation found eight postholes in the plaza, a cistern pit about 1.8 × 0.9 m and 1.3 m deep, and secondary buttress footings. Stratigraphy included 11 lenses in one plaza profile.

**Ceramics**
- Indigenous pottery totalled 16,872 sherds, with 99% Piman Plainware and Redware. Piman Red-on-brown (122 sherds) and Piman Black-on-red (39 sherds) were rarer. Polychrome sherds (19) probably came from the Acoma–Laguna–Zuni area.
- Historic wares included about 152 Puebla Blue-on-white majolica sherds, 382 Guanajuato green-glaze sherds, 39 Chinese blue-on-white porcelain sherds, and English ironstone and transferwares. Chronological shifts were from Mexican majolica and porcelain to English wares after the Gadsden Purchase.

**Glass, metal and other artifacts**
- Bottle glass weighed over 17,900 g. Glass was scarce in Early and Middle contexts, which the authors attribute to fragility and transport costs.
- Metal artifacts were dominated by hardware (211 square-cut nails, 139 wire nails) and horse gear. A Spanish coin, three Mexican quarter-reals and a U.S. Flying Eagle cent were recovered.
- Buttons totalled 34 across six materials. Firearm remains included 29 lead bullets, 16 cartridge cases and 7 gunflints.

**Stone and bone**
- 779 stone artifacts were recovered: 24 cores, 640 flakes, 9 metates, 10 manos, 3 handstones, and 17 projectile points and fragments. Stone flaking was concentrated in the Early and Middle periods.
- 17 worked-bone artifacts were recorded.

**Sociocultural hypotheses**
- Acculturation was tested through male- and female-related artifacts, comparing the [[Deetz Model]] (missionization) with the [[Deagan Model]] (mestizaje). The test was framed around change between the Early and Middle periods.
- Hypotheses on caste and class could not be tested because the data were inadequate.
