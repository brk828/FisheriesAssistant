# Defining Biodiversity

#biodiversity #conservation-biology #all-taxa

## Executive Summary

This 1994 essay by [[Peter H. Raven]], published in *Nature Conservancy* magazine, defines [[Biodiversity]] as the total of all plants, animals, [[Fungi]] and microorganisms, their variation, and their interactions. It estimates that 8 to 10 million species exist, most of them unnamed, and argues that human activity is driving an extinction rate far above the background rate. The essay also includes a companion piece by [[Kent H. Redford]] describing how [[The Nature Conservancy]] is widening its conservation approach from species alone to genes, communities and ecosystems, through its [[Last Great Places Initiative]] and [[Bioreserves]].

## Background & Description

**Definition and units.** Biodiversity is described as the set of living organisms that make up the fabric of Earth, capturing solar energy, forming communities that have altered the atmosphere, soil and water over billions of years, and sustaining the planet's processes. The basic unit is the [[Species]], designated by two-part Latin names (e.g., *Homo sapiens*). Examples given include dozens of oak species (*Quercus*) in the United States, such as the California live oak (*Quercus agrifolia*) and the eastern white oak (*Quercus alba*).

**Components.** The companion sidebar lists five components that can be measured:
- [[Genetic Diversity]]: variation in genes within a species or population.
- [[Species Diversity]]: the variety of species at local, regional or global scales.
- [[Higher Taxonomic Diversity]]: variety among genera and families.
- [[Community Diversity]]: groups of interacting species in the same habitat.
- [[Ecosystem Diversity]]: communities interacting with their physical environment.

**Known and unknown species.** The essay reports the following counts of named species:
- About 9,000 birds, 4,500 mammals, 20,000 butterflies and perhaps 250,000 plants.
- About 69,000 fungi named, though as many as 1.5 million may exist.
- 4,800 named bacteria, described as a tiny fraction of the total.
- 1.4 million named organisms overall, with most named species coming from temperate regions.

For the tropics, where perhaps 7 to 8 million kinds may exist, fewer than half a million have been named. The essay puts this at roughly one named species for every 15 to 20 that exist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Latitude and tropical richness.** Between two-thirds and three-quarters of all species are estimated to occur in the [[Tropics]], and more than half of them live exclusively in [[Tropical Rain Forests]]. These forests have been cut nearly in half and now cover about 7 percent of Earth's surface.

**Evolutionary history.** Life was exclusively aquatic for most of Earth's history. Species moved onto land about 430 million years ago, and forests had evolved by about 330 million years ago. Modern humans appeared perhaps 500,000 years ago, when more species existed than at any earlier time.

**Area and extinction.** The essay cites the [[Species-Area Relationship]]: an area of 10 square miles holds, on average, twice as many species as one of 1 square mile. Reducing a habitat to a tenth or less of its original extent may eventually drive half its organisms to extinction. Western Ecuador's forest, cut to about a tenth of its original size since 1945, is given as a possible example.

**Past mass extinction.** About 65 million years ago, at the end of the [[Cretaceous Period]], an asteroid impact near the Yucatan Peninsula is thought to have caused an extinction in which about two-thirds of land species, including the dinosaurs, disappeared.

**Human-driven change.** The essay reports that:
- World population grew from 2.5 billion in 1950 to 5.5 billion today.
- Roughly a third of the world's forests have been cleared without replacement.
- A fifth of the world's topsoil, an estimated 24 billion tons, has been lost.
- Atmospheric carbon dioxide has increased by about a third.
- Stratospheric ozone at middle latitudes has decreased by 5 to 10 percent.
- Biological diversity is disappearing at 1,000 to 10,000 times the average rate of the past 65 million years.
- Humans use about 40 percent of net terrestrial photosynthetic productivity and about a third of all available fresh water.

## Key Findings

- **Biodiversity is more than species counts.** Using only species richness led conservation efforts to focus on a few ecosystems while other threatened ones were overlooked, and it could not keep pace with tropical inventories.
- **Most life is unknown.** Only a small fraction of the estimated 8 to 10 million species has been named, and this gap is largest for fungi, bacteria and tropical organisms.
- **Extinction is projected to be severe.** Removing 90 percent or more of tropical rain forest within about 30 years is expected to eliminate half of the organisms in those areas. The essay projects that by the end of the next century perhaps two-thirds of all species could be lost, a rate comparable to the Cretaceous extinction but caused by one species.
- **Biodiversity underpins human welfare.** The essay links biodiversity to food, medicines, fiber, building materials, solar biomass and unknown future products, noting that each organism is a source of genes that may be useful in ways not yet imagined.
- **Ecosystem-scale conservation is the proposed response.** The Nature Conservancy plans to combine protected areas with surrounding multiple land uses in [[Bioreserves]], optimizing each land use to conserve several biodiversity components at once. Its approach is most advanced in the United States, where plant and vertebrate data are reasonably good, while Latin America, the Caribbean and the Pacific are still focused on communities and ecosystems because species data are sparse.
- **Ethical conclusion.** Raven argues that humanity holds custody of the organisms that could later help restore Earth's stability, and that stabilizing population and consumption is an urgent necessity.
