# Life History of the Virgin River Spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1971 University of Nevada, Las Vegas master's thesis by William E. Rinne documents the life history of the [[Virgin River Spinedace]], an endemic member of the [[Plagopterini]] tribe of western cyprinids. Field and laboratory work ran from December 1969 to February 1971 in a study reach of the [[Santa Clara River]] near [[Magotsu Creek]], Utah. The study covered distribution, habitat, interspecific relationships, food habits, reproduction (maturity, spawning periodicity, fecundity) and age and growth. The work was partly motivated by the goal of assessing the species as a bait minnow. The author concludes that the species is still largely intact but threatened by habitat alteration, reservoirs, water diversion and introduced fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The Plagopterini tribe comprises three genera: *Meda* and *Plagopterus* (both monotypic) and *Lepidomeda*, which contains four recognized species (*L. albivallis*, *L. altivelis*, *L. vittata* and *L. mollispinis*). Two subspecies of *L. mollispinis* were described: *L. m. pratensis* (Big Springs spinedace, now extinct) and *L. m. mollispinis* (Virgin River spinedace), the subject of this study (Miller and Hubbs, 1960).
- **Conservation status context:** Within the tribe, one species (*L. altivelis*) and one subspecies (*L. m. pratensis*) are extinct. Habitat deterioration from human-initiated changes and competition from introduced exotic species were identified as threats (Minckley and Deacon, 1968).
- **Study objectives:** (1) elucidate the biology of *L. mollispinis* in the Santa Clara River; (2) assess its potential as a bait minnow. Laboratory experiments on growth, survival, bait preference, temperature tolerance and reproduction were run under the supervision of [[James E. Deacon]] and [[Al Espinosa]] from November 1968.
- **Related tribe research:** Prior work includes [[Miller and Hubbs 1960]] on systematics and distribution, Miller (1963) on *L. vittata*, Minckley and Carufel (1967) on *L. vittata* ecology and reproduction, Barber and Minckley (1966) on [[Meda fulgida]], Peters (1970) on [[Woundfin]], and Barber et al. (1970) on *M. fulgida* life history.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The primary reach ran from about one kilometer above Gunlock, Utah, upstream to 0.8 km above the confluence with Magotsu Creek, with elevations from 1207 m (Station I) to 1310 m (Station V). The valley is deep and narrow, flanked by sandstone and volcanic plateaus. Riparian vegetation includes box elder (*Acer*), willow (*Salix*), cottonwood (*Populus*) and catclaw (*Acacia*). The stream is clean and moderately swift, with wide shallow riffles (5–15 cm deep), pools 0.5–2 m deep, and runs. Numerous springs enter the channel. Stream gradient was estimated at 10 m/km.

**Aquatic flora and fauna.** Twelve aquatic plant species were recorded, the most conspicuous being watercress (*Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*), with buttercup (*Ranunculus aquatilis*) and horned pondweed (*Zannichellia palustris*) also present. Aquatic invertebrates include snails (*Physa virgata*, *Gyraulus parvus*, *Planorbella subcrenata*), clams (*Pisidium ventricosum*), amphipods (*Hyalella azteca*), mayflies, stoneflies, beetles, dipteran larvae and water bugs. Full checklists appear in the thesis tables and are summarized here only by group.

**Climate and water quality.** Winters are mild and summers hot. Turbulence and shallow water prevent thermal stratification. Daily water temperatures rise from a low in December–January to a high in July–August. Upper-reach temperatures were consistently cooler than lower-reach temperatures. Water quality records (USGS, 1950–1956) above Winsor Dam were used as a proxy. Discharge peaks in spring and drops to a minimum in late summer, with flash floods from mid-summer thunderstorms.

**Distribution.** The species occurs throughout the Virgin River System in Utah, Arizona and Nevada. It is most abundant in permanently flowing clean tributaries. Populations were confirmed in [[Beaver Dam Creek]], the Santa Clara River, La Verkin Creek, Ash Creek, North Creek and the North and East forks of the Virgin River within Zion National Park. Four mainstem Virgin River sites where it had been recorded previously yielded no specimens. Key findings on range loss include the [[Lower Gunlock Reservoir]], which inundated about 3 km of stream, and diversion at Winsor Dam.

**Interspecific relationships.** Five other fish species co-occur with the spinedace: speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), mountain sucker (*Catostomus clarki*), flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*), brown trout (*Salmo trutta*, introduced) and [[Bonneville Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus hydrophlox*, non-native). The redside shiner is considered the greatest current threat through competition for space and food. Brown trout may displace spinedace from suitable pools, and the trout feed on spinedace.

## Key Findings

**Habitat and movement**
- The species prefers clean, cool, relatively swift streams of pools, runs and riffles, with pools 0.5–2 m deep most often used.
- Movements of about 25 m up or downstream were common over one month. The maximum recorded displacement was about 1.5 km.
- Spinedace form loose aggregates in pools and school when alarmed.

**Food habits (283 stomachs examined, Dec 1969–Nov 1970)**
- Age Group I (n = 193), II (n = 64) and III (n = 26) fish were examined.
- Diptera remains were the most frequent item (74% of stomachs), followed by plant material (64%), dominated by green algae.
- Insects are the primary food when available; plant material is eaten mainly when insects are scarce.
- Ephemeroptera (mayflies) dominated stomach volume in May and June (about 90% or more), likely linked to mass emergence.
- Snails were abundant but little used, and the amphipod *Hyalella azteca* was never found in stomachs.

**Reproduction**
- Sexual dimorphism is weak; females become more robust during spawning, and males show tubercles and spawning coloration.
- Spawning is annual, lasting from spring to early summer (mid-April to late June, with a few individuals into July). Little spawning occurs after July, and gonads are undeveloped in July–August.
- Maturity-index peaks were about 11.3% (females) and 4.5% (males), with males ripe at ≥4.5% and females at ≥11% (Age Group III females' pattern differs).
- Five ovarian egg classes were identified. Class I (ripe ova) diameter was 1.98–2.85 mm; Class II (mature) 1.28–2.19 mm; Class III (maturing) 0.28–1.23 mm; Class IV (immature) <0.2 mm.
- Spawning behavior was observed in May and June 1970. Spawning was brief, reportedly triggered by female entry into patrolling males' areas.
- Environmental cues: spawning was linked to water temperatures around 13–17°C, photoperiod near 13.8 hours, and high spring discharge.

**Fecundity**
- Secondary fecundity (mature ova) was regressed against standard length, body weight, ovary weight and maturity index, all showing positive relationships. Ovary weight gave the strongest fit (r = 0.87).
- Total fecundity of ovaries ranged roughly from 2,000 to 4,000 ova (preliminary estimate).
- Age Group II and III fish were more fecund than Age Group I, but Age Group I dominated the spawning population.
- Evidence suggests Age Group III fish may spawn twice per season.

**Age and growth**
- Scales and opercles proved unreliable. Length-frequency analysis was used instead.
- Age Group 0 (young-of-year) made up about 50% of samples; Age Group I about 41.1%; Age Group II 5.4%; older fish 2.4%.
- Growth is fastest in the first year (about 21 mm gained), roughly 10 mm in the second year and about 5 mm in later years.
- Maximum length recorded was 112 mm, though the species was previously described as seldom exceeding 85 mm.
- Females outnumber males in all age groups, suggesting higher male mortality.

**Conclusions**
- Habitat is the principal limiting factor. Shallow, clean pools with moderate turbulence, low turbidity and cool water are needed.
- Dam construction, stream diversion and introduced predators or competitors (especially the redside shiner) represent the most serious threats.
- Insect food availability likely regulates spawning and growth.
- Ecology resembles that of *L. vittata* and [[Meda Fulgida]], and the same habitat alteration risks apply.
