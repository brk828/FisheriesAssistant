# Geomorphic and Sedimentary Response of Rivers to Tectonic Deformation

#fluvial-geomorphology #sedimentology #neotectonics

## Executive Summary

This review evaluates how [[Alluvial Rivers]] respond to subtle epeirogenic tilting and warping of continental interiors, and whether those responses can be preserved and recognized in the sedimentary record. Because low-gradient rivers are very sensitive to changes in grade, their form, pattern, and deposits can act as sensitive indicators of incipient or small-scale deformation in both modern and ancient settings. The authors identify five longitudinal responses and two lateral responses to tilting, and they stress that nearly all of these effects can also be produced by non-tectonic causes. Tectonic interpretations are therefore considered valid only when non-tectonic causes are eliminated and the features are closely associated with historically active structures.

## Background & Description

The paper is a review and critique rather than a single field study. Its authors, John Holbrook (Southeast Missouri State University) and S.A. Schumm (Colorado State University), were published in *Tectonophysics* 305 (1999), pp. 287–306. The manuscript was received 28 April 1998, revised 30 June 1998, and accepted 11 August 1998.

Epeirogeny is the broad, gentle warping of crustal interiors, and it can arise from many causes, including [[Salt Tectonics]], transpression and transtension, intraplate stress, and sediment loading. Regardless of cause, the usual result is tilting of the contemporary topographic surface. The paper focuses on individual alluvial rivers, which flow through their own sediments and are not confined by bedrock. Drainage-network analysis is noted as a complementary approach but is treated separately.

The authors define two end-member tilting geometries:

- **Longitudinal tilting**, parallel to the floodplain orientation, which affects the channel profile.
- **Lateral tilting**, normal to the floodplain orientation, which drives channel migration across the valley.

Melton (1959) estimated that 25–75% of streams in non-glaciated areas are tectonically influenced or controlled. Large features such as the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the Tibetan Plateau are easy to identify, but smaller and incipient warpings are subtle and difficult to recognize, especially in ancient deposits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses fluvial geomorphology and sedimentology rather than biology, so ecological content is limited. The most relevant environmental points concern how tilting changes flow regimes, floodplain conditions, and habitat-forming sediments:

- **Flooding and wetlands:** Reduced slopes raise flood frequency. Where deformation dams river courses, the result can be permanent swamps or lakes. The authors note that swamp development promotes organic preservation as carbonaceous overbank fines, coal or lignite, and hydric paleosols.
- **Lake formation:** Lake deposits near the [[Blytheville Arch]] on the St Francois River are cited as evidence of deformation-related impoundment.
- **Sediment supply and climate:** Clay preservation depends on supply from source areas, so overbank fine-grained deposits can reflect climate rather than tectonics.
- **Bedrock control:** Resistant bedrock and armoring can produce water impoundment and grain-size changes that are unrelated to tectonics.

## Key Findings

### 1. Five longitudinal profile responses to tilting
Alluvial rivers respond to longitudinal deformation through:
1. Deflection around uplifts and into subsidence zones.
2. Aggradation in backtilted reaches and degradation in foretilted reaches.
3. Compensation for slope change through shifts in [[Channel Pattern]].
4. Increased overbank flood frequency in foretilted reaches and decreased frequency in backtilted reaches.
5. Increased bedload grain size in foretilted reaches and decreased grain size in backtilted reaches.

### 2. Deflection
Rivers tend to be deflected by surficial warping because it is one of the most readily observable effects of deformation. Streams gravitate toward subsided zones, while a superposed river crosses an uplift when its incision rate substantially exceeds the uplift rate. Low-gradient rivers are especially sensitive. [[Amazon River]] tributaries in the upper Amazon Basin were diverted by Tertiary uplifts generating only tens of meters of relief over several kilometers. Deflection can also result from bedrock fracture control, resistant materials, or climatic and eustatic aggradation, so it must be tested against structures.

Examples of deflection in the rock record include:
- Pliocene–Recent fluvial deposits of the Sorbas Basin, southeast Spain, with paleocurrents deflected around modern uplift and into structural lows.
- The Lower Cretaceous [[Antlers Formation]] of central Texas, with sand thickness increasing as much as 2× in pre-Cretaceous structural lows (the Kingston and Sherman synclines).

### 3. Longitudinal profile adjustment
Up-warping produces convex terraces, valley floors, and thalwegs that mimic the underlying structure, and subsidence produces concavity. The [[Wiggins Uplift]] of southern Mississippi, which uplifts as much as 4 mm/year, is the central example:
- The [[Pearl River]], Tallahala Creek, and Bogue Homo Creek all have convex terraces coincident with the uplift.
- The Pearl River's projected channel profile shows no convexity, because its greater discharge and erosive power let it re-establish grade. It has 12 m of incision below its lowest terrace near the uplift axis.
- Tallahala and Bogue Homo creeks, which have less stream power, retain convexity.

Profile deformation is a lasting indicator mainly for low-stream-power rivers. Even large rivers, such as the [[Mississippi River]] over the [[Lake County Uplift]] (related to the 1811–1812 [[New Madrid Seismic Zone]] earthquakes), retain convexity temporarily.

### 4. Aggradation and degradation
In an idealized case, a river aggrades upstream and downstream of an uplift and incises the steepened dome. The [[Rio Grande]] north of Socorro, New Mexico, is cited as a current example, crossing a broad dome over the Socorro magma body. Profile-related aggradation and degradation may be preserved as thinning and intraformational angular unconformities. Schwartz (1982) interpreted Early Cretaceous reactivation of the [[Boulder Batholith]] in southwestern Montana from radial paleocurrents, thinning above the uplift, and increased lithic content of flanking sandstones.

### 5. Channel pattern adjustment
Decreased slope can shift rivers from braided to meandering, straight, or anastomosing patterns, while most changes are intrapattern (for example, changes in sinuosity):
- On the Lake County Uplift, a roughly 10 m high bulge in the New Madrid seismic zone, the low-gradient (~0.0001) Mississippi reduces sinuosity on the up-dip flank and increases it on the down-dip flank.
- In the gravel-bed [[Jefferson River]] of Montana, sinuosity is higher upstream and downstream of the uplift axis because sediment choking distributes material laterally.
- Flume studies show that aggrading braided streams increase in braid index, number of braid bars, and number of linguoid dunes, while degrading streams decrease in braid bars and channel width.
- In the Lower Cretaceous [[Mesa Rica Sandstone]] of northeastern New Mexico, very low-sinuosity channels (width/depth ratio of 10–20) on the up-stream flank of the Sierra Grande uplift contrast with higher sinuosity on the downstream flank. The authors argue for tectonic causation because the anomaly coincides exactly with the structure.

The authors recommend [[Architectural-Element Analysis]] (Miall, 1985, 1996) for reconstructing these patterns where exposure permits.

### 6. Cross-section adjustment
Decreased slope lowers bankfull discharge and stream power while increasing width/depth ratio and flood frequency. Jefferson River data (Jorgensen, 1990) illustrate this: channel width, width/depth ratio, and sediment storage decrease, while bankfull discharge increases, as the river passes from above to over the uplift axis. Overall channel shape is not a consistent indicator of deformation, because the Nile widens over steepened zones. The most consistent effect is reduced incision and shallower depths on lowered slopes, which promote more frequent floods.

### 7. Grain-size change
Tectonically steepened slopes generally increase bedload grain size. The Guadalupe River in Texas coarsens where it crosses the Sam Fordyce fault zone. However, grain size is also controlled by sediment availability. The Bogue Homo and Tallahala creeks have coarser bedload across the Wiggins uplift because the uplift exposes the gravel-rich Citronelle Formation, so the effect is indirect. Grain-size variation therefore requires "extreme caution" as a tectonic indicator.

### 8. Lateral tilting: avulsion and combing
- **[[Avulsion]]:** Cross-valley tilt rates above a threshold given in the text as >7.5 × 10⁻? radians/ka (exponent illegible in the scanned source) force rivers to avulse toward the down-tilt side. Avulsion produces isolated sand ribbons and immature channel belts. Dumont and Hanagarth (1993) identified five successive Holocene stages of down-tilt shifting for the Beni River in the Beni Basin, Bolivia (described as Peru in the text's citation context).
- **[[Channel Combing]]:** Slow down-tilt migration, with tilt rates reportedly 2–3 orders of magnitude smaller than avulsion rates, produces wide channel belts with asymmetric meander mosaics concave toward the subsidence axis.

Leeder and Alexander (1987) studied the [[Madison River]] of Montana and predicted three architectural features: preferential up-tilt dip of lateral-accretion surfaces, randomly oriented sand bodies in untilted settings, and climbing sand-body bases under aggrading conditions. Mack and James (1993) tested these models on Plio-Pleistocene rift deposits in New Mexico. Fluvial deposits in asymmetric basins had a narrower effective floodplain, more multistory channel sandstones, fewer and less mature paleosols, and a lower proportion of planar cross-beds than in symmetric basins.

### 9. Caveats and limitations
- Tectonic inferences require stratigraphic evidence of abnormal sediment accumulation or removal, elimination of bedrock control, nickpoint migration, and changes in discharge and sediment load.
- Profile deformation is generally the least reliable indicator of ancient tilting because it is temporary and easily modified, though it can be a good indicator of recent warping.
- Age control is difficult, and differential compaction can produce false thickness anomalies.
- The authors conclude that no single indicator is sufficient, and that interpreters should check as many indicators as possible.

### 10. Applications
Fluvial analysis is presented as a cost-effective way to locate modern warping before expensive geodetic or geophysical surveys. In ancient settings, fluvial sediments may be the only evidence of the timing, location, and magnitude of deformation. The authors also note the value of fluvial studies in petroleum and coal exploration, and for assessing mesoscale (10³–10⁴ years) structural behavior in mid-continent deformation.
