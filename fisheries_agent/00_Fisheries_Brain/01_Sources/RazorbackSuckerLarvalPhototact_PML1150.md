# Razorback Sucker Larval Phototactic Behavior in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This student special-problems report (1995, supervised by Dr. W.L. Minckley) investigates whether the nighttime catch rate of [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae in [[Lake Mohave]] varies with [[Lunar Phase]] or with location. The larvae respond positively to bright light, so they can be captured with a [[Q-Beam Fish Light]] and a fine-mesh dip net. The author combined his own March–April 1995 collections with [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (USBR) data from February–April 1994. The analysis found no detectable lunar effect on catch rate, strong differences among coves, and an apparent decline in catch over the course of each night that the author attributes to collector fatigue. The author regards the work as a preliminary step toward recovery-oriented research.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a rare and endangered fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its historical range extended from Wyoming into northwestern Mexico. The species has declined sharply in range and abundance since the mid-twentieth century, for reasons that remain unclear. Lake Mohave supports the largest known population, and a recovery plan has been developed and implemented there. Life-history gaps remain, and the report argues that these gaps could be vital to survival.

Larval records in Lake Mohave were sparse until spawning areas were identified and sampling improved. Early captures came from plankton tows and midwater trawls. The key advance was the discovery that larvae show a positive phototactic response to bright light at night. Light-based collecting made it possible to obtain hundreds to thousands of larvae in a few hours (Bozek et al. 1984, as cited in the report). The author's central question was whether the phototactic response, and therefore catch efficiency, follows a pattern related to the moon, time of night, shift, or location.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and collecting context**
- Sampling took place in coves of Lake Mohave. Many cove names appear in the USBR records, including [[Yuma Cove]], [[Tequila Cove]], [[Arizona Bay]], [[Redtail Cove]], [[Hog Farm Cove]], [[Nine Mile Cove]] and [[Great West Cove]]. The report's cove-name cross-reference lists more than 150 numbered coves.
- Collecting was conducted from a boat in two to three feet of water with the bow beached, soon after dark until dawn. At each site, one light per side of the boat was used, and at least one person scooped larvae with a fine-mesh dip net.
- Identification relied on field recognition. The author states that razorback sucker was the only fish spawning at the time of the study, which supported confident identification.

**Sampling design**
- Captures were tallied in 15-minute increments, with the time, location, weather, lunar phase and water conditions recorded on a standard tally sheet. Tests were run during near-full moon (89–98% illumination) and near-new moon (1–4%) periods.
- Two crews, an early shift and a late shift, were rotated through the night. Relative effort was summarized as catch per person per attempt.

**Data sources**
- The author's own collections were taken on March 14–16 and April 1–2, 1995.
- The USBR data set covers February, March and April 1994 and included location, begin/end times and larval counts. Later USBR master sheets for March–April 1995 at [[Tequila Cove]] and [[Yuma Cove]] are summarized in the report as catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) tables.

## Key Findings

**1. Lunar phase does not appear to affect catch rate.**
- Catch totals were compared across lunar phases ranging from about 1% to 98% illumination (Graphs D, G, H, I, L). The author found no consistent rise or fall in catch approaching full moon.
- The author suggests that the light intensity of the fish light may overwhelm lunar illumination. This interpretation is based on comparisons, not a controlled experiment.

**2. Catch varies strongly among locations.**
- Among the 1994 USBR records (Graph E/F), the most productive coves were:
  - [[Tequila Cove]]: 2,107 larvae, 34.8% of the total
  - [[Yuma Cove]]: 1,870 larvae, 30.9%
  - [[Arizona Bay]]: 765 larvae, about 12.6%
- Other coves, such as Redtail Cove (28 larvae) and several Hog Farm and Nine Mile sites, yielded few larvae. The total across all listed coves was about 6,047 larvae.
- Lunar-phase comparisons at the two most consistently productive sites (Tequila Cove, Graph H; Yuma Cove, Graph I) also showed no clear pattern.

**3. Time of night and shift effects.**
- Per-hour contribution by group (Graph C) showed that catch peaked in the early evening hours (roughly 8:00–11:00 pm) for several groups. Catch was relatively steady through the night for some crews (Graph B).
- The author concludes that human factors, such as fatigue and individual technique, are a plausible source of variation, rather than a true decline in larval availability. Groups B and C performed better in the early hours and had both early and late shifts, while the other groups did not.
- Graph L, comparing 1994 and 1995 data, suggested that catch rate begins to fall after the first few hours of a shift, which the author attributes to fatigue.

**4. Date and season.**
- Comparison of similar days across February–April 1994 (Graph J) showed no evidence that particular times of the month produce higher catches.
- April 1995 showed the largest surge in activity among the days compared in Graph K. The April 5, 1994 Tequila Cove record was also fairly active. The author suggests Tequila Cove may be a hot spawning site, pending further testing in April 1996.

**5. Limitations acknowledged by the author.**
- The author states that many factors affecting phototactic behavior were not controlled. These include variation in water conditions, collector skill, and light intensity.
- Collector skill varied, and the author notes that some collectors consistently caught more larvae than others over long periods.

**6. Conclusion.**
- The author concludes that lunar phase has little or no effect on catch rate or activity of razorback sucker larvae.
- He recommends further research on phototactic behavior, given its potential value for understanding and managing this species.
