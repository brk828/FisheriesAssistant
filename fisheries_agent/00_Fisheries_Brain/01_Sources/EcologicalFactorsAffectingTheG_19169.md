# Ecological Factors Affecting the Growth of Smallmouth Bass and Longear Sunfish in Jordan Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 paper by [[Leonard Durham]], published in the *Illinois Academy of Science Transactions* (Vol. 47), reports age and growth analyses of [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Longear Sunfish]] in the lower four miles of [[Jordan Creek]], a small tributary of the [[Salt Fork of the Vermilion River]] in Vermilion County, Illinois. The stream was split into two contrasting reaches: a shaded lower wooded area and an open upper area used for farming. The study compared water temperature, [[Bottom Fauna]] biomass, fish population density, and growth between the two reaches.

Smallmouth bass grew faster in the upper open reach, while longear sunfish grew at nearly the same rate in both reaches. The author attributes the bass result to a combination of warmer water, more bottom organisms, fewer bass, and more minnows. The longear result is attributed to a balance between possible benefits (warmer water, more invertebrates) and increased inter- and intraspecific competition for food.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Jordan Creek has been studied intensively by the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] since summer 1950. Work was carried out jointly with the [[Illinois Department of Conservation]]. Smallmouth bass were the main focus because they are the most important game fish in the stream.
- **Species studied:**
  - *Micropterus dolomieu dolomieu* (Smallmouth Bass), Lacépède. The source OCR reads "dolomicu dolomiew"; corrected here.
  - *Lepomis megalotis peltastes* (Longear Sunfish), Cope.
- **Study reach:** The lower four miles of Jordan Creek, naturally divided into two habitat types of about equal length:
  - **Lower area:** wooded, densely shaded, falls an average of 24 feet per mile, mean flow 18.6 cubic feet per second (cfs). Soils erode easily, so little farming occurs.
  - **Upper area:** open, falls 9.7 feet per mile, mean flow 12.9 cfs. Extensive sand and silt accumulate in its long pools. Farming is common.
- **Methods (summary):**
  - Water temperature: recording [[Thermograph]]s in the lower part of each area, checked periodically against a mercury thermometer. Mean daily temperatures were averaged from readings at two-hour intervals.
  - Bottom fauna: samples taken with a [[Surber Sampler]] each month in both areas. Crayfish were not adequately sampled by this gear, so their relative weights are considered unreliable.
  - Fish: population counts and weights, plus age determination from [[Scale Age Determination]] using a nomograph and manila strips ([[Carlander and Smith Nomograph Method]], 1944). Fish were measured, scaled, and returned to the stream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature regime**
- Upper (open) area temperatures were higher than lower (wooded) area temperatures in nearly every week from June 6–13 to October 4–9, averaging 2.9°F higher. The single exception was June 28–July 4, when rain lowered the upper-area temperature (−0.4°F).
- After the first or second week of October, the lower area was no longer densely shaded, and temperatures in the two areas became more similar.
- Cloudy or rainy days sometimes brought upper-area temperatures to or below lower-area levels.
- The lower area fluctuated less daily, due to shade and several springs flowing into it.
- Maximum daily temperatures in the upper area exceeded the lower area by as much as 12°F. Minimum daily temperatures in the upper area were as much as 3°F lower.
- Peak temperatures on August 31, 1951: 94°F upstream and 84°F downstream.
- Temperature data are from one season (1951). The author judged this season typical based on field observations and preliminary review of later data.

**Bottom fauna (1952)**
- *Annelids and insects* (Table 2): In the June 4 collection, lower-area weights were slightly higher than upper-area weights. Caddisfly larvae, stonefly nymphs, beetle larvae and adults, midge larvae, and some mayfly nymphs made up most of this group.
- *Crayfish*: about twice as high in the lower area on June 4.
- *Mollusks*: slightly higher in the lower area on June 4.
- After June 4, total bottom invertebrate weight in every upper-area collection was at least twice that of the lower area.
- Few annelids were found in the samples.

**Fish population density (Table 3, 1950)**

| Smallmouth Bass | 213 / 48.2 | 156 / 24.1 | 369 / 72.3 |

- Average weight per fish for bass was 0.23 lb (lower) and 0.15 lb (upper).
- Average weight per fish for longears was 0.06 lb (lower) and 0.04 lb (upper).
- Average weight per minnow was 0.012 lb (lower) and 0.006 lb (upper).

## Key Findings

### 1. Study sample and methods
- Age and growth analyses covered **439 smallmouth bass** (199 collected in 1950, 240 in 1952). The body length–scale length correction factor was **1.1 inches** for bass.
- Age and growth analyses covered **400 longear sunfish** (1950) and **415 longear sunfish** (1952). The correction factor was **0.25 inches** for longears.
- Fish from each reach were analyzed separately, then combined for stream-wide averages. The author assumed the two reaches held distinct populations. Marked-fish studies (Larimore, 1952) suggested most bass and sunfish stay in about the same location across successive growing seasons.

### 2. Temperature
- Upper-area mean daily temperatures were higher than lower-area temperatures for most weeks of summer 1951, with an average difference of **2.9°F** across the period noted above.
- Upper-area temperatures had greater extremes: up to **12°F** higher maximum and up to **3°F** lower minimum.

### 3. Bottom fauna
- In June 1952, the lower area held slightly more annelids, insects, and mollusks, and about **twice** as many crayfish.
- From July through September 1952, the upper area always had **at least twice** as many bottom invertebrates by weight.

### 4. Smallmouth bass
- Bass grew **faster in the upper open area** than in the lower wooded area, except in the first year of life, when growth was nearly identical.
- The 1950 population had **156 bass upstream vs. 213 downstream** (about three-fourths as many upstream). Downstream bass weighed **48.2 lb vs. 24.1 lb** upstream, about twice as much.
- *Note on source text:* The summary (Item 4) states "27 percent more bass downstream," while the Table 3 counts give 213 vs. 156 (about 37% more downstream, or 27% fewer upstream). The figures are reported here as given in the table.
- Stream-wide average calculated total lengths (1950, all fish): **3.4, 6.8, 9.5, 11.1, 12.5, 14.0 inches** at annuli 1–6.
- Stream-wide average calculated total lengths (1952, all fish): **3.3, 6.3, 9.5, 11.4, 12.8, 13.9 inches** at annuli 1–6.
- Legal size (**10 inches**) was reached in the **third or fourth year** of life. The author notes this compares well with growth reported for smallmouths in other waters (Carlander, 1950).
- The oldest bass were in their **seventh year**, but most disappeared before that age.
- The longest bass measured **15.4 inches** total length.
- Legal-length bass collected: **68** in 1950 and **73** in 1952 (stream-wide). In the upper open area, legal-length bass rose from **17** (1950) to **35** (1952).

### 5. Longear sunfish
- Growth was **almost the same** in both reaches. In 1950, longears from the upper area (140 fish) grew slightly faster than those from the lower area (260 fish). In 1952, growth was almost identical for 213 upper-area and 202 lower-area fish.
- Stream-wide average calculated total lengths (1950, all fish): **1.1, 2.2, 3.3, 4.2, 4.8, 5.2 inches** at annuli 1–6.
- Longears of desirable size (**6 inches or longer**) were scarce. The oldest were in their **seventh or eighth year**, and most were taken from the lower area.
- Compared with [[Michigan Longear Sunfish Growth]] (Hubbs and Cooper, 1935) and [[Indiana Fish Growth]] (Hile, 1931), Jordan Creek longears grew **more slowly in the first two or three years** but **faster in later years** (age groups IV to VII). By these later ages, Jordan Creek fish exceeded comparable Michigan and Indiana fish in length and weight.
- In 1950, there were **almost 4 times** as many longears upstream as downstream (1,567 vs. 448). Upstream longears weighed **56.9 lb vs. 28.3 lb** downstream, about twice as much.

### 6. Minnows
- Minnows were **2.5 times** as numerous upstream (23,116 vs. 9,245). Upstream minnows weighed **23 percent more** (131.6 lb vs. 107.0 lb).

### 7. Interpretation and hypotheses
- **Factors favoring bass growth upstream:** warmer water (which may increase digestion and metabolism; Markus, 1933, for largemouth bass), more bottom organisms, more minnows (a food source for bass), and lower bass density (less intraspecific crowding).
- **Factors affecting longears:** longears are unlikely to eat many minnows (except very small ones), so the minnow population may increase competition for food with longears. This could offset benefits from warmer water and more invertebrates.
- The author concludes that growth in both species reflects an **interplay of many factors**, not a single cause.

### 8. Summary of conclusions (from the author's summary)
1. The study covered the lower four miles of Jordan Creek, divided into an unshaded upstream area and a shaded downstream area.
2. Mean daily water temperatures were higher upstream during the summer of 1951, with greater extremes.
3. Bottom fauna in 1952 followed the pattern described above.
4. Smallmouth bass grew faster in the upper open area.
5. Longear sunfish grew at almost the same rate in both areas.
6. Faster bass growth upstream may reflect warmer temperatures, more bottom organisms, less interspecific crowding, and more minnows. Longear growth may not reflect these advantages because of increased inter- and intraspecific competition upstream.
