# Riparian and Stream Enhancement Management and Research in the Rocky Mountains

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[William S. Platts]] and John N. Rinne, published in the *North American Journal of Fisheries Management* (1985, vol. 5, no. 2, pp. 115–125), assesses past stream enhancement research in the Rocky Mountains, the adequacy of that research, and the studies needed to improve future projects. The authors conclude that research on stream improvement in a long-term watershed context is lacking, that not all streams can be enhanced, and that enhancement should be attempted only after literature techniques have been carefully judged appropriate for a given site. Riparian fencing and rehabilitation of bank vegetation are presented as the most reliable enhancement tools, while artificial instream structures frequently fail where grazing and other watershed stressors continue.

## Background & Description

The paper is a review and position piece rather than a field study. Its stated scope covers riparian and stream habitat organized into five components: riparian, water column, stream bank, channel, and biotic. The authors define "enhancement" as returning riparian and stream habitat to a more productive condition by natural or artificial means, typically through log pool-forming structures, willow planting, flow deflectors, or cleaning spawning gravel.

The authors note that many Rocky Mountain streams are artificially stressed to a state "barely adequate for fish life," and that some naturally lack suitable habitat for species such as the salmonids. These two conditions define the situations that fishery biologists usually address with enhancement projects.

The document is the work of two [[U.S. Forest Service]] researchers, affiliated with the Intermountain and Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Stations. It is followed by an extensive reference list (roughly 90 entries), which is omitted here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian vegetation.** Trees provide shade, bank stability, and instream cover when fallen; they also create pools and riffles and control gradient. Summer solar radiation accounts for about 95% of midday heat input to Rocky Mountain streams, making canopy cover important at lower elevations on streams of Order 6 or less. Brush cover also insulates against [[Anchor Ice]] formation in winter.

**Bank stability and grazing.** Resilient willows and grasses form mats during floods that reduce near-bank velocity and trap sediment. Late summer or fall grazing reduces the vegetative mat and exposes bank soils; rest-rotation systems can maintain mats in fair to good condition two years out of three, but removing more than 65% of grass cancels the benefits of rest.

**Threatened and endangered fishes.** Several southwestern species, including the [[Woundfin]], [[Gila Trout]], and [[Apache Trout]], have very restricted ranges. The Gila trout and Apache trout occur naturally in about 24 high-elevation streams (above 2,000 m). Competition from introduced fishes and habitat degradation are cited as major causes of decline, and the 1973 [[Endangered Species Act]] frames protection efforts.

**Water and channel.** Channelization reduces sinuosity and stream length; one Montana study found channel length 30% shorter in man-made channels, and thalweg distance was reduced 34% in streams channelized during highway construction.

## Key Findings

**Riparian fencing is the most consistently effective tool.** All authors who examined fenced or protected riparian habitats reported enhancement (Table 1 of the source). In a Horton Creek, Idaho study, heavily grazed reaches were four times wider and one-fifth as deep as fenced reaches, and fish numbers were 7.6-fold and biomass 10.9-fold greater in the fenced areas. On Big Creek, Utah, the ungrazed exclosure was significantly narrower and deeper with more bank undercut.

**Fencing costs are substantial.** Four-wire fencing costs about $6,000 per stream mile when fenced on both sides, and fencing all fishable streams on [[Bureau of Land Management]] lands (15,000 miles) would cost about $90 million. Annual maintenance runs $60 to $200 per stream mile. Fishery benefits alone would require an average of 47 fisherman days per mile per year to offset fencing costs, while U.S. Forest Service streams averaged 13.5 fishing trips per mile annually.

**Instream structures often fail under continued grazing.** Duff placed 43 structures in grazed and ungrazed reaches of Big Creek in 1970–1971. By 1980, only three remained functional in grazed reaches, while most in rested reaches still worked. Single log structures were ranked most effective among four types studied.

**Fish response to enhancement is mixed.** Gunderson (1968) reported fish populations up to 27% higher and biomass 44% higher without livestock grazing. Gard (1961a) documented better condition, survival, and growth of brook trout in an improved headwater stream. However, Gard (1972) found no differences between improved and unimproved sections of Sagehen Creek, and Patterson (1976) detected no fish population increases after riprapping. Whitney and Bailey (1959) recorded a 90% fish loss after channelization for highway construction.

**Macroinvertebrate responses vary.** Gard (1961b) reported a 5- to 8-fold increase in summer standing crops in pools created by rock and log dams, while washed-out beaver dam sites showed up to a 22-fold decrease. McKirdy (1964) found 11 of 16 sites with log structures had more invertebrates, 3 had fewer, and 2 were unchanged.

**Core recommendations.** The authors argue that:
- Enhancement should be limited to degraded streams that will not recover naturally within a reasonable period.
- Artificial enhancement must never substitute for watershed stewardship; "cookbook" approaches transferred from Midwestern low-gradient streams to Rocky Mountain systems have usually failed.
- Projects should be evaluated environmentally, sociologically, and economically, with benefit-cost ratios and long-term effectiveness assessed.
- Research priorities include compatible grazing strategies, long-term studies of enhancement structures, and effects on fish communities.
