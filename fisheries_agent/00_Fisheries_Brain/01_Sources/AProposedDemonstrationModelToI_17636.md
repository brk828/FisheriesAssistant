# BIOTA Project: A Proposed Demonstration Model to Identify Key Areas of Biodiversity in Arizona

#biodiversity #conservation-planning #ecosystem-management #multi-agency-coordination

## Executive Summary

The BIOTA (Biodiversity Team Assessment) Project is a collaborative proposal developed in 1993 by representatives from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona Association of Conservation Districts]], [[Arizona Gap Analysis Program]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. This demonstration model aims to identify key areas of biodiversity conservation value across Arizona through a structured, multi-stakeholder process. The project explicitly has no policy, regulatory, or statutory purpose, but rather seeks to provide an objective scientific framework for future conservation discussions and actions. The assessment integrates two comprehensive databases: the [[Heritage Data Management System]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department) and the [[Gap Analysis Program]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service/University of Arizona). The project evaluates biodiversity from three perspectives: representative biological communities, species richness of native plants and animals, and populations of endangered wildlife. Key areas are selected to capture 60-80% of qualifying biodiversity values while emphasizing conservation effectiveness—the ability of areas to maintain biodiversity over time through intact ecological processes. The proposal includes extensive public involvement through statewide conferences, a multilateral steering committee with representation from public agencies, tribal governments, organizations, and private interests, and careful consideration of economic factors and land ownership patterns.

## Background & Description

The BIOTA Project emerged from recognition that Arizona needed a coordinated, science-based approach to identifying priority areas for biodiversity conservation. The proposal was prepared during November 1992 through August 1993 and presented to the [[Arizona Interagency Natural Areas Coordinating Council]] for evaluation and implementation. Participation in proposal development did not constitute endorsement by participating agencies or organizations; a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) was circulated to approximately 12 participating agencies and organizations.

The project structure includes five phases: (1) Organization of the project, including finalization of the proposal, commitments to participate, funding commitments, and development of detailed methodology with peer review; (2) First Conference to reach the broadest possible audience describing goals and methods and soliciting input; (3) Investigation Phase including information preparation, biodiversity analysis, socio-economic analysis, documentation of heritage values and historical land use, and report preparation; (4) Second Conference to report results and lay groundwork for constructive follow-up; and (5) Project Follow-up focusing on regional meetings and decentralized, multilateral coordinating groups.

The organizational structure features a hierarchical arrangement: a Cooperators' Council of high-level representatives chaired by prominent leaders; a Steering Committee providing working oversight with representatives from participating agencies and groups; Advisory Teams of experts advising project investigators and conference organizers; and Staff including Project Coordinators, Project Investigators from AGFD Heritage Data Management System and USFWS Gap Analysis program, Conference Coordinators, and miscellaneous contractors as needed.

The project defines three facets of biodiversity as "biodiversity values": (1) Representative biological communities—characteristic assemblages of plants and animals associated with relatively minor human disturbance; (2) Species richness—total number of native terrestrial and aquatic vertebrate species (rare and common) in a given area; and (3) Populations of endangered wildlife—plants and animals rare throughout their geographic range, generally found in 20 or fewer locales worldwide (specifically taxa with Heritage ranks G1-G2, T1-T2, and selected G3, representing 200-250 taxa in Arizona).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The BIOTA Project emphasizes [[Conservation Effectiveness]]—the ability of an area to maintain biodiversity values over time. Factors considered include viability of populations, habitat quality, functioning of natural ecological processes, manageable boundaries, feasibility of maintaining natural processes (fire, flooding, etc.), and current or projected land use and threats. Watersheds and larger units generally have greater conservation effectiveness, and connectivity between areas may also be considered.

The assessment methodology employs computer-assisted analysis using polygons (two-dimensional areas of any shape) established around clusters of biodiversity values representing "hot spots." These polygons are enlarged to boundaries of effective conservation units such as watersheds. Algorithms—conceptual models in computer program form—compare alternative sets of polygons to identify optimal solutions that efficiently capture at least 60% of qualifying biodiversity values in units of high conservation effectiveness. The algorithms consider adequacy of representation of biological communities and endangered wildlife, with a defined minimum representation of each type included where possible.

The project recognizes that the resulting set of key areas will not include every biologically important site in the state. Important conservation actions are expected to occur outside key areas, and future evaluations will result in somewhat different sets based on new information. The assessment is designed to facilitate future updates incorporating new data.

Economic considerations are integrated through a computer-based model assessing factors such as relative amounts of public and private land, land and resource values, and demographic trends. Cultural, geological, and historical features within key areas are documented and described, though these are not used as selection criteria for identifying key areas.

## Key Findings

**Operating Principles and Scope**: The BIOTA Project operates under explicit principles that it has no policy, regulatory, or statutory purposes and respects without qualification the right of all participants to exercise complete discretion in how and whether to use results. Individual private landowners are not singled out based on biodiversity values of their properties. The project aims to help interpret and communicate a conservation ethic while coordinating existing databases to facilitate future updates.

**Participation and Governance**: The project structure ensures broad participation while maintaining organizational and individual autonomy. Initial Steering Committee membership represents diverse stakeholders including federal agencies ([[National Biological Survey]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Forest Service]]), state agencies ([[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State Land Department]]), counties and local government, [[Arizona Association of Conservation Districts]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], corporate land and resource managers, and American Indian tribes. A 10-member Coordinating Group meets more frequently with the Core Project Team. Decisions are made by consensus with quorum requiring at least one-third of members present.

**Data Sources and Methodology**: The project utilizes the most comprehensive biodiversity information and analytical tools available: the [[Heritage Data Management System]] tracking occurrences of all threatened and endangered species and state-rare species, and the [[Gap Analysis Program]] database with geographical analysis capabilities using [[Geographic Information System]] (GIS) technology. The Status of Information Review ensures all secondary sources are accessed and entered into principal databases. Advisory Team Review examines databases for data quality and accuracy, with data validation conducted as needed. The project does not entail additional field surveys except as needed for validation.

**Target Coverage**: The set of key areas aims to include 60-80% of qualifying occurrences of each of three biodiversity facets: high species richness, representative biological communities, and populations of endangered wildlife. Each key area is configured to ensure high conservation effectiveness. This 60-80% target recognizes that complete coverage is neither feasible nor necessary for an effective conservation framework.

**Sensitive Information Protocols**: Published maps and descriptions list biodiversity values, cultural and historic features only for entire key areas, not for specific tracts or locations within key areas. This protects privacy of private landowners and prevents irresponsible collection of vulnerable species. Each Native American tribe is individually approached to explain the project and inquire how the tribe wishes to participate. Tribes preview all maps and descriptions to ensure no proprietary or sensitive information is inadvertently published.

**Relationship to Other Processes**: The BIOTA Project differs from the [[Natural Diversity Scorecard]] process by including evaluation of species richness, ecological representation, and conservation effectiveness to greater degrees and utilizing Gap Analysis Program database with geographical analysis capabilities. The project emphasizes species based on global rarity rather than state rarity, though mechanisms for evaluating state-rare species sites through other processes are acknowledged.

**Geographic Scale Considerations**: The assessment occurs at state scale because the Gap Analysis Program database for Arizona is more nearly complete than databases in adjacent states and Mexico. The rarity of species is assessed at global scale, but identification of key areas occurs in state context. Future assessments at broader geographic scales (regional or national) are anticipated as other states and nations develop comparable databases.

**Implementation Timeline**: Phase One involves development of detailed Project Design and Methodology. Phase Two involves actual production of the list of key biodiversity areas. The Arizona Game and Fish Commission decided to participate in September 1994, with the first Steering Committee meeting scheduled for October 15, 1994. The MOU allows work to begin prior to first Steering Committee meetings in both phases.

**Expected Outcomes**: The project is intended to set the stage for more cooperative and coordinated implementation of conservation in areas including endangered species conservation, ecosystem planning and management, and proactive land protection through mitigation, land exchanges, habitat conservation planning, and voluntary conservation agreements. The process and results should provide an invaluable framework for conservation discussions and actions, though how agencies, organizations, tribes, and individuals use the information remains separate from project scope.
