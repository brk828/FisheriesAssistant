# Improving the Evaluation of Conservation Programs

#conservation-biology #program-evaluation #peer-review

## Executive Summary

This essay argues that formal evaluation of [[Conservation Program Evaluation|conservation programs]] is rare but increasingly important for improving their effectiveness. The authors, a team of conservation scientists and managers from zoological, governmental, academic and NGO institutions, recommend that programs be evaluated by external peer reviewers every five years, supported by more frequent (annual) internal reviews. They stress that evaluations must assess organization and process as well as biological outcomes, and that agreement on goals and success criteria should be reached at the outset. The paper also addresses resistance to evaluation and the institutional barriers that limit it.

## Background & Description

- **Publication:** Kleiman et al., *[[Conservation Biology]]*, Volume 14, No. 2, April 2000, pp. 356–365. Received November 30, 1998; accepted May 26, 1999.
- **Authors:** Multi-institutional team including the [[Smithsonian National Zoological Park]], the Denver Zoological Foundation, Yale University School of Forestry and Environmental Studies, the U.S. Geological Survey, the University of Idaho, the [[Wildlife Conservation Society]], the [[USDA Forest Service]] and a Seattle-based consultancy.
- **Scope:** A conceptual essay rather than a data study. It draws on examples from endangered-species recovery and reintroduction programs. It contains no new field data, sample sizes or statistics.
- **Abstract:** Provided in English and Spanish. The Spanish version is a full translation of the English abstract.
- **Defining evaluation:** Following [[Lasswell Policy Framework|Lasswell]] and [[Clark and Brunner]], evaluation is defined as assessing success and failure in terms of goal attainment and accountability for outcomes, gathering information on past decision performance, and disseminating results to appropriate audiences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's examples come from species conservation programs, mainly for terrestrial and avian taxa, with the [[Columbia River Salmon]] review as the only aquatic example:

- [[Golden Lion Tamarin]]: long-term monitoring of reintroduced animals led scientists to change how captive-bred tamarins were prepared for release.
- [[Black-Footed Ferret]]: used to illustrate the balance between biological and social considerations in success criteria.
- [[Hawaiian Crow]] and [[California Condor]]: peer-review evaluations by the National Research Council and the National Audubon Society and American Ornithologists' Union.
- [[Florida Manatee]]: used as a model of organizational learning in a recovery program.
- Columbia River salmon: a National Research Council review of the status of seven salmon species.

The essay also discusses the [[Endangered Species Act]], including a 1999 review of habitat conservation plans, and the [[Government Performance and Results Act]] (GPRA) of 1993, which motivated calls for peer review of research.

## Key Findings

**1. Evaluation should assess both substance and process.**
- Substantive criteria are usually biological (e.g., population numbers, genetic heterozygosity, habitat diversity). Process criteria concern how the program is organized, funded, and learns.
- A program may meet biological goals inefficiently or with negative side effects, or run smoothly while failing its goals.

**2. Internal and external evaluations serve different purposes.**
- [[Internal Evaluation]] should be regular and frequent (annual is recommended), with participants reviewing goals and methods and adjusting activities through [[Adaptive Management]].
- [[External Evaluation]] should be less frequent, broader, and conducted about every five years by well-chosen peer reviewers. Reviewers are less subject to rewards or sanctions, groupthink, conflicts of interest and peer pressure.
- Integrating both is recommended, and the authors suggest that external reports be formally managed by funding agencies.

**3. Evaluation must start with shared goals and context.**
- Participants and reviewers should agree on the problem definition, aims, objectives, and context, including past and recent trends and projected future developments.
- Vague or contested goals hinder evaluation. Clear, measurable goals, timetables and responsibilities should be set.

**4. Conservation is a continuous, feedback-driven process.**
- Following the [[Lasswell Program Phases]] (initiation, estimation, selection, implementation, evaluation, termination), the authors argue that conservation should be treated as a looped process rather than a linear one.
- Strategic planning should use stepwise milestones and multiple routes to success.

**5. Learning and innovation are central.**
- Programs may progress without hypothesis testing or adequate documentation, leaving managers without a scientific basis for adjustment.
- [[Prototyping]] (small-scale, systematically evaluated trials) and [[Case Study Analysis]] are proposed for building transferable "model" programs.
- Managers in bureaucratic cultures may avoid new methods because the fear of failure outweighs the rewards of efficiency.

**6. Stakeholder inclusion and resources shape evaluation success.**
- The best evaluations include all relevant participants, and conflicts should be managed rather than avoided.
- Resistance comes from fear of criticism, budget limits and lack of resources; evaluation costs are often a small percentage of program budgets.
- Some organizations are developing internal evaluation mechanisms for the programs they fund.

**7. Scale and focus determine evaluation design.**
- Evaluations can cover one aspect, a whole program, a time frame of one to two years, or the program's life (roughly 15–20 years).
- Multi-jurisdictional programs are harder to evaluate; broader evaluations need more time and resources.

**Conclusion:** Evaluation is critical to improving the effectiveness and efficiency of conservation programs. Periodic external evaluations, backed by routine internal reviews, should be a standard part of conservation management.
