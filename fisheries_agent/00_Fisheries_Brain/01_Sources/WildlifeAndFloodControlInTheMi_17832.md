# Wildlife and Flood Control in the Mississippi Valley

#ichthyology #marine-biology #waterfowl

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by [[Gordon Gunter]] of the [[Gulf Coast Research Laboratory]] at the Twenty-Second North American Wildlife Conference (1957), examines how [[Flood Control]] on the [[Mississippi River]] has altered the river's floodplain and the wildlife and aquatic life that depend on it. Levees, [[Canalization]], and [[Reservoir]] construction have cut the river off from its [[Mississippi River Floodplain]], raised high-water stages, and reduced shallow nursery and feeding habitat. The author concludes that the decline in wildlife and aquatic life stems from habitat loss, though the exact changes are undocumented. He proposes studying [[Transient Reservoirs]], which would reconnect swamps to the river, as a way to reduce flood heights and restore habitat, and he recommends a thorough, multidisciplinary study of the idea.

## Background & Description

The Mississippi River watershed is one of the largest in the world, after the Amazon and Congo. It covers 1,244,000 square miles, about one-third of the United States, plus 13,000 square miles in Canada. Average annual rainfall is about 30 inches, and roughly one-fourth of it reaches the sea by the river. Annual discharge at the mouth is estimated at around 800,000,000,000 cubic yards. The river carries about two million tons of sediment per day into the sea, or more than 730,000,000 tons per year.

The valley was incised during the ice ages, when sea level was at least 400 feet lower than today. After the ice melted, the river filled its valley with recent [[Alluvium]], a process termed [[Alluvial Drowning]] by Russell and Howe (1935). This produced some of the most fertile land on earth. The [[Mississippi River Floodplain]] covers 35,000 square miles. Sediment deposited by high water forms [[Natural Levees]], which slope away from the channel at about seven feet per mile. Between these levees and the valley escarpments lie large flood basins that are cut off from the channel at low water.

Historical accounts document the river's natural behavior. Le Page du Pratz (1758) noted that the river lost water during floods, which never returned to it, and described how natural levees form. The high-water crest usually comes between March and May. Spanish and French explorers recorded spring floods in 1543 and 1684, and New Orleans' initial settlement was delayed by floods in 1717. Elliott (1932) listed 58 recorded major floods between 1717 and 1929.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

In its natural state, the lower river overflowed its banks annually and spread across the flood basins. The shallows of both freshwater and marine systems serve as the main rearing grounds for aquatic life. Most aquatic organisms spawn in shallows, and the overflow areas once served as nursery habitat for animals that retreated into the river as floodwaters receded. The flood basins also sheltered large flocks of [[Waterfowl]].

The river's four major [[Distributaries]] once carried water to the sea, especially at high stages. [[Bayou Manchac]] was cut off in 1828, two west-bank distributaries were closed in 1868 and 1904, and [[Bayou Atchafalaya]] grew to carry more water at high stages than any other American river except the Mississippi and Ohio. Changes to these distributaries and to the lower river have affected the [[Estuarine Habitat]] around the river mouth, including [[Oyster Beds]] where salt water has encroached.

Climate may also have played a role. Evidence suggests a drier climatic cycle began around 1870 (Price and Gunter, 1943). The author notes this may have contributed to the changes observed.

## Key Findings

**Flood history and levee expansion**
- The first levee at New Orleans was built between 1717 and 1727. It was 5,400 feet long, about three feet high, and formed a [[Ring Levee]].
- The total levee system now exceeds 2,500 miles, and some levees are 35 feet high.
- The [[Carrollton Gage]] at New Orleans registered 14.95 feet in 1882, 16 feet in 1890, and 21 feet in 1912.
- The greatest flood of historic times occurred in 1927. The [[Corps of Engineers]] took complete charge of flood control on the river in 1928.
- Present peak water heights are reported as seven times higher than before levees were built.

**Reservoirs and river control**
- The Corps of Engineers had built at least sixty major reservoirs in the watershed by 1952, with at least as many under construction. The [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] has also built reservoirs. The author counts about 300 large and small reservoirs in the watershed.
- Reservoirs have created new aquatic habitat and waterfowl stopover areas, reduced silt in downstream river stretches, improved groundwater recharge, moderated local climate, and lowered high-water stages on the lower river.

**Effects on wildlife and aquatic life**
- Levees cut swamps off from the river, and the swamps were progressively destroyed. Wildlife and aquatic life have declined because their habitat was largely destroyed. The author notes that no baseline data exist, since wildlife biologists did not exist when the decline began.
- Channel straightening and faster flow have reduced shallow water in the main channel. Canalization and levees increase silt transport to the sea.

**Proposed benefits of transient reservoirs**
- Setting levees back from the river in sparsely populated swampy areas would create transient reservoirs. These could lower flood heights, restore silt deposition, raise groundwater levels, restore wildlife habitat and economically important plant life, and possibly allow lower levee heights.
- Controlled flow through the closed distributaries could benefit the estuary and oyster beds. [[Bayou Manchac]] outlets could benefit the area from [[Lake Maurepas]] to the river and the oyster bottoms in [[Mississippi Sound]].
- Towns within 30 to 40 miles of the lower river already pipe water from it, and the author expects demand for Mississippi water to grow as the country needs more of its available supply.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The author concludes that the proposal deserves serious study across sociological, economic, engineering, and biological aspects.
- In the discussion, Chairman Bellrose (Upper Mississippi) agreed that setting bottomlands back to overflow conditions would benefit fisheries, waterfowl management, and flood reduction. Gunter recommended that the problem be studied fundamentally and that the case be made to decision-makers.
