# The Trout Fishery of Crowley Lake, Mono County, California

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #salmonidae #reservoir-ecology #stocking-programs

## Executive Summary

This administrative report documents the highly successful trout fishery at [[Crowley Lake]], an artificial reservoir in southern [[Mono County]], [[California]]. The fishery is managed as a trophy fishery through annual planting of approximately 300,000 subcatchable [[Rainbow Trout]] averaging 10 per pound, planted near season's end (July 31). These fish exhibit exceptional growth rates, reaching approximately one pound and 12 inches within eight months. The management program produces trout at approximately $0.15 per pound returned to anglers, compared to $0.90 per pound for catchable trout programs in nearby waters. The 1958 season yielded an estimated 142,149 trout totaling 160,000 pounds, with 53% of planted subcatchables eventually harvested. Opening weekend accounts for nearly 25% of seasonal angling pressure and 35% of seasonal catch. High biological productivity results from high total dissolved solids (187 ppm), shallow mean depth (34.85 feet), and abundant bottom fauna (153 pounds per acre standing crop).

## Background & Description

[[Crowley Lake]] was created in April 1941 when the Long Valley Dam was placed in operation by the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power to impound the [[Owens River]]. The reservoir reached spillway level in spring 1946. At maximum storage, the lake measures approximately six miles long and three miles wide, with maximum depth of 114.5 feet and surface area of 5,272 acres. Volume at spill level is 183,743 acre-feet with mean depth of 34.85 feet.

The [[Owens River]] drainage historically contained no native game fish. Native species include sucker ([[Catostomus]] sp.), chub ([[Siphateles]] sp.), and dace ([[Rhinichthys]] sp.). Game fish introductions prior to reservoir creation included [[Rainbow Trout]] ([[Salmo gairdnerii]]), [[Brown Trout]] ([[Salmo trutta]]), [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] ([[Salmo clarkii henshawi]]), [[Golden Trout]] ([[Salmo aguabonita]]), and [[Eastern Brook Trout]] ([[Salvelinus fontinalis]]), though only rainbow and brown trout were present in significant numbers when the lake filled.

Bottom types consist chiefly of mud and ooze, grading to gravel and sand in shoal areas. The lake is strongly bicarbonate in character with relatively high concentrations of silica, sodium, chloride, and phosphate. Total dissolved solids of 187 ppm are quite high compared to nearby lakes. Eighty-three percent of total bottom area lies beneath less than 50 feet of water, contributing to high biological productivity.

Winter conditions are severe; the lake almost invariably freezes over its entire surface, with ice-cover typically forming in late December and disappearing in early April. Midsummer surface temperatures reach the low 70s. During summer, blooms of zooplankton ([[Cladocera]]) and phytoplankton ([[Volvox]] and [[Gloeotrichia]]) are common, occasionally creating oxygen depletion in shoal areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lake's bottom fauna productivity was studied in 1953, revealing mean standing crop of 153 pounds per acre between May and August, with total standing crop exceeding 400 tons. Bottom fauna consists primarily of midge larvae and pupae (Diptera, [[Tendipedidae]]). Although productivity per unit area is not extraordinarily high, the extensive shallow areas result in substantial overall food production relative to trout population size.

Analysis of [[Rainbow Trout]] stomach contents (n=18) revealed that 100% contained [[Tendipedidae]] pupae, comprising 89.3% of total food volume. [[Cladocera]] were present in 33% of stomachs, making up 10.4% of total volume. Tendipedid larvae were rarely consumed, presumably due to their bottom-dwelling habit. Later in fall, rainbow trout consume chiefly small [[Tui Chub]] ([[Siphateles]] sp., approximately 2 inches long) and [[Cladocera]].

Nine [[Brown Trout]] stomachs examined contained approximately equal volumes of ramshorn snail ([[Helisoma]] sp.) and chironomid pupae.

Nongame fish populations (suckers and chubs) exist without apparent detriment to trout populations. Young nongame fish are consumed in large numbers by trout, particularly during late fall when aquatic insect populations are low. This may contribute to unusually good overwinter growth of trout.

The severity of winter conditions and degree of reservoir fluctuation exert considerable influence on trout growth. Exceptional growth occurs during winters with short-duration ice-cover and constant or rising reservoir levels. Comparison of 1959-60 versus 1962-63 overwinter periods demonstrates this: fish planted in 1962 reached mean weight of 18.9 ounces by May compared to 15.3 ounces for 1959 plant. The 1962-63 winter had only 7 weeks ice-cover (versus 13 weeks in 1959-60) and increasing surface acreage (3,950 to 4,450 acres versus decreasing from 4,300 to 3,600 acres).

## Key Findings

**Growth Rates:**
- Subcatchable rainbow trout planted in August at 10 per pound (5.8 inches fork length, 1.6 ounces) reach approximately one pound and 12 inches by the following May (about 8 months later)
- By mid-June: mean 13 inches, 20 ounces
- By late July: mean 14 inches, 26 ounces
- Age 2+ fish at season opening: approximately 17 inches, 37 ounces
- Age 2+ fish in July: approximately 18 inches, 46 ounces
- Age 3+ fish (rare): approximately 20 inches, 4 pounds
- [[Brown Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]] grow at similar rates

**Comparative Growth:**
[[Crowley Lake]] rainbow trout mean total lengths at end of first, second, and third years: 10.1, 15.8, and 19.9 inches, compared to nearby [[Convict Lake]] calculated lengths at annuli formation of 3.8, 6.4, and 11.1 inches.

**Condition Factors:**
- Rainbow trout: mean K = 1.30 (significantly higher than nearby waters)
- Brown trout: mean K = 1.06 (similar to nearby waters)
- Cutthroat trout: mean K = 0.94 (relatively poor, likely due to spawning stress)

**1958 Season Creel Census Results:**
- Total estimated seasonal catch: 142,149 trout (160,000 pounds)
- Species composition: 88% rainbow, 8% brown, 4% cutthroat
- Opening weekend: 12,243 angler days (23.89% of seasonal total), 48,930 trout caught (34.4% of seasonal total)
- May total: 33,447 angler days (65.27% of seasonal total), 117,186 trout caught (over 60% of seasonal total)
- Mean catch per angler hour: 0.70 opening weekend, 0.50 remainder of May, 0.53 May overall
- June: 0.22 catch per angler hour
- July: 0.19 catch per angler hour
- Season overall: 0.38 catch per angler hour

**Species Composition by Month:**
- May: >90% rainbow trout
- June: 76% rainbow trout
- July: 71% rainbow trout
- Brown and cutthroat catch rates remained relatively constant throughout season while rainbow catch rates declined sharply

**Harvest and Survival:**
- Estimated 53% of subcatchable rainbows planted in 1957 were eventually taken by anglers (minimum estimate, boat anglers only)
- Marking study: 5% of 1957 plant was fin-clipped; identical percentage of 1958 rainbow catch bore this mark
- Survival into third year negligible; season's catch approximately 95% age 1+, 5% age 2+

**Fish Production:**
- Net yield: 27.7 pounds per acre (deducting planted fish weight from total harvest)
- Mean surface area during study period: approximately 4,800 acres
- Maximum productive capacity estimated at approximately 50 pounds per acre

**Cost-Benefit Analysis:**
- 1958 rainbow trout planted: 26,943 pounds costing $19,297
- 1958 rainbow trout harvested: approximately 125,000 pounds
- Cost per pound returned to angler: $0.15
- Comparable cost for catchable trout programs in nearby waters: $0.90 per pound (assuming 85% recovery)
- [[Crowley Lake]] program produces 6 pounds of trout for same cost as 1 pound from catchable programs

**Stocking History (1941-1962):**
- Total subcatchables planted: 2,668,022
- Total fingerlings planted: 3,345,939
- Total catchables planted: 671,820
- Total all trout planted: 8,441,697
- Current management: approximately 300,000 subcatchable rainbow trout annually, planted at or near season's end (July 31)

**Special Management Measures:**
- Season opens Saturday nearest May 1, closes July 31 (three months earlier than general area)
- Reduced bag limit: 7 trout (versus standard limit)
- [[Hot Creek]] fall spawning strain rainbow trout used predominantly
- Beginning 1958: [[Mt. Whitney]] spring spawning strain planted to increase natural recruitment
- [[Brown Trout]] stocking discontinued in 1954; natural reproduction in tributary streams adequate
- Experimental plants of [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]] (yearlings) successful; fingerlings less successful
- [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] fingerlings not particularly successful
- [[Eastern Brook Trout]] fingerlings totally unsuccessful

**Natural Reproduction:**
- [[Brown Trout]]: excellent natural reproduction in tributary streams; entire catch from natural production
- [[Rainbow Trout]]: historically minimal natural spawning by [[Hot Creek]] fall spawning strain (females retain fall spawning, males revert to spring spawning)
- [[Mt. Whitney]] spring spawning strain: considerable spawning activity observed in tributary streams beginning March through May
- 1963 season: naturally produced rainbow trout about 9 inches long observed in catch

**Angler Use Patterns:**
- Opening weekend 1947: 5,920 anglers, nearly 9,000 trout, over 17,000 pounds
- Opening weekend 1961: nearly 19,000 anglers, over 72,000 trout, 40.5 tons (record)
- Strong correlation (r = 0.92) between numbers of subcatchables planted previous year and opening weekend catch per angler hour
- Shore anglers take relatively few fish; boat anglers dominate fishery
- Boat counts: close correlation between Highway 395 northbound boat counts and opening day boats on lake (ratio 1:1.14)
- Sunday boat use averages 87.5% of Saturday use

**Trophy Fish:**
- Only [[Brown Trout]] taken at trophy size
- Lake record brown trout: 19 pounds 10 ounces
- Brown trout in 8-10 pound range taken rather frequently
- [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]] seldom exceed 3 pounds due to heavy angling pressure and rapid harvest

**Future Management Considerations:**
- Increased planting of subcatchables as additional rearing facilities become available
- Lower bag limit may become necessary to extend quality fishing later in season
- Possible alternate-week opening during season
- Experimental barbless fly-only area with 12-inch minimum size limit tested fall 1963-1964
- Continued experimental planting of yearling [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]]
