# Ecological Analysis of Aravaipa Creek and Environs: Quarterly and Annual Progress Reports (1976–1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled project document, submitted by W. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]] to the [[Bureau of Land Management]] under Contract No. YA-512-CT6-98, reports the first year (August 1976 through early June 1977) of an ecological study of [[Aravaipa Creek]], a perennial desert stream in Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona. The work combined physico-chemical monitoring, algal pigment sampling, benthic invertebrate surveys, quantitative electrofishing, historical photography, and a riparian flora catalogue. Data are described as tentative. The study concludes that the stream supports an unusually abundant native fish fauna of seven species, while primary production is low and largely limited by flood scour.

## Background & Description

The project began in late June 1976 with account activation, hiring of a graduate assistant and an undergraduate field assistant, procurement of a Hydrolab "Surveyor" meter, and site selection. Localities were coded by two-letter prefixes (e.g., AC = Aravaipa Creek proper, VC = Virgus Canyon, TC = Turkey Creek) plus elevation, and taxa were coded with a symbolic scheme. Data were stored on the Arizona State University Univac 1110/42 system.

Aravaipa Creek arises near Klondyke at about 1,007 m (3,303 ft), remains perennial for 35 to 45 km, and enters a canyon cut in Galiuro Volcanics. Adjacent lands (Aravaipa Canyon Natural Area and the George Whittell Wildlife Reserve) total about 3,237.5 hectares. Historical photographs from 1890 to 1900, housed in regional archives, show a denser riparian community and more ground litter than at present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physico-chemical conditions.** The stream is alkaline, with pH typically near 7.6 at night and up to about 8.4 to 9.0 by day. Conductance ranged from 400 to 500 µmhos/cm (corrected to 25 °C). Water temperatures ranged from 5.0 °C to near 30.0 °C, with heating rates of 1.56 to 2.97 × 10⁻² °C/min and cooling rates of 0.28 to 1.17 × 10⁻² °C/min. Nighttime dissolved oxygen often fell to about 80% saturation. Nitrate-nitrogen was relatively high upstream (0.05 to 0.30 mg/l) and declined downstream (0.01 to 0.05 mg/l), while phosphate-phosphorus stayed between 0.04 and 0.15 mg/l.

**Hydrology.** Discharge is bimodal, with peaks in winter (January to March) and summer (July to September). Mean discharge is about 30 cfs (median 23 cfs over 24 years). Scour-level events, about ten times the mean, occurred on 0.8% of 1,583 days (1966 to 1970). Flash spates periodically removed algae, emergent plants, and pool habitat.

**Primary producers.** Diatoms and green algae (including [[Cladophora]]) dominate. Chlorophyll a ranged from undetectable to 19.6 mg/m² in the first sampling round (38 samples: 20 at 0.0 to 0.9 mg/m², 14 at 1.0 to 9.9, and 4 above 10.0). Watercress (Nasturtium officinale) was decimated by summer floods and began recolonizing in September.

**Benthic invertebrates.** 83 taxa have been recorded. Abundance ranged from about 334 to 13,500 individuals/m² and biomass from 1.0 to 13.0 g/m². Diversity was 6 to 20 taxa per sample. Side-canyon faunas resembled temporary ponds, lacking the current-loving Simuliidae, Hydropsychidae, and swift-water Baetis that dominate the mainstream.

**Riparian vegetation.** A catalogue lists 150 vascular plant species in 128 genera and 58 families for the Primitive Area. Canyon vegetation is cottonwood–willow with seep willow understory, transitioning to mesquite and desert scrub downstream. Introduced saltcedar (Tamarix pentandra) is a noted threat.

## Key Findings

**Fish community (the central biological result)**
- All seven native fish species are present and unusually abundant. Agosia chrysogaster (longfin dace) is the most abundant fish, a pattern recorded since monitoring began in 1963.
- [[Tiaroga Cobitis]] (loach minnow) has expanded to occupy about 95% of the channel, from about 3,225 ft down to 2,500 ft. Previously it was known only to about 2,600 ft.
- [[Meda Fulgida]] (spikedace) ranges from 3,200 ft to about 2,600 ft, with three year classes present, indicating recent successful reproduction.
- [[Rhinichthys Osculus]] (speckled dace) ranges from 3,225 to 2,595 ft and is the second most abundant minnow upstream.
- [[Gila Robusta Grahami]] (roundtail chub) occupies deeper pools from about 3,100 to 2,550 ft, at densities of 1 or 2 large adults per pool. Juveniles were present throughout.
- [[Pantosteus Clarki]] (Gila mountain-sucker) and [[Catostomus Insignis]] (Gila coarse-scale sucker) remain abundant. The latter concentrates in deeper canyon water.
- Introduced [[Chaenobryttus Cyanellus]] (green sunfish) occurs only from about Virgus Canyon (2,795 ft) to about 2,590 ft, and was very rare. Its population appeared to decline after August flooding.

**Sampling results**
- Electrofishing captured 99% or more of numbers and biomass within three passes in blocked segments. Larger fishes are most readily sampled, while small bottom-dwelling species such as Tiaroga are least efficiently captured.
- Seining was dropped for quantitative work because it indicated errors up to 20% in numbers and more for biomass.
- October 1976 and April 1977 rankings by number were identical: Agosia, Pantosteus, Rhinichthys, Catostomus, Meda, Tiaroga, Gila. Estimated density was 11,000 to 15,000+ fish/ha, with biomass near 105 kg/ha.
- Fish density and biomass were greatest upstream, lowest in and near the canyon, and intermediate downstream. Diversity was highest in the heterogeneous canyon section.

**Ecosystem interpretation**
- Respiration appears to be fueled mainly by organic loading of sediments rather than by the water column. Primary productivity is generally low, consistent with frequent scour.
- Nitrate declines strongly downstream, which may reflect uptake by riparian trees. The authors describe this as speculative and untested.
- Several suggestions for future work were given: tagging of large fishes to track movement, larval fish studies in spring, quantitative fish sampling in winter, and mapping of riparian vegetation along 100 m transects.

**Historical and administrative**
- Of 432 photographs in hand, 92 are dated and only 24 can be located with confidence. The 1890 to 1900 series documents a denser riparian gallery and less channel incision than at present.
- Personnel changed during the project: Terry Tompkins resigned and was replaced by Mark Foster (M.S. student). Dr. Stuart Fisher joined to study nutrients in desert streams.
- A benthos thesis by Dale A. R. Bruns (August 1977, M.S.) is appended, and a microchemistry report by Milton R. Sommerfeld accompanies the work.
