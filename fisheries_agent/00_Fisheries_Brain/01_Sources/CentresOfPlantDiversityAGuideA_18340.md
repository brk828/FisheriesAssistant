# Cuatro Ciénegas Region (CPD Site MA10), Mexico

#botany #plant-diversity #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This data sheet, from the WWF/IUCN *Centres of Plant Diversity* series (Volume 3, The Americas), describes the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in Coahuila, northern Mexico. The c. 2000 km² region is a closed-drainage intermontane basin within the eastern [[Chihuahuan Desert]], and its warm springs, gypsum dunes and mountain woodlands support a rich, highly endemic flora. The flora comprises 879 taxa (860 species in 458 genera of 114 families), including many endemics. The sheet concludes that the region's ecosystems and biota are suffering accelerating damage from water drainage, habitat loss, grazing, gypsum mining and collection of cacti, and it calls for urgent protection. Its authors are Donald J. Pinkava and José A. Villarreal-Quintanilla.

## Background & Description

- **Location and extent:** Northern Mexico, between latitudes 26°45'–27°05'N and longitudes 102°05'–102°20'W. Area c. 2000 km².
- **Elevation:** From c. 740 m on the basin floor to over 3000 m in the [[Sierra de la Madera]].
- **Basin geometry:** c. 40 km east-west and 25–30 km north-south. The [[Sierra de San Marcos]] nearly bisects it.
- **Hydrology:** The basin is naturally closed-drainage. Its seven major above-ground drainage systems include the [[San Marcos River]] (locally the Mesquites River), and the [[Churince River]] runs into [[Laguna Grande]]. Springs are cool or thermal (25°–38°C), emerging from travertine-lined tubes or from pits called *posos*. Canals now drain the basin eastward to the Rio Grande.
- **Climate:** Mean annual precipitation is under 200 mm. Air temperatures range from below 0°C in winter to over 44°C in summer.
- **Land use and values:** Grazing and forestry are the main uses, with medicinal plants also harvested. Other values include scenery, aquatic resources and refuge for fauna. Agriculture has been practised since the 16th century, and the sheet notes the region remained little developed as of 1959.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Major vegetation groupings** (Pinkava 1984):

1. **Basin grassland:** Salt-tolerant species such as *Distichlis stricta*, *Monanthochloe littoralis* and *Sporobolus airoides* dominate the saline valley floor.
2. **Aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats:** Successional stages of posos, from sedges and grasses on new slumping banks to aquatic *Nymphaea ampla*, *Utricularia obtusa* and *Chara* in mature pools. Shore and tree species include *Prosopis glandulosa* and *Salix nigra*.
3. **[[Gypsum Dunes]]:** Dunes of white gypsum blown from evaporating lakebeds, up to 6–9 m high. They host gypsophilous endemic Compositae and are stabilised by mesquite and *Acacia greggii*.
4. **Transition zone:** Interrupted shrub and tree islands ("mogotes") around the basin and its lagunas.
5. **Desert scrub:** *Larrea tridentata* with agaves, yuccas and cacti on bajadas and lower slopes.
6. **Chaparral:** Dense oak and heath growth in sheltered arroyos and canyons.
7. **Pine-oak and oak woodlands:** Higher, moister slopes with oaks, pinyons and junipers.
8. **Montane forest:** Mesic forest in upper north-facing canyons, with *Pseudotsuga menziesii*, *Abies durangensis* var. *coabuilensis* and *Pinus strobiformis*.

**Endemism hotspots:**
- c. 12 endemics in the Laguna Grande–Laguna Churince complex
- c. 10 endemics in the canyons and bajadas of the Sierra de la Madera's east-facing slope
- 2 endemic taxa in bajadas and canyons above Poso Anteojo

**Fauna:** The basin supports fishes, turtles, lizards, crustaceans, snails and scorpions. The sheet describes it as showing evolutionary processes such as isolation, adaptation, niche segregation, character displacement, speciation and endemism.

**Threats:** Drainage of water, temperature imbalances and habitat loss are accelerating. Gypsum dune extraction has been industrial-scale since 1979. Other pressures include overgrazing by goats and horses, agricultural and pasture expansion, trash deposition, and cactus collection for sale in the U.S.A., Europe and Japan. Population growth may cause extinctions.

## Key Findings

- **Flora:** The native vascular flora of the basin and surrounding sierras comprises 879 taxa, including 860 species in 458 genera of 114 families (excluding the unstudied Sierra de la Fragua). Cultivated plants add 6 genera and species in 3 families.
- **Endemism:** Of 49 taxa described from type localities in the region, 45 are accepted and 23 are endemic. The summary box gives "at least 33 endemics" and the social values section gives "at least 56 endemics", of which c. 33 are faunal. The counts are reported as they appear in the source and are not reconciled.
- **Unique features:** The basin has notable thermal posos and white gypsum dunes. The sheet states there are only three such dune formations in North America.
- **Threat assessment:** Anthropogenic damage is accelerating, and the gypsum dunes and endemic species are particularly at risk.
- **Conservation status:** Mexico's SEMARNAP, with World Bank funding, is studying the region to set protection and management policy, including restoration and reforestation. The basin is included in The Nature Conservancy's Parks in Peril Program.
- **Recommendations:** The sheet calls for urgent preservation of representative portions of the region. It recommends establishing a biological station for research and education, and protecting endemic-rich areas in coordination with local people. It also recommends studying the uses and properties of endemic species as potential genetic resources.
- **Traditional use:** Many plants are used in rural traditional medicine. Wood extraction from the Sierra de la Madera is the most extensive exploitation and threatens *Abies durangensis* var. *coabuilensis*.
