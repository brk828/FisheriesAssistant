# Whitefish

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Salmonidae #Coregonidae

## Executive Summary

This article provides an overview of whitefish species in New York waters, focusing primarily on the [[Adirondack Whitefish]] or [[Labrador Whitefish]] (Coregonus clupeaformis). Whitefish are highly valued food fish with an international reputation for flavor, occurring in at least 48 New York lakes including [[Lake Erie]] and [[Lake Ontario]]. The species exhibits bottom-feeding behavior, fall spawning habits, and has been successfully propagated artificially since 1858. Commercial catches show large fluctuations, and recreational harvest remains limited despite abundant populations in many waters.

## Background & Description

Whitefish are distinguished by their small mouths positioned low on the head, adapted for bottom feeding. This feature differentiates them from similar species like [[Cisco]] (lake herring) and [[Smelt]], which have terminal mouths. Unlike [[Shad]] or [[Mooneye]], whitefish possess an adipose fin anterior to the tail.

The [[Round Whitefish]] can be recognized by its small size, slender body, and parr markings (blotches along the sides). This species persists in several Adirondack lakes but is seldom caught by anglers, serving primarily as forage for other fishes.

The "common whitefish" group includes the [[Gulliver Lake Whitefish]] and two or more subspecies of Coregonus clupeaformis. The Adirondack or Labrador whitefish is the most commonly distributed and caught form, though the Gulliver Lake whitefish appears to be the native Adirondack form. Historical references describe the [[Otsego Whitefish]] as "the world's greatest finned table delicacy."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Whitefish exhibit inoffensive, bovine feeding behavior, grazing on small organisms on or near the bottom. Preferred prey includes snails and crustaceans, though diet variety is considerable. Several instances of whitefish consuming considerable numbers of small fish have been recorded, though this is unusual.

The species demonstrates remarkable tolerance for conditions ruinous to carnivorous, aggressive [[Lake Trout]]. The ability to subsist on small bottom organisms provides a critical competitive advantage over fish-eaters.

Whitefish are fall spawners, depositing eggs over stony shoal areas similar to those selected by lake trout. Spawning of these two species sometimes occurs simultaneously on the same shoal, with developing eggs of both species found together under the same stones.

The species occurs in lakes with deep, well-aerated water. Introductions into suitable habitats have been generally successful.

## Key Findings

**Artificial Propagation**: Artificial propagation was successfully accomplished in 1858 and has been conducted regularly in New York hatcheries since 1890. All stocking uses eyed eggs or fry, as rearing to fingerling size in hatcheries has not been feasible. Fry stocking supplements natural production to maintain fishing quality.

**Distribution**: Whitefish occur in at least 48 New York lakes, including Lakes Erie and Ontario.

**Recreational Fishery**: Anglers typically fish over baited areas marked by buoys. Bait commonly consists of corn, rice, or oatmeal scattered near anchored buoys. Hooks are baited with small sections of cut fish. Successful whitefish anglers are specialists and comparatively few. The species' small, soft mouth results in many fish being hooked and lost. In most inland lakes, catch represents only a small fraction of available populations, though total yield is considerable.

**Commercial Fishery**: In Lakes Erie and Ontario, constant demand ensures good markets and consistent fishing effort. Large catch fluctuations are the rule rather than the exception.

- **Lake Erie** (New York waters, last 20 years): Range from 21,611 pounds (1935) to 433,081 pounds (1940)
- **Lake Ontario**: Range from 26,031 pounds (1943) to 147,709 pounds (1927)

Despite investigation by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], reasons for these fluctuations remain largely unknown. Much more basic research is required.

**Management Recommendations**: Ideal management requires fitting regulations, environmental control, and stocking to each situation. In the majority of whitefish lakes, better use of the resource depends primarily upon improved harvesting methods rather than increased stocking.
