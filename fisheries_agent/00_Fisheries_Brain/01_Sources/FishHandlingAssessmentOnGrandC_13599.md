# Fish Handling Assessment on Grand Canyon Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #humpback-chub

## Executive Summary

This memorandum, dated September 25, 1991, was written by the principal investigator of the [[BIO/WEST]] [[Humpback Chub]] studies to the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program manager and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] contracting officer's technical representative. It responds to a request for information on the potential impacts of field work on native and endangered fish in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. It summarizes eleven monthly trips (October 1990 through August 1991), reports handling totals, gear-specific results, and anecdotal observations of stress and injury, and describes steps taken to reduce stress. The author notes that full analysis awaits two more field trips and the 1991 Annual Report (February 1992). The memo reports little evidence of stress and no adverse effects from PIT tagging, while noting net scars, tag-related lesions, and three mortalities.

## Background & Description

The memo accompanies a copy of the Data Collection Plan, which includes a Fish Handling Protocol. The plan and associated protocols are to be updated for the 1992 Work Plan.

The study covers humpback chub (*Gila cypha*-group native cyprinid, as commonly referenced in the field; the document itself gives only the common name), an endangered native fish of the Colorado River. Other native species handled include [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Sport and predatory species, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Striped Bass]], and [[Channel Catfish]], were also encountered.

Field methods included:
- **Netting:** [[Gill Netting]] and [[Trammel Netting]]
- **Trapping:** [[Hoop Trap]] and [[Minnow Trap]]
- **Electrofishing:** [[Electrofishing]] with a Coffelt CPS system and spherical electrodes
- **Seining**
- **Tagging:** [[PIT Tagging]] and [[Radiotelemetry]] (radio implants with surgery)
- **Diet analysis:** [[Stomach Pumping]] as a nonlethal alternative to sacrifice

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal conditions:** Cold mainstem temperatures of 45–50°F were described as more conducive to handling than the warmer waters of some tributary streams.
- **Study area focus:** Mainstem Colorado River, with sampling at the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR).
- **Seasonal timing:** Work ran October 1990 through November 1991, with September 1991 data not yet entered in the database.
- **Stressors in the environment:** Entanglement in nets, air trapped in the intestine (possibly from air bladder pressure or swallowed air), and fatigue possibly linked to activity in the LCR.

## Key Findings

**Handling totals (October 1990 – August 1991, eleven trips)**

| Metric | Value (as reported) |
|---|---|
| Total humpback chub handled | 569 |
| Total recaptures (all marks) | 128 (21.9%) |
| Total PIT tagged by BIO/WEST | 468 |
| Recaptures on BIO/WEST PIT tags | 38 (8.1%) |
| Fish greater than 200 mm TL | 459 |
| Fish less than 200 mm TL | 110 |
| Radiotagged humpback chub | 42 (plus 6 in September) |

**Capture by gear type**
- Netting (gill and trammel): 484
- Trapping (hoop and minnow): 32
- Electrofishing: 81
- Seine: 2

*Note:* Gear totals sum to 599, which exceeds the 569 handled total. The memo does not explain the difference, so the discrepancy should be checked against the source database.

**Stress and injury observations**
- Little evidence of stress (lethargy, slimming, inability to upright, color change) was noted in humpback chub.
- Still photographs replaced video of each fish to reduce handling time.
- **Net scars:** Seen on humpback chub, flannelmouth suckers, bluehead suckers, and trout. They are superficial and not considered significant to health. Most marks came from 2-inch mesh gill nets, which were phased down. Trammel nets were judged less stressful because they entangle and hold fish before gilling occurs. Scar analysis will rely on photographs.
- **Electrofishing:** No burn marks and only one deformity were observed on humpback chub. The deformity was at the posterior margin of the dorsal fin on a fish of about 175 mm TL. The fish showed stress, was held overnight, and was swimming strongly the next morning. It did not appear to affect swimming and was released. Burn marks were reported on trout, but no percentage of occurrence has been analyzed.
- **PIT tagging:** No adverse effects were noted.
- **Old tags:** Open lesions and inflammation were reported on fish with [[Carlin Tags]], [[Floy Tags]], or scars from lost tags. These tags are being removed and fish are being PIT tagged.

**Mortalities (three reported)**
1. A recaptured radiotagged fish (PIT tag 7F7F3F3626, 432 mm TL).
2. A fish captured in a minnow trap (51 mm TL).
3. A fish found unentangled in a trammel net (PIT tag 7F7D08186A, 340 mm TL), at the LCR mouth. It may have drifted into the net already fatigued.

**Intestinal air trapping**
Humpback chub often have trouble upright-swimming because of air trapped in the intestine. Gentle abdominal massage helps them expel air and swim normally.

**Stomach pumping pilot study**
- Eight adult humpback chub were stomach-pumped in January 1991 to test feasibility for nonlethal diet analysis.
- No stress was observed, and the procedure appeared to relieve the trapped-air problem.
- One of the eight was recaptured later with no detrimental effects.
- Planned use extends to large predators (rainbow trout, brown trout, striped bass, channel catfish) to reduce sacrifice for gut examination.

**Radiotelemetry impact**
- A full evaluation of radiotelemetry impacts (implant and surgery) will be included in the 1991 Annual Report.
- Of 60 fish anticipated for radiotagging in year one, 48 were tagged. The author considered this sufficient to meet study objectives.

**Stress-reduction measures implemented**
1. Handling restricted to experienced biologists, with a senior biologist on every team. No volunteer or inexperienced handler handles fish unsupervised.
2. Video of each fish eliminated to reduce handling time.
3. All fish processed on site, except radiotag implants, to shorten holding time.
4. Constant recirculation in live wells holding many fish.
5. Gill and trammel nets checked at intervals of no more than 2 hours.
6. Electrofishing runs shortened to prevent crowding in live wells.
7. Radiotagging limited to the number needed for surveillance and monitoring.
8. Old Carlin and Floy tags removed and fish PIT tagged.
9. Stomach pumping adopted to avoid sacrificing fish for diet analysis.

**Conclusion:** The author concludes that the summary gives reviewers enough information to assess fish handling impacts on humpback chub in Grand Canyon. Final conclusions are deferred to the 1991 Annual Report.
