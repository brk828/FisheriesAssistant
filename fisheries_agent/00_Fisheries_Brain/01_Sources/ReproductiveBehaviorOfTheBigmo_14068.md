## Executive Summary
This note synthesizes a field study documenting the reproductive behavior of the [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*) in Crab Orchard Lake, Illinois, conducted 24–26 April 1982. The study represents one of the first direct, detailed field observations of spawning behavior for this species, which had previously been poorly described due to its typical inhabitation of turbid waters and the short duration of its spawning period. Spawning occurred in shallow (0.5–0.75 m), vegetated water at 18–20°C, involving group "rush" behavior, adhesive egg deposition on decaying vegetation, and no parental care.

## Background & Description
- **Sexual dimorphism (breeding season):** Males develop extensive breeding (nuptial) tubercles on the head, sides, and tail; females lack tubercles entirely.
- **Body size correlation:** Larger individuals within spawning groups (lacking tubercles) were inferred to be females; smaller, tuberculate individuals were inferred to be males.
- **Egg morphology:**
  - Shape: Spherical
  - Texture: Adhesive, opaque
  - Diameter: 2.4–2.7 mm (mean = 2.6 mm, N = 12)
  - Comparative data: [[Makeyeva 1981]] reported swollen egg membrane diameter of 1.4–1.8 mm (mean = 1.6 mm) in a separate study — roughly 1 mm smaller than eggs measured in this study, possibly reflecting pre- vs. post-swelling measurement differences or population variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat during spawning:** Shallow water (0.5–0.75 m) in the S end of "Grassy Bay," Crab Orchard Lake — a 2672-ha impoundment in Williamson Co., Illinois.
- **Substrate:** Hard-packed clay with some gravel, overlain by decomposing [[American Lotus]] (*Nelumbo lutea*), which served as the primary egg deposition substrate.
- **Spatial restriction:** Spawning was confined to a ~2–3 ha area near the center of the bay; no spawning was observed near the shoreline or in adjacent Grassy Creek.
- **Water temperature range at spawning:** 18.3–20°C (18–20°C range cited overall).
- **Temporal pattern:** Spawning activity occurred across a very brief window (24–26 April 1982), consistent with anecdotal reports from bow-hunters of a typical 2–3 day spawning period in southern Illinois.
- **Diel/weather independence:** Spawning was observed under both sunny and cloudy conditions, and at dawn, midday, and dusk — indicating no strict diel or weather-dependent spawning trigger.
- **Interspecific interaction:** Two [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) were found in the spawning area, potentially attracted by the disturbance to feed opportunistically on eggs — suggesting a facultative egg-predation interaction, though carp were not observed spawning.
- **Comparative regional data:** Other studies (Canfield 1922; Swingle 1957; Bardach et al. 1972) reported broader spawning temperature thresholds (14.4–26.7°C), suggesting geographic/regional plasticity in spawning temperature triggers relative to this Illinois population.
- **Saskatchewan comparison:** [[Johnson 1963]] found via gonadal examination that ripe/near-ripe males occurred from 30 April–7 July, and the last unspawned female was caught 23 May — indicating a much more protracted spawning season at higher latitude compared to the compressed 2–3 day window observed in Illinois.

## Key Findings
1. **Spawning group structure:** Typically triads — two tuberculate males aligned parallel alongside one larger, non-tuberculate female, all facing the same direction; groups occasionally included up to 5+ individuals.
2. **The "rush" behavior:** A rapid (2–3 sec), noisy surface rush covering 1–3 m, usually in a straight line (occasionally curved), during which dorsal fins and upper backs broke the surface — likely serving to synchronize gamete release and/or mechanically induce spawning.
3. **Post-rush behavior:** Spawning groups sank to the bottom, sometimes assuming a vertical "head stand" posture with tails breaking the surface accompanied by vigorous tail action — presumably corresponding to gamete release, though turbid water prevented direct visual confirmation.
4. **Simultaneous spawning intensity:** Over 100 spawning rushes were observed total; as many as 25 simultaneous spawning rushes were recorded within the observer's field of vision on peak days (24–25 April).
5. **No parental care:** Eggs were broadcast/scattered over decaying vegetation and left completely unattended; no nest preparation or site attachment behavior was observed.
6. **Behavioral consistency with historical accounts:** Findings corroborate early descriptions by Baird (in [[Mosher 1885]]) of *Ictiobus* spawning (female accompanied by 3–8 males, noisy surface rush, repeated performance) and are broadly similar to spawning behavior described for the related [[Black Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus niger*) by [[Yeager 1936]], where eggs were deposited on logs, bushes, and treetops in flooded swamp margins.
7. **Data gap addressed:** This study fills a significant literature gap, as no prior spawning behavior had been directly observed for *I. cyprinellus* (only indirect gonadal evidence existed from [[Johnson 1963]]) and none had been described at all for the [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*).
8. **Male tubercle function:** Breeding tubercles on the head, sides, and tail of males are hypothesized to function in maintaining physical contact with the female during the spawning rush, supporting a hypothesis previously proposed by [[Morris and Burr 1982]].
