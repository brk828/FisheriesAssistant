# Intentional Introductions Policy Review: Findings, Conclusions, and Recommendations (ANS Task Force, 1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-nuisance-species

## Executive Summary

This proposed Report to Congress, prepared by the [[Aquatic Nuisance Species Task Force]] under Section 1207 of the [[Nonindigenous Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act]] (P.L. 101-646, 1990), reviews approaches for reducing "adverse consequences associated with intentional introduction of aquatic organisms." The document defines "intentional" to include both direct introductions and indirect releases (escapes, accidental releases, aquarium dumping) from public and private facilities.

The report finds that nonindigenous aquatic species provide substantial socio-economic benefits, but are also a major factor in the loss of biological diversity. Its central finding concerns sport fishing: in the Task Force's analysis of extinction and endangerment records, introductions related to sport fishing dominate. The report makes eight recommendations, centered on education, research, federal authority, a federal import permit system, interjurisdictional panels, a [[Model State Code]], and industry codes of good business practices. Its guiding principles are that decisions should be based on ecosystem considerations and that recommendations should generally apply only to new introductions. A public comment period ran from August 1993 to October 25, 1993. A letter from W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University, 22 September 1993) criticizes the report for limiting its scope to new introductions and for leaving "ecosystem" undefined.

## Background & Description

**Document type:** Proposed Report to Congress, transmitted by the Co-Chairs of the ANS Task Force (Fish and Wildlife Service and NOAA) on 20 August 1993, with an enclosed Options Paper (Appendix A), appendices on the [[ICES Code of Practice]], the [[American Fisheries Society]] position on introduced aquatic species, the research protocol, and [[Executive Order 11987]], and a reprinted American Fisheries Society position statement on [[Transgenic Fishes]].

**Legal basis:** Section 1207 of the [[Nonindigenous Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act]] (signed 29 November 1990). The Act defines a nonindigenous species as "any species or other viable biological material that enters an ecosystem beyond its historic range." The Task Force interprets "historic range" as the area occupied at the time of European colonization of North America.

**Process and participation:**
- Initial Committee meeting: November 1991.
- Contact list: over 350 names by December 1991; over 450 names for the Options Paper mailing list.
- Public meeting, 26 February 1992 (Washington, D.C.): 25 participants.
- Four public meetings on the Options Paper, June-July 1992: Portland (OR), Vicksburg (MS), Valrico (FL), and Baltimore (MD).
- Total participants: over 200 individuals, groups, and organizations, including fish and wildlife agencies from nearly every State.
- Recommendations presented to the Task Force on 20 November 1992.

**Key definitions adopted:**
- *Intentional introduction:* "The knowing import or introduction of nonindigenous species into, or transport through, an area or ecosystem where it was not previously established." Escapes and releases are treated as the "virtual inevitable consequence" of an intentional introduction.
- *Aquatic nuisance species:* a nonindigenous species that threatens "the diversity or abundance of native species or the ecological stability of infested waters," or activities dependent on those waters.
- *Species:* includes subspecies, populations, and stocks. Transfers of differentiable stocks of the same species count as introductions.
- *New introduction:* movement of a species into an ecosystem where it does not presently occur. Ongoing introductions into ecosystems where the species is already established (e.g., [[Brown Trout]] in the Madison River, MT) are largely excluded from the recommendations.

**Scope exclusions:** Microbial and transgenic organisms were not covered by the specific recommendations. The Task Force considers transgenic organisms to be nonindigenous species and recommends that NRC or OTA review them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Economic dependencies on nonindigenous species:**
- **Aquarium trade:** Florida growers sold $7.1 million of imported tropical fish for resale in 1991, plus nearly $33 million in Florida-produced tropical fish (Florida Agricultural Statistics Service 1992). Conniff (1989) reported that 1 of every 11 U.S. homes held aquaria, and that hobbyists had "spent about $1 billion" on aquarium livestock, equipment, and food.
- **Aquaculture:** U.S. aquaculture production in 1988 totaled over 289,000 metric tons, valued at $539 million (National Research Council 1992). [[Channel Catfish]] are cultured mainly within their native range but are also produced outside it (e.g., California). Rainbow trout producers were located in 48 of 50 States in 1987. [[Pacific Oyster]] (*Crassostrea gigas*) underpins much Pacific Coast marine aquaculture. Tilapia and carp are not native anywhere in the United States.
- **Sport fisheries:** Brown trout, native to Europe and western Asia, are widely introduced. Salmon in the Great Lakes, not native there, support a "multi-billion dollar" fishery. Millions of anglers fish for [[Largemouth Bass]] outside its native range.

**Escape and containment:**
- Shelton and Smitherman (1984) state that "escape is virtually inevitable" in fish culture.
- Welcomme (1988) concludes that aquaculture species "eventually escape."
- Tilapia have been prohibited from introduction in several States because they are aggressive and compete with native species for spawning sites and space (Parker 1989).

**Pathogens and genetics:**
- *Gyrodactylus salaris*, a monogenean parasite, devastated wild and farmed Atlantic salmon in Norway (Johnsen and Jensen 1991).
- Infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus appears to have been introduced into Japan with sockeye salmon eggs in the 1970s and has since affected native masou salmon.
- The American crayfish introduced to Europe carried *Aphanomyces astaci* (crayfish plague). The fungus has little impact on the American crayfish in its native range but has eliminated native crayfish from much of Europe (Thompson 1990).
- Hatchery and aquaculture strains may reduce wild fitness, cause loss of genetic diversity through interbreeding, and produce outbreeding depression (Waples 1991).

**Aquatic plants and other organisms:**
- [[Hydrilla]] has spread through intentional (aquarium dumping) and unintentional (boat trailers) transport, clogging water bodies.
- Schardt and Schmitz (1990) reported that most of Florida's nonindigenous aquatic plants "were deliberately transplanted."
- [[Melaleuca]] was imported to Florida for forestry and has extensively invaded its wetlands.

**Jurisdictional and habitat context:** Many introductions fall between federal and State jurisdiction, including interstate waters, waters that feed interstate systems, and State waters that may affect federally significant resources such as national parks and marine sanctuaries. The report notes that habitat alteration (channelization, dam construction) often coincided with introductions, making it difficult to separate their effects.

## Key Findings

**1. Extinctions (Miller et al. 1989, analyzed in Table 1):**
- Factors were analyzed for three genera, 27 species, and 13 subspecies of North American fishes extinct over the past 100 years.
- Habitat degradation was cited in 73% of extinctions.
- Introduced species were cited in 68% of extinctions.
- Table 1 totals: 2 (7.4%) unintentional, 6 (22.2%) unclear intent, and 19 (70.4%) intentional introductions.
- "Over two-thirds" of cases citing introduced species were apparently intentional.

**2. Endangered Species Act listings (Table 2):**
- Adequate information existed for 69 of 92 U.S. species.
- Habitat degradation: 63 of 69 cases (91%).
- Pollution: 28 of 69 cases (41%), comprising 27 cases as cause of decline and one as potential threat.
- Introduced species: 48 of 69 cases (70%), comprising 40 as cause of decline (58%) and 8 as potential threat (12%).
- Sport fishing introductions were cited in 35 of the 48 introduced-species cases (73%).
- Most-cited groups: Centrarchidae (bass and sunfish), with [[Largemouth Bass]] the most-cited individual species; then Ictaluridae (catfish and bullheads); then bait species, led by red shiner (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) and fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*).
- [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] were cited in seven and six cases, respectively, mainly for hybridization or predation.
- Biological control ([[Mosquitofish]], *Gambusia*), ornamental, and private aquaculture introductions were also cited.

**3. Stated policy position:**
- The Task Force supports education, cooperation, and accountability over punitive measures.
- It does **not** support total bans on nonindigenous species use, extensive federal permitting of interstate movements, or Federally mandated Model Code linked to fines or withheld funds.
- Prevention is considered key; most recommendations center on the decision-making process.

**5. Proposed permit system (Recommendation 4A/4B):**
- Applications would include source, characteristics, health status, purpose, destination, containment conditions, and disposal plans.
- Direct open-water releases would require an environmental assessment (e.g., following the ICES protocol).
- Species with little information would require stricter containment.
- Categorical exclusions could apply to well-documented species (e.g., tropical aquarium fishes in northern States) or to species already established in an area.
- Exceptions to prohibitions would require an interagency multidisciplinary panel.

**6. Model State Code components (Table 3):** Authority over importation, transport, possession, and introduction (including aquarium species); approved, restricted, and prohibited lists; permits; environmental evaluation (including pathogen status and availability of native alternatives); consultation with affected jurisdictions; public involvement; stock-to-stock genetic integrity clauses; containment for facilities; introducer responsibility for escapee control; monitoring; and, if federally incentivized, information sharing and escape notification.

**7. Examples from States:**
- **Texas:** a prohibited-list approach, with all other species reviewed.
- **Illinois:** an approved list, with an interagency advisory committee for aquaculture species.
- **Florida:** draft rules requiring hurricane contingency plans and banning release of nonindigenous marine animals. Outdoor facilities holding restricted species must have levees at least one foot above the 100-year flood level, and the State monitors and removes nonindigenous aquatic plants.
- **Hawaii:** cost-based fines for escapes that trigger eradication programs.
- **Montana:** prohibits transplants unless scientific investigation and public hearing show no significant harm.
- **Arizona:** new species introduced into the Colorado River drainage require advance approval of the Colorado River Wildlife Council.

**8. Limits of the report's scope:** The Task Force did not give a State-by-State summary of regulations, which it considered too fluid. It deferred to the forthcoming OTA study (expected summer 1993).

**9. Critical comment (W. L. Minckley, 22 September 1993):**
- The report is "a solid document" within its self-imposed limits, but its focus on new introductions leaves existing sport-fish versus native-fish conflicts unaddressed.
- The term "ecosystem" is undefined and open to convenient interpretation, for example a State declaring itself an ecosystem.
- Interjurisdictional problems should be handled by panels that include "professional, objective, and non-aligned (non-agency) biologists," which recommendation 6A does not specify.
- Gaps remain in managing organisms already in place and in balancing non-native species against biodiversity values.
