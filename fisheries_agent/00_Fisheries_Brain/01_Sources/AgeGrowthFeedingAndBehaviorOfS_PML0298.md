# Age, Growth, Feeding, and Behavior of Sacramento Squawfish in Bear Creek, Colusa County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the life history of the [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*), a large predatory cyprinid endemic to the middle and lower elevation streams of California's Sacramento-San Joaquin, Pajaro-Salinas, and Russian river drainages. Fieldwork was conducted in [[Bear Creek]], a small stream in Colusa County where squawfish are often abundant. The author assessed age and growth, feeding, and behavior to supply basic life history data for a habitat where little such information existed. The study found a flexible growth pattern in young-of-year fish, a diet that shifts from insects to fish as squawfish grow, and sedentary, nonaggressive behavior in large adults. The author argues these traits together explain the species' success in small streams.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The Sacramento squawfish is a large, predatory cyprinid. Its published life history is limited to an early study by [[Tait and Murphy]] (1950) and several distributional studies.
- **Life strategy:** The species is thought to partition food and space among life stages and to combine long life with moderate fecundity. The author notes these claims could not be fully evaluated with existing data.
- **Prior research:** Most work has focused on interactions with [[Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus* sp.) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), or on distribution and abundance within California drainages. Several of these studies, including Taylor et al. (1982) and Moyle et al. (1982), describe the squawfish as one of the most abundant fishes in waters ranging from intermittent streams to large rivers, and as the top predator in these systems.
- **Objective:** To examine squawfish life history in a small stream supporting large numbers of squawfish, and to provide comparative data for future studies. Traits examined were age, growth, feeding, and behavior.

**Study site:** Bear Creek lies at an elevation of 456 m on the eastern slope of the coastal mountains in Colusa County. It flows about 30 km south to its confluence with [[Cache Creek]], a tributary of the Sacramento system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Bear Creek held a large population of small mature fish. Older fish were rare, with fish older than age IV seldom found.
- **Trophic role:** Squawfish occupy the top predator niche in these streams. Diet changes with body size, so the species uses different food resources at different life stages.
- **Behavioral context:** Large squawfish were sedentary during summer daytime observations, showing little movement within or between pools. Their nonaggressive behavior toward conspecifics and similar-sized [[Sacramento Sucker]] (*Catostomus occidentalis*) suggests reduced competition for space in small streams.
- **Seasonal feeding:** Feeding intensity varied through the year, which is discussed in the Key Findings below.

## Key Findings

**Age and growth**
- Young-of-year squawfish in Bear Creek grew throughout summer and into early fall.
- Mean back-calculated lengths ranged from 65 mm SL (standard length) at age I to 257 mm SL at age IV.
- Mean back-calculated lengths differed among year-classes only at age I, ranging from 55 mm SL (1979 year-class) to 84 mm SL (1977 year-class).
- Fish older than age IV were rare, while many small mature fish of ages III and IV were present.

**Feeding and diet**
- Young squawfish (≤100 mm SL) consumed mainly insects and occasionally small fish.
- Fish became the dominant prey, making up more than 50% of diet by weight, at 100 to 150 mm SL.
- Diet diversity calculated by size class (based on summed contents of all individuals in a size group) was highest for fish 51 to 100 mm SL.
- Individual diet diversity (based on individual gut contents) did not differ among size classes.
- Stomach fullness of fish ≤100 mm SL declined from May through December 1980.
- Individual diet diversity of fish ≤100 mm SL did not change over the same period.

**Behavior**
- Large squawfish (100 to 500 mm SL) were sedentary during summer daytime observations, with little movement within or between pools.
- Large squawfish did not interact aggressively with each other or with similar-sized Sacramento sucker.

**Conclusions**
- The author attributes the species' success in small streams to three traits: a flexible growth pattern in young-of-year fish, partitioning of food among life stages, and the sedentary, nonaggressive behavior of large squawfish.
- The study's data are intended to inform future comparative research on squawfish life history in small streams.
