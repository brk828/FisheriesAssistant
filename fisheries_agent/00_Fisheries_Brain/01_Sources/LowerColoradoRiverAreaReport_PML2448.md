# Desert Fishes Council 2001 Symposium: Native Fish Status in Chihuahua and the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Proceedings of the Desert Fishes Council (Volume XXXII, 2001 Symposium, published December 15, 2002) contains two linked items. The first is a Chihuahua, Mexico survey of fish assemblages in the endorheic [[Cuenca Guzmán]] basin, conducted in 1990. The second is a multi-agency status report on native fishes of the [[Lower Colorado River]] and its delta. Both report native fish declines driven by habitat loss, nonnative species, pollution and overharvest. The lower Colorado report also documents an active recovery program covering barriers, nonnative removal, renovation and reintroductions.

## Background & Description

**Chihuahua survey (Cuenca Guzmán).** The native ichthyofauna of the endorheic systems of the Cuenca Guzmán in northern Chihuahua has been reduced and is being displaced by nonnative fishes. A 1990 basin-wide survey aimed to determine the status of the [[Chihuahua Chub]] (*Gila nigrescens*). The English and Spanish texts differ slightly on the river name (Santa María in the English, Santa Clara in the Spanish summary), and the Spanish summary is the more complete account of the sampling.

**Lower Colorado River report.** The report is a compilation of agency updates (USDA Forest Service, Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona State University, and Nevada Department of Wildlife). A century ago the free-flowing lower Colorado River and delta supported a suite of indigenous fishes. Today that community is largely gone: most species are extirpated, others are threatened or endangered, and none is self-sustaining in the long term. The lowermost river and delta in Mexico are described as dying from water deprivation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drivers of decline:** Habitat destruction, degradation and fragmentation, pollution, nonnative species and overharvest are identified in both regions as threats to native biodiversity.
- **Management conflict:** Priorities differ among states, federal agencies, Indian tribes, municipalities and private irrigation and domestic water users. The authors state that sport fishing and native fish needs are in direct opposition, with little hope of compromise.
- **Policy shift:** A revised [[Biological Opinion]] on the [[Central Arizona Project]] moved emphasis from general recovery and nonnative control toward a focused approach on barriers, renovation and repatriation.
- **Nonnative pressure:** Predators and competitors such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Red Shiner]] and [[Common Carp]] are repeatedly cited as limiting native recovery, for example red shiner contributing to low [[Woundfin]] numbers.

## Key Findings

### Cuenca Guzmán, Chihuahua (1990 survey)

- **Río Santa María:** Of seven native species previously reported, five were found. Native species recorded were Chihuahua chub, Rio Grande sucker, red shiner, and Mexican stoneroller, with a *Cyprinodon* sp. (an undescribed pupfish) encountered uncommonly.
- **Nonnative species:** Black bullhead (*Ameiurus melas*), western mosquitofish and common carp were recorded, with black bullhead, common carp and western mosquitofish occurring at most sites sampled.
- **Río Casas Grandes abundances:** Red shiner and Rio Grande sucker were common. Chihuahua chub and Mexican stoneroller were uncommon. Fathead minnow and *Cyprinodon* sp. were rare.
- **Tributaries:** Nonnative rock bass, bluegill and largemouth bass were found in Río Casas Grandes tributaries.
- **Outlook:** The authors expect continued habitat loss, pollution, nonnative species and overharvest to reduce native diversity over time, even though most documented natives persist.

### Lower Colorado River and Delta (agency updates)

- **Overall status:** Native fishes are essentially gone from the reach, and the report calls for management that moves beyond general recovery.
- **[[Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area]]:** 8,309 native fish were collected, including longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*), Sonora sucker and desert sucker. About 18,217 nonnative fish were also collected (figure from the Spanish summary; the English OCR is partly illegible), with red shiner the most abundant species.
- **Upper Verde River:** Small native fishes remain rare. Native species made up about 21% of abundance during spring sampling. Cattle grazing was removed from most US Forest Service lands along the river. No speckled dace or spikedace were captured.
- **Fossil Creek:** The Childs-Irving project planned full flows to return in 2004. The Arizona Game and Fish Department declined to support needed renovation for native fishes, a reversal of its earlier position.
- **Barriers and renovation:** Barriers were added to mitigation procedures at six locations, and two barriers were built in Aravaipa Creek. Feasibility studies were done for barriers on Fossil and Coniz creeks and the East Fork White River. The [[U.S. Geological Survey]] La Crosse lab was developing alternative piscicide and control techniques.
- **[[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** Mark-recapture, nonnative removal and larval surveys were completed in the Virgin River. A total of 5,100 woundfin were released.
- **[[Virgin Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*):** Numbers were down from previous years.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** 2,121 averaging about 10 inches were stocked into the Verde River, with none captured there or in the Salt River afterward. Forty were stocked into Lake Mohave, 2,130 into the Colorado River at Parker Strip, and 62 were captured at Lake Havasu. Restored off-channel areas appear to provide favorable habitat, though recruitment at Senator Wash Reservoir is questionable.
- **[[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*):** 5,209 were stocked into Lake Mohave in 2000 and none in 2001. Lake Havasu received 1,557 in 2000 and 1,287 in 2001. Ten were reared to 300 mm at Davis Cove.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*):** The lower basin status was reviewed, and a comprehensive bibliography was compiled covering biology, ecology, life history, habitat and taxonomy of roundtail chub and the related *G. nigra*.
- **[[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*):** Six streams were evaluated for reintroduction. The population remains stable in Sabino and Lousy canyons.
- **Spinedace:** Razorback-sized and [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*) efforts included stocking 99 fish into Yeager Canyon. Flagstaff Arboretum pond was renovated to remove fathead minnow.
- **[[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae apache*):** Fish from federal and state hatcheries were stocked into White Mountains streams. Renovations were carried out at Pistol Butte, Wohlenburg, Draw and Flash creeks. Genetic analysis is ongoing. Spruce Creek-lineage Gila trout fingerlings were placed in Raspberry Creek, a Blue River tributary. The Dude Creek population has been monitored quarterly since September 1999, with fish persisting but no reproduction documented.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*):** Cold Spring and Finley Tank retain the species, which may be stocked into Lousy Canyon.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):** Fish from a coastal spring in the Sancayetano Mountains were stocked into Lousy Canyon, the first reintroduction into a natural setting in more than seven years. Breeding and increased presence were documented. A Safe Harbor Agreement allows stocking into flood control ponds managed by the Arizona Department of Transportation. This was the first Gila topminnow stocking on National Forest System lands since 1985. At Sharp Spring the species makes up under 5% of the fish community, and Heron Spring retains only Gila topminnow.
- **Nonnative fishes in the lower basin:** Juvenile blue tilapia (*Oreochromis aureus*) were collected below the Bunkerville diversion, and flathead catfish were noted as predators in restored off-channel habitats.
