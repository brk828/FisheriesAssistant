# Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River #recovery-plan

## Executive Summary

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is the largest cyprinid in North America, historically reaching lengths of nearly 1.8 m and weights exceeding 36 kg. This voracious predator served as the top carnivore of the [[Colorado River]] system. Listed as endangered in 1967 and protected under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, the species has experienced dramatic population declines and range contractions. Historically abundant throughout the mainstem Colorado River and major tributaries from Wyoming to the Gulf of California, the species is now restricted primarily to the upper basin in Colorado and Utah. This recovery plan, prepared by the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team and approved by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1987, outlines comprehensive strategies for population monitoring, habitat protection, propagation, reintroduction, and public education necessary to prevent extinction and achieve recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] belongs to the genus *Ptychocheilus*, known from fossil records dating to the Miocene. It is the largest of four living species in this genus and the largest minnow (Family [[Cyprinidae]]) in North America. The species exhibits an elongated, dorsoventrally compressed pike-like body with a long, flattened head. The mouth is large and nearly horizontal with pharyngeal tooth formula 2,5-4,2 and long, slender gill arches adapted for grasping prey. The dorsal fin has nine rays positioned posteriorly with origin slightly behind pelvic fin insertion. Scales are small and embedded, numbering 80-95 in the lateral line. Adults display strong countershading: dark olivaceous backs becoming lighter along sides and culminating in white bellies. Young typically have a dark, wedge-shaped spot at caudal fin insertion.

Historically, [[Colorado Squawfish]] were distributed throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]], including the mainstem from Wyoming to the Gulf of California in Mexico, and major tributaries including the [[Gunnison River]], [[White River]], [[Yampa River]], [[Dolores River]], [[San Juan River]], [[Uncompahgre River]], [[Animas River]], [[Gila River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]]. Early records indicate abundance sufficient that stranded fish were used as animal food and fertilizer in Arizona. The species enjoyed a favorable reputation as food and game fish, commonly called "white salmon of the Colorado" or simply "salmon."

Present distribution is severely restricted to the upper Colorado River system in Colorado and Utah. Recent populations exist in: the [[Green River]] from the mouth of the Yampa River to its confluence with the Colorado; the lower 199 km of the Yampa River and [[Lodore Canyon]]; the [[White River]] in Utah and Colorado; the [[Duchesne River]]; [[Lake Powell]]; and the mainstem [[Colorado River]] from Palisade to Lake Powell. Spawning has been confirmed in the lower 32 km of the Yampa River and in [[Gray Canyon]] of the Green River. The species is considered extirpated from the [[Gila River]] system (last specimen 1950), the lower Colorado River mainstem below Glen Canyon Dam, and the Green River above [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Colorado Squawfish]] evolved in the harsh, dynamic environment of the historic Colorado River—a system characterized by extreme flow variability (3,000 to 380,000 cfs), high turbidity (0.62% silt content by volume), elevated mineral salts, and dramatic temperature fluctuations. The species developed as the top carnivore in an assemblage of uniquely adapted native fishes including [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*).

Habitat preferences vary by life stage. Young-of-the-year (up to 64 mm TL) and juveniles (65-200 mm) occupy shallow backwater areas with little or no current over silt and sand substrates. At approximately 200 mm TL, coinciding with the shift to piscivory, fish select deeper water with greater velocity. Adults inhabit large-river environments across various depths and velocities over silt, sand, gravel, and boulder substrates. Seasonal habitat use varies substantially: during peak runoff, adults move to backwaters or flooded riparian areas; after runoff subsides, they occupy run-riffle areas for spawning; post-spawning, adults use deeper pool reaches for feeding and overwintering.

Temperature preferences determined in laboratory studies show juvenile squawfish acclimated to 20°C preferred 26.6°C, while those acclimated to 26°C selected 23.7°C and those at 14°C selected 21.9°C. Adults selected temperatures ranging from 21.5° to 25.7°C with optimal preference at 25.4°C. Optimum growth of 45-100 mm TL fish occurred at 25°C. Field observations indicate spawning occurs between late June and mid-August when water temperatures reach 18-25°C, with peak activity at 22-25°C.

[[Colorado Squawfish]] exhibit complex migratory behavior. Radiotelemetry studies document migrations up to 320 km, both upstream and downstream, to reach restricted spawning areas. Definite homing behavior has been documented for some individuals, though this may reflect limited spawning habitat availability rather than true homing. Not all adults migrate annually, possibly because they are immature or do not spawn every year. Two major spawning sites have been identified: the lower 32 km of Yampa River canyon and Gray Canyon of the Green River.

The species displays ontogenetic dietary shifts. Fry feed on zooplankton and insect larvae. At approximately 60 mm length, fish begin consuming other fish, which comprise nearly 86% of the diet by 22-59 mm TL, with [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) as major prey. By 200 mm TL, the diet becomes almost exclusively piscivorous. Adults feed on most native fishes and many introduced species present in the river system.

Growth rates show mean back-calculated total lengths at annulus formation: Age I = 44 mm, II = 95 mm, III = 162 mm, IV = 238 mm, V = 320 mm, VI = 391 mm, VIII = 499 mm, X = 600 mm. Wild fish do not mature until reaching 400 mm total length at Age VI. Hatchery-reared fish mature at Age V (males) and Age VI (females).

Larval drift patterns vary by location. In the Green and Yampa Rivers, larvae appear to drift downstream upon hatching and rear in different reaches from spawning sites. However, in the Colorado River, similarities between larval and post-larval distributions suggest little drift occurs between spawning and rearing areas. The most important rearing area in the Colorado River extends from Moab, Utah, to the confluence with the Green River (River Miles 0-60).

Tributaries play critical roles in the species' life history. The [[Yampa River]], [[White River]], and [[Duchesne River]] provide essential spawning, feeding, and overwintering habitat. Movement of juveniles from the Green to White River and downstream movement of larvae from the Yampa to Green River have been documented. During 1977, a low-flow year in the Yampa, young-of-the-year were not collected in downstream nursery areas where they were present during normal water years, suggesting Yampa River flows are important to reproductive success in downstream Green River areas.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Decline**: The [[Colorado Squawfish]] has experienced catastrophic population declines and range contractions. The species is extirpated from the entire lower Colorado River basin, including the [[Gila River]] system (no specimens since 1950), and from upstream reaches above major reservoirs including the Green River above [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], Wyoming, and the San Juan River above [[Navajo Reservoir]], New Mexico. Current populations are restricted to approximately 1,800 km of the upper basin in Colorado and Utah.

**Causes of Decline**: Multiple interacting factors contributed to decline:

1. **Habitat Loss and Fragmentation**: Over 20 dams constructed on mainstem and tributaries since 1913 have created a series of reservoirs connected by altered stream reaches, blocking migration routes and eliminating spawning and rearing habitat.

2. **Hydrologic Alterations**: Dams have replaced natural flood-drought cycles with stable discharges driven by irrigation and hydroelectric demands. Flow modifications have altered channel morphology, reduced backwaters and sloughs used as nursery areas, and changed sediment transport regimes. High flows in 1983-1984 resulted in negligible recruitment, with Age-0 fish growing to only 25 mm TL by October, suggesting low survival probability.

3. **Thermal Regime Changes**: Cold tailwaters from hypolimnetic releases have eliminated populations below dams. Below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], altered temperatures were the major factor in loss of native fishes from 105 km of the Green River. Below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], thermal changes caused vast faunal modifications in 389 km of the Colorado River through Marble and Grand Canyons.

4. **Water Quality Changes**: Reservoirs trap nutrients and sediments formerly carried downstream. Water passing from hypolimnion layers carries far less dissolved materials and fine particulates. Reduced turbidity may affect feeding efficiency and competitive interactions. Channel erosion below dams changes substrates from shifting sand to armored cobble and boulder.

5. **Introduced Species**: Approximately 60 nonnative fish species have been introduced to Arizona waters and 42 to the upper basin (of 55 total species). Top predators including largemouth and smallmouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*, *M. dolomieui*), sunfishes (*Lepomis* spp.), striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*), walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*), and catfishes (*Ictalurus punctatus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*), plus competitors such as threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*) and red shiner now dominate reservoir and stream habitats. Native species evolved in harsh Rio Colorado environments appear ill-adapted to interactions with nonnative species.

**Life History Requirements**: Spawning requires specific conditions: water temperatures of 18-25°C (peak 22-25°C), suitable substrate in run-riffle areas, and adequate flows. Spawning occurs late June through mid-August. Larvae require warm water temperatures for growth and survival; reduced growth at lower temperatures during high-flow years results in small fish (25 mm TL) by October with presumably low overwinter survival. Young-of-the-year and juveniles require shallow backwaters with minimal current over silt substrates. Adults require large-river habitats with variable depths and velocities, seasonal access to spawning areas, and migration corridors potentially extending 320 km.

**Critical Habitats**: Primary essential habitats identified include: (a) Green River from confluence with Colorado River to Echo Park; (b) lower 240 km of Yampa River; (c) lower 240 km of White River; (d) Colorado River from Palisade to Lake Powell. Two confirmed spawning sites exist: lower 32 km of Yampa River canyon and Gray Canyon of Green River. Major rearing areas include the Colorado River from Moab to the Green River confluence (RM 0-60) and areas downstream from the Dolores River, between Dolores confluence and Westwater Canyon, and downstream from the Gunnison River confluence.

**Recovery Objectives**: The species will be considered eligible for reclassification to threatened when naturally self-sustaining populations are maintained in the upper Colorado River basin and further fragmentation is prevented in the primary habitats listed above. Downlisting in the lower basin requires meeting upper basin criteria. Delisting in the upper basin requires: (a) downlisting criteria met; (b) primary essential habitats, migration routes, river flow and water quality parameters legally protected; and (c) lower basin delisting criteria met. Lower basin delisting requires either: (a) naturally self-sustaining populations established in three lower basin areas with legally protected habitats, or (b) determination after 10 years of reintroduction efforts that suitable habitat no longer exists (species declared extinct in lower basin).

**Recovery Actions**: The plan outlines four major recovery components:

1. **Determine Population Status and Life History**: Locate and monitor all populations; compile historical data; survey potential habitats; determine population dynamics and define self-sustaining populations; expand life history information including spawning requirements, feeding behavior, age distribution, growth rates, and migration patterns; develop fish passage requirements.

2. **Implement Management Plan**: Monitor and assess impacts of development projects on flow, temperature, and water quality; identify and assess impacts of nonnative species competition; study parasitism and disease; enforce existing laws (ESA, Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act, Lacey Act); ensure Section 7 ESA compliance by Federal agencies; assess effectiveness of current regulations; discontinue introductions of detrimental nonnative species; identify and monitor essential habitat; determine critical biological, chemical, and physical habitat components; establish criteria for suitable habitat; manage and restore primary habitat; develop methods to restore movement where dams restrict passage; determine effectiveness of habitat enhancement.

3. **Reintroduce into Historic Range**: Establish hatchery facilities at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico (broodstock holding) and construct new FWS facility for propagation; develop propagation techniques to maximize production; maintain diversified gene pool; inventory and select reintroduction areas; restore or prepare stocking sites; develop stocking and monitoring plans; determine stocking rates and sizes; conduct stocking program; monitor reintroduced populations. In 1985, over 96,000 fingerlings and 442 larger fish (355-405 mm TL) were stocked in the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]], Arizona, with preliminary data indicating survival of at least 5 months for larger fish.

4. **Information and Education**: Conduct nationwide and local programs to advise public of aesthetic and recreational value of Colorado squawfish and preservation efforts; emphasize potential sportfishing value and "good-eating" reputation from early literature; inform agencies of enforcement responsibilities.

**White River Status**: The [[White River]] was impounded by [[Taylor Draw Dam]] in 1984 above Rangely, Colorado. The Recovery Team maintains that the lower 240 km may remain important for recovery. Before recovery objectives can be amended, either: (a) upstream and downstream passage must be restored and scientific data must document that essential habitats are not impaired by the dam, or (b) scientific data must document that loss of this range and population will not impair species recovery. Pre- and post-impoundment studies (1983-1985) captured 41 Colorado squawfish and sighted an additional 21 in the 125-mile reach between the reservoir and the Colorado-Utah state line, with 69% observed within 10 miles below the dam.

**Cultural and Economic Importance**: Historically, Colorado squawfish were important food sources for Native Americans and early settlers along the lower Colorado River and tributaries. Commercial fishermen operated on the lower Salt River from settlement until about 1910 and in Salt River Canyon until the 1930s or longer. Individuals weighing 2.7 to 15.4 kg were commonly caught along the lower Colorado River prior to 1949. The species has substantial sportfishing potential given its large size, willingness to take artificial lures, and palatability. Arizona plans to reintroduce and manage Colorado squawfish for sportfishing in the Salt River. The proposed fishery management plan for [[Kenney Reservoir]] on the White River in Colorado includes development of a sportfishery for the species.

**Implementation Priorities**: Priority 1 actions (essential to prevent extinction) include population monitoring, spawning site protection, flow maintenance, and broodstock maintenance. Priority 2 actions (maintain current status) include habitat monitoring, Section 7 consultations, and hatchery production. Priority 3 actions (full recovery) include reintroductions, habitat restoration, and public education. Estimated costs are provided in 1987 values. Cooperating agencies include U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Reclamation, National Park Service, and state wildlife agencies from Colorado, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, and California.

**Research Needs**: Additional information is needed before specific quantifiable recovery goals can be established. Interim objectives include obtaining and analyzing population data to define viable, self-sustaining populations and establish specific quantifiable goals for downlisting and delisting. Critical research needs include: reliable aging techniques; understanding of competition with nonnative species; effects of parasitism and disease; water chemistry tolerance levels; instream flow requirements; effectiveness of fish passage devices; habitat enhancement techniques; optimal stocking rates and sizes; and genetic diversity requirements for hatchery broodstocks (number of brood fish needed for natural genetic diversity for at least 20 generations).
