# Suitabilities and Relative Resistances of Twelve Fish Species as Bioassay Animals for Oil-Refinery Effluents

#ichthyology #toxicology #bioassay #Centrarchidae #Cyprinidae #pollution-ecology

## Executive Summary

This research evaluated twelve fish species for their suitability as bioassay animals for testing [[Oil-Refinery Effluent]] toxicity. The study, conducted at [[Oklahoma State University]] from February 1960 through December 1961 under a National Institutes of Health grant, assessed each species based on collection ease, laboratory maintenance, transport survival, and test reactions. Median tolerance limits (TL₉₆) were calculated for each species and subjected to statistical analysis. [[Lebistes reticulatus]] (guppy) served as the reference species. Results showed [[Esox lucius]] was the most sensitive (approximately one-fourth as resistant as the guppy), while [[Fundulus kansae]] and the guppy showed similar resistance. Three species—[[Menidia audens]], [[Notropis girardi]], and [[Notropis stramineus]]—were rated as excellent bioassay animals, combining good ecological suitability with measurable sensitivity to effluents.

## Background & Description

The study investigated species from multiple families: [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes), [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows), [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), [[Esocidae]] (pikes), [[Fundulidae]] (killifishes), [[Atherinidae]] (silversides), [[Poeciliidae]] (livebearers), and [[Cichlidae]] (cichlids). Test specimens averaged 0.6-2.2 grams in weight and 4.0-6.7 cm in total length. Oil-refinery effluents came from crude units, coking units, catalytic crackers, catalytic reformers, thermal reformers, and compound houses collected June-December 1961.

Effluent characteristics: pH 8.9-10.0 (average 9.4), ammonia 65.0-94.0 ppm (average 76.9), phenol 4.2-29.0 ppm (average 11.4), sulfide 0.0-1.6 ppm (average 0.22), chemical oxygen demand 260-465 ppm (average 326). Dilution water from [[Lake Carl Blackwell]] had pH 7.9, hardness 142.0 ppm, dissolved solids 218.0 ppm.

Species descriptions included:
- [[Chaenobryttus gulosus]] (warmouth): distributed central and eastern United States, found in ponds and lakes with dense vegetation
- [[Esox lucius]] (northern pike): northern United States and Canada, prefers clean waters with weed beds
- [[Fundulus kansae]] (plains killifish): Great Plains region, shallow streams
- [[Hybognathus hankinsoni]] (brassy minnow): Great Lakes drainage to Colorado and Kansas
- [[Ictiobus cyprinellus]] (bigmouth buffalo): Mississippi Valley, large rivers and oxbow lakes
- [[Lepomis humilis]] (orangespotted sunfish): North Dakota to Texas, sluggish pools
- [[Menidia audens]] (Mississippi silversides): Lower Mississippi Valley
- [[Notropis girardi]] (Arkansas River shiner): Arkansas River Basin
- [[Notropis stramineus]] (sand shiner): Rocky to Appalachian Mountains, sandy streams
- [[Notropis whipplei]] (steelcolor shiner): Mississippi Valley, swift water
- [[Pimephales vigilax]] (bullhead minnow): Mississippi River system, sluggish pools
- [[Tilapia nilotica]] (Nile tilapia): African native, introduced to southeastern United States 1957

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Test procedures followed [[Doudoroff Method]] bioassay protocols. Fish were held minimum 10 days prior to testing when practical. Holding tanks treated with terramycin to prevent finrot bacterial infection. Exploratory bioassays preceded full-scale tests to determine concentration ranges. Full-scale bioassays used 10 fish per concentration, two replications, with survival counts at 1, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 96 hours.

Dissolved oxygen maintained above 2.0 ppm throughout tests using oxygen bubbling when necessary. Temperature held constant at approximately 75°F. Tests terminated when all fish died or at 96 hours. Species showed varying oxygen tolerance: [[Chaenobryttus gulosus]] survived 1.0 ppm, [[Esox lucius]] survived 1.4 ppm, [[Tilapia nilotica]] survived 1.2 ppm.

Transport methods varied: plastic bags with oxygen atmospheres or water tanks with oxygen through breaker stones. [[Menidia audens]] failed transport in plastic bags but succeeded in oxygenated tanks. Holding periods ranged from one week to over five months depending on species.

Food requirements varied: some species required live food ([[Daphnia magna]]), others accepted dry food, [[Esox lucius]] required fish prey. Species requiring frequent feeding and showing high respiration rates were in poor condition after 96-hour tests even in controls.

## Key Findings

**Suitability Rankings:**

*Excellent bioassay animals:*
- [[Menidia audens]]: frequents shallow water, does not outgrow test size, transported well, ate both dry and live food, remained quiet in tests, no oxygenation required
- [[Notropis girardi]]: does not outgrow usable size, minimal transport mortality, adapted well to holding, ate dry food immediately, no oxygenation required
- [[Notropis stramineus]]: suitable size maintained, transported and held well, dry food sufficient, survived low oxygen tensions

*Good bioassay animals:*
- [[Chaenobryttus gulosus]]: prolonged spawning provides continuous supply, transported and held well over one month, required live food, withstood low oxygen
- [[Lepomis humilis]]: prolonged spawning period, seldom outgrows test size, transported and held well, required live food, remained quiet, low oxygen tolerance

*Fair bioassay animals:*
- [[Notropis whipplei]]: initially difficult to adapt (snout injuries first two weeks), required frequent feeding with dry food, nets needed for jumping, oxygenation required
- [[Pimephales vigilax]]: transported well, ate dry food, held three weeks, undesirably nervous in tests, nets required, some oxygenation needed
- [[Tilapia nilotica]]: cannot survive below 48°F outdoors, transported and held excellently, dry food sufficient, leaping required nets, difficult to distinguish dead from live fish

*Poor bioassay animals:*
- [[Esox lucius]]: suitable size available only briefly, required fish food, reacted violently to stimuli throughout six-week holding, high respiration required oxygenation, leaping behavior
- [[Fundulus kansae]]: required much attention, holding water fouled quickly, held poorly, nets required, oxygenation required
- [[Hybognathus hankinsoni]]: difficult to seine, did not hold well, became thin and shrunken, could not survive on laboratory diets
- [[Ictiobus cyprinellus]]: frequent water changes needed, susceptible to parasites (Lernaea sp.), easily disturbed, nets required, oxygenation needed

**Relative Resistance Rankings (96-hour median tolerance limits):**

Statistical analysis (two-way classification, Doolittle technique, Duncan's multiple range test) showed significant differences among species (P < 0.0005). Ranked from most to least resistant:

1. [[Lebistes reticulatus]] (reference): TL₉₆ = 22.46% effluent
2. [[Fundulus kansae]]: TL₉₆ = 22.20% (99% of reference)
3. [[Tilapia nilotica]]: TL₉₆ = 20.16% (90% of reference)
4. [[Ictiobus cyprinellus]]: TL₉₆ = 20.10% (90% of reference)
5. [[Lepomis humilis]]: TL₉₆ = 17.19% (77% of reference)
6. [[Notropis stramineus]]: TL₉₆ = 17.19% (77% of reference)
7. [[Chaenobryttus gulosus]]: TL₉₆ = 16.55% (74% of reference)
8. [[Pimephales vigilax]]: TL₉₆ = 15.66% (70% of reference)
9. [[Hybognathus hankinsoni]]: TL₉₆ = 13.93% (62% of reference)
10. [[Menidia audens]]: TL₉₆ = 12.37% (55% of reference)
11. [[Notropis whipplei]]: TL₉₆ = 12.26% (55% of reference)
12. [[Notropis girardi]]: TL₉₆ = 11.94% (53% of reference)
13. [[Esox lucius]]: TL₉₆ = 6.30% (28% of reference)

Most resistant species was nearly four times as resistant as most sensitive. Distribution was normal with few resistant, few sensitive, and majority intermediate.

**Methodological Observations:**

Test solution pH initially alkaline (8.3-8.9) due to effluent and dilution water, shifted toward neutrality (7.2-7.6) during tests from food wastes, respiration CO₂, and effluent compound volatility. Some species reduced pH faster than others.

Species with high metabolic rates depleted oxygen rapidly. [[Ictiobus cyprinellus]], [[Tilapia nilotica]], and [[Notropis whipplei]] required oxygenation of all test solutions and controls. Species with low respiration rates ([[Menidia audens]], [[Notropis girardi]], [[Notropis stramineus]]) more advantageous where oxygenation facilities unavailable.

Behavioral responses varied: some species jumped in toxic solutions requiring cover nets ([[Esox lucius]], [[Fundulus kansae]], [[Notropis whipplei]], [[Pimephales vigilax]], [[Tilapia nilotica]]). Others remained calm ([[Lepomis humilis]], [[Menidia audens]]). [[Tilapia nilotica]] showed spiraling movements and loss of equilibrium before death.

Water quality issues: [[Fundulus kansae]] and [[Ictiobus cyprinellus]] caused water "fouling" (brown color, odor, milky appearance with foam) requiring frequent changes. [[Hybognathus hankinsoni]] and [[Fundulus kansae]] collected from high-salinity waters may have suffered from low total dissolved solids in holding water.

Four tests conducted per species (except [[Esox lucius]] with three due to specimen shortage) using different effluent samples. 48-hour tests produced TL₄₈ values similar to 96-hour TL₉₆ values, suggesting shorter test duration feasible for oil-refinery effluents.
