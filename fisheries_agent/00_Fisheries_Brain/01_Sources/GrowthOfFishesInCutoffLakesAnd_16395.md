# Growth of Fishes in Cutoff Lakes and Streams of the Little River System, McCurtain County, Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 survey by the [[Oklahoma Fisheries Research Laboratory]] examined the fish community of the [[Little River System]] in southeastern Oklahoma. A five-person crew sampled the main rivers, sixteen tributaries, and ten [[Cutoff Lakes]] during June and July 1955. The study compared species composition and growth rates across three habitat types to judge whether cutoff lakes support distinct fish populations and whether annual hatchery stocking is justified. The authors found that growth rates were broadly similar across habitats, and they concluded that stocking the cutoff lakes is unnecessary and futile because seasonal flooding flushes these pools and replaces their populations each year.

## Background & Description

The study area lies in the south-central [[Ouachita Mountains]] of [[McCurtain County]]. Oak-pine forests cover boulder-strewn sandstone and shale slopes, and loblolly pine-hardwood forests occupy the thin, poorly drained bottomland soils. Ridge crests rise 400 to 1,000 feet above the valleys, reaching a maximum of 2,900 feet above sea level. Spring floods regularly inundate the bottomlands and cutoff lakes for extended periods. [[Lumbering]] is the main land use, and only about 10 percent of the area is in farm crop production.

The streams are locally known for [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Green Sunfish]] angling. Long-time residents report that fishing success has declined steadily since lumbering began in 1912, and that most small tributaries have stopped flowing in summer over the past 80 years. The [[Oklahoma Game and Fish Department]] receives yearly requests to stock these waters, but the need for stocking has long been questioned. The study aimed to document the principal species in each habitat and compare their growth rates to guide future management.

**Habitat characteristics**
- **Tributaries:** steep gradients, bedrock bottoms with large boulders, and little gravel or rubble.
- **Lower Little River:** moderate gradient with somewhat finer bottom materials.
- **Cutoff lakes:** 6 to 12 feet deep, steep-sided, with gravel and silt bottoms, and surrounded by dense button-bush (*Cephalanthus*) and cutgrass (*Zizaniopsis*).

**Water chemistry**
- Tributaries were slightly acidic (pH 6.2–6.8) and soft (total alkalinity 16–45 ppm).
- Principal rivers were slightly higher (pH 6.6–7.2; alkalinity 6–56 ppm).
- The eight cutoff lakes sampled averaged pH 7.1 and alkalinity 34 ppm, both slightly higher than in the flowing streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Fish were collected with [[Rotenone Sampling]], small-mesh seines, and experimental [[Gill Netting]]. Scale and spine samples were taken from representative individuals of each species, and length-weight data were recorded for the rest. Small specimens were preserved in 10 percent formalin. Age was determined by counting and measuring [[Scale Annuli]] on magnified scale images (×45). Lengths at each year of life were estimated with a nomograph that assumed a zero intercept. Growth rates are based on grand average calculated total lengths and exclude age-groups at capture.

**Sample composition.** The totals include only measured fish and exclude minnows, darters, and other small species. Sample sizes were:
- Tributaries: 3,932 fish (sixteen sites sampled by rotenone and seining)
- Main rivers: 713 fish (lower Mountain Fork and Little Rivers, gill nets only)
- Cutoff lakes: 3,201 fish (ten lakes of 1 to 18 surface acres, rotenoned fully or partially)

**Fishery composition by habitat (percent of catch, as reported):**

| Habitat | Sport fishes | Pan fishes | Coarse fishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main rivers | 10 | 30 | 60 |
| Tributaries | 12 | 85 | 3 |
| Cutoff lakes | 20 | 30 | 50 |

The authors noted that the high coarse-fish share in the main rivers may be unrealistic because gill nets were the only gear used there. With matched sampling, the sport and coarse fish proportions in the main rivers and cutoff lakes would probably have been more similar.

**Species distribution.** The authors reported that numbers of largemouth bass, smallmouth bass, bluegill, and green sunfish differed significantly among the three habitat types (no test statistics are given in the text). Largemouth bass and bluegill were most numerous in cutoff lakes, smallmouth bass were most abundant in tributaries, and green sunfish were most common in tributaries. Other taxa noted in Table I include [[Spotted Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], pickerel, several bullheads, gars, buffalo, carpsuckers, and redhorse. Sunfish (notably longear sunfish) dominated some tributary samples.

## Key Findings

**Growth rates**
- Growth was similar across all three habitat types for the eight species compared. Main streams grew slightly faster and tributaries grew slowest.
- Channel catfish were the only species with a marked difference, growing faster in cutoff lakes than in the rivers.
- After slow first- and second-year growth, the game fishes of the Little River System closely match or exceed the Oklahoma stream-fish state average.

**Game fish growth (time to reach the 10-inch legal length)**
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: slow first-year growth of 3.5 to 4.5 inches; legal length reached in the third summer.
- [[Spotted Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]]: four summers required.
- [[Channel Catfish]]: fastest-growing game species; legal length reached in the third summer, with notable individual variation in both river and lake habitats.

**Panfish and other growth**
- [[White Crappie]] and [[Black Crappie]]: very slow first-year growth; about 8 inches reached late in the third or early in the fourth summer.
- [[Bluegill]]: very few individuals exceeded 5.0 inches, and those were at least three years old.
- [[Green Sunfish]]: individuals over 5 inches were common, averaging 6.4 to 7.3 inches at the end of year three. This explains their prominence in anglers' creels.

**Management conclusions**
- Frequent flooding flushes the cutoff lakes and introduces a new stream-fish population each year. The authors therefore judged that [[Stocking Management]] of the cutoff lakes is unnecessary and futile.
- Dominance of green sunfish and smallmouth bass in the tributaries and upper reaches suggests that hatchery-reared fish have habitat requirements the streams do not meet.
- Surveys found ample natural reproduction of all sport fishes.
- The decline in fishing success over the previous 25 years is more likely due to watershed land-use practices that altered stream habitat than to increased angling pressure or poor natural reproduction.

**Relevance to future reservoirs.** The data were intended to help predict the fish resource that would develop in the proposed [[Flood Control Reservoirs]] on these rivers.
