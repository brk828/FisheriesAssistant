# Relict Fauna of Ennerdale Water: A Problem of Distribution

## Executive Summary
This 1959 *Nature* communication by W. Pennington addresses a biogeographic puzzle: the presence of two crustaceans, **[[Mysis Relicta]]** and **[[Limnocalanus Macrurus]]**, in Ennerdale Water — a freshwater lake in Cumberland, England, situated 368 ft above sea level. These species are typically diagnostic of "relict lakes" in Scandinavia and the Baltic that were once part of arctic/sub-arctic seas during the last glacial period. Because Ennerdale's elevation far exceeds any plausible glacio-isostatic sea-level fluctuation, earlier authors (Eastwood, Tattersall) had speculated that seawater was forced up the valley by ice, potentially explaining the presence of these marine-relict crustaceans. Using stratigraphic and pollen-analytic evidence from lake-bottom sediment cores, Pennington demonstrates conclusively that **no seawater was ever present in Ennerdale Water** during the relevant glacial or post-glacial periods, refuting the marine-incursion hypothesis and leaving the origin of the relict fauna as an open distributional problem.

## Background & Description
This paper is geological/paleoecological rather than morphological in focus; however, key organismal evidence includes:

- **[[Mysis Relicta]]**: A glacial relict mysid shrimp, believed derived from the marine ancestral form *Mysis oculata*, modified during the freshwater Ancylus Lake phase in the Baltic region.
- **[[Limnocalanus Macrurus]]**: A relict calanoid copepod, similarly derived from the marine *Limnocalanus grimaldii*, undergoing analogous freshwater modification during the Ancylus Lake stage.
- **Diagnostic freshwater microfossils** used to reconstruct paleosalinity:
  - **[[Melosira Arenaria]]** — a freshwater diatom, one of the first colonizing taxa as glacial conditions ameliorated (Zone I → Zone II transition).
  - **[[Fontinalis Squamosa]]** — an aquatic moss found in epiphytic association with *M. arenaria*, notably in calcareous springs today.
  - Additional late-glacial pollen assemblage: *Betula* (excluding *B. nana*), *Myriophyllum alterniflorum*, *Filipendula ulmaria*, *Thalictrum*, *Artemisia*, *Juniperus*, *Helianthemum*, Gramineae, and Cyperaceae.
- **Diatactic (banded) varve clays**: Sedimentological "morphology" of lake-bottom deposits — each varve shows a coarse lower layer and fine upper layer with a sharp boundary. Scandinavian geological research (cited) shows these varves form *only* in fresh water, since salinity exceeding 1/50 of normal seawater causes flocculation and disrupts this banding, producing an unsorted single layer instead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Relict lake fauna**: *Mysis relicta* and *Limnocalanus macrurus* are characteristic indicator species of so-called "relict lakes" — water bodies that were once arms of the sea (the **[[Yoldia Sea]]**) and became isolated as freshwater bodies (the **[[Ancylus Lake]]** stage) following post-glacial changes in relative land/sea level, primarily documented in the Baltic region.
- **Ennerdale Water's ecological trajectory**: Contrary to the Baltic relict lakes, stratigraphic and diatom evidence shows Ennerdale Water has remained a **deep freshwater lake of approximately its present shape** continuously since the early post-glacial period (Pollen Zones IV–VII), with no phase of brackish or marine water intrusion.
- **Glacial paleo-lake context**: Ennerdale is geologically unique among English Lake District lakes for having hosted **Glacier-Lake Ennerdale**, a proglacial lake formed during retreat stages of the New Drift Glaciation, dammed by Irish Sea ice. This paleolake drained completely before the subsequent Scottish ice re-advance and never re-formed — meaning it cannot have served as a conduit for trapped seawater.
- **Comparative stratigraphic correlation**: The Ennerdale core's pollen and diatom stratigraphy (Zones I–VII) closely parallels the well-documented sequence from **[[Windermere]]** (north basin) and **Kentmere**, reinforcing a shared purely-freshwater climatic and depositional history across the Lake District.
- **Sediment community structure**: Diatom assemblages in Zone II (post-Zone I amelioration) and later zones are dominated by freshwater and epiphytic (on freshwater plants) species, with no planktonic marine organisms or brackish indicators detected at any depth, including in the deep eastern basin (despite slumping disturbance of some strata there).

## Key Findings
1. **No marine incursion occurred**: Diatactic varve clays from Zones I and III, dating to the earliest late-glacial phase, are proven (via Scandinavian sedimentological studies) to form only in fresh water — directly disproving the hypothesis that Scottish ice re-advance trapped seawater in Ennerdale.
2. **Chronological impossibility of Tattersall's hypothesis**: Glacier-Lake Ennerdale (the New Drift Glaciation proglacial lake) had already drained and disappeared long before the Scottish ice re-advance, invalidating any proposed mechanism linking that re-advance to introduction of seawater via the glacier-lake.
3. **Complete freshwater continuity through the critical isolation period**: During Pollen Zones IV–VI — the exact interval when Baltic relict lakes were undergoing land/sea-level driven isolation of *Mysis* and *Limnocalanus* — Ennerdale Water's sediments show abundant freshwater diatoms throughout, with the Boreal/Atlantic transition (Zone VI/VII) clearly marked at 450 cm depth, proving continuous deep freshwater conditions.
4. **Eastern basin corroboration**: Even in the deep upper eastern basin, where slumping disturbed some late-/post-glacial strata, underlying horizontal laminated clays contain no organic remains and confirm the absence of seawater; overlying organic muds match Windermere's freshwater diatom record.
5. **Unresolved biogeographic problem**: Since the sedimentological evidence firmly excludes any marine or brackish phase in Ennerdale's history, the presence of *Mysis relicta* and *Limnocalanus macrurus* cannot be explained by the same relict-lake (marine isolation) mechanism proposed for Baltic lakes — the paper explicitly leaves the question of how these species colonized Ennerdale unresolved, framing it as a distinct "problem of distribution."
6. **Methodological contribution**: The study demonstrates the power of combining **[[Pollen Analysis]]**, diatom stratigraphy, and varve sedimentology for reconstructing paleosalinity and paleolimnological history, correlating Ennerdale's record with the broader Late-glacial and post-glacial stratigraphic framework established for **[[Windermere]]** and other Lake District sites (per Godwin & Eastwood's zonation).
