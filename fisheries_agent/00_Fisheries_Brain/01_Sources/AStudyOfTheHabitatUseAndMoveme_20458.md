# Habitat Use and Movement of Rare Fishes in the Green River, Jensen to Green River, Utah (August-September 1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by Paul B. Holden (BIO/WEST, Inc.) for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Western Energy and Land Use Team (Contract No. 14-16-0009-77-959, dated October 28, 1977) examines habitat use, movement and potential spawning areas of four rare fishes along 175 miles of the [[Green River]] from Jensen to Green River, Utah. Sampling took place in August (Jensen to Sand Wash) and September (Desolation and Gray canyons) 1977. The study focused on young [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]], which are federally listed as endangered, and also addressed [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], which were rare and under consideration for listing. The work was conducted under a low-water year and was intended to inform recovery planning and water-development decisions.

## Background & Description

The upper [[Colorado River]] basin supports two endangered fishes, the Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the humpback chub (*Gila cypha*), and two rare species, the bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The report states that the principal research need is to define the requirements for subsistence of these species. The author identifies two factors as most important for self-sustaining populations: (1) adequate spawning and rearing habitat, and (2) interaction with exotic fishes. The lower and middle Green River in Utah was described as the least altered large-river habitat in the upper basin and as the only area with reproducing Colorado squawfish populations, whereas adults elsewhere show a lack of recruitment.

Study objectives were to:
- Determine the physical and chemical parameters of habitats used by rare fishes, with emphasis on young fish.
- Determine whether spawning occurs in the study area and locate sites.
- Determine movements of the size (age) classes encountered.
- Identify fish species associated with the rare species.
- Assess the relative importance of river habitats to rare fishes.

Methods used boats (Jensen to Sand Wash) and a rubber raft (Sand Wash to Green River). Habitats were classed as backwaters, eddies, runs and riffles. Measurements included velocity (Marsh-McBirney model 201 meter), maximum depth, substrate, temperature and turbidity. Fish were collected mainly by seining, supplemented by electrofishing and a fry shovel. Preferred habitat was expressed as electivity curves under the Cooperative Instream Flow Service Group methodology. Rare fishes over about 100 mm TL were tagged with numbered fingerling tags, and fish under 100 mm were marked by anal fin clipping.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions.** Water temperature varied strongly on a daily cycle. Early-morning temperatures were 3 to 5 C cooler than late afternoon. Backwaters were the warmest sites, often 3 to 8 degrees warmer than the main river in late afternoon. Morning temperatures near Jensen in August were 15 to 17 C, and 19 to 21 C downstream. A September low of 10.5 C was recorded in Desolation Canyon (River Mile 30). Daily water-level fluctuation from releases at [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] was greatest at Jensen (16 inches) and only 3 to 4 inches in Desolation Canyon. Flows were lower than normal in 1977, and the Yampa River was exceptionally low.

**Turbidity and chemistry.** Turbidity was highest after rainstorms, and the report attributes this to an easily eroded watershed. Dissolved oxygen, pH, alkalinity, ammonia, nitrate, nitrite, sulphate, conductivity and salinity were measured, and the author reports they changed little during the study and stayed within the normal range. Because habitats were chemically similar, fish could not select chemical conditions.

**Associated fauna.** Nineteen species totaling more than 19,500 specimens were collected. [[Red Shiner]] was the most abundant (13,711), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (918) was the most abundant native species. [[Carp]] (388), [[Channel Catfish]] (947) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (1,951) were common. Bonytail chub was the rarest native fish, with two individuals. Smallmouth bass, green sunfish and northern pike were also noted.

**Backwater dynamics.** Backwaters were classed as short-lived (changing with a 6-inch rise or fall) or long-lived (protected from minor level changes). Young squawfish and humpback chubs used both types, young sunfish and bullheads used only long-lived backwaters, and fatheads were most abundant in long-lived sites. The 1977 low water extended the life of long-lived backwaters.

## Key Findings

**Sample effort and distribution**
- More than 330 samples were taken: 125 in runs, 121 in backwaters, 59 in eddies and 19 in riffles.
- Young-of-the-year (YOY) Colorado squawfish (35-64 mm) occurred only in the lower study area. Juveniles (65-200 mm) were concentrated at the upper and lower ends, and six subadults were captured, three of them in a 3-mile section near Ouray.
- Six YOY [[Razorback Sucker]] were tentatively identified from lateral line scale counts (80-88). The report describes them as likely the first young razorbacks recorded from the upper Colorado basin, pending taxonomic confirmation. Only two adult razorbacks were seen.
- Chubs in Desolation and Gray canyons were provisionally referred to humpback chub, although some features resembled roundtail chub. One adult and one juvenile bonytail chub were identified.

**Habitat preferences**
- YOY squawfish (30-64 mm) were found only in backwaters with no current, firm silt substrate and 1-2 ft depth.
- Juvenile squawfish (65-200 mm) also preferred silt-bottomed backwaters with no current, but were less selective for depth.
- Subadult and adult squawfish (200+ mm) used a range of velocities and depths and preferred sandy eddies and runs over backwaters.
- YOY razorbacks preferred eddies with about 0.2 fps velocity, 1.0 ft depth and soft silt.
- YOY humpback chub preferred backwaters with no current and firm silt, but also used eddies and runs at 0.4-0.8 fps.
- Adult humpback chub were mainly in sand-bottomed eddies and runs.
- No size class of squawfish or humpback chub selected temperature.
- Electivity reliability (CISFS criteria): YOY squawfish and humpback chub data were rated "good"; the other curves were rated "fair."

**Movement**
- Twenty juvenile squawfish were tagged and 13 more were anal-fin clipped. Four subadults were tagged. One tagged juvenile was recaptured at its release site 4.5 hours later.
- A 2.5-day study in one backwater seined five times and captured 4, 31, 29, 11 and 12 YOY squawfish. Recaptures were 0, 1, 1 and 1 in the last three samples. Marked fish were not found nearby, and the results were judged inconclusive.
- Partial anal fin clipping was preferred over Bismark brown Y dye, which caused stress within 5 to 15 minutes.

**Spawning**
- Squawfish spawning in 1977 was inferred to occur in Gray Canyon, below Range Creek, based on the concentration of YOY there. Spawning above that point was not evident.
- Razorback spawning was inferred near and just below Ouray, and humpback chub spawning in Desolation and Gray canyons.
- Early-August temperatures near Jensen (15-20 C) were below the reported squawfish spawning threshold of 21 C, which may explain the absence of spawning upstream.
- Rare fishes spawn at 55-70 F according to earlier literature.

**Competition and predation**
- Red shiners were the most frequent associate of all rare-fish size classes (75-100% of samples).
- YOY squawfish were most often found with red shiners, fathead minnows, juvenile carp and YOY channel catfish.
- Exotic species dominated YOY collections of both squawfish and humpback chub.
- Smallmouth bass were considered a potential predator, but the author expected turbidity and river conditions to limit their reproduction.

**Management implications**
- Pump intakes for Ouray National Wildlife Refuge ponds and irrigation should avoid slow backwaters and be placed in swift runs.
- Artificial long-lived backwaters could increase exotic fish as well as rare fish, so the author cautioned against them. Short-lived backwaters may be sufficient for YOY squawfish.
- Changes in flow must be considered carefully, since reproduction in 1977 occurred only near Green River, and the author questions whether 1977 low flows reduced spawning-rearing habitat.
- The author states that several years of data are needed for excellent reliability of seasonal estimates for juvenile and adult fish.
