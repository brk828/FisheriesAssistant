# Desert Fishes Council 1992 Abstracts: Gila Chub Studies in the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

These abstracts from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] 1992 proceedings cover four topics. A population genetics abstract models mitochondrial DNA diversity in a "Mohave" population, where the species is not named in the text. An electroshock study examines injury in [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) from the Colorado River. Two studies on [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) address seasonal population estimates in the [[Little Colorado River]] and the species' present distribution in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]]. The work supports conservation and management of these endangered [[Gila Chub]] species.

## Background & Description

- **Genus context:** Both fishes belong to the genus *Gila* in the family Cyprinidae, the minnows and carps. The proceedings are devoted to the [[Desert Fishes]] of the American Southwest.
- **Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*):** Restricted to tight, canyon-bound reaches of the Colorado River and its tributaries. It reaches its greatest abundance in the lower basin, where it uses the Little Colorado River for spring reproduction and summer residence.
- **Roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*):** Studied as adults collected from the Colorado River near the Colorado/Utah state line.
- **Study methods mentioned:** [[Mark-Recapture]] sampling, hoop and trammel nets, [[Electrofishing]], necropsy, X-ray photography, the CAPTURE program for population estimation, and [[Stepping-Stone Model]] analysis of genetic data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Little Colorado River (LCR):** A tributary in the northern Grand Canyon that serves as the main spring spawning and summer residence habitat for humpback chub in the lower basin.
- **Study reaches on the LCR:**
  - Confluence, 0.0 to 1.4 km upriver
  - [[Powell Canyon]], 1.5 to 7.0 km above the confluence, on the Navajo Indian Reservation
  - [[Salt Canyon]], 8.0 to 15.0 km above the confluence, on the Navajo Indian Reservation
- **Mainstem distribution:** Chubs were found from River Mile (RM) 57.0 to 221.8. Concentrations occurred near tributary mouths and canyon reaches, including [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Shinumo Creek]], [[Blacktail Canyon]], [[Middle Granite Gorge]], [[Havasu Creek]], and [[Pumpkin Spring]].
- **Genetic context:** The first abstract concerns a "Mohave" population whose mtDNA diversity is unusually high. The authors link this to a possibly declining population of older individuals and to post-glacial colonization events.

## Key Findings

**1. Genetic diversity in the "Mohave" population (no species or sample size given)**
- Unusually high mtDNA diversity is consistent with a population that is not declining but is composed only of older individuals.
- Simulations show that Nei's diversity measure stays high despite a steady loss of genotypes from the population. It drops rapidly only when the population nears extinction.
- A [[Stepping-Stone Model]] with migration estimated as Nm (approximately 10) can explain the diversity pattern. Hypothesized post-glacial colonization events are compatible with this pattern but are not required to explain it.

**2. Electroshock effects on roundtail chub**
- Sample: 40 adult roundtail chub, according to the English text. The Spanish summary says 14 fish, so the figure is inconsistent in the source and should be checked against the full paper.
- Gear: pulsed DC output of 120 V and 8 amps.
- No abnormal behavior was noted immediately after shocking.
- Internal necropsy found hemorrhaging around the spine in 5% of the fish.
- X-ray photographs showed no dislocated or splintered vertebrae.
- The Spanish summary reports no burns and does not give a hemorrhage percentage.

**3. Monthly population estimates for humpback chub in the LCR**
- Sampling: monthly from July 1991 to the present, using hoop and trammel nets, with fish PIT/tag-marked and recaptured.
- Modeling: the best-fitting of 7 models in the CAPTURE program was applied. Fish within each reach were treated as a closed population, which is justified because sampling periods lasted only 6 to 8 days.
- Seasonal patterns in 1992:
  - Confluence density peaked in early March as adults staged for upriver movement, then declined by May.
  - Powell Canyon density peaked in late March and declined gradually into July.
  - Salt Canyon density peaked in April and stayed high through the summer.
- Conclusion: chubs migrate into the LCR in spring and remain in the upper reaches through summer.

**4. Present mainstem distribution of humpback chub**
- Sample: 1,392 humpback chub were captured from October 1990 through June 1992. Of these, 95% (1,324 fish) were found in a 13-km reach between RM 57 and 65, roughly equidistant upstream and downstream of the LCR confluence at RM 61.3.
- Bimonthly sampling between RM 56 and 226 showed distribution from RM 57.0 to 221.8.
- Concentrations occurred downstream of the LCR at several tributary and canyon sites, listed above.
- The present distribution is compared with historical records in the paper.

**Management implications:** The authors state that understanding migration timing and seasonal density is essential for managing this endangered species. Electroshock injury data suggest that shocking surveys of roundtail chub carry a measurable risk of spinal hemorrhage.
