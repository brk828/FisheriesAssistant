# Fishes of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a monograph-style regional fish survey, *Fishes of Arizona* by W. L. Minckley, published 30 June 1973 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department. It covers the state's inland fish fauna: habitat types, identification keys to families and species, collection and preservation methods, and detailed species accounts with distribution maps. The fauna is a mix of a small native assemblage (about 8 or 9 original families, many of them endemic desert and Colorado River taxa) and a large, rapidly growing group of introduced sport, bait, forage and aquarium fishes. The text stresses that native fishes are declining or near extinction, and that introductions drive much of that loss. Source material is largely the book's front matter, habitat introduction, identification and collection guidance, and species accounts; the source is a scanned book with OCR noise, and the structure below follows its content.

## Background & Description

- **Scope and authorship:** Minckley (Department of Zoology and Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory, Arizona State University) compiled information from literature, museum specimens, and field collections, including Arizona Game and Fish Department and federal agency collections. The book is 1–293 pages with 127 figures and 42 distribution maps.
- **Fauna size:** At the time of writing, at least 22 families of bony fishes were in Arizona or its nearby waters. The original fauna included "at best 8 or 9 families," with 3 entering only the lowermost Colorado River.
- **Fish habitat types** described in the introduction:
  - Flowing streams and rivers (the Colorado River mainstream, Gila, Little Colorado, Salt, Verde, San Pedro, Santa Cruz).
  - Springs and cienegas, many small and local.
  - Closed basins (Willcox Playa, Hualapai Dry Lake), which were essentially fishless historically.
  - Artificial reservoirs (Lake Powell, Mead, Mohave, Havasu; Salt River lakes Roosevelt, Apache, Canyon, Saguaro).
  - Canals and ditches, and low-elevation ponds or "tanks."
- **Taxonomic approach:** The book follows the American Fisheries Society list of common and scientific names. Each species account gives identification features, status, distribution maps and biology. Keys use fin-ray and scale counts, pharyngeal teeth, and body proportions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat modification:** Dams, impoundments, channelization, groundwater pumping, grazing and drought have changed most native habitats. The lower Colorado River, once a braided channel with marshes and oxbow lakes, is described as having lost its native fauna. Many smaller rivers and creeks are now dry except in wet years.
- **Thermal and flow regimes:** Dam-regulated waters are colder in summer and warmer in winter, with reduced turbidity and more stable flows. Native fishes evolved in turbulent, silt-laden flows and are poorly suited to these changes.
- **Introductions:** Sport, bait and aquarium releases, some illegal, have added many species. Examples cited include golden shiner and red shiner (competitors with native spinedace and trout), threadfin shad (forage added to reservoirs), smallmouth bass (reproductive impacts on native chub larvae in the Black River), walking catfish (a pest risk), and northern pike.
- **Native-fish decline:** Colorado River squawfish, razorback sucker, woundfin, spikedace, Gila trout and Arizona trout are discussed as declining, endangered, or in restricted refuges. Hybridization with rainbow trout threatens native trout, and stocking persistently swamps native genomes.
- **Bait trade:** Transport of bait minnows is identified as a major vector for spreading unwanted species.

## Key Findings

- **Native fish conservation is urgent:** Several native taxa (Colorado River squawfish, woundfin, spikedace, Virgin River spinedace, Little Colorado spinedace, Yaqui chub and Yaqui sucker) are described as endangered, extirpated from much of their range, or extinct in the United States.
- **Introduced species dominate:** The introduced component is large, and many taxa are now established in reservoirs and at low and mid elevations. Golden shiners, carp, mosquitofish and sunfishes are among the widespread introductions.
- **Forage and game management trade-offs:** Threadfin shad were introduced to feed game fishes (largemouth bass and crappie grew faster after establishment), but they may compete with young game fish for plankton in small lakes. Coho and sockeye salmon were stocked for sport and as predators of shad; natural reproduction of coho in the Colorado River is judged unlikely.
- **Fish identification recommendations:** Specimens should be preserved in 10 percent formalin (later transferred to alcohol), with field notes recording locality (township, range, section, county, date and collector), habitat and color notes, since preservation fades colors. Hybrids are common among sunfishes and trouts, and a fish that cannot be identified should be sent to a specialist.
- **Priority research gaps:** The Colorado River mainstream, especially the Grand Canyon and Lake Powell, is poorly sampled. Life histories for many native species (for example, the bonytail chub, humpback chub and Colorado River squawfish) are incomplete.
- **Special features of key taxa:**
  - Colorado River squawfish was once the top predator of the basin, reaching about 1.85 kg in the Green River in the study cited and historically far larger. Essentially extinct in Arizona by the early 1970s.
  - Flathead catfish, introduced to the Gila system and the lower Colorado, is a predator of other fish and an established sport species.
  - Gila trout and Arizona trout (*Salmo gilae* and *Salmo apache*) are native high-elevation trouts whose persistence depends on removing introduced salmonids and on hatchery propagation.
  - The Colorado River was historically a salmon-named fishery ("white salmon"), and early residents harvested squawfish, humpback suckers and bonytails in large numbers for fertilizer and food.
