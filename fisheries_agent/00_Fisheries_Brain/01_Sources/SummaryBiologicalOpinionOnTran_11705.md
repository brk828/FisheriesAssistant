# Biological Opinion on Central Arizona Project Water Delivery to Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #endangered-species-act #non-native-invasions

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion (dated April 15, 1994) addresses the transportation and delivery of [[Colorado River]] water through the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) aqueduct to water users in the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona and New Mexico. The opinion focuses specifically on the potential for CAP operations to introduce and spread non-native aquatic species into habitats occupied by seven federally listed species: threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*); endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*).

The FWS determined that the proposed action is likely to jeopardize the continued existence of spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, and razorback sucker, and is likely to adversely modify critical habitat for spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker. The opinion is not likely to jeopardize desert pupfish, Colorado squawfish, or bald eagle. A comprehensive Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) was developed requiring construction of four drop-structure barriers, continued operation of three existing electrical barriers, monitoring programs, annual transfer of $500,000 for 25 years for native fish conservation and research/non-native fish control, and implementation of public education programs.

## Background & Description

### Listed Species Descriptions

**[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*): Listed as threatened July 1, 1986. A small silvery fish with a well-developed dorsal fin spine. Originally distributed throughout much of the Gila River drainage above Phoenix, now restricted to [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona), upper [[Gila River]] (Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico), [[Verde River]] (Yavapai County, Arizona), and [[Eagle Creek]] (Greenlee County, Arizona). Critical habitat designated March 8, 1994, includes portions of Verde and Gila Rivers and Aravaipa Creek.

**[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Listed as threatened October 28, 1986. A small, elongated, olive-colored fish with small white or orange spots at the base of dorsal and caudal fins. Once common in the Gila River above Phoenix, now found in only six locations: Aravaipa Creek, upper Gila, San Francisco and Tularosa Rivers (New Mexico), White River, and Blue River (Arizona). Critical habitat designated March 8, 1994.

**[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): Listed as endangered March 11, 1967. A small livebearing fish once among the most common species in the Gila River and tributaries. Nine naturally occurring populations remain, all but one in the [[Santa Cruz River Basin]]. One natural population exists in three small adjacent spring systems near Bylas on the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]].

**[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Listed as endangered October 23, 1991. Grows to over two feet in length with a distinctive abrupt, sharp-edged dorsal ridge behind the head. Once common throughout the Colorado River basin, now exists sporadically in only about 750 miles of the upper basin. In the lower basin, a substantial population exists only in [[Lake Mohave]]. Critical habitat designated March 21, 1994, includes portions of Verde, Gila, and Salt Rivers within the Gila River basin.

**[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*): Listed as endangered March 31, 1986. A small fish historically common throughout much of the lower Gila, lower Colorado, and Sonoyta River systems. Natural populations now exist in three Imperial County, California locations; [[Quitobaquito Spring]] (Pima County, Arizona); Rio Sonoyta (Sonora, Mexico); and scattered sites in the lower Colorado River delta.

**[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Listed as endangered March 11, 1967. A large silvery minnow growing up to six feet long. Once common throughout the Colorado River system including the Gila River basin, natural populations now found only in scattered areas of the upper Colorado River system in Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico. Experimental, non-essential populations designated in Salt River (Roosevelt Dam to U.S. Highway 60 bridge) and Verde River (Horseshoe Dam to Perkinsville) on July 24, 1985.

**[[Bald Eagle]]** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*): Listed as endangered March 11, 1967. A large fish-eating eagle. Desert-nesting population breeds earlier than northern counterparts. Currently nests along Bill Williams, Agua Fria, Verde, Salt, and Gila Rivers and tributaries in central Arizona.

### Project Description

The [[Central Arizona Project]] was constructed to provide long-term, non-groundwater water source for municipal, industrial, and agricultural users in Arizona. Water is taken from the [[Colorado River]] at [[Lake Havasu]] and conveyed across the state in a series of large open aqueducts. Storage became available in December 1992 following enlargement of [[Lake Pleasant]] on the [[Agua Fria River]]. The system was declared completed in October 1993 with an expected project life of 100 years.

The CAP aqueduct crosses or connects to multiple tributaries of the Gila River:

- **[[Hassayampa River]]**: CAP crosses approximately 24 miles upstream of confluence with Gila River. Several irrigation districts in vicinity.
- **[[Agua Fria River]]**: CAP crosses downstream of Lake Pleasant's New Waddell Dam. CAP water stored in Lake Pleasant beginning December 1992.
- **[[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]]**: CAP crosses Salt River below [[Granite Reef Dam]], below the confluence of Salt and Verde Rivers. Two major [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) canals receive water from Granite Reef Dam with CAP delivery points in same area. Electric fish barriers on both SRP canals upstream of CAP delivery point.
- **[[Gila River]]**: 21 CAP water delivery turnouts between Salt River siphon and Picacho Pumping Plant. Direct route for fish transfer exists via [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]] (SCIP) through [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]].

Field collection data confirm fish populations exist in the aqueducts, originating largely from Lake Havasu, with documented reproduction of some species in the aqueduct and additional source from Lake Pleasant storage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Environmental Baseline

The aquatic habitats and native fish community of the Gila River basin are already seriously deteriorated. Several native fish species have been extirpated and others occupy only limited areas. Of the 14 native fishes remaining or restocked, 86% are federally listed as endangered or threatened (8 species) or are Federal candidates (4 species). Minckley and Douglas (1991) believe the pattern of decline is moving beyond loss of individual species to collapse of entire aquatic faunas.

Primary causes of decline include:
- **Habitat alteration**: Impoundment, diversion, channelization, and watershed manipulation. Major dams (San Carlos, Roosevelt, Horseshoe Reservoirs) directly removed habitat and modified downstream flows.
- **Intermittent flows**: Major portions now flow only intermittently (San Pedro River, lower Salt River, Gila River near Virden NM, near Safford AZ, below Ashurst-Hayden Dam).
- **Habitat simplification**: Alterations created new habitat types more favorable to non-native than native species.
- **Non-native species**: Introduction and spread has been a major factor. Species with substantial adverse impacts include [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*).

Fragmentation of aquatic habitats has disrupted historic patterns of recolonization or augmentation of damaged native fish populations. Additional impacts from CAP will be cumulative to this already degraded baseline.

### Non-native Species Threat

Five species were initially identified as likely to be transported by CAP and adversely impact native fishes: [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*), [[Blue Tilapia]] (*Tilapia aurea*), [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*), and triploid [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*). None currently found in Gila River basin above Ashurst-Hayden Dam, but all present in Colorado River system.

Additional non-native fish documented in CAP and interconnected SRP canals include: [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), grass carp-bighead carp hybrids, [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*), [[Guppy]] (*Poecilia reticulata*), [[Shortfin Molly]] (*Poecilia mexicana*), [[Swordtail]] (*Xiphophorus variatus*), [[Yellow Bass]] (*Morone mississippiensis*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*), [[Convict Cichlid]] (*Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*), [[Firemouth Cichlid]] (*Cichlasoma meeki*), [[Rio Grande Cichlid]] (*Cichlasoma cyanogutattum*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*), and [[Redbelly Tilapia]] (*Tilapia zilli*).

Non-native aquatic organisms beyond fish may also be transported, including [[Asian Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*), [[Crayfish]] (*Procamberus clarkii*), [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), [[Zebra Mussel]] (*Dreissena polymorpha*), and [[Giant Rams-horn Snail]] (*Marisa cornuarietis*).

### Entry Points and Pathways

**Direct entry points**:
- Lake Havasu at aqueduct head (documented source of nine non-native species)
- Lake Pleasant (tilapia and white bass likely)
- Florence-Casa Grande Canal (direct connection to Gila River above Ashurst-Hayden Dam)

**"Bait bucket" introductions**: Accidental or intentional release by private citizens. CAP proximity to high-density human populations (Phoenix-Tucson area) and easy canal access increases this risk. Several species already in CAP aqueduct thought to have entered via bait bucket transfer.

### Time Frame Considerations

CAP will operate for at least 100 years. Delivery of water gives any species entering the aqueduct continuous access into the Gila River basin throughout this period. The longer CAP operates, the higher the risk of undesirable non-native species entering the system. Once established, effects of non-natives on native species will continue far beyond the project life.

## Key Findings

### Jeopardy Determinations

**Jeopardy**: The proposed delivery of CAP water is likely to jeopardize the continued existence of:
- Threatened spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)
- Threatened loach minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)
- Endangered Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*)
- Endangered razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

**Adverse Modification of Critical Habitat**: The proposed action is likely to adversely modify critical habitat of spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker.

**No Jeopardy**: The proposed action is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of endangered desert pupfish, Colorado River squawfish, or bald eagle.

### Sub-basin Specific Effects

**[[Hassayampa River]]**: Moderate to high probability of CAP introduction of non-natives; moderately adverse consequences to listed species. Probability moderated by long, normally dry stream stretches. Listed species present include stocked populations of desert pupfish, Gila topminnow, and Colorado squawfish at [[The Nature Conservancy Hassayampa Preserve]] and stocked Gila topminnow in isolated upper watershed springs.

**[[Agua Fria River]]**: Introduction of non-natives virtually certain; moderate consequences to listed species. Fish movement from CAP into Lake Pleasant and up into Agua Fria River unrestricted. Striped bass first recorded in lake November 1993, less than one year after first CAP water entered (December 1992). Barrier constructed on [[Tule Creek]] (tributary supporting large stocked Gila topminnow population) will provide some protection, though non-native presence near barrier increases bait bucket transfer probability.

**[[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]]**: Low probability of CAP introduction into listed species habitats, but severe consequences if introductions occur. Probability limited by electric barriers on SRP canals, Granite Reef Dam, and multiple upstream dams (Stewart Mountain, Mormon Flat, Horse Mesa, Roosevelt on Salt; Bartlett and Horseshoe on Verde). However, non-natives continue spreading upstream, most likely by bait bucket transport. Electric barriers not totally effective; operational failures documented (December 23, 1993 SRP barrier outage allowed grass carp upstream movement; June 1990 Pima Lateral barrier outage). One-third of remaining spikedace habitat is in upper Verde River. One of five remaining loach minnow populations in White River (upper Salt basin). Loss would seriously damage survival potential.

**[[Gila River]] below [[Coolidge Dam]] and [[San Pedro River]]**: Highest risk area. Numerous entry points into Gila River. [[Ashurst-Hayden Dam]] not an effective fish barrier due to structural features and operation. No barrier to upstream fish movement exists in Gila River above Ashurst-Hayden and below Coolidge Dam. Non-natives entering this area have open access to San Pedro River system, including [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Redfield Creek]], and [[Babocomari River]]. Matter (1991) concluded four of five studied non-native species would likely spread into these areas even with existing electric barriers. Severe potential consequences: includes bald eagle nest at Coolidge Dam, spikedace and loach minnow in Aravaipa Creek, recent spikedace record in Gila River near Cochran (7 miles above Ashurst-Hayden Dam), and important recovery habitat for multiple listed species in upper San Pedro River basin.

**[[Gila River]] above [[Coolidge Dam]]**: Less likelihood of CAP-mediated introductions, but most severe consequences. Largest remaining populations of spikedace and loach minnow in upper Gila, San Francisco, and Blue Rivers with largest portions of critical habitat. Spikedace also in Eagle Creek. Natural Gila topminnow populations in three springs near Bylas. Transfer of non-natives over Coolidge Dam would not result directly from CAP but becomes substantially more likely as CAP introduces non-natives below dam. [[San Carlos Reservoir]] supports heavily-used warm water sport fishery. Likely scenario: angler collects bait fish below dam for use in reservoir, dumps excess bait into reservoir. Once in reservoir, high risk of spread into upper Gila, San Francisco, and Blue Rivers and tributaries.

### Species-Specific Effects

**[[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]**: Both species particularly vulnerable due to short life span (approximately two years). Loss of two consecutive years of reproduction would result in population extirpation. Can sustain serious adverse impacts from non-native invasion even if non-native fails to establish long-term. Introduction of non-natives via CAP has potential to affect all remaining populations. Verde River spikedace and White Mountain loach minnow at low risk due to intervening dams.
