# Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*): 1990 Five-Year Status Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG) Five-Year Status Report, dated July 1, 1990, reviews the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*[[Cyprinodon Macularius]]*), a small endemic fish listed as endangered by California in 1980 and by the federal government in 1986. The report recommends that the species **retain its endangered classification**. Its native populations survive in only two short stream segments: [[San Felipe Creek]] and [[Salt Creek]] (with its associated [[San Sebastian Marsh]]) in Imperial and Riverside counties. Both are invaded by non-native animals and plants, and non-native fishes, notably [[Tilapia]], are considered the most serious threat. The report also describes artificially maintained refuge populations, population trends, and a set of recommended management actions.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and identification.** The desert pupfish belongs to the family [[Cyprinodontidae]]. The species was described by Miller (1943), and California populations were later designated a subspecies by Miller and Fuiman (1987). The species was listed as endangered by California in 1980 and by the federal government in 1986.

**Morphology.** Desert pupfish are small (less than 75 mm total length) and chunky, with strong sexual dimorphism:
- **Males** are larger and deeper-bodied. During the breeding season they turn bright blue with yellow or orange fins. They have black eyes and a black terminal band on the tail.
- **Females and non-breeding males** are tan to olive, with five to eight vertical bars on their sides.

**Life history.** Males may reach sexual maturity at about 15 mm standard length. Most individuals do not breed until their second summer. Year-round breeding occurs in habitats where water temperature remains constantly warm. The species can complete its life cycle within a single year.

**Diet.** Diet varies seasonally. The fish feed opportunistically on insect larvae, detritus, vegetation, and small crustaceans. The report notes that the species is an effective natural mosquito-larvae control agent, which has been used as a justification for introductions of [[Mosquitofish]].

**Phylogenetic note.** The report's species name is *Cyprinodon macularius*. The genus name derives from Greek roots meaning "carp with teeth," and the common name "pupfish" was coined by Carl L. Hubbs after the fish's playful, aggressive behavior.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** Black (1980), cited in the report, lists the following requirements: abundant filamentous algae, relatively shallow and warm water, and a lack of predators and competitors. The species tolerates wide ranges of temperature, dissolved oxygen, and salinity, which suggests that its main limitation is biotic (non-native species) rather than physicochemical.

**Historic distribution.** The species once occurred in the backwater sloughs, springs, and lower Colorado River drainage of Arizona, the Sonoran Desert of northern Sonora, Mexico, and the lower Colorado Basin, including the Salton Sink in California. Early California records date to Cowles (1934). The Salton Sea formed in 1905 after an irrigation canal break, creating new habitat, but lakeshore populations were later reduced after the 1950s, when native fishes were largely displaced by introduced species (Barlow 1961; Deacon and Minckley 1974).

**Current distribution.** The current distribution consists of:
- Two naturally occurring populations, in San Felipe Creek and Salt Creek with its wetland.
- Roughly eight introduced populations in artificially maintained refuges, such as [[Palm Canyon Pond]], [[The Living Desert]], [[Oasis Springs]], and [[McCallum Pond]], most of which depend on human intervention to supply water.
- Small numbers of fish along the margins of the [[Salton Sea]].

**Threats.**
- **Non-native fishes.** Tilapia, mosquitofish, mollies (*Poecilia* spp.), and African cichlids have replaced pupfish in nearly all habitats. Tilapia are a particularly serious threat, and the report links introduced fishes to the decline of more than 20 native fish species worldwide.
- **Invasive plants.** A non-native aquatic plant, whose genus name is illegible in the scan, has high growth rates and can crowd out habitat. Cattails (*Typha* spp.) can also become a problem where the plant is especially invasive.
- **Water management.** Increased groundwater pumping or pesticide use associated with agriculture could reduce the quantity and quality of water available to pupfish. Only one of the pupfish habitats, San Sebastian Marsh, has been studied in detail regarding water supply.
- **Natural disturbance.** Volcanic eruptions, drying of intermittent watercourses, and flooding periodically isolate populations.

## Key Findings

**1. Population status.** Native populations are restricted to short segments of two creeks and remain at low abundance.

- *Historic:* Early observers reported the species as "abundant" at the Salton Sea. Barlow (1961) counted schools of about 10,000 juveniles in one shoreline pool and approximately 1,500 adults in another. No quantitative historic abundance data exist.
- *1986:* Three pupfish made up 1.2% of the total catch in an irrigation drain near the southeast shore of the Salton Sea.
- *1988:* No pupfish were found in a similar survey.
- *Spring 1988:* Numerous pupfish were observed in San Felipe Creek, and 42 were captured in minnow traps in Salt Creek.
- *1989:* Only 49 pupfish were trapped in San Felipe Creek (after a flood), and two were trapped in Salt Creek.
- *May 1990:* Pupfish were captured at three locations along the Salton Sea (mouths of San Felipe and Salt creeks and the Avenue 81 drain), representing an estimated 5% of the total fish captured.
- *Spring and June 1990:* Surveys of San Felipe Creek recorded 119 and 125 pupfish, respectively.

The report notes that population counts from minnow trapping are only semi-quantitative, because trap placement depends on where fish occur and on water level in a given year or season.

**2. Refuge populations.** Refuge populations are monitored unevenly:
- [[Mccallum Pond]] (Riverside County): monitoring in 1989 recorded 23 pupfish caught in five minnow traps set for 15 minutes each, suggesting a stable population.
- Oasis Springs: monitored quarterly from October 1988 to July 1989. Counts ranged from roughly 1,500 pupfish (lowest, April 1989) to a higher October 1988 figure.
- Other refuges: no formal monitoring, but staff reports suggest populations are doing well.
- The refuge table lists founder populations of 17 to 203 fish for several sites, with introductions dating from 1972 to 1985.

**3. Recommended actions.**
- **Retain endangered status** and incorporate the report's findings into the forthcoming U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) recovery plan.
- **Install a barrier** on San Felipe Creek to prevent upstream movement of tilapia, to be built during winter 1990–1991, after flooding has reached the tilapia downstream of the construction site.
- **Create additional habitat** at Oasis Springs, if hydrologically sound.
- **Remove invasive vegetation** as necessary.
- **Monitor** pupfish populations annually and visually on a quarterly basis.
- **Reactivate the Desert Pupfish Advisory Committee**, which last met in 1986, to meet at least annually.
- **Determine intermixing** between the two pupfish subspecies at the Living Desert.
- **Acquire adjacent land** along Salt Creek, which The Nature Conservancy is planning to purchase and transfer to the BLM.
- **Update the BLM Salt Creek Area of Critical Environmental Concern Management Plan** (1980), which is ten years old.
- **Conduct a thorough survey** of pupfish habitat in the Salton Sea and adjacent tributaries, given reported increases in salinity and a potential decrease in tilapia after unusually cold winters.

**4. Hydrologic connectivity.** Before 1985, the connection between San Felipe Creek and the Salton Sea was intermittent. A constant flow now links them. Where this connection allows tilapia to enter pupfish habitat, the barrier is intended to protect the native population.

**5. Information gaps.** The report identifies gaps in the data on abundance trends, refuge genetics, and the status of populations at refuges with irregular monitoring, and it calls for further research on habitat requirements, refuge design, and genetics (building on Turner's 1983 and 1984 work and Edds 1984).
