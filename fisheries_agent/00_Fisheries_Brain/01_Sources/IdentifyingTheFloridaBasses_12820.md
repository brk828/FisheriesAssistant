# Identifying Florida's Black Basses: Is There a Smallmouth Bass in Florida?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary
This 1949 article by John F. Dequine (reprinted from *Florida Wildlife*, Game & Fresh Water Fish Commission) addresses whether northern Smallmouth Bass occur in Florida. The author reports that, despite many claims of "record Smallmouth" catches, every fish he examined was a Florida Largemouth. No biologist or ichthyologist had verified a Smallmouth in the state. He explains how the black basses can be told apart and notes the newly described [[Suwannee Bass]]. He concludes, echoing [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Reeve M. Bailey]] (1949), that there is "no valid evidence that the smallmouth has become established in Florida."

## Background & Description
- Black basses were long thought to be two species. They are now recognized as a **largemouth group** and a **smallmouth group**, divided into at least ten varieties: two in the largemouth group and eight in the smallmouth group.
- Four of the five varieties illustrated occur in Florida. The fifth, the northern Smallmouth, is shown for comparison.
- Taxonomic basis: Hubbs and Bailey, "A Revision of the Black Basses (*Micropterus* and *Huro*) with Descriptions of Four New Forms" (1940) and "The Black Basses of Florida, with Description of a New Species" (1949). The latter first fully described the Suwannee Bass.
- The [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] was a leading centre for freshwater fish taxonomy.

**Diagnostic characters (largemouth vs. smallmouth group)**
- **Dorsal fins:** separated in the largemouth group, with greater variation in spine length. They appear joined in the smallmouth group, with spines more nearly equal.
- **Scales on the soft dorsal and anal fin bases:** present only in the smallmouth group. They are small and embedded, and sometimes need a diligent search.
- **Maxillary (upper jaw):** usually extends past the eye in largemouths, but this is not fully reliable. It must be measured with the mouth closed.
- **Colour:** variable with water and even handling, such as time in a livewell. The smallmouth group is usually darker.
- **Scale counts:** generally dependable when combined (lateral line, cheek, rows above and below the lateral line), despite occasional overlap.
- Fin ray counts, body measurements and internal organs are less practical for sportsmen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Florida Largemouth:** the original range is believed to run from the mouth of the [[St. Johns River]] south and west to the mouth of the [[Suwannee River]], excluding the Suwannee itself. Hatchery stock from the Winter Haven and Welaka hatcheries has spread it across northern Florida and into southern Georgia and Alabama. The Northern Largemouth is generally restricted to north Florida, with overlap.
- **Northern Smallmouth:** its southward spread appears to have stopped about a hundred miles north of the Florida line, despite numerous plantings.
- **Spotted Bass:** known from Ohio, Kentucky, Tennessee, northern Alabama and other states. Only two Florida specimens are known, both from the Flint River near its junction with the Chattahoochee. It rarely exceeds 4 pounds.
- **Suwannee Bass:** first reported by University of Florida biologists from [[Ichetucknee Springs]], Columbia County, in 1941. It has since been found in the [[Santa Fe River]], the Withlacoochee River (Madison County) and the main Suwannee down to the mouth. It is small: the pictured specimen was over five years old and weighed under 12 ounces. The largest reported was 4.5 pounds. Local residents recognized it as distinct before 1900.
- **Historical stocking:** about 1908, 10,000 Smallmouth fingerlings from the Federal Bureau of Fisheries were planted in Lake County lakes, including Lake Seneca. In 1910 fingerlings were reportedly planted in the Ochlockonee River, the St. Marks basin, Orchard Pond and Buck Lake. In summer 1930 Smallmouth and Rock Bass were planted in the Chipola, Wakulla, Wacissa and Suwannee Rivers and in Blue Lake, Hillsborough County.

## Key Findings
- The "record" 14-pound Smallmouth credited to [[Lake Apopka]] (1932) is considered not credible. The author and two associates, experienced with both species, examined, tagged, weighed and measured over 3,000 adult bass from Lake Apopka and connected Lake County lakes and saw only Florida Largemouth. Outdoor writers Rube Allyn and Herb Mosher judged photographs of the fish to show a Largemouth.
- The author's tests of claimed Smallmouths all failed. An ~11-pound mounted "smallmouth" brought to the Commission was a largemouth. A mounted 1933 prize fish in Lake County was also a Florida Largemouth. An hour of seining in a Citrus County lake produced only Largemouths.
- The 9-lb 13-oz Lake Seneca fish (O. W. Milton, reported 1931) matched a Florida Largemouth in scale counts, fin ray counts and dorsal fin separation. Jaw length could not be assessed because the mount's mouth was open.
- Other experts also found no Florida Smallmouths: Dr. Lloyd Meehean (Welaka hatchery, about 3 years of searching) and Dr. [[Coleman Goin]] (University of Florida).
- **Causes of misidentification:**
  - Florida Largemouth scale counts resemble the northern Smallmouth more than the northern Largemouth.
  - Taxonomy of the black basses was only recently clarified.
  - Maxillary length and colour are unreliable on their own.
- Dr. Bailey suggests another smallmouth-group species may occur in the Chipola and other west Florida streams. Unusual bass have also been reported from streams draining to the northeastern Gulf of Mexico.
- The author cannot state definitively that no Smallmouths exist in Florida, only that no biologist has seen one. He invites specimens for examination at laboratories in Tallahassee, Welaka, Okeechobee, Williston, Gainesville, Jacksonville and Wewahitchka.
