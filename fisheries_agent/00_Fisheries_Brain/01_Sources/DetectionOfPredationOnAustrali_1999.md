# Detection of Predation on Australian Native Fishes by Gambusia Holbrooki

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #poeciliidae #melanotaeniidae #invasive-species

## Executive Summary
This short communication investigates predation by the invasive [[Gambusia Holbrooki]] (eastern mosquitofish) on juvenile Australian native fishes, a phenomenon long speculated but not empirically confirmed via gut-content analysis. Using clearing-and-staining techniques, a controlled feeding trial, and examination of regurgitated fixative residues, the authors developed methods to identify rapidly-degraded fish prey within Gambusia guts for up to 12 hours post-feeding — a substantial improvement over the ~4-hour window previously reported (Meffe 1985). Analysis of 631 wild-caught Gambusia from the upper Orara River, NSW, confirmed that 18 individuals (2.8%) had ingested native melanotaeniid or pseudomugilid larvae, and 3 had cannibalized conspecific juveniles, providing direct empirical support for the hypothesis that [[Gambusia]] preys on native fish larvae in Australian waterways.

## Background & Description
Differentiation of sympatric juvenile fishes within Gambusia gut contents relied on species-specific morphological and developmental traits (see comparative table below), assessed both in unstained specimens and after clearing/staining (bleaching in caustic peroxide, clearing in boracic trypsin, staining with alizarin red).

**Digestive degradation timeline (melanotaeniid larvae, non-cleared specimens):**
- **<2 h post-feeding:** Small larvae (<5 mm TL) identifiable
- **Up to 3 h:** Larger larvae identifiable
- **4 h:** Larger larvae identifiable only via otoliths and semi-digested skin melanophore pattern
- **≥6 h:** No longer identifiable without clearing/staining

**Degradation timeline (cleared and stained specimens):**
- **Up to 6 h:** Larger larvae readily discernible; vertebral column remains articulated; cranial bones aggregated about otoliths
- **8 h:** Otoliths discernible; vertebral column disarticulated; individual vertebrae stain poorly amid faecal matter
- **12 h:** Only rectal faecal pellets with a few centra remain detectable

Recently hatched, non-calcified melanotaeniid larvae could not be identified even with clearing and staining, indicating a detection limit tied to skeletal ossification stage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Study site:** A 10 km stretch of the upper [[Orara River]] near Karangi, New South Wales (30°15′S, 153°00′E), where Gambusia occurred sympatrically with native juvenile fishes (October 1997–January 1998).
- **Predator-prey community:** Gambusia co-occurred with juveniles of [[Retropinna Semoni]], [[Melanotaenia Duboulayi]], [[Rhadinocentrus Ornatus]], [[Pseudomugil Signifer]], and [[Hypseleotris Galii]].
- **Trophic interaction:** Gambusia is documented globally as an aggressive competitor and predator of native/endemic small fishes, having contributed to extirpation of [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]] and [[Heterandria Formosa]] in North America via direct predation (Schaefer et al. 1994).
- **Feeding behavior:** Captive Gambusia (74 post-juveniles, held in outdoor 4 L aquaria) fed on live wild-harvested melanotaeniid larvae, demonstrating active larvivory and occasional cannibalism of conspecific juveniles.
- **Regurgitation as an ecological signal:** Stressed/handled Gambusia regurgitated recently ingested prey (0.5–1.0 h post-feeding), an underexploited data source for detecting piscivory in field-collected specimens.

## Key Findings
- Developed methodology (clearing/staining + regurgitate examination) extended the **detectable post-feeding predation window from ~4 h to 12 h**, substantially reducing underestimation of piscivory in gut-content studies.
- Among **631 wild-caught Gambusia**, **21 individuals (3.3%)** contained an ingested fish:
  - **18 (2.8%)** contained an ingested melanotaeniid or pseudomugilid larva (confirming predation on native fishes).
  - **3** contained a cannibalized conspecific Gambusia juvenile.
- Regurgitated material recovered from fixative solutions included **10 melanotaeniids, 1 Gambusia, and 1 H. galii** — a novel technique not previously reported in piscivory studies (cf. Dionne 1985; Meffe 1985; Nesbit and Meffe 1993).
- Confirms long-standing speculation (Arthington and Lloyd 1989; Arthington 1991) that **Gambusia preys on melanotaeniid eggs and larvae** in Australian freshwater systems, providing the first direct gut-content evidence of this interaction outside of cannibalism.
- The acidic gut environment of Gambusia causes rapid dissolution of ingested fish skeletal material, explaining historically low detection rates of piscivory in routine dietary analyses relative to more persistent food items (e.g., lignified plant debris, arthropod exoskeletons).
- The study underscores that **true predation rates on native fishes are likely underestimated** in prior literature due to methodological limitations in detecting rapidly-degraded prey items.
- Broader significance for **conservation of Australian native fishes** remains uncertain, as the study did not assess reciprocal predation pressure from native species or quantify population-level impacts — highlighting a need for further research into [[Invasive Species Impact]] on endemic Australian freshwater fish communities.
