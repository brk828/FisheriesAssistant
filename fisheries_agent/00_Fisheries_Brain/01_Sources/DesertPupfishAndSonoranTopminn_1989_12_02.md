## Executive Summary

This 1989 memorandum from the Arizona Game & Fish Department (AGFD) to the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] summarizes annual monitoring results for two federally protected endangered fishes: the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) and the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), along with status updates for the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) and [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) at the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge. The report catalogs reintroduction outcomes across dozens of sites in Arizona (springs, tanks, creeks, and constructed ponds), documents the persistence of natural populations, and highlights the pervasive threat of invasive [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) to native poeciliid and cyprinodontid populations. The data represent a snapshot of active recovery efforts under the U.S. Endangered Species Act for two of the Southwest's most imperiled desert fishes.

## Background & Description

The source document is a management/monitoring report and does not provide detailed morphometric data. However, taxonomic distinctions are implicit in the site tables via subspecies/species abbreviation codes:

- **CYMAMA** — *Cyprinodon macularius macularius*, the nominate subspecies of [[Desert Pupfish]], used in the majority of Arizona reintroductions.
- **CYMAER** — Likely a hatchery-propagated or hybrid lineage of desert pupfish (Bubbling Ponds Hatchery, Arizona State University sites).
- **CYMA??** — Uncertain/unconfirmed pupfish subspecies identity at a given site, indicating taxonomic ambiguity or incomplete verification.
- **POOCOC** — *Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*, the [[Gila Topminnow]].

Both target species are small-bodied, live-bearing or egg-laying desert fishes adapted to extreme thermal and hydrological variability characteristic of isolated desert springs and cienegas. No length, weight, or fin-ray count data are included in this report.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types utilized:** Natural and artificial habitats including springs, spring-fed creeks, cienegas, livestock tanks, and constructed ponds — reflecting the reliance on human-assisted refugia for endangered desert fish recovery.
- **Ownership/management context:** Sites are distributed across U.S. Forest Service (USFS), Bureau of Land Management (BLM), Arizona State Parks, private landowners, The Nature Conservancy (TNC), USFWS (Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico), and San Carlos Apache Reservation lands — illustrating a multi-jurisdictional conservation network.
- **Primary threat — invasive species:** The [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), listed as "exotics" or "POOCOC" competitor in numerous sites, is identified as a major driver of reintroduction failure through predation and competitive exclusion of native [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] populations. Several sites (e.g., Phoenix Zoo, Arizona State University, Kino Spring) failed explicitly due to exotic species presence.
- **Habitat desiccation:** A significant proportion of unsuccessful reintroduction sites failed due to the habitat "drying" (e.g., Buffalo Corral Pond Spring, Green Tanks, Mesquite Flat Trough, Pasture Well), underscoring the fragility of small isolated desert water bodies used for translocations.
- **Co-occurring native fauna:** At San Bernardino NWR sites, [[Yaqui Topminnow]] co-occurs with [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*), and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), forming a native fish assemblage vulnerable to mosquitofish invasion gradients (e.g., Black Draw transect showing declining topminnow percentage downstream toward the international border, corresponding with increasing mosquitofish dominance).
- **Parasitism:** [[Longfin Dace]] in Leslie Creek were noted to be heavily parasitized with black spot disease (trematode metacercariae), an ecological stressor noted incidentally during surveys.
- **Physical barriers as conservation tools:** A 2-foot cement barrier below the gauging station on Leslie Creek appeared to exclude topminnow from upstream reaches, while a "recently constructed gabion" near Twin Pond on Black Draw correlates with shifts in native/exotic fish ratios, suggesting these structures may function (intentionally or not) as barriers against upstream invasion by mosquitofish.

## Key Findings

**Gila Topminnow Reintroductions (Arizona, 1989)**
- Approximately 45 sites reported as "successful" reintroductions, spanning springs, tanks, and creeks across USFS, BLM, state, private, and tribal lands.
- Population sizes were categorically reported as >500 or <500 individuals; several sites had populations coexisting with exotic species (marked "Y" for exotics present), including Bain Spring, Cave Creek, Cow Creek, Horse Creek, Humbug Creek, and Watson Wash.
- Roughly 20 sites were documented as unsuccessful, primarily due to habitat drying or exotic species introduction (notably *Gambusia affinis*).

**Desert Pupfish Reintroductions (Arizona, 1989)**
- 17 successful sites reported, dominated by the CYMAMA subspecies, concentrated in tank and pond habitats with fewer creek/spring occurrences (e.g., Buehman Canyon, Peoples Canyon).
- Several sites carried subspecies-identity uncertainty (CYMA??), notably at Tucson-area private facilities (AZ Historical Society, Tohono Chul Park, Bog Hole).
- Failures again linked mainly to desiccation and exotic species competition (e.g., Arizona State University site failed due to exotics).

**Natural Populations Status (Gila Topminnow & Desert Pupfish)**
- Strong natural population persistence (100% topminnow composition) recorded at Cienega Creek, Cottonwood Spring, Monkey Spring, and Redrock Canyon (Sonoita/San Rafael Valley area).
- Complete extirpation (0% topminnow) documented at North Fork of Ash Creek (San Carlos Reservation) and Sheehy Spring (San Rafael Valley), likely due to mosquitofish displacement.
- [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] population at Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument remained robust at 100% pupfish composition — a critically important isolated refuge population.
- Intermediate/declining topminnow percentages (2–52%) at Bylas Spring, Santa Cruz River, Sharp Spring, and Sonoita Creek indicate ongoing competitive pressure from non-native poeciliids.

**Yaqui Topminnow & Yaqui Chub Status (San Bernardino NWR, 1989)**
- Multiple spring sites (Bunting, Cottonwood, Middle, Cienega, Upland, Tule) maintained 100% Yaqui topminnow composition, representing strongholds for the subspecies.
- Mosquitofish had achieved complete dominance (100%) at several interconnected sites (Unnamed Pond, Bathhouse Spring, 2 PhD Ponds) despite being indirectly hydrologically connected to topminnow-only Upland Spring, raising concern about invasion risk.
- Black Draw sampling transect revealed a stark upstream-to-downstream (toward the border) shift from Yaqui topminnow dominance (94% at source) to mosquitofish dominance (88–100% near the border), illustrating a clear invasion gradient.
- Leslie Creek supported robust native assemblage (Yaqui chub n=178, longfin dace n=127) with topminnow present only in low numbers (n=2), confined to shallow pools below a barrier structure.
- Twin Pond was fish-less, attributed to radioactivity contamination concerns; Robertson Cienega was also fish-less following redirection of Border Springs flow.

**Data & Methodological Notes**
- Population size categories used a binary threshold (>500 / <500 individuals) rather than precise counts for most reintroduction sites.
- Percentage-based composition data (e.g., at San Bernardino NWR) were restricted to Poeciliid species comparisons only, per report methodology notes.
- The report reflects a broader recovery strategy of establishing multiple redundant, geographically dispersed refuge populations to buffer against stochastic extirpation events (drying, invasion) — a core tenet of the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] conservation approach for endangered Southwestern cyprinodontiforms.
