# Freshwater Fisheries and Pollution Control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

D. W. M. Herbert of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] reviews how waste discharges harm freshwater fish and how that harm can be predicted and controlled. The paper combines a field survey of [[China-Clay Wastes]] in Cornish rivers with laboratory [[Toxicity Testing]] of soluble poisons, mainly on [[Rainbow Trout]]. Its central proposal is a "trout standard": effluents should be limited so that they do not harm trout, which are generally the most sensitive British species tested. The paper also argues that toxicity of mixtures can be predicted by simple additive rules, and that water quality (especially dissolved oxygen, pH, hardness and carbon dioxide) changes poison toxicity in predictable ways. The discussion records contributions from several engineers and anglers, including questions on particle size, acclimatisation, synergism and intermittent discharge.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with the economic and recreational value of freshwater fisheries. A Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food committee (Lord Bledisloe) estimated about one million anglers in England and Wales, roughly 2 to 3 per cent of the population (the scanned figure is ambiguous). The author cites about 11 per cent of the population as anglers in the U.S.A. Annual catches of [[Atlantic Salmon]], sea-trout and eels add economic weight.

Pollution is identified as a major hazard in a densely industrialised country. River boards hold statutory powers to control discharges, but they need reliable predictions of an effluent's effect on fish. Requirements should be neither unnecessarily rigorous nor insufficient. The author is a Water Pollution Research Laboratory scientist who has led work on pollution effects on fisheries since 1951.

Pollutant types covered include heat, oxygen-demanding organic matter, pH extremes, suspended solids and soluble poisons. Soluble poisons are the paper's main focus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community effects.** Fish depend on plants and invertebrates, which respond to velocity, temperature, mineral content and substrate. Harm can occur directly through toxicity, or indirectly through reduced food, damage to eggs and young, or avoidance of polluted reaches.

**Cornish china-clay survey (28 April to 24 May 1960).** Researchers measured trout density, bottom fauna and drifting invertebrates alongside suspended solids in clean and polluted reaches.

| Site | Suspended solids (p.p.m.) | Brown trout (fish/1,000 ft²) | Bottom fauna (g/1,000 ft²) | Drift (mg/m³) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clean control streams | — | 21 | 640 | 77 |
| [[River Camel]] (light pollution) | 59 | 28 | 572 | 14 |
| [[River Fal]] (polluted) | 1,061 | 3 | 182 | 48 |
| [[River Par]] (polluted) | 5,837 | 3 | 36 | 57 |

- Trout density in the polluted Fal and Par was about one-seventh of control levels.
- Drift was similar across sites, but bottom fauna was far lower in polluted reaches, so food was scarcer.
- Trout fry were absent from polluted stretches, probably because settled solids clogged gravel needed for egg development.
- Gill epithelium thickening was seen in many Fal trout, a condition also produced by laboratory exposure to inert suspensions.

**Laboratory suspended-solids tests.** Mineral solids at 30 and 90 p.p.m. were not very harmful to trout. At 270 and 810 p.p.m. they clearly reduced survival.

**Avoidance and food web.** Fish can detect and avoid some low oxygen and low pH conditions. Sticklebacks and minnows avoided several poisons, but detection was lost at concentrations that were still toxic. Sewage-polluted streams where trout could not survive still supported dense populations of *Asellus* and *Chironomus*, which coarse fish ate voraciously. Some insecticides affected bottom fauna more than fish: trout in cages survived DDT spraying, but bottom organisms were substantially reduced.

## Key Findings

1. **Trout are the usual test species and generally the most sensitive.** Schweiger's order of sensitivity to heavy metal salts was trout > char > carp >> tench. Tench tolerated mercury, cadmium, nickel and cobalt at 2 to 6 times trout concentrations. For cyanide, tench (the most resistant species tested) tolerated about 24 times the concentration that killed [[Rainbow Trout]] in one day.

2. **Life stages differ.** Gardiner found trout eggs and fry much more resistant than adults to phenol. Fluoride at 1 to 5 p.p.m. delayed hatching of fish eggs (presumed goldfish), while adult goldfish survived 200 p.p.m. NaF for at least ten days.

3. **Field and laboratory results agree on solids.** Solids at 1,000 p.p.m. or more distinctly reduce trout abundance, although 6,000 p.p.m. did not eliminate the species. Solids at 60 p.p.m. were probably harmless where spawning grounds exist nearby. The exact critical concentration between 60 and 1,000 p.p.m. could not be determined because suitable graded stretches were not found.

4. **A "trout standard" is proposed.** Until more research is done, permissible discharges of poisons should be based on concentrations harmless to trout. Under the suggested rule, a continuous discharge diluted only by dry-weather flow should kill no more than 5 per cent (or 1 per cent) of trout-sensitive fish within three months. Because real flows rarely stay at dry-weather levels, actual dilution will usually be greater.

5. **Interpretation of short tests.** Results of [[Ammonium Chloride]] tests showed a steep response, so the 48-hour threshold approximates the long-term threshold. [[Probit Analysis]] was used to estimate concentrations killing 5 and 50 per cent of fish. [[Synthetic Detergents]] showed no clear threshold, with lethal effects after several months at concentrations harmless within days. Extrapolating the five-per-cent tangent to 90 days gave an estimated safe concentration of 4 p.p.m. for the [[Demulsifier]] tested in half-strength sea water.

6. **Complex effluents depend on receiving-water quality.** Copper sulphate was about fourteen times as toxic to trout in water of 12 p.p.m. hardness as in 320 p.p.m. Toxicity generally rises as dissolved oxygen falls below saturation and as pH rises (for ammonia). The authors recommend using dilution water from the receiving stream, or one closely resembling it, in tests.

7. **Dissolved oxygen effects are consistent for many poisons.** [[Lloyd Gill Model]] predicts that reduced oxygen lowers threshold concentrations of zinc, lead, copper and monohydric phenols by about the same proportion. Table 2 factors were 1.00 at 100 per cent saturation, 0.95 at 80, 0.85 at 60 and 0.71 at 40 per cent.

8. **Ammonia requires a gill-surface correction.** Only un-ionised ammonia is toxic. Carbon dioxide excreted at the gills lowers local pH, which reduces un-ionised ammonia near the gill. Table 3 factors at 3.4 p.p.m. free CO₂ were 1, 0.84, 0.64 and 0.46 (at 100, 80, 60 and 40 per cent oxygen). At 19.8 p.p.m. they were 1, 0.91, 0.79 and 0.62. Predicted ammonia thresholds were usually within ±12 per cent of independent tests.

9. **Mixture toxicity is additive in threshold terms.** For zinc sulphate and ammonium chloride, the threshold is reached when the sum of threshold fractions equals one (Z_a/Z_t + A_a/A_t = 1). The same rule held for zinc plus copper and for monohydric phenols plus ammonia. Survival times in toxic mixtures lay between the single-poison curves. Only three poison pairs have been examined so far, and no synergism was found in that work.

10. **Mixture predictions hold across oxygen levels.** Table 4 gave observed and calculated mixture thresholds of 0.36 vs 0.33, 0.29 vs 0.25 and 0.19 vs 0.15 at 100, 66 and 33 per cent oxygen saturation. Predictions were slightly low but close enough to validate the approach.

11. **Waste water prediction works in the laboratory and the field.** For spent still liquor from a coke oven, predicted toxicity from chemical analysis matched bioassay results (Fig. 7). In a Hertfordshire stream below a gas works, ten caged trout per day were tested against predicted toxicity. Observation and expectation agreed in 80 per cent of 40 trials, with a further 10 per cent classed as "near misses". Fluctuations in water quality, sampled every two hours, explain some disagreement.

12. **Recommended research priority.** The author argues that toxicity tests of whole effluents are often the quickest and most economical method, and that they are more humane than repeated fish bioassays. Priority waste types are gas and coke-works effluents, sewage effluents with metal salts, plating wastes, and paper and board mill wastes.

13. **Discussion points.** Mr. Herbert noted that trout were killed within minutes by 0.2 p.p.m. cyanide, and that a person would need to drink about 300 litres at once to ingest a lethal dose. Trout survived a month in 10 p.p.m. arsenic trioxide, which is unsuitable for domestic supply. Minnows (*Phoxinus laevis*) are generally more resistant than trout, so a larger safety margin would be needed if minnows were test animals. Acclimatisation gives only a small gain in tolerance, so standards should not be relaxed on that account. Intermittent discharges can waste river self-purification capacity, and discharging during daylight may suit effluents with high oxygen demand.
