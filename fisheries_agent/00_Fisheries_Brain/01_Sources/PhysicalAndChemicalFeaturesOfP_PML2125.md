# Physical and Chemical Features of Playa Lakes in Southeastern California, USA

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #playa-lakes

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Dennis M. Kuby]] (Arizona State University, published 1982 in *Archiv für Hydrobiologie*, Suppl. 62) reports a limnological survey of ephemeral playa lakes in the deserts of southeastern California, sampled on three field trips (19 Jan–1 Feb 1978; 30 Apr–5 May 1978; 23 May–4 Jun 1979). Of 45 playas visited, the lakes were shallow, well mixed and had extremely low water-volume-to-sediment-area ratios, so sediments strongly shaped overlying water. Salinity ranged from under 100 ppm to 325,000 ppm. Dilute lakes were mainly Na-HCO₃-CO₃ waters; saline lakes progressed to chlorosulfate and nearly pure chloride brines. Principal component analysis (PCA) separated turbid, organic-rich dilute lakes from clearer saline lakes and split saline lakes into two chemically divergent groups, which the author linked to the HCO₃/(Ca+Mg) mole ratio of inflow waters.

## Background & Description

Playas are smooth, barren flats at the lowest points of intermontane basins in semiarid to arid western North America, largely within the [[Basin and Range Province]]. Evaporation exceeds precipitation in every such zone. Ephemeral playa lakes form at irregular intervals and are classed as *argillotrophic* systems, characterised by clay-derived turbidity and a bacteria-particulate organic detritus ("feindetritus") food base. Because of their ephemeral, unpredictable and isolated character, limnological data for them were scarce before this survey.

The study area covers roughly 85,000 km² of southeastern California, including parts of the [[Great Basin]], [[Mojave Desert]] and [[Colorado Desert]], bounded by the Colorado River, the Transverse Ranges and the Sierra Nevada. Geology spans Precambrian to late Tertiary formations; Quaternary valley fill includes lacustrine silts, clays and salts left by Pluvial-period lakes. The region is among the hottest and driest in the continental United States. Annual precipitation ranges from 4.3 cm (Death Valley) to 13.2 cm (Deep Springs), and pan evaporation exceeds 300 cm/yr at lower elevations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and sediment.** Playa lakes are shallow: only South Panamint and East Cronese exceeded 1 m depth, and most were under 25 cm when sampled. Surface areas ranged from 2.3 to 29,020 ha, with over 50% under 1,000 ha. Shorelines were mostly subcircular to elliptical, and surface gradients were virtually flat.

**Hydrological regime.** Playas were classed as fed by permanent sources (e.g., Deep Springs, Tecopa Soda, South Panamint, Harper, Koehn) or as ephemeral (Ford's, East Cronese). Water coverage in the winter trip was 65% of playas, falling to 45% and 50% on the two summer trips.

**Sediment types.** Following [[Morris Playa Classification]], fine-grained, surface-water-discharging playas with dense clay sediments and deep water tables were contrasted with coarse-grained, groundwater-discharging playas with porous sediments and near-surface water tables. Fine-grained systems retain salts and resist groundwater input, whereas coarse-grained systems allow evaporative pumping and enrichment of surface salts.

**Thermal and oxygen conditions.** Temperatures ranged from 2.0 °C (under ice) to 16.0 °C in Jan–Feb and from 17.5 °C to 35.5 °C in May–Jun. Thermal stratification was rare (only at El Mirage Lake). Wind-mixed shallow waters kept dissolved oxygen near or above saturation; no oxygen depletion was recorded.

**Climate.** Summer highs exceed 49 °C at low elevations, and 56.7 °C was recorded at Death Valley. Winter temperatures fall below freezing.

**Biota.** Diatoms were present in all but the most turbid and saline lakes but were generally sparse. Blue-green algae occurred, and invertebrates such as rotifers, brine shrimp, dipterans, corixids and beetles were recorded. Their abundance was linked more to habitat heterogeneity and instability than to water chemistry. Fairy shrimp (*Branchinecta mackini*) were studied in [[Rabbit Dry Lake]] by other workers.

## Key Findings

**Physical and morphometric results**
- Shoreline development index (D_L) was ≤ 1.50 in 25 of 45 playas (range 1.02–3.00); ellipticity median was 0.50.
- Area and shape correlations were positive but weak (Spearman r_s = 0.34 for D_L; 0.19 for E).

**Salinity**
- Salinity ranged from under 100 ppm to 325,000 ppm; the modal class was 100–1,000 ppm.
- 20 of 45 samples (7 lakes) were saline (> 3,000 ppm); 5 lakes were hypersaline (> 40,000 ppm).
- South Panamint Lake showed a roughly 50-fold change (3,800 to 190,000 ppm) between winter and summer. East Cronese increased 6.5-fold.

**Ionic composition**
- Dilute lakes were Na-HCO₃-CO₃ waters, with Na dominant in all but one lake (Ford's, with Na < 10 eq.%).
- Anions shifted from CO₃ toward Cl and SO₄ with rising salinity (Spearman CO₃–Cl r_s = −0.89; CO₃–SO₄ r_s = −0.31).
- South Panamint, Koehn and Cadiz terminated as nearly pure chloride waters (< 10 eq.% SO₄ + CO₃).
- Mg was generally lower than Ca, regardless of salinity, unlike other California closed-basin lakes.

**Turbidity, organic matter and light**
- Turbidity and loss on ignition (LOI) were positively correlated (r = 0.44, P < 0.01; r = 0.66 after removing the Coso Junction Lakes).
- Turbidity was negatively correlated with dissolved solids (r = −0.63; −0.86 after removal of Coso Junction data). LOI was negatively correlated with salinity (r = −0.79).
- Reduced turbidity in saline lakes was interpreted as allowing light penetration, which may favour autotrophy over argillotrophy.

**pH, silica and phosphorus**
- pH ranged from 7.5 (South Panamint) to 10.0 (Deep Springs); values exceeded 8.5 in 36 of 45 samples.
- Total phosphorus ranged from 0.11 ppm PO₄ (South Panamint) to 73.31 ppm PO₄ (concentrated Deep Springs). Silica ranged from 3.5 ppm (Ford's) to 534.62 ppm (Deep Springs).

**Principal component analysis**
- The first three components explained 85% of variance (PC1 49%).
- PC1 represented a gradient from dilute, turbid, organic-rich waters to hypersaline, clear, low-organic waters.
- PC2 separated saline lakes into two diverging groups: Group 1 (Deep Springs, Tecopa, Soda; n = 9) had higher pH, alkalinity, silica and phosphorus with low Ca and Mg; Group 2 (Koehn, South Panamint, Cadiz, Harper; n = 12) retained Ca and Mg in excess.
- PC3 (8% of variance) was related mainly to pH and total phosphorus and was difficult to interpret.

**Controls on salinity and chemistry**
- The author attributed Na dominance in dilute waters to silicate hydrolysis, fractional dissolution of efflorescent salts and cation exchange on clays, not to evaporation alone.
- Major ion composition in saline lakes was governed by the HCO₃/(Ca+Mg) mole ratio of inflow waters, via early precipitation of alkaline earth carbonates and gypsum. Low-ratio inflows (Cadiz, South Panamint, Koehn, Harper) produced Ca-Na-Cl brines; high-ratio inflows (Deep Springs, Soda, Tecopa) produced Na-Cl-SO₄-CO₃ brines.
- Silica and phosphorus followed the same two-group pattern: low-ratio lakes had lower pH, silica and phosphorus. Silica was explained using the evaporative-concentration model of [[Jones et al. 1967]].

**Trophic conclusions**
- The dilute-to-saline transition marks a shift from argillotrophic, heterotrophically based lakes to potentially autotrophic systems. The author suggests this distinction may justify separate terms for playa and salina.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Turbidity was measured by a linear-response assumption in distilled-water dilutions.
- Dissolved oxygen could not be accurately measured above sea-water salinity; conductance could not be measured above 100,000 μS/cm.
- Alkalinity titrations were affected by suspended clay particles and carbonate-phase minerals.
