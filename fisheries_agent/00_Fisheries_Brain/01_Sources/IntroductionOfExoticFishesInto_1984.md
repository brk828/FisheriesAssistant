# Introduction of Exotic Fishes into Canada

## Executive Summary
This historical account by E. J. Crossman traces the origins and management philosophy behind the introduction of exotic fish species into Canada, beginning in the late 1800s. The paper frames fisheries management history as a series of "ages" — law enforcement, regulations, hatcheries, introductions, and (by the late 1960s) eradication. It centers on two case studies: the [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), introduced largely for sport fishing purposes, and the [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), introduced as a cheap food fish following the collapse of native [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) stocks in Lake Ontario. The carp introduction, championed by Samuel Wilmott ("the father of fish culture in Canada"), is presented as an early and ultimately regretted attempt to use an exotic species to compensate for anthropogenic extirpation of a native fishery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native context (Lake Ontario):** The [[Atlantic Salmon]] population in Lake Ontario had been extirpated ("rehabilitation" attempts by Samuel Wilmott failed), creating an ecological and economic vacuum that managers sought to fill with an exotic species.
- **Brown Trout introduction:** Introduced as a sport fish to diversify angling opportunities, reflecting the broader late-19th century trend of importing foreign game fish species into Canadian waters, following the lead of the United States.
- **Common Carp introduction:** Deliberately introduced into Ontario waters as a substitute food fish niche where salmon had been lost — intended as "a cheap article of food" for the "poor man's table" (McCrimmon 1968:20).
- **Outcome:** Within 19 years of introduction, the Ontario Department of Fish and Game concluded that the "promiscuous introduction of carp on this continent had been attended with nothing but evil results" (Crossman 1968:8), indicating significant negative ecological impact, though specific ecological mechanisms (e.g., habitat degradation, competition) are not detailed in this excerpt.

## Key Findings
- Fisheries management history can be periodized into distinct "ages": law enforcement, regulations, hatcheries, introductions (1850s–1900s), and eradication (by the time of writing, ~1968) (Crossman 1968).
- Canada's exotic fish introductions in the late 1800s closely followed United States precedent.
- Most early exotic introductions (e.g., [[Brown Trout]]) were sport-fish oriented and considered relatively "innocent" in intent — aimed at increasing angler species diversity.
- The [[Common Carp]] introduction represents a documented early case of using an exotic species to *compensate* for the human-caused extirpation of a native species ([[Atlantic Salmon]]) in a specific ecosystem (Lake Ontario).
- Despite optimistic intentions, the carp introduction was assessed as ecologically harmful within two decades, yet the species persisted and remains established in Canadian waters as of the paper's writing ("the exotic is with us still").
- This case is presented as a cautionary historical precedent for later 20th-century exotic fish policy debates in North America.
