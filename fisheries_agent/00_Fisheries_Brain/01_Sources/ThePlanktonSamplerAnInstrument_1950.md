## Executive Summary
This 1950 technical paper by Clarke and Bumpus describes the design, operation, and calibration of the **Clarke-Bumpus Plankton Sampler**, a closing plankton net fitted with an internal flow meter. Developed at [[Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution]], the instrument was designed to solve a persistent problem in aquatic ecology: obtaining **quantitative** (not merely qualitative) estimates of plankton abundance by simultaneously collecting organisms and measuring the exact volume of water filtered. While the sampler was originally engineered to target the **copepod** fraction of marine [[Zooplankton]], empirical use demonstrated its versatility across a wide range of taxa and both marine and freshwater systems. The paper details mechanical operation, deployment protocols, calibration procedures, and comparative validation against other sampling methods (Juday plankton trap, plankton pump, continuous plankton recorder).

## Background & Description
*(Instrument design substitutes for organismal morphology in this source; target-organism size classes are noted where discussed.)*

- **Sampler body**: Brass tube, 5 inches in diameter, ~6 inches long, weight ~10 lbs total.
- **Net**: Interchangeable conical nets (~2 ft long), various mesh sizes, secured via bayonet-lock ring; mesh as fine as No. 10 (109 threads/inch) usable, though fine mesh increases clogging risk.
- **Shutter mechanism**: A rotating disc shutter (analogous to a stove-pipe damper) mounted on vertical pivots at the tube's front, mechanically triggered by messengers sliding down the support cable to open/close the sampling aperture at precise depths.
- **Flow meter**: Internal propeller geared to a cyclometer-type counter, recording revolutions proportional to filtered water volume; calibrated at 3.8–4.5 liters/revolution across five test units (approx. 4.0 L/rev as a standard estimate).
- **Frame and cable attachment**: Pivoting frame allows the tube to self-orient horizontally regardless of cable angle; swivel-mounted via spring pin and gate lock to maintain forward-facing orientation while under tow.
- **Target organism size range**: Effective for organisms retained by silk netting — spans small eggs/larvae and phytoplankton up to larger zooplankton such as [[Sagitta]] (chaetognaths), [[Euphausiids]], and [[Amphipods]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Sampling domain**: Designed for **horizontal or oblique tows** through the pelagic water column; not intended for vertical hauls, targeting mid-water and near-surface plankton assemblages in both marine and inland (freshwater) systems.
- **Depth-stratified sampling**: Multiple samplers can be rigged simultaneously at different depths on a single cable, enabling reconstruction of **vertical distribution profiles** of plankton within a water column — a key method for understanding [[Diel Vertical Migration]] and stratified community structure.
- **Applicable environments**: Validated in marine settings (Woods Hole coastal waters, Lofoten area of Norway) and freshwater lentic systems (Trout Lake, Wisconsin), demonstrating cross-ecosystem utility.
- **Community representativeness**: Because tows integrate plankton over a wide horizontal area, the sampler is argued to better represent patchy or irregularly distributed plankton populations compared to point-based methods like the [[Juday Plankton Trap]] or plankton pump.
- **Trophic context**: Though the source doesn't address fish directly, the copepod, euphausiid, and amphipod taxa targeted form critical prey resources at the base of marine and freshwater food webs supporting planktivorous fish.

## Key Findings
- **Net size does not bias catch composition**: Statistical comparison (Winsor & Clarke, 1940) of 5-inch, 12-inch, and 30-inch diameter nets showed no significant difference in catch variability or species proportion — validating the small 5-inch aperture as sufficient for reliable quantitative sampling, including of larger, more active plankton.
- **Calibration stability across tow speeds**: Flow meter ratings varied <5% across boat speeds from 0.5 to 4 knots, meaning precise knowledge of vessel speed is unnecessary within this range.
- **Clogging is not a quantitative error source**: Because the meter records only the water that actually passes through the net, net clogging does not compromise volumetric accuracy — provided flow remains above the minimum threshold of ~0.5 knot.
- **Cross-validation with other methods**: Comparative studies (Neess 1949 vs. [[Juday Plankton Trap]]; Wiborg 1948 vs. plankton pump in the [[Lofoten Area]], Norway) supported the sampler's accuracy and consistency relative to established alternative techniques.
- **Utility for large-sample, wide-area studies**: The instrument's design permits both intensive small-area studies and broad synoptic surveys via combinations of horizontal and oblique tows, positioning it as a flexible tool for large-scale quantitative plankton ecology work, foundational to subsequent zooplankton monitoring programs.
- **Operational robustness**: Despite its light weight (10 lbs), the sampler was engineered for durability in rough field conditions, usable from research vessels down to small rowboats, broadening its accessibility for varied research contexts.
