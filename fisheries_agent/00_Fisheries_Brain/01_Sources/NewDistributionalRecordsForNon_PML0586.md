# Florida Scientist Vol. 46 Issue 1 Excerpt: Manatees, Non-Native Fishes and Water Column Sampling

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #poeciliidae #trichechidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from *Florida Scientist* (vol. 46, no. 1, 1983) contains three components: the end of a manatee study from Turkey Creek, Brevard County; a paper on new distributional records for non-native fishes in Florida's coastal waters; and a methods paper on a tube-type water column sampler for shallow lagoons. It also includes a bibliography for the manatee paper. The fish and manatee studies both concern human impacts in Brevard County, specifically boat traffic, herbicide use and the introduction of exotic species.

## Background & Description

**Manatee study.** The [[West Indian Manatee]] (*Trichechus manatus*) occurs in Florida waters, and the excerpt reports on animals in [[Turkey Creek]]. The study recorded 45 individual manatees (39 adults and 6 calves). Animals were seen alone or in small groups, usually of 2 or 3. The author notes that more knowledge of manatee distribution and abundance is needed for land-use planning.

**Non-native fishes.** Dial and Wainwright (Florida Atlantic University, Boca Raton) report new distributional records for five established non-native species in Brevard County:
- [[Jack Dempsey]] (*Cichlasoma octofasciatum*)
- [[Blackchin Tilapia]] (*Tilapia melanotheron*)
- [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*)
- [[Southern Platyfish]] (*Xiphophorus maculatus*)
- [[Green Swordtail]] (*Xiphophorus helleri*)

The authors state that all five were already known elsewhere in Florida. Fish were collected from [[Satellite Beach]], [[South Patrick]] and [[Indian Harbor Beach]] in 1980 and 1981, with the sites listed as FAU collection numbers.

**Water column sampler.** Youngbluth and colleagues (Harbor Branch Foundation) describe a simple rigid PVC tube sampler for shallow, well-mixed water. It has a 7.62 cm inside diameter, is 4.57 m long and holds 0.021 m³. The study was conducted in the [[Indian River Lagoon]], which has a mean depth of 1.5 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Manatee threats.** Boat and barge collisions were a documented cause of manatee death in Florida. U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service data (April 1974 to March 1981, personal communication) attribute about 22% of Florida manatee mortality and 26% of Brevard County mortality to boats or barges. Boats travelling at excessive speed posed the most obvious threat in Turkey Creek, and manatees with fresh scars were seen.

**Herbicide and habitat effects.** The Melbourne Tillman Drainage Control District applies [[2,4-D Herbicide]] annually in Turkey Creek to control [[Water Hyacinth]]. The authors suggest that this reduces food available to manatees and that weed removal around docks and boat ramps has the same effect. They note that bioconcentration of 2,4-D residues in manatees is possible because animals ate sprayed plants, but that no data currently support this concern. The authors also cite reports that 2,4-D breaks down rapidly in water and has not been linked to adverse effects on animal health worldwide.

**Seasonal distribution.** Manatees were present in Turkey Creek throughout most of the year. Their distribution shifted in winter, apparently in response to lower ambient temperatures.

**Fish habitat and salinity.** The cichlid species tolerate high salinity and use estuaries in their native ranges. In the [[Banana River]], salinities range from 9.3 to 34.5 ppt, with a mean of 21.8 ppt. The *Xiphophorus* species are relatively [[Stenohaline Tolerance|stenohaline]] and need low salinity, so they are not expected to colonize estuaries. In the collections, *C. octofasciatum* was found only at salinities of 2 to 9 ppt, in shallow water with dense littoral vegetation, and was absent from higher-salinity adjacent water.

**Temperature limits.** Low temperature is the main barrier to northward expansion. Shafland and Pestrak (in press) predict suitable winter temperatures for *T. melanotheron* as far north as [[Daytona Beach]] and for *T. mossambica* as far north as [[Gainesville]]. Salinity may moderate [[Lower Lethal Temperature]] tolerance, which could allow further northward spread in brackish and marine water. Both tilapia species survived the cold winter of 1981 (water temperatures of 8 to 14 °C).

## Key Findings

**Manatee study (Turkey Creek)**
- Boat and barge strikes caused about 22% of manatee deaths in Florida and 26% in Brevard County (April 1974 to March 1981, USFWS data).
- Boat speed was the main observed threat in Turkey Creek, with near-misses and scarred animals documented.
- Herbicide use (2,4-D) and weed removal may reduce food supplies for manatees. Bioconcentration was hypothesized but not demonstrated.
- Forty-five individual manatees were identified (39 adults, 6 calves), mostly alone or in groups of 2 to 3.

**Non-native fishes (Brevard County)**
- Five non-native species (three cichlids, two poeciliids) were found established in coastal canals and the Banana and Indian River estuary.
- *C. octofasciatum* made up 3.4% of fish by number (15 individuals, 18 to 116 mm standard length) in collection FAU 80-33. A second collection (FAU 80-35B) had 9 individuals, comprising 8.3% by number and 79.6% by weight.
- *T. melanotheron* is spreading rapidly. It has been established in [[Tampa Bay]] since 1958 and supported a commercial catch of about 18,000 kg in 1968. A single 200 lb catch was reported from the Banana River.
- In [[Lithia Springs]], *T. melanotheron* became dominant and native centrarchid abundance was well below expected levels. Competition and grazing were suggested as causes.
- *T. mossambica* appears to be present but its status remains unclear. Adults were observed and collected, but no juveniles were seen.
- *X. maculatus* was collected in the [[Satellite Beach Civic Center]] pond. Three fish were taken in February 1980, versus 44 in a spring 1981 collection, which may partly reflect seasonal reproductive peaks.
- Diet overlap is likely between tilapia and native estuarine bottom feeders. The authors consider the risk to native estuarine fishes, as seen in California, to be the most immediate concern.
- The likely introduction routes were fishermen (for tilapia, to start a commercial fishery) and home aquarists (for the *Xiphophorus* and cichlid species).

**Water column sampler**
- Samples from the tube sampler and the [[Niskin Bottle]] (5 L, 0.005 m³) were statistically similar for inorganic nutrients, active phytoplankton chlorophyll *a* and copepod nauplii.
- The tube sampler captured about twice as many [[Copepod Nauplii]] as a 25 cm inside-diameter, 64 µm mesh net.
- Tube sampling used 4 depth intervals (0 to 0.9, 0 to 1.8, 0 to 2.7 and 0 to 3.7 m) with two samples per interval. The Niskin bottle sampled 5 discrete depths with two samples each.
- The tube is lowered slowly (about 30 to 60 cm/s) so that it fills evenly. A 5.08 cm ball valve replaced the original rubber closure, which did not seal reliably.
