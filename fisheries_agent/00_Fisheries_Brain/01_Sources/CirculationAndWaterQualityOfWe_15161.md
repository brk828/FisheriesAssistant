# Circulation and Water Quality of Western Lake Ontario: A Study for Water Supply Design Purposes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1965 Ontario Department of Lands and Forests Research Report (No. 62, Technical Series) by D. H. Matheson (City of Hamilton Municipal Laboratories) and D. V. Anderson (University of Toronto, Mathematics) presents a limnological survey of the western end of [[Lake Ontario]]. The study was designed to guide the siting and design of a new Hamilton waterworks intake. It combined water chemistry, bacteriology, algal counts, temperature profiling, and current tracking (drogues and drift bottles) across a triangular area from Hamilton to Bronte to Grimsby. The main conclusion is that [[Burlington Bay]] is the principal pollution source affecting the western lake, that pollutant transport is strongly wind-controlled, and that the new intake should be placed near the existing site but extend farther into the lake.

The document contains no fish or fish-specific data. Its scope is the physical, chemical, and biological water environment relevant to intake design.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** In December 1959 the City Engineer of Hamilton asked the Department of Municipal Laboratories to plan a study of western Lake Ontario to support design and location of new waterworks intakes. The City appropriated $20,000, and the Public Utilities Commission of Burlington made an additional contribution.
- **Scientific context:** Lake Ontario was then less studied than other Great Lakes, and general circulation and nearshore conditions were poorly documented. Earlier current data came from [[Harrington Drift Bottle Survey]] (1892–94), which the authors regard as largely of historical value.
- **Research vessels:** The Royal Canadian Navy chartered a 40 ft tug, the PLAINSVILLE, which the Ontario Department of Lands and Forests had outfitted for science. A 30 ft whaleboat was loaned by the [[Great Lakes Institute]] while negotiations were ongoing.
- **Publication history:** A preliminary report was published in 1962, interim and final internal reports followed, and this Research Branch volume appeared in 1965 after a long delay.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pollution sources and indicators**
- [[Burlington Bay]] is the most obvious pollution source. It receives city sewage and industrial wastes, expected to increase with growth. Other large sources were unknown. Diffuse inputs from smaller towns and streams probably add nitrogen and phosphorus to the lake.
- [[Cladophora]] algal blooms and shoreline decay in the 1950s were the main trigger for the Lake Ontario Anti-Pollution Association (formed 1959).
- Hamilton intake coliform density rose about fivefold from 1935 to 1960. Total chlorine dose almost doubled from 1940 to 1960.
- Phenol peaks above Bay-derived levels were recorded in 1959, source undetermined, and were not seen at comparable levels in 1960.
- The Niagara River influence on the north shore was judged negligible, with its outflow generally moving east along the American shore.

**Parameters measured**
- Ammonia nitrogen (4–6 ppm in the Bay), a nutrient and the main driver of chlorine demand.
- Mineral constituents (pH, alkalinity, hardness, silica), which differ strongly between Bay and lake water.
- Phenol, which causes taste and odour problems.
- Synthetic detergents (Syndet, about 0.7 ppm in the Bay in 1964), used as a tracer of Bay water.
- Coliform bacteria, counted by membrane filtration.
- Diatoms and other algae, which interfere with filtration.

**Physical processes**
- The lake shows a seasonal level cycle plus wind-driven fluctuations and seiches. A gauge was installed at Stoney Creek in November 1960.
- Nearshore bottom has a steep drop at the beach bars, then a gentle, smooth slope.
- A thermocline in summer strongly influences whether Bay effluent stays at the surface or mixes downward.
- Bay outflow through the [[Burlington Canal]] can form a two-layer exchange, with warm surface outflow and cold inflow at depth. Velocities of about 30 ft/min were recorded at the surface and near the bottom, with zero flow midway.

**Circulation**
- Surface and deep currents in the west basin were generally clockwise, except when a strong wind opposed them. This contradicts the counter-clockwise cyclonic flow suggested by Harrington (1895).
- Surface currents respond quickly to wind. A sustained east wind deepens the thermocline, and a prolonged west wind raises it.
- Drogues in the same thermal layer moved together. Drogues in different layers could move differently.
- Of 24 drift bottles released on 22 July 1960, 14 cards were returned (about 30% of all bottles released). Most recoveries fit a clockwise pattern.
- Local upwelling and sinking are likely near shore, with sand accumulation on the east sides of south-shore structures possibly reflecting clockwise flow or easterly surf.

**Pollutant behaviour**
- On 7 June 1960 a clockwise surface current carried a polluted tongue northeast from the canal. On 8 September the pollution was diffuse and the current pattern was not evident.
- Pollution tends to concentrate near shore. East winds push it inshore, and west-wind upwelling traps it in a narrow beach band. In calm conditions it moves in discrete masses through shallow water.
- In summer, effluent moves in surface layers where biodegradation is strongest. In winter, spring and late fall (homothermal conditions), Bay emissions mix to the bottom and reach the intakes at higher concentrations and more often.
- On 23–26 November 1960 an ammonia peak and matching coliform increase appeared in the 48-inch intake but not in the 60-inch intake, showing that the two intakes can draw different water masses.

**Chlorine demand**
- Chlorine demand is approximately 1.0 ppm plus ten times the ammonia nitrogen concentration. Demands above 3 ppm were observed, and demands above 5 ppm were expected from ammonia levels, though only briefly.

**Management conclusions**
- The new Hamilton intake will be placed near the present intakes, not farther east as a casual reading might suggest, and will extend much farther into the lake. The farther offshore the intake, the better, because pollution is concentrated near shore.
- The authors stress that long-term study is needed. The 1960 work demonstrated the value of a limnological survey but did not resolve how weather and other factors control mixing. A continuous current recording station at Hamilton was proposed.
