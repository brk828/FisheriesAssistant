# Rio Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #poeciliidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan addresses three federally listed fishes of the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] of southwestern North America: [[Yaqui Chub]] (endangered), [[Beautiful Shiner]] (threatened), and [[Yaqui Catfish]] (threatened). It also covers the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (Yaqui form) and a group of candidate and native species, including [[Mexican Stoneroller]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Yaqui Sucker]], and the [[San Bernardino Ranch Snail]]. The plan describes species biology and distribution, the historical and present habitat of the region, the causes of decline, and conservation actions. Its central goal is self-sustaining populations, centered on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], that allow downlisting and delisting under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The plan includes recovery criteria and a 1986 addition proposing a habitat-based management framework.

## Background & Description

**Legal and conservation status.** The three Yaqui fishes were recently listed under the Endangered Species Act. The Arizona Game and Fish Department assigned Group I and Group II statuses, and the American Fisheries Society and Desert Fishes Council gave differing assessments. Of these, the Yaqui chub and Sonoran topminnow are Group II (threatened with extirpation). The beautiful shiner, Yaqui sucker, and Yaqui catfish are Group I (extirpated from Arizona but extant elsewhere).

**Geography.** The Rio Yaqui drains about 73,000 km² of mountainous terrain, mostly in northwestern Mexico. Its discharge averages about 2800 ha-m/year. The U.S. portion (Cochise County, Arizona, and Hidalgo County, New Mexico) makes up less than 1.0% of the watershed. Critical habitat for three listed species is designated on the San Bernardino NWR (934 ha) near Douglas, Arizona. The refuge was purchased by The Nature Conservancy in 1979 and transferred to USFWS in 1982.

**Species accounts (summarized):**

- **[[Yaqui Chub]]** (*Gila purpurea*): Maximum length about 15 cm TL. Males turn steel-blue when breeding. Historically known from only two Mexican localities. Current U.S. populations derive from fish moved from [[Leslie Creek]] in 1969 and 1976.
- **[[Beautiful Shiner]]** (*Notropis formosus*): Rarely exceeds 50 mm TL. Breeding males have yellow or orange fins. Subspecies status is unresolved, with northern (Bavispe) and southern (Papigochic) Yaqui populations differing consistently. Not recorded in the U.S. since 1970.
- **[[Yaqui Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus pricei*): Up to 50 cm TL and dark-colored. Taxonomic relationships are obscure and biology is largely unknown. Extirpated from the Santa Cruz basin, Arizona, by the late 1950s.
- **[[Sonoran Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): A small live-bearer (males seldom exceed 25 mm TL). It is the most northern member of its tropical, coastal genus.
- **[[Mexican Stoneroller]]** (*Campostoma ornatum*): Up to about 15 cm TL. Mexican populations were formerly named *C. pricei*, but this name was synonymized.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*, Rio Yaqui form): Up to 75 mm TL. A Rio Yaqui subspecies is soon to be described.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*): Regularly exceeds 40 cm TL in larger streams. Former U.S. occurrence in the Yaqui system is debatable.
- **[[Yaqui Sucker]]** (*Catostomus bernardini*): Often reaches 50 cm TL. Extirpated from the U.S. when Astin Spring dried.
- **[[San Bernardino Ranch Snail]]** (*Pyrgulopsis cochisei*): A miniscule hydrobiid endemic to two spring areas on the refuge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Most Rio Yaqui fishes live at intermediate and lower elevations in springs, [[Cienega]] wetlands, creeks, and moderate-sized rivers. Stream habitats typically alternate riffles and pools, with undercut banks, boulder and woody cover, and gravel bottoms. Yaqui chub occurs most often on cut banks along pool margins, and its largest populations are in early-stage cienega succession. Sonoran topminnow tolerates pH 6.6–8.9, dissolved oxygen of 2.0–11.0 mg/l, and salinity from fresh water to 35 g/l.

**Associated biota.** On the San Bernardino NWR, the fauna is dominated by longfin dace. In the Rio Yaqui, Mexican stoneroller, longfin dace, Yaqui sucker, and Sonoran topminnow were the most frequent native associates. The most common non-native associate was river carpsucker, at six localities.

**Historical conditions.** Early 1846–1892 descriptions recorded perennial springs, marshes, and cottonwood-willow riparian zones in San Bernardino and Douglas valleys. Pollen records indicate cienega habitat 800–4000 years before present, with earlier arroyo cutting. Heavy cattle grazing, logging, and mining affected the region, and Douglas Valley agriculture began in 1910.

**Drivers of decline.**

- **Arroyo cutting** around 1900 lowered water tables, destroyed cienegas, and made streams intermittent. Cienegas had regulated flows and dampened floods.
- **Groundwater pumping** for agriculture (more than 100 km² irrigated by 1965) desiccated springs such as Croton Spring and Cienega in the Sulphur Springs basin.
- **Introduced species**, especially [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which is considered the major factor in declines of the Gila form of Sonoran topminnow and a serious threat on the refuge. Largemouth bass, black crappie, and bluegill in a pond likely caused the local extirpation of beautiful shiner.
- **Dams and diversions.** Three mainstream reservoirs in Mexico, with more under planning, pose future risks.
- **Direct habitat destruction**, including cattle trampling at Astin Spring and capping of an artesian well at Twin Ponds (~1970).

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** Yaqui chub descended from 224 individuals moved from Leslie Creek to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in 1976. Its largest Arizona populations are in Leslie Creek, North and House ponds, and Black Draw, though numbers fluctuate widely. Beautiful shiner has not been collected in the U.S. since 1970. Quantitative abundance data are unavailable for most species.
- **Mexican abundance (1978 survey):** Mexican stoneroller comprised more than 20% of fishes in the upper Rio Yaqui. Yaqui sucker made up more than 40% of fishes in the Rio Papigochic subbasin. Yaqui catfish was mostly represented by single specimens.
- **Reintroductions:** Stocking of Yaqui chub and topminnow into [[Leslie Creek]] in 1969 was successful. Proposed construction of a fishing lake there was disallowed for hydrologic reasons. Current water rights claims in the basin (158.7 ha-m/year) exceed average annual runoff by about 80.8 ha-m/year.
- **Recovery criteria:**
  - *Yaqui chub:* downlist after six stable populations on the NWR and secure populations in Leslie and West Turkey creeks. Delist after viable populations are established in the Mexican historic range.
  - *Beautiful shiner:* delist after five stable NWR populations, reestablishment of the Guzman form in the Mimbres River, and secure populations in Mexico.
  - *Yaqui catfish:* delist after four stable NWR populations and secure populations in Mexico.
  - *Yaqui topminnow:* downlisting requires complete federal control of Ranch habitat, elimination of mosquitofish from all seven existing habitats, and stable populations surviving at least five years.
  - *Candidate and native species:* maintain diversified fauna, including three Mexican stoneroller populations on the NWR and at least the Rucker Canyon population in Arizona. Also establish four longfin dace populations, one roundtail chub population, six Yaqui sucker populations, and two or more San Bernardino Ranch snail populations.
- **Habitat management framework (1986 addition):** Recovery depends on maintaining existing habitats, securing new ones, reclaiming marginal habitat, constructing habitat for introductions, and continued management of all presently suitable habitats.
- **Regulatory measures:** Arizona Game and Fish Commission rules restrict live baitfish use in Cochise County, prohibit unpermitted fish transport or stocking, and prompted negotiations on public health mosquitofish use. The refuge is managed to remove mosquitofish and sunfishes through ichthyocide renovation and desiccation.
- **Knowledge gaps:** Taxonomy of the Rio Yaqui fishes, especially beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish, remains unresolved. Biology of Yaqui catfish, roundtail chub, and San Bernardino Ranch snail is largely unknown.
