# Partial Checklist of Fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #centrarchidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This April 1969 report, prepared by W. L. Minckley (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University) for the Office of River Basins, U.S. Department of the Interior, presents a partial checklist of the fishes of the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] from about Lee's Ferry, Arizona, to Mexico. The checklist is built from published literature and original collections. The document lists **17 families, 52 genera and 854 species** as printed in the source; this total does not match the roughly 90 species actually itemized, so it is likely an OCR or typographic error and should be verified against the original. Introduced species are marked with an asterisk, and the author excludes bait fishes that have not become established and early introductions by the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries that failed to persist.

The narrative accompanying the checklist covers each family in turn. It is organized around three themes: the decline of native species (especially the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]-related forms), the spread of introduced competitors and predators (notably [[Red Shiner]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Mosquitofish]]), and the growing importance of introduced sport and forage fishes such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Rainbow Trout]].

## Background & Description

The checklist follows the common names of the Bailey et al. (1960) *Check-list of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada*. It is a partial survey rather than a complete inventory, and the author states that acknowledgements are not always given for reasons of space.

Families covered include:

- **Acipenseridae:** [[White Sturgeon]], introduced in the mid-1960s; no evidence of reproduction.
- **Elopidae:** [[Machete]] (*Elops affinis*), a rare native.
- **Clupeidae:** [[Threadfin Shad]], introduced and used as forage.
- **Salmonidae:** [[Kokanee]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Arctic Grayling]], and two native trouts, [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]].
- **Esocidae:** [[Northern Pike]], introduced to high-elevation lakes.
- **Characidae:** [[Mexican Tetra]], apparently established below Morelos Dam.
- **Cyprinidae:** the largest native family in the basin, including the [[Longfin Dace]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Chub]], [[Gila Chub]], several spinedaces, [[Woundfin]], [[Moapa Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and introduced species such as [[Golden Shiner]], [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]].
- **Catostomidae:** suckers, including [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Gila Sucker]], [[Humpback Sucker]] and introduced buffalofishes.
- **Ictaluridae:** bullheads, [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]].
- **Cyprinodontidae:** [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Whitewater Killifish]] and an introduced Rio Grande killifish.
- **Poeciliidae:** [[Mosquitofish]], [[Gila Topminnow]] (the only native livebearer) and several introduced aquarium livebearers.
- **Serranidae and Centrarchidae:** [[Striped Bass]], [[White Bass]], [[Yellow Bass]], and all sunfishes and basses, which the author describes as introduced.
- **Percidae, Cichlidae, Gobiidae, Mugilidae, Eleotridae:** perches, cichlids, a mudsucker, [[Striped Mullet]] and [[Spotted Sleeper]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author repeatedly links the fauna to changes in water quality, flow and temperature caused by impoundments, irrigation and drying of springs. Several themes recur:

- **Habitat specialization and extinction risk.** Many natives, such as the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Moapa Dace]] and [[Pahranagat Spinedace]], are endemic, monotypic or restricted to isolated tributaries, springs or headwaters. The author considers these highly vulnerable to dams, desiccation, pumping and introductions.
- **Competition and predation by introduced fishes.** Red shiners are described as capable of "totally displacing" the [[Spikedace]], and Mosquitofish are called "one of the larger threats" to native fishes. Common Carp are implicated in the decline of native species through turbidity and feeding habits.
- **Parasitism.** Native chubs and dace are highly susceptible to ectoparasites such as *Lernaea*, and infestation rises where introduced fishes are abundant.
- **Salinity.** The author suggests that increasing salinity in the lower river may favor the Machete and [[Mullet]] species.
- **Thermal and oxygen tolerance.** Several natives (e.g., [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Whitewater Killifish]]) live in extreme thermal or low-oxygen springs, which the author regards as scientifically valuable.

## Key Findings

- **Introduced species dominate the sport fishery.** Nearly all sunfishes, basses, trouts, and many minnows and catfishes are introduced. [[Largemouth Bass]] is described as the major game fish, and [[Channel Catfish]] as "an exceedingly important species."
- **Native species are declining.** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is "exceedingly rare" below [[Lake Mead]]. The [[Humpback Sucker]] has apparently disappeared from the Gila Basin within the last 20 years and shows little recent reproduction. The [[Bonytail Chub]] is "greatly doubted" to persist in the future.
- **Some natives are already extinct locally.** The White River subspecies of *Lepidomeda mollispinis*, the Pahranagat spinedace (*Lepidomeda altivelis*) and the Gila populations of the bonytail are reported extinct. The [[Desert Pupfish]] is described as "widely extinct."
- **Competitive threats are rising.** The [[Red Shiner]], introduced in the early 1950s, is spreading throughout the basin, possibly via bait-bucket transfers. The [[Mosquitofish]] is described as likely to occupy the entire lower basin.
- **Scientific value is high.** Endemic and monotypic species (e.g., [[Gila Trout]] as a relict, [[Meda Fulgida]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Woundfin]] and [[Moapa Dace]]) are regarded as scientifically important, and several are the subject of ongoing studies.
- **Specific recommendations.** The author recommends curtailing the spread of [[Red Shiner]] until more information is available and calls for a research program on the competitive effects of [[Golden Shiner]]. He also suggests developing spawning and refugium areas in canals to sustain [[Channel Catfish]] populations, and recommends active management to keep the [[Colorado Squawfish]] from disappearing.
- **Prognosis varies by taxon.** The [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]] is described as having a good prognosis if headwater quality is maintained. The [[Gila Topminnow]] is severely endangered, restricted to two spring areas in Arizona. The [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]] have a future that is difficult to predict.
- **Data gaps.** The author notes that the status of several introduced species (e.g., [[Mexican Tetra]], [[Walleye]] and [[Striped Bass]] reproduction) is still uncertain, and that a third tilapia species may be a hybrid.
