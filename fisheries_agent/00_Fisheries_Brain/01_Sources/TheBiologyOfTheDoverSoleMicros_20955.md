# The Biology of the Dover Sole (*Microstomus pacificus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pleuronectidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game *Fish Bulletin* (No. 85, 1952, by Frederick B. Hagerman) describes the biology of the [[Dover Sole]], *Microstomus pacificus*, and the trawl fishery that depended on it. The study drew on about 3,200 specimens collected from July 1948 to October 1949, mainly from commercial landings at [[Eureka]] with some sampling at [[Fort Bragg]]. The work covers morphology, length-weight relationships, age and growth, maturity and fecundity, spawning, larval development and distribution, migration, food habits and conservation. Its management goal is to keep the stock at its most productive level by balancing recruitment against total mortality.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and naming:** The species belongs to the family Pleuronectidae and is technically a flounder, not a true sole. It is the only *Microstomus* on the eastern Pacific coast. It was often confused with the Atlantic [[Lemon Sole]] (*Microstomus kitt*). Many regional names were used, including slippery sole, slime sole, rubber sole, smear dab and lemon sole. The name "Dover sole" was adopted in California in 1948 because it had better marketing value.
- **Morphology:** The fish is invariably dextral and slender, with body depth 2.8–3.2 in standard length. Dorsal rays number 90–116 and anal rays 80–96. Lateral line scales number 137–146. Teeth are present on the blind side only, forming a continuous cutting edge. This separates it from the close relative *Embassichthys bathybius*, which has teeth on both sides. The skin is very mucous and slimy.
- **Coloration:** The eyed side is brown to mottled, with two yellowish-brown spots along the lateral line in young fish. Adult males usually have black fin rays, while females are dark brownish. Two aberrant forms were recorded in the Eureka region, including a golden xanthochroic individual taken near Redding Rock in 1951.
- **Sampling methods:** Fish were measured to the nearest millimeter from market piles. Sex and gonads were recorded, and scales and [[Otolith]] samples were taken for age analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Dover sole are found over muddy bottoms in greatest numbers. They have the widest bathymetric range of the commercial flatfishes. Juveniles inhabit shallow nursery grounds close to shore, while large fish move into deeper water.
- **Seasonal and depth migration:** In summer (June–August), the main population feeds in shallow water of 100 fathoms or less, which is thought to be a prey-rich feeding ground. In November the fish move outward and downward, probably triggered by spawning, and by December most are in depths that are not fished. In April they return to grounds at about 140 fathoms.
- **Sexual segregation:** Females move inshore more strongly than males. In a July 1949 analysis of eleven market samples, the proportion of males increased with depth.
- **Pelagic larval phase:** Eggs and larvae are buoyant and pelagic for several months. They drift with ocean currents, which likely affects the species' distribution. Larvae have been taken as far as 280 miles offshore and as far south as 60 miles below the Mexican boundary.
- **Diet:** No vertebrates were found in the stomachs examined. Stomach and intestine contents were dominated by mud and sedentary invertebrates such as small bivalves, scaphopods, polychaetes, sipunculids, echinoids and ophiuroids.
- **Associated flatfishes:** The commercial range overlaps with [[Petrale Sole]] (*Eopsetta jordani*), [[English Sole]] (*Parophrys vetulus*) and [[Rex Sole]] (*Glyptocephalus zachirus*). These species move into shallow, protected areas during winter and spring and draw trawling effort away from Dover sole.

## Key Findings

**Fishery history and landings**

- Trawling began on the California coast in 1876. Dover sole was initially discarded as "trash fish" because more valuable flatfishes were abundant.
- The fishery changed from the two-boat [[Paranzella Net]] (used in California until 1940) to the single-boat [[Otter Trawl]], which had become universal on the Pacific coast by 1941.
- Cod-end mesh changed from 14 inches in 1915 to commercial five-inch mesh by 1948.
- Landings began in small amounts in 1943. Quick-freezing and filleting made the species marketable. Landings in the Eureka and Fort Bragg areas exceeded 9,600,000 pounds in 1950, about 38 percent of the total California sole catch.
- Landings are strongly seasonal. In 1949, January and February together totaled 8,800 pounds, while August totaled 1,728,657 pounds. In 1950, January and February totaled 6,049 pounds, while August totaled 2,383,961 pounds.
- Block-area logs and dealer receipts show that the bulk of the catch came from Cape Mendocino to Cape Sebastian, mainly on the 100-fathom contour and over muddy bottoms.

**Length, weight and sex structure**

- Length-weight regressions were fitted by least squares on log-transformed data:
  - Females (1,738 fish): Y = 0.0002389 X^2.97
  - Males (488 fish): Y = 0.000244 X^2.95
  - Here Y is weight in pounds and X is total length in centimeters. The relationship closely follows the cube law.
- The sex ratio in the commercial catch is about 3:1 (female to male). Males are rarely larger than 50 cm.
- Larger fish in the north: Fishing in Oregon yielded larger fish than fishing off Eureka or Fort Bragg. October 1948 female samples had modal lengths of 40.0 cm at Fort Bragg, 41.0 cm at Eureka and 42.5 cm in southern Oregon.
- Length-frequency analysis was judged unsatisfactory for age estimation because about 10 age classes are present in the catch and growth may vary geographically.

**Age and growth**

- Age was estimated from [[Otolith]] (sagitta) readings and scales. Both gave concordant results in most cases. Scale circuli provided clear winter checks.
- Annuli form from October to February. Accessory false checks, including spawning checks, can occur and must be excluded.
- Fish enter the commercial catch at about 30 cm total length and at an average age of about five years.
- Most of the catch is 8–12 years old at 39–46 cm (the summary gives 8–10 years). Fish aged 13–15 years, at 48–55 cm or more, are almost all female.
- Females grow faster and reach a larger ultimate size. By age six, females average 30 mm longer than males.

**Maturity and fecundity**

- Males begin to mature at about 30 cm. Fifty percent are mature at 32 cm and nearly all at 39 cm.
- Females begin to mature at about 33 cm. Fifty percent are mature at 35 cm and nearly all at 45 cm.
- Egg counts were made on both ovaries of eight mature females, which were sampled as stage B (developing). Estimated totals ranged from 37,188 to 229,615 eggs. Fish of about 36–38 cm produced 37,000–50,000 eggs. The summary states that large females produce about 230,000 eggs.

**Spawning cycle**

- Six ovarian stages (A–F) were defined, from immature through spent to resting.
- Spawning peaks from December to February, with a few spawning in November and March. About 30 percent of mature females had spawned by February, and nearly all were spent by April.
- Oocytes grow about tenfold between July and October. Only one group of mature eggs is spawned per season.
- Eggs are 2.05–2.57 mm in diameter, averaging 2.33 mm. They are spherical, translucent and have an undivided yolk with no oil globule.

**Larval development and distribution**

- Postlarvae can be identified by vertebral counts and fin-ray counts. Vertebrae number 51–54 (mean 51.8). Dorsal rays range from 94 to 116 and anal rays from 80 to 96.
- Metamorphosis is delayed. The left eye does not finish migrating until about 35 mm, and even a 48 mm specimen was incomplete.
- Pelagic specimens as small as 26 mm were taken in August. Two pairs of spinous protuberances on the head of postlarvae may serve as a diagnostic feature.
- Larvae were taken at 32 stations: 19 north of San Francisco and 13 south. Stations included an area off Point San Luis, more than 200 miles south of the commercial fishery, where many young fish were found near shore.
- Young fish become demersal at 50–55 mm and are found inshore along the bottom.

**Conservation and regulation**

- The stock is thought to have better survival prospects than other commercial flatfishes because of its wide depth range, seasonal unavailability to the fishery during spawning, winter storms, large escapement and separate nursery grounds.
- Dover sole has the greatest escapement from trawl nets of any flatfish studied. In Clark's 1935 gear experiments, the minimum length retained in a five-inch mesh cod end was 300 mm, compared with 210 mm for English sole, 230 mm for petrale sole and 230 mm for rex sole. Reasons include heavy mucus, body flexibility and the lack of a first interhaemal spine.
- In 1949, California and Oregon prohibited trawling inside the three-mile limit, and California required trawler logs.

**Summary of management implications**

- Dover sole is heavily exploited and has a fast-growing, late-maturing life history with a long pelagic larval phase.
- The author recommends that management be based on biological facts, including size at maturity, growth, spawning seasons, and the effects of gear selectivity and closed areas.
