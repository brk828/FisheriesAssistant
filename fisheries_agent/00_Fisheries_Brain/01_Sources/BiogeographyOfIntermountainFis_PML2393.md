# Biogeography of Intermountain Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 symposium paper by Gerald R. Smith analyzes the distribution of the native fishes of the [[Intermountain Region]], an area bounded by the [[Sierra Nevada]], [[Grand Canyon]], [[Rocky Mountains]], and [[Snake River Plain]]. The fauna comprises 83 species, with the abstract listing 26 genera and the body text 28 genera and 7 families. Distributions were shaped by extensional faulting and volcanism in the [[Great Basin]], range and plateau uplift, and cyclic [[Pleistocene Pluvials]] and interpluvials. The study divides the region into 48 barrier-bounded drainage units and applies correlation, cluster, and principal components analyses, together with fossil records, to separate dispersal from extinction. Its central conclusion is that the pattern is dominated by extinction: the species-area slope is steep, colonization is rare, and postsettlement introductions and habitat destruction are accelerating losses.

## Background & Description

The fishes are assigned to seven families. The Salmonidae include [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Redband Trout]] (*Salmo* sp.), [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*), and several [[Whitefish]] species from [[Bear Lake]]. The Cyprinidae are the largest group and include [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Longnose Dace]] (*Rhinichthys cataractae*), [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*), [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and several [[Spinedace]] species of *Lepidomeda*. The Catostomidae (suckers) include [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) and the [[Mountain Sucker]] (*Catostomus platyrhynchus*). The Cottidae are represented by [[Mottled Sculpin]] (*Cottus bairdi*) and related sculpins. The Cyprinodontidae include [[Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon*) and [[Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys*) species from the [[Death Valley]] system.

The paper treats 46 species (55 percent) and 6 genera (13 percent) as endemic. Many taxa have fossil records from [[Lake Idaho]] and [[Lake Bonneville]], and the paper uses these records to infer earlier distributions. Several species are listed as endangered, vulnerable, or extinct under Miller (1977), including [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and climate.** The [[Great Basin]] contains more than 100 drainage basins among roughly 160 north- or northeast-trending ranges. Faulting began in the Eocene or Oligocene, and most ranges formed in the past 20 million years through crustal extension of 50 to 300 km. The region has a [[Interarc Basin]] structure, with basaltic volcanism radiating outward and the [[Yellowstone Hotspot]] moving eastward along the [[Snake River Plain]] at about 4 cm per year. Isolation increased with aridity (Axelrod 1950), but Pleistocene pluvials connected many drainages.

**Pluvial history.** Lake levels fluctuated across the Pleistocene. The [[Pinedale Glaciation]] pluvial left the clearest record, with high stands between 25,000 and 13,000 years BP, a low stand near 11,000 years, and unstable low stands from about 8,000 years BP to the present. The [[Sangamon Interglacial]] is dated to about 130,000 years BP, and some lacustrine deposits are dated to about 600,000 years through an ash layer.

**Barriers.** Barriers were defined as mountains or deserts uncrossed by continuous aquatic habitat, or one-way restrictions such as falls. Strong barriers include the [[Wasatch Range]], the [[Sierra Nevada]], the southern margin of the Snake River Plain, and the eastern Nevada zone near the origin of Basin and Range tectonism. Barrier strength was calculated as B = x/T, where x is the number of species stopped and T is the mean species total of the two basins.

**Latitudinal and altitudinal effects.** Cool-stream fishes live at lower altitudes in the north and higher altitudes in the south. As a result, passes that connect drainages are accessible in the north but not in the south, and mountain barriers weaken northward. Warm-water cyprinodonts are limited by valleys and lowlands at high latitudes, which is one reason cyprinodonts and sculpins do not overlap latitudinally in the region.

**Human impacts.** Overgrazing from 1880 to 1940 removed vegetative cover and destabilized rivers. Diversion and groundwater overuse drained habitats. Introduced species, mostly from the Mississippi Basin, now outnumber native species in most intermountain waters. Native species evolved under extreme climatic selection with little competition or predation and proved poorly adapted to these introductions.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna and endemism:** 83 species, 26 to 28 genera, and 7 families. 51 of 83 species occur in only one of the 48 drainage units, and 18 of these are endemic to that unit. 33 are more widespread outside the study area.
- **Widest distributions:** *Rhinichthys osculus* occurs in 32 of 48 units and *Gila bicolor* in 21 units. The paper interprets both as extinction-resistant generalists rather than strong colonizers.
- **Basic northern fluvial fauna:** Cluster analysis identified a group including *Cottus bairdi*, *Prosopium williamsoni*, *Salmo clarki*, *Rhinichthys cataractae*, *Richardsonius balteatus*, and related taxa. These species share ecology and dispersal patterns, occupy headwaters, and are often dispersed by [[Stream Capture]].
- **Species–area relationship:** The slope for Great Basin fishes is z = 0.59 (r = 0.66), steeper than the z of about 0.4 reported for Great Basin mammals. This indicates high extinction and low colonization.
- **Extinction dominance:** The paper argues that the patterns largely reflect extinction, especially in the south, where pluvial aridity caused both isolation and loss. Departures from geographic similarity are interpreted as extinction effects.
- **Vicariance:** About two dozen vicariant sister-species pairs were associated with strong barriers (B = 0.53 to 1.0, mean 0.83 for 13 barriers). Examples include *Richardsonius balteatus* and *R. egregius*, *Gila bicolor* and *G. alvordensis*, and *Cottus echinatus* and *C. ericksoni*. Some pupfish and killifish pairs in Death Valley probably arose after the pluvials.
- **Phylogenetic augmentation:** Adding 22 higher taxa to 83 species yielded 105 taxa in the analysis. The paper argues that higher taxa, such as the genus *Chasmistes*, record older dispersal patterns.
- **Postsettlement losses:** Five species have become extinct, and 18 are rare, vulnerable, endangered, or threatened. Many local populations are also lost. The paper recommends watershed-level management and restraint in exotic introductions.
- **Field survey:** Of 374 collections from 1930 to 1960 in the Great Basin, Upper Snake, and Upper Colorado drainages, the mean was 2.4 native and 0.9 introduced species per collection. Trout waters typically held 0–2 native and 1–4 introduced species. The paper describes this as a nonequilibrium situation in which manmade colonizations are increasing and extinctions are accelerating.
