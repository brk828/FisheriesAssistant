## Executive Summary

This study by Gerald A. Cole (1957) presents a six-year (1950–1956) qualitative survey of net plankton in Tom Wallace Lake, a small (2.34 ha, mean depth 4.1 m) eutrophic artificial impoundment in Jefferson County, Kentucky. Rather than fish, the paper documents the taxonomic composition, seasonal succession, and ecological dynamics of phytoplankton (Cyanophyta, Chrysophyta, Chlorophyta), Protozoa/Protista, and zooplankton (Rotatoria, Cladocera, Copepoda, Ostracoda). Key findings include a shift from an early *Melosira*-dominated diatom flora to blue-green algal blooms coincident with fertilizer application, later replacement by flagellate-dominated primary production, puzzling multi-year population fluctuations in taxa such as *Dinobryon*, *Kellicottia bostoniensis*, and *Ceriodaphnia lacustris*, and inferred trophic structure suggesting a dominance of filter-feeding and small-particle-feeding zooplankton with *Chaoborus punctipennis* larvae likely filling the secondary consumer niche.

## Background & Description

**Study System:** [[Tom Wallace Lake]], an artificial impoundment in Jefferson County, Kentucky, constructed in 1946 (filled by winter 1946–47, with 1947 as the lake's first year). The lake is eutrophic, with pronounced thermal stratification and an anaerobic summertime hypolimnion. Its only natural inflow is an insignificant intermittent brook.

**Methodology:** 55 plankton samples (40 preserved, 15 examined fresh) were collected between September 1950 and December 1956 using No. 20 silk bolting cloth tow nets, via shore casts and boat-based vertical/horizontal tows. Sampling was irregular, with midsummer data notably sparse.

**Water Chemistry Context:** Annual application of 6-8-6 commercial fertilizer (2 tons/year) was discontinued after the first five years. Total alkalinity of surface waters increased over the study period: 22 ppm (Sept. 1950) → 44 ppm (1955) → 38 ppm (1956, following an unusually rainy summer).

**Taxonomic Scope (Annotated List of Planktonic Organisms):**
- **Cyanophyta (blue-green algae):** *Anabaena planktonica*, *A. spiroides*, *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*, *Aphanocapsa* sp., *Chroococcus* sp., *Coelosphaerium naegelianum*, *Merismopedia* sp., *Microcystis aeruginosa*, *Oscillatoria* sp., *Trichodesmium lacustre* — largely warm-water plankters (epilimnion >15°C).
- **Chrysophyta (diatoms):** *Asterionella formosa*, *Fragilaria* spp., *Homeocladia sigmoides*, *Navicula* spp., *Surirella robusta*, *Synedra* spp. — rarely abundant, never bloom-forming.
- **Chlorophyta (green algae):** *Botryococcus*, *Closterium* spp., *Coelastrum microporum*, *Cosmarium botrytis*, *Crucigenia* spp., desmids (*Staurastrum*, *Pleurotaenium ehrenbergii*), *Spirogyra* spp., *Sphaerocystis*, and others — not abundant, no consistent dominant species.
- **Protozoa and Protista:** Includes flagellates ([[Dinobryon sertularia]], [[Ceratium hirundinella]], *Eudorina elegans*, *Mallomonas caudata*, *Peridinium cinctum*), ciliates (*Codonella cratera*, *Vorticella* sp.), and rhizopods (*Arcella vulgaris*, *Difflugia lobostoma*).
- **Rotatoria (rotifers):** Most speciose zooplankton group (mean 4.4 species/sample). Dominant taxa: *Keratella cochlearis*, *Polyarthra* spp.
- **Cladocera:** Mean 3.2 species/sample. Dominant taxa: *Bosmina longirostris*, *Daphnia longispina*, *Chydorus sphaericus*, *Ceriodaphnia lacustris*, *Daphnia pulex*, *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*.
- **Copepoda:** Dominated by *Diaptomus pallidus* (calanoid) and *Tropocyclops prasinus* (cyclopoid); mean 1.88 species/sample.
- **Ostracoda:** Only *Cypridopsis vidua* and *Physocypria pustulosa*, both adventitious/benthic.
- **Adventitious Plankters:** Microbenthic cladocerans, copepods, ostracods, water mites, and *Chaoborus punctipennis* larvae (dipteran predators) occasionally captured in net hauls despite being primarily benthic/deep-water organisms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal Classification of Species:** The study distinguishes oligothermal (cold-water, collected <15°C) and polythermal (warm-water, collected ≥17°C) species across multiple taxa:
- *Oligothermal:* *Synchaeta* sp., *Kellicottia bostoniensis*, *Mallomonas caudata* (absent in midsummer).
- *Polythermal:* Most Cyanophyta, *Difflugia lobostoma*, *Eudorina elegans*, *Peridinium cinctum*, *Keratella gracilienta*, *Filinia*, *Conochilus*, *Pedalia*, *Trichocerca*, *Ptygura libera*, *Conochiloides*, *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*.

**Sedimentary Reconstruction:** Varved profundal sediments allowed reconstruction of pre-sampling plankton history (1947–1949). The 1947 stratum was dominated by *Melosira* filaments, which declined over 1948–1949 and were never recorded in net samples after 1950 — potentially linked to nutrient loading from fertilizer application. This pattern parallels diatom stratigraphy documented in Linsley Pond, Connecticut ([[Patrick 1943]]) and *Melosira* decline associated with pollution in Crystal Lake, Minnesota.

**Trophic Structure Inference:** The overall community structure suggests:
- Primary production dominated by pigmented flagellates (*Dinobryon*, *Ceratium*) rather than diatoms or persistent blue-green blooms (post-1952).
- Zooplankton dominated by filter-feeders (*Daphnia*, *Bosmina*, *Ceriodaphnia*, *Diaphanosoma*) and small-particle feeders (*Keratella*, *Filinia*, *Conochilus*, *Kellicottia*), suggesting an important unsampled nannoplankton component or reliance on planktogenic detritus.
- Secondary consumers were scarce among net zooplankton; *Tropocyclops prasinus* may be partially carnivorous. The predaceous rotifer *Asplanchna* was rare. The dominant secondary consumer niche was likely filled by nocturnally foraging *Chaoborus punctipennis* larvae, recorded at densities up to 55,000 individuals/m² in profundal sediments ([[Neff 1955]]), though rarely captured in daytime plankton tows.

**Comparative Context:** Findings are compared with [[South Park Pond]] (2.5 miles away, lower alkalinity, richer desmid flora, established *Mesocyclops edax* and *Diaphanosoma brachyurum* populations) and with Woods Reservoir, Tennessee ([[Yeatman 1956]]), where *Dinobryon sertularia* and *Ceratium hirundinella* co-occurred, contrasting with Tom Wallace Lake patterns.

## Key Findings

1. **Diatom decline:** An early *Melosira*-dominated flora (1947) disappeared within ~3 years, likely due to nutrient enrichment from fertilizer application; diatoms remained unimportant in net plankton throughout the study, with no winter maxima (similar to Lake Providence, Louisiana; [[Moore 1950]]).

2. **Blue-green algal bloom dynamics:** *Microcystis aeruginosa* bloomed heavily during fertilized years (pre-1952) but was never again conspicuous after fertilization ceased. *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae* and *Trichodesmium lacustre* bloomed the first unfertilized year (1952), after which blue-greens became largely unimportant — possibly limited by scarce midsummer sampling.

3. **Desmid decline:** Desmids were more prevalent in spring and during early lake years (1951–1952); their decline may correlate with rising total lake alkalinity (22→44 ppm over the study period).

4. **Erratic *Dinobryon* population dynamics:** *Dinobryon sertularia* exhibited irregular, unpredictable bloom-and-disappearance cycles across the six years, appearing in only 3 of 6 summers, with no clear correlation to temperature, competing with blue-green blooms (notably absent during heavy *Microcystis*/*Ceratium* years). *D. bavaricum* anomalously replaced *D. sertularia* in summer/fall 1955.

5. **Long-term absences and reappearances:** *Kellicottia bostoniensis* was absent from April 1951 to fall 1954; *Ceriodaphnia lacustris* was absent from December 1952 to April 1955 — both without clear environmental explanation, echoing similar enigmatic entomostracan population shifts reported by [[Pennak 1955]] in a Colorado mountain lake.

6. **Late-arriving species:** *Ptygura libera*, *Conochiloides* sp., *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*, *Daphnia pulex*, and *Mesocyclops edax* were not detected until several years into the study, possibly reflecting poor dispersal/colonizing ability (*Mesocyclops edax* framed as a potential "fugitive species" per [[Hutchinson 1951]]).

7. **Community richness metrics:** Typical zooplankton assemblages consisted of ~4 rotifer species, ~3 cladoceran species, 1 cyclopoid copepod (*Tropocyclops prasinus*), and 1 calanoid copepod (*Diaptomus pallidus*) per sample.

8. **Trophic structure:** Net phytoplankton was dominated by flagellates rather than diatoms or persistent cyanobacteria; most zooplankton were filter- or small-particle feeders, implying a significant unsampled nannoplankton contribution to the food web. Secondary consumers were poorly represented among net-caught zooplankton, with *Chaoborus punctipennis* larvae inferred as the primary nocturnal predator in the system.

9. **Vertical stratification evidence:** A single record of *Keratella valga* from a 3 m subsurface Kemmerer sample (absent at surface, where *K. cochlearis* dominated) suggests fine-scale vertical stratification in rotifer populations not captured by standard surface tows.
