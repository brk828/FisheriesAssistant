# Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team: Native Fishes of the Colorado River System

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service document from January 1979 describes the status and conservation efforts for four native fish species of the [[Colorado River]] system that have declined dramatically due to habitat modification. The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], formed in 1974, seeks to restore healthy populations of vanishing fishes through collaborative efforts among federal and state agencies. The document provides identification guides and distribution maps for the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], all of which face extinction threats from dam construction, water diversion, and competition with introduced species.

## Background & Description

### Historical Context

More than 200 years ago, over 725 fish species inhabited the continental United States. By 1979, at least 12 species and four subspecies had become extinct. Over 35 species were listed or proposed for protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, including four species from the [[Colorado River]] system.

### River System Characteristics

The [[Colorado River]] drainage originates in the high Rocky Mountains of northcentral Colorado and southwestern Wyoming, flowing 1,700 miles to the [[Gulf of California]] with an elevation drop exceeding two miles. Historically, the river exhibited extreme flow variability:

- Dry-weather low: 3,000 cubic feet per second (measured at Yuma, Arizona)
- Spring-flood high: 380,000 cubic feet per second
- Annual silt load: over 100,000 acre feet

### Native Fish Fauna

Fewer than ten fish species evolved in this extreme environment of fluctuating streamflows, muddy water, and high summer temperatures. These unique fishes developed specialized morphological adaptations:

- Humped or ridged backs
- Wing-like fins
- Streamlined bodies

Significantly, 70 percent of the [[Colorado River]]'s native fishes were endemic (found nowhere else in the world).

### Species Descriptions

**[[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius)**
- Largest minnow in North America
- Historical weight: 50 to 80 pounds
- Identification: No hump; mouth extends to rear of eye; usually 9 dorsal and 9 anal fin rays; olive-green back with silvery-white belly; round tail; wedge-shaped dark spot at base of tail in juveniles
- Status: Endangered species protected by Federal and State laws
- Top carnivore; efficient predator
- Made extensive spawning migrations

**[[Roundtail Chub]] (Gila robusta)**
- Still common in much of the upper [[Colorado River]] system
- Maximum length: seldom exceeds 13 inches
- Identification: No hump; mouth extends only to front of eye; usually 9 dorsal and 9 anal fin rays; silvery-gray, darker on dorsal surfaces
- Distribution: Throughout upper basin except [[San Juan River]] drainage below [[Navajo Reservoir]]
- Often mistaken for endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]]

**[[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha)**
- Identification: Rounded, nearly scaleless hump; snout overhangs mouth; usually 9 dorsal fin rays and 10 anal fin rays; silvery-gray, darker on dorsal surfaces
- Size: Usually less than 13 inches long; seldom exceeds two pounds
- Status: Endangered species protected by Federal and State laws
- First scientifically described in 1946 near [[Grand Canyon]]
- Feeds actively at water's surface

**[[Bonytail Chub]] (Gila elegans)**
- Identification: No hump; mouth extends to front of eye; body thin, pencil-like in front of tail; usually 10 or more dorsal and 10 anal fin rays; silvery-gray, darker on dorsal surfaces
- Status: Proposed for Federal protection as endangered species; protected by State laws
- Most abrupt decline of any native mainstream fish
- Feeds in schools at water's surface

**[[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus)**
- One of the largest suckers in the United States; may weigh 12 pounds
- Identification: Hump narrow when viewed from top; snout overhangs mouth; usually 14-15 dorsal fin rays; olive-brown to black on back; yellowish-white belly
- Status: Proposed for Federal protection as threatened species; protected by State laws
- Requires warm, flowing water for spawning
- Long-lived species

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Modification

Over the past 100 years, the [[Colorado River]] system has undergone drastic modification—physically, chemically, and biologically. More than 20 major dams have been constructed on the river and its larger tributaries, fundamentally altering riverine habitat:

**Upstream Effects:**
- Naturally turbulent, silty waters impounded to form deep, clear reservoirs

**Downstream Effects:**
- Rapid water level fluctuations due to periodic dam releases
- Clear, cold water discharge
- Elimination of shallow backwaters vital as nursery, resting, and feeding sites

### Introduced Species

Many non-native fish species now thrive in the modified [[Colorado River]]:
- Sport fish: bass and trout in reservoirs
- [[Catfish]] and [[Carp]] in main channel
- Numerous minnow species escaped from bait buckets

These introduced species compete with native fishes for food, spawning sites, and nursery areas.

### Water Development Impacts

Impounding waters of the [[Colorado River]] and tributaries has provided water for:
- Irrigation of crops
- Development of energy resources
- Generation of electricity

However, these developments have created a precarious ecological balance affecting both fish and human populations.

## Key Findings

### Population Status and Distribution

1. **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Virtually disappeared from lower basin; rare in upper basin. Once abundant in [[Salt River]] and [[Gila River]] of lower basin, supporting commercial fisheries. Large dams and water diversion structures have disrupted spawning success.

2. **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Found only sporadically in canyon areas. Population disappeared from [[Lodore Canyon]] on upper [[Green River]] after [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] began operation. Small numbers remain in [[Little Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. Scientists fear extinction before biology is fully understood.

3. **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Experienced most abrupt decline of any native mainstream fish. Common in [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] in 1960s; virtually nonexistent there by 1979. Still found occasionally in [[Desolation Canyon]] on lower [[Green River]].

4. **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Once abundant in mainstream rivers from Wyoming to Mexico. Adult populations in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mojave]] (lower basin) and [[Saguaro Reservoir]] on [[Salt River]] probably not reproducing; expected to disappear as old fish die. Found in small numbers in remaining free-flowing stretches of upper basin.

### Conservation Efforts

The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], formed in 1974 and sponsored by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, coordinates restoration efforts. Team members include:
- Biologists from fish and wildlife agencies of Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, and Utah
- U.S. Department of Interior representatives
- Special consultants from private sector

### Research Needs

Critical information gaps identified:
- Reproductive biology of [[Colorado Squawfish]] under current conditions
- Complete life history of [[Humpback Chub]]
- Spawning requirements and recruitment dynamics for all species

### Management Recommendations

The document emphasizes that survival of most native fishes depends on stretches of rivers left relatively unchanged. As water demands grow in the southwestern United States, planning must balance human needs with maintenance of ecological balance upon which both humans and fish depend.
