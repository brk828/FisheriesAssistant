# Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge Land Protection Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pupfish

## Executive Summary

This April 1984 Land Protection Plan, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) Region 1 in Portland, Oregon, proposes acquiring private and public land at Ash Meadows, Nye County, Nevada, to establish the [[Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge]]. The plan was issued as a draft for comment, with comments requested by April 30, 1984. It argues that the area holds an extraordinary concentration of endemic plants and animals, several of them federally listed or proposed for listing as endangered. Continued agricultural and development activity was judged likely to cause the extinction of all endemic species. The preferred alternative is fee acquisition of about 14,074 acres of private land, withdrawal of 2,681 acres of public domain land, and cooperative management of 6,693 additional public acres. The plan relies on a $5 million appropriation from the [[Land And Water Conservation Fund]], with [[The Nature Conservancy]] acting as interim owner.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Ash Meadows lies in southern Nye County, Nevada, and southeastern Inyo County, California, about 90 miles northwest of Las Vegas and 40 miles east of Death Valley National Monument. It covers roughly 48,000 acres of spring-fed wetlands and arid uplands at 2,100 to 2,300 feet elevation, and is described as the most arid region of the United States.

**Endemism.** The area is habitat for at least 26 plants and animals found nowhere else in the world. Four endemic fishes were listed as endangered by the USFWS:
- [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*)
- [[Warm Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*)
- [[Ash Meadows Amargosa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes*)
- [[Ash Meadows Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus nevadensis*)

The [[Ash Meadows Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys merriami*) also appears in the endemic list, marked in Table II as unseen for several years and probably extinct.

Other endemic taxa include seven plants and one insect proposed as endangered (for example, king ivesia, Ash Meadows sunray, Ash Meadows milk-vetch, and the Point of Rocks Springs naucorid). Table II lists about 26 endemic taxa in total, including dry-upland plants, spring and riparian plants, about a dozen spring snails and *Tryonia* species, one mammal (the Ash Meadows vole, probably extinct), and two insects. Table III sorts these by status: federal endangered, federal proposed, federal candidates, and state-protected categories. The plan notes that Cuatrocienegas in northeastern Mexico has the highest endemic concentration in North America, at approximately 50 species.

**Land ownership.** Per Table I, the Area of Management Concern totals 23,488.09 acres: 9,414 acres of public land and 14,074.09 acres of private land. The largest single holding is the Bureau of Land Management tract (9,373 acres). The Nature Conservancy owns 12,654 acres. The remaining private holdings (about 1,420 acres) are split among roughly two dozen small owners, with several tracts under 10 acres. The National Park Service manages 40 acres containing Devils Hole National Monument.

**Background of the conflict.** [[Preferred Equities Corporation]] (PEC), a land-development firm, had received partial approval from Nye County and the State of Nevada for a municipal and agricultural community of about 50,000 residents. The plan estimates that this would have eliminated about 80 percent of the habitat occupied by endemic species and indirectly affected the remaining 20 percent. On February 7, 1984, The Nature Conservancy purchased 12,654 acres from PEC.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The plan identifies several threats:
- **Physical disturbance and agriculture** have reduced endemic habitat to a portion of its former extent.
- **Groundwater removal** threatens spring discharge. Hydrologic data identify a limited area where pumping most affects spring levels that sustain listed fishes.
- **Mining** and other activities on public lands could harm listed species.
- **Exotic species** such as saltcedar (*Tamarisk* sp.) displaced mesquite woodlands, and exotic plants and animals compete with natives.
- **Secondary impacts** of development, including pollution and groundwater mining, would persist even if federal protection covered taking and harassment.

The [[Carson Slough]] marsh, now largely lost, is a major restoration target. Restoring it is expected to support waterfowl production and hunting. The plan also calls for reestablishing mesquite woodland and endemic populations in areas where they were eliminated.

Water is central to the proposal. Under the PEC subdivision review, the State Engineer found that the spring-based quasi-municipal permits (totaling about 4,007.81 million gallons annually) could not support the combined 11,676 lots proposed for Calvada Lakes Units 1 and 2. A U.S. Supreme Court case, [[Cappaert V. United States]], limits groundwater extraction that would lower water levels at Devils Hole. Water rights are to be acquired with the land, and their priority use would be conservation of listed and candidate species.

Recovery plans for the Devils Hole and Warm Springs pupfishes state that habitat protection is their primary recovery requirement. Critical habitat for the Ash Meadows Amargosa Pupfish and Ash Meadows Speckled Dace lies within the proposed boundary.

## Key Findings

**Acquisition proposal (preferred alternative)**
- Acquire 14,074 acres of private land, including the 12,654 acres of TNC land, which will be acquired first. The remaining 1,420 acres will be acquired as owners are willing to sell.
- Withdraw 2,681 acres of public domain land for refuge purposes, and cooperatively manage 6,693 acres under a Memorandum of Understanding between the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] and the USFWS.
- The total refuge area would be 16,755 acres, with the Area of Management Concern covering 23,488.09 acres.
- Use the $5 million authorized by Congress to fund the purchase.

**Alternatives considered**
- **No action:** TNC lacks the financial resources to hold the land indefinitely. If the federal government does not buy, the land would likely be sold for development, and all endemic taxa could become extinct in the foreseeable future. Litigation would be expected.
- **Acquisition or management by others:** Rejected. TNC and other private groups lack funds for continuous ownership. National Park Service management would prohibit hunting, which is scarce in southern Nevada. The [[Nevada Department Of Wildlife]] lacks the funds and expertise, though it wants a cooperative role.

**Existing protection (as of 1984)**
- The four endemic fishes are protected from take under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- Other endemic species are not federally protected.
- The State of Nevada recognizes four plants (Ash Meadows milk-vetch, spring-loving centaury, Ash Meadows gumplant, Ash Meadows stickleaf) as critically endangered. This prohibits collection without a permit but does not protect habitat.
- No listed, proposed, or candidate species is protected from long-term development impacts.

**Socio-cultural and economic findings**
- Fewer than 20 people live in Ash Meadows. The nearest towns are Amargosa (1,800 people, about 15 miles northwest) and Pahrump (5,000 people, about 20 miles southeast).
- PEC sold more than 25,000 lots in Pahrump over 12 years, but those lots are now occupied by fewer than 2,500 people. This suggests that Ash Meadows development would proceed slowly.
- Refuge Revenue Sharing Act payments would exceed what TNC pays now by no more than $12,500.
- Planned mitigation includes sale of public lands near Amargosa, use of excess subsurface water for Amargosa, and a road linking Pahrump and Amargosa that would be realigned to avoid listed species.
- Tourism and educational use would increase, and archaeological sites would be protected. Ash Meadows is judged highly sensitive for archaeology.

**Coordination and public input**
- Discussions with landowners and local governments began in early 1981.
- Public hearings on February 11, 1983 (Las Vegas) drew comments mostly favoring listing and total protection. The May 26, 1983 hearing (Amargosa) drew comments mostly opposed.
- Support letters came from Governor Richard Bryan, Senator Paul Laxalt, and the director of the Nevada Department of Wildlife.
- A Section 7 consultation (Case 1-5-84-FW-1) received concurrence. The Nevada Division of Historic Preservation and Archaeology reported extensive prehistoric sites.
- A public hearing on the listing of endemic plants and the acquisition was scheduled for April 24, 1984.

**Recommendations**
- Acquire TNC lands (12,654 acres) immediately.
- Withdraw the 2,681 public acres for the refuge in the near future.
- Enter the remaining 6,693 public acres into BLM planning and agree on a Memorandum of Understanding for cooperative management.
- Acquire remaining private parcels on a willing-seller basis.
- Remove roads not yet built from PEC's plans, and allow farming or grazing only where it benefits wetland or endemic species.
