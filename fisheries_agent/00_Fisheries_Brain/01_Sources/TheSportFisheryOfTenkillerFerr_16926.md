# The Sport Fishery of Tenkiller Ferry Reservoir, Oklahoma

#ichthyology #freshwater-fisheries #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #reservoir-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1967 Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory bulletin (Gasaway, 1967) reports results of an intensive creel survey conducted on [[Tenkiller Ferry Reservoir]], Oklahoma, from May 1964 to June 1965, comparing findings to prior surveys (1953–1956). The study found daytime fishing pressure of 33.1 man-hours/acre and night fishing pressure of 2.4 man-hours/acre (Dec 1964–June 1965), representing a significant decline from the 110.9 man-hours/acre recorded in 1955–56. [[Crappie]] dominated the fishery, accounting for 50% of daytime effort and 91% of night effort, followed by [[Black Bass]], [[Catfish]], and [[White Bass]]. Catch rates and yield were substantially higher in the upper (riverine, shallower, more turbid) third of the reservoir than in the lower (deep, clear) end, except for [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], and possibly [[Walleye]], which were more abundant in the lower reservoir. Total annual yield was 11.85 lbs/acre, well below the national reservoir average of 22.6 lbs/acre (Jenkins, 1967). Long-term declines were most pronounced for [[Bluegill]] and [[Black Crappie]].

## Background & Description

- **System**: [[Tenkiller Ferry Reservoir]] is an impoundment of the Illinois River, located in Sequoyah and Cherokee Counties, northeastern Oklahoma, approximately 13 miles upstream of the confluence with the Arkansas River.
- **Physical characteristics** (at power pool elevation 630 ft MSL):
  - Length: ~34 miles
  - Shoreline: ~130 miles
  - Surface area: 12,500 acres
  - Storage volume: 630,000 acre-feet
  - Maximum width: ~2 miles
  - Maximum depth: 130 feet
  - Impoundment began: July 1952
- **Water quality**: Very clear water overall, becoming thermally and chemically stratified each summer (June–September) throughout the reservoir except in the shallow extreme upper end.
- **Spatial structure**: The reservoir was divided into three creel survey zones — Area 10 (lower, deep, clear), Area 20 (middle), and Area 30 (upper, shallower, more turbid, riverine) — each further divided into sub-areas for sampling.
- **Study design**: A stratified random sampling design with sub-sampling (following methodology of [[Lambou 1967]]) was used to estimate fishing pressure, catch rates, yield, and species composition, both for daytime (5 a.m.–8 p.m.) and nighttime (8 p.m.–12:30 a.m.) fishing, including effort at 7 heated fishing docks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal/chemical stratification**: Occurs annually June–September across the reservoir except in the shallow upper reaches, influencing fish distribution and possibly catchability.
- **Turbidity gradient**: The lower reservoir (Area 10) has the clearest water of any Oklahoma reservoir at the time; turbidity increases substantially toward the upper reaches (Area 30), correlating with higher fishing yield there — potentially due to increased fish activity/catchability in turbid water rather than higher abundance.
- **Depth gradient**: Water depth decreases directly with distance from the dam, contributing to habitat differences between lower and upper reservoir zones.
- **Standing crop vs. harvest inversion**: Cove rotenone samples (1955–1960) indicated a *higher* standing crop of [[Centrarchidae|centrarchids]] and total sport fish in the lower end (Area 10: 307.3 lbs/acre) compared to the upper end (Area 30: 97.4 lbs/acre) — the opposite pattern from angler harvest, which was much greater in Area 30. This suggests no direct relationship, or possibly an inverse one, between standing crop and sport fish harvest in this system.
- **Predictive modeling**: Using [[Jenkins 1967]]'s reservoir harvest prediction formula (incorporating reservoir area, shore development, dissolved solids, growing season length, and reservoir age), predicted harvest was nearly identical across the three reservoir areas (~7.2–8.4 lbs/acre), indicating that the observed harvest differences (especially Area 30's 26.78 lbs/acre estimated harvest) are driven by factors other than shore development or dissolved solids — likely fishing pressure, depth, and turbidity.
- **Species introductions**: [[Walleye]] were not introduced into the reservoir in substantial numbers until 1961 and remained rarely encountered in the 1964–65 survey.

## Key Findings

### Fishing Pressure
- Total daytime fishing effort (13 June 1964–20 June 1965): ~414,000 man-hours (33.1 man-hours/acre).
- Area 30 (upper third) alone received as much or more fishing pressure than the other two areas combined.
- Night fishing (1 Dec 1964–30 June 1965): ~30,080 man-hours (2.4 man-hours/acre); annualized estimate ~4.1 man-hours/acre.
- 59.5% of night fishing occurred at only 7 heated fishing docks.
- Combined annual estimate: ~444,080 man-hours (35.5 man-hours/acre).
- Daytime effort breakdown: 56.7% boat, 23.7% bank, 19.6% heated dock.
- Night effort breakdown: 28.2% boat, 12.3% bank, 59.5% heated dock.
- Fishing pressure declined significantly from 110.9 man-hours/acre (1955–56) to 33.1 man-hours/acre (1964–65) (T = 3.202, .01>P>.001).
- Most fishing occurred on weekends; a notably large amount of night fishing occurred on Monday nights.

### Target Species / Effort Allocation
- 50% of daytime effort and 91% of night effort targeted [[Crappie]].
- [[Black Bass]], [[Catfish]], and [[White Bass]] followed in popularity.
- Crappie fishermen were proportionally more common in the upper reservoir; bass fishermen more common in the lower reservoir.

### Catch Rates & Yield
- Overall daytime catch rate: 0.58 fish/hour or 0.34 lbs/hour.
- Catch rates for total catch, bass, crappie, and carp were significantly higher in Area 30 (upper) than Area 10 (lower) (e.g., Total: T=4.683, P<.001; Bass: T=4.429, P<.001; Crappie: T=3.995, P<.001).
- Daytime yields significantly higher in upper reservoir for [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Crappie]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[White Bass]], and [[Freshwater Drum]].
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Spotted Bass]] (and possibly [[Walleye]]) yields were significantly *greater* in the lower reservoir.
- Total annual daytime yield: 18.6 fish/acre and 11.1 lbs/acre.
- Night yield relatively uniform across areas; annualized nighttime harvest ≈0.72 lbs/acre (6.1% of total annual harvest).
- Total annual harvest (day + night): **11.85 lbs/acre** — well below the 121-reservoir national average of 22.6 lbs/acre (Jenkins, 1967).
- [[Flathead Catfish]] averaged significantly larger size in Area 30 vs. Area 10 (T=2.653, .02>P>.01).
- Daytime-caught fish averaged heavier (0.62 lbs) than night-caught fish (0.49 lbs) overall (T=2.025), though [[Bluegill]] were an exception, being heavier at night (T=2.013).

### Temporal Trends (1953–1965)
- Average weight of fish caught changed little over the decade, though [[Freshwater Drum]] average weight continually decreased, and [[White Bass]] average weight may have slightly increased.
- Significant declines in yield (lbs/acre) were documented only for [[Bluegill]] (10.6→0.18 lbs/acre) and [[Black Crappie]] (3.1→0.03 lbs/acre) (both P<.001), though this coincided with the large drop in overall fishing pressure.
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] showed a possible slight increase in yield over time.
- [[Freshwater Drum]], [[Green Sunfish]], and bullheads yields remained roughly stable.
- Catch composition shift: [[Black Bass]] species made up a large share of the catch immediately post-impoundment but declined thereafter; [[White Crappie]] now dominate the catch (a major increase); other [[Sunfish]] species declined sharply as a percentage of catch; [[White Bass]] contribution increased after 3–4 years post-impoundment.
- Catch rate (number/hour) showed a possible but non-significant decline from 1954 (1.4/hr) to 1964-65 (0.58/hr); lbs/hour catch rate was stable (0.34 in both 1955-56 and 1964-65).
- Crappie now constitute 72% of the numeric catch, up substantially from earlier years.

### Species Notes
- Four [[Blue Catfish]] (*Ictalurus furcatus*) were reported caught, but identification was considered uncertain.
- No bullheads, [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]], [[Warmouth]], or [[Sauger]] were encountered during the 1964–65 survey.

## Management Implications

- The pronounced difference in yield/catch rate between the upper (shallow, turbid) and lower (deep, clear) reservoir sections was larger than expected, and warrants further investigation into causal mechanisms (fishing pressure vs. habitat/turbidity effects) to inform sport fishery management.
- Notable exceptions ([[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], possibly [[Walleye]]) favoring the lower/clearer reservoir suggest species-specific habitat preferences that could guide stocking or access-development strategies.
- Overall reservoir yield remains below regional norms, and understanding the causes of below-average harvest, along with differential species decline rates as the reservoir ages, are identified as priority areas for future research.
- A parallel, more intensive study combining creel survey with fish population dynamics was underway at [[Canton Reservoir]], Oklahoma, intended to provide comparative data.
