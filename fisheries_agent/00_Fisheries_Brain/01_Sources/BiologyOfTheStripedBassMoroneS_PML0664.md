# Biology of the Striped Bass (*Morone saxatilis*) in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percichthyidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University M.S. thesis by Gary Bruce Edwards (May 1974) examines the biology of [[Striped Bass]] introduced into the [[Colorado River]] between [[Davis Dam]] and [[Parker Dam]]. Following a 1962–1969 stocking program by the Arizona and California Game and Fish Departments (about 93,000 fish total), the population established itself. Evidence of limited reproduction appeared between 1966 and 1969, and the first spawn capable of producing a dominant year class occurred in 1970. The study covers reproduction, food habits, age and growth, and angling success. The author concludes the introduction is a success as a trophy sport fishery, but that long-term persistence without further stocking remains unresolved.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The striped bass is a temperate bass in the family Percichthyidae, order Perciformes. Its relatives in North American fresh and brackish waters are the white bass, white perch and yellow bass (*Morone* species).
- **Native range:** Atlantic coast of North America, from the St. Lawrence River to the St. Johns River, with Gulf of Mexico tributaries. Its center of abundance is Cape Cod to northern North Carolina, including [[Chesapeake Bay]]. Distinct races have been described, including a distinctive Gulf of Mexico race.
- **Western introductions:** Fish from New Jersey were planted in the [[Sacramento River]], California, in 1879 and 1881, and have since dispersed from San Diego to the Columbia River, Oregon.
- **Colorado River stocking:** Initial stocking began on 13 August 1962 (17,000 fingerlings, 200 yearlings). Fish were collected at the [[Tracey Fish Screens]], California. Additional plants brought the 1962–1964 total to 82,935 fingerlings and 896 yearlings. A final plant in October 1969 (9,000 fingerlings, 175 yearlings, sourced from South Carolina sac fry) brought the total to about 93,000.
- **Study area:** The river flows about 450 km to the Gulf of California. The 81.0 km free-flowing reach below Davis Dam and the upper [[Lake Havasu]] area were the focus. The climate is hot and arid, and vegetation follows the [[Lower Sonoran Life Zone]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and dams:** [[Davis Dam]] (completed 1953) regulates flow for the Mexican Water Treaty of 1944 and downstream demands. [[Parker Dam]] (completed 1938) forms Lake Havasu and feeds the [[Colorado River Aquaduct]]. Minimum daily flow below Davis Dam is 2,547 m³/min and maximum is about 64,516 m³/min.
- **Water temperature:** Warm-water conditions (annual average about 18 °C; January minimum about 9 °C) allow near year-round growth in the river, unlike most native populations.
- **Prey base:** [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) dominate prey. A primary source is [[Lake Mohave]], and many shad were observed dying after passing through Davis Dam penstocks.
- **Trout fishery:** Catchable [[Rainbow Trout]] are stocked below Davis Dam, creating a potential predation conflict.
- **Spawning habitat:** Spawning occurs in the river from Davis Dam to about 16 km downstream, where strong and turbulent currents prevail. Spawning in native populations is linked to water temperatures of at least 14.5 °C, peaking at 15.6–18.4 °C.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction**
- Spawning occurs from Davis Dam to about 16 km downstream, April through mid-June, with a few ripe fish in July.
- Of 1969 bass sampled, 84% were taken in this reach, and 53% of these were females. The sex ratio was roughly 1:1, unlike the typical male-biased ratio on spawning grounds.
- Females matured in age-group III (50% mature, type-3 ova) and nearly all were mature by age-group IV (sample of 15 fish for ages III–IV combined).
- Mature ova per kg of body weight averaged 231,643 (range 149,142–308,086), considerably higher than in other populations (222,000 per kg in Oregon; 176,000 per kg in the Roanoke River).
- Ovary-to-body weight ratio was 1:7.6 in the Colorado River, compared with 1:12 in Chesapeake Bay.
- Evidence of a substantial spawn was lacking until about 1969. The first dominant year class came from a 1970 spawn.

**Food habits**
- The sample comprised 100 adult stomachs collected February–November 1969. 45% were empty.
- Spear-gun catches had 71.4% full stomachs (15 of 21), versus 50.6% for angling (40 of 79).
- Threadfin shad occurred in 67 of 56 non-empty stomachs (reported as "67 (67.8%)"), with 1,489 of 1,476 identifiable natural food items (the largest single count being 257 shad in one stomach).
- By reconstructed weight, shad made up 2.46 kg of 4.98 kg total (about 49%). Trout were second at about 27% of total volume.
- Other prey included centrarchids (three largemouth bass, one green sunfish), one European carp and two salamanders (angler bait). Overall, the bass are a generalized, total carnivore.
- Angling records suggest a diel feeding pattern, with about 58% of catches taken between 1800 hours and midnight (March–September 1969).

**Predation on trout**
- Rainbow trout were the second most important food item, but were far less abundant than shad (21 trout vs. 1,439 shad).
- A tagged-trout study (20 bass, 13-day period) found 18 of 21 trout were tagged, suggesting higher predation shortly after stocking. The author recommended curtailing trout stocking directly below Davis Dam during peak bass concentration.

**Age and growth**
- Scale analysis (200 fish, 1966–1972) was validated for ageing. Calculated growth showed introduced fish (1961–1965 year classes) grew about 182 mm in year one, while river-spawned fish (1966, 1968, 1970, 1971 year classes) reached 207 mm.
- By age-group six, Colorado River bass reached fork lengths up to 932 mm, exceeding the combined six-year growth of other populations by about 20%.
- Females began outgrowing males in the fourth year, and were 25 mm longer at six years (1966 year class).
- The largest specimen was about 1.02 m fork length (seven years old). A 1972 female measured 1.2 m and weighed 21.3 kg, the state record.

**Angling success**
- Angling for striped bass began in 1966 and became a major trophy fishery. Usage of cut anchovy bait after 1972 increased angler success.
- Bullhead City Striped Bass Derby records (1968–1972) show increasing catches, with a 1971 mean length decline of 225 mm due to the dominance of two-year-old fish.
- Economic value is growing, with three annual derbies offering prizes up to $1,000.

**Conclusions**
- The study concludes the introduction appears successful, but three questions remain: how long threadfin shad can sustain the population, whether reproduction will maintain the stock without restocking, and what population density the forage base can support.
- Edwards states that further data on reproduction, young-of-the-year food habits and shad availability must be collected before success can be confirmed.
