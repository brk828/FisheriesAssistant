# Fisheries Report of the Lower Colorado River (1951)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 report, prepared by the [[Fisheries Task Group]] for the [[Colorado River Great Basin Field Committee]] and printed by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission, describes the present and potential fishery and angling recreation of the [[Lower Colorado River]], from [[Hoover Dam]] to the Mexican border. Field surveys covered the entire reach over one year. The report concludes that the fishery is diversified and largely unexploited, that growing angling pressure from freshwater reservoir closures in the Southwest makes the river increasingly important, and that continued research and management are needed. The excerpt provided covers the introduction, the [[Lake Mohave]] section (Section I), fragments of the lower-river section (Section VII), and the conclusions and recommendations. Sections II–VI are not included.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Describe the current and potential fishery and angling recreation, and outline a path for future research and management, with some firm recommendations based on field surveys.
- **Authorship:** Biologists from the fish and game agencies of Nevada, Arizona and California, plus a representative of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Named field contributors included A. L. Jones, Jack Hemphill, Richard D. Benson, Gerald Duncan and Richard A. Hagner (names as legible in the scan).
- **Structure:** The river is divided into seven sections, each considered individually.
- **Nomenclature:** Common and scientific fish names follow American Fisheries Society Special Publication No. 1.

**Species referenced (17 fishes; 8 aquatic plants):** Key fishes included [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], black and white crappie, [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Redear Sunfish]], brown and yellow bullheads, [[Channel Catfish]], [[Carp]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Striped Mullet]], [[Tenpounder]] and [[Gambusia]]. Aquatic plants included sago pondweed, spiny naiad, widgeon grass, horned pondweed, cattail, rush, tule and spike rush.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Mohave (Section I):**
- Bounded by Hoover Dam to the north and Davis Dam to the south, lying within Black and Eldorado Canyons. The lake is 67.5 miles long with a maximum width of about four miles near Cottonwood Landing.
- Water levels are regulated under the Mexican Water Treaty. Levels drop during June and July and reach approximately 527 feet by August (partly garbled in the scan).
- Mohave is characterized as highly turbid with no direct connection to the river; seepage maintains its level.
- Total dissolved solids rise through evaporation, which at times exceeds 10 feet per year. Dissolved salts reach levels "far beyond the tolerable limits" of fish and other aquatic life during some periods, and the lake was occasionally flushed by floodwater.
- Fishing is for bass, bluegill and catfish, with a limited bullfrog fishery.

**Lower-river reach (Section VII, Imperial Dam to Mexican border):**
- **Zone 1:** About 14 miles long (partly garbled) with a shifting sand-bed channel, a drainage area over 30 square miles, and annual precipitation under five inches. Runoff is minimal.
- **Zone 2:** About 35 miles of shifting channel across a flood plain, with a braided channel up to half a mile wide. Turbid water carries large sand loads but little dissolved organic matter, so physical conditions are unfavourable to aquatic life and inhibit the fishery.
- Air temperatures range roughly from the 30s°F to over 100°F. Water temperatures reach about 90°F.
- The fishery is dominated by carp, channel catfish and striped mullet. Largemouth bass, bluegill, black crappie and yellow bullhead are also present, and green sunfish and razorback sucker are known. Tenpounders are occasionally taken.
- Emergent plants (cattail, bulrush, tule, spike rush, widgeon grass) predominate.

**Other water bodies:**
- **[[Laguna Lake]] (Laguna Reservoir):** Over 1,500 acres with a boat-launch facility; a popular bass, bluegill and catfish area.
- **[[Mittry Lake]]:** About 2,000 surface acres (scan uncertain), averaging about eight feet deep, highly turbid and largely isolated from the river. Limited flushing comes from the Gila Canal, and salinity is a problem.
- **[[Haughton Lake]]:** About 58 surface acres, maximum depth about 30 feet, supplied by seepage from the river. Good bass fishing and good bluegill, with fair results reported.
- **Laguna Dam area:** Heavy fishing occurs in the spillway tailrace. A sudden drop in water level strands fish on rocks and in shallow pools, and an estimated 1,000+ catfish were observed stranded, some weighing up to eight pounds.

**Human use:**
- Public access is good, with paved and unpaved roads crossing the area. No point is more than 25 miles from a town.
- Fishing is mostly at night for catfish in summer. Anglers concentrate at Laguna Dam, targeting catfish, mullet and carp in that order.
- Winter use is heavy, with up to 60 house trailers and camps between Laguna and Imperial Dams.
- Most anglers are local. Bait fishing predominates, and economics limit the gear available to each angler.

## Key Findings

1. **Fishery status:** The Lower Colorado River fishery is diversified and, with minor exceptions, is not being used to its fullest extent.
2. **Recreational importance:** Drought drying of lakes and reservoirs in the Southwest makes the river the last frontier for a growing number of freshwater anglers.
3. **Lake Mohave:** Water level fluctuation and rising salinity are key constraints.
4. **Stranding loss at Laguna Dam:** Sudden water-level drops strand large numbers of fish, mainly carp and striped mullet with many game fish (chiefly channel catfish, including some spawners over 20 pounds). Stranding losses are difficult to quantify, but the report calls for rescue efforts and better flushing practice.
5. **Recommendations for development (from the report):**
   - Stocking of warm-water fishes already present in the river is not recommended, consistent with practice elsewhere.
   - Laguna Dam flushing techniques should be studied and modified if possible to benefit the fishery.
   - Mittry Lake should be developed to its full potential through a proper water supply and an aquatic-plant planting program.
   - Further study is needed on introducing new forage fish species.
6. **Conclusions and overall recommendations:**
   - The sport fishery is a valuable recreational resource for the growing number of Southwest anglers.
   - Management depends on continuous data collection on fish populations and the effects of harvest.
   - Research should continue under the three adjoining states with federal cooperation, including: the most efficient trout stocking program for cold-water areas; general fishery study; the practicability of introducing submerged aquatic plants; and the cost-effectiveness of various management techniques.
