# Hatchery and Laboratory Phase of the Colorado River Endangered Fishes Study

## Executive Summary
This report (Joe Valentine, USFWS Colorado River Fishery Project, 1979-1981) documents captive propagation and laboratory research on four endangered/threatened Colorado River Basin fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The project spanned three hatcheries ([[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Utah Fisheries Experiment Station]]) and two university laboratories ([[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]], [[Idaho Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]]). Work focused on broodstock development, induced spawning, larval rearing, temperature preference, swimming performance (stamina tunnel testing), and toxicological bioassays of heavy metals and organic contaminants. 1980 was reported as the most successful spawning year on record for Colorado squawfish, and a critical wild-egg collection expedition to Black Rocks rescued humpback chub propagation efforts. Interspecific hybridization (bonytail x roundtail; bonytail x humpback) was also documented, with hybrid vigor observed in the humpback-bonytail cross.

## Background & Description
- **Colorado Squawfish** ([[Ptychocheilus lucius]]): Wild broodstock collected 1973-1979 from the Yampa, Green, and Colorado Rivers. Fry grew slowly — approximately 100 days to grow from 7 mm to 50 mm.
- **Humpback Chub** ([[Gila cypha]]): Faster-growing than squawfish; some individuals gained ~1 mm/day, reaching an average of 50 mm in 72 days (high size variance noted). Fish shipped to University of Idaho reached 100 mm; those at Logan, Utah reached 75 mm by September before growth ceased at 62°F.
- **Bonytail Chub** ([[Gila elegans]]): Mojave-collected adults were all female in available broodstock; hybrids created via cross-fertilization. Humpback-bonytail hybrids reached 125 mm by December (rapid growth); roundtail-bonytail hybrids reached <75 mm by December, and some exhibited **deformed backbones** (possible developmental/genetic or rearing artifact).
- **Razorback Sucker** ([[Xyrauchen texanus]]): Test subjects sized 150-300 mm used in temperature preference trials.
- **Colorado Squawfish (juvenile testing size)**: ~100 mm length used in temperature gradient trials; minimum size threshold of 50 mm required for standardized bioassay protocols.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Spawning habitat (Colorado squawfish)**: Gravel beds with water upwelling through substrate, depths of 0-75 cm, requiring water temperatures of 68-70°F to trigger maturation (approximately one month post-threshold).
- **Rearing habitat**: Fry transitioned from natural zooplankton prey to commercial trout feed as they outgrew natural food supply in fertilized ponds — mirrors ontogenetic dietary shifts observed in wild populations.
- **Wild collection localities**: 
  - Humpback chub: [[Little Colorado River]] (Arizona) and [[Black Rocks]] area of the [[Colorado River]] (Colorado).
  - Colorado squawfish broodstock: [[Yampa River]], [[Green River]], [[Colorado River]].
  - Bonytail: [[Mojave]] populations.
- **Temperature preference (laboratory-derived)**:
  - Razorback sucker: final preferred temp 22-23°C (acclimated at 20°C and 26°C); daylight/dark preferences of 23°C and 27°C (not significant).
  - Colorado squawfish (~100 mm): mean preferred temps of 20.3°C, 28.5°C, and 22.8°C when acclimated at 14°C, 20°C, and 26°C respectively (horizontal gradient system); higher variability (SE up to 3.3°C) than razorback sucker (SE ~1°C).
- **Swimming performance / flow ecology**: Razorback suckers sustained 1 hour of swimming at velocities <1.25 ft/sec, but less than 2 minutes at ≥2 ft/sec. Larger fish outperformed smaller conspecifics at intermediate velocities (1.25-2 ft/sec), suggesting body size mediates rheotactic capability important for riverine habitat use.
- **Northern squawfish** ([[Ptychocheilus oregonensis]]) field ecology (Idaho): Spawning biology, early life history, habitat selection, and movement studied via scuba observation in the [[Clearwater River]], [[St. Joe River]], and [[St. Maries River]], Idaho — used as an ecological/physiological surrogate for the more imperiled Colorado squawfish.

## Key Findings
1. **Broodstock status (1979-1980)**: 27 wild adult Colorado squawfish held at Willow Beach (collected 1973-1979); ~500 hatchery-origin fish (from 1974 cohort) selected as broodstock, distributed across Willow Beach (100), Utah FES (140), Dexter (270), and Utah State (16).
2. **Security/loss event**: 14 wild squawfish stolen from Willow Beach on November 27, 1980, including 6 of 8 mature females — a significant setback to the wild genetic broodstock.
3. **Record spawning year**: 1980 marked the best natural spawning on record for Colorado squawfish; three females released an estimated 25,000 eggs naturally (June 23-24).
4. **First successful induced spawning**: Carp pituitary injections successfully induced spawning in wild and domestic Colorado squawfish for the first time — wild fish produced ~40,000 eggs (released) plus ~1,000 hand-stripped eggs; domestic broodstock yielded 78,000 hand-stripped eggs.
5. **Differential hatch success**: Wild-origin eggs from natural spawning had up to 95% hatch rate; domestic broodstock eggs hatched poorly (only 1,000-2,000 of 78,000), suggesting domestication or handling stress effects. Hatching typically occurred within 96 hours at 20°C.
6. **Humpback chub propagation crisis and rescue**: Captive broodstock (32 total fish) failed to spawn naturally; hand-stripping yielded only 750 fry. A targeted wild-egg collection expedition to Black Rocks (Valdes 1980) secured 18,000 eggs from wild fish, of which ~82% hatched — critical for meeting production targets.
7. **Production shortfall**: Expected humpback chub fingerling harvest of ~12,000 yielded only 6,200 in final inventory — highlighting substantial rearing mortality/loss. Survivors distributed to Utah FES (1,000) and University of Idaho (5,200).
8. **Hybridization results**: Bonytail eggs fertilized with roundtail sperm achieved 75% hatch success; fertilized with humpback sperm achieved 62% success. Growth was markedly asymmetric — humpback-bonytail hybrids thrived (125 mm by December) while roundtail-bonytail hybrids underperformed and showed skeletal deformities.
9. **Temperature preference methodology**: Two systems employed — an electronic "bow-tie" shuttle box (self-selecting temperature via fish-triggered photocells/solenoids) and a horizontal temperature gradient trough (used for fish too small to trigger shuttle box photocells).
10. **Toxicology/bioassay program**: 96-hour static bioassays testing 8 inorganic and 8 organic compounds (heavy metals, pesticides, herbicides) at 12°C, 17°C, and 22°C across three water types simulating Willow Beach water (730 mg/L TDS), artesian well water (175 mg/L TDS), and Palouse River water (32 mg/L TDS). 63 bioassays completed on Northern squawfish (surrogate species) prior to October 1980 due to Colorado squawfish size constraints (<50 mm).
11. **Species hardiness comparison**: Preliminary bioassay data suggest Colorado squawfish are more tolerant ("tougher") to tested chemicals than Northern squawfish.
12. **Culture performance**: Under lab conditions at 19°C, juvenile Colorado squawfish grew 20 mm and humpback chub grew 40 mm over 6 weeks — comparable to hatchery growth rates, validating laboratory rearing protocols.
13. **Program timeline**: Final reports were scheduled for completion by September 30, 1981, with author expressing hope that hatchery-lab work would continue beyond the dissolution of [[Colorado River Fishery Project]].
