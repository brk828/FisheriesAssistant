# Mound Springs of the Great Artesian Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gobiidae #hydrobiidae #aquatic-invertebrates #limnology

## Executive Summary

This review by W. F. Ponder (Australian Museum) describes the mound springs fed by artesian water from the [[Great Artesian Basin]] (GAB), one of the largest artesian systems in the world, occupying about 22% of the Australian continent (about 1.76 million km²). The springs host rare flora and endemic invertebrates and fish, and their ecological simplicity makes them valuable for evolutionary and ecological research. Many springs have become extinct in the last century, and most of the survivors are threatened by degradation, chiefly from stock, altered flows and artesian bore extraction.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Artesian springs occur in arid regions worldwide, including the [[Nubian Aquifer]], eastern Nevada carbonate aquifers and the GAB. The GAB springs lie mainly on the basin fringes, where they provide permanent water in an arid landscape.
- **Mound formation:** Springs typically sit on faults and deposit solutes as calcareous travertines, sometimes ferruginous or siliceous. Williams and Holmes (1978) estimated that a spring discharging about 200 l h⁻¹ with 4 g l⁻¹ solutes would build a 3 m high mound in 1000 years. Many mounds are largely sand and clay capped with carbonate. Cyanobacteria contribute to terrace formation.
- **Structure:** A typical spring has a head with a vent and pool, outflow channels, and a marshy tail. Springs are "active" when flowing and "extinct" when dry.
- **Organisation:** Springs form spring groups, complexes and eleven supergroups (Habermehl 1982), such as the [[Lake Eyre Supergroup]] and [[Dalhousie Supergroup]].
- **Numbers:** Estimates of the number of springs range from 350 to 600 groups and complexes; about 400 individual springs occur near Hermit Hill, South Australia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry**
- Spring water is chemically similar to nearby artesian bores. Total dissolved solids (TDS) are generally 2000–4000 mg l⁻¹, with Lake Eyre springs 2000–10000 mg l⁻¹ and Dalhousie Springs 650–2000 mg l⁻¹. Values as low as <1700 mg l⁻¹ and above 32000 mg l⁻¹ are known.
- pH ranges from about 7 to nearly 10. Nitrates reach up to 14 mg l⁻¹; phosphate is low.
- Vent temperatures range from near ambient to about 45°C. Paralana Spring reaches 62°C and is radioactive.
- Water usually lacks dissolved oxygen at the surface but rapidly saturates. Some pools with little vegetation have low oxygen.

**Flow and flooding**
- Dalhousie Springs have the highest flow, about 670 l s⁻¹. The total GAB spring discharge is about 1500 l s⁻¹ (Habermehl 1980).
- Flooding is a key disturbance: a Hermit Hill spring took more than a year to recover after near-total submersion, whereas Blanche Cup Spring's fauna was largely unchanged.

**Flora**
- Only three botanical studies exist, none on algae. Dalhousie Springs are dominated by *Melaleuca glomerata*, *Cyperus gymnocaulos*, *Typha domingensis* and *Phragmites australis*.
- *Eriocaulon carsonii* is the only plant considered endemic to artesian springs.

**Fauna**
- Eight fish species occur in South Australian mound springs, and only two (*Neosilurus* sp. and *Craterocephalus dalhousiensis*) are endemic to the springs. The introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) is abundant in many Queensland springs and threatens native species.
- Hydrobiid snails: the Lake Eyre supergroup has two endemic genera and nine species; the Dalhousie supergroup is a radiation of a single genus.
- Crustaceans: the phreatoicid isopod *Phreatomerus latipes*, the amphipod *Afrochiltonia* spp., and the endemic ostracod *Nearawa dirga* are notable endemics.

**Thermal and salinity tolerance**
- The desert goby tolerates 5–41°C and unacclimated salinity up to 37 g l⁻¹ (60 g l⁻¹ acclimated). Snails at Dalhousie Springs are active between 18 and 45°C.
- Endemic invertebrates have much lower salinity tolerance than the fish, which may explain why invertebrates are restricted to springs while fish occur in other waterbodies.

## Key Findings

1. **Endemism and speciation:** Endemic invertebrates are almost never found in other waterbodies. Speciation is greatest in hydrobiid snails, and the conditions are judged conducive to rapid speciation.
2. **Isolation and island biogeography:** Fatchen (1984) found that flora diversity increased with spring number rather than spring area. Hydrobiid snails and crustaceans are reduced in isolated springs, so "it is more important to maintain a number of springs in a group than to attempt to salvage one."
3. **Refuge role:** During the last 400 000 years of hydrologic oscillation, mound springs may have served as stable refuges for aquatic life (Bowler 1982).
4. **Degradation:** Stock trampling, fouling, damming and digging have caused extensive degradation, and some modifications have caused extinction of endemic invertebrates.
5. **Extinction:** Artesian bore extraction (about 211 Ml day⁻¹ in South Australia alone, against 83 Ml day⁻¹ for all springs in that State) has caused many extinctions. All north-western New South Wales springs are apparently extinct, as are many in Queensland.
6. **Conservation:** The purchase of Mount Dare Station, incorporating Dalhousie Springs, as a national park is recognised as a major step, but other spring groups need protection, management and conservation of artesian water.
7. **Research gaps:** Algae, microflora, oligochaetes, insect larvae and most Queensland springs remain largely unstudied, and no protein or enzyme analysis of endemic invertebrates has been carried out.
