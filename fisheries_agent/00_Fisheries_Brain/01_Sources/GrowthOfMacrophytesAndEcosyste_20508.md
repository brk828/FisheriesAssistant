# Growth of Macrophytes and Ecosystem Consequences in a Lowland Danish Stream

#macrophytes #freshwater-biology #potamogeton-pectinatus

## Executive Summary

This study, by Sand-Jensen, Jeppesen, Nielsen and colleagues (Freshwater Biology, 1989), examines the seasonal growth of submerged macrophytes in the [[River Susaa]], a small, nutrient-rich lowland stream in [[Zealand]], Denmark. Over three years (1979–1981) the authors tracked biomass, discharge, light availability, water velocity and sedimentation at three 400 m reaches. Macrophyte growth was closely tied to light availability, and discharge controlled light through depth and turbidity. Dominant plants, [[Potamogeton Pectinatus]] and [[Sparganium Emersum]], together with the filamentous alga [[Cladophora]], reduced water velocity, promoted sedimentation of organic material, and structured habitat for invertebrates, microalgae and fish. The paper concludes that managed lowland streams are far from climax vegetation, and that changes in hydrology and management strongly alter stream ecosystem function.

The document also contains a reference list from an earlier work and the truncated opening of a second article (Garden et al., on leaf litter decomposition under acid rain in a buffered lotic system). Only the Susaa study is summarized in full here.

## Background & Description

Low-gradient streams are a feature of temperate lowland landscapes. Their low water velocities and fine sediments favour submerged macrophyte growth. Macrophyte abundance is regulated by management and physico-chemical conditions, which the plants themselves modify. Submerged plants reduce velocity, raise water level and promote sedimentation, and they shade benthic algae. They also shelter fish and invertebrates and offer substrate for invertebrates, microalgae and bacteria.

Little was known about seasonal macrophyte development in lowland streams, or why development differs between streams and between years. European lowland streams usually receive ample inorganic nitrogen and phosphorus, so the authors predicted that physical factors, mainly hydrology and light, would explain most of the variation. Earlier work had shown that bank shading reduced summer macrophyte biomass in shallow Danish streams, and that biomass in deeper English streams declined exponentially with depth as light attenuated.

The study had two aims: (1) to relate seasonal plant development to discharge and light, and (2) to test the response of macrophyte species to harvesting and dredging. The broader goal was to show the key ecological role of macrophytes by quantifying their effects on light, velocity, sedimentation and [[Benthic Microalgae]] populations.

### Study Sites

- **River Susaa:** the largest river of Zealand, 87 km long, draining 815 km² of clay-dominated glacial deposits. Soils are rich in carbonates, giving high bicarbonate alkalinity (about 4 meq l⁻¹). About 69% of the catchment is farmland, mostly drained.
- **Pindso:** 13 km from the source; reach 2.5–5 m wide, slope 0.28–0.84 m km⁻¹.
- **Vetterslev:** 38 km downstream; two reaches, 5–9 m wide, slope 0.15–0.59 m km⁻¹.
- Flow was regulated at all stations. Sediments were clay and sand at Pindso and clay, sand and gravel at Vetterslev, covered in summer by several centimetres of organic detritus.
- Sewage input was 0.5 l s⁻¹ at Pindso (about 3% of median minimum flow) and 50 l s⁻¹ at Vetterslev (about 37%). Vetterslev therefore carried higher nutrient loads, roughly 0.5 mg PO₄-P l⁻¹ and 7.7 mg NO₃-N l⁻¹, compared with 0.14 mg PO₄-P l⁻¹ and 5.6 mg NO₃-N l⁻¹ at Pindso.
- Groundwater input was low. The river was fed mainly by drainage water and secondary groundwater reservoirs, with less than 10% from the primary groundwater table.

### Management History

Macrophytes had been harvested once or twice each summer in earlier years to limit flooding of fields, and banks were cut in autumn. During 1979–1981, Pindso was cut by hand or dredged, with material placed on the banks. At Vetterslev, plants were cut about 10 cm above the sediment using a boat with rotating knives. Marginal cutting continued throughout, and submerged plants were moderately cut at Vetterslev in 1980 and 1981.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Light.** Surface photon flux density was measured continuously about 40 km away. The attenuation coefficient of water was 2.4 m⁻¹ (median, n = 24) and was the same at all stations. The plant attenuation coefficient, k_p, for [[Potamogeton Pectinatus]] was constant over the season at 0.024 ± 0.003 (SD) m² g⁻¹ DW. A conservative 10% surface reflection was assumed. Shading from banks and emergent vegetation was neglected, so light estimates were used mainly for May–June.

**Hydrology.** Discharge was highly variable on seasonal and daily scales, with low groundwater input. Summer discharge at Vetterslev in July was 0.4–1.8 m³ s⁻¹ in most years. Discharge was low in 1979 and high in 1980 and especially 1981, when summer was extraordinarily wet. Heavy rain alternating with hot, dry weather produced rapid discharge peaks.

**Velocity and macrophytes.** Water velocity rose with discharge and fell with macrophyte biomass because plants add frictional resistance. Summer velocity was therefore lowest when discharge was low and biomass high. Macrophytes reduced velocity by 1.3–3.5 fold, with a larger relative effect at low discharge (Table 2). Velocity during summer increased from 1979 to 1981 because discharge rose, and plant biomass did not fully offset this.

**Sedimentation.** Winter high flows left light-coloured mineral sediment. In summer, low velocity allowed dark organic detritus to settle on top. Organic accumulation paralleled macrophyte development and reached several centimetres.

**Habitat.** Submerged macrophytes provided substratum and shelter for invertebrates and microalgae and had large surfaces (up to 7 m² per m² of bottom). Dense stands and organic sediment may have lowered oxygen for [[Brown Trout]], which is a key species in most Danish streams.

## Key Findings

### Macrophyte Development and Biomass

- Above-ground growth was restricted to late May through November, when light was high and discharge and velocity low. High autumn–winter discharge in 1980 and 1981 removed all above-ground parts, and plants regrew each spring from tubers (P. pectinatus) or rhizomes (S. emersum).
- Total maximum above-ground biomass was about 130 g DW m⁻² at Pindso in 1979 and 1980 and 147 g DW m⁻² at Vetterslev in 1980. It rose to 192 g DW m⁻² at Pindso and 256 g DW m⁻² at Vetterslev in 1981.
- Growth rates were linearly related to available light, with 69–86% of the variation in growth rate explained by light (Fig. 7).
- Cumulative light from 1 April explained most of the biomass increase (r² = 0.72–0.93), including the differences in timing and rate among years (Fig. 8). High spring and early-summer discharge may have delayed development by up to 3 weeks.

### Species-Specific Traits

- **[[Potamogeton Pectinatus]]:** grew rapidly in May–June and concentrated biomass near the surface in July–August. Its maximum growth rate was about 0.06 log₁₀ units day⁻¹ (doubling time about 5 days). Biomass stayed stable for 40–50 days and then declined exponentially from September to November. The plant dominated at Pindso (more than 90% of biomass) and co-dominated with S. emersum at Vetterslev. Its decline was accelerated by high autumn discharge.
- **[[Sparganium Emersum]]:** had long, flexible linear leaves that offered little resistance to flow. It was less susceptible to high velocity, declined later and more slowly, and replaced its leaves continuously from a basal meristem. It regrew well after cutting that left the meristem intact.
- **[[Cladophora]]:** developed rapidly during sunny, low-discharge periods but was washed out by high flows. Maximum biomass fell from 52–72 g DW m⁻² in 1979 to 2–15 g DW m⁻² in 1981, and the high-biomass period shortened over the study.

### Management Effects

- Cutting caused more damage to P. pectinatus than to S. emersum. Cutting removed apical meristems, so P. pectinatus had to regrow from rhizomes, while S. emersum kept its basal meristem.
- Dredging at Pindso probably favoured P. pectinatus, whose deep tubers survive, over S. emersum, whose rhizomes and meristems are lost. S. emersum was absent from Pindso.
- Frequent disturbance keeps vegetation in early successional stages dominated by opportunistic species such as Cladophora and Elodea canadensis. Species richness was low compared with undisturbed streams.

### Microbenthic Algae and Sediment

- Benthic microalgae peaked only from mid-April to late May. Early growth was limited by low light and high turbidity, and later growth by macrophyte shading. A secondary peak occurred in September 1981 after cutting.
- Organic sediment reached 700–900 g organic DW m⁻², about ten times maximum macrophyte biomass.
- Sudden high discharge, autumn senescence and cutting resuspended and removed accumulated organic material.
- Organic sediment was higher in 1981, probably because of greater macrophyte biomass and higher particle export after heavier rainfall.

### Ecosystem Role

- The authors present a conceptual model (Fig. 12) in which rooted macrophytes regulate water depth, velocity, sedimentation, shading and substrate, which in turn shape benthic microalgae, invertebrates and fish.
- Invertebrate densities in macrophyte stands were high, and chironomids and simuliids were common. Trout populations were linked to the physical changes produced by macrophytes and to food supplied by invertebrates.
- Hydrology strongly influenced ecosystem processes, so human changes such as groundwater management, irrigation, channelization, deforestation and macrophyte cutting are likely to have major impacts.

### Conclusions

- Light availability, mediated by discharge, explained most of the seasonal and inter-annual variation in macrophyte biomass.
- Managed lowland streams support vegetation well short of climax, with a more stable seasonal discharge pattern (higher in summer, lower in winter) favouring macrophyte development.
- Macrophytes are a key structuring element of lowland stream ecosystems, so management that alters their growth has wide consequences.
