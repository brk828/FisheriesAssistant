# Control of Aquatic Weeds in Farm Ponds

#aquatic-plants #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-herbicides

## Executive Summary

This 1961 article, written by aquatic biologist [[Alex Montgomery]] and reprinted from the *Journal of Soil and Water Conservation*, reviews methods for controlling nuisance [[Aquatic Weeds]] in [[Farm Pond]]s. It was issued by the [[Chemical Insecticide Corporation]], which sells aquatic herbicides, so its recommendations favor chemical control. The article argues that most farm ponds become infested with weeds unless they are well built and actively managed, and that weed control is an important part of [[Fishery Management]]. It compares four control approaches, then describes the herbicides available at the time and the importance of treatment timing. The text ends mid-discussion, without a formal conclusion.

## Background & Description

Aquatic plants can be useful or harmful depending on conditions and how the water is used. Some plants supply food and cover for fish and game, but in small farm ponds most plants are considered undesirable because they interfere with intended uses of the pond. The author notes that the large number of privately owned farm ponds, and growing interest in managing them for recreation, has made weed control a significant concern.

Montgomery identifies four control methods:

1. **[[Fertilization]]**: Adding nutrients to produce enough [[Phytoplankton]] to shade the pond bottom and reduce light penetration. It prevents new growth but does not remove established infestations. The fertilizer formula and rate must suit regional water conditions, climate, and growing seasons.
2. **[[Manual Removal]]**: Cutting or pulling plants. It works well on emergent species such as cattails, arrowhead, waterlilies, and pickerelweed if carried out before seed production. Three to five cuttings may be needed in the first year, and a fertilization program is recommended to slow regrowth.
3. **[[Winter Draw-Down]]**: Lowering water levels in severe-winter regions to expose weed-infested bottoms to freezing and thawing. It gives seasonal control of some submerged plants. The author believes draw-down may need to continue for three years, and possibly in later years, to prevent re-infestation.
4. **[[Chemical Aquatic Weed Control]]**: Applying chemicals to target plants. The author states that this is usually the most economical and effective option.

No single chemical controls all aquatic weeds. Different plant types respond to different treatments, so specific chemicals must be matched to specific plants. Until recently, only a few herbicides were available.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Aquatic plants are grouped by growth form, and each group responds to different treatments:

- **[[Submerged Aquatic Plants]]**: Grow beneath the water surface.
- **[[Emergent Aquatic Plants]]**: Rooted on the bottom with leaves extending above the surface, including cattail and bulrush.
- **[[Floating Aquatic Plants]]**: Include waterlilies, watershield, and waterlettuce.
- **[[Filamentous Algae]]** and plankton algae: Treated separately.

Environmental and safety considerations recur throughout the text:

- Sodium arsenite is toxic to warm-blooded animals and can accumulate in residues after repeated use.
- Pelleted 2,4-D components are described as non-toxic to warm-blooded animals and fish at the concentrations used.
- Water inflow can dilute herbicides below effective levels, so inflow must be considered when planning treatments.
- Seasonal conditions affect both plant growth and herbicide performance.

## Key Findings

**Chemical treatments and reported rates**

- **[[Sodium Arsenite]]**: Used almost exclusively for submerged weeds. It is non-selective and kills submerged plants of all types at 4 to 10 parts per million (ppm). Its drawbacks are high toxicity to warm-blooded animals and residue buildup. The author notes it remains in extensive use because many aquatic plants can be controlled only with it.
- **[[2,4-D]] and [[2,4-D-2,4,5-T Combinations]]**: Used against emergent and floating plants. Ester formulations are mixed at a fractional-pound rate (the OCR-garbled source gives "¥Y pound") plus 1 pint of detergent in 10 gallons of water, then sprayed on exposed plant parts. Adding fuel oil to the carrier improves control of hardier narrowleaf plants such as cattail and bulrush.
- **[[Copper Sulphate]]**: The most economical and effective option for filamentous and plankton algae. It is broadcast or dragged in a loose woven bag at 0.5 to 1.0 ppm, equal to 1.35 to 2.7 lbs per acre-foot, depending on water hardness.
- **2,4,5-Trichlorophenoxypropionic Acid**: A newer herbicide for broadleaf submerged and emergent plants, applied at 1.5 to 3 ppm. Dilution from water inflow must be considered.
- **[[Pelleted 2,4-D]]**: Described as one of the most promising newer products. The pellets contain 25 percent active insoluble ester of 2,4-D with components that release it slowly. The rate is nearly 150 lbs per surface acre. It controls many broadleaf submerged and emergent plants, is easy to apply by hand or with seeding equipment, and gives prolonged control on some species. Because of its slow release, it resists water-dilution losses and suits spot treatment and flowing-water areas.

**Timing and persistence**

- Spring and early-summer treatments work fastest because plants are young and actively growing.
- Late-summer and fall treatments require higher rates and produce slow, sometimes erratic results because mature plants are tougher.
- No permanent cure exists. Most herbicides damage initial infestations, after which control shifts to treating regrowth from viable seed or missed spots. The author stresses that consistent follow-up treatment reduces long-term effort and prevents re-infestation.

**Context**

- The article is a trade-sponsored reprint. Its statements on effectiveness reflect the manufacturer's perspective, and the text gives no independent sample sizes or experimental data.
- Two photographs of the [[Ocean Township Municipal Pond]] in [[Monmouth County]], [[New Jersey]], show weed cover before and after pelleted 2,4-D treatment, with the caption attributing the photos to SCS (Soil Conservation Service) photographer Clark.
