## Executive Summary

This document is a **preliminary draft (January 1995)** management prospectus prepared by the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service Arizona Fishery Resources Office, addressing conservation of four indigenous "big-river" fishes native to the **Colorado River Lower Basin**: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. All four species are federally listed as **Endangered** under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. The plan is explicitly designed as a lower-basin complement to existing **Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Programs**, emphasizing coordination across a wide network of federal, state, tribal, academic, and non-governmental stakeholders (Appendix B). The document outlines a tiered conservation strategy — from preventing extinction and securing genetic variability, to stabilizing, expanding, and ultimately achieving self-sustaining wild populations (Figure 1) — and catalogs the broader indigenous fish assemblages present across five defined **Bio-regions (I–V)** of the basin (Appendix A). Note: this source is a management/policy planning document rather than a primary research or morphological study; it references but does not report new empirical data.

## Background & Description

The source document does not provide direct morphological descriptions of the target species. Morphological and taxonomic notes are inferred only from cited literature:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) — a large-bodied cyprinid (pikeminnow), the largest native minnow in North America; taxonomic authority per USFWS 1991a recovery plan.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — distinguished by a pronounced dorsal keel ("razorback"); subject of genetic/hybridization studies with the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Buth et al. 1987) indicating introgressive hybridization in both hatchery and native stocks.
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*) — a cyprinid within genus *Gila*; hatchery broodstock origin and allozyme variation examined by Minckley, Buth & Mayden (1989).
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) — a cyprinid noted for a pronounced nuchal hump; related to *Gila seminuda*, a taxon shown to have originated via introgressive hybridization between *Gila robusta* and *Gila elegans* (DeMarais et al. 1992).
- Genetic studies (Ammermann & Morizot 1989; Dowling & DeMarais 1993; Dowling & Minckley 1993) document biochemical/mitochondrial DNA variability relevant to distinguishing populations and informing broodstock management, rather than gross external morphology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geographic/Habitat Framework**: The plan divides the Lower Colorado River Basin into five **Bio-regions (I–V)**, each supporting distinct indigenous fish assemblages (see Appendix A map, Appendix A species tables):

- **Bio-region I**: Mainstem Colorado River fauna including [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] (extirpated), [[Roundtail Chub]] (extirpated), [[Woundfin]] (extirpated), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (extirpated), [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Bio-region II**: Broader assemblage including [[Apache Trout]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] (extirpated), [[Virgin Chub]], [[Virgin Spinedace]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], multiple sucker taxa (*Catostomus*, *Pantosteus* spp.).
- **Bio-region III**: [[Longfin Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Woundfin]] (extirpated), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (extirpated), [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Desert Pupfish]].
- **Bio-region IV**: Highest diversity region including [[Apache Trout]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Gila Chub]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]] (extirpated), and the extinct [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]].
- **Bio-region V**: Yaqui drainage-associated fauna including [[Yaqui Shiner]] (extirpated), [[Sonora Chub]], [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Sucker]], [[Yaqui Catfish]] (extirpated), [[Yaqui Topminnow]], [[Mexican Stoneroller]], and the [[Desert Pupfish]] subspecies *eremus*.

**Trophic/Community Interactions**: Cited literature references predator-prey and competitive interactions affecting native fish persistence, including:
- Rainbow trout predation impacts on [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (Blinn, Runck & Clark 1993).
- Species replacement dynamics between native [[Sonoran Topminnow]] and introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (Meffe 1983).
- General historical decline of native fish fauna attributed to habitat alteration and introduced species (Miller 1961; Minckley 1991).

**Habitat Threats & Considerations** (per plan objectives): altered hydrographs/water releases, dam operations (Glen Canyon), and need for habitat restoration are identified as core management concerns, though specific hydrological data are not detailed in the extracted text.

## Key Findings

- All four "big-river" fishes covered by this plan — [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]] — are federally listed as **Endangered**, with formal USFWS recovery plans already existing except for the Razorback Sucker (per Appendix A "Recovery Plan" column, which shows "No" for razorback sucker in most bio-regions, despite Endangered status).
- The plan proposes an integrated, tiered management framework (Figure 1) progressing through: **(1) Prevent Extinction → (2) Perpetuate Genetic Variability/Stabilize Populations → (3) Expand Populations/Self-Sustaining Populations → (4) Recovery**, each tier paired with specific procedures (e.g., securing broodstock, genetic management, habitat restoration) and success metrics (e.g., completion of life cycle without human intervention).
- **Genetic management** is emphasized as a core action item: securing broodfish and germplasm for gene banking and refugia, to be integrated with parallel Upper Basin genetic protocols (Williams & Wydoski 1994; Wydoski 1994).
- Numerous regional populations are noted as **extirpated** (denoted by asterisks in Appendix A) from specific bio-regions, including Bonytail from Bio-regions I, II, and IV, and Colorado Squawfish from Bio-regions I, III, and IV — underscoring substantial range contraction across the basin.
- The **Monkey Spring pupfish** (*Cyprinodon* sp.) is documented as **Extinct**, illustrating the severity of decline among lower basin desert fish taxa.
- Successful conservation is framed as requiring broad multi-stakeholder coordination (Appendix B), spanning federal agencies (USFWS, BOR, BLM, NPS, Forest Service), state wildlife agencies (AZ, NM, NV, CA), numerous Native American tribal nations, academic institutions, and NGOs (e.g., [[Desert Fishes Council]], The Nature Conservancy).
- Prior applied conservation work cited includes on-site rearing of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave (Burke et al. 1992), reintroduction evaluations of Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish in the Salt and Verde rivers (Hendrickson 1993), and population viability analysis for Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Basin (Gilpin 1993).
- The document remains an internal preliminary draft; accompanying correspondence (April 1995) indicates it required substantial revision and closer alignment with existing Upper Basin recovery plans before finalization.
