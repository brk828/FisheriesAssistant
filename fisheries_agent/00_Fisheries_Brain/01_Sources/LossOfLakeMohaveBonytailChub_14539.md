# Loss of Lake Mohave Bonytail Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #fish-conservation

## Executive Summary

This memorandum documents a significant mortality event involving endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) collected from [[Lake Mohave]] in May 1993. Of 15 fish captured and transported to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] for broodstock purposes, 10 died (67% mortality) due to handling stress, fungal infection, and communication failures. The incident revealed the absence of written protocols for capture and handling of endangered species, prompting development of standardized procedures.

## Background & Description

[[Bonytail Chub]] are endangered cyprinids collected annually from [[Lake Mohave]] as potential broodstock for [[Dexter NFH&TC]]. Historically, collections yielded four or fewer fish per year through [[Arizona State University]] netting surveys conducted at the end of the academic year. Since 1987, all bonytail caught during surveys were transported to Dexter to increase genetic variation of broodstock, which originated from 10 fish. The fish being captured may be siblings of those held at Dexter, as some individuals from the original Willow Beach transfer group were released into the lake while others were retained. None of the fish captured since 1987 had successfully spawned; all died either at Dexter or [[Willow Beach]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The netting effort occurred May 10-13, 1993, in [[Lake Mohave]]. Trammel nets were set off selected points in the evening and pulled the next morning, with nets run every morning and evening. The survey team included Chuck Minckley (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service), Paul Marsh ([[Arizona State University]]), and Kirk Young ([[Arizona Game and Fish]]). Water temperature at the hatchery was 67-68°F, while transport water was 72+°F. Fish were held in live wells during collection, then transferred to larger "Nalgene" tanks for transport to the hatchery without stress reduction chemicals.

## Key Findings

**Collection Results**: 16 bonytail were netted during May 10-13, 1993. One fish bore a fin mark and was PIT tagged and returned to the lake; the remaining 15 were transported to [[Willow Beach]] for holding and treatment prior to shipment to Dexter.

**Mortality Timeline**:
- 2 fish died at Willow Beach during the nearly 2-week holding period
- 1 fish died in transit to Dexter
- 7 fish died after arrival at Dexter (by June 14, 1993)
- 4 fish remained alive in raceways at time of report
- Total mortality: 10 of 15 fish (67%)

**Handling Procedures**: Fish were tempered from 72+°F to 67-68°F hatchery water over a 1-hour period. Hatchery crew noted fish were "badly marked up by the net" upon arrival. Fish received formalin flow-through treatments at 1:6000 concentration on May 11, 12, 13, and 14 to reduce fungal infection potential.

**Treatment Gap**: Glenn Green, who was treating the fish, took the weekend plus one day off (May 15-17). No communication occurred regarding continued treatment needs, resulting in no treatments during this 3-day period. By May 17, one fish showed fungal development; by May 18, eight fish appeared heavily fungused.

**Emergency Treatment**: Following consultation with Roger Hamman at Dexter, seven fish were painted with potassium permanganate (KMnO₄), but treatment was halted due to over-stress concerns. Dr. Carmichael provided a stress reduction formula for holding fish in tanks.

**Transport Decision**: On May 24, despite concerns about fish condition, the decision was made to ship to Dexter based on belief that Dexter had better capability to save the fish than Willow Beach, even though Dexter personnel "did not believe they were in decent shape."

**Pathology**: Fish lost after arrival at Dexter did not appear fungused but showed sloughing of necrotic tissue from the dorsal part of the caudal peduncle, indicating either serious bacterial infection or damage to nerves and vessels due to handling.

**Protocol Violations**: The only existing protocol—that no fish will be moved onto a hatchery without examination by a hatchery biologist—was ignored. No written protocol existed for capture and handling of endangered species.

**Corrective Action**: A protocol for all handling of endangered species was being drafted for submission to the Regional Office by June 18, 1993, with expected distribution by June 25, 1993.

**Contributing Factors**: Overhandling or poor handling techniques combined with lack of communication between personnel created the mortality event. The absence of standardized procedures for endangered species handling was identified as the fundamental systemic problem.
