# "Pit Digging" and Related Behavior in Pupfishes (Genus Cyprinodon)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Minckley]] and [[Arnold]] (October 1969, *Journal of the Arizona Academy of Science*, vol. 5) describes "pit digging," a stereotyped excavation behavior in several [[Pupfish]] species (genus *Cyprinodon*). Some species excavate shallow circular depressions in soft sediments of shallow waters. Earlier authors had interpreted these pits as nests, as an artifact of territoriality, or as incidental to reproduction. The authors report pit digging in detail for one species, [[Cyprinodon atrorus]], observed in a semi-natural outdoor pool at [[Arizona State University]], and record it in a number of other forms from Arizona, Texas, New Mexico, Mexico, Florida, and California. They conclude that pit excavation and defense are largely food-oriented in most populations studied, with reproductive motor patterns used in defense, and that a shift from defending temporary feeding sites to defending permanent feeding and breeding territories may explain the behavior's evolution.

## Background & Description

Pit digging was previously described in several contexts:

- [[Raney]] et al. (1953) reported pits made by [[Cyprinodon variegatus]] in Florida and considered them nests.
- [[Barlow]] (1961) proposed that territory size restriction in crowded populations of [[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]] limited male activity so that males dug and plowed in one spot.
- [[Cyprinodon variegatus]] used depressions for spawning, possibly by chance.
- [[Jordanella floridae]] also makes pits unrelated to reproduction (Peters, 1941).

The paper uses the motor-pattern terminology of [[Barlow]] (1961), with motor patterns proposed by others given in quotation marks and described at the end of the paper. Excavation uses three basic motor patterns:

- **[[Digging]]:** Excavation with the mouth.
- **[[Plowing]]:** Pushing sediment aside with the head and body.
- **[[In-Place-Churning]]:** A violent undulating movement of the body and fins against the bottom. This pattern is often used first, creating a small depression that is then enlarged by digging and plowing.

A fourth movement, the **[[Chafing Movement]]**, involves a fish turning onto its side against the bottom. It appears unrelated to excavation and often partly obliterates pits. Similar movements occur in other species.

Pit size varies with species and conditions. Pits are excavated only in soft, flocculent sediments or fine sand, in shallow, quiet water. Under ideal conditions they reach up to 20 cm in diameter and 2 to 5 cm in depth. Fish under 15 mm standard length rarely take part in excavation. Both males and females excavate, with females generally less aggressive and less frequently involved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonality and conditions.** In the field, pits were recorded only from early spring to early autumn, matching the main activity season of most species. In the laboratory, digging continues throughout the year. The authors note that activity may be rare or sporadic in some populations, possibly because of water chemistry, light, food availability, or other factors. Because time control in field observations was poor, negative observations may be almost meaningless.

**Field versus artificial pits.** Tracks of small animals can collapse in soft sediment and resemble pits. Such cases were excluded unless actual excavation was seen. Large pits are relatively impermanent but can persist for up to five days under conditions of continuing activity in artificial pools.

**Geographic records.** The authors reviewed populations across the range:

- **Death Valley (California):** *Cyprinodon nevadensis* and *Cyprinodon salinus* from Saratoga Springs and Death Valley populations dig pits in aquaria; field pits were noted at some sites. A new species of *Cyprinodon*, *Cyprinodon diabolis*, from Devils Hole, does not excavate.
- **Lower Colorado River Basin:** Three populations of *Cyprinodon macularius* were studied; all dug pits in aquaria, and one population from Monkey Spring, Arizona, also dug in the field.
- **Northern Chihuahuan Desert (Mexico):** Two populations of *Cyprinodon* from Mexico were studied; pits were positively noted at both sites.
- **Rio Grande System:** Pits were noted at some localities but not others. A population from Devils River, Texas, was affected by the filling of Amistad Reservoir in 1965.
- **Pecos River Basin:** Several populations were kept in laboratory tanks, and pits were observed in natural habitat at some sites.
- **Red River System:** *Cyprinodon rubrofluviatilis* digs pits in nature, mostly by in-place churning and plowing.
- **Cuatro Ciénegas Basin (Coahuila, Mexico):** Pit digging by *Cyprinodon atrorus* occurs, and the male breeding territory corresponds to the area of pit digging where substrate is suitable.

**Experimental pool.** The core study used a semi-natural outdoor pool of about 25 square meters at Arizona State University. The pool had been relatively undisturbed since 1959 and resembled small spring environments of pupfishes in nature. Climate was similar to the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin, the source of the fish stock. The stock was introduced in March 1964, stabilized by August, and was studied until April 1967, when it was replaced with another species.

**Diet.** Fish taken specifically from pits had sand grains, chironomids, and ostracods in their foreguts. Ten fish caught at random from deeper water, where pits were absent, had mostly sediment and algae (over 80 percent of foregut content), with limited invertebrates. The sediment fauna included ostracods, oligochaetes, chironomid larvae, hydracarines, molluscs, and talitrid amphipods.

## Key Findings

**Pit size and depth.** In the field and in the artificial pond, *Cyprinodon atrorus* pits ranged from less than 2 cm wide and 0.5 cm deep to maxima of 16 cm and 4 cm. In aquaria, pit sizes were often modified by the owner's environment but were generally similar to field pits.

**Timing.** Digging by *Cyprinodon atrorus* occurred mainly between 0900–1000 and 1300–1400 hours, at water temperatures above 20 °C. Digging alternated with attentive inspection of the coarse substrate exposed in pit centers, feeding, and, in larger pits, inspection of the walls.

**Observations, 9–11 April 1967 (1200–1500 hours).** Plowing and in-place churning were the most frequently recorded excavation techniques. Twelve active pits, dug within the day, occupied an area of about 20 square meters, all in less than 25 cm of water and more than 10 cm offshore from a zone of defended breeding territories with no pits.
- The largest depression was about 11 cm in diameter and 3 cm deep (dug to concrete).
- Fifteen separate males spent an average of 17 minutes (range 3–38 minutes) at two pits, within a total observation time of 240 minutes. One male defended and fed in one pit for the longest recorded occupation.
- Eleven females averaged nine minutes (range 2–13 minutes) in defense and feeding at the same two pits.

**Defense behavior.**
- Females defended pits as vigorously as males but for shorter periods. Females used charging almost exclusively against either sex; tailbeating was seen only three times in recorded female–female encounters, and charging alone was used in 26 female defenses against males.
- Females rarely patrolled a depression but fed and excavated continuously when undisturbed.
- Ninety percent of 117 male observations included brief patrol of the site.
- Of 40 defensive actions by males against other males, 75 percent were charges, with arching and tailbeating making up the rest. One fish performed a complex movement called "looping" (described by Itzkowitz, 1967).
- Females rarely challenged males: 17 encounters were recorded. Nine initiated a breeding sequence from the male, but females usually fled; one spawn was realized, out of the pit, in soft, flocculent sediment. The other eight encounters ended with a brief male charge, female retreat, and resumed feeding by the male.

**Age and sex patterns.**
- Active defense of pit areas was observed only in adult or sub-adult fish.
- Young fish often move into pits with an adult and feed without interference. Charges toward young fish are brief, often under 1 cm. Young fish flee rapidly and then resume foraging.
- Pit excavation by fish under 18 mm standard length was not observed.
- Non-reproductive males often excavate and feed in pits, staying peripheral to spawning areas, whereas breeding territories lie in marginal shallows.

**Conceptual conclusions.**
- Pit excavation and defense appear largely food-oriented in most species studied. Reproductive motor patterns are commonly used in defense, but reproductive behavior is rare in these contexts, except possibly in *Cyprinodon bifasciatus*.
- Some authors have suggested that pit digging may be a [[Displacement Activity]], as in sticklebacks.
- Alternatively, feeding may be primarily on algal mats, travertines, or micro-organisms, with pits trapping particulate matter. This could be valuable to the defender.
- Defense of temporary feeding depressions may have evolved into defense of permanent feeding and breeding territories, which would show how [[Territoriality]] could develop.
- *Cyprinodon bifasciatus* and *Cyprinodon variegatus* occupy habitats shared with many other fish species, so competition for space and food may be important in these populations.

**Limitations.** The authors stress that the data are sketchy and cut across evolutionary lines within the genus. Qualitative and quantitative differences in pit building, and its apparent absence in some species (though possibly spurious), indicate the need for refined analyses. Some data on hybrids are only random notes.
