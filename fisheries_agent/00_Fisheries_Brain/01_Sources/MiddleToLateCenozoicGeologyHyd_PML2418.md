# Middle to Late Cenozoic Geology, Hydrography, and Fish Evolution in the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Geological Society of America special paper (Spencer, Smith and Dowling, 2008) combines geologic, paleontologic and molecular evidence to reconstruct the cenozoic drainage history of southwestern North America. The authors argue that fish distributions, read against tectonic history and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) divergence times, provide a time-calibrated record of past river connections. Their central conclusion is that the [[Colorado River]] integrated into its modern course at ca. 5 Ma and became the largest hydrographic change in the region, while earlier fish exchanges occurred across the [[Continental Divide]] and with the [[Snake River]] system.

## Background & Description

The paper is one of a series in the volume *Late Cenozoic Drainage History of the Southwestern Great Basin and Lower Colorado River Region*, arising from a 2005 conference at the Desert Studies Center, Zzyzx, California. Its authors are Jon E. Spencer (Arizona Geological Survey), Gerald R. Smith (University of Michigan) and Thomas E. Dowling (Arizona State University).

The geologic record of ancient rivers is described as "remarkably poor." Sediment cannot easily be tied to source catchments, and Oligocene-Miocene extension and volcanism in the [[Basin and Range Province]] buried or destroyed most pre-late Cenozoic river courses. Fish distributions are therefore used as an alternative proxy for past hydrography.

The paper's method relies on a molecular clock. Pair-wise percent sequence divergence in mtDNA (cytochrome b) between sister lineages is divided by fossil-calibrated rates to estimate the time since isolation. The authors note that standard deviations of such estimates often range between 10% and 21%, and that rates vary with metabolic rate, body size and temperature, with small, warm-water fishes showing faster change.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tectonic stages.** The authors divide the regional history into four stages:
1. Late Jurassic-Cretaceous magmatism and thrust faulting built a western mountain belt that likely formed the continental divide.
2. Latest Cretaceous-early Tertiary [[Laramide Orogeny]] uplifted the Rocky Mountains and created lake-holding basins (Green River, Washakie, Uinta, Piceance Creek).
3. Oligocene-Miocene extension fragmented the western belt into the Basin and Range Province, with closed drainages that gradually filled with sediment.
4. Over the last 10-15 m.y., Baja California separated from mainland Mexico, opening the [[Gulf of California]] and linking nearby basins to the sea.

**Fish dispersal logic.** Freshwater fishes are restricted by salinity, temperature, gradient, current velocity, competition and predation. Capture of small headwater drainages transfers only small, cool-water species, so the species found across a divide indicate the size of the connection.

**Key geologic units.** The [[Bidahochi Formation]] (late Miocene, Arizona) contains fossil fishes. The [[Bouse Formation]] (latest Miocene-early Pliocene, lower Colorado River trough) records early Colorado River inflow. The Sespe Formation (Los Angeles area) and the Claron Formation (southwestern Utah) also bear on paleodrainage.

## Key Findings

- **Eocene Atlantic fauna:** Eocene fishes of the Green River Formation (Wyoming) and basins in southeastern British Columbia belong to Atlantic drainage groups, with no evidence of Pacific connection. This supports the Mesozoic mountain belt as the continental divide before Rocky Mountain uplift.
- **Eocene paleodrainage disputes:** The authors discuss whether middle to late Eocene Lake Uinta drained westward to coastal Oregon (Tyee Formation). They note that Green River fish fossils belong to eastern North American families absent from Pacific drainages.
- **Mazatzal Quartzite clasts:** The quartzite cobbles in the Sespe Formation are judged inconsistent with an east-west transport route from the Mazatzal Quartzite, which lies within an eastward-flowing system feeding the Baca Basin, New Mexico. The clasts may have come from buried or eroded sources in the Mojave-Sonora region.
- **Basin and Range drainage loss:** Oligocene-Miocene extension "broke apart older drainages," and pre-Pliocene drainage patterns are described as "poorly known to completely unknown."
- **Colorado River integration:** The Colorado River "established its present course at ca. 5 Ma." The Bouse Formation was deposited across formerly closed basins, not in a drowned Colorado River mouth as Howard (1996) proposed.
- **Miocene fish exchange:** Fossil fishes show north-south connections in the Great Basin. Topminnows (Fundulidae) were found in Nevada in the middle-late Miocene. Muskellunge (*Esox*) from the 11 Ma Deer Butte Formation in Oregon are related to *Esox columbianus* of the Pliocene Ringold Formation.
- **Snake River links:** The Bidahochi Formation fish are closely related to fossils from the Snake and Sacramento Rivers. The authors argue this implies a major drainage transfer, not a small headwater exchange, from the ancestral Snake River to the upper Colorado Plateau drainage in the late Miocene.
- **Green River versus Colorado fauna:** The Green River shares all 13 of its species with the Colorado and Snake Rivers. The Green and Platte faunas share only one common species (mountain sucker, *Catostomus platyrhynchus*), indicating a strong barrier through the Miocene.
- **Divergence timing:** The Colorado River and Rio Grande *Gila* chubs diverged at an estimated minimum of about 4.7-5.6 Ma, and the Colorado River and Rio Grande suckers differ by 10.9%. Among the lower Colorado River taxa, divergence times range from 5.1 to 1.9 Ma. Rainbow trout and speckled dace are estimated at around 8 Ma.
- **Mountain sucker phylogeny:** Divergence of the Apache and Gila trout from their sister groups is dated to about 12 Ma, based on fossils. Pantosteus diverged from its sister subgenus about 12 Ma.
- **Pluvial lakes and capture:** Great Basin fish faunas were "historically richer" than modern ones. Pleistocene cooling and pluvial lakes allowed fish transfers between the Bonneville Basin, Snake River and Sacramento drainages.
- **Continental Divide and Gulf opening:** The authors state that the transfer of the upper Colorado River catchment from the Atlantic to the Pacific side is "a dominant theme" of the Cenozoic hydrographic history.
- **Conclusion:** The Colorado River's 5 Ma arrival "was probably the largest Cenozoic hydrographic change in southwestern North America." Late Miocene Colorado Plateau runoff did not flow down the ancestral Rio Grande or Platte River.
