## Executive Summary

This 1995 interagency memorandum from the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDWR) critiques a proposed "Flooded Bottomlands Restoration Program" intended to benefit endangered fish species—primarily the [[Razorback Sucker]]—in the Upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. Rather than presenting novel empirical data, the document is a management-science critique highlighting fundamental unknowns in floodplain restoration ecology, particularly the risk that restoring floodplain/backwater habitat connectivity could disproportionately benefit invasive [[Nonnative Fish Species]] over native, imperiled [[Catostomidae|catostomids]]. The authors argue for a conservative, phased, hypothesis-driven approach (inventory → ranking → screening → pilot studies → land acquisition) rather than large-scale irreversible construction, and identify specific candidate and reference sites (e.g., Old Charlie Wash, Leota Complex, Walter Walker, Adobe Creek, Moab) for pilot-scale testing of [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment dynamics.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphometric or anatomical descriptions of the [[Razorback Sucker]] adult or larval forms. However, several life-history/size-based parameters relevant to morphological development are referenced as critical unknowns:

- **Young-of-year (YOY) stage**: Program explicitly plans to "inoculate" floodplain sites with YOY razorback suckers, indicating a focus on early larval/juvenile life stages as the vulnerable and target cohort.
- **Size-at-stocking**: A central unresolved research question is "what sizes of razorback suckers need to be produced from flooded bottomlands for enhanced survival and ultimately recruitment" — implying that body size at the time of floodplain egress/stocking is a key determinant of post-release survivorship.
- **Growth rate as a proxy**: Floodplain sites are proposed as growth-rate assessment arenas, using somatic growth (rather than detailed morphology) as the primary measured trait, tied to habitat productivity.

No further taxonomic, meristic, or morphological detail (fin ray counts, coloration, hump development typical of adult razorbacks) is provided in this document.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The memo frames the [[Razorback Sucker]]'s ecological requirements against a backdrop of contested floodplain habitat function within the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]:

- **Floodplain/bottomland habitat**: Seasonally flooded backwater and bottomland areas connected to the mainstem river are hypothesized to serve as nursery/rearing habitat for drifting larval suckers, supporting growth prior to recruitment into the adult population.
- **Competing littoral niche occupation**: Many established nonnative fish species in the upper basin are described as **littoral specialists**—occupying vegetated, low-velocity, high-temperature habitats—which are ecologically similar to the very floodplain habitats proposed for restoration. This creates direct niche overlap between the restoration target ([[Razorback Sucker]]) and established [[Nonnative Fish Species]].
- **Connectivity risk**: Direct, uncontrolled hydrologic connections between constructed floodplain habitats and the mainstem river are flagged as a potential vector enhancing nonnative fish recruitment/access rather than native species benefit.
- **Geomorphic and productivity context**: The authors (citing geomorphologist recommendations, e.g., Schmidt) stress that floodplain ecological function depends on sediment transport, sediment deposition, sediment geochemistry, and geomorphic evolution relative to historical ("naturally functioning") floodplain sites — factors largely ignored in the original proposal's site-selection criteria.
- **Predation/contaminant risk**: Larval razorback suckers occupying floodplain habitats are exposed to two major risk categories treated with unequal rigor in the original proposal: contaminant exposure (uncertain lethality) versus nonnative fish predation (near-certain lethality — "We are sure of the fate of a larval razorback if it is eaten").

## Key Findings

- **Central management concern**: Floodplain restoration could inadvertently enhance nonnative fish populations at the expense of the native community, given habitat niche overlap with littoral nonnative species; UDWR refuses to sanction uncontrolled mainstem-connected habitat construction until this is resolved.
- **Knowledge gap**: There is currently no reliable scientific basis for how to best manage flooded bottomlands to benefit endangered species in the basin — a claim the memo asserts is not supported by existing data despite being treated as settled fact in the original proposal.
- **Faulty prioritization**: Original site-selection criteria overweighted spatial factors (area size, proximity to known spawning bars) and underweighted biological/geomorphic attributes (nonnative fish distribution, productivity, sediment dynamics).
- **Five critical research questions identified** for guiding pilot-scale construction:
  1. Optimal razorback sucker size-at-release for enhanced survival/recruitment.
  2. Relationship between floodplain productivity and time required to reach that size threshold.
  3. Effect of existing nonnative fish community composition on native target species survivorship.
  4. Whether floodplain habitat construction favors native vs. nonnative fish, contingent on site-specific nonnative density/size structure.
  5. How construction efforts should be spatially directed to benefit the full native fish assemblage, not just [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Recommended phased approach**: Inventory → re-ranking (incorporating productivity, nonnative community, geomorphology, habitat quality) → contaminant/nonnative screening → small-scale reversible pilot projects → deferred land acquisition/large-scale construction.
- **Proposed pilot sites** for short-term razorback sucker stabilization: Old Charlie Wash and the Leota Complex, involving nonnative fish exclusion, YOY inoculation, and growth-rate monitoring.
- **Proposed evaluation sites** for long-term floodplain function studies, segmented by research question:
  - Contaminant impacts: Walter Walker, Adobe Creek.
  - Nonnative fish impacts: Gravel pit, Old Charlie, Leota, Moab.
  - Productivity/growth outcomes: New site on lower Green River, plus other sites.
- **Monitoring recommendation**: Basin-wide monitoring of individual site actions should be integrated into the basin-wide Integrated Stocking/Species Management Plan (ISMP) rather than funded under capital improvements.
- **Institutional caution**: The memo explicitly opposes certain proposed Statements of Work and recommends delaying property acquisition activities, redirecting those funds toward propagation facilities and screening/ranking research instead.
