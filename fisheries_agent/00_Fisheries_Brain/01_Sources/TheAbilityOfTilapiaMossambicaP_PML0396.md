# Depth Tolerance of Tilapia mossambica and Chloramine-T Treatment of Ichthyophthiriasis

#ichthyology #aquatic-biology #cichlidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The source is a 1973 issue of the *Journal of Fish Biology* (vol. 5, pp. 783–798) containing two papers. The first, by M. S. Caulton and B. J. Hill, experimentally tests how deep [[Tilapia Mossambica]] can go and how its [[Swimbladder]] compensates for pressure. Juveniles tolerated pressure equivalent to at least 33 m. Compensation depth fell as fish grew: small adult males reached about 20 m, small adult females about 13 m, and large adults about 11.5 m. The authors argue that adults are largely confined to shallow water and that their swimbladders regulate gas partly through active oxygen secretion. The second paper, on [[Chloramine-T]] for treating [[Ichthyophthiriasis]] (white spot disease), is truncated in the source. Only its introduction and the start of its methods are present, which describe toxicity tests on [[Rasbora Heteromorpha]] (harlequin fish). No results or conclusions from that paper survive.

## Background & Description

**Paper 1: Depth tolerance in Tilapia mossambica**

[[Tilapia Mossambica]] (Peters) is a cichlid heavily harvested as a food fish in natural and man-made lakes and ponds. Little was known about its ability to live in deep water. Two lines of evidence raised the question:

- Tentative identification of juveniles at 20 m in [[Lake Sibaya]].
- Echo-sounding work by Bruton (1973) suggesting that adults do not go below 15 m.
- Similar reports from [[Lake Kariba]], where adults apparently occur only to 15 m. This is despite a lake maximum depth of 110 m and a thermocline between 10 and 36 m.

The authors tested depth tolerance and swimbladder physiology experimentally, using a [[Pressure Chamber]] described by Hill & Caulton (1974).

**Methods**

- Three size classes were used: juveniles (total length under 135 mm), small adults (135–195 mm, caught at Lake Sibaya near East London), and large adults (205–250 mm, caught in freshwater ponds near Kenton-on-Sea).
- Fish were held in 1 m deep tanks at 22 ± 1 °C.
- Two fish were tested at once in separate compartments. Water flowed at about 5 L/min. Oxygen drop across the chamber was about 3%.
- Pressure was raised in 12 h steps of about 2 m equivalent per increment. Fish were scored for neutral buoyancy, which marked the maximum compensation depth.
- Ten fish per size class were tested, including 10 males and 10 females among small adults. Juveniles were not sexed.
- Swimbladder gas was withdrawn by needle and analysed with a PO₂ electrode (accuracy about 1%). Oxygen content was calculated at 760 mmHg and 22 °C.

**Paper 2: Chloramine-T and ichthyophthiriasis**

The introduction is by D. G. Salmon and co-authors (author list partly truncated) at the Ministry of Agriculture. It notes that ichthyophthiriasis is a serious disease of fresh water fish, caused by the ciliate protozoan *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*. Standard treatments cited include methylene blue, acriflavine, formalin and malachite green. Chloramine-T is described as a disinfectant with a mild, colourless solution and an antiseptic action linked to hypochlorous acid. Schäperclaus (1954) reportedly used it against the disease at one concentration but abandoned it, apparently because of toxicity. The authors note that toxicity may depend on water hardness, through the formation of metal complexes. The methods section describes toxicity tests on harlequin fish (*Rasbora heteromorpha*). The rest of the paper is missing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Depth and the lake bottom**

Results suggest that adult [[Tilapia Mossambica]] use only part of the deep lake bottom. Juveniles can move vertically over a wide depth range. This may let them escape predators and widen their feeding area. Larger fish lose this ability with growth. The authors conclude that adults are limited to relatively shallow water (about 15 m) and make restricted use of lake and reservoir bottoms.

**Swimbladder regulation**

Compensation depends on regulating swimbladder gas, which is adjusted partly by [[Oxygen Secretion]] into the bladder at depth. Oxygen tension in the swimbladder rose faster than pressure alone would predict. The authors take this as clear evidence of secretion. The surface gas of the fish was already about 52% oxygen. Swimbladder volume changes with depth are important for vertical movement. A 2 m vertical shift from 2 m depth was estimated to change swimbladder volume by about 16%.

**Reservoir management**

The authors suggest that bush clearing in new reservoir basins need not extend below 15 m if the fishery depends on large adult [[Tilapia Mossambica]].

**Disease context**

Ichthyophthiriasis affects fish in fresh water and is a major cause of mortality in hatcheries and other intensive culture. Chloramine-T was considered as an alternative to standard treatments. The toxicity of chemicals to fish varies with water hardness, which affects treatment choice.

## Key Findings

**Paper 1: Depth tolerance of Tilapia mossambica (Caulton & Hill, 1973)**

- **Juveniles:** Tolerated a pressure increase or decrease equivalent to 33 m in 10 min. Neutral buoyancy was regained afterward. Compensation to at least 33 m (the maximum tested) was observed.
- **Small adult males:** Maximum compensation depth of 20 m. Males were significantly more capable than females at maximum depth (P < 0.05).
- **Small adult females:** Maximum compensation depth of 13 m.
- **Large adults:** Mean maximum compensation depth of 11.5 m (range 9.5–13 m). Small adults had significantly higher oxygen tension at maximum depth than large adults (P < 0.02).
- **Oxygen tension in swimbladder:** Lower in juveniles than in medium adults at the surface. No significant sex difference at the surface (P > 0.1). At maximum compensation depth, males had significantly higher oxygen tension (P < 0.05).
- **Gas increase:** Females raised swimbladder oxygen tension 3.9 times for a 2.3-fold pressure increase. Males raised it 4.4 times for a 3-fold increase. The authors attribute this to oxygen secretion.
- **Timing:** Adults needed about 4–7 days for maximum compensation at 22 °C (value partly illegible in scan). Small adult males reached 10 m faster than females, which needed about 50 h. Large adults needed about 80 h of decompression to regain neutral buoyancy.
- **Comparison:** Bishai (1961) reported that adult [[Tilapia Nilotica]] (90–150 mm) compensated to a 260 mmHg pressure increase (about 10 m) in 10–12 h. The authors suggest Bishai's apparatus may have raised ambient oxygen, shortening the apparent time.
- **Conclusion:** Growth reduces depth-compensation ability. Small adult females mature earlier and at smaller size than males, and appear physiologically ahead.

**Paper 2: Chloramine-T for ichthyophthiriasis (truncated)**

- The paper was designed to test the toxicity of chloramine-T to fish and its efficacy against *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*. Chloramine-T's toxicity depends partly on water hardness.
- Methods for toxicity tests on ten harlequin fish (*Rasbora heteromorpha*) are described, but no results survive in the source.
- Earlier work by Schäperclaus (1954) indicated potential efficacy at one concentration, but toxicity was a concern.
