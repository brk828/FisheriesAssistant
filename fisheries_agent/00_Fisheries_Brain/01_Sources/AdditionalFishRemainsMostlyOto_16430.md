# Additional Fish Remains, Mostly Otoliths, from a Pleistocene Deposit at Playa del Rey, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleoichthyology #otoliths

## Executive Summary

This Los Angeles County Museum of Natural History contribution (No. 119, December 31, 1966) by [[John E. Fitch]] reports a resampling of the [[Playa del Rey Pleistocene Deposit]]. A 200-pound field sample of washed fossiliferous screenings was examined under a binocular microscope at six magnifications. The author recovered 1,174 identifiable otoliths representing 34 "species," plus elasmobranch teeth and other remains. Twelve bony-fish species and two elasmobranchs were new to the deposit, raising the known total from this locality to 62 species (at least). The study argues that microscope-aided searching is essential for accurate fossil fish faunas, since naked-eye sorting is biased toward large otoliths. The fauna indicates a quiet-water embayment and a period of warmer local ocean temperatures.

## Background & Description

The Los Angeles County Museum of Natural History relies on volunteer and staff sorting of fossil screenings. Most prior sorting, led by [[Invertebrate Paleontology]] personnel, targeted mollusks, so small vertebrate remains were often missed. The author, a [[Research Associate]] at the Museum and marine biologist with the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], noticed that he overlooked otoliths while sorting mollusks and vice versa. He tested the "perusal-by-eye" method by microscopically examining discarded fine material.

The sampling method involved:
- Wet-screening through three stacked sieves (2, 1, and 0.5 mm).
- Drying residues in the sun.
- Screening coarse material through a ¼-inch mesh, then examining the remainder under a binocular microscope.

The Playa del Rey locality had previously been reported in [[Fitch 1964]], which listed 1,376 otoliths gleaned over three decades or more.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Depositional setting:** Fine-grained silty sand typical of a quiet-water embayment 10 to 12 fathoms deep, after [[Valentine 1961]].
- **Deep-water and offshore taxa:** Bathypelagic species such as [[Stenobrachius leucopsarus]] and [[Symbolophorus californiense]], and bottom-living species such as [[Coelorhynchus scaphopsis]] and [[Argentina sialis]], occur as rare otoliths. The author suggests these were carried in the stomachs of predators or scavengers, with gulls and other fish-eating birds a likely vector.
- **Southern warm-water indicators:** Six southern fish species seldom recorded north of Mexico occur in the deposit, including [[Coelorhynchus scaphopsis]] and [[Prionotus stephanophrys]]. The author links them to higher local ocean temperatures at the time of deposition.
- **Preservation and taphonomy:** Many otoliths are eroded or worn, consistent with digestion in predators.
- **Anthropogenic threat:** Fossil deposits in southern California are being lost to freeway construction, housing and cut-and-fill projects, which motivated the resampling.

## Key Findings

**Sampling efficiency**
- 1,174 otoliths (34 "species") were recovered microscopically from the 200-pound sample; 19 species were common to both samplings, 9 occurred only in the earlier diggings, and 12 only in the resampling.
- Naked-eye sorting favored large otoliths: [[Genyonemus lineatus]], [[Otophidium]] spp., and [[Seriphus politus]] comprised 15.8, 16.3, and 20.1% of the eye-sorted yield but 9.9, 9.7, and 9.9% of the microscope yield.
- Small otoliths were under-recovered by eye: [[Engraulis mordax]] made up 4.9% of the earlier sample but 16.5% of the resampling.
- [[Porichthys]] spp. yielded 24.0% by microscope versus 12.8% by eye, due to odd shape and many otoliths of 2 mm or less.
- Of the 12 species new in the resampling, 8 were small, 3 medium, and 1 large. Eight of them were rare, though [[Icelinus quadriseriatus]] yielded 10 otoliths.

**Elasmobranchs**
- Two elasmobranch species were new to the site: [[Dasyatis dipterurus]] (diamond stingray) and [[Heterodontus francisci]] (horn shark).
- The author states that a microscope is an "absolute necessity" for finding the tiny teeth of seven common inshore elasmobranchs, which pass through an 18-mesh (1 mm) screen.

**Fauna totals (resampling)**
- Elasmobranchs: at least 11 species in nine families, represented by 58 teeth, 15 vertebrae, 5 caudal stings and 4 "wing" spines.
- Teleosts: at least 34 species in 21 families, represented by 1,182 otoliths, 32 vertebrae, 18 teeth, 2 fin spines and 1 jaw fragment.
- Known species from the deposit after resampling: 62 (at least).

**Notable species accounts**
- [[Engraulis mordax]] (northern anchovy): 194 additional otoliths, against 68 previously reported.
- [[Myliobatis californicus]] (bat stingray): 32 additional teeth, against 205 previously reported.
- [[Citharichthys stigmaeus]] and [[Citharichthys sordidus]] (sanddabs): 181 and 7 otoliths identified, plus 47 undeterminable.
- [[Porichthys notatus]] (plainfin midshipman): 278 additional otoliths.
- [[Occa verrucosa]] (warty poacher): the only species from the deposit not reported south to the same latitude in recent times; several hundred small-mesh tows in Santa Monica Bay caught none.

**Conclusions**
- Microscope-aided sorting is required to recover an accurate fauna from fossil fish deposits.
- The assemblage reflects a quiet-water embayment and warmer local ocean conditions during deposition.
