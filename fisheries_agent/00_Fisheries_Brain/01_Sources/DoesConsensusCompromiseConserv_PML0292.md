# Does Consensus Compromise Conservation? Critique of the Upper Colorado River Recovery Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This policy critique examines whether [[Consensus-Based Management]] protects endangered fishes, using the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] as a case study. The authors argue that the Program has delivered real gains in water development and stakeholder inclusion, but has failed the fishes. They contend that the consensus model is vulnerable to domination by powerful constituent groups and by bureaucratic procedural goals, so success is measured by process rather than [[Species Recovery]]. Three recommendations follow: judge success by population response, keep a statutory jeopardy threat in place, and reduce funding-driven power imbalances.

## Background & Description

The study concerns four endangered fishes of the Upper Colorado River: the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Water development, dams, irrigation, exotic sportfish introductions, and fish poisonings have sharply reduced native fish populations since the [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922. The Recovery Program was created by a 1987 Cooperative Agreement to allow continued water development while recovering these species. Its participants include the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, water users, and environmental groups.

The Program was shaped by the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Under Section 7, water projects that threaten listed species can be blocked unless a "Reasonable and Prudent Alternative" is proposed. Water users feared that reduced flows needed for fish would trigger such denials, which made water rights a highly politicized resource. Colorado, Utah and Wyoming chose to negotiate rather than litigate, amend the Act, or halt development.

The authors built their analysis from secondary sources (Fish and Wildlife Service appraisals, newspaper coverage, and law review articles) and from in-depth interviews with 22 key informants. Interviewees came from multiple constituent groups, and their identities were kept anonymous. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors describe a river transformed into "ruler-straight channels" and diverted for irrigation, reservoirs and urban growth. They report that the Program's status reports include both optimistic and pessimistic population assessments. By the most optimistic estimates, fish numbers have not changed during the Program's lifetime. At worst, two species are thought to be locally extirpated.

Scientific evidence on population trends is uneven. The authors note that small fish populations are very difficult to measure, and that studies on razorback sucker populations and juvenile recruitment conflict. Studies cited include Modde et al. (1996) on razorback sucker status and Behnke & Benson (1980) on habitat conditions. Stanford (1994) is cited for instream flow needs and evidence of continued decline. This scientific uncertainty fuels disagreement among participants, because each interest group can cite supporting studies.

## Key Findings

**Program achievements.** The Recovery Program has stimulated research on rare and poorly documented fishes. Over 200 water development projects involving nearly 700,000 acre-feet of water were approved from 1988 onward, and none has been litigated for Endangered Species Act compliance. The authors also note that stakeholders were included in the decision process, though possibly "at least superficially."

**Failure at species recovery.** The fish populations are not recovering. Authors argue that the fishes will likely remain listed for the indefinite future, and many local biologists believe they will require ongoing human intervention.

**Consensus as a tradeoff.** The authors acknowledge that a consensus approach may be the only politically palatable route in such a contested setting, so some shortcomings may have been unavoidable. They argue, however, that the key indicator of success should be recovery, not open communication while populations decline.

**Weakness 1: Procedural goals over species recovery.** The Program relies on five recovery elements: habitat management; habitat development and maintenance; native fish stocking; nonnative species management; and research, monitoring and data management. The Fish and Wildlife Service assumes that completing these actions offsets the impacts of water development. Progress is measured through checklist-style Annual Status Reports and Sufficient Progress Reports, which assure the Program Director that the Program qualifies as a Reasonable and Prudent Alternative. For example, meeting a native fish stocking target counts as success even if stocked fish never reproduce.

**Displacement of goals.** Drawing on [[Robert Merton]]'s concept of displacement of goals, the authors argue that organizational means have become ends in themselves. Agencies focus on nonnative species management and hatcheries rather than on water use and habitat flows, which are more controversial. Examples include a state wildlife division supporting hatcheries rather than addressing sport fishing threats in native habitat, and water users backing nonnative fish eradication rather than flow management.

**Accountability gap.** Because success is defined by completed actions, no participant is clearly responsible when populations decline. A former state agency director asked who should bear the consequences if actions are implemented but species populations do not respond. The authors conclude that the Program has no accountability mechanism for population decline.

**Weakness 2: Unequal power among stakeholders.** Participation is broad, but voices are not equal. Water developers are described as controlling funding through lobbying and congressional proposals, and hydropower revenue adds to Program funding. Environmentalists represent a broader constituency, lack a unified agenda, and are reluctant to challenge the Program because of the pending reauthorization of the Endangered Species Act. The authors note that a legal challenge might produce a Pyrrhic victory, which has left environmentalists unable to threaten a lawsuit over lack of progress. The consensus rule gives each group veto power, which sometimes blocks harmful actions but more often produces inactivity and marginal policies.

**Recommendation 1: Measure success by population response.** Require positive responses in endangered populations as the marker of success. Shift attention from monitoring and stocking to population recovery, including releasing water from dams and reservoirs and protecting existing wild fishes.

**Recommendation 2: Maintain the threat of a jeopardy opinion.** Consensus should not guarantee immunity from a jeopardy finding. Keeping this statutory sanction in place would keep recovery the top priority, prohibit further development amid continued decline, and make participants more invested in fish status. The authors also call for a short-term alternative goal alongside the ultimate goal of delisting.

**Recommendation 3: Reduce funding-driven power imbalances.** Transfer funding responsibility toward the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]], whose mission aligns with species recovery, and away from the Bureau of Reclamation, which is primarily interested in water development. The authors acknowledge that shifting these funds would be extraordinarily difficult. They also suggest broader participation in fundraising to diversify lobbying.

**Broader implications.** The authors argue that replacing uncertainty with the certainty of a process, a concept drawn from [[Lance Gunderson]]'s work on adaptive management, is common in resource management. They recommend fixed, measurable targets expressed in terms of the actual resource, such as fish population figures. A Colorado River scientist's remark that the fish "don't seem to give a rip" about the Program's successes underlines the authors' central point.
