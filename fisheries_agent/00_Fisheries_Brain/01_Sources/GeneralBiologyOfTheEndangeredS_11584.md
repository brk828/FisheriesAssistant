# General Biology of Endangered Species on the Klamath Reclamation Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter reviews the general biology, status and habitat needs of three endangered taxa affected by the [[Klamath Reclamation Project]]: the [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), the [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and the [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*). The eagle section focuses on breeding and wintering populations in the [[Klamath Basin]] and on how water management shapes prey availability. The sucker sections cover taxonomy, identification, distribution, life history and the population decline in [[Upper Klamath Lake]] and its tributaries. The document is a background chapter that describes existing conditions; it does not set out a management plan.

## Background & Description

**Bald Eagle.** The bald eagle is a generalized predator and scavenger closely tied to aquatic habitats. Its main foods, in descending order of importance, are fish (live or carrion), waterfowl, mammalian carrion, small birds and mammals. Individuals do not reach sexual maturity until 4 or 5 years of age. The species declined across most of its range, including Oregon and California, mainly from habitat loss, shooting and pollution. It was listed for protection under the Act on February 14, 1978. It is listed as endangered in 43 states (including California) and threatened in 5 (including Oregon). A [[Bald Eagle Recovery Plan]] was completed in 1986. In 1990 the Service proposed reclassifying the species from endangered to threatened in the lower 48 states, and the Pacific Region Recovery Team found this justified in the seven northwestern states. The Klamath Basin is one of 45 Recovery Zones in that region.

**Lost River Sucker.** The species was listed as state threatened in California in 1974, federally endangered in 1988, and endangered in Oregon on October 13, 1991. Its taxonomy has changed many times since [[Edward Drinker Cope]] first described it in 1879 as *Chasmistes luxatus*. Names used over time include *Catostomus rex*, *Deltistes*, and *Catostomus luxatus*. The current name, *Deltistes luxatus*, was adopted in the 1991 American Fisheries Society list. Adults can reach about 1 m total length. Juveniles under 100 mm SL are difficult to separate from [[Shortnose Sucker]] juveniles. Key traits include a long snout, a wide median notch in the lower lip, and 25 to 33 gill rakers in juveniles.

**Shortnose Sucker.** The shortnose sucker was listed as a California state threatened species in 1974, federally endangered in 1988, and endangered in Oregon in 1991. Its taxonomy has been more stable than that of the Lost River sucker and it has been classified as *Chasmistes brevirostris* since 1952. Adults have a large head, an oblique terminal mouth and thin lips. Juveniles are darker and deeper-bodied than juvenile Lost River suckers, with 33 to 48 gill rakers and 74 to 83 lateral line scales. Adults vary in scale and gill raker counts across published sources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Eagle prey and reproduction.** Prey availability is the main limiting factor for eagle populations when secondary factors such as weather, contaminants and disturbance are absent. Eagles get prey in two ways: when live prey behaves in a catchable way (for example, fish basking near the surface) or when carcasses are exposed. Only part of the available prey is found and taken. In the Klamath Basin, prey comes from three classes, each shaped by water management: fish, breeding waterfowl and small mammals during the breeding season; concentrations of migratory waterfowl in fall and winter; and small mammals exposed by late-winter irrigation flooding. Irrigation deliveries reach the [[National Wildlife Refuges]], which support hundreds of thousands of migratory waterfowl and so sustain the wintering eagles.

**Rotenone effects.** Rotenone treatments that removed rough fish reduced eagle reproduction in northern Michigan and in a northern California reservoir. In 1990, [[Osprey]] abandoned young at Oregon's [[Hyatt Reservoir]] after rotenone sharply reduced prey.

**Sucker habitat.** Suckers depend on lake margins, tributary spawning reaches and river systems. Spawning uses gravel substrate with little sand. Upper Klamath Lake is [[Eutrophication|hyper-eutrophic]], with high pH (8.5 to 10) and dissolved oxygen falling below 1 mg/l, which is linked to the sucker decline. Causes include agricultural development, the loss of the northern marsh that trapped nutrients, altered lake flushing and lower lake levels under dam regulation. Lack of stratification allows blue-green algae to flourish. Hybridization between the two sucker species and other catostomids was a concern, but recent genetic work found that hybridization has not occurred at an appreciable rate.

**Threats to suckers.** The Fish and Wildlife Service listing identified dams, instream flow diversions, marsh drainage, dredging of Upper Klamath Lake and other water manipulation as the main causes of decline. It also cited the absence of successful recruitment for about 18 years. Exotic species competition and late-summer die-offs in the lake were also identified.

## Key Findings

**Bald eagle status and breeding**

- The Klamath Basin Recovery Zone had 79 occupied breeding sites in 1990, close to the Recovery Plan goal of 80.
- Mean reproduction over the previous 5 years was 0.93 young per occupied site, near the Recovery Plan standard of 1.0.
- Breeding adults begin laying between early March and mid-April. Incubation lasts 5 weeks and eaglets stay on the nest for 10 to 12 weeks.
- Pairs potentially affected by the Project include 2 at [[Gerber Reservoir]] and more than 30 at Upper Klamath Lake.
- Winter counts during the 1980s ranged from 500 to 1000 eagles, making the basin one of the most important wintering areas in the continental United States.
- Weather, contaminants and disturbance are not believed to impose serious limits at present. A 1982 productivity drop was attributed to storms, and [[Frenzel 1984]] found no significant contaminant effect in the early 1980s.

**Lost River sucker**

- Spawning occurs March to May, with migration starting around 5.5 °C and spawning recorded at 8 to 19 °C.
- Spawning depths were 21 to 70 cm, with velocities of 31 to 90 cm/s.
- Fecundity was reported at 44,000 to 218,000 eggs for fish 518 to 750 mm fork length.
- Eggs hatched in 8 days at 13.5 °C in the laboratory; swim-up occurred after 10 days.
- Sexual maturity in Upper Klamath Lake is at ages 6 to 14, most often at age 9. The oldest recorded individual was 43 years old.
- In 1984 and 1985, population estimates in the Williamson and Sprague rivers were 23,123 and 11,861 Lost River suckers, respectively.
- Lost River suckers were a major food fish for Indigenous people and were heavily snagged from the 1950s. A 10-fish-per-day bag limit was introduced in 1969.

**Shortnose sucker**

- Spawning occurs in April and May, with reported water temperatures of 9 to 17 °C.
- Spawning depths were 21 to 60 cm, with velocities of 41 to 110 cm/s.
- Fecundity was reported as 58,000 and 46,000 eggs for fish about 360 to 445 mm fork length (as stated in the source).
- Sexual maturity occurs at ages 5 to 8, most often at 6 or 7. The oldest recorded individual was 33 years old, from [[Copco Reservoir]].
- In 1984, 2,650 shortnose suckers were counted in the Williamson and Sprague rivers.
- The species appears to be native only to Upper Klamath Lake and its tributaries. Other basin populations may have arrived through the A-canal of the Klamath Irrigation District.

**Distribution and management gaps**

- Lost River suckers persist in Upper Klamath Lake, Clear Lake Reservoir, and the Upper Klamath River above Copco Reservoir. Their presence in the Lost River is poorly documented, and a viable population there is considered unlikely.
- In 1991, a few Lost River suckers were seen spawning below Anderson Rose Dam, which raised the possibility that the species survives in Tule Lake.
- Clear Lake Reservoir is the only place where both species occur together in the Lost River system.
- Life-history data for Clear Lake Reservoir are lacking. Juvenile suckers there are stranded in intermittent tributary pools.
- The U.S. Forest Service planned to inventory remaining endangered sucker populations in 1992.
- Copco Reservoir shortnose suckers are mostly adults over 16 years old. Adult shortnose suckers have been documented gathering in the upper reservoir before spawning upriver. Predation by [[Yellow Perch]] on young suckers is suspected as a cause of the missing year-classes.
- The main spawning sites in the lake are Sucker Springs and the tributaries. Gravel was added at Sucker Springs in 1987, and suckers were then observed using the improved area. The USFWS suggested a lake level of about 4,142.1 feet as a lower limit for spawning there.
