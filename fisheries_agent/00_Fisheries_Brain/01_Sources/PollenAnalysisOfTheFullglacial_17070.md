# Past Environments: Pollen Analysis and the Full-Glacial Landscape of the American Southwest

#palynology #paleoecology #pleistocene-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This document is a symposium transcript on the paleoecology of arid North America, centred on Paul S. Martin's paper "Pollen Analysis and the Full-Glacial Landscape." It contains no fish data. Its subject is terrestrial and lacustrine: pollen records from [[Playa]] basins, dry lakes, and related deposits. The paper argues that [[Pollen Analysis]] combined with [[Radiocarbon Dating]] shows major vegetation shifts across the Southwest during the late Pleistocene glacial interval. Nine full-glacial pollen spectra show pine, spruce and fir, and [[Artemisia]] replacing their modern dominants, implying vertical vegetation displacements of roughly 800 to 1200 m. The discussion section records methodological debate about deflation, evaporite stratigraphy, sampling location, and the role of long-distance wind-borne pollen.

## Background & Description

The paper frames a long-standing disagreement. Pleistocene climate in unglaciated America has been "fraught with discord." Early work by Huntington (1907), Gilbert (1890), and Russell (1885, 1889) linked pluvial lakes to glaciation. Plant ecologists such as Spalding (1909), Shreve (1919, 1951), and Axelrod (1950) largely argued for stable desert vegetation. Antevs (1954) proposed a 900 to 1200 m lowering of life zones in New Mexico during the glacial maximum.

Martin identifies two obstacles. First, fossil plant remains are scarce in fans and playas, which are the main Pleistocene deposits. Second, the [[Rancho La Brea]] tar pit fauna has often been treated as a full-glacial record, although radiocarbon dates of 14,000 to 15,000 years B.P. place it in the late-glacial period. Martin notes that the period of maximum ice advance in eastern North America was about 20,000 years ago, with rapid ice-margin change between 23,000 and 17,000 years ago. Only fossil evidence of that age, he argues, is relevant to glacial-maximum conditions.

The paper uses modern surface pollen samples as analogues for each fossil spectrum. It classifies vegetation into five modern formations, following Shantz and Zon (1924): desert, grassland, woodland, ponderosa pine parkland, and boreal forest. Each is characterised by a pollen signature, for example, alpine deposits combine large amounts of herb pollen with spruce and pine, while boreal forest deposits have little herb pollen and more than ten per cent spruce and fir.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The discussion stresses that playa stratigraphy is complicated by post-depositional change:

- **Wind deflation:** Malde reports that at [[Death Valley]] salt crust movement left accumulated salt thousands of feet above the basin floor, representing "several inches" of material removed. Damon reports that the margin of [[Willcox Playa]] carries a desiccated, mud-cracked zone about five feet above the present playa floor, which he interprets as a minimum of five feet of deflation.
- **Evaporite behaviour:** Damon notes that evaporites are not necessarily deposited in sequence. Sodium salts occur throughout the Willcox core, and sulphates may be converted to sulphide by bacterial action. Surface salts are often blown out of the playa after storms.
- **Sampling location:** Wendorf observes that the basin centre is the only place where pollen is preserved, because it stays moist. Leopold recommends comparing a central drilling locality with a marginal one, and notes that spruce zones served as stratigraphic markers in his cores.
- **Dunes and reworking:** Wendorf suggests that deflation, infilling, and redeposition may have occurred many times at the High Plains basins, which would make uniformity of sediments difficult to establish.
- **Taphonomic caution:** Hester and Haynes emphasise that fossils such as snails, and humic-acid-rich soil carbon samples, must be accompanied by a description of their depositional environment, since redeposition can cause ecological misinterpretation.
- **Continuity of water:** Potter argues that continuous aquatic spores and pollen would show whether the central basin of [[San Augustin Plains]] held water throughout the period. Wendorf asks whether the lake dried during the [[Altithermal]]. Potter considers this possible.

## Key Findings

**Methodological framework**

- Martin's paper argues that pollen analysis combined with radiocarbon dating addresses two major problems in arid-America Pleistocene interpretation: correlation with events elsewhere, and the erratic distribution of climate-sensitive fossils.
- A total of nine full-glacial pollen records is available, two of them unpublished. Five surface-pollen tables serve as modern ecological equivalents.
- The full-glacial spectra imply vegetation displacements of at least 800 m in most localities. Pollen records alone establish this displacement only for the Chuska Mountains.

**Site-by-site results**

- **[[Searles Lake]], California** (radiocarbon-dated parting mud, 10,000 to 24,000 B.P., average of 14 levels, N = 6000): pine 52 per cent (modern 43 per cent), with Artemisia 14 per cent (modern 3 per cent). The closest modern analogue is pinyon-juniper woodland at 1800 to 1950 m in the [[Panamint Mountains]]. Reaching Searles Lake would require a displacement of at least 1200 m.
- **[[Tule Springs]], Nevada** (N = 400): pine 73 per cent, against 9 per cent in the modern Mojave Desert sample. The record suggests ponderosa pine parkland descended more than 1000 m during the Wisconsin glacial interval.
- **[[Llano Estacado]], Texas and New Mexico**: Rich Lake level L-513A (17,400 ± 600 B.P.) has pine 88 per cent, spruce 5 per cent, and Artemisia 4 per cent. Martin estimates a vertical vegetation displacement of at least 1000 m. Hafsten's modern stock-tank samples recorded pine at 10 to 32 per cent through wind transport.
- **[[Willcox Playa]], Arizona**: the full-glacial sample at 1.9 to 2.1 m (A-352, 23,000 ± 500 B.P., N = 410) contains pine at 99.0 per cent. The modern playa sample has 17.5 per cent pine, and the modern Catalina Mountains equivalent has 68.4 per cent. The authors cite this as a record with no modern lake analogue.
- **[[San Augustin Plains]], New Mexico** (Y-1053, 23,070 ± 650 B.P., BHM core at 2060 m): pine 77 per cent, spruce 21 per cent, with no grass pollen. Martin postulates boreal forest around San Augustin Lake about 20,000 years ago.
- **[[Laguna Salada]], Arizona**: a spruce and fir maximum lies at 170 to 190 cm, below A-256 (7300 ± 110 B.P.). The full-glacial peak in spruce also occurs at 3 cm in a middle core. Martin describes the records as "quite similar" to San Augustin Plains.
- **[[Deadman Lake]], Chuska Mountains, New Mexico** (N = 576 full-glacial, 1087 modern): pine 34 per cent (modern 67 per cent), Artemisia 46 per cent (modern 9 per cent). The unusual Artemisia frequency may reflect a subalpine Artemisia community or wind-blown pollen. Bent and Wright infer a treeline depression of 800 m.

**Interpretive conclusions**

- The evidence supports the view that major transformations in the vegetation of arid America accompanied late Pleistocene glaciation. Martin states that "no full-glacial record fails to reveal a major change in pollen composition."
- Martin argues that the full-glacial pattern agrees with Wurm-Wisconsin climatic changes elsewhere in the Northern Hemisphere (Flint and Brandtner 1961).
- Long-distance transport of pollen has to be considered. Desert herb pollen reaches the Catalina, San Juan, and Sangre de Cristo Mountains, and pine pollen is carried into deserts. Wind-transport therefore complicates the modern equivalents used.
- The Sonoran Desert's full-glacial history is unknown. Its dominant species are largely insect-pollinated, so pollen evidence is unlikely to survive in basin sediments.
- Biogeographic consequences include the probable displacement of ranges such as that of the pygmy kangaroo rats (*Microdipodops*), and the relative monotony of Southwestern plant communities, possibly the legacy of repeated Pleistocene migration.

**Discussion points still unresolved**

- Dahl asks whether higher glacial precipitation in the Rocky Mountains would reduce the temperature depression implied by a firn-line depression of over 3000 feet.
- Potter objects that a few sites with pine and spruce should not be extrapolated across Texas, Oklahoma, and Colorado, since tree stands often form small "tree islands" within grassland.
- Potter reports that peat in the Turtle Mountains of North Dakota shows spruce to the top, while no spruce remains there today.
- Martin's full-glacial map is acknowledged to be provisional, and the northern Plains lack sufficient pollen records to settle the position of the boreal forest.
