## Executive Summary

This 1937 Ohio Division of Conservation bulletin synthesizes limnological and fisheries survey data collected from 1930–1936 across impounded waters in Ohio, building on fish collection records dating to 1920. The study examines the ecological transformation of stream habitats into reservoir systems, focusing on water chemistry (oxygen, turbidity, pH, CO₂), forage fish ecology, siltation rates, and management recommendations for the emerging Muskingum Watershed Conservancy District (14 dams, ~77,780 acres flood area). Key findings establish the [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Bluntnose Minnow]] as dominant forage species in impounded waters, document the ecological shift from riverine to lentic fish assemblages following impoundment, and provide practical guidance for reservoir habitat engineering, stocking policy, and seasonal fishing regulation.

## Background & Description

- **Gizzard Shad**: Silvery coloration, lacks spines, soft-bodied — described as an attractive baitfish and "dainty morsel," though not caught by hook and line. Functions as a highly efficient plankton strainer ("excellent tow net"), feeding almost entirely on microscopic algae.
- **Bluntnose Minnow**: Generalized feeding morphology well-suited to a forage role; deposits eggs on the underside of submerged objects, with males guarding the egg mass.
- **Fathead Minnow**: Feeds via a mud/algae-grazing habit; diet dominated by benthic algal material rather than zooplankton or macroinvertebrates.
- **Species richness by reservoir** varied substantially with reservoir size/age: 14 species (Griggs Reservoir), 17 (Lake Alma), 19 (O'Shaughnessy Reservoir), 20 (Lake Milton), 27 (Lake Loramie), 32 (Indian Lake), 44 (Lake St. Marys), 48 (Portage Lakes), 52 (Buckeye Lake).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Forage fish base**: [[Gizzard Shad]] is the dominant forage fish in larger impounded waters (natural colonization or introduction), converting phytoplankton directly into game-fish biomass without competing with game species for food, cover, or breeding habitat. Diet studies (Tiffany) found up to 140 algal forms across 200 individuals examined, with one fish containing 50 algal taxa.
- **[[Bluntnose Minnow]]** occupies the broadest ecological niche among Ohio's 163 fish species — found in more streams, lakes, ponds, and reservoirs, at higher abundance, across a wider range of habitats than any other species.
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]** thrives in oxbow habitats and is recommended for pond propagation; algal gut-content analysis identified 128 algal forms from 203 individuals.
- **Trophic role**: Young gizzard shad ("hickory shad") identified as the single most important forage item for adult [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[White Crappie]], black crappie, [[White Bass]], brown bullhead, and [[Channel Catfish]] during summer months.
- **Community shift after impoundment**: A 1929 survey of the free-flowing Meander Creek yielded 30 stream-adapted species (rock bass, smallmouth bass, hog sucker, bigeye chub, blacknose dace, creek chub, redside dace, silver-jaw minnow, stone roller, sand/banded/rainbow/fantail/greenside darters, northern muddler, etc.). After flooding to form [[Meander Reservoir]] in 1932, only 12 species were recorded — total fish biomass increased, but riverine specialist diversity collapsed.
- **Riffle habitat value**: Ohio stream riffles averaged ~150 lbs of insect larvae per acre in early summer, serving as critical fish-food habitat and crayfish/forage-fish refugia; impoundment eliminates nearly all of this production except some mayfly taxa.
- **Depth-dependent benthic productivity**: Insect larvae and other benthic fish-food organisms decline rapidly in pools deeper than ~40 inches (Ludwig; Krecker).
- **Winter habitat**: Open lake waters under ice were not oxygen-depleted even after cutting ~2 ft of ice, but shallow, vegetation-choked bays showed oxygen depletion — a reversal of summer productivity/oxygen dynamics in the same habitats.
- **Vegetation dynamics**: Early-season aquatic vegetation offers ideal spawning/rearing conditions, but dense mid/late-summer growth can drive nighttime oxygen depletion severe enough to cause fish kills, necessitating vegetation removal.

## Key Findings

- **Dominant hook-and-line species** in impounded Ohio waters: [[White Crappie]] becomes dominant initially, with [[Bluegill]] and [[Largemouth Black Bass]] increasing as coarser aquatic vegetation establishes.
- **Siltation rates**: O'Shaughnessy Reservoir (1,120 acres) silted 6% of volume in 9 years; Griggs Reservoir (364 acres) silted 25.6% of volume in 30 years.
- **Minnow bait trade**: An estimated 5–7 million minnows removed annually from Ohio streams by dealers alone (excluding angler-caught bait); over 600,000 minnows reported taken from a single county (Allen Co.) in one year.
- **Meander Reservoir case study** (filled Feb. 1932, 2,000 acres, 10 billion gallons):
  - Dissolved oxygen crashed from 0.8 ppm (10m, Aug 1932) to 0.00 ppm below 27 ft (Aug 1933), recovering to 3 ppm at 12m by June 1933 and 4.9 ppm at 12m by Oct 1936.
  - Minimal initial stocking (750 bullheads + 500 crappies, April 1932) produced 131,569 fish removed for restocking other waters by spring 1935, and 101,308 fish (≈10 lbs/acre) by fall 1936.
  - 1935 vs. 1936 removals by species (fall counts, pounds): Crappies 116,651→80,558 (13,659 lbs); Suckers 11,022→896 (1,792 lbs); Bluegills 2,410→12,378 (2,475 lbs); Bullheads 1,380→3,872 (887 lbs); Largemouth Bass 43→975 (1,460 lbs); Smallmouth Bass 3→105 (96 lbs); Rock Bass 2 (3 lbs); Gizzard Shad 2,524 (578 lbs).
- **Parasitology**: Bangham found very low parasite loads in [[Gizzard Shad]].
- **Growth rate**: Gizzard shad average ~4 inches by end of first year (Grimm).
- **Management recommendations for the Muskingum Watershed Conservancy District** (14 dams, >$40 million cost, operational ~1938) included: continuous seasonal biological monitoring; a dedicated fish management agent; university-partnered limnological studies (Western Reserve, Wooster, Kent State, Marietta, Akron); a minimum one-year closed fishing season post-flooding; closed spawning season (Dec 1–June 30); protective status for forage fish, mussels, turtles, and fur-bearers pending population assessment; retention of tree stumps, brush, and cut limbs as structural fish habitat in graduated depth zones; and designation of one small (Charles Mill) and one large (Piedmont/Senecaville) reservoir as unstocked experimental controls.
- **Recommended forage species for active propagation/stocking**: gizzard shad, bluntnose minnow, golden shiner, brook silverside, lake shiner, steelcolor minnow, Johnny darter, log perch, fathead minnow, and crayfish.
- **Tagging/fin-clipping** recommended as the primary method for future population and movement studies across the reservoir system.
