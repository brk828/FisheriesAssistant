# Observations on the Food of Cladocera

#limnology #zooplankton #cladocera #daphnia #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This posthumously published study by Homer G. Fisher (summarized by Will Scott following Fisher's death in 1917) investigates the diet of three freshwater cladoceran species — [[Daphnia Pulex]], [[Simocephalus Vetulus]], and [[Daphnia Hyalina]] — through direct microscopic examination of gut/alimentary tract contents. Across three research settings (a laboratory culture, a university pond population, and a natural lake/creek population), Fisher consistently found only one type of organized material in the intestines of all specimens: a minute, pear-shaped **flagellate** of undetermined systematic position. Based on its constant presence across diverse collection times, locations, and rearing conditions, and the absence of any other identifiable organized material, Fisher concluded that this flagellate constitutes the primary food source for these [[Cladocera]] species.

## Background & Description
- **Prey organism (flagellate):**
  - Minute, pear-shaped body, visible only under oil immersion lens.
  - Possibly represents two or more cryptic species, though sizes were too small to resolve at the time.
  - Exhibited typical flagellate locomotion in living material (distinguished from passive Brownian motion by directed movement and cessation upon fixation with Flemming's solution).
  - Some individuals observed in the process of division (asexual reproduction), supporting their identity as living organisms rather than inert particulate matter.
  - Uniform pear shape across individuals cited as evidence against a non-biological (particulate/detrital) origin.
- **Host organisms (cladocerans):** All specimens examined were parthenogenetic females; no morphological description of the cladocerans themselves is given beyond alimentary tract anatomy (anterior vs. anal end of intestine).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic relationship:** The flagellate appears to be a primary planktonic food source for multiple cladoceran species, suggesting a foundational role in freshwater [[Plankton|planktonic food webs]].
- **Habitat distribution of prey:** Confirmed present in open lake water (Eagle/Winona Lake) via net-straining and filter-paper filtration of lake water independent of daphnid guts — demonstrating the flagellate is a free-living component of the water column, not an intestinal parasite.
- **Host collection habitats:**
  - *Daphnia* spp. collected via plankton pump from lake center at 4–5 meters depth.
  - *Simocephalus vetulus* collected via silk net from creek mouths and lake edges among aquatic macrophytes — indicating a more littoral/vegetated niche compared to the pelagic *Daphnia*.
- **Spatial distribution within host gut:** Flagellates concentrated almost exclusively in the anterior intestine, with only sparse occurrence near the anal end (interpreted as passive displacement during sample preparation rather than a parasitic distribution pattern).

## Key Findings
1. **Single dominant food item identified:** In all three cladoceran species examined (99 total individuals across all sites), the only organized material recovered from gut contents was the pear-shaped flagellate — no other food particles were consistently observed.
2. **Ruled out artifact/Brownian motion:** Staining (Flemming's triple stain) revealed internal organization confirming the bodies were living flagellates, not suspended debris undergoing passive Brownian movement.
3. **Ruled out parasitism hypothesis:** The flagellates' free-living presence in filtered lake water, independent of any host, combined with their anterior-intestine concentration (consistent with ingestion/feeding rather than infection), supports a food-source interpretation over a parasitic one.
4. **Consistency across independent populations:** The flagellate was found in cladocerans reared in laboratory jars (Carnegie Institution), collected from Hill Pond, and collected from Eagle Lake and Cherry Creek — three ecologically distinct settings — strengthening the generality of the conclusion despite modest sample sizes (15, 32, and 89 individuals respectively across the three locations).
5. **Methodological approach:** Diet determination was achieved not through examination of loose food suspensions (which proved inconclusive) but through direct dissection/crushing and microscopic (oil immersion) examination of alimentary canal contents, supplemented by stained smears — a methodological lesson applicable to future [[Zooplankton]] feeding studies.
6. **Unresolved taxonomy:** The systematic identity of the flagellate was not determined; Fisher noted the possibility of multiple cryptic species within the observed population, a question left open at the time of his death.
