# Habitat Use and Preference of Gila Topminnow (Poeciliidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the habitat requirements of the federally endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*). The authors combined field observations of aggregations in [[Cienega Creek]], Arizona, with controlled experiments in outdoor pools testing responses to three types of cover and to a choice of water depths. Topminnow showed relatively specific habitat preferences despite their broad tolerances: strong selection for cover, consistent use of the upper third of the water column, avoidance of measurable water velocity, and diel shifts toward warmer water. The authors attribute the persistence of topminnow in Cienega Creek to environmental heterogeneity, a long expanse of perennial water, and the absence of introduced fishes, and they argue that recovery depends on managing large, heterogeneous stream reaches rather than isolated tanks or spring heads.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow historically was widespread and abundant across the [[Gila River System]] of Arizona, New Mexico, and northern Sonora, Mexico. Before 1940 it may have been the most abundant low-desert fish in the [[Colorado River Basin]], below about 1,800 m elevation. It was listed as federally endangered in 1967. Declines resulted from major habitat alteration over the preceding century and from interaction with the non-native [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which has been present since the late 1920s.

Only 10 or 11 native populations remained in the U.S. at the time of writing. Topminnow had been transplanted to more than 200 sites, but only about 19 or 20 of these populations persisted ([[Species Introduction]] outcomes reviewed by Hendrickson and Brooks 1991). The authors note that these transplants were largely trial-and-error because habitat requirements were poorly known.

The study had two objectives:
1. Characterize the microhabitat conditions used by topminnow in nature.
2. Test fish responses to different types of cover and to a choice of water depths in controlled settings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Cienega Creek is a tributary of the Santa Cruz River in Pima County, about 90 km southeast of Tucson and 8 km north of Sonoita. It is shallow and meandering, with deeply incised pools, quick runs, narrow riffles, and long shallow glides. Topminnow occupy more than 16 km of the stream, from 3.2 km upstream of the Gardner Canyon confluence down to a constriction called the Narrows. [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) also occur there, and no introduced fish species had been documented. The authors describe the creek as the largest expanse of perennial lotic habitat for topminnow without mosquitofish.

**Field sampling.** Data were collected in July 1991 and May 1992 at six sites (two pools, two backwaters, and two glides) in the mid-reaches of the creek on the BLM Empire-Cienega Resource Conservation Area. Two backwater or glide sites were altered by scouring flows in winter 1991, so two new sites were selected in 1992. Aggregations of 50 to 500+ fish were delineated from the bank after at least 15 to 20 minutes of observation. Depth, velocity (at 0.6 of the depth), and [[Substrate Classification]] were measured every 20 cm across transects perpendicular to flow. A "plant material" substrate category was added. Temperature was recorded in occupied and unoccupied areas, in morning and mid-afternoon. Availability was estimated from transects at about 1 m intervals across each entire macrohabitat.

**Experimental setup.** Twenty-five topminnow (18 females, 7 males) were collected from Cienega Creek in June 1991 and held at the University of Arizona fisheries facility, where they reproduced in a 3.65 m diameter by 1.2 m deep pool. Experiments ran in small vinyl-lined pools (1.7 m diameter by 0.9 m deep) in winter (February to March) and summer (June to August) 1992. Each trial used ten fish (five of each sex), which acclimated for at least 24 hours and were used in only one morning and one afternoon trial.

**Cover trials.** Cover covered about 25% of pool surface area on one side. Treatments were floating [[Styrofoam Sheets]], floating [[Algal Mats]] held by screened rings, and anchored [[Plastic Strips]] simulating submerged vegetation. Fish positions were scored every 5 minutes for one hour from an observation ladder.

**Depth trials.** Wooden "steps" created three equal-area horizontal zones with depths of 55.8 cm, 25.4 cm, and 10.2 cm. Horizontal location and vertical position (upper, middle, or lower third) were recorded every 5 minutes.

**Statistics.** The [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]] compared depth distributions, and the [[Chi-Squared Test]] compared substrate distributions, with areas used versus available. A [[Student's T-Test]] assessed cover preferences, and a [[Two-Way Anova]] analyzed depth, water column position, and time of day.

## Key Findings

### Field microhabitat
- Aggregations occupied water 1 to 80 cm deep. Topminnow were also seen in water deeper than 1 m.
- Depth use differed from availability (p < 0.05) in both pools in 1991 and 1992 and in one glide each year. Use did not differ from availability in one glide and both backwaters. Water was generally deeper where fish were found.
- Topminnow consistently occupied the upper third of the water column, regardless of total depth.
- No consistent substrate preference was found. Fish occurred most often over or within plant material.
- Fish used areas with no measurable velocity, even when velocities up to 10 cm/s were available. Riffles, runs, and cascades were rarely occupied.
- Young-of-year fish concentrated in the shallowest (1 to 5 cm) and quietest margins, including water in vehicle tire tracks.
- Diel shifts occurred. Near dawn, fish were near mid-channel, where water was 1 to 2 °C warmer than at the margins. By mid-morning to afternoon, they moved to margins that were warmer than mid-channel. The margin difference is reported as "22 C" in the source, which is likely an OCR error for a value around 2 °C.

### Experimental cover preference
- Topminnow strongly selected all three cover types over open water, especially in winter.
- Winter mean use of the styrofoam side was 77% (P < 0.001). In summer, selection was significant only in the afternoon (P < 0.05).
- Algal mats drew a mean of 75% of fish (P < 0.001) in both seasons and both time periods.
- Plastic strips drew a mean of 84% of fish (P < 0.001). Winter selection of the covered side was 94%, compared with 73% in summer.
- Concealment was greater in winter. Fish were most often concealed within the plastic strips and were virtually invisible there.
- Strongest preference was for anchored plastic strips. Algal mats were also favored, probably because they supply invertebrate and algal food.

### Experimental depth preference
- No difference in use among the three depth zones (P > 0.10).
- Fish strongly selected the upper third of the water column (P < 0.001), at 74% of observations in the morning and 66% in the afternoon.
- The upper stratum averaged 1.5 °C warmer than the middle third in the morning and 1.3 °C warmer in the afternoon. It was 3.5 °C warmer in the morning and 5.1 °C warmer in the afternoon than the lower third.
- Because surface water was also warmer, the authors could not determine whether fish selected near-surface position, warmth, or some combination of cues.

### Synthesis and conservation implications
- Topminnow show broad tolerances but relatively specific preferences: cover, surface position, low velocity, and warm water. Their clear response to small temperature differences (about 1 to 2 °C) suggests that thermal requirements deserve further study.
- Persistence in Cienega Creek is attributed to environmental heterogeneity, a long expanse of perennial water, and the absence of [[Mosquitofish Co-Occurrence]] effects. This makes the creek resemble many historic habitats.
- Many introduction sites are isolated tanks, spring heads, or short intermittent reaches, which the authors consider less likely to support recovery.
- Tyus (1992) cautioned that habitat data from remnant and declining populations may be suboptimal. The authors recommend managing for large, heterogeneous stream reaches similar to Cienega Creek.
