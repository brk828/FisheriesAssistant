# Growth of Bluegills and Crappies in Reelfoot Lake, Tennessee

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sunfish-and-crappies

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Robert J. Schoffman]], published through the [[Reelfoot Lake Biological Station]] and the [[Tennessee Academy of Science]], re-checks the age and growth of [[Bluegill]], [[White Crappie]], and [[Black Crappie]] in [[Reelfoot Lake]], Tennessee. Scale samples from 1947–1950 are compared with earlier studies from 1937–1948. Growth rates in length and weight remained high across the study period, and game fish were being harvested during rapid growth. The author attributes persistent angler complaints mainly to lake conditions, such as summer clay-silt pollution, rather than to fishing regulations, and recommends lake improvement over restricting fishing.

## Background & Description

**Study history.** The investigation began in 1937 to determine age and growth of game and rough fish in Reelfoot Lake. Bluegills were re-checked in 1947 and white crappies in 1948, and this paper adds a 1950 re-check of bluegills and white crappies plus a first check on black crappies. Because conditions in the lake change, the author judged repeated monitoring necessary.

**Species studied.**
- [[Bluegill]] (*[[Lepomis Macrochirus]] macrochirus*): 201 fish (1947) and 177 fish (1950), total 378.
- [[White Crappie]] (*[[Pomoxis Annularis]]*): 204 fish (1948) and 218 fish (1950), total 422.
- [[Black Crappie]]: 310 fish (1938) and 199 fish (1950), total 509.

**Methods.**
- Samples came mainly from commercial catches using hoop nets of 1.5-inch square mesh.
- The 1937 collections also used [[Wire Set Net]]s (basket type, poultry netting, set in hollow stumps). These were prohibited in 1941, so no 1947 or 1950 specimens came from them.
- Fish were weighed on a two-pound spring scale (quarter-ounce graduations) and measured to the nearest 1/8 inch.
- Age was expressed in "summers of life." Ages were determined from [[Scale Age Determination]] using a magnifying apparatus with polarized light, described in an earlier paper by the author (1939). Annual rings were counted on scales.

**Regulatory context.**
- 1937: legal length was 6 inches for bluegills and 9 inches for crappies.
- 1939: sale of [[Black Bass]] was prohibited.
- 1941: wire set nets were prohibited.
- 1947: crappie size limit reduced to 8 inches.
- May 1949: all [[Size Limit]]s removed. Commercial fishermen were limited to 25 bluegills per day and 20 crappies (white or black) per day, with the same [[Creel Limit]] applying to sportsmen. Warmouth bass was limited to 25 per day.
- 1951: the Tennessee legislature removed the commercial creel limit and allowed sale of game fish from Reelfoot Lake, except black bass, with a bill of sale originating from the lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water level and habitat.** Between studies, channels were dug to open all parts of the lake, giving free water flow at all times and raising the water level. Summer 1951 was unusually wet, keeping the lake above its established level until late August. The author reports high water retarded vegetation growth and kept summer sport fishing above average.
- **Sediment pollution.** Heavy rains caused Reelfoot Creek, Indian Creek, and drainage ditches to overflow, carrying clay silt from surrounding hills. The water turned much of the lake a "mucky brown," and bluegills would not bite during these episodes. The author identifies this as a key problem for fishing.
- **Food and season.** The author reports no appreciable change in the length of the growing season or in food supply between study periods.
- **Fishing decline.** No creel censuses exist. Based on fifteen years of personal observation, the author concludes that only summer fishing is declining, due to changing biological conditions in the lake (Schoffman, 1950). Fall crappie fishing in 1949 and 1950 was reported as the best the north end of the lake had experienced, and summer 1951 was better for crappies than any previous summer.

## Key Findings

### Bluegill
- Age groups 3 and 4 made up 75% of 1947 specimens; age groups 4 and 5 made up 77% of 1950 specimens. This reverses the pattern seen in 1937 and 1947.
- Age group 4 was the most common group in both years (56% in 1947, 42% in 1950).
- Length: 68% of total growth (to the sixth summer, 9.5 inches in 1947) was completed in the first summer in 1947, and 65% in 1950.
- Weight: 30% of total weight (to the sixth summer, 11.8 ounces in 1947) was acquired in the first two summers in 1947, and 33% in 1950. Cumulative weight acquired for third through sixth summers in 1947 was 39%, 50%, and 66%; in 1950, 47%, 65%, and 76%.
- Weight increments remained large in older age classes, indicating fish were still in a fast-growing phase.
- In 1950, bluegills were shorter and heavier for each size group than in 1947.

### White Crappie
- Age group 3 made up 41% of the 1948 catch and 35% of the 1950 catch. The oldest group (age 8) was 2% in 1948 and 2.2% in 1950.
- Length: 60% of total growth (to eighth summer, 14.19 inches in 1948) was completed in the first two summers in 1948, and 58% in 1950.
- Weight: 20% of total weight (to eighth summer, 24.25 ounces in 1948) was acquired in the first two summers in 1948, and 21% in 1950. Cumulative weight for third through seventh summers in 1948 was 28%, 45%, 64%, and 69%; in 1950, 21%, 29%, 39%, 55%, and 75% (as printed in the text).
- In 1950, white crappies were shorter and heavier for each size group than in 1948.

### Black Crappie
- Age group 4 was the most common in both studies: 43% in 1938 and 42% in 1950.
- The oldest group was age 7 in 1938 (8.4% of total) and age 6 in 1950 (12%).
- Length: 67% of total growth (to seventh summer, 11.66 inches in 1938) was completed in the first two summers in 1938, and 61% in 1950.
- Weight: 29% of total weight (to seventh summer, 4.11 ounces in 1938) was acquired in the first two summers in 1938, and 33% in 1950. Cumulative weight for third through sixth summers in 1938 was 46%, 58%, and 75%; for 1950 (third through fifth summers) 48% and 56%.
- In 1950, black crappies were shorter and heavier for each size group than in 1938.

### Overall Conclusions
- All three species maintained or increased growth rates over a fifteen-year span despite changing regulations.
- Fish were harvested during a rapidly growing period, which the author considers desirable and evidence that the "crop" is being harvested.
- Angler complaints persist. The author attributes them mainly to lake conditions (summer sediment, water level, vegetation) rather than to commercial fishing, and recommends lake improvement over restricting fishing.
- The author notes that the 1950 shorter-but-heavier pattern may partly reflect removal of size limits in 1949, though the 1937 and 1950 lengths and weights are nearly the same.

### Caveats
- The paper contains internal inconsistencies in years and sample figures (for example, some tables label years differently from the text, and one table gives 422 white crappies from 1948 and 1950 without clearly separating the subsamples). Figures are reported as printed.
- Weight percentages are based on the oldest fish still growing rapidly, so they would be lower if older fish were included.
