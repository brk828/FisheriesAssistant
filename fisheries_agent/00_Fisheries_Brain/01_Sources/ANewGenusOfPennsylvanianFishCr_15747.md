# A New Genus of Pennsylvanian Fish (Crossopterygii, Coelacanthiformes) from Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coelacanth

## Executive Summary

This 1963 University of Kansas Museum of Natural History study by [[Joan Echols]] describes *Synaptotylus*, a new genus of Late Pennsylvanian coelacanth fish from the Rock Lake shale member of the [[Stanton Formation]] near Garnett, Kansas. The type and only referred species, *Synaptotylus newelli* (Hibbard), is defined mainly by a basisphenoid bearing knoblike antotic processes joined by a low ridge to small basipterygoid processes. The author proposes two new subfamilies within [[Diplocercidae]], retaining the genus in [[Rhabdodermatinae]] with [[Rhabdoderma]] based on reduced endocranial ossification and retained basipterygoid processes. The paper also argues that scale characters are unreliable for species and genus assignment, and reviews the depositional environment as lagoonal.

## Background & Description

- **Material:** Specimens were collected in 1931–1932 by H. H. Lane, C. W. Hibbard and W. K. McNown; quarried in 1954 by F. E. Peabody, R. W. Wilson and R. Weeks; and collected in 1955 by R. R. Camp. Localities were about six miles northwest of Garnett, Anderson County, Kansas (sites including KAn-1/D).
- **Preservation:** Most remains are fragmentary and dissociated. Only the type (K.U. no. 786F) and half of another specimen are nearly complete. Referred specimens number 23 catalogued K.U. numbers.
- **Size:** Parasphenoid length estimated at 15–20 mm; pelvic fin about 25 mm total length.
- **Diagnostic traits:** Knoblike antotic processes; parasphenoid ventral surface entirely toothed; paired frontals; elongate supratemporals; oval scales with posteriorly converging ridges; three anteriorly diverging pelvic apophyses.
- **Etymology:** *Synaptotylus* from Greek *synaptos* ("joined") and *tylos* ("knob, projection"), referring to the connected antotic and basipterygoid processes.
- **Synonymy:** *Coelacanthus arcuatus* Hibbard, 1933 is a junior synonym of *S. newelli*. Moy-Thomas (1937) had earlier placed both Hibbard species in the synonymy of *Rhabdoderma elegans*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Depositional setting:** Peabody (1952) interpreted the Rock Lake shale as lagoonal. The flora, adapted to well-drained soil, is of Pennsylvanian age. Marine invertebrates occur at some horizons, while plant remains and land-animal fragments suggest continental input.
- **Associated fauna:** At least one shark (*Hesperoherpeton garnettense*), undescribed labyrinthodonts, and the reptiles *Petrolacosaurus kansensis*, *Edaphosaurus ecordi* and *Clepsydrops* sp. The author calls it "the earliest known reptilian fauna" from a rich vertebrate assemblage.
- **Salinity debate:** Echols cites Wehrli (1931) and Aldinger (1931), who regarded related forms as euryhaline. Keller (1984) held that most Carboniferous fishes became freshwater adapted, while Schaeffer (1953) considered all Carboniferous and Permian coelacanths freshwater. Fishes may have lived in the lagoon, the adjacent sea, or upstream streams, with carcasses washed into the lagoon.
- **Feeding mechanics:** The loss of basipterygoid articulation is linked to a more efficient prey-swallowing mechanism, allowing the palatoquadrate and mandible to swing laterally to expand the oral cavity. Comparable changes in actinopterygians are cited from Schaeffer and Rosen (1961).

## Key Findings

1. **New genus and subfamilies:** *Synaptotylus* is erected. The family Diplocercidae is split into Diplocercinae (two large unpaired endocranial bones) and Rhabdodermatinae (reduced, partly paired ossification with retained basipterygoid processes), the latter holding *Rhabdoderma* and *Synaptotylus*.
2. **Phylogenetic position:** *Synaptotylus* is most closely related to *Rhabdoderma*, though its fin girdles and basal plates are intermediate between *Rhabdoderma* and *Coelacanthus*. The pectoral girdle resembles *Eusthenopteron* more closely than *Rhabdoderma* overall.
3. **Taxonomic caution:** Ornamentation and scale ridge patterns vary with individual, size and body location. The author recommends that isolated scales not be assigned to species and be assigned to genus only cautiously.
4. **Evolutionary trend:** Major endocranial change occurred during the Carboniferous. Devonian coelacanths had two large unpaired endocranial bones and both processes; Carboniferous forms show reduced ossification with retained processes; later forms lost the basipterygoid processes. The Pennsylvanian was the period of greatest change.
5. **Environment:** The genus occurs in layers with marine invertebrates, plant remains, vertebrate parts and dark carbonaceous material. Freshwater, lagoonal and marine habitats remain possible.
