# Contributions to a Guide to the Cypriniform Fish Larvae of the Upper Colorado River System in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management (BLM) Biological Sciences publication, authored by Darrel E. Snyder of the [[Colorado State University]] Larval Fish Laboratory, provides interim identification data for the cypriniform fish larvae of the [[Upper Colorado River System]] in Colorado. It is a partial precursor to a planned comprehensive guide. It offers a standardized developmental terminology, descriptions of identification characters, morphometric and meristic tables for 20 species, and a preliminary key to metalarvae. The work responds to the fact that the larvae of many native fishes, several of them endangered, remained undescribed, which hampers environmental impact assessment in a basin facing energy development.

## Background & Description

The document is a preface-to-appendix style technical report comprising acknowledgments, a preface, an introduction, a terminology section, identification characters, methods, species accounts, comparative tables and literature cited. Its stated purpose is to supply larval identification information for management decisions under the [[Bureau of Land Management]] and the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]].

**Scope and origin**
- Prepared under contract YA-512-CT8-129 and published December 1981 (the OCR reads "198]").
- Specimens came mainly from the Yampa, White, Colorado and Gunnison rivers, collected 1976–1979, plus rearing from artificially fertilized eggs (1978–1981) and loaned or donated material from several museums and agencies.
- Of the 20 cypriniform fishes of the basin in Colorado, only five non-native species (Cyprinus carpio, Pimephales promelas, Notropis lutrensis, Richardsonius balteatus, Catostomus commersoni) had adequate larval descriptions when work began in 1977.

**Standard terminology**
The author adopts the [[Snyder Larval Terminology]] (after Balon's hierarchy of developmental intervals) for the larval period, which has three phases:
- Protolarva: no distinct median-fin rays yet.
- Mesolarva: principal rays forming in median fins.
- Metalarva: full adult principal-ray complement and pelvic fins present.

The larval period ends when finfolds are absorbed, fin rays are complete and segmented, and all criteria for the juvenile period are met.

**Methods**
- Measurements are given as percent standard length (SL), recorded to 0.1 mm, with repeatability within 0.1 mm.
- Myomere counts follow the [[Siefert Myomere Method]] for preanal and postanal counts.
- Juveniles were cleared and stained with Alizarin Red for internal meristics.
- Preserved specimens were stored in buffered formalin (3–5%), which is preferred over alcohol for limiting shrinkage and pigment fading.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat zones:** The basin's historic habitats were four zones: cold headwater streams and lakes; intermediate cool-to-tepid rivers; large warm, turbid rivers; and major reservoirs. All are represented in Colorado.
- **Historic fauna:** Only 13 native fish species were recorded about a century ago, most of them cypriniforms, and six are endemic to the system.
- **Current status:** At least 46 species have been reported in Colorado's portion of the system, 33 of them non-native. Of the seven endemic fishes present or formerly found in Colorado, five are considered endangered, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The Colorado cutthroat trout (Salmo clarki pleuriticus) is also listed. The status of [[Mottled Sculpin]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] needs study.
- **Threats:** Dams, reservoirs, diversions, overgrazing, riparian vegetation loss, competition with non-native fishes, and water withdrawals for coal and oil shale development.
- **Egg ecology:** Cypriniform eggs are typically demersal, lack oil globules, and measure 1.0–2.0 mm in cyprinids and 2.5–3.5 mm in catostomids (about 2.0 mm in Ictiobinae and Erimyzontini). The text notes exceptions, such as pelagic eggs of *Notropis atherinoides* and *N. amoenus*, which reach about 3 mm.
- **Distribution table:** Table 1 rates relative abundance (C, O, R) and endemism/threat status across 12 subregions. Figure 1 maps the basin.

## Key Findings

- **Identification approach:** Cypriniform larvae in the Upper Colorado basin can be separated into cyprinids and catostomids by the snout-to-vent length ratio. Cyprinid larvae are generally under 72% SL, and catostomids are above it. The two families also differ in the ratio of postanal to preanal myomeres. Cyprinids have about 1/2 or greater postanal to preanal, while catostomids have 1/3 or less.
- **Myomere ranges:** Total myomere or vertebra counts were 28–51 for 70 cyprinid species and 32–52 for 27 catostomids (Snyder 1979).
- **Caudal rays:** Cyprinids usually have 19 principal caudal rays, and catostomids usually have 18.
- **Species accounts:** The document presents accounts for 20 species. Each has tables of morphometrics, meristics and developmental size events, plus illustrations of protolarvae, mesolarvae, metalarvae and juveniles. Examples:
  - *Cyprinus carpio*: 19 principal caudal rays, 15–18 pectoral rays, 35–38 vertebrae, 36–39 total myomeres. Recently hatched protolarvae ~5.3 mm SL.
  - *Gila cypha*: 45–48 total myomeres, 19 caudal rays, adult vertebrae 45–49. Metalarvae appear at about 15.2 mm SL.
  - *Xyrauchen texanus*: 14–16 dorsal rays, 18 caudal rays, 49–50 vertebrae; included in the key for dorsal ray counts.
- **Preliminary key:** A key to metalarvae of the Upper Colorado River System (about 25 couplets) separates taxa by snout-to-vent length, dorsal and anal ray counts, myomere totals, eye diameter (6% SL), body depth at vent (7% SL), pigmentation and gut form. The authors note it is not absolute and that specimens with atypical counts may not key out.
- **Pigment:** Melanophore patterns are often species-diagnostic, but cultured larvae can look different from field material.
- **Terminology:** The authors note over 60 synonymous terms for larval developmental intervals in past literature. They recommend the Snyder terminology, which was used in the Second Symposium on Larval Fishes in 1978.
- **Literature reliability:** Some published illustrations are misidentified. For example, specimens illustrated as *Micropterus salmoides* in Taber (1969) are probably a *Lepomis* species.
- **Future plans:** The planned guide will add more species, keys to protolarvae and mesolarvae, graphed morphometrics, and a reproductive summary table. Readers are asked to report errors.
- **Comparative tables:** Table 2 summarizes fin ray, lateral-line scale, vertebra and myomere counts for all 20 species. Table 3 gives egg diameters and developmental size thresholds. For example, *Cyprinus carpio* eggs are 1.3–2.1 mm, and *Catostomus latipinnis* eggs are 3.8–3.9 mm.
