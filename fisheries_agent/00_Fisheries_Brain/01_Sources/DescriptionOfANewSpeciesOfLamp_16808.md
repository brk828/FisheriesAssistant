# Some Aspects of the Relation Between Stream Foods and Trout Survival

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by [[Norman Reimers]] (United States Fish and Wildlife Service, Reno, Nevada) examines why wild and hatchery trout die in large numbers during winter in small, exposed streams. Field work at [[Convict Creek]], California, from April 1952 to June 1956 combined bottom and drift food sampling, stomach analysis, [[Digestion Experiments]] at controlled temperatures, and multi-month [[Starvation Experiments]]. The author concludes that food scarcity is a secondary, qualitative factor in overwinter survival, while adverse physical conditions of the stream (ice, slush, scouring, flow fluctuation, exposure) are the primary cause of losses.

## Background & Description

Winter trout losses are large but poorly understood, because continuous observation of stream conditions is impossible and carcasses rarely show a reliable cause of death. Disease, predation and accidents were judged too minor to explain most losses. Earlier work by [[Needham and Slater]] (1944), [[Needham, Moffett, and Slater]] (1945) and [[Maciolek and Needham]] (1952) pointed to cold water, ice and snow as agents of mortality and to winter food as a possible factor.

The study had three aims: (1) winter trout food and feeding, (2) the speed and effectiveness of digestion at various water temperatures, and (3) the effect of prolonged starvation on condition and viability across seasons.

The study site, Convict Creek, is 6 to 15 feet wide, has a gravel and rubble bottom with long riffles and few deep pools, and a grade of about 2 percent. It is typical of alpine trout streams, being repeatedly exposed to snow and ice cover, long periods of very low water temperature, and daily flow changes. Rainbow trout came from the [[Hot Creek State Fish Hatchery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Winter food supply.** Ice reduces light, periphytic algae and grazers. Anchor ice can briefly dislodge bottom organisms, but repeated anchor ice causes scouring, and ice and slush dams expose riffles to drying, freezing and rewatering. In 1952 the creek was bridged by snow and ice from early January to mid-April, and rock surfaces lost their algal growth.

**Dominant organisms.** Immature Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Diptera dominated both bottom and drift samples. Oligochaetes were abundant on the bottom but rarely eaten by trout.

**Comparative ice literature.** The author notes conflicting findings on anchor ice: [[Benson]] (1955) in Michigan saw little depletion, [[O'Donnell and Churchill]] (1954) in Wisconsin reported extensive scouring, and [[Brown, Clothier, and Alvord]] (1953) in Montana found little depletion but freezing of shoreline bottom.

## Key Findings

**Bottom fauna (square-foot riffle samples, organisms per square foot)**
- February 1942: 373 (134 lb/acre, five samples)
- January–March 1951: 107 (mild winter)
- Mid-April 1952 under ice bridges: 36 (21.4 lb/acre, 12 samples)
- April 25, 1952, open riffles: 41 (32.4 lb/acre, 12 samples)
- February and March 1953: 139 and 162
- April 1956: 137 (six samples), similar to early February levels

**Drift food (15-minute net sets)**
- February–March 1951 mean: 14.4 organisms
- February 4 to March 27, 1953: 12.5 to 19.8 per set (mean 15.5, 36 sets)
- April 1956: 7.8 per set (six samples)
- Higher flow sets yielded 17 organisms versus 18 under normal flow, so the feeding advantage was small. Trout fed on ice clumps and debris with little selectivity.

**Stomach contents (66 brown trout, February 17, 1953; mean length 17.2 cm)**
- 733 organisms in total, 11.1 per fish (versus 14.5 per fish in 40 fish in early 1951)
- Average stomach volume 0.16 cc (versus 0.29 cc in 1951)
- Debris made up 60.4 percent of ingested volume, leaving about 0.06 cc of actual food per stomach
- Oligochaetes were nearly absent from stomachs despite making up 15.8 percent of bottom fauna
- Two of 66 stomachs were empty

**Digestion (half-gram meals, hours to complete digestion)**

| Food organism | 49–53°F | 43–44°F | 35–36°F | 32–33°F |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Helodrilus (soft-bodied) | 12 | 18 | 25 | — |
| Gammarus (intermediate) | 13 | 18 | 26 | 43 |
| Arctopsyche (hard-bodied) | 16 | 24 | 44 | 70 |

Digestion slowed sharply below 35°F, but no undigested food was found in feces at 32–33°F. The author argues that winter nourishment depends more on steady, modest food availability than on abundance.

**Starvation trials**
- **Winter 1953–54 (120 days, brown trout, 32–41°F):** Exposed outdoor group (254 fish) lost 17.7 percent; sheltered indoor group (97 fish) lost 9.3 percent. The author attributes the difference to ice, slush, snowbanks and fluctuating flow.
- **Fall–spring 1954–55 (248 days):** Rainbow trout (50) reached 50 percent mortality and brown trout (60) 61.7 percent. Condition fell 31 percent (rainbow) and 21 percent (brown). Feeding began too late for a fair recovery test.
- **Stream-stocked rainbow trout, same period:** 36 percent mortality (222 days), versus 18 percent for starved fish, with condition down 28 percent.
- **Summer–fall 1955 (120 days):** Brown trout mortality was 82 percent, versus about 2 percent for rainbow trout (two groups of 50). Summer stream temperatures reached 72.5°F.
- **Overwinter 1955–56 (180 days):** Survival was 98 percent for rainbow trout and 92 percent for brown trout. Feeding for 56 days restored brown trout condition (+10.2 percent).
- **Pond trial (1,200 sq ft, Nov 1955–Apr 1956):** All 100 hatchery rainbow trout survived, with condition down 22 percent. Stream groups of rainbow trout survived 64 to 89 percent.

**Conclusions**
1. Healthy trout can fast for long periods, especially in cold water.
2. Planted trout make few immediate demands on stream food.
3. Competition for food among planted trout, or between planted and wild trout, is unlikely to be a critical factor in survival to creel.
4. Overwinter losses of catchable trout in small, exposed streams are driven mainly by physical conditions. Food is a secondary, qualitative factor.
5. Body condition at the start of a fast predicts survival more than fish origin does. Hatchery trout outperformed wild brown trout in starvation tests in every season.
