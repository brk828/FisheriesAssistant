# Comparison of Morphometric Measurements of Gila Trout and Ord Creek Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This report by Danny M. Regan (Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit) compares body measurements of 16 [[Gila Trout]] and 16 [[Ord Creek Trout]] as part of a broader study of Gila trout ecology and its taxonomic relationship to other trouts. Funding came from the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish under [[Federal Aid Project F-22-R]]. Sixteen measurements were taken from each fish and expressed in thousandths of standard length. A [[Student's T-Test]] at 95% confidence found significant differences in 12 of the 15 ratio measurements (standard length was the divisor). The author recommends further study and, if the differences hold, classifying the Ord Creek trout as a new species, proposed as *Salmo apache* ([[Apache Trout]]).

## Background & Description
- **Gila trout** was described by [[Robert R. Miller]] in 1950 from specimens collected at Main Diamond Creek on July 18, 1939. Miller's description notes a yellow cutthroat mark under the lower jaw, yellowish sides fading to silvery above the lateral line, and a golden opercle.
- **Ord Creek trout** had not been formally classified; Miller was preparing a description of this new group.
- The most obvious difference is spotting: Gila trout have round to oval spots that are fine and dense on the dorsal and caudal fins (the largest are about two-thirds of pupil diameter). Ord Creek trout have fewer spots, many as large as the pupil.
- **Collections:** Gila trout came from [[Main Diamond Creek]], New Mexico, where it is the only fish species. There are no records of introductions, so the population is considered a pure strain. The stream has been closed to fishing since 1935. Ord Creek trout came from [[Ord Creek]], Arizona, where three species were obtained. Brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) and brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) were stocked in recent years, and the Ord Creek trout is believed to be the only native species. The stream has been closed to fishing since 1960.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Both populations live in small, protected headwater streams closed to angling (Main Diamond Creek since 1935, Ord Creek since 1960).
- Main Diamond Creek is a single-species system with no stocking record. Ord Creek has stocked non-native [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] alongside the presumed native trout, so introgression or competition is a possible concern, though the report does not discuss it.
- The Ord Creek trout is native to the [[Apache Indian Reservation]].
- No water chemistry or habitat measurements are given.

## Key Findings
**Methods:**
- 16 Gila trout and 16 Ord Creek trout were measured with dividers and a dial caliper. Measurements were straight point-to-point lines to the nearest 0.1 mm, and distorted parts were gently restored to a normal shape.
- Each measurement was divided by [[Standard Length]] (mm) and expressed in thousandths. Range, mean, standard error and standard deviation were reported, and a t-test (Snedecor, 1962) was applied with a tabular t of 2.042 at 95%.
- The measurements were snout length, orbit length, head length, predorsal length, upper jaw length, pectoral length, prepelvic length, pelvic length, dorsal fin basal length, adipose length, dorsal fin depressed length, body depth, head depth, head width and body width.

**Results (mean Gila vs. Ord Creek, calculated t):**

| Character | Gila | Ord Creek | t | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Snout length | 73 | 67 | 2.727 | Significant |
| Orbit length | 76 | 69 | 3.500 | Significant |
| Head length | 292 | 278 | 3.590 | Significant |
| Predorsal length | 534 | 507 | 6.000 | Highly significant |
| Upper jaw length | 155 | 150 | 1.190 | Not significant |
| Pectoral length | 184 | 196 | 4.138 | Significant |
| Prepelvic length | 566 | 576 | 2.128 | Reported as not great enough to rule out one population (t exceeds 2.042) |
| Pelvic length | 140 | 160 | 8.000 | Highly significant |
| Dorsal fin basal length | 150 | 163 | 3.939 | Significant |
| Adipose length | 116 | 101 | 4.839 | Significant |
| Dorsal fin depressed length | 242 | 265 | 6.389 | Highly significant |
| Body depth | 256 | 275 | 4.138 | Significant |
| Head depth | 186 | 199 | 0.930 | Not significant |
| Body width | 154 | 136 | 5.294 | Highly significant |
| Head width | 151 | 136 | 3.947 | Significant |

- Gila trout had larger snout, orbit, head, predorsal, adipose, body width and head width values. Ord Creek trout had longer paired and dorsal fins (pectoral, pelvic, dorsal base, dorsal depressed) and deeper bodies.
- The author's conclusion lists 12 significant differences and 3 non-significant ones (upper jaw, prepelvic, head depth). Prepelvic length is counted as non-significant even though its calculated t (2.128) is slightly above the tabular value (2.042); this is an inconsistency in the source.
- The most prominent differences were dorsal fin depressed length, predorsal length, pelvic length and body width.

**Recommendations:**
1. Conduct a complete study of the overall differences between the two groups.
2. Make meristic (count) data on both groups.
3. Take additional morphometric measurements not covered here.
4. If the differences hold, classify the Ord Creek trout as a new species.
5. Use the name *Salmo apache*, common name [[Apache Trout]].

**Limitations:** The sample was small (n = 16 per group) and the groups were assumed to be random samples of their populations. The report also compares only morphometrics, not meristics or genetics.
