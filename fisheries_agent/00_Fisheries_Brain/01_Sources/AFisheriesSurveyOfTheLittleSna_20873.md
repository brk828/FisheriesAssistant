# Fisheries Survey of Streams in the Little Snake River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 Wyoming Game and Fish Commission completion report (Project No. 154-FR-34-1) documents a stream-by-stream survey of the [[Little Snake River]] drainage in south-central Wyoming. The survey assessed fish populations, habitat, water chemistry, and benthic fauna to set management policy for sustained yield of game fish. It found that the only native trout, the [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], had declined sharply after introductions of [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Brown Trout]]. Water use, watershed disturbance, and historic [[Copper Pollution]] were identified as major limits. The report gives stocking, access, and protection recommendations for roughly forty fishable streams.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and personnel.** The survey was run by Fisheries Management Crew No. 5 under Project Leader Jack Kanaly, with Fred Williams, Robert Millis, and George Kidd. It responded to a problem the report describes: planting policies were often set by geographic boundaries rather than biological conditions, and rising fishing pressure, agricultural, domestic, and industrial water use, and pollution required management changes. Stated objectives were (1) to set management policies for each water based on biological, physical, and chemical conditions, and (2) to create a guide for similar surveys elsewhere in Wyoming. No earlier investigation of these streams was on record.

**Setting.** The drainage covers over 1558 square miles (997,120 acres) and includes mountains to 10,500 feet, rock slide areas, timbered slopes, sagebrush hills, meadows, and fertile river bottoms. Life zones are Upper Sonoran, Transition, and Canadian. Main land uses are sheep and cattle ranching, limited farming, and some [[Uranium Exploration]]. Domestic use, irrigation, and stock watering are the main water uses; big game (deer, antelope, elk) also depend on the water.

**Fisheries history.** About forty streams could support game fish at least in part. The native [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii pleuriticus*) survived in only a few streams. Records show cutthroat first planted in Big Sandy Creek in 1933, with rainbow (1936), brook (1936), and brown trout (1936) following. A 1955 gill-net set in [[Battle Lake]] caught 145 brook trout and one cutthroat, and the report credits that single brook trout planting with opening the way for brook trout across nearly the whole drainage. Early creel reports (1946) listed rainbow, brook, cutthroat, and brown trout, and by 1950 brook trout dominated the harvest.

**Methods.** Local sportsmen and residents were interviewed, and reconnaissance used aerial photos and county, Forest Service, and geological maps. Field work ran from July 26 to August 23, 1955, near minimum seasonal flow. Study sections were set on major streams, with at least one site per stream. Measurements included bottom type (% silt, rubble, sand, gravel, boulder), pool and riffle depth, width, flow (c.f.s.), velocity, pH (Helige disc comparator), alkalinity (titration with N/50 sulfuric acid), temperature, and visual turbidity. Fish were sampled by 115-volt AC [[Electrofishing]], keyed with Simon (1946), and food organisms were rated A (abundant), C (common), or R (rare). Benthos was identified with Needham and Needham (1953). Trout stomachs were examined where feasible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mainstem Little Snake River.** Clear and fast above [[Dixon]], but silty and yellow-gray below it, with silt up to six inches deep locally. Flow measured 24 c.f.s. at the Galloway Ranch on August 11, 1955, and fluctuates seasonally. Water is warm and turbid between Dixon and [[Baggs]], and [[Muddy Creek]] dumps heavy silt in at Baggs, making the river unfit for game fish.
- **Watershed disturbance.** Forest fires, timber operations, overgrazing, and irrigation have increased runoff and erosion. Signs include silt deposits, eroded gullies, trash piles along streams, springtime flooding, and drying of smaller streams. Drought over the previous six years and rising water and land use were cited as likely causes of the fishery decline.
- **Irrigation diversion.** On several creeks, so much water is diverted that fisheries potential is eliminated (e.g., [[Big Gulch Creek]], [[Roaring Fork]]).
- **Beaver dams.** Beaver ponds provided most fishable habitat on small streams, but on some streams they also blocked migration and may have reduced natural reproduction. Dams on [[Big Sandstone Creek]] were often washed out each spring.
- **Copper contamination.** Past mining (operations ended 1916–1917) left [[Haggerty Creek]] polluted enough that practically no aquatic life survives there.
- **Benthic fauna.** Table 5 lists dominant bottom fauna by water. Common groups included Physa snails, Trichoptera (caddisflies), Plecoptera (stoneflies), Ephemeroptera (mayflies), Diptera (midges and flies), and Coleoptera (beetles). Alkalinities ranged from about 29 to 174 ppm (OCR-garbled values; treat as approximate). Silt-covered rocks greatly reduced useful trout food insects.

## Key Findings

**Fish populations and species**

- Only about forty streams were judged capable of supporting game fish at least in part.
- The mainstem Little Snake River electrofishing at five points between Baggs and Savery yielded only three game fish (a 13-inch whitefish, an 11-inch rainbow, and a 19-inch [[Channel Catfish]] below Dixon). Eight rough-fish species were abundant; bluehead and flannelmouth suckers dominated.
- [[Battle Creek]] (upper section) yielded 46 brook trout (3.7–7 inches) and 8 cutthroat (5.2–7.4 inches) in 400 feet, judged an adequate population.
- [[Haskins Creek]] yielded 41 brook and 11 cutthroat trout in 450 feet (1.5–8.9 inches), with most fish 4.0–4.4 inches. Small size was attributed to heavy fishing, overproduction of brook trout, and short life expectancy.
- [[Smith Creek]] yielded 51 brook trout in 200 feet (3.5–7.5 inches), with excellent cover.
- [[Douglas Creek]] held only cutthroat trout (average 6 inches, largest 8 inches) and was recommended as a "cutthroat sanctuary."
- [[Solomon Creek]] held 34 cutthroat (3–7 inches); it had never been stocked and was also recommended as a sanctuary.
- On [[North Fork Little Snake River]], 6 small cutthroat were taken in 400 feet; it was also recommended as a cutthroat sanctuary.
- [[Little Savery Creek]]: three trout in three stations (a 10-inch rainbow, an 11-inch cutthroat, and a 26-inch brown). A 1957 photo caption describes an eight-pound brown trout from this creek.
- [[Big Sandstone Creek]] yielded 14 cutthroat (3.1–9.0 inches) in its headwaters, but no permanent population could be established without costly stream improvement.
- [[Muddy Creek]] electrofishing produced a good brook trout population (2 to 8 inches) in the lower half. Headwaters shocking produced only two brook trout after 6,873 two-inch brook trout were planted in 1950.
- [[Lost Creek]] yielded 55 brook trout (2.2–7.3 inches) in 300 feet, with about 40% of fish missed. Its planting policy was changed to brook trout because cutthroat could not be established.
- [[Tiny Creek]] yielded 14 brook and two cutthroat trout; growth was described as extremely good with excellent body condition.

**Habitat and management**

- [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] were of concern, and the report recommends protecting remaining populations in sanctuary streams.
- [[Stocking Recommendations]] were tabulated (Table 4). Recommendations include catchable rainbow in Fish Creek (800 planned for 1957) and Big Savery Creek, fingerling brook trout in beaver ponds on Stoney Creek and Eagle Creek areas, and channel catfish in the Little Snake River between Baggs and Dixon.
- [[Haggerty Creek]] copper readings were 8.64 ppm at the mine dump (22.15 ppm as CuSO4), 0.49 ppm just below the dump on September 9, and 0.05 ppm about four miles downstream. No stocking was recommended until the pollution was diverted or eliminated.
- [[Civilian Conservation Corps Stream Improvements]] built around 1933–1934 made up 25% of fish cover on Battle Creek, and a 1936 device was still providing trout cover.
- Several streams were recommended for no stocking because of adequate populations, inaccessibility, or closure to fishing (e.g., [[Fulton Creek]], [[Dirtyman Creek]] pond areas, [[Strawberry Creek]]).

**Overall conclusions**

- Native cutthroat populations had declined, largely because of introduced brook trout competition and changed physical, chemical, and biological conditions.
- Management should match planting to stream biology: stock where habitat and access support fish, protect sanctuary streams, and avoid stocking polluted or silt-choked lower reaches.
- Long-term fishery improvement depends on controlling watershed erosion, irrigation diversions, and pollution.
