# Evaluation of Habitat Conditions and Species Composition Above, In, and Below the Atomizer Falls Complex of the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 University of Arizona Master of Science thesis by William Patrick Mattes evaluates water chemistry, physical habitat, and fish distribution along the lower 21 km of the [[Little Colorado River]] in Arizona, from [[Blue Springs]] (river kilometer 11.40 to 21.06). Water chemistry and physical habitat change gradually downstream, and fish distribution correlates with those changes. The study links the absence of [[Humpback Chub]] from the upper reach to elevated carbon dioxide and calcium bicarbonate from Blue Springs during base flow, together with reduced turbidity and the lack of travertine dams and deep pools above the [[Atomizer Falls Complex]]. [[Speckled Dace]] occur farther upstream but are absent from Blue Springs itself. The author recommends carbon dioxide tolerance studies for humpback chub and other native fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*)**: a cyprinid adapted to swift water, with large falcate fins, a nuchal hump, an inferior mouth, and a flattened head. It is endemic to Grand Canyon canyon-bound reaches of the Colorado River system, and the largest known population occurs at the Little Colorado River confluence. Glen Canyon Dam is considered the main cause of its decline, having shifted the Colorado River from warm, fluctuating flows to cold, steady flows.
- **Speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*)**: the only native fish found above area 7 in this study.
- **Other species noted**: flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, common carp, channel catfish, rainbow trout, fathead minnow, plains killifish (historical and recent records cited).
- **Study design**: 15 study areas (table of river kilometers and descriptions) sampled July 1992 to July 1993 using mini-hoop nets, minnow traps, seines, underwater and bank observation, with water chemistry analyzed by ANOVA and Tukey HSD tests and habitat by Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests.
- **Study area**: about 69,807 km² basin; mean annual rainfall 20.25 cm; the river becomes perennial below Blue Springs, flowing about 5.94 m³/s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry gradient**: pH, CO₂, alkalinity, hardness, and turbidity change gradually from 11.40 km to 21.06 km. Blue Springs water is mineralized, carbon dioxide-rich, and warm compared with surface runoff.
- **Physical habitat**: depth, velocity, and substrate varied among areas. Areas 5 and 6 (below the falls) had slower velocities and smaller substrates, largely from calcium carbonate precipitate.
- **Seasonal regimes**: summer rain runoff (July and September 1992), spring runoff (April 1993), and base flow (June and July 1993) produced different monthly trends.
- **Physiological limits**: high CO₂ and bicarbonate can cause anesthesia and death in fishes by blocking gill gas exchange. Acclimation may allow some tolerance, and the author notes no CO₂ tolerance data exist for humpback chub.
- **Dynamic habitat**: 1993 floods deposited sand on banks, scoured pools, and increased average base-flow depth, indicating ongoing habitat change.

## Key Findings

- **Longitudinal gradient**: CO₂, hardness, and alkalinity decreased, while pH and turbidity increased, moving downstream from 21.05 km to 11.40 km. The sharpest changes occurred between 14.7 km (above Chute Falls) and 13.9 km (below Atomizer Falls), a distance of about 750 m.
- **Blue Springs water**: measured CO₂ of 571 mg/L and temperature of 22.6 °C. Downstream at 19 km, CO₂ was 205 mg/L (23.5 °C); at 21.06 km, 172 mg/L (22.6 °C).
- **Humpback chub distribution**: absent above Chute Falls (14.7 km); present in low densities between 14.3 and 14.5 km; more numerous below Atomizer Falls (13.9 km); abundant near Big Canyon/Mecca Falls area (11.4–12.8 km). Captured only in areas 1, 2, 6, and 7 in the sampling records.
- **Speckled dace distribution**: found in all areas except Blue Springs and about 1 km of main channel downstream. June 1993 observations recorded 241 juveniles, with 14 (6%) in the top third of the water column, 96 (36%) in the middle third, and 141 (58%) in the bottom third.
- **Habitat use**: Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests found no significant differences between habitat sampled and habitat used by speckled dace.
- **Turbidity and travertine**: humpback chub appeared most abundant where turbidity was high and travertine dams formed deep pools. The author considers these habitat factors insufficient alone to explain their absence, since chemistry at base flow is the stronger candidate.
- **Seasonal timing**: humpback chub move upstream in April before spawning, and fish arrive at the Atomizer Falls complex during June and July base flow, suggesting they may be blocked by the chemical barrier at base flow.
- **Flood limitation**: short summer floods may not give fish time to travel the roughly 7 km from the Atomizer Falls complex to Blue Springs. Historical records of chub and squawfish below Grand Falls before the 1940s suggest higher past discharge may have diluted Blue Springs long enough to allow passage.
- **Recommendation**: studies should determine CO₂ tolerance of humpback chub size classes under varied temperature, dissolved oxygen, and CO₂ conditions, and should extend to other native species of the lower 21 km.
