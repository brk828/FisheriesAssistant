## Executive Summary

This document is a Bureau of Reclamation (Reclamation) memorandum responding to a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) draft Biological Opinion regarding the transportation and delivery of Central Arizona Project (CAP) water to the [[Gila River Basin]]. The FWS draft opinion concluded that CAP water delivery would likely jeopardize four federally-listed fish species — including the [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], and [[Loach Minnow]] — primarily through the risk of nonnative fish transport via the CAP aqueduct system. Reclamation's rebuttal argues that this concern is disproportionate given the far larger, ongoing threats to native fish from state- and federally-sanctioned sport fishery stocking, aquaculture, grazing-related habitat degradation, and the aquarium/bait trade. The memo is a critical case study in the political and scientific tension between single-project ESA Section 7 consultations and cumulative, multi-agency threats to imperiled freshwater fish in the American Southwest.

## Background & Description

- The controversy originated during environmental compliance work for the **Pima Lateral Feeder Canal (PLFC)**, built to deliver 30,000 acre-feet of CAP replacement water to the [[Gila River Indian Community]], with a reciprocal agreement to maintain a minimum pool in [[San Carlos Reservoir]] to protect its sport fishery.
- This raised concern over nonnative fish transferring from the CAP canal system into the [[Gila River]] below **Ashurst-Hayden Diversion Dam**.
- Interim water deliveries were permitted contingent on construction of **electrical fish barriers** and a Reclamation-funded study (per an approved Scope of Work, SOW) examining long-term impacts of potential nonnative fish introductions.
- The SOW, reviewed and endorsed by FWS in April 1990 and January 1991 correspondence, focused on **five specific nonnative fish species** not yet present in the upper Gila River.
- The final draft Biological Opinion (May 30, 1991) abruptly shifted focus: it did not evaluate the five originally-scoped species as most concerning, and instead flagged **new species of concern** (guppies, sailfin mollies, golden shiners) and even species **not yet present** in the system (rudd, sheepshead minnow, tench). Critically, the [[Gila Topminnow]] — omitted from FWS's original March 1990 list of three protected species — was newly identified as jeopardized.
- New unaddressed pathways of concern included fish transfer to the **Salt River** headwaters (past an electrical barrier and four dams) and the **Verde River** headwaters (past two dams).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical introduction pattern**: A cited figure ("Introductions of the Warmwater Fishes of Arizona, 1850–1990," Arizona Game and Fish Department) documents decades of deliberate nonnative sport fish stocking in Arizona waters, including bass, catfish, sunfish, and crappie species — establishing a long precedent of anthropogenic exotic introductions predating CAP.
- **Nonnative species implicated**:
  - [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]] — cited in the draft Opinion itself as having "substantial adverse impacts" on native fish; over 1.27 million rainbow trout stocked in the New Mexico Gila Basin over 5 years, nearly half near proposed critical habitat for [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]].
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] stocked in Evans Lake near proposed critical habitat.
  - [[Striped Bass]] and [[Tilapia]] — aquaculture recently legalized by Arizona in areas downstream of CAP, anticipating their introduction via the CAP itself.
  - [[Red Shiner]] — already implicated in the decline of native minnows; permitted as bait fish on the Salt, Verde, and Gila Rivers.
  - [[Bighead Carp]] documented in Bartlett Lake; [[Tilapia]] documented in Saguaro Lake and Lake Pleasant despite regulatory controls.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] — documented in Horseshoe Lake (no barrier to upstream Spikedace critical habitat) and Lake Mojave (largest known Arizona population), yet FWS continues stocking rainbow trout there; draft Opinion itself states "razorback suckers do not survive well in the presence of nonnative fish populations."
- **Habitat degradation drivers**: Overgrazing of watersheds and riparian corridors on federal lands is repeatedly identified (by Reclamation and cited experts) as a compounding factor that both degrades native fish habitat and facilitates nonnative fish persistence/competitive advantage.
- **Barrier efficacy questioned**: Multiple experts (Dr. Minckley, Dr. James Johnson) are cited as skeptical that fish barriers do more than **postpone** (not prevent) upstream nonnative fish colonization.
- **Reproductive failure of native stock**: Dr. James Johnson (FWS Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit) reported that over 20 million native fish released from the **Dexter National Fish Hatchery** over 10 years failed to produce any known successful reproduction in the wild, attributed to habitat loss and nonnative fish presence.
- **Expert opinion**: Dr. W.L. Minckley (author of *Fishes of Arizona*) suggested aquaculture escapes may pose a greater threat to native fish than water-delivery-canal introductions, and reportedly recommended treating **all of Arizona as critical habitat** given the inevitability of future species introductions.

## Key Findings

- **Species named as jeopardized** in the FWS draft Biological Opinion: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and a fourth unspecified federally-listed species (implied [[Razorback Sucker]]) — four total federally-listed fish in the Gila River Basin.
- **Inconsistency in FWS guidance**: The species initially scoped for study (5 nonnative species, agreed upon in the 1990 SOW) were superseded late in the process by a different, expanded set of concern species, undermining Reclamation's ability to plan mitigation during earlier project phases.
- **Identified transfer pathways** for nonnative fish via CAP: canal-to-river transfer below Ashurst-Hayden Diversion Dam, farm sump escapes within the 500-year floodplain, transfer during infrequent flood events into normally dry tributaries, and bait-bucket transfer by anglers.
- **Comparative threat assessment**: Reclamation argues that ongoing, larger-scale nonnative fish stocking programs (sport fishery management by AGFD and NMGFD), aquaculture operations, and bait-fish trade — many of which have federal funding ties (e.g., Dingell-Johnson Act funds) or occur on federal lands — represent a substantially greater cumulative threat to listed species than the CAP transfer risk alone.
- **Regulatory gap identified**: Critical habitat for [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], proposed by FWS in 1985, had still not been finalized by 1991 despite a statutory deadline of June 18, 1987 under Section 4(b)(6)(C)(ii) of the Endangered Species Act — a procedural failure Reclamation argues is more consequential than the CAP nonnative fish transfer issue.
- **Policy conflict**: Reclamation highlights the contradiction of being required to build fish barriers and restrict fishing under the draft Opinion while FWS and state agencies concurrently promote sport fishing, permit nonnative baitfish use, and fund aquaculture expansion in the same watersheds.
- **Outcome/position**: Reclamation maintains, per its original Biological Assessment, that CAP water delivery is not likely to jeopardize the listed species, and calls for equivalent Section 7 scrutiny of other federally-tied nonnative fish stocking, grazing, timber, and aquaculture/aquarium trade activities across the Southwest.
