# Accumulation of Radioactive Fallout Materials in the Biota of Doe Run, Meade County, Kentucky, 1959–63

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 paper, reprinted from the proceedings of the Stockholm symposium *Radioecological Concentration Processes*, reports a multi-year study by L. A. Krumholz (University of Louisville) of how [[Nuclear Fallout]] accumulated through the biota of [[Doe Run]], a spring-fed limestone stream in Meade County, Kentucky. Data were gathered from May 1959 to October 1964. The key feature of the study is a pre-test baseline: radioactivity in the stream biota was near background before the Soviet nuclear test series began in September 1961. After that date, every component of the stream ecosystem showed a measurable rise in [[Gross Beta Radioactivity]] that persisted through the end of the study.

## Background & Description

The study was supported by the [[U.S. Atomic Energy Commission]] under a contract with the University of Louisville. Its original aim was to document the biota and physical-chemical setting of a stream far from known industrial or radioactive sources, and to test whether fallout accumulated seasonally and moved through the [[Food Web]].

Doe Run rises as a torrent spring near Ekron, Kentucky, and flows about 9.7 miles through Mississippian limestone to the [[Ohio River]] at Ohio River Mile 642.2, roughly 36 miles downstream of Louisville. Three old mill dams (at Miles 1.65, 2.36 and 2.82) had silted in. A fourth, earthen dam at Mile 7.6 was closed on 9 July 1961, creating [[Doe Valley Lake]]: about 4 miles long, roughly 500 acres, and backed up to about Mile 3.5 by spring 1962.

Key physical and chemical data:
- Source elevation 575 ft; mouth about 374 ft; average gradient 20.7 ft per mile, ranging from more than 35 ft per mile in the riffle section to about 7 ft per mile near the mouth.
- Discharge at the spring source ranged from less than 5 to more than 600 cubic feet per second (cfs), and was largely rainfall-driven. Flow at the culverts under State Highway 1638 probably exceeded 2000 cfs in March 1961.
- Source water temperature was consistently 13–14 °C.
- Dissolved oxygen was 75–80% of saturation at the source (8.0–8.5 mg/l), and usually at least 90% downstream.
- pH was circumneutral at the source and reached slightly above 8.3 downstream.
- Methyl purple alkalinity was about 150–250 mg/l along the whole stream.
- Turbidity tracked discharge, because high flows carried heavy suspended clay.

Five permanent stations (I–V) were set up to represent major stream community types:
- **Station I:** main spring source, rubble and sand.
- **Station II:** swift riffles and cataracts between Miles 1.8 and 2.1, with heavy [[Fissidens]] and *Myriophyllum* growth.
- **Station III:** Miles 3.0–3.3, marl deposition, heavily disturbed by visitors near a highway culvert.
- **Station IV:** Miles 4.7–4.9, flooded by the lake in July 1961.
- **Station V:** Mile 7.7, silty muck at the edge of the Ohio River backwater.

Early in 1961 the lower valley was cleared for impoundment. Later work concentrated on the upper 3.5 miles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling effort.** A total of 5,996 samples were assayed for gross beta radioactivity: 5,456 from Doe Run and 540 from sinkhole ponds. Doe Run station totals were 1,214 (I), 1,882 (II), 1,527 (III), 458 (IV) and 375 (V). Sinkhole samples came from a pond near the source (268), Stations A (51) and B (47) on the B. S. Jordan farm, and 174 from 92 other ponds. The Doe Run samples comprised 277 water samples, 9 bottom sediment samples, 56 marl samples, 3 detritus samples, and 5,111 samples of plants and animals.

**Biota.** The study covered:
- **Plants:** 31 kinds, represented by 1,275 samples. More than 120 plant kinds were recorded in the stream overall.
- **Invertebrates:** 34 kinds or groups, represented by 2,666 samples. Minckley (1963) recorded more than 120 invertebrate kinds.
- **Vertebrates:** 22 kinds (16 fishes, 3 frogs, 1 salamander, 1 turtle, 1 mammal), represented by 1,170 samples, with separate tissues analysed for some animals.
- **Fishes:** representatives of 13 families were collected, but only three species, the [[Common Sucker]], [[Blacknose Dace]] and [[Banded Sculpin]], maintained flourishing populations in the upper reaches. Minckley recorded 66 fish species in the wider area.

**Dominant plant communities.** For about two miles below the source the plants formed nearly monospecific stands: *Fissidens fontanus* (moss), *Vaucheria geminata* (alga), *Potamogeton foliosus*, and *Nitella flexilis*. Below Dam 1, *Fissidens* again dominated alongside *Myriophyllum heterophyllum*. Diatoms (46 species in 21 genera) were largely controlled by discharge.

**Key species relationships.** The isopod [[Asellus Bivittatus]] was tightly linked to *Fissidens*: as moss abundance fell, isopod numbers fell in parallel. Its main food was diatoms. Population size was governed mainly by discharge and moss abundance. The banded sculpin was the most abundant fish and the top carnivore of the system. Its diet at upstream sites was mainly crustaceans, and downstream it shifted toward insect larvae and fishes. The crayfish *Cambarus tenebrosus* was distributed throughout the stream, whereas *Orconectes rusticus rusticus* was restricted to the area below Dam 1 and seemed to need warmer water for reproduction.

**Sinkhole ponds.** Geiling and Krumholz (1963) sampled 93 ponds from November 1960 to December 2061 (sic, as printed: 1961). More than 140 planktonic kinds were recorded, dominated in spring, summer and autumn by *Peridinium cinctum* and *Ceratium hirundinella*. Rotifers were the most common zooplankton, with *Keratella cochlearis* in 119 of 125 collections. Ponds act as concentration basins because evaporation exceeds dilution during hot weather, whereas streams flush radionuclides away.

## Key Findings

**1. Fallout accumulation rose sharply after September 1961.**
- The pre-test data showed amounts near background. Only 27 of 115 Doe Run water samples taken before September 1961 exceeded 5 pCi/l.
- Within about three months of the first Soviet tests, there was a marked increase in all components of the stream community. By the end of 1961, very few samples did not contain considerably more radioactivity than before.
- The Discussion reports at least a twofold increase from the August 1961 low to the end of the study, and more than threefold for isopods, plecopterans, and all plants except watercress.

**2. Seasonal differences were not detectable.** Plants may have peaked in spring and early summer, but the authors found no clear seasonal variation in accumulation across organisms, and no reliable separation of seasonal means.

**3. Water exposure was uniform.** Water at Stations I, III and V had essentially the same radioactivity over the study period, so all organisms experienced similar exposure. Differences among organisms were attributed to their individual metabolism.

**4. Plants differed in accumulation.**
- *Fissidens* at Station II consistently accumulated more radioactivity than any other plant. In five samples (3.6 to 9.4 g), amounts exceeded 100 pCi/g wet weight, and two exceeded 120 pCi/g.
- Only *Myriophyllum* (two samples) and *Lemanea* (one sample) also exceeded 100 pCi/g.
- Watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*) never exceeded 20 pCi/g, whereas the partly terrestrial forget-me-not (*Myosotis scorpioides*) accumulated much more. The author called the watercress result unexplained.

**5. Animals accumulated less than the top plants.** Isopods, amphipods and plecopterans accumulated the most among animals, but no animal sample exceeded 50 pCi/g. Amphipods and plecopterans never reached 30 pCi/g. The snail [[Goniobasis]] accumulated little, probably because calcium from the limestone competed with radiostrontium, and most activity was in soft tissue rather than shell.

**6. Fishes and crayfishes accumulated little.** Both crayfish genera and the fishes sampled took up only small amounts, fewer than the herbivores. Crayfish diets were dominated by allochthonous riparian leaf detritus, and fish accumulated fallout mainly because their prey did not accumulate much.

**7. Accumulation declined up the food chain.** Fig. 15 shows that the amounts of radioactivity fell at each trophic step. The author identified seven trophic "levels": three in primary production (algae, bryophytes, tracheophytes) and four in consumers (primary consumers, omnivores, detritus feeders, and carnivores).

**8. Sinkhole ponds accumulated more than the stream.** Pond water usually held more than twice the radioactivity of stream water. 73 of 81 pond samples exceeded 5 pCi/l, compared with 27 of 115 stream samples before September 1961. The highest stream value was 69 pCi/l (late 1962 to early 1963). Two ponds exceeded 65 pCi/l in November and December 1961.

**9. Radiochemical analyses were limited.** 184 composite samples were analysed for ⁹⁰Sr and ¹³⁷Cs (59 from Station I, 78 from Station III, 47 from Station V). Seasonal differences could not be assessed with confidence. The author noted that much of the gross beta activity was traceable to these two radionuclides.

**10. Trophic and diversity patterns.** Biotic diversity peaked at Station II, about two miles downstream, then declined toward the mouth. Ten kinds of organisms were found at all five stations for much of the year: diatoms, *Vaucheria*, Cyanophyta, *Goniobasis*, amphipods, isopods, decapods, trichopterans, *Tipula* and *Cottus*.

**11. Study limitations.** Gross beta counts do not identify radionuclides and so cannot give dose. The author noted that the activity levels were very low, and that sample sizes for some small invertebrates were limited by the amount of material that could be collected without damaging the population.
