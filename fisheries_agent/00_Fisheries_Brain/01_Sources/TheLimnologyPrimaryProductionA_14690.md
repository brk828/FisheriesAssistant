## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by A. Sreenivasan investigates the limnology, primary production, and fish yield of **Ayyangulam Pond**, a small (1.4 ha), shallow, eutrophic temple pond in Tiruvannamalai, Madras State, South India. Unlike similar temple tanks studied by [[Ganapati 1940]], this pond lacked a permanent *Microcystis* bloom blanket, though blue-green algae (*Oscillatoria* and *Microcystis*) still dominated the [[Phytoplankton]] community. Primary production was measured via light-and-dark bottle methods and natural diel changes in oxygen and alkalinity, ranging from **6.0 g C/m²/day (December 1962)** to **11.0 g C/m²/day (June 1962)**. The pond supported a stocked fishery (milkfish, white carp, catla) with an average annual yield of **1,438 kg/ha** over 7 years — a notably high yield for unfertilized water. Fish production represented **1.65% of primary production**, exceeding the 0.98% conversion efficiency reported by [[McConnell 1963]] for a desert impoundment, and demonstrating strong potential for increased yield via plankton-feeder stocking.

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily a limnological/productivity study rather than a morphological one, it does report specific growth data for stocked species:

- **Milkfish (*[[Chanos chanos]]*)**: Average growth of **1–1.36 kg per annum**.
- **White carp (*[[Cirrhina cirrosa]]*)**: Growth rate described as "equally good" as milkfish.
- **Catla (*[[Catla catla]]*)**: Exceptional individual growth — up to **10.43 kg in 2.5 years**, indicating a very fast growth trajectory under the pond's trophic conditions.

No detailed anatomical/morphometric descriptions (fin counts, body proportions, etc.) are provided in the source text; emphasis is on somatic growth rate as a productivity indicator.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Structure:**
- Square-shaped, granite-walled temple pond used for bathing/washing; not stocked artificially with fertilizer, and free of fecal/sewage pollution (unlike other productive South Indian ponds).
- Max depth 7 m (full water level), average depth ~3.0 m; substantial annual water-level fluctuation (~3 m).
- Surrounded by high walls limiting wind-driven mixing → shallow [[Epilimnion]] / mixing depth (per [[Ruttner 1953]]).

**Thermal & Chemical Stratification:**
- Weak overall annual temperature variation (tropical water characteristic), but diel thermal stratification occurred in evenings during March–June, producing a thin warm surface layer.
- "Klinograde" oxygen curve: strong surface supersaturation not persisting below 1.0 m.
- Daytime reduction in methyl orange (bicarbonate) alkalinity paired with increased phenolphthalein alkalinity — attributed to **photosynthetically-driven carbonate precipitation**, a hallmark of this eutrophic biotope, contrasting with findings in [[Clear Lake]] (Goldman & Wetzel 1963) and [[Lake Erie]] (Verduin 1960).
- Hard water (264–314 ppm as CaCO₃); high conductivity (1,075–1,370 µmho); moderate calcium (43.6–52.1 mg/L); high silicate (50–66 mg/L SiO₂).
- Notably, **soluble inorganic phosphate and nitrate were never detected** — described as a recurring feature of Madras State ponds despite high eutrophic productivity.

**Trophic Structure:**
- Dominant plankton: blue-green algae, specifically *Oscillatoria* and *Microcystis*, present in all sampling periods; other genera included *Anabaenopsis*, *Aphanocapsa*, *Chlorella*, *Synedra*, and others depending on season.
- Primary producers support a fish assemblage dependent on plankton feeding — the paper explicitly recommends stocking additional **plankton-feeding fishes** to capture unused primary/secondary production.

**Biogeographic Context:** Freshwater tropical inland pond ecosystem in South India, functioning analogously to (but distinct from) fishpond systems studied in Israel ([[Hepher 1962]]) and Malaya ([[Fish 1960]]).

## Key Findings

- **Primary Production Range:** 6.0 g C/m²/day (Dec 1962, lowest) to 11.0 g C/m²/day (June 1962, highest); average of 9.0 g C/m²/day, equating to **45,950 kg C/year** across the 1.4 ha pond.
- **Seasonal Drivers of Productivity:** Lower December yields attributed to (1) cooler temperatures, (2) plankton deterioration, and (3) dilution from rain-driven higher water levels (supported by higher Secchi disc visibility and lower conductivity that period).
- **Methodological Finding:** Natural CO₂/O₂ change methods generally yielded **higher** apparent photosynthetic estimates than light-and-dark bottle methods — consistent with [[Verduin 1960]]'s results for Lake Erie.
- **Fish Yield:** Total annual pond fish production ranged **1,121–3,687 kg** (801–2,633 kg/ha) over 7 years (1956–57 to 1962–63), averaging **1,438 kg/ha/year** — a high yield for an unfertilized system.
- **Plankton-to-Fish Conversion Efficiency:**
  - Fish production = **0.104%** of total plankton biomass (193,000 kg/year, per [[Laevastu 1957]] conversion).
  - Fish production = **1.65%** of primary production — exceeding [[Odum 1960]]'s benchmark of 1–2% for "high yield" systems and surpassing the 0.98% conversion reported by McConnell (1963) for a desert impoundment.
- **Growth Performance:** Stocked species (milkfish, white carp, catla) exhibited strong growth rates, suggesting the pond's food base (dominated by blue-green algal plankton) was well exploited, with room for further yield increases via additional plankton-feeder stocking.
- **Chemical Anomaly:** Absence of detectable phosphate and nitrate despite high productivity — an unusual and unexplained regional pattern warranting further investigation into nutrient cycling in these systems (possible rapid uptake/cycling or non-standard nutrient sources).
- **Buffering Capacity:** High bicarbonate alkalinity buffered the pond against large pH swings despite intense photosynthetic activity — pH generally >8.2 except in December (as low as 7.6) and during CO₂ accumulation events in March 1963 (pH dropped to 7.3).
