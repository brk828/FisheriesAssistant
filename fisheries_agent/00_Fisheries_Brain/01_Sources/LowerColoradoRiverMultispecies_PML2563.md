# Imperial Ponds Conservation Area Restoration Development Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This plan, prepared in January 2008 for the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCR MSCP), describes the design, construction, planting, monitoring and management of the Imperial Ponds Conservation Area on [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] in Arizona. The LCR MSCP is a 50-year [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] covering at least 26 species from Lake Mead to the Mexican border, with [[Bureau of Reclamation]] responsible for implementation. The site was first built as four interconnected ponds (the DU2 Ponds) under a joint action by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], Ducks Unlimited and Reclamation. Those ponds were shallow, could not be isolated from one another without compromising water quality, and their surrounding soils could not be flushed of salts, so planted cottonwoods and willows survived poorly. A December 2004 expert group and a July 2005 conceptual design led to the current plan, which reconfigures the site into a mosaic of backwaters for native fish, cottonwood-willow forest, managed marsh and raised waterfowl fields.

## Background & Description

The plan's stated objectives are to (1) build a mosaic of land cover types required by LCR MSCP target species, (2) provide opportunities for species and restoration research, and (3) use adaptive management to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of habitat creation. The project area lies within the refuge's Intensive Management Area, which is closed to the public.

Land ownership and water rights are summarized as follows:

- **Refuge:** Established February 14, 1941, by Executive Order 8685 as a refuge and breeding ground for migratory birds and other wildlife. It encompasses 25,765 acres along the Lower Colorado River.
- **Water entitlement:** Granted by the 1964 Supreme Court Decree in *Arizona v. California* and by Secretarial reservation. Annual quantities are capped at 28,000 acre-feet diverted from the mainstream or 23,000 acre-feet of consumptive use, whichever is less, with a priority date of February 14, 1941. The refuge has agreed to make Colorado River water available for the ponds, cottonwood-willow irrigation and marsh.
- **Management:** Reclamation and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service are jointly responsible for long-term operation and maintenance under a Land Use Agreement. Imperial NWR handles law enforcement and public use regulation, and wildfire management includes rapid irrigation response of affected and adjacent fields.

Compliance is covered by the 1994 refuge Comprehensive Management Plan and Environmental Assessment, the LCR MSCP Programmatic EIS/EIR, the Final Biological Assessment and Habitat Conservation Plan, cultural resource inventories under Section 106 (no properties/no effect concurrence from the Arizona State Historic Preservation Office), and Clean Water Act Nationwide Permit 27. Herbicide, fertilizer and pesticide use must be carried out by licensed applicators under Arizona and Yuma County rules, using refuge-approved products, with records kept by the land manager.

The overall land cover targets are:

| Land cover | Target area | Primary focus |
|---|---|---|
| Backwaters | 80 acres | Native fish ([[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]]) |
| Cottonwood-willow forest | 34 acres | [[Yellow-Billed Cuckoo]] and other covered species |
| Managed marsh | 12 acres | [[California Black Rail]] |
| Raised fields | Up to 70 acres | Migratory waterfowl |

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The original site failed for two linked reasons. The ponds were too shallow and could not be isolated, so water quality could not be managed for fish in more than one pond. Salt-laden soils, with depth-to-water under 1 foot in the fields, prevented flushing, which produced poor cottonwood and willow survival. The new design addresses both problems by raising fields above the water table and building hydrologically separate ponds.

Native fish and the wider community are central to the design. Hummocks planted with California bulrush provide emergent cover, spawning gravel is placed in shallow areas, and riprap offers escape cover. The spatial pattern of emergent vegetation and open water is intended to support invertebrates that feed fish and insect predators of mosquito larvae. Marsh birds, including [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and [[Western Least Bittern]], are also targeted through hummock tops set within 12 inches of the water surface.

Threats to the fish community are managed through design. The inflow system uses a wedge-wire screen with 0.02-inch mesh to exclude non-native fish, and the drainage ditch is oversized to prevent backflow between ponds. Water renovations and refills are scheduled outside mid-April to late May, when [[Threadfin Shad]] eggs and larvae would otherwise be entrained by the screened intake.

## Key Findings

**Backwater (fish) development**

- Six isolated ponds totaling 80 acres were designed and constructed, ranging from about 9 to 21 acres each. The design target depth distribution is 20% less than 5 ft, 60% between 5 and 10 ft, and 20% at 10 to 12 ft or greater.
- Each pond has its own inlet and outlet. Fresh water is pumped from Martinez Lake at a total capacity of 6,000 gpm (1,000 gpm per pond) through a [[Wedge-Wire Screen]] system and a 24-inch pipeline.
- Each pond has a deep "fish kettle" of about 10 by 10 ft beside its outlet, to concentrate fish during salvage and to reduce the volume treated during any non-native fish eradication.
- Primary purpose: refugia for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] over the 50-year term. Secondary uses include fish production for the [[Fish Augmentation Program]] and species and habitat research.
- Initially, two ponds will be stocked, one with razorback sucker and one with bonytail, using fish from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Two ponds will receive no fish so water management can be pushed to extremes without risk to endangered fish.
- A fishery management plan will follow the initial 8 to 10 year research phase. The LCR MSCP Fishery Research Advisory team was being assembled in 2007.

**Backwater monitoring**

- Annual questions cover water quality, food availability, survival, condition, growth, spawning, recruitment, habitat use and non-native fish presence.
- Fish sampling is seasonal: larval light traps in spring, snorkel counts in summer to avoid handling stressed fish, and active netting and electrofishing in fall for population data. Fish are measured, weighed, sexed, scanned for PIT tags and condition-scored.
- Water quality is profiled with multiprobe equipment for temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH and conductivity. Summer samples cover major ions, chlorophyll a, total dissolved solids and contaminants (Hg, Se, As, ClO4). Zooplankton, phytoplankton and macroinvertebrates are also sampled on set schedules.
- Vegetation is mapped annually by percent cover using snorkel transects.

**Cottonwood-willow development**

- About 100 acres of fields were filled 3 to 4 ft with roughly 500,000 cubic yards of excavated pond material, which also improved farm drainage. Approximately 34 acres will become cottonwood-willow forest.
- The irrigation pump is being upgraded to deliver up to 20 cfs, with the delivery pipe increased from 18 to 24 inches. About 4,000 linear feet of concrete-lined canal will be built, and the 13 fields will be reconfigured into 17 smaller fields (five for cottonwood-willow) served by 10 turnout structures.
- Soil preparation of one to two years is planned, with flushing of salts. A salt-tolerant cover crop may be used to limit [[Saltcedar]] establishment.
- Planting is a random mix of Goodding's willow and Fremont cottonwood, with coyote willow nearest the turnouts because it needs the most water.
- Irrigation is initially weekly after planting, then every 10 to 14 days through September, then monthly in October and November.
- Structural management aims to create two distinct vegetation layers consistent with the [[Anderson-Ohmart Classification]], using pruning, thinning and selective harvest.

**Cottonwood-willow monitoring**

- Pre-monitoring point counts were conducted in June 2006.
- Planting survivorship and growth are tracked for the first 2 years. Previous projects showed most planted trees die in the first two years.
- Avian area searches each June follow the system-wide LCR protocol. [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and yellow-billed cuckoo surveys begin in Year 3 or when either species is detected.
- Fixed-radius vegetation plots (0.04 ha) are measured from Year 2 onward, annually for Years 3 to 6, every 2 years for Years 7 to 10, then every 5 years.
- Small mammal trapping for the [[Yuma Hispid Cotton Rat]] begins in Year 3 with a minimum of 500 trap nights per year.
- [[Anabat Bat Detectors]] record bat activity quarterly.
- Diversity indices (Renkonen similarity and Shannon-Weaver) will compare avian communities between years.

**Marsh development**

- Field 18 (about 12 acres) will be converted to a bulrush-dominated marsh to duplicate conditions in Fields 16 and 17, which are occupied by [[California Black Rail]]. Field 16 was selected as the model because it had the highest recorded black rail occurrence among Imperial NWR marsh units.
- The HCP specifies water depths of no more than 1 inch for black rail. Monitoring at Field 16 found surface water ranging from dry or moist ground to about 9 inches over each irrigation cycle.
- Field 18's design maintains 1 ft of graded relief across the field to create shallow standing water (up to 1 inch) and promote water movement.
- Field 18 is currently unmanaged, dominated by saltcedar and common reed, with water tables 12 to 18 inches below ground. Natural colonization is expected to take 1 to 1.5 years, supplemented by bulrush plantings.
- Irrigation will initially be three times per week, matching Fields 16 and 17. Adjustments will follow one year of intensive water data recorded at Field 16 at six locations.
- Marsh vegetation management emphasizes periodic prescribed burning, with the USGS and Imperial NWR developing final recommendations.

**Marsh monitoring**

- Marsh bird surveys follow the Standardized North American Marsh Bird Monitoring Protocol (Conway 2006). Each survey point gets a 10-minute playback of black rail, Yuma clapper rail, western least bittern and Virginia rail calls, twice a year in April and May.
- Vegetation is assessed annually by visual cover-class estimates, supplemented by aerial photos classified under the Anderson-Ohmart system.
- Piezometers with pressure transducers measure water elevation in Fields 16 and 18.
- Adaptive management triggers include poor marsh vegetation development and unsuitable water levels.

**Reporting**

- An annual report prepared by Reclamation will summarize project status, mitigation implementation, monitoring results, acres converted, and acreage meeting performance standards.
- A final report is due no later than 180 days after all mitigation measures are complete, anticipated in 2055.
