# Abstracts from the conference 'Geologic and biologic evidence for late Cenozoic drainage rearrangements in North America: Implications for aquatic biogeography I'

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-biogeography #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #paleoichthyology

## Executive Summary

This compilation of GSA symposium abstracts uses [[Fish Paleontology]] and [[Fish Biogeography]] as proxy evidence for reconstructing late Cenozoic drainage rearrangements across the western United States, driven by [[Basin And Range Extension]], the [[Yellowstone Hotspot]], and [[Rio Grande Rift]] tectonics. Fish distributions—together with mollusks, crayfish, unionid mussels, and DNA phylogeography—are used to infer former river connections, lake spillovers, and drainage captures that are otherwise difficult to detect from geology alone. Key fish taxa discussed include [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii*), [[Gila Chub]] (genus *Gila*), and suckers (*Catostomus* and *Chasmistes*), whose differing dispersal susceptibilities make them sensitive indicators of drainage connectivity at different spatial scales.

## Background & Description

The regional tectonic framework (Hamilton, Abstract 5801) describes profound Oligocene–Quaternary reorganization of western U.S. topography via subduction, San Andreas–style strike-slip transport, Basin-Range extension, and Neogene uplift of the Colorado Plateau and Rocky Mountains. This tectonic backdrop created and destroyed aquatic connections repeatedly, generating "biogeographic islands" for fish populations (Hamilton).

Smith (Abstract 50652) articulates the methodological basis for using fish paleontology to reconstruct drainage histories, proposing several operating principles:
1. Fishes rapidly colonize newly connected habitats (e.g., post-glacial dispersal).
2. Fishes lack time to evolve adaptations during Pleistocene climate shifts—they immigrate rather than adapt in place.
3. Shared species across adjacent drainages imply former aquatic connection.
4. Divergence of geographically isolated sister species implies a persistent barrier lasting 1–several million years.
5. Certain fish families/body plans are restricted by temperature, salinity, gradient, or elevation, making them useful paleoenvironmental indicators.
6. Fish eggs are not bird-dispersed—so distributions require direct aquatic connectivity.
7. Stable isotope analysis of fish bone enhances paleoenvironmental interpretation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish-based drainage reconstructions span several major western drainage systems:

- **Snake River / Columbia system**: The [[Salmon River]] captured the [[Snake River]] ~3 Ma; the Snake River was connected via Oregon to the [[Klamath River]] system and [[Pit River]] during the Miocene. The [[Snake River Plain]] once connected to Missouri River headwaters, with the Missouri tributary to Hudson Bay in the early Pliocene. Lower Columbia River fishes were isolated from marine access for much of the Pliocene (Smith, Abstract 50652).

- **Yellowstone Hotspot region**: Pierce & Morgan (Abstract 51131) link fish, mollusk, and geomorphic evidence to drainage shifts along the migrating [[Yellowstone Hotspot]] track, including a ~3–5 Ma connection between [[Lake Idaho]] and the [[Sacramento River]], later captured by the [[Hells Canyon]] drainage just after 3 Ma.

- **Bonneville/Virgin/Colorado system**: Upper Snake River tributaries were formerly linked through the pre-Bonneville Basin to the [[Virgin River]] and lower [[Colorado River]]. [[Death Valley]] and the [[Owens River]] connected to the lower Colorado River and Pacific in the Pliocene; the lower and upper Colorado were separate basins in the Miocene, with the Pliocene [[Bouse Embayment]] being marine (Smith, Abstract 50652; Dowling et al., Abstract 50823).

- **Bear River system**: Kaufman (Abstract 50552) documents Quaternary rerouting of the [[Bear River]] by lava flows and pluvial lake overflow (Lake Thatcher, Lake Bonneville), which had major biogeographic consequences—[[Bear Lake]] hosts several endemic fish species, and captured lake systems mixed fish/mollusk faunas from disparate sources.

- **Great Basin phylogeography**: Molecular phylogenetic analysis of DNA sequences (Dowling et al., Abstract 50823) supports historical connections between the [[Lahontan Basin]] and Snake River, and between the [[Bonneville Basin]], Virgin River, and pluvial White River systems, with both Pleistocene and pre-Pleistocene divergence events shaping ichthyofaunal diversity.

- **Sierra Nevada spillover event**: Adams et al. (Abstract 51959) describe a probable Middle Pleistocene spillover of pluvial [[Lake Beckwourth]] (Feather River/Pacific drainage) into the [[Lake Lahontan]] basin, potentially transferring fish between Sierra Nevada west-slope and Lahontan drainages.

- **NW Nevada / SE Oregon fish transfer dynamics**: Behnke & Curry (Abstract 52279) provide a comparative framework for interbasin fish transfer susceptibility (see Key Findings below), documented across the Coyote, Alvord, Summit Lake, Quinn River, and Lahontan basins.

- **New Mexico basins**: Mack (Abstract 8422) notes the absence of stratigraphic evidence for a Pliocene [[Gila River]] or [[Rio Mimbres]], despite extensive Rio Grande rift extension—relevant context for the timing of fish colonization in these systems.

- **Comparative non-fish biogeography**: Related aquatic and terrestrial taxa used as biogeographic proxies in the session include the snail genus *Tryonia* (Hershler et al., Abstract 50173), freshwater crayfish of the Central Highlands/Ozark Plateau (Crandall & Fetzner, Abstract 51022), unionid pearly-mussels of the southeastern U.S. (Lydeard, Abstract 50480), and tiger salamanders/painted turtles (Shaffer, Abstract 52248)—all reinforcing the broader theme of Cenozoic drainage-driven vicariance and dispersal.

## Key Findings

- **Differential susceptibility to interbasin transfer** (Behnke & Curry, Abstract 52279): Fish species vary systematically in how easily they cross drainage divides:
  - *Headwater specialists* (e.g., **cutthroat trout**, *Oncorhynchus clarkii*) are most susceptible to interbasin transfer via alluvial fan distributary shifts, neotectonic warping, and landslide-driven drainage rearrangement — because these are frequent, small-magnitude events.
  - *Small-to-medium minnows* (e.g., **Gila chub**, genus *Gila*) are transferred primarily by drainage capture events.
  - *Lowland species* (**suckers**, *Catostomus* and *Chasmistes*) require large-scale regional rearrangement of major rivers/lakes for transfer — a rarer, higher-magnitude event.
  - This inverse frequency–magnitude relationship explains why there are more isolated cutthroat trout populations than isolated sucker populations across the northwestern Great Basin.

- **Dated isolation events using cutthroat trout populations**: Genetic distance among five isolated cutthroat populations (Whitewater Creek/Coyote Basin, Virgin Creek/Alvord Basin, Mahogany Creek/Summit Lake Basin, upper Quinn River, and Pyramid Lake/Lahontan Basin) is roughly similar. The Summit Lake landslide (~7,000–16,000 years ago) caused isolation of Summit Lake cutthroats from the Lahontan Basin population — providing a calibration point for other divergence estimates.

- **Gila chub transfer between Alvord and Lahontan basins** likely occurred earlier in the Pleistocene than the cutthroat trout transfers, with Mahogany Creek hypothesized as the "unstable switch" point for this exchange.

- **Fish evidence contradicts hotspot-based paleoelevation models**: Smith (Abstract 50652) notes that fish records indicate *low* elevations in northern Utah and southern Idaho at 6 Ma — contradicting current Yellowstone Hot Spot models that predict high elevation for the region at that time. This is a direct case of fish biogeographic data constraining (and challenging) geophysical/tectonic hypotheses.

- **Fish fossils as lacustrine environment indicators**: In the Glacial Lake Dakota core record (Yansa & Murphy, Abstract 50910), abundant fish fossils co-occurring with plant macrofossils and laminated silty clays confirm a sustained lacustrine environment in an abandoned meltwater channel — illustrating the use of fish fossil abundance as a proxy for paleolimnological conditions independent of drainage-connectivity inference.

- **Integration of molecular and fossil data**: Dowling et al. (Abstract 50823) emphasize that combining phylogenetic (DNA sequence) divergence-time estimates with geological data yields a more complete reconstruction of both Pleistocene and pre-Pleistocene drivers of ichthyofaunal diversification in western North America — a methodological throughline connecting nearly all fish-related abstracts in this session.
