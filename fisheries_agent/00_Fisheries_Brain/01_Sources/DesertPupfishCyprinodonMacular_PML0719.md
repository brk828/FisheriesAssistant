# Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*): Proposed Endangered Listing and Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a proposed rule from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], published in the [[Federal Register]] in 1984. It proposes to determine the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) to be an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and to designate four areas as [[Critical Habitat]]. The species has been drastically reduced in numbers and distribution because of competition for food and space with exotic fishes, predation by exotic fish species, and habitat loss from dam construction, streambank erosion, stream channelization, groundwater pumping, water pollution, and the lining and dredging of irrigation drains. The Service requested data and comments from the public. Comments were due by July 16, 1984, and requests for a public hearing by July 1984 (the exact day is garbled in the scan).

## Background & Description

- **Morphology:** The desert pupfish is a small, laterally compressed fish. Adults rarely exceed 75 mm in total length.
- **Life history:** The species matures rapidly and spawns during the warm months. Eggs are usually laid in a flocculent substrate in shallow water. Breeding males become colored blue on the dorsal fin and sides, and they hold feeding and breeding territories. Individuals typically survive for about a year.
- **Physiological tolerance:** The species tolerates a wide range of temperature, salinity, and low dissolved oxygen. The document notes that this adaptability makes it a valuable research organism, and that studies of the species have contributed to knowledge of fish ecology, behavior, and physiology.
- **Historical range:** In the early 20th century, the species was widespread in the lower [[Gila River]] and its tributaries, the [[San Pedro River]] and [[Santa Cruz River]], the [[Colorado River]] and its delta backwaters, and the [[Salton Sea]] area. It also occurred in springs, marshes, and shallow waters in Arizona, California, Sonora, and Baja California.
- **Taxonomic and scientific interest:** Earlier studies of the genus are cited, including work on tolerance to low oxygen (Carlson and others) and on temperature and salinity effects on growth and food conversion in a euryplastic fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Decline and habitat change.** Beginning in the 1980s, many desert rivers experienced cycles of severe erosion. These cycles removed permanent water from numerous pupfish streams and dried shallow littoral areas that the species preferred. Miller (1961) linked the increase in erosion to changes in land and water use. Dams, diversions, and channelization dewatered the lower Gila River and other Arizona drainages, and the species was forced into mainstream channels of the Colorado River, where it was outcompeted by native and exotic fishes.

**Specific threats cited:**
- Competition for food and space with exotic fishes.
- Predation by exotic fish species.
- Habitat loss from dam construction, streambank erosion, and stream channelization.
- Groundwater pumping, which affects the aquifers that feed springs and streams.
- Water pollution, including agricultural pesticides.
- Lining and dredging of irrigation drains and canals.
- Geothermal exploration, identified as a potential threat in areas near the species' habitat.
- Salinity changes, since the species tolerates salinity but can be displaced by exotic competitors when conditions shift.

**Status history.** The species was included in the Service's 1982 Review of Vertebrate Wildlife for listing as an endangered or threatened species. Notices of review in 1983 indicated that populations had been reduced to low levels in most habitats, to the point where long-term survival was in doubt. The document states that the species is currently under consideration for listing, and that the Service has substantial data on its status.

**Critical habitat areas.** The proposal identifies four areas, each defined by a stream segment or spring with a buffer zone:

1. **[[Quitobaquito Spring]]**, Pima County, Arizona. This spring is about 1/2 acre and lies within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. A riparian buffer zone surrounds the spring.
2. **[[San Felipe Creek]]**, Imperial County, California. The segment runs from the State Highway 86 bridge crossing upstream to the eastern boundary of Section 31, T.12 S., R.10 E. The stream length is partly garbled in the scan; the text describes it as roughly one-half mile of channel, with a buffer of about 100 feet on either side.
3. **[[Carrizo Wash]]**, Imperial County, California. The segment runs from the confluence of Carrizo Wash with San Felipe Creek upstream to the southern boundary of a section in T.12 S., R.10 E. The text gives a stream length of roughly 19 miles, but the figure is uncertain due to scan errors.
4. **[[Fish Creek Wash]]**, Imperial County, California. The segment runs from the confluence of Fish Creek Wash with San Felipe Creek upstream, with a stated length of approximately three-fourths of a stream mile.

**Constituent elements of critical habitat.** The proposed elements are clean, unpolluted water free of exotic organisms and exotic fishes, and small, slow-moving desert and spring pools with marshy track areas.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed action:** The Service proposes to list the desert pupfish as an endangered species and to designate critical habitat, which would apply to the four areas described above.
- **Primary causes of decline:** Exotic fish competition and predation, habitat destruction from dams and channelization, groundwater depletion, pollution, and irrigation drain modifications.
- **Regulatory amendments:** The rule proposes adding the desert pupfish to the list of endangered and threatened fishes in 50 CFR 17.11(h) and adding its critical habitat in 50 CFR 17.95(e). Under the proposed rule, taking, possessing, selling, or delivering the species would be unlawful, and some federal actions that could affect critical habitat would require consultation.
- **Authorship and contacts:** The rule was prepared by the Sacramento Endangered Species Office, with primary authors Kathleen E. Franzreb and M. Lorentzen. Public comments were directed to the Regional Director of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in Portland, Oregon, with contact by Sanford R. Wilbur, Endangered Species Coordinator.
- **Research value:** The species' tolerance of extreme conditions and its ecological sensitivity make it a useful model for studying fish ecology, behavior, and physiology.
