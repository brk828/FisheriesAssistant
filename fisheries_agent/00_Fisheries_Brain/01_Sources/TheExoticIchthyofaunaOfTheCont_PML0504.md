# The Exotic Ichthyofauna of the Contiguous United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #exotic-fishes

## Executive Summary

This review, written from the Department of Biological Sciences at Florida Atlantic University in Boca Raton, catalogues exotic (non-native) fishes established in open waters of the contiguous United States. It records **39 established exotic species**, with most introduced after the Second World War, and adds **57 non-established** exotic fishes. The author also gives a preliminary estimate that **at least 168 native fishes** have been transplanted beyond their natural ranges, mostly as game fish and bait. The paper concludes that introductions are poorly studied, often driven by goal-oriented management, and frequently harmful to native fauna, and that manipulating open waters this way is "unwarranted, needless and costly."

## Background & Description

The paper is organized by introduction purpose: sport and food fishes, [[Biological Control]] agents, [[Aquaculture]] species, [[Forage Fish]], [[Aquarium Fish Trade|Aquarium Fishes]], and fishes from other sources. It ends with preliminary observations on international and intranational transplants of native species.

Established exotic species are grouped by family in the abstract: about 13 cichlids, 7 cyprinids, 7 poeciliids, 3 loricariids, 2 gobiids, 2 sciaenids, and one species each of Anabantidae, Clariidae, Cobitidae, Osmeridae and Salmonidae. The main established families and representative species are:

- **Salmonidae**: [[Brown Trout]], the only introduction generally accepted as having beneficial, self-sustaining populations.
- **Cyprinidae**: [[Common Carp]] (imported 1870s), [[Tench]], [[Goldfish]] (first exotic fish established in North America, introduced in the late 1600s), and [[Grass Carp]] used for vegetation control.
- **Cichlidae**: [[Blue Tilapia]], [[Mozambique Tilapia]], [[Redbelly Tilapia]], [[Oscar]], and several aquarium cichlids.
- **Sciaenidae**: [[Bairdiella]] and [[Orangemouth Corvina]], both marine species from the Pacific coast of Mexico introduced to the [[Salton Sea]] in the early 1950s.
- **Clariidae**: [[Walking Catfish]], which spread across about 20 counties in peninsular Florida.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salton Sea.** The Salton Sea formed in 1905–07 when the lower Colorado River was diverted into the Salton Sink. The lake became progressively more saline; the document reports salinity reaching 35 (per mille) by the 1960s-era survey it cites, extirpating freshwater species. Marine species introduced later, and [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (or a hybrid) invaded after 1976 and became the dominant fish there.

**Tilapia and native fish declines.** Where tilapias established in warm climates, population explosions were accompanied by moderate to dramatic declines of native fishes, including transplanted natives. When blue tilapia populations reached 1842 kg/ha in one Texas reservoir, most native fishes ceased reproducing.

**Desert and thermal spring systems.** Hobbyist releases into thermal springs in northwestern states and desert ecosystems of the American Southwest have had adverse effects on endemic, often endangered native fishes. Examples include [[Pike Killifish]] (established south of Miami since November 1957), [[Guppy]] (established in thermal springs in several western states), and [[Convict Cichlid]] in southern Nevada thermal springs.

**Habitat disturbance.** Most successful aquarium-fish establishments occurred in disturbed habitats and sub-tropical climates; southern Florida, where 80 percent of U.S. aquarium fishes are cultured, was a major source.

## Key Findings

- **Established exotics:** 39 species established in open waters of the contiguous U.S.; all but nine introduced since the Second World War.
- **Non-established exotics:** 57 further species recorded, of which 14 were formerly established and 9 were intentionally released but failed to establish.
- **Status of established species (as reported in the French abstract):** 15 expanding, 14 with localized distributions, 3 with fairly wide, stable ranges, 4 apparently declining, and 3 with insufficient data.
- **Native transplants:** a preliminary review found at least **168 native fishes** moved beyond natural ranges.
- **Reasons for transplants:** sport fishing dominates. An estimated 51 species were probably moved as game fishes and 58 probably released from bait buckets. Eleven were moved for species survival, and two to expand ranges.
- **Comparison:** transplants of native fishes, planned or otherwise, "far outnumber" introductions of exotic fishes in the U.S.
- **Mixed-species bait and seining:** incidental non-target fishes, including juvenile centrarchids, were often moved unrecorded, so some small centrarchids listed as game introductions may actually be bait releases.
- **Biological control:** grass carp was the subject of the few early impact studies, begun in the mid-to-late 1970s. Several biological control fishes, including tilapias and grass carp, were released without prior testing.
- **Aquarium releases:** no introduced aquarium fish has been shown to have beneficial value in U.S. open waters. Releases are "needless but to be expected."
- **Forage introductions:** only one exotic forage fish, [[Wakesagi]], was introduced, released into six California reservoirs for trout forage. Its current status is unknown.
- **Management critique:** introductions have long been accepted "mostly without question" as "potential cure-alls." Fishes are introduced to fill "vacant niches," which can cascade. One case documented by Allen and Roden (1978) involved two predatory game fishes introduced to reduce a forage base; one predator eliminated the other, and then the population collapsed.
- **Conclusion:** the author states that an introduction is an introduction, whether intranational, intracontinental or foreign, and that impacts "ranging from negligible to major are to be expected." Manipulating open waters in such ways is unwarranted, and the paper calls for stronger ecological grounding in fishery management.
