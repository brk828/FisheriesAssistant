# Sampling at ASU Research Park, May 1990

## Executive Summary
This memo from Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) reports results of a fish community assessment conducted 15–16 May 1990 in three artificial lakes (A, B, C) at the ASU Research Park in Tempe, Arizona. The study is notable for tracking populations of two endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes — [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] — that have been stocked or are potentially reproducing in an urban lake system. Trammel and experimental gill nets captured 624 fish across 13 taxa, dominated numerically by non-native [[Threadfin Shad]]. Key findings include evidence suggestive of possible **in-lake natural reproduction of Razorback Sucker**, continued survival and apparent growth of stocked Bonytail, and the persistent failure to capture the elusive [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (Colorado Squawfish) despite angler reports of its presence.

## Background & Description
- **Bonytail (Gila elegans)** (n=5, captured only in Lake B via trammel net):
  - Mean total length (TL): 36.2 ± 2.2 cm (range 33.4–39.5 cm)
  - Mean weight: 349 ± 87 g (range 238–466 g)
  - Sex ratio: 3 males (all ripe/reproductively mature), 2 females
  - Growth comparison: April 1989 cohort averaged 33.2 cm TL (n=8), max 34.6 cm — indicating measurable growth over ~1 year.

- **Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus)** — two statistically distinct size classes captured, with **no overlap** in TL or weight:
  - **"Small" group** (n=11): TL 27.2 ± 3.8 cm (range 21.5–37.0 cm); weight 246 ± 130 g (range 88–600 g)
  - **"Large" group** (n=7): TL 48.5 ± 2.5 cm (range 44.5–51.0 cm); weight 1233 ± 247 g (range 880–1600 g)
  - Note: fish at the upper size boundary of the "small" group (~37.0 cm) had not been recorded in samples since September 1987, suggesting a temporal gap consistent with a distinct recruitment cohort.

- **Scale/Age Analysis** (from 4 deceased "small" Razorback Sucker retained for ageing):
  - Three specimens (21.0, 23.7, 25.7 cm TL): showed a clear inner zone of closely-spaced circuli and an outer zone of more distantly-spaced circuli — possible single annulus.
  - Fourth specimen (26.0 cm TL): exhibited two distinct bands, suggesting possibly two annuli / two cohorts.
  - Otoliths and vertebrae were extracted for cross-validation of age estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Three interconnected artificial lakes at an urban research park, part of a long-term monitoring program (since 1986) for reintroduced native Sonoran Desert fishes alongside a diverse non-native assemblage.

- **Lake-specific dominance patterns** (trammel net catch):
  - Lake A: Razorback Sucker dominant (7 individuals, 58% of trammel catch)
  - Lake B: Largemouth Bass (39%) and Bluegill (28%) dominant
  - Lake C: Bluegill (44%) and Largemouth Bass (43%) dominant
- **Gear/Effort**: Trammel nets (6 sets, total effort 669 m²) deployed in all lakes; experimental gill nets (2 sets, 140 m² effort) deployed only in Lakes A and C, capturing all Threadfin Shad exclusively.
- **Human interaction**: An angler reported catching 56 Bonytail via hook-and-line in 2 hours (all released), unaware of the species' native/endangered status despite proximity to interpretive educational signage — highlighting a public outreach gap regarding [[Native Fish Conservation]] at the site.
- **Colorado Pikeminnow (Squawfish)**: Not captured since March 1988 despite reliable angler reports of continued catches, suggesting low mobility/activity levels that render the species refractory to passive net sampling.

## Key Findings
1. **Possible in-lake reproduction of Razorback Sucker**: The discovery of two non-overlapping size cohorts, absent evidence of unauthorized restocking (only one authorized stocking event occurred in 1986), and inconsistency with known hatchery growth-variation patterns (per W.L. Minckley, Dexter National Fish Hatchery) together support the hypothesis that the "small" cohort represents **natural recruitment** at ASU Research Park, likely spawned in winter-spring 1989 or autumn 1988.
2. **Sampling gaps may explain missed larvae**: 1989 larval sampling began in mid-February, potentially missing an autumn/early-winter spawning event; by the time sampling started, young fish may have outgrown the phototactic larval stage targeted by sampling gear.
3. **Small Razorback Sucker absent from extensive seining**: None were found among >2,000 fish seined in autumn 1989, suggesting they occupy deeper water or unsampled microhabitats (e.g., macrophyte beds) rather than being genuinely absent.
4. **Growth evidence for stocked Bonytail**: Comparison of 1989 vs. 1990 length data (33.2 cm → 36.2 cm mean TL) is consistent with survival and growth of a single stocked cohort over one year.
5. **Aging methodology initiated**: Scale, otolith, and vertebrae samples were collected from Razorback Sucker to validate age/cohort structure, with plans for continued scale sampling from future captures.
6. **Community imbalance**: Non-native [[Threadfin Shad]] overwhelmingly dominates lake biomass/numbers (80% of total catch), with native Sonoran species restricted to a small fraction of total catch, underscoring the challenges of maintaining native species presence in a mixed-community reservoir system.
7. **Future monitoring plans**: Seining planned for summer 1990, additional netting in autumn 1990, and earlier larval sampling (starting ~October) to better detect autumn-winter spawning; if in-lake reproduction of Razorback Sucker is confirmed, a dedicated recruitment dynamics study will be developed.
8. **Public education deficit**: Documented case of an angler unknowingly and repeatedly catching an endangered species despite on-site interpretive signage, indicating need for improved [[Angler Outreach]] efforts at the Park.
