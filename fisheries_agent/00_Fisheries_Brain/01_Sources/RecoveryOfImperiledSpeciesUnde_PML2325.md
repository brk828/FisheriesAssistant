# Recovery of Imperiled Species Under the Endangered Species Act: A Case for Recovery Management Agreements

#conservation-biology #endangered-species-act #species-recovery

## Executive Summary

This concepts-and-questions paper argues that the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) needs a more nuanced definition of "recovery." The authors contend that many listed species will not be fully independent of management even after delisting. Human pressures such as habitat loss, disruption of natural disturbance regimes and [[Invasive Species]] are expected to grow, so the number of species that depend on continuing intervention will likely increase. The authors propose viewing recovery as a continuum rather than a "recovered/not recovered" dichotomy, defining "[[Conservation-Reliant Species]]", and formalizing long-term commitments through [[Recovery Management Agreements]] (RMAs). The paper frames these ideas as a way to secure continuing management after delisting, with shared responsibility among federal, state, local, tribal and private partners.

## Background & Description

- **Scope.** The paper is a policy and conceptual analysis, not a field study. It draws on US delisting records, recovery plans and ESA text. The species discussed are mostly terrestrial birds, mammals and plants. Aquatic taxa appear only as examples, notably [[Chinook Salmon]] and hatchery-reared salmon.
- **Legal framework.** The ESA's stated goal is to recover listed species to the point where Act protection is no longer necessary (ESA secs 2(b), 3(3)). Under the current linear process, a species is listed, a [[Recovery Plan]] is prepared by the responsible agency ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] or [[NOAA Fisheries]]), and protection continues in the interim through take prohibitions (sec 9), consultation requirements (sec 7) and federal funding to states (sec 6(d)).
- **Protection gaps.** Few species have targeted legal protection beyond the ESA. Statutes such as the [[Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act]] and the [[Marine Mammal Protection Act]] are narrower. General laws such as the [[Clean Water Act]], state wetland laws and local zoning protect habitat only incidentally and are unlikely to be sufficient for most listed species.
- **Threats.** The most common threats to imperiled species in the US are habitat degradation and invasive species (Wilcove et al. 1998). The authors note that human alteration of species and habitats has accelerated for millennia, and that globalized economies and a six-fold increase in human population over the past 150 years have changed the temporal and geographic scale of these effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Listing trends and recovery status**

- Since the ESA took effect in 1973, the number of listed endangered and threatened species has risen steadily (Figure 1, USFWS data as of 31 October 2001).
- The text states that of 1260 listed species in the US and its possessions, 13 have been delisted ("recovered") and 22 have been downlisted from endangered to threatened. Web Table 2 is cited for a total of 27 species reclassified to threatened. The paper does not reconcile the 22 and 27 figures.
- The authors argue that it is unrealistic to expect many more recoveries after about 30 years of the Act, given the complexity of threats, limited recovery funding and the decades or centuries many species took to reach acute vulnerability.

**Inconsistent recovery goals**

- The USFWS grizzly bear recovery goal for the conterminous 48 states would leave the species occupying less than 5% of its pre-Columbian range.
- Bald eagle recovery plans, by contrast, call for a range approximating its pre-Columbian extent.

**Examples from delisting rules (Panel 1)**

- The [[Aleutian Canada Goose]] was listed because of fox introductions to its nesting islands. Removing the foxes eliminated the threat. Continuing management relies on existing instruments such as the [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]], flyway councils and state waterfowl regulations.
- [[Robbins' Cinquefoil]] was threatened by collecting and trampling. Delisting in 2002 depended on a management agreement requiring fencing and on-site personnel to prevent collection.
- The authors identify common features of species delisted under agreements: threats are known and treatable, threats are pervasive and recurrent (for example, predators, non-native species or parasites), management actions are identified and can be implemented, and government or private partners can carry out the work long term.

## Key Findings

**1. Recovery is better understood as a continuum.** Recovery exists at several levels of human intervention (Figure 2):

- **Captive-only persistence:** The Guam Micronesian kingfisher, the Hawaiian crow and ten Hawaiian plant species exist only in captivity in the US and its possessions. The authors argue these cannot count as recovered because they are inconsistent with the ESA's purpose of conserving ecosystems.
- **Captive-bred releases:** The [[California Condor]] and the Attwater's prairie chicken persist only through releases of captive-bred birds.
- **Ongoing habitat management:** [[Kirtland's Warbler]] needs prescribed burns to maintain jack pine habitat. [[Least Bell's Vireo]] requires control of brown-headed cowbird parasitism. The [[Least Tern]] and Hawaiian land birds need protection from human disturbance and predators.
- **Persistence under existing regulation:** The [[Peregrine Falcon]] and [[Gray Wolf]] can maintain populations under existing non-ESA mechanisms once threats are removed.

**2. Conservation-reliant species need continuing management.** The authors define these as species that can maintain a self-sustaining wild population only if effective ongoing management continues. Examples include invasive species control, prescribed fire, grazing or public-access restrictions, predator control, scheduled dam water releases and limited translocations for genetic diversity. Species that depend on captive-bred releases, or on manipulating large fractions of a population rather than its habitat, are excluded. Examples of the latter include barging salmon smolts past dams to overcome migration barriers (Figure 7). The authors state that a population sustained only by captive propagation, removal from the wild or artificial migration is inconsistent with the ESA.

**3. Conservation-reliant species deserve a new reclassification.** The authors propose that threatened species that are stable only under management could be recognized as a distinct intermediate category. Their statutory restrictions could then be replaced by more flexible regulations, provided the needed management is supplied.

**4. Recovery Management Agreements should be created.** The authors propose RMAs, enforceable agreements that guarantee continuing management after delisting. Key elements include:

- **Biological standards** based on known and treatable threats, and management actions that address the threats.
- **Legal requirements**, formalized as an enforceable contract between the federal wildlife agency and a managing entity with the authority and financial resources for the foreseeable future. Candidates include federal land agencies, state, tribal, county or municipal governments, or non-governmental organizations.
- **Transfer of management authority** from the federal agency to the conservation manager, which can help integrate recovery with land-use planning.
- **Early operation.** The RMA should be operational well before a species is downlisted or delisted, so that managers can confirm the actions are mitigating threats.
- **Monitoring and adaptive management**, with agreements that can be revised as threats change.
- **Funding and accountability** to ensure obligations are met.

**5. Land-use change poses a challenge.** Changes such as hedgerow removal, shifts in conservation programs or timber prices can alter the conditions on which management depends. The authors argue that continuing management must be part of the recovery strategy because many at-risk species face irreversible or ongoing human alterations.

**6. Cost and feasibility remain open questions.** The text includes a per-year cost estimate for recovery, but the figure is garbled in the scanned copy and cannot be reliably reported here. The authors acknowledge that there is a developing consensus that additional efforts are needed to improve the outcomes of recovery. They argue that the cost of RMAs is justified because it sustains recovery gains that would otherwise be lost.

**7. Open questions the authors pose.** They ask whether a species that depends on continuing management can be classed as recovered, and whether "recovered" should be reserved for species that can survive unaided. They also ask whether a reasonable certainty of continued intervention is sufficient for delisting.

**8. Acknowledgments and context.** The concepts emerged from a conference in November 2004 that considered the ESA at 30 years. The paper notes that recovery of the Puerto Rican parrot after Hurricane Hugo shows how natural disasters and human activity can change a population's trajectory over time.
