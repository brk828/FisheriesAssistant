# Growth and Movement of Smallmouth Buffalo, Ictiobus bubalus (Rafinesque), in Watts Bar Reservoir, Tennessee

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #freshwater-ecology #population-dynamics #radionuclide-ecology

## Executive Summary

This doctoral thesis investigates the population characteristics of [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) in [[Watts Bar Reservoir]] and the [[Clinch River]], Tennessee, focusing on age distribution, growth rates, dispersion, and radionuclide accumulation. The study analyzed 1,271 commercially-caught fish collected June–September 1962, plus 655 fish from 1960–1961 tagging operations. Scale impressions provided age and growth data; autoradiographic analysis of scales revealed movement patterns between contaminated and non-contaminated areas. The research was conducted as part of the [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]] (ORNL) [[Clinch River Study]], examining ecological impacts of low-level radioactive waste releases from ORNL into [[White Oak Creek]]. Key findings include: year class 6 dominated the commercial catch (the youngest fully vulnerable class); annulus formation occurred prior to June; survival rates ranged 19–49% for year classes 6–9; absolute growth averaged 422 mm at age 3, increasing to 609 mm at age 9; and only 0.08% of Watts Bar fish showed radionuclide accumulation in scales, versus 6% in the Clinch River and 77% in White Oak Creek. A novel autoradiographic technique successfully traced individual fish movements through contaminated zones, proving superior to conventional tagging for life-history reconstruction.

## Background & Description

The [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus* Rafinesque) is a member of the [[Sucker Family]] ([[Catostomidae]]), widely distributed from Lake Erie south to Mexico, common in the Mississippi, Missouri, Ohio, and Tennessee river systems. Adults reach sizes exceeding 30 inches and 25 pounds, though commercial catches average near 3 pounds. The species is a bottom-feeder preferring muddy or silty substrates, consuming aquatic insects, mollusks, small invertebrates, algae, and occasionally plant seeds. Spawning occurs in early spring in sloughs and shallow weedy areas; females (8–10 pounds) produce 300,000–400,000 eggs scattered on the bottom without parental care. High egg and larval mortality is offset by large population densities; yields up to 700 pounds per acre have been reported from Missouri lakes.

[[Watts Bar Reservoir]] on the main Tennessee River (formed by Watts Bar Dam at TRM 529.9) contains 38,600 acres at full pool with 783 miles of shoreline. [[White Oak Creek]], the major source of radioactive contamination, enters the [[Clinch River]] at CRM 20.8 (20.8 river miles upstream from the Clinch-Tennessee confluence at TRM 567.7). White Oak Creek water is diluted an average of 450 times at entry to the Clinch River; Clinch River water is diluted 5.6 times entering the Tennessee River. The study was part of ongoing ORNL investigations into radionuclide distribution in aquatic ecosystems receiving low-level radioactive waste.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Watts Bar Reservoir]] smallmouth buffalo population inhabits a system influenced by commercial fishing pressure, impoundment effects, and low-level radioactive contamination. Commercial harvest increased dramatically from 15,687 pounds (1958) to 161,303 pounds (1962), representing 272% of the 1961 catch. This fishing pressure likely reduced population density and improved food availability, evidenced by increasing growth rates in successive year classes. The species competes directly with [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) for food and habitat, though both remain abundant.

Radionuclide contamination originates from ORNL operations releasing waste into [[White Oak Creek]]. Major radionuclides include strontium-90, cesium-137, cobalt-60, and ruthenium-106. Fish scales and bones contained detectable ruthenium-106, cesium-137, cobalt-60, zirconium, and zinc isotopes. Calcium was the most abundant element in scales (0.142 mg/g fresh weight, 308,000 ppm ash content), with 23+ other elements present. The stable strontium-calcium ratio was 0.394 × 10⁻³. Mineral residue comprised 46.05% of scale weight.

The contaminated zone is effectively limited to White Oak Creek (≈5 acres surface area, <0.02% of Watts Bar's 38,660 acres). Only fish residing in this small area accumulated sufficient radionuclides for detection. Radiometric surveys showed 0.08% of Watts Bar fish, 6% of Clinch River fish, and 77% of White Oak Creek fish had measurable artificial radionuclide accumulations, emphasizing the importance of proximity to contamination source.

## Key Findings

**Age Distribution and Survival:**
- Watts Bar smallmouth buffalo in commercial catches ranged from age 4 to 15 years
- Year class 6 was modal in the catch (June–August 1962); year class 5 became most numerous in September as fish grew into vulnerable size (≥400 mm)
- Recruitment complete at age 6; commercial fishing pressure equal on all fish ≥ year class 6
- Survival rates: 49% (year class 6→7), 35% (7→8), 26% (8→9), 19% (9→10)
- No evidence of dominant year classes or cyclic abundance patterns
- Annulus formation occurs prior to June
- Population corresponds to theoretical stable age distribution with declining numbers in successive year classes due to mortality

**Growth Rates:**
- Length-weight relationship: W = 0.9976 L³ (isometric growth; 100 g/cm increase for fish >31 cm)
- Absolute growth (average total length): 422 mm at age 3, 441 mm at age 4, 453 mm at age 5, 465 mm at age 6, 487 mm at age 7, 522 mm at age 8, 609 mm at age 9
- Largest annual growth increment during second year of life (characteristic of species, possibly due to food habit changes)
- Calculated total length at end of first year increased steadily from 98 mm (year class 9) to 134 mm (year class 4), indicating improving habitat conditions 1954–1958
- Growth compensation evident during fourth and fifth years: fish with slow early growth accelerated, while fast-growing fish slowed
- Watts Bar fish larger at ages 3–5 than Grand Lake, Oklahoma fish; smaller than Wister Reservoir, Oklahoma and Reelfoot Lake, Tennessee fish at most ages
- Relative growth highest in second year (125% average), declining steadily thereafter
- No evidence of Lee's phenomenon (underestimation of early growth in older fish)

**Radionuclide Accumulation:**
- Smallmouth buffalo a relatively minor accumulator population-wide
- Scale mineral composition: 46.05% mineral residue; calcium dominant (308,000 ppm ash); 23+ elements detected including Na, K, Mn, Zr, Fe, Al, Pb, Si, Co, Cr, Sn, Zn, Mo, Ni, Rb, Sr, Ti, V, B, Cu, Li, Ag
- Radionuclides detected in scales/bone: ¹⁰⁶Ru, ¹³⁷Cs, ⁶⁰Co, ⁹⁰Sr (assumed present but not analyzed), ⁹⁵Zr, ⁶⁵Zn
- Radiometric surveys: 0.08% Watts Bar fish, ~6% Clinch River fish, ~77% White Oak Creek fish showed accumulations
- Gamma spectrometry of bones/scales: ¹⁰⁶Ru (108–347 × 10⁻⁷ µc/g), ¹³⁷Cs (108–198 × 10⁻⁷ µc/g), ⁶⁰Co (135 × 10⁻⁷ µc/g)
- Strontium-90 assumed present in bone (not analyzed in this study; other ORNL data showed 3.0–297.0 µµc/g in white crappie bone)
- Residence in contaminated areas did not affect growth rates (fish from contaminated areas fell within normal length ranges for their year classes)

**Dispersion and Movement:**
- Conventional tagging (1960–1961): 656 fish tagged, 13 returns (2.0% return rate)
- Tagged fish moved 0 to >450 miles over 38–577 days (0.01–0.22 river miles/day)
- Most movement downstream; one fish moved 17.5 miles down Clinch River, then 18 miles upstream in Tennessee River
- Tagged fish showed length losses (5–20 mm) and weight losses (70–190 g in 4 of 5 fish), indicating detrimental tag effects
- **Autoradiographic analysis (novel technique):** 11 fish with readable scale autoradiograms provided detailed movement histories
- All normal scales from an individual produced identical concentric-circle radionuclide patterns
- Patterns associated with growth in contaminated areas; radionuclides incorporated only during actual scale formation in contaminated zones
- Fish typically sedentary for first 2 years post-hatching, then moved upstream into tributary areas
- Most movements (12 of 16 instances) coincided with annulus formation (late winter/early spring)
- No mass migrations observed
- Two fish hatched in contaminated area, left at age 2, returned at ages 4–5
- Nine fish hatched in non-contaminated areas, entered contaminated area at ages 2–5

**Scale Autoradiography Technique:**
- Scales cleaned by soaking in tap water until epidermis decayed (≤2 weeks), then dried flat between blotting paper
- Mounted on subbed glass slides (gelatin + chrome alum) with Eastman 910 cement
- Exposed on No-screen X-ray film (10 days to 9 months; ~2,000,000 counts over background needed for readable image)
- Developed in Kodak D-19, fixed in DuPont X-ray Fixer
- Regenerated scales: if regenerated in contaminated area, radionuclides distributed evenly over regenerated portion; if regenerated in clean area, no accumulation until normal growth resumed
- Scale growth sequence (contrary to classic concept): lateral fields first, then posterior field, finally anterior field and entire margin
- Fibrillary plate layer accumulates radionuclides evenly; bony surface layer shields beta emissions except where growth occurred in contaminated area, creating concentric patterns
- Technique superior to conventional tagging: provides continuous life history vs. single-point recapture data; no detrimental effects on fish

**Cesium-134 Tagging Experiment:**
- Laboratory experiment: bluegill and warmouth fed earthworms tagged with ¹³⁴Cs (1.1 × 10⁵ dpm)
- Only one fish (warmouth) grew during experiment; its scales showed narrow line of ¹³⁴Cs accumulation at growth margin
- ¹³⁴Cs accumulated primarily in soft tissues (testes, muscle, liver/spleen: 3.75–8.75 × 10⁻² µc/g dry weight) rather than scales/bone (0.65–1.24 × 10⁻² µc/g)
- Cesium not suitable for scale tagging; strontium-90 (bone-seeker, T½ = 27.7 years) proposed as better candidate
- Proposed method: remove key scales, hold fish in pond with bone-seeking radionuclide, allow scale regeneration with radionuclide incorporation, release and later identify by autoradiography

**Population Analysis Applications:**
- Proposed technique combining autoradiography with capture-recapture: (1) select study area with adequate radionuclide concentration, (2) block area with nets, (3) conduct conventional capture-recapture estimate, (4) autoradiograph scales to identify resident vs. transient individuals, (5) calculate separate population estimates for resident and transient segments
- Catch curve analysis: convex right limb attributed to increasing natural mortality with age (not continued recruitment or increased fishing pressure)
- Commercial fishing increased food availability, improving growth rates in successive year classes
- Segmented population possible (different tributary populations with different growth rates combining in main reservoir), but data insufficient to confirm
