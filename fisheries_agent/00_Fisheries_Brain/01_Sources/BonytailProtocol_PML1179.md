# Bonytail Chub Handling, Collection and Sampling Protocol Correspondence (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a set of 1993 draft protocol materials and interagency memoranda concerning the handling, collection, holding, transport and tissue sampling of [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), an endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] cyprinid. The core documents are a June 1993 memorandum from Chuck Minckley (Parker Fishery Resources Office) to the Associate Manager of Fisheries, titled "Bonytail Protocol," and a June 1993 memorandum from the Arizona Fishery Resources Office (Pinetop, AZ) on a "Native Fish Handling Protocol." Supporting pages include notes from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] and a set of fish-health and tissue-handling instructions. The material is a draft in poor scan quality, so many passages are partially illegible; the points below reflect only what can be read with reasonable confidence.

## Background & Description

The documents concern [[Bonytail Chub]], a native [[Colorado River]] fish that was being collected, held and transported for propagation and research. The protocol was drafted because the species was endangered and its handling involved several agencies. The collection and holding work involved:

- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (Dexter NFH&TC), which was to receive transported fish.
- [[Parker Fishery Resources Office]] and the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] (Pinetop, AZ), which led portions of the planning.
- Roughly a dozen participating agencies and institutions, including the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], [[National Park Service]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], fish health staff, tribal and university researchers, and [[Arizona State University]] (per OCR).
- A [[Genetic Analysis]] component involving tissue samples sent to a University of Texas laboratory and to the [[Arizona State University]] laboratory.

The correspondence also refers to a [[Native Fish Handling Protocol]] intended to set common procedures across all cooperating parties.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The protocol is aimed at minimizing stress and mortality during capture, holding and transport of a native, imperiled fish. The memoranda emphasize:

- Keeping fish alive and healthy through holding, transport and handling, with attention to water quality and temperature.
- Limiting capture-related stress through controlled [[Trammel Net]] and [[Hoop Net]] use (limits on set duration and checking frequency).
- Using approved chemicals only, with field and hatchery staff trained in their use, including for [[Anesthesia]] or treatment.
- Coordinating notification of all parties before sampling, so that multiple agencies do not duplicate or disturb the same fish.
- Keeping dead or tissue-sampled fish as voucher specimens and preserving tissue for later analysis.

Because the source is an early draft, the protocol's quantitative thresholds (for example, net set times and holding periods) cannot be confirmed from the scan.

## Key Findings

1. **Coordinated notification and planning.** The Parker memorandum outlines a basic protocol that requires planning when collections occur and advance notice to all participants. It also calls for [[Fish Health]] personnel to be contacted before collection to advise on handling and to be present where appropriate.

2. **Hatchery holding and transport.** Fish collected at [[Lake Mohave]] (per OCR) were to be held in the field and then transported to Dexter NFH&TC. The memo notes that holding and transport must be arranged in advance and that the hatchery must be ready to receive the fish.

3. **Gear standards.** The Arizona Fishery Resources Office memorandum recommends setting standards for sampling gear. Trammel nets should not be left unchecked for long periods (the text suggests a limit of about 4 hours without pulling; a 12-hour set is also mentioned, and the scan is unclear). Hoop nets should be checked regularly, and captured fish held in live wells or holding containers to avoid excessive mortality.

4. **Lead agency responsibility.** The Pinetop memorandum argues that the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service should take lead responsibility for studies involving native fishes, even when work is done by others. It recommends that all parties (state, university, tribal and federal agencies) be notified of sampling and research efforts.

5. **Permits.** The memorandum notes that collection permits for the species should be obtained in advance, and that a single lead entity should be identified for each study.

6. **Chemicals.** The Pinetop memo asks whether captured fish should be anesthetized or treated before release, and states that field and hatchery personnel need approved chemicals and clear instructions on their use.

7. **Tissue and voucher sampling.** The protocol and attached notes call for:
   - Fin clips and tissue samples placed in labeled vials (ethanol-preserved) and frozen at low temperature (noted as -80 °C) for [[Genetic Analysis]].
   - Preserved voucher specimens kept separate from tissue samples.
   - Tissue divided between laboratories (University of Texas and Arizona State University), with results from both groups compared and a third laboratory used if results disagree.
   - Samples and data organized by the lead agency, with known and unknown-age fish handled separately.

8. **Hatchery and research use.** Some fish were to be retained at Dexter NFH&TC for propagation and research, with the remainder released after appropriate holding and sampling. The memos also refer to a possible study of age and growth using [[Otolith]] or similar hard parts.

9. **Outstanding issues.** The correspondence repeatedly flags that the final protocol was not yet in place, that communication among all parties must be improved, and that a formal written protocol is needed before further collections proceed.
