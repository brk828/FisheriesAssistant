# Bonytail protocol

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 document set is a draft proposal and internal correspondence from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) on collecting, handling, holding and transporting wild [[Bonytail]] from [[Lake Mohave]] to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] for [[Captive Propagation]] and recovery. It sets out a planning checklist, lists participating agencies, gives handling and transport rules, and includes memoranda on sampling gear, handling standards, and the allocation of tissues and carcasses from a small group of bonytail for genetic, aging and morphological studies. The source is a protocol and coordination document, not a research study, so it reports no population or survival data.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Bonytail]] is a Colorado River endemic cyprinid. The document does not give a binomial name; *Gila elegans* is used here from general knowledge and is consistent with the [[Gila Taxonomy Group]] referenced in the text.
- **Conservation context:** The preface frames captive propagation as a viable option for the recovery of imperiled species threatened with extinction, while noting that it "is not an end in itself" and carries risk to the species involved.
- **Core problem identified:** Recent collection efforts failed because of poor coordination among three elements: extensive planning, interagency communication, and committed resources (personnel, funds, and equipment). The drafters aim to prevent repeats of these failures.
- **Related species:** Memoranda also mention the [[Razorback Sucker]] and other native Colorado River fishes, which are handled under the same concerns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Capture site:** Fish are collected from [[Lake Mohave]], with [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] proposed as a possible holding site on the lake.
- **Gear limitations:** A memo from the Parker office states that [[Electrofishing]] and standard [[Hoop Net]] sets are ineffective for bonytail. Larger traps with long leads "might work" but would require a larger commitment. [[Trammel Net]]s are the recommended gear.
- **Water and health requirements:** Holding water must be well aerated to maintain adequate dissolved oxygen. Transport and holding water should include approved stress-reducing and antibacterial agents.
- **Disease risk:** Handling and holding are designed to limit bacterial and secondary fungal outbreaks, and fish are quarantined on arrival at Dexter.
- **Environmental exposure:** The Arizona Fishery Resources Office notes that some native suckers (including Razorback Sucker) are repeatedly collected, and asks whether they should be held to check for latent mortality.

## Key Findings

### Planning and coordination

- The draft lists eight planning questions covering the purpose of captivity, the contribution to recovery, the minimum number of fish required, retention time, the use of progeny (reintroduction, further somatic-tissue work, combined use with other captive stock), and whether all required permits (ESA, NEPA, NPR) are in place.
- Operational questions cover who collects the fish, how often gear will be run, who holds the fish, where they will be held, how long capture efforts will continue, how long fish will be held Lakewide or at Willow Beach before transport, and whether fish health specialists have approved the transfer.
- Agencies that should take part in planning include the Arizona Game and Fish Department, Nevada Department of Wildlife, Arizona State University, the Arizona Ecological Services Office, the Bureau of Reclamation, the National Park Service, the Regional Fisheries and Endangered Species offices, Dexter NFH & TC and Willow Beach NFH.
- The Arizona Fishery Resources Office argues that FWS should lead these surveys because it bears ultimate responsibility for the species. It also requests that collection permits be issued study by study for species under FWS "lead" responsibility, and that all parties be notified with ample lead time.

### Handling procedure

- Handling should be kept to an absolute minimum. Fish should be removed from the net by cutting the mesh around them, or moved with soft-mesh dip nets, and should be lifted only when essential, supported underneath with both hands.
- Fish should never be grasped by the caudal peduncle.
- Fish should go immediately into water containing approved therapeutic agents to reduce stress and prevent infection (citing Carmichael and Tomasso, 1986).
- Fish held at the capture area should be transported to Dexter as soon as possible. They should ideally stay on the truck for no more than three days, with five days as a maximum.
- Fish held at Willow Beach must remain there until they have recovered from capture and have been cleared for transport by the Regional Fish Health Biologist.
- Anesthetics such as MS-222 may be considered for hauling, but only after appropriate testing on adult or fingerling bonytail at Dexter.

### Quarantine and arrival procedure at Dexter

- On arrival, fish are quarantined while receiving continued treatment for possible bacterial or fungal outbreaks, and are exposed to several prophylactic treatments.
- Once judged healthy, fish are individually anesthetized, checked for external parasites, given a tag, and then moved to long-term holding ponds.

### Sampling effort

- A Parker FRO memo recommends running trammel nets every 6 hours.
- The Arizona Fishery Resources Office recommends that trammel nets not be left unpulled for more than 4 to 5 hours, gill nets be checked very frequently, and traps may be set for up to 12 hours. The memo notes that electrofishing has not been addressed.

### Tissue, aging and morphological studies

The 28 June 1993 correspondence and the associated memos allocate tissues and carcasses from a small group of bonytail (reported in the text as 15 fish of concern):

- **Biochemical genetics (allozymes):** Tissues are to be sent to Don Morizot, University of Texas, Science Park. A second aliquot is kept at Dexter at -80 °C.
- **Aging:** Otoliths, vertebrae and scales are sent to Monty Millard (Fairbanks FRO) under a "blind" design, with known- and unknown-age fish unmarked. Results are compared with those of an Arizona State University group (Wendell Minckley and Paul Marsh). If the two groups disagree, a third group chosen by Wayne Starnes repeats the analysis. Fish with no consensus are classed as "un-age-able."
- **DNA:** Tissues are sent to Tom Dowling at Arizona State University for mitochondrial and nuclear analyses.
- **Morphology:** Mike Douglas at Arizona State University is to videotape the process. Carcasses are then sent to the ichthyology collection for cataloging, with disposition handled by the taxonomy group.
- **Frozen-specimen procedure:** Starnes recommends that tissue be sampled from frozen carcasses before formalin fixation. Tissue is divided into three tubes: DNA, allozymes, and a reserve kept at Dexter. Vials are labeled and tagged with the specimen number. Carcasses are shipped to ASU on dry ice. Starnes notes that frozen allozyme results may be degraded, while DNA and some enzyme attributes may also be affected.
- **Permits:** Receipt and analysis of the specimens are said to be covered by Gila Taxonomy federal permit 93-07, renewed 26 May 1993, which covers Region 2 and the upper basin.

### Key recommendations

- Involve fish health specialists, and have one on site during collection.
- Notify all parties early and in writing.
- Confirm approved chemicals for field and hatchery use, and ensure staff know how to apply them.
- Modify [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] to be more friendly to endangered species, including a set of solar-heated raceways for Colorado River fishes.
- Ensure hatchery staff understand that caring for endangered fish is an important responsibility.
