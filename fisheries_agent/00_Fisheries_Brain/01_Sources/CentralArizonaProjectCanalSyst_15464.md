# Central Arizona Project Canal System Fishery Investigations, 1988 Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation progress report presents preliminary 1988 results of fish monitoring in the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal system. The work was led by [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] with the Arizona Game and Fish Department, Fish and Wildlife Service, and Arizona State University. Its goals were to track fish losses from [[Lake Havasu]], document the fish community forming in the canal, and provide data for mitigation planning. Sixteen species have been recorded in the canal since 1986. Fish are entrained from Lake Havasu, and at least threadfin shad, carp, and sunfish are reproducing in the canal. Striped bass are concentrated in Reach 1, and in the absence of shad they appear to be preying heavily on other species. The authors conclude that sampling efficiency falls as diversion rates rise, and they plan to drop summer sampling in 1989.

## Background & Description

The CAP conveys Colorado River water about 824 feet (251 m) uphill from Lake Havasu through the [[Havasu Pumping Plant]] and a 7-mile tunnel under the Buckskin Mountains. Water then enters open canals divided into the [[Granite Reef Aqueduct]], the [[Salt-Gila Aqueduct]], and the Tucson Aqueduct. The Granite Reef Aqueduct is 190 miles long in 12 reaches. Reach 1 is 17 miles long and is wider, providing storage of about 4,000 acre-feet. Standard canal cross-sections are 24-foot bottom widths, about 18-foot depths, and 1:4 side slopes, with flows up to 3,000 cfs (85 m³/s) at full operation.

Diversions grew from 107,636 acre-feet in 1986 to 388,000 in 1987 and 499,800 in 1988. The 1988 total was about half of Arizona's allocated 1.1 million acre-feet and represented a 29 percent increase over 1987.

The study was a cooperative effort begun in 1986. It is one of three efforts monitoring CAP fisheries, alongside work at Lake Havasu and [[Lake Pleasant]]. A final report covering 1986 to 1989 is scheduled for spring 1990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community and predator-prey dynamics.** The canal is a simplified, homogeneous habitat with no protective cover. The authors describe a short food chain in which prey populations collapse when predator numbers are high. In Reach 1, threadfin shad, green sunfish, bluegill, and snails were once abundant but are now absent or rare, which the authors attribute to predation by large numbers of [[Striped Bass]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Downstream, sunfish populations are large but stunted.

**Velocity and lining effects.** Operational velocities rose with diversion rates. The report cites the [[Coachella Canal]], where lining in 1980 removed cover and doubled velocities. Species diversity there fell from 16 species to 5, dominated by small channel catfish, threadfin shad, and large carp.

**Introductions.** Some species are attributed to [[Bait Bucket Introductions]]. These include desert and Sonora suckers, goldfish, and largemouth bass released by the public. The report also notes tilapia recorded in Lake Pleasant and Saguaro Lake in 1988.

**Water quality.** Canal waters were generally well mixed. Water temperatures ranged from 11.2 to 29.9 °C, dissolved oxygen from 7.9 to 12 mg/L, pH from 7.3 to 10, and conductivity from 840 to 894 µmhos/cm.

## Key Findings

**1. Fish collection and composition.** About 4,800 fish were collected in 1988: 3,698 by [[Electrofishing]], 590 by [[Gill Netting]] (the text gives 594), and 512 by angling. Threadfin shad (40.2%), bluegill (26.7%), and carp (26.6%) dominate the canal fish community. Largemouth bass fell from third to fifth in dominance.

**2. Sampling method bias.** Electrofishing and gill netting agreed closely for most species, except carp. Of 1,139 carp collected by electrofishing, 1,100 were under 100 mm and too small for gill nets. Angling was highly selective: 344 of 383 striped bass (89.8%) and 93.3% of black crappie were taken by angling. Gill net efficiency dropped sharply with higher flows, as filamentous algae matted the nets. Summer gill netting "failed miserably."

**3. Angler success.** Catch per unit effort was 3.81 fish per hour in 1988, up from 3.26 in 1987. [[Striped Bass]] made up 67.2% of the 1988 harvest, up from 13.4% in 1987. Black crappie harvest fell from 56.2% to 5.5%.

**4. Striped bass in Reach 1.** Of 634 striped bass taken over three years, only seven (1.1%) were found downstream of Reach 1. Five of those were taken in 1988 and were year class 0 or I. Average length rose from 257 mm (1986) to 375 mm (1987) and 427 mm (1988). Only one fish under 380 mm was caught in 1988, so the authors found little evidence of recruitment. Two ripe females were collected in Reach 1 in spring 1988.

**5. Predation and prey collapse.** [[Threadfin Shad]] have not been collected in Reach 1 since summer 1987. Their K factor fell from 1.63 to 1.09 in 1987. The authors suspect predation by striped and largemouth bass. Striped bass stomachs contained large numbers of snails and clams and, in some cases, hundreds of fish eggs, with eggs exceeding 15% of body weight. About 80 stomachs were collected, and their analysis was postponed to 1989.

**6. Condition factors.** Condition declined for striped bass (1.6 to 1.1) and largemouth bass (1.4 to 1.2) in 1987. Tagged fish returns suggest striped bass adapted better to the changing food base. Striped bass body weight increased 1.0% per month in Reach 1, while largemouth bass decreased 1.7% per month. At Salt-Gila, bluegill condition factors of 1.25 compared with values above 1.8 elsewhere, which the authors describe as bordering on starvation.

**7. Egg and larval entrainment.** The 1988 study examined 238 individual samples from four sites, yielding 1,060 eggs and 5,540 larvae. No striped bass eggs were found in 1988. Larvae were entrained from Lake Havasu at 1.13 fish per cubic meter in 1987 and 0.39 in 1988. The 1988 rate may reflect a 45% increase in diversions. In 1987, [[Threadfin Shad]] made up 71.1% of samples, sunfish 18.4%, and carp 9.8%.

**8. Evidence of reproduction.** Egg and larval data suggest that threadfin shad, carp, and possibly sunfish are spawning in the canal. SCUBA divers observed largemouth bass and bluegill spawning in the Bouse Hills and Hassayampa forebays. No bass larvae were observed, and no eggs or larvae were found in bluegill redds. Carp juveniles (3–15 cm) were collected for the first time, with 1,100 taken at Little Harquahala.

**9. Fish passage at Hassayampa.** [[Fish Passage]] netting over 10 sets (120 hours, April to July) collected 79 fish. Composition was 53% bluegill, 20% threadfin shad, 20% channel catfish, 4% yellow bullhead, 3% black crappie, and 1% green sunfish. About 90% were dead, and many had severe lacerations. Extrapolated passage was about 2 fish per hour (0.00023 fish per cubic meter). The authors note these fish likely represent injured or dead passage rather than total passage.

**10. Tagging.** 298 fish were tagged with Floy tags. 22 were recovered, nearly all in the release reach. One dead largemouth bass was found 17 miles upstream of its release point, and anglers recaptured striped and largemouth bass near the Buckskin Mountain Tunnel. These findings indicate upstream movement.

**11. Sampling limitations.** The authors cannot sample juvenile or adult passage through the Havasu Pumping Plant, because fish enter the tunnel before any sampling point. Large carp and channel catfish appear to evade electrofishing and small-mesh gill nets during normal operation. Fyke nets and a large-mesh trammel net are planned for Reach 1.

**12. Management recommendations.** For 1989, the authors plan to drop summer sampling because flows above 2,000 cfs and algal matting make capture unreliable. Spring, fall, and winter sampling will continue, with electrofishing and gill netting as primary methods and fyke and trammel nets added. A final report is planned for spring 1990.
