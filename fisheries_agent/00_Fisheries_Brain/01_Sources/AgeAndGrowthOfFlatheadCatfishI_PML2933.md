# Age and Growth of Flathead Catfish in Southwestern Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This paper, published in *California Fish and Game* (1990, vol. 76, no. 4, pp. 224-233) and attributed to Paul C. Marsh, estimates age and growth of [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) in four southwestern U.S. rivers: the [[Lower Colorado River]], [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]]. Ages were determined from pectoral spine cross-sections, with back-calculated lengths corrected for lumen enlargement. The lower Colorado River sample (n = 98, ages 0-9) provided the most robust data and showed fast growth, comparable to the fastest populations in the species' range. The study concludes that spine-based ageing is valid for this species when lumen enlargement is properly corrected.

## Background & Description

The flathead catfish is a large [[Ictaluridae|Ictalurid]] catfish native to Gulf of Mexico drainages, from the Texas and Oklahoma region eastward. The document describes it as an economically and recreationally valuable sportfish that was widely introduced across the United States, including the southwest, where it has become a significant predator on native fishes. Earlier work cited in the paper includes studies by [[Minckley]] and Deacon (1968) and Marsh and Brooks (1989) on predation by introduced ictalurids.

The paper's stated purpose is to describe age and growth in the southwestern populations, which had been poorly documented. Because the species was difficult to obtain in several rivers, sample sizes varied greatly among sites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling**

- **Lower Colorado River:** Sampled by boat electrofishing and trammel netting over a reach of approximately 32.5 km (OCR reading), with a 59 km length upstream from [[Lake Roosevelt]] also referenced.
- **Verde River:** Collections were made in accessible reaches from Childs (Yavapai County) downstream to [[Bartlett Reservoir]] (Maricopa County), using boat- or raft-mounted electroshockers and 50 m trammel nets.
- **Gila River:** Collected over a 77 km reach from the Arizona-New Mexico border (Greenlee County) downstream to Safford (Graham County), using a 115 V AC, 850 W bank shocker.
- **Gear:** Trammel nets had 2.5 cm inner and 20.3 cm outer mesh.

**Thermal and seasonal conditions**

- Spawning occurred from spring through early summer, based on captures of small fish without discernible annuli.
- Mid-summer water temperatures reached 28 °C in the lower Colorado River, but no false annuli were observed among larger fish in response to this thermal stress.
- Spring annulus formation coincided with resumption of growth after cooler winter temperatures.

## Key Findings

**Methods and validation**

- Length was measured to the nearest mm total length (TL) and weight to the nearest 20 g.
- Age was determined from left pectoral spine cross-sections, polished and examined under reflected light; spine diameter and annulus radii were measured to 0.01 mm.
- Back-calculation used the Carlander (1982) approach with a constant intercept of 15 mm TL at spine formation for all populations, to reduce variability among populations.
- The regression of TL on spine radius (based on the lower Colorado River sample, r = 0.989) was TL = 17.415 + 15.20s (OCR reading), with separate regressions not possible for the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers due to small samples.
- Back-calculated lengths matched modes in length-frequency distributions of 884 flathead catfish collected from the lower Colorado River in 1982-1986 (Marsh and Minckley 1987).
- The spine method was previously validated by tag returns in the Des Moines River, Iowa (Muncy 1957), and in Lake Carl Blackwell, Oklahoma (Turner 1977).

**Lumen enlargement and bias correction**

- Lumen enlargement was present in fish from all rivers and was most severe in the lower Colorado River population, caused by natural resorption of calcium phosphate in the spine's central region.
- More than 90% of lower Colorado River fish aged 3 years and older lacked a discernible first annulus.
- Depending on year class, 2% to 7% of fish aged 4 years and older lacked the second annulus.
- Uncorrected enlargement biases age estimates toward younger ages and faster growth.
- Correction: mean annulus radii and standard deviations for annuli 1-3 were calculated from sections with little or no enlargement (Table 1). If a lumen radius exceeded the mean plus 1 SD, the fish's age was increased accordingly.

**Population results**

- **Lower Colorado River (n = 98):** Ages 0-9 years; year classes 1977-1986. Young-of-the-year averaged 71 mm TL at capture. Average annual growth varied among year classes and was greatest in the third year. Growth was rapid, at least through age 9.
- **Gila River (n = 24):** Year classes 1981-1986; ages 1-5 years. Young-of-the-year averaged 103 mm TL. The largest fish was 520 mm TL and 5 years old. Growth was consistent for a given age across year classes, and was greatest in the first year of life.
- **Salt River (n = 34):** Year classes 1980-1985; ages 1-6 years. No young-of-the-year were captured. The largest fish was 707 mm TL and 6 years old.
- **Verde River:** Sample sizes were too small for separate growth analysis, and the length distribution was skewed toward larger individuals.

**Comparisons and conclusions**

- Growth in the lower Colorado River was among the fastest reported for the species, comparable to the Coachella Canal, California (Visano et al. 1983), and faster than in the Salt River.
- Reservoir populations were reported to show more variable growth rates but generally similar ranges to river-dwelling stocks (Cuier et al. 1979).
- The authors conclude that growth of flathead catfish in the lower Colorado River is rapid, at least through age 9, and as fast as anywhere in the species' range.
