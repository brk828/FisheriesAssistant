# Status of the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Minckley (*The Southwestern Naturalist* 28(2): 165-187, 1983) assesses the status of the [[Razorback Sucker]] in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. Outside [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada) the species survives only as scattered individuals. The Lake Mohave population is composed of large, slow-growing fish, estimated to be nearly 30 years old, that likely hatched while the reservoir was filling in the early 1950s. The species made up about 12.5% of fishes taken by trammel netting there from 1974 to 1982, yet no recruitment was detected in that period. The author concludes that predation by introduced fishes on early life stages is the most important single factor in the species' extirpation. Artificial propagation and reintroduction in Arizona are under way.

## Background & Description

- The razorback sucker is one of four endemic "big-river" fishes of the Colorado basin, alongside the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. It is the only one of the four listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior at the time of writing.
- Habitat for all four has declined through river development for domestic, industrial, agricultural, and power needs.
- Sampling by the author and colleagues since 1963 documented reduced ranges for all native fishes, though many local populations remained abundant.
- Lake Mohave, filled in the early 1950s, is about 11 km² at full pool (197 m elevation), up to 43 m deep, and has gravel and cobble shorelines and sand-silt deeper substrates. Only two native species persist there: razorback sucker and bonytail chub.
- Methods: trammel nets (30.5-213.3 m long), supplemented by gill nets, fyke and hoop nets, seines, and electrofishing. Catch rates are expressed as individuals per 100 m² of netting per 24 hours. Ageing used scale annuli, which proved unreliable beyond about six annuli.
- Morphological work covered 83 adults from Lake Mohave (collected 1966-1975), and fecundity was estimated from five ripe females.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical distribution:** The species was once widespread, occurring in the mainstream Colorado (including the delta in Mexico), the Salton Sea (until salinity rose and it disappeared by 1929), the Gila, Verde, Salt, and San Pedro rivers, and Tonto Creek. Historical abundance was high: in the early 1900s "wagon loads" were collected in Phoenix-area canals, and a commercial fishery operated in [[Saguaro Lake]] as late as 1949.
- **Reservoir pattern:** Large, old adults persist for years in reservoirs, but the author reports they disappear as the reservoirs age (Roosevelt Lake, Saguaro Lake, Lake Mead, Lake Havasu).
- **Predation and reproduction:** Spawning has been observed in shallow, gravelly areas in Lake Mohave, Lake Havasu, Lake Mead, and the Paria and Senator Wash systems. Introduced predators, including [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and mosquitofish, have been documented eating eggs, larvae, and juveniles.
- **Lake Mohave fish fauna:** 29 species have been reliably reported, only six of them native. Introduced species dominate.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] were recorded in 1975 and 1982, all over 50 cm TL.
- **Disease:** Blindness, eye damage, and spinal deformities were common (for example, 11.5% of blinded fish had orbits overgrown with epidermis). Pathogens included *Myxosoma* sp., *Aeromonas hydrophila*, and *Lernaea* sp.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** Razorback suckers are now scattered outside Lake Mohave. In Lake Mohave they comprised about 12.5% of trammel-net catches in 1974-1982, with no recruitment detected.
- **Age and growth:** Growth since 1964 averaged about 0.5 cm per year. Females (47-74 cm TL) are larger than males (37-64 cm TL). Scale-based age estimates of 11-20+ years were uncertain. The author proposes the cohort hatched in the early 1950s, making the fish roughly 30 years old.
- **Reproduction:** Sex ratios were male-biased on spawning grounds (for example, 3.57 in 1981) and female-biased offshore. Fecundity averaged 100,800 ova per female (range 74,600-144,000) for five females, or about 1,812 ova/cm SL. Fertilized eggs and hatched larvae have been recorded, but the percentage of fertile ova and larval survival are unknown.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males have longer pelvic and anal fins, nuptial tubercles, and a thinner urogenital papilla. Females are larger and have a longer, rounder papilla. Differences were significant at P < 0.05.
- **Primary threat:** The author concludes that predation by introduced fishes on early life stages is the most important single factor in extirpation. Competition is considered secondary, and dams block re-dispersal of native fishes.
- **Recovery:** Hatchery propagation began at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery in 1974. In 1981, 20,000 fingerlings (about 8 cm TL) were produced at Dexter National Fish Hatchery and stocked into Arizona habitats. In 1982, more than 1.8 million larvae were produced and released. The author notes that reintroductions may succeed in reaches with few non-native fishes.
- **Management implications:** Changes to Hoover Dam operations, pump-storage development, and Lake Mohave water-level regulation may affect remaining populations. The author stresses the critical lack of information on big-river fishes.
