# Libby Lake Brook Trout Ecology (1961)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Cooperative Research Project report (University of Wyoming and Wyoming Game and Fish Commission, October 1961, Jack A. Sinn) examines the [[Brook Trout]] population of [[Libby Lake]], a 25.5 acre subalpine lake in the [[Snowy Range]] of the [[Medicine Bow National Forest]]. It was the first study in the series to address a lake larger than nine acres, testing whether management policies developed for small high-country lakes also apply to larger ones. The study covered plankton and benthos, water chemistry, fish standing crop, growth, feeding, spawning, and a stratified [[Creel Census]] during summer 1961.

## Background & Description

The [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) is well suited to cold subalpine lakes, beaver ponds and small streams of southeastern Wyoming. Concern centred on "stunted" populations stabilizing at six to nine inches, attributed to high reproductive potential and competition for food, and/or loss of larger fish through fishing or mortality.

The study built on earlier [[Cooperative Research Project 2]] work: Stewart (1959) on scale-based ageing ([[Scale Age Determination]]), Hepworth (1959) on [[Towner Lake]] and [[Little Telephone Lake]], Royer (1960) on [[Little Brooklyn Lake]], and Call (1960) on [[Beaver Ponds]] and trout. Those lakes were all under nine acres.

Libby Lake sits at 10,750 ft elevation, with a maximum depth of 40 ft, mean depth 15.9 ft, and volume of 405.9 acre-feet. It is the last of a stair-step chain of glacial "[[Paternoster Lakes]]" feeding Libby Creek. Its inlet is blocked by boulders, so trout cannot spawn there; the outlet creek has gravel and rubble suitable for spawning. Aquatic vegetation is limited to a few small *Elodea canadensis* beds. The fishery is mainly brook trout, with a few [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*); no other fish species were encountered.

A cover letter dated September 17, 1962 accompanies the report, supplying a chart omitted from the Game and Fish Laboratory Research Report FW-3-R-9 (July 15, 1962).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Surface water reached a maximum of 56 °F on August 4, 1961. Temperatures fell within the 55–66 °F range that Benson (1953) identified as optimal for brook trout feeding and activity only for about one month, which the author suggests may limit production. [[Thermal Stratification]] was present on August 10 and 18 (hypolimnion at 31 and 35 ft) and was breaking down by September 3, when fall overturn was under way. Dissolved oxygen stayed high except at depth during stratification. Alkalinity and pH were stable (mean pH 7.5). Secchi disk readings averaged 12.2 ft. Iron concretions in the bottom mud suggest groundwater iron input.

**Plankton and benthos.** Net plankton averaged 166,756 organisms per liter, dominated by diatoms (*Asterionella* bloom on July 14). Rotifers averaged 982 per liter, mostly *Kellicottia longispina*. Microcrustaceans averaged about 17 per liter, mostly copepod nauplii. The benthos averaged 146 organisms per square foot, with [[Tendipedidae]] (midge larvae and pupae) most numerous (about 102 per square foot) and tubificid oligochaetes (*Limnodrilus*) second. Benthic standing crop was 51.5 lb per acre (wet weight). Libby Lake's midge density is far below [[Towner Lake]] and [[Little Brooklyn Lake]], which the author attributes to those lakes being shallow and productive across their whole bottom, while Libby Lake showed limited production below 30 ft.

**Comparative context.** Libby Lake's volume is about 12 to 19 times that of Towner and Little Brooklyn lakes. The report also appends a table of chemical and physical analyses of 45 water samples from southwestern Wyoming lakes (July 1961), grouped as supporting, potential supporting, nonsupporting and miscellaneous waters, covering conductance, dissolved solids, alkalinity, and major ions.

## Key Findings

**Fish standing crop and population**
- A [[Petersen Method]] single-census mark-recapture estimate (Bailey's modification) used 724 marked nonresident brook trout (328 Group A, ≥6 in; 396 Group B, ≤5.9 in).
- Final estimate on July 31: 2,950 Group A and 542 Group B fish (the Summary gives 551 for Group B, a discrepancy within the report).
- Age-group estimates: I = 542; II = 1,673; III = 935; IV = 277; V = 65 (total 3,492).
- Standing crop: 26.0 lb per surface acre (662.1 lb total).
- The age-II count was about three times age-I, the reverse of expected. Possible causes: the 1959 hatchery plant, estimate error, or late-summer immigration of age-I fish from the outlet stream.

**Growth**
- Calculated annual total length increments at the end of years 1–5: 2.8, 2.9, 2.3, 1.9 and 1.2 in.
- Body–scale regression: L = 1.12 + 0.128·S.
- Mean summer [[Coefficient of Condition]] was 38.4 (July 6 mean 36.2; the report cites a usual eastern U.S. range of 38–45).
- Principal growth period ran from early July to mid-August. Most age I and II fish had formed a new annulus by July 6.
- Instantaneous growth of age-II fish in July was 0.0315, exceeding rates reported by McFadden (1961) for Lawrence Creek, Wisconsin.
- Growth Walford plots estimated ultimate length at 15.5–18 in for all lakes except Towner Lake.

**Feeding**
- Stomach fullness: 79.5% in July 6 fish, falling to 36.0% by September 19 (Group A and B values also tabulated).
- Dominant food shifted seasonally: [[Tendipedidae]] made up 64% of diet in July; adult aquatic and terrestrial insects 36% in August; zooplankton (mainly *Daphnia pulex*) 67% in September.
- Oligochaetes were absent from stomachs, supporting the view that brook trout feed in open water rather than on bottom mud.

**Harvest and creel census**
- Stratified creel census (June 24–September 6, 55 sample days) estimated 1,829.5 fishing hours.
- Estimated seasonal catch: 1,354 brook trout and 11 cutthroat trout.
- Bank fishermen: 1,004 brook trout; boat fishermen: 350.
- Average catch per hour: 0.6334 (bank) and 1.0801 (boat).
- Average fish length in the catch: 9.1 in (range 6–12.5 in).
- Creel census showed light fishing pressure and low harvest, with wide confidence limits driven by variable angler skill.

**Spawning**
- No spawning in Libby Lake itself; the inlet is boulder-choked.
- Adult trout concentrated in the outlet creek: 141 (September 17) and 130 (September 27) in one pool, plus 12 and 37 in the reach between pool and trap.
- A trap captured only two 9 in brook trout, so it was unclear whether spawners came from the lake or Libby Creek.
- Towner Lake inlet counts were 82 adults on September 17, 1961, compared with 7 and 13 in 1958. Little Brooklyn inlet counts were 52, compared with 8 and 23 in 1958.
- Sex ratio: 61 males to 60 females (121 fish).

**Management conclusions**
- The lake appears self-supporting; stocking is not recommended until fishing pressure increases.
- Fishing pressure was light, consistent with the high proportion of older (age IV–V) fish.
- Growing season, governed by water temperature, is the likely main limit on productivity.
- Harvest is below estimated production, so the lakes are not over-harvested.
- Future work: at least 25 age I or II fish sampled every 10 days from June through September for a better growth curve; evaluate food utilization efficiency; and mark fish to test upstream migration from Libby Creek.
