# Letter Regarding Gila Topminnow Recovery Plan Revision Materials

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #poeciliidae #conservation-genetics #gila-topminnow

## Executive Summary

This 1992 letter from Dean A. Hendrickson (Curator of Ichthyology, Texas Memorial Museum) to Francisco Abarca González (Arizona Game and Fish Department) presents a substantial re-evaluation of the proposed [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) Recovery Plan revision. Hendrickson argues, based on emerging allozyme and mtDNA genetic data, that the previously favored **hierarchical, sub-basin-specific management model** (treating each remnant population as a genetically distinct unit) is both **genetically untenable and logistically unmanageable**. Instead, he proposes managing Arizona's Gila topminnow populations as a **single, basin-wide panmictic unit**, mirroring pre-historic gene flow patterns disrupted only recently (circa the arroyo-incision cycle at the turn of the 20th century). The letter outlines a detailed, prioritized management and reintroduction strategy, recommends new experimental and genetic monitoring approaches, and proposes revised recovery/delisting criteria based on statewide genetic diversity rather than simple population counts.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[Gila Topminnow]], a small poeciliid fish native to the [[Gila River Basin]], listed as endangered, subject to a federal Recovery Plan under revision in the early 1990s.
- **Key remnant natural populations** (defined as all individuals naturally occurring within a single sub-basin):
  - **San Rafael Valley population**: Sharp Spring + Sheehy Spring + Santa Cruz River
  - **Sonoyta Creek population**: Monkey Spring + Cottonwood Spring + Red Rock Canyon + Sonoyta Creek
  - **Ciénega population**: Ciénega Creek
  - **Gila population**: Bylas Springs complex
- **Historical genetic hypothesis debated**:
  - Original Recovery Team position: sub-basins were isolated in pre-historic times → each should be managed as a discrete, panmictic-within-but-not-among unit.
  - Hendrickson's revised position (supported by [[Vrijenhoek]] allozyme data and [[Joe Quattro]] mtDNA data): Arizona Gila basin populations were **historically a single panmictic unit**, only recently fragmented (~turn of 20th century, arroyo incision), and are themselves a genetic subset of more diverse populations in the **Rio de la Concepción** and **Rio Sonora** (Mexico).
- **Key genetic threats/exotic species**: *Gambusia* (mosquitofish) identified as a major invasive threat requiring removal and barrier construction at sites including Bylas, Sharp and Sheehy Springs, and Redrock Canyon.
- **Cited genetic authorities**: Vrijenhoek's lab (allozyme studies, heterozygosity-fitness correlations), W.L. Minckley (population sampling concerns), Joe Quattro et al. (mtDNA haplotype study, submitted to *Conservation Biology*, unpublished at time of writing).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types**: isolated desert springs, cienegas, and headwater streams within the Sonoran Desert, spanning the Gila River drainage (Arizona) and Rio Sonora / Rio de la Concepción drainages (Sonora, Mexico).
- **Historical hydrogeomorphic context**: Sub-basin isolation of populations is attributed largely to the **arroyo incision cycle** beginning near the turn of the 20th century, not deep pre-historic vicariance — implying isolation is a recent anthropogenic/geomorphic artifact rather than an ancient evolutionary divergence.
- **Invasive species pressure**: *Gambusia* invasion is the principal biotic threat to remnant native populations, necessitating removal efforts and construction of invasion barriers (e.g., at Bylas, Sharp/Sheehy Springs, Redrock Canyon, Cottonwood Spring).
- **Cross-border conservation context**: Genetic diversity reservoir exists in Mexican populations (Rio de la Concepción, Rio Sonora), which are more variable than the depauperate Arizona populations; ongoing habitat degradation in Mexico and geopolitical/trade factors (referenced NAFTA) are cited as long-term risks and potential opportunities for cross-border management.
- **Reintroduction site categories** proposed:
  1. Sites near parental natural populations (hydrographically proximal) — stocked with matched native stock to prevent genetic contamination of natural populations.
  2. "Outer ring" sites geographically isolated from natural populations (e.g., San Simon basin, lower Santa Cruz tributaries, Gila drainage below San Carlos Dam including San Pedro, Salt/Verde, Agua Fria, Hassayampa) — used for experimental mixed-stock/hybrid introductions.

## Key Findings

- **Genetic panmixia**: Both allozyme data (Vrijenhoek) and mtDNA data (Quattro et al., unpublished) indicate **no significant genetic differentiation among Arizona Gila basin populations** — all share the same mtDNA haplotype and are a genetic subset of more diverse Mexican populations (Rio de la Concepción, Rio Sonora).
- **Heterozygosity–fitness correlation**: Vrijenhoek's lab research found a **positive correlation between heterozygosity and fitness** in topminnow, corroborated in other taxa, suggesting Arizona's low-diversity remnant populations may have reduced fitness.
- **Recommended management shift**: Abandon the hierarchical, sub-basin-specific reintroduction approach in favor of managing Arizona Gila topminnow as a **single basin-wide panmictic unit**, more consistent with pre-historic gene flow and more logistically feasible.
- **Eight-point prioritized action plan**:
  1. Protect/purchase remaining natural habitats; remove *Gambusia*; erect invasion barriers; establish refugia stocks (≥2 per remnant population).
  2. Protect proven-stable reintroduction sites (e.g., Hidden Water, Boyce Thompson) without unnecessary new stockings.
  3. Redesign reintroduction program as a large-scale field experiment using hydrographically matched stocks for "inner ring" sites and mixed/hybrid stocks for "outer ring" experimental sites.
  4. Conduct controlled lab/outdoor mesocosm experiments incorporating Mexican stocks (Rio de la Concepción, Rio Sonora) to test heterozygosity-fitness hypotheses.
  5. Pursue advanced genetic studies (including museum specimens of extinct stocks) to detect fine-scale inter-population differences.
  6. If hybrid fitness increases are confirmed, incorporate Mexican alleles into Arizona field reintroductions.
  7. Conduct intensive field surveys (Arizona and Sonora, Mexico) to locate additional natural populations.
  8. Maintain dynamic, periodically revised management plans reflecting new genetic/habitat knowledge.
- **Proposed recovery criteria overhaul**: Reject simple population-count-based recovery metrics in favor of **statewide genetic diversity tracking** over time; recovery/downlisting should be defined by stabilization of overall genetic variability rather than static population persistence, better reflecting the naturally dynamic (populations "come and go") historic condition.
- **Delisting caveat**: True delisting should require establishment of genuinely self-sustaining populations (not just human-managed panmixia); author suspects the species may never be fully delisted absent major reductions in Mexican habitat degradation, referencing potential (but unlikely) influence of NAFTA on removing political barriers to reduced protections.
- **Experimental design proposal**: A theoretical population genetics model (to be developed with a population geneticist) should guide both mesocosm/outdoor experiments and field reintroductions, testing fitness differences between pure native stocks and inter-population/inter-country hybrids; requires large founder population sizes (~500 individuals/site) and centralized inter-agency data tracking.
- **Logistical/bureaucratic concerns**: Large-scale experimental reintroduction faces major hurdles including multi-agency approval processes, need for short-timeframe synchronized stocking, verification of founder genetic composition, and periodic effective population size (N_e) estimation.
- **Unresolved caveat**: Possible **outbreeding depression** and undetected local adaptation (not revealed by allozyme/mtDNA data) could counter the panmixia/hybridization strategy; author flags this as a needed area of further study before large-scale mixing of Arizona and Mexican stocks.
