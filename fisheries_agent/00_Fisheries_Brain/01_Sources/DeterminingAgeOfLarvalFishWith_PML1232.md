# Determining Age of Larval Fish with the Otolith Increment Technique

#ichthyology #marine-biology #otolith-aging

## Executive Summary

This 1986 review by Jones (Fishery Bulletin, Vol. 84, No. 1) evaluates whether [[Otolith Increment Technique]] can accurately age field-captured larval fish. The technique assumes that growth increments in [[Otolith]] microstructure are deposited daily. The author reviewed the literature to test this hypothesis: larval age equals otolith increment count plus age at first increment deposition under field conditions. Daily deposition is well supported under optimal laboratory conditions, but it is frequently interrupted under suboptimal or extreme conditions, and many species have never been tested under suboptimal conditions. The paper concludes that the technique should be validated both under optimal conditions and under conditions that mimic nature before it is applied to field populations.

## Background & Description

- **Historical origin:** [[Reibisch]] (1899) first observed annular rings in the otoliths of the European plaice (*Pleuronectes platessa*). [[Pannella]] (1971) identified fine daily increments in temperate species, and later (1974) used them to read patterns in tropical adult fish.
- **Application to larvae:** [[Brothers Et Al. 1976]] first applied the technique to larval fish, verifying daily deposition in the northern anchovy (*Engraulis mordax*) and California grunion (*Leuresthes tenuis*) reared from eggs.
- **Scale of use:** The technique had been used to estimate larval age in at least 29 species, in both freshwater and marine settings, often without adequate validation.
- **Core assumption:** Either surviving larvae grew under moderately good conditions, or larvae exposed to suboptimal conditions survive and their increment deposition is unaffected. The second assumption depends on a [[Zeitgeber]], an external factor (such as light) that entrains a daily (diel) clock.
- **Stated consequences:** Inaccurate age estimates also distort estimates of [[Growth Rate]] and [[Mortality]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Factors affecting increment deposition.** Candidate triggers include [[Photoperiod]], feeding, and temperature. Their effects are mostly species-specific:

- **Photoperiod:** Abnormal photoperiods disrupted daily deposition in *Fundulus heteroclitus* (Radtke and Dean 1982) and *Tilapia mossambica* (Taubert and Coble 1977). Constant light did not disrupt it in *Oncorhynchus tshawytscha* (Neilson and Geen 1982) or *Scophthalmus maximus* (Geffen 1982). Studies of *Tilapia nilotica* conflicted on whether the timing of increment formation relative to light is species-specific.
- **Feeding:** Frequent discrete feedings induced subdaily increments in *O. tshawytscha* (four feedings per day), while feeding time had no effect in *T. nilotica* or *T. mossambica*.
- **Starvation:** Tested in only three species. Starvation disrupted deposition in *S. maximus* (Geffen 1982) and *Morone saxatilis* (Jones 1984). In *Oncorhynchus nerka*, 2 weeks of starvation had no effect (Marshall and Parker 1982), possibly reflecting age-specific sensitivity.
- **Temperature:** [[Brothers]] (1978) linked diel temperature cycles to daily deposition in temperate stream populations. Other studies found small temperature changes had no effect on deposition rate. Below about 10 °C, growth and increment formation ceased in *Lepomis cyanellus* and *O. nerka*.

**Field conditions.** Laboratory studies typically used constant temperature and salinity, artificial food, and low light, whereas field larvae experience fluctuating conditions. Laboratory-grown larvae also often have fainter increments than field-captured larvae. Enclosures and flow-through chambers were identified as useful intermediate steps for testing increment deposition under field-like conditions.

## Key Findings

1. **Daily deposition is well supported under optimal conditions.** About 17 of 20 species tested showed daily deposition under optimal laboratory conditions. Six species held in the laboratory and sampled over known periods also showed daily deposition.

2. **Deposition is often nondaily under suboptimal or extreme conditions.** In studies that examined these conditions, deposition was not daily in more than half of the tests, and in the 2-to-3-year range reviewed for many species it was not tested at all.

3. **Initial increment timing varies and is not predicted by development.** The hypothesis that slowly developing embryos begin deposition at or before hatch, while rapidly developing embryos begin at yolk-sac absorption or first feeding, was not supported. Clupeids show contrasting patterns: [[Atlantic Herring]] and [[Northern Anchovy]] differ despite sharing suborder Clupeoidei.

3. **Herring results are the most controversial.** [[Gjøsæter]] and Øiestad (1981) reported roughly daily deposition, with increments beginning at first feeding (4–5 d) and 99 increments in 97-day-old larvae, though the author noted small sample sizes. [[Lough Et Al. 1982]] found only three increments in 18-day-old larvae, which the author judged easy to dismiss because the larvae did not survive longer.

4. **Growth rate affects deposition.** [[Geffen]] (1982) found deposition approached daily (0.92 increments/d) only at very fast growth (0.42 mm/d) in *Clupea harengus*, and at 20 °C with constant light and 24 °C with a 12L:12D cycle in *S. maximus*. Faster growth in larger containers was also associated with more increments.

5. **Only one species has independent confirmation of daily deposition.** Among the literature summarized, *Oncorhynchus nerka* was the only species where independent investigators confirmed daily deposition (Wilson and Larkin 1980; Marshall and Parker 1982).

6. **Field applications are extensive but variably validated.** Table 5 lists field applications across many species. Some applications lacked laboratory validation and assumed both a given age at first deposition and daily deposition thereafter. Growth estimates for *Parophrys vetulus* from otolith increments were 2–3 times faster than estimates from modal progression of length frequencies (Laroche et al. 1982), implying that mortality estimates could change considerably if the increment method proves accurate.

7. **Statistical and methodological cautions.** The author recommends checking model fit and weighting before using linear regression, and reporting confidence intervals and standard deviations. Relating increment width to environmental variables gave either no correlation or potentially spurious correlation. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) generally matched light microscopy counts under optimal conditions, except for larval cod, but SEM is too costly for routine field surveys and is recommended mainly to confirm nondaily deposition.

8. **Recommendations.** Test increment deposition for each new species under varied conditions, as recommended by the 1982 Bergen otolith workshop. Evaluate cost-effectiveness against length-frequency analysis, perform sensitivity analyses, and consider other otoliths such as the lapillus.
