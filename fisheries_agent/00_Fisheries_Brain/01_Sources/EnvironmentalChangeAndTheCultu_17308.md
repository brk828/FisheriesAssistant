# Environmental Change and the Culture of Common Carp in Medieval Europe

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This article, by historian Richard C. Hoffmann, approaches the European domestication of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) as a problem in [[Environmental History]]. It argues that the economic expansion of roughly 1000 to 1400 CE altered European inland waters, damaging [[Lotic Habitats]] and anadromous fish populations while favouring lentic and flood plain species. Medieval Europeans responded to falling supplies of favoured fish with new [[Fishpond Culture]] technology, centred in western and interior Europe, in which carp became a central species. The paper combines an inventory of carp remains and early verbal references with critical source analysis. It concludes that carp spread slowly up the Danube and into the Rhine-Maas drainage between the seventh and eleventh centuries, largely without elaborate culture, and that it was taken up by western fish culturists and spread widely in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The text is published in *Guelph Ichthyological Review* 3:57-85 (1995).

## Background & Description

The paper frames the question as one of historical method: the event of carp domestication cannot be observed and must be reconstructed from [[Primary Sources]], both written and archaeological. Hoffmann defines [[Environmental History]] through three interdependent variables: human culture (mentality and cognition), human economy (production and action), and ecological process. The approach is applied to pre-industrial European fisheries (roughly 500-1750), with a focus on fresh water.

Common carp is described in the text as an omnivore that feeds on bottom-dwelling and plant-associated small animals, prefers warm, turbid, slow and still waters with abundant vegetation, spawns in early summer, tolerates low oxygen and can survive some days out of water if kept cool and damp. The fish is noted to grow faster than [[Bream]], the most similar native western European cyprinid.

The appendices address classical evidence. Aristotle's *kyprianos* and Latin *cyprinus* appear to refer to a different fish, probably associated with coastal or brackish waters rather than carp; the equation with carp is attributed mainly to [[Jan Dubravius]] in the late 1530s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat change.** Medieval deforestation and plowing increased runoff, erosion and siltation. Sites cited include the Leine valley, the upper Thames, the Lys and Scarpe, Lac d'Annecy, the Oude Rhin mouth and the Vistula bay. Watermills multiplied (about 56 recorded in Domesday Book, 1085, versus a few hundred in England in the 880s, by the source's account; roughly 14 mills on the Aube in the eleventh century rising to about 200 by the early thirteenth). Dams created still water and reduced [[Migratory Fish]] access.

**Barriers and migration.** Mill dams blocked salmonid runs; a Scottish statute of 1214 required dam openings. Rhine delta dikes built in the eleventh and twelfth centuries are argued to have contributed to sturgeon decline.

**Urban and nutrient loading.** Sewage, tannery and textile effluents, and monastic latrines over streams increased nutrients. Konstanz, on Lake Constance, showed loss of clean-water plants and mass growth of a eutrophic filamentous alga in the 13th-16th centuries (Kuster 1989). Parisian effluent in the Seine was described as polluted by the early 1400s.

**Fishing pressure and shifting fish communities.** Sturgeon fell from 70% to 10% of fish consumed at 17 south Baltic sites between the eighth and twelfth/thirteenth centuries (Benecke 1986). Salmon catches in lower Normandy fell from 300-350 fish per year (1100-1300) to under 100 by 1400. Eel and common carp rose: at Sint-Salvators abbey, after about 1450, carp comprised 40% and eel 20% of fish. At Gaiselberg castle, carp made up 60% of fish bones from the twelfth to sixteenth centuries, mostly after 1400.

**Ecological effects of carp.** Carp foraging uproots vegetation and releases nutrients, increasing turbidity and eutrophication, to the disadvantage of clear-water species. The paper cites [[Welcomme]] (1984) and [[Taylor Et Al 1984]] on exotic fish impacts.

## Key Findings

- **Medieval economic change drove aquatic habitat change.** Clearance, erosion, mills, dams and urban waste reduced clear, running, and cool water and increased silt, nutrients and standing water.
- **Fish consumption shifted.** Before about 1200, diets were dominated by anadromous and freshwater species (e.g., the late eleventh-century Cluny list of six freshwater taxa against one marine taxon). Later, eel and carp increased while sturgeon, salmonids and pike-perch declined in body size and abundance.
- **Fishponds were the key response.** Fishpond construction began in the eleventh century and increased in the twelfth and thirteenth. Lay and ecclesiastical landowners both built them; Cistercians are shown to be no more innovative than lay neighbours.
- **Carp diffusion occurred in three phases:**
  1. Roughly seventh to eleventh centuries: slow spread up the Danube and into west-flowing middle Rhine tributaries, without evident fish culture.
  2. Twelfth to early fourteenth centuries: radiation across the lower Rhine/Maas, Paris basin and Burgundy, linked to fish culture.
  3. After the mid-fourteenth century (post-[[Black Death]]): extension to the periphery, including England, southern Scandinavia and east central Europe.
- **Carp arrived in Britain late.** The Duke of Norfolk's 1460s fishponds record the first documented carp in a specific British water. In France, Count Thibaut V of Champagne stocked carp at Igny-le-Jard in 1258.
- **Documented evidence base.** An inventory of 56 dated sub-fossil finds from 47 sites (Hoffmann 1994b), plus 36 verbal references before 1300, was updated with a second-to-fourth century Roman fort find at Iza and two additions (about 1100 and about 1450).
- **Origin claims rejected.** The paper finds an East Asian origin implausible, given neolithic carp in southeastern Europe, the absence of Chinese contact at the time, and differing pond-management practice. A direct Roman domestication is not supported; Roman fishponds were mainly coastal and marine, and carp are unambiguously absent from the Roman record.
- **Regional geography.** Fresh marine fish could be moved only about 150 km inland, so interior Europe developed a "carp belt" from Sologne and Berry to Oswiecim-Zator. England and Italy relied more on marine fish and aquatic species other than carp.
- **Impact of carp.** Carp became established in western Europe with little competition from medieval fauna. Their introduction is treated as ecologically significant, comparable to nineteenth-century North American introductions.
