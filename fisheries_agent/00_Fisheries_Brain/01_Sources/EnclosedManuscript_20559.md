# Fax Correspondence and Policy Opinion on Endangered Fish Propagation in the National Fish Hatchery System (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1994 facsimile from [[David M. Schleser]], Aquatic Biologist at the [[Dallas Aquarium]], to [[W.L. Minckley]] of Arizona State University transmits an unformatted manuscript (dated 11 May 1994) and a cover letter (dated 16 May 1994). The author argues that the [[National Fish Hatchery System]] (NFHS) should shift from gamefish production toward endangered species propagation, and that [[Private Hatcheries]] and [[Public Aquariums]] should be included in that effort. He identifies escapes, exotic introductions, and long-term institutional commitment as the main problems, and he says they are solvable. He also describes the Dallas Aquarium's own propagation work and supports [[Refugia]] built within a species' historic range.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Facsimile transmission with cover letter and a multi-page opinion manuscript. It is not a research study and reports no original data sets.
- **Sender:** [[David M. Schleser]], Aquatic Biologist, Department of Park and Recreation, [[Dallas Aquarium]], Dallas, TX.
- **Recipient:** [[W.L. Minckley]], PhD, Arizona State University, Department of Zoology, Tempe, AZ 85287-1501. The fax was copied to [[Paul Loiselle]], who had asked about Minckley's tasks for the NFHS review panel.
- **Purpose:** The cover letter says the author and Loiselle were considering a paper at the annual meeting of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (November) on desert fish and endangered species propagation programs.
- **Context:** The 1994 Dallas Aquarium pledge to visitors (safe, clean, fun, wholesome, inexpensive, family experience) appears on the first page.
- **Taxa referenced (common names only; no scientific names given):** desert fishes, Edwards Aquifer (TX) fishes, aquatic amphibians, sea-run salmon, sturgeons, and [[Paddlefish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Escapes and effluent:** Effluent from most traditional private fish hatcheries is discharged into the nearest waterway. The author notes contamination of some protected desert springs by escapes from upstream private catfish and bass hatcheries.
- **Exotic introductions:** Ecosystems must adjust to some degree to exotic species, and outcomes cannot be predicted. The author states that the tropical fish hobby, including tropical fish hatcheries, has been responsible for many deliberate and accidental introductions, some into habitats containing endangered species.
- **Disease and genetics:** Concern is raised that hatchery stocks may carry exotic parasites and pathogens. The paper also flags genetic management, inadvertent domestication, and hybridization.
- **Regulatory contrast:** Federal facilities have tighter controls on escapes. [[San Marcos Hatchery]] propagates only native species, and [[Dexter Hatchery]] handles effluent in a way that makes exotic introductions virtually impossible.
- **Habitat loss:** Many historic habitats have been modified so extensively by human activity that successful reintroduction is "out of the question." Texas has developed remote refugia near or within historic ranges, including the refugia at [[Balmorhea State Park]]. The species at that site are not legible in the OCR text.
- **Amphibian decline:** The author refers to a worldwide die-off of aquatic amphibians that he says is currently occurring.

## Key Findings

- **Shift in federal role:** The author argues that federal hatcheries are moving away from gamefish production toward technology and research centers focused on endangered species. He attributes this partly to changes in funding and to state hatcheries leading gamefish production. [[San Marcos Hatchery]] was transferred to the [[National Biological Survey]]; Dexter was reclassified as a technology center.
- **Recommendation for NFHS:** The NFHS should continue this change of direction and pursue endangered species propagation, especially for species of no direct economic importance.
- **Economic potential:** Some threatened or endangered species (sea-run salmon, sturgeons, paddlefish) may have economic and commercial value. Successful large-scale hatchery production and in-situ management could benefit both conservation and the economy, but long-term economic impacts should not become the overriding consideration.
- **Expanded partnerships:** "Private hatcheries" should include public aquariums and, in some cases, the organized hobby. Networking and pooling of efforts are described as a necessity given limited NFHS space, money, and manpower.
- **Two main problems, both described as solvable:**
  - *Escapes:* contamination from effluent and accidental release outside native ranges.
  - *Long-term commitment:* projects often depend on one or two employees and are abandoned when staff change.
- **Case-by-case permitting:** Permits for private hatcheries to propagate endangered species should be evaluated individually but encouraged.
- **Reintroduction caution:** Intentional release of endangered species outside their natural range raises unpredictable ecological outcomes and possible legal consequences for sport fisheries and recreation, especially if a "critical habitat" designation follows.
- **Refugia:** Species-specific refugia constructed within a species' historic range may be "the only hope" for some species. The author opposes introducing endangered species into existing systems where they are not native.
- **Dallas Aquarium track record:** The aquarium has propagated a number of rare North American desert and Edwards Aquifer (TX) fishes and aquatic amphibians for approximately four years. In that time, thousands of fish were raised and hundreds were distributed to other zoological institutions through the [[Freshwater Fish Taxon Advisory Group]] of the [[American Zoological Association]]. Work has been published and presented and carried out with [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Texas Parks and Wildlife Service]], and the University of Nuevo León, Mexico.
- **Agency cooperation:** The author reports "virtually complete cooperation" from federal and state agencies in obtaining permits and specimens.
- **Institutional commitment:** Non-charismatic species do not raise attendance or revenue, which reduces institutional commitment. The author believes public aquariums have underused grants and reputational benefits from conservation work.
- **Public education:** Aquariums should display and hold stocks of endangered species for education, even without propagation. Held stocks could serve as genetic reservoirs if a disaster affects NFHS stocks.
- **Biological rationale:** The high fecundity and short generation time of most fish mean that even short-term commitments can significantly benefit species survival.
