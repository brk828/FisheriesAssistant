# Evaluation of Tilapia Populations to Support a Commercial Fishery in the Lower Colorado River Area

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985–1986 final report from the [[University of Arizona]] (School of Renewable Natural Resources) assesses whether [[Tilapia]] populations in the lower [[Colorado River]] area near Yuma, Arizona, could support a commercial fishery. Contaminant levels in fish flesh were judged the highest-priority question. Composite fillet samples from two [[Drainage Canals]] and two [[Gila River]] sites were analyzed for metals and pesticides. Most contaminants were below detection limits, and no detected value exceeded the EPA action levels or guidelines cited. The authors caution that the small sample size does not rule out higher burdens elsewhere. They also assess incidental catch, public-use conflict, and site suitability, ranking the Colorado River least suitable and the drainage canals most suitable for commercial harvest.

## Background & Description

The project was requested after meetings with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. Its goal was to evaluate the potential for a viable commercial fishery on tilapia (primarily *Tilapia gulea* as printed in the document; the species name is partly garbled by OCR) in aquatic habitats near Yuma, including the Colorado River, the Gila River, and agricultural drainage canals. Beyond contaminant data, the report covers management considerations: harvest-suitable areas and practical constraints on commercial harvest.

The lower Gila River and the large drainage canals were considered the systems most likely to carry biocides and other contaminants from agricultural lands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions.** High salt and dissolved-solids concentrations in the drainage canals and lower Gila River effectively ruled out [[Electrofishing]]. The canals also had high water velocity and heavy loads of filamentous algae and debris.

**Fish fauna.** The drainage canals have the most depauperate fish fauna of the three systems. This is attributed to high salinity, lack of suitable habitat, and isolation from more natural areas. The Colorado and Gila rivers host a variety of valued sport fish, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[Common Carp]].

**Habitat structure.** Colorado and Gila river habitats have irregular bottoms and abundant structure (vegetation, debris, pilings, rocks, boulders). Tilapia aggregations in the canals were largest where lining was damaged or cracked, or where other structures provided refuge.

**Public use.** Angling and other public uses of the lower Colorado and Gila rivers are high, and the report expects user conflicts with any commercial operation.

## Key Findings

### Sampling and Methods

- **Sites (four):** [[Main Drain]] at San Luis; [[Salt Canal]] (Main Outlet Drain, near the Gila–Colorado confluence); Gila River 3.5–4.0 km above the Colorado confluence; and [[Castle Dome Wash]] at its confluence with the Gila River.
- **Design:** Two composite samples per drainage canal and two per Gila River site. Each composite held the flesh of three to five fish. This follows the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Resource Contaminant Assessment and [[EPA Priority Pollutant Monitoring]] approach.
- **Processing:** Fish were weighed and measured, and the left-side fillet was removed, wrapped in acetone-rinsed aluminum foil, and frozen in the field.
- **Capture gear:** Stationary nets (gill, block, trap, hoop) were impractical in the canals because of current and debris. The most productive method was seining with a large gill-net panel (minimum stretch mesh 2 in.). Table 1 records tilapia weights (roughly 100–670 g) and lengths (roughly 17–37 cm) by site and gear.

### Tissue Contaminant Results

- **Laboratory:** [[Western Technologies Inc.]] (Phoenix, Arizona) analyzed eight samples for metals and pesticides.
- **Mercury:** Not reported. It was lost during the tissue digest procedure.
- **Detection:** Concentrations of nearly all analytes were below detection limits at all sites. Lead was the most consistently detected metal, at low values across samples. Scattered low-level detections of other analytes appear in Table 2, with inconsistent units (ng/kg in the summary table, mg/kg in some lab sheets).
- **Analytes tested:** Arsenic, beryllium, cadmium, lead, selenium, [[DDT]] and its metabolites (DDE, DDD), toxaphene, aldrin, dieldrin, endrin, and heptachlor epoxide.
- **Standards:** No value exceeded [[EPA Action Levels]] or the guidelines recommended by the National Academy of Sciences and National Academy of Engineers (1973).
- **Sample insufficiency:** Some Salt Canal samples were too small to digest.
- **Quality control:** Duplicate and digest spike recoveries were reported for several analytes in the appendix. Values are OCR-garbled in places.

### Conclusions on Contamination

- The authors reasoned that unacceptably contaminated flesh would make a commercial fishery unwarranted. The data showed no indication of high contaminant levels.
- The small number and size of samples do not rule out high burdens in some local areas.
- Tilapia generally showed lower contaminant levels than other fish collected in the same waters, according to [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] personnel. The authors caution that these results should not be generalized to other species likely to be captured alongside tilapia.

### Incidental Catch

- Incidental catch risk depends on the non-target species present and the selectivity of harvest gear.
- The drainage canals would have the least incidental catch if only they were opened to harvest.
- In the Gila River, crews captured more than two non-target fish for each tilapia netted (Table 3). The Colorado River was expected to have an unfavorable catch ratio.
- Hoop, trap, or similar maze gear would allow safer release of incidental catch. These could not be used in the high-flow canals and would require constant attention in heavily used Gila and Colorado areas. Their efficacy for tilapia was untested.
- [[Entangling Nets]] (gill nets, trammel nets) would be ineffective and impractical in the canals, and would probably catch many non-target species in the main rivers.
- [[Towed Gear]] (seining, trawling) would be very difficult in the rivers because of structure. It might be most effective in concrete-lined canals.

### Conflicts with Public Use

- Anglers are likely to blame any decline in sport harvest on commercial operators, regardless of the actual scale of incidental catch.
- Commercial techniques not open to sport anglers could also make enforcement harder.

### Site Suitability

- Suitability depends on tilapia abundance, abundance of valued sport fish, and public-use levels.
- **Ranking (least to most suitable):** Colorado River, then Gila River, then drainage canals.
- **Colorado River:** Harvest records (Jacobson 1981; Marsh 1983) show no indication that tilapia are abundant. The river supports many sport fish and has heavy public use.
- **Gila River:** Tilapia abundance is probably higher than in the Colorado River because the Gila is slower and warmer. Substantial numbers of largemouth bass were captured, and public access is heavy at easy-access points.
- **Drainage canals:** Few other species live or reproduce there, so commercial harvest is least likely to affect other fish or public use. However, the abundance and growth rate of tilapia in the large canals are unknown.

### Overall Recommendation

- The authors call for further research on whether the productive habitat in the large drainage canals and the standing stock of tilapia can sustain a strong, long-term commercial harvest, provided other considerations do not preclude it.
- Successful commercial fisheries typically harvest abundant, highly productive species that range widely. Only a fraction of the standing crop is taken each year, yet this still represents a relatively large catch.
