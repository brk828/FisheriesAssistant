# Trip Report: Cold Spring Seep, August 7, 1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae #conservation-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This trip report documents a status check of two artificial ponds at [[Cold Spring Seep]], a Bureau of Land Management (BLM) site near Safford, Arizona, conducted on August 7, 1990, by USFWS biologist Sally Stefferud. The ponds were constructed in the early 1980s and stocked with two federally listed desert fish species: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) in 1985, and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) in April 1990. The northern pond was found to be healthy, with abundant, actively breeding topminnow and evidence of reproducing pupfish. The southern pond had nearly dried up and appeared to have lost both species (extirpation), attributed to an insufficient water source rather than BLM action. The report concludes that no immediate management action is warranted beyond continued monitoring of the northern pond, and recommends against continued reliance on the southern pond for native fish conservation.

## Background & Description

Limited morphological detail is provided in this field report, but the following qualitative observations were recorded:

- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*):
  - Observed across a "large range of sizes," indicating a healthy, multi-cohort population.
  - **Black males** were noted as abundant — a coloration associated with breeding condition/dominance in poeciliid males.
  - Field identification distinguished topminnow from the visually similar, non-native **mosquitofish** (*Gambusia* sp.) via dip-netting and direct examination, confirming species purity at the site.

- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*):
  - **Large blue males** were observed patrolling the center of the pond — the blue coloration is characteristic of breeding-condition male pupfish.
  - Females and smaller males exhibited a distinct spatial/behavioral pattern, staying near pond edges rather than the center.
  - **Juvenile pupfish** were captured via dip-net, confirming multiple size classes were present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Two small artificial ponds constructed at a natural desert seep on BLM land, functioning as refugial habitat for two imperiled native fish taxa.
- **Northern Pond**:
  - Water clear, pond full, with active outflow — indicating a robust, functioning water source.
  - Supported a stable, breeding population of [[Gila Topminnow]] and a smaller but reproducing population of [[Desert Pupfish]].
  - Spatial partitioning observed in pupfish: large males held central territories, while females/small males occupied pond margins — suggestive of typical pupfish breeding-territory behavior.
- **Southern Pond**:
  - Severely reduced water level (less than 6 inches deep), slightly murky with an orange cast, and dominated by extensive submergent vegetation — signs of a degrading, drying habitat.
  - No fish detected despite approximately 10 minutes of visual observation and active dip-netting.
  - Believed to represent a **local extirpation event** of both [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] due to natural water source insufficiency, not anthropogenic disturbance.
- **Regulatory context**: Per the December 1, 1990 Biological Opinion governing pupfish stocking at this site, fish loss due to natural drying or water source failure (absent direct BLM action) does not constitute "incidental take" and requires no mitigation.

## Key Findings

- [[Gila Topminnow]] population in the northern pond was abundant, size-diverse, and actively breeding as of August 1990.
- [[Desert Pupfish]], stocked only four months prior (approximately 150 individuals, roughly two-thirds into the northern pond), were present but not abundant in the northern pond; juvenile recruitment confirmed successful reproduction.
- Species identity of topminnow was field-verified against non-native **mosquitofish** to rule out misidentification/contamination.
- The southern pond has likely lost both fish species due to inadequate water supply; the seep source feeding this pond is deemed insufficient to sustain fish through dry periods.
- Author's recommendation: continue routine monitoring of the northern pond; no corrective management action needed at this time; southern pond considered unsuitable long-term habitat for native fish conservation.
- Report circulated to key regional stakeholders in desert fish conservation, including BLM (Safford), Arizona Game and Fish Department, Arizona State University researchers ([[W.L. Minckley]], [[Paul Marsh]]), and Dexter National Fish Hatchery — reflecting this site's role within a broader interagency desert fish recovery program.
