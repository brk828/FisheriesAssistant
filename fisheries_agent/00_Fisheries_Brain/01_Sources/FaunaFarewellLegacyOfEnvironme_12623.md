# Farewell to Environmental Abuse: Water Management, Desert Fishes, and the Decline of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Fall 1992 [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) feature, the second part of a series on environmental abuse, reports on how water management in the arid West is driving the loss of native fishes, birds, and riparian habitat. ASU researchers [[W. L. Minckley]], [[Michael E. Douglas]], and [[Robert Ohmart]] attribute the decline mainly to federal water development and to non-native species, and they call for habitat protection and conservation action. A [[Cambridge Reports Research International]] / [[Morrison Institute For Public Policy]] survey of 805 Arizona residents reports strong public support for environmental protection, though it was funded by [[Browning Ferris Industries]], a solid-waste company. The source is a journalistic article, not a peer-reviewed study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Desert Fishes
- About one-third of North America's roughly 810 native freshwater fishes live in the arid West (Minckley and Douglas).
- Nearly half of the 224 fish taxa listed as threatened or endangered, or under consideration for listing, live in western waters. This figure excludes 17 western native fishes that have gone extinct in recent decades.
- Of the 80 fish species in Arizona waters, only 30 are native. "All but about five are on some kind of endangered list" (Minckley).
- The [[Colorado River]] south of [[Lake Mohave]] is described as the only major river in North America with no native fishes left.
- Western native fishes appear especially vulnerable to [[Non-Native Species]] because of their antiquity and isolation. They may lack the competitive abilities and predator defenses of fishes in more species-rich areas.

### Lower Colorado River Birds and Habitat
- The lower Colorado River forms about 300 miles of the Arizona border with Nevada and California.
- The area supports more than 400 bird species, including the yellow-billed cuckoo, vermilion flycatcher, and elf owl.
- Birds were chosen as indicators because they "respond quickly to environmental changes and are excellent indicators of ecological health and change."
- Mature [[Cottonwood-Willow Forest]] is described as among the richest bird habitats in North America.
- Fewer than 200 acres of cottonwood-willow forest remain (Ohmart's estimate). Flooding by river-management agencies and salt poisoning have destroyed most native vegetation.
- [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix*), which moved from the Gila River, is now the most dominant tree species on the Colorado River. Ohmart says water management favors it over native trees.

### Causes of Salinity and Water Loss
- In [[Lake Mead]] alone, almost 1 million acre-feet of water evaporate each year.
- Salt from evaporation leaches into the river through irrigation return drains. Natural springs and native soils contribute additional salt.
- By the time water reaches Yuma, it must be desalinated for agriculture in Mexico.

### Public Opinion (Arizona Survey, April 1992)
- Survey sample: 805 Arizona residents, random-sample telephone survey by Cambridge Reports; 81% of respondents lived in Maricopa and Pima counties. Margin of error: ±3.4%.
- The survey included 65 questions and a 43-page report, "Arizonans and the Environment: Attitudes Toward the Key Environmental Issues Facing the State."

## Key Findings

### Causes of Decline (Scientists' Views)
1. **Federal water development is the primary problem.** Minckley, Douglas, and Ohmart identify federal water projects, including dams, diversions, irrigation works, and power plants, as the main drivers of habitat loss. Udall writes that these projects are increasingly demanding more water.
2. **Human-introduced non-native species compound the threat.** Competition, predation, and other interactions with non-natives are recognized as major factors in native fish decline.
3. **Conservation is possible but unevenly applied.** Minckley and Deacon argue that scientists now know how to protect endangered fishes through habitat protection, relocation, and hatchery breeding. They describe federal agencies' enthusiasm for Endangered Species Act duties as "varying degrees." Under the [[Endangered Species Act Of 1973]], both state and federal agencies could pursue habitat preservation, since the government owns most western lands.

### Bird and Habitat Decline
4. **Bird populations are declining sharply.** From 1976 to 1986, seven river-habitat bird species that were common or abundant at the turn of the century declined by 32% to 57%.
5. **Projected extirpations.** Ohmart predicts that by the year 2000, eight to 10 bird species "will be extirpated from the lower Colorado." He also says the area "will be dead" in about 50 years.
6. **Research basis.** The [[Birds Of The Lower Colorado River Valley]] study ran mostly from 1972 to 1984 and was funded by $1.5 million in contracts from the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]]. Ohmart calls it one of the most detailed ecological projects in the world.
7. **Restoration potential.** Two 70-acre plots revegetated by ASU scientists during the 12-year study remain among the richest wildlife sites on the river. The [[Havasu Wildlife Refuge]] is cited as a positive example of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service efforts to restore cottonwood-willow stands.

### Policy Positions and Recommendations
8. **Recommended actions:** Save critical habitat, relocate fishes to safer locations, or breed them in hatcheries. Ohmart's 1988 report calls for state and federal action to preserve the river's ecology.
9. **Warning:** Without action, the Colorado River "may simply become a barren ditch," and environmental pressure must be "focused and organized" soon.
10. **Agency progress:** Most western states now employ at least one biologist responsible for native non-game fishes, which the authors contrast with the situation 20 years earlier.
11. **Criticism of management:** Ohmart faults Bureau of Reclamation policies under President Reagan and Interior Secretary James Watt for much of the recent destruction ("a travesty").

### Public Attitudes (Survey Results)
12. **Perceived environmental decline:** 53% of Arizonans rated environmental quality as worse than five years earlier.
13. **Priority issues:** 37% named air pollution as the top environmental priority. Waste disposal and water pollution each received 17%.
14. **Regulation:** 73% favor stricter air-pollution regulations even if they cost jobs. 60% say there is too little governmental regulation of environmental protection. A slim majority favor a command-and-control approach over free-market solutions.
15. **Rivers and habitat:** 80% favor designating remaining free-flowing rivers and streamside habitats as "wild and scenic." 74% would do so even if preservation restricts privately owned land.
16. **Economy versus environment:** 58% believe economic growth need not conflict with environmental quality. If forced to choose, 61% would sacrifice economic growth and 15% would sacrifice the environment. 57% say more jobs are created than lost by preserving the environment; 28% say the opposite.
17. **Willingness to pay:** Respondents said they are "willing to pay higher consumer prices to preserve the environment."
18. **Self-rated behavior:** 58% recycle; 51% buy recycled products; 46% reduce water use; 45% conserve energy; 21% frequently cut back on driving.
19. **Performance ratings:** 80% or more rated the environmental performance of the federal government, large companies, and the average American as fair or poor. Only 51% rated themselves that low.

### Key Limitation
20. The article is a popular news feature. Its claims are drawn from interviews, books, and a survey funded by an industry sponsor. The findings are presented as the authors' views and have not been independently verified in this source.
