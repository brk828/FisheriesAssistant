# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center: 1993 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #endangered-fishes #catostomidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 annual report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center (Dexter NFH & TC), in Dexter, New Mexico, covers station operations, staffing, culture results, health problems, cooperation, outreach, facilities, and future needs for the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] endangered fishes program. In FY 93 the station held and cultured 16 federally listed threatened or endangered species. Its work centers on propagation for recovery and reintroduction, with research on temperature, parasites, and genetics. The report also records water-rights problems, the loss of the artesian well, and a request for a genetics program and more staff and construction funds.

## Background & Description

The station is in the Pecos River Valley of northern Chihuahuan Desert country, at an elevation of 3,500 feet, with about 12 inches of annual rainfall and a 180–200 day growing season. It was authorized under the White-Sands-era Emergency Relief Act and built in 1932 on land of the New Mexico State Game and Fish Commission. Following passage of the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, the station was selected to lead recovery work for imperiled fishes of the Southwest. It was designated a Technology Center in FY 91, one of only six in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

Fish culture water comes from five shallow aquifer wells (150 ft deep, combined 2,500 gpm). The water is about 64°F, alkaline (pH 7.5–8.5), with total hardness averaging 210 mg/L and total dissolved solids of 1,500 mg/L. Facilities include a hatchery building, raceways, fiberglass tanks, and about 22 surface acres of ponds, of which 23 are now lined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report states that the Southwest's native freshwater fauna has been greatly reduced by water diversion, nonnative species, and habitat loss. Of 600 freshwater fish species in the United States, 88 are listed as threatened or endangered. Sixteen of these were held or cultured at Dexter in FY 93. Apart from four species for Region 1, all come from New Mexico or neighboring states and Mexico.

Several stressors are documented:
- **Parasites:** [[Anchor Worm]] (*Lernaea* sp.) infested the newly received suckers; [[Ich]] (*Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*) repeatedly affected Woundfin; tapeworms (*Bothriocephalus*) were present in Beautiful Shiner, Woundfin, Virgin River and Pahranagat Roundtail Chub; Yellow grub occurred at low levels.
- **Water quality:** Nitrogen supersaturation caused losses in Colorado Pikeminnow.
- **Habitat loss:** Low flows and irrigation releases at Clear Lake, California, led to a 1991 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service "jeopardy opinion" for two suckers.

## Key Findings

### Station Operations and Personnel
- Staff in FY 93 numbered eight positions, including a Center Director and a Geneticist. Dr. J. Holt Williamson was selected as Geneticist on September 18, 1993.
- The station held and cultured 16 listed species during FY 93, with no dedicated endangered species funding; all operations were paid from the Fish Hatchery O&M subactivity.
- A 1993 Congressional appropriation of $1.4 million supported design and construction of new administrative, lab, and genetics facilities.

### Razorback Sucker
- On March 3, 1993, 20 females were spawned using hCG. A total of 114,985 eggs gave a mean of 5,749 eggs per female.
- Swimup fry totaled 27,825, a 19.5% egg-to-swimup survival.
- Of 903 adults distributed, many were released into Lake Havasu. Distribution totaled more than 73,000 fry and 615 fingerlings.
- Scoliosis, lordosis, and cranial disparity appeared at high frequency. The report suspects an environmental cause, probably faulty incubator heating, but genetic links are also possible.
- Stocking size was changed in 1991 to a minimum of 15 inches for razorbacks and initially 8 inches for squawfish. The station maintained 254 domestic broodfish.

### Colorado Pikeminnow
- 18 females were spawned on June 2, 1993, with carp pituitary. Eggs totaled 2,231,931, averaging 322,405 per female. Swimup fry averaged 195 per gram.
- An imprinting study using morpholine and phenethyl alcohol was in its second phase.
- Many fish from 1990–91 year classes showed cranial problems after repeated nitrogen supersaturation events.

### Bonytail Chub
- 18 females and 47 males were spawned on April 28, 1993. Total eggs were 568,847, averaging 31,603 per female. Swimup fry totaled 160,242, a 33.4% survival rate.
- The station maintained 400 domestic F1 broodfish.

### Woundfin
- Woundfin were maintained in raceways and lined ponds. A Malachite Green/Formalin treatment was used, but it was banned by the FDA in 1991, which removed the main chemical control for Ich.
- A 1993 heat-treatment experiment used temperatures up to 35°C. Survival dropped with peak temperature (mean maximum of 34°C in dead fish vs. 31.5°C in survivors; r = -0.8, p < 0.01). Results were inconclusive regarding parasite control, but heat delayed and extended the spawning season, with 257 fry produced from treated fish.
- A 1993 pond spawned about 60 adults and 4,000 young-of-year fish.

### Warner Sucker, Lost River Sucker, and Shortnose Sucker
- In 1991, 75 Warner Suckers, 24 Lost River Suckers, and 85 Shortnose Suckers were transported to Dexter after a Bureau of Reclamation emergency salvage. Many shortnose suckers died during parasite treatment.
- In FY 93, surviving Lost River and Shortnose Suckers were sampled for genetics and aging, with tissues sent to the Bureau of Reclamation.
- Spawning attempts with Warner Suckers in March failed to obtain any ova. The station entered FY 94 with 27 adults.

### Chihuahua Chub, Pahranagat Roundtail Chub, and Virgin River Roundtail Chub
- 450 Chihuahua Chub were stocked into the Mimbres River tributary (Whitewater Creek) in October 1992. The population survived a flood, and 141 adults were maintained.
- Pahranagat Roundtail Chub produced 21,629 swimup fry in 1991. The station maintained 75 adults plus fingerlings.
- Virgin River Roundtail Chub recruitment was lower in Hypalon-lined ponds than in earthen ponds. 109 adults were held over winter.

### Yaqui Catfish
- Of 201 surviving captive fish, 152 were pure Yaqui Catfish, 30 were hybrid intergrades, 5 were backcrosses, and 14 were unresolved.
- 31 pure fish were returned to Mexico in June 1992. About 112 pure Yaqui Catfish were held at the station.
- Spawning trials in 1993 with hormone-induced ovulation and pen, pond, and raceway methods all failed. The authors suggest the trials started too late.

### Desert Pupfish, Leon Springs Pupfish, Gila Topminnow, and Big Bend Gambusia
- Leon Springs Pupfish performed well, with over 5,000 individuals moved to winter ponds. Desert Pupfish recovery was low (250 fish).
- Emergency rescue from Diamond Y Spring was carried out after a Texaco pipeline rupture.
- Shipments included 150 Desert Pupfish and 900 Leon Springs Pupfish to EPA Gulf Breeze, FL, and over 1,000 Gila Topminnow were held.

### Fish Health and Specimens
- The report recommends closer health screening of new introductions, because Lernaea is often introduced from wild-caught fish.
- The demand for preserved and frozen specimens remains high.

### Cooperation, Outreach, and Facilities
- The station worked with more than 20 federal, state, tribal, and Mexican agencies and universities.
- About 2,400 visitors toured the hatchery; seven guided tours were given to school and community groups.
- Since 1991, lined ponds have been expanded to 23, with about five of the remaining 27 scheduled for lining.

### Future Outlook and Funding Requests
- Dexter's water rights appear fairly secure, although junior water rights from after the mid-1940s could be affected by litigation.
- The artesian water right (318 acre-feet) was traded for shallow water. The Bureau of Reclamation bought 390 acre-feet of shallow water for the trade.
- A Genetic Management Plan is proposed for all Region 2 threatened and endangered fishes. Start-up genetics lab funding was requested at $125,000 (or $200,000 for a full lab) for FY 94–95.
- Staff requests include 1.5 FTE, a GS-7/9 lab technician, and an additional $520,000 for FY 95 operations, plus $57,200 for remaining construction.
