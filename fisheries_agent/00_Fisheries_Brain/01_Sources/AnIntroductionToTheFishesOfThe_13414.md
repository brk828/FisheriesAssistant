# An Introduction to the Fishes of the Genus Nothobranchius (Part I–III)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This three-part series by [[Bruce J. Turner]], published in *African Wild Life*, introduces the African annual killifish genus [[Nothobranchius]]. The genus is described as one of the least-studied vertebrate groups in Africa, and the only true annual fishes on the African landmass. The article covers taxonomic history, morphology and sexual dimorphism, annual life history and habitat, geographic distribution, collecting and transport methods, laboratory propagation and feeding, mating behaviour, and threats from habitat destruction. Part III includes a non-fish item on a Tanganyika rhino translocation and a note on the journal *Koedoe*, which the source includes as unrelated material.

## Background & Description

**History.** [[W. G. H. Peters]] named *Cyprinodon orthonotus* in 1844 from a fish collected near Beira, Mozambique. In 1868 Peters placed it in the new genus *Nothobranchius*. The genus was later recognised as valid in 1929, largely through the work of [[G. S. Myers]] and [[E. Ahl]], with approximately fourteen described species at that time.

**Relationship to Aphyosemion.** The source's Table 1, following Myers, separates *Nothobranchius* from the closely related *Aphyosemion* by head depth relative to width, preorbital bone size relative to eye diameter, and dorsal fin position. The author notes that on morphological grounds the separation is largely artificial, but he suggests it be kept for convenience because *Nothobranchius* is more uniform and recognisable.

**Morphology.** Fish are heavy-bodied and elongate, with high dorsal and anal fins and a rounded caudal fin. Adults seldom exceed 80 mm total length and are usually under 60 mm. Males are described as the most highly coloured freshwater fishes in Africa, with red, blue and yellow pigments and metallic blue, silver and green iridescence. Females are mostly drab, with metallic splotches on the opercle or a few dots. Sexual dimorphism is said to peak in this genus.

**Taxonomic revision.** The author was conducting a revision using electrophoresis, egg membrane-pattern analysis, population hybridisation and osteological comparison. He expected it to take at least five years, with live material procurement as a major obstacle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Annual life cycle.** The habitats are temporary, holding water only in the rainy season and often drying completely in the dry season. Eggs survive buried in the mud for up to six to eight months, hatch with the next rains, and fry can mature and spawn in eight to ten weeks. Some eggs survive several dry seasons.

**Habitat variety.** The source argues against the stereotype of an isolated seasonal pool. Some populations depend on lake rises (e.g. *N. orthonotus* in south-eastern Nyasaland), seasonal rivers, freshwater swamps, or pits dug for building clay on Zanzibar (7 to 9 feet deep). Waters are generally stagnant or semi-stagnant. Lowland flooding can mix *Nothobranchius* with lungfish, [[Clarias]] catfish and [[Tilapia]].

**Predators and competitors.** When dry-season waters recede, predatory fish can enter. In 1963 heavy rains let a stream overflow into the Kruger National Park pond, and predators such as *Barbus trimaculatus* and *Clarias gariepinus* probably eliminated the young of *N. orthonotus*. Dense crowding late in the dry season is thought to cause starvation deaths.

**Water chemistry (Table 2).** Two samples are compared. The Beira ditch was sampled late in the dry season, and the Kruger National Park pool was sampled in the rainy season. The source notes the table shows concentration changes through evaporation. Reported values for the Transvaal sample include pH 6.7, chloride 59 ppm, sodium 38 ppm, calcium 7 ppm, magnesium 6 ppm, fluoride 0.25 ppm, and total solids 145 ppm. Other values were not obtainable.

**Geographic distribution.** Species have been described from Natal, Mozambique, Zanzibar, Tanganyika, Somalia, Katanga, Chad, northern Nigeria, Gambia and the Seychelles. Populations are also known from Transvaal, Nyasaland, Northern Rhodesia, Kenya, Uganda and Sudan. East Africa, especially the coastal plain from northern Natal to central Somalia, is the most productive area. The author predicts many more populations will be found.

**Threats.** Swamp drainage, DDT spraying and road building threaten these fish. Populations near Dar-es-Salaam and Kisumu, Kenya, were reported destroyed. In northern Natal, sugar cane conversion of the Mkuzi River swamps made the rediscovery of *N. mkuziensis* "less than nil." The author suggests re-settling populations, laboratory maintenance, or giving them to aquarists.

**Similar fish.** In East Africa, [[Micropanchax]] killifishes often occur alongside *Nothobranchius*. They can be told apart by their almost total lack of pigment, translucent body, smaller size, weaker scale pattern, and higher pectoral fin placement.

## Key Findings

- *Nothobranchius* is described as the only true annual fish genus on the African continent. The other true annuals are neotropical (*Cynolebias*, *Pterolebias*, *Austrofundulus*, *Rachovia*).
- Lifespan is reported as usually not exceeding 14 to 15 months, even under good care.
- Males readily cross-mate with females of other species and related genera such as *Aphyosemion* and *Cynolebias*. Hybridisation experiments are therefore easy.
- **Collection:** Adult specimens for preservation are best taken several weeks into the dry season. Live specimens are best collected toward the end of the rainy season. Males are bellicose, so sexes should be separated. Medium-sized fish survive transport best.
- **Anaesthesia:** [[Quinaldine]], a coal tar derivative, is effective at 7 ppm. It reduces oxygen demand and male aggression, with rapid recovery.
- **Laboratory care:** Fish need little special care. Light and white-bottomed tanks should be avoided because they bleach fish. One third of the water should be changed every fortnight. Diet includes mosquito larvae and pupae (ideal), beef heart, Tubifex, and Drosophila.
- **Disease:** [[Oodinium]] ("velvet") is the main threat and is usually fatal within 3 to 7 days. Treatment with a one drop per litre dose of 10% [[Malachite Green]] solution worked well, with [[Acriflavine]] as a less effective alternative.
- **Breeding method:** The author favours the method of R. S. LaCorte using fine silicate sand and zeolite. Eggs are sieved from the substrate, then incubated in specially prepared, dyed and boiled peat moss. Hatching begins about 100 minutes after water is added. Optimum incubation is tied to the length of the natural dry season.
- **Resting eggs:** Some eggs stay undeveloped, or start development after many months. Development can pause at almost any stage.
- **Rearing:** The first food is the rotifer *Brachionus rubens* (or *Philodina*), then newly hatched [[Artemia]] nauplii. Males are removed early to prevent aggression toward females.
- **Spawning:** The male courts with fin displays, undulation, and chasing, then uses his under-jaw and pelvic region to force the female to the bottom. An egg is released in about one of every three spawning acts. Sperm remains viable for about 24 minutes. Well-fed females can produce up to about 45 eggs per day for up to three weeks. A male can service three or four females.
- **Part III, non-fish item:** The source also reports that the Tanganyika Game Division moved rhinos to Rubondo Island, about 55,000 acres in size, with three rhinos released so far. One calved after arrival.
