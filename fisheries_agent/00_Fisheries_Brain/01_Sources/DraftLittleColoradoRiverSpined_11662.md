# Little Colorado River Spinedace Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #conservation-biology #recovery-plan #threatened-species

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan addresses [[Lepidomeda vittata]] (Little Colorado River Spinedace), a federally listed threatened species restricted to north-flowing tributaries of the [[Little Colorado River]] in eastern Arizona. The species was described by Cope (1874) and is a member of the tribe Plagopterini. Listed as threatened in 1987, the spinedace has experienced dramatic population fluctuations and range contractions due to habitat loss, habitat modification, competition and predation from non-native fishes, and their parasites. Critical Habitat includes 31 miles of [[East Clear Creek]], 8 miles of [[Chevelon Creek]], and 5 miles of [[Nutrioso Creek]]. The recovery objective is delisting through securing all extant populations, establishing refugia within historic range, and re-establishing populations that persist for a minimum of ten years.

## Background & Description

### Morphology and Identification

[[Lepidomeda vittata]] exhibits the following diagnostic characteristics: mouth moderately oblique; strong second "spine" of dorsal fin; dorsal fin moderately high and acute (depressed length 2.0-2.3 in predorsal length); anal fin-rays eight (rarely nine); scales in lateral line usually >90, generally embedded and difficult to count; pharyngeal teeth in two rows (1 or 2, 4-4, 1 or 2); vertebrae number 41-43. Adults generally <100 mm total length.

Sexual dimorphism is minimal. Males' pectoral fins consistently extend beyond pelvic fin insertion, while females' are shorter. Breeding males develop intense reddish-orange or weak yellow-orange at bases of paired fins; females show watery yellowish or reddish-orange coloration. Males develop tubercles on bodies during breeding season.

Live coloration: nearly vertical dark lines extending dorsally from midside shine like polished silver; venter white; upper sides olivaceous; back olivaceous, bluish or lead grey; fine black puncticulations overlay silvery sides; conspicuous cream-colored spots at dorsal fin origin and near terminal ray bases when viewed from above; scales show light bluish to greenish-brass reflections.

### Distribution

**Historical**: Original collections from Little Colorado River between mouth of [[Zuni River]] and [[Sierra Blanca]] (White Mountain). May have occurred in New Mexico in Zuni watershed south of Gallup (no records confirm). Prior to 1939, only four collection records existed. Miller (1961) stated no reason to doubt the species was once abundant in mainstem upper Little Colorado River and cool tributaries ([[Silver Creek]], [[Showlow Creek]], [[Chevelon Creek]], [[Clear Creek]]) rising on northern slopes of White Mountains and [[Mogollon Rim]].

By 1960, extensive collecting revealed extirpation throughout known range except single specimen from [[Clear Creek]]. 1961 surveys found large population in [[East Clear Creek]]. Between 1963-1966, spinedace reappeared in much of former range. Populations again declined, considered endangered by 1973. New population discovered in lower [[Chevelon Creek]] in 1977.

**Present (1994)**: Five populations confirmed in 1983 surveys at 11 localities. Collections in 1990s continued to find spinedace in Little Colorado River mainstem, [[East Clear Creek]], [[Chevelon Creek]], two new localities in [[Nutrioso Creek]] (Apache County), and new population in [[Rudd Creek]] (tributary of Nutrioso). Population levels in [[East Clear Creek]] declined markedly since 1983; species again considered rare in that system.

### Habitat Requirements

Spinedace occupy permanent flowing stream sections with varied substrates (sand, gravel, rocks, boulders, silt, bedrock). Water color varies from greenish brown to clear. Stream width averages 15 m; fish taken in water up to 2 m deep. Prefer midwater portions of clear flowing pools of medium depth with fine gravel bottoms. Avoid deepest heavily shaded pools and relatively shallow open areas; predominate in pools that are open but have undercut banks or boulders for cover.

During high discharge, species becomes more widely distributed with adults at upper ends of pools and shallow lower ends of riffles. During spate conditions, occur in eddies lateral to current. Recent studies document wide tolerance for physico-chemical factors. Found in pools with slow to moderate current adjacent to riffles; water depths 16-88 cm over bedrock, sand, gravel, cobble and mud substrates.

In [[Nutrioso Creek]], significant differences in light penetration between upper (absorption coefficient 0.59) and lower (1.81) reaches. Nearly three-fold difference in alkalinity between upper (111 mg/L) and lower (328 mg/L) sites demonstrates tolerance for varied water chemistry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Life History

**Reproduction**: Limited spawning observations exist. C. Hubbs observed presumed males following apparent females and nibbling about vent. Eggs presumably randomly deposited over bottom or on aquatic vegetation/debris. Spawning occurs early summer to early autumn (June-July based on 1983 length-frequency distributions). Females with mature eggs collected only during May; fish taken June-July lacked eggs.

High proportions of females with mature ovaries found in both May and October suggest females may spawn more than once annually. Three size classes of eggs observed; fecundity estimates 650-1000 total eggs. Egg diameters range 20-50 microns at first discernable stage to 1.0-1.28 mm for mature eggs. Fish <50 mm total length lack mature eggs; generally found in fish >64 mm. When gonadalsomatic values approach 20%, >90% of eggs are mature.

**Growth/Survival**: Preliminary data indicates hatchlings are 6-8 mm total length, reaching 25 mm in one month. After 10 weeks fish are 50-60 mm, reaching 75-80 mm by end of first year. Collection of original transplants in Arboretum pond indicates they live at least three years.

**Genetics**: Mitochondrial DNA and allozyme surveys indicate considerable population structure with most variation found among samples. Distribution of variation suggests three distinct populations within Little Colorado River drainage. All populations should be maintained to conserve genetic variation (Tibbets et al. 1994).

### Food Habits

Stomach analyses from [[Nutrioso Creek]] and [[Chevelon Creek]] indicate spinedace are predators. Aquatic insects, primarily chironomid larvae and ephemeropteran nymphs, most common in May. July diets include cladocerans, detritus, Heteroptera, Coleoptera, filamentous green algae, and terrestrial invertebrates (Formicidae, Diptera, Thysanoptera, Crustacea). Smaller fish contain higher percentage of small aquatic insects. October diets include terrestrial insects, chironomid larvae, corixidae, elmidae. January diets dominated by adult dipterans, Formicidae, Coleoptera, chironomid larvae, plecopteran nymphs, corixidae; no terrestrial insects identified.

### Co-occurring Fishes

**Native species**: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) - most common co-occurring species; [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*); [[Little Colorado River Sucker]] (*Catostomus* sp.); [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) - now rare; [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*) - historical occurrence.

**Introduced species**: [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*L. cyanellus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus zebrinus*). All are potential predators on spinedace eggs/larvae or adults, particularly salmonids, sunfish, and catfish.

### Reasons for Decline

**Population Fluctuations**: Documenting decline complicated by dramatic population fluctuations - very abundant at times then essentially disappearing. Fluctuations observed between years, months, and days. Changes between years attributed to climatic conditions (drought, increased rainfall). Shorter duration variations remain unexplained but may be unique characteristic historically beneficial for exploiting/evacuating habitats quickly. Human-related habitat changes may have reduced effectiveness of this strategy.

**Habitat Modification**: Undocumented but significant impacts from: 1) reduction in stream discharge from dam construction on Little Colorado River; 2) alteration in flow patterns; 3) changes in sedimentation. Dam construction occurred on [[Chevelon Creek]], [[Willow Creek]], [[East Clear Creek]], [[Silver Creek]], plus numerous lakes and diversions throughout watershed. Most obvious impact is flow reduction and direct habitat loss.

**Sedimentation**: Increased sedimentation rates from logging practices (improper road construction/maintenance) and overgrazing by ungulates adversely impact spinedace. Modification of existing channels and habitat by increased runoff destroys permanent flowing stream habitats over varied substrates.

**Chemical Treatment**: Manipulation of fish populations by piscicide no longer widely practiced. However, treatment of upper [[Chevelon Creek]] with piscicide twice in late 1960s is suspected cause for spinedace extirpation from that area.

**Non-native Fish Impacts**: Impact of introduced fishes can be subtle (displacement from preferred habitat), indirect (competition), or direct (predation). [[Red Shiner]] implicated in decline of two other Plagopterines: [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Theories include invasion of: 1) previously unoccupied niches; 2) vacant niches left by extirpated native minnows; 3) areas occupied by native species with consequent displacement through competition/predation.

Laboratory and in situ experiments demonstrate high [[Rainbow Trout]] predation rates on spinedace even with natural refuges and abundant macroinvertebrate prey present. Spinedace show little predator avoidance, suggesting limited evolutionary interaction with large non-native piscivores. Rainbow trout significantly influence spinedace distribution and behavior; may be responsible for disjunct distribution. High turbidity habitats appear to be few places where co-occurrence possible, but preliminary experiments in lower [[Nutrioso Creek]] suggest even elevated turbidity doesn't prevent significant trout predation impact. [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] further exacerbate salmonid influence.

[[Green Sunfish]] also impacts spinedace, presumably to lesser degree due to lower abundance in lotic systems. However, centrachids occur sympatrically with some populations and strongly implicated in [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment failure in [[Lake Mohave]]. Additional suspected impacts include competition for food and parasites introduced with non-native fish. Exotic Asian tapeworm established in [[Virgin River]] fishes; will probably enter upper Little Colorado River watershed. Total impact of yet another non-native parasite unknown but presumed negative.

## Key Findings

### Recovery Goals and Criteria

**Recovery Objective**: Delisting of the species.

**Recovery Criteria**: 
- Secure and maintain all extant populations
- Establish refugia in most natural, identifiable habitats within probable historic range
- Re-introduced populations not considered established until persisted minimum ten years

### Priority Actions Needed

1. **Secure natural populations and habitats**
   - Prioritize existing populations by protection need
   - Develop secure refugia for four extant populations within natural drainages
   - Establish/maintain at least two refugia in [[East Clear Creek]] drainage (above and below [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]])
   - Establish/maintain refugia in [[Silver Creek]] drainage (main spring at Silver Creek State Fish Hatchery)
   - Establish/maintain refugia in [[Rudd Creek]]-[[Nutrioso Creek]] drainage
   - Establish/maintain refugia in lower [[Chevelon Creek]] drainage and upstream of Chevelon Lake
   - Maintain refugia at The Arboretum in Flagstaff
   - Acquire and protect lands and water rights to assure perennial flow and natural hydrograph
   - Enforce existing laws and regulations (Section 7 and Section 9 of Endangered Species Act)
   - Terminate introduction of non-native fishes into spinedace habitat

2. **Re-establish populations**
   - Identify systems for reintroduction based on flow data, habitat parameters, previous occurrence
   - Determine genetic composition of current populations using MtDNA and allozyme analysis
   - Use genetic information to determine stocks for introductions (limit stocking to sub-basin containing parent stock)
   - Develop genetic pedigree to prevent loss of genetic variability
   - Maintain maximum genetic heterozygosity in introduced populations
   - Enhance habitat as necessary (reduce/cease cattle grazing, remove non-native fish)
   - Remove introduced fishes from reintroduction habitat
   - Install barriers to prevent non-native fish reinvasion
   - Conduct multiple stockings over 1-2 years following initial reintroduction
   - Monitor all reintroductions annually

3. **Monitor all populations**
   - Establish standard monitoring locations at extant sites
   - Implement standard sampling techniques consistent across investigators and through time
   - Establish and maintain central database for all spinedace information
   - Determine variation in abundance and population structure
   - Develop standard methods for quantifying abundance (spray marking, mark-recapture, depletion sampling)
   - Monitor subsequent to spawning to determine recruitment

4. **Better define habitat requirements**
   - Determine acceptable levels of natural variation (absolute numbers, population structure, reproduction)
   - Describe spawning characteristics (substrate, water velocity, temperature)
   - Determine recruitment levels
   - Define minimum population size for self-sustaining populations
   - Characterize physical parameters (temperature, current velocity, substrate)
   - Characterize chemical parameters (dissolved oxygen, CO2, alkalinity, pH)
   - Characterize biological characteristics of community to maintain natural biodiversity

5. **Develop genetic information and pedigree**
   - Chemical analysis of MtDNA and allozymes shows three distinct populations within drainage
   - All populations must be maintained to conserve genetic variation
   - Develop protocol to ensure highest genetic variability maintained in captive-produced fish

6. **Research interactions with non-native fish**
   - Examine direct interactions experimentally under laboratory and field conditions with: [[Apache Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Speckled Dace]], crayfish, salamanders
   - Examine indirect interactions (competition, habitat displacement) experimentally

7. **Develop captive breeding program**
   - Develop procedures for propagating, holding and maintaining spinedace at Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center
   - Supply fish for reintroduction, education, and research
   - Develop genetic protocol to maintain highest genetic variability

8. **Information and education**
   - Target statewide Arizona media with recovery information
   - Provide national exposure through federal channels
   - Inform local media and residents directly impacted by recovery actions
   - Maintain dialogue between State, Federal and private organizations
   - Encourage publication in peer-reviewed, open literature
   - Circulate information among all interested persons
   - Present research at professional, scientific meetings
   - Conduct periodic meetings with species experts and public

### Current Status (1994)

**Listed Status**: Threatened throughout range (listed 1987)

**Critical Habitat**: 31 miles [[East Clear Creek]] (Coconino County) from confluence with Leonard Canyon upstream to [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] and from upper end of reservoir to Potato Lake; 8 miles [[Chevelon Creek]] (Navajo County) from confluence with Little Colorado River upstream to confluence with Bell Cow Canyon; 5 miles [[Nutrioso Creek]] (Apache County) from Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest boundary upstream to Nelson Reservoir Dam.

**Population Ranking by Threat Level**: 
1. [[Silver Creek]] - most imminent danger (possibly extinct)
2. [[East Clear Creek]] - marked decline since 1983, again rare
3. [[Chevelon Creek]]
4. [[Rudd Creek]] and [[Nutrioso Creek]]
5. Little Colorado River mainstem

**Downlisting Timeline**: Expected to occur in 20__ if downlisting criteria met (specific year not determined in draft).
