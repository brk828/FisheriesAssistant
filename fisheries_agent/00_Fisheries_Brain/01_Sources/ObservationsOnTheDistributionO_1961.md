# Observations on the Distribution of Nuptial Tubercles in Some Catostomid Fishes

## Executive Summary
This 1961 paper by Branley A. Branson (Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science) examines the distribution and morphology of nuptial (breeding) tubercles across numerous genera within the [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), a North American freshwater fish family. Based on direct observation of preserved specimens (supplemented by literature data), the study documents tubercle patterns on the head, fins, and body scales of breeding males (and occasionally females) in genera including [[Cycleptus]], [[Ictiobus]], [[Moxostoma]], [[Thoburnia]], [[Hypentelium]], [[Minytrema]], [[Erimyzon]], [[Chasmistes]], and [[Catostomus]]. The author proposes that these tubercle arrangements carry taxonomic and phylogenetic significance, building on prior work by [[Bailey And Suttkus 1952]] and [[Robins And Raney 1956]]. Branson advances a hypothesis — contrasting with Gibbs' (1957) view for Cyprinella — that well-defined rows of head tubercles represent the ancestral (primitive) condition in Catostomidae, with scattered patches or tubercle loss representing derived states, illustrated via a hypothetical dendrogram.

## Background & Description
**General structure**: Nuptial tubercles are horny, epidermal outgrowths (also termed "breeding tubercles," "pearl organs") likely under endocrine control, developing on the head, scales, and fins of breeding males and, in some species, females.

**Key genus/species-specific patterns:**

- **[[Cycleptus]]** (Neosho River specimen): Extremely dense tuberculation. Hundreds of tiny white organs cover the head dorsally and ventrally (denser dorsally); 70–80 large tubercles (0.5–1.0 mm) on opercles/subopercles; a band of 180–200 organs from subopercle to eye margin curving around the orbit. Scales above the lateral line average 16 organs each (12–25); below, 9.5 organs (6–17), with posterior scales bearing nearly double the tubercles of anterior scales. All fins (pectoral, pelvic, anal, dorsal, caudal) densely tuberculate with ray-specific counts ranging from 10–100+ organs per ray segment. Non-breeding Lake Texoma specimens (~200 examined) entirely lacked tubercles, confirming seasonal/hormonal dependency.

- **[[Ictiobus]]**: Minute white tubercles in a head patch beginning above/behind each eye, narrowing centrally; a ring encircles the nostrils. Related **[[Carpiodes]]** apparently lacks tubercles; data unavailable for **[[Xyrauchen]]** and **[[Lagochila]]**; only immature **[[Pantosteus]]** specimens examined (inconclusive).

- **[[Moxostoma]]** (Ozark forms, e.g., *M. poecilurum*): Tubercles largely restricted to fins rather than body. Pelvic fin rays bear 3–8 small tubercles per ray; anal fin tubercles arranged bilaterally, smallest proximally/ventrally, largest distally/dorsally; caudal fin abundantly tuberculate (20–25 organs per ray), largest on ventral rays. *M. carinatum* similar, with additional tubercles on caudal peduncle and snout in large specimens. *M. duquesnei* broadly matches Trautman's (1958) findings with minor caudal fin differences. Southeastern species (*M. rupiscartes*, *M. cervinum*, *M. robustum*, *M. mascotae*, *M. austrinum*) show additional head, nape, and body scale tuberculation absent in Ozark forms, with documented sexual dimorphism (females bearing smaller/fewer tubercles, mainly on anal fin).

- **[[Thoburnia]]**: Considered possibly congeneric with Moxostoma ([[Bailey 1959]]). Author's specimens lacked tubercles, but *T. atripinne* photographs show patterns resembling *M. cervinum* with additional fine head tubercles.

- **[[Hypentelium nigricans]]**: Oklahoma/Arkansas specimens match Trautman's (1957) Ohio description. Pelvic and anal fin rays bear 8–10 tubercles each; caudal fin rays bear 9–15, best developed on lower lobe. No caudal peduncle tubercles observed in 56 White River specimens.

- **[[Minytrema melanops]]**: Highly detailed, geographically variable pattern. Anal fin rays bear 14–20 tubercles each with size gradients along ray length; rays six and seven bear notably larger, callous-ridge-connected tubercles. Head bears four distinctive rows of nipple-shaped tubercles (two above eye, two anterolateral). Notable regional variation identified between Kansas/Ozark and Ohio specimens (Trautman): Ohio specimens have well-developed caudal fin tubercles (absent in Kansas fish), three cheek rows vs. two, and a tuberculate nape patch absent in Kansas material — suggesting need for intermediate-population sampling before taxonomic conclusions.

- **[[Erimyzon]]** (*E. oblongus*, *E. sucetta*, *E. tenuis*): Marked sexual dimorphism in anal fin morphology; only males bear well-developed tubercle rows (rays 3–8, 3–20 tubercles per ray). Head bears three large yellowish tubercles (four in *E. tenuis*, per Moore 1957) morphologically similar to those of Minytrema, supporting a close phylogenetic relationship.

- **[[Chasmistes brevirostris]]**: Exceptionally well-developed and numerous tubercles across pelvic, anal, caudal, and pectoral fins, plus extensive head/body tuberculation (hundreds of middorsal organs, opercular patches, snout bands). Represents one of the most heavily tuberculate catostomids documented.

- **[[Catostomus macrocheilus]]**: Sparse tuberculation — only a few small tubercles on anal fin rays 4–8 (2–5 per ray) and a small caudal fin patch on proximal lower six rays (2–6 tubercles per ray).

- **[[Catostomus (Deltistes) luxatus]]**: Head pattern resembles Chasmistes but lacks a continuous band behind the nostrils; entire snout heavily tuberculate instead. Body distribution unknown (only head material available).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: All examined taxa are freshwater catostomids inhabiting rivers, streams, and lakes across the central and southeastern United States (e.g., Neosho River, White River, Lake Texoma, Elk County Kansas streams) and Pacific/western drainages (*Chasmistes*, *Catostomus luxatus*).
- **Reproductive biology**: Nuptial tubercles function in male-male combat, courtship, substrate adhesion during oviposition, maintaining male position against the female during spawning, and nest/egg-tending behavior — paralleling functions described in [[Pimephales]] minnows (see companion article "Notes on Captive Pimephales" in same volume).
- **Seasonality**: Tubercle expression is tightly linked to breeding condition; non-breeding individuals (e.g., summer-collected *Cycleptus* from Lake Texoma) show complete absence of tubercles, indicating hormonal/seasonal regulation rather than permanent structural features.
- **Sexual dimorphism**: Variably expressed across genera — pronounced in *Erimyzon* and several *Moxostoma* species (females bearing smaller/fewer tubercles restricted mainly to anal fin), while males of most genera show substantially more extensive tuberculation across body and fins.

## Key Findings
1. **Taxonomic utility**: Nuptial tubercle arrangement varies substantially and consistently among catostomid genera and species, supporting prior claims ([[Bailey And Suttkus 1952]]; [[Robins And Raney 1956]]) that tubercle patterns carry diagnostic/taxonomic value.
2. **Phylogenetic hypothesis**: Contrary to Gibbs' (1957) model for *Cyprinella* (where tuberculate rows on the head = primitive, scattered tubercles = derived), Branson proposes that in Catostomidae, well-defined rows of head tubercles represent the **primitive condition**, with a graded reduction toward scattered patches or complete tubercle loss representing derived states — illustrated in a hypothetical dendrogram (Fig. 8).
3. **Primitive genera anomaly**: *Ictiobus* and *Carpiodes*, considered more primitive catostomids, either lack tubercles entirely or show only scattered (non-row) patterns — complicating a straightforward primitive-to-derived tubercle-row hypothesis.
4. **Minytrema/Erimyzon relationship**: Both genera display well-defined, homologous head tubercle rows, morphologically similar tubercle shapes (nipple-like protrusions), supporting their proposed close phylogenetic relationship (congeneric affinity suggested).
5. **Geographic variation**: Significant intraspecific/interpopulation variation documented in *Minytrema melanops* between Kansas/Ozark and Ohio populations (tubercle row counts, caudal fin tuberculation, nape patches), highlighting the need for broader geographic sampling before firm taxonomic conclusions.
6. **Correlation with nesting behavior**: Following [[Hubbs And Black 1947]] (on *Pimephales*/*Ceratichthys*), the author suggests tubercle line reduction may functionally correlate with nesting habits, warranting further ethological study linking tubercle morphology to breeding behavior across the family.
7. **Data gaps**: No tubercle data were available for *Xyrauchen*, *Lagochila*, or mature *Pantosteus*; body distribution unknown for *Catostomus (Deltistes) luxatus* due to limited specimen material (head only).
