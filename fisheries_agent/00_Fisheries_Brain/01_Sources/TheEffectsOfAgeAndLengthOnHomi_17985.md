# Age and Length Effects on Homing Performance in Oligocottus maculosus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This study by G. J. S. Craik (1981, *Canadian Journal of Zoology* 59: 598–604) tests whether homing performance in the intertidal sculpin [[Oligocottus Maculosus]] changes with age and body length. The author reciprocally transplanted fish between two groups of [[Tidepool]] pools at [[First Beach]], [[Barkley Sound]], and tracked whether they returned to their [[Home Range]] or [[Home Pool]]. Homing success rose with size, peaked at about 5–7 cm (age 2), and declined in the largest fish. Juveniles of about 2.3–2.7 cm moved widely between pools and began showing home range fidelity at roughly 3 cm, which the author interprets as a period of spatial "learning" and "memorization."

## Background & Description

*Oligocottus maculosus* is a small cottid (sculpin) of the intertidal zone of the northeast Pacific. Earlier work had shown that adults can [[Homing Behaviour|home]] after being moved to a distant pool, but age and size effects had received little attention in intertidal fishes. Most earlier studies used older fish or only a few broad size classes, all measured as total length. The author cites [[Green 1967]] and [[Khoo 1971]], who reported no clear size differences in homing among fish of about 4–9 cm.

Key terms follow the methods of [[Khoo 1971]] and [[Hayne 1949]]:
- **Home pool:** the pool where a fish was first captured.
- **Home range:** the area covered by the fish during normal travel. It includes the home pool alone or together with adjacent pools.
- **Homing success:** the proportion of released fish that returned to the home range.

Age was not measured directly. It was estimated from an [[Age-Length Regression]], log y = −0.9137 + 1.459 log x, which the author derived from otolith ages and lengths of 365 fish from First Beach (Craik 1978).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study took place on a rocky intertidal shelf. Fish were captured with minnow traps and dipnets from two groups of six tidepools. Each group covered an area about 15 m in diameter, and the two groups were about 60 m apart among many other pools. Fish were measured for total length, sexed, and marked with uniquely coloured embroidery beads.

Several related observations shaped the interpretation:
- Juvenile [[Oligocottus Maculosus]] move from high pools into lower pools before settling. They show extensive movement between pools.
- Other intertidal fishes showed pool fidelity patterns that varied with size. These included *Clinocottus analis* (Williams 1957), *Girella nigricans* (Williams 1957), *Blennius pholis* (Gibson 1967), and *Acanthocottus bubalis* (Gibson 1967). Several of these species move to deeper subtidal water as they grow.
- Growth rates appear to differ between populations. Craik (1978) reported that fish at [[Port Renfrew]] grew more slowly than fish at First Beach.

Fish held in the laboratory were fed chopped mussel (*Mytilus californianus*). Lab-held fish were kept for periods ranging from 3 weeks to over 6 months.

## Key Findings

### Methods Overview
- Ten homing experiments were analysed by [[Age Class]] and by length class. Fish were reciprocally transplanted between the two pool groups, so that density was approximately maintained in each.
- Fish were relocated using baited minnow traps set for about 4 h around low tide every 2 weeks, plus visual inspection of pools. Each experiment ran for at least 5 weeks.
- Statistical comparisons used the variance test for homogeneity of the binomial distribution, following [[Snedecor and Cochran 1967]].
- Three replacement experiments returned fish to their home pool, to test whether size affects the extent of the home range.

### Age Effects
- Homing success (return to the home range) differed significantly among the three age groups (p < 0.05). Test statistic for all ages: 87.75 (significant).
- One-year-old fish were relatively poor homers compared with 2-year-old fish, and to a lesser extent with 3-year-old fish.
- Among fish that returned to the home range, all age groups returned to the home pool in equal proportions.
- Significantly fewer fish remained in the transplant area with increasing age. Younger age groups also showed significant variability among experiments (p < 0.05).

### Length Effects
- Homing success improved with length and peaked at about 5–7 cm total length (age 2). Homing was stable across this range.
- Homing success declined in fish larger than about 7 cm.
- Among size classes, the test statistic was 110.66 (significant, p < 0.05) for all sizes combined. Within the smaller classes, significant differences among experiments appeared in 3.0–3.4 cm (10.10) and 4.0–4.9 cm (30.92).
- Percentages remaining in the transplant area declined with length, except in the largest size class.
- The proportion of homers returning to the home pool did not change significantly with size (Table 2).

### Juvenile Experiments
- Fish of about 2.3–2.7 cm moved extensively between tidepools.
- Homing and home range fidelity began at about 3 cm.
- In the laboratory-holding trial, fish of 2.3–2.7 cm (Groups A and B) did not return to the home pool after holding for 5 or 7 weeks. Group A (n = 24, 7 weeks) and Group B (n = 14, 5 weeks) both showed 0% homing.
- Group C (fish of 2.7–3.3 cm when collected; n = 42 in text, 43 in table; held 3 weeks) showed 14% overall homing, with 17% of the 2.5–2.9 cm fish and 14% of the 3.0–3.4 cm fish returning. The proportion remaining in the transplant area ranged from 42% to 62% across size classes.
- Removal of 2.3–2.7 cm fish from the field impaired their homing when they were replaced. Removal of larger fish (Group C) did not appear to affect homing relative to fish that had remained in the field. The author concludes that information needed for homing is acquired by about 3 cm.

### Replacement Experiments
- In fish of 4.0–4.9 cm, 59–97% (depending on release date) were found in the home range. The proportion in the home range was 84–100% in the 5.0–5.9 cm class.
- Larger fish held in the laboratory for over 6 months still returned to the field, which suggests that once acquired, homing ability is not lost.

### Author's Conclusions
- Age and size explain at least part of the variability in homing performance in *O. maculosus*.
- Acquisition of homing information appears to occur between about 2 and 3 cm and possibly continues to about 4 cm. The author suggests that the process is similar to "learning" and "memorization" of the intertidal environment.
- Reduced homing in the largest fish may reflect small sample sizes, impaired memory, reduced swimming ability, or early senility. The author cites [[Gerking 1957]] on earlier senescence in faster-growing fish.
- Port Renfrew fish, which grow more slowly and show no decline in homing among large fish, may acquire homing information later. The author argues that smaller size classes must be examined to determine the size and age at which Port Renfrew fish adopt a home pool or home range.
