# Streams of Baja California: A Locality Guide to Permanent Lotic Habitats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-habitat

## Executive Summary

This paper, published in the NABS Bulletin (Fall 1994), is a field-based annotated gazetteer of running-water habitats on the Baja California Peninsula, Mexico. Authors Chad M. Murvosh and Richard K. Allen traveled the length of the peninsula in May–June 1978 and again in late May 1979, collecting aquatic insects and cataloging stream localities. The paper covers approximately 96 mapped locations drawn from fieldwork, literature and local information, and gives an annotated list of 96 numbered stream records. Each record indicates whether permanent water is likely, along with access routes, map discrepancies and key historical sources, chiefly Nelson (1921/1966) and Follett (1960). The guide's stated purpose is to help biologists locate permanent water, verify type localities and avoid wasted search effort in a remote and poorly mapped region. A second, unrelated section (a NABS conservation committee column and a note on water quality in Central and Eastern Europe) follows the Baja article.

## Background & Description

- **Study region:** The peninsula extends from about 23° N latitude to the U.S. border and is divided into Baja California (north) and Baja California Sur (south). The authors recommend using latitude and longitude rather than state names for locality data.
- **Access:** Much of the interior was historically remote. Mexican Highway 1 (the Transpeninsula Highway) has increased access, though back-country roads are often unsigned, poorly mapped or washed out.
- **Hydrology:** Most channels are ephemeral. Truly perennial streams fed by stable groundwater are described as rare. Many intermittent streams have permanently flowing reaches. Permanence was judged mainly from the aquatic fauna present, and local information was used where fauna were absent.
- **Climate:** Most precipitation falls in winter, largely as snow at higher elevations. May through August are the driest months. A 1978 hurricane caused severe flooding, washed out highways and bridges, and deposited sand that braided river channels.
- **Cartography:** Sources include the AAA road map, the Nelson–Goldman expedition map, the Clark and McMahan maps and 1:250,000 and 1:50,000 Mexican topographic sheets. Place names often differ between maps, and the authors give alternate names in parentheses.
- **Historical sources:** Edward W. Nelson and Edward A. Goldman surveyed the peninsula in 1905–1906. Their report, *Lower California and its Natural Resources* (1921; reprinted 1966), is the main historical source on stream flow. Follett (1960) provides fish collection records.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fauna noted:** Mayflies (notably *Baetis* species, with *Baetis byblis* and *Baetis caelestis* named from type or collection localities), water penny beetles (*Psephenus*), caddisflies, stoneflies, black flies, snails, amphipods and aquatic beetles (*Helichus*). Permanence was often inferred from water penny beetles and other permanent-water taxa.
- **Fish:** Fundulus lima is noted as the only endemic freshwater fish of Baja. Records also include trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) from Boca de la Sierra and native trout collected at San Antonio in 1905.
- **Water conditions:** Recorded temperatures ranged from about 22 to 33 °C, with pH values of about 7.3 to 7.8 where measured. Some springs were sulfurous or heavily mineralized, and several streams showed fecal or livestock pollution.
- **Human pressures:** Irrigation diversions, livestock waste, a 1978 flood, and groundwater extraction for a federal irrigation project were all noted as possible threats to flow.
- **Oases:** Several spring-fed oases are described as relict habitats, including those at San Ignacio, Mulegé, Comondú, La Purísima, San Javier and Santiago. Grismer and McGuire (1993) is the primary modern reference for them.

## Key Findings

- The authors documented about 96 stream and spring localities, of which the annotated list covers 96 numbered entries. Sites with likely permanent water were marked with squares and doubtful sites with circles on the maps.
- Permanent water is uncommon. Many records are of dry channels with only occasional pools, and several named streams were found dry in one season but flowing in another.
- Some of the most reliable permanent sites include Rio San Ignacio (San Ignacio), Rio San Telmo, Rio Santo Domingo (Colonia Guerrero), Rio San Fernando, Arroyo Calamajué, Arroyo San Jacinto, Arroyo San Javier, Arroyo Caduano, Rio San José (San José del Cabo), Las Parras (Rio Pozo) and Mulegé River.
- Several sites are identified as permanent but hard to reach: Rio San Rafael higher reaches (Mike's Sky Rancho), El Tule, Arroyo Grande and the Santo Tomás headwaters.
- The authors note that the Nelson–Goldman account, although largely overlooked, is the essential historical reference for stream flow and should be consulted by anyone planning extensive fieldwork.
- Practical advice includes using 4-wheel drive, obtaining current local information, checking road and race schedules (such as the off-road race near Mike's Rancho in early June) and visiting the sites in the dry season for the most reliable results.
- Several discrepancies between maps and place names remain unresolved, and the authors state that many sites need further exploration. The guide is intended as a starting point rather than a definitive inventory.

**Second section (NABS notes):** The column also reports the composition of the NABS Conservation and Environmental Issues Committee (38 members, co-chaired by Cathy Pringle and Nick Aumen) and lists conservation publications from non-governmental organizations. Pringle's note on Central and Eastern Europe reports that air particulate emissions in the region were 60 times and SO₂/NOₓ emissions 30 and 8 times higher than in Western Europe per $1000 of GNP. It states that 82% of Polish rivers were unsuitable for industrial use, and that high-quality drinking water in Poland fell from 32% to under 5% over twenty years. It also notes that 22 million tons of toxic waste crossed borders into the region since 1989. These figures are reported as stated in the source.
