# Fishes of Eagle Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the fish fauna of [[Eagle Creek]], a second-order stream in eastern Arizona that still supports much of its original native fish community. The stream holds 20 recorded species: 9 native (including the reintroduced [[Razorback Sucker]]) and 11 non-native. The paper reports the first records of [[Spikedace]] from the stream (juveniles collected in 1985) and documents a single 1950 collection of [[Loach Minnow]], which has not been seen since. Based on an intensive July 1987 survey, the authors describe a sharp upstream-downstream split: native fishes dominate above a natural fish barrier (a diversion dam), while non-native fishes dominate below it. The authors attribute this pattern to the barrier and to flood-driven displacement of exotics, and they call for ongoing monitoring because non-native species could quickly become established upstream.

## Background & Description

**Focal species.** Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) and loach minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*) are each the sole member of their genus. Both are endemic to middle-elevation, flowing-water habitats of the [[Gila River]] basin in Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora, Mexico. Both are federally listed as threatened and protected by Arizona and New Mexico. Both are part of a wider decline of native fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]].

**Study site.** Eagle Creek lies in Graham and Greenlee counties and drains about 2000 km² of watershed. It rises in mixed-conifer forest near 2800 m on the White Mountains, crosses about 20 km of broad grassy valley, then runs about 64 km through steep canyons to the Gila River. Gradient ranges from 90 m/km or more in the headwaters, to 4.9 m/km in the upper valley, to 7.4 m/km in the downstream canyons.

**Hydrology and water quality.** For 1946–1969, mean discharge was 0.94 m³/s. Interbasin transfer from the [[Black River]] raised this to 1.2 m³/s, and withdrawal above a gauge about 20 km upstream from the mouth reduced it to 0.86 m³/s. Mean annual discharge ranged from 0.21 to 2.74 m³/s. Peak flows occur twice yearly, from winter precipitation and snowmelt and from summer monsoon flash floods. Water temperature runs from freezing in winter to about 24 °C in summer at high elevation, and from 4–10 °C in winter to near 30 °C in summer in the lower reaches. In 1976–77, dissolved oxygen stayed near 100% saturation, pH ranged from 7.6 to 9.2, specific conductance from 140 to 370 µmhos/cm, and total dissolved solids from 134 to 324 mg/L.

**Habitat.** The substrate is mostly cobble and boulder, with sand on margins and runs and local silt in pools. The green alga *Cladophora glomerata* can choke riffles after long low-flow periods. Vascular macrophytes are rare. Benthic invertebrates (mainly aquatic insect larvae and nymphs) are abundant and diverse. Riparian vegetation ranges from absent or flood-resistant shrubs in narrow scoured canyons to gallery cottonwood-willow-sycamore forest in wider reaches with terraces.

**Study methods.** The authors divided the stream into five reaches. Only the four downstream reaches were sampled in 1987 (13–18 July), with backpack and bank electrofishing, gill and trammel nets, seines and angling. Sampling across almost four decades (1950–1987) varied widely in method and effort, so the authors caution that abundance and distribution interpretations are tentative.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Eagle Creek is described as relatively undisturbed, though the authors note this may be only apparent. Eastern Arizona has been affected by heavy cattle grazing, open-pit copper mining and logging. A 1945 aqueduct transferred water from the Black River into Willow Creek, which feeds the Eagle Creek system. Diversion structures have been built, washed out and rebuilt since 1897. In 1984 a 5.0 m vertical-fall dam replaced the earlier low dams. This structure is a fish barrier except during major floods, and it creates a small impoundment above it.

The native trout (*Oncorhynchus* sp.) of the headwaters has an unresolved identity. It has been referred to [[Apache Trout]] or [[Gila Trout]] depending on the source. Introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] have hybridized with or replaced the original population. Genetic work on nearby [[Chitty Creek]] found only rainbow trout alleles and mitochondrial DNA in 22 fish sampled in 1989. Arizona Game and Fish Department managed Chitty Creek for rainbow trout, while the U.S. Forest Service treated the creek as a high-priority stream for endangered Gila trout. The authors recommend a single common management goal.

Spikedace populations expand and contract in response to natural variation in desert stream conditions. The authors suggest that an undetected population was present when Miller visited in 1950, and that the species' absence from that collection most likely reflects this natural variation rather than absence from the stream.

## Key Findings

**Species inventory.** Eagle Creek holds 20 recorded species: 9 native and 11 non-native. Of these, the native trout, loach minnow and [[Black Bullhead]] were not taken in 1987.

**Loach minnow.** The species was recorded only in 1950 (UMMZ 162744). It was not found in 1970s or early 1980s surveys, or in the 1987 survey, which used methods that effectively capture it elsewhere. The authors suspect it persists in low numbers in the canyon reach.

**Spikedace.** Twelve juveniles (12–22.5 mm TL) collected in 1985 were the first records from the stream. In 1987 the species was locally common in the canyon-bound reach: 337 fish (24.7%) between Sheep Wash and P-Bar Ranch, 59 (17.1%) between P-Bar Ranch and the diversion dam, and 1 (0.2%) below the dam. None were taken at or above Sheep Wash.

**Relative abundance by reach (1987).** Native species made up 98.2% of fish in the Honeymoon–Sheep Wash reach, 97.8% in the Sheep Wash–P-Bar reach, and 91.0% in the P-Bar–diversion reach, falling to 23.2% below the diversion dam. Non-native species rose from 1.8%, 2.2% and 9.0% above the dam to 76.6% below it. Species richness was 6, 10, 10 and 14 across the four reaches. Native richness stayed between 5 and 6 per reach, while non-native richness climbed from 1 to 8.

**Dominant species below the dam.** [[Red Shiner]] made up 57.9% of fish in the diversion-to-Gila reach (242 specimens). [[Fathead Minnow]] made up 12.2%, [[Longfin Dace]] 15.6% and [[Common Carp]] 1.2%.

**Native suckers and dace above the dam.** [[Speckled Dace]] formed 40.9% of fish at Honeymoon–Sheep Wash. [[Sonora Sucker]] (27.5%) and [[Longfin Dace]] (16.0%) led the Sheep Wash–P-Bar reach. [[Desert Sucker]] (21.7%) and [[Sonora Sucker]] (18.3%) led the P-Bar–diversion reach. [[Roundtail Chub]] was present throughout.

**Razorback sucker.** Stocking began in 1981. Two subadults (21.1 and 22.3 cm SL) were caught in the impoundment above the diversion dam in August 1986. In July 1987, 10 fish (47–72 mm SL, mean 58 ± 8 mm) were caught below Sheep Wash. These were presumed survivors of 33,750 juveniles (about 60 mm SL) stocked near the mouth of Willow Creek on 22 June 1987.

**Non-native introductions.** The authors attribute the presence of [[Rainbow Trout]] to sport-fishing plantings. [[Red Shiner]] and fathead minnows likely came from baitfish releases. Channel catfish may have been stocked without authorization, may have entered from upland livestock ponds, or may have come from the Gila River. Smallmouth bass are unexplained: the species was not known to be stocked, but the authors believe it came from the Black River via the Willow Creek pumping route. [[Mosquitofish]] probably arrived by several routes, including bait, mosquito-control planting and stock-pond floods. Largemouth bass were likely immigrants from the Gila River.

**Threat assessment.** The authors cite evidence that red shiners contributed to the decline of spikedace in the Gila River system, and that predatory basses and catfishes prey on native suckers and minnows. They also note that mosquitofish have displaced native topminnows elsewhere.

**Barrier effects.** The diversion dam blocks upstream movement of non-native fish. Floods wash exotics downstream, and they cannot return above the barrier. The 1977–78 and 1983 floods removed most non-native fish, but by 1987 red shiners and channel catfish had re-entered the upstream reach. The authors also note that the small impoundment above the dam may shelter lentic-adapted exotics during floods, and that anglers may move baitfish upstream.

**Recommendation.** Because the native fauna remains largely intact but could change quickly if non-native populations become established above the dam, the authors recommend frequent monitoring of Eagle Creek and development of management strategies to protect the native fauna.
