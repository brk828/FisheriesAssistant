# Status of the Pecos Gambusia, Gambusia nobilis (Pisces: Poeciliidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This contract report, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] by Anthony A. and Alice F. Echelle of Baylor University (contract #14-16-0002-79-133, dated 22 October 1979), assesses the status of the endangered [[Gambusia Nobilis]] (Pecos gambusia) across its four extant areas in the Pecos River drainage. Surveys from 6 June to 2 September 1979 examined ecological correlates of abundance, electrophoretic and morphological evidence of hybridization with [[Gambusia Affinis]] and [[Gambusia Geiseri]], and genetic divergence among populations. The authors estimate about 1.2 million or more adults in total, identify 14 essential habitat areas, and recommend three possible introduction sites.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Gambusia Nobilis]] (Baird and Girard 1853) is the Pecos gambusia, a small poeciliid fish. The report's Table 10 and Appendix tables list diagnostic characters such as dorsal ray count (typically 8 in *G. nobilis* versus 6 in *G. affinis*), gonopodial characters in males, and the anal spot in females.
- **Study purpose:** The study aimed to (1) examine correlates of occurrence and abundance across the species' range, (2) search for new localities, (3) assess hybridization with [[Gambusia Affinis]] and [[Gambusia Heterochir]]-related taxa and [[Gambusia Geiseri]], and (4) characterize genetic distinctness of populations to guide management.
- **Prior work:** The report builds on [[Bednarz 1979]] (New Mexico populations), [[Hubbs and Springer 1957]] (species revision and historical Comanche Springs records), [[Hubbs and Peden 1969]] (Balmorhea hybrids), and [[Hubbs et al. 1978]] (Leon Creek eradication program).
- **Study areas and methods:**
  - Four areas: [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Bottomless Lakes State Park]] (Roswell, Chaves Co., NM); [[Blue Spring]] (Eddy Co., NM); the [[Balmorhea]] spring-irrigation system (Reeves Co., TX); and [[Leon Creek]] (Pecos Co., TX).
  - Short seine hauls restricted to single habitat types; fish returned to water after counting.
  - Recorded variables included conductivity, spring influence (0 to 3 scale), current, overhead cover, and emergent vegetation.
  - Principal components analysis using BMDP4M (not run for Blue Spring).
  - [[Electrophoresis]]: 12 enzymes encoding 16 presumptive loci in 11 populations.
  - Morphological data: standard length, head and body ratios, dorsal rays, and gonopodial or anal-spot characters following [[Peden 1970]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and habitat**
- Natural populations occur only in the Pecos River drainage at low elevations: the maximum elevation is given as about 1051 m in the summary and "below 1060 m" in the abstract.
- The species was found across a wide range of habitats: springheads, spring runs, downstream areas with no marked spring influence but abundant overhead cover, sedge-covered marshes, and gypsum sinkholes without surface flow.
- In all four areas the densest populations were in marshy waters directly influenced by springflow.
- Comanche Springs (Fort Stockton) populations have been extinct since the 1950s, when the springs went dry.

**Salinity and conductivity**
- Abundant populations occurred from near 1200 µmhos/cm at Blue Spring to 16,000 in Leon Creek. A moderately abundant population occurred in sinkhole 27 at 32,500 µmhos/cm, roughly 30 ppt total dissolved solids, which the authors consider near the upper tolerance limit.
- Absence or low abundance was associated with habitat instability, conductivities above 32,500 µmhos/cm, and lack of predator cover.
- Bednarz (1979) reported that 28,940 µmhos/cm at two sinkholes exceeded tolerance, and recorded 52,525 µmhos/cm at sinkhole 26 (the present study recorded 66,500 there, with only [[Cyprinodon Pecosensis]] taken).

**Habitat stability and competition**
- *G. nobilis* favors stable spring-fed situations. *G. affinis* predominates in shallow, exposed, thermally unstable pools and in downstream reaches where water is periodically diverted for irrigation.
- *G. geiseri* appears to be a recent introduction to the Pecos drainage and is a potentially stronger competitor than *G. affinis* in spring habitats. Its competitive effect is salinity-dependent: it dominates the freshwater Balmorhea springs but is restricted to the Diamond-Y Spring headpool and outflow in saline Leon Creek.

**Predation and thermal ecology**
- Green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) are implicated in losses at Lake St. Francis, Geyser Spring, and the Diamond-Y Spring headpool.
- Critical thermal maxima of 38.1 to 39.3 °C were reported for *G. nobilis* (Gehlbach et al. 1978), 1 to 2 degrees lower than *[[Cyprinodon Elegans]]*. Laboratory thermal preferences were 21 to 25 °C in the morning and 26 to 30 °C in the afternoon for *G. nobilis*, versus 31 °C for *G. affinis* (Winkler 1979).
- Flash floods are a threat to some sites, such as Geyser Spring and Cottonwood Draw, but most occupied habitats are considered safe from them.

**Food**
- Bednarz (1979) described *G. nobilis* as a carnivorous surface feeder. Hubbs et al. (1978) described it as opportunistic, so food availability is not considered a major limiting factor.

## Key Findings

**Population status (estimated adults)**
- [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]]: 26,550 to 28,650 (Bednarz 1979, as summarized by the authors).
- [[Blue Spring]]: 900,000, accepted as a working estimate, though the authors judge Bednarz's marsh area overestimated (about 20,000 m² rather than 30,240 m²) and the 1:2.5 nobilis:affinis ratio is not representative of all sites.
- [[Balmorhea]] area: 113,000, about 88% in the East Sandia Spring headpool (more than 100,000 adults), 9% in the Phantom Lake Spring irrigation system (about 10,000), and a small remainder in Giffin Spring (about 3,000).
- [[Leon Creek]]: more than 1 million, with perhaps 100,000 in the Diamond-Y Spring outflow and marsh. These are described as intuitive approximations.

**Range and new sites**
- No new major areas of occupation were found, though the species was found at one new site on the refuge.
- A single specimen was recorded from the Pecos River about 15 km SSE of Fort Sumner by Hatch (pers. comm.).
- Three artificial populations exist in New Mexico: Dexter National Fish Hatchery (Leon Creek stock), Carlsbad sewage facility (Blue Spring stock), and Living Desert State Park pools (source unknown).
- Of 20 sinkholes stocked in 1973 at the refuge, the effort was unsuccessful in 17. Bednarz attributed failures mainly to high total dissolved solids.

**Hybridization**
- *G. nobilis* × *G. geiseri*: negligible. At Giffin Spring, only 2 of 71 females (2.8%) had mixed genotypes. The authors report the frequency is negligible relative to *G. nobilis* × *G. affinis*.
- *G. nobilis* × *G. affinis*: can be sufficiently frequent to be detrimental locally, especially when *G. nobilis* is rare.
  - Leon Creek lower watercourse: 12 (32%) of 38 males and 33 (28%) of 115 females were heterozygous at one or more loci. Adding unmixed 7-ray specimens gives 18 (47%) and 57 (50%) intermediate males and females.
  - Apparent hybrids and backcross products made up 2 to 39% of Gambusia in three lower-watercourse samples (Table 5).
  - Blue Spring: 10 (4%) of 276 specimens had mixed genotypes (as stated in the report; the sampling description gives 205 specimens from five sites). Six (80%) of the genetic intermediates came from sites 8 and 12, where *G. affinis* outnumbered *G. nobilis* about 20:1.
  - Bitter Lake Unit 3: 10 hybrid intermediates among 312 preserved specimens; Unit 5: one hybrid female among 80 frozen specimens.
- Hybridization rate scales with relative rarity: when one species is rare, mixed genotypes per individual of the rarer species tend to be high (Fig. 16).

**Genetic relationships**
- Average Nei's genetic identity (I) among *G. nobilis* populations was .93. This compares with .99 for *G. affinis* and .98 for *G. geiseri*. Excluding Balmorhea, *G. nobilis* averages I = .99.
- The Balmorhea population is the most divergent of the four extant populations (average I = .68 with the other three). Its maximum I to other *G. nobilis* (.89) is nearly equal to its I to *G. affinis* (.88).
- Balmorhea differs in allele frequencies at GPI-A and IDH-A and is fixed for a PGM-B allele not found elsewhere. It also has a golden-yellow color polymorphism (about 1 in 20 males at East Sandia Spring). The authors suggest formal subspecies recognition, with further study recommended.
- Genetic distance sequence: *G. affinis*–*G. heterochir* D = .026; *G. affinis*–*G. nobilis* D = .152; *G. nobilis*–*G. heterochir* D = .157; *G. geiseri*–*G. heterochir* and *G. affinis* D = .236; *G. geiseri*–*G. nobilis* D = .243. The authors argue this may require rearranging current *Gambusia* classification.

**Essential habitat areas and management**
- Fourteen presently occupied sites were judged essential for survival, using two criteria: presently occupied areas only, and areas requiring minimal management. Units 3 and 5 of the refuge are considered marginal, not essential.

**Possible sites for introduction**
- Four criteria: permanent water within the natural range; isolation or conditions that exclude immigrant competitors and predators; protection from flash floods; and water quality within tolerance (TDS below 30 ppt).
- Three sites recommended:
  1. Lake St. Francis, a sinkhole on Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge. The 1973 introduction failed, possibly from green sunfish predation; the sunfish has since disappeared.
  2. Cottonwood Lake, [[Bottomless Lakes State Park]]: about 10 ppt, 9 m deep, with steep sides; possible competitors are *Fundulus zebrinus* and *Notropis lutrensis*.
  3. The pupfish refugium at [[Balmorhea State Park]], Reeves Co., Texas, considered an especially desirable site because the Balmorhea population is divergent and appears to be declining.

**Key management implications**
- Hybridization with *G. affinis* poses no serious threat to most existing populations, owing to ecological segregation and selection for pure genomes, but the rarity-hybridization relationship has implications for management.
- Competition with *G. geiseri* appears to be the critical factor for *G. nobilis* abundance, particularly in freshwater spring systems such as Balmorhea.
