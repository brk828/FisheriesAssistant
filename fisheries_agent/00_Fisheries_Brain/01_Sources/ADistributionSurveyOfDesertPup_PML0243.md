# Distribution Survey of Desert Pupfish Around the Salton Sea, California (1991)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (CDFG) Inland Fisheries Division, documents a 1991 survey of [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in non-refugium habitats around the [[Salton Sea]]. Baited minnow traps were set in [[Irrigation Drains]], [[Shoreline Pools]], [[Salt Creek]], and [[San Felipe Creek]] during April, May, and June 1991. Pupfish were found in most drains, in 9 of 14 trapped shoreline pools, at the mouth of Salt Creek, and throughout lower San Felipe Creek. The survey was qualitative, and the authors recommend expanded surveys, study of drain maintenance effects, and further work on interactions with introduced fishes.

## Background & Description

The desert pupfish is a member of the family Cyprinodontidae. Historically it was widespread in portions of southeastern California and northern Mexico, occurring in backwaters of the [[Colorado River]] and in springs, streams, and seeps within the Salton Sink (Miller 1943). Its current California range is limited to:

- shoreline pools of the Salton Sea
- two natural streams
- eight artificial ponds
- several irrigation drains flowing to the Salton Sea (Bolster 1990)

The species is endangered because of habitat alteration and the introduction of exotic species. It was listed as endangered by the California Fish and Game Commission in 1980 and by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) in 1986. The draft USFWS recovery plan calls for protecting natural habitat, creating refugia for specific genetic stocks, and periodic monitoring of pupfish populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Survey methods**

- **Trapping:** Wire mesh minnow traps baited with cat food were placed in perforated plastic bags and left overnight for about 20 hours. Traps were set at the mouth and along the length of each drain or pool.
- **Habitat data:** For each trap, the team recorded location, approximate depth, substrate type, water quality, surrounding aquatic and terrestrial vegetation (with relative abundance), and trap placement and retrieval times.
- **Trap placement:** Traps were placed in clearer, slow-moving water between 2 in. and 2 ft deep. Drains and pools that were heavily vegetated or stagnant were not sampled. The number of traps per drain depended on drain length and habitat quality.
- **Visual surveys:** Three segments of Salt Creek and San Felipe Creek were surveyed visually. The Alamo and New rivers were not trapped because they lacked suitable pupfish habitat.
- **Taxonomic grouping:** Sailfin and shortfin mollies (*Poecilia latipinna* and *P. mexicana*) were lumped as "mollies" in the field. Two African cichlids (*Tilapia mossambica* and *T. zillii*) were lumped as "tilapia."

**Study area context**

- **Irrigation drains:** The [[Imperial Irrigation District]] (IID) and [[Coachella Valley Water District]] (CVWD) documented 66 drains discharging to the sea. The northern portion of the sea (Riverside County) and the southern portion (Imperial County) were surveyed separately.
- **Shoreline pools:** Sampling was limited to selected pools on the southern and eastern shores. Ten of the 14 pools surveyed were accessible only by airboat.
- **Salt Creek:** Access was restricted by dense [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix ramosissima*) and giant reed. The lower section was surveyed from the railroad tracks at Highway 111 west to the mouth. A more accessible upper section was trapped quarterly.

**Environmental variability**

Irregular supplies of waste irrigation water cause frequent changes in drain and sea water levels. These changes may affect trapping efficiency by altering drain connections to the sea and discouraging fish from entering traps. Strong winds also shifted water levels. Vegetation removal by the IID and CVWD may affect pupfish distribution and abundance. Maintenance occurs more frequently in the CVWD service area on the north end of the sea.

## Key Findings

**Irrigation drains**

- Pupfish were found in most drains surveyed. The northern portion of the sea had higher occupancy than the south.
- **North end:** 24 of 27 surveyed drains (89%) contained pupfish. Four clear, shallow drains were not trapped but were confirmed as habitat by dip-netting or visual identification. Pupfish were netted in the Avenue 82 drain and the County Line stormwater channel. Counts per north-end drain ranged from zero (Johnson, Lincoln, and Buchanan 0.5 drains) to 155 (Avenue 78 drain).
- **South end:** 17 of 30 surveyed drains (57%) contained pupfish. Counts per drain reached as high as 44.
- Overall, about 72% of the drains surveyed contained pupfish (the source OCR is partially garbled, but this matches the reported 24/27 and 17/30 figures).

**Shoreline pools**

- 9 of 14 trapped pools (64%) contained pupfish. Trapping captured zero to 30 pupfish per pool.
- One pool between Trifolium 23 and Trifolium 23N yielded pupfish by dip-netting rather than trapping.
- The authors state that more sampling is needed to determine whether this distribution represents shoreline pools as a whole.

**Natural tributaries**

- Pupfish were caught at the mouth of Salt Creek.
- Visual surveys indicated that pupfish inhabit the entire lower San Felipe Creek. Many young fish and a few adults were seen.
- The only exotic species observed in San Felipe Creek was the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which appeared to favor larger, deeper pools.
- During the survey, San Felipe Creek flow was intermittent and restricted to shallow, disjunct pools between the Salton Sea and Highway 86.

**Associated species**

- In addition to desert pupfish, 12 fish species and one crustacean were trapped. The most commonly caught species included mollies (two species), tilapia (two species), longjaw mudsucker, mosquitofish, red shiner, porthole livebearer, variable platyfish or swordtail, orangemouth corvina, largemouth bass, green sunfish, and the [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*).
- Other organisms recorded included tadpoles, water beetles, water scavengers, pileworms, and snails.

**Factors affecting trap results (Discussion)**

- **Trap placement:** Pupfish were often caught in only one of five or six traps set in apparently homogeneous habitat. Several drains where initial trapping failed later yielded pupfish.
- **Trap density:** A large number of traps may saturate an area and increase capture chances. Inconsistent habitat evaluation could lead to varied results.
- **Bait:** The survey used an inexpensive fish-flavored bait. Differences in bait type and amount may affect efficiency.
- **Timing:** Matsui (1981) reported that Salton Sea desert pupfish breed from late March through September, so surveys should coincide with this period. Population densities fluctuate widely.
- **Temperature and light:** Pupfish movements respond to daily changes in water temperature and possibly light intensity (Barlow 1959). Shoreline pool results may reflect trap placement relative to these changes.

**Management recommendations**

1. Expand surveys to cover all potential shoreline pool habitats and other potential pupfish habitat not sampled in 1991. Repeat surveys at least every two years.
2. Study the effects of continued drain maintenance. Pupfish appeared to survive maintenance, but part of one drain was left undisturbed during the procedure.
3. Develop formal agreements among the USFWS, CDFG, and irrigation districts to regulate the frequency and extent of drain maintenance.
4. Investigate other human impacts on pupfish in drains, including water diversion, bait fishing, agricultural runoff, and water quality.
5. Study pupfish interactions with introduced species. Schoenherr (1991) noted that some *Cyprinodon* species have declined through interaction with mosquitofish, and Matsui (1981) documented behavioral interference by tilapia and sailfin mollies with pupfish reproduction.
6. Study pupfish tolerance of human impacts and exotic species to aid future management of this endangered species.
