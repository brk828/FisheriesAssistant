# Long-Term Streamflow Records Reconstructed from Tree Rings

#hydrology #dendrohydrology #tree-ring-reconstruction #streamflow #paleoclimatology

## Executive Summary

This monograph (Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research Papers No. 5, University of Arizona Press, 1975, by Charles W. Stockton) tests whether surface-runoff information can be extracted from tree-ring width series and used to extend short streamflow records. Two pilot watersheds, [[Bright Angel Creek]] (101 sq. mi., Arizona) and the [[Upper San Francisco River]] (1,653 sq. mi., Arizona-New Mexico), were studied in detail using [[Douglas Fir]] ring-width chronologies, and the method was then applied to the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The work is a hydrology and dendroclimatology study; it contains no fish, invertebrate, or in-stream aquatic biological data.

The author argues that short hydrologic records (often under 40 years) carry large estimation errors, and that tree rings offer a long, annually resolved natural archive. Reconstructed runoff at [[Lees Ferry]] extends the record from 1896–1961 to 1564–1961.

## Background & Description

- **Author:** Charles W. Stockton, affiliated with the [[Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research]] (University of Arizona) since 1966; B.S. geology and M.S. hydrogeology (Colorado State University), Ph.D. hydrology (University of Arizona).
- **Context:** Contribution No. 22 of the [[Lake Powell Research Project]], supported by NSF grant GI 34840 (RANN Division) and the U.S. Department of Interior Office of Water Resources Research.
- **Core problem:** Covariance stationarity is assumed by most hydrologic techniques, yet short records are unreliable. Rodriguez-Iturbe (1969) reported errors of 2–20% in the mean, 15–60% in variance, and up to 200% in first-order autocorrelation for records of 40 years or less.
- **Key concept:** The "Noah and Joseph effects" of [[Mandelbrot and Wallis]] describe long-term persistence in hydroclimatic series that short records cannot capture.
- **Methods (cross-linked in text):** [[Correlation Analysis]], [[Spectral Analysis]], [[Cross Spectral Analysis]], [[Principal Component Analysis]], [[Multiple Linear Regression]], variance component analysis, and the [[Water Year]] convention (October 1 to September 30).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study watersheds**
- *Bright Angel Creek:* small north-rim basin dominated by April–May snowmelt (about 50% of annual runoff in spring; mean annual runoff 4.729 in., 25,480 acre-ft., 1924–1966). Mean annual precipitation 25.01 in. (1926–1966) with winter dominance. Karst conduits supply substantial baseflow.
- *Upper San Francisco River (above Glenwood, N.M.):* runoff concentrated December–May; mean annual flow 46,767 acre-ft. (1928–1966), about 0.532 in. Monsoon (July–September) precipitation dominates, and the runoff regime differs strongly from Bright Angel Creek.

**Climate regime:** The region has a semiannual precipitation pattern. Winter precipitation comes from westerly systems, while summer monsoon moisture arrives from the Gulf of Mexico. Snowpack and [[Evapotranspiration]] mediate the water balance.

**Tree sampling:** [[Douglas Fir]] (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*) was the only species sampled, chosen to limit species-related variance. Samples came from ridge crests and well-drained sites, avoiding stream valleys and snow-drift zones.
- Bright Angel Creek: 5 sites × 5 trees × 2 cores (50 trees).
- Upper San Francisco River: 5 sites (Fox Mountain, Alpine, Luna, Tularosa Divide, Rainy Mesa) with 10 trees × 2 cores each (100 trees), with Alpine and Luna each split into north and south subsites.
- Upper Colorado: 17 tree-ring chronologies, 1561–1964.

Site elevations ranged from roughly 5,800 to 9,200 ft. across the study basins.

## Key Findings

1. **Hydroclimatic regimes differ sharply.** Bright Angel Creek is snowmelt-dominated with winter-controlled precipitation, while the Upper San Francisco River depends on winter rain, snowmelt, and summer monsoon inputs. Runoff at the San Francisco gauge is maximal in March, and the Bright Angel Creek autospectrum shows low-frequency power at 3- and 5-year periods.

2. **Non-stationarity in Bright Angel Creek runoff.** A two-period Student t-test (1924–1944 vs. 1945–1965; means 5.53 in. vs. 3.98 in.; |t| = 2.5137 > 2.021) indicated a significant downward shift, attributed to a shift in seasonal precipitation distribution rather than watershed development.

3. **Site-level tree-ring quality varies.** Variance components showed that Point Imperial (site 3, Bright Angel Creek) carried the most hydrologic signal (δ_t² = 63.6%), followed by Dragon Creek (53.9%) and Upper Watershed (39.0%). Quarry Pit Ridge had the lowest (15.8%). On the Upper San Francisco River, Tularosa Divide had the highest year-to-year component (71%) but the most persistence.

4. **Ranking of Upper San Francisco River sites.** Based on variance components and mean sensitivity, the order was Fox Mountain, Rainy Mesa, Luna, Alpine, and Tularosa Divide.

5. **Ring-width to runoff correlation.** Product-moment correlations between mean tree-ring index and annual runoff were about 0.7 for both basins, so roughly 50% of runoff variance is explainable. For Bright Angel Creek, the Eq. (10) model explained r² = 0.512 (standard error 1.56 in.).

6. **Reconstruction results.**
   - Bright Angel Creek (Eq. 10, 1753–1966): reconstructed mean 5.81 in. vs. gaged 4.73 in.; reconstructed variance 4.20 sq. in. vs. 4.39 sq. in. Under-prediction of high-flow years was noted.
   - Upper San Francisco River, four-site Eq. (11): explained 72% of variance (13% from prior runoff); reconstructed 1753–1966 mean 0.65 in. vs. gaged 0.54 in.
   - Equation (12) (model 3) gave a smoother reconstruction, mean 0.46 in.

7. **Validation.** Split-sample tests showed that a calibration on 1937–1966 reconstructing 1927–1936 explained only 15% of variance, illustrating sensitivity to shifts in moisture regime. The 1927–1956 calibration applied to 1957–1966 explained 60%. Independent comparison with [[Alma New Mexico]] gauge data for 1905–1913 showed underestimation in the wet year 1905, linked to a preceding drought in 1904.

8. **Upper Colorado River (Lees Ferry).** Reconstruction Eq. (14) explained 82% of variance over 1896–1961 (r = 0.91, standard error 2.02 million acre-ft.). The long-term mean is about 13 million acre-ft./yr, versus 15.03 for the gaged record. The reconstructed variance is 13.95 (17.01 corrected) vs. 18.42 gaged. Extended droughts occurred in 1565–1600 and 1870–1890, with no equivalent in the gaged record.

9. **Management implication.** The author concludes that the two wettest periods of the past 400 years were each preceded by extended low-runoff intervals, information considered valuable for reservoir management such as [[Lake Powell]].

10. **Limitations noted.** Ring-width response saturates in wet years, so the method is less useful for flood prediction. Snow accumulation and microsite effects weaken the signal at some sites, and Lees Ferry verification against [[Laguna Gauge]] data (1878–1922) was described as "not vigorous," with a correlation dropping to 0.40 for 1878–1896.
