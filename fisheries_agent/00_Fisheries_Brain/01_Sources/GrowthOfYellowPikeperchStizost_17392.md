# Growth of Yellow Pikeperch (Stizostedion vitreum vitreum) in Some Iowa Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stizostedion

## Executive Summary

This 1948 study by Kenneth D. Carlander, published in the *Iowa State College Journal of Science*, reports the age and growth of [[Yellow Pikeperch]] (also called [[Walleye]]) from six [[Iowa Lakes District]] lakes sampled from 1941 to 1944. A total of 216 fish were aged from their scales using the [[Scale Annuli Age Determination]] method and growth was calculated with the [[Direct Proportion Method]]. Growth in Iowa lakes was about average, or slightly above average, compared with published values from other North American waters. Growth varied among lakes, and the author links that variation to population density, productivity, depth, latitude, and length of growing season.

## Background & Description

The yellow pikeperch is one of the most important game fishes in Iowa's natural lakes. The [[Iowa Conservation Commission]] has planted thousands of fry and fingerlings for many years to maintain pikeperch angling, and it has also used lake improvements and catch limits in managing the fishery. The study was carried out under the Iowa Cooperative Fishery Research Unit, and the author describes it as a general overview intended for comparison with more detailed later studies.

Growth was estimated from scale rings. The author notes that a [[Lake of the Woods]] study had found an S-shaped body-scale relationship, so the direct proportion method gives only approximate growth rates. The author judged these data accurate enough for comparisons. Growth was calculated from [[Standard Length]] in millimetres and reported as [[Total Length]] in inches. Total length was equal to 1.198 standard lengths for pikeperch up to 450 mm standard length (based on 83 fish measured both ways), and to 1.187 standard lengths for larger fish.

Fish were collected by seine, gillnet, and angling from these lakes:

- **Mud Lake** (Emmet County), April 1944
- **East Okoboji**, **West Okoboji**, and **Spirit Lake** (Dickinson County), 1940 to 1944 in various months
- **Welch Lake** (Dickinson County), September to October 1941
- **Diamond Lake** (Dickinson County), August to September 1941

The smallest fish examined was 6.9 inches total length and less than one year old. The largest was 26.7 inches and twelve years old. Age is designated by Roman numerals. The oldest recorded yellow pikeperch, reported by Juday and Schloemer (1938), was in the XVIII age class, but very few pikeperch reach ten years of age.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author treats fish growth as highly responsive to the environment, which makes it a useful indicator of conditions in a given water body. Several environmental and population factors are discussed:

- **Lake depth and productivity:** Growth was slowest in the deepest lake, [[West Okoboji Lake]], and most rapid in the shallowest, [[East Okoboji Lake]]. The author notes that shallow lakes are usually more productive than deep ones.
- **Population density:** Rapid growth in [[Mud Lake]], [[Diamond Lake]], and [[Welch Lake]] was probably associated with low population densities. These lakes are subject to frequent winter kill and were sometimes used as rearing areas, with fish seined out at the end of the growing season. Population density is cited as an important modifier of growth (Eddy and Carlander, 1940). Rapid growth in [[Norris Reservoir]], Tennessee, was partly attributed to a recent introduction and low density.
- **Latitude and temperature:** Pikeperch grow more slowly in the northernmost lakes. The author cites similar southward increases in [[Largemouth Bass]] growth (Bennett, 1937) and in several sunfish and Minnesota species. The proposed causes are longer growing seasons and higher summer temperatures, which raise metabolic rates. In [[Lake of the Woods]], growth was faster in summers with high July and August mean temperatures.
- **Condition:** The coefficient of condition, K, was calculated as K = W × 10⁵ / L³ (W in grams, L in millimetres standard length), following [[Hile]] (1936). The K values showed no consistent trend with length or season in the Iowa fish. Higher K values in 3- to 6-year-old fish were reported in [[Trout Lake]] and Lake of the Woods, but this was not pronounced enough in Iowa to affect comparisons.

## Key Findings

**Growth rate and age structure**

- The combined calculated mean total lengths at each annulus were 5.0, 9.2, 12.4, 15.0, and 17.1 inches for annuli I through V, based on all 216 fish. Annual increments declined steadily from 5.0 inches in the first year to 4.2, 3.4, and 2.8 inches in the next three years.
- Length at a given age varied widely. Some Age III fish were smaller than the largest Age I fish. Speaker (1936) reported pikeperch reaching 13.5 inches in less than one year in an Iowa rearing pond.
- Fish collected at different times of year are not directly comparable. Mud Lake fish had not yet begun their second summer's growth, while Spirit Lake fish had completed it.

**Comparison with other waters**

- For Age Classes III, V, and VII, Iowa lakes showed about average growth, or slightly faster than average, among the waters compared in Table 2 (Lake Nipigon, Lake Erie, Prairie Provinces, Lake of the Woods, Quetico, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Ohio, and others).
- Calculated growth in Iowa lakes was also about average (Table 4). Among the comparison waters, [[Bear Lake]], Wisconsin, showed the fastest growth and [[Clear Lake]], Wisconsin, the slowest.

**Lake-specific patterns**

- Among lakes with older fish, growth was slowest in West Okoboji, intermediate in Spirit Lake, and fastest in East Okoboji. The author regards growth in the other lakes as faster still, but those fish were too young for valid comparison.

**Conservation and management implications**

- The author suggests that growth analysis can show where conditions favour fish, identify overcrowded populations that do not need additional planting, evaluate earlier stocking success, and provide a scientific basis for catch regulation.
- Lake richness, population density, and growing-season length are proposed as the main factors modifying growth between waters.

**Condition**

- The average K of 202 Iowa yellow pikeperch was 1.64, somewhat higher than values reported elsewhere. The Iowa range was 1.46 to 5.70. For comparison, [[Lake of the Woods]] fish had a mean K of 1.47.
- Lakes with the fastest growth were not necessarily those with the highest K values, so growth rate and condition are not simply linked.

**Limitations**

- The author states the sample is relatively small and drawn from several lakes, so the paper is intended to give a general picture rather than a definitive analysis. Causal factors could not be determined with the available data.
