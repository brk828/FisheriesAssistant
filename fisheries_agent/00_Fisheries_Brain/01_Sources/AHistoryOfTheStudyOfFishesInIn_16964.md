# A History of the Study of Fishes in Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #history-of-science #freshwater-fishes #Amblyopsidae #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Shelby D. Gerking traces the development of ichthyological research in Indiana from the earliest natural history surveys (1820s) through the mid-20th century. It documents how Indiana University became a major center of American ichthyology under [[David Starr Jordan]] and [[Carl H. Eigenmann]], and how their students and successors expanded the field into fish taxonomy, cave biology, population dynamics, and applied fisheries management. The narrative connects taxonomic description, evolutionary biology (notably studies of cave-dwelling blind fishes), and early quantitative fisheries science, situating Indiana as a foundational hub for training many of the discipline's most influential American ichthyologists.

## Background & Description

- **Earliest records (1820s–1870s):** [[Constantine Samuel Rafinesque]]'s *Ichthyologia Ohiensis* (1820) marked the first ichthyological work touching Indiana, based on an 1818 survey of the Falls of the Ohio River near Jeffersonville. Rafinesque described roughly 100 new species but preserved no type specimens, complicating later verification.
- **New Harmony period:** The 1826 "Boatload of Knowledge" brought naturalists [[Thomas Say]] and French ichthyologist [[Charles Alexandre Lesueur|C. A. LeSueur]] to [[New Harmony]], Indiana. LeSueur's *American Ichthyology* (1827), published in New Harmony, was a landmark descriptive work on North American fishes.
- **Physician-naturalists:** [[Jared P. Kirtland]] and [[J. T. Plummer]] contributed early species lists from Ohio River tributaries and the Richmond, Indiana area (1841, 1851).
- **Edward Drinker Cope** visited [[Wyandotte Cave]] in the 1870s to study the cave blindfish (*Amblyopsis spelaea*), publishing "Life in Wyandotte Cave" (1872).
- **Jordan's Day (1874–1891):** [[David Starr Jordan]] arrived in Indiana in 1874, teaching at Shortridge High School, then [[Butler University]], before joining [[Indiana University]] in 1879. With [[Herbert E. Copeland]], he launched systematic surveys of Indiana fishes, laying groundwork for his monumental *Fishes of North and Middle America* (completed with [[Barton Warren Evermann]] in 1900). Jordan trained a cohort of major ichthyologists including [[Charles H. Gilbert]], [[Barton Warren Evermann]], [[Joseph Swain]], [[Oliver P. Jenkins]], [[Amos Butler]], [[Albert B. Ulrey]], [[Stephen A. Forbes]], [[Oliver P. Hay]], and [[John Otterbein Snyder]].
- **Eigenmann's Day (1891–1925):** [[Carl H. Eigenmann]], a Jordan protégé, led the Zoology Department and became the first Dean of the Graduate School. His two major research legacies were the taxonomy of South American freshwater fishes and the study of cave vertebrates, framed as a model system for degenerative evolution. He co-founded the [[Indiana University Biological Station]], first at Wawasee Lake (1895), later at [[Winona Lake]] (1899), to support evolutionary research using northern Indiana's glacial lakes.
- **Lake Maxinkuckee Survey (1899–1908):** Evermann and [[Howard Walton Clark]] conducted one of the most complete early ecological lake surveys in the U.S., published in 1920 as *Lake Maxinkuckee, a Physical and Biological Survey*, involving contributions from limnologist [[Chauncey Juday]].
- **Later Years (1920s–1950s):** [[Will Scott]] established the *Investigations of Indiana Lakes and Streams* (beginning 1928) and formalized the cooperative Indiana Lake and Stream Survey with the state Department of Conservation. His students — including [[Ralph Hile]], [[James Wilding]], and [[Herman P. Wright]] — advanced age-and-growth methodology, respiratory physiology, and post-glacial faunal dispersal studies. [[William E. Ricker]] succeeded Scott (1939–1950), advancing quantitative population dynamics and fisheries statistics, later followed by [[David G. Frey]] (from 1950).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat diversity studied:** Indiana's aquatic research history spans the Ohio River and its tributaries, the Wabash River, glacial lakes of northern Indiana (e.g., Lake Maxinkuckee, Winona Lake, Bean Blossom Creek), and karst cave systems (Wyandotte Cave), reflecting a broad range of lotic, lentic, and subterranean freshwater environments.
- **Cave ecosystems and evolution:** Eigenmann's cave vertebrate research (40+ papers, most active 1898–1903) used Indiana and Cuban cave systems to study degenerative evolution, culminating in *The Cave Vertebrates of America* (1909). [[Fernandus Payne]] extended this work experimentally, showing that blind cavefish locate food via touch rather than vision — an early example of sensory ecology tied to habitat-driven evolutionary loss of traits.
- **Glacial lake ecology and post-glacial colonization:** Herman P. Wright's snail migration studies established faunal recolonization pathways into northern Indiana following retreat of the Wisconsin glacier; these pathways were later corroborated for fish distributions, linking invertebrate biogeography to fish community assembly.
- **Population dynamics and fisheries management:** Ricker's work on vital statistics of fish populations (mortality, fishing effort) — informed by his prior work on the Pacific salmon populations of [[Cultus Lake]] — directly influenced Indiana fisheries policy, notably the removal of seasonal catch restrictions on panfish, a management shift that subsequently spread nationally.
- **Water quality and respiratory physiology:** James Wilding's doctoral research on oxygen thresholds in three fish species addressed minimal respiratory requirements under degraded conditions such as pollution, an early contribution linking fish physiology to water-quality management.

## Key Findings

- Indiana University functioned as a principal training ground for American ichthyology (c. 1875–1925), producing or hosting Jordan, Eigenmann, Gilbert, Evermann, Snyder, Hay, and others who shaped taxonomy and fisheries science nationally.
- [[W. J. Moenkhaus]] conducted some of the earliest statistically-based studies distinguishing populations within a species, using the [[Logperch]] (*Percina caprodes*) and [[Johnny Darter]] (*Etheostoma nigrum*) (1894–1898), and performed early fish hybridization experiments using saltwater cyprinodonts.
- [[Eigenmann and Beeson's "Fishes of Indiana" (1893)]] represented the most comprehensive statewide fish survey of its era; a single-mile stretch of Bean Blossom Creek yielded 40 species, illustrating high local freshwater biodiversity.
- The [[Lake Maxinkuckee Survey]] (1899–1908, published 1920) remains cited as one of the most complete ecological lake surveys ever conducted, integrating fish, plankton, and physical limnology.
- Ralph Hile's scale-based age-and-growth methodology, developed at Indiana, became a standard technique in fisheries biology and was later applied to Great Lakes rock bass and cisco population studies, contributing to understanding of subspeciation in herrings (Coregonidae).
- Ricker's *Methods of Estimating the Vital Statistics of Fish Populations* (1948) and related work established foundational quantitative fisheries science methods, recognized internationally (invited Baldi Lecture, 1956 International Congress of Limnology).
- Indiana's fish collections suffered a major setback when Jordan's original collection was destroyed by fire in 1883; subsequent collections (Eigenmann's South American material, and later state distributional collections) were dispersed among the U.S. National Museum, California Academy of Sciences, University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, and Indiana University's Jordan Hall of Biology (from 1955).
- The *Investigations of Indiana Lakes and Streams* series (from 1928) constituted one of the first state-sponsored technical journals dedicated to aquatic biology in the United States, reflecting the institutionalization of applied fisheries and limnological research in Indiana.
