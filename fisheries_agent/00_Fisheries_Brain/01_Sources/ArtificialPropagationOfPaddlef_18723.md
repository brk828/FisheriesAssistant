# Artificial Propagation of Paddlefish and Simazine as an Algicide for Bass Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #polyodontidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two short hatchery reports from the *Progressive Fish-Culturist* (January 1963). The first, by Charles A. Purkett, Jr. of the [[Missouri Conservation Commission]], describes the first hatchery propagation of [[Paddlefish]] (*Polyodon spathula*) from eggs stripped from wild females in the [[Osage River]] in 1961, and their rearing to 17 months of age. The second, by J. R. Snow of the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], tests the herbicide [[Simazine]] as a control for filamentous green algae in [[Largemouth Bass]] ponds at the [[Marion National Fish Hatchery]]. Both are applied fish-culture studies, and both were preliminary, reporting modest sample sizes and incomplete outcomes.

## Background & Description

**Paddlefish (Part 1).** Paddlefish eggs and newly hatched larvae had been described for the first time in 1960 ([[Purkett 1961]]). That discovery gave basic biological information, but larger numbers of fish were needed for further work. Eggs were obtained from fish snagged in the Osage River, Missouri. Paddlefish are a large, filter-feeding freshwater species, and the study notes that they normally become adhesive soon after fertilization.

**Simazine (Part 2).** Substituted symmetrical triazines had been shown to have strong herbicidal activity. Simazine (2-chloro-4,6-bis(ethylamino)-s-triazine) had shown algicidal effects in earlier work. The Marion hatchery had long struggled with [[Hydrodictyon]] (*Hydrodictyon reticulatum*), a noxious net alga, and earlier control methods were either ineffective or toxic to fish food organisms and fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water temperature:** Paddlefish eggs were stripped at 62 °F and incubated at 55–57 °F. Hatching was rapid at 57–59 °F and slowed by 58 °F. A flood on May 6–7 submerged the trays with warm water, and temperatures for that period were not recorded.
- **Pond management:** The Hunnewell pond was fertilized with 0-20-0 to sustain a plankton bloom. Supplemental feeding used Purina trout starter.
- **Algal control:** Filamentous green algae (including *Hydrodictyon*, *Pithophora*, *Cladophora*, *Oedogonium*, *Zygnema*, *Mougeotia*, and *Spirogyra*) were to be controlled when they first appeared at the pond surface.
- **Zooplankton:** Pond water used in bioassays contained a heavy concentration of zooplankton, mainly Cladocera. Live zooplankton was identified as an unmet need for holding paddlefish larvae.
- **Winterkill:** An early freeze and persistent ice cover led to a winterkill in the Hunnewell pond, which was found on April 3, 1962.

## Key Findings

### Paddlefish Propagation (Purkett)

- **Source and spawning:** Three ripe female paddlefish were obtained from fishermen on April 29, 1961. The fish had been spawning when caught and had been held tied to the bank for several hours.
- **Fertilization:** Eggs were stripped into an 8-gallon jar. Silt was added to prevent adhesion, and milt from three males was added while the jar was rotated for about 30 minutes. Some clumping occurred, but most eggs stayed free.
- **Incubation and hatching:** Eggs were spread in five trays of a Roberson trout-egg incubator at Bennett Spring Trout Hatchery and treated daily with malachite green. Hatching began on May 10 (12 days after spawning) and was largely complete by May 22. Fungus and silt affected some eggs. Survival was low but was not precisely determined. About **3,000 larvae** hatched and lived long enough to be placed in rearing facilities.
- **Rearing trials:** Larvae were reared in four settings: aluminum hatchery troughs, experimental aquaria with running warm-lake water, aerated aquaria without flowing water, and a warm-water pond at the Hunnewell Fish Hatchery.
  - Fish in troughs and aerated aquaria began feeding by day 4 on Purina trout starter. Most food was taken in suspension, and low-quality floating material made the diet inadequate. These fish could not be held for more than 2 weeks.
  - All fish in the running-water aquaria from the warm-water lake died within a few days. No cause was identified.
- **Pond stocking:** The half-acre Hunnewell pond received 500 fish on May 12, 60 on May 28, and 20 on June 7, 1961. Pond fertilization and 1 lb of trout starter per day were used.
- **Sampling:** A 100-foot seine took 75 fish (estimated 5–8 inches) on June 28, 1961, and 53 fish on August 8, 1961. Neither haul was expected to capture the whole population. A representative sample of 53 fish is given in Table 1, with lengths from about 8.4 to 16.0 inches and weights from 1.3 to 9.0 ounces. Ten fish were removed for the [[Missouri State Fair]] before the second haul.
- **Winterkill and survivors:** The pond was drawn down on April 3, 1962. Only two paddlefish remained alive, measuring 20.7 in (14 oz) and 21.7 in (17 oz). One dead fish appeared to have had its rostrum trapped between mud and ice, which may have been the cause of many deaths.
- **Growth to 17 months:** The two survivors were moved to another brood-stock pond with channel catfish, which was fertilized with 16-20-0 water-soluble fertilizer. On October 26, 1962, they measured **35.5 in (6 lb)** and **34.0 in (5.5 lb)**.
- **Conclusions:** Paddlefish can be propagated in hatcheries. The main constraint is a dependable egg supply, since netting ripe fish and collecting eggs from anglers' catches are both unreliable and time-consuming. [[Meyer and Stevenson 1962]] tried stimulating gonads with a dried chorionic gonadotrophin, without success in obtaining fertilized eggs, but the method was considered worth further study. The author suggested that with intensive fertilization and feeding, paddlefish might be farmed commercially, possibly alongside rice production.

### Simazine Algicide Trials (Snow)

- **Prior work:** [[Grigsby 1958]] reported algicidal effects at 5 ppm on *Chlamydomonas*. [[Walker 1959]] applied a 10% pelleted Simazine formulation at 100 lb/acre, which killed submerged plants (*Potamogeton*, *Chara*) but not cattail, and phytoplankton then developed.
- **Laboratory bioassay:** Zooplankton-rich pond water was treated with Simazine at 2 and 4 ppm and stocked with largemouth bass fry (about 3/8 inch) at 21.3–23.0 °C for 96 hours.
  - At 2 ppm, 1 fish and a few Cladocera died.
  - At 4 ppm, 2 fish and about 10% of the Cladocera died.
  - These results suggested that Simazine could be used in production ponds with reasonable safety.
- **Field trial design:** Two bass spawning ponds (with brood fish) and four fingerling rearing ponds were treated with 80% wettable-powder Simazine at 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 ppm active ingredient. Solution was applied at 15 gallons per surface acre, mostly around the pond margins, using a knapsack sprayer.
- **Monitoring:** Observations were made each weekday for 2 weeks and then weekly until draining, including checks for sick or dead fish and for algal control. The excerpt ends before the field results are reported, so efficacy and fish-safety outcomes are not included here.
