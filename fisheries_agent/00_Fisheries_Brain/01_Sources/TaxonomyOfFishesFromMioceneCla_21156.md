# Taxonomy of Fishes from Miocene Clarkia Lake Beds, Idaho

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paleontological taxonomy paper by G. R. Smith and R. R. Miller describes the fish fossils from the Miocene [[Clarkia Lake Beds]] in northern Idaho. The assemblage contains three species (possibly four) from three families: a large trout tentatively assigned to the Eurasian genus [[Hucho]], a minnow referred to [[Gila]] (probably [[Gila turneri]]), and a sunfish described as the new species [[Archoplites clarki]]. The specimens are unusually well preserved, and the authors use them to infer the biogeographic history and paleoclimate of western North America.

## Background & Description

Miocene freshwater fishes of western North America were poorly known before this study. Available samples showed low diversity, representing nine families, with [[Salmonidae]], [[Cyprinidae]] and [[Centrarchidae]] among the most widely distributed. The Clarkia material was collected by C. J. Smiley and colleagues. Several dozen specimens were studied, and the authors note remarkably detailed skeletal preservation.

Most fossils come from transitional brown, ashy silts and silty clays (level 376-397 cm) below the ash in the type section at locality P-33. Additional specimens come from horizons at 120-150 and 206-236 cm at P-33 and from the Emerald Creek locality (P-37). Taphonomy is treated separately by Smith and Elder.

**Hucho sp.** (Salmoniformes, Salmonidae): A single large trout was collected at P-33 on June 12, 1980. It is estimated at 668 mm total length and 588 mm standard length. Diagnostic traits include large jaws and teeth, coarse scales, 55-58 vertebrae, 22 or 23 scale rows on the caudal peduncle, and 12 dorsal rays. The caudal fin has 10/9 principal rays.

**Gila sp.** (Cypriniformes, Cyprinidae): Nineteen specimens were studied. Seventeen are adults of 9.5-18 cm, and two are small, 55-65 mm long. Diagnostic traits include 8 dorsal rays (in 11 specimens), 8 or 9 anal rays, 19 caudal rays, 42 post-Weberian vertebrae (in two specimens), conical and hooked pharyngeal teeth in two rows, and a deeply forked caudal fin.

**Archoplites clarki** (Perciformes, Centrarchidae): Named for Capt. William Clark of the [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]]. The holotype (UMMP V 74202) is an imprint 123 mm standard length and 155 mm total length. It has 13 dorsal rays, 13 anal rays, and 4 predorsal bones. The description is based on 21 specimens plus the holotype, with standard lengths of 63-200 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Clarkia lake supported a fish fauna of low diversity by modern standards. The authors note that two of the three (possibly four) species belonged to groups widely distributed in the Miocene of western North America, and these occurred in other warm-water faunas of low species diversity.

Paleoclimate is inferred from both the flora and the fishes. The [[Clarkia Flora]] suggests a warm-temperate climate comparable to the southern Appalachians. The distribution of Archoplites indicates warm winters in northwestern North America. The salmonid, however, indicates moderately cool summers at Clarkia, since salmonids are rare in modern lakes dominated by centrarchids except at Great Lakes latitudes. Hucho is a relatively southern salmonid, found today in southern Europe and Japan.

Biogeographic context is also important. Miocene centrarchids ranged farther north than they do today, probably to Alaska. Miocene cyprinids extended from southern Nevada to northern Idaho and probably crossed from Asia via the [[Bering Strait]] in the early Miocene or late Oligocene. Hucho was probably also present in Alaskan fresh waters during the Miocene.

## Key Findings

- **Three families, three or four species:** The Clarkia fauna includes one salmonid, one centrarchid, and one or two cyprinids.
- **Hucho affinities:** The Clarkia trout differs from [[Eosalmo driftwoodensis]], an Eocene grayling-like trout from British Columbia, and from [[Oncorhynchus]], [[Salvelinus]] and [[Salmo]] in vertebral number, scale size and dorsal ray count. Its closest affinity is the Eurasian genus Hucho, especially [[Hucho perryi]] of Japan. The assignment is described as preliminary, with circumstantial support from Hucho remains in late Miocene [[Lake Idaho]] sediments.
- **Gila assignment is uncertain:** The authors refer the Clarkia minnows to Gila sp. because of taxonomic uncertainty and the lack of suitable type material. The forms resemble [[Gila turneri]] of the Miocene Esmeralda Group in Nevada. The authors describe them as generalized midwater fishes, not particularly primitive cyprinids.
- **Dorsal origin variation:** In most Gila specimens the dorsal fin originates over the pelvic origin, but in two larger specimens it originates behind. The authors suggest this may indicate two species, but they consider the character unreliable in fossils because of possible displacement during preservation.
- **New sunfish species:** [[Archoplites clarki]] is the most abundant of the three Clarkia species. It was a large sunfish that probably had predatory habits similar to those of [[Micropterus]] (black bass) in eastern North America. Its lineage is the most primitive and most western of known Miocene sunfish groups.
- **Archoplites diagnosis:** The genus is diagnosed by teeth on the endopterygoid, serrate preopercle and lacrimal, and long pelvic spines. Related fossil forms are placed in [[Plioplarchus]] and [[Boreocentrarchus]]. Specimens from [[Trout Creek, Oregon]] are intermediate in character.
- **Scale size:** The four largest A. clarki specimens (ca. 200 mm S.L.) have scales 4-5 mm in diameter. Isolated scales of 6.0 and 7.9 mm are attributed to the same species, with six and nine growth rings respectively.
- **Paleoclimate:** The Miocene distribution of Archoplites suggests warm winters, while the Hucho record suggests moderately cool summers at Clarkia.
- **Later changes:** Three salmonid genera, including Hucho, coexisted with Archoplites in southern Idaho later in the Miocene. By the late [[Pliocene]], following a cooling trend, Archoplites was restricted in the western U.S., Hucho was extinct in Lake Idaho, and other salmonids were abundant and widespread.
- **Related fossil localities:** The authors list similar late Miocene or Pliocene fishes from Montana, North Dakota, California, Utah and Nevada, and from the [[Bidahochi Formation]] in Arizona. Related Pliocene material is known from the [[Glenns Ferry Formation]] in Idaho.
