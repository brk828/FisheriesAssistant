# Non-native Fishes Inhabiting the Streams and Lakes of Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #invasive-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Laird and Page, 1996, Vol. 35, Art. 1) catalogs the non-native fishes established or occasionally found in Illinois streams and lakes. Of the 22 species treated, 13 were established by 1996 (six by 1979, seven more since) and nine are periodically stocked or stray in from neighboring states. The authors conclude that none of the introduced fishes has benefited native species or ecosystems, and that most, perhaps all, have had negative environmental impacts. The document serves as an identification guide (family and species keys supplementing [[Smith 1979 Fishes of Illinois]]) and a species-by-species summary of origin, biology, expansion potential and impact.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Keys, descriptions and summaries for 22 non-native species across 10 families, plus a family-level key covering native and introduced families.
- **Definition:** A non-native species is one that did not occur in Illinois historically. An established non-native reproduces in the wild as a result of human influence. The white catfish and Rio Grande cichlid were once considered established but are no longer thought to reproduce in Illinois.
- **Identification notes:** Diagnostic counts (lateral scales, fin rays, gill rakers, pharyngeal teeth) separate closely related species. Lengths given are total lengths.
- **Introduction pathways:** Four established species (sea lamprey, alewife, threespine stickleback, white perch) arrived through the [[Welland Canal]], built in the 1820s. Goldfish and Oriental weatherfish escaped from ponds or were released by aquarists. Round goby arrived in ship ballast water, as did [[Zebra Mussel]]. Six species were deliberately introduced to improve fishing (common carp, grass carp, bighead carp, threadfin shad, rainbow smelt, inland silverside). The six Lake Michigan salmonids were also stocked for angling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native fish richness decline:** Native fish species fell from 187 at the turn of the century (Forbes and Richardson 1908) to 174 in 1996, a loss of 13 species attributed largely to environmental degradation, probably including exotic introductions.
- **Vegetation loss:** Many native species depend on aquatic plants for cover, spawning and food. Siltation from poor agriculture and herbicide use are depleting plants, and grass carp and common carp add pressure. Threatened and endangered fishes such as the bigeye chub, bigeye shiner, starhead topminnow and banded killifish are strongly vegetation-dependent.
- **Plankton competition:** Bighead and silver carp feed on plankton, the food of larval fishes and native mussels.
- **Lake Michigan:** The sea lamprey, introduced via the Welland Canal, caused major declines in lake trout and cisco. Alewife die-offs in the late 1960s fouled Lake Michigan beaches.
- **Waterfowl:** Species dependent on aquatic vegetation are negatively affected by common and grass carp.

## Key Findings

**Overall conclusions**
- No introduced fish has enhanced a native species or natural ecosystem in Illinois; most, and perhaps all, have had negative impacts.
- Economic and recreational benefits of introductions are accompanied by environmental and economic tradeoffs.

**Species-level findings (selected)**
- **[[Sea Lamprey]]:** First found in Illinois in 1936 on a Lake Michigan lake trout. Parasitic adults kill fish; control relies on the lampricide [[TFM]]. Lieffers (1990) found 88% of sampled invertebrate populations declined after TFM application, and Bills and Johnson (1992) showed TFM toxicity rises as pH falls. Adults live 1–1.5 years; fecundity 24,000–107,000 eggs.
- **[[Alewife]]:** Found in Lake Michigan in 1949; reproduces only in the main body of the lake. Expansion beyond Lake Michigan is not expected. Schools of 5,000–6,000 individuals were observed.
- **[[Threadfin Shad]]:** First found in Illinois in 1957; now common in the Ohio River and present in the Illinois River. Intolerant of cold (fatal at 4°C), so unlikely to become common in northern Illinois. Potentially depletes plankton.
- **[[Common Carp]]:** Present since the earliest surveys; roots in substrate, increasing turbidity and destroying vegetation. Possibly displaces carpsuckers and buffalos. Expansion not expected to change.
- **[[Grass Carp]]:** Reproducing in Illinois (Raibley et al. 1995), with records in the Mississippi, Illinois, Chicago and Kaskaskia rivers. Herbivory threatens vegetated habitats used by the blacknose shiner, pugnose shiner and Iowa darter. Likely to become more numerous, especially in large rivers.
- **[[Silver Carp]] and [[Bighead Carp]]:** Filter-feeding plankton consumers. Silver carp has not been found reproducing in Illinois but is likely to establish. Bighead carp young-of-the-year were collected (Burr and Warren 1993), suggesting established status. Both may deplete zooplankton.
- **[[Rudd]]:** Spreading as bait minnow from Arkansas bait-fish trade. No reproduction documented in Illinois, but establishment is likely if use as bait continues.
- **[[Round Goby]]:** First collected in Illinois in 1993 (Calumet River drainage); now abundant in southwestern Lake Michigan. Eats eggs and young fish; likely to harm native sculpins and benthic species.
- **[[Rainbow Smelt]]:** Common in Illinois Lake Michigan waters; may contribute to declines in lake herring through larval competition.
- **[[White Perch]]:** Reproducing in Illinois and rapidly expanding; likely to become statewide and compete with white and yellow bass.
- **[[Striped Bass]]:** First appeared in 1974 as an escapee from Kentucky impoundments; reproduces in the Ohio River. Highly piscivorous, with potential effects on native game and endangered fishes.
- **Salmonids (Lake Michigan):** Coho and chinook salmon, pink salmon, rainbow trout and brown trout are stocked or stray into Illinois waters. Coho and chinook help control alewives. Atlantic salmon are too rare to have an ecological impact. Pink salmon do not reproduce in Illinois.
- **[[Inland Silverside]]:** Likely to reproduce in Illinois given abundance in the Ohio River; may deplete invertebrates.
- **[[Threespine Stickleback]]:** Reproducing in Illinois waters of Lake Michigan (first collected 1988 in Cook and Lake counties); eats fish eggs and may compete with native sticklebacks.
- **[[Goldfish]] and [[Oriental Weatherfish]]:** Goldfish are common only in severely degraded areas (little impact). Oriental weatherfish reproduces in Illinois, with first collection in 1987 and a range limited to Cook County and Lake Michigan.

**Recommendations and management implications**
- Authors recommend monitoring non-native impacts using the keys and species accounts.
- Restoration of aquatic vegetation and water quality is identified as essential to native fish survival.
