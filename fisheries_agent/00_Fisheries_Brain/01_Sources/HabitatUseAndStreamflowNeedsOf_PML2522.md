# Habitat Use and Streamflow Needs of Rare and Endangered Fishes in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared under the [[Flaming Gorge Studies Program]] (1991), synthesizes sixteen-plus studies of four federally listed or imperiled Colorado River fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It evaluates how their habitat use and life-history stages respond to streamflow in the Green River, Utah, downstream of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and below the [[Yampa River]] confluence. The authors conclude that flow regulation has reduced spring peaks, elevated winter baseflows, and produced unnatural daily fluctuations, which together degrade spawning, nursery, and overwintering habitat. They recommend synchronized spring releases, gradually declining summer flows, and stable low winter flows, alongside bottomland restoration and control of non-native fishes. The report is the second of two Green River basin documents; the first addressed the Yampa River (Tyus and Karp 1989).

## Background & Description

The Green River, the largest sub-basin of the upper Colorado River basin (about 116,000 km²), was studied from its confluence with the Colorado River to the Yampa confluence at km 552 in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Its native fish fauna includes cyprinids, catostomids, salmonids, and sculpins. Of these, four large-river species have declined: Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, Humpback Chub, and Bonytail Chub. Colorado Squawfish, Humpback Chub, and Bonytail Chub are federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). The Razorback Sucker was proposed for federal listing and is protected by state statutes in Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, and Utah.

Major threats include habitat loss, flow and temperature regulation, sediment changes, and proliferation of more than about 10 common non-native species, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Northern Pike]], and [[Walleye]]. Closure of Flaming Gorge Dam in 1962 altered the historic flow regime. Daily peaking operations produced extreme fluctuations, and winter baseflows at Green River, Utah, rose from about 79.24 m³/s to 107.54 m³/s (1957–1984 comparison). Historic spring peaks of the Green River below the Yampa confluence were largely maintained by Yampa runoff, but peaks below the dam itself became nearly nonexistent.

The Flaming Gorge Studies were a 1986 interagency effort between the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], executed by the [[Colorado River Fishes Project]] in Vernal, Utah. Studies used radiotelemetry, capture-recapture, drift nets, seines, and remote sensing to link flows to fish life stages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types.** Adult Colorado Squawfish use eddies, runs, backwaters, and seasonally flooded shorelines, while juveniles and larvae concentrate in shoreline [[Backwater Habitat]]. Humpback Chub favor high-gradient, whitewater reaches and shoreline eddies in canyons such as [[Yampa Canyon]], [[Whirlpool Canyon]], [[Desolation Canyon]], and [[Gray Canyon]]. Razorback Sucker spawn on cobble, gravel, and sand riffles and use flooded bottomlands such as Old Charley Wash and Stewart Lake Drain.

**Flow–habitat linkages.** Flow determines backwater formation and persistence. Aerial and video remote sensing ([[Remote Sensing Habitat Mapping]]) found that backwater area peaked at summer flows of about 28.3–50.94 m³/s at the Jensen gauge, while flows above 50.94 m³/s reduced backwater development. Gradually decreasing summer hydrographs maximized backwater availability.

**Non-native species interactions.** Stable, moderate regulated flows appear to favor introduced fishes from more mesic environments. Red shiner, fathead minnow, and green sunfish showed aggressive behavior toward young squawfish ([[Behavioral Interactions Study]]). Channel catfish were abundant in humpback chub eddies and were found lodged in adult squawfish throats. Northern pike and walleye, introduced in the 1960s–1977 stocking period, expanded in range and abundance, and pike recaptures moved 78 and 110 km within about a year.

**Food webs.** Backwater invertebrates, particularly chironomid larvae, dominated young squawfish diets. Mormon crickets (*Anabrus simplex*) migrating across the river were eaten by both native and introduced fishes, and pesticide control of crickets was flagged as a risk to fish food webs.

**Riparian change.** Invasion of [[Saltcedar]] into the flood zone, under controlled flows, stabilizes banks, traps sediment, and reduces ephemeral backwater and eddy habitat.

## Key Findings

### Colorado Squawfish

- **Early life stages:** Of 11,379 age-0 squawfish captured (1979–1988), postlarvae were concentrated about 150 km downstream by autumn. Abundance and growth were inversely correlated with high summer and autumn flows, which inundated nursery habitat. Overwintering mortality was not significant in 1985–86, 1987–88, and 1988–89.
- **Backwater use:** Of 1,194 young squawfish seined, 85% were captured in shoreline backwaters. Of 922 fin-clipped, 230 (25%) were recaptured.
- **Migration:** Of 153 radio-tracked fish, 63% were highly mobile; 41% migrated to known spawning sites. Spawning migrations began about 28 days after peak spring flow at water temperatures of 9°C or greater. Migrations to known spawning sites averaged 140.7 km (range 32–372.8 km).
- **Spawning:** Spawning occurred in Yampa Canyon and Gray Canyon during declining flows and temperatures near 23°C, with an optimum period of about 26 days. Spawning was not triggered by a single flow or temperature event.
- **Winter:** Adults moved more under fluctuating flows exceeding 5 cm/h, with about 190% greater movement in adult squawfish.
- **Age structure:** Aged fish ranged from 3 to over 31 years, and age–size relations resembled those of historic collections.

### Razorback Sucker

- **Population:** The upper Green River population was estimated at 948 fish (95% CI 758–1,138) from 410 captures (68 recaptures). Lower Green River numbers were too low for an estimate (13 captured, 1 recaptured).
- **Spawning:** Ripe fish were captured from mid-April to early June at water temperatures of about 14°C (range 9–17°C). Spawning migrations of 30–106 km were detected in 17 fish.
- **Recruitment:** No recruitment has been documented since the 1960s. Aged fish from the Green River averaged 27 years, suggesting old standing stocks.
- **Threats:** Predation by non-native fishes, habitat loss, lower spring temperatures, and curtailed spring flooding are cited as causes.

### Humpback Chub

- **Distribution:** Only 133 juvenile and adult fish were captured in Dinosaur National Monument in spring sampling (1986–1989). Humpback chub spawned in shoreline eddies at river temperatures of about 20°C, most often in May–June, and extended into July in the high-flow year of 1986.
- **Competition:** Channel catfish comprised 35% of the angling catch with mature humpback chub, indicating a potential for competition and predation.

### Bonytail Chub

- Extremely rare; 36 captured in the Yampa and upper Green rivers (1968–1970) and only sporadic captures afterward. Decline in Echo Park is linked to altered flow and temperature and reduced macroinvertebrate taxa. Reintroduction success depends on dam operations.

### Flow Recommendations

- **Spring (March 21–June 21):** Increase Flaming Gorge releases in average and high water years (about 70% of years) to mimic Yampa spring timing and duration. Maximum releases of 113.2 m³/s, synchronized with Yampa runoff for three to six weeks, were proposed, followed by at least 56.6 m³/s for an additional week. Water temperatures should range from 14°C to 22°C during squawfish migration and reach at least 20°C by June 1 in the Ouray reach.
- **Summer (June 22–September 22):** Decrease flows gradually (no more than about 14.2 m³/s per day), targeting no more than 51.0 m³/s at Jensen, with weekly fluctuations within about 25% of the target. Water temperatures should average 22–25°C in Gray Canyon from July 1 to August 15.
- **Autumn (September 23–December 21):** Maintain low flows until November or until most age-0 squawfish leave backwaters.
- **Winter (December 22–March 20):** Stabilize low winter flows, maintain ice cover until spring breakup, and avoid flows that scour shorelines.
- **Flood pulses:** Periodic high flows of at least 3–5 days near average annual historic levels were recommended to control saltcedar and woody vegetation.

### Other Management Options

- **Bottomland restoration:** Evaluate and restore flooded areas such as Old Charley Wash, Stewart Lake Drain, and locations near Ouray National Wildlife Refuge, and remove barriers to fish access.
- **Flow re-regulation:** Explore options to allow higher spring and lower winter flows without loss of power generation.
- **Control of introduced fishes:** Investigate native–non-native interactions with controlled experiments. Current stable flows appear to favor introduced species.
- **Population augmentation:** Consider stocking of razorback sucker and bonytail chub, but only after understanding chemosensory cues, larval drift, and homing behavior.

### Overall Conclusions

- The Yampa River is essential to maintaining the spring hydrograph and usable habitat on the Green River, so the two rivers must be managed as one system.
- Reproduction and survival appear highest in years whose hydrographs approximate natural flow conditions.
- Caution is needed in interpreting habitat use, because observed use may reflect temporary or marginal habitat.
