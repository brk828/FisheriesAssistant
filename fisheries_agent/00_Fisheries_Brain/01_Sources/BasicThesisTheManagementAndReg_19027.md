# Risk Perception, Risk Management and Public Trust

#ichthyology #marine-biology #risk-perception

## Executive Summary

This presentation-style document (apparently a 1993 slide deck with accompanying clippings and articles) argues that the management and regulation of hazards must rest on an understanding of how people think about risk. It contrasts risk assessment treated as pure science with risk assessment treated as politics, documents a paradox in which societies that are on average healthier and safer grow more concerned about risk, and examines why trust in institutions is the decisive factor in whether risk communication succeeds. Its case studies include the 1991 Peruvian [[Cholera Epidemic]] linked to halted chlorination, the 1982 [[Tylenol Poisonings]], and the [[Harvard Lifesaving Study]] on cost-effectiveness of life-saving interventions. The document contains no fish, invertebrate or aquatic-ecosystem data; its relevance to aquatic science is indirect (water disinfection and contamination risk).

## Background & Description

The material is a compilation of slides, newspaper reports, journal excerpts and survey results on [[Risk Perception]]. Key framing points include:

- Perceived risk is described as a "battleground" of strong, conflicting views.
- The authors contrast two models: "Risk Assessment as Science" (public perceptions irrelevant) versus "Risk Assessment as Politics" (public perceptions central), and propose a "New Model" in which science and public values jointly guide policy.
- Risk-related research spans identification, measurement, evaluation, and perception/communication, and is drawn from fields such as [[Toxicology]], epidemiology, engineering, microbiology and radiation biology.
- Limitations of [[Risk Assessment]] include reliance on theoretical models and subjective judgments, difficulty extrapolating from animals to humans, high doses to low doses, acute versus chronic exposure, synergistic effects and sensitive subpopulations. The slides stress that communicators must distinguish uncertainty from ignorance.

Descriptions of risk measures show that conclusions depend on the metric chosen. A coal-mining example (Wilson & Crouch, 1982) shows that accidental deaths per million tons of coal and deaths per thousand employees give different trends; the slides conclude that whether coal mining is "getting safer" depends on the measure. A list of mortality-expression options (per capita, per facility, per unit of product, per dollar of output) illustrates the same point.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although the document does not address aquatic ecosystems directly, several environmental cases are discussed:

- **Drinking water and disinfection:** Chlorine protects against microbial risk, but chlorination byproducts such as [[Trihalomethanes]] (THMs) are suspected carcinogens. The US standard for THMs was cited as 100 parts per billion (p.p.b.), and an EPA study estimated a cancer risk of about 1 in 10,000 at 100 p.p.b.
- **Cholera outbreak:** The article reports more than 300,000 cases and 3,516 deaths in Peru and roughly two dozen other countries in South and Central America. Infection is attributed to contaminated bilge water from a Chinese freighter in the harbour at Lima, spreading first through shellfish and fish (ceviche) and then through a water supply with old pipes and open, unchlorinated wells.
- **Regional EPA rankings (1990):** Indoor radon, indoor air pollution, pesticides, and drinking water contamination were consistently ranked as high health risks. Point-source discharges to surface waters and physical habitat modification were ranked the highest ecological risks, while active and abandoned hazardous waste sites were ranked as low health risks.

## Key Findings

**Perception and paradox**
- Public concern about risk has risen even as average health and safety have improved. A comparison of risk today versus 20 years ago shows that respondents in Sweden and Canada rated chemicals, drinking water, climate change and infectious disease as higher risks today.
- Expert and layperson rankings diverge sharply. In a 1977 ranking, laypersons placed nuclear power first and motor vehicles second, while experts placed motor vehicles first and smoking second; nuclear power was ranked 20th by experts.

**Qualitative risk factors**
- Public risk judgments are shaped by qualities such as voluntary versus involuntary exposure, dread, catastrophic potential, delayed effects, unknown-to-science status, and inequity. Factor analysis places nuclear and chemical risks high on "unknown" and "dread" dimensions.

**Chemical concern**
- In a 1992 HWC survey, a majority of respondents reported trying hard to avoid chemical contact, while Chemical Company executives and college students showed contrasting free-association responses to the word "chemicals."

**Social and demographic influences**
- In the 1992 U.S. National Risk Survey, women rated many technology risks higher than men. Nine hypotheses are offered for this gap, including risk aversion, less technical knowledge, caregiving roles, and distrust of institutions.
- Among physical and life scientists, gender differences were reported for nuclear energy, waste storage and transport risks.
- "Low-risk-perception individuals" were more likely to accept imposing very small risks without consent, to trust experts and government and industry, and to oppose local authority to close a plant.

**Trust as the central variable**
- "Acceptance of any risk is more dependent on public confidence in risk management than on quantitative estimates of risk" (C. Starr).
- Trust is described as fragile and asymmetric: negative events weigh more than positive ones, bad news is seen as more credible, and risk is easier to demonstrate than safety.
- In a Koren & Klein study of newspaper coverage, a positive JAMA radiation-cancer study received more and longer press coverage (354 ± 181 vs. 192 ± 178 words; P = .04) than a negative study, indicating media bias against null results.
- Trust-decreasing events (e.g., concealed safety problems, delayed inspections) were rated as having greater impact than trust-increasing events in a survey of a hypothetical nuclear plant.

**Policy failures and successes**
- The Peruvian cholera epidemic is presented as a case of risk miscalculation, in which fear of chlorination by-products led to halted disinfection. Researchers question whether EPA emphasised the microbial risk of not disinfecting enough.
- EPA priorities were found to correspond more closely with public opinion than with its own task force's risk estimates (EPA Journal, 1987).
- The regional "Unfinished Business" study (1990) found that EPA regions agreed broadly on rankings but differed where regional conditions differed, such as acid rain effects.
- A Harvard study of 587 interventions found that medical care generally saves lives at lower cost than workplace-safety or environmental measures, and that about 60,000 deaths per year could be averted by reallocating $21.3 billion spent on 185 programs.
- The 1982 Tylenol case showed substantial stock losses for Johnson & Johnson, supporting the view of brand names as quality-assuring mechanisms (Mitchell).

**Recommendations**
- The "system" can amplify distrust through sensational media, special-interest groups, adversarial litigation and proliferating risk studies.
- Proposed directions: make risk assessment sensitive to public views and improve public participation mechanisms, rather than restricting public input as in the Florida law example.
