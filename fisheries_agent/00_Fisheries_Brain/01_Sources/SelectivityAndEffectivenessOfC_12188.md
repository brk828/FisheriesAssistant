# Selectivity and Effectiveness of Certain Types of Commercial Nets in the T.V.A. Lakes of Alabama

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #fisheries-management #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by C. E. White, Jr. (Alabama Department of Conservation) evaluated the effectiveness and selectivity of commercial netting gear — [[Trammel Net|trammel nets]], [[Riprap Net|riprap nets]], and [[Gill Net|gill nets]] — for harvesting rough fish in the [[T.V.A. Lakes|Tennessee Valley Authority (T.V.A.) impoundments]] of Alabama (~180,000 acres). Conducted from November 1955 through May 1956 under supervision of state fishery biologists but operated by resident commercial fishermen, the study tested nylon webbing of 3-, 3.5-, and 4-inch bar measure across multiple net-set configurations (whip sets vs. fixed sets). A total of 10,425 fish and turtles weighing 42,689 pounds were captured, of which 95.4% by weight were classified as [[Rough Fish|rough fish]]. The **whip-set trammel net** using **3-inch bar measure** mesh was found to be the most effective and reasonably selective gear, substantially outperforming fixed trammel nets, riprap nets, and gill nets. The findings support commercial netting as a viable [[Rough Fish Removal|rough fish removal]] management tool to improve sport fishing in large reservoir systems.

## Background & Description

This paper is a fisheries-gear and population-catch study rather than a morphological description of a single species; morphological data are limited to size-class and species composition of catches.

- **Species Diversity Captured:** 20+ taxa recorded, including:
  - Rough/commercial fish: [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Buffalo (Fish)|Buffalo]] (Bigmouth *Megastomatobus cyprinella*, Black *Ictiobus niger*, Smallmouth *Ictiobus bubalus*), [[Carpsucker]] (*Carpiodes* spp.), [[Catfish]] (Blue *Ictalurus furcatus*, Channel *Ictalurus lacustris*, Flathead *Pylodictis olivaris*, Brown Bullhead *Ictalurus nebulosus*), [[Gar]] (Longnose, Shortnose, Spotted *Lepisosteus* spp.), [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*), [[Redhorse]] (*Moxostoma* sp.), [[Shad]] (Gizzard, Threadfin), [[Skipjack Herring]] (*Pomolobus chrysochloris*), [[Spoonbill (Paddlefish)]] (*Polyodon spathula*).
  - Game fish: [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*), [[White Bass]] (*Lepibema chrysops*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Sauger]] (*Stizostedion canadense*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*).
  - Non-fish bycatch: freshwater turtles (4.4% of total weight).
- **Size Selectivity:** Overlap in size ranges of species caught across 3-, 3.5-, and 4-inch mesh nets was substantial enough that minimum size retained per mesh size could not be determined for any species.
- **Net Construction Details** (gear morphology, not organism):
  - Trammel nets: three-net panel system; outer walls of 14-inch bar measure No. 9 nylon (3 meshes deep), inner webbing 3-, 3.5-, or 4-inch bar measure No. 3/No. 4 nylon (23–31 meshes deep).
  - Riprap nets: similar to trammel but outer walls only ½ mesh deep, allowing inner webbing to hang without tension; wall bar measure varied with net depth.
  - Gill nets: standard single-wall design, No. 3/No. 4 nylon, 3- or 4-inch bar measure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Large, main-stem reservoir impoundments of the Tennessee River system in Alabama, characterized by open water, old pre-impoundment channels, sloughs, and creek arms — habitats exploited differently by various net-set configurations (semicircle, double semicircle, circle, spiral, fencerow, and fixed sets).
- **Species Aggregation Behavior:** [[Carp]], [[Buffalo (Fish)|Buffalo]], and [[Carpsucker]] were observed to congregate in large, dense schools during spawning, feeding, and at other times for unclear reasons — a behavior that made **whip sets** (in which fishermen actively drive fish into stationary nets) especially effective, since fishermen could pursue schools rather than passively wait for fish to encounter fixed gear. One single 400-yard whip set captured 1,033 pounds of fish, 99% of which were buffalo, carp, and carpsucker combined.
- **Trophic/Community Composition:** Rough fish (carp, buffalo, catfish, carpsucker, gar) dominated the community catch (~95.4% of total weight), reflecting the trophic structure of these reservoirs where rough fish biomass substantially outweighs game fish biomass. Game fish (white crappie, white bass, largemouth bass, sauger, bluegill) made up a small minority (<5%) of catch weight across all gear types, consistent with the ecological rationale for targeted rough-fish removal to reduce competition with sport species.
- **Interaction with Fishery Management:** The study is directly tied to the [[Rough Fish Removal|rough fish removal]] management paradigm described by Dequine (1952) and Hulsey (1957), wherein reduction of rough fish biomass in impoundments correlates with improved sport fishing outcomes, also corroborated by observations following natural fish kills in Alabama impoundments.

## Key Findings

- **Overall catch:** 10,425 fish/turtles totaling 42,689 lbs captured Nov 1955–May 1956; 95.4% by weight were rough fish.
- **Species dominance by weight:** Carp (35.1%), Buffalo (30.8%), Catfish (8.5%), Carpsucker (8.3%), Gar (5.7%), Turtles (4.4%). Game fish: White crappie (3.6%), White bass (0.6%), Largemouth bass (0.4%); Sauger and bluegill each <0.05%.
- **Gear effectiveness ranking (pounds of rough fish per 100-yard-net-day):**
  1. **Whip-set trammel net (3-inch mesh):** 163 lbs/100-yard-net-day — most effective gear overall, 114 lbs more than 3.5-inch and 138 lbs more than 4-inch whip-set trammel nets.
  2. **Fixed trammel net (3-inch mesh):** 28 lbs/100-yard-net-day (3.5-inch mesh slightly higher at ~30 lbs, likely due to chance; both outperformed 4-inch at ~12 lbs).
  3. **Riprap net (3-inch mesh):** 19 lbs/100-yard-net-day, versus ~17 lbs (3.5-inch) and ~9 lbs (4-inch).
  4. **Gill net (3-inch mesh):** 12 lbs/100-yard-net-day, versus ~4 lbs (4-inch) — least efficient gear overall despite good selectivity.
- **Mesh size effect:** Across all gear types, effectiveness (catch per net-day) consistently **decreased as bar measure increased from 3 to 4 inches**.
- **Selectivity:** Gill nets and whip-set trammel nets were most selective for rough fish (97% rough fish by weight); fixed trammel and riprap nets were slightly less selective (94% rough fish by weight). Combined across gear types, 3-inch mesh caught 94% rough fish, 3.5-inch caught 98%, and 4-inch caught 99% rough fish by weight — showing a selectivity/effectiveness tradeoff (larger mesh = more selective for rough fish but far less total catch).
- **Riprap 3-inch mesh** had the highest percentage of game fish bycatch by weight (8%) of any gear/mesh combination tested — indicating lower selectivity against game species despite decent rough-fish catch rates.
- **Double whip-set mesh-passage experiments:**
  - 3-inch vs. 3.5-inch paired nets: 65% of total catch weight passed through the 3.5-inch mesh and was retained by the 3-inch mesh.
  - 3-inch vs. 4-inch paired nets: 88% of total catch weight passed through the 4-inch mesh and was retained by the 3-inch mesh.
  - This confirms substantial escapement through larger mesh sizes, reinforcing 3-inch bar measure as optimal for rough fish harvest.
- **Behavioral/operational insight:** The superior performance of whip-set trammel nets was attributed primarily to the **active pursuit technique** (fishermen driving fish into nets) combined with the natural schooling/aggregation tendency of carp, buffalo, and carpsucker, rather than passive gear encounter rates — corroborated by comparable findings in Starrett and Barnickol (1955) on the Mississippi River.
- **Management implication:** The combination of high catch efficiency and acceptable rough-fish selectivity makes the **3-inch bar-measure whip-set trammel net** the recommended gear for commercial rough fish removal programs in T.V.A.-type reservoirs, supporting broader [[Sport Fishery Improvement|sport fishery improvement]] goals via reduced rough fish biomass.
