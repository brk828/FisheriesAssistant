# Selected References on Electrofishing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #electrofishing #fisheries-methodology

## Executive Summary

This document is a **1980 annotated bibliography** compiled by R.S. Wydoski of the National Fisheries Center-Leetown, cataloging scientific literature on **[[Electrofishing]]** as a fisheries sampling and management technique. Rather than presenting original morphological or ecological data on a single species, the source synthesizes decades of research (primarily 1938–1978) spanning four major thematic categories: (1) general techniques and equipment design, (2) use of electrofishing in population sampling, (3) physiological and lethal effects of electric current on fish, and (4) effects of electrofishing on aquatic invertebrates. The references collectively span freshwater and marine systems and involve numerous taxa, including [[Salmonidae]], [[Centrarchidae]], [[Ictaluridae]], [[Cyprinidae]], [[Petromyzontidae]] (lampreys), and various tunas and coastal pelagic fishes.

## Background & Description

While this bibliography does not describe a single species' morphology, several referenced studies address **physiological and anatomical responses** to electric fields across taxa, which are relevant to understanding species-specific electrosensitivity:

- **Electrical resistivity of tissue**: Nakatani (1954) measured average specific electrical resistance of [[Salmonidae|salmonids]], a key determinant of an individual fish's susceptibility to a given electric field.
- **Body size and electrotaxis**: Klima (1972) examined voltage and pulse-rate thresholds for inducing electrotaxis across twelve coastal pelagic and bottom-dwelling marine fishes, implying morphology-dependent responses (e.g., body length, orientation to field lines).
- **Internal injury**: Spencer (1967) documented internal injuries (e.g., spinal hemorrhaging, hematomas) in [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] caused by electric current exposure — a key morphological/pathological consequence of the technique.
- **Blood chemistry alterations**: Bouck and Ball (1966) found that capture method (including electrofishing) affects blood characteristics and mortality in [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*).
- **Reproductive tissue damage**: Multiple studies (Elder 1954; Godfrey 1956; Kmioteck & Helm 1961; Marriott 1973) reported damage to eggs, sperm, and fertility in salmonids and northern pike following electric shock exposure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The references span diverse aquatic habitats and demonstrate how electrofishing performance and effects are mediated by environmental/ecological context:

- **Freshwater lotic systems (streams/rivers)**: Numerous studies (Larimore 1961; Penczak & Zalewski 1973; Peterman 1978; Alabaster & Harley 1962) evaluate electrofishing efficiency in flowing water, where conductivity, current velocity, and channel morphology affect capture success.
- **Lentic systems (lakes/ponds)**: Latta & Myers (1961), Klein (1967), and Sanderson (1960) applied electrofishing in standing water bodies for population estimation of species like trout and largemouth bass.
- **Hatchery environments**: Combs (1968) used electrical grids to control trematode cercariae in hatchery water supplies, an applied ecological/parasitological use case.
- **Marine and estuarine systems**: Bary (1956) and Miyake & Steiger (1958) examined electric field effects on marine fishes including tuna, indicating cross-habitat applicability of the technique despite differing water conductivity.
- **Migratory behavior manipulation**: Burrows (1957), McLain (1957), and Maxfield et al. (1959) explored using electric fields to divert or guide migrating salmonids and squawfish (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*), relevant to fish passage and dam management.
- **Invertebrate community effects**: Bisson (1976), Elliott & Bagenal (1972), and Fowles (1975) documented electrofishing-induced drift and disturbance of benthic invertebrate fauna, showing non-target ecological impacts within stream ecosystems.
- **Sea lamprey control**: McLain, Smith, and Moore (1965) applied electrofishing as a control method for invasive [[Sea Lamprey]] populations in Lake Superior, an example of electrofishing as an ecosystem management tool rather than pure sampling.

## Key Findings

- **Equipment evolution**: Early literature (1940s–1960s) documents the transition from AC to pulsed DC current systems, driven by findings (e.g., Webster et al. 1955; Pratt 1951) that pulsed DC improves capture efficiency and reduces fish injury relative to AC.
- **Water conductivity as a critical variable**: Multiple studies (DeMont 1971; Lennon & Parker 1958; Lennon 1959; McCauley 1960) identify water resistivity/conductivity as a primary factor governing electrofishing effectiveness, prompting techniques like salt addition to standardize field strength.
- **Population estimation biases**: Bohlin & Sundstrom (1977) and Junge & Libesvarsky (1965) show that unequal catchability and size-selectivity bias population estimates derived from removal methods using electrofishing, a methodological caveat for [[Mark-Recapture]] studies.
- **Gear selectivity**: Funk (1958) and Timmons (1975) found that electrofishing gear exhibits size- and species-selectivity compared to other gear types (e.g., gill nets, seines), affecting community composition estimates.
- **Physiological costs of capture**: A substantial body of work (Schreck et al. 1976; Burns & Lantz 1978; Curry & Kynard 1978) documents electrofishing-induced stress responses, galvanonarcosis, and behavioral effects in species such as [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]].
- **Mortality and reproductive impacts**: Collins et al. (1954) and Pugh (1962) linked pulsating DC exposure to salmon fingerling mortality, while several studies found reduced egg/sperm viability following shock exposure during spawning.
- **Non-target impacts on invertebrates**: Electrofishing was shown to increase invertebrate drift and disturb benthic fauna (Bisson 1976; Fowles 1975), indicating that the technique's ecological footprint extends beyond the target fish community.
- **Species-specific electrosensitivity**: Comparative studies (Mitra & Biswas 1969; Namboodiri & Verghese 1969; Klima 1972) demonstrate that narcotizing/electrotaxis thresholds vary substantially among taxa (e.g., Indian carps [[Catla Catla]], [[Cirrhinus Mrigala]], versus coastal pelagic and bottom fishes), underscoring the need for species- and habitat-specific calibration of electrofishing parameters.
