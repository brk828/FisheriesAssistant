# Syrische Süßwasserfische und Entwicklungsbiologie: Natur und Museum Issue Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This issue of *Natur und Museum*, published by the [[Senckenberg Naturforschende Gesellschaft]] in Frankfurt am Main, contains several articles. Its central fish-focused contribution is Friedhelm Krupp's study of the distribution of Syrian freshwater fishes under human influence. Krupp reports that Syria's freshwater fauna is shaped by three zoogeographic origins, by the drying of rivers and wetlands, by salinisation and by deliberate and accidental introductions of non-native species. Other articles in the issue cover the development of yolk-rich eggs (Otto Larink), trampling damage by Shelducks and Eiders (Jürgen Wietfeld), the history of the biogenetic law (Franz M. Wuketits), a crustacean museum in Tokyo, book reviews and society news. Only the fish article is summarised in detail here.

## Background & Description

Krupp's article is based on field collections and reports in the [[Middle East]], especially Syria. The author draws on earlier collections, including those of [[Wolfgang C. Beckman]] (1962), who worked for the [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) between 1955 and 1959, and on [[Hecker]]'s (1843) description of fishes from the Qwaiq. The region between the Mediterranean coast and the Tigris is described as one of the oldest cultural areas of the Earth. Ancient cities such as [[Dura Europos]] and [[Mari]] on the [[Euphrates River]] show that intensive irrigation agriculture once existed in areas that are now desert. Krupp suggests that rainfall was then much higher, and that the vegetation and water supply were far richer.

Syria is described as a land bridge between Europe, Asia and Africa. Its fish fauna therefore combines elements from three regions:

- **Palaearctic (Eurasian) origin:** Cobitidae (loaches).
- **Ethiopian (African) origin:** Cichlidae (cichlids) and Clariidae (air-breathing catfishes).
- **Oriental (South Asian) origin:** Cyprinidae (carps and minnows).

Krupp reports about 90 fish species described from Syrian waters. The origin of some of them remains unclear.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Euphrates.** The Euphrates is described as a foreign river crossing a desert, with agriculture limited to narrow strips irrigated along its banks. Its fauna is dominated by barbs (*Barbus* sp., *Tor* sp., *Capoeta trutta*), catfishes and [[Abu Mullet]] (*Liza abu*). Abu Mullet is often the only fish in oxbow lakes that are too saline for barbs.

**Orontes.** The Orontes is the most important river of western Syria. It supports many cyprinids (*Garra*, *Capoeta*, *Chondrostoma*, *Alburnus*, *Leuciscus*, *Barbus*, *Phoxinellus*) and also the [[Mystus halepensis]] catfish, [[European Eel]] (*Anguilla anguilla*), *Clarias* and loaches (*Cobitis*, *Schistura*). Slow or standing waters hold large numbers of [[Cyprus Toothcarp]] (*Aphanius cypris*).

**Qwaiq.** This river in northern Syria ends in an endorheic basin with no outflow. Earlier records list cyprinids, cobitids, clariids, bagrids (*Mystus*), and spiny eels (*Mastacembelus*). Its fauna has declined sharply.

**Southern Syria.** Inland drainages here are characterised by Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae and the northernmost natural occurrence of Cichlidae.

**Coastal streams.** Small, fast-flowing streams that enter the Mediterranean are dominated by loaches (*Cobitidae*) and suckering barbs (*Garra* sp.).

**Water loss.** Groundwater decline has dried many streams that once flowed year-round. The Nahr ay-Sardi, a tributary of the Orontes, is dry in summer but flows strongly after winter rains. In March 1979, *Schistura* sp., *Garra rufa* and *Capoeta capoeta* were abundant there. Fish recolonise the stream from the Orontes, which keeps a steady level through springs, and some may survive in residual pools.

**Salinity.** Salinity limits colonisation in many irrigation and drainage channels. Even so, a drainage channel of the Gab wetland with 508 mg chloride supported five species: [[Zill's Tilapia]] (*Tilapia zillii*), *Gambusia affinis*, *Aphanius cypris*, *Hemigrammocapoeta* and *Garra rufa*, plus *Phoxinellus zeregi*. Mosquitofish occurred at 1.6% salinity and Abu Mullet at 1.8%.

## Key Findings

1. **Historical decline of the Qwaiq.** In 1979, the middle Qwaiq was largely dry, and local villagers collected fish from residual pools. Only four species were found: *Phoxinellus zeregi*, *Garra variabilis*, *Alburnus sellal* and *Chondrostoma regium*. Krupp concludes that most of the river's species have probably been lost. Beckman (1962) had been unable to refind several species recorded from the river.

2. **Wetland drainage and fisheries collapse.** The [[Amik Lake]] (Antioch) and the [[Gab Swamp]] on the Orontes were both highly productive. Up to 600,000 eels were caught annually at Amik, and up to 700 tonnes of catfish were taken from the Gab in one period (Gruvel 1930). Both lakes were drained in the mid-20th century and converted to farmland. Freshwater fish yields in the Gab fell by about half after drainage.

3. **Creation of artificial fish habitats.** Dams such as [[Assad Lake]] on the Euphrates and newer reservoirs on the Orontes now hold fish stocks. Krupp notes that irrigation canals are mainly concrete-lined and hold small barbs, loaches and toothcarps in high densities but few species. Vegetated drainage ditches generally provide better habitat.

4. **Aquaculture history.** Intensive fish farming in Syria began only recently. Beckman was sent by the FAO in 1955 to assess the potential for a fishery. The first experimental hatchery opened in 1958 at Qal'at al-Mudiq on the edge of the Gab. In 1959 the first [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) were imported from Egypt and the tributaries of [[Lake Tiberias]]. Carp now make up more than half of the fish offered at some markets. Wild carp have also spread into natural waters of the Orontes.

5. **Introduction of non-native fishes.** Several species have been introduced:
   - **Tilapia.** Krupp notes that Wunder (1960) first judged the winter temperatures of northern Syria to be below the lethal limit for tilapia. This proved wrong. Four species, *T. nilotica*, *T. aurea*, *T. galilaea* and *T. zillii*, are now established far north of their natural range. *T. mossambica* has caused the decline or local extinction of native fishes in parts of southern Asia.
   - **Mosquitofish.** [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), a live-bearing poeciliid from the southern United States, was introduced in 1930 near Lake Tiberias to control mosquito larvae. It had reached Spain in 1921, then Italy, Corsica and Cyprus. By 1962 it was known only from southern Syria, but it now occurs across the country, especially in the Orontes basin.
   - **Catfish and spiny eel.** The [[European Catfish]] (*Silurus glanis*) and [[Spiny Eel]] (*Mastacembelus simach*) were not reported from the Orontes in earlier surveys, yet they are now abundant enough to be fished commercially. The presence of spiny eel in the middle Orontes suggests that it reproduces there. Krupp notes that it was once considered a "snake" and not eaten, but it was introduced after gaining economic value.

6. **Vertical transfers between river systems.** Krupp argues that fish were moved between river systems by irrigation canals. He also warns that interpreting historical distributions from old zoological records is often unreliable. Early records are often incomplete and seldom give precise identifications.

7. **Conservation concern.** Krupp argues that Syria's valuable native food fishes are rarely considered in fisheries planning, while foreign species are imported. Introductions risk altering the fauna and disturbing ecological balance.

**Other articles (summary only).** Larink's article discusses the development of yolk-rich eggs, using a functional and biophysical perspective, and questions the continuing influence of Haeckel's biogenetic law. Wuketits's article reviews the history of this theory, including critiques by [[Ernst Haeckel]] (as cited in the text). Wietfeld's piece concerns trampling damage by Shelducks and Eiders, and the crustacean museum article covers a Tokyo collection. The text of these articles was not available in full.
