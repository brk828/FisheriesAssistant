# Morphological Correlates of Ecological Specialization in Darters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This reprint bundle contains two items. The main paper by [[Lawrence M. Page]] and [[David L. Swofford]] (1984) examines how [[Darters]] (genera [[Percina]], [[Ammocrypta]] and [[Etheostoma]]) have specialized ecologically, and which morphological traits correlate with feeding, habitat occupation and reproduction. Using [[Canonical Correlation Analysis]] on 78 species, the authors show that habitat is a prime influence on darter morphology. The second item is a short technical note by Page and Kevin S. Cummings describing a portable [[Camera Box]] for photographing preserved small fishes.

## Background & Description

Darters are small benthic percids. Their three genera contain about 150 of the roughly 750 freshwater fish species of North America north of Mexico, or about 20%. Most species are less than 80 mm standard length (SL), and the largest reach about 150-170 mm SL. Darters are compared with [[Minnows]], another highly speciose North American group (about 220 species), which are mainly small midwater invertivores.

The authors frame darter specialization around three areas: feeding, habitat (space occupation) and reproduction. Survival depends mainly on occupying space, avoiding predators and disease, and feeding. Specialization in one area can constrain another.

Key morphological trends described include:

- **Body size reduction:** Darters have evolved progressively smaller and more benthic. Mean maximum SL is 91.8 mm in *Percina* (n = 30), 71.7 mm in *Ammocrypta* (n = 7) and 62.2 mm in *Etheostoma* (n = 93). Sexual size dimorphism is probably universal, with males larger than females in all but two subgenera.
- **Benthic habit:** Most darters live among their food organisms. Many have lost the [[Swimbladder]]. Based on Miller's categories, most darters are epibenthic, and some are [[Cryptobenthic]].
- **Mouth diversity:** Mouth size and position vary greatly, while diets overlap heavily (especially on fly larvae). The authors suggest that diet separation among coexisting species is based on where darters feed rather than what they eat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and size.** Size is constrained by habitat and water stratum. Gravel-run species are the largest (mean maximum SL 93.9 mm, n = 26), while pool species are the smallest (56.9 mm, n = 44). Riffle species average 70.6 mm (n = 53) and sand-run species 61.0 mm (n = 7). Midwater species (mean 95.2 mm, n = 29) are larger than benthic ones (62.8 mm, n = 101). The authors argue that midwater darters are more visible to predators and therefore stay larger, while small benthic darters conceal themselves in the substrate.

**Feeding.** Juveniles eat mainly microcrustaceans, and adults eat immature aquatic insects. Large species make a second shift to larger insects and crustaceans. Very small species, such as *E. fonticola* and *E. microperca*, keep eating mainly microcrustaceans as adults. Growth efficiency and foraging theory are invoked to explain the trend toward small size.

**Cryptic behavior.** Sand-run species (*Ammocrypta* and *E. vitreum*) use transparency and burial, with eyes and snout protruding. Their eyes sit more dorsally. Riffle species use disruptive vertical bars, and quiet-pool species use X- or W-marks that match sandy or debris-laden substrates.

**Lateral line.** Interrupted head canals occur only in small species. The authors suggest these interruptions may be incomplete development, and that exposed neuromasts may improve sensing in slow or standing water.

## Key Findings

- **Canonical correlations between habitat and morphology are strong.** The first three canonical R values for habitat and meristic variables were 0.929, 0.736 and 0.726. Species were analyzed as units, with 5 habitat and 66 morphological variables scored for adults.
- **Meristic counts track body size.** Mean lateral scale count correlated with maximum SL at r = 0.761 (the strongest reported). Transverse scale rows (r = 0.737), scales below (r = 0.714) and above (r = 0.702) the lateral line, and pored caudal fin scales (r = 0.625) also correlated. Lowest correlations were for anal spines (r = 0.123, P = 0.28) and branchiostegal rays (r = 0.163, P = 0.16).
- **Midwater species in medium to large streams have high meristic counts** (first canonical pair), and riffle darters in shallow rocky habitats have more dorsal rays and anal spines (second pair, considered to have little ecological significance).
- **Sluggish-water darters have a lower percentage of pored lateral line scales** and fewer pectoral rays. Fast-water darters have more pectoral rays, which the authors link to support against current.
- **Habitat and miscellaneous traits:** Midwater darters over rocky substrates tend to be large and to have a basicaudal spot and a midlateral stripe. Benthic darters over sand or mud are small and lack these traits. Males are larger than females in species that defend territories. Species of *Boleichthys* and *Ozarka* are considered non-territorial, and females are larger in them.
- **Habitat and mensural traits:** Midwater darters in large streams are large overall. Benthic darters in fast, shallow, rocky habitats have deep caudal peduncles, deep bodies, long pectoral fins and narrow interorbital distances. Narrow interorbital width is linked to benthic, dorsally oriented eyes.
- **Habitat guilds.** The authors characterize five habitat-based groups. Gravel-run darters are large, fusiform midwater fish. Riffle darters are benthic with reduced fusiformity, deep caudal peduncles and vertical bars. Sand-run darters are translucent, benthic, and lack swimbladders. Quiet-pool darters are small, dully pigmented and have blunt snouts. Flowing-pool species may be midwater or benthic.
- **Swimbladder loss.** Only a few species were examined. A large swimbladder (about 26% SL) occurred in midwater *P. cymatotaenia*, and moderate swimbladders (15-16% SL) in *P. sciera*, *P. phoxocephala* and *P. maculata*. Most benthic species examined lacked swimbladders.
- **Reproductive strategies.** Spawning behavior is known for 61 species (7 *Percina*, 54 *Etheostoma*), with four types:
  - **Egg-burying:** Found in all *Percina* studied and some *Etheostoma*. Eggs are buried and not guarded.
  - **Egg-attaching:** Known only in *Etheostoma*, with 1-3 adhesive eggs per mating placed on plants or rocks. This behavior is considered derived from egg-burying.
  - **Egg-clumping:** Found in some *Nothonotus* species, with eggs deposited under stones and guarded by males.
  - **Egg-clustering:** Eggs form a single-layer cluster on the underside of a stone in *Boleosoma* and *Catonotus*. This behavior also occurs in the minnow *Pimephales*.
- **Sexually selected traits track reproduction.** Modified scales occur only in *Percina* (all likely egg-buriers). Breeding tubercles are found in egg-buriers and egg-attachers. Swollen head flesh is extreme in egg-clustering subgenera and may have a secretory function. Knobs on dorsal fin elements may mimic eggs and stimulate the female to deposit eggs in an existing nest.
- **Guild classification needs correction.** The authors dispute several classifications by [[Balon et al. 1977]]. For example, *P. caprodes* spawns in gravel and buries its eggs, and *E. blennioides* is an open substrate spawner rather than a nest spawner.
- **Conservation note:** *E. sellare* is endangered. Its spawning behavior is unknown, but if it clumps or clusters eggs, management should ensure suitable sites.
- **Camera box technique (Page & Cummings):** Preserved fish in about 50% formalin are fixed on pins in a clear plexiglass shelf and photographed in a 10 mm plywood box with white or gray backgrounds, a 45° white plexiglass diffuser, a strobe and a 35 mm camera. Colors are largely retained for several hours after death, but iridescence is lost quickly. The strobe used is no longer manufactured.
