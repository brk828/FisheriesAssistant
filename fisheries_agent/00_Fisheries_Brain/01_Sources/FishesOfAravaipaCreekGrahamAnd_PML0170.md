# Fishes of Aravaipa Creek, Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This survey documents the fish fauna of [[Aravaipa Creek]], a spring-fed, relatively unmodified tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] in southeastern Arizona. The authors (Barber and Minckley) carried out an intensive survey between 6 November 1964 and 1 May 1965, supplemented by earlier collections by Simon (1943) and Miller (1960). The creek supports seven of the 12 known native fish species of the San Pedro drainage, and the paper treats it as a refugium for these fishes at a time when most southwestern aquatic habitats had been altered or lost. Two introduced species, [[Black Bullhead]] and [[Green Sunfish]], were also collected, and the authors judged that neither was likely to establish a large population. The study also describes the stream's hydrology, temperature, water chemistry and vegetation, and the historical loss of marshes and downstream mesquite expansion.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Aravaipa Creek rises at about 1,310 m above sea level in a structural trough south of the Willcox playa and flows northwest to enter the now-dry San Pedro River at 660 m, about 16.8 km south-southeast of Winkelman, Arizona. Only about 40 creek-km of its channel hold permanent surface water. The creek cuts through a steep-walled canyon (walls often exceeding 250 m) in Tertiary andesites and Precambrian rocks of the Galiuro Range, with a broad valley and floodplain (up to 2 km wide) above it.

**Climate and vegetation.** Temperatures range from below -5 °C to above 38 °C. Rainfall at Klondyke in 1915–16 indicated about 50 cm per year, probably an overestimate of the true average. The headwaters lie in grassland with sparse juniper, while the lower course passes through upper Sonoran vegetation of cacti, shrubs and grasses, with mesquite groves along the terraces. Riparian vegetation includes cottonwood, sycamore, willow, ash and box elder, with walnut, oak and acacia further from the water.

**Hydrology and channel.** Gradient averages 5.7 m/km above the canyon and 8.2 m/km within it. Currents exceed 1 m/s in riffles and are below 5 cm/s in pools. Discharge is mostly spring-fed. Annual mean discharge at the mouth-of-canyon gauge ranged from 0.50 m³/s (1938) to 1.46 m³/s (1935), with a seven-year average of 0.79 m³/s (1932–38). Discharge measured on 6 November 1964 ranged from 0.34 to 0.68 m³/s.

**Collecting methods.** Most collections were made with seines ranging from 1.2 m common seines (0.39 cm mesh) to a 19 m bag seine (0.64 cm mesh), and gill nets 16 to 40 m long. Collections were made at 16 numbered stations, numbered consecutively from downstream to upstream, with Station 4 being an artificial pond beside the creek fed by an irrigation diversion.

**Specimen repositories.** Collections are housed at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ) and the Arizona State University Department of Zoology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human impacts.** Water demand, land-use change and introduced species have modified the native aquatic fauna across the American Southwest. Aravaipa Creek was one of the few habitats still largely intact. Historical evidence (malaria records at Camp Grant, water-rights records, trapper accounts) indicates that extensive marshes once existed along the lower creek and the San Pedro River. Sacaton grass stands were replaced by [[Mesquite Expansion]] in the early 1900s. Pumping for irrigation began in the upper valley at least 40 years before the survey, though no decline in average flow had been observed at one ranch.

**Temperature and water quality.** Water temperature ranged from 8.9 °C (December) to 20.6 °C (6 November 1964). Summer temperatures can exceed this and have caused fish mortality. Percolation through coarse stream-bed sediments often keeps water near or below 27 °C. Cliffs and riparian shading moderate summer temperatures. Water is slightly turbid, light brown from fine suspended silt, and hard, with marl nodules on riffles. Ground water of the lower San Pedro valley usually contains less than 600 mg/L of dissolved solids, but up to 9,000 mg/L has been recorded. Some Aravaipa basin waters exceed 1.5 mg/L fluoride. No organic pollution was observed.

**Habitat structure.** Substrates are almost entirely unconsolidated stony material, except where cliffs meet the water. Aquatic vegetation is sparse, limited to watercress and filamentous algae. Pools are mostly behind man-made [[Irrigation Diversions]], up to 150 m long and 15 m wide, with the deepest water under 1.5 m in the main channel.

**Habitat partitioning.** The authors argue that the scarcity of congeneric species in the Southwest helps explain the strong habitat separation among the minnows. [[Roundtail Chub]] is a deep-pool fish. [[Longfin Dace]] occupies flat, sandy bottoms in shallow pools. [[Spikedace]] lives in eddies and swift water below riffles. [[Loach Minnow]] is restricted to riffles. [[Speckled Dace]] overlaps with the loach minnow and spikedace, but swims actively above the substrate on riffles.

## Key Findings

**Native species (seven persisting, plus one hybrid):**

- **[[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*):** Taken at Stations 4–7, 12 and 14. Its lateral-line scale counts (mean 78.6) placed Aravaipa fish between *G. r. robusta* and *G. r. intermedia*, so the authors referred the population to intergrades. Largest populations were in pools behind irrigation diversions. Thirty-nine adults were gill-netted at Station 4. Simon had collected 61 specimens near Station 2 in 1943. In Aravaipa Canyon, only six were caught at a single station, usually in pools and beneath ledges.
- **[[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus osculus*):** Rare. One immature specimen was taken in the survey. Earlier collections included one specimen by Simon (1943) and 35 by Miller (1960).
- **[[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*):** Dominant in the lower creek, making up more than 80% of the fauna at Stations 5–6 and some other sites. Its abundance dropped in the canyon, which the authors attributed both to a real decline and to increases in other species. Up to 150 individuals were taken in a single seine haul.
- **[[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*):** Scarce and declining across its range. One adult was taken at Station 6, and seven young at Station 16 in May. Simon's six adults and Miller's 106 specimens (1960) point to a marked decline since the 1950s. The total catch during the survey was 53 individuals, including fish kept alive for other studies. Specimens occurred only in gravel and small-rubble areas less than 20 cm deep.
- **[[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*):** Found at Station 6 (April, not November) and at Stations 8 and 10–14. It was substantial in the upper canyon. Most specimens were on uniformly turbulent riffles in water under 30 cm deep. One group was taken in water flowing over 70 cm/s.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*):** Frequent, especially in pools behind irrigation diversions and in the Station 4 pond. Small individuals were restricted to turbulent water.
- **[[Gila Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*):** Most abundant in Aravaipa Canyon and present at all stations upstream of 1 and 2. Large individuals favored pools, while small adults and young were mainly riffle fish.
- **Hybrid (*C. insignis* × *P. clarki*):** Three specimens from Stations 6, 11 and 16. Similar hybrids are known from other Gila basin streams.

**Introduced species:**

- **[[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*):** Seven specimens from the Station 4 pond. The authors judged that the swift current and lack of muddy pools make establishment in the creek unlikely.
- **[[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*):** Three gill-netted at Station 4. Its preference for slow, deep, protected pools should limit its spread into the main channel.

**Species of hypothetical occurrence:**

- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) was said to occur in the Station 4 pond but was not collected.
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) (94 specimens from the San Pedro River, Simon 1943) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) probably occurred in lower Aravaipa when permanent marshes existed.

**Conclusions and management:**

- Aravaipa Creek is a refugium for seven native fishes of the San Pedro drainage. Its relatively intact fauna contrasts with the disrupted patterns found in many other Arizona streams, especially those invaded by the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*).
- The authors recommended that the creek be maintained as a reserve, with its native fauna protected from further introductions and habitat change. They noted that the creek's fish fauna is changing and that some native species are declining.
