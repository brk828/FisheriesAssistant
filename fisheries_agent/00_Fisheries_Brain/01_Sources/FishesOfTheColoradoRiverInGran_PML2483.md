# Fishes of the Colorado River in Grand Canyon National Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This survey documents the fish fauna of the [[Colorado River]] between [[Lee's Ferry]] and the upper reaches of [[Lake Mead]] (Pierce Landing, River Mile 280) in [[Grand Canyon]]. Fieldwork comprised 13 float trips and 112 collections from 35 sites between September 1970 and June 1975 (with some 1976 samples also reported). Nineteen species were recorded: four native and fifteen introduced (about 79%). Two hybrid combinations were also taken.

The river has been altered by the closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, which replaced natural floods with hydropower-driven fluctuations and cold, clear releases. The authors conclude that the [[Humpback Chub]] has been severely reduced and that the [[Colorado Squawfish]] has been extirpated from the area. The [[Humpback Sucker]] was not collected at all. Three native species (the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]]) appear to maintain adequate populations, largely by spawning in tributaries.

## Background & Description

The authors frame the work within a documented decline of native fishes across the American Southwest. Isolated spring populations and small tributary residents have been most vulnerable to habitat loss and competition from exotics. Large-river species are harder to assess. Nearly 300 river miles of the Grand Canyon had been largely inaccessible to ichthyological work until the growth of commercial float trips on large pontoon rafts. This rising visitor pressure, along with concern for vanishing wildlife, motivated the study.

The Colorado River through Grand Canyon is naturally poor in species. The authors estimate that perhaps eight, and at most ten, species once lived there. Most of these forms are unique and occur nowhere else naturally outside the Colorado basin.

**Native species recorded:**
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*)

**Notable absences:** [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) were not collected. The [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) was not confirmed as an adult, and the young specimens could not be positively identified.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and temperature.** Since the closure of Glen Canyon Dam, the river's seasonal floods have been replaced by water-level variations tied to electricity generation. Water is discharged from the lower portion of the dam, which alters the natural temperature regime. During the study, the river was consistently 10°C or colder in the upper section and warmed gradually to a high of 16°C about 250 miles downstream. Tributary temperatures followed seasonal patterns. Still waters behind the dam trap silt, so the discharged water is extremely clear. Before impoundment the river was normally turbid.

**Tributaries.** Tributaries such as the [[Paria River]], [[Little Colorado River]], [[Kanab Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]], [[Shinumo Creek]] and [[Crystal Creek]] serve as spawning and nursery habitat. Many small streams were also sampled, and some supported speckled dace populations without any suckers present. The Paria River mouth was a key spawning area and is invariably turbid, which prevented direct observation on many visits.

**Exotic species.** The fifteen introduced species were widely distributed and in many cases were recorded only as adults, suggesting that upper river conditions are not favorable for spawning even for tolerant species such as [[Carp]].

## Key Findings

**Native fish status**
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]:** Widespread from Lee's Ferry to Mead Reservoir. Adults, young or both were taken from 14 tributaries and nine main-channel sites. Spawning runs from March to late May. Ripe males were observed at the Paria River in April 1975 and 1976. Spawning appeared to occur mainly in tributary lower reaches, although the authors suspect some main-channel spawning occurs. Post-spawn fish drop downstream and move into the main Colorado when the Paria cools.
- **[[Bluehead Sucker]]:** Taken at all tributary sites except a few. Spawning was observed in Crystal, Kanab, Havasu and Shinumo creeks, and at the Paria and Deer Creek mouths. Spawning in Kanab Creek (May 1975) occurred at 16°C. At Shinumo Creek in June 1975 and June 1976, hundreds of individuals were observed in a few meters below the falls. The authors found no spawning after mid-June, though sporadic summer spawning may occur at the Paria mixing zone.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]:** Found in the main river from River Mile 0 to 245 and in most tributaries, including very small ones (for example Hermit, Boucher, Blacktail Canyon, Stone and Travertine creeks) where no suckers were present. Color patterns ranged from banded to unicolored. Reproduction occurs from late spring through early fall. Tuberculate males were taken from late April to mid-November. Size data show early spawning in Boucher Creek (six young, 7.3–12.7 mm standard length, mean 10.3 mm) and late spawning at the Paria mouth (34 young, 12.4–20.3 mm, mean 15.5 mm).
- **[[Humpback Chub]]:** Distribution in Grand Canyon appears greatly reduced, now near the [[Little Colorado River]] mouth and extending a few miles below it. Records include one adult at Shinumo Creek (August 1, 1975, photographed and released), four adults at the Little Colorado River (June 2, 1976), and young or small juveniles at River Miles 44, 61.5, 65.8, 69, 71 and 108.7. Young ranged from roughly 24.5 to 35.5 mm standard length. No ripe individuals or spawning were observed. The authors believe spawning occurs at various times during summer, but the pattern is disrupted by the altered environment.
- **[[Humpback Sucker]]:** No specimens were collected. Hybrids with flannelmouth sucker were found. The authors suggest the species has been displaced from the Grand Canyon section because water temperatures are too cold for spawning, it does not use small tributaries, and the dam blocks access to suitable spawning areas. Hybridization may have occurred after Powell Reservoir acted as a barrier while temperatures remained suitable for spawning.

**Extirpation and population concerns**
- The authors are convinced that the [[Colorado Squawfish]] was extirpated from the Grand Canyon area by impoundment and the conditions that followed.
- They believe the cold bottom-water releases from Glen Canyon Dam have had a devastating effect on the humpback chub population.
- They consider the Little Colorado River, in its present physical and chemical state, essential to humpback chub survival. They identify the Little Colorado River and the main Colorado River downstream to Shinumo Creek as critical habitat.

**Exotic species (fifteen recorded)**
- [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*): Presumably introduced as forage. Collected at Spencer Creek, Emory Falls and Scorpion Island.
- [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*): One specimen seined at River Mile 194.5.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and hybrid trout: Taken from many tributaries and main-river sites. No young or juveniles were found near the Paria mouth, so spawning there is likely unsuccessful.
- [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): Widely distributed, with schools seen from Lee's Ferry to Mead Reservoir. Young or juveniles were collected only at the Little Colorado River mouth and some lower sites.
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*): Collected at several sites but not above the Little Colorado mouth. Native to the lower Mississippi Valley.
- [[Rio Grande Killifish]] (*Fundulus zebrinus*): Abundant at Spencer Creek and spawning in ten sites. The authors describe a dramatic dispersal downstream from the Little Colorado River mouth to the head of Mead Reservoir.
- Other exotics included [[Golden Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and green sunfish. Most were sampled at only a few sites, but several reached tributary mouths or reservoir arms.

**Management recommendations**
- The authors recommend restricting angling and any harassment within critical habitat, especially around the Little Colorado River.
- They call for a continuous, long-range fish fauna study, arguing that many forces are rapidly exterminating native fishes.
