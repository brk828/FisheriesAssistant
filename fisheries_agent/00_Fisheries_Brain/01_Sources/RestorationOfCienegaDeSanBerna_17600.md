# Restoration of Cienega de San Bernardino, Sonora

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 2000 proposal submitted to the [[North American Wetlands Conservation Act]] (NAWCA) program to restore the [[Cienega de San Bernardino]], a binational desert wetland straddling the Mexico–USA border. The Mexican portion lies within [[Rancho San Bernardino]], a 4,050 ha (10,000 acre) property administered by the [[Fundacion de Refugio de San Bernardino]], a non-profit founded and privately funded by [[Josiah Austin]] and Valer Austin. The project aims to re-establish the ciénega and its watershed by repairing eroded channels, raising water tables, improving water-use efficiency, restoring native species, eradicating non-native species, and educating local residents.

The total estimated budget is $908,500, of which NAWCA would provide $304,000. The project is a partnership with the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (SBNWR), [[Instituto Nacional de Ecologia]] (INE), [[IMADES]], SEMARNAP, and [[UNAM Hermosillo]], and it has endorsements from U.S. and Mexican agencies and The Nature Conservancy.

## Background & Description

**Ecological importance.** The ciénega formerly was a large riparian wetland within Chihuahuan Desert grasslands, extending for more than 2.5 km (1.6 mi) into Sonora along Rio San Bernardino (Black Draw in the USA). It is now reduced to remnants tied to artesian wells, springs and artificial ponds, but remains an oasis for stopover, breeding and year-round habitat.

**Physiographic and geological importance.** Ciénegas form in stream channels where perennial water reaches the surface in stable flows. Once mature, they are controlled by permanently saturated soils and act as groundwater-storage reservoirs that stabilize base flow and regulate downstream hydrography. Their permanence is supported by pollen dates ~60 km (~40 mi) west of the CSB indicating more than 3,000 years of continuous habitat.

**Social and economic importance.** The region's economy is based on ranching. After recovery, the Mexican portion is planned for controlled cattle production, intended to show local ranchers how livestock operations can coexist with biodiversity management.

**Historical and cultural importance.** Evidence of Amerinds on the site dates from more than 10,000 years ago. Apaches, Spanish settlers, a 1822 land grant and Mormon Battalion travelers (1846) all used the area. Ranching and irrigated agriculture followed the wetland's drying, and property passed through [[Texas John Slaughter]]'s holdings (1884) before the USA parcel became part of SBNWR (1982).

**Recognition.** E. A. Mearns sampled the area in 1892. The region is considered an ecoregion of conservation priority, recognized by CONABIO and the World Wildlife Fund, and several protected areas have been proposed or established nearby.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Birds.** More than 250 bird species are recorded. Waterfowl numbers are low but diversity is high, with at least 24 species recorded. At least 18 raptors occur, and the site is part of a north–south corridor for Neotropical migrants.

**Mammals, herpetofauna and invertebrates.** At least 42 mammals are known, including jaguar records in nearby mountains. At least 10 amphibians and 27 reptiles are recorded, including Chiricahua leopard frog, ornate box turtle, Gila monster and Mexican garter snake. The area supports more than 240 butterfly species, the greatest butterfly richness recorded in North America according to the source, and more than 65 dragonfly and damselfly species, one known only from this ciénega. The [[San Bernardino Springsnail]] is endemic.

**Fishes.** Nine native fishes were formerly present, and five persist or have been reintroduced. These fishes represent 47% of the Rio Yaqui freshwater fish fauna in Mexico and 25% of the native fish fauna of Arizona. Eight of the nine are listed or of special concern by the Mexican government or USFWS, and viable populations of all nine persist in or adjacent to the ciénega in Mexico. The [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*), an endangered cyprinid, is the focus of a 12-year management partnership with SBNWR.

**Plants.** Nearly 500 vascular plant taxa are present, most native. At least 77 native grasses occur, along with the rare Huachuca water umbel (federally listed as threatened) and other wetland plants.

**Degradation.** The area was severely overgrazed for roughly 300 years. Overstocking in the 1800s triggered erosion and [[Arroyo Cutting]], which lowered water tables and drained wetland and riparian communities. Grassland decline, desertscrub expansion and wetland loss followed.

**Management history.** After the USA parcel was acquired and restored, livestock were removed from the Mexican portion in 1998 under the Austins' management. The entire property has since been newly fenced.

## Key Findings

**Objectives (first year):**
1. Restore existing wetlands, design and build new habitats, and develop easier management.
2. Prevent water loss through increased water-use efficiency.
3. Stabilize and expand native species populations.
4. Reduce negative impacts on native biota by eradicating and excluding non-native species.
5. Educate local and regional residents on managing natural and semi-natural wetlands.

**Restoration targets:**
- Enlarge existing Mexican wetlands from ~15 ha to more than 100 ha (~35 to more than 250 acres).
- Restore riparian (60 ha), terrace (250 ha) and upland (3,650 ha) areas.
- Repair channels with rock-filled wire-basket [[Gabion Erosion Control]] and cottonwood–willow plantings.
- Create potholes and shallow depressions to retain water for waterfowl.
- Remove mesquite and other woody invaders, and reseed with native grasses.
- Install a non-native fish exclusion barrier as part of the erosion-control system.

**Work plan and monitoring:**
- Data acquisition includes vegetation and core sampling with UNAM Hermosillo, GPS surveys with IMADES, aquifer pumping tests, and stream gages on Rio San Bernardino and Arroyo Grande.
- Monitoring covers groundwater, indicator species (such as pothole use by waterfowl and raptor nesting in cottonwoods), quarterly photography and cross sections, and post-flood damage assessment, following an [[Adaptive Management]] approach.

- The Nature Conservancy (Northwest Mexico Program) and IMADES expressed support.
- The USFWS Arizona Field Supervisor noted that the project will require the landowner to adopt long-term land management practices.
