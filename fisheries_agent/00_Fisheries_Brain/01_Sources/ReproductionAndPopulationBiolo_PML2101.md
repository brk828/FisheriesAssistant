# Bonytail and Razorback Sucker Status and Reproduction in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document set contains two items on endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in the [[Colorado River]] system. The first is a December 1986 fishery management letter reporting two razorback suckers caught with bonytail chubs in a golf course pond in Blythe, California, and planned seining for more bonytails. The second is a July 1981 research proposal by Paulson, Deacon and Baker, submitted to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], to study population status and spawning success of both species in [[Lake Mohave]]. The proposal reports no new field data; it sets out objectives, sampling designs and a schedule. Its central premise is that both species are very scarce and that recruitment appears to fail, probably because of predation.

## Background & Description

- **Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*)**: Historically widespread in the Colorado River system. A small population persists in Lake Mohave, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has classified it as endangered.
- **Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**: Still present in most river areas and "fairly common" in the [[Black Canyon]] area below [[Hoover Dam]] and in Lake Mohave. Some researchers (notably Minckley, 1980) argue that the Lake Mohave population consists only of adults and is headed for extinction.
- **Historical context**: Both species were abundant in [[Eldorado Canyon]] in the 1950s (Jonez and Sumner, 1954). Carp were suspected of eating bonytail eggs. Juvenile razorbacks were observed in Eldorado Canyon after 1954 spawning, and one juvenile was captured in Black Canyon in March 1979.
- **Prior sampling**: Work before 1981 concentrated in Carp Cove of Cottonwood Basin (Gustafson and Minckley, 1975) and Black Canyon (Bryant, 1977; Paulson et al., 1980).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reproductive limitation**: Bonytail abundance is extremely low, and young razorbacks are apparently absent. Both species have been seen spawning in shallow areas of upper Lake Mohave. Eggs from hand-stripped ripe fish produced viable offspring in hatcheries. Juvenile bonytails have not been observed in the lake.
- **Predation hypothesis**: Survival of young is considered nearly nonexistent because of predation by introduced [[Non-Native Predatory Fish]] (including sunfish-family centrarchids) and [[Carp]]. Jonez and Sumner (1954) noted that razorbacks occurred where centrarchids were also very abundant. Minckley (1980) considered predation the major factor limiting reproductive success of native species in Lake Mohave.
- **Spawning season**: Peak spawning is not precisely known. Based on earlier surveys, spawning appears to occur during winter (December–February).
- **Study areas**: Three reservoir areas were proposed for sampling: Eldorado Canyon, Little Basin (Arizona Bay) and Cottonwood Basin. Recent surveys showed both species in the latter two.

## Key Findings

**1986 Letter (Imperial National Wildlife Refuge)**
- On 2 December 1986, Scott Yess (Fishery Biologist) captured two (?) razorback suckers, apparently mixed with bonytail chubs, in a golf course pond in Blythe, California.
- The razorbacks were approximately 500 mm (OCR-uncertain) and healthy.
- No bonytails were collected in the two experimental [[Gill Net]]s. Yess planned to seine the pond for bonytails.
- The letter was copied to Jerry Burton and to the California Department of Fish and Game.

**1981 Research Proposal: Objectives**
1. Determine population abundance, age structure and growth of bonytails and razorbacks in Lake Mohave.
2. Determine whether both species spawn successfully in Lake Mohave.
3. Identify the primary cause(s) of mortality in offspring, if successful spawning occurs.

**Population Biology Approach**
- **Scuba surveys** (November 1981) to locate sites of greatest abundance in the three areas.
- **Trammel netting**: four to six nets (300 ft each) set at each site in December 1981, January 1982 and February 1982. Nets were checked every 3 hours over 48 hours. Fish were weighed, measured, individually tagged with disc-angler tags, assessed for reproductive condition and external condition (parasites, lesions), and released.
- **Analysis**: Population size was to be estimated with the [[Schnabel Index]]. Additional netting in November or December 1982 would measure growth of tagged fish over one year.
- **Hatchery control**: Brood stock collected by the Fish and Wildlife Service was to be tagged and monitored at the Dexter or Willow Beach hatcheries to measure tag loss and mortality from handling.

**Reproductive Success Approach**
- **Weekly scuba surveys** for two months in one spawning area, counting spawning fish, depth and redd numbers.
- **Remote underwater video** monitoring of spawning behavior from shore.
- **Egg and fry measures**: egg counts from redd contents, hatching times and fry emergence.
- **Predator exclusion**: enclosures (dimensions as scanned: 10 × 16 ft, OCR-uncertain) around three redds to compare fry survival with open areas and quantify predation.

**Assessment**
- The proposal reports no quantitative results. Its conclusions are conditional: successful spawning appears to occur, but young survival is likely near zero because of predation. The sampling design was intended to test that predation hypothesis directly.
