# Largemouth Bass Tagging

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report by J. B. Kimsey of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (Inland Fisheries Branch) evaluates six types of external tags for [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), tested in the field at [[Clear Lake]] and in aquaria and hatchery ponds. The study was motivated by the rapid shedding of conventional tags, which prevented long-range mortality and harvest-rate data. The author concludes that most tested designs were unsuitable: strap and jaw tags were shed too quickly as bass grew, Petersen disk tags were forced out, and the Einar Lea hydrostatic tag tended to saw out or foul. The two-wire [[Disk-Dangler Tag]] and the [[Plastic Spaghetti Tag]] performed best, with no shedding recorded in the aquarium. The study is described as continuing and "not entirely conclusive."

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** Early in the [[California Warmwater Fisheries Investigations]], a reliable tag for largemouth bass was needed because conventional tagging did not yield long-range mortality or harvest-rate data, mainly due to rapid tag shedding.

**Funding and acknowledgments.** A portion of the work was performed under [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] California F-13-R, "Black Bass Tagging," supported by Federal Aid to Fish Restoration funds. [[Dr. Earl S. Herald]] provided an 800-gallon observation tank at [[Steinhart Aquarium]] for a year and a half. Almo Cordone, Ed V. Dwyer, Leonard O. Fisk, and R. R. Bell assisted with tagging, recording, and observation.

**Tags evaluated.** Six tag types were tested, with a round jaw tag included as a variant:

- [[Strap Tag]]: No. 1 monel strap tags, 2 mm wide and 8.5 mm long, applied to the lower jaw (and a rounded upper-jaw variant).
- [[Jaw Tag]] (round, bird-band style): monel, 3.5 mm wide and 8 mm in diameter, placed over the premaxillary-maxillary overlap.
- [[Petersen Disk Tag]]: two cellulose nitrate buttons, each 0.5 inch in diameter and 0.045 inch thick, on soft tantalum wire 0.020 inch in diameter.
- [[Staple Tag]]: two-wire tag with a disk on one side, after [[Calhoun]] (1953).
- [[Disk-Dangler Tag]] (Atkins modification): two wires with a pendant, rigidly fixed disk.
- [[Plastic Spaghetti Tag]]: white No. 20 vinylite tubing with a figure-eight knot, no jacket or core.
- [[Einar Lea Hydrostatic Tag]]: a sealed message in a tubular plastic hydrostatic tag on a stainless steel bridle.

**Fish sizes.** Fork lengths ranged from about 6.2 to 20.25 inches across experiments, depending on the trial.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Growth and tag retention.** Tag retention was tightly linked to growth rate. Fast-growing yearling bass outgrew jaw and disk tags, while slow-growing fish retained them. Tags sized to allow for growth were often too cumbersome and interfered with feeding and swimming.
- **Tissue response.** Tags caused sores, pockets of necrotic tissue, and abrasion of fins (e.g., the soft dorsal edge in the spaghetti tag trial). Some tags caused interference with oral valves.
- **Tag loss and natural mortality.** The author reasons that the lack of tag returns after the first year in several studies was more likely due to shedding than to natural or tagging mortality.
- **Possible behavioral bias.** Jaw tags may alter harvest rates by irritating feeding. A tagged bass may find usual prey less available, which could make plugs and artificial lures more attractive to anglers.
- **Substrate and vegetation.** Shedding in hatchery ponds was attributed to constant rubbing against the pond bottom and sides. Tags also fouled on submerged brush, debris, weeds, and nets.
- **Comparative species.** Methods were compared with work on [[Striped Bass]], [[White Catfish]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Yellowtail]], and [[Tuna]], as well as [[Rainbow Trout]] in a tagging study that reported color-related attack by conspecifics.

## Key Findings

**Strap and jaw tags**

- **Clear Lake strap tags:** 108 tags were attached to fish 6.5 to 9.0 inches in fork length. Three (2.8 percent) were returned by anglers. The longest confirmed elapsed time between tagging and return was 41 days. Use was discontinued after the 48th day because every net-recaptured fish had a large, raw tag sore.
- **Aquarium jaw tag trial:** Sixteen fish (7.25 to 8.0 inches) were tagged on August 4, 1953 (five flattened lower, five rounded lower, six rounded upper). All tags caused serious sores within two weeks. By December 1, 1953, four lower and two upper jaw tags had been shed. By July 7, 1954, only two lower rounded tags remained.
- **Growth effect:** The 14 fish that shed tags grew a mean 28 percent in length. The two retaining tags grew 6 percent. One retained bass gained 17 percent in fork length over nearly two years, about one-third of the expected increase, supporting the view that jaw tags are retained best when growth is slow.
- **Hatchery round jaw tag:** Applied August 10, 1954 to 50 fish (6.2 to 9.0 inches). Twenty were shed within 30 days. This tag interfered least with feeding among the jaw tags.
- **Field returns from other studies:**
  - P. A. Douglas (unpublished), San Vicente Reservoir: 52.9 percent six-month return from 70 introduced bass (mean 2.6 lb, mean 15.5 inches fork length). Half were returned in four months and an additional 2.9 percent in the sixth month. A second group of 149 bass (mean 1.1 lb, estimated mean 12.5 inches) gave 51.7 percent in three months, with 6 percent more in the next eight months.
  - Chance (1955), Tennessee Valley reservoirs: 41.6 and 41.2 percent resident jaw tag returns, 95 and 94 percent complete in the first year (fish size not reported).
  - Manges (1950), Norris Reservoir, Tennessee: 18.4 percent overall return from four studies, with one known second-season recapture (fish size not reported).
  - Dequine and Hall (1950), six Florida lakes: 22.9 percent average return, 92.4 percent complete within 30 weeks, with three returns after one year (fish size not reported).

**Petersen disk tag**

- Tested in three positions: back between occiput and dorsal fin insertion, back between spinous and soft dorsal fins, and caudal peduncle.
- Growth in width versus fork length (Figure 3) showed that the dorsal positions would be forced out as bass grew.
- Of 12 dorsal tags (6 per location), all were on their way out at seven months. Of 10 caudal peduncle tags, 6 were completely shed and 4 were retained but being forced backward and upward.
- Tags loose enough to allow growth caused severe sores. Conclusion: Petersen disk tags do not allow for growth and are shed too quickly under normal growth. A smaller tag may be useful on the caudal peduncle.
- Red, white, and yellow tags were used; bass did not preferentially attack any color.

**Staple tag**

- Placed on 240 bass at Clear Lake in summer 1953 (8.5 to 19.0 inches, mean 10.1 inches fork length).
- Developed a large sore and rotated into a disk-dangler position, which caused early abandonment.
- Two-year return: 66 tags (22.2 percent). Of the returns, 39 (59.1 percent) came in year one and 25 (37.9 percent) in year two. Two more were returned in the first half of year three.

**Disk-dangler (Atkins) tag**

- Clear Lake: 82 tags on bass 9.0 to 20.25 inches. 26 returned (31.7 percent) over two years: 14 (56 percent of returns) in year one and 11 (44 percent) in year two.
- Aquarium (July 1954 to February 1955, 10 tags on yearling bass): none shed, and fish were in excellent external condition.
- Growth allowance: bass gain about 0.1 inch in width per 2 inches of fork length. A 9.0-inch fish is about 0.6 inch wide at attachment, so the tag may last about two years before the wire presses on the neural spines and pterygiophores.
- Two fish died en route to the hatchery, and a third casualty was dissected. Each had a necrotic pocket at the forward wire, while the hind wire had not formed one. Later examples showed the pocket disappearing as the wire frame stabilized into a triangle.
- Force diagrams: with a 10-degree disk movement, the forward wire of a rectangular frame moved about three times as far as in a triangular frame. Recommendation: apply the wire frame in a triangular shape and eliminate the long disk stalk.

**Plastic "spaghetti" tag**

- 10 tags placed dorsally in the aquarium; none shed and no external sores, though initial swelling and redness occurred.
- Dissection of three fish at seven months showed no irritation; the tag hole was lined with membrane-like scar tissue.
- Possible hang-up on brush was observed in brown bullheads (one tag torn out) but not adequately tested in bass.
- Knots can come untied, a demonstrated risk in fast-swimming marine fish; the author suggests metal clips and is testing spaghetti tags secured with No. 1 strap tags.

**Einar Lea hydrostatic tag**

- Four tags were used in an 11-month aquarium trial. Two dorsal tags were retained, but wounds never healed and the bridles allowed considerable movement. After transfer to hatchery ponds, both bridles had shifted and were cutting upward rapidly.
- The two caudal peduncle tags were shed after six months, attributed to the backward pull of the cartridge during limited swimming.
- The thin stainless steel bridle can saw out or foul on debris, weeds, and nets. The sealed-message principle was judged sound, and an improved bridle was under consideration.

**Summary conclusions**

- Strap tags do not yield adequate mortality data because of rapid shedding; a tag large enough for growth is too cumbersome in yearling fish.
- Twenty of 50 round jaw tags were shed within 30 days.
- Petersen disk tags are shed too quickly under normal growth.
- Two-wire staple tags stabilize only after turning over, becoming disk-danglers with exposed wires that suffer excessive abrasion.
- Two-wire disk-danglers are long-lasting, and none had been shed; a triangular wire frame is recommended.
- Plastic spaghetti tags were not shed and caused no sores, but hang-up on brush needs testing and fastening may need improvement.
- Einar Lea hydrostatic tags are unsatisfactory for largemouth bass unless the bridle is improved.
