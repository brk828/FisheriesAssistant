# Aggressive Behavior Among Females of the Siamese Fighting Fish, *Betta splendens*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osphronemidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 laboratory study by [[James C. Braddock]] and [[Zora I. Braddock]] (Department of Zoology, Michigan State College) describes in detail the aggressive behavior of adult female [[Siamese Fighting Fish]] (*Betta splendens*) when two females are placed together in a small aquarium. Earlier literature focused mainly on males or reproduction, and only briefly asserted that females form hierarchies. Across 51 pairings of 45 individuals, 35 pairs fought. The authors describe a pre-fight phase of challenging and other activities, a fight phase dominated by mutual biting, jaw locking and jockeying for position, and a post-surrender phase in which the winner stays aggressive and the loser withdraws. Fights were highly variable in duration (2 to 134.5 minutes) and produced substantial fin damage, and the study found no reliable behavioral predictor of outcome other than relative size.

## Background & Description

- **Species of interest:** *Betta splendens*, the Siamese fighting fish, long known for aggressive behavior. It is easily bred, readily available, and relatively hardy, which makes it attractive for laboratory study.
- **Prior literature:** Much existing material was popular or semipopular and often inaccurate. Studies such as [[Noble 1939]] and [[Smith 1937]] addressed male fighting or reproduction. [[Noble 1939]] stated that females form hierarchies but gave no details.
- **Stated purpose:** A survey recording, in detail, the activities that occur when two females are placed together, to serve as a foundation for later, more detailed work.
- **Study fish:** Adult females obtained from a commercial distributor in Detroit. Ages were unknown but estimated at no more than nine months. Six individuals measured averaged 3.2 cm from the anterior end to the base of the tail fin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Laboratory conditions:** Observations were made between March 6 and June 18, 1953, under lights set from 8:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. Room air temperature was maintained at 27 ± 1°C by a recording thermometer, with a heater keeping it above 25°C.
- **Water:** Aged and aerated building tap water (two weeks in 20-gallon crocks), originating from college-maintained deep wells.
- **Housing:** Fish were kept singly in 1-gallon jars containing gravel, one snail and a stalk of *Myriophyllum*. Observation aquaria measured 30.5 × 26.5 × 26.5 cm, with gravel and *Valisneria*.
- **Diet:** Daily grated frozen shrimp, with occasional laboratory-reared *Drosophila*.
- **Isolation and recovery:** Fish were isolated physically and visually for at least 7 days before each pairing. After fights they were held in weak [[Methylene Blue]] solution for 3 to 7 days to prevent fungal infection, and no pair was re-tested before wounds had healed.
- **Relative size coding:** Pairs were scored as similar, or greater by 1, 2 or 3. The largest difference measured was 9 mm.

## Key Findings

**Sample and design**
- 45 individuals were used in 51 pairings. Ten were paired once, 24 twice, five three times, three four times, one five times and two six times. No pair was repeated.
- 35 pairings produced fights. The remaining 16 pairings showed no fighting and were reserved for separate analysis.

**Period prior to fighting**
- Duration ranged from 2 to 20 minutes, with a mean of 6.6 minutes. In 28 of 35 cases it was 8 minutes or less.
- Challenging was universal: every individual challenged, and all challenge forms totaled 605. Individual challenges numbered 207 and mutual challenges 199. Biting was concentrated just before fighting began.
- Other activities included chasing (19), retreating (38), biting (62), and exploring (28). Surfacing for air occurred throughout, and social activity ceased during it.
- No correlation was found between the duration of this phase and the frequency of any activity, or with size difference.

**Fight phase**
- Fights lasted 2 to 134.5 minutes, with a mean of 51.5 minutes. About two-thirds lasted 60 minutes or less, with no clear modal value.
- Fights began after a definite aggressive display by the attacked fish. The authors infer that mutual aggressiveness is required, since a female did not attack a non-aggressor.
- In the 21 fully recorded fights, individual challenges totaled 1,062, mutual challenges 360 and jockeying for position 1,235. Single bites totaled 1,460, with 657 exchanges of single bites, 166 exchanges of multiple bites and 289 mutual bites at the mouth.
- Jaw locking occurred in 29 of 35 fights, totaling 406 instances across brief and long locks. Long locks were more common in longer fights.
- Individual challenges correlated with fight duration, while mutual challenges did not. Jockeying increased after the first 30 to 45 minutes, likely replacing mutual challenges as fatigue set in.

**Color and pattern**
- Before fighting, body and fin color became more saturated and stripes tended to disappear. By the start of fighting, 37 of 70 pair-mates were in full color.
- Dorsoventral stripes were most characteristic of breeding females. Striping and color appeared to be controlled by separate mechanisms.
- Winners largely stayed in full color without stripes. Losers faded and frequently acquired stripes after surrender.

**Outcome and post-surrender behavior**
- Every fight ended with a definite retreat by the loser, following an aggressive display by one or both fish.
- In the five minutes after surrender, all 53 individual challenges came from winners. Winners chased losers 40 times and bit them 7 times. Losers never chased winners and bit once.
- Winners were more likely to keep fins erect and darker, while losers' fins drooped.

**Injuries**
- Caudal and anal fins were most often damaged. Injuries were recorded in 65 of 70 pair-mates, and three individuals probably died.
- Winners were more damaged than losers in 8 experiments, suggesting injury severity did not decide outcomes.

**Predictors of outcome**
- No pre-fight activity predicted the winner. Only in fights longer than 60 minutes did winners give individual challenges and bites about twice as often as losers.
- Relative size predicted the winner in 27 of 35 fights. The smaller fish won 3 times, and 5 pairs were equal in size.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- The authors argue that the pre-fight phase builds a threshold of aggressiveness, and that crowding prevents this build-up. This may explain why groups of 20 or more females can coexist in a 12-gallon container without fighting, forming hierarchies without contact fights.
- Female fighting may relate to territoriality or to defending against females that would eat eggs, though the authors call this speculative and call for further work.
- Selective breeding for fighting ability has occurred in Siam, but US stock has been selected for color only. The authors propose breeding belligerent and non-belligerent strains as a future study.
- Overall, the authors describe the behavior as complex and variable compared with other fishes, and hence a promising subject for further research.
