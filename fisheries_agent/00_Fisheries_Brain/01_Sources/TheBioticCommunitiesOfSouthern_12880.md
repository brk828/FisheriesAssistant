# Biotic Communities of Southern Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report (Nevada State Museum Anthropological Papers No. 13, Part 4, by [[W. Glen Bradley]] and [[James E. Deacon]], October 1967) assigns the native and introduced vascular plants and vertebrate animals of southern Nevada to biotic communities and describes their present distribution and relative abundance. Its stated purposes are to summarize community ecology information, provide a framework for further studies, and provide a basis for comparison with paleoecological work. The study area is the Nevada portion of the Mohave Desert, extended to include the entire Nevada portion of the [[Colorado River Drainage]] for aquatic habitats. Fish are a major focus: the region holds 37 species of fish, with a high proportion of isolated endemics, and the aquatic and hydric communities are treated in detail.

## Background & Description

**Authors and affiliation:** W. Glen Bradley and James E. Deacon, Biology Department, Nevada Southern University, and Desert Research Institute, Las Vegas, Nevada.

**Study area:** The area covers Clark County and small parts of southern Lincoln and southeastern Nye counties. It is part of the [[Basin and Range Province]], with elevations from about 500 feet near the Colorado River to 11,972 feet on Charleston Peak. Few permanent streams occur; the [[Virgin River]] and [[Moapa River]] drain into [[Lake Mead]], and [[Meadow Valley Wash]] is intermittent.

**Methods:** Field observations and collections by the University of Nevada at Las Vegas Biology Department (1959 to the time of writing) were combined with literature review. Fish were collected with seines, traps and a shocker. Vegetation was sampled by random collecting, species lists, line transects, quadrats and step-point methods. Mammal collecting used the technique of Calhoun (1949). Few quantitative abundance records exist for plants, amphibians, reptiles and birds.

**Biogeographical context:** Southern Nevada lies where the [[Sonoran Desert]], [[Mohave Desert]] and [[Great Basin Desert]] meet. The flora of [[Clark County]] comprises 887 vascular plant species, of which 704 have ecological distribution data in Appendix I.

**Classification:** The authors recognize zonal communities (creosote bush, blackbrush, juniper-pinyon, fir-pine, bristlecone pine, pseudo-alpine), transzonal communities (desert riparian, saltbush, riparian and cliff) and hydric/aquatic communities (desert spring and marsh, stream riparian, stream, lake).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish communities**

- The Nevada portion of the Colorado River drainage contains 16 native species; 14 are endemic to the Colorado River system and six are restricted to southern Nevada.
- *Moapa coriacea* occurs only at its type locality.
- *Salmo clarki* and *Ptychocheilus lucius* no longer maintain native populations in this part of their former range; *Lepidomeda altivelis* is extinct here.
- The [[Death Valley Drainage]] in Nevada (Pahrump Valley and Ash Meadows) has five native species, four of them endemic. *Empetrichthys merriami* is probably extinct.
- Physical factors (available water, humidity, temperature) largely limit the distribution of fishes, amphibians and reptiles.

**Desert springs and marshes**

- Most springs are thermal, with water temperature varying only a few degrees annually.
- Aquatic vegetation includes Chara, Nasturtium, Naias, Potamogeton, Ruppia and algae.
- Fish populations decline as waters cool and flow into marsh habitat; cyprinodontids are almost absent in cooler flowing waters, while cyprinids reach greatest abundance in flowing water.
- Snails and aquatic insects are very abundant.
- Introduced [[Bullfrog]] populations occur in most of these habitats.

**Stream communities**

- The Moapa River heads in warm springs (headwater temperature relatively constant at 85° F, maximum 90° F) and drops to a low of 57° F in the lower river in March 1964.
- Virgin River and Meadow Valley Wash headwaters are cooler (Meadow Valley Wash 57° F in June 1962).
- Lower Colorado River backwaters, eddies and sloughs are the primary habitat for several introduced sport fishes.

**Lake community**

- [[Lake Mead]] holds about 32 million acre-feet, covers 163,000 surface acres, is about 115 miles long and reaches 500 feet maximum depth.
- [[Lake Mojave]] holds 1,820,000 acre-feet, covers 30,000 surface acres, is 65 miles long and about 100 feet maximum depth.
- Wide water-level fluctuation and high flow prevent significant rooted aquatic vegetation; plankton is the primary food source at the base of the food chain.
- The [[Threadfin Shad]] is the most abundant fish in both lakes.

**Terrestrial and montane context**

- Desert shrub communities hold the bulk of species (for example, the creosote bush community has 429 total species recorded in Table 4).
- Montane communities are impoverished in reptiles and mammals at higher elevations; the bristlecone pine and pseudo-alpine communities have no reptiles.

## Key Findings

- **Study scope:** The area is the Nevada Mohave Desert, plus the Nevada Colorado River drainage for aquatic habitats.
- **Fish diversity:** 37 species of fish are known from southern Nevada (Appendix II lists 42 species in its checklist, including introduced forms).
- **Endemism:** About 2.1% of vascular plants, 45.0% of fishes and 1.4% of mammals are endemic to southern Nevada.
- **Colorado River isolation:** The Colorado River system is described as faunistically the most isolated river system in North America.
- **Native Colorado River fishes:** Of 16 native species, 14 are endemic to the Colorado River system; six are restricted to southern Nevada.
- **Extinctions and extirpations:** *Empetrichthys merriami* is probably extinct; *Lepidomeda altivelis* is extinct in the area; *Vegas Creek* and its native *Rhinichthys osculus* population became extinct after development of Las Vegas. The Vegas Valley frog (*Rana pipiens fisheri*) is also extinct.
- **Dam effects:** Filling of [[Lake Mead]] (first filled 1941) and [[Lake Mojave]] (first filled 1951) almost eliminated *Ptychocheilus lucius*, reduced mayfly and caddisfly populations, increased plankton and created a sport fishery.
- **Introduced fishes:** Introductions have nearly always adversely affected native populations. [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*, listed as *P. mexicana* in the text) were noted in remnant waters and springs.
- **Lake Mead fish:** Threadfin shad is the most abundant fish; game species include largemouth bass, channel catfish, black crappie and bluegill; carp are numerous; Lake Mojave has a trout fishery (largely *Salmo gairdneri*); kokanee salmon were being planted.
- **Plant and vertebrate totals (Table 4):** Desert communities contain 429 total vascular plant and vertebrate species (creosote bush 363 total in Table 4); hydric-aquatic communities 405 total; the text states over 400 species occur in each of the desert, montane and hydric-aquatic groupings.
- **Table 4 totals:** Desert communities 429 species; montane communities 407 species (total column as printed); hydric-aquatic communities 405 species.
- **Community richness:** Desert springs and marshes (276 species) and stream riparian (253 species) are richer than stream (54 species) and lake (70 species) communities.
- **Reptiles:** 77% of southern Nevada reptiles occur in the creosote bush community; 36.7% are restricted to it. Only 12.8% occur in fir-pine, with none restricted there.
- **Amphibians:** Ten species are listed in Appendix III. The tree frog (*Hyla regilla*) apparently has been eliminated from the lower Colorado River stream riparian community.
- **Birds:** Approximately 290 bird species are assigned to communities (Appendix V). The fir-pine community holds the most species (89 in Table 9).
- **Mammals:** 73 mammal species are listed in Appendix VI; the creosote bush community has 27.2% of its mammals restricted to it.
- **Biogeographic patterns:** Southern-affinity biota (shrub flora, reptiles, rodents, some birds) entered along the Colorado River and Death Valley region; northern-affinity biota (woodland and forest flora, rodents, carnivores, ungulates) persists on isolated Spring and Sheep Ranges.
- **Ecological patterns:** Fishes, amphibians and reptiles show impoverishment along environmental gradients; birds, mammals and vascular plants show replacement patterns.
- **Age of communities:** Bradley (ms.) suggests the lower-elevation plant communities assumed their present positions within the last 10,000 years; fossil pollen spectra similar to modern ones first appeared about 7,000 to 7,500 years ago.
- **Conservation concern:** The authors note that endemism is marked in the most isolated areas (higher Spring Range and isolated springs and streams), and that marshes along the lower Colorado River below Davis Dam were eliminated by channel stabilization work completed after 1960.
