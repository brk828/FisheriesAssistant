# Colorado Squawfish Pond Survival and Antibiotic Delivery in Largemouth Bass

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This communication contains two short fish-culture studies. The first reports the survival of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) reared from swim-up fry and fingerlings in [[Earthen Ponds]] at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico, during 1987. Fry survival was low (27.7 to 36.0%), while fingerling survival averaged 92.2%. The authors attribute the poor fry result mainly to a depleted zooplankton population caused by [[Clam Shrimp]] (*Cyzicus* sp.). The second study compares methods for administering an antibiotic to [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). It tests [[Hyperosmotic Infiltration]] (HI) against [[Intraperitoneal Injection]] (IP) and a control, and finds that IP gave significantly higher survival, while HI did not differ significantly from the control.

## Background & Description

**Colorado Squawfish.** This is the largest native cyprinid in North America. It formerly reached lengths of up to 6.0 ft (1.8 m) and weights of 80 lb, but specimens over 15 lb have rarely been found in the past 15 years. The species is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and is believed to be extirpated from the lower basin, though it persists in much of the upper basin. It is listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior and by seven western states. A propagation program began at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in 1974 and moved to Dexter in 1981. Reproductive biology ([[Spawning Induction]] studies by Hamman) and early life history are better understood, but survival under culture conditions from fry to fingerling was poorly documented.

**Largemouth Bass.** The study's second focus is this sportfish. The authors note that some species, including largemouth bass, are frequently fed only live prey, which limits [[Medicated Feed]] treatment. The paper reviews standard antibiotic routes: IP injection, incorporation into food, and addition to tank water. Hyperosmotic infiltration was originally developed for fish vaccination.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hatchery water supply (Water Well 1, Dexter).** The incoming water is highly mineralized and warm, with the following values reported:

- Total solids: 3,680 ppm
- Total hardness (as CaCO3): 2,100 ppm
- Sulfate: 1,900 ppm
- Sodium: 317 ppm
- Alkalinity (as CaCO3): 260 ppm
- Conductivity (25°C): 3,900 µS/cm
- pH: 7.35
- Temperature: 64°F

**Pond plankton.** Ponds were fertilized to build plankton. Natural zooplankton, including rotifers and cladocerans, served as the main food for fry. A pest bloom of clam shrimp increased turbidity about 34 days before fry stocking. This crustacean competes with fry for food, and its hard shell and large size make it unsuitable as fish food. The bloom was controlled with [[Trichlorfon]] at 0.25 ppm, but the treatment also reduced plankton populations.

**Disease context.** The paper cites bacterial infections such as [[Columnaris Disease]] and bacterial gill disease as the main reasons for antibiotic treatment. Antibiotics such as [[Oxytetracycline]] and nitrofurazone are commonly used, and the authors note that dosing by food is hard to standardize because individual fish eat different amounts.

## Key Findings

**Colorado Squawfish: Fry Culture**

- Source: 25 females (1974 year class) induced to spawn with acetone-dried carp pituitary. Mean female weight was 4.7 lb and mean fecundity was 82,576 eggs.
- Swim-up fry stocked June 1987 (total 337,620) into three ponds of 0.89–0.98 acre, about 3 ft deep.
- Stocking density: 105,316–137,438 fry/acre. Stocked fry numbered 95,340–104,420 per lb.
- Pre-stocking fertilization: alfalfa pellets at 300 lb/acre and superphosphate at 50 lb/acre, then six weekly alfalfa pellet applications at 100 lb/acre.
- Fry were reared 48–51 days at 67–75°F and supplementally fed a commercial trout starter diet from day 1.
- Survival: 27.7–36.0% (lowest in the most affected pond, with the clam shrimp bloom cited as the probable cause).
- Final size: 966–1,155 fish/lb.

**Colorado Squawfish: Fingerling Culture**

- 111,776 fingerlings stocked into two ponds of 0.98–1.08 acre, about 3 ft deep.
- Stocking density: 52,978–55,424 fingerlings/acre; initial size 980–1,088/lb.
- Fed a commercial trout diet (granules 1 and 2) at 5.0% of body weight daily.
- Reared 86–90 days at 64–77°F.
- Survival averaged 92.2%.
- Final size: 230–240 fish/lb.
- Food conversion ratio (feed fed/weight gained): 4.83 and 4.89 in the two ponds.

**Recommendations and Conclusions (Squawfish)**

The authors conclude that the low fry survival likely reflected depleted zooplankton at stocking, caused by the clam shrimp bloom. They suggest that treating ponds during flooding could stop this branchiopod from repopulating before the critical plankton-growth period. Given the cultural progress so far, they consider that hatchery work may help restore the species to its historic [[Colorado River Basin]] habitats.

**Largemouth Bass: Antibiotic Administration**

- Compared three routes for administering the antibiotic oxytetracycline (per the text's description of the compound): HI, IP injection, and a control treatment.
- Survival of IP-injected fish was 53.3% over the 21-day period after bacterial infection (the scan is partly illegible; the value is as reported).
- IP survival was significantly higher than that of both the HI and control groups (p < 0.001).
- HI survival was higher than control survival, but the difference was not significant (p = 0.110 as scanned).
- The authors suggest HI effectiveness could be improved with a follow-up treatment using a chemical surfactant.
