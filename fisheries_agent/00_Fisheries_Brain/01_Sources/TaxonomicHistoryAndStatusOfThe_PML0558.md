# Taxonomic History and Status of the Gila Chub, Gila intermedia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report, prepared for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program (December 1, 1995), reviews the taxonomic history and current status of the [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) and its systematic relationship with the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). An appendix addresses morphologically intermediate, problematic populations. The author concludes that *G. intermedia* is a valid full species maintaining evolutionary independence from *G. robusta*, that the subspecies *G. r. grahami* should be dropped, and that intermediate stocks deserve protection regardless of their evolutionary origin.

## Background & Description

*Gila intermedia* is a relatively small, thick-bodied, coarse-scaled cyprinid. Diagnostic characters follow [[Minckley 1973]] and [[Rinne 1976]], with the key traits summarized below:

- Lateral-line scales usually fewer than 80.
- Dorsal fin-rays usually eight or fewer.
- Anal fin-rays eight or fewer.
- Pelvic fin-rays 8 or 9.
- Head length divided by caudal peduncle depth usually 3.0 or less.
- Breeding males show red or orange coloration on the cheek, lips, fin bases and ventrolateral surfaces.

Rinne's (1976) quantitative comparison with *G. robusta* reported that 97% of 390 *G. intermedia* had fewer than 80 lateral-line scales, compared with 80% of 670 *G. robusta*. Strongly inscribed basal radii occurred in 63% of about 70 *G. intermedia*, versus 14.6% of 137 *G. robusta*. Dorsal fin-rays were 7 or 8 in 94% of *G. intermedia*, and 9 or more in 83% of *G. robusta*. DeMarais (unpublished data) found that these meristic and morphometric characters are strongly correlated, so one or two characters (dorsal fin-ray count and/or caudal peduncle depth) allow rapid, nearly as accurate field identification as a full analysis.

**Taxonomic history.** The species was first collected in 1851 from southern tributaries of the Gila River and was originally described as *Gila gibbosa* by Baird and Girard (1854); because that name became invalid as a homonym, the officially recognized original description is Girard's 1856 *Tigoma intermedia*, with type material from the Rio San Pedro. It was placed in five cyprinid genera over time. Miller (1945) treated it as *G. robusta intermedia*, a status widely followed for over two decades. Re-elevation to full species status was contested until the American Fisheries Society list of 1991 recognized it as valid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native distribution.** *G. intermedia* is endemic to the Gila River basin of Arizona-New Mexico and Sonora, Mexico. Its historic range likely included suitable habitat throughout the basin except the Salt River drainage above Roosevelt Lake. Several populations were lost, probably through draining of [[Cienega]] wetlands over the past century. The species persists in most major sub-basins (Verde, Agua Fria, San Pedro, Santa Cruz) but appears extirpated from Fish Creek in the lower Salt River drainage.

**Sympatry and habitat partitioning.** The Colorado River basin contains six recognized taxa: *G. elegans*, *G. cypha*, *G. intermedia*, *G. r. robusta*, *G. r. jordani* and *G. semidnuda*. *G. elegans* occupies large rivers, *G. intermedia* small streams and marshes, and *G. robusta* and *G. semidnuda* are intermediate in both habitat and phenotype. Because these species coexist, earlier workers grouped them as "ecological subspecies" of *G. robusta*.

**Outside the basin.** The upper Bill Williams is the only other drainage where *G. intermedia* could plausibly occur. Low divides separate it from Verde headwaters, and stream-capture transfer is considered possible. Characteristic Gila basin fishes such as longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and Gila sucker (*Catostomus insignis*) occur there.

**Hybridization.** Evidence for introgressive hybridization is central to the genus. The Virgin River chub (*G. seminuda*) was shown to have a hybrid origin (DeMarais et al. 1992), and [[Dowling and DeMarais 1993]] documented widespread ancient hybridization among Gila species. Allozyme and mtDNA data, however, provide little resolution between *G. intermedia* and *G. robusta*.

## Key Findings

- ***G. intermedia* is a valid species.** Consensus now supports full species status, and adjacent populations with *G. robusta* maintain independent evolutionary trajectories, with no evidence of ongoing gene exchange.
- **Phylogenetic placement.** DeMarais (1992) allozyme cladistics place *G. intermedia* as sister to *G. robusta*, and mtDNA analysis (Dowling, unpublished) supports a close relationship.
- **Dropping *G. r. grahami*.** The author concludes that this subspecies is not valid, and its "grahami" populations should be referred to *G. intermedia* or *G. robusta* after careful evaluation of diagnostic characters.
- **Problematic populations.** Morphologically intermediate stocks, concentrated in upper Verde and lower Salt river tributaries, cannot be assigned objectively with current data. Terms "intergrade" and "hybrid" are considered inappropriate for them.
- **Stream of capture is unreliable.** Because both species occur in the same streams (e.g., upper vs. lower Eagle Creek), designations based solely on locality may be incorrect.
- **Oak Creek.** Gila chubs are established in Spring Creek, near Page Springs, and conform morphologically to *G. intermedia*. Their absence from the Oak Creek confluence is unexplained, and self-reproduction in Oak Creek mainstream is considered unlikely because *G. robusta* occurs there and the stream is more typical of *G. robusta* habitat.
- **Rye Creek.** Few chubs were collected, and their sample sizes were too small for statistical validation. Some showed eight dorsal fin-rays, a *G. intermedia* trait. Further upstream sampling and gene-flow estimates with Tonto Creek are recommended.
- **Conservation.** Because Gila evolved through divergence and reticulation, the author argues for protecting the entire species complex, including morphologically intermediate stocks, to preserve genetic diversity and adaptive potential.
