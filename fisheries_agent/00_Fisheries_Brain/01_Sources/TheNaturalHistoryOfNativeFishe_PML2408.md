# The Natural History of Native Fishes in the Death Valley System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This book, Science Series 30 of the [[Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County]] (published October 31, 1978, by David L. Soltz and Robert J. Naiman, illustrated by Carol Mortensen), synthesizes what was then known about the native fishes of the [[Death Valley System]] and their aquatic habitats. The system held 10 native fish species, nine of them found nowhere else on Earth. The fauna is dominated by [[Pupfish]] (genus Cyprinodon), which have evolved into distinct species and subspecies in isolated springs and streams since the late Pleistocene. The authors argue that these fishes illustrate evolution in North America, that their survival depends on intact groundwater-fed ecosystems, and that groundwater mining, exotic species, habitat diversion and hybridization have driven most populations to decline or extinction.

## Background & Description

The Death Valley System is part of the [[Great Basin]], a region of more than 90 valleys separated by more than 160 mountain ranges. Death Valley is the hottest and driest part of the United States: rainfall averages 4.22 cm per year, while evaporation is about 380 cm per year. Its fishes are remnants of Pleistocene lakes and rivers that connected the basins, which in turn had links to the [[Colorado River]] and [[Lahontan Basin]] systems.

The native fauna comprises three families, five genera, 10 species and 11 recognized subspecies:

- **Killifishes (Cyprinodontidae):** five pupfish species and two killifish species (one extinct). Killifishes are small (2.5 to 10 cm), with soft-rayed fins and scales on the top of the head.
- **Minnows (Cyprinidae):** speckled dace, Owens chub and Mohave chub (the latter two are subspecies of the [[Tui Chub]]). Minnows are scaleless on the head, with forked caudal fins and no jaw teeth.
- **Suckers (Catostomidae):** the Owens sucker, with fleshy, papillae-covered lips.

Pupfishes are the most heat- and salinity-tolerant group. The authors report that they tolerate upper lethal temperatures of 41 to 44°C and salinities up to about 2.5 times that of seawater in the Cottonball Marsh species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Most springs and streams are fed by groundwater moving underground through permeable carbonate rocks. Spring water at [[Ash Meadows]] is 8,000 to 12,000 years old (Winograd, pers. comm.). Ash Meadows springs discharge more than 2,700 hectare-meters annually, and the authors estimate that as much as 35% of the groundwater reaching Ash Meadows travels about 145 km from the White River Groundwater Basin.

**Pleistocene context.** The Pleistocene held at least four pluvial periods. During the last pluvial, [[Lake Manly]] covered most of Death Valley, reaching about 160 km long, 10 to 17 km wide and 185 m deep. Climate warming after roughly 8,000 to 12,000 years ago left isolated springs as refuges.

**Habitat types.** Fishes live in three settings: constant-temperature spring heads, fluctuating streams and marshes, and thermal streams with temperature gradients. Springs are predictable, competitive and structured; streams and marshes are unpredictable, with daily temperature swings of 15 to 20°C in some places.

**Biodiversity beyond the water.** The Amargosa River corridor supports more than 100 bird species, 10 amphibian and reptile species and 20 mammal species. Ash Meadows has 10 endemic flowering plants.

**Human history.** The Panamint Indians harvested Salt Creek pupfish, and the Ash Meadows area was used by prospectors and the 49'ers.

## Key Findings

- **Evolution in isolation.** Pupfish populations have been isolated for 12,000 to 20,000 years in some cases. Devils Hole pupfish, the most divergent, are the smallest of about 25 pupfish species. Three factors drive divergence: selection pressure, length of isolation, and population size (genetic drift).
- **Pupfish count.** More than 20 isolated pupfish populations exist, which have evolved into 10 distinct types: four species and six subspecies of a fifth species.
- **Social systems.** Amargosa pupfish show four social systems: territorial breeding (large springs), loose aggregations (flowing water), monarchistic dominance hierarchies (very small springs such as [[Mexican Spring]]), and non-territorial spawning ([[Devils Hole Pupfish]]).
- **Population extremes.** The Mexican Spring population was 20 to 47 individuals, probably the world's smallest self-sustaining vertebrate population before the spring dried. Devils Hole numbers fluctuate from about 150 to 400 fish, down from a maximum of 800.
- **Thermal tolerance.** Acclimation shifts thermal tolerance. Juvenile pupfish tolerate higher temperatures than eggs or adults. Amargosa pupfish eggs develop normally at 20 to 36°C.
- **Salinity.** The [[Cottonball Marsh Pupfish]] has been recorded at 160‰ in nature, but field fish avoided water above 70‰. Pupfish can recover from osmotic stress in about eight hours.
- **Oxygen tolerance.** Desert pupfish have the lowest recorded minimum oxygen tolerance for any fish, 0.13 to 0.22 mg/L.
- **Productivity.** In a warm stream near Tecopa, annual production of Amargosa pupfish is nearly 10 times that of any carnivorous fish population studied to date.
- **Extinctions and losses.** The [[Ash Meadows Killifish]] is extinct (last specimen seen in 1948). Among the subspecies, Raycraft Ranch and Pahrump Ranch killifish, Shoshone pupfish and Tecopa pupfish are extinct. Owens pupfish fell from millions in 1916 to a few hundred by the 1930s. Its rediscovery in 1956 followed a presumed extinction.
- **Main threats.** The authors identify four causes of decline: exotic species, pesticides, surface-water diversion and groundwater mining. Groundwater mining is the most serious. In Pahrump Valley, groundwater was mined at 3,200 hectare-meters per year for over 20 years.
- **Hybridization.** The [[Mohave Chub]] and Owens chub have hybridized so extensively that only one genetically pure population of each remains (Soda Springs and Owens Gorge).
- **Devils Hole legal protection.** The 1976 [[Cappert v. United States]] Supreme Court decision upheld federal authority to appropriate groundwater to preserve Devils Hole and natural aquatic ecosystems.
- **Remaining fauna.** Of the 18 native fishes (including subspecies) listed in Table 2, only five are considered healthy and undepleted.
- **Recommendations.** Authors call for protection of entire aquatic ecosystems, not single species, along with refugia, legal protection, research, and long-term financial and philosophical commitment. Transplanting is considered a last resort because transplanted populations diverge from parent populations.
