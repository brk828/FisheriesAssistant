## Executive Summary

This paper reports a 10-year (1939–1948) continuous creel census of [[Lake Pawhuska]], a 95-surface-acre artificial impoundment in Osage County, Oklahoma. It represents one of the earliest long-term, uninterrupted creel studies tracking a reservoir fishery from initial filling through population establishment and into a period of "normal" annual fluctuation. Over the study period, 23,442 fisherman-days yielded 44,072 fish. From 1943–1948, when individual weights were also recorded, 11,964 fishermen harvested 12,021.8 pounds of fish, averaging 21.1 lbs/acre/year. The four dominant taxa by number were [[Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Bass]], and miscellaneous [[Sunfish]] species; by weight, [[Black Bass]], [[Crappie]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Bullhead]] ranked highest. The study demonstrates classic reservoir "boom-and-bust" dynamics, with peak harvests in the early impoundment years (1941–1942) followed by a marked decline attributed to overpopulation-driven growth suppression rather than population collapse.

## Background & Description

Species-specific size and growth data recorded during the census (1943–1948):

- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: Largest individuals reached 14 lbs 8 oz (recorded 1943). Two individuals collected in 1948 averaged 11.5 lbs at age 6, implying a growth rate of roughly 2 lbs/year assuming stocking occurred no earlier than 1937. Average weight in the creel during 1943–1948 was 5.42 lbs — the largest average individual size of any species censused, explaining its disproportionate contribution to total harvest weight (21.28%) despite low numbers (2.0% of total fish).
- **[[Black Bass]]** (largemouth + spotted, combined category): Average individual weight 1.22 lbs (1943–1948).
- **[[Crappie]]** (white + black, combined category): Average individual weight 0.36 lb across 1943–1948; lowest annual average size was 0.33 lb (1947), highest was 0.44 lb (1948).
- **[[Bluegill]]**: Average individual weight 0.26 lb, relatively stable across years.
- **[[Black Bullhead]]**: Average weight increased markedly from ~1 lb in 1943 to ~2.25 lbs in 1948, a trend interpreted as indicating a population no longer self-replenishing, leaving only larger/older survivors in the creel.
- Minimum legal harvest sizes enforced: 6 inches total length for crappie; 10 inches for bass and channel catfish. No sub-legal fish were recorded in creel data, though anecdotal catch-and-release of undersized fish (including yellow perch "stragglers") is noted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System type**: Small (95-acre), deep (max 55 ft), clear-water impoundment fed by a 1,600-acre tall-grass pasture drainage; siltation negligible.
- **Habitat structure**: Abundant [[Chara]] and smartweed growth along most of the shoreline provided cover/spawning habitat; ~5 acres of shallow water (≤5 ft depth).
- **Thermal regime**: Monthly mean air temperatures ranged from 33.5°F (January) to 81.4°F (July/August), with extremes of 21.4°F (Jan 1940) and 87.5°F (Aug 1943), reflecting a warm-temperate to subtropical continental climate typical of southern impoundments.
- **Community composition**: Dominant sport species were [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Crappie]] (white and black), [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. Gill-net surveys (1946, 1948) also documented [[Black Bullhead]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Orange-Spotted Sunfish]], [[Redear Sunfish]], and [[Spotted Sucker]] as established but minor components.
- **Species shifts**: Relative dominance between white crappie and black crappie shifted between 1946 (white crappie dominant) and 1948 (black crappie dominant), suggesting community-level species replacement dynamics within the [[Crappie]] guild.
- **Trophic/feeding notes**: Bait preference data indicate crappie and bass were primarily caught on live minnows (implying piscivorous/predatory foraging), bluegill on worms, green sunfish on flies, and catfish on cut bait, liver, worms, and prepared baits (benthic/opportunistic feeding).
- **Night vs. day activity**: Night angling (1943, 1944, 1946–1948) disproportionately captured [[Channel Catfish]] (73.22% of night catch by weight) and [[Bullhead]] (12.73%), consistent with nocturnal/crepuscular foraging behavior in ictalurids, versus bass (7.61%) and crappie (4.93%).

## Key Findings

- **Total harvest (1939–1948)**: 44,072 fish removed by 23,442 fisherman-days (1.88 fish/fisherman-day average).
- **Species composition by number**: Crappie 36.91% (16,269), Bluegill 27.31% (12,035), unidentified sunfishes 16.23% (7,150), Bass 14.09% (6,210), Bullheads 2.97% (1,311), Channel catfish 2.00% (883), Yellow perch 0.43% (190), Redear sunfish 0.05% (24).
- **Species composition by weight (1943–1948, 12,021.8 lbs total)**: Bass 30.09%, Crappie 27.30%, Channel catfish 21.28%, Bluegill 10.32%, unidentified sunfish 5.71%, Bullhead 5.30%.
- **Yield metrics**: Annual average total harvest = 21.1 lbs/surface acre (1943–1948). By species: Bass 6.28 lbs/acre/yr, Crappie 5.76 lbs/acre/yr, Channel catfish 4.49 lbs/acre/yr, Bluegill 2.18 lbs/acre/yr, unidentified sunfish 1.27 lbs/acre/yr, Bullhead 1.12 lbs/acre/yr.
- **Temporal/seasonal patterns**: June accounted for 24.1% of all fishing effort and 28.26% of the 10-year fish catch — largely an artifact of closed spawning seasons (April–May) enforced in 1939–1942, which concentrated angling pressure at reopening. Catch-per-fisherman rose steadily January–June, dipped in July–August, and peaked again in fall (Sept–Nov), possibly reflecting more skilled/dedicated anglers in cooler months.
- **Boom-and-bust population dynamics**: Harvest peaked in 1941–1942 (17.83% of 10-yr catch in 1942 alone) then declined sharply after 1943. The authors interpret this not as population collapse but as a shift toward **overpopulation-driven growth stunting**, wherein dense year-classes grow too slowly to reach legal/"keeper" size and are thus underrepresented in the creel — a key cautionary finding for reservoir fisheries management.
- **Night fishing efficiency**: Night anglers averaged 1.52 lbs/trip, exceeding day-fishing rates, driven by large-bodied catfish and bullhead catches.
- **Species-specific declines**: Yellow perch and redear sunfish effectively disappeared from the creel after 1942, suggesting failure to establish self-sustaining populations post-introduction.
- **Management implication**: The study underscores the value of continuous, long-duration creel census data for distinguishing between true population decline and size-selective availability shifts caused by density-dependent growth suppression in impounded fisheries — directly relevant to [[Reservoir Fisheries Management]] and [[Stunted Fish Populations]] research.
