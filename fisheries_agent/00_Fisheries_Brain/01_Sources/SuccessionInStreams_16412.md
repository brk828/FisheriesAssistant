# Succession in Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review by Stuart G. Fisher ([[Arizona State University]], Department of Zoology) argues that [[Ecological Succession]] theory, which has historically been botanical and terrestrial, can be fruitfully applied to running waters. The paper examines two aspects of stream succession: [[Site-Specific Temporal Succession]] following disturbance, and [[Longitudinal Succession]] along the upstream-downstream gradient. Fisher concludes that streams are a valuable setting for testing general successional theory and proposes a redefinition of succession as a sequence of communities in which each is influenced by its predecessors, in time or in space. The discussion draws mainly on studies from [[Sycamore Creek]] in the Sonoran Desert, with comparisons to temperate and other stream systems.

## Background & Description

The introduction traces succession to early phytosociologists ([[Cowles]], [[Clements]], [[Gleason]]) who treated it as temporal change in terrestrial plant communities. Succession is defined as change in community composition after disturbance at a site, assessed by species composition, richness and dominance. Fisher notes that generality of successional pattern remains contested (e.g., Drury and Nisbet, 1973; McIntosh, 1980), and that the concept has been stretched to cover lake eutrophication, seasonal phytoplankton change, spatial zonation and ecosystem-level properties.

Two conceptual frameworks are contrasted:

- **Gleasonian individualistic school:** succession arises from life-history attributes and interactions of individuals ([[Connell and Slatyer Models]]).
- **Clementsian holistic school:** the community is an organic entity, with succession analogous to organismal development, as in [[Odum's Ecosystem Development]] generalizations.

Tansley's (1929) division of causes into allogenic (abiotic, external) and autogenic (biotic) forcing is used throughout. Fisher stresses that these causes overlap in reality and that Connell and Slatyer's hypotheses are not mutually exclusive.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disturbance regimes in streams.** Flooding is the best-documented disturbance, but dredging, pesticides, coal ash, drought and hailstorms also alter stream communities. Colonization of new substrates, natural and artificial, is also documented. Return intervals for severe scouring floods usually extend two years or more, allowing several generations of organisms between events.

**Why streams are poor successional settings in practice.** Large rivers rarely flood with enough intensity to reset the biota; small temperate streams flood too often; and seasonal change can confound successional patterns. Fisher describes the "ideal" successional stream as one where (1) flooding is severe, (2) the biota experiences several generations between disturbances, (3) minor disturbances are absent, and (4) seasonal effects are minimal. Hot desert streams, where successional change completes within one season, approximate this ideal.

**Recovery and life span.** Recovery after flood damage ranges from about a month to several years. Diatom communities recover in one to two months, insects are intermediate depending on taxon and voltinism, and fish may take several years where immigration is excluded. Recovery is accelerated when colonization routes remain open. In Sonoran Desert streams, floods can remove nearly 99% of the insect fauna but last only hours; riparian adults oviposit immediately after recession, so recolonization is rapid.

**Time scales.** Fisher links successional time to the interval between resetting events. Frequently flooding streams (e.g., heterotrophic New England streams with weekly spates) may show little change, while autotrophic Arizona streams flooding about once a year show marked succession. Longer cycles include detrital turnover times near 100 years in Northwestern U.S.A. streams, and the drainage of Southwest [[Cienega]] wetlands about a century ago.

**Longitudinal gradient.** Fish, macrophyte and invertebrate distributions have traditionally been explained by physiographic variables such as slope, depth, stream order, temperature, substrate, velocity and discharge. Fisher argues that this view ignores the influence of upstream communities.

## Key Findings

1. **Initial colonization is highly stochastic.** Post-flood colonists in Sycamore Creek are always diatoms, yet these assemblages are replaced by bluegreen- or Cladophora-dominated communities depending on local conditions. Fisher suggests that the sere is not determined at its outset, contrary to Blum (1956).
2. **Egler's initial floristics model** may apply to stream insects and algae, but is difficult to test in systems dominated by small organisms.
3. **Direct evidence for interactive autogenic succession is scant.** Diatom inhibition, spontaneous mat detachment and dominance shifts within the first month at Sycamore Creek are cited as examples of biotic effects, but they are limited.
4. **Collective properties behave largely as expected.** Biomass, chlorophyll *a* and primary production increase during succession, and species diversity often declines to moderate levels after an early increase. At Sycamore Creek, Odum's generalizations hold except for attributes shaped by stream openness (e.g., P/R ratios do not approach unity; net production is exported).
5. **Streams are not more resistant to perturbation after succession.** Disturbance by flooding is too severe to be biologically ameliorated.
6. **Longitudinal succession should be recognized.** Upstream communities alter the aqueous medium (through organic matter processing and nutrient spiralling) and thereby influence downstream communities. Interaction is inequitable, favoring upstream-to-downstream effects, and is irreversible in time but not in space. At Sycamore Creek, dissolved inorganic nitrogen declines sharply below spring heads owing to diatom and Cladophora assimilation, and nitrogen-fixing bluegreen algae dominate downstream where dissolved nitrogen is undetectable.
7. **Supporting conceptual advances.** Fisher credits Hynes's work on terrestrial detritus, the [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al., 1980) and [[Nutrient Spiralling]] as underpinnings for spatial succession.
8. **Redefinition of succession.** Succession is proposed as "a sequence of communities in which the ascendancy of each is influenced by its predecessors," resolved in time or space, with influence that may be weak or strong, direct or indirect.
9. **Proposed experiments.** Fisher suggests recirculating "Round River" channels, artificial channels with and without upstream biota, and partial-scour experiments. He predicts that under facilitation, streams recover from the bottom up; under inhibition, from the top down.
10. **Patchiness is a methodological barrier.** Sycamore Creek algae occur in distinct patch types differing in composition and chlorophyll *a*, so bank-to-bank sampling coalesces several successional states. Fisher calls for patch-level analyses comparable to Horn's (1975) Markovian replacement matrices.
11. **Time since disturbance** should be reported in all stream studies, whatever their focus.
