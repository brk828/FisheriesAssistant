# Food Habits of Lake Trout from Squaw Lake, Northern Quebec

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This short field note documents opportunistic dietary observations of [[Lake Trout]] (*Cristivomer namaycush namaycush*, synonym of *Salvelinus namaycush*) in Squaw Lake, a barren, oligotrophic lake in Northern Quebec, during summer 1952. The study, incidental to herpetological fieldwork, found a marked seasonal shift in diet — from fish remains and aquatic invertebrates in early season to near-total reliance on adult mosquitoes and mosquito larvae during peak fly season. This is notable because large lake trout are typically considered highly piscivorous specialists; the observations suggest strong food limitation and dietary plasticity in resource-poor northern lake systems.

## Background & Description
- Species examined: **Lake Trout** (*Cristivomer namaycush namaycush*), also referenced under current taxonomy as [[Salvelinus Namaycush]].
- Individuals were in notably **poor body condition**, interpreted as evidence of intense intraspecific/food competition:
  - 19 in — 1.5 lb
  - 24 in — 3 lb
  - 27 in — 5.5 lb
- These length-to-weight ratios are described as unusually low ("poor condition") relative to expected condition factors for the species.
- One 8-inch juvenile/small individual was examined with a firm, distended stomach — distention caused entirely by ingested mosquitoes and mosquito larvae, despite small body size.
- One 24.5 in (3 lb) trout showed no fight response when hooked, later explained by an embedded injury (see Key Findings).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat description**: North shore of Squaw Lake is barren of macroscopic aquatic vegetation; substrate is broken slate, clearly visible through the water column, indicating an oligotrophic, low-productivity environment.
- **Trophic ecology**: 
  - Pre- and during spring ice break-up: diet composed of **fish remains** and **aquatic invertebrates**, consistent with the species' typical role as an apex piscivore in northern lake food webs — see [[Piscivory in Salmonids]].
  - As the fly season progressed (through late June–early July), diet shifted heavily toward **terrestrial/emergent insect prey**, specifically adult mosquitoes and mosquito larvae, likely reflecting a seasonal pulse in insect abundance and reduced availability/energetic payoff of forage fish.
  - Of 13 lake trout sampled June 28–July 7, **12 of 13** had stomachs filled/plugged with insect material (mosquitoes and larvae) and only 1 contained fish remains — a striking reversal of expected size-based piscivory.
  - Even large trout (up to 27 in) were found subsisting on mosquitoes and mosquito larvae, which is atypical given that large lake trout are generally considered almost exclusively piscivorous (see [[Ontogenetic Diet Shift]]).
  - One individual's stomach contained only **caddis fly larvae**, suggesting benthic/inactive prey foraging at the lake bottom rather than active pursuit of fish or emergent insects.
- **Competitive context**: The author links the barren habitat (lack of cover/forage structure, low productivity) to intensified intraspecific food competition, manifesting as poor body condition across sampled size classes.

## Key Findings
1. **Seasonal dietary shift**: Lake trout diet transitioned from fish/invertebrate-based (early season) to overwhelmingly insect-based (mosquitoes and larvae) as the fly season progressed — a notable deviation from typical piscivorous feeding strategy in large salmonids.
2. **High incidence of insectivory in large individuals**: 12 of 13 trout sampled over a 10-day period (June 28–July 7) had stomachs dominated by insects rather than fish, including individuals up to 27 inches in length.
3. **Poor body condition**: Length/weight data (19 in/1.5 lb; 24 in/3 lb; 27 in/5.5 lb) suggest food-stressed populations, attributed to intense competition in a resource-poor, macrophyte-barren lake environment.
4. **Anomalous behavior explained by injury**: A lethargic, non-resistant 24.5 in trout was found to have a large triple hook embedded in its stomach wall with attached wire leader and line — indicating prior angling injury impairing normal behavior and possibly feeding capacity (only caddis fly larvae found in stomach, suggesting reduced foraging on active prey).
5. **Evidence of benthic feeding**: The caddis-fly-larvae-only stomach content in the injured fish suggests it had shifted to feeding on relatively inactive, bottom-dwelling prey, possibly due to its compromised condition.
6. **Broader implication**: Findings suggest that in unproductive, structurally simple lake systems, even top-level piscivores like [[Lake Trout]] may rely heavily on seasonally abundant insect prey (e.g., mosquito emergences), challenging assumptions about strict piscivory in large-bodied salmonids — relevant to [[Optimal Foraging Theory]] and [[Diet Plasticity in Fish]].
