# Oxygen Consumption and Metabolic Reserves During Starvation and Aestivation in Bulinus (Physopsis) africanus

#malacology #freshwater-biology #planorbidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 study by J. Heeg, published in *Malacologia*, examines how the freshwater snail [[Bulinus (Physopsis) Africanus]] (Planorbidae) responds physiologically to being out of water, comparing fed, starving-in-water and aestivating (out-of-water) individuals. Aestivating snails had a lower oxygen consumption rate, a temperature coefficient (Arrhenius slope) distinct from fed and starving snails, and drew on metabolic reserves more slowly over 21 days. Only a subset of the population could effect this metabolic shift, which the author proposes explains variation in out-of-water survival among planorbid populations.

## Background & Description

- **Organism:** [[Bulinus (Physopsis) Africanus]] (Krauss), a pulmonate planorbid snail, a schistosomiasis intermediate host studied within a [[Bilharzia Research Context]].
- **Prior work:** [[Von Brand Starvation Studies]] (1948, 1950, 1957) showed reduced oxygen uptake during starvation in several gastropods, including [[Biomphalaria Glabrata]]. [[Cridland Desiccation Resistance]] (1967) showed aestivating aquatic pulmonates tolerate up to 50°C. [[Coles Aestivation Research]] (1969) reported that reactivation of *B. (P.) nasutus* raised oxygen uptake before feeding.
- **Aims:** Determine whether starvation and aestivation differ in oxygen consumption (and thus metabolic pathway), and whether reserves are consumed more slowly during aestivation.

### Methods (summary)
- **Source populations:** Seepage basin of the Vernon Hooper Dam (Shongweni), pools in the Mpolweni River (Krantzkloof Nature Reserve), and the Bilharziasis Research Unit, Nelspruit. Results did not differ significantly among the three.
- **Culture:** Snails kept at 15–27°C fluctuating (simulating field conditions), medium-hardness standard water, lettuce and lucerne diet; individuals isolated and culled if abnormal.
- **Oxygen measurement:** [[Warburg Respirometry]] at 25°C, with flask constants derived from live weight and a density of 1.13 g/cc (n = 10).
- **Reserves:** Tissue fractionated into musculature, [[Alimentary System]] and [[Reproductive System]]; lipid by Soxhlet ether extraction, protein by micro-Kjeldahl, carbohydrate by Dreywood's anthrone method.
- **Standardisation:** A "standard snail" with 25 mg dry tissue weight (excluding kidney) was used to compare results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Aestivation is a survival strategy in temporary waters; the study site habitats (Shongweni, Krantzkloof) dry partially and seasonally, though never completely.
- In South Africa, July–November is the period when many streams and pools dry; July–August has minimal cloud cover, maximal insolation, and the year's highest soil temperatures (Schulze, 1965), making high-temperature tolerance essential.
- The author links population differences to selection pressure: populations in truly ephemeral waters are expected to contain more successful aestivators than permanent lake populations (cf. [[Olivier Vector Survival]], 1956, who found similar contrasts in *Biomphalaria glabrata* and *Tropicorbis centimetralis*).
- Comparative aestivation physiology: [[Pila Aestivation Anaerobiosis]] (Meenakshi, 1964) involved anaerobiosis and lactic acid, whereas [[Pila ovata]] (Coles, 1968) was aerobic with reduced oxygen uptake; the Planorbidae studied fit the aerobic pattern.

## Key Findings

1. **Reduced oxygen uptake during aestivation.** After 21 days, oxygen consumption (µl O₂/mg dry tissue/h) fell in both categories, more so in aestivators (Table 1):

| Treatment | n | Rate at start | Rate after 21 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fed | 52 | 1.44 ± 0.035 | 1.40 ± 0.024 |
| Starved | 50 | 1.44 ± 0.034 | 0.81 ± 0.015 |
| Aestivating | 51 | 1.37 ± 0.035 | 0.47 ± 0.014 |

2. **Heterogeneous responses.** Aestivators split into three patterns: some could not fall below the starvation rate, some fell to less than half the starvation level, and some dropped and then re-elevated.

3. **Defining successful aestivation.** Snails surviving 28 days out of water (n = 13) had significantly lower rates than those that died (n = 17) after day 9; the dead did not differ from starving snails (n = 20). Successful aestivators were operationally defined as those within 2× SE of the survivors' mean. Initial rates did not differ among categories, ruling out surface-area effects.

4. **Mortality in water.** Over 30% of starving snails died within 21 days; only one survived 28 days.

5. **Temperature coefficient differs.** Arrhenius plots (5–50°C) showed no significant difference between fed and starving slopes (suggesting substrate- or enzyme-limited depression), but the aestivating slope differed significantly (P < 0.001), implying a change in activation energy. Aestivators survived above 40°C: at 45°C for up to 4 hr, at 50°C for about 2 hr, with up to a 5-fold rise in oxygen consumption before death.

6. **Reserve economy.** Protein was the main substrate used in both states (confirming [[Von Brand Starvation Studies]], 1957). The greatest depletion occurred early (first 5 days). The clearest difference was in the alimentary system: after 21 days, starved snails lost about 3.56 mg dry tissue there versus 1.39 mg for aestivators (Table 3). Carbohydrate in the reproductive system stayed relatively constant after the first week.

7. **Standard snail totals (Table 3, 25 mg dry tissue):**

| Substrate | Fed | Starved (loss) | Aestivating (loss) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein (mg) | 15.91 | 9.91 (6.00) | 12.14 (3.77) |
| Carbohydrate (mg) | 1.66 | 0.79 (0.87) | 1.21 (0.45) |
| Lipid (mg) | 1.26 | 0.51 (0.75) | 0.73 (0.53) |
| Dry tissue (mg) | 25.00 | 17.90 (7.10) | 21.01 (4.00) |

8. **Oxygen budget.** Oxygen calculated from metabolite losses agreed well with measured consumption for aestivators; starving snails showed a discrepancy over the first two weeks, attributed to experimental error.

9. **Metabolic shift and survival.** Preliminary experiments suggested water content, not metabolic economy alone, mainly determines survival time when aestivating. The author nonetheless concludes that metabolic reduction, combined with altered temperature coefficients, underpins survival of both starvation and high temperatures in aestivation.

10. **Parallel with temperature acclimation.** The rotated rate/temperature curve resembles Type III high-temperature acclimation in [[Prosser Acclimation Classification]] (1958), suggesting aestivation and acclimation may share a mechanism responding to multiple proximate factors (acclimation itself was not tested).

**Conclusion:** Aestivation in *B. (P.) africanus* is a physiologically distinct, economical state that not all individuals can enter, which may explain intra- and interpopulation variation in desiccation tolerance across planorbids.
