# Intra-Specific Variation in the Bigmouth Shiner (Notropis dorsalis)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Notropis

## Executive Summary
This 1959 study by Underhill and Merrell analyzes intra-specific variation in nine meristic characters (scale and fin ray counts) of the [[Bigmouth Shiner]] (*Notropis dorsalis*) using 1,080 specimens collected from 1952-1956 across the Minnesota, Mississippi, St. Croix, and Missouri river drainages in Minnesota. The study systematically examines annual, intra-stream, inter-stream, intra-basin, and inter-basin variation to test whether observed differences represent genetically-based **micro-geographic races** or environmentally-induced **ecophenotypes**. The authors conclude that the high, complex variation found—particularly the fact that intra-basin variation often equals or exceeds inter-basin variation—argues against simple environmental (temperature) control and instead supports a genetic basis for the differences, suggesting the populations are best interpreted as micro-geographic races. The paper offers a significant methodological caution for [[Taxonomy|taxonomic]] and subspecies-defining work reliant on [[Meristic Characters]].

## Background & Description
- **Species**: Bigmouth Shiner (*Notropis dorsalis*), a small cyprinid fish.
- **Sample Size**: 1,080 specimens collected 1952-1956.
- **Collection Method**: Quarter-inch mesh seines.
- **Geographic Scope**: Streams tributary to the Minnesota, St. Croix, Mississippi, and Missouri rivers in Minnesota, USA.
- **Characters Measured** (following [[Hubbs and Lagler (1949)]] methodology):
  - Scales in the lateral line
  - Scales above the lateral line
  - Scales below the lateral line
  - Scales before the dorsal fin (pre-dorsal scales)
  - Scales on the caudal peduncle
  - Rays in the dorsal fin
  - Rays in the anal fin
  - Rays in the pectoral fin
  - Rays in the pelvic fin
- **Statistical Methods**: Followed [[Simpson and Roe (1939)]] and [[Snedecor (1946)]], including analysis of variance and standard error comparisons.
- **Error Controls**:
  - Checked for counter drift/systematic error via repeated subsampling (35- and 20-individual groups) from a 212-specimen sample (South Branch, Zumbro River) — no significant change detected.
  - Checked for sampling bias via comparison of consecutive weekend samples from the Zumbro River — close agreement for most characters.
  - Checked for **sexual dimorphism**: comparison of 81 females vs. 131 males (Zumbro River) revealed **no significant differences** in any measured character (see Table II).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- The Bigmouth Shiner shows a strong habitat preference for **small to moderate-sized streams with shifting sand bottoms**, and is notably uncommon in large-river habitats.
- The sluggish, silt-laden **Minnesota River** is hypothesized to act as a **barrier to inter-stream migration**, effectively isolating individual stream populations within the basin.
- The species is a **late-summer spawner** (July–August, per [[Starrett (1951)]]), a timing which the authors argue exposes developing embryos to relatively stable (non-fluctuating) ambient temperatures, unlike spring-spawning species subject to volatile May/June temperature swings.
- [[Starrett (1951)]] also noted that Bigmouth Shiner populations are subject to marked annual fluctuations in abundance (common one year, scarce the next), which combined with hypothesized low migration rates could contribute to genetic drift between neighboring stream populations.
- No marked ecological, atmospheric, or temperature differences were identified between sampled streams/basins that could account for the morphological differences found — all basins studied fall within similar (70–72°F) isotherms and flow through glacial till of similar age.
- This ecological homogeneity across sample sites is central to the paper's argument against a purely direct-environmental (ecophenotypic) explanation for variation, in line with prior findings in [[Gasterosteus Aculeatus|three-spined stickleback]] ([[Heuts 1947]], [[Heuts 1949]]) and [[Salmo Trutta|sea trout]] ([[Taning 1952]]).

## Key Findings

### Character Variability Categories
The nine meristic characters fell into three distinct groups based on variability:
1. **Low annual/inter-basin variation**: Dorsal, anal, and pelvic fin ray counts — nearly identical across years and basins.
2. **High variation**: Scales above and below the lateral line — extreme variability, cautioned against use in intra-/inter-basin comparisons.
3. **Intermediate variation**: Lateral line scale counts, pre-dorsal scale counts, caudal peduncle scale counts, and pectoral fin ray counts.

### Sexual Dimorphism
- No evidence of sexual dimorphism found across any measured character (Table II, Zumbro River sample: 131 males, 81 females).

### Annual Variation
- Significant annual differences detected in scales above/below the lateral line and lateral line scale counts (Credit River, Savage, MN, 1952–1956 samples).
- Lateral line scale count in 1954 differed significantly (>0.6 scales) from 1952 and 1955 averages, though not from 1953 or 1956; overall probability that inter-annual differences were due to chance was <1/1000.
- No correlation found between annual variation and any discernible ecological/atmospheric shifts.

### Intra-Stream & Inter-Stream Variation
- Comparison of three tributaries of the Zumbro River (North Branch, South Middle Branch, South Branch) revealed substantial variation in lateral line scale counts but little variation in caudal peduncle scales or pectoral fin rays (Table IV).
- Distance between sampling stations (20–80 river miles) did not correlate with the magnitude of morphological difference — the closest two stations (South and South Middle Branch, 20 miles apart) differed almost as much as the most distant pair (North and South Branch, 80 miles apart).
- Differences within a single stream system reached nearly one full scale count, comparable in magnitude to inter-basin differences.

### Intra-Basin Variation
- **Minnesota River tributaries** (Table V): Redwood River population had significantly more lateral line scales than Cottonwood, Little Cottonwood, and Blue Earth River populations; no evidence of a geographic cline.
- Maximum intra-basin difference in lateral line scales was 1.2 scales — nearly double the annual variation observed in the Credit River and 20% greater than intra-stream (Zumbro) variation.
- **Mississippi River tributaries** (Table VI) showed a similarly complex pattern of variation with no consistent geographic trend.
- No correlation found between different meristic characters within the same population (e.g., Redwood River had high scale counts but only mid-range pectoral fin ray counts).

### Inter-Basin Variation
- Pooled basin comparisons (Table VII: Upper Mississippi, St. Croix, Minnesota, Lower Mississippi, Missouri) showed:
  - Upper Mississippi sample (n=16) had significantly higher lateral line scale counts than all other basins, though based on a small sample.
  - St. Croix River sample had significantly fewer caudal peduncle scales than other basins, but this difference disappeared when compared to specific within-basin samples (e.g., Zumbro, Prairie Rivers).
  - Minnesota and Lower Mississippi populations differed significantly in pectoral fin ray counts at the pooled level, but this significance vanished when individual stream samples were compared directly — indicating pooling can mask or exaggerate true biological signal.
- Inter-basin variation was generally **no greater** than intra-basin or annual variation, and in some cases was smaller.

### Pre-Dorsal Scale Count & the "Piptolepsis" Phenomenon
- Extreme range: 8–28 scales; sample means ranged from 18.61±1.05 to 24.31±0.42; coefficient of variation 7.0–13.3.
- Some individuals (up to 10% in certain samples) exhibited a scaleless or partially-scaled nape phenotypically resembling the scaleless western form **piptolepsis**, particularly in Minnesota and Missouri River tributary samples — despite these locations being far outside the known range of that western form.
- This finding raises doubt about the taxonomic reliability of nape scalation as a subspecies-diagnostic character.

### Taxonomic & Genetic Implications
- The authors argue that if only single samples per basin had been collected, researchers might have erroneously assigned subspecific status to each basin population.
- Because intra-basin variation was found to be as great as or greater than inter-basin variation, the study strongly cautions against using limited sampling for subspecies delineation (relevant to debates around the "75%/90% rule" for subspecies).
- The lack of correlation between variable characters (e.g., lateral line scales vs. pectoral rays) within the same populations argues against simple, uniform environmental (e.g., temperature) control, since a single dominant environmental factor would be expected to shift correlated characters uniformly.
- Comparisons with related literature ([[Taning 1952]] on *[[Salmo Trutta]]*, [[Svardson 1952]] on [[Coregonid|coregonids]], [[Heuts 1949]] on [[Gasterosteus Aculeatus]], and [[Lagler and Bailey 1947]] on [[Etheostoma Nigrum|Johnny Darter]] subspecies) support the idea that meristic character limits are genetically fixed, with environment modifying expression within a genetically-determined reaction range.
- The authors conclude that Bigmouth Shiner populations are best understood as **micro-geographic races** with an underlying genetic basis, though they acknowledge the critical "common-environment rearing" experiment needed to fully resolve genetic vs. environmental causation was not performed in this study.
- A companion ongoing study on [[Notropis Cornutus|Common Shiner]] and [[Notropis Deliciosus|Sand Shiner]] reportedly shows substantially lower annual and inter-population variability compared to the Bigmouth Shiner, suggesting this degree of variability may not be universal among cyprinids.
