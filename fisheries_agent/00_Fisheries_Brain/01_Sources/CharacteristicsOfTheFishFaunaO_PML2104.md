# Fish Fauna of Malya Dam, Lake Victoria Region, Tanzania, and the Effects of Multispecies Tilapia Stocking

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This paper (A. I. Payne) describes the fish fauna of [[Malya Dam]], a small impoundment completed in 1947 in the [[Lake Victoria]] region of [[Tanzania]]. The dam had been stocked with several [[Tilapia]] species, and the survey aimed to assess the resulting fishery and to inform a national approach to stocking. Of the species now present, the endemic [[Tilapia Esculenta]] dominates the catch and is the economically most important fish in the dam. Introduced [[Tilapia Rendalli]] and [[Tilapia Zillii]] are less abundant, and Zillii's tilapia failed to establish. The study concludes that stocking non-native tilapia is not a sound policy and that stocking with native species should be preferred.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Malya Dam has an average surface area of about 70 ha and a maximum depth of 9.4 m after the rains. It is fringed by papyrus, has no inflowing rivers, and is filled by rainfall and seasonal runoff from surrounding grassland. It lies at about 3° S latitude, roughly 75 miles by road from Lake Victoria.
- **Origin of fauna:** The dam was colonised after 1947 by several species. The fauna is described as including at least seven species, most of which probably colonised naturally. [[Tilapia Rendalli]] was introduced in the mid-1950s, [[Tilapia Zillii]] in three batches in the late 1950s, and the endemic [[Tilapia Esculenta]] is considered a natural colonist.
- **Earlier work:** Methods and limnological features were the same as those in an earlier Payne paper (1971). Chemical data were compared with other Tanzanian dams (Bailey 1966).
- **Sampling approach:** Fish were sampled with gill nets of different mesh sizes (including 2-in and 3-in mesh) set from dusk until dawn, on several dates between March and July. Catch totals were recorded by mesh size and fish length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry** ([[Water Chemistry]], Table 1):
- pH roughly 6.6–7.7.
- Total alkalinity about 35–39.5 ppm.
- Nitrate and phosphate concentrations were low, as reported for several other Tanzanian dams.
- Dissolved oxygen was high, at about 86–94% saturation, across the sampled layers.

**Temperature:** Early-morning surface temperature from March to July ranged from 20.6 °C to 21.9 °C.

**Diet and trophic roles:**
- [[Tilapia Esculenta]] is planktivorous and insectivorous, feeding largely on zooplankton and small arthropods, particularly [[Chironomids]].
- [[Tilapia Rendalli]] is herbivorous, grazing on vegetation and algae in the marginal zone.
- Tilapia that occupy the planktivore and herbivore roles compete with one another. The author suggests that the herbivorous [[Tilapia Rendalli]] already occupied the habitat when [[Tilapia Zillii]] was introduced, which may explain the failure of Zillii's tilapia to establish.
- Dam size matters: stocking with herbivorous tilapia was of little value in the largest dam, presumably because its feeding is restricted to the dam periphery, which becomes proportionally less important as dam size increases.

**Habitat and reproduction:** The papyrus-fringed shoreline and seasonal swamp inflow provide the habitat for the herbivores. Spawning activity by [[Tilapia Esculenta]] peaks around December, coinciding with the rainy season. This pattern is similar to that reported for southern [[Lake Victoria]].

## Key Findings

### 1. Species composition
- The dam supports at least seven fish species. Most are cichlids, and the largest share of the catch is [[Tilapia Esculenta]].
- Cichlid and non-cichlid contributions to the catch differ only marginally, but the endemic and economically important [[Tilapia Esculenta]] dominates the planktivore niche.
- Despite intensive efforts, [[Tilapia Zillii]] could not be established, probably because of competition with the herbivorous [[Tilapia Rendalli]] introduced earlier.

### 2. Growth and size
- Malya *T. esculenta* grow more slowly than those in [[Lake Victoria]]. Growth data suggest individuals reach about 9–10 cm in their first year, with a larger maximum size.
- Length frequency distributions for each mesh size and month show that the 3-in mesh catches the larger fish while the 2-in mesh catches juveniles.
- The mean length in 2-in nets increased from about 12.05 cm in March to about 13.85 cm later in the season, reflecting recruitment into the catch.

### 3. Length-weight relationship
- A double-logarithmic length-weight relationship was derived for *T. esculenta* in Malya Dam, with an intercept of −5.055 and a slope of 3.37. The equation is reported as "weight = −5.055 + 3.37 log length" in the original text, a form that should be checked against the source before reuse.

### 4. Maturity
- The minimum length at maturity for *T. esculenta* in Malya Dam is 19.0 cm.
- Comparisons from other sites: Smith Sound (southern Lake Victoria) 20.5 cm (Garrod 1959); open-water Tanzanian waters 23 cm (Lowe-McConnell 1956); Jinja area 18.0 cm (Garrod 1959); off the Mwanza islands 26 cm (Lowe-McConnell 1956); Malya fishponds 13.5 cm.
- The Malya value is within the Lake Victoria range but at its lower end and clearly below the nearest lake sites.

### 5. Spawning
- A spawning peak occurs around December in Malya Dam. In a March sample, no *T. esculenta* were in breeding condition.
- Catch per unit effort rises from March to July, apparently because of recruitment of juveniles into the fishery and the increased availability of fish to the nets.

### 6. Fishery management implications
- Fishing with 2-in mesh nets takes large numbers of young fish, which the author regards as an overexploitation risk. The fishermen's use of 3-in nets was judged a more appropriate minimum.
- The lower-than-lake minimum length at maturity may reflect heavy exploitation of the population, a hypothesis the author considers plausible.
- The dam's relative isolation means it has no migration route to replenish stocks; fish cannot escape the nets by migrating to inaccessible areas, as they may in Lake Victoria.

### 7. Stocking policy
- Stocking a non-native tilapia species is not a reliable policy. The author recommends that stocking with native tilapia species be considered, since stocking with exotic tilapia may produce unforeseen effects.
- Multiple stocking of tilapia species did not appear to be of much benefit, and more attention should be paid to the biology of non-cichlid fish in such systems.

### 8. Conclusions
- The biological pattern of *T. esculenta* in Malya Dam is broadly similar to that in Lake Victoria, with the main differences being slower growth and an earlier attainment of maturity at a smaller size.
