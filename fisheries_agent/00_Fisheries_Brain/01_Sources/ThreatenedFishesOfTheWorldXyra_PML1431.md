# Threatened Fishes of the World: Razorback Sucker, Gila Spikedace, and Bonytail

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compilation of *Environmental Biology of Fishes* "Threatened fishes of the world" accounts (1996, with a 2004 entry) covers three endangered North American freshwater fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]] and [[Gila River Basin]]: the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), the [[Gila Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), and the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). A fourth fragment, on fish nematode parasites, is also included. Each species account covers identification, distribution, habitat, reproduction, threats and conservation recommendations. All three species have declined sharply, and their remaining populations depend on habitat protection, non-native fish control and hatchery-based augmentation.

## Background & Description

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Catostomidae).** Also called humpback sucker, this endangered (U.S.A.) sucker is recognized by a sharp-edged, elevated back ridge behind the head. The page 1 text describes its olive to brownish coloration with a lateral band, and the figure caption credits a photograph by Paul C. Marsh. The page 2 text is largely a discussion of madtom (*Noturus*) defensive coloration and venom glands, drawing on works by Barlow, Endler and Thayer. It is part of the same issue, and the page 2 text and its reference list are partly illegible after OCR.

**Gila Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*, Cyprinidae).** This small, slender minnow reaches a maximum of about 75 mm TL. It belongs to the tribe Plagopterini (spiny-rayed minnows), with two spinose rays at the leading edge of the dorsal fin. Its scales are small and deeply embedded. Breeding males are brassy, especially on the head and fin bases. It is federally listed as threatened (U.S.A.).

**Bonytail (*Gila elegans*, Cyprinidae).** The bonytail is a moderate-sized, unique cyprinid of western North America. Its name was applied for a time to almost any *Gila*, which obscured its ecology. It is federally endangered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and range contraction.**
- *Razorback Sucker:* Historically widespread throughout the large Colorado River basin. Populations are now much reduced.
- *Gila Spikedace:* Historically found in large to moderate streams of the Gila River basin, including the Gila, Salt, Verde, Aravaipa, San Pedro and San Francisco systems, and possibly Sonora, Mexico. Its present range is limited to Aravaipa and Eagle creeks and the upper Verde River in Arizona, and the Gila River and its forks in New Mexico. Its monotypic genus is endemic to the region.
- *Bonytail:* Endemic to larger Colorado River basin streams of Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, Nevada and adjacent states, and northern Mexico. Populations are now too small for reliable assessment, and recent captures are scattered (e.g., Lake Havasu, Yampa and Green rivers).

**Habitat use.**
- *Gila Spikedace:* Lives in flowing water less than 1 m deep. Adults use shear zones along sand-gravel bars, eddies below riffles and shallow areas above bars. Young occupy shallow pool margins over fine sediments. Habitat use varies with season, life stage and geography.
- *Bonytail:* Adults occupy pools and eddies rather than turbulent areas. Young are presumed to use quiet, shallow habitats. In impoundments the species is limnetic.

**Feeding.**
- *Gila Spikedace:* An opportunistic, drift-feeding carnivore that eats mainly aquatic insects.
- *Bonytail:* A surface and drift-feeding omnivore that eats terrestrial and aquatic insects, plant debris and algae. It becomes zooplanktivorous in reservoirs.

**Predators and competitors.**
- Natural predators of the Gila Spikedace included Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, now extirpated from its range) and roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*). Non-native cyprinids, ictalurids, poeciliids and centrarchids now prey on spikedace of all sizes.
- Bonytail predators historically included Colorado squawfish and aboriginal humans. Its larvae and juveniles are now preyed upon by a suite of non-native fishes.

**Threats.** Across the three species, the main threats are:
- Habitat destruction and alteration, including dewatering, dams, altered flow regimes and channel management.
- Interactions with non-native fishes, notably [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) for spikedace.
- Hybridization, notably between bonytail and humpback chub (*Gila cypha*) and roundtail chub.
- Continuing water development.

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker**
- Spawns in rivers during spring. Eggs hatch in about 5–8 days, depending on temperature.
- Hatchery propagation can produce substantial numbers of young. The source text suggests that populations are unlikely to persist without supplementation.
- Recovery priorities include protecting existing populations, restoring historic habitats, and improving captive holding and propagation methods.

**Gila Spikedace**
- Spawning appears to be cued by discharge and temperature in spring to summer. Females are usually accompanied by two males and spawn in riffles.
- Fertilized eggs are adhesive and demersal. Fecundity is 90–250 ova, correlated with body size and age. Ova are about 1.5 mm in diameter.
- Growth is 40–48 mm TL in the first year and 59–74 mm in the second. Typical longevity is 1–2 years, occasionally 3 and rarely 4.
- The tribe Plagopterini contains 7 taxa in 3 genera, 2 of which are monotypic. One taxon is recently extinct, and the rest are federally endangered, threatened or candidates for listing.
- The USFWS (1991) recovery plan calls for protecting populations, designating critical habitat, determining the effects of non-native fishes, quantifying habitat requirements, and restoring habitat with monitoring.

**Bonytail**
- Spawns at about 18 °C in June–July in the upper Colorado River (Vanicek & Kramer 1969). In Lake Mohave, an aggregation of about 500 fish was observed in May. Typically one female is accompanied by several males, and eggs are broadcast over gravel at depths to 9 m (Minckley 1973).
- Eggs hatch in 5–11 days, depending on temperature. Recruitment to wild populations has been undocumented for several decades, possibly partly because of identification difficulties with young fish.
- The species is readily propagated in hatcheries, where it is often absent from non-native fish communities. Hatchery-based augmentation of wild populations is the central conservation approach.
- The authors conclude that bonytail "soon will be extinct in the wild" unless measures are taken. These include obtaining adequate wild stock, an aggressive augmentation or reintroduction program, evaluating recruitment failure, and documenting its life history and habitat needs.

**Madtom Defensive Coloration (page 2 fragment)**
- Some madtoms are nocturnal and hide under rocks during the day when visually oriented predators are active. Madtoms also have venom glands at the base of their spines, which provides additional protection.
- Some madtom species show bold color patterns. The authors state that the color patterns and degree of patterning deserve further study.

**Fish Nematode Parasites (page 4 fragment)**
- The text recommends mimicking natural conditions experimentally when studying nematode behaviour in ectothermic fishes, because the life cycles of many fish nematodes are tied to seasons.
- The reference list draws on general nematode and fish parasite works (e.g., Hoffman 1967; Moravec 1994; Anderson 1992).
