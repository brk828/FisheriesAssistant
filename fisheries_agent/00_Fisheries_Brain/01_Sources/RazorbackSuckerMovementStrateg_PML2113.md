# Razorback Sucker Movement Strategies across a River-Reservoir Habitat Complex

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tracked adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a federally endangered catostomid, at the San Juan River-[[Lake Powell]] inflow area in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]] from April 2018 to March 2019. Using [[Passive Acoustic Telemetry]] receivers and a [[PIT Tag]] antenna below the [[Piute Farms Waterfall]], the authors examined seasonal movement, home-range size, environmental correlates and depth use of 69 analysed fish. Fifty-eight percent of fish moved from the reservoir upstream to the waterfall, while 42% remained within the reservoir. Movement and home-range size varied seasonally, and monthly activity tracked river temperature rather than discharge, which was unusually low and constant during the study.

## Background & Description

Razorback Sucker are federally listed as endangered and experience a recruitment bottleneck across the Colorado River basin. The basin is among the most altered river systems in the world, with dams, diversions and geomorphic barriers fragmenting habitat and modifying flow, temperature and turbidity regimes. Competing hypotheses for the bottleneck include altered flow and temperature, habitat loss and degradation, nonnative species and food limitation. Apart from [[Lake Mead]], all Razorback Sucker populations depend on intensive stocking; nearly 624,000 individuals were stocked in the upper basin from 2001 to 2019.

The species uses main-stem rivers, tributaries, off-channel wetlands and reservoirs. Adults aggregate at reservoir river inflows, possibly to access turbid, productive water and submerged vegetation. Such aggregations at Lake Powell resemble those at Lake Mead, the only place where wild-spawned recruitment has consistently occurred.

The Piute Farms Waterfall, about 15 m tall, blocks upstream movement into the San Juan River. It formed through sediment deposition and channel incision as reservoir levels fell, and has been a barrier since 2001. Prior studies had not clarified how often fish move between reservoir and river, how much time they spend in each habitat, or what habitats they use at inflows.

Study objectives were to quantify fine-scale movement and vertical distribution using passive telemetry, and to test three questions: (1) whether activity and home-range size change seasonally; (2) whether activity correlates with river temperature and discharge; and (3) whether fish use different water depths along the river-reservoir gradient.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** Lake Powell (second-largest US reservoir, over 33 billion m³, surface area 65,300 ha) was formed after [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed the Colorado River in 1963. The San Juan River joins about 30 km downstream of the study area. The inflow area spans riverine, transition and lacustrine zones, which shift with reservoir level.
- **Sites:** The Great Bend (canyon-bound, sand and silt substrates) and Neskahi Bay (north shore boulder-steep, south shore coves with fine gravel) were the main reservoir sites.
- **Hydrology:** San Juan River discharge was low and constant, with little snowmelt runoff and no clear ascending limb. Reservoir elevation declined through the study, shifting zones downstream.
- **Temperature:** River temperature was consistent with long-term seasonal patterns. Mean monthly temperatures peaked at 26.2 °C in summer.
- **Receiver array:** About 18 km of acoustic receivers (SUR-3, Sonotronics) were placed in a gated design roughly every 2 km. Assumed maximum detection range was 500 m, conservative relative to a field-estimated 700–900 m.
- **Detection efficiency:** Generally high (0.78–0.99), higher in Neskahi Bay (0.93 ± 0.06) than in the Great Bend (0.82 ± 0.05).

## Key Findings

**Sample and tagging**
- Of 133 fish analysed in the table summaries, 69 fish were retained for analysis after excluding 24 fish detected at only one receiver location.
- 86 fish were captured below the waterfall by raft electrofishing; 47 were captured in Neskahi Bay. Of the waterfall-tagged fish, 29 were released downstream and 57 translocated upstream.
- 111 fish received acoustic transmitters; 22 received depth transmitters. Tags were under 7% of body weight. Surgical recovery was rapid, and all fish survived release.

**Movement and residency**
- 58% of fish (overall) were detected at the waterfall at some point; 42% (29 fish) remained within the reservoir inflow area for the entire year.
- Fish detected in the reservoir moved as much as 326 km, yet kept home ranges under 18 km (linear).
- Over half of fish in the study moved between river and reservoir in different seasons, indicating intraspecific variation in movement strategy.
- Detections at the waterfall occurred in all seasons except winter, showing the species is not only entering riverine habitat during spawning.

**Seasonal patterns**
- Total distance moved differed among seasons (P < 0.001). Median distance was 94 km in spring, 50 km in summer, 2 km in autumn and 4 km in winter.
- Home-range size was about six times larger in spring and summer than in autumn and winter.
- Fish spent most time toward the upstream end of Neskahi Bay in all seasons.

**Environmental correlates**
- Monthly activity increased significantly with mean monthly river temperature (LR = 16.9, P < 0.001).
- Discharge had no detectable relationship with activity (P = 0.276).
- The authors suggest temperature alone may drive movement, though this inference is limited by a single year of unusually low flow.

**Depth use**
- Average depth was 1.4 ± 1.3 m, with a maximum of 5.7 m. Fish generally remained in shallow water (< 2 m).
- Fish moved toward shallower water near the inflow in all seasons (P < 0.01) and were deeper in summer and autumn than in spring (P < 0.001).
- The season × river-kilometre interaction was not significant (P = 0.092).

**Management implications**
- The authors hypothesise that reservoirs provide warmer temperatures for longer periods than regulated rivers, which may benefit warmwater fishes.
- The study concludes that facilitating fish passage at sites such as the Piute Farms Waterfall could give fish access to important habitat and support long-term population stability.
- Inclusion of reservoir inflow habitats in management plans is recommended.
- Limitations include a single year of data, lack of receivers between the inflow and the waterfall, and study restricted to adults. Activity and home-range estimates are therefore considered conservative.
