## Executive Summary

This document summarizes the outcomes of the **Biology Subcommittee** meeting for the **[[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCRMSCP)**, held on August 9, 1996, in Phoenix, AZ. The meeting convened 42 biologists and stakeholders to identify and prioritize **Interim Conservation Measures (ICMs)** for endangered species in the Lower Colorado River Basin while a comprehensive long-term program was being developed. Fish species were identified as top-tier priorities, specifically the **[[Bonytail Chub]]**, **[[Razorback Sucker]]**, and **[[Desert Pupfish]]**. The core of the fish-focused effort centers on the **Native Fish Work Group's** ongoing propagation and genetic rescue program at **Lake Mohave**, aiming to prevent extinction via captive rearing, larvae collection, and reservoir stocking. Budgetary constraints ($4.5 million total program budget, with only $200,000 initially committed to ICMs) were a significant point of discussion, alongside criteria for prioritizing species based on extinction risk and feasibility of short-term intervention.

## Background & Description

The **LCRMSCP** was established to address the conservation needs of multiple threatened and endangered species along the Lower Colorado River, balancing water management operations with species preservation. The Biology Subcommittee was tasked with identifying "Critical Biological Needs," prioritizing them, and developing actionable options.

Three fish species were flagged as top priorities:
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) — a critically endangered cyprinid.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — an endangered catostomid with an aging, senescent wild population.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*) — a small-bodied cyprinodontid restricted to isolated desert habitats.

These fish were considered alongside four bird species (southwestern willow flycatcher, yellow-billed cuckoo, California black rail, Yuma clapper rail) and one amphibian (relict leopard frog), reflecting a broader ecosystem-based conservation approach rather than a single-species focus.

The **Native Fish Work Group**, formed in 1990, has been the primary operational body driving razorback sucker and bonytail chub recovery efforts in **[[Lake Mohave]]** and **[[Lake Havasu]]**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary Habitat**: Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu (Lower Colorado River reservoirs), with supplemental **ephemeral backwater ponds** used for rearing juvenile fish to sub-adult size.
- **Key Threat to Razorback Sucker**: The wild Lake Mohave population is aging without natural recruitment; without intervention, the genetically wild stock faces imminent extinction by senescence.
- **Key Threat to Bonytail Chub**: Wild populations in both lakes are of "unknown size" with very limited natural recruitment; the population is sustained almost entirely by hatchery stocking (e.g., Dexter National Fish Hatchery stock).
- **Habitat Interventions Identified**:
  - Restoration of wetlands and backwaters
  - Large-scale cottonwood/willow revegetation (supports riparian-dependent species, indirectly benefiting instream habitat)
  - Salt cedar (tamarisk) management
  - Flow restoration and flood-irrigation to mimic natural flood pulses
  - Off-mainstem ("off-site") rearing facilities (e.g., Boulder City Golf Course ponds, Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery (WBNFH), CRIT lands ponds)
- **Co-occurring Ecosystem Stressors**: River flow regulation, loss of riparian/mesquite bosque habitat to wildfire, and cowbird brood parasitism affecting avian species sharing the same riparian corridors as fish nursery habitats.

## Key Findings

### Prioritized Fish-Related Interim Conservation Measures (ICMs)
Ranked by subcommittee vote count:

1. **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Provide space and funding to rear small fish (**17 votes**)
2. **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Collect wild larvae in Lake Mohave (**12 votes**)
3. **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Genetic characterization of broodstock & augmentation (**12 votes**)
4. **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Harvest larvae in Mohave / monitor population status (**5 votes**)
5. **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Create refugia; develop rearing space (unranked/lower votes)
6. **[[Desert Pupfish]]**: Create refugia; assess use of **Salton Sea** and **Cienega de Santa Clara** as habitat (unranked/lower votes)

### Razorback Sucker Program Status (Lake Mohave) — as of July 1995
- **Objective**: Replace the aging wild population with a minimum of 50,000 adult fish by year 2000 (Lake Mohave); increase Lake Havasu population to 25,000 adults.
- Biannual population sampling ongoing since early 1980s.
- **Yuma Cove** berm fortified (1991); **Davis Cove** blocked (1992) to create protected rearing habitat.
- Stocking history of sub-adults (≥250mm) into Lake Mohave: 1992 (129 fish); 1993 (542 fish); 1994 (2,189 fish).
- **1995 swim-up fry collection**: 20,146 total — 10,800 to ephemeral ponds, 7,730 to WBNFH.
- Projected annual stocking capacity under existing regime: **2,500–5,000 sub-adults (250–300mm)** per year into Lake Mohave.
- **Unfunded need identified**: additional $60,000 to expand wild larvae collection from 20,000 to a 40,000+ goal; additional $100,000+ for rearing facility expansion (30 acres needed to produce 10,000 additional sub-adults).

### Bonytail Chub Program Status (Lake Mohave/Havasu) — as of July 1995
- **Objective**: Increase populations in both Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu to 25,000 individuals each by year 2005.
- Approximately **160,000 fingerlings (5–150mm)** stocked into Lake Mohave from 1981–1995 (e.g., 41,300 in 1981; 43,500 in 1990; 6,800 in 1992).
- Natural recruitment observed but considered minimal; most recruitment traced to captive-reared stock (Dexter National Fish Hatchery).
- **1996 commitment**: USFWS to stock **25,000 bonytail (250–300mm)** into Lake Mohave and **6,000 sub-adults** into Lake Havasu.

### Larval Rearing Pipeline for Razorback Sucker
- Larvae collected from Lake Mohave (Jan–April) → Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery (WBNFH) hatch house → outside raceways (grown to ~125mm) → ephemeral ponds (grown to 300mm) → released to Lake Mohave (Fall).
- For a target batch of 40,000 larvae: ~35,000 survive to 25mm+, ~20,000 reach 100mm at WBNFH, with a probable net production of **~10,000 fish at 300mm**.
- **1995 actual cohort** (20,000 larvae collected): probable net production of only **~4,500 fish at 260mm**, illustrating substantial attrition through the rearing pipeline.

### Funding & Governance Constraints
- Total LCRMSCP development budget: **$4.5 million**, prioritizing long-term plan development over immediate ICMs.
- **$200,000** committed off the top by the Steering Committee for first-year ICMs; only Reclamation's **$100,000** had been obligated at time of meeting (largely directed to razorback sucker/Native Fish Work Group efforts in Lake Mohave).
- Additional **$100,000** committed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) but not yet obligated, earmarked for augmented survey efforts (Lake Mohave, Lake Mead), off-site rearing, and CRIT lands rearing facility improvements.
- Prioritization criteria for species/actions included: immediacy of need, extinction/extirpation risk, feasibility within a 2-year window, sensitivity to river operations, leverage potential with existing programs, and availability of existing programs to receive Steering Committee funds.

### Related Non-Fish Priority Taxa (Contextual)
- **Southwestern Willow Flycatcher** — protect existing populations (17 votes); status determination (6 votes)
- **Relict Leopard Frog** — control of exotic species (9 votes); population status assessment (Virgin and Muddy River drainages)
- **Yellow-billed Cuckoo, California Black Rail, Yuma Clapper Rail** — habitat/status assessments and riparian revegetation (5 votes)
