# Pathways for Aquatic Biota Transfer Between Watersheds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-biota

## Executive Summary

This 1995 report by Herbert R. Ludwig Jr. and Jay A. Leitch (North Dakota Water Resources Research Institute, North Dakota State University) catalogs the ways living aquatic organisms can move between adjacent watersheds. The work was motivated by uncertainty over how isolated the [[Hudson Bay Watershed]] is from the [[Mississippi River Watershed]], and by opposition to the [[Garrison Diversion Unit]], an interbasin water transfer project. The authors identify 17 pathways, both natural and human-caused, and conclude that the annual probability of biota crossing watershed boundaries "approaches 1.0" because of the large number of events. They argue that focusing resources on a single pathway, such as treating GDU water, may do little to reduce overall transfer risk.

## Background & Description

**Watersheds and barriers.** Continental divides are described as biophysical barriers that limit aquatic species ranges, and neighboring watersheds often hold discrete aquatic ecosystems. Shared fish species are attributed to past connections during glaciation. The GDU would move water from the Missouri River (Mississippi watershed) into the Hudson Bay watershed in eastern North Dakota, ultimately crossing from the United States into Canada. Congress authorized the project in 1986 (Public Law 99-294), and Canadian concern centers on possible damage to commercial fisheries.

**Costs of control.** Filtration and disinfection of GDU water were estimated to cost up to $58 million to construct, plus $4.5 million in annual operating costs (Kopchynski 1994).

**Definitions.** The report defines *introduced* (moved by humans), *exotic* (native range entirely outside the country), *non-indigenous* (beyond natural range, including feral species and most hybrids), and *bait bucket transfer* (release of biota into a watershed where it was previously absent through sportfishing).

**Pathway classification.** The 17 pathways fall into two groups:
- **Biophysical (natural):** high-water connections, animal transport (birds, terrestrial animals, insects), extreme meteorological events, and unknown mechanisms.
- **Anthropogenic:** intentional introductions (authorized stocking for sport, forage, biocontrol, endangered species reintroduction; unauthorized releases by hobbyists, aquaculturists and anglers) and accidental introductions (angler releases, watercraft and live wells, road dust-control spray, water projects, aquaculture escapes).

Related concepts include [[Anthropogenic Transfer Mechanisms]], [[Biophysical Barriers]], [[Non-Indigenous Species]], [[Interbasin Water Transfer]] and [[Bait Bucket Transfer]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biophysical barriers.** Land is the greatest barrier between neighboring watersheds, since aquatic species rarely survive overland. Climate limits tropical species (for example, an African tilapia would be unlikely to survive a North Dakota winter). Oceans, waterfalls and shallow waters also block dispersal, and lack of "vacant niche" can prevent establishment.

**Glacial history and high water.** About 9,000 years ago, parts of the present Hudson Bay watershed drained south from [[Glacial Lake Agassiz]] into the Gulf of Mexico. The boundary is flat and poorly defined, in places a "non-contributing" zone 10 to nearly 50 miles wide. In 1938 and 1939, two walleye tagged in 1937 in Cutfoot Sioux Lake (Mississippi watershed) were recaptured in Bowstring Lake (Hudson Bay watershed), described as the only proof of inter-basin travel through flooded marshes. Control structures usually keep [[Lake Traverse]] and [[Big Stone Lake]] separated, but after a roughly 10-inch rainfall near Sisseton, South Dakota, water flowed through the Browns Valley box culvert on July 25, 1993, reversed on August 6, and stopped by August 13.

**Birds and animals.** The boundary lies within the Mississippi and Central [[Waterfowl Flyways]], where millions of waterfowl move among lakes and potholes. Birds may carry algae, crustaceans, snails and protozoans externally, and fish-eating birds (cormorants, pelicans, gulls) have been hypothesized to transfer fish, though this is unsubstantiated. Digestive passage has been documented for seeds, invertebrate cysts (brine shrimp in mallards) and slime mold propagules. Beaver, muskrat, crayfish and raccoons may carry small organisms, and dragonflies and other insects may disperse algae and ciliates over long distances.

**Extreme weather.** Historical accounts of "rains of fishes" document fish lifted by waterspouts and tornadoes. Gudger (1921) reviewed 44 accounts, and Bajkov (1949) described seven species falling in Marksville, Louisiana. Some fish were found alive.

**Aquaculture and bait.** Aquaculture has been linked to the greatest share of introductions nationally, and nearly all exotic aquaculture species have escaped into open waters. The authors judge aquaculture transfer risk across watersheds in the study region to be low because most cultured species already occur in both basins, with tilapia confined to warm power-plant effluent.

## Key Findings

**Scale of the problem**
- 17 transfer pathways identified; the authors judge the annual probability of cross-watershed transport to approach 1.0 because of the number of events.
- The authors state that every pathway has some potential to disperse aquatic species, and that single-pathway controls may be ineffective.
- About 20 percent (151 of 743) of native fishes of Canada and the continental United States have expanded their ranges through human activity (Courtenay and Taylor 1986).

**Bait bucket transfer (strongest anthropogenic pathway)**
- Baitfish regulations exist in 54 of 57 United States states and Canadian provinces and territories; enforcement varies.
- Litvak and Mandrak (1993) found 18 of 28 fish species in Toronto bait dealers' tanks could potentially be released outside their native ranges; 41 percent of anglers interviewed said they released unused baitfish.
- Ludwig (1995) estimated the annual probability of bait bucket transfer between the Mississippi and Hudson Bay watersheds in North Dakota and Minnesota to be close to one. The probability of carrying species of concern was not investigated.
- North Dakota anglers travel an average of 33 miles to an angling destination (Baltezore and Leitch 1992).
- Restocking cycles are linked to baitfish release: white sucker is a legal bait only in the Missouri and Red River systems but repeatedly reappears in restored waters.

**Intentional introductions**
- Sportfish introductions are the most common type. Between 25 and 50 percent of freshwater fish caught by anglers in the continental United States come from introduced populations (Moyle et al. 1986).
- Anglers spent about $24 billion in 1991.
- Of 39 established exotic species, only three are considered beneficial: brown trout, bairdiella and orangemouth corvina (Welcomme 1984, 1981).
- Of 70 exotic fish established in the United States, about 46 escaped aquarium culture or were introduced by hobbyists.
- Biological control has caused harm: grass carp (long-term establishment viewed negatively) and [[Mosquitofish]] (established to the detriment of native species).

**Accidental introductions**
- Ballast water from transoceanic ships introduced [[Zebra Mussel]] into the Great Lakes, and also spiny water flea, [[Ruffe]], tubenose goby and round goby.
- Zebra mussels and Asian clams clog intake pipes; estimated plant redesign and maintenance costs exceed $6.2 billion (1991 dollars) over 10 years.
- Ruffe threaten Great Lakes fisheries; annual damage is estimated at about $7 billion.
- Water projects, road dust-control spraying, and boat and trailer movement can carry biota; Eurasian watermilfoil spread is attributed to itinerant boat trailers.

**Discussion and policy implications**
- The Mississippi and Hudson Bay watersheds are vulnerable to biophysical transfer because of the flat terrain between them.
- The GDU is one water-project pathway among many. Because it draws from a single point, the authors argue its risk may be overstated relative to bait buckets and aquaculture, which draw from many sources, though passage through GDU control structures is not zero.
- Pretreating GDU water may be an inefficient solution; resources and regulation should target the pathways with the highest probability of meaningful transfer.
- Information on bait species and non-target organisms (such as accompanying pathogens) is not available for the region. A comprehensive risk analysis on nine species of concern is recommended.

**Legal barriers**
- The [[Lacey Act]] (1900, strengthened by later amendments) prohibits importing illegally taken or transported wildlife, but enforcement is after damage occurs.
- Executive Order 11,987 (1977) restricting exotic introductions was not implemented.
- The [[Endangered Species Act]] (1974) limits import and sale of threatened and endangered species only.
- North Dakota prohibits white sucker use as bait in many waters and periodically chemically "restores" waters by killing all fish.
