# Perspectives on the Ichthyofauna of the Arid Zones of Northern Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontiformes

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Salvador Contreras-Balderas]] (Facultad de Ciencias Biológicas, Universidad de Nuevo León) reviews the endemic freshwater fishes of Mexico's arid north. It covers three biotic provinces: the [[Sonoran Province]], the [[Chihuahua-Potosinan Province]] and the [[Tamaulipan Province]]. The fauna includes five endemic genera and at least 58 primary and secondary endemic species. The author argues that industrial growth, irrigation, pollution, groundwater decline, and introduced species are driving losses, and he calls for conservation measures that allow rational resource use. The paper is a regional survey and advocacy document, not a sampling study with a formal design.

## Background & Description

The arid north of Mexico has three biotic provinces. The Sonoran Province covers most of Sonora. The Chihuahua-Potosinan Province lies between the Sierra Madre Oriental, the western Sierra Madre and the Eje Volcánico. The Tamaulipan Province covers Coahuila, Nuevo León and Tamaulipas, from the Sierra Madre Oriental to the coast. These regions have almost no surface water. Their basins are endorheic, cryptorheic, or rarely exorheic. Springs are often high in dissolved solids, and streams are intermittent or fragmented by drought and drain into senescent lagoons.

Desert waters hold isolated, relictual endemic fishes, many of which are rare, restricted to a single spring, and precarious. Ecologically the species fall into primary and secondary groups, meaning intolerant or poorly tolerant of salinity respectively. Their biogeography can be correlated with the hydrographic history of the basins, as was done for the [[Colorado River]] region (Hubbs and Miller, 1948).

The knowledge base comes mainly from [[Robert R. Miller]] (University of Michigan) and [[Carl L. Hubbs]] (Scripps Institution of Oceanography), who included the Mexican border region in their work. The author's own provinces have been under observation since 1960.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Endemism and scale.** Five endemic genera are known: *Stypodon*, *Characodon*, *Xenoophorus*, *Ataeniobius* and *Cualac*. Seven of the 58 endemic species belong to these genera. The remainder belong to more widely distributed genera. Together they represent roughly one-third of the known ichthyofauna of these regions. The author notes that endemism is lower than in the Colorado River region but still significant, especially in the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Valley]]. Diversification depends on how long a basin has been invaded or isolated. The fragmented [[Río Bravo]] basin is an example.

**Threats.** The paper lists destruction of terrestrial vegetation, irrigation works, mining, industrial pollution, falling water tables, introduction of exotic species, chemical treatment of waters to remove fauna, and natural climate change. Miller (1961) had treated all these causes earlier. The author adds that chemical poisoning is absent in Mexico, unlike the United States.

**Status.** Several endemics are extinct or seriously threatened in the United States, but their condition is less critical in Mexico. Fourteen areas are singled out as especially important, and the Tamaulipan Province has fewer threatened endemics, though the area around [[Monterrey]] is significant.

## Key Findings

**Colorado River (Sonora, Baja California, USA).** The ichthyofauna is primitive and highly endemic. Thirteen species have nearly disappeared, and most no longer live in the Mexican portion. These include *Gila robusta*, *Ptychocheilus lucius*, *Plagopterus argentissimus*, *Agosia chrysogaster*, *Tiaroga cobitis*, *Meda fulgida*, *Catostomus latipinnis*, *C. insignis*, *C. clarki*, *Xyrauchen texanus*, *Cyprinodon macularius* and *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*.

**Río Concepción (Sonora).** A poorly explored cryptorheic river. It has one endemic, *Gila ditaenia* (Miller, 1945), along with *Agosia chrysogaster*, *Campostoma ornatum* and *Catostomus insignis*.

**Río Sonora (Sonora).** Shares *Gila purpurea* with the Yaqui River. *Catostomus wigginsi* is exclusive to this river (Miller, 1958).

**Río Sauz (Chihuahua).** A small senescent endorheic basin. Reported extinct by Hubbs and Springer (1957), its fauna was rediscovered by Minckley and Koehn (1965). Differentiation is slight, with one endemic subspecies of *Notropis lutrensis* under study. Its fish populations are precarious because of climate change and irrigation.

**Laguna de Bustillos (Chihuahua).** An endorheic basin with *Gila nigrescens* (collected by Meek in 1904), plus *Pimephales promelas* and *Cyprinodon eximius*. A cellulose plant pollutes the lagoon. In more than 40 hauls in 1964 only one salamander (*Ambystoma*) was captured. Largemouth Bass and Bluegill were introduced to some springs where native fishes were no longer found, though the cause is unknown.

**Jiménez-Parral (Chihuahua).** Springs host *Gambusia gaigei*, nearly extinct in the Big Bend region of Texas but temporarily safe there (Hubbs and Broderick, 1963).

**Laguna de Santiaguillo (Durango).** Holds an unnamed *Gila*, a *Cyprinodon* slightly different from northern congeners, and *Chirostoma jordani* toward the south. The water is disappearing due to climate change and irrigation.

**Cuatro Ciénegas (Coahuila).** An intermontane valley and the author's principal center of speciation. Its many spring pools, called pozas, are cold or thermal, and it is rich in endemic fishes and mollusks. Described endemics include *Lucania interioris*, *Xiphophorus gordoni*, *Gambusia longispinis* and *G. marshi*. Undescribed endemics include *Dionda episcopa*, *Notropis*, *Cyprinodon* and *Cichlasoma* species. The author calls this endemism unique in North America. Pools are being drained or connected by canals, and exotic *Cichlasoma cyanoguttatus* has been introduced. The paper urges protection, a scientific station, and multiple-use water management.

**Parras (Coahuila).** A small endorheic satellite of the Nazas basin. Its endemics include the monotypic *Stypodon signifer*, *Dionda episcopa punctifer*, *Cyprinodon latifasciatus* and *Characodon lateralis*. Only *Gila* sp. survives. The others are considered extinct (Miller, 1961, 1964). Irrigation has altered the basin, and Common Carp, Mosquitofish-like *Poecilia reticulata* and probably Goldfish have been introduced.

**Saltillo (Coahuila).** Springs around the city belong to the San Juan River basin and are fragmented by climate change and heavy water use. One is the type locality of *Dionda episcopa punctifer*, and its validity is being checked.

**Ex Chorro de Arteaga (Coahuila).** A spring-fed stream system of about 5 km in a national park with partial protection. Its only native fish, *Gila modesta*, is a relict and the southeasternmost member of its genus. The population is about 3,000 to 4,000 individuals. Park works altered the habitat and introduced Goldfish. The author urges eradication of the carp to protect the native species. The true type locality was rediscovered in 1963.

**Ex Salado (San Luis Potosí–Zacatecas).** A flat, karstic, endorheic region with few permanent streams. *Xenoophorus exsul* is known near Venado and Moctezuma. Its survival is considered very uncertain given scarce water and restricted habitat.

**Río Verde (San Luis Potosí).** A Pánuco basin section with limestone springs similar to Cuatro Ciénegas. It holds *Cualac tessellatus*, *Ataeniobius toweri* and *Xenoophorus captivus*, some considered rare or threatened, and others such as *X. erro* and *Goodea gracilis* that are poorly studied.

**Monterrey (Nuevo León).** Springs within 30 km at La Huasteca, Apodaca and La Pastora held populations of *Xiphophorus couchianus* ("Monterrey Platy"). The Huasteca type locality dried up in 1963 due to urban water works and drought, and the population was lost. The author proposes reintroduction using stock from the American Museum of Natural History or the New York Aquarium. La Pastora's spring and 1 km stream are the main refuge but face irrigation pressure. At Apodaca, a spring and 200 m stream hold a small population. A company's planned discharge was blocked by the municipal president.

**Overall outlook.** Several species are extinct and others threatened. Not all endemics can be saved, but protection is possible if conservation is built into water use. The author recommends a national or state park and a research station for the Cuatro Ciénegas valley, with rational water use to support the poor regional economy.
