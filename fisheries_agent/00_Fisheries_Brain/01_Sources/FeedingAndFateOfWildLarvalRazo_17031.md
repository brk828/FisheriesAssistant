# Feeding and Fate of Wild Larval Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study in *Environmental Biology of Fishes* by Paul C. Marsh and Daniel R. Langhorst tests whether food limitation explains the disappearance of wild larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. The reservoir's largest wild population has shown no recruitment since the 1950s, even though spawning is successful and larvae are seasonally abundant. The authors compared larval diets and zooplankton availability in the reservoir with those in an adjacent, isolated [[Backwater]] where larvae survived longer and grew larger. Food availability and primary dietary constituents were similar in both habitats, so the authors conclude that nutritional factors do not explain the loss of larvae. They identify predation by introduced fishes, particularly [[Green Sunfish]], as the likely significant cause of larval mortality.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system of western North America. It was once widely distributed and abundant, but its numbers and range are now greatly reduced. Larvae are defined here as fish from hatching (6.5 to 8.6 mm) until full complement of fin rays in all fins (about 35 mm). Specimens examined had active swimming ability, and most were early postlarvae. Jaws were formed and the mouth was open, so the larvae were presumed capable of feeding, although yolk had not been fully absorbed in the smallest individuals.

Before the study, the reservoir's wild larvae disappeared at about 12 mm total length (TL) by late spring. The authors listed three possible explanations: mortality, in-reservoir migration to unknown places, and transport out of the system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** Lake Mohave is a mainstem Colorado River reservoir impounded by [[Davis Dam]], immediately downstream of [[Hoover Dam]]. Sampling covered about 5 km of shoreline between [[Arizona Bay]], which holds a large seasonal aggregation of spawning adults and larvae, and [[Hammerhead Cove]]. The backwater, separated from the reservoir by a wave-formed gravel spit, had maximum depths of 1.7 to 3.7 m and surface areas of 0.85 to 2.10 ha. Water temperature paralleled the reservoir but averaged 1.1 °C cooler in winter and 1.8 °C warmer in summer. Shallow areas held dense stands of watermilfoil (*Myriophyllum spicatum*) and curly leaf pondweed (*Potamogeton crispus*).

**Experimental manipulation.** Before stocking, the backwater's non-native fishes were removed by [[Ichthyocide]] treatment in autumn 1984. Those species were [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Bluegill]]. In January–February 1985, 30 female and 150 male adult razorback suckers from Arizona Bay were stocked. They spawned successfully, and larvae were first captured on 7 March 1985.

**Zooplankton community.** The reservoir was dominated by [[Cladocera]] (*Bosmina* and *Daphnia* together made up nearly two-thirds of numbers), followed by [[Copepoda]] (about one-third), with rotifers of minor significance. Total density averaged 1,554 organisms m⁻³. The backwater had greater zooplankton species richness, with rotifers making up more than 50% of the water-column community, and copepods less abundant. Total density was 377 organisms m⁻³, about 25% of the reservoir value. The most abundant kinds were the same in both habitats.

**Methods.** Larvae were collected mainly at night with a spotlight and dipnet, then preserved in 5% buffered formalin. Zooplankton were sampled with duplicate Clarke-Bumpus tows (80 mesh) in the reservoir and duplicate 38-L grabs in the backwater. Food selection was assessed with the [[Linear Index of Food Selection]] (L = r − p), which ranges from −100 to +100. Positive values indicate preference, negative values avoidance, and values with |L| < 10 were treated as not different from random feeding.

## Key Findings

**Larval growth and survival**
- Reservoir larvae averaged 10.6 ± 0.3 (SE) mm TL (n = 410). Neither mean nor maximum length increased over the sampling period, and the largest reservoir fish was 12.2 mm. The authors judged that the largest specimens were at most a few weeks old, based on comparison with known-age, experimentally reared fish.
- Backwater larvae (n = 367) started at about 10 mm, grew to more than 16 mm by late April, and reached a maximum of 20.3 mm. This is nearly twice the size of the largest wild reservoir larvae.

**Diet and selection (reservoir)**
- Larvae strongly preferred [[Bosmina]], which made up 51.0% of the water-column zooplankton and 90.3% of larval diet, giving L = 39.3. Copepods were avoided (water column 30.7%, diet 1.2%, L ≈ −29.5; the printed value lacks a sign, but the text reports avoidance).
- Reservoir larvae selected zooplankters of 400 to 999 × 10⁻⁴ mm³ and avoided those of 100 to 399 × 10⁻⁴ mm³. This pattern closely followed their selection for Bosmina.
- Of 124 reservoir larvae examined, 83 (67%) had empty stomachs.

**Diet and selection (backwater)**
- Backwater larvae selected Bosmina (L = 28.5) and Daphnia (L = 4.9), showed no preference for copepods (L = 0.2), and strongly avoided rotifers (water column 57.9%, diet 10.7%, L = −47.2).
- Diet was more diverse than in the reservoir, including larger prey such as Chironomidae and Trichoptera larvae. The authors found no evidence that mean prey size increased with fish length.
- Of 75 backwater larvae examined, 28 (42%) had empty stomachs.

**Interpretation**
- Taxa and sizes most selected by backwater larvae were more abundant in the reservoir, but reservoir larvae showed neutral or negative selection for them. The authors suggest food density differences may partly explain this, but they found no behavioural, pigmentary, or habitat differences between the zooplankters of the two habitats that would account for it.
- The main conclusion is that the type, number, and size of available foods do not account for total larval mortality in the reservoir. Papoulias (unpublished data) also found that larval survival, though not growth, was independent of productivity in experimental ponds at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

**Alternative explanations**
- *In-reservoir migration:* rejected. Extensive gillnet and other sampling since the early 1970s, which had taken thousands of juvenile-sized individuals of other species, never captured small razorback suckers.
- *Down-lake transport:* considered possible but unlikely to be the sole cause. Razorback populations downstream of Davis Dam and in Lake Havasu are very small, so transport would itself have to result in high mortality.
- *Mortality from predation:* supported as the most likely cause. The backwater was breached by high reservoir levels (196.8 m) and storm waves on 4 April 1985. Non-native fishes then invaded within days, and nearly 40% of green sunfish captured over 24 hours contained an average of four razorback larvae. No larvae could be collected by 5 May, when the backwater was isolated again, and the authors attribute the total loss of this stock to predation.

**Supporting evidence on predation**
- Predation on razorback eggs and larvae has also been recorded at Senator Wash Reservoir (California) and Lake Mohave. Predation by introduced catfishes on stocked juveniles was reported in the middle [[Gila River]].
- Brooks (unpublished data) found that predation losses of young suckers were directly related to green sunfish density in experimental ponds.

**Methodological limitation**
- Most earlier predation studies were not quantitative. Stomach contents are hard to identify after mastication or prolonged digestion. Serological methods such as [[Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay]] may help but are time-limited by rapid prey digestion, so close-interval sampling is needed.

**Management implications**
- Confirming predation as the cause of larval loss is considered critical for recovery. Reducing predator populations, for example by [[Ichthyocide]] reclamation of selected streams before stocking, is proposed as a test.
- Stocking at a size less vulnerable to predation may restore adults but will not ensure self-perpetuation if larval predation prevents recruitment.
- The authors judge large-scale reclamation largely infeasible on the mainstem. They suggest recovery is more likely in backwaters, oxbows, and smaller tributaries where repeated removal of non-native fishes is practical.
