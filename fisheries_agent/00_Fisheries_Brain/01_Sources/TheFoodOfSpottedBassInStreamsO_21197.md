# The Food of Spotted Bass in Streams of the Wabash River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study by Philip W. Smith and Lawrence M. Pace, published in the [[Transactions of the American Fisheries Society]], examines the diet of [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*) in tributaries of the middle [[Wabash River]]. The authors analyzed stomach and intestinal contents of 114 bass (23 to 320 mm total length) collected from March through November. Immature aquatic insects were the principal food across all sizes. Small bass also consumed plankton, while larger bass added crayfish and fish. The authors conclude that stream-dwelling spotted bass are more insectivorous and less piscivorous than conspecifics in impoundments.

## Background & Description

The study arose from a life-history investigation of the [[Dusky Darter]] (*Percina sciera*) conducted in 1967 and 1968. Spotted bass were the most abundant potential piscine predator in the study area, so specimens were preserved for dietary analysis. No evidence of predation on darters was found.

Prior work on spotted bass diet came mainly from reservoirs and lakes, including:
- Howland (1931), who reported crayfish and minnows from stream bottoms, working with [[Kentucky Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus pseudaplites*).
- Dendy (1946), who studied [[Norris Reservoir]], Tennessee.
- Rosebury (1950), who examined 62 bass from [[Clayton Lake]], Virginia.
- Cross (1967), who reported crayfish and insects as principal foods in Kansas streams.
- Applegate et al. (1967), who described diet in [[Bull Shoals Reservoir]], Arkansas.

**Sampling methods:** Fish were collected by minnow seine and electrofishing, preserved in formalin, and dissected. Recorded data included total and standard length, stomach fullness (full to empty), stomach and intestinal contents, and parasites. Bass were grouped as 23–75 mm, 76–152 mm, and 153–320 mm. Collections by month were: March 5, April 7, May 1, June 8, July 27, August 1, September 17, October 10, and November 28. No specimens were available for December through February.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Specimens came mainly from the middle [[Embarras River]] of east-central Illinois, with additional fish from the [[Little Embarras River]], the Middle Fork, the [[Little Wabash River]], and other Wabash tributaries in Indiana.
- **Seasonal feeding:** Feeding appeared greatest in summer. Fourteen of 27 July stomachs were half-full or fuller. In November, 25 of 28 stomachs were empty and 11 contained no identifiable food. Feeding appeared to decline as water cooled in late October. Young-of-the-year fish had visible fat deposits by early November.
- **Diel activity:** Only two paired collections (one night, one afternoon, in March and July) were compared. Six of 10 nocturnal and nine of 18 diurnal bass had empty or nearly empty tracts. The authors suspected spotted bass move into shallow water at night to feed.
- **Seasonal terrestrial prey:** Terrestrial arthropods appeared in summer and early fall, a period when they were otherwise unavailable.
- **Prey taxa in the broader community:** Common fishes in the middle Embarras included [[Dusky Darter]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. The second most abundant local fish, *Pimephales vigilax*, was absent from bass stomachs.

**Principal findings:**
- **Aquatic insects** were the principal food of bass of all sizes. Dipteran larvae and pupae (chironomids and simuliids) ranked first. Ephemeropteran naiads, including [[Baetidae]], [[Caenidae]], and [[Heptageniidae]], contributed significantly to bulk in mid-sized bass.
- **Plankton** (cladocerans and copepods) was eaten only by bass under 75 mm. Cladocerans occurred in 32.2% of these fish. One 50-mm bass contained 106 cladocerans. Copepods occurred in 23.5%. One 50-mm bass held 156 chironomid larvae.
- **Crayfish**, mainly *Orconectes virilis*, were absent from small bass. They occurred in 15% of subadults and 38.9% of adults, and made up 19.8% of total prey numbers in the largest group.
- **Fish** were rare in small bass (one 57-mm bass). They occurred in 40.0% of the 153–320 mm bass, making up 17.3% of their total food. Two instances of three prey fish were found in single stomachs, suggesting bass did not restrict feeding to small prey.
- **Terrestrial insects** were more common in larger bass, which consumed ants, wasps, grasshoppers, beetles, flies, and dragonflies.
- **Comparison with impoundments:** In Bull Shoals Reservoir, 56% of 50–75 mm bass contained fish, versus 5% in this stream study. Only 17.3% of the largest stream bass contained fish. Stream bass begin fish predation at a larger size.
- **Parasites:** Only one bass had external parasites (two leeches on the caudal fin). Seven bass had one to five intestinal nematodes, and five bass of 25–50 mm had one to seven young proteocephalid tapeworms.

**Conclusions:**
- Stream-dwelling spotted bass are "more strongly insectivorous, less piscivorous" than impoundment populations.
- Food intake peaks in summer and drops sharply with autumn cooling.
- Dietary shifts with size reflect a transition from plankton and insects to crayfish, fish, and terrestrial prey.
