# Lampetra spadicea (Anguila de Jacona): A Restricted Mexican Freshwater Lamprey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary

This article, published in the *Zoologia Informa* series on Mexican freshwater fishes (No. 26, November 1993), is a taxonomic, biological and conservation review of [[Lampetra Spadicea]], known as the *Anguila de Jacona*. Authors Oscar J. Polaco and Celia Guerra-Magaña resolve the uncertain provenance of the type specimen, confirm the type locality as Jacona, Michoacán, trace the species' nomenclatural history, and synthesize the biology of the species largely from the work of [[Alvarez Del Villar]] (1966). The species is one of only two lampreys known from Mexican freshwaters and the only ones in Mexico with such a restricted distribution. The authors argue that it has no economic value but high biological value as a relict of an ancient lineage, and that it is a strong candidate for extinction.

The issue also contains a *Triatominae* (Chagas vector) transmission study, a review of Mexican aquaculture policy from 1950 to 1980, and an article on authorship problems in scientific papers. The reference list in the excerpt belongs to the *Triatominae* paper.

## Background & Description

**Classification.** The family [[Petromyzontidae]] is of marine origin, with species in both marine and freshwater habitats. Lampreys are described as highly specialized parasitic vertebrates from an ancient lineage. They have a cartilaginous skeleton, no jaws or paired fins, and a single median nasal opening (the basis for the name "monorrhine"). Their skin lacks scales. The two Mexican freshwater species are *Lampetra spadicea* and *L. geminis*.

**Original description.** [[Tarleton H. Bean]] described the species in 1887 from a single adult, 187 mm long, catalogued as USNM 38005 at the United States National Museum. The specimen was sent by [[Alfredo Dugés]], a resident of Guanajuato. Bean did not record a collection locality. In 1892 he reported two larger specimens (260 and 267 mm) from Tanganzicuaro (USNM-43766). A Spanish translation of the original description, with notes and drawings by Dugés, was published in *La Naturaleza* that year.

**Type locality resolved.** The provenance of the holotype had been considered uncertain, with different authors citing the Río Lerma, Tanganzicuaro, or Guanajuato. The authors resolved this through three lines of evidence:
- The Dugés Museum at the University of Guanajuato holds a jar with a larva and an adult, labelled in Dugés's hand "Lampetra spadicea, Tarleton H. Bean, Anguila, Michoacán."
- Herrera's 1896 museum catalogue lists specimen number 3 as coming from Jacona, Michoacán.
- Dugés's own 1884 *Elementos de Zoología* originally stated that lampreys occurred at Jacona, near Zamora. Annotations in the author's personal copy cross out "pero no los tengo clasificados" and add "Lampetra spadicea, Bean, de Michoacán," a correction consistent with the species being described three years later.

The authors conclude that the type locality is **Jacona, Michoacán**.

**Nomenclature.** The species has been placed in several genera and subgenera:
- Regan (1911) placed it in *Entosphenus* as *spadiceus*.
- Creaser and Hubbs (1922) described the subgenus *Tetrapleurodon* with *L. spadicea* as its only member.
- Jordan et al. (1930) raised *Tetrapleurodon* to genus rank.
- Berg (1931) returned the species to *Lampetra* (subgenus *Lampetra* s. str.).
- Bailey (1980) argued that the species belongs in *Lampetra* but retained *Tetrapleurodon* as a subgenus to reflect its distinctive features. The authors follow this usage.

**Common name.** The species has been called anguila, anguila de Jacona, lamprea de Jacona o Chapala, lamprea de Chapala, and "Mexican lamprey." The authors propose **anguila de Jacona**, which combines the indigenous name with the original locality.

**Morphology.** Adults have an eel-like cylindrical body and a circular suctorial mouth lined with horny teeth. The oral disc has a supraoral lamina with two main cusps, four lateral teeth on each side (the first and last bicuspid, the middle ones bi- or tricuspid), and an infraoral lamina with nine alternately large and small teeth. The tongue is modified into a rasping organ. There are seven branchial openings on each side of the anterior body.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The species is restricted to the Río Duero, its tributaries, and Lake Chapala in the lower Río Lerma. Alvarez (1970) and Dugés (1924) reported it from Lake Cuitzeo (Michoacán) and from Guanajuato. The authors consider these records unsupported by documented specimens and exclude them from the range. A distribution summary is given in [[Lyons Et Al]] (in press), cited as "Distribution and Abundance of the Mexican Lampreys."

**Life cycle.** Lampreys remain in freshwater but migrate upstream during their life cycle.
- **Spawning and hatching:** Spawning occurs in the cold season (November to January) in the headwaters of the Ríos Celio and Duero. Fertilized eggs develop over about three weeks, and larvae reach roughly 1 cm.
- **Larval stage:** Larvae bury themselves in soft or slightly sandy substrate and only the anterior part of the body protrudes. When disturbed they swim rapidly to a new site. The larval stage lasts about three years, and larvae reach up to 150 mm before metamorphosis.
- **Metamorphosis and migration:** After metamorphosis, juveniles move to Lake Chapala, attach to fish with their sucking disc, and feed on them while growing. They reach sexual maturity two or more years later. Alvarez del Villar (1966) also recorded adults in rivers outside the breeding season, so some individuals may not migrate to the lake.
- **Reproduction:** Adults over 300 mm return upstream to their natal streams. After a prenuptial courtship that includes amplexus, males and females release gametes externally. Development is synchronous and the species is [[Semelparity|semelparous]]: a single spawning is followed by death.
- **Eggs:** Spherical, 0.8 to 0.95 mm in diameter, with smooth surfaces and no adhesive filaments.

**Feeding.** The postlarval (parasitic) phase is hematophagous. Specimens over 200 mm have intestines full of blood or blood residues, along with fish scales from catostomids, cyprinids and atherinids. The authors note that the dentition and rasping tongue suggest the parasitism may be closer to predation. Larvae are [[Filter Feeding|filter feeders]] with a planktonic diet, including diatoms (*Rhizolenia*, *Pleurosigma*, *Pinularia*, *Stauroneis*, *Amphora*, *Cocconema*), filamentous green algae, rotifers, ciliates, and occasional euglenoid flagellates.

**Habitat requirements.** Little is known about the ecology of the species. Larvae and short-lived adult phases occur in streams with slow current, clear cold water, and soft, low-gravel substrates covered with silt and clay. Postmetamorphic individuals live in Lake Chapala or in rivers of the lower Lerma. Because of its filter-feeding habits, the species apparently cannot tolerate polluted water. The reported water temperature range is 16.2 to 22.5 °C (Alvarez del Villar, 1966), and temperature does not appear to limit reproduction or larval development.

## Key Findings

- **Type locality confirmed:** The holotype of [[Lampetra Spadicea]] (USNM 38005, 187 mm) came from Jacona, Michoacán, based on museum labels, Herrera's catalogue, and Dugés's annotated personal copy of *Elementos de Zoología* (1884).
- **Taxonomy:** The authors recognize the species within *Lampetra* as subgenus *Tetrapleurodon*, following [[Bailey]] (1980). They propose the common name "anguila de Jacona."
- **Restricted range:** The species occurs only in the Río Duero system and Lake Chapala. Records from Lake Cuitzeo and Guanajuato are considered undocumented.
- **Semelparous life cycle:** Individuals spend about three years as filter-feeding larvae, then metamorphose, feed parasitically on fishes in [[Lake Chapala]] for two or more years, return to natal streams to spawn once, and die.
- **Shrinkage during metamorphosis:** Alvarez del Villar (1966) documented length reduction during the first year of larval life, which he attributed to rainy-season conditions. Greater water filtration is needed to find sparse plankton, and floods expose and displace larvae, forcing them to bury again. The authors cite [[Crotonei]] (1926) for the broader phenomenon.
- **Biological significance:** As the only freshwater lampreys of a very ancient lineage in Mexico, the species has high evolutionary value. The authors state that it has no economic importance.
- **Conservation status:** Pollution, channelization and loss of streams, the disappearance of fish of suitable size for prey, and the restricted range make the species a strong candidate for extinction. The authors identify basic biological research as the first step toward conservation, since little is known despite more than a century of records.
- **Other content in the issue:** A study of *Trypanosoma cruzi* transmission capacity in five *Triatominae* species (Nogueda-Torres, Ramírez-Moreno & Cortés-Jiménez), an overview of Mexican aquaculture policy 1950–1980 (Ramírez Granados), and a discussion of authorship problems in scientific articles (Guzmán del Próo). These are outside the scope of this lamprey note.
