# Drainage History of the Bonneville Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-molluscs

## Executive Summary

Taylor and Bright reconstruct the late Cenozoic drainage history of the [[Bonneville Basin]] from the distribution of living and fossil freshwater molluscs, with comparisons to [[Fish Biogeography]] studies. The paper argues that the basin was never a single faunistic unit, that its present drainage configuration was assembled through a sequence of Miocene to Quaternary stream captures, lava damming and volcanic diversions, and that the [[Bear River]] was only permanently diverted into the basin about 30,000 years ago. It also argues that post-Pleistocene aridity, which eliminated [[Lymnaea Bonnevillensis]], was more severe than any earlier episode.

## Background & Description

The authors base the analysis on the premise that living western freshwater molluscs date back to the Pliocene or Miocene in practically all species studied, so modern distributions can indicate past habitats and drainages. The paper focuses on perennial-water species, because seasonal-water species are shaped largely by passive overland transport and are therefore poor indicators of drainage.

Dispersal is classified into group dispersal (long-term spread with differentiation) and individual dispersal, which is either active (at a "snail's pace") or passive. Passive dispersal occurs within habitats (glochidia carried by fishes, transport on vegetation or wood, and drift in current) or overland (carriage by insects such as waterbeetles, Belostomatidae and Corixidae, and birds, although the authors find no credible evidence for bird-gut transport). Criteria for judging overland transport include geographic replacement, correlation with geologic features, restriction to part of a drainage, and absence from suitable habitat across narrow divides.

Key examples cited:
- *Physa gyrina*: Marsh (1980) recorded a maximum stream drift of 533,000 individuals/m²/sec in Minnesota, at an average density of 1417/m².
- *Corbicula* and *Dreissena*: introduced bivalves with a free larva that drifts for days, enabling rapid spread.
- *Lymnaea techella*: a desiccation-tolerant species living in temporary water bodies.
- *Petrophysa zionis*: restricted to rock faces in Zion Canyon, Utah.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional diversity.** The Bonneville Basin has 58 living native freshwater mollusc species, plus six known only as Pleistocene or Holocene fossils. Thirty of these 64 are restricted to perennial water and are useful for drainage history. Richness is governed by habitat diversity, climate, and endemism. Utah is richer than Arizona and Montana, Wyoming, or Colorado when normalized for area, but less rich than Idaho. Around eight local endemics occur, nearly all in the hydrobiid genus *Fontelicella*.

**Faunal structure.** The "Bonneville Basin" is not a faunistic unit. The Sevier Lake Basin has its own endemics, while the Great Salt Lake Basin is most closely related to the upper Snake River. Lake Bonneville had little effect on molluscan distribution, and its Pleistocene fauna is low in endemism and similar to that of the much smaller [[Bear Lake]]. Both contrast with the rich, endemic fauna of [[Lake Thatcher]], which contained 43 species.

**Geologic and climatic drivers.** The Snake River Plain, the eastern Snake River volcanic trend, and the migrating thermal anomaly that produced volcanic centres from southeastern Oregon to Yellowstone all shaped the drainage. The authors estimate a diversion of the Little Wood and Big Lost Rivers at about nine million years ago, based on a migration rate of 3.5 cm/yr.

**Holocene change.** Recession of Lake Bonneville concentrated salts and made the lake uninhabitable. Bear Lake's diverse fauna also went extinct after a Holocene environmental change, possibly linked to the river cutting into toxic rock units.

## Key Findings

- **Miocene:** The northwestern basin drained west to the Pacific system, while an eastern fauna containing *Valvata idahoensis* hints at external drainage toward the lower Colorado River. The Bonneville Basin had not yet reached its modern form by mid-Miocene.
- **Latest Miocene (Hemphillian):** Molluscan faunas are widespread and abundant, bracketed within about 10 million years, and show a sharp east–west faunal split. The authors infer erosion across southeastern Idaho during this interval.
- **Pliocene:** A large lake with a rich endemic fauna occupied Cache Valley, Idaho–Utah (Cache Valley Formation), with *Lake Idaho* in the Glenns Ferry Formation as a related faunal counterpart. Marsh Creek and Gentile Valleys were initially drained southward, then reversed northward at about the end of the Tertiary.
- **Bear River:** Assembled in the Quaternary from separate streams. Its upper segment, now contributing about 12% of basin inflow, was permanently diverted into the basin about 30,000 years ago and may have triggered the Lake Bonneville highstand and spillover into the Snake River about 16,000 years ago. The total Bear River contributes about 40% of present inflow.
- **Pleistocene dating:** The Huckleberry Ridge ash (2.01 million years) in the Thatcher basin shows that southward drainage was truncated before two million years ago.
- **Quaternary species distributions:** *Valvata utahensis*, *Amnicola limosa*, and *Lymnaea bonnevillensis* each show distributions that illustrate passive dispersal and drainage fragmentation. The utahensis–newberryi pattern, a biogeographic abstraction (an "operational taxonomic unit"), is estimated at about two million years old.
- **Holocene extinctions:** *Lymnaea bonnevillensis* went extinct after about 13,000 years ago, and the Bear Lake fauna after about 8,000 years B.P. Post-Pleistocene aridity is described as geologically unique.
- **Ichthyological comparison:** Fishes can occupy headwaters that molluscs cannot, and fish distributions mostly reflect habitat rather than drainage history. The authors criticize Smith (1981) for proposing a Miocene–Pliocene drainage of the Bonneville Basin into Hudson Bay, arguing that faunal similarities were wrongly assumed to reflect a through-flowing stream. They also criticize Minckley and others (1986) for misusing the biogeographic pattern concept.
- **Conclusion:** Molluscs are a reliable tool for reconstructing drainage history because their dispersal and habitat limits are well defined, and different organism groups yield different, complementary data.
