# Influence of Las Vegas Wash Density Current on Nutrient Availability and Phytoplankton Growth in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary
Baker and Paulson describe how the saline, nutrient-rich [[Las Vegas Wash]] inflow forms a [[Density Current]] in [[Las Vegas Bay]], [[Lake Mead]], and how this current controls nutrient availability and [[Phytoplankton]] growth. The current acts as an underflow in winter, an interflow along the thermocline in summer, and is partly mixed in spring. It works as a "natural diffuser" of nutrients, so the bay's trophic status is far lower than the very high phosphorus loading would suggest. Planned desalination and a bypass pipeline for the Wash would reduce the density difference, increase mixing, and could raise nutrient availability and produce nuisance algal conditions in the bay.

## Background & Description
- Density currents form in reservoirs because of temperature- or salinity-induced density differences. [[Anderson and Pritchard]] (1951) showed that the [[Colorado River]] formed an underflow in Lake Mead in winter, an overflow in spring and an interflow in summer and fall.
- Lake Mead was formed in 1935 by [[Hoover Dam]]. It occupies a 183 km reach of the Colorado River, has a maximum depth of 180 m, and holds 36x10^9 m³ (the largest US reservoir by volume). Its major reaches are Gregg, Temple, Virgin and [[Boulder Basin]]. [[Lake Powell]] was formed upstream in 1963.
- The lake lies in the arid Mohave Desert (annual precipitation under 12.7 cm). The Colorado River supplies 98% of inflow. The Muddy and Virgin Rivers enter the Overton Arm, and Las Vegas Wash enters Las Vegas Bay, an embayment of Boulder Basin.
- Two secondary sewage treatment plants about 17 km upstream supply over 95% of the Wash flow (2.26 m³/s). Ammonia is low for sewage effluent because of ammonia stripping in the Wash, a mechanism that is not completely understood. High nitrate (over 100 mg/L) and total dissolved solids (TDS; up to 20,000 mg/L) come from contaminated groundwater, which originates from salt mounds formed by industrial effluent discharged into unlined evaporation ponds until 1978.
- Wash water concentrations (Table 1; mg/L): mean total phosphorus 3.52 (range 2.53-5.98); phosphate range 1.29-3.08; nitrate range 2.43-6.57; ammonia range 0.21-6.73; TDS range 2120-3040 (USGS data).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Methods:** Stations were sampled about monthly over 3-4 days from October 1977 to September 1978. Water was collected with a 3-L van Dorn sampler at the surface and 1-7 other depths. The study measured ammonia, nitrate, phosphate and total phosphorus, plus chlorophyll-a (composite of surface, 3 and 5 m). Productivity was measured in situ with the [[Carbon-14 Method]] at 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10 and 15 m (3-6 hr incubations). A Hydrolab analyzer recorded temperature, oxygen, pH and conductivity. USGS data supplemented these.
- **Budget (Table 2):** Las Vegas Wash supplies less than 1% of inflow (7.23x10^6 m³/yr vs 1.077x10^10 m³/yr for the Colorado River) but a substantial share of nutrient loads. Total phosphorus is 263.1x10^3 kg/yr (Wash) vs 198.7x10^3 kg/yr (Colorado River), and hypolimnetic output at Hoover Dam is 123.1x10^3 kg/yr. Inorganic N:P is 82.8:1 for the Colorado River (phosphorus-poor) and 4.9:1 for the Wash (phosphorus-rich).
- The U.S. Water and Power Resources Service identified the Wash as a major salinity source to the lower Colorado River and plans to cut its salinity by about 50%.

## Key Findings
- **Seasonal current behavior:** Conductivity traced the current easily.
  - Winter (isothermal): cooler, higher-TDS wash water formed an underflow through the bay. In January-February it extended into Boulder Basin along the old stream channel.
  - Stratified period: the wash water was warmer than the hypolimnion and stayed in the metalimnion as an interflow along the thermocline.
  - October: the current followed the bottom of the inner bay, then flowed out along the thermocline at 22 m as a narrow interflow.
  - Spring: substantial mixing occurred (April conductivity tongues) because the wash warmed faster than the lake. High TDS still confined the current mainly to the bottom of the inner bay.
  - Summer: broad stratification gave a broad distribution, and inner-bay mixing was high (about 1200 µmhos).
- **Nutrients:** Phosphate was always high in the inner bay and declined seaward. It rose to 57 µg/L in the inner bay in summer, and winter mixing raised it in the middle-outer bay and Boulder Basin. Nitrate was stripped from the epilimnion in summer by phytoplankton uptake, and winter nitrate exceeded 200 µg/L after fall overturn.
- **Productivity:** Phytoplankton productivity and chlorophyll-a were highest in the inner bay and declined toward Boulder Basin.
- **Nitrogen limitation:** Growth in Las Vegas Bay was nitrogen-limited. N:P ratios were below the roughly 10N:1P needed, except in a few winter months, and bioassays (C.R. Goldman, unpublished) agreed. Excess phosphorus accumulated in the inner bay, producing a spurious correlation between phosphate and productivity or chlorophyll-a. Boulder Basin has become more nitrogen-limited than the less productive, phosphorus-limited upper basins (Virgin, Temple, Gregg).
- **Loading vs. trophic state:** Using the [[Vollenweider-Dillon Phosphorus Model]], [[Goldman]] (1976) calculated phosphorus loading in the bay at 34 times the "dangerous" rate. Yet the inner bay is only slightly eutrophic, and the rest of the bay and Boulder Basin are mesotrophic-oligotrophic. Nitrogen limitation, hypolimnetic discharge at Hoover Dam (which reduces nutrient retention) and especially the density current explain this. The underflow acts as a natural diffuser and prevents point-source problems.
- **Management implication:** The planned desalination would route effluent around the Wash by pipeline, which could warm the inflow by 2-6°C. At present the Wash cools by about 3°C over its length (spring 18.6-15.5°C; summer 25.4-22.0°C). With a 3°C rise and a 50% salinity reduction, surface flows would occur in spring, increasing nutrient availability. Nuisance algal conditions could develop, so desalination may harm water quality in Las Vegas Bay.
