# Conservation Genetics of Endangered Fish Populations in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 *Science* report by Vrijenhoek, Douglas, and Meffe uses protein electrophoresis to compare genetic diversity in remnant Arizona populations of the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] ([[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]]) with populations in Sonora, Mexico, where the species remains abundant. Arizona populations, at the northern periphery of the range, are substantially less variable. Genetic variation is partitioned mainly among three geographically distinct groups (about 53% of total diversity). The authors use these results to recommend which source stocks should be used for restocking. The document also contains two unrelated fragments: the tail end of an endocrinology article on atrial prohormone processing, and a neurophysiology paper on swimming in the pteropod mollusk *Clione limacina*. Neither concerns fish.

## Background & Description

- **Focal species:** *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (Pisces: Poeciliidae), a small, short-lived, viviparous fish. It was once the dominant fish in lowland streams, springs, and marshes of the [[Gila River]] system in Arizona.
- **Decline:** Over roughly 40 years, habitat destruction and introduced predators, particularly the nonnative mosquitofish [[Gambusia Affinis]], reduced large populations to small, isolated demes in tiny headwater springs and stream segments.
- **Legal status:** The species was placed on the federal Endangered Species List in 1973.
- **Sampling:** The study covered 5 Arizona and 16 Sonoran populations (21 total). Electrophoretic surveys screened 25 protein-determining gene loci, of which 12 were polymorphic.
- **Concept:** Habitat fragmentation can subdivide a species into partially isolated local populations (demes) that lose variation through inbreeding and [[Genetic Drift]], which may reduce fitness and limit future adaptive potential.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range gradient:** Genetic variability is highest near the center of the range, in the Río Matapé, lower Río Sonora, and Río Yaqui. It declines toward the northern periphery and in upstream reaches of most rivers.
- **Drift in peripheral populations:** Population fluctuations that drive drift are thought to be most severe in partially isolated peripheral populations. The authors note that low heterozygosity may reduce growth rate, developmental stability, survivorship, and competitive ability, though the immediate consequences in Arizona fish are unknown.
- **Three genetic groups:** A dendrogram based on Rogers' genetic distances, clustered by the Distance-Wagner method, separates three groups:
  - Groups 1 and 2 correspond to morphologically defined subspecies previously recognized in Arizona.
  - Group 3, the most divergent, occupies partially isolated headwater tributaries of the Río Mayo above a man-made impoundment.
- **Divergence times:** Estimated times for the group splits are about 1.7 Myr (groups 1–2), 2.8 Myr (groups 2–3), and 4.3 Myr (groups 1–3). These are consistent with Pliocene–early Pleistocene separation of the drainage basins. The authors state that a Nei distance of one unit was assumed to equal about 18 Myr.
- **Intergradation:** Groups 1 and 2 intergrade in the Río Matapé and lower Río Sonora, where populations carry alleles diagnostic of both groups. Flooding on the Sonoran coastal plain permits admixture, but it is less likely in montane reaches.
- **Barriers:** Arizona Gila tributaries are separated by impoundments and dry reaches. Some of these populations are the least genetically variable.
- **Downstream connection:** The downstream Río Mayo population (location T) groups more closely with coastal-plain Río Yaqui populations than with upstream populations in its own river.

## Key Findings

**Genetic variation partitioning** (hierarchical analysis; mean H_t = 0.107 across 25 loci):

| Component | Share of total diversity |
|---|---|
| Among the three geographic groups | 52.8% (abstract rounds to 53%) |
| Between colonies within rivers | 25.5% (abstract: 26%) |
| Within local colonies | 21.3% (abstract: 21%) |
| Between rivers within groups | 0.4% (negligible) |

**Population variability examples** (Table 1; P = % polymorphic loci, H = mean % heterozygous loci per individual):
- Sharp Spring (Arizona, location D): P = 62%, H = 3.7%. This is the most variable Arizona population.
- Monkey Spring (Arizona, location C): P = 23%, H = 0. Genetically invariant by the authors' criteria.
- San José de Pima, Sonora (location N): P = 60%, H = 11.6%. The highest heterozygosity in the table.
- Several Arizona and Sonoran sites (e.g., Bylas Spring, Cienega Creek, Río Bocobachi, Río Arizpe, Río Cedros, San Bernardino Ranch in one case) show 0% heterozygosity or very low values.

**Conservation recommendations:**
1. **Restocking source:** The current plan, which draws on a hatchery population from Monkey Spring, is flawed. Monkey Spring is a thermally stable, isolated springhead whose fish are genetically invariant and show low fecundity. The authors instead recommend wild or first-generation fish from thermally fluctuating habitats, such as Sharp Spring, which is the most variable Arizona population and has high female fecundity.
2. **Group discreteness:** Because 53% of diversity lies between groups, the three groups should be kept separate in nature. Repopulation in extreme southeastern Arizona should use stock from San Bernardino Ranch (location E) or other upper Yaqui basin localities (group 2).
3. **Controlled intragroup mixing:** Experimental mixing of stocks within each group may increase local diversity. The authors suggest intragroup hybrids may be more successful colonists than stock from a single population, and that this could reverse effects of recent habitat-driven subdivision.
4. **Feasibility:** Experimental studies of hybridization, fitness, and adaptive plasticity are practical in this small, short-lived, viviparous fish, though time is limited for most endangered species.

**Management actions already under way:** Remaining natural habitats in Arizona are protected from development, and efforts to control *Gambusia affinis* are in progress. Several Gila drainage springs and marshes are being restocked from the Monkey Spring hatchery population.

**Unrelated fragments in the document:**
- *Page 2 (endocrinology):* Discussion of proteolytic conversion of atrial natriuretic prohormone in heart and plasma, with a reference list. No fish or aquatic data.
- *Final section (neurophysiology):* A study of the swimming central pattern generator in *Clione limacina*, a pteropod mollusk. Four pedal interneurons with reciprocal inhibition and postinhibitory rebound produce reverberating alternating activity. This concerns a marine invertebrate, not fish, and is included here only for completeness.
