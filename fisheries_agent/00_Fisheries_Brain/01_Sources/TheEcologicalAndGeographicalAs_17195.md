# Ecological and Geographical Aspects of the Fecundity of the Plaice

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pleuronectidae

## Executive Summary

This synthesis by T. B. Bagenal compiles roughly 30 years of work on the fecundity (number of ripening eggs prior to spawning) of the [[European Plaice]] across most of its geographical range. Fecundity is lowest in the Southern Bight of the [[North Sea]], rises in all directions from there, and is again lower in Faxa Bay (Iceland) and the Barents Sea. The author argues that food availability, linked to population density, is the most important controlling factor. Only the [[Baltic Sea]] and [[Trondheim Fjord]] populations are so distinct that a racial or genetic explanation is required. The paper also stresses fecundity as a density-dependent population-regulating mechanism.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[European Plaice]] (*Pleuronectes platessa* L.), an economically important flatfish studied widely by fishery workers. A biological summary is given by Wimpenny (1953).
- **Distribution:** Occasionally in the Mediterranean; common along the Atlantic coasts of France, around the British Isles, the North Sea, Faroes, Iceland and Norway to the Barents Sea; found in the Kattegat, Skagerrack and Baltic at least as far as Bornholm.
- **Fecundity definition:** Only the opaque, yolk-laden eggs due for shedding in the coming season were counted. Ovaries were preserved in a modified Gilson's fluid and eggs were counted by subsampling.
- **Data sources:** Research vessels (e.g., *Calanus*, *Mizpah*, *Sarsia*, *Maria Julia*), fish markets, and fishing boats. Earlier works by [[Simpson]] (North Sea, Irish Sea), [[Kandler]] (Kattegat), and [[Kandler & Pirwitz]] (Baltic) are integrated.
- **Methods:** Log-transformed regression of fecundity on length (F = aL^b), covariance analysis, and expected fecundity standardized to a 37 cm fish. Fiducial limits are reported at 95%.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food and density.** Areas with high population density (North Sea, Iceland, Barents Sea) show lower fecundity. Areas where plaice are scarcer show higher fecundity, consistent with food limitation.

**Larval drift and nursery grounds.** The Southern Bight spawning eggs and larvae drift north-eastwards to nursery grounds along the Dutch, German and Danish coasts, which are considered low-mortality. Flamborough larvae are more widely dispersed. Irish (Dingle, Schull) and La Rochelle plaice eggs are exposed to strong gales and currents that scatter them, which is associated with higher fecundity. Icelandic eggs drift into the Faxa Bay nursery grounds.

**Annual variation.** In the [[Clyde Sea Area]], expected fecundity of a 37 cm plaice changed markedly around 1957/58 while co-located witch and long rough dab did not show the same pattern. Because nearby species were unaffected, the author concludes that hydrographic factors such as temperature are unlikely causes. Changes in fishing mortality, which reduced population density and so increased food per fish, are proposed as the explanation.

**Salinity.** Salinity appears to have only a slight effect on fecundity.

**Baltic and Trondheim Fjord.** These populations are sharply distinct, with high fecundity (Baltic around 307–371 thousand eggs at 37 cm; Trondheim Fjord 271 thousand) and marking experiments show little mixing with outside stocks.

## Key Findings

1. **Geographical pattern.** Mean expected fecundity of a 37 cm plaice is 84,000 eggs in the Southern Bight of the North Sea, rising to 153,000 in Dingle Bay (Ireland). Iceland and the Barents Sea are relatively low (Iceland 106 thousand; Barents Sea 80 thousand per Milinski 1938, based on nine fish).
2. **Length dependence.** Fecundity increases steeply with length. A 30 cm plaice carries about 60,000 eggs, while a 45 cm fish carries over 280,000.
3. **Individual variability.** At 37 cm, Clyde fish ranged from 65,000 to 220,000 eggs, with a mean of about 153,000.
4. **Annual variation.** Clyde fecundity was low (137 thousand) in 1956 and 1957 and higher (159 thousand) in the following four years. Age did not explain this, since mean age was 5.1 years in both low and high years.
5. **Age effect.** Among fish of equal length, older fish do not carry more eggs (following Simpson 1951 and Gerking 1959).
6. **Genetic vs. environmental control.** Within-population changes across all age-groups point to environmental, not genetic, control of local fecundity variation. Baltic and Trondheim Fjord levels, however, persisted through density changes and are considered genetic.
7. **Fecundity and growth.** A direct correlation exists between fecundity and growth rate (mean length at age 4), suggesting food supply controls both.
8. **Condition.** Where samples can be compared (Irish data), higher fecundity corresponds to better condition. No evidence of a negative correlation was found, so parental condition does not appear to be a compensating factor.
9. **Egg size.** Dried egg density at Dingle (0.78 g/cc) exceeded that of Rye Bay (0.68 g/cc), indicating greater food reserves. This provides no support for the [[Svardson Compensation Hypothesis]] that high-fecundity fish produce smaller eggs.
10. **Population regulation.** Fecundity may act as a density-dependent regulator. The southern North Sea fecundity is 47.4% lower than that of the Firth of Clyde, according to the author's calculation.
11. **Conclusion on causes.** Most geographical differences can be explained by plasticity under food-driven variation. The Baltic and Trondheim Fjord populations require a racial or genetic explanation.
