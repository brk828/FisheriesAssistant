# Stability of Periphyton and Macroinvertebrates to Flash-Flood Disturbance in a Desert Stream

#macroinvertebrates #periphyton #stream-ecology #desert-stream

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates the [[Resistance]], [[Resilience]] and [[Ecological Succession]] of [[Periphyton]] and [[Macroinvertebrates]] in [[Sycamore Creek]], a spatially intermittent stream in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of Arizona, over June 1984 to July 1987. Thirty-five [[Flash Flood]] disturbances (spates) were recorded, with peak discharge from 0.2 to 58.4 m³/s. Biota showed low resistance, especially to events large enough to move substrata, while resilience was very high. Macroinvertebrate densities in five long successional sequences declined sharply in late stages. The authors attribute these "crashes" to a [[Food Quality Hypothesis]] in which [[Nitrogen Limitation]] reduces [[Bacterial Conditioning]] of detritus, lowering the quality of material repeatedly reingested by [[Collector-Gatherers]]. The paper has no fish component.

## Background & Description

The study addresses how disturbance regimes shape stream ecosystem stability. The authors define disturbance as a discrete, partly unpredictable event causing significant mortality, and they treat resistance (deflection of state variables) and resilience (recovery rate) as measurable components of stability. They argue that succession is a major part of resilience and that short-lived stream organisms make successional patterns easier to resolve than those of long-lived forest systems.

Sycamore Creek drains a 505 km² mountainous watershed, with study sites at 600-700 m in Sonoran desertscrub. Mean precipitation is about 15 cm per year, and modal streamflow is low (0.01-0.05 m/s in summer, 0.1-0.5 m/s in winter). Summer flash floods rise and recede within hours, whereas winter flows persist longer. Stream water temperature ranges from 20-30 °C in summer to 10-20 °C in winter. Riparian trees such as [[Platanus Wrighti]] and [[Populus Fremontii]] provide little shade, so light reaches the bed and algal standing crops can exceed 200 g AFDM/m² when interflood periods are long.

Methods included:
- **Discharge and disturbance magnitude:** Peak discharge was estimated from high-water marks and a [[Manning Equation]]-based expression (Eq. 1), and continuously monitored by pressure transducer from 1986.
- **Periphyton:** Algal patch mapping along fixed transects, with patch-weighted core sampling and chlorophyll a extraction.
- **Macroinvertebrates:** Five 80 cm² cores per date, elutriated through 250 µm mesh, with biomass from length-mass regressions or dry:wet mass ratios.
- **Nutrients:** Nitrate, ammonium and soluble reactive phosphorus, with inorganic nitrogen flux calculated as concentration × discharge.
- **Statistics:** Arcsine square-root transformed resistance, regression-based resilience, and stepwise multiple regression.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disturbance regime.** Sycamore Creek averages about six spates per year (14, 11 and 10 in the three years). Of the 35 spates, 49 distinct events were treated separately because magnitude differed between the two study sites. Spates above roughly 2-3 m³/s were capable of moving coarse sand and altering channel geomorphology. Every spate raised discharge at least an order of magnitude above base flow, and the largest floods reached thousands of times base flow.

**Trophic structure.** Algae are the basis of the food web, supporting both direct grazing and, more often, detritivory on autochthonous material. Ingestion by macroinvertebrates is high, estimated at four times macroinvertebrate biomass per day. Assimilation efficiency is low (about 10%), so most ingested organic matter (about 90%) is egested and must be reingested.

**Nutrients.** Nitrogen availability limits autotrophic processes in this stream. Dissolved inorganic nitrogen flux was closely tied to late-stage invertebrate density, which links primary production to consumer populations.

**Comparative context.** The authors cite a tundra river fertilization study showing that phosphorus enrichment increased chlorophyll, heterotrophic microbial activity, insect abundance and fish growth. This is presented as evidence that nutrient limitation can propagate to higher trophic levels.

## Key Findings

**1. Resistance is low for both groups, and it declines with disturbance magnitude.**
- Resistance was measured for 27 spates. Periphyton resistance (as chlorophyll a) was negatively correlated with peak discharge (r = −0.50, p = 0.0073).
- Among algal assemblages, [[Diatoms]] were most resistant, followed by invertebrates, [[Filamentous Green Algae]] and [[Cyanobacteria]]. Diatom-dominated assemblages declined with discharge (r = −0.53, p = 0.004), as did filamentous green algae (r = −0.45, p = 0.026).
- Relatively small floods (about 1-2 m³/s, roughly 100 times summer base flow) virtually eliminated the chlorophytes and cyanobacteria. Spates above about 8 m³/s (about 1000 times base flow) were needed to reduce diatoms and invertebrate density by more than 90%.
- Substratum type (cobble riffles vs. sand-gravel runs) did not significantly affect chlorophyll resistance (p = 0.6), and the authors suggest that sediment scour from adjacent runs may have overridden substratum stability.

**2. Apparent diatom "resistance" reflects high resilience.** Diatom-dominated assemblages can make up very little of preflood standing crop yet over 90% of postflood cover, so rapid colonization within hours of recession inflates their apparent post-spate standing crop.

**3. Resilience is very high.** Recovery was rapid relative to other streams and ecosystems, which the authors attribute to warm temperatures, stable low discharge, high light and high production rates.
- Twenty sequences of postflood succession were analyzed (21 in the Results, with 15 having invertebrate data), with 33% of sequences under one month long and 24% exceeding two months.
- Sequence lengths ranged from 1 to 158 days.
- Maximum chlorophyll a ranged from 75 to 404 mg/m². Eleven sequences showed linear increases and nine showed asymptotic increases, with stable standing crops reached within 12-100 days.
- Invertebrate densities reached 20,000 individuals/m² in 8-36 days and 40,000 individuals/m² in 13-52 days. Maximum densities were 15,000-146,000 individuals/m².
- Maximum invertebrate biomass ranged from 0.50 to 5.43 g/m².

**4. Seasonal differences.**
- Chlorophyll a recovered faster in summer and autumn than in winter-spring (ANOVA on log-transformed slopes, p = 0.024), and seasonal differences in time to preflood levels were significant (p < 0.0001).
- Time to reach 40,000 individuals/m² was significantly shorter in summer and autumn (p < 0.01). Invertebrate recovery slopes did not differ among seasons (p = 0.26).

**5. Dominant taxa and recovery.** Oligochaeta and Chironomidae dominated most sequences. Ephemeroptera ([[Baetis Quilleri]], [[Tricorythodes Dimorphus]]), the tipulid [[Cryptolabis]] sp., Trichoptera ([[Cheumatopsyche Arizonensis]], [[Helicopsyche Mexicana]]) and the snail [[Physa Virgata]] were occasionally abundant.

**6. Invertebrate density is poorly explained by disturbance variables.** Multiple regression explained only about 40% of variance in invertebrate density. The best predictors were DIN flux (r = 0.50), chlorophyll a (r = 0.41), days since disturbance (r = 0.27) and day length (r = 0.28). Disturbance-related variables explained most chlorophyll a variance but were only weakly correlated with invertebrates. The authors conclude that other unmeasured factors must contribute.

**7. Late-stage crashes.** In 8 sequences density increased linearly or asymptotically. In 5 sequences it rose quickly and then declined sharply, with biomass following the same pattern. Four of these five were the longest sequences (over two months). In each case DIN flux was low throughout or at the end of the sequence, and cyanobacteria became abundant only after invertebrate declines.

**8. Nitrogen flux predicts invertebrate density late in succession.** For samples collected more than one month after disturbance, invertebrate density was significantly correlated with DIN flux (r = 0.74, p < 0.0001).

**9. Proposed mechanism: food quality limitation.**
- Fecal material contains only about half the nitrogen of ingested food, so feces are of lower quality than food.
- Bacterial conditioning can increase N content only if microbes immobilize N from the medium. When N supply is limiting, conditioning fails, and repeated reingestion degrades food quality until populations crash.
- The authors present this [[Food Quality Hypothesis]] as the most plausible explanation, but they acknowledge that N-quality and density links were not directly demonstrated.

**10. Alternative hypotheses were considered.**
- Food quantity is unlikely, since benthic [[Ash-Free Dry Mass]] continued to rise during invertebrate crashes.
- Increased predation was not supported by consistent successional patterns in interstitial predators, though adult beetles, hemipterans and large odonates were not adequately sampled.
- Synchronous emergence is unlikely because the declining taxa reproduce continuously with asynchronous life histories.

**11. Testable predictions.** The authors propose two tests: (1) C:N ratio and bacterial colonization of benthic detritus should increase with time since disturbance, and (2) N enrichment should prevent late-stage invertebrate crashes.

**12. Conclusions.**
- Over periods longer than a year, disturbance by spates is a dominant organizing factor in this desert stream.
- Biota show little resistance to spates that move substrata (above about 2-3 m³/s), but recovery via succession is usually rapid.
- Nitrogen limitation can influence not only primary producers and grazers but also detritivorous consumers, indicating strong element-limitation control of productivity at higher trophic levels.
