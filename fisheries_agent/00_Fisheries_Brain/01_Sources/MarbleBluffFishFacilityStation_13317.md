# Marble Bluff Fish Facility Station Development Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-passage

## Executive Summary

The [[Station Development Plan]] for the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]] (MBFF), prepared by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) Region 1 Division of Engineering, assesses why the facility fails to pass migrating [[Cui-ui]] and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (LCT) from [[Pyramid Lake]] to spawning grounds on the [[Truckee River]]. The plan concludes that the existing fishway is inadequate, that rehabilitation is not feasible, and that a new 3.1-mile concrete channel with a river trap upgrade should be built in three phases. The estimated grand total is $19,042,000 (preliminary, based on February 1984 cost indices).

## Background & Description

**Historical context.** [[Derby Dam]] and the [[Truckee-Carson Canal]] (completed 1905) diverted Truckee River water to agriculture in the Carson basin. Lake level then declined, reducing cui-ui spawning success and contributing to the extinction of the Pyramid Lake strain of LCT. The cui-ui was listed as endangered by the FWS in 1967; a recovery plan was approved in 1978, and it is also listed as endangered by the Nevada Department of Wildlife. The [[Cui-ui Recovery Plan]] aims to downlist the species to threatened.

**Facility.** The [[Marble Bluff Dam]], fishway, [[Numana Dam]] fish ladder, and associated structures were authorized in 1956 under the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Washoe Project and completed in 1976. The FWS has operated and maintained the facility since 1977. The site lies about 30 miles northeast of Reno on a 623.2-acre right-of-way within the Pyramid Lake Indian Reservation.

**Current management.** The Bureau of Reclamation maintains the dam. The FWS operates the fishway and fish handling facilities, with staff from the [[Lahontan National Fish Hatchery]] running the fishway and sluice gates, and the Great Basin Complex office in Reno handling trapping, run enumeration, and egg collection. Operating expenses were $54,903 in FY 1983, supported by two full-time equivalent positions. Visitor use is minimal due to remoteness.

**Existing facilities (summary).**
- Fish handling building (2,964 sq ft, good condition, but crowder and vertical fish sweep ineffective due to silt)
- Fishway: 3.1 miles, 20 ft × 5.5 ft, clay liner (fair), with four ladders in poor condition
- Numana Dam fish ladder (fair) and three fish screens (good)
- Fishway road (poor, drainage and flooding problems) and fencing (poor, 2.6 miles to replace)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake decline.** Before diversion, Pyramid Lake covered roughly 130,000 acres. Since 1905 the lake level has dropped about one foot per year on average. Short-term rises since 1966 have not reversed the long-term downward trend.
- **Delta formation.** Increased bank erosion upstream has built a delta at the Truckee River mouth. In most years it forms an impassable barrier to upstream migrants.
- **Water quality and habitat.** Inadequately treated sewage and nonpoint pollution cause nutrient loading and toxic conditions below Reno and in Pyramid Lake. Riparian vegetation removal has raised water temperatures and accelerated bank erosion. Lake productivity may decline further as levels fall and nutrients rise.
- **Flow variability.** River inflow is probably the strongest attractant for cui-ui. The fishway discharges only a small fraction of river flow in most years. Lake elevation fluctuations of nearly 25 feet since 1977 have repeatedly stranded or submerged the terminal ladder.
- **Sediment and wind.** Sand, silt, tumbleweeds, and mudslides from the bluffs fill the fishway and handling building. Silt and sand have made the crowder, sweep, and truck loading well ineffective.

## Key Findings

**Facility performance**
- Only 10 to 20 percent of the cui-ui run entering the fishway reached the fish handling building in recent years.
- The original design (patterned on [[Ice Harbor Dam]], 1:10 slope, 1-foot drop between pools) produced velocities too high for cui-ui. Design changes (doubled weirs, alternated overflow sections, removed bottom orifices) reduced velocities but did not remove the barrier.
- The building bypass ladder is ineffective: high velocity and turbulence deter cui-ui, and LCT avoid the subsurface openings.
- The [[River Fish Trap]] does not attract or capture fish as intended and is unsafe for staff and fish.
- In April and May 1984, the spillway flow attracted roughly 11,000 cui-ui and 600 trout to the river trap while no fish passed the fishway.
- The terminal structure cannot handle lake fluctuations; [[Fishway Delta]] formation requires dredging before and during runs.
- Bird predation (especially [[Pelican Predation]]) is worsened by the fishway's shallow depth of 3 feet.

**Knowledge gaps**
- The number of fish that must be passed to build an optimum, self-sustaining cui-ui population is unknown.
- Population dynamics data (larval survival and required recruitment) are lacking.
- "Self-sustaining population" and "optimum numbers" have no quantified definitions.

**Design criteria** (furnished by the Great Basin Complex office)
- Design volume: 50 to 500 cfs; the system must also function at 50 cfs.
- Maximum near-bed velocity: 2 fps. Maximum velocity over weirs: 4 fps.
- Design depth: 6 feet across all volumes, to shelter cui-ui and deter pelicans.
- Lake surface design range: 3780 to 3815 feet. Upstream pool: 3854.0 to 3862.0 feet.
- Optimal weirs combine a several-foot-wide overflow (for LCT) and several-foot-square bottom orifices (for cui-ui), with stilling pools.

**Recommended design concept**
- Rehabilitate the river trap: extend the wing wall about 240 feet, excavate the canal to 6 feet, add resting areas, install an electric weir, and redesign the fish lift.
- Replace the existing fishway with a 3.1-mile [[Concrete Channel]] (about 16,350 ft long) with 297 permanent weirs, 30 adjustable weirs spaced 50 feet apart, and a [[Radial Gate]] at the river junction.
- Weir hydraulics at design flows: 0.00 ft over weirs at 50 cfs, 1.42 ft at 200 cfs, and 2.92 ft at 500 cfs. Velocities: 2.00 fps between weirs, 5.70 fps over weirs, and 4.01 fps through orifices.
- Build a fish trap, office, and maintenance building, an irrigation and vegetative barrier to reduce windblown sand, a gravel maintenance road, and two vehicular bridges.
- Relocate the upstream exit a few hundred feet into the impoundment so fish can reorient.

**Cost estimates (preliminary, 1987 construction season, ENR index 4,113.20, February 1984)**

| Phase | Scope | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Initial | River trap rehabilitation | $613,400 |
| Intermediate | Fishway reconstruction (concrete, excavation, weirs, gate, bridges, building) | $17,385,700 |
| Final | Irrigation/barrier, heavy equipment, security fence, Numana screen, riprap | $1,042,900 |
| **Grand total** | | **$19,042,000** |

The largest intermediate items are concrete channel and permanent weirs ($13,648,200), excavation ($1,258,700), and equipment mobilization ($1,013,300). Heavy equipment in the final phase totals $521,400.

**Operations and maintenance:** The O&M base rises from $55,000 (current) to $80,000 (initial), $200,000 (intermediate), and $180,000 (final), with FTE rising from 2 to 4.

**Planning assumptions:** The FWS will continue cooperating with Nevada, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and Reclamation. The FWS has no responsibility to artificially propagate cui-ui at Marble Bluff. Numana Dam facilities are integral to the MBFF. The long-term lake decline will continue until adequate average annual inflow is secured. Redevelopment must account for tribal authority, unstable site conditions, and variable river flows.

**Facility objective:** Design a fish-passage facility that lets cui-ui and LCT migrate to and from historic spawning grounds, consistent with the [[National Objectives for Species of Special Emphasis]] and the Regional Resource Plan, which lists cui-ui among the Interior Basin fishery resources of greatest concern. The cui-ui population has declined over 90 percent in the last 40 years.
