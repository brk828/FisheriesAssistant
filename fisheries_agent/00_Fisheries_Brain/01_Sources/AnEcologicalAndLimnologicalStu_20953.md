# Ecological and Limnological Studies of the Green River in Dinosaur National Monument (1962 Field Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by [[Harold K. Hagen]] and [[Joseph E. Banks]] of [[Colorado State University]] for the [[National Park Service]] (contract No. 14-10-0232-686, June 1963), documents the ecology and limnology of the Green River in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Its main focus was the rare endemic fishes, studied through a single graduate student's field season (June to November 1962) and a re-inventory of 1961 stations by a 14-student crew in October 1962. The October 1962 comparison showed a large loss of fish and invertebrates, which the authors attributed to a September 1962 stream pollution event (a rotenone-related fish kill). Fish were absent from the [[Gates of Lodore]] station, and invertebrate counts fell sharply compared with 1961.

## Background & Description

**Scope and rationale.** The study was designed to explain the distribution and life histories of rare fishes endemic to the Green River. Physical data (streamflow, temperature, turbidity) were largely omitted because they were already recorded by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, USGS, and state agencies of Utah, Wyoming, and Colorado. A concurrent contract with Utah University covered the aquatic insects, plankton, and plants, so the invertebrate material is largely summarized. Terrestrial plants had been inventoried in 1961 by Dr. Holmgren of Utah State University.

**Field methods.**
- Summer work: a single student collected for the whole season using throwlines, seines (including a one-man push seine), gill nets, electrofishing, Surber square-foot bottom samplers, and plankton nets. Hoop and fyke nets were of little value.
- October 1962: 14 students in two survey crews resampled the 1961 stations. One crew used electric shocking, gill nets, and seines. The second crew applied a [[Rotenone]]-based toxin in a side channel at over 2 ppm at the [[Gates of Lodore]].
- Fishes were preserved in 10% formalin and invertebrates in alcohol. Representative fish went to the Dinosaur National Monument museum.

**Species of note.** The study focused on the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), the bony-tailed and round-tailed chubs (*Gila robusta*), the [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], and the bluehead mountain sucker (*Pantosteus delphinus*). The report also covers the exotic [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), the [[Channel Catfish]], and the [[Green River Spring Dace]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure.** Steep canyon walls deflect the current and create pools, eddies, and backwaters that serve as fish refuges. Sediment accumulates on inside bends, while outside bends are scoured to bare rock. Stream grade varies across the Monument:
- Near Lodore: about 2.5 ft/mile.
- Lodore Canyon: average 18.2 ft/mile, easing to about 4 ft of drop at [[Echo Park]], where the [[Yampa River]] joins.
- Whirlpool Canyon: more than 12 ft/mile.
- [[Island Park]] and Rainbow Park: 3.3 ft/mile.
- [[Split Mountain]] Canyon: nearly 22 ft/mile.

**Temperature.** The Green River ran up to 4–5 °F cooler than the Yampa. The authors suspected this difference limited the upstream range of squawfish, humpback suckers, and humpback chub in the Yampa. A 1952 Colorado report (Baily and Alberti) identified Lily Park as the warmest zone of the Yampa. Daily ranges reached about 65–72 °F at Echo Park in late summer.

**Water chemistry.** Water-temperature and air-temperature correlation at Island Park (September 23–October 28) gave r = -0.85. Water temperature and dissolved oxygen were strongly linear (r = 0.95). Dissolved oxygen ranged from 10.6 ppm at 48 °F (high) to 8.1 ppm at 62 °F (low). In Lodore, pH was 7.4–7.6 and dissolved oxygen 6.5–7.3 ppm. Total alkalinity readings (M.O.) were erratic, and the observer noted probable titration errors.

**Turbidity.** River depth changed by 5–7 inches or more per day during high water. Secchi visibility at Island Park (September–October 1962) ranged from about 1/4 inch during high turbidity to roughly 13 inches at the clearest. Gill nets silted in during May–July and could not be used effectively until late summer.

**Bottom and vegetation.** At Lodore, the bottom was almost entirely fine sand and silt, with no aquatic vegetation for several miles. Echo Park had rubble with some silt, and the Yampa had sand with boulders. Periphyton increased sharply after the water cleared in late September, and filamentous algae washed downstream during rains and fouled throwlines.

**Adaptations.** The authors linked several traits to current and turbidity: the enlarged nuchal hump, enlarged fins, and reduced scalation in chubs. The mayfly *Lachlania powelli* clings to debris on the bottom.

## Key Findings

- **Summer 1962 fish observations:**
  - Carp occupied nearly every niche of the turbulent Green, including the upper Jones Creek trout habitat.
  - Humpbacked carp were seen in the Green and Yampa, and the hump was first noted in 1961.
  - Squawfish were present but scarce. Young fish favored quiet backwaters.
  - Throwline catches came only from the shoreline, with no fish caught beyond the third hook, about 10–15 ft out.
- **Parasitism (1962):** Three primary parasites were recorded: the copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea* ("anchor worm"), the leech *Myzobdella moorei*, and a sporozoan (*Myxosoma* sp.). Parasitism was highest in the Yampa River (41.5%), followed by Echo Park (31.3%) and Split Mountain (22.2%). The leech was found only on channel catfish.
- **The September 1962 fish kill:**
  - Rotenone reached the Brown's Park bridge at about 7:00 a.m. on September 8, and fish were in distress for about 13 hours.
  - Dead fish were found from Lodore to the Split Mountain campground, mostly flannelmouth suckers, channel catfish, and dace. Hybrid suckers (humpback × flannelmouth) were also found.
  - Dead carp and catfish were counted at Echo Park and Rainbow Park.
- **October 1962 resampling:**
  - No fish were found at Gates of Lodore, despite several hours of seining and a rotenone-treated side channel.
  - Echo Park produced 26 fish in 1962, against hundreds in one 1961 hole alone.
  - Fish species declined at all stations. Echo Park held only three of its 11 1961 species. Island Park also lost many species.
- **Invertebrate decline (1961 vs. 1962, average number per square foot):**
  - Lodore: 22.9 in 1961 vs. 6.0 in 1962 (74% decrease).
  - Echo Park: 12.6 vs. 1.8 (86% decrease).
  - Split Mountain: 18.5 vs. 12.0 (about 35% decrease).
  - Island Park: 1961 data unavailable (1962 value 30.5).
- **Authors' conclusions:** Upstream pollution "seriously reduced" aquatic food organisms in the Monument. Invertebrates were expected to recover faster than fish. The authors hoped a genetic reserve of the rare fishes survived.
- **Stomach analysis:** Fish remains were found in adult squawfish, chubs, carp, catfish, and one flannelmouth sucker. Analysis was not expected to be complete before fall 1963. Early evidence suggested the fishes were opportunistic rather than selective feeders.
- **Floods and sediment:** The river was the highest the boatman had seen in six years. Sandbars were undermined and caved into the river, adding to turbidity.
- **Upcoming plans:** The authors urged sampling in summer 1963, before the [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] was filled. The report ends with a November 4 drop of about 7 inches, attributed to closure of the Flaming Gorge dam.
