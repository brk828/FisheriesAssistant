# Fish Conservation and the Enigma of Introduced Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This workshop paper, presented at the Australian Society for Fish Biology workshop on introduced and translocated fishes (Magnetic Island, 1989), reviews the history, scale and consequences of [[Introduced Fishes]] in the United States. The author argues that introductions, whether deliberate or accidental, have rarely produced direct benefits and frequently harmed native habitats and biota. He contends that fisheries managers often pursue "socio-economic" justifications while ignoring ecological principles, and that professional policies on introductions have been largely ignored. The paper calls for greater ecological caution, peer review of candidate introductions, stronger regulation of the [[Aquarium Fish]] and [[Aquaculture]] industries, and public education to prevent releases.

## Background & Description

The paper is a conservation essay built on quantitative inventories of established exotic and translocated fishes in the U.S. Its author, Walter R. Courtenay, Jr., of Florida Atlantic University, draws on decades of distribution surveys. The paper distinguishes between:

- **Exotic fishes**: species from outside the country, including marine species introduced to Hawaii.
- **Translocated fishes**: native species moved beyond their historic ranges within the country, often through [[Translocation]] for sport fishing, bait or reservoir stocking.

The author also stresses that **importation is not synonymous with introduction**. Many imported species never establish, and the paper argues that this distinction is often overlooked in debates over import regulation.

Table 1 of the paper lists exotic fishes established in the United States, with family, state distribution and approximate year of release. It spans 19 families, with [[Cichlidae]] and [[Poeciliidae]] especially well represented. Representative entries include:

- [[Carassius Auratus]] (goldfish), established since the 1680s, with a record of 1680s introduction and presence in all states except Alaska and Florida.
- [[Cyprinus Carpio]] (common carp), introduced in 1831 and established in all states except Alaska.
- [[Salmo Trutta]] (brown trout), introduced in 1884 and established across a very large number of states.
- [[Ctenopharyngodon Idella]] (grass carp), established in several southern and central states from the 1960s.
- [[Poecilia Reticulata]] (guppy), established in several states, with Hawaii records dating to 1922.
- Several cichlids, including [[Oreochromis Mossambicus]] and other tilapias, recorded mainly in Florida, Hawaii and Texas.

Table 1 is a state-by-state inventory; the full list is not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scale of establishment**

- The paper's abstract states that the 50 U.S. states host 64 established exotic fish species.
- In the contiguous 48 states, 46 exotic species are established as reproducing populations.
- At least 168 native species have been translocated and established beyond their historic ranges in the contiguous 48 states. Adding [[Alaska]] and [[Hawaii]] raises this to a conservative 178 species.
- Alaska has no established exotic fishes but hosts two established translocated species.
- Hawaii hosts eight established and six non-established translocated fishes from the continental U.S., and many of its exotic fishes are marine.
- A further 15 exotic species formed reproducing populations that later failed, mainly due to cold intolerance.
- 48 more exotic species have been collected from open waters without establishing, and eight others were identified only to genus.
- Hawaii's introductions raised the U.S. total to 64 established and 73 non-established exotic species.

**Timeline of introductions**

- Before 1900, the continental U.S. had six established exotic fish species. Including Hawaii, the total was 10 (17%) before 1900 and 13 (23%) before World War II.
- Most introductions occurred after the late 1950s, aided by jet aircraft and styrofoam shipping containers.
- The pet fish industry expanded markedly in the 1960s, centred in warm states such as Florida and California.
- In the late 1970s and early 1980s, nearly 200 million exotic fishes were imported annually. The paper notes this may represent only about 20% of the U.S. exotic aquarium market, with the rest produced by domestic culture facilities.

**Pathways of introduction**

- *Intentional*: new food and sport species, biological control, and protection of endangered species.
- *Unintentional*: escapes from [[Aquaculture]] facilities, release of unwanted [[Pet Fish Trade]] stock, canal construction, and ballast water discharge from intercontinental ships.
- *Public transfers*: individuals moving fish from one water body to another to see whether they will survive. The author notes that good intentions by agencies can be undone by such actions.

**Documented impacts**

- Introductions in the U.S. have often yielded neutral to damaging results for habitats and native species, frequently combined with other human modifications of aquatic systems.
- Damming of rivers by federal agencies prompted state agencies to use introductions to replace lost riverine species. This benefited fisheries in the eastern and central U.S. but caused serious damage to native biota in western states.
- In the Rio Grande, Colorado River and San Joaquin basins, and adjoining states with relatively low native diversity, populations declined sharply after habitat modification and the arrival of introduced species.
- The paper notes that the cost of control and [[Eradication Programs]] is very high. Federal and state agencies spend millions, probably billions, of dollars annually on such programmes, with limited success.

**Delayed and unpredictable impacts**

- Introduced species may show few or no adverse effects for decades before population explosions and range expansions. Proposed triggers include a "critical mass" in population size, genetic drift, or slight alterations in native populations. The author describes the introduction of any species as a potential "[[Time Bomb Effect]]".
- The author emphasises that most biological pollutants cannot be contained, unlike chemical or thermal pollutants. He also notes that any species imported into a country could become a permanent part of its biota.

## Key Findings

- **Introductions have a poor track record.** The paper states that most introductions have not met their intended goals, and that direct benefits to society have been rare worldwide. Neutral to damaging effects on habitats and native species are common.
- **Establishment is strongly linked to human behaviour.** Fish culture and irresponsible releases by the public, rather than deliberate policy, account for most established exotic and translocated fishes.
- **Policy has been poorly followed.** The [[American Fisheries Society]] adopted a [[Position Statement]] in 1972 on exotic species and a broader revised statement in 1986 covering both exotic and translocated organisms. The author reports that the 1972 statement was rarely followed.
- **Key safeguards were dropped.** The 1986 revision omitted two provisions from the 1972 statement: [[Peer Review]] of research before purposeful introduction, and a request for stronger federal testing of imported species for parasites and pathogens.
- **Agency roles are in conflict.** Many agencies charged with protecting native resources also promote introductions. The paper identifies the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] as funding state programmes that promote introductions opposed by neighbouring states sharing drainages. It also criticises the [[Agency for International Development]] and the [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] for international species transfers without testing for environmental consequences.
- **Jurisdictional shifts risk further problems.** Entrepreneurs and politicians are moving aquaculture from fisheries agencies to agriculture agencies. The author argues that agriculture agencies do not treat introduced aquatic species as pests and so will not respond to escapes.
- **Risk prediction is limited.** Predation effects may be predictable, but trophic and behavioural interactions often are not. Research funding has favoured control and eradication after problems arise over pre-introduction risk testing.
- **Recommended actions.** The author calls for:
  - greater ecological and conservation concern in introduction policy;
  - caution, and use of introductions only as a final option;
  - peer review and independent testing of candidate species;
  - regulation of the pet and aquaculture industries to prevent releases, with industry-funded consumer education;
  - cooperation between industry and regulators as a more effective alternative to litigation.
