# Dexter National Fish Hatchery & Technology Center Annual Report FY2002

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report covers Fiscal Year 2002 (October 1, 2001 to September 30, 2002) at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] & Technology Center in southeastern New Mexico. The station is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service facility devoted to the refugium holding, culture, research and reintroduction of imperiled fishes of the American Southwest. In FY2002 it held 16 listed species and one species of special concern. Most of these fish come from New Mexico or neighboring states and northern Mexico. The report covers species status, hatchery production, broodstock genetics, facility construction, budget and personnel, partnerships, outreach, and resource needs.

## Background & Description

The hatchery was authorized under the White Act of 1930 to produce warmwater game fish for the Southwest. The land, then owned by the New Mexico State Game and Fish Commission, was acquired by the federal government on August 31, 1931 for $3,265.90. The station covers 640 acres in Chaves County. Operations began in 1932 with [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Channel Catfish]] distributed to reservations, reservoirs and farm ponds. After the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, the station was selected in 1974 as a holding facility for threatened and endangered southwestern fishes. By 1978 it was fully devoted to that work. Its three goals are to:

1. Establish a refuge for critically imperiled fishes.
2. Develop spawning and culture techniques.
3. Produce and rear species for reintroduction.

The station was designated a Technology Center in FY1991, one of only seven in the Fish and Wildlife Service.

**Setting and infrastructure**

- Elevation 3,500 ft, average rainfall 12 inches, growing season 180–200 days, on the northern fringe of the Chihuahua Desert.
- Five shallow aquifer wells (150 ft deep) pump a combined 2,000+ gallons per minute.
- Well water is a constant 64°F, pH 7.8–8.5, total hardness 2,100 ppm and total dissolved solids 3,500 ppm.
- Water rights total 2,185.5 acre-feet per year (10,927.5 acre-feet per five-year period).
- Culture facilities include 46 earthen or lined ponds (0.1–1.0 acre), four fiberglass raceways, circular and rectangular fiberglass tanks, and 80 ten-gallon and 20 forty-gallon aquariums.
- Phase III construction began in June 2002, adding 25 earthen ponds, four outdoor raceways, plastic lining for ponds 7A and 9A, and water lines to the back of the A-series ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The station sits in an arid setting where drought and rising water demand threaten aquatic resources across the Southwest. Hatchery effluent collects in two sumps on the southeast side and supplies year-round water to adjacent wetlands. Operations are concentrated in the northwest portion of the property. The remainder is co-managed with the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish as a Wildlife Management Area under a Cooperative Agreement dated August 1981. This area is open to seasonal hunting for upland game and migratory birds.

Several species depend on wild source populations that are threatened by drought. In 2002, drought reduced flows in the [[Pecos River]], which prompted a salvage operation. Captive refugium stocks act as insurance in case wild populations are lost. Disease is a recurring concern. Wild-caught fish brought to the hatchery can carry parasites and pathogens, such as yellow grub in [[Chihuahua Chub]] and tapeworm and bacterial infections in [[Virgin River Chub]].

## Key Findings

### Species status and holdings

- **[[Bonytail]] (Endangered):** 162 captive broodstock, derived from 10 wild fish from [[Lake Mohave]], representing five original genetic families. The FY2002 genetic analysis by Dr. Allan Strand (College of Charleston) assigned fish to their correct families 93.4% of the time, using both genetic distance and maximum-likelihood methods. A simulation of 1,000 permutations gave the same 93.4% result. Fish produced were used for Lake Mohave and [[Lake Havasu]] augmentation.
- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (Endangered):** Three hatchery-reared broodstock strains (1991, 1981 and 1995 year classes, as reported). About 200,000 two-inch fingerlings were raised for San Juan River recovery stocking in 2002. Two shipments of 7,000 fish each went to Colorado facilities.
- **[[Chihuahua Chub]] (Threatened):** Refugium culture since 1979. In 2001, 75 wild fish were collected from the Mimbres River, and the captive broodstock totaled 84 adults and subadults. Some 10,000 fingerlings were reared for the Mimbres River in FY2003, but none were stocked in FY2002. Wild fish were chronically infected with yellow grub. Praziquantel caused high mortality, so ivermectin is to be tested.
- **[[Woundfin]] (Endangered):** A three-year interagency agreement with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] was signed August 5, 2002. Three ponds produced 2,313 young of the year. Adult survival ranged from 12.1% to 69.5%. Fish showed sunburn after two years of indoor culture. Ponds were fertilized to bloom for shade.
- **[[Guzman Beautiful Shiner]] (Threatened):** Held in refugium since 1979, with about 2,000 fish on hand. None were distributed in FY2002.
- **[[Pecos Bluntnose Shiner]] (Endangered):** 312 fish received from a drought salvage on the Pecos River. All were quarantined indoors and planned for a pond in April 2003.
- **[[Virgin River Chub]] (Endangered):** 66 adults retained from a 1989 collection. Roughly 50 young were observed in August 2002. More than 400 fish were transported from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Arizona, with three mortalities in transport and quarantine. A total of 483 fish were inventoried and appeared to be juveniles.
- **[[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]] (Endangered):** Refugium culture since 1985, aiming to increase stock to about 500.
- **[[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (Endangered):** Eggs were collected during the May 2002 egg salvage on the Rio Grande. Captive survival rose to 65%. In 2002, 18 pairs of wild stock produced 30,000 eggs, and 200 wild adults were kept as a refugium. About 12,000 VIE-tagged fish were stocked at Corrales Siphon and Bernalillo Bridge. VIE tag retention was 98% over one month and above 80% over eight months. A goal of 100,000+ fish was set for FY2003.
- **[[Yaqui Sucker]] (Special Concern):** Refugium culture since 1996. Ten of 17 broodfish from Mexico were added to the captive broodstock. Five females were injected with HCG, but only two produced a significant number of eggs.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (Endangered):** The station has been the major producer for the Lower Colorado Basin. Holdings include 133 F1 adults and 612 F1 adults/subadults from wild paired matings. A three-year Bureau of Reclamation agreement supports rearing to 12 inches or greater. A recirculation study tested fry survival at reduced water hardness, reducing Dexter well water from 2,500 ppm to roughly 250–400 ppm in 16 treated and 16 untreated tanks.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]] (Endangered):** Refugium since 1983, with about 500 fish.
- **[[Leon Springs Pupfish]] (Endangered):** Culture since 1984, with about 1,500 fish.
- **[[Comanche Springs Pupfish]] (Endangered):** An emergency refugium population from Phantom Lake Springs, Texas. Fish will be moved to the station and isolated for six months because the wild population may be extirpated. A captive stock management and genetics plan was drafted.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]] (Endangered):** Refugium since 1976, with about 500 fish.
- **[[Big Bend Gambusia]] (Endangered):** Refugium since 1974, with about 500 fish.
- **[[Pecos Gambusia]] (Endangered):** Phantom Lake Springs stock held since 1999. About 40 adults are on hand, and genetic analysis is underway.
- **[[Yaqui Catfish]] (Threatened):** Four adults remain. Genetic identification found they are hybridized with channel catfish, so they are used mainly for display and education.

### Facilities, budget and operations

- Five buildings were constructed or renovated since 1990, with a new boiler installed in the administration building.
- Five wells were redrilled, new pumps were installed, and three backup generators were added, completing Phase II.
- Southwestern Electric buried 1.5 miles of electrical cable at a total project cost of $191,723 and added a 250 kW diesel generator.
- Other purchases included a 3/4-ton pickup ($16,146), a sediment sieve ($5,660), two mowers ($9,877) and two additional phone lines ($500).
- Pickup and equipment spending is reported by species in an FY2002 expenditure chart, which lists percentages of the total budget by species.

### Partnerships and outreach

- Partners include the Upper Colorado and San Juan River recovery programs, Bureau of Reclamation, Middle Rio Grande and Pecos ecosystem teams, New Mexico Game and Fish, Arizona and Colorado agencies, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, New Mexico State University, University of New Mexico, Clemson University and the University of Houston.
- Outreach included National Fishing Week clinics, Environmental Education Week, Ruidoso River cleanup and Bitter Lake Dragonfly Festival events.
- The syndicated show *Game Warden Journal* (episode #468) filmed the Bonytail spawning.
- Dr. Holt Williamson authored a chapter in the second edition of *Fish Hatchery Management*.

### Resource needs

- An independent quarantine building with separate water supply and diagnostics lab, able to hold fish for six months.
- Replacement of the seven-year-old ABI 373 automated DNA sequencer.
- A national internet-accessible genetic database for imperiled species.
- Four unfilled positions (two biologists and two support staff). The analysis lab built in 1997 remains unused for lack of staff and equipment.
- Visitor center renovation to meet ADA standards, plus new aquarium systems and displays.
- A fire alarm for the shop building, built in 2000 without one, is planned for Phase III funding.
