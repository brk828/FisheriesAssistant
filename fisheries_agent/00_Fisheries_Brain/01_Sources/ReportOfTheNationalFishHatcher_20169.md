# National Fish Hatchery Review Panel Report (1994)

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #conservation-policy #hatchery-operations #ecosystem-management

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive review of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's National Fish Hatchery (NFH) program was conducted by an independent panel convened by the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]] in 1994. The panel assessed 78 federal hatcheries across 35 states that produce 220 million fish of 69 species annually, consuming approximately 80% of the Fishery Resources program budget ($54.1 million in 1993). The review concluded that fundamental changes are needed in the direction and management of the NFH program to support [[Ecosystem Management]] rather than simply maximizing fish production. The continuing decline of aquatic habitats and fisheries resources—with nearly 400 taxa requiring special protection and 40 species/subspecies extinct since 1900—mandates a shift from production-focused operations toward habitat restoration, protection of [[Threatened and Endangered Species]], and maintenance of biodiversity. The panel found that while hatcheries have historically been viewed as solutions to fishery declines, they have sometimes contributed to problems through introduction of non-native species, genetic pollution, disease transmission, and domestication effects that reduce fitness of wild populations.

## Background & Description

The federal fish hatchery system began in 1871 at [[Bucksport Maine]] and 1872 at [[McCloud River California]]. Three hatcheries established between 1879-1889 remain operational: [[Neosho NFH]] (Missouri), [[Leadville NFH]] (Colorado), and [[Craigbrook NFH]] (Maine). Major expansion occurred following the [[White Act]] (1930), [[Coordination Act]] (1934), and [[Mitchell Act]] (1934), with construction peaking 1934-1960. The system once reached 96 facilities but declined to 78 by 1994 through closures and state transfers beginning in the 1980s.

The current system includes 78 hatcheries (52 coldwater, 13 warmwater, 13 mixed), 9 Fish Disease Control Centers, and 6 Fish Technology Centers. Production serves multiple mandates: mitigation (38 hatcheries involved), restoration, threatened/endangered species preservation, tribal/treaty obligations, and recreational fishing. Of the 78 facilities, many are aging and would require approximately $270 million for complete restoration to full capability.

The panel comprised 16 members representing universities (4), state agencies (2), federal agencies (National Biological Survey, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Reclamation, [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], U.S. Forest Service, Pacific States Marine Fisheries Commission), private conservation (Water Watch), commercial aquaculture, and two retired USFWS employees as co-chairs. The review involved interviews with state directors, university researchers, professional societies, industry groups, field biologists, and visits to all USFWS Regional Offices through three formal meetings in 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The decline of North American fishes stems from five broad categories identified by [[Moyle and Leidy]] (1992): (1) competition for water, (2) habitat alteration, (3) pollution, (4) introduction of exotic species, and (5) commercial exploitation. Twenty-seven species and thirteen subspecies became extinct in North American waters since 1900. The panel identified habitat alteration and destruction as the major causes of decline, with hatchery-based mitigation alone proving insufficient.

The [[Pacific Northwest]] exemplifies the crisis: despite record smolt releases, [[Pacific Salmon]] runs continue declining. Multiple factors contribute: hydropower dams physically injure/kill migrating smolts, ecosystem changes from riverine to reservoir systems favor predatory species ([[Northern Pikeminnow]], [[Walleye]]), clear-cut logging and livestock grazing cause stream warming and siltation of spawning beds. Similarly, [[Atlantic Salmon]] populations decline despite intensive stocking efforts in [[New England]].

Hatchery operations themselves can harm native fauna through: (1) domestication reducing fitness under natural conditions ([[Frankham and Loebel]] 1992; [[Reisenbichler]] 1994), (2) genetic pollution when hatchery fish interbreed with wild stocks, (3) disease and parasite introduction, (4) competition and predation by stocked fish, (5) saturation of waters attracting intensive fisheries that overharvest wild fish indiscriminately. The [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] designation of [[Evolutionarily Significant Units]] (ESUs) for [[Snake River Sockeye Salmon]], [[Snake River Fall Chinook]], and [[Snake River Spring Summer Chinook]] underscores the need to protect geographic populations with evolutionary significance.

The panel emphasized that artificial propagation should not substitute for aggressive habitat restoration. Given hospitable environments, hatchery fish can establish reproducing populations, but this capability cuts both ways—non-native stocks can overwhelm indigenous species. Examples include reservoir creation destroying native warmwater habitat while stocking creates coldwater fisheries, or tailwater discharges below dams creating trout habitat where none existed naturally, requiring perpetual stocking without spawning habitat.

## Key Findings

**Primary Mission Redirection**: The panel concluded that the primary mission of federal fish hatcheries must shift from numerical production to supporting ecosystem management and habitat restoration. Hatchery functions should focus on: (1) maintaining aquatic diversity of ecosystem components, (2) restoring diminished/depleted/destroyed aquatic resources, (3) conducting technological and culture research, (4) fish health management for cultured and wild organisms, (5) objective evaluation of hatchery product success.

**Priority Hierarchy**: Applications should follow this order: (1) threatened and endangered species, (2) international and tribal treaty obligations, (3) mandated mitigation, (4) sport fish restoration, (5) recreational fishing only if consistent with national goals, done with state funding, and not conflicting with ecosystem management.

**Mitigation Program Failures**: Thirty-eight hatcheries operate under mitigation mandates (statutory or de facto), but the panel found that former mitigation concepts have failed in many cases, particularly in the Pacific Northwest. Continuation of present production levels would be "expensive, wasteful, and fruitless." The panel strongly recommends reopening mitigation issues with Congress and project sponsors to negotiate support for fish passage research, scale down production, and seek reimbursement for operations lacking pass-through funding. A 1991 audit identified approximately $1.5 million in unreimbursed costs for just two projects (four hatcheries).

**Recreational Fishing Controversy**: The majority panel position holds that using federal tax dollars for enhanced recreational fishing is not a federal responsibility since states receive license fees and tourism revenues. Federal obligations should end once self-sustaining populations are established in federally-created waters. No federal fish should stock private waters (including farm ponds) or support "put and take" fisheries. However, a minority report (Val S. Nash) argues that federal hatcheries historically provided fish for federal lands and that unilateral withdrawal would destroy partnerships and collapse recreational fisheries in multiple southeastern states (Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, Tennessee, West Virginia) where states lack immediate financial resources to assume production.

**Genetic and Health Management**: The panel found that sophisticated genetic management technologies exist but have been inconsistently applied. Before any stocking, comprehensive assessments should address: availability of suitable habitat, adequate food base, appropriate spawning sites, and potential impacts on native biodiversity. Competent population geneticists must be consulted from the beginning of any propagation effort, especially for threatened/endangered species where maintaining heterozygosity is critical with limited founder populations. Fish Disease Control Centers should evaluate health status of wild fish selected as broodstock to prevent disease introduction.

**Threatened and Endangered Species**: The panel found widespread support for using federal hatcheries to prevent extirpation of endangered species, but emphasized this must be a last resort. Rigorous population viability analysis should precede hatchery use unless taxa face imminent extinction risk. Domestication beginning at capture makes immediate habitat restoration imperative. Programs should terminate if self-sustaining wild populations cannot be restored within 3-5 generations or if habitat no longer exists/cannot be restored, with responsibility then transferred to aquaria as zoo populations.

**Facility Assessment and Divestiture**: Not all 78 NFHs will be needed under ecosystem management. Regional ecosystem management teams should evaluate each facility using criteria including: consistency with ecosystem management goals, mandated obligations, species production capability, cost-effectiveness, potential for contract/lease operation, flexibility for adaptation to non-traditional species, suitability as aquatic ecosystem assessment/restoration center, physical condition, and demonstration of non-disruption to ecosystem structure/function. Excess facilities should be divested through state transfer, leasing, or closure.

**Budget and Organizational Restructuring**: The current allocation of ~80% of Fishery Resources budget to hatchery operations is excessive given habitat restoration needs. The panel recommends revising budget preparation to focus on ecosystem management project needs rather than hatchery operations, with NFH operations becoming defined parts of Regional budgets. Regional ecosystem management teams should determine fish production needs (numbers, species, stocks, strains) to support ecosystem projects, then compare with NFH broodstock registry capabilities.

**Partnership and Communication Failures**: The panel found that policy changes occur without adequate consultation with state partners, who perceive violations of long-standing partnerships. Any changes in fish provision policies should involve advance discussion and negotiation with affected states/tribes, with time-phased implementation allowing states to develop political support and funding for new responsibilities. Negotiated phase-ins from full federal support through cost-sharing to full state takeover should be standard, with outside/contributed funds encouraged to facilitate transfers.

**Private Aquaculture Relationships**: Commercial producers focus on maximum production using domesticated, uniform, often inbred strains adequate for put-and-take fishing but unacceptable for ecosystem management or threatened/endangered species work. However, the panel recommends evaluating feasibility of leasing excess federal facilities to private operators with federal fish health assistance. The private sector should receive greater access to disease-free stocks in the federal broodstock registry to control pathogen spread. Lead federal responsibility for assisting private aquaculture should transfer to USDA, with an inter-agency group ensuring consideration of impacts from feral populations and exotic imports.

**Public Education Deficiencies**: Federal hatcheries attract thousands of visitors annually but perpetuate the impression that their role is solely producing fish ("the more the better"). Public and political perceptions remain inconsistent with Service goals. New outreach approaches must emphasize hatchery roles as vital support to ecosystem stewardship, explaining what species/strains are propagated, how they're used in ecosystem management, and why. The "more is better" stocking approach has been passé for years, but public attitudes haven't changed.

**Personnel and Expertise**: Federal hatchery managers comprise the largest assemblage of trained fish culturists globally, with expertise to propagate virtually any species, strains, or races requested by ecosystem managers. Most were initially educated in aquatic natural resources and gained specific hatchery training within the Service, making them professionally flexible for reassignment to ecosystem management, particularly aquatic habitat assessment, restoration, and management. This expertise should be shared through publications, workshops, and newsletters to enhance Service leadership and provide relevant information to user groups.

**Implementation Timeline**: The panel recognized that recommended changes require time due to budget processes and need to inform Congress and state cooperators. In the interim, NFHs should immediately implement prudent changes: interact with ecosystem managers to identify needed stocks/strains, develop broodstock in anticipation of needs, modify facilities, conduct field observations of spawning habitat, and collaborate with field biologists on habitat suitability assessments and hatchery fish performance evaluations.

**Specific Regional Issues**: In Region 1 (Pacific Northwest), major concern centers on depleted anadromous stocks; Region 2 focuses on native fishes and endangered species; Region 4 addresses recreational fishing on federal lands. Fish Disease Control Center services vary: Region 1 serves only federal hatcheries, while Regions 2 and 6 serve federal first with state/private on "as time permits" basis. This regional variation may be a strength under geographic ecosystem management but creates inconsistency in service delivery.

**Snake River Case Study**: In 1993, only a single male [[Sockeye Salmon]] returned to [[Redfish Lake]] on the [[Salmon River]], stimulating pressure to introduce non-indigenous salmon stocks. The panel strongly opposes such introductions because heavy overlays of hatchery fish may cause severe genetic or other competition to low numbers of native fish, hastening their decline toward extinction. If stocking is politically mandated, only very closely-related stocks should be used.

**Service Planning Documents**: The panel commended the Service for developing recent planning documents including "An Ecosystem Approach to Fish and Wildlife Conservation" (1994), "Action Plan for Fishery Resources and Aquatic Ecosystems" (1994), and "Vision for the Future" (1991, updated). These represent major steps toward making the NFH program more responsive to aquatic resource needs. However, vision statements without implementation strategies and action plans result in disjointed programs. The identified priorities are consistent with panel recommendations, but specific implementation strategies remain needed.

**Cost Recovery and Reimbursement**: The Service has attempted to have project beneficiaries pay for hatchery operations built to mitigate fishery damage from water projects. A 1991 audit indicated the Service should receive compensation from project sponsors, but limited progress has been made due to lack of agreements on which costs beneficiaries should assume and assessment procedures. The Bureau of Indian Affairs formerly provided funds for four hatcheries fulfilling treaty/trust obligations but ceased in 1988, with the Service operating these from appropriated funds since then.
