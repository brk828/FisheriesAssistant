## Executive Summary

This 1978 U.S. Geological Survey/Arizona Game and Fish Department document is a **drainage map and glossary**, not a conventional research paper, cataloging perennial streams, wetlands, and former wetlands ("cienegas") across Arizona. Compiled by D.E. Brown (Arizona Game and Fish Department), N.B. Carmony, and R.M. Turner (USGS), the map correlates stream elevation bands with fish thermal guilds — distinguishing cold-water fish habitat (e.g., [[Arizona Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]]) from warm-water fish habitat (e.g., [[Longfin Dace]], [[Red Shiner]]). The accompanying annotated bibliography (compiled by N.B. Carmony) documents extensive historical evidence of hydrological decline across the state — streams and wetlands that were perennial and fish-bearing in the 19th century have since become ephemeral, dry, or dominated by wastewater flow, largely due to groundwater depletion, diversion, and impoundment.

## Background & Description

The map's core purpose is to classify Arizona's streams by:
- **Flow permanence**: perennial (continuous or interrupted), intermittent, ephemeral, or wastewater-dominated.
- **Flow regulation status**: unregulated (natural [[Base Flow]] from groundwater discharge) vs. regulated (dams/reservoirs altering discharge, temperature, and sediment load).
- **Base flow magnitude**: categorized as <10, 10–50, and 50–250 cubic feet per second.
- **Elevation**: four bands (<3500 ft, 3500–5500 ft, 5500–7500 ft, >7500 ft) used as a proxy for water temperature regime and thus fish community composition.
- **Wetlands**: extant (by size: <500 acres / >500 acres) and former wetlands, historically termed "marshes" or "cienegas," many of which have dried due to arroyo cutting, groundwater decline, or diversion.

The glossary formally defines key hydrological terms including [[Base Flow]], [[Cienega]], [[Reach]], [[Regulated Flow]], and stream classifications by relation to time (perennial, intermittent, ephemeral) and space (continuous vs. interrupted).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The map explicitly links **stream elevation to fish thermal guild distribution** in Arizona:

- **<3500 ft (unregulated streams)**: Habitat exclusively for warm-water fish species, specifically [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).
- **3500–5500 ft**: Streams mainly populated by warm-water fish species.
- **5500–7500 ft**: Streams mainly populated by cold-water fish species.
- **>7500 ft**: Habitat exclusively for cold-water fish species, specifically [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*, now *Oncorhynchus apache*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*S. gairdneri*, now *O. mykiss*).

**Environmental degradation themes** recurring throughout the annotated bibliography:
- **Groundwater decline** driving loss of perennial flow (e.g., Santa Cruz River near its mouth, once perennial per Lee 1904, now ephemeral).
- **Diversion for irrigation** causing downstream drying (e.g., the Gila River below the Pima villages, dried by Indigenous irrigation diversion as observed by Bartlett in 1852).
- **Arroyo cutting/channel trenching** (Bryan, 1925) draining historic marshes and cienegas (e.g., Aravaipa Creek's former headwater marsh, San Simon Cienega, Cienega Creek near Vail, Whitewater Draw).
- **Regulated flow impacts**: Reservoirs alter discharge patterns, temperature, and sediment loads, creating conditions described as "unfavorable to native aquatic fauna."
- **Wastewater-dominated base flow**: Some streams' base flow is now mainly or entirely municipal, industrial, or agricultural wastewater, a category distinct from natural groundwater-fed base flow and posing water-quality risks to native fauna.
- Historical fish presence is used as **indirect evidence of past perennial flow** — e.g., Minckley (1973) noted fish collections from headwaters of San Bernardino Creek suggesting recent-past perennial conditions in a now-diminished system.

## Key Findings

- **Two-tiered thermal fish guild framework**: Arizona's fish fauna is ecologically stratified by elevation into cold-water and warm-water assemblages, with abrupt guild dominance shifts around the 5500 ft contour.
- **Named indicator/type species**:
  - [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Red Shiner]] as sole warm-water indicators at lowest elevations.
  - [[Arizona Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] as sole cold-water indicators at highest elevations (>7500 ft).
- **Widespread historical hydrological decline**: Numerous specific streams and cienegas documented in 19th-century accounts (San Bernardino Creek, San Simon Cienega, Aravaipa Creek marsh, Santa Cruz River, Cienega Creek, Rillito Creek, Whitewater Draw, Kanab Creek, Little Colorado River) have transitioned from perennial/marshy to ephemeral or dry, representing significant historical loss of aquatic and riparian [[Habitat]] for native fish species.
- **Flow regulation and wastewater inputs** are flagged as creating environments unfavorable to native aquatic fauna, implicating anthropogenic alteration (dams, diversions, effluent discharge) as a primary driver of fish habitat degradation in Arizona.
- **Methodological note**: The map itself functions as a baseline ecological/hydrological inventory (scale 1:1,000,000, 1978) intended to document remaining perennial fish habitat and wetlands amid ongoing statewide desiccation trends.
- Related/adjacent species not detailed but implied within the broader native fish fauna discussion: [[Gila Chub]] and other Sonoran Desert native fishes referenced contextually in Minckley's (1973) *Fishes of Arizona*, cited as the authoritative source for Arizona fish distribution data underlying this map's classifications.
