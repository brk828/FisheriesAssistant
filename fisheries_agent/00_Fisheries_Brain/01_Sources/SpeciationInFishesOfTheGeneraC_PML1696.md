# Speciation in Fishes of the Genera Cyprinodon and Empetrichthys in the Death Valley Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

Robert Rush Miller (1950, *Evolution* 4: 155–163) examines speciation in six species of two cyprinodontid genera, [[Cyprinodon]] and [[Empetrichthys]], inhabiting the isolated spring, stream and lake remnants of the [[Death Valley System]] in the [[Great Basin]]. The paper argues that [[Population Size]], [[Duration of Isolation]], [[Ecological Conditions]] (temperature, salinity) and [[Generation Time]] all modify evolutionary rates in these desert fishes. Warm springs emerge as hotspots of rapid differentiation.

## Background & Description

The Great Basin's [[Pleistocene]] history produced numerous isolated desert basins. During late Pleistocene [[Pluvial]] periods, lakes and rivers connected the four now-isolated basins into one integrated system, the Death Valley System. Miller notes that the fish fauna is small but highly endemic: 3 families, 5 genera, 10 species and about 24 kinds including subspecies. One genus, *Empetrichthys*, is endemic, and nine species are confined to the system. The high endemism at species level suggests a [[Wisconsin]] or pre-Wisconsin origin for the fauna.

The present account is limited to the six species of *Cyprinodon* and *Empetrichthys* that were treated in detail by Miller (1948).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cyprinodon** is a genus of small fishes tolerant of a wide range of physical and chemical conditions. Four distinct species occur in the region:
- *C. rodiocus* in Owens Valley, along the eastern base of the Sierra Nevada.
- *C. salinus* in Salt Creek, on the floor of Death Valley.
- *C. nevadensis* in the Amargosa River drainage, from the south end of Death Valley to just across the California–Nevada border. It has been divided into six subspecies.
- *C. diabolis* of Devil's Hole, an isolated spring in Ash Meadows, Nevada.

All four share a basic scale pattern that distinguishes Death Valley species from those elsewhere, which Miller treats as evidence that the Death Valley waters were once continuous.

**Empetrichthys** is restricted to warm springs and their outflows. It is represented by two species: *E. merriami* in Ash Meadows, and *E. latos* from Pahrump Valley, Nye County, Nevada, which has been treated as a distinct subspecies. Pahrump Valley is a wholly enclosed basin, and *E. latos* is the sole native fish there. In Ash Meadows it lives alongside *C. nevadensis* and the cyprinid *Rhinichthys osculus*. Only 22 specimens of *Empetrichthys* were secured in Ash Meadows over six years, whereas the species is "rather common" in Pahrump Valley.

Miller relates *Empetrichthys* to the Andean genus *Orestias*, whose members are rare, although the connection is not established.

## Key Findings

- **Population size (Sewall Wright effect):** In small, closely inbreeding populations, chance fixation of genes can outweigh selection. *Cyprinodon diabolis* is the clearest case. It occupies a single hot-spring hole about 20 feet long, 8 feet wide and a few inches to a few feet deep. Its population fluctuates between about 50 and 400 individuals, so the minimum effective breeding population may fall below 50.
- **Duration of isolation:** A positive correlation exists between time since isolation and degree of differentiation. Populations isolated since early Wisconsin time are now distinct species. The six *C. nevadensis* subspecies in the Amargosa basin indicate recent isolation, possibly only a few centuries to a few thousand years ago. Miller cautions that postpluvial disruption may not explain all speciation.
- **Temperature and meristic characters:** Spring-outlet populations, which are cooler than their source springs, have significantly more body scales than the source-spring fish. Each spring complex appears to contain a distinct genetic stock. Temperature is therefore associated with meristic differences, although part of the variation may be direct environmental effect.
- **Salinity:** Increasing salinity has an effect similar to decreasing temperature, producing more meristic units. *C. salinus* of the briny Salt Creek has smaller scales than any other species in the genus. The Amargosa race of *C. nevadensis* shows higher scale counts than the Saratoga Spring race in less saline water.
- **Generation time:** Warm springs (up to about 43 °C) support spawning every month of the year. Stream populations of *Cyprinodon* breed roughly from April to October and produce 2–3 generations per year. Warm-spring fish may produce 8–10 generations annually. Laboratory-reared *C. nevadensis* reached maturity and spawned at only 2 months old.
- **Experimental rearing and hybridization:** Three subspecies of *C. nevadensis* reared in concrete pools retained the meristic values of their natural populations across generations. Interspecific crosses generally lowered fertility. The most successful interspecific matings were between *C. nevadensis* and *C. maculariu*s, and success varied with the direction of the cross. Miller concludes that rapid generation turnover and high mutation rates in small isolated populations likely produced the differentiation seen in *C. diabolis*.
- **Paleontological context:** Fossil evidence is fragmentary. Miocene or early Pleistocene fossils of *Fundulus* and a *Cyprinodon* species were found in the Death Valley and Mohave regions. The presence of *Fundulus* suggests a lowland connection to the sea from the southwest, and *Cyprinodon* probably entered through the Colorado River route, with *Empetrichthys* derived from an ancestral stock. Miller stresses that these historical reconstructions remain tentative.
