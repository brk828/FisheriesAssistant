# Freshwater Drum, *Aplodinotus grunniens*, in the Navigational Impoundments of the Upper Mississippi River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by Robert L. Butler analyzes the population dynamics of the [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) in the 26-pool navigational impoundment system of the [[Upper Mississippi River]], using scale samples collected between 1944 and 1948. The paper calculates total and fishing mortality rates for heavily and lightly fished populations, compares mortality between the river's northern and southern sections, and investigates the environmental drivers of year-class strength. Key findings show that year-class strength was strongly linked to **May air and water temperatures**, with strong recruitment in 1941 and 1944 and weak recruitment in 1942, 1943, and 1945 — but was **not** correlated with water level (river stage) during spawning. The drum is identified as the third most economically important species in the river's commercial fishery, behind [[Common Carp]] and [[Buffalo Fish|Buffalo (Ictiobus sp.)]].

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Aplodinotus grunniens* (Freshwater Drum, historically called "sheepshead" in regional literature), family [[Sciaenidae]].
- **Economic ranking**: Third most abundant/valuable commercial species in the Mississippi River fishery, after [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and [[Buffalo Fish]] (*Ictiobus* sp.).
- **Study basis**: Doctoral dissertation research (University of Minnesota) synthesizing scale, weight, and length data collected by the Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee (formed December 1943 by IL, IA, MN, MO, WI conservation departments and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service).
- **Geographic scope**: 26 navigation impoundments spanning ~650 mid-channel miles between Hastings, MN, and Caruthersville, MO, divided into a **northern unit** (pools 2–11, Dubuque, IA to Hastings, MN) and a **southern unit** (pools 12–26).
- **Sampling methods**: Multi-gear test netting (hoop/buffalo nets, trap nets, wing nets, trammel nets, gill nets, seines, otter trawls, rotenone) plus commercial market sampling (Lansing, IA; Bay City, WI; Muscatine, IA) from 1944–1948.
- **Sample size**: 2,350 scale samples used (94.4% of those collected) for age/growth analysis; 1,521 drum (northern unit) and 819 drum (southern unit) used specifically for mortality/catch-curve calculations.
- **Age validation**: Scale-reading methodology and growth rates previously validated by [[Butler & Smith 1950]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **River morphology**: The Upper Mississippi River (1,163 miles, Lake Itasca to mouth of Missouri River) is impounded into 26 navigation pools averaging 25 miles long, each with two habitat types: a lake-like zone near the dam and an upper riverine zone with noticeable current.
- **Climate gradients**: Northern unit lies in a prairie/northeastern climatic gradient (mean annual temp ~44°F); southern unit lies in a prairie/southeastern gradient (mean annual temp ~56°F). July temperatures range from 73°F (Minneapolis) to 77°F (mouth of Missouri River).
- **Hydrology**: Discharge peaks in April (snowmelt) with elevated flows through May–June (spring rain); low flows occur December–February. Impoundment during dry seasons causes sedimentation in backwaters and above dam structures, reducing backwater/lateral channel volume over time.
- **Water chemistry**: Glacial till substrate produces fertile but seasonally variable water chemistry tied to discharge fluctuations.
- **Habitat types sampled**: Backwater, slough, running channel, lake (Lake Pepin, pool 4A), and main river channel (navigationally maintained at ~9.5 ft depth).
- **Spawning ecology**: Freshwater Drum spawn **pelagically** — eggs and larvae float on the surface, a trait described as unique among North American freshwater fishes. Eggs hatch in 24–48 hours; larvae remain on the surface film for a similar duration before swimming down. Spawning occurs at water temperatures of **66–72°F**.
- **Competitors/predators**: [[White Bass]] (*Roceus chrysops*) share habitat and surface-feeding behavior with young drum but do not appear to consume drum eggs/larvae based on food-habit studies. [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) and Lake Erie [[Burbot]] (*Lota lota maculosa*) consume yearling/1–2-year-old drum, but predation pressure on eggs/larvae appears minimal — implicating early-life (egg/larval stage) temperature-driven mortality as the primary driver of year-class strength, rather than predation.

## Key Findings

### Fishery & Commercial Catch
- Upper river drum fishery accounted for **10% of the total U.S. drum catch** (1944–1951), ranging **0.6–2.0 million lbs annually**.
- Peak income year: **1944** (2,187,845 lbs; $185,967).
- Northern section (pools 3–11) produced the largest catch, concentrated in pools **4A, 8, and 9**; Pool 9 alone averaged **170,000 lbs/year**.
- Southern section catches were concentrated in pools **12 (36,500 lbs)** and **19 (62,300 lbs)**.
- No closed seasons existed; regulations varied by state (bar-mesh restrictions, 10–12 inch minimum size limits, or no size limit at all). Sport fishery contribution was negligible compared to commercial harvest.

### Mortality Rates
- **Heavily fished pools (northern section)**:
  - Pool 4A: annual mortality (a) = 0.746; instantaneous rate (i) = 1.368
  - Pool 8: a = 0.513; i = 0.722
  - Pool 9: a = 0.656; i = 1.074
- **Lightly fished pools (3, 5A, 6, 10, 11)**: a = 0.466; lightest pools (3 & 6 only): a = 0.434, i = 0.568 — lowest recorded rates, likely approximating natural mortality.
- **Fishing mortality (m)**, estimated using n = 0.257 (natural mortality proxy from pools 3 & 6, age-groups V–VII):
  - Pool 8: m = 0.344
  - Pool 9: m = 0.537
  - Pool 4A: m = 0.658
- **Southern section**: test-net-derived a = 0.770 (i = 1.469) — higher than northern heavily fished pools, consistent with less restrictive regulations, though influenced by a strong 1941 year class. Market-sample-derived rates showed lower mortality (a = 0.585) than test-net estimates, suggesting sampling method significantly affects mortality estimates.
- Catch per mile per year was highest in pool 8 (5,416 lbs/mile) compared to pool 4A (2,786 lbs/mile) despite pool 4A's higher mortality rate — indicating mortality rate and total yield are not always proportional.

### Year-Class Strength & Environmental Drivers
- Strong year classes: **1941 and 1944**.
- Weak year classes: **1942, 1943, and 1945**.
- Year-class strength correlated positively with **May air and water temperatures** (deviation from long-term 1939–1948 mean) at multiple stations (Winona, Minneapolis, Keokuk, St. Louis, Cairo) — warmer Mays associated with strong recruitment; cooler Mays with weak recruitment.
- **No correlation** found between water level (river stage) during May–June spawning period and year-class strength (compared across Lake Pepin/pool 4A and pool 9, 1941–1945).
- June temperature correlation with year-class strength was weaker/less consistent than May.
- High fecundity (per [[Daiber 1953]]) combined with lack of significant egg/larval predation suggests that **temperature-driven mortality during the egg and larval (pelagic) stage** is the primary determinant of year-class strength, though the physiological mechanism was not directly tested in this study.

### Comparative Context
- [[Lake Winnebago]] (Wisconsin) drum populations showed up to tenfold yield variation (1954–1960) and dramatic shifts in age-composition under intense fishing pressure (Wirth, 1958).
- Similar year-class fluctuations documented in [[Lake Erie]] drum populations (Van Oosten, 1938).
