# Rare and Endangered Fishes of the United States: A 1964 Conservation Policy Address

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Acipenseridae #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a December 10, 1964 conference address by R. H. Stroud, Executive Vice President of the [[Sport Fishing Institute]], delivered at the National Conference on Conservation Issues in Washington, D.C. Stroud reviews the emerging federal effort to identify threatened aquatic fauna, criticizes the confusion in existing species lists, and proposes a standardized six-category status system and a joint [[Atlas of Rare and Endangered Fishes]]. He presents a tentative classification of roughly 29 endangered, 16 depleted and at least 9 peripheral fish taxa, with additional species assigned to the threatened, restored and abundant categories. The text is a policy statement rather than research, so its "findings" are classifications and recommendations, many of which are provisional by the author's own account.

## Background & Description

The address was given in the context of two new developments. The [[Federal Committee on Rare and Endangered Fish and Wildlife]] had been formalized, and the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund Act]] had recently passed, which Stroud viewed as providing previously lacking means to protect endangered species. The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] of the Interior Department had also formed a special team to study dozens of dwindling American fish and wildlife species.

Stroud observed that the public, agencies and legislators often withheld support for basic biological research until a crisis occurred, after which "crash programs" were launched with poor planning. He noted that the Federal Committee considered some 60 species of birds, fishes and mammals to be at risk of losing their status as viable species.

Stroud's own method was a survey. He wrote to 157 state and academic fishery authorities, asking for nominations and appraisals of species. The responses yielded more than 70 fish species and subspecies across 15 states, of which 45 were new nominations. The survey also included 6 reptile species and 5 aquatic mammal species. Some nominated species may already be extinct or nearly so.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Stroud framed species decline as the result of land and water development. He argued that the activities of a large, technologically advanced human population had altered much of the landscape, displacing competitive life forms and often sharply reducing their numbers. He urged governmental bodies to assess the effects of land, water and resource management on threatened or scarce aquatic resources.

Several factors complicated assessment:

- **Definitions:** There was no general agreement on terms or their meanings.
- **Taxonomy:** Stroud described the long-running dispute between "splitters" and "lumpers" over what counts as a good species. Some taxonomists favored a genetic-unit or population-based approach over the species concept.
- **Distribution data:** Knowledge of species distribution was inadequate in many cases.
- **Range changes:** The [[Sacramento Perch]] illustrated how status can change. Introductions in alkaline lakes in Nebraska, South Dakota and North Dakota significantly extended its range, so Stroud judged it no longer appropriate for an "endangered" listing.

Stroud also noted that the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] released a list of freshwater fishes described as "rare, restricted and/or threatened" (though not necessarily endangered), which was much longer than the Interior Department's list.

## Key Findings

**1. Lists disagreed sharply.** The Interior Department's "Rare and Endangered" list named only seven fish species: [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Arctic Grayling]], [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], Ohio muskellunge, [[Maryland Darter]] and Olympic mudminnow. The Michigan list was considerably longer (the count is partly illegible in the scan). Only three species appeared on both lists: the grayling, the darter and the mudminnow. The [[National Wildlife Federation]] publicized 12 species from the Michigan list as seriously endangered.

**2. Proposed action.** Stroud recommended analyzing all published and unpublished fish collection records, from state fishery biologists and museum taxonomists, to produce an atlas. The [[American Fisheries Society]] had passed a resolution calling for such an atlas as a joint venture with the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]]. Stroud argued that a composite listing, reviewed by state, academic, private and federal agencies, was necessary because single-author lists reflect the authors' special interests.

**3. Proposed six-category status system.** Stroud defined the following categories:

- **Endangered:** Taxa that have suffered progressive or substantial decline toward extinction, or that are severely restricted in range or habitat, or that are poorly known.
- **Threatened (second category):** Taxa not necessarily endangered, but potentially vulnerable because of extreme specialization. They would likely become endangered if exploited or if habitat were perceptibly altered.
- **Depleted:** Taxa substantially reduced in abundance or range but still able to support limited exploitation. These require monitoring.
- **[[Peripheral Species]]:** Taxa abundant elsewhere but present in small numbers in the U.S. at the edge of their natural range, and sensitive to marginal conditions.
- **Restored:** Once-endangered taxa showing increasing abundance through natural range extension or protection, though not yet able to support significant exploitation.
- **Abundant:** All other taxa.

**4. Tentative fish classification.** Stroud labeled the classification tentative and subject to change. Representative entries follow.

- **Endangered (about 29 species or subspecies):** Includes the [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser brevirostrum*), [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser oxyrinchus*), Columbia River white sturgeon (*Acipenser transmontanus*), [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus albus*), Lahontan cutthroat trout (*Salmo clarkii henshawi*), Mohave chub (*Gila mohavensis*), Maryland darter (*Etheostoma sellare*), Utah Lake sculpin, Arctic grayling and several cavefish and springfish taxa.
- **Depleted (about 16 species):** Includes Atlantic shad, bonytail chub, streamline chub, river redhorse, Amargosa pupfish, Guadalupe bass and Atlantic tomcod, among others.
- **Peripheral (at least 9 species):** Includes Mexican stoneroller, Sonora chub, Yaqui chub, rainwater killifish, Rio Grande darter and [[Sacramento Perch]] (*Archoplites interruptus*). Stroud treated the Sacramento perch as the example of a peripheral species that had been wrongly listed.
- **Abundant:** Three species were nominated in this category, subject to reassignment.

**5. Footnote annotations.** The list used these flags: (1) possibly extinct; (2) taxonomic validity doubtful; (3) population status confused; (4) possibly endangered; (5) possibly rare or unique; (6) possibly depleted; (7) possibly peripheral. Several entries carry more than one flag. Some entries are marked as new nominations by the SFI survey.

**6. Policy position.** The Sport Fishing Institute's stated policy urges government bodies to weigh the effects of development on scarce aquatic resources, and to maintain habitat diversity to sustain those resources.
