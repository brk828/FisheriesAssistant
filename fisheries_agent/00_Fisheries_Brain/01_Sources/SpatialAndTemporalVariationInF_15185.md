# Spatial and Temporal Variation in Fishes of Riffle Habitats: A Comparison of Analytical Approaches for the Roanoke River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Percidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[William J. Matthews]] compares three analytical approaches for partitioning spatial and temporal variation in riffle fish assemblages of the upper [[Roanoke River]] system, Virginia. Six sites were sampled by [[Kick-Set Sampling]] in five surveys between May and November 1978. The approaches were a two-way [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA) on individual species following [[Lewis Variance Components Model]], [[Morisita's Index of Similarity]] on whole assemblages, and [[UPGMA Cluster Analysis]]. Across methods, spatial variation generally exceeded temporal variation, and the author concludes that adding sampling sites is preferable to adding repeated collections at fewer sites.

## Background & Description

Stream fish studies have typically emphasized either longitudinal (spatial) or temporal variation, rarely both. The paper argues that understanding the relative magnitude of these components matters for population and community modelling (e.g., [[Lotka-Volterra Equations]] assume equilibrium or predictable fluctuation) and for planning survey effort.

The study area comprised six mainstream or large-tributary sites (fourth and fifth order; widths 8–30 m; gradients 2.5–22.2 m/km) upstream of Salem. Each site included about 100–150 m of stream, with riffles making up 25–50% of habitat. At typical flow, riffle current speeds were 10–60 cm/s and depths 20–40 cm. Inter-station distances were about 4–10 km. Small tributaries (1–2 m wide) were excluded because they held only a subset of mainstream riffle species.

Fish were collected by seining with a 4.6 m seine (4.7 mm mesh), with riffles sampled for about 30 min per site using kick-sets. Five surveys were conducted: 18–31 May, 7–23 July, 8–17 August, 20 September–4 October and 14 October–1 November 1978. The design yielded 30 space × time samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Species pool:** 2287 fish of 26 species were collected from the 30 riffle samples. Total abundance and species richness were relatively constant across periods (Table 1), with no evidence that sampling depleted riffle populations.
- **Focal taxa:** Nine most common species were analyzed individually. These include benthic darters such as [[Etheostoma flabellare]] (the most abundant darter), [[Percina roanoka]] and [[Etheostoma podostemone]], the sucker [[Moxostoma rhothoecum]], and minnows such as [[Phoxinus oreas]], [[Notropis albeolus]], [[Notropis cerasinus]], [[Nocomis leptocephalus]] and [[Campostoma anomalum]].
- **Habitat use:** Benthic darters and the sucker were overwhelmingly captured in riffles relative to pools. Minnows showed more variable riffle use; the author suggests juvenile minnows often occupy riffles while adults favour pools.
- **Season:** The warm-season window included spawning for many species, which may alter benthic habitat use (Winn, 1958). Winter was excluded.
- **Sampling-error check:** Seven repeated samples at Station 5 over 24 h (17–18 August 1988, per Appendix A; the text gives 1978 elsewhere) had a mean Morisita's index of 0.94, supporting the assumption of low sampling error.

## Key Findings

**Species-level ANOVA (Table 3)**
- Five of the nine common species showed no significant spatial or temporal variation. Four of these were minnows, and two (*N. albeolus* and *N. cerasinus*) were considered likely riffle transients.
- Four species varied significantly: *Phoxinus oreas*, *Moxostoma rhothoecum*, *Percina roanoka* and *Etheostoma flabellare*.
- For all four, fixed spatial (station) variance exceeded temporal variance. The ratio S²s:S²d ranged from 1.3 to 42 (for *E. flabellare*, 42.00).
- *M. rhothoecum* and *P. oreas* were dominated by ephemeral spatial variance; *E. flabellare* and *P. roanoka* by fixed spatial variance. The fixed-versus-ephemeral distinction was weak for *P. oreas* and *P. roanoka*.
- Data passed assumption checks: of 99 normality cases, only six skewness and five kurtosis cases were detected, all slight, and variances were homogeneous.

**Autocorrelation**
- Mean correlation between spatially nearest stations was 0.439, versus 0.397 for non-neighbours. For time, values were 0.391 (nearest) versus 0.438 (non-adjacent). Autocorrelation was judged low overall.

**Morisita's similarity (Tables 4–5)**
- Mean within-station similarity across time was 0.64, virtually identical to the mean for spatially adjacent stations at one time (0.64). Spatial and temporal differences in whole assemblages were therefore roughly equal.
- Neither station nor date effects on similarity were significant (station F = 1.404, df 5/18; date F = 0.381, df 3/20).

**Cluster analysis (Figure 3)**
- None of the 30 collections clustered by month. Stations 2, 6 and 7 tended to cluster together (Station 2 all in cluster I; Station 6 in cluster II except July; Station 7 all in cluster I), indicating spatial structure.

**Overall conclusions**
- Spatial variation generally exceeded temporal variation across methods, though the similarity index suggested near-parity.
- To survey riffle fishes adequately in this or similar systems, the author recommends increasing the number of sites rather than the number of collections per site. Repeated sampling is still advised to capture transient movements such as spawning migrations.
- The two-way ANOVA with variance partitioning had not previously been applied to stream fish distributions, and the author suggests it complements the more common similarity and multivariate methods by identifying which species drive assemblage patterns.
