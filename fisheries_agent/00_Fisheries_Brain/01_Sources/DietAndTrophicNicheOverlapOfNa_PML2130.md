# Diet and Trophic Niche Overlap of Native and Nonnative Fishes in the Gila River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study characterizes trophic linkages between native and nonnative fishes in the upper [[Gila River]] basin of southwest New Mexico, one of the few unimpounded drainages west of the Continental Divide. Gut contents and stable isotopes (δ13C and δ15N) from six reaches were used to assess diet, trophic position, resource overlap and the effects of nonnative predators. Nonnative predators, notably [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]], occupied higher trophic positions than native fishes, but nonnative predator density was not significantly associated with changes in native diets or trophic positions. The authors conclude that nonnative fishes are apex predators in these food webs and likely threaten native fish persistence, though the magnitude of impact remains uncertain.

## Background & Description

The upper Gila basin has low human influence, with land largely in federal wilderness and forest. Native fishes have nonetheless declined where nonnative fishes became established, with natural drought cycles acting as a compounding stressor (Propst et al. 2008). Native fishes are dominated by small-bodied minnows and suckers, several of which are listed by the State of New Mexico or federally (threatened or endangered), including [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. Nonnative species include predators and competitors such as Smallmouth Bass, Yellow Bullhead, [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Green Sunfish]], Red Shiner and [[Western Mosquitofish]].

Study sites were on the West, Middle and East forks of the Gila River, the Heart Bar reach below their confluence, the San Francisco River near Glenwood, and the mainstem Gila near Cliff, New Mexico. Catchment areas ranged from 295 to 4828 km². Fishes were collected in June to July 2007 and 2008 by seining and [[Electrofishing]]. Large-bodied fishes (>100 mm maximum total length) were divided into juvenile, sub-adult and adult age-classes; small-bodied species were grouped together.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trophic framework:** Trophic position was calculated two ways: from diet (weighted by prey trophic levels, algae and detritus = 1.0, predaceous invertebrates and fish = 3.0) and from δ15N, standardized to ephemeropteran baseline using a 3.4‰ fractionation per level.
- **Isotope integration:** Gut contents reflect diet over short periods (<24 h), while dorsal muscle integrates diet over weeks to months.
- **Environmental gradients:** Sites differed in elevation, stream size and water temperature. Middle Fork was about 5 °C warmer than West Fork in summer, with smallmouth bass and yellow bullhead dominating the warmer reach and nonnative trout more prevalent in the colder West Fork.
- **Invertebrate sampling:** Dominant taxa included Ephemeroptera (Baetidae, Heptageniidae, Leptohyphidae), Trichoptera (Hydropsychidae), Megaloptera (Corydalidae) and Diptera (Tabanidae). Invertebrates were grouped by functional feeding group to assign trophic levels.
- **Analytical tools:** Percent similarity and [[Principal Coordinates Analysis]] characterized diet overlap; [[Discriminant Function Analysis]] classified size-groups; [[Multivariate Analysis Of Covariance]] tested isotope differences; [[IsoSource]] modelled prey source contributions.

## Key Findings

- **Sample size:** Diets of 996 individuals (7 native and 9 nonnative species) and tissue isotopes from 787 fishes were analysed.
- **Native fish diets:** Small-bodied native fishes were primarily insectivorous, with chironomid and simuliid larvae most frequent. Adult suckers were omnivorous, consuming algae and detritus (16.0–74.0% of volume). Headwater chub was the only native species that was piscivorous, with fish in 18.0% of adult and 27.0% of sub-adult guts.
- **Nonnative diets:** Nonnative species consumed a broader range of invertebrates and more fish. Mean fish content of adult nonnative diets was about 25%: yellow bullhead 12%, channel catfish 6%, green sunfish 31%, smallmouth bass 23%, rainbow trout 8%, flathead catfish 24%, brown trout 10%. Of fish prey, 64% were suckers.
- **Empty guts:** About half (50%) of smallmouth bass and 22% of yellow bullhead had empty stomachs; rainbow and brown trout had 4% for both species.
- **Isotopes:** Nonnative adult and sub-adult predators were more enriched in δ15N (11.2–14.8‰) than native fishes. Riffle-dwelling speckled dace and loach minnow had the most depleted δ13C.
- **Trophic position agreement:** Diet- and isotope-based trophic positions were significantly correlated (r² = 0.49, P < 0.001), with a slope of 0.54 (95% CI 0.39–0.78). Isotope estimates were generally higher for omnivorous native fishes, possibly reflecting animal assimilation or lower trophic fractionation in herbivores.
- **Size-group overlap:** Discriminant analysis correctly classified 61% of species/age-classes overall, with the highest accuracy for nonnative large-bodied fishes (74%). Nonnative large-bodied fishes were the most distinct group, with the most enriched δ15N (10.4‰ estimated marginal mean).
- **Nonnative predator effects:** Constrained analysis of principal coordinates found no significant association between nonnative predator density and native diet (pseudo-F = 1.02, P = 0.42). Correlations between native trophic positions and predator density ranged from −0.59 to 0.05, all non-significant (P > 0.1). Where nonnative trout were present, native fishes had higher, not lower, trophic positions, contrary to the prediction.
- **IsoSource results:** Predaceous invertebrates were major contributors to yellow bullhead and trout diets at some sites, while juvenile suckers contributed minimally at most sites.
- **Conservation implications:** The authors argue that nonnative predators are apex predators preying on native fishes, that mitigation through removal or preclusion may be necessary where feasible, and that native fishes in the upper Gila may be near a threshold of decline, as seen in the upper [[Verde River]].
