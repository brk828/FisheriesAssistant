# Influence of Turbid Inflows on Vertical Distribution of Larval Shad and Freshwater Drum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #Clupeidae #Sciaenidae #larval-fish-ecology

## Executive Summary
This study by Matthews (1984) examined how turbid floodwater inflows into [[Lake Texoma]] (Oklahoma-Texas) altered the vertical distribution behavior of larval [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Threadfin Shad]] (collectively *Dorosoma* spp.), and [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*). Using paired Tucker-trawl tows across the 1981 and 1982 spawning seasons, the author demonstrated that during periods of high turbidity, larval shad became concentrated in a reduced surface volume of water, while larval freshwater drum — normally concentrated near the bottom during daylight — became distributed throughout the water column. Critically, this vertical shift decoupled larvae from their zooplankton prey, which remained deeper in the water column. Declines in larval shad abundance in both years followed periods when zooplankton density fell below ~100/liter, coinciding with or following high turbidity events, suggesting nutritional stress as a key factor in larval shad population dynamics within reservoir systems.

## Background & Description
- **Size classes sampled:** Larval shad most frequently collected at 7–15 mm total length (TL); *D. cepedianum* and *D. petenense* are morphologically indistinguishable at this size (per [[Shelton 1972]]).
- **Net selectivity:** Tucker trawl (1-m², 505-µm mesh) efficiently collects shad up to 15 mm TL; substantial numbers of 15–20 mm individuals are caught; individuals >20 mm are rare, indicating net avoidance develops with size/juvenile recruitment.
- **Freshwater drum development:** Eggs and prolarvae are pelagic/buoyant (per [[Davis 1959]], [[Swedberg and Walburg 1970]]), facilitating potential downstream transport during flood events.
  - 11 May 1982 (pre-flood): larvae ~5 mm TL.
  - 15 May 1982 (during inflow): ~6 mm TL, yolk sac partially to nearly absorbed.
  - 26 May 1982: 6–7 mm TL.
  - 3 June 1982: 7–8 mm TL, with some larger individuals present.
- **Species composition shift between years:** In 1981, both gizzard and threadfin shad were represented (winterkill of threadfin shad in Jan–Feb 1982 at water temps <6°C eliminated threadfin shad from 1982 larval samples, so 1982 data represent almost exclusively gizzard shad).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Limnetic zone of [[Lake Texoma]], a 36,000-hectare mainstream impoundment of the Red and Washita rivers; study reach ~18 m deep, 2–3 km wide, 40 km uplake from the dam.
- **Trophic role:** Larval shad and freshwater drum are visual, sight-feeding planktivores (per [[Hunter 1981]]) that rely on zooplankton prey including *Bosmina*, *Daphnia*, *Ceriodaphnia*, rotifers, copepods, and copepod nauplii.
- **Normal vertical distribution:**
  - Larval shad: distributed fairly evenly through the water column under clear conditions (e.g., 558/1,000 m³ at surface vs. 604/1,000 m³ at 6 m before turbid inflow, 11–15 May 1981).
  - Larval freshwater drum: normally concentrated near the bottom during daylight hours (per [[Taber 1969]]), consistent with pre-flood sampling (11 May 1982: 150/1,000 m³ at 12 m depth, scarce near surface).
- **Turbidity-driven niche shift:**
  - Turbid conditions caused both taxa to shift toward the surface, a pattern consistent with laboratory findings for [[Lake Herring]] (*Coregonus artedii*; [[Swenson and Matson 1976]]) and [[Atlantic Herring]] larvae ([[Johnston and Wildish 1982]]).
  - This created a spatial mismatch with zooplankton prey, which — per [[Dirnberger 1983]] — migrated deeper in the water column during high turbidity periods, opposite to the larvae's shift toward the surface.
- **Predator-prey dynamics:** Turbidity is known to reduce reactive distance and feeding efficiency of visually foraging fish ([[Vinyard and O'Brien 1976]], [[Moore and Moore 1976]], [[Gardner 1981]]), compounding the risk of nutritional stress when larvae and prey become vertically segregated.
- **Inflow dynamics:**
  - 1981 inflow: subsurface turbid layer at ~4 m depth, forming a surface-to-6 m turbid layer as the plume advanced.
  - 1982 inflow: surface flow extending to ~5 m depth (per [[Dirnberger 1983]]), raising reservoir level from 186.2 to 192.7 m — the second-highest level ever recorded.
  - Estimated downlake water transport velocity during 1982 flood: ~4 cm/second, sufficient to potentially transport fish larvae (per [[Barnes and Kilambi 1977]]).

## Key Findings
1. **Turbidity alters vertical distribution of larval shad:** During turbid inflow events in both 1981 and 1982, larval shad concentrated near the surface in a reduced volume of water (e.g., 10 June 1981: 3,922/1,000 m³ at surface vs. 422/1,000 m³ at 6 m under Secchi = 25 cm).
2. **Turbidity alters vertical distribution of larval freshwater drum:** Normally bottom-oriented larvae shifted to occupy the full water column, including the surface, under high turbidity (15 May 1982 turbid plume: drum abundant at all three depths sampled, including surface).
3. **Prey-larvae spatial mismatch:** Zooplankton prey moved deeper during turbid periods (per [[Dirnberger 1983]]) while larval fish moved shallower, increasing the potential for nutritional stress via reduced prey encounter rates.
4. **Zooplankton threshold linked to shad decline:** In both years, the sharpest declines in larval shad abundance occurred after total zooplankton density fell below ~100/liter — a threshold associated with starvation in laboratory studies (per [[Houde 1978]]).
5. **Gut content evidence of starvation:** In 1981, zooplankton in larval shad guts decreased markedly during increasing turbidity in early-mid June, and remained low even after water clarity improved, suggesting fish may have passed a "point of no return" (per [[Ehrlich 1974]], [[May 1974]]). In 1982, larval shad collected 3 June (during rapid population decline) had **zero food items** in 110 individuals examined.
6. **Net avoidance/size-selectivity ruled out as primary driver:** Size distributions of larvae sampled across the 1982 decline (17 May–11 June) do not support trawl size-selectivity as sufficient explanation for the observed drop in catch, since small (5 mm TL) individuals were still present shortly before the population crash.
7. **Possible larval transport:** Freshwater drum larvae increased dramatically in the study area during turbid inflow events in both years, raising the possibility of import from uplake or tributary sources; however, consistent size/developmental staging of drum larvae across the inflow period argues against wholesale replacement by transported individuals, favoring an explanation of in-situ upward migration in response to turbidity.
8. **Broader implication:** Turbidity from floods can substantially affect larval fish survival in reservoirs via combined behavioral (vertical distribution) and trophic (prey mismatch) mechanisms, an important but understudied factor in [[Reservoir Fish Population Dynamics]].
