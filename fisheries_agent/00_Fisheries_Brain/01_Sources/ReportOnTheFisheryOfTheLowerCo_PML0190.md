# Lake Havasu and the Lower Colorado River Fishery: A 1950–1951 Creel Census and Ecological Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1952 fisheries management report, prepared by Richard D. Beard for the California Department of Fish & Game (Region V), describes the fishery of the lower Colorado River reservoir-river system, with emphasis on [[Lake Havasu]], a 25,000-acre [[Reservoir]] impounded by [[Parker Dam]] in 1938. Field work began in July 1950 to establish the biological conditions affecting the sport fishery and to provide a basis for a [[Fisheries Management]] program. The study divided the river into six sections; Lake Havasu received most of the effort. The central finding is a marked decline in [[Largemouth Bass]] angling quality after about 1947, with the fishery stabilizing at a lower level by 1950–1952. Catch and effort data, littoral sampling, and comparisons with other reservoirs are presented.

## Background & Description

The study area covers the lower [[Colorado River]] from the California–Nevada state line downstream to the [[Imperial Reservoir]]. Because habitats differed widely, the river was divided into six sections:

- **Section 1:** Lotic river from the California–Nevada line about 20 miles downstream to Davis Dam. Unstable bottom, no aquatic plants, maximum temperature near 75°F, limited fish population, and marginal for trout. Channel was being dredged by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation.
- **Section 2:** [[Lake Havasu]], a large reservoir primarily supporting Largemouth Bass and Bluegill.
- **Section 3:** River below Parker Dam about 15 miles to Headgate Rock Dam. Stable bottom, abundant aquatic plants, warm water, and a large fish population, primarily a Channel Catfish and Bluegill fishery. Smallmouth Bass had been stocked.
- **Section 4:** River from Headgate Rock Dam about 100 miles to Imperial Reservoir, meandering across an alluvial valley with an unstable bottom and few aquatic plants. Channel Catfish predominated.
- **Section 5:** Imperial Reservoir, a group of small-to-medium lakes with dense aquatic plant growth and a high-quality largemouth bass fishery.
- **Section 6:** River from Imperial Dam to the international boundary, with unstable bottom and high flow fluctuations that limit productivity. Catfish predominated in the catch.

Lake Havasu is a man-made reservoir with a surface area of about 25,000 acres, formed by the completion of Parker Dam in 1938. It serves flood control, power generation, and as a forebay for the [[Metropolitan Water District]] aqueduct. The shoreline consists largely of coves: steep and rocky in the lower end and shallow with sloping gravel beaches in the upper end. Substrate is sand and gravel with silt in the more protected areas. Maximum depth is 75 feet immediately behind the dam. Riparian vegetation includes willows, salt-cedar and arrowweed. The [[Colorado River]] enters at the upper end, averaging about 16,000 cfs, while the [[Bill Williams River]] enters at the lower end with intermittent flood flows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Limnology and water quality.** Because Lake Havasu was in its 12th year as a reservoir, the author noted ongoing ecological change. Dissolved salts declined gradually, which was believed to indicate a decline in basic fertility. The lake's water level stays relatively constant, unlike many western reservoirs, with drawdown limited to the top ten feet for pumping efficiency. Water temperatures from the river entering the reservoir were relatively cool due to subsurface releases at Hoover and Davis dams. Surface temperatures between June 1 and September 7, 1951 ranged from 66°F to 76°F, averaging 71°F. No thermocline was found in the four summer depth series. Dissolved oxygen was adequate, above 3 ppm at all depths. Water clarity was exceptional, with Secchi readings normally 10 to 20 feet.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Sago pondweed (*Najas marina*) was the dominant submerged plant from 1938 to 1947, then declined sharply, with near-total failure in 1948. Linear-leaved pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) is now the dominant plant, found in scattered patches in shallow cove ends. The decline could not be attributed to water level fluctuation.

**Benthos and plankton.** The lake is described as very poor in bottom-dwelling invertebrates and aquatic insects, with plankton present only in limited amounts. Crayfish were absent from Lake Havasu but present in nearby waters.

**Fish fauna.** Of the fish species in the reservoir, only the Razorback Sucker was considered to have adapted to the new habitat. No authentic records of the Colorado Squawfish or Bonytail Chub were recorded for a number of years. A list of 15 to 16 fishes was compiled in a species table; the most abundant were Largemouth Bass, Bluegill, Green Sunfish, and Black Crappie.

**Regulations.** Angling regulations were very liberal: the season was open all year, and the bag limit was ten fish for both bass and channel catfish. No size limit applied to any species at the time. A 10-inch size limit on bass and channel catfish had applied before 1952.

## Key Findings

**Fishery history and decline**
- The quality of the Largemouth Bass fishery was good for the first eight to nine years after impoundment. It declined noticeably in the 10th and 11th years (1948 and 1949), and in the last three years (1950 to 1952) it remained fairly constant but at a much reduced level.
- The author attributed the decline partly to reduced fertility and aging of the bass population, with less aquatic vegetation for cover.

**Catch and effort**
- Voluntary concessionaire records from 1945 to 1947 were judged the most reliable. They indicated a bass catch of 1.7 bass per angler day, while channel catfish catch was 0.1 per angler day, with questionable accuracy.
- Creel census data (October 27, 1950 to June 16, 1951) covered 38 census days and 1,338 interviewed anglers during the nine-month period. Bass quality indices were 0.50 bass per angler day and 0.09 per angler hour. Bluegill was 0.28 per angler day, channel catfish 0.01, and crappie 0.01. About 90 percent of anglers sampled targeted bass.
- Bass fishing quality (bass per angler day) was 1.4 in 1945–1947 and 0.5 in 1950–1951, a substantial decline.
- In the 12-month period July 1, 1950 to June 30, 1951, total angler use was estimated at 30,351 angler days (about 1.27 angler days per surface acre). Total boat trips were estimated at 12,338, with an average of 2.46 anglers per boat.
- Estimated total catch for the year was 33,091 bass, the largest catch species. Bass made up 59 percent of catch by weight and 21 percent by number. Bluegill made up 67 percent by number.
- Bass production was 0.76 pounds per surface acre, which the author considered extremely low despite light angling pressure.

**Littoral fish populations**
- Seine sampling showed small forage fishes (bluegill and green sunfish under 3 inches) concentrated in the sago pondweed beds.
- A rotenone sampling of a half-acre cove (August 3, 1950) and a one-half-acre cove (June 6, 1951) provided size-class data for sub-adult fishes. Bluegill, green sunfish and bass were the most common fish collected.
- Gill netting of seven sets of standard gill nets in spring and summer produced adult catches of bluegill, crappie, catfish and carp.

**Growth and reproduction**
- Largemouth bass growth in the second and third year classes compared favorably with other waters, but growth in the zero and first year classes appeared retarded.
- Bass spawning extended from late March to early June, peaking in mid-April. Spring 1951 and 1952 spawning appeared very successful. One day in April 1952 alone, eight schools of fry were observed along about 1.5 miles of shoreline.
- Summer predation pressure from adult crappie and bluegill is believed to heavily reduce the fry and fingerling population.

**Diet**
- Subadult bass stomach contents (40 specimens, 2.2 to 8 inches) consisted mainly of bluegill and green sunfish, 0.5 to 0.75 inches long. Forage fish of this size was scarce.

**Comparison with other waters**
- Lake Havasu's bass yield (0.76 pounds per acre) and bass per acre (0.60 numbers per acre, 1950–1951) were lower than those of Lake Norris (Tennessee), Lake Mead, and Clear Lake (California). Average bass length in the sport catch compared favorably with Clear Lake and Norris Reservoir.
- The author concluded there was no indication of overfishing, although Lake Havasu had a relatively low carrying capacity.

**Management implications**
- The author suggested that the fishery is limited mainly by productivity rather than angling pressure.
- About 55 percent of the catch had been legal for at least one season before capture, and 19 percent had passed through two or more seasons, suggesting most fish are harvested after reaching catchable size.
