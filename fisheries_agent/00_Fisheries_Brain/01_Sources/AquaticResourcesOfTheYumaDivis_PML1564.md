# Aquatic Resources of the Yuma Division, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #fish-community

## Executive Summary

This draft final report (May 1985), prepared by Paul C. Marsh and W. L. Minckley for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] under contract 2-07-30-X0214, documents the physical, chemical and biological attributes of the 32.5-km Yuma Division of the [[Lower Colorado River]] from November 1982 to August 1984. The study was designed as a preproject baseline but was overtaken by unanticipated high flows in 1983–84, which rose from about 10 m³/s to a maximum daily average near 750 m³/s in August 1983. The study therefore became an opportunistic survey. High flows inundated hundreds of hectares of riparian land and created extensive shallow, warm backwater habitat, which proved to be the basis of fish production in the reach. Benthic macroinvertebrates, zooplankton and phytoplankton were sparse. The fish community comprised 19 species collected, dominated by introduced fishes, with the native [[Striped Mullet]] the only native species present.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] is a highly modified desert river. Its lower reaches have been channelized, dredged and impounded, and nonnative species have replaced most native fishes. The Yuma Division is the ninth of ten operational divisions downstream of Davis Dam. It runs from [[Laguna Dam]] to [[Morelos Dam]] and forms part of the international boundary. An estimated 90% of the division had been modified by riprap, levees, dikes, channelization or inundation (Minckley 1979, cited in report).

The division functions as a delivery channel with no major withdrawals. Its only significant natural inflow is the [[Gila River]], which is now usually dry, while perennial flow derives from irrigation return, groundwater and municipal effluent. Prior to closure of upstream dams, mean discharge at Yuma was about 565 m³/s. Under regulation it had varied from 3 to 30 m³/s until early 1983.

The study was framed by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Reclamation), which proposed selective clearing of floodplain riparian vegetation to restore channel capacity. Original objectives were to: (1) test accuracy of environmental impact statement predictions, (2) assess effectiveness of aquatic mitigation and enhancement, and (3) provide planning information for future projects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites and habitats.** Sixteen designated sites (8 main channel, 8 backwater/off-channel) were sampled at low flow. High flows obliterated or altered most of them. Six generalized habitat types were then used: (I) deep main channel, (II) main channel beaches, bars and banks, (III) deep inundated floodplain, (IV) shallow inundated floodplain, (V) connecting channels, and (VI) isolated pools (Table 3).

**Methods.** Two sampling regimes were used: site-transect sampling at low flow (Nov 1982, Feb, May and Aug 1983 and 1984), and survey sampling at high flow. Fishes were collected by [[Electrofishing]], [[Seine Netting]], [[Hoop Net]] trapping, gill and trammel nets. Benthos was taken with a 232 cm² [[Ponar Grab]]. Zooplankton, phytoplankton and [[Chlorophyll]] were sampled with a 3.2-L horizontal water sampler. Water physicochemistry was measured with a Hydrolab multiparameter probe.

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Temperature and dissolved oxygen followed predictable seasonal patterns. Other parameters, including pH, conductivity and turbidity, remained relatively stable. Dissolved oxygen was the only parameter approaching levels potentially harmful to biota, but no fish mortality was observed except in drying isolated pools.

**Benthic macroinvertebrates.** The benthos was depauperate: only 37 taxa were recorded, and most were rare. Chironomid dipterans and oligochaete worms dominated. Causes proposed included sandy substrates, unstable substrates after high flows, predation by fishes, and possible pesticide effects. Aerial mosquito control with organophosphates and Bti (Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis) was applied in both 1983 and 1984.

**Plankton and chlorophyll.** Twenty-one zooplankton taxa were identified. Copepods predominated, and densities were inversely related to flow. Phytoplankton included 94 species among 49 genera, with diatoms dominant. Chlorophyll concentrations were low, with mean chl a falling from 2.5 µg/L in low flow to 1.0 µg/L in high flow.

**Fish community and habitat use.** Backwaters were the principal fish habitat. Their warm, shallow, low-velocity water provided spawning, nursery and refuge habitat. Main channel beaches and bars were occupied largely by Red Shiner. Isolated pools were ephemeral "death traps" that dried in summer.

## Key Findings

- **Hydrology:** Mean daily flow at Laguna Dam rose from 9.15 m³/s (November 1982) to nearly 750 m³/s (August 1983). Flows stayed near 500 m³/s through the end of fieldwork.
- **Habitat creation:** The main new habitat was extensive shallow backwater, which replaced the earlier main channel-and-backwater pattern.
- **Fish community:** 19 species collected. Red Shiner made up more than one third of all fishes (36.9%). Blue Tilapia was about 18% of all fishes, and Common Carp, Mosquitofish, Threadfin Shad and Sailfin Molly were each about 7–12%. Largemouth Bass was 1.58% and Striped Mullet 0.54%.
- **Method selectivity:** Nearly 92% of fishes were taken by seine. Eight of 18 species were missed by at least one method.
- **Population shifts:** Threadfin Shad, Common Carp and Largemouth Bass increased in backwaters in 1983 but collapsed by 1984. Sailfin Molly virtually disappeared after high flows. Mosquitofish and Red Shiner stayed high. Blue Tilapia expanded strongly in summer 1984 (96.6% of an isolated pool sample in June 1984).
- **Threatened native fish:** Striped Mullet was the only native fish present, with 345 individuals taken, and no evidence of reproduction in the division.
- **Predicted future:** Blue Tilapia was expected to become the predominant fish. Flathead and Channel Catfish were expected to remain stable. Sustained low flows would likely reduce backwater-dependent species.
- **Caveat:** Authors state that accurate assessment of cause and effect cannot be made for the fish community as a whole. Data gaps include population dynamics, growth and recruitment.
