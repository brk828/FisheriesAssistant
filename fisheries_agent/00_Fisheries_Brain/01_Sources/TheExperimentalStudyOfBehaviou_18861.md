# The Experimental Study of Behaviour in Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This is a review chapter by F.E.J. Fry (Department of Zoology, University of Toronto), published in the *Proceedings of the Indo-Pacific Fisheries Council* (III), pages 37–42, in 1958. It surveys laboratory and aquarium methods for studying fish behaviour, with emphasis on the **ecological level** of organisation, because fisheries workers are most concerned with the economic consequences of behaviour. The review covers physiological methods (diel rhythms and work capacity), the classification of orientation responses, and the design of [[Experimental Gradient Apparatus]] used to test fish reactions to light, temperature, current and chemicals. It closes with a long bibliography. The text is a methods-oriented synthesis rather than a set of new experimental results.

## Background & Description

The review begins with Scott's (1958) classification of behaviour into levels of organisation: ecological, social, organismic (psychological), physiological, organ, cell and genetic (see [[Levels Of Behavioural Organisation]]). Fry notes that the review cannot be complete, since fields such as learning (organismic level) and genetics receive different attention. The ecological and social levels were the focus of fisheries workers, and the social level had already been covered in a chapter of *The Physiology of Fishes* (Brown, 1957).

Two illustrative observations are given:

- **Sensory capacity:** Fish should not be judged by human sensory abilities. Fry cites the demonstration that fish use the [[Sun Compass Orientation]] (Hasler et al., 1958), which implies a sense of time that humans do not possess.
- **Genetic behaviour patterns:** Young [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) feign death and hang limply in the water when disturbed. Young [[Maskinonge]] (*Esox masquinongi*) do not show this trait, and the hybrid behaves like the pike.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diel rhythms.** Fish rhythms have been measured mainly by quantifying random movement. Methods include direct observation under infra-red light, tethered recording arms (Spencer, 1939), loose-mesh barrier recorders (Wikgren, 1956), oxygen consumption (Clausen, 1936), opercular counts (Pora and Nitu, 1952), weight changes on a suspended false bottom in flatfish (Harder and Hempel, 1954), and water turbulence recorded by mechanical devices. A [[Girella]] (*Girella punctata*) shows strong light-seeking in daytime but not at night (Kawamoto and Niki, 1952). Fry stresses that apparatus defects can change results. Jones (1956) found that [[Minnow]] (*Phoxinus phoxinus*) activity rhythms reversed depending on whether shelter was provided.

**Work capacity.** Capacity for work is usually measured by inducing rheotropic or pseudo-rheotropic swimming in a [[Swimming Flume]] or rotating chamber. Fry notes that flumes give a better rheotactic stimulus because visual and turbulence cues reinforce each other, whereas in rotating chambers the moving bottom opposes the visual field. Simple designs include annular troughs and Hoar's "rheotactic tubs."

**Orientation.** Fraenkel and Gunn (1940) separate primary orientation (the normal resting or moving position relative to fixed marks) from secondary orientation (directed movement triggered by external stimuli). Their scheme (Table II) divides responses into kineses (undirected, e.g. orthokinesis and klinokinesis) and taxes (directed, e.g. klinotaxis, tropotaxis and telotaxis), plus transverse orientations such as the light compass reaction and ventral gravity reaction.

**Observer error and context.** Blum's (1934, 1954) work on the copepod *Harpacticus fulvus* showed that a negative phototaxis in a cylinder appeared as a positive photokinesis in a shallow dish, which Blum called a "phototactic ratchet." Ozaki (1951) found that an isolated individual of a schooling fish did not react to light of various wavelengths, but did respond to light when paired with a conspecific. Hasler (1956) found that minor metal irregularities in a tank oriented fish more strongly than an artificial sun. Tait (personal communication) observed brown trout in a permanent temperature gradient for over a year. Dominant fish held territories regardless of season, while subordinate fish showed the expected seasonal change in thermal preference (see [[Thermal Preference]]).

## Key Findings

- **Apparatus classification.** Fry groups gradient apparatus by direction:
  - *Horizontal linear:* the Shelford-type tank (first used, Shelford and Allee, 1913), with inflows at each end and a central drain. Used for temperature, salinity and oxygen, and extended to light gradients (Jones, 1955).
  - *Horizontal annular:* allows continuous swimming in one direction. Examples include the Hoar (1956) temperature ring and a valve system shifting the gradient to four points 90° apart.
  - *Horizontal transverse:* divided troughs requiring lateral choice. These were the main tools for [[Current Velocity Preference]] research (e.g. Carney and Adkins, 1955) and were used extensively at the University of Washington.
  - *Rosette:* radial choice chambers around a central basin, used for colour response (Kawamoto and Takeda, 1950, 1951; Ozaki, 1951, 1952) and salmon odour tests (Wisby, 1952). A model at the Ontario Fisheries Research Laboratory uses an odd number of chambers, with fish trapped once they fully enter a chamber.
  - *Concentric:* isopleths radiate from a point, as in a circular pond fed at the periphery and drained at the centre (Davidson, 1949).
  - *Vertical linear:* the simplest design for temperature selection, using a double heat exchanger. Fry notes that a full vertical gradient cannot be made from 0°C freshwater because of anomalous expansion below 4°C.
- **Measuring responses.** The usual index is the number of individuals in each gradient segment, tested against chance with the chi-square (χ²) test, whose magnitude expresses reaction intensity. Ozaki (1952) used three indices: visits multiplied by stay duration, number of visits, and number of colours visited.
- **Temperature selection.** Temperature preference is fairly sharp, so a gradient of about 10 centigrade degrees is usually adequate (e.g. Pitt, Garside and Hepburn, 1956; Garside and Tait, 1958). Long-term experiments must add cold water at the tank bottom because fish consume dissolved oxygen.
- **Stability of gradients.** Density differences are controlled by water-tight partitions (Baggerman, 1957; Houston, 1957) or by constrictions with vigorous stirring for temperature gradients. Dissolved-substance gradients are stabilised by a slight vertical temperature gradient.
- **Research gaps.** Fry notes that learning (organismic level) is largely excluded, and that apparatus must be designed so that the same sense organs can be tested in both kinesis and taxis conditions.
- **Scope.** The bibliography (pp. 106–116) contains a very large number of references, from early 20th-century work to 1959. It covers fish, invertebrate and plankton orientation, and sensory physiology.
