# Origin of Broodstock and Allozyme Variation in Hatchery-Reared Bonytail, an Endangered North American Cyprinid Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Minckley, Buth and Hayden, documents how a broodstock of the critically endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) was founded from wild fish caught in [[Lake Mohave]], and evaluates genetic variation in the hatchery-produced stock. Allozyme analysis of 24 volunteer F2 juveniles from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] found six polymorphic loci out of 45 examined. Genotype frequencies agreed with [[Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium]] expectations. Mean heterozygosity was lower than in the congener [[Arroyo Chub]] (*G. orcutti*) but comparable to western North American cyprinid averages. The authors conclude the stock is genetically variable enough for reintroduction and support its continued use, while urging a wider search for remaining wild populations.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The bonytail is a streamlined, riverine cyprinid that once inhabited the larger streams of the entire [[Colorado River]] system (Wyoming, Colorado, USA, to northwestern Mexico). It is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973.
- **Current status:** Since 1976 only one individual was recorded in the upper Colorado River (1984). Fewer than 10 were taken in the Green River sub-basin, and no more than five in Cataract Canyon. The largest remaining number, 34 specimens, came from Lake Mohave (1975-1988). Lake Mohave was impounded in 1954.
- **Hybridization concerns:** Some upper-basin specimens show morphological signs of hybridization with [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*) or [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), raising questions of genetic contamination. Lake Mohave specimens showed no external signs of hybridization.
- **Causes of decline:** Habitat destruction and alteration through water developments, introduction of non-native fishes, and other human-induced factors. Large adults persist, but no recruitment into the adult population has been detected since perhaps the 1950s.
- **Management response:** In the 1970s the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) decided to establish a broodstock and develop holding, culture and reintroduction capabilities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Mohave as refuge:** The main surviving population is in this lower Colorado River reservoir, which is also the site of wild broodstock capture.
- **Parallel decline:** The authors note that [[Razorback Sucker]] in Lake Mohave shows the same pattern of adult persistence without recruitment.
- **Spawning evidence:** Jones and Sumner (1954) estimated about 500 bonytail broadcasting gametes over a gravelly shelf in Lake Mohave. At Dexter, presumed spawning behavior (milling, chasing, nudging) was observed among more than 200 fish.
- **Wild genetic diversity:** Wild populations are so reduced that an accurate measure of genetic diversity in nature may no longer be possible. Comparison with other species is therefore the only available reference.
- **Recovery framing:** The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] (USFWS 1988) called for transfer of any bonytail found in the wild into captivity as insurance against extinction.

## Key Findings

### 1. Broodstock origin and capture

- An original stock of 18 *G. elegans* was caught in trammel nets from Lake Mohave: February 1976 (3), April 1979 (2), April 1980 (5), March 1981 (8).
- The first 10 fish (1975-1980) were large (>45 cm TL) old females, raising concern that males had disappeared through differential mortality. Only five of these 10 were successfully transported and maintained at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Arizona.
- Otolith ages from four dead fish were 34, 40, 42 and 49 years.
- Seven fish from 1981 (five males, two females) were transported successfully. After hormone-induced gonadal development in May 1981, five females and five males were spawned. Females measured 45.9-55.4 cm TL and males 45.4-48.0 cm TL.

### 2. Artificial propagation

- Hatching averaged 95% (range 91-99%) from an estimated 125,450 ova from five females.
- About 80,000 larvae were transferred immediately to grow-out ponds at Dexter.
- Roughly 1,700 F1 adults (1981 year class) were present at Dexter in 1988.
- A technique developed by [[Hamman]] (1981) used five pairs of wild-caught fish, with eggs from each female fertilized by sperm from a different male. This was successfully repeated in 1983 and 1984 spawning trials with hatchery-reared fish.
- Stocking into Lake Mohave (1981-1987) totaled about 130,000 fish, including 54,941 original F1, manually cultured F2 (about 28,700), and F2 "volunteers" (46,629).

### 3. Recaptures and survival

- Field efforts after 1981 yielded 15 additional bonytail (1983, 1985 and 1988). Two were taken by fishermen and not retained, and eight died in transit or shortly after from injuries, secondary infections or unknown causes.
- Six large fish captured in 1988 were in good condition at Dexter.
- Otoliths from four of five 1988 specimens that died showed six annuli, consistent with F1 fish stocked in 1981. Wild or hatchery origin cannot be determined without sacrifice, because lower-basin bonytail cannot be reliably aged by scales.
- Growth rates suggest that fish stocked in 1981 could have reached about 40 cm TL by 1985 and about 40-50 cm TL by 1988.

### 4. Allozyme analysis

- Soluble gene products of 45 loci were resolved from skeletal muscle, liver and brain tissues of 24 volunteer F2 juveniles (1985 year class, 65-95 mm SL).
- Six loci were polymorphic: sAat-A, Gpi-B and Pgm-A (muscle); Est-2 (brain); and Cat-1 and Iddh-A (liver).
- Electromorph distributions at each polymorphic locus agreed with Hardy-Weinberg expectations. Allelic association was maximal, with an exact probability of 1.00 at five of six variable loci.
- Mean number of alleles per locus was 1.2 ± 0.1 for both 45-locus and 29-locus comparisons.
- The proportion of polymorphic loci was 0.133 (45 loci) and 0.138 (29 loci).

### 5. Comparison with congeners and cyprinids

- Only the arroyo chub (*G. orcutti*) had enough homologous loci (29 in common) for direct comparison.
- Mean heterozygosity was lower in bonytail than in arroyo chub. The text gives approximately 0.061 ± 0.030 for arroyo chub versus 0.025 ± 0.017 for bonytail, so arroyo chub is about twice as variable. (The Table 3 values are partly garbled in the scan, so these figures should be checked against the original.)
- Avise (1977) reported a mean of 0.038 ± 0.009 for western North American cyprinid genera (including *Gila*). Bonytail heterozygosity is slightly below this mean but direct-count values are comparable.

### 6. Conclusions and recommendations

- The authors judged the Dexter stock genetically variable enough for reintroduction. They saw no unusual morphological abnormalities, and the fish reproduce naturally (though in hatchery ponds) and survived to adulthood when stocked in Lake Mohave.
- They noted that the Dexter broodstock may represent only part of the species' genetic variability, and that reintroducing low-variability fish could alter the genetic structure of small wild populations.
- They recommend continued use of the Dexter stock for reintroduction in the lower Colorado River basin, and strong support for an expanded search for other bonytail populations.
