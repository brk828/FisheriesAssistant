# Biological Survey of the Sierra del Carmen and Santa Rosa Mountains, Coahuila (1936)

#wildlife-survey #ornithology #mammalogy #herpetology #botany

*Note on scope: this document is a terrestrial field survey. It contains no fish, and no freshwater or marine fauna are reported beyond a few birds and mammals that use water sources. The aquatic content is limited to springs, tanks, and rivers as habitat.*

## Executive Summary

A 1936 field report by Ernest G. Marsh Jr., a Student Naturalist Technician working for the [[National Park Service]], documents a biological survey of the [[Sierra del Carmen]] and [[Santa Rosa Mountains]] in northern [[Coahuila]], Mexico. The survey lay across the [[Rio Grande]] from the [[Big Bend National Park]] in Texas. Fieldwork ran from early July to late September 1936. The report covers birds (an annotated list), reptiles and amphibians, mammals, photographs, and a taxonomic plant list. The author concludes with a recommendation for continuous, cooperative wildlife research between Mexico and the United States to support the ecological rehabilitation of the Big Bend.

The source is a survey report and proposal, not a fish study. Its main results are descriptive species accounts, observations on predator-livestock conflict, and a call for a bi-national research program.

## Background & Description

- **Author and role:** Ernest G. Marsh Jr., Student Naturalist Technician, National Park Service. Affiliated with the Department of Botany, [[University of Texas]], Austin. The report was submitted March 10, 1937 (scan reads "Uarch 10, 1937").
- **Objective:** Begin a biological survey of the Yuzquie-Zoquillas region of northern Coahuila, with emphasis on the Sierra del Carmen, lying roughly 30 to 100 miles south of the US boundary.
- **Dates:** The report heading reads "July 2 to September 22, 1936." The narrative states the author returned to Austin on September 25.
- **Route:** Entry via [[Piedras Negras]] and [[Muzquiz]]. Work proceeded through [[La Mariposa Ranch]], [[Santa Anna Canyon]], [[El Zacate]], [[La Encantada]], [[Vivoras Canyon]], [[Jardin del Sur]], [[Piedra Blanca]], and [[La Bavia Ranch]]. The author camped at Vivoras Canyon (7,500 ft) from July 2 and moved to Jardin del Sur on August 23.
- **Logistics:** Collecting permits came from the Mexican Department of Caza, Pesca y Forestal. Bird ammunition and film were supplied from the NPS regional office in Oklahoma City. The University of Texas supplied presses and blotters for plant collection. Entry into Mexico was delayed about eighteen days by customs officials at Piedras Negras, who required a federal ruling from Mexico City on each question.
- **Report structure:** Route narrative, photographic summary (with 40 colored slides prepared by the University of Texas Visual Instruction Bureau), annotated bird list, reptile and mammal accounts, plant taxonomic list, and a suggestion for further work.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Landscape and habitat**
- The Sierra del Carmen has a fault-line escarpment about 3,000 ft high, with the [[Fresno Mesa]] (about 40 square miles of grassland) lying to the west. Canyons, rocky limestone hills, and pine-oak forest occur in the upper mountains, with mesquite, huisache, and grassland on the plains.
- Upper canyons contain conifers (pines and firs), and a 150 ft Rocky Mountain longleaf pine is pictured. Oak, madrone, and cypress are also noted.
- Desert grasses, sotol, lechuguilla, and maguey dominate lower slopes.

**Water and aquatic habitat**
- The author describes the junction of three mountain springs forming the Rio de las Cabinas (River of Cypress), called the only permanent natural body of water in northern Coahuila. Cypress trees line the clear water.
- Water tanks (stock ponds) such as those at [[Western Hills Tank]] and La Bavia were important for waterfowl and shorebirds, as well as for many mammals.
- The Sabinas River is noted near Muzquiz. Beaver are absent from the Sabinas but occur in small numbers in the Rio Grande above Boquillas (three to six killed annually by trappers).
- No fish are reported, and no fish surveys were conducted.

**Land use and disturbance**
- The author describes the Sierra del Carmen as much less degraded than the Texas Big Bend. Large areas had been free of livestock for twenty years or more.
- Domestic stock in northern Coahuila had more than doubled over the previous fifteen years.
- Livestock grazing, hunting by American parties, and the presence of predators all shape wildlife numbers.

**Predator and prey relationships**
- Golden Eagles were reported to kill young calves. Seven weaned calves were killed at La Mariposa in spring 1934.
- Wolves were increasing; Santa Domingo reported 200 cattle killed by wolf packs in 1934 and 1935.
- Bears were reported as increasing. Three bears had been taken in the region since 1932, and there were no rigid preservation rules.
- Mountain lions were regarded as numerous. Several were reported killed around Jardin Ranch and Piedra Blanca over five years.

## Key Findings

### Birds
- The annotated bird list covers numerous species, with notable counts:
  - Green-winged Teal: approximately 100 birds on Western Hills tanks on September 4.
  - Pintail: several hundred seen near Muzquiz in December.
  - Band-tailed Pigeon: flocks of as many as one hundred, feeding on acorns and wild cherries. One female collected on August 9, 1936, held twenty-three acorns in her crop.
  - Mearns' Quail: one covey of about fifty birds fed for a week in mesquites on the mesa.
  - Wild Turkey: present in small numbers between Piedra Blanca and Santa Domingo.
  - Mexican Cormorant: one male collected at La Bavia tank, July 24, 1936, considered a rare visitor from the south.
- Recorded raptors include the Golden Eagle, Harris' Hawk, Swainson's Hawk, and Sharp-shinned Hawk.
- Common passerines include the Couch Jay, Western Crow, Cowbird, Western Meadowlark, Lloyd's Bush-tit, and Canyon Towhee.

### Reptiles and Amphibians
- Green Rattlesnake (*Crotalus molossus*): nine specimens seen from 9,000 ft in Vivoras Canyon to 3,000 ft in the Western Hills; one specimen collected.
- Western Diamondback Rattlesnake (*Crotalus atrox*): the skin of a five-foot specimen was seen at Piedra Blanca.
- Bull snake (*Pituophis sayi affinis*): a seven-foot specimen collected August 15 in Shuperosa Canyon.
- Coral snake (*Elaps fulvius*): reported as common in the northern Carmen, with specimens killed at Jardin and Piedra Blanca.
- Lizards recorded include the Texas horned lizard (*Phrynosoma cornutum*), the Sceloporus spiny lizards, and the Sceloporus variabilis.

### Mammals
- Bats: Big Brown Bat was very numerous from the lower plains to about 8,000 ft. Little Brown Bats were abundant over the Western Hills tank.
- Carnivores: Mexican Coati (reported from Mariposa and Encantada), Ring-tailed Cat, Raccoon (declining; last one from northern Del Carmen trapped in 1930), Badger, Coyote, Gray Wolf, Mexican Cougar, Jaguar, Ocelot, and Bobcat are described.
- Ungulates: White-tailed Deer (27 deer passed one glade in half an hour), Sonora White-tailed Deer (or Arizona Flagtail), Mexican Mule Deer (rare, few records), Antelope (restricted to Encantada flats; no change in numbers reported over ten years), and Texas Bighorn Sheep (one killed at Encantada in 1932; a skull found at about 9,000 ft south of La Sierra del Jardin).
- Rodents: Couch Rock Squirrel (common in the Del Carmen; a colony at Jardin del Sur lost its fear of humans), Rio Grande Ground Squirrel, Texas Antelope Squirrel (two sightings), and Pocket Gopher (probable, not confirmed).
- Peccary (javelina): common on plains and foothills from 2,000 to 3,500 ft in the Santa Rosa Mountains; the author describes defensive behavior in detail.

### Plants
- The author collected several thousand specimens representing about 850 species. The list is a provisional taxonomic enumeration (field numbers up to 1046), with some names classified as new species (e.g., *Quercus marshii*, *Aristolochia kershii*, *Nama lumbrica*).
- Families represented include Poaceae (grasses), Fagaceae (oaks), Fabaceae (legumes), Asteraceae (composites), Euphorbiaceae, Malvaceae, Cactaceae, and Pinaceae.
- The plant collection was being prepared for the National Park Service, and a separate paper was planned.

### Recommendations
The author recommends that wildlife research in the Sierra del Carmen become continuous through a cooperative program between the Mexican Department of Caza, Pesca y Forestal and the [[National Park Service]]. Reasons given:

1. Such a program would be welcomed and beneficial to the Mexican department.
2. It would promote cooperation between the two nations' corresponding departments.
3. It would help speed the rehabilitation of the Big Bend National Park area over time.

The author argues that the Sierra del Carmen is structurally and biologically nearly identical to the Texas Big Bend and the [[Chisos Mountains]], and that it has been much less affected by livestock and hunting. He proposes that areas be chosen in the Sierra del Carmen for extended study, so that results could be applied to the Big Bend. He states that experimental plots in the Big Bend itself would not give natural conditions, and that the original state of the wildlife can be better understood in the undisturbed Mexican mountains.
