# Habitat Use by Hatchery-Reared Adult Razorback Suckers Released into the Lower Colorado River, California-Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Bradford and Gurtin]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department, North American Journal of Fisheries Management, 2000) examined how hatchery-reared adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered catostomid, used available aquatic habitat in the Imperial Division of the [[Colorado River]]. The Imperial Division was designated as critical habitat under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1994, but little habitat-use information existed for it. Eighty-seven ultrasonically tagged fish (413-592 mm total length) were released into the lower 9.6 km of the reach and tracked every 2-3 weeks from December 1995 to November 1998. Fish used habitat non-randomly: backwaters and the impoundment were selected, the main channel was avoided, and side channels were used roughly in proportion to availability. The authors conclude that off-channel backwaters are important to the species and that their loss could impair recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The razorback sucker was historically abundant in the main stem and major tributaries of the Colorado River, but was nearly extirpated from the lower basin following river regulation and introduction of nonnative fishes. Extant lower-basin populations are mostly in [[Lake Mohave]], with smaller numbers in [[Lake Mead]] and Senator Wash Reservoir.
- **Listing:** Listed as endangered in November 1991; much of its historic range, including the 100-year floodplain of the Imperial Division, was designated critical habitat in April 1994.
- **Study fish:** Three release groups (25, 35 and 27 fish) were derived from [[Lake Mohave]] broodstock at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico, and held in outdoor ponds for at least 18 months before surgery. Transmitters were ultrasonic (73-80 kHz, approx. 8 g) with PIT tags added.
- **Study area:** About 64 km of the lower Colorado River between [[Imperial Dam]] and Adobe Ruins was the management reach; the researchers restricted fieldwork to the lower 9.6 km because of logistic constraints. Main-stem temperatures typically ranged 9-31 °C and regulated flows 65-346 m³/s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat classes:** Available habitat was mapped from aerial photographs with ground truthing and divided into four types: main channel (48% of the total surface area, 279.65 ha), backwaters (26 sites, 0.06-28.10 ha), side channels (9 sites, 0.21-4.02 ha), and the impoundment (1.32 ha, immediately upstream of Imperial Dam). Backwaters were permanent, hydraulically connected but low-flow waters, often with constricted entrances from sedimentation and vegetation.
- **Floodplain vegetation:** Floodplain areas supported cattail, giant reed, bulrush, salt cedar, mesquite and palo verde.
- **Upper vs. lower basin:** The upper basin has steeper, more channelized, erosion-prone main stems with pools, runs, bars and eddies. The lower Imperial Division is low-gradient, depositional, and relatively homogeneous, lacking pools, riffles and eddies. Upper-basin backwaters are typically gravel pits or seasonally flooded lowlands, whereas Imperial backwaters are large, permanently flooded coves.
- **Threats to habitat:** Sedimentation and plant succession are restricting or blocking backwater entrances. Historic flood events that created and renewed backwaters no longer occur, so the authors suggest periodic dredging of entrances.
- **Predation:** Channel catfish and flathead catfish preyed heavily on juvenile razorback suckers (45-168 mm TL). Adults of study-fish size appeared to survive in areas with large piscivores such as [[Flathead Catfish]].

## Key Findings

- **Non-random habitat use:** Use differed significantly from availability in the first, second and third years (χ² = 173.03, 290.49 and 219.34; df = 3; P < 0.01) and for pooled data (χ² = 619.61, df = 3, P < 0.01).
- **Selection pattern (pooled data, Jacobs' D):**
  - Backwaters: 61-64% of observations versus 42% of availability, D = 0.42 (selected for).
  - Impoundment: 13-19% of use, D = 0.63 (selected for, strongest selection).
  - Main channel: about 13% of use versus 48% of availability, D = -0.72 (selected against).
  - Side channels: about 4% of use, D = -0.12 (used roughly in proportion to availability).
- **Overall:** Backwaters and the impoundment together accounted for 83% of fish contacts while making up only 47% of the study area; the main channel held 13% of contacts despite covering 48% of the area.
- **Seasonality:** Overall pattern did not change seasonally. Backwater use was 59-65% in spring, summer and winter and rose to about 82% in fall. Differences among seasons were statistically significant (χ² = 17.72, df = 9, P = 0.04), but the authors attributed this largely to fewer fall contacts and possible transmitter expiration or vegetation interference. No difference was found for the January-April spawning period.
- **Movement:** Of 87 fish released, 74 were contacted at least once and 54 at more than one site; maximum contacts for one fish was 30. Forty-five fish used more than one habitat type, 7 used all four, and 63 (72%) used two or more habitat types when inferred use was included. Most movement occurred in winter and spring and was mainly among backwaters. Some fish were sedentary while others moved widely.
- **Egress:** Fish could leave the study area through the main channel at the north end or through irrigation canals at Imperial Dam, but remote stations largely failed, so exit movements are poorly documented.
- **Management implications:**
  - Backwaters are important habitat and their loss could affect species recovery.
  - Side channels are essential corridors linking the main channel and backwaters, and should be maintained.
  - Results may inform [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the Endangered Species Act for federally managed critical habitat.
- **Limitations:** Only hatchery-reared fish were used, as wild lower-basin adults were scarce; the authors judged them likely to behave similarly to wild fish but possibly somewhat naive to river conditions. Availability of hatchery fish in the study may not fully represent wild behavior.
