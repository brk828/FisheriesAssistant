# Profitable Stream Positions for Salmonids: Relating Specific Growth Rate to Net Energy Gain

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Kurt D. Fausch]] (1984, *Canadian Journal of Zoology*) develops a model of "potential profit," the net energy gain available to a stream salmonid at a fixed holding position, calculated as energy from drifting invertebrates minus the metabolic cost of swimming to hold station. Juvenile [[Coho Salmon]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] were tested in six experiments in a recirculating [[Stream Aquarium]] at 15 °C. Specific growth rate was fitted to potential profit with the [[Michaelis-Menten Model]] (Monod form). Potential profit predicted growth better for coho than for either trout species, and dominant fish that held the most profitable positions grew fastest. The predicted ranking of positions from the drift and flow map closely matched the observed [[Dominance Hierarchy]] (r = 0.97).

## Background & Description

Growth of stream salmonids is treated as a function of net energy gain, consistent with [[Bioenergetic Models]] and [[Optimal Foraging Theory]]. Juvenile salmonids hold relatively fixed "focal points" and make short forays to capture drifting invertebrates. Prior work (Chapman and Bjornn 1969; Everest and Chapman 1972; Fausch and White 1981) proposed that fish should sit in low velocity water close to fast current, balancing swimming cost against food supply.

Growth-resource relationships in algae, diatoms and fish rations are hyperbolic and defined by a resource threshold (maintenance level) and a maximum specific growth rate. The author argues that fish holding the most profitable positions should therefore also grow fastest.

The experimental system followed [[Fausch and White 1983]]: a 7.28 m sinuous Plexiglas channel (30 cm wide, 30 cm deep) with pools up to 15 cm deep, riffles about 5 cm deep, and flow produced by air-lift pumping. Dissolved oxygen was 100% saturation. Water chemistry was held by a biofilter (CaCO3 alkalinity 100–128 ppm; pH 8.08–8.53; hardness 120–140 ppm). Light was 40–55 µE·m⁻²·s⁻¹ over 90% of the stream area on a 12 h photoperiod.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish and holding positions:** Fish formed linear dominance hierarchies through interference competition. Coho salmon never rested on the bottom; trout used pectoral fins to rest on the stream bed and subordinate trout hid in gravel against the wall.
- **Water velocity:** Riffle velocities averaged 20–30 cm·s⁻¹; pool velocities ranged from near 0 to 20 cm·s⁻¹.
- **Drift:** Invertebrate drift was simulated by continuous introduction of frozen [[Daphnia]] at the upstream riffle. Drift energy was a linear function of water velocity at each cross section, with the slope declining exponentially with distance downstream owing to predation and sinking.
- **Energy conversion:** Standard 2 mm Daphnia yielded 5938 cal·g⁻¹ dry weight (SE 19.6). A fish would need to capture 19.7 standard Daphnia to gain 1 cal.
- **Swimming cost:** Calculated from [[Stewart 1980]] bioenergetic equations for coho salmon and rainbow trout, transformed to 15 °C.
- **Foraging:** Fish foraged to about two body lengths, mainly in a quarter-sphere in front of and above the focal point, consistent with [[Wankowski 1981]] (1.9–9.9 body lengths for juvenile Atlantic salmon).

## Key Findings

1. **Potential profit predicts growth best for coho salmon.** Coho always achieved higher specific growth rates than trout for a given resource level in concurrent experiments, and maintained growth to lower resource thresholds.
2. **Resource thresholds (Michaelis-Menten parameter T, cal·h⁻¹):** coho 0.0 (experiment 1) and 2.5 (experiment 3), 1.1 (experiment 5); brook trout 5.5 (experiment 4); brown trout about 2.1 (experiment 2, linear estimate) and 4.0 (experiment 6).
3. **Experiment summary (Table 1):** six experiments, 13–22 fish each, 9–18 day periods. Coho salmon in experiments 1, 3 and 5; brown trout in 2 and 6; brook trout in 4. Initial fish length was 33.7–54.1 mm FL.
4. **Growth outcomes:** In experiment 1, 19 of 22 coho lost weight. In experiment 2, only 2 of 17 brown trout grew. Only 3 brook trout grew or maintained weight in experiment 4. In experiment 5 all 16 coho grew. Only 5 brown trout in experiment 6 grew or maintained weight.
5. **Dominance and profit:** Dominant fish held positions with the highest potential profit and highest specific growth rate. The dominance order generally matched the order along the growth-profit curve.
6. **Position prediction validated:** In experiment 5, a map of potential profit at 89 points was compared with the observed rank of 16 coho positions. The ranks were nearly identical (Pearson r = 0.97, p << 0.001), so fish appear to choose positions by food supply, flow and dominance.
7. **Methodological caveats:** Variability arose from acclimation time (increased from 4 to 14 days), drift measurement error, limited swimming-cost equations for small fish, unmeasured agonistic energy costs, and trout use of pectoral fins for bottom-resting. The author notes that resource thresholds should be compared only between concurrently run experiments.
8. **Generality of the model:** The author argues the Michaelis-Menten form applies across bacteria, algae, zooplankton, sockeye salmon and fish rations. It is also proposed as a tool for predicting competitive ability between species in sympatry.
9. **Fitness link:** Specific growth rate is argued to correlate with fitness in fish, because faster-growing fish have more energy for reproduction. This supports the claim that dominant fish maximizing potential profit also maximize fitness.
10. **Confirmation of earlier ideas:** Results support [[Everest and Chapman 1972]], [[Jenkins 1969]] and [[Fausch and White 1981]], which proposed that juvenile salmonids select focal points on water velocity and food supply to maximize net energy gain while minimizing swimming cost.
