# Genetics Management Plan for the Endangered Fishes of the San Juan River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (24 March 2003) presents a Genetics Management Plan (GMP) for the endangered razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and Colorado pikeminnow (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the [[San Juan River]] Basin. Prepared for the [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]] (SJRIP) by Larry W. Crist and Dale W. Ryden, the plan provides a conceptual framework for maintaining the genetic integrity and evolutionary potential of wild and captive-reared stocks. It addresses captive propagation, genetic risks such as [[Hybridization]], [[Inbreeding Depression]] and [[Outbreeding Depression]], breeding designs such as the [[5 X 5 Di-Allele Breeding Matrix]], and stocking guidelines. Its central decision is that wild fish are too few to achieve recovery through natural recruitment alone, so augmentation will proceed using broodstock from San Juan River Arm of [[Lake Powell]] parentage and [[Nearest Neighbor]] sources.

## Background & Description

The two species are among four large endemic Colorado River fishes listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (the other two being the bonytail and humpback chub). Only the razorback sucker and Colorado pikeminnow occur in the San Juan River, where portions are designated critical habitat (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1994).

**Razorback sucker:** A large monotypic catostomid endemic to the Colorado River Basin, formerly widespread in warm-water reaches from Wyoming to Mexico. It is now extirpated from much of its historic range. Its federal listing took effect 22 November 1991.

**Colorado pikeminnow:** The largest of the four *Ptychocheilus* species and the largest North American minnow, with maximum weights likely exceeding 80 lbs. It is a generalist adapted to large seasonal flow variation, high silt loads and turbulence. It has declined from historic levels and now persists primarily in the upper basin.

The plan draws heavily on earlier documents, including the [[Genetics Management Guidelines]] (Williamson, Wydoski and Visscher 1996) and the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] genetics documents (Williamson and Wydoski 1994; Czapla 1999).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water development in the Upper Colorado River Basin has fragmented fish habitat with dams and diversions, altered flow regimes affecting streamflow magnitude, water temperature and channel maintenance, and enabled establishment of nonnative fishes that compete with and prey on natives (Miller et al. 1982; Tyus and Saunders 1996). The Program includes the Ute, Jicarilla and Navajo nations, the States of Colorado and New Mexico, the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Bureau of Indian Affairs, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Bureau of Land Management and water development interests.

Hybridization is a notable risk for razorback sucker. Introgressive hybridization with flannelmouth sucker is low (0.5% toward flannelmouth suckers, Buth et al. 1987) but could increase if habitat or flow and temperature cues are altered. Razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker and bluehead sucker are sympatric in the San Juan River, but large-scale introgression is considered relatively unlikely because the species co-evolved. No hybridization has been documented in the Colorado pikeminnow.

## Key Findings

- **Management goal:** Maintain genetic integrity and evolutionary potential, prevent immediate extinction of any wild San Juan stock, conserve genetic diversity of wild stocks, and identify priority stocks for augmentation if native stocks are too depleted.
- **Strategy decision (19 February 2002):** The San Juan River Biology Committee judged that too few wild fish remain for recovery through all-natural management (Strategy 1) or augmentation using San Juan parent stock alone (Strategy 2, Priority 1).
  - Razorback sucker: augmentation proceeds with broodstock from both San Juan/Lake Powell and [[Nearest Neighbor]] sources (Strategy 2, Priorities 1–3). Lake Mohave, which holds the greatest genetic diversity, is an acceptable source.
  - Colorado pikeminnow: no San Juan-origin fish are in captivity, so augmentation uses Nearest Neighbor broodstock (Strategy 2, Priority 2).
- **Genetic risk framework:** Risks include hybridization and introgression, inbreeding depression, outbreeding depression and loss of rare alleles. Risk assessment is handled plan-by-plan, and managing these risks is essential for a "no jeopardy" opinion under Section 7 consultation.
- **Breeding design:** A minimum effective population size of 50 (25 males and 25 females) is the target for broodstock development. The [[5 X 5 Di-Allele Breeding Matrix]] (10 unrelated parents, 25 family lots, effective population size of 10) is the minimal model when wild adults are few.
- **Stocking precautions:** Fish should be marked (preferably with PIT tags) and stocked as equal family lots from pedigreed matings. Escapement from refugia must be prevented, and stocking must not significantly affect indigenous fishes.
- **Razorback sucker genetics:** Allozyme data (Buth 1994) showed little structure among populations. The mtDNA study (Dowling and Minckley 1994) found haplotype diversity highest in Lake Mohave (0.97) and zero in the upper Colorado River, with a clinal reduction consistent with serial bottlenecks after glaciation. The Dexter National Fish Hatchery broodstock was less diverse than its Lake Mohave source.
- **San Juan razorback status:** Wild razorback suckers are extremely depleted. Historic San Juan collections are sporadic, and intensive surveys from 1990 to 2002 captured no additional wild adults. Stocked adults survived, and larval fish were collected in five consecutive years (1998–2002), but no recruitment had been documented as of the report.
- **Colorado pikeminnow status:** Populations in the San Juan are less abundant than in the Green or Colorado sub-basins. A small population persists and is reproducing and recruiting, with most wild fish occurring in a 23-mile reach between Hogback Diversion and Four Corners. Stocked early-life-stage fish showed low survival but fast growth in those that survived. Allozyme studies found no significant differentiation among stocks.
- **Recommendations:** Do not remove wild adult Colorado pikeminnow from the San Juan. Develop broodstock from Nearest Neighbor stocks using a 5 X 5 or equivalent design, implement augmentation with genetic baselines, and monitor progeny for inbreeding depression and introgression. Retain existing San Juan/Lake Powell fish for broodstock, and collect any wild adult razorback sucker for spawning and return.
