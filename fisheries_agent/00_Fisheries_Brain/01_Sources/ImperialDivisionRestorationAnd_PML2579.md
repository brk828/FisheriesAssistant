# Imperial Division Restoration and Enhancement: Environmental Assessment Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This environmental assessment, prepared by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] with several state and federal partners, proposes restoring, enhancing and protecting backwaters, wetlands, riparian woodland and aquatic habitat along the Lower Colorado River in the Imperial Division. The area lies between [[Imperial Dam]] and the northern boundary of the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]. Habitat loss followed the 1983 flood, which silted channels and backwaters and allowed invasive vegetation to dominate. The preferred alternative combines hydraulic dredging of the historic California channel, a new Arizona channel, amphibious-excavator work on backwaters and wetlands, and revegetation with native trees. Reclamation can fund 50 percent of the cost, and the remainder is being sought from another source. The document concludes that the project yields a net habitat gain, requires no mitigation, and will not increase consumptive water use.

## Background & Description

The project was developed by an interagency work group. The group drew on a 1995 inventory of backwaters that identified issues and concerns, and it operates under the [[Lower Colorado River Management Work Group]]. Participating agencies include the Arizona Game and Fish Department, Arizona State Land Department, California Department of Fish and Game, California Division of Parks, [[Bureau of Land Management]], Bureau of Reclamation and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

The stated purpose is to restore, enhance and protect streamflow, water storage and flood-storage capability, and riparian-wetland-aquatic habitat for fish and wildlife and recreation, consistent with water conservation laws and public use. The study reach is unique on the Lower Colorado River. Historically, about 5 percent of the riparian, wetland and aquatic habitat in the area has been lost over recent years, mainly through natural succession to upland vegetation.

Project components are summarized below.

- **Preferred alternative (California side, Imperial Dam to Ferguson Lake):** dredge 3.3 miles of the California channel to about 30 feet wide and 10–12 feet deep; add two dredge channels (970 and 1,560 feet) as fire breaks; excavate about 9,000 feet of finger channels to restore about 50 acres of wetland; open inlets and outlets to 10 isolated backwaters.
- **Preferred alternative (Arizona side, Imperial Dam to Martinez Lake):** dredge a new 30-foot-wide inlet channel to restore about 1.8 acres of open water; use an amphibious excavator to build a 10-foot-wide channel linking 10 isolated backwaters (about 1.5 miles); create fire breaks protecting about 50 acres of native trees; plant 1,000 native trees.
- **Imperial National Wildlife Refuge:** six or seven projects, including a connective channel at the Island Lake complex, Martinez Lake Marsh dikes, Ferguson Lake, Norton Lake, Adobe Lake and Draper Lake. Refuge work aims to restore about 2,000 acres of wetlands and backwaters and remove about 1,000 acres of cattails, cane, phragmites, salt cedar and arrowweed.
- **Implementation:** the work would take 2 to 3 years, depending on equipment, weather, river flows and substrate. Excavator work is most efficient in low-water winter months.

Alternatives considered:

- **Alternative One:** restore the Arizona channel to pre-1983 conditions by dredging a 30-foot channel. Backwaters would not be connected by channels, but their inlets and outlets would be opened.
- **Alternative Two:** restore the California and Arizona channels with an amphibious excavator 10–12 feet wide and 8–10 feet deep, with no hydraulic dredging.
- **No Action:** no dredging or excavation. Wetland marsh would continue to decline into upland vegetation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation.** The project area is dominated by phragmites, cane, salt cedar, saltbush, wolfberry and arrowweed. Small stands of native willow, cottonwood, screwbean and honey mesquite are interspersed among them. Cattails and California and three-square bulrush form emergent stands. Submergent vegetation includes several pondweed species and spiny naiad. No threatened, endangered or candidate plant species occur in the project area. Mitigation is not required because there is a significant net gain in desirable vegetation.

**Fish and wildlife.** The Colorado River is a major flyway for migratory waterfowl, neotropical birds, shorebirds and marsh birds. Common resident species include white-winged and mourning doves, Gambel's quail, jackrabbits, cottontails, coyotes, muskrat and beaver. The reach is an important warm-water fishery for Arizona and California anglers. Major sportfish include largemouth and smallmouth bass, striped bass, sunfish, and channel and flathead catfish. Forage and baitfish include shiners, minnows, carp and threadfin shad. Appendix B lists about 50 waterfowl, wading, shorebird, gull and tern, marsh and passerine species common to the area.

**Water quality.** The water is generally of normal pH and low in pesticides and other contaminants. Some backwaters are becoming eutrophic. Others show poor water quality, with low dissolved oxygen in summer, odor and hydrilla growth, depending on flow and water temperature. The project is expected to improve water quality in backwaters, channels and wetlands. Dredging would cause temporary turbidity increases. Dredged material would be deposited into the main river channel where possible and later removed from the [[Imperial Dam Silting Basin]].

**Threatened and endangered species.** Five federally listed endangered bird species and one endangered fish species with designated critical habitat may occur in the area. These include the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]. The project provides habitat for the [[Bald Eagle]] (about 20–30 wintering birds), with the [[Brown Pelican]] and [[Peregrine Falcon]] occasional visitors. Biological opinions from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] were favorable for the Yuma clapper rail and razorback sucker, but the Arizona plan has since changed, requiring further [[Section 7 Consultation]]. Other listed or special-status species expected to benefit include the yellow-billed cuckoo, black rail, Yuma hispid cotton rat and California leaf-nosed bat.

**Cultural resources.** A [[Class III Cultural Resource Survey]] identified 31 recorded sites in the study area: 9 prehistoric, 21 historic and 1 of recent origin. Seventeen of these sites lie on the floodplain and are unverified. One site, the Martinez Lake Site (AZ X:3:13), is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The project is not expected to affect any identified cultural resources. If resources are found, ground-disturbing work would stop until mitigation is agreed upon with the State Historic Preservation Officer.

**Land use, recreation and water budget.** Most lower-division land is withdrawn for Reclamation use, and the refuge is managed by the Fish and Wildlife Service. State-owned floodplain land would require state approval and a right-of-way agreement or memorandum of understanding. Recreation is highly valued and includes boating, water skiing, fishing, hunting, camping and bird watching. Sites include Squaw Lake, Hidden Shores RV Village and Martinez Lake. Restrictions such as no-wake signs would limit disturbance. The water budget (Appendix D) estimates that open-water evaporation was about 889 surface acres at 7.5 acre-feet per acre per year in the mid-1950s. Present lower-division open water is about 457 acres, with a loss of about 3,427.5 acre-feet per year. Restoration would not raise consumptive use under the Supreme Court decree in Arizona v. California.

## Key Findings

- **Habitat loss:** about 5 percent of riparian, wetland and aquatic habitat in the Imperial Division was lost in recent years through natural succession. The project boundary covers about 6,100 acres and 150 backwater lakes.
- **Restoration targets (preferred alternative):** about 13 acres of open water on the California side and 8 acres of cattail/bulrush wetland; about 170 acres of riparian woodland and marsh protected; about 50 acres of wetland restored with an amphibious excavator; about 1.8 acres of open water on the Arizona side; and about 100 surface acres of backwater lakes reconnected.
- **Refuge targets:** about 2,000 acres of wetlands and backwaters restored, with about 1,000 acres of invasive vegetation removed.
- **Revegetation:** about 1,000 mixed cottonwood, willow and mesquite trees planted, with more planting as funds allow.
- **Impacts:** short-term, minor impacts from dredging and recreation. Long-term benefits to fish and wildlife are expected to outweigh them.
- **Mitigation:** not required for vegetation, fish and wildlife, water quality, threatened and endangered species, land use or recreation.
- **Funding and consultation:** Reclamation funds 50 percent of the cost. Section 7 consultation is required for the revised Arizona plan.
- **Water use:** restoration would not increase consumptive water use under the Arizona v. California decree.
