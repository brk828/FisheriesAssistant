# Status of Freshwater Fishes of the United States: Overview of an Imperiled Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Melvin L. Warren Jr.]] and [[Brooks M. Burr]], published in *Fisheries* (Vol. 19, No. 1, January 1994), assesses the diversity, endemicity and conservation status of native freshwater fishes across the conterminous United States. The country holds about 790 native freshwater species, roughly 90% of them nongame fishes, and the South is the核心 of diversity. Imperilment is widespread and most acute in the arid West and the southeastern states, and the authors argue that federal protection lags far behind the scientific assessment of need. They call for watershed-scale management, public education and advocacy by fisheries professionals.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The authors present fish data state by state rather than by drainage basin, acknowledging that fish "do not recognize state lines" but arguing that political units match how policy and management are actually organized.
- **Global context:** About 22,000 fish species are known worldwide. North America has at least 1,061 native freshwater species, and 790 (75%) occur in the United States. By comparison, Europe has 250, Asia 1,500, Africa 1,800 and South America 2,200 freshwater fishes. The Mississippi basin alone has at least 375 species, more than all of Europe.
- **Nongame fishes:** These make up about 89–90% of U.S. fish species. They lack the public appeal of game fishes and are often dismissed as "minnows" useful only as bait or food.
- **Methods:** Status data were drawn from [[USFWS Endangered Species Lists]] (1991) and the [[American Fisheries Society]] list of 1989 (Williams et al. 1989). Species counts came from [[Page and Burr Field Guide]] (1991). The authors defined four regions: Midwest, Northeast, South and West.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diversity patterns**
- The South is the most diverse region. Alabama and Tennessee each have 257 species, Kentucky 220, Georgia 219, Virginia 201, Mississippi 200 and Arkansas 189. Alabama and Tennessee each hold about one-third of U.S. freshwater fishes.
- The West is least diverse. Arizona and Utah have 26 species each, Idaho 36, Nevada 39, Washington and Colorado 47 each, and Wyoming 49.
- Species richness does not follow the simple species-area model, which predicts a doubling of species with each tenfold increase in area. The authors attribute this to historical factors such as [[Pleistocene Glaciation]], orogeny and ancient geological events.

**Endemism**
- Freshwater fishes are highly localized. Eight families, 128 genera and 966 species are endemic to North America.
- Western endemism is high. 69% of native fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]] are endemic, compared with 34% in the Mississippi basin. The Great, Klamath and Sacramento basins combined have 58% endemics, and coastal drainages of the Gulf states about 30%.
- Key endemic hotspots include the Mobile basin (about 40 endemics), the [[Tennessee River Drainage]] (at least 33), the Eastern Interior Highlands (at least 53), the Colorado River drainage (22) and the Sacramento–San Joaquin drainages (12).

**Threats (proximate causes)**
The authors identify four main causes of decline: physical habitat loss and alteration, chemical pollution, overexploitation and introduction of nonindigenous species. Additional pressures include dams and flow regulation, groundwater pumping, acid rain, sedimentation from agriculture (doubled in 20 years in parts of the Midwest and South), wetland drainage and invasive cichlids in Florida.

## Key Findings

1. **Imperilment is widespread.** In 1989 the AFS listed 364 North American freshwater fish species and subspecies as endangered, threatened or of special concern, a 45% increase in 10 years, equal to about one-third of the native fauna. Another 27 species and 13 subspecies were documented as extinct during the 20th century, 16 of them since 1964.
2. **Imperilment exceeds that of other taxa.** The Nature Conservancy ranked 7% of U.S. mammals and birds as extinct or imperiled, compared with 20% of fishes, 55% of freshwater mussels and 36% of crayfishes. Only 4% of federally protected aquatic species with recovery plans showed significant recovery.
3. **Regional patterns.** Imperiled taxa number 132 east of the Continental Divide and 122 to the west, but proportionally the West is more affected. Nevada (43 imperiled species), California (42) and Tennessee (40) have the most. Nevada (ratio 1.10), Arizona (0.85) and California (0.72) have the highest ratios of imperiled to total fishes. Tennessee and Texas each have 0.16, and Alabama 0.12, a level the authors describe as the South being "on the brink of an extinction crisis."
4. **Family-level imperilment.** Among the five most diverse families, imperilment ranges from 7.0% (Centrarchidae) to 50.8% (nonanadromous Salmonidae). Minnows (30.7%) and darters (27.2%) are imperiled roughly in line with their representation in the fauna. Suckers (34.7%) and bullhead catfishes (36.6%) are imperiled disproportionately. The authors read the broad pattern as evidence of "widespread and pervasive degradation of aquatic habitats."
5. **Endemic fish losses.** In the Colorado River basin, 80% of 32 endemic fishes are seriously imperiled. In the Tennessee River drainage, 16 of 33 endemics (nearly 50%) are imperiled.
6. **Federal listing backlog.** The states with the most severe backlogs are Alabama (−14), Tennessee (−12), Georgia (−11), Mississippi (−9) and Nevada (−10). Many eastern states show negative differences between federal and professional lists, while most western states show agreement. The authors conclude that funding and staffing for the [[Endangered Species Act]] are inadequate.
7. **Stream habitat is scarce.** Of 5,200,000 km of streams in the conterminous 48 states, only about 2% (under 100,000 km) are of sufficient quality for federal wild, scenic or recreational status. Only 42 free-flowing rivers over 200 km long remain.
8. **Recommendations.** The authors call for: (1) watershed-wide ecosystem management; (2) a nationwide network of aquatic preserves; (3) restoration of degraded habitats; (4) long-term fish community research programs; and (5) a national environmental ethic that recognizes natural rights of aquatic ecosystems. They also urge complementary legislation to protect whole aquatic communities proactively.
9. **Public advocacy.** Fisheries professionals must act as advocates. Citing Aldo Leopold, the authors note that conservation has long concentrated on "show pieces" rather than "small cogs and wheels."
