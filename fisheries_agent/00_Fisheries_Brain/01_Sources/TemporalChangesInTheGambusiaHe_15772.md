# Temporal Changes in the Gambusia heterochir × G. affinis Hybrid Swarm Following Dam Reconstruction

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #poeciliidae #hybridization #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This study documents temporal changes in a long-standing hybrid swarm between the endangered endemic [[Gambusia heterochir]] (Clear Creek Gambusia) and [[Gambusia affinis]] (Western Mosquitofish) in [[Clear Creek]], [[Menard County]], Texas, following reconstruction of the uppermost dam in 1979. Quarterly monitoring from January 1980 through winter 1983-84 revealed dramatic decreases in hybridization incidence in the upper spring pool after the repaired dam prevented upstream immigration of G. affinis. The study provides evidence that naive immigrant females played a significant role in hybrid production and documents an unexpected increase in G. heterochir abundance downstream, possibly facilitated by the introduced [[Lucania parva]] (Rainwater Killifish) through interference competition with G. affinis.

## Background & Description

[[Gambusia heterochir]] is restricted to the spring-fed headwaters of Clear Creek, a tributary of the [[San Saba River]] in west central Texas. The species was first discovered in 1953, by which time three impoundments had extensively modified the original spring run habitat. The uppermost dam created a stenothermal headspring pool above (inhabited primarily by G. heterochir) and a shallow eurythermal pool below (dominated by G. affinis), with a hybrid zone along the dam edges.

The two species exhibit ecological differences correlated with their respective habitats: pH differs (6.2-6.7 above dam vs. 6.8-7.6 below), aquatic macrophytes differ ([[Ceratophyllum]] above vs. [[Myriophyllum]] below), and amphipod species differ (endemic [[Hyalella texana]] above vs. widespread [[Hyalella azteca]] below). Previous studies (1956-57, 1968-69) documented that the hybrid swarm had persisted for decades, with hybrids consistently accounting for more than 10% of Gambusia at most localities in the hybrid zone.

By 1978, dam deterioration from tree damage and nutria tunneling had created multiple breaches allowing unrestricted upstream immigration of G. affinis. The dam was reconstructed August 27 - September 1, 1979, with mortared limestone, concrete reinforcement, and installation of new gate boards creating approximately one decimeter drop at the spillway—sufficient to preclude upstream Gambusia migration.

[[Gambusia heterochir]] was listed as Endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior in 1967 due to its extremely limited distribution and threats from genetic swamping and competition with G. affinis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Clear Creek has been extensively modified from its original condition as a clear spring run extending approximately 5 km to the [[San Saba River]] confluence. Human activities date to pre-Columbian times (abundant artifacts on cliffs) and Spanish colonial period (17th century). By the 1880s, the upper dam was constructed for irrigation purposes. Subsequent dams built in the 1930s created a 3 km eurythermal pool below the upper dam, allowing G. affinis populations to flourish and restricting endemic biota (G. heterochir, H. texana, Ceratophyllum sp.) to the area above the upper dam.

The hybrid swarm likely originated after dam construction altered the natural habitat gradient. Previous studies hypothesized that naive female G. affinis immigrating through damaged dam sections would mate with subdominant G. heterochir males, producing F1 hybrids. Reproductive studies confirmed only partial mechanical isolation and low-level conspecific mate preference between the species, allowing hybridization potential. Temporal differences in activity patterns provided some additional reproductive isolation.

The study employed modified minnow traps at 16 locations along the dam (8 stations above with two traps each, 8 below with one trap each) sampled quarterly. Traps were adapted with fine nylon screening to capture small Gambusia. A seining station (Station R) was established at downstream stenothermal springs beginning October 1980. Fish were allocated to species or hybrid categories using the hybrid index code based on morphological and meristic characteristics for adults; juveniles were classified by visual inspection.

## Key Findings

**Dramatic Reduction in Hybridization Above Dam**: Following dam reconstruction, G. heterochir abundance in trapping stations above the dam increased from approximately 70% in 1980 to about 95% by end of 1983. Hybrid abundance decreased four-fold from about 5% initially to approximately 1.2% by 1983. G. affinis declined from almost 35% in 1980 to less than 10% in 1983. Seasonal fluctuations in abundance, initially large (up to 40% difference), dampened considerably by 1983 (only 2-8% variation).

**Unexpected Downstream Changes**: Below the dam, G. heterochir increased markedly from less than 1% in all previous studies (1953, 1956-57, 1968-69, 1971) to approximately 70% by January 1984. At the downstream stenothermal Station R, G. heterochir rose from 0-7% before dam repair to approximately 80% by end of sampling period. Hybrid proportions remained relatively stable (5-20%) below dam but increased at Station R compared to historical levels.

**Sample Sizes**: Total of 25,157 Gambusia collected over study period. Above dam collections ranged from 299 to 2,252 individuals per sampling date. Below dam collections ranged from 3 to 383 individuals. Station R seine collections ranged from 186 to 890 individuals.

**Hybrid Index Stability**: Despite 30+ years (60+ generations) of hybrid swarm existence, hybrid index scores showed few changes in overall range of values. Adult males: G. affinis had index values <59, G. heterochir ≥83, hybrids 66-75, with backcross combinations intermediate. Adult females: G. affinis ≤80, G. heterochir >95, hybrids 86-90. The presence of fewer presumed backcross hybrids than F1 hybrids suggests backcrossed individuals may be at selective disadvantage.

**Juvenile vs. Adult Hybrid Proportions**: Proportion of hybrids in juvenile samples was generally much lower than in adult samples at all stations (statistically significant, P<0.001 overall). Juveniles represented 76% of collections above dam where adults were more abundant, 100% below dam, and 93% at Station R. This pattern suggests either mating preference or early developmental mortality of hybrids, with subsequent increase in hybrid proportions among adults indicating possible heterotic phenomena.

**Introduction of Lucania parva**: The rainwater killifish, not native to the [[Edwards Plateau]], was discovered in Clear Creek shortly after dam renovation (first noted 1980). Numbers varied seasonally with highest levels in winter, lowest in spring/summer. No Lucania were captured above the dam. Below dam, Lucania abundance showed consistent increases suggesting recent introduction (likely as bait) and population not yet at carrying capacity. Correlation between G. affinis and Lucania relative abundances was r=-0.43 (P=0.06).

**Lucania-Gambusia Interaction Hypothesis**: The ecological similarity between Lucania and G. affinis (both eurythermal, similar food habits and habitat preferences) suggests interference competition. At [[Leon Creek]] (Pecos County, Texas), where Lucania, G. affinis, and spring-adapted [[Gambusia nobilis]] co-occur, Lucania and G. affinis showed extensive niche overlap and both differed from the stenothermal-adapted Gambusia. Authors hypothesize that Lucania introduction may explain increased G. heterochir abundance below dam by competitively suppressing G. affinis rather than directly affecting G. heterochir.

**Confirmation of Naive Immigrant Hypothesis**: The rapid decrease in hybrid abundance (much faster than G. affinis decline) above the dam after blocking immigration confirms that sexually naive immigrant females played significant role in hybrid production, as originally suggested by Hubbs (1971). This finding has implications for fish stocking programs where naive juveniles might exacerbate genetic contamination problems.

**Conservation Status**: While G. heterochir appears relatively secure in the pool above the dam following reconstruction, the species remains restricted to extremely limited habitat. Complete recovery would necessitate removal of dams that allowed establishment of large G. affinis and L. parva populations, though the introduced Lucania may have inadvertently benefited the endangered species through competitive effects on G. affinis.

**Other Fauna**: In addition to Gambusia (N=25,157), 1,855 other vertebrates were collected: [[Dionda episcopa]] (N=589 total, 587 above dam), [[Ictalurus natalis]] (3 total), Lucania parva (1,225 total), [[Lepomis megalotis]] (18 total), and Rana tadpoles (20 total). Numerous [[Micropterus salmoides]], other Lepomis, [[Nerodia]] (water snakes), and nutria were observed but not captured in sampling gear.
