# Management Concerns for Endangered and Threatened Fishes in Western Warmwater Springs and Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #conservation-management

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-207), authored by [[James E Johnson]] of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, reviews management of endangered and threatened fishes that depend on western warmwater springs and streams. Of 83 federally listed U.S. fish taxa, 59 (71%) are western species, and 50 warm-riverine or spring-dependent taxa are protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The paper argues that past management favored non-native sport fishes at the expense of native non-game species, that conventional warmwater stream practices may fail for native fishes, and that barriers, [[Chemical Renovation]], and habitat protection have mixed results. It concludes that continuous, long-term management, improved coordination, monitoring, instream flow protection and aquifer protection are required.

## Background & Description

The document is a technical review and recommendations paper rather than a field study. Its core concern is the decline of native fishes in western warmwater habitats, which the author ties to damming, diversion, pumping, pollution, and the spread of non-native species.

Key counts drawn from the text:
- The April 15, 1990 [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] listing included 94 fish taxa: 83 from continental U.S. fresh waters and 11 foreign taxa.
- Of the 83 U.S. taxa, 59 are western, 21 eastern, and 3 occur between the Mississippi River and the 100th Meridian.
- Of the 59 western taxa, 2 are lake species and 7 are cold-water salmonids, leaving 50 warmwater riverine or spring taxa (53% of listed fishes; 60% of the U.S. total).
- Table 1 lists these protected warmwater western species with scientific names and U.S. locations (e.g., Colorado squawfish, Woundfin, Devils Hole pupfish, Clear Creek gambusia, Fountain darter). Because the table is long, it is summarized here rather than reproduced.

The [[American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee recognized 254 U.S. fishes with survival difficulties (Williams et al. 1989), about 60% western. Habitat loss was cited for 93% of these taxa, and invasion of non-native fishes is second only to habitat loss as a reason for listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat decline:** Large western warmwater rivers (Colorado, Gila, Rio Grande, Pecos, Platte, Sacramento/San Joaquin) are most impacted by damming and diversion, while smaller streams are more easily altered by local actions. Springs are disappearing through groundwater pumping and diversion to croplands.

**Texas springs:** 23% of the 281 largest Texas springs had completely failed by 1975 (Berne 1975), and many Edwards Aquifer springs are projected to fail after the turn of the century (USFWS 1984).

**New Mexico:** Overappropriation has lowered water levels and spring flows, particularly in the southeast. At [[Bitter Lakes National Wildlife Refuge]], sinkhole water levels are more than 3 m below canals built in the 1930s, isolating [[Pecos Gambusia]] and [[Pecos Pupfish]] populations genetically.

**Non-native species:** Invasion by species such as [[Mosquitofish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Sheepshead Minnow]], and [[Golden Shiner]] is a major threat to every western native fish. Endemic species generally cannot compete with broadly adapted non-natives.

**Extinction:** [[Miller et al. 1989]] attributed extinction of 40 North American fish taxa, 52% of them western, mainly to habitat loss.

## Key Findings

- **Management history:** Before the 1970s, state and federal agencies focused on non-native sunfishes, catfishes and reservoir fisheries, since anglers' license fees fund management. Few native warmwater fishes are classed as game species.
- **Legislation:** The [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) forced federal agencies to consider listed fishes in water development and habitat management, and states later developed their own non-game programs.
- **Recovery plans:** Beginning about 1975, recovery plans typically had four objectives: protect populations and habitats, protect gene pools (often through captive propagation), reintroduce species to historic habitats, and provide public education. Most plans are general and offer few details on habitat restoration.
- **Barriers:** Barriers protect isolated reaches from non-native invasion. Examples include the [[Bylas Springs]] barriers (both failed and are being rebuilt), the reconstructed Clear Creek lowhead dam (1978), and planned barriers on Aravaipa Creek and San Bernardino Creek. Barriers have generally only slowed invasion, partly due to illegal fish transfers. Protecting the Bylas Springs Sonoran topminnow population remained unsuccessful after nearly a decade of effort, although mosquitofish control kept two of three populations from disappearing.
- **Chemical renovation:** Renovation was used on woundfin, Sonoran topminnow, Big Bend gambusia, Pahrump killifish, desert pupfish and Leon Springs pupfish. Like barriers, it rarely removes all non-natives but can provide a period of reduced competition.
- **Stream improvement:** Habitat structures used in eastern streams may not suit western fishes. Behnke (1980) proposed backwater creation for Colorado squawfish, but Holden (1980) and Johnson (1980) argued this could benefit non-natives more.
- **Livestock removal:** Fencing can cause dense vegetation growth that eliminates surface water. At Bylas Springs, San Bernardino NWR and School Spring, grazing had altered habitats, so limited grazing has been suggested for some aquatic habitats.
- **Spring ownership:** Public purchase of spring lands (e.g., [[Devils Hole]] National Monument, Ash Meadows NWR, Diamond Y Spring) reduces illegal stocking but cannot protect springs if recharge areas or aquifers are compromised.
- **Illegal introduction example:** Golden shiners were illegally stocked into Quitobaquito Spring (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, AZ), forcing removal of desert pupfish before restocking.
- **Recommendations:**
  - Improve coordination with dedicated biologists for individual species or habitats.
  - Implement regular baseline monitoring.
  - Use [[Instream Flow]] methods to set flows for all life stages, and clarify state water-right priorities for listed species.
  - Enact and enforce state laws to protect aquifers and springflows.
  - Study the biology and genetics of endemic fishes rather than assuming eastern or coldwater life histories.
  - Enact strict laws against illegal fish introductions.
  - Commit to long-term, continuous management, comparable to management of game fish.
- **Conclusion:** Many western warmwater streams are being lost to water demand. Legislation exists, but more funding and staffing are needed. The author states that managing aquatic resources is necessary for future generations.
