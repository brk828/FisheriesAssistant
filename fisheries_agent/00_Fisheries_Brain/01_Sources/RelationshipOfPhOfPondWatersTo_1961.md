# Relationship of pH of Pond Waters to Their Suitability for Fish Culture

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #limnology #pond-management #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary
This 1961 symposium paper by H.S. Swingle (Alabama Polytechnic Institute) synthesizes data on how pond water pH governs suitability for [[Pondfish Culture]]. Rather than focusing on a single fish taxon, the paper addresses water chemistry as the primary ecological variable controlling fish survival, growth, and reproduction in managed ponds. Key species referenced include [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), used as bioindicators for acid/alkaline tolerance thresholds. The work emphasizes standardized pre-daybreak pH measurement (via electrometric glass electrodes) as the most reliable proxy for classifying [[Freshwater Pond Chemistry]] and establishes management protocols (liming, fertilization) tied to pH ranges.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Freshwater ponds classified by pH regime:
  - [[Soft Water]]: pH 3.0–7.2 (pre-daybreak), highly variable due to weak buffering; can swing to pH 9.5–10.0 during peak photosynthesis.
  - [[Hard Water]]: pH 7.5–8.5, buffered by calcium/magnesium bicarbonates, minimal diurnal fluctuation.
  - [[Alkali Water]]: pH 9.5–11.0, found in dry-land areas, dominated by sodium/potassium carbonates.
- **Diurnal pH fluctuation:** Driven by [[Photosynthesis]]/CO₂ dynamics — phytoplankton and submerged vegetation deplete free CO₂ and bicarbonate during daylight, raising pH; a soft water pond may shift from pH 6.5 at daybreak to pH 9.5 by afternoon.
- **Trophic effects of pH:**
  - Acid waters (pH 4.5–6.0) support phytoplankton growth after fertilization but **suppress zooplankton and benthic invertebrate (chironomid larvae) production**, starving higher trophic levels (e.g., bluegill growth arrested).
  - Alkaline waters (pH 9.5–10.5) limit phytoplankton to surface layers only, since CO₂ becomes locked as unavailable carbonate above pH 10.0; rooted aquatic plants, however, can utilize carbonate and continue growing.
- **Soil–water interaction:** Bottom sediment pH acts independently of water column pH; acid bottom soils (below pH 5.5) suppress benthic organism production even after water column is limed, forming a critical but underappreciated niche constraint.

## Key Findings

### pH Toxicity Thresholds
- **Acid death point (general pondfish):** pH 4.0 ([[Ellis et al. 1946]])
- **Acid danger point (Common Carp):** pH 4.8–5.4 (Schaeperclaus 1933; California Water Pollution Control Board 1952)
- **Alkaline danger point (Common Carp):** pH 9.0 (Schaeperclaus), though no mortality observed up to pH 10.0 in American ponds; upper toxic limit cited as pH 10.8 (Ohle, in Mortimer 1954)
- **Alkaline death point (general pondfish):** pH 11.0 — near-universal mortality threshold
- Rapid pH shifts can prove lethal even within otherwise tolerable ranges (California Water Pollution Control Board 1954), highlighting *rate of change* as an independent stressor.

### Optimal Production Range
- **Most desirable pH range (pre-daybreak):** 6.5–9.0, given adequate fertility.
- Waters at pH 6.0–6.5 (acidity due to CO₂, not mineral acids) can still yield excellent production since pH rises above 7.0 during daytime photosynthesis.

### Low-Production Zones
- pH 4.8–6.0: Low yields despite non-lethal conditions — attributed to phytoplankton dominance without zooplankton/benthic support.
- pH 9.5–10.5: Low yields due to carbon limitation (all CO₂ locked as carbonate above pH 10.0), restricting phytoplankton to surface microzones.

### Management Interventions
- **Liming (water):** Effective correction for pH < 6.5 using [[Calcium Carbonate]] (limestone) or [[Dolomite]]; reaction ties CO₂ into bioavailable bicarbonate form:
  `CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → Ca(HCO₃)₂`
- **Liming (strong acid waters, pH <4.5):** Requires [[Calcium Hydroxide]] (hydrated lime), applied cautiously to avoid over-liming to pH >6.5, which would convert all CO₂ to unavailable carbonate:
  `Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O`
- **Soil liming is essential**: Correcting water pH alone is insufficient if bottom soil pH remains below 5.5; Swingle's hatchery trial showed no increase in bluegill production until soil (not just water) was limed.
- **Application rates (Schaeperclaus 1933, loamy sand soils):** 1,500 kg CaO/ha for pH 4 soils; 1,000 kg/ha for pH 5 soils. Using CaCO₃: ~2,000 kg/ha for pH 5, and 4,000–6,000 kg/ha for pH 4.
- **CaO/Ca(OH)₂ for parasite control:** Applied to damp (drained) pond bottoms only; ponds must remain unflooded ~2 weeks to allow conversion to carbonate before refilling, preventing lethal pH spikes (≥11).
- **No liming needed** when water pH >6.5 and soil pH >6.0 pre-daybreak — excess lime can depress production (Swingle 1947).

### Fertilization Strategy Linked to pH
- **Acid waters (<pH 5.5):** Must be limed before fertilization; use non-acid-forming fertilizers (sodium nitrate, calcium nitrate) to avoid compounding acidity.
- **Alkaline waters (pH 8.5–9.5, pre-sunrise):** Use acid-forming fertilizers (ammonium sulfate) — phytoplankton uptake of ammonium leaves sulfate ion behind, reducing alkalinity.
- **Waters ≥pH 10:** Should not be fertilized at all due to CO₂ limitation regardless of nutrient input.

### Methodological Notes
- Colorimetric pH methods and pH papers are unreliable (error margin of 1–3 units); [[Electrometric Glass Electrode]] methods are recommended.
- Proper reporting of pH data requires context: time of day, light conditions, vegetation density, sample depth, and water classification — otherwise the value is scientifically uninterpretable.
- Best practice: serial pH measurements from before sunrise to sunset to capture full diurnal range.
