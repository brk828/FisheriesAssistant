# A Key to Nevada Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1952 paper, published in the Bulletin of the Southern California Academy of Sciences (Vol. 51, Part 3) by [[Ira La Rivers]] of the University of Nevada, Reno, is the fourth revision of a dichotomous key to the fishes of [[Nevada Fishes]]. The key separates the state's native and introduced fauna into families, genera and species, and in several places to subspecies. The author notes that few publications existed for the Nevada fauna at the time, so the key relied heavily on the work of [[Hubbs and Miller]] and other regional authors. The paper also proposes revised common names, because earlier names were inconsistent or cumbersome. It is an identification tool and taxonomic synthesis, not a field study, and it reports no new population or sample data.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Fishes of Nevada, including native Great Basin species and many introduced sport and aquarium species.
- **Sources:** The key draws on [[Schultz 1936]] (Pacific Northwest), the Hubbs and Miller series on Nevada fishes, [[Tanner]] on Utah and Nevada, and California works by Curtis, Murphy and Neale. The author describes the Hubbs and Miller body of work as the most important single contribution to knowledge of the Nevada fauna.
- **Collection:** The [[University of Nevada Biology Museum]] had few Nevada specimens when the first draft was made. Efforts by [[Thomas J. Trelease]] of the Nevada Fish and Game Commission and university students had assembled a collection holding nearly every recorded Nevada species by 1952.
- **Review:** [[Robert R. Miller]] annotated the second and third mimeographed drafts. The author states that remaining errors are his alone.
- **Nomenclature:** The 1952 checklist by La Rivers and Trelease was the first attempt at an official state list. The author revised some common names, for example rejecting "Bonytail" for a genus whose members have both pencil-shaped and thick caudal peduncles, and abandoned most subspecies names as unwieldy. See [[Common Names Stabilization]].

**Key structure:** The key begins by separating fish with thoracic or jugular pelvic fins (couplet 1) from those with abdominal pelvic fins (couplet 10). It then proceeds through orders and families, including Cottidae, Percidae, Centrarchidae, Poeciliidae, Cyprinodontidae, Ictaluridae, Salmonidae, Catostomidae and Cyprinidae. Diagnostic characters include fin ray and spine counts, lateral line scale counts, pharyngeal tooth formulae, intestine length, vomer shape and barbel presence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native and relict fauna:** Many native taxa are restricted to isolated remnants of past drainages. Examples include the [[White River System]], a disrupted Pleistocene system in eastern and southeastern Nevada, and the [[Lahontan Basin]] of western Nevada. The Bonneville Basin, Colorado River, Columbia River and Snake River drainages also contribute species.
- **Desert springs and endemics:** Several species occur in single springs or small spring systems. These include the [[Devil Pupfish]], confined to [[Devil's Hole]] at [[Ash Meadows]], the [[Moapa Dace]] at Warm Springs in warm water, and the [[Soldier Meadows]] springfish-related taxon at a single warm-water locality in Humboldt County.
- **Pahrump and Amargosa systems:** The [[Pahrump Valley]] supports the Pahrump Springfish, divided into three subspecies, each confined to a single ranch spring. The [[Amargosa Pupfish]] occurs in the Amargosa River system of Nevada and California.
- **Large river and lake fishes:** The [[Cui-ui Sucker]] is known only from [[Pyramid Lake]], and the [[Humpback Sucker]] is native to the Colorado River. The [[Woundfin]] occurs in the Virgin River and elsewhere in the Colorado system.
- **Introductions:** Many species were introduced from the eastern United States, Europe and the Pacific Northwest. These include [[Largemouth Blackbass]], [[Smallmouth Blackbass]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]], [[White Crappie]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Goldfish]]. The paper cites [[Miller and Alcorn 1945]] for the introduction history.
- **Habitat disturbance:** The author cites fish rescue work during the Sacramento Valley introductions and Trelease's 1952 article "Death of a lake," which addresses Pyramid Lake. These are the only specific environmental-decline references in the text.

## Key Findings

1. **Family-level diagnoses:** The key separates the Nevada fauna using fin, scale, dentition and barbel characters. Examples are Cottidae (scaleless sculpins), Centrarchidae (sunfishes and blackbasses), Salmonidae (trouts, salmon and charr), Catostomidae (suckers) and Cyprinidae (minnows, dace and chubs).
2. **Species and subspecies counts stated in the key:**
   - Cyprinidae and Catostomidae group: 25 species and 4 subspecies of carp, sucker and related fishes.
   - Native minnow family group: 12 species and 7 subspecies of chubs, dace and shiners.
   - Springfish and pupfish: seven species and three subspecies in the Poeciliidae and Cyprinodontidae group, plus six species and three subspecies in the Cyprinodontidae.
   - Catfish: 4 species.
   - Suckers: nine species, with the Catostomus group listed as five common sucker species.
3. **Endemic taxa:** The key identifies several taxa restricted to one locality, including the Devil Pupfish, Moapa Dace, Cui-ui Sucker, and the Pahrump Springfish subspecies (Empetrichthys latos latos, E. l. pahrump and E. l. concavus), each confined to a single ranch spring.
4. **Speciation and subspecies:** The [[Amargosa Pupfish]] has two Nevada subspecies (pectoralis and mionectes), separated by pectoral ray counts of 17 and 16. The [[Colorado Gila]] has four Nevada subspecies. The Speckled Dace (Rhinichthys nubilus) has four subspecies, each tied to a drainage.
5. **Taxonomic problems:** The author notes unresolved issues, including the Brown Trout subspecies, which hatcheries have mixed so thoroughly that they can no longer be told apart in the United States. Rainbow and Cutthroat trouts have also hybridized through hatchery practice.
6. **Revised nomenclature:** The author proposes different common names from the 1952 checklist and rejects most subspecies names, favoring simpler designations.
7. **Limits of the work:** The paper reports no field sample sizes, abundance data or survey results. Its value lies in its identification framework and the summary of the state's fauna at that time.
