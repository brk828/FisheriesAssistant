# The Serial Discontinuity Concept: Extending the Model to Floodplain Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #river-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This conceptual paper by [[J. V. Ward]] and [[J. A. Stanford]] (*Regulated Rivers: Research & Management*, 1995, vol. 10, pp. 159-168) extends the [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]] (SDC) from single-thread, longitudinal river models to [[Floodplain Rivers]]. The original SDC treated dams as disruptions of longitudinal gradients along the [[River Continuum Concept]] (RCC). The authors add the lateral channel-floodplain dimension using a simple three-reach model (headwater, braided and meandering reaches). They predict how dams in each reach would alter channel stability, thermal heterogeneity, [[Ecological Connectivity]], the [[CPOM-FPOM Ratio]] and [[Biodiversity]]. The paper is a coarse-resolution, idealized framework rather than an empirical study. Its main conclusion is that regulation of meandering floodplain reaches is likely to produce the largest losses of habitat and biodiversity, a conclusion supported by only limited comparative data.

## Background & Description

**Original SDC.** [[Ward and Stanford]] (1983) proposed that impoundments displace biotic and abiotic patterns upstream or downstream by a "discontinuity distance." The size and direction of that displacement depend on the variable and on dam position along the river continuum. The original model assumed single-thread, constrained channels, with shoreline vegetation as the main link to the surrounding landscape. The authors note that highly managed rivers may approach this assumption, but that natural fluvial dynamics should frame anthropogenic disturbance.

**Three spatial dimensions.** Following [[Ward (1989a)]], river systems interact laterally (channel-floodplain), vertically (channel-aquifer) and longitudinally (channel-channel). The original SDC considered only the longitudinal dimension. This paper incorporates the lateral dimension and largely sets aside the vertical one.

**Model river system.** The hypothetical temperate river has three reaches arranged in sequence:

- **[[Headwater Reach]]:** high-gradient, bedrock-constrained, heavily canopied, groundwater-fed. It corresponds to the headwaters of the river continuum.
- **[[Braided Reach]]:** alluvial, with multiple thalwegs around islands. It grades from unstable "bar braided" segments (transient sand and gravel bars) to vegetation-stabilized "island braided" segments (after Schumm, 1985).
- **[[Meandering Reach]]:** sinuous single-thread channels that migrate slowly across a forested floodplain through bank erosion and deposition.

Deltaic and estuarine systems were excluded. The authors acknowledge that other configurations occur.

**Figure framework.** Figures 2-6 share one format. The upper curve shows the original SDC pattern without floodplain influence. The lower curve shows the pattern when the floodplain is treated as part of the ecosystem. Arrows mark hypothetical dam positions. Thin lines show how a dam modifies conditions downstream. The hypothetical dams are deep-release reservoirs that thermally stratify but do not release oxygen-deficient or gas-supersaturated water. The authors stress that the curves are highly idealized and that an open headwater canopy would change the predicted gradients.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Why floodplains matter.** Research on regulation had mostly concentrated on within-channel processes, largely ignoring river-floodplain interactions. The authors cite earlier work (e.g. [[Junk et al. (1989)]] on the flood pulse concept) to argue that the diverse water bodies and alluvial forest mosaic of floodplains are integral to river function. Lateral interactions are most important in braided and meandering reaches. Headwater floodplains have inundation that is too brief and irregular for flood-adapted strategies to develop.

**Connectivity and flow.** Connectivity is defined as exchanges of water, sediment, nutrients, detritus and organisms between water bodies and between aquatic and riparian systems (after [[Amoros and Roux (1988)]]). In the meandering reach, the flood season delivers sediment and nutrients, floodplain detritus is released, and fish move onto the flooded surface to spawn and feed. Regulation severs these pathways.

**Thermal regime.** Temperature regime is treated as a primary determinant of community composition and biodiversity. Small-scale variability is the key difference from the original model. Water in deep-release reservoirs does not vary on a diel basis, which affects downstream thermal patterns.

**Detritus.** Organic matter is divided into coarse (CPOM, >1 mm; food of shredders) and fine (FPOM, <1 mm; food of collectors) fractions, following [[Cummins and Klug (1979)]]. The paper predicts a bimodal CPOM/FPOM pattern in natural floodplain rivers, with high coarse detritus in headwater and meandering reaches.

## Key Findings

The paper's findings are predictions and hypotheses drawn from the model. Data are cited only where noted.

**1. Channel stability**
- Headwaters are the only highly stable reach. Regulation has little influence there.
- Braided reaches are unstable. Regulation tends to increase their stability sharply.
- Meandering reaches have intermediate stability. Regulation accelerates hydrarch succession, arrests alluvial forest succession and truncates formation of floodplain water bodies.
- Example: the [[South Platte River]] changed from a wide, largely unvegetated braided system to a single-thread channel with an alluvial forest corridor in less than a century (Schumm, 1985).

**2. Thermal heterogeneity**
- Headwater thermal heterogeneity is naturally low, so damming headwaters should have little thermal effect below the dam.
- The expanded model places maximum heterogeneity in the meandering reach. Damming there should moderate channel temperatures, isolate floodplain water bodies and reduce habitat diversity.
- Spatial variability is large. Differences of up to 13°C between thalweg and bank were reported by Penaz et al. (1968). Mosley (1983) recorded substantial temperature differences among branches of the braided [[Ashley and Rakaia Rivers]] in New Zealand.

**3. Ecological connectivity**
- The original SDC placed the greatest connectivity in headwaters, based on allochthonous detrital inputs. Damming headwaters would therefore have the largest impact.
- In the three-reach model, connectivity peaks in the meandering reach. Regulation there should produce the largest reduction in connectivity.

**4. CPOM/FPOM ratio**
- The original SDC predicted a steady downstream decline. The floodplain model predicts a bimodal pattern.
- Headwater dams should still greatly reduce the ratio, as in the original model.
- In the braided reach, converting an unstable channel to a vegetated single-thread channel should increase coarse detritus.
- In the meandering reach, regulation should lower the ratio by removing early successional stages, drying floodplain water bodies and killing alluvial forest.

**5. Biodiversity (macrozoobenthos and related taxa)**
- Original SDC: biodiversity peaks in middle reaches, attributed to habitat heterogeneity and moderate disturbance.
- Expanded model: biodiversity is low in the braided reach because of channel instability (though Statzner and Higler, 1986, see braided reaches as overlap zones of mountain and floodplain species). The highest biodiversity is expected in the meandering reach if lotic, semi-lotic and lentic habitats are all included.
- Headwater regulation should further reduce already low biodiversity by cutting off detrital energy inputs.
- The authors note that almost no data exist on braided-reach biodiversity responses to regulation.

**6. Comparative data from the Danube**
Based on [[Löffler (1990)]], comparing two Danube floodplains:

| Group | Disconnected site | Connected (intact) site |
|---|---|---|
| Molluscs (zoobenthos) | 16 species | 35 species |
| Macrophytes | 20 species | 60 species |
| Fish | 4 species | 30 species |

These figures come from a single comparison and are not statistically tested in the paper.

**7. Overall conclusions**
- Floodplains should be treated as integral parts of the river ecosystem, not as peripheral features.
- The meandering reach of regulated rivers is most vulnerable, since most ecological linkages and habitat diversity are concentrated there.
- Many lower meandering reaches in developed countries have been isolated from their floodplains, which may have produced a "false impression" of their natural ecology (Petts et al., 1989).
- The model is meant as a flexible framework, to be adapted for specific systems and regulation scenarios.
