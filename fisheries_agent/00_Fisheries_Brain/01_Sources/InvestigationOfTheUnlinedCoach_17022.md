## Executive Summary

This 1980 cooperative study by Arizona State University and the U.S. Water and Power Resources Service investigated the benthic macroinvertebrate community of the unlined [[Coachella Canal]] in southeastern California, ahead of a planned conversion to a concrete-lined channel. While the study's primary focus is invertebrate community structure, it is explicitly framed around predicting impacts on the canal's [[Sport Fishery|recreational fishery]], reportedly dominated by [[Largemouth Bass]] at densities of 1,500–3,000 individuals per mile (St. Amant et al. 1974). The paper argues that macroinvertebrate production, especially filter-feeding taxa like the Asiatic clam ([[Corbicula Manilensis]]) and the hydropsychid caddisfly ([[Smicridea Utico]]), underpins forage availability for canal fishes, and that loss of aquatic macrophyte beds in the new lined canal could indirectly depress fish recruitment and long-term population viability.

## Background & Description

Although this is a community-ecology paper rather than a species-morphology study, several taxon-specific size/structural notes are recorded:

- **[[Corbicula Manilensis]] (Asiatic clam):** Two size classes distinguished — juveniles (shell width < 2 mm) and larger adults (shell width 1–2 cm). Juveniles dominated numerically at Station 1 (west bank); large adults (mean 1,277/m² across 3 replicates) dominated biomass at Station 8 (west bank).
- **[[Smicridea Utico]] (hydropsychid caddisfly):** Larval form associated with pebble/coarse substrates; considered the second-largest biomass contributor after *C. manilensis*.
- **Chironomidae (midges):** Present as larvae; densities far below typical lotic benchmarks (max <2,300/m² here vs. >50,000/m² cited as unremarkable in other lotic systems, per Merritt & Cummins 1978).
- **Fish taxa (from literature context, not directly sampled):** Species mentioned include *Tilapia zillii*, *Tilapia mossambica* (=*Oreochromis mossambicus*), *Menidia audens* (Mississippi silverside), *Acanthogobius flavimanus* (oriental goby), and *Ictalurus punctatus* (channel catfish, notably a documented blind individual per Hauser 1976). No morphometric fish data were collected in this study; these are prior-literature references establishing precedent for canal fish research.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure:** The unlined canal presented a gradient of substrates — predominantly fine-to-medium sand with sparse pea-gravel, and localized silt/clay accumulation in low-current nearshore zones. Current velocities were uniform (~1–2 FPS) in open water, slower nearshore, and faster near siphons/bridge constrictions. Rooted vegetation (*[[Phragmites]]*, *[[Typha]]*, submergent *[[Potamogeton Pectinatus]]* and *[[Myriophyllum Spicatum]]*) was restricted to shallow (<1 m) margins and covered limited area, but created critical low-flow detrital microhabitats.

**Functional feeding groups identified:**
- **Filter feeders:** *C. manilensis*, many Chironomidae, *S. utico* — numerically and biomass-dominant.
- **Sediment/detritus ingesters:** Oligochaeta.
- **Predators:** Turbellaria, Hydracarina, *Hetaerina americana*, *Hyponeura lugens*.
- **Collector-gatherers:** Baetidae, *Nectopsyche* sp., *Chrysops* sp.
- **Scrapers:** Nearly absent (e.g., *Physa* snails), suggesting very low attached microalgal (periphyton) production on canal substrates.

**Fish-invertebrate trophic linkage (central ecological argument):** The paper explicitly ties canal fish population health to macroinvertebrate food-web support, positing that:
1. Filter-feeding taxa depend on zooplankton, phytoplankton, and fine organic detritus, sourced from (a) upstream input via the [[All-American Canal]], (b) eolian deposition, or (c) autochthonous production (phytoplankton/periphyton vs. macrophyte-derived).
2. Aquatic vegetation beds serve dual roles as a primary production/detritus source for caddisflies and other invertebrates, and directly as fish habitat (feeding, shelter, refuge from predators for juveniles).
3. Concrete lining is predicted to eliminate macrophyte establishment due to steep side-slopes, high current velocities, and operational constraints (efficient water conveyance), fundamentally altering both invertebrate detrital resources and fish nursery habitat.

**Predicted response of key taxa to canal lining:** *C. manilensis* and Oligochaeta populations (substrate-dependent) are expected to decline sharply due to loss of unconsolidated sediment habitat, but since neither is heavily utilized as fish forage, this decline is predicted to have minimal direct fishery impact. Conversely, *S. utico* may successfully colonize hard concrete surfaces and could increase in abundance, potentially benefiting canal fishes as a food resource — contingent on whether its energy source is macrophyte-derived (in which case losses would offset gains) or channel-water/eolian derived (in which case production could persist or increase).

## Key Findings

- **17 total macroinvertebrate taxa** were identified across 9 stations (27 total sampling sites: east bank, mid-channel, west bank), collected 23–24 October 1980 using a 15.24 × 15.24 cm Eckman dredge.
- **Community dominance:** *[[Corbicula Manilensis]]*, 2 Oligochaeta taxa, Chironomidae, and *[[Smicridea Utico]]* collectively comprised >90% of individuals; *C. manilensis* overwhelmingly dominated biomass.
- **Spatial distribution — nearshore vs. mid-channel:** Nearshore (bank) habitats supported >20× more individuals than mid-channel (mean 3,678/m² bank vs. 158/m² mid-channel) and more than double the taxonomic richness (17 vs. 7 taxa), though proportional taxon composition was similar between zones.
- **Station-level heterogeneity:** Station 1 (west bank) had the highest total density (24,282/m²), driven by juvenile *C. manilensis*. Station 8 (west bank) — uniquely characterized by very low current and silt/organic substrate — had the highest biomass due to large adult clams (mean 1,277/m² for the 1–2 cm size class).
- **Substrate-density relationship:** Highest invertebrate numbers correlated with detritus/coarse particulate organic matter presence; *S. utico* was most abundant on pebble substrates (Stations 1, 2, 5, 7, east bank).
- **Depauperate fauna overall:** Densities were far below typical lotic benchmarks (e.g., Chironomidae <2,300/m² here vs. >50,000/m² reported elsewhere), attributed to the harsh, shifting-sand substrate environment. Mayflies (Ephemeroptera) were poorly represented and aquatic beetles (Coleoptera) were entirely absent — both groups typically substrate-dwelling, reinforcing the substrate-limitation hypothesis.
- **Fishery context (background, not directly measured):** The unpublished [[St. Amant Et Al. 1974]] report estimated 1,500–3,000 game fish (predominantly [[Largemouth Bass]]) per mile in the Coachella Canal, though forage/non-recreational fish species were largely unstudied — a data gap this project's broader initiative aims to address via concurrent fish collections during the November 1980 dewatering event.
- **Management implication:** The study recommends monitoring macroinvertebrate colonization in the new lined canal as an early, rapid indicator (versus the slower-responding fish community) of likely long-term impacts on the sport fishery, since invertebrates should reach equilibrium much faster than fish populations following the habitat conversion.
- **Critical uncertainty flagged:** The ultimate energy source sustaining canal secondary production (imported plankton/detritus vs. eolian input vs. autochthonous macrophyte/phytoplankton production) remains undetermined but is identified as the key variable governing whether the new lined canal will support a comparable invertebrate — and by extension, fish — community.
