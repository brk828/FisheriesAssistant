# Birth Activities of Some North American Scorpions

#arachnology #scorpiones #vejovidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Stanley C Williams]] describes the birth and early post-birth behaviour of 14 North American scorpion species from seven genera and four families ([[Buthidae]], [[Chactidae]], [[Diplocentridae]], [[Vejovidae]]). Observations were made over roughly five years in western North America, with the most extensive data on [[Vejovis Spinigerus]], [[Vejovis Vorhiesi]], [[Uroctonus Mordax]] and [[Centruroides Sculpturatus]]. Births occurred at any hour of the day but within a limited seasonal window. A litter could take several hours to deliver and was carried on the mother's back, where the young never touched the ground. The study also compares these births with Old World scorpions described by Shulov and colleagues (1960).

## Background & Description

Scorpion reproduction has been hard to study because of the animals' secretive, long life cycles and the difficulty of rearing them through a full life cycle in the laboratory. Earlier work covered courtship and mating, embryology, post-birth growth and gametogenesis, but parturition had been documented in only a few studies. Williams aimed to describe birth behaviour comparatively across a broad sample of North American taxa.

The taxa examined were:

- **Buthidae:** four species of [[Centruroides]] (C. gertschi, C. exilicauda, C. sculpturatus, C. vittatus)
- **Chactidae:** [[Broteas Alleni]]
- **Diplocentridae:** [[Diplocentrus Whitei]]
- **Vejovidae:** [[Anuroctonus Phaeodactylus]], [[Hadrurus Arizonensis]], [[Uroctonus Mordax]], and the Vejovis species V. confusus, V. mesaensis, V. minimus, V. spinigerus and V. vorhiesi

Some species were studied in depth, while others rest on single observations. Work was done in the field and in laboratory facilities at Arizona State University. The author notes that the study was partly supported by a National Science Foundation Summer Fellowship.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Under natural conditions, females gave birth in protected places such as beneath rocks, under bark and in burrows. Of about 75,000 scorpions observed in western North America over five years, none were seen giving birth on open ground or in places without good shelter. Burrowing vejovids were rarely seen giving birth in the field. In the laboratory, [[Anuroctonus Phaeodactylus]] and [[Hadrurus Arizonensis]] showed conspicuous burrowing for several days before birth. Centruroides females were seen more often in the field because their shelters under logs and bark were easier to observe.

Birth timing tracked the warm season. Laboratory births ran from early June to late November, and field observations gave a similar but shorter window. Some species bore young over a broader span, such as C. sculpturatus (June through September, with young on backs from late May to late October). Others, including V. vorhiesi and H. arizonensis, appear to produce one litter per year within a narrow period. Low-altitude V. spinigerus has a longer activity season than high-altitude V. vorhiesi, which the author links to differences in their reproductive and developmental timing.

## Key Findings

**Timing of parturition**

- Births occurred at all times of day and night, with no preferred hour evident.
- Parturition length varied with brood size, young size and complications. The shortest recorded birth was a H. arizonensis delivering 10 young in one hour. The longest was a V. spinigerus delivering 69 young over about 7½ hours.
- In a colony of 50 mature V. vorhiesi collected in Portal, Arizona, in mid-July, females began delivering about two weeks later. All were born at the same developmental stage, and the whole colony gave birth within a two-week span.

**The birth process**

- Before delivery the mother adopted a [[Stilting Posture]], raising her body on her last two pairs of walking legs. This posture was held until the young reached her back.
- In a typical V. spinigerus birth (a 1.27 g female, 69 young, beginning 11:30 AM MST on 27 July 1967), the genital operculum opened after about an hour. The young emerged head first, one at a time, each encased in a thin transparent [[Birth Membrane]].
- The mother caught the young in a [[Birth Basket]] formed by the distal segments of her first two walking-leg pairs. The newborn then freed themselves from the membrane, climbed a leg, and ascended to her back without touching the ground.
- Four mechanisms helped the young reach the back safely: the basket itself; a sticky outer membrane that held the young together; shed membranes that formed a landing platform; and a mother that stayed completely still throughout.
- The two main threats to newborns were failure to escape the membrane and falling to the ground.

**Post-birth association**

- First-instar nymphs reached the back within 45 minutes of birth and stayed there for the first few weeks. The first stadium lasted one to two weeks, and the whole litter molted within a 24-hour window.
- In the vejovids, first-instar young took up a highly organised orientation: they faced forward with the prosoma down and the metasoma curled so the aculeus pointed toward the mother. In the buthids, first instars were randomly positioned in layers up to three or four deep.
- In the second instar, young became more elongate and lost the vejovid orientation. After about two weeks they began dropping off the mother one by one. Burrowing species first sheltered under rocks, then excavated simple burrow cells.
- Neither first-instar nymphs nor the mother fed on the litter. Young did not eat or drink during their time on the back. Mothers sometimes ate young that fell off, but the mother's integument was intact after the young had left, which the author took as evidence against the folk belief that young feed on their mother.

**Developmental stage at birth**

- Births fell into three developmental categories, based on the observations of this study and of Shulov and colleagues (1960):
  - *Early state:* Vejovis species, [[Centruroides]] species and Euscorpius italicus. Young were born still in the birth membrane, ascended the back and first moulted several days to over a week later.
  - *Intermediate state:* Old World buthids such as Orthochirus innesi, Leiurus quinquestriatus and Compsobuthus acutecarinatus. Young escaped the membrane within minutes and moulted within 8 to 10 minutes of birth, before ascending.
  - *Precocious state:* Nebo hierochonticus and Scorpio maurus. Young were largely free of the membrane at birth, were capable of locomotion, and did not moult for about 10 days.
- Parturition in the New World species (Uroctonus mordax, Vejovis and Centruroides) seldom exceeded one minute per young. The Old World O. innesi and N. hierochonticus took about 4 and 10 minutes per young, respectively.
- The author suggests the transition may be a continuous spectrum, so the terms viviparous, ovoviviparous and oviparous may not fit scorpions well.

**Phylogenetic and comparative notes**

- The birth process was strikingly similar across the four families. The authors suggest the pattern of gestation, parturition and post-birth maternal association was well established early in scorpion evolution, possibly before the families diverged in the Carboniferous (per Birula, 1917).
- Scorpion young leave the mother with no pouch and no food requirement, which the author compares to the premature birth and migration to the pouch in marsupials.
- Mothers of [[Centruroides Sculpturatus]] were often seen on the ground at night while carrying young, kept normal locomotion and feeding, and took part in courtship dances. Vejovid mothers carrying litters were rarely seen on the surface, showed reduced activity and feeding in the laboratory, and did not court. The author concludes that vejovids usually stayed in their burrows while carrying young.
- A single [[Centruroides Gracilis]] specimen produced two litters in one year, and a female of [[Anuroctonus Phaeodactylus]] gave birth to a second litter without a new mating.

**Gestation**

- Gestation is probably shaped by temperature and altitude. Baerg's (1954) estimates for Centruroides species were roughly 4½ to 7½ months in Jamaican C. insulanus and about 8 months in Arkansas C. vittatus. The present data were consistent with these figures.
