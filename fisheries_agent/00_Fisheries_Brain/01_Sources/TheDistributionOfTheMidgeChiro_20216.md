# Distribution of the Midge *Chironomus riparius* in a Polluted River System and Its Environs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Chironomidae

## Executive Summary
A 1965 survey was carried out in the Colne Valley, near the Maple Lodge sewage works, an area with a history of complaints about swarms of the non-biting midge [[Chironomus riparius]]. Larval densities in the water courses, previously very high, were now low (average below 10,000/m² at all stations). Adult *C. riparius* rarely made up more than 30% of insects on sticky traps, and other chironomids from nearby [[Gravel Pits]] were often as numerous. Seven emergences were identified between March and November. Laboratory tests of three granular [[DDT]] formulations gave larval mortality below 70%, attributed to patchy distribution of the insecticide on the mud.

## Background & Description
- *C. riparius* (subfamily Chironominae, family [[Chironomidae]]) is generally associated with organically polluted water courses. About 400 chironomid species are known in Britain.
- Adults do not bite and, as far as is known, do not transfer pathogens. Complaints arise from large mating swarms, which consist mainly of males and are governed by light intensity, temperature and wind velocity.
- Life history: females lay about 600 eggs in a gelatinous mass; 90% lay 2–4 days after emergence and 10% lay a second batch. Eggs hatch in 4–5 days at 18 °C. There are 4 larval instars, and overwintering occurs in the fourth instar. Larvae are detritus feeders that build tubes in mud, with fourth instars reaching about 10 cm depth. Pupation does not seem to occur at low temperatures.
- Background: [[Edwards]] (1957) found larval densities exceeding 100,000/m² in Flakes Channel in 1954–55, and about 40,000/m² in 1962. Effluent discharge to Flakes Channel ceased in 1964 and was redirected to the [[Grand Union Canal]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Study area:** [[Flakes Channel]], Troy Arm, the Grand Union Canal (upstream and downstream of the effluent outfall), the [[River Colne]] and nearby gravel pits. Six routine stations (A–F) were sampled monthly, with four cores each, and about 4000 yd (3656 m) of channel was initially surveyed.
- **Oxygen:** Outside the canal upstream of the effluent (A), dissolved oxygen was generally below 6.5 mg/l. At stations E and F it fell to nil in October–November 1965, when flow was low. The mud became covered with the sulphur bacterium *Beggiatoa*, and larvae left their tubes for the air–water interface. Larval numbers at E and F were extremely low at the end of this anaerobic period.
- **Co-occurring invertebrates:** [[Tubificidae]] (densities up to 10,000/m² in Flakes Channel), [[Tanypodinae]] (carnivorous; up to 750/m² at A, E and F) and *Asellus aquaticus*. There was no evidence of competition between *Chironomus* and tubificids.
- **Organic carbon of mud (April):** 5.8–10.9% across stations. This was similar to other Hertfordshire river muds, and increased downstream of the effluent.
- Larvae were evenly distributed across Flakes Channel and Troy Arm, but in the canal were most numerous near the bank with the outfall.

## Key Findings
- **Larval density:** Averages stayed below 10,000/m² at all stations in 1965. This is comparable to some lakes and reservoirs, e.g. [[Mundie]] reported 36,000/m² (Kempton Park East Reservoir, winter) and 10,000/m² (Staines South Reservoir, May). Late-summer increases occurred in the canal downstream of the effluent (B, C).
- **Organic carbon and density:** Average larval density correlated with mud organic carbon (r = 0.88; P<0.01). April–December averages ranged from 137/m² (A, 6.6% C) to 1480/m² (B, 10.8% C) and 1309/m² (D, 10.9% C).
- **Adult catches:** *C. riparius* numbers were low in spring and early summer and rose rapidly in August, with emergences continuing until mid-November. From April to September it generally made up less than 10%, and rarely more than 30%, of the total insect catch. Only in March and November did it exceed 50%.
- **Sex ratio:** Male to female ratios on sticky traps were 2:1 to 3:1; suction traps gave a ratio nearer unity.
- **Emergence rhythm:** In March 1966 pupal ascent and emergence occurred between noon and 8 p.m., with a peak near 3 p.m.
- **Generations:** Using [[Harding's Method]] (probability-paper analysis), seven emergences were found in 1965. The interval between peaks was related to water temperature, but not precisely enough to predict development time.
- **Other chironomids:** Chironomids often made up 50–60% of the catch and occasionally over 80%, owing to *Tanytarsus* spp. (notably *T. sylvaticus* and *T. mancus*) and orthocladinines (*Cricotopus* spp.). *T. sylvaticus* larvae were not found in cores, but pupal exuviae occurred on a nearby gravel pit, so these midges probably came from gravel pits.
- **Trap comparison:** Sticky-trap and [[Johnson-Taylor Suction Trap]] catches of *C. riparius* correlated (r = 0.73; P<0.01) over 12 weeks, but total insects did not. In a 6-week comparison, *C. riparius* made up 12.2% of sticky-trap and 4.8% of suction-trap catches; Psychodidae made up 17.4% vs 48.6%. Sticky traps took a higher proportion of chironomids and were chosen for their low cost and simplicity.
- **Chemical control tests:** Three granular [[DDT]] formulations (5% active; about 70% pp'-DDT, nearly 30% pp'-TDE plus op'-DDT) were applied at 1.1 kg/hectare to aquaria with larvae at 20,000/m². Larval kill after nine days was 63%, 40% and 67% (formulations 1, 2, 3).
  - The best formulations had the smallest particle size or disintegrated most easily.
  - Of 36 cores per tank, over 30% contained less than 2 µg DDT+TDE; only about 50% of the added pesticide was recovered in mud and larvae, and water held negligible amounts.
  - Dead larvae averaged 0.021 µg and survivors 0.019 µg (not significantly different).
  - Application at the start of overwintering would probably give higher mortality because of longer exposure.
- **Conclusion:** Midge nuisance now appears less severe in this system, with other chironomids from gravel pits contributing substantially to swarms.
