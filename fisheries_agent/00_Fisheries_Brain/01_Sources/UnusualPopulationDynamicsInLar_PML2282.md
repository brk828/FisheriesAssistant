# Unusual Population Dynamics in Largemouth Bass Caused by a Seasonally Fluctuating Food Supply

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This field study documents a rare case of large-scale natural mortality among 2-year-old [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in [[Deer Island Lake]], a 40.7 ha backwater of the [[Lower Colorado River]] in Arizona. The authors, Michael K. Saiki and Jerry C. Tash of the Arizona Cooperative Fishery Research Unit, attribute the die-off to starvation. Each spring, adult [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) enter the backwater and spawn, and their young temporarily raise the trophic carrying capacity for piscivorous bass. Bass that grew rapidly on abundant shad in 1973 were left with inadequate forage when shad became scarce in autumn and winter. In spring 1974, shad were present but either too small or too large to be eaten by 2-year-old bass. Rising water temperatures between May and June then raised metabolic demands, and the fish died, presumably from starvation.

## Background & Description

The study addresses starvation as a suspected but poorly documented cause of natural fish mortality. The authors state that their literature search found no field studies documenting starvation in fish populations, although laboratory work exists (e.g., [[Ivlev]] 1961; James 1939; Niimi 1972). The stated purpose is to provide field evidence that starvation caused the bass mortalities.

Study site and methods:
- **Site:** Deer Island Lake, surface area 40.7 ha, mean depth about 2 m. Water exchange with the river occurs through a culvert 75 cm in diameter, grated with vertical bars spaced 2 cm apart.
- **Period:** October 1973 to August 1974.
- **Sampling:** Fish collected by night [[Electrofishing]] from five areas of the backwater.
- **Analyses:** Length-frequency distributions (following [[Tesch]] 1971); condition coefficient (K_n) from weight and total length; seasonal diet by frequency of occurrence and dry biomass (following [[Windell]] 1971).
- **Seasons:** spring (March–May), summer (June–August), autumn (September–November), winter (December–February).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Trophic dynamics.** Bass diet shifts with size. Fry feed mainly on zooplankton, juveniles on aquatic insects, and adults on fish, crayfish, and other large organisms. The authors describe this as a Type IV survivorship pattern (Slobodkin 1961), in which mortality falls mainly on the young.

**Density-dependent regulation.** Weatherley (1972) proposed that in fish, density-dependent growth replaces density-dependent mortality: intraspecific competition slows growth and reduces fecundity and recruitment, while slow-growing fish face longer predation exposure. The authors argue this buffer works only when forage declines gradually. A rapid forage collapse can instead trigger density-dependent mortality.

**Shad dynamics and temperature.** Shad numbers fell in autumn and winter through cessation of reproduction, emigration, predation, and exposure to temperatures below 9 °C (Strawn 1965). Declining water temperatures also slowed bass metabolism (Johnson and Charlton 1960), so the food shortage was not immediately visible.

**Other causes ruled out.**
- *Disease:* no pathogen infestations found in captured fish, and disease selective for only 2-year-old bass was judged unlikely.
- *Predation:* herons, cormorants, grebes, water snakes, frogs, and fishes were judged too small to prey on 25–35 cm bass, and no large predator concentrations were observed in spring 1974.
- *Fishing:* U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service creel data showed low harvest at the time.
- *Emigration:* 2-year-old bass were too wide (body width–length relationship, Fig. 3) to pass through the grated culvert.

**Human influence.** The authors attribute the situation to human modification of the backwater: the grated culvert permits migration only by small fish. They note that starvation could also occur in shallow water bodies such as oxbows and sinkholes that partly dry and crowd fish into small pools.

## Key Findings

- **Population decline:** The 1972 year-class of bass dominated in January 1974 (913 fish measured across six sampling occasions, January–June 1974). By May it had dropped to nearly half its former abundance, and by June it had nearly vanished.
- **Condition collapse:** Mean condition coefficients for 25.1–35.0 cm bass were lowest in May (1.42 at the reported May figure in Table 1 for that size class is higher; the lowest value in the 25.1–35.0 cm class is 1.31 in the 3-month interval reported in the table). Field observations described these fish as extremely emaciated in March 1974, while smaller and larger bass were plump. Poor condition was not due to reproduction, since spawning began in April.
- **Diet switch:** Bass of 25.0–34.9 cm fed mainly on large crayfish by biomass, and on fingerling shad in summer. Bluegill fry dominated diets of smaller bass in autumn and winter.
- **Shad availability:** Shad were abundant in October 1973 (14.6–19.9 cm), scarce or absent from November through February, and returned in May 1974 as two size classes: fry (2.1–4.6 cm) and adults (16.0–19.0 cm).
- **Size-based prey limitation:** Bass of 25–35 cm can swallow threadfin shad only up to 10.8–15.5 cm (Lawrence 1958, 1961), so neither shad size class was usable in spring 1974.
- **Conclusion:** The authors conclude that the bass that disappeared had starved. The die-off was driven by a sharp, short-term forage collapse, and density-dependent growth could not buffer it.
