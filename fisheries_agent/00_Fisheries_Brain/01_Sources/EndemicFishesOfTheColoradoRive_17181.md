# Endemic Fishes of the Colorado River System: A Status Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #cottidae #cyprinodontidae #elopidae #mugilidae #poeciliidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 status report, published by the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] and prepared by its [[Endemic Species Committees]], lists the endemic fishes of the Colorado River system and describes their status state by state across seven states: Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming. The document covers species from eight families, including suckers, minnows, sculpins, killifishes, pupfishes, tenpounder, mullet, topminnows and trouts. Several species are listed as threatened or endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The report attributes declines to dam construction, river channelization and the introduction and dominance of exotic species. Its stated purpose is educational, and it also summarizes each state's authority and management policy for threatened and nongame species.

## Background & Description

- **Origin of the Council:** An interstate committee was formed in 1956 to coordinate protection and management of Colorado River fish and wildlife. It was formalized as the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] on March 31, 1964.
- **Membership:** The Council includes fish and game or wildlife agencies of the seven basin states. Federal agencies participate as nonvoting members, including the Bureau of Land Management, Forest Service, [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], Bureau of Reclamation, National Park Service, Coast Guard and Army Corps of Engineers.
- **Council purposes:** These include promoting sound management and use of fish and wildlife resources, studying federal projects affecting the drainage, advising on interstate and intrastate planning, and recommending coordinated laws and regulations.
- **Committee structure:** A Technical Committee advises the Council. Technical subcommittees are organized as Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming) and Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada) sections. The Prohibited and Protected Fishes, Amphibians and Reptiles Committee oversees the [[Endemic Species Committees]], which consolidate management programs for endemic species and coordinate study groups and recovery teams.
- **Purpose of the report:** The booklet is educational. It notes that endemic or native species serve as environmental indicators, so a decline in a species' population can signal a corresponding decline in its environment.
- **Species list and notations:** The Table of Contents lists species by family, marking federally listed or proposed taxa. Taxonomy follows the [[American Fisheries Society]] Special Publication No. 6 (1970), *A List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pressures on endemic fishes:** The report identifies the construction of dams, the channelization of large river stretches, and the introduction and dominance of exotic species as the main factors affecting endemic fish populations.
- **Environmental indicators:** Population status within the native range is presented as a proxy for environmental condition.
- **Habitat examples from the text:**
  - White River Killifish (*Crenichthys baileyi*) occurs in isolated springs of the relict White River system.
  - Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*) is associated with the [[Salton Sea]] area in California.
  - Kendall Warmsprings Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus thermalis*) has a restricted range in Wyoming.
  - Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was extirpated from Wyoming due to habitat loss from the construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].
- **Monitoring and study:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in Salt Lake City supports a long-term turbidity, temperature and sediment monitoring program on the [[Yampa River]] near Maybell and at Echo Park on the [[Green River]]. The University of Nevada studies the Virgin River system (Utah, Arizona, Nevada) under contract with the same service office.
- **Utah State study:** Utah State University completed a 1973 study, *Distribution, Abundance and Life History of the Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin*.

## Key Findings

### Species status highlights

- **Humpback (Razorback) Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Listed as endangered or threatened in several states. Wyoming reports it as extinct. The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] is investigating its true status and life history.
- **Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*):** Endangered and fully protected in Colorado, where occurrence is extremely unusual, with very few documented records during the past decade.
- **Bonytail (*Gila elegans*):** Listed as endangered and fully protected in several states. Arizona lists it as uncommon, and Wyoming lists it as extinct.
- **Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):** Extinct in Wyoming because of habitat loss from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. Declined sharply in Colorado over the past decade. The Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team is investigating its life history and recovery measures.
- **Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** Protected in all listed states, with a [[Woundfin Recovery Team]] studying its status and life history.
- **Gila Trout (*Salmo gilae*):** Rarely found in Arizona. In New Mexico it is found in seven streams closed to fishing. A Gila Trout Recovery Team has been established.
- **Arizona Trout (*Salmo apache*):** Described by Miller (1972) as a new species. An official recovery team has been established.
- **Colorado Cutthroat Trout (*Salmo clarki pleuriticus*):** Threatened in Colorado and may be taken by angling only.
- **Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*):** Believed extinct in Arizona, where reintroduction and propagation have been initiated. Listed as extinct in California, with a related subspecies restricted to the Salton Sea area.
- **Kendall Warmsprings Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus thermalis*):** Endangered in Wyoming. Permits are not issued to seine, trap or use the dace for bait; the only legal take is hook and line. The U.S. Forest Service protects the habitat and stream area.
- **Moapa Dace (*Moapa coriacea*):** Endangered and protected in Nevada.
- **Gila Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):** Locally common in Arizona but possibly in jeopardy. In New Mexico, the Gila drainage population is probably extirpated and may be reintroduced.
- **Monkey Spring Pupfish (*Cyprinodon* sp.):** Believed extinct in Arizona.

### Management and policy by state

- **Arizona:** The Department of Game and Fish has authority over all wildlife. Two biologists are assigned to nongame species, with emphasis on federally listed species. The department published *Threatened Wildlife of Arizona*.
- **California:** The Department of Fish and Game has a cooperative agreement with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. It has three full-time wildlife biologists and two full-time fishery biologists assigned to rare, endangered and nongame species.
- **Colorado:** The Division of Wildlife has statutory authority over all wildlife. The Nongame Staff includes one supervisor, one secretary and five biologists. Emphasis is on recovery through captive breeding, translocation or reintroduction. Federal grant-in-aid funds under the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]] support recovery programs. On June 3, 1977, the Governor signed a bill creating a state income tax check-off allowing taxpayers to donate 1, 5 or 10 dollars of their refund to the nongame program.
- **Nevada:** The Department of Fish and Game has limited funds for nongame species, with priority given to birds and mammals. It employs two nongame biologists.
- **New Mexico:** The Department of Game and Fish established an Endangered Species Program in 1974 with four biologists. Its authority covers all vertebrates, crustaceans and mollusks.
- **Utah:** The Division of Wildlife Resources manages endangered, protected and nongame species, including reptiles and amphibians. Three nongame biologists are employed, with emphasis on nonaquatic species.
- **Wyoming:** The Game and Fish Department keeps an unofficial list of rare and endangered species and publishes a pamphlet describing them. All management units are responsible for nongame and threatened species.

### Report-level conclusions

- The report presents a cooperative, multi-state effort to prevent extinction of endangered endemic fishes.
- It notes that increased state effort to prevent extinction of endangered species was an early result of the compilation work.
- Several species have unknown or uncertain status (e.g., Flannelmouth Sucker and Mountain Sucker in Nevada, Bonytail in one state), and the report highlights the need for further study and recovery team investigations.
- Taxonomic uncertainty is noted for Kendall Warmsprings Dace, which differs little superficially from the [[Speckled Dace]].
