# Ecological Studies of the Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary, Part I: Zooplankton, Zoobenthos, and Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-ecology

## Executive Summary

Fish Bulletin 133 (California Department of Fish and Game, 1966), compiled by D. W. Kelley, is Part I of a series of studies on the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]]. It was produced under the Delta Fish and Wildlife Protection Study, organized in 1961 to assess how future water development would affect fish and wildlife. The bulletin covers zooplankton, zoobenthos and fishes of San Pablo and Suisun Bays, plus zooplankton and zoobenthos of the upper estuary known as the Delta. Fish studies of the Delta itself were deferred to a second bulletin. Chlorinity (salinity) emerges as the dominant factor controlling distribution of plankton, benthos and fish in the bay portion, while residence time (net water velocity), temperature and dissolved solids govern the Delta.

## Background & Description

The estuary is formed where the [[Sacramento River]] and [[San Joaquin River]] meet in California's Central Valley. The Delta was once an extensive tidal marsh, but about 738,000 acres of land and water are now largely reclaimed for agriculture, with about 39,000 acres under water and roughly 700 miles of navigable channels. Downstream, Suisun Bay leads through the 6-mile [[Carquinez Strait]] into San Pablo Bay, which covers 73,000 acres, almost 60 percent of it less than 6 feet deep at mean lower-low tide.

The study area was about 25 miles long, from the Delta through San Pablo Bay. Field work ran about 2 years, with analysis in the third year. The project was financed under the California Water Bond Act through a contract between the Departments of Water Resources and Fish and Game. The bulletin contains no direct management recommendations; its stated practical result was a water plan for the estuary "compatible with all of its uses."

Human impacts are substantial. Reclamation of the Delta marsh began about 1870 and was essentially complete by 1930. Mean outflow to the bay fell from 33.6 million acre-feet (1900) to 15.9 million (1960). Over five million people live around the estuary's periphery, and many exotic species (striped bass, American shad, [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Asiatic Clam]]) have been introduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The estuary has a mild marine climate, though Delta summers are warmer and winters colder than the bay. Tides rise and fall twice per lunar day; the mean range is about 6.5 feet in South San Francisco Bay and close to 3 feet in the Delta. Currents in the Golden Gate and Carquinez Strait reach 8 feet per second, and Delta ebb velocities of 2.5 feet per second are common. A tidal change of 6 ‰ chlorinity was not unusual in San Pablo Bay over about 8 hours.

**Salinity.** The salinity gradient from fresh to sea water is usually about 50 miles long. The bay is generally well mixed. Flood flows create a freshwater tongue over saltier water, and the mesohaline zone moved upstream through Suisun Bay in the summers of the study.

**Hydrology and water quality.** Inflow from the Sacramento, San Joaquin and Mokelumne rivers averages about 22 million acre-feet per year (1921–1957). Water from the Sacramento and Mokelumne is soft and low in dissolved solids; San Joaquin water is hard, with much higher total dissolved solids. Dissolved oxygen is usually above 80 percent of saturation. Operation of the [[Delta Cross Channel]] and the [[Tracy Pumping Plant]] alters flow direction in the south Delta, including reversals in Old and Middle rivers.

**Species context.** Dominant native and introduced taxa include the copepods Acartia clausi and Eurytemora affinis, the mysid Neomysis awatschensis, and the bottom-dwelling amphipods Corophium spp. Native large-bodied fishes such as the Sacramento perch and tule perch became uncommon, while carp and white catfish spread. Commercial fishing for salmon, striped bass, shad and catfish had been outlawed; only minor fisheries for shrimp, carp and bait remained.

## Key Findings

### Zooplankton of San Pablo and Suisun Bays (Painter)
- Based on 383 plankton samples taken monthly from January through December 1963 with Clarke-Bumpus samplers.
- Concentrations ranged from under 10,000 to more than 500,000 zooplankters per cubic meter in the main channel, peaking in May.
- Acartia clausi, Eurytemora affinis and Neomysis awatschensis made up the bulk of the net zooplankton.
- Adult copepods were most abundant near the bottom of the channel; shallow "flats" supported higher standing crops than adjacent deep water in most months.
- Chlorinity was the prime factor determining longitudinal distribution. Genera fell into seaward, freshwater and broad-range groups, roughly matching the Venice System of estuarine classification.

### Zoobenthos of San Pablo and Suisun Bays (Painter)
- Monthly collections were made at 27 stations along eight transects during 1963, yielding 1,107 benthic samples.
- More than 40 benthic taxa were recognized; 11 were of principal importance as fish and game food or were very numerous.
- Biomass of molluscs, arthropods and annelids was far greater in San Pablo Bay than in Suisun Bay.
- Chlorinity was the factor contributing most to benthic distribution, with a distinct faunal break in the eastern Carquinez Strait. Marine and estuarine forms lay to the west; estuarine and freshwater forms to the east.
- Substrate and depth could not be separated as influences because they were closely correlated.

### Fishes Collected in Carquinez Strait, 1961–1962 (Messersmith)
- Midwater trawling with two square-mouthed trawls made 1,233 twenty-minute tows; more than 750,000 fish were caught.
- Forty-eight species in 25 families were taken.
- The [[Northern Anchovy]] made up 63 percent of the catch, concentrated June through August.
- The [[Pacific Herring]] was second at 27 percent, mostly caught March through July.
- Sacramento smelt, jacksmelt, striped bass and American shad together accounted for just over 9 percent.
- Unexpected records included a Pacific saury, a Pacific hake and nine lingcod.

### Fishes and Decapods of San Pablo and Suisun Bays (Ganssle)
- Sixty species were recorded in a 2-year survey (1963–1964). Of these, 31 were marine, 8 euryhaline, 13 freshwater and 8 anadromous.
- Freshwater fishes were few and restricted to the upper estuary; marine fishes to the lower end. Few species were resident; the Sacramento smelt and pond smelt were the clearest examples.
- Ocean salt penetrated farther upstream in the drier second year (1964), and marine species increased in number.
- Neomysis awatschensis was the key food link: young striped bass fed heavily on it, and it was also the main food of American shad.
- Striped bass young-of-the-year were particularly abundant in the Suisun Bay flats, suggesting these shallows are important nursery habitat.
- The [[Chinook Salmon]] downstream migrants peaked April–June and in November.

### Seasonal Distribution of Crustacean Plankters in the Delta (Turner)
- Monthly samples were collected at 20 Delta stations from March 1963 to February 1964.
- Peak standing crop occurred August through October; lows in December and January. A notable dip occurred in May–June after high flows.
- Standing crop was closely correlated with water temperature except during the flood period.
- Stations with low "net velocity" (an index of residence time) held more zooplankton. Velocities above 0.3 feet per second were always associated with low populations.
- San Joaquin River water supported roughly double the copepod and cladoceran numbers of Sacramento–Mokelumne water under comparable conditions. Total dissolved solids in San Joaquin water were consistently more than three times as great.

### Distribution and Concentration of Neomysis awatschensis (Turner and Heubach)
- Twenty-two Delta stations were sampled monthly from March 1963 to February 1964; 15 were resampled March–August 1964.
- Populations were highest in the western Delta in summer, when salinity incursion was greatest.
- Very low concentrations occurred upstream of areas with net water velocity above 0.3 feet per second; none were found where it exceeded 0.4 feet per second.
- Densities were greater in deeper channels at all net flows. In August 1963 at Antioch, concentrations were 4.8/m³ at 5 feet, 35.2/m³ at 18 feet and 84.5/m³ at 30 feet.
- Laboratory tests found mortality began below 5.0 ppm dissolved oxygen, with 100 percent mortality at 2 ppm (at 10–17°C).

### Zoobenthos of the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta (Hazel and Kelley)
- Bottom samples were taken with a Peterson dredge at 25 fixed fresh-water stations; 597 samples were sorted in full.
- Animals from 85 taxa were collected. Only Corophium spinicorne, C. stimpsoni, the Asiatic clam Corbicula fluminea, tendipedids and oligochaetes were abundant enough for distribution analysis.
- C. spinicorne preferred cobble, peat and "mixed" surfaces, usually near shore. C. stimpsoni favored fine and medium sand in deeper, low-flow channels.
- Mean C. stimpsoni concentrations were highest at net velocities between 0.05 and 0.2 feet per second.
- Corophium species were found in significant numbers as far downstream as Honker Bay, but rarely below it.
