# Toxaphene as a Fish Toxin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This early field study by Jack E. Hemphill of the [[Arizona Game And Fish Commission]] evaluates [[Toxaphene]], a chlorinated camphene insecticide, as a selective agent for [[Fish Population Control]] in farm fishponds and reservoirs. The work was prompted by fish kills in Arizona fishponds linked to aerial insecticide drift onto crops. Laboratory bioassays on [[Common Carp]] were followed by field treatments of two small Apache County lakes in 1951. Both treatments killed large numbers of rough fish, and the lakes were subsequently restocked with [[Rainbow Trout]]. The author concludes that partial population control is achievable in large lakes at low cost, provided treated fish cannot escape to untreated water, and that toxaphene poses no greater hazard than herbicides regularly used in the same settings.

## Background & Description

- **Origin of interest:** Fish kills occurred in Arizona farm fishponds after aerial dusting of agricultural crops with insecticides. Ponds near fields being dusted, or near wind-drifted spray, suffered partial to total fish mortality, and young populations were hit hardest. Toxaphene was identified as the causative insecticide.
- **Chemistry:** Toxaphene is a chlorinated camphene manufactured by the Hercules Powder Company of Wilmington, Delaware. It is completely insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents, and has a density of 1.6. It may be absorbed through the skin.
- **Toxicity reference values:** The estimated fatal oral dose is 60 mg/kg body weight, and continued ingestion of small quantities may cause liver damage (Merck Index).
- **Laboratory baseline:** Experiments showed that 1 part per 10 million (0.1 ppm) killed carp at 65°F in a hatchery bioassay.
- **Breakdown:** Toxaphene decomposes under alkaline conditions and breaks down into nontoxic substances at 100°F.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lyman Reservoir (St. Johns):**
- Treated in August 1951 with a mix of emulsified toxaphene and aromatic liquids, dust applied directly into the outboard propeller wash, and sprayed emulsions.
- Conditions at treatment: surface water 70°F, 69°F at maximum depth of 12 ft, pH 8.6, total dissolved salts 370 ppm, Secchi disc reading 6 in, surface area 58 acres, total volume 385 acre-feet.
- Dosage: 0.1 ppm concentration, applied at a rate given in the text as approximately 0.? lb of 40-percent toxaphene per acre-foot (OCR-garbled figure).
- The reservoir holds 32,000 acre-feet and covers 1,300 surface acres at full pool. Pre-treatment fish biomass exceeded 340 lb per surface acre.

**Becker Lake (Springerville):**
- Treated in October 1951 at 0.1 ppm. The 106-acre lake was heavily infested with carp and [[Yellow Perch]], and carp introduced about 1910 had eliminated nonemergent vegetation.
- Conditions: 52°F at surface, 51°F at maximum depth of about 28 ft, pH 8.2. A white volcanic ash turbidity reduced Secchi visibility to 12 in.

**San Carlos Reservoir (Gila River):**
- Experimental treatment destroyed about 800 tons of an estimated 1,100-ton carp population, plus large numbers of crappie, yellow bass (*Morone interrupta*), and a small percentage of [[Channel Catfish]].
- The author estimates partial control in large lakes at about 5 cents per acre-foot treated.

## Key Findings

**1. Lyman Reservoir kill and recovery**
- Carp began dying within 12 hours and die-off continued for 48 hours. Estimated losses included 220 tons of carp, about 3,000 lb of [[Bonytail]], 29 [[Largemouth Black Bass]], about 17 [[Brown Trout]] (OCR-uncertain count), and several hundred bullhead catfish.
- A heavy rain two days after treatment added 300 acre-feet of water and nearly halted the die-off.
- Draining after the rain revealed about 2,000 large carp, 3,000 bonytail, roughly 112 largemouth bass averaging 6 lb (OCR-uncertain count), and 192 channel catfish weighing 3 to 12 lb. No channel catfish were noted during toxicity. Later higher-concentration use was effective on this species.
- Turbidity fell from 6 in to 4 ft, and a plankton bloom persisted through subsequent seasons.
- An experimental stocking of 3,600 six-inch rainbow trout in June 1952 produced fish averaging 16 inches the following May, with a catch of 1.2 fish per hour. Earlier trout plants had reportedly "disappeared" from anglers' catches.

**2. Becker Lake kill and recovery**
- Yellow perch showed distress about 30 hours after application, followed by golden shiners and small carp. Within 48 hours, many 2- to 4-lb carp were circling at the surface. Carp died over the next 11 days, after which no fish activity was found.
- Periodic sampling over two years found no rough fish.
- 25,000 rainbow trout fry were planted in spring 1952. A sample of 121 fish grew at an average of 1.2 inches per month during the first year. Turbidity dropped from about 1 ft to about 2 ft in winter (OCR-uncertain figure), and the lake did not appear "milky" despite high winds.

**3. Effects on non-target organisms**
- Insect life was severely affected but not eliminated.
- Frogs and water snakes were the only vertebrates other than fish showing toxic symptoms.
- Zooplankton rebound after toxin dissipation was used as the indicator for restocking, and stocking was done as soon as possible to exploit fertilization from decaying fish.
- Treated waters with pH 8.0 or higher were restocked with desirable fish within 4 weeks.

**4. Livestock and human safety**
- Calculations show a cow would need to drink about 19,000 gallons of water per day from water treated at 0.1 ppm to show toxic symptoms.
- On four occasions, livestock restricted for 60 days to treated lake water showed no toxic symptoms.
- Toxaphene is "no more dangerous" than herbicides regularly used when precautions are followed. No danger to human consumption of destroyed fish is expected because toxaphene breaks down at 100°F.

**5. Recommendations and limitations**
- Partial population control of large lakes is feasible at about 5 cents per acre-foot treated.
- The critical limitation is that distressed fish must have no opportunity to migrate into fresh water.
- Water used for domestic purposes should not be treated.
- Streams or lakes with large inflows would give a very poor kill.
- Deep lakes have not yet been treated. A good kill there is unlikely because dust-form toxaphene falls rapidly to the bottom.

**Cited literature:** Lawrence, J. M. (1950), toxicity of new insecticides to pondfish species, *Progressive Fish-Culturist* 12(3): 141–146, which the author states is borne out in these findings for large reservoirs.
