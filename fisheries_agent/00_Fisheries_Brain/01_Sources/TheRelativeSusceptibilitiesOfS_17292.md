# The Relative Susceptibilities of Some Species of Fresh-Water Fish to Poisons—I. Ammonia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by [[Jan R. Ball]] at the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] compares the acute toxicity of [[Ammonia Toxicity|ammonia]] to four coarse fish species (bream, perch, roach and rudd) and [[Rainbow Trout]]. The study tests the common belief that [[Salmonid Fish]] are more sensitive to pollutants than [[Coarse Fish]]. Trout were more sensitive over a 1-day exposure, but their mortality later dropped. The coarse fish kept dying, and when tests ran until the toxicity curves became asymptotic, all species were roughly equally sensitive. The paper also argues that the slope of the concentration-response curve matters as much as the lethal concentration when setting permissible limits in the field.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Trout are commonly used in toxicity trials in Europe and North America, but earlier studies by [[Wuhrmann]] and [[Wuhrmann And Woker]] gave conflicting results on whether salmonids are always the most sensitive. Most earlier experiments lasted less than one day, and their criterion of reaction was overturning, which made them hard to apply to field mortality.
- **Programme goal:** The laboratory was testing common pollutants on coarse fish in order to define concentrations at which a coarse fishery could be maintained or established. Rainbow trout were tested in parallel as a reference.
- **Ammonia relevance:** Ammonia is described as a common and important pollutant in many British rivers ([[Herbert]] et al., 1965).
- **Study species:**
  - Bream (*Abramis brama*), perch (*Perca fluviatilis*), roach (*Rutilus rutilus*) and rudd (*Scardinius erythrophthalmus*) were netted at [[Buckenham Broad]] in November 1966.
  - Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdnerii*) came from the Berkshire Trout Farm, Hungerford, and were held for at least one month before testing.
- **Holding conditions:** Stock fish were kept in four 400 L glass tanks with a constant flow of aerated borehole water matching the test water. Stock fish were fed commercial trout food, and trout also received maggots and chopped raw ox-liver. Fish were not fed on the day before testing or during tests.
- **Stock mortality:** Coarse fish losses were 7% during the 4 weeks before testing. Mortality among stock fish stayed below 1% during the tests. Trout mortality was below 1% over the holding month.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test water and chemistry**
- Diluent was borehole water with 360 mg/L total dissolved solids, total hardness of 290 mg/L as CaCO₃, and alkalinity of 230 mg/L as CaCO₃. It was vigorously aerated to keep dissolved oxygen high.
- Toxicant stock was technical-grade ammonium chloride dissolved in deionised water.
- Test conditions by species (pH, dissolved oxygen, temperature and total ammonia range) are listed in Table 1 of the source. Dissolved oxygen ran roughly 88–98% saturation across the tests.

**Toxicity theory**
- Toxicity depends mainly on the concentration of **undissociated ammonia**, calculated from total ammonia, pH and the temperature-appropriate pKa.
- Because pH and temperature fluctuated during the tests, the exposure concentration of undissociated ammonia varied somewhat. The author judged this unlikely to affect the comparison between species.

**Exposure system**
- Fish were tested in batches of 5 or 10 in 40 L perspex aquaria, with flow at 70–100 mL/min.
- A [[Serial Dilution Apparatus]], based on the design of Mount and Warner (1965), delivered the concentrations. Two dilution devices were used together, with dilution factors of one half and two-thirds. Tracer tests with lithium and methylene blue showed deviations under 10% of the target concentration. Each device ran on a half-cycle time of 3 minutes.
- Fixed concentrations were 1, 2, 3.1, 4, 4.7, 7.1, 8, 10.7 and 16 times a selected value, with two control aquaria.

**Statistical approach**
- [[Median Lethal Concentration]] (LC₅₀) values and 95% confidence limits were calculated with the [[Litchfield And Wilcoxon Method]].
- Survival data were plotted on double-logarithmic paper. Tests ran until the curve became asymptotic to the time axis, giving the [[Asymptotic LC50]]. Survival data were statistically homogeneous across intervals.
- Body weight was not consistently related to survival time, although the coarse fish varied more in weight than the author wanted.

## Key Findings

**1. Short-term versus long-term sensitivity**
- Over exposures up to one day, trout were much more susceptible to undissociated ammonia than the coarse fish.
- After that, trout mortality fell rapidly, while coarse fish mortality changed little until at least two days had passed.
- The ultimate asymptotic values are similar across the four species, although the time needed to reveal them differs widely.

**2. Asymptotic LC₅₀ values (undissociated ammonia, mg N/L; 95% CL in parentheses; slope S)**

| Species | Asymptotic LC₅₀ | S |
|---|---|---|
| Perch | 0.29 (0.24–0.34) | 1.22 |
| Roach | 0.35 (0.30–0.40) | 1.47 |
| Rudd | 0.36 (0.31–0.42) | 1.13 |
| Bream | 0.41 (0.36–0.47) | 1.31 |
| Trout | 0.41 (0.34–0.49) | 1.23 |

- The author concludes there is little difference between species on an asymptotic basis. Perch may be somewhat more susceptible, but the text also says the perch data did not suit the statistical method and no replicate test was possible, so no toxicity curve was drawn for perch.

**3. Slope matters for field limits**
- Roach and rudd have nearly identical 5-day LC₅₀ values, but their concentration–mortality slopes differ.
- At 0.24 mg N/L (two-thirds of the 5-day LC₅₀), roach mortality would be about 16%, while rudd mortality would be under 1%.
- Where the slope is low, a larger safety margin is needed to cover concentration fluctuations.

**4. Management implications**
- Controlling an ammonia effluent to protect one species selectively would be impractical.
- A level of undissociated ammonia harmful to trout would also harm all four coarse species.

**5. Reconciling earlier and field data**
- Earlier findings by [[Wuhrmann And Woker]] (1948), which found chub, barbel and roach more resistant than trout, came from experiments lasting only a few hours. The author notes that differences would be expected to be large over such short periods.
- Results from [[Merkens And Downing]] (1957) were also under one day, and the author suggests the species differences would largely have disappeared with a further 24 hours.
- The findings partly explain the field observations of [[Edwards And Brown]] (1967) in the [[Trent River Authority]] area. Those authors found only small differences in water quality between reaches holding roach, chub or trout, which is consistent with the ammonia data.

**6. Permissible-concentration approaches cited**
- [[Jones]] (1964) proposed one-tenth of a 48-hour LC₅₀ as a permissible concentration.
- [[Edwards And Brown]] (1967) suggested fish populations can generally persist where the summed fractions of 48-hour LC₅₀ values for soluble poisons do not exceed 0.3–0.4.
- [[Herbert]] (1965) favoured extrapolating a concentration expected to kill no more than 5% of fish over three months.
- The author stresses that slope, and factors such as life-stage and population variation, should be considered alongside the LC₅₀ value. Most fish toxicologists, he notes, omit the slope.
