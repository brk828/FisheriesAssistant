# Swimming Ability of Non-Native Fishes and Interactions with Exotic Fish Exclusion Structures at Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 report, prepared by Brian E. Bagley, [[Paul C. Marsh]], and W.L. Minckley of Arizona State University for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Phoenix Area Office), reviews the literature on the swimming and leaping capabilities of non-native fishes. The goal is to inform the design of [[Exotic Fish Exclusion Structures]] (EFES) that would block upstream invasion into [[Aravaipa Creek]] and the [[San Pedro River]]. The review was a literature search and data compilation, not new field or laboratory work. It found that the literature is limited and cannot support specific design criteria for minimum height, minimum velocity, or maximum depth. The authors instead recommend that Reclamation engineers and modelers work with qualified biologists to develop a design, and they set out general hydraulic principles for it.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory context.** A [[Biological Opinion]] issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service on 15 April 1994 found that Central Arizona Project water deliveries to the Gila River basin would jeopardize four native fishes: [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It also found adverse modification of critical habitat for three of them. Among the alternatives proposed to remove jeopardy were EFES barriers at:

- Aravaipa Creek: two EFES in close proximity, between the Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness lower boundary and the mouth, to be completed within 3.5 years.
- San Pedro River: two EFES in close proximity, between Redington and Fairbank, to be completed within 6 years.

The Opinion specified that barrier design be mutually agreed upon by Reclamation, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

**Study area significance.** Of 30 indigenous Arizona fish taxa, 29 are federally listed or candidates for listing. Aravaipa Creek still supports seven indigenous taxa, more than any other habitat in the state. Its relatively unmodified watershed and channel, and the absence of non-indigenous predators and competitors, explain this. The [[Central Arizona Project]] canal system raises the risk of non-native fishes entering from the Colorado River and central Gila River systems, especially via the lower San Pedro River.

**Study species.** Seven non-native species were selected for review based on their presence in the area, their potential to cause damage, or their body form and ecology, which might resemble target taxa. Species-specific data were found for five: [[Common Carp]], [[Rudd]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]]. Data were not found for [[Redshiner]] or [[Flathead Catfish]].

**Methods.** The literature search covered:

- Technical libraries (Denver, CO) and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Office of Information Transfer (Fort Collins, CO).
- Online and CD-ROM databases, Arizona Game and Fish Department management files, and personal libraries.
- Keywords including burst speed, sustained and prolonged speed, darting, jumping, leaping, velocity barrier, and invasion.
- Case histories from the Virgin River, Tule Creek barrier, Salt River diversion dam, and Aravaipa Creek.

Quantitative data on speed (feet per second) and jumping ability (metres) were tabulated. The authors compiled a full bibliography of roughly 100 references.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Swimming performance.** Swimming speeds are divided into three categories:

- Burst speed: maintained for less than 20 seconds.
- Prolonged speed: 20 seconds to 200 minutes.
- Sustained speed: more than 200 minutes.

The fastest recorded speed was more than 91 ft/s (about 62 mph) for the marine [[Swordfish]]. The fastest freshwater value cited was about 8.2 ft/s (5.0 mph) for a 24 cm [[Smallmouth Bass]] (Webb 1983). Based on body type, none of the seven selected species would be considered specialized for speed (Videler 1993). The authors regard the tabulated values as conservative. Body size, water temperature, dissolved oxygen, pollution, conditioning, and individual variation all affect performance. Most studies also measured sustained or environmentally affected speeds in small laboratory fish rather than maximum velocities, so their applicability to field conditions is unknown.

**Leaping ability.** Leaping ability is as variable as swimming speed. Key observations include:

- [[Sea Lamprey]] were blocked by a physical barrier about 1 ft (12 in) high on Great Lakes rivers (Hunn & Youngs 1980).
- Salmonids can use kinetic energy in submerged waves below falls to gain lift for leaping (Stuart 1962).
- The only leaping record for a target species is Ellis (1974). Green sunfish leaped from a pond into a culvert, and traps placed up to about 30 inches above the pond captured fish as small as 3.4 inches at the greatest height sampled.

**Natural and artificial barriers.**

- In New Mexico, natural falls of about 6.5 ft seemed to exclude non-native trouts from [[Gila Trout]] streams in McKenna and Spruce creeks (Mello & Turner 1980).
- A Tennessee survey of 30 locales found that dams above 6.0 ft blocked most fish passage. A height of 8 ft or more was thought necessary to preclude upstream movement of [[Common Carp]].
- The [[Roaring River]] "apron-type" barrier in Tennessee (built 1973) is about 230 ft long, 11.3 ft high, and 50 ft thick. Bulow et al. (1988) found that none of seven tagged species passed upstream, but discharges exceeded 1000 cfs on seven occasions (maximum above 4350 cfs), against a normal flow of about 250 cfs. The structure backed water about 1.0 mile upstream, creating a silt-laden pool.

**Failures in the American Southwest.** Numerous EFES 1.5 to 30 ft high were built to protect [[Yaqui Topminnow]] habitat at the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] in Arizona. Over seven years, all failed to exclude [[Mosquitofish]]. These fish moved through overland sheet flow during rainstorms and climbed inclines exceeding 3.0 ft of total height. Human transfer of mosquitofish above the barriers was also apparent. A similar problem with Gila topminnow and mosquitofish occurred at Bylas Springs, Arizona (Marsh & Minckley 1990). A 10-ft log barrier on Ord Creek, Arizona, also failed to stop upstream movement of [[Rainbow Trout]]. The authors suggest the structure may have been adequate and that upstream transport resulted from human intervention.

## Key Findings

**1. Limited literature, no design criteria.** The authors could not provide the minimum height, minimum velocity, and maximum depth criteria that Reclamation requested. These variables interact in complex ways that cannot be simulated effectively. The authors offered to work with Reclamation engineers and other qualified specialists to develop a design.

**2. Hydraulic principles for EFES design.**

- Large fish swim faster and leap higher than small fish, so water downstream of any EFES should be kept as shallow as possible.
- Vertical structures that create downstream pool habitat must be avoided, because pools provide resting and launching zones.
- A relatively expansive apron is preferred. Turbulence and water depth over it should be minimized.
- Flow over the apron should be as [[Laminar Flow]] as possible, with no zones of turbulence or slow water that offer refuge.
- Fish swimming in swift, laminar water may face drag forces up to five times greater than those experienced while swimming below the surface (Videler 1993).
- Eddies and turbulence provide rest between leaps, so they should be eliminated.

**3. Human intervention.** Regardless of EFES type, human transfer of fish will remain a problem and must be countered by all available means, including public education.

**4. Trade-offs in design.** No EFES appears to be both environmentally acceptable and sufficient at all discharge levels.

- A high structure would create an upstream impoundment. This is unacceptable because it would favour non-indigenous fishes and because landowners strongly oppose such impoundments on aesthetic and other grounds.
- A structure effective at low, modal, and moderate discharges (perhaps 300–500 cfs), including on the falling limb of floods, should block access by non-indigenous taxa like those examined.
- Laminar flow and high velocity over the structure should be sought at discharges above about 300–500 cfs.
- At the highest discharges (above roughly 1000–5000 cfs), upstream movement through constrained channel reaches should be largely precluded in any case.

**5. Design recommendations.** Any EFES must be designed for essentially laminar flow across the full width and length of the apron. This eliminates low-velocity, turbulent, or non-uniform zones that fish could use for resting or launching. The authors suggest curved surfaces rather than corners or angles at side junctions, between apron and vertical face, and on the apron itself.
