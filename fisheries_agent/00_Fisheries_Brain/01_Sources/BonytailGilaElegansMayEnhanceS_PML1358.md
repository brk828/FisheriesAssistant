# Bonytail May Enhance Survival of Razorback Suckers by Preying on Juvenile Red Swamp Crayfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This U.S. [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] study tests whether juvenile [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) can reduce predation and competition pressure from [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*) in ponds used to rear endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Crayfish are an introduced predator, and standard [[Minnow Trap]] control with 6.34 mm mesh lets crayfish under about 17 mm carapace length (CPL) escape. Juvenile bonytail readily ate young crayfish of 3 to 15 mm CPL in laboratory aquaria. Crayfish consumption was not reduced by an alternate food source, but cover reduced predation only slightly. The authors conclude that integrating bonytail and razorback sucker rearing programs could benefit both endangered species.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] supports a highly differentiated fish fauna, about 7% of which is endemic. Of 13 native fishes in the mainstem below Grand Canyon, only bonytail and razorback sucker persist, and both are federally listed as endangered. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] has been re-established immediately below [[Davis Dam]].

Razorback suckers in [[Lake Mohave]] formed a large population in the early 1950s as the reservoir filled, but recruitment has been extremely low since then. Most adults are over 40 years old, and the indigenous population is declining. Predation by introduced sport fishes is identified as the main cause of near-total recruitment failure. Both species spawn annually in Lake Mohave, but their larvae do not survive.

Recovery relies on a head-start program. Wild larvae are reared to about 150 mm total length (TL) at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and moved to ponds without predatory fish. Sub-adults near 300 mm TL are repatriated to the lake. Repatriates first appeared in breeding aggregations in 1993 and made up about 21% of the Lake Mohave razorback population by 1999. A similar effort for bonytail was being started. The study supports the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] and the Bureau's own recovery program.

Red swamp crayfish were introduced to the system and colonized rearing ponds. They add a predator that can significantly reduce larval and juvenile razorback survival, and [[Odonata]] nymphs are also a known predator. The bonytail diet includes aquatic and terrestrial insects, plant material, and filamentous algae. Crayfish are not native to the Colorado River, and no earlier study had shown that bonytail eat juvenile crayfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Trapping limits.** Adult crayfish are removed with modified 6.34 mm (0.25 inch) barrel-type minnow traps, but reproduction by the remaining adults keeps densities high enough to suppress razorback survival. Traps also preferentially catch males, while berried females and young stay hidden. The authors note that mesh size limits removal of small crayfish, and that crayfish fecundity makes control harder.

**Crayfish biology.** Red swamp crayfish in warmer climates can mate year-round and produce up to three generations per year. Eggs and instar 1 stay attached to the mother's abdomen, so they are likely protected from bonytail. Instar 2 (about 3 mm CPL) is the first stage that separates from the mother, and it was the smallest size tested.

**Hatchery setting.** Experiments were run at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, about 17 km (11 mi) downriver from Hoover Dam. Bonytail were 100 to 200 mm TL, hatchery-raised on trout pellets, and held for seven days before trials. Crayfish came from adults collected at Lake Mohave backwaters in September 1999. Hatchery broods ranged from about 150 to 500 young, with growth averaging 1 to 2 mm per week.

**Pond complexity.** Rearing ponds are more complex than 38-L aquaria and contain a more diverse food supply. Ponds also provide ample escape cover for crayfish.

**Possible risk to larvae.** [[Johnson and Hines]] (1999) suggested that bonytail might prey on razorback larvae in clear backwater ponds. The authors argue this risk is small because larvae are reared to about 150 mm TL before pond release, and because the two species evolved together in sympatry, so bonytail are unlikely to be a greater threat than the exotic crayfish.

## Key Findings

**Experiment 1: Trap escape size.** After a four-day trial in 6.34 mm mesh cages, 73% of crayfish under 14 mm CPL were outside the cage, while 91% of those over 17 mm CPL were inside. No crayfish over 20 mm CPL escaped. Crayfish between 14 and 17 mm CPL were the transition zone. Table 1 lists the sizes tested, with one crayfish unaccounted for in one trial.

**Experiment 2: Size-specific predation.**
- Groups of juveniles of four size classes (104, 131, 147, and 172 mm mean TL) were offered 3, 4, 6, and 7 mm CPL crayfish. The smallest bonytail (104 mm) ate most 6 and 7 mm crayfish within 1 hour and most 3 mm crayfish within 4 hours.
- The 131 mm class ate almost all crayfish of these four sizes within 4 hours, but only 20% of 10 mm crayfish and none of the larger sizes.
- The 147 mm class took 20% of 15 mm crayfish.
- The largest class (172 mm) ate 10, 4, 6, and 7 mm crayfish most rapidly and consumed about half of the 3, 11, 14, and 15 mm crayfish within the trial period.
- Single-crayfish trials gave these results: 9 mm CPL, 100% eaten (N=5); 10 mm, 80% (N=5); 13 mm, 17% (N=6); 14 mm, 33% (N=3). Crayfish of 16, 17, and 21 mm CPL were not eaten.
- Most eaten crayfish were under 8% of the predator's TL. One 107 mm TL bonytail ate a 13 mm CPL crayfish, about 13% of its length.

**Experiment 3: Alternate food.** Over two hours, bonytail ate significantly fewer pellets when crayfish were present (M = 7.7, SD = 2.1) than when pellets were offered alone (M = 9.3, SD = 2.5; t(2 df) = 5.0, p = 0.02). Crayfish intake was not reduced by pellets (M = 34 with pellets, SD = 7.0, versus M = 27 without, SD = 10.2; p = 0.29). Bonytail therefore preferred crayfish to pellets even though they were habituated to pellets.

**Experiment 4: Cover.** After 16 hours, none of 50 crayfish remained in tanks without cover, 14% remained in tanks with horizontal cover, and 26% remained with vertical cover. Cover reduced short-term predation only slightly, and vertical cover gave more protection than horizontal cover. Crayfish are vulnerable once they move away from cover.

**Consumption rate.** Discussion estimates an average consumption of about 15 crayfish per hour. If sustained for part of each day in ponds, this could remove large numbers of young crayfish before they can prey on young razorbacks.

**Conclusions.**
- Juvenile bonytail may reduce numbers of crayfish under 15 mm CPL in razorback rearing ponds, sizes that trapping cannot remove.
- Combining bonytail and razorback sucker rearing programs could improve survival of both species, since crayfish predation would decline and razorback survival would rise.
- Trapping mainly removes males and non-reproductive females, so reducing reproductive females and young requires other methods.
