# Inventory of Fish Species and the Aquatic Environment of Fifteen Backwaters of the Topock Gorge Division of the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game administrative report (Administrative Report No. 76-4, September 1976, by Charles W. Marshall, Region 5, Inland Fisheries) inventories fifteen [[Backwater]] areas of the [[Topock Gorge]] Division of the [[Colorado River]]. The gorge is one of the few unchannelized California reaches of the river. Surveys combined mapping, physical and chemical water data, [[Plankton]] and [[Benthic Fauna]] sampling, and fish inventories using gill nets and electrofishing. The authors document progressive siltation, cattail encroachment, and the fishery value of each backwater, and caution that dredging or channelization would harm both fish and wildlife habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Topock Gorge Division covers about 22.5 km (14 miles) of river and adjacent backwater, lying within the [[Havasu Lake National Wildlife Refuge]] near Topock, Arizona, and Lake Havasu. The river forms the California border; the division is one of the few unchanged sections in California.
- **Regulated river:** The Colorado River is among the most intensively managed streams in the world; all water produced is diverted for agricultural, industrial, or domestic use, and the river no longer reaches the Gulf of California. Only three California sections (about 36.7 miles total) remain relatively unaltered, including Topock Gorge.
- **Climate:** The area is one of the most severe deserts in the United States, with annual precipitation of 7.6 to 12.9 cm (3 to 5 inches), summer maxima commonly above 43 °C (110 °F), and spring sand or dust storms.
- **Flow regime:** Seasonal flows range from about 461 m³/s (17,000 cfs) to about 57 m³/s (2,000 cfs), with a mean yearly flow of about 311 m³/s (11,000 cfs). Peak flow occurs March through July. Daily fluctuations are large; in April 1973 the monthly mean of 421 m³/s was accompanied by daily flows from 294 to 490 m³/s.
- **Historical management:** [[Parker Dam]] (begun 1934, completed 1938) created Lake Havasu, forming many of the gorge's coves and backwaters. The refuge was established in 1941. Sediment from [[Hoover Dam]] (1938 onward) and [[Davis Dam]] (1953) has caused aggradation, flood threats at Needles, and dredging and channelization efforts. A 1966 Bureau of Reclamation plan to channelize the full gorge was partly begun and then suspended by Secretary Udall in June 1969.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types:** Each backwater's shoreline was mapped into three bank categories: Desert Upland Edge (mesquite, salt cedar, arrowweed), Marsh (cattail and bulrush), and Rock Cliffs.
- **Area:** About 975 ha (2,410 acres) of open water occur at high flows, of which about 223 ha (550 acres) are backwater. At low flows, open water falls to about 603 ha (1,490 acres).
- **Physical and chemical methods:** Depths were measured with a depth indicator, Secchi disc turbidity, temperature, [[Total Dissolved Solids]], and dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide.
- **Biological sampling:** Zooplankton were collected by vertical hauls, and benthos by Ekman dredge, sieved and counted. Fish were sampled with monofilament gill nets (38 m, several mesh sizes), and a 4.9-m electrofishing boat operated at 240 or 425 volts.
- **Succession:** Sedimentation is transforming open water into cattail beds. Aerial photographs show vegetative encroachment advancing nearly 6.9 m (20 ft) per year in some locations.

## Key Findings

- **Fifteen backwaters surveyed:** Backwaters #1 through #15 were mapped from aerial photographs taken 30 March 1971 and surveyed on the California side of the gorge.
- **Dredging and siltation:** Dredge spoil has covered parts of some backwaters; in Backwater #1, about 35% of the shoreline remains spoil with vegetation. Siltation rates were described as low in several backwaters where water enters only after losing its velocity.
- **Water quality:** Several backwaters had surface temperatures higher than the main channel. Backwater #1 showed no obvious water quality impairment from limited exchange with the river.
- **Biological productivity:** Benthos in most backwaters was dominated by silt, with Chironomus and Corbicula present in varying numbers. Zooplankton hauls yielded counts from a few hundred to over 3,000 organisms per 3.1-m haul, with cladocerans and copepods the main groups.
- **Fish community:** Across the backwaters, the estimated species composition by number was carp (about 61%), with largemouth bass, bluegill, and channel catfish contributing much of the rest by weight. The report notes that electrofishing captured no channel catfish but nearly as many largemouth bass as gill nets. One razorback sucker was captured in Backwater #16.
- **Fishery ranking:** The report ranks backwaters by fishery habitat value. Backwaters #2, #8, #9, and #11 are highlighted for good depth, diverse habitat, and fish populations, while Backwaters #14 and #15 show high siltation and poor habitat value.
- **Vegetation:** Cattail and bulrush encroachment is widespread, and submergent plants such as pondweed and spiny naiad occur in some areas.
- **Management recommendations:** Any major dredging for siltation control would have two major impacts on fish and wildlife: large volumes of spoil would need disposal, and dredging of the river channel could lower water levels in both the river and backwaters, accelerating vegetative encroachment. Natural levees protect most backwaters and influence the surviving open areas.
- **Document scope:** The report includes a species checklist, numerous water-quality and biological tables for each backwater, and an extensive reference list covering Colorado River and Coachella Canal fisheries, triploid grass carp studies, and Lake Havasu and Lake Pleasant investigations.
