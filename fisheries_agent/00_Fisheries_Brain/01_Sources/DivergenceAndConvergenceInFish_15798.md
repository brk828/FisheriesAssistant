# Divergence and Convergence in Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #typhlichthys

## Executive Summary

Carl H. Eigenmann's 1905 *Biological Bulletin* paper argues that natural selection drives two opposite trends in fishes. Divergence produces diversity among related species, as seen in the South American [[Characins]]. Convergence produces similar form in unrelated groups living under similar conditions. The paper's main case study is the blind fishes of North American [[Cave Ecosystem|cave]] systems. Eigenmann describes the cave as a uniform, food-poor "unit of environment" that erases differences among related species, and he names one new species, *Typhlichthys wyandotte*, and one new species, *Typhlichthys osborni*, alongside the previously known [[Typhlichthys Subterraneus]].

## Background & Description

The paper opens with a theoretical framework. Under a geometric rate of increase, individuals with heritable characters that give a personal advantage are preserved. Over time this produces "specific divergence in structure, shape, color, food-habits and breeding-habits" (in Eigenmann's words). Environmental pressure tends toward convergence, which is illustrated with plant annuality and with a quotation from Scott (1891) on parallel skull changes across mammal families.

The **characins** are a freshwater family ranging from the southern United States to south of Buenos Aires. They make up about one third of the South American freshwater fauna. Their adaptive lines include:
- mud-eaters with long intestines and no teeth;
- flesh-eaters with shear-like teeth;
- conical-toothed forms with needle-like teeth and large fangs.

Eigenmann lists characins that parallel North American fishes. Examples include *Elopomorphus*, *Potamorhina* and *Psectrogaster* (shad and herring analogues), *Salminus* and *Catadasis* (salmon analogues), *Tetragonopterus* (minnow analogue), *Prochilodus* (mullet analogue), *Mannostomus* (topminnow analogue) and *[[Characidium Fasciatum]]* (darter analogue).

Other convergence examples come from outside fishes and from marine-freshwater comparisons. A goby (*[[Philypnus Dormitator]]*) from riffles of the San Juan River, Cuba, resembles North American [[Darters]]. Darters also resemble marine [[Gobies]] and blennies. The burrowing lizard *Rhineura* resembles an earthworm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Eigenmann defines a cave as a "little world" with the following features:

1. **Isolation:** The cave often connects to the surface through a single narrow vestibule. Epigean (surface) species rarely pass this twilight zone.
2. **Darkness and food supply:** With no sunlight there are no green plants, so all food must be imported. Fauna abundance is often inversely proportional to cave size and directly proportional to how readily openings admit food.
3. **Stable temperature:** Seasonal and daily temperature swings are small. Temperature depends on cave shape and air access, and some temperate caves hold ice year-round.
4. **Still air:** Air currents are weak, and are driven mainly by barometric change and by moving water. At Mammoth Cave a water cascade in one dome creates a local air current. Water flow in cave rivers changes quickly with surface [[Freshets]], although water temperature changes are much smaller than those in surface rivers. No detailed water-temperature records were available.

Under these stable conditions, markers that separate related species, such as coloration and ornamentation, are absent. Related species therefore look alike. Eigenmann notes that [[Amblyopsis]] and [[Typhlichthys]] look alike at the same size, and that [[Lucifuga]] and [[Stygicola]] (Cuban blind fishes) could be told apart only after close examination of many specimens.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution of *Typhlichthys subterraneus*:** Mostly known from Mammoth Cave and Glasgow, Kentucky. It has been wrongly recorded from Missouri and Indiana, and is restricted to the region south of the Ohio River and east of the Mississippi.
- **Corydon specimen:** A single specimen from a well near Corydon, Indiana (north of the Ohio River), was described as ***[[Typhlichthys Wyandotte]]* sp. nov.** It is 42 mm long (snout to caudal base). It is more slender than southern specimens, and its head width is greater than 6 in body length. Its first anal ray is nearer the middle caudal ray base than the anus. Repeated collecting failed to find more specimens.
- **Mitchell's Cave and Horse Cave:** From 29 November to 2 December, many specimens were collected: five from Mitchell's Cave at Glasgow (*subterraneus*) and forty-two from Horse Cave. The Horse Cave fish looked distinct alive, with a pointed head, puffed cheeks, conspicuous white eye spaces, and white caudal fat masses. Its size range is given as 20–50 mm.
- **Description of *[[Typhlichthys Osborni]]* sp. nov.:** Named after [[Henry Fairfield Osborn]]. It has the most extreme cave-adapted features. Its head is 4½ times in body length (to caudal base), its cheeks are much swollen, and its orbital fat mass is round and projects like a dome. Its eyes are less than 0.1 mm in diameter (text partly garbled by OCR).
- **Behaviour in aquaria:** Fish moved readily between connected aquaria through siphon overflows. One specimen crossed both siphons within two to three hours. Horse Cave fish stayed under leaves, while Glasgow fish swam more.
- **Diagnostic key for the three species:**
  - *wyandotte*: head width more than 6 in body length; first anal ray nearer caudal base than anus.
  - *subterraneus*: head width 5 in body length; eyes average 0.16 mm (smallest 0.14 mm); orbital fat mass elongate and inconspicuous; cheeks slightly swollen.
  - *osborni*: head width 4½ in body length; orbital fat mass round, conspicuous and projecting; cheeks much swollen.
- **Central conclusion:** Cave conditions produce strong convergence. The blind cave fishes of Missouri and Kentucky ([[Typhlichthys Rose]]) resemble *Typhlichthys subterraneus* closely enough that expert ichthyologists could not reliably separate them, even though their surface ancestors belonged to distinct genera. Eigenmann regards the cave's uniform environment as a strong test of how far convergence can go.
