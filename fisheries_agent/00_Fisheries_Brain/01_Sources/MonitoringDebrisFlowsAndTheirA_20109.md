# Monitoring Debris Flows and Their Effects in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-cypha

## Executive Summary

This transition-monitoring proposal, prepared by [[Theodore S. Melis]] of the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Desert Laboratory (August 21, 1995), requests funding for fiscal years 1996 and 1997 to monitor [[Debris Flows]] and their effects on the geomorphology, sediment, and aquatic resources of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]], Arizona. The work is funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Upper Colorado Region) and sponsored by [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]]. The proposal argues that debris flows from unregulated side canyons continue to reshape the river below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], building [[Debris Fans]] and [[Rapids]] and altering [[Eddy Complexes]] and [[Backwater Habitats]] that are critical for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]]. It proposes a long-term database, testable hypotheses, coordinated field work with [[Northern Arizona University]] and the [[Arizona Game & Fish Department]], and a predictive probability model for debris-flow occurrence.

## Background & Description

**Debris flows** are episodic, water-based slurries of poorly sorted sediment, ranging from clay to boulders, initiated during intense rainfall by failures in weathered bedrock or colluvium. They commonly contain 70 to 90 percent sediment by volume. In Grand Canyon, they build debris fans and the associated rapids that control the river's geomorphic framework.

The study area is the Colorado River corridor between Lee's Ferry and Diamond Creek. Debris-flow potential exists in at least 529 drainage basins there. Historical data indicate more than 100 debris flows in the park since 1872, with apparent increased frequency in the latter third of the 20th century.

The proposal is a response to a memorandum from the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies program, addressing items III.C(3) and IV.D(1-3). It is shaped by a 1994 biological opinion from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and is intended to support [[Adaptive Management]] of the dam and river.

Logistic and financial details are summarized below under Key Findings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geomorphic controls on the river:** Debris flows deliver sand, fine sediment, and large boulders to the river, altering navigation, sand bars, stage-discharge relations, riparian vegetation, and archaeological sites. Debris-fan constrictions create eddy complexes and the backwaters within them.

**Effect of dam regulation:** Before regulation began in 1963, large river floods (possibly as large as 11,000 m³/s) periodically reworked debris fans. Since 1963, such reworking has been limited to discharges at or above typical power-plant peak releases (>890 m³/s). Interim flows, implemented in August 1991 at 142 to 510 m³/s, generally do not inundate most sand bars and debris fans. Suspended sediment load fell by almost 90 percent immediately after dam closure, and debris-flow-deposited material is now less likely to be redeposited. Tributaries below the dam remain unregulated, so the river-canyon "quasi-equilibrium" has shifted in favor of the tributaries.

**Fish habitat:** Juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) use backwaters, which provide warmer temperatures for development and increased aquatic productivity. Backwaters are created and maintained by debris-fan constrictions. Adult distribution has been linked to recirculating eddy complexes. For reasons not yet understood, humpback chub appear attracted to areas of frequent or recent debris-flow deposition.

**Other biota and resources:** Debris flows disrupt riparian vegetation, damaged the Roaring Springs water-delivery system (1966 and 1995), and buried agave roasting pits in Lava and Dragon Creeks (1966). Shannon and others (1994) studied benthic communities and trophic interactions, which the proposal cites in connection with physical habitat.

## Key Findings

### Debris-Flow Characteristics (from the Abstract and Background)

- Debris flows occur in **529 tributaries** of the Colorado River in Grand Canyon.
- Between **1984 and 1994**, debris flows created **4 new rapids** and enlarged **17 existing rapids and riffles**.
- Interpretation of about **1,300 historical and aerial photographs** spanning **120 years** and **168 tributaries** indicates debris-flow recurrence ranging from **one every 10 to 15 years** in certain eastern tributaries to **less than one per century** elsewhere, with an average of roughly **20 to 50 years** in individual tributaries.
- Estimated peak discharges in **18 drainages** range from about **100 to 300 m³/s**.
- Deposits generally contain **15 to 30 percent sand-and-finer sediment** and an original water content of **10 to 25 percent by weight**.
- Since 1963, documented movement of boulders **0.9-1.2 m** in diameter from newly aggraded fans occurred only at river discharges at or above **>890 m³/s**.
- Debris flows buried or eroded at least **22 sand bars** along the river.
- Howard and Dolan (1981) reported that **25 percent** of all debris fans in Grand Canyon were affected by tributary floods between **1965 and 1973**.

### Hypotheses Tested

**Frequency**
- H₀: Debris-flow frequency is increasing, related to hydroclimatic change.
- Hₐ: Frequency is naturally highly variable on decadal-to-century scales and generally random through recent geologic time.

**Magnitude**
- H₀: Magnitude is related to the scale of hydroclimatic forcing (storm types).
- Hₐ: Magnitude is related mainly to drainage-basin characteristics (sediment availability, drainage area).

**Sand storage in mainstem pools**
- H₀: Sand storage in low-velocity pools upstream of aggraded fans is significantly enhanced after debris flows.
- Hₐ: It is not significantly affected at a local scale.

**Interim flows and reworking**
- H₀: Interim flows are mostly ineffective at eroding fans and rapids aggraded since 1991.
- Hₐ: Interim flows can erode them, particularly when tributary floods push river flow above **>890 m³/s**.

**Backwater habitat**
- H₀: Under interim flows, the number and area of backwaters will continue to decrease.
- Hₐ: They will equilibrate until another perturbation (spike or debris flow) changes them.
- Additional hypotheses address whether backwater distribution is related to fan and eddy geomorphology and to downstream-increasing sand availability between Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead.

### Methods

- **Debris-flow sampling** is event-driven, following the timing and location of natural floods, using standard methods (Melis and others, 1994), GCES metadata standards, topographic surveys, and repeat photography.
- **Backwater evaluation** uses repeat aerial and oblique photography, bathymetric and topographic data from NAU and AGFD monitoring, and GIS integration of databases.
- **Logistics:** one annual 14-day river trip in February-March of each fiscal year, with additional trips as needed after summer monsoon debris flows. One dedicated integrative fieldwork trip is proposed. Much of the work occurs in a GIS framework.

### Timetable

- **FY1996 and FY1997:** annual 14-day monitoring expedition each February-March; analysis of spring data in spring-summer; cooperative integration with NAU and AGFD beginning October 1995 (FY96) and October 1996 (FY97).
- Funding period: **October 1, 1995 to September 30, 1997**.

### Deliverables

1. Report on the reworking of debris fans (1987-1997) and comparison of interim and non-interim flow effectiveness (final draft by end of FY1997).
2. Report on debris-flow frequency and a predictive probability model based on historical data, drainage characteristics, and climate (final draft by end of FY1997).
3. Journal paper on magnitude and flow transformations of Grand Canyon debris flows (final draft by end of 1996).
4. Journal paper on relations between mainstem geomorphology and tributary characteristics and the distribution of endangered fish-habitat types (final draft by end of FY1997).
