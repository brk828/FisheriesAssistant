# Secondary Production and Life History of the Introduced Asiatic Clam in a Central Arizona Canal

#malacology #aquatic-ecology #corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This study estimates the [[Secondary Production]] and life history of an introduced population of [[Asiatic Clam]] ([[Corbicula Fluminea]]) in a small earthen canal in the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]], central Arizona. Sampling ran for 12 months (November 1981 to October 1982) in [[Lateral 9.5]]. The clams showed a monthly mean density of 2,255 m⁻², a single annual spawning, a 2-year life span, and overlapping cohorts. Annual production by the [[Size-Frequency Method]] was 25.62 g DM m⁻², and cohort production by a composite [[Allen Curve]] was 29.32 g DM m⁻². The authors describe these as among the highest single-species mollusc estimates reported, and argue that clams play a major role in organic matter and nutrient dynamics in [[Canal Ecosystems]].

## Background & Description

*C. fluminea* is a freshwater bivalve native to Asia. It was first recorded in North America in 1938 and reached California in 1953. It appeared in Phoenix irrigation canals in 1956 and is now abundant in lakes, rivers and canals across the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. In its native range it is a food resource. Where introduced, it is a nuisance that fouls water-delivery infrastructure through shell accumulation and sediment aggradation (see [[Sediment Aggradation]]).

Arid southwestern North America now carries much of its flowing water in canals. These support diverse fish assemblages and macroinvertebrate communities that differ from those of natural lotic systems. *Corbicula* is the predominant macroinvertebrate in these canals. Previous studies report variable life histories across populations, which the author attributes to local substrate, temperature and water chemistry.

The study aimed to estimate secondary production and describe the life history of *C. fluminea* in a canal, to improve understanding of canal ecology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study reach.** Sampling took place in a 100 m reach of Lateral 9.5 of the [[Consolidated Canal System]] in Mesa. The earthen canal is 15.2 km long and serves irrigation and floodwater conveyance. Key physical features:

- Trapezoidal cross-section, side slopes 1.5:1
- Mean water width 7.7 m; mid-channel depth 0.95 m
- Mean velocity 0.07 m s⁻¹, fairly constant flow over an 11-month operational period
- Autumn stilling and partial dewatering (not drying) during maintenance
- Substrate predominantly silt, with minor sand and rare fine gravel
- Sparse algae and macrophytes during the study
- Midday water temperature 12.5 °C (January) to 30.5 °C (September)

**Sampling.** Five benthos samples were collected on each of 10 dates, using a hand-operated [[Ekman Grab]] (232 cm²) to a depth of at least 15 cm. Samples were preserved in 10% formalin and processed in the lab. The author judged five samples adequate because preliminary collections (N = 20) showed relatively uniform clam distribution.

**Biomass conversion.** Tissue dry mass was estimated from the regression log DM = −2.118 + 3.117 (log SL), taken from [[Aldridge and McMahon]] (1978). A direct dry-mass check on a fresh subsample (SL 2–25 mm, N = 60) agreed within 5%.

**Ecosystem role.** The author outlines several roles for clams:

- As filter feeders, they can strongly regulate particulate organic transport where density is high (see [[Filter Feeding]]).
- Their [[Pseudofaeces]] bind with sand and precipitate, storing organic matter in the sediment.
- Dead clams remain in place because of their infaunal habit and shell mass, so tissue material can enrich sediment and water, stimulating decomposers and primary production.
- Fish use clams only lightly as food, though pseudofaeces may contribute detrital food.

**Canal stability.** Canals lack the floods that periodically reset southwestern desert streams, so clam populations can accumulate massively. The author notes this stability as a condition for high production and in situ utilization of dead tissue, a contrast to natural streams where aquatic insects dominate production and are exported downstream.

## Key Findings

### Population and life history

- **Density:** Monthly mean density ranged from 215 ± 52 m⁻² (October 1982) to 9,988 ± 5,934 m⁻² (February 1982), with an annual mean of 2,255 m⁻².
- **Temporal pattern:** Density was low in November, rose rapidly to February, declined gradually to June, and then remained constant.
- **Size:** Monthly mean shell length stayed under 2 mm through the period of greatest abundance and into April. It then increased linearly to 18.0 mm in October 1982. The largest individuals recorded reached 28 mm.
- **Recruitment:** Length-frequency data indicate recruitment in winter (November–February), coinciding with peak numbers.
- **Reproduction:** Marsupial larvae were found only in June and July, so the population spawns once per year in mid-summer. Spawning occurs only after clams exceed about 14 mm SL, so first-year clams do not reproduce. Reproductive state was examined in 187 clams over 10 mm SL.
- **Life span:** The population was treated as hemivoltine, with a 2-year life span, a single spawning period and overlapping cohorts.
- **Sampling bias:** Clams over 20 mm were sampled poorly in January–March because they sat deeper in the sediment. In February 1983, a graded core series (10 samples, 5 cm increments to 30 cm) found large clams mostly at 15–20 cm depth. The author interprets the June–October large-clam mode as a cohort recruited in 1980–1981.

### Secondary production

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual production (P, size-frequency) | 25.62 g DM m⁻² (256 kg ha⁻¹) |
| Annual turnover ratio (P:B) | 2.70 |
| Mean annual standing crop (B) | 9.50 g DM m⁻² |
| Cohort production (P, composite Allen curve) | 29.32 g DM m⁻² |
| Cohort turnover ratio | 5.81 |
| Mean cohort standing crop | 5.05 g DM m⁻² |

- About 40% of annual production came from clams over 20 mm SL. The annual estimate is therefore probably an underestimate, because large clams were under-sampled in colder months.
- Assuming a 2.5-year life span (CPI = 913 days) would lower annual production to 20.48 g DM m⁻². The author notes that the two sources of error may partly offset each other, but the net effect is unknown.
- The close agreement of the cohort and annual estimates (29.32 vs 25.62 g DM m⁻²) suggests that errors in the assumptions were small.

### Comparison with other molluscs

- [[Lake Arlington]], Texas (*C. fluminea*): 10.4 g C m⁻² (about 20.7 g DM m⁻²), TR 4.1 (Aldridge and McMahon, 1978).
- Lake Chad, Africa (*C. africana*): maximum P = 10.6 g DM m⁻², TR 2.9 (Leveque, 1973, cited in Waters, 1977).
- Largest annual estimate for any mollusc in Waters' (1977) review: 15.6 g DM m⁻² for *[[Bellamya Unicolor]]*.
- Lateral 9.5 production (25.6 g DM m⁻²) was substantially higher than these. The summary states that annual production exceeds estimates for *Corbicula* in other waters by up to threefold.

### Comparison with other *Corbicula* populations

- Most populations spawn twice a year, in spring and autumn. Lateral 9.5 spawns once, in mid-summer.
- Populations in [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] (California) and Lake Arlington spawn twice and mature at about 10 mm, whereas Lateral 9.5 clams mature at about 14 mm.
- Maximum sizes elsewhere (40–50 mm in California, over 40 mm in Texas) exceed the 28 mm recorded here.
- The author attributes these differences to local substrate, water chemistry and thermal regime, and notes that year-to-year variation can occur within a single habitat.

### Conclusions

- Clams in this small canal achieve very high secondary production, which helps explain their observed impacts on sediment deposition.
- Clam production implies a major role in the organic matter and nutrient dynamics of canal ecosystems.
- Quantifying that role would improve understanding of lotic ecosystems in general and canals in particular.
