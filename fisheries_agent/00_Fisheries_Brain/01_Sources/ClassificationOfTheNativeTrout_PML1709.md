# Classification of the Native Trouts of Arizona and Description of *Salmo apache*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 *Copeia* paper by Robert Rush Miller reviews the native trouts of Arizona and formally describes a new species, [[Salmo Apache]] (Apache trout). The species was known from the White Mountains of eastern Arizona and had been misidentified for nearly a century as a [[Cutthroat Trout]] or as [[Salmo Gilae]]. Miller argues that two trout species once inhabited Arizona, that *S. apache* is the only one still native to the state, and that its survival owes much to protection by the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]]. The paper combines morphology, meristics, coloration, karyotype, osteology, and biogeographic history. It also proposes an evolutionary framework for western North American *Salmo*. A short appended note reports rockfish killed by an underwater Navy detonation off San Clemente Island, California.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** Cope and Yarrow (1875) referred three specimens collected in August 1873 from the White River to the Colorado cutthroat trout. Jordan and Evermann (1896) assigned Little Colorado River specimens to the same taxon. Miller had earlier tentatively referred all Arizona trout to [[Salmo Gilae]] (1950). Re-examination of specimens and records led him to recognize two species.

**Type series.** The holotype (UMMZ 162769) is an adult female, 145.5 mm SL, collected on 17 May 1950 by R. R. Miller and H. E. Winn from the East Fork of the White River, [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], Apache County. Thirty-two paratypes (UMMZ 162770, 60–197 mm SL) accompanied it. Additional material from 1873 to 1968 is listed in the paper. Many of these were used to establish the diagnostic ranges below.

**Diagnostic characters (summary):**
- Sides and belly yellow to golden yellow in life; no red lateral band ("rainbow" band).
- Spotting on body and fins resembling that of [[Salmo Clarki]], including a prominent oval spot behind the eye.
- Tips of dorsal, anal, and pelvic fins milky white to orange.
- 142–175 scales in the lateral series (142–170 in 33 fish, mean 155.9).
- 58–61 vertebrae.
- 22–40 [[Pyloric Caeca]] (mean about 30).
- Vestigial [[Basibranchial Teeth]] in some specimens.
- Diploid chromosome number 2n = 56 with 106 arms.

**Life history and reproduction.** Life history is poorly known. Spawning is thought to occur in March and April, with females laying roughly 200–600 eggs. Incubation takes 5–6 weeks. Hybridization with [[Salmo Gairdneri]] is possible. Propagation of the upper Ord Creek stock at the [[Sterling Springs Hatchery]] began in 1963.

**Size.** Individuals are typically under 25 cm total length, with adults between 12 and 18 cm. Specimens to 30 cm were reported, and a maximum total length of 46 cm was reported by an informant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Type habitat.** The East Fork of the White River, averaging 6 m wide, was a rapid, snow-fed stream with boulder riffles, flowing through boreal forest of yellow pine and fir. Water temperature rose from 7 °C in the morning to 11 °C at 1300 h. Trout occurred mainly in deep pools, undercut ledges, and brush piles. Sampling used a 3.7 m seine. No non-trout fishes were collected. Elevation was estimated at 2,300–2,400 m.

**Predation and competition.** Cope and Yarrow (1875) observed that [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) may drive trout into shallower water. Later, Hemphill (1960) reported that rising water temperatures pushed trout habitat upstream, allowing native suckers and chubs to dominate.

**Historic decline and recovery.** Before about 1945, trout were found in streams down to roughly 1,800 m. The species declined to near extinction within about 20 years. A 1960 survey by the Arizona Game and Fish Department found only Ord Creek contained uncontaminated trout among 10 streams sampled. About 1940, the White Mountain Apache Tribe closed headwater streams to fishing and stocking. Restoration has involved cooperation among federal and state agencies. By 1971, the Arizona Game and Fish Department produced about 20,000 fingerlings, and the species had been re-established in at least 12 streams.

**Historic range.** The species formerly occurred in the Little Colorado River basin, which is a direct tributary to the Grand Canyon. Miller concluded that *S. apache* was likely native there, since specimens were collected before 1896 and an 1897 diversion canal post-dated those records.

## Key Findings

- **New species recognized.** *Salmo apache* is distinguished from its relatives by a unique combination of color, spotting, proportions, fin ray counts, scale and vertebral counts, pyloric caeca, basibranchial teeth, and karyotype.
- **Two native species in Arizona.** *S. gilae* was native to Oak Creek, Arizona, until about 1900, and was extirpated there. *S. apache* is the only remaining native trout of Arizona.
- **Conservation success.** Protection by the White Mountain Apache Tribe and cooperating agencies preserved pure stocks in Ord Creek and other headwaters. Limited sport fishing is now possible.
- **Hybridization in Oak Creek.** By 1913, native *S. gilae* from Oak Creek was affected by introduced [[Salmo Gairdneri]]. Miller later revised his view that all Oak Creek specimens were hybrids.
- **Karyotype.** The karyotype (2n = 56, 106 arms) is most readily derived from an interior-type cutthroat karyotype by Robertsonian fusion of 16 acrocentric chromosomes. Other species show different counts: [[Golden Trout]] 2n = 58 with 104 arms, rainbow trout 2n = 60 with 104 arms, and [[Redband Trout]] 2n = 58.
- **Osteological variability.** Epural number, postorbital bones, and caudal skeleton features vary too much within *Salmo* to resolve phyletic lines. The supraethmoid shape resembles *S. clarki* and *S. gilae* most closely.
- **Phylogeny proposed.** Miller recognizes four or five phyletic lines in western North American *Salmo*: [[Salmo Chrysogaster]] (Mexico) as the most primitive, the cutthroat trouts, the rainbow trouts, the redband trout, and possibly *S. gilae*. *S. apache* is most likely derived from the cutthroat line in the Little Colorado basin.
- **Chrysogaster age.** Fossil vertebrae from Chapala, Mexico, suggest a [[Salmo Chrysogaster]] lineage at least three million years old.
- **Golden trout origin.** The golden trout (*S. aguabonita*) probably derives from an ancestor that entered the Sacramento–San Joaquin Valley from the north. The [[Redband Trout]] of the McCloud River may be its living relative.
- **Taxonomic caution.** Hybridization ability alone does not justify synonymizing these trouts. Miller urges that karyotype studies always record precise locality and voucher specimens.

### Appended Note: Rockfish Killed by Underwater Detonation

On 8 September 1970, a U.S. Navy subsurface detonation about 2.6 km off Pyramid Head Light, [[San Clemente Island]], killed fishes. Dead fish began surfacing about 30 minutes after the blast and continued for roughly 2½ hours. Fish were dipnetted from the surface over about 3 km. Most were juvenile rockfish (25–75 mm), though some were up to nearly 45 cm. Fourteen species were recorded, including [[Sebastes Rufinanus]] (141–145 mm). Two unidentified rockfish matched no known California species. The note reports that the species total in Californian waters rose to 57. Specific counts for the total number of fish killed are partly illegible in the scanned text.
