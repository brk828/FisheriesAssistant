# Razorback Sucker Studies on Lake Mead, Nevada: 1999-2000 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report (PR-578-4, December 2000) presents the fourth year (July 1999 to June 2000) of a cooperative study of the [[Razorback Sucker]] population in [[Lake Mead]]. The study was commissioned by the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]] (SNWA) and conducted by [[BIO/WEST, Inc.]] (BIOMEST in the source), with support from the [[Nevada Division of Wildlife]] (NDOW), the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR), the [[National Park Service]], the [[Colorado River Commission of Nevada]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS).

During the year, 39 adult razorback suckers were captured, 25 of them recaptures. Sonic telemetry, larval sampling, video surveillance, and fin-ray aging were used to study spawning, habitat use, growth, and age structure. Eleven larvae were found in the upper lake near the Colorado River inflow, suggesting a previously unknown spawning area. No juveniles were collected. Several findings point to netting and fin-clipping stress as causes of mortality and negative growth.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was once widespread, but it is now listed as endangered by the USFWS (1991). Declines are attributed to [[Mainstem Dams]] that replaced warm riverine habitat with cold tailwaters and reservoirs, and to competition and predation by [[Nonnative Fishes]].

Reservoir populations were typically sustained by adults that recruited in the first years after impoundment, and they tended to disappear about 40 to 50 years after reservoir creation. [[Lake Mead]] was formed in 1935 when Hoover Dam closed. Razorback suckers were common in the 1950s and 1960s, declined in the 1970s, and were not collected by NDOW or the Arizona Game and Fish Department from 1980 to 1989. From 1990 to 1996, 61 fish were collected (34 at Blackbird Point, 27 at Echo Bay). Two larvae were collected near Blackbird Point in 1995. NDOW stocked 26 subadults into Las Vegas Bay in 1994 and 14 into Echo Bay in 1995, all PIT-tagged.

For comparison, [[Lake Mohave]] held an estimated 75,000 fish in the 1980s but only about 9,000 by 1999.

The study's primary objectives were to: (1) determine population size; (2) determine habitat use and life history; and (3) determine use of known spawning locations. Added objectives in 1998 (tied to USFWS Reasonable and Prudent Alternative Provision #10) were to search for new concentrations with larval light-trapping and to enhance juvenile sampling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** The two primary areas were [[Echo Bay]] (Overton Arm) and [[Las Vegas Bay]] (including the Blackbird Point spawning area). Telemetry also covered Overton and Muddy Arms, Boulder Basin, and Virgin Basin. Trammel netting was added at the Colorado River inflow and southern Grand Wash Bay.

**Lake elevation.** Average elevation was about 1,190 ft amsl in October 1996 and peaked at about 1,216 ft in October 1998. It fell to about 1,206 ft in July 1999, rose to 1,214 ft in January 2000, and was 1,204 ft in June 2000. Declining levels in late 1999 and 2000 built a large delta at the [[Las Vegas Wash]] inflow, limiting boat access to the flowing channel.

**Water conditions and spawning.** Spawning at Echo Bay occurred at about 17 ft depth and was turbid. Turbidity at Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay limited video observation in 2000.

**Trammel netting timing.** Netting was avoided July to October (warm water, stress risk) and during the March to May spawning season, except for May netting at the Grand Wash and Colorado inflow areas.

## Key Findings

**Population and captures**
- 39 adults captured in year four (15 at Las Vegas Bay, 24 at Echo Bay); 25 were recaptures.
- 115 individual razorback suckers handled lakewide in the first four years (excluding larvae).
- Trammel catch rates: 0.58/net night at Echo Bay (40 net nights); 0.29/net night at Las Vegas Bay (51 net nights) and 0.25 including all bay netting.
- Catch-per-effort trend: Echo Bay/Overton Arm 0.85, 0.32, 0.48, 0.58 (years 1-4); Las Vegas Bay 0.29, 0.34, 0.10, 0.25.
- Ripe males were caught from mid-December 1999. The first male with milt was caught January 27, 2000, and the first female expressing eggs on February 25, 2000.

**Spawning and larvae**
- Spawning was recorded on video at Echo Bay on March 9, 2000 (one spawning sequence in 30 minutes). The Echo Bay site was silting over by the end of the season.
- Larvae at Echo Bay totaled 73 in 2000 (95 in 1999; 197 in 1998). Larvae at Las Vegas Bay totaled 32 (145 in 1999). No larvae were found at the Las Vegas Wash inflow in 2000 (21 in 1999).
- Lakewide sampling of 258 sites yielded 11 larvae at three sites, near Grand Wash (6 larvae, April 29), the Colorado River inflow (4, April 30), and God's Pocket (1, May 14). This suggests a spawning area near the Colorado River inflow or in the river itself.
- A 515 mm adult taken by the Arizona Game and Fish Department in August 1999 near Grand Wash suggests the species occurs there. BIOMEST's 20 net nights in May 2000 caught no razorback suckers.

**Telemetry and habitat use**
- Sonic-tagged fish were usually located within about 200 yards of shore, at average depths under 60 ft, and were mostly stationary.
- Fish 464 (tagged 1998) was captured December 13, 1999, fin-clipped, and recaptured January 7, 2000. Its fin was abraded and it was in poor condition. It did not move afterward, so it likely died from netting stress and fin clipping.
- Sonic tag 3434 was externally attached on June 14, 2000. The fish moved away from the release site within 24 hours, suggesting external tagging is viable, but tag longevity is unknown.
- Fish 247 (Las Vegas Bay) and fish 464 used a narrower range than fish in earlier years. Echo Bay and Blackbird Point were confirmed as the only known spawning areas in Lake Mead.

**Growth**
- Mean annual growth was 8.3 mm at Echo Bay (range -3.7 to 32.8 mm) and 10.8 mm at Las Vegas Bay (range -0.7 to 27.4 mm). The combined mean was 9.1 mm.
- Fish recaptured within 16 to 48 days all showed negative growth, which suggests capture stress from trammel netting reduces growth.
- Growth remains higher than in [[Lake Mohave]] (about 2.0 mm/yr per Pacey and Marsh 1998) and the [[Green River]] population.

**Age structure (fin-ray aging)**
- Fin-ray sections from pectoral fins were used. Twelve adults from late 1999 and early 2000 were aged: 5 definitive ages (13 years for several), 6 potential ages or ranges, and 1 unreadable.
- Ages ranged from 12 to 19+ years. Two groups of recruitment were suggested, around 1980-1983 and around 1986, with some fish from 1978-1980 and 1987-1993.
- Fin clipping caused mixed healing. Of four recaptured fin-clipped fish, two had not healed after one month and one of those died. A less-intrusive fin-ray sectioning tool was developed.

**Juveniles**
- No juvenile razorback suckers were collected from July 1999 to June 2000 (fyke nets, gill nets, minnow traps, and electrofishing; 25 fyke net-nights at Echo Bay and 120 gill net-nights at Las Vegas Bay per Table 7).

**Recommendations for year five**
- Continue focused trammel netting, electrofishing, and fyke netting at Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay to locate juveniles.
- Continue sonic tracking and monitoring of the Echo Bay and Blackbird Point spawning sites.
- Search for new spawning populations near Grand Wash and the Colorado River inflow using larval light-trapping; initiate trammel netting and sonic tagging if concentrations are found.
- Age all trammel-netted fish using pectoral fin rays.
- Sample Las Vegas Wash and the flowing portion of Las Vegas Bay, and use weekly video surveillance at spawning sites.
