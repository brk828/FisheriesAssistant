# Grand Canyon National Park: 1937 Visitor Guide

#ichthyology #marine-biology #national-park-guide

## Executive Summary

This 1937 National Park Service visitor guide covers [[Grand Canyon National Park]] in Arizona, which was established as a national park by act of Congress in 1919 and covers 1,009.08 square miles. The booklet combines a visitor's rules summary, a historical timeline of exploration, a geological account of the canyon, and practical details on access, seasons, lodging, trails, and services for the [[South Rim]] and [[North Rim]].

Aquatic content is limited. The document describes the [[Colorado River]] as the canyon's central waterway and mentions trout fishing in [[Bright Angel Creek]] and [[Clear Creek]], but it names no fish species and contains no fish survey data. The note therefore records the aquatic and ecological context only.

## Background & Description

- **Park status and extent:** The guide lists the park as established in 1919, covering 1,009.08 square miles. Only the first 105 miles of the Colorado River's 217-mile canyon, west of Marble Canyon, lie within park boundaries. The canyon is 4 to 18 miles wide, with an average depth of about 1 mile.
- **Elevation:** Park elevations range from 1,850 feet at the Colorado River to 9,100 feet on the [[North Rim]]. The North Rim is about 1,200 feet higher than the South Rim. The South Rim averages about 7,000 feet.
- **Management:** The park is administered by the [[National Park Service]] under the Department of the Interior. The 1937 superintendent is named as Miner R. Tillotson.
- **Access:** The South Rim is reached by road (State Route 64 from [[U.S. Highway 66]]), by the Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway branch line, and by air. The North Rim is reached through the [[Kaibab Forest]] and is open only from May 30 to September 30.
- **Historical exploration:** The guide lists milestones including Coronado-expedition members reaching the canyon, the first American visitors (James O. Pattie and his father), [[Powell Expedition|Major J. W. Powell]]'s first successful boat trip down the Colorado River, and the construction of the [[Bright Angel Trail]] and [[Kaibab Trail]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado River**
- The river is 300 feet wide at the gaging station in the canyon and flows 2½ to 20 miles per hour.
- Depth varies from 12 to 42 feet.
- It carries an average of nearly 1,000,000 tons of sand and silt past the gaging station every 24 hours.
- The river has two main sources, in southwestern Wyoming and northern Colorado, and flows about 2,000 miles to its delta at the upper end of the [[Gulf of California]].
- [[Boulder Dam]] lies 260 miles downstream. Its reservoir backs water to within about 60 miles of the park's western boundary, so the guide states it has no effect on the river within the park.

**Wildlife**
- The park holds about 180 bird species, 60 mammal species, 25 reptile species, and 5 amphibian species.
- Large mammals include Rocky Mountain mule deer, mountain sheep, antelope, cougar, coyote, and bobcat.
- Two squirrels are noted: the [[Abert Squirrel]] on the South Rim, and the white-tailed [[Kaibab Squirrel]], which the guide says is found nowhere else except on the North Rim.

**Climate and vegetation**
- Four distinct climatic and plant zones result from altitude variation. The lower canyon has Mexican desert conditions, the high North Rim has Canadian-type vegetation, and the South Rim lies in an intermediate zone.
- The guide attributes canyon erosion to running water on a slowly rising plateau. Aridity limits vegetation and soil, so brief torrential storms can cause more erosion than a season of rain in a humid region.

**Geology**
- The guide describes rock layers from the Archean, Algonkian, and Paleozoic eras.
- Upper canyon walls are horizontal Paleozoic strata that include marine fossils, indicating deposition in ancient seas, as well as land fossils.
- The cliff-and-slope profile results from alternating resistant and weak beds.

## Key Findings

1. **Aquatic systems:** The Colorado River's large sediment load and seasonal variability are the main aquatic features described. Its flow is affected by Boulder Dam only outside the park.
2. **Fishing regulations:** Fishing is allowed in certain park streams from June 1 to September 30, and an Arizona State license is required. The guide does not name species or give catch data.
3. **Trout water:** Trout fishing is noted in [[Bright Angel Creek]] and the new [[Clear Creek]] trail area.
4. **Endemic squirrel:** The Kaibab squirrel is restricted to the North Rim.
5. **Visitor rules:** Removing or harming natural features, including wildlife, is prohibited. Hunting is banned, dogs are restricted, and the speed limit is 35 miles per hour. Campfires are allowed only in designated spots.
6. **Trail access:** The [[Kaibab Trail]] is the only cross-canyon trail, running 20.6 miles from the South Rim to the North Rim. It crosses the river on the [[Kaibab Suspension Bridge]], which is 440 feet long. Descent and ascent are strenuous, with a climb of about 4,600 feet from the South Rim and 5,850 feet from the North Rim.
7. **Visitor services:** The guide lists accommodations at [[El Tovar Hotel]], [[Bright Angel Lodge]], and [[Phantom Ranch]], along with rim drives, mule and horse trips, and airplane scenic flights.
8. **Cultural context:** The region has about 300 ruins of ancient pueblo dwellings, and four Indian nations live in or near the area. These are the [[Navajo Indians]], [[Hopi Indians]], [[Havasupai Indians]], and [[Paiute Indians]].
