# Fishes of Some of the Lakes and Ponds of Central Massachusetts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Esocidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document reprints a Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game report on the fishes of central Massachusetts lakes and ponds, based on biological surveys in the summers of 1944 and 1945 (53 lakes and ponds surveyed). It covers game fishes (trouts, black basses, pickerel), pan fishes (yellow perch, white perch, sunfishes, black crappie, bullhead), other fishes (suckers, carp), and forage fishes (minnows, killifish, darters). For each group it gives distribution, habitat preferences, spawning behaviour, diet from stomach analyses, stocking history, and abundance in survey and creel data. The central finding is that the chain pickerel, yellow perch and brown bullhead dominated the surveyed waters, while stocked trouts and several basses were poorly established in many lakes.

## Background & Description

The survey was part of the Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game's programme for the lakes of central Massachusetts. The text is a reprint, with editing, of the 1948 fisheries report, and it cites a broad literature (see [[Literature Cited]] in the original, not reproduced here). Abundance was assessed by a relative frequency ranking (Table 10) based on the number of lakes in which each species was collected or reliably reported, and was cross-checked against summer [[Creel Census]] returns from 13 lakes, with 1,663 anglers recorded.

Species accounts are grouped as follows:

- **Trouts (Salmonidae):** [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Brook Trout]].
- **Catfishes (Ictaluridae):** five species, including the [[Brown Bullhead]] and the [[Tadpole Madtom]].
- **Pickerels (Esocidae):** [[Chain Pickerel]] (the only one common in the lakes), with redfin pickerel and northern pike discussed for identification.
- **Perches (Percidae) and pan fishes:** [[Yellow Perch]] and [[White Perch]].
- **Sunfishes (Centrarchidae):** [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], pumpkinseed, bluegill, and others.
- **Suckers (Catostomidae):** [[White Sucker]] and [[Lake Chubsucker]].
- **Minnows and other forage fishes:** [[Golden Shiner]], [[Fallfish]], common shiner, bridled shiner, spot-tailed shiner, [[Banded Killifish]], and [[Johnny Darter]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and oxygen.** Water temperature is the main habitat factor the report emphasises. Brook trout thrive in streams with a midsummer maximum near 66°F and need cool bottom waters with enough oxygen. Brown trout tolerate warmer water than brook trout and do best in lakes with a minimum of five parts per million of oxygen by weight; the maximum summer temperature for brown trout is garbled in the scan. Smallmouth bass spawn at 59–65°F, largemouth bass at 64–73°F, and black crappie begin spawning at 68°F. Yellow perch spawn at 44–49°F in April and May. Minnow spawning temperatures are also given: the common shiner needs a minimum of about 60–65°F.

**Lake type.** Lake type sorted the species. Trout and smallmouth bass need clear, cool, deep lakes with thermal stratification and gravel or rock shoals (Belding's 1927 guide, based on 269 Massachusetts lakes, recommended lakes over 100 acres). Chain pickerel, golden shiner, largemouth bass and bullhead favour warm, shallow, weedy, mud-bottomed water. Yellow perch and white perch are less specific and occur in both warm shallow and cold deep lakes.

**Predation and competition.** The report notes that predators and competitors drove brook trout out of the spring-fed ponds. Chain pickerel have a reputation as trout predators, although the authors (citing Kendall and Goldsborough) think the harm is overestimated and that indirect competition for food is more important. The survey observed pickerel gaining the upper hand in trout waters that also held pickerel. Golden shiners and large fallfish compete with game fishes for food.

**Diet.** Stomach analyses from survey specimens show a strong fish diet in the larger predators (see Key Findings), with insects, crustaceans and plants dominating the diets of perch, white perch, and the smaller bullheads.

**Game fishes**

- **[[Brown Trout]]:** stocked in 11 lakes; 7 were found lacking the chemical, physical and biological requirements for the species. Four stomachs (average 14.1 in) were 75% fish and 25% insects. The largest specimen was 5.9 lb and 22.5 in, from Whalom Pond, Lunenburg, in August 1945.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]:** stocked in 13 lakes; 9 lacked the requirements. Five stomachs (average 13.4 in) were 47.9% crustaceans, 41.8% fish, 10.0% insects and 0.4% mollusks. The report stresses that stocked ponds should have screened outlets, because rainbow trout tend to migrate downstream.
- **[[Brook Trout]]:** stocked lightly in 7 lakes; 5 lacked the requirements. Not found in any surveyed lake.
- **[[Chain Pickerel]]:** the most widespread species. It made up about 11% of the creel catch from 13 lakes. Stomachs of 38 adults (15 lakes, 1944) were 84.8% fish; 78 adults (23 lakes, 1945) were 97.0% fish.
- **[[Smallmouth Bass]]:** stocked in 24 lakes; 12 lacked the requirements. Poorly represented in 17 of 53 lakes, and only slightly over 1% of the creel catch. Stomachs of 42 adults (11 lakes, 1944) were 51.2% fish.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]:** found in 19 of 53 lakes. At Quacumquasit Pond, 82 bass were recorded: 51 largemouth and 31 smallmouth. Stomachs of 12 adults (1945) were 90.7% fish.

**Pan fishes**

- **[[Yellow Perch]]:** collected from 46 of 53 lakes and the most-taken species in the 13-lake creel census, at over 25% of the 1,663-angler catch. The report notes that many lakes have undersized perch, a source of complaint.
- **[[White Perch]]:** relatively abundant in 30 of 53 lakes, third in creel frequency, and over 14% of the recorded catch. Diet was dominated by insects (76.1% in 157 fish, 1945).
- **[[Brown Bullhead]]:** the report gives the northern brown bullhead as collected from 29 ponds and reliably reported from nine, but Table 10 lists 38 lakes; the discrepancy is in the source. Bullheads made up over 22% of the creel catch and averaged 8.6 in in 1,173 fish. Survey specimens (178 from 21 lakes) averaged 7.7 in and 3.7 oz.
- **[[Black Crappie]]:** collected or reliably reported from 16 of 40 stocked ponds, with 335 taken in the creel census (6.39% of the catch).

**Other fishes and forage fish**

- Four native suckers occur in western Massachusetts; only the white sucker and the [[Lake Chubsucker]] were found in surveyed lakes.
- In 21 ponds forage fish were so scarce that no samples could be obtained, suggesting that game fish there feed largely on their own young.
- **[[Golden Shiner]]:** the most widely distributed minnow in the lakes (22 of 53). It matures in one to two years and has a long spawning season, so it is rated highly as forage fish. It avoids cold, clear trout lakes.
- **[[Fallfish]]:** recorded from five lakes, including Webster Lake and Lake Quinsigamond. Adults can reach about 18 in and become competitors of game fish. Not recommended for stocking.
- **[[Banded Killifish]]:** found in three ponds in the 1944–1945 surveys (eight in 1942). Males are patterned with broad vertical bands.
- **[[Johnny Darter]]:** recorded only from North Pond, Hopkinton and Milford.
- **[[Tadpole Madtom]]:** taken from one pond; the report records this as the first Massachusetts record.

**Management points**

- Stocking must match habitat requirements. The report repeatedly notes that many stocked lakes lacked the required conditions, and that the poor showing of stocked trout and basses reflects both specialised environmental demands and indiscriminate stocking.
- Hubbs and Eschmeyer's list of twelve traits of an ideal forage fish is presented as a planning framework.
- The carp is not native to Massachusetts and was found in only three lakes, where it was judged to be of no benefit.
