# Depth Distribution of Striped Bass and Other Fish in Lake Texoma during Summer Stratification

## Executive Summary
This 1982 field study examined how summer thermal/chemical stratification in [[Lake Texoma]] affects the vertical (depth) distribution of fish, with particular focus on [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*). Using weekly echolocation surveys paired with vertical physicochemical profiling (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity) at six main-basin stations, the researchers found that before stratification (May-mid June) fish were distributed throughout the water column. As stratification developed in July-August, an anoxic, warm hypolimnion formed below a "chemocline" (a discontinuity layer defined more strongly by oxygen, pH, and conductivity than by temperature), and fish—including adult and subadult striped bass—became compressed into a narrow band of the epilimnion (8-12 m) immediately above this chemocline. This produced conditions matching the "temperature-oxygen squeeze" hypothesized by [[Coutant 1985]] to threaten striped bass survival. Despite months of exposure to epilimnetic temperatures above 25°C combined with hypolimnetic dissolved oxygen below 2 mg/L, **no striped bass die-off was observed** in 1982, nor in comparable stratification conditions documented in 1983-1984, indicating Lake Texoma's striped bass population tolerates this squeeze without significant mortality.

## Background & Description
This paper is primarily ecological/behavioral rather than morphological, but relevant physical/size data include:
- **Striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*)**: Subadults and adults in Lake Texoma commonly exceed 2 kg; many individuals in the reservoir population exceed 5 kg.
- **Calibration specimens**: Two freshly killed striped bass of 0.5 kg and 1.8 kg were used to calibrate echolocator "hook" (inverted-U) signal identification at known depths (5, 8, 15 m).
- **Juvenile striped bass** (from cited laboratory work by Hill et al. 1981): standard length 55-82 mm, used in pH-gradient behavioral trials.
- Fish detected near-surface early in the study (21 June, ~4-8 m depth) were noted as likely small-bodied [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) based on relative size on echolocator traces; medium-to-large fish (consistent with striped bass) predominated thereafter with no clear size stratification by depth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Habitat structure of Lake Texoma:**
- 36,000-hectare impoundment of the Red and Washita Rivers; 102,870-km² agricultural watershed (SW Oklahoma/N Texas).
- Red and Washita River arms are shallow (max ~18 m); the **main basin** (12 km long, 3-8 km wide) is 22-26 m deep and is where subadult/adult striped bass congregate in summer.
- Red River water is highly saline (conductivity >4,000 µmhos upstream) versus Washita River (400-1,000 µmhos); differential dissolved solids drive a density-based stratification (a "halocline"/chemocline) distinct from classic thermal stratification (per [[Hubbs Et Al 1976]]).
- Turbid water: Secchi depth 1.0-1.5 m (Red River arm), 1.5-1.8 m (main basin).

**Seasonal movement pattern of striped bass** (per telemetry by [[Summers 1982]] and gill-net data):
- Adults migrate upstream into the Washita and Red Rivers to spawn in spring.
- By late May, adults/subadults >2 kg return downlake, concentrating in the deep main basin.
- May-September: large oval-shaped movement patterns within the main basin, with much time spent north of the dam.
- No adults captured uplake (station 8) during summer, confirming main-basin residency.

**Stratification dynamics and fish response:**
- **Pre-stratification (10 May-14 June)**: No significant thermal/DO/pH stratification; only transient conductivity discontinuities (e.g., saline underflow event 25 May). Fish distributed broadly through the water column (modal depths ranged 6-22 m across dates) — vertical position not linked to physicochemical structure.
- **Onset of stratification (late June-early July)**: Discontinuity developed at 14-18 m; DO dropped below 3 mg/L near bottom; epilimnion exceeded 25°C for first time (28 June). Fish disappeared from the bottom.
- **Full stratification (mid-July-August)**: A sharp "chemocline" developed — nearly anoxic hypolimnion, conductivity 100-200 µmhos higher, pH 0.5-1.0 unit lower below the chemocline than above; no classic thermocline (entire column >25°C, epilimnion >28°C). Fish compressed into a narrow zone (8-12 m) just above the chemocline.
- **Wind-driven seiche event (22-23 August)**: Strong SW winds caused an internal seiche, shifting the chemocline from 10 m (upwind) to 18 m (downwind) across the basin — the only occasion when substantial numbers of fish were found below the chemocline in anoxic water, possibly due to temporary loss of positional cues.
- **Late-season breakdown (31 August-13 September)**: Chemocline deepened progressively; by 13 September the water column was nearly homogeneous again, and fish redistributed broadly through the column as in spring.

**Community composition** (from uplake gill-net data, station 8, April 1981-September 1982, n>3,300 fish):
| Species | % of catch |
|---|---|
| Striped Bass (*Morone saxatilis*) | 43% |
| Freshwater Drum (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) | 26% |
| Blue Catfish (*Ictalurus furcatus*) | 10% |

| Smallmouth Buffalo (*Ictiobus bubalus*) | 5% |

| River Carpsucker (*Carpiodes carpio*) | 3% |

## Key Findings
- **Temperature-oxygen "squeeze" confirmed but non-lethal**: From 8 July through at least 7 September 1982, the entire water column of the main basin was either >25°C or had DO <2.0 mg/L — matching the hypothetical squeeze proposed by [[Coutant 1985]] as a threat to adult striped bass survival — yet **no mortality was observed**, despite >650 person-hours of observation.
- **Critical depth zone identified**: At the peak of stratification (16 August), nearly all fish were concentrated between 10-12 m deep. At 12 m: temperature = 28.5°C, DO = 7.2 mg/L, conductivity = 840 µmhos, pH = 8.1. At 14 m (just below): temperature = 27.0°C, DO = 0.5 mg/L, conductivity = 920 µmhos, pH = 7.4 — illustrating the sharpness of the chemocline.
- **Oxygen avoidance is a primary driver**: Fish consistently avoided hypolimnetic water with DO <2-3 mg/L, consistent with prior findings in Lake Texoma ([[Hubbs Et Al 1976]]) and other reservoirs.
- **Role of temperature ambiguous**: Two competing (non-distinguishable) hypotheses are proposed for why fish congregated at 8-12 m: (1) active avoidance of hot epilimnetic water above, stopped by oxygen limitation below; or (2) an innate depth preference for 8-12 m independent of temperature/oxygen, since fish also clustered at this depth on other dates when neither temperature nor oxygen was limiting.
- **pH and conductivity as possible secondary cues**: Chemocline-associated pH shifts (0.5-1.0 units) and conductivity differences (100-200 µmhos) could function as directional habitat-selection cues (per prior work on [[Red Shiner]] and juvenile striped bass), though changes were too small to cause direct physiological (osmotic) stress, especially in an anadromous species like striped bass accustomed to much larger conductivity swings during river spawning migrations.
- **No thermal refugia available**: Unlike Tennessee reservoirs studied by Coutant, where striped bass could escape into cool tributaries or springs, Lake Texoma has no cool tributary inflows or submerged springs in summer, leaving fish with no true escape from the squeeze other than the narrow near-chemocline zone.
- **Population remained robust across multiple years**: Similar stratification patterns were documented in 1983 and 1984, with no significant striped bass die-offs recorded in any of the three years. Adults examined in late winter/early spring had well-developed gonads, and age-1 striped bass were consistently caught in gill nets, indicating successful reproduction despite chronic summer exposure to marginal conditions.
- **Historical context**: The findings both support and refine earlier work (e.g., [[Hile 1936]] on cisco, [[Dendy 1945]], [[Borges 1950]], [[Gebhart And Summerfelt 1975]] on white crappie) demonstrating that vertical stratification of oxygen and temperature commonly compresses fish into narrow water-column strata in reservoirs and lakes — but this study is notable for documenting a population thriving despite chronic, season-long exposure to theoretically stressful conditions.
