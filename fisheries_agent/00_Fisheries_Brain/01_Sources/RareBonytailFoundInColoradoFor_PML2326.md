# Rare Bonytail Rediscovered in Colorado and the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Effort

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

A 1985 *Wildlife News* article reports the first documented bonytail ([[Bonytail]]) in Colorado in nearly twenty years, netted in the [[Colorado River]] near the [[Black Rocks]] area in July 1984. The fish was identified by taxonomy specialists and released unharmed. The article frames the bonytail as the most severely affected of four federally endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]], and describes the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] and state-led efforts to reintroduce and study the species. A short companion item profiles [[Dave Deden]], named 1985 Wildlife Landowner of the Year, whose land supports a healthy [[Rocky Mountain Bighorn Sheep]] herd, and describes a disease-prevention capture and treatment operation on that herd.

## Background & Description

The bonytail is a chub of the family Cyprinidae. The specimen was about 18 inches long. Researchers from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) crew, led by Lynn Kaeding, were conducting a routine inventory with nets in about 20 feet of water when they recognized the fish as unlike their other samples. Photographs and measurements were sent to fish taxonomy specialists across the country, who confirmed the identification. The last previous Colorado record was from the Green River in 1966.

Historically, the bonytail ranged from Wyoming to Mexico and inhabited the Yampa, Green, Colorado, White, Gunnison, East, Dolores, and San Juan rivers within Colorado. Today it is the rarest of the Colorado River fishes and the closest to extinction.

The article also notes that the species list for the recovery effort includes the bonytail, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The razorback sucker was then endangered in Colorado and proposed for federal endangered listing.

The companion item (pages 1 to 2 of the scan) covers [[Dave Deden]], honored at the 12th Annual National Western Stock Show in Denver. His land includes a healthy bighorn sheep population on Jackass Flats near Ouray.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article attributes the decline of these fishes to several factors:

- Dam construction and water flow reduction
- Siltation and habitat loss
- Predation and competition from exotic species
- An eradication program in the 1960s to make way for sport fish

Of 29 major dams on the Colorado River system, the most significant impact came from the loss of the historical flow and timing of the river, according to aquatic researcher Charlie Haynes.

Remnant older bonytails persist in [[Mojave Reservoir]] on the California-Arizona border and in [[Havasu Reservoir]] in California. Several thousand bonytail fingerlings were recently stocked, but spawning efforts have apparently been unsuccessful. The article cites Jim Bennett, the Division of Wildlife program specialist coordinating the project: "As far as reintroduction efforts go, we first need to know more about their habitat requirements."

The Colorado River system has been altered so much that Bennett describes the river as "a smaller, colder river, entirely different from what these species are accustomed to."

Historical comparison also appears in a 1983 Colorado State University Cooperative Extension bulletin by [[Robert Behnke]] and [[Del Benson]], *Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin*. Its authors describe the bonytail's decline using the extinction of the [[Passenger Pigeon]] as an analogy.

## Key Findings

**Bonytail rediscovery**
- A bonytail was netted, tagged, measured, and released in the Black Rocks area of the Colorado River, about four miles east of the Utah state line, in July 1984.
- This was the first bonytail documented in Colorado in twenty years.

**Recovery and research program**
- Division of Wildlife researchers have sampled hundreds of miles of river since 1978.
- The program has inventoried nursery habitats and handled literally thousands of larval fish.
- The project is partly funded by the Colorado nongame income tax check-off program.
- Radio transmitters were implanted in Colorado squawfish to track their movements.
- A federal hatchery in New Mexico is being considered as a source of captive bonytail stock.
- The recovery team includes the USFWS, the Colorado Division of Wildlife, the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management, the Bureau of Reclamation, and state wildlife agencies from Utah, Arizona, California, and Nevada.

**Bighorn sheep and Dave Deden**
- Deden was named 1985 Wildlife Landowner of the Year.
- Biologists netted 39 Rocky Mountain bighorn sheep to treat them.
- Meg Gallagher injected the sheep with oxytetracycline and gave oral fenbendazole to control lungworm.
- The treatment aimed to prevent a pneumonia die-off, like the one previously seen in the herd at Waterton Canyon near Denver.

**Conservation messages**
- The bonytail is the rarest of the Colorado River fishes and is nearest to extinction.
- The authors caution that without recovery action the bonytail could meet the fate of the passenger pigeon.
