# Native Southwestern Fishes: Reintroduction, Management and Status

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of Desert Fishes Council papers and related items (1985–1986) on the recovery of native fishes in the American Southwest. It covers the reintroduction of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Woundfin]] into Arizona under the new experimental listing category of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It also covers management of the [[Razorback Sucker]] in the upper and lower [[Colorado River]] basins, a fertilization trial for larval razorbacks, a larval-ecology study at [[Lake Mohave]], a flow-modelling study for [[Cui-ui]] in the [[Truckee River]], and a checklist of inland fishes of the Baja California Peninsula. The main message is that stocking is one phase of recovery, and that long-term survival depends mainly on protecting existing habitat.

## Background & Description

The paper that opens the compilation (J. E. Johnson, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Albuquerque) describes the decline of southwestern fishes in the twentieth century. Deacon et al. (1979) listed 203 US and Canadian endangered fishes, of which 46% occurred in the Southwest. Johnson and Rinne (1982) estimated that 81% of Arizona's native fishes and 42% of New Mexico's native fishes were protected under state or federal endangered-species legislation.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has written [[Recovery Plans]] for 21 southwestern species (listed in Table 1 of the source). Common recovery actions include protecting habitat, holding genetic stock in refugia such as [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], reintroducing species to waters where they were extirpated, and public outreach. [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2 (Albuquerque) has been especially active in reintroductions.

The species accounts give the following background:

- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): extirpated from the [[Gila River Basin]] since 1950 (last Gila River specimen collected by R. R. Miller and party in 1950). It still maintains spawning populations in the upper Colorado and lower Green rivers.
- **Woundfin** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): extirpated from much of its range; since early in the twentieth century it has been restricted to the Virgin River and LaVerkin Creek.
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): declining across the Colorado River basin, and the subject of differing management strategies in the upper and lower basins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat loss is the central problem. Johnson describes how physical changes above and below mainstream dams have left little habitat for Colorado squawfish. Spawning is not reported from reservoirs or from cold, hypolimnetic waters below dams. Exotic fishes may also reduce squawfish survival. Squawfish were not found in the Gila, Salt or Verde rivers despite repeated collecting from 1965 to 1985. Recent work in the upper Colorado (Tyus et al. 1985) indicates that long-distance spawning migrations may be more important than previously believed.

Woundfin's probable historic range also included the upper Gila, Salt, Agua Fria, Hassayampa and perhaps San Pedro and Santa Cruz rivers. No known barriers prevented access to these streams, so the absence of records may partly reflect few early collections.

Predation is a major threat to reintroduced fish. In the Arizona razorback study, [[Introduced Catfishes]] (*Pylodictis olivaris* and *Ictalurus punctatus*) could remove a significant proportion of stocked fish. Stocked razorbacks of 62–213 mm TL moved downstream at night.

Razorback sucker status differs by basin:

- **Upper basin:** the species is rare. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service collected 23 razorbacks in the upper Colorado since 1982, compared with 165 subadult and adult Colorado squawfish and 300 adult [[Humpback Chub]] over the same period with similar effort. Colorado Division of Wildlife collected 7 razorbacks from 1979 to 1984, with 50 or more squawfish handled per razorback. No evidence of recruitment was found in recent years.
- **Lower basin:** the species was historically the most abundant fish in the lower Colorado River. Only 33 razorbacks, all adults, were collected in the riverine sections and canals since 1969. Seven subadults under 555 mm were taken in 1984–1985 from equalizing reservoirs off the Coachella Canal, which suggests possible mainstream spawning in the Imperial Division.

## Key Findings

**1. Squawfish reintroduction into Arizona (1985)**
- The experimental nonessential listing was published as final regulation on 27 August 1985 (proposal published 10 April 1985). The listing took effect 30 days later.
- On 26 August 1985, 296 Colorado squawfish were stocked into the Verde River near Perkinsville, Arizona. Fish were over 1 year old and 12–16 inches total length.
- On 28 August 1985, a second group of squawfish and 30,000 fry were stocked into the Salt River above Lake Roosevelt. The exact count is garbled in the scan.
- By the end of September 1985, over 117,000 squawfish had been reintroduced into Arizona waters. Stocking is planned for up to 10 more years, with monitoring by the Service and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

**2. Woundfin propagation**
- Propagation at Dexter NFH has been unsuccessful compared with other species. Spawning occurred and several thousand fish were produced, but numbers are insufficient for stocking.
- Wild woundfin from the Virgin River will be moved into at least three selected streams in spring 1986.
- Stocking of the Gila and San Francisco rivers was delayed at the request of New Mexico Department of Game and Fish until impacts on the [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] are assessed. Both are proposed as threatened species.

**3. Experimental listing policy**
- The 1982 amendment to the Act created two subcategories, essential and nonessential, for experimental populations.
- Criteria include: reintroductions must be within the probable historic range; no critical habitat can be declared for nonessential populations; the public must comment before reintroduction; and experimental populations must be geographically isolated from threatened or endangered populations of the same species.

**4. Razorback sucker management in Arizona**
- Management began in 1981 under a 10-year memorandum of understanding between the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Fish and Wildlife Service.
- By 1985, more than 10 million juveniles, fingerlings and fry had been stocked at 28 sites on the Gila, Salt and Verde rivers and their tributaries. Few stocked fish were recaptured more than a few weeks after release.
- In 1985 the Service stocked 3,151,708 young razorbacks in the Salt, Verde and Gila rivers. The cumulative total for the first five years exceeds 9,400,000 (scan partly garbled).
- Two razorbacks were recaptured in 1985. A concerted search in 1985 failed to find any in the Salt, Verde or Gila rivers.
- Recommendations include stocking upstream where predators are absent or few, stocking in winter, releasing small numbers over a long period, and restricting intensive monitoring to one river system.
- Other fishes reintroduced from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] stocks include the Colorado squawfish, [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Bonytail Chub]], yaqui chub, Big Bend gambusia and [[Desert Pupfish]].

**5. Larval razorback sucker studies**
- **Fertilization trial (Papculias, Arizona State University):** Larvae were stocked in ponds at three fertilization rates. Survival did not differ significantly among treatments. Growth did, with the low treatment giving significantly smaller fish and lower total biomass than the high and medium treatments.
- **Lake Mohave (Langhorst, Arizona State University):** Larvae were abundant in the littoral zone from mid-January into April 1984–1985 and grew to about 10–12 mm before disappearing. Trawling failed to locate larvae in open water. Larvae in a predator-free backwater reached about 20 mm in one month and were then eliminated by predation after the backwater was breached. Nearly 40% of invading green sunfish contained an average of 3.9 larval razorbacks each. Food was mainly zooplankton (*Bosmina* and *Daphnia*).

**6. Razorback sucker in the Green River (Tyus)**
- Historically widespread in mainstem rivers, the species is now found mainly in the Green River. Recent data suggest it may be more numerous there than in other upper-basin locations.
- Spawning was documented, but few young were collected, so recruitment appears very low.

**7. Differing management philosophies**
- The upper basin supports federal listing under the Act as a threatened species with critical habitat. Listing would provide funding (Section 6) and habitat protection (Section 7).
- The lower basin aims to re-establish the species within its historic range rather than list it.

**8. Cui-ui and Truckee River flows**
- Cui-ui from Pyramid Lake, Nevada, historically migrated into the lower Truckee River each spring. Derby Dam blocked access, and the Marble Bluff Dam fishway (completed 1976) provided only partial access. River flows were often inadequate for spawning.
- A population model links water management scenarios to flows, habitat suitability and cui-ui population trends, to predict which flow scheme would benefit the species most.

**9. Baja California inland fish checklist**
- The checklist compiles inland fishes of the Baja California Peninsula from literature records and museum specimens at the Universidad Autónoma de Baja California collection.
- It lists about 59 species (38 native, 21 exotic) in 44 genera, in roughly 28 families (family count partly illegible in the scan). The authors classify species by ecological and zoogeographical origin, using categories such as primary, secondary, peripheral, complementary and accidental freshwater fishes.

**10. Overall conclusion**
- Reintroduction programs are only one part of recovery. Long-term survival of southwestern native fishes depends mainly on protecting existing habitats.
