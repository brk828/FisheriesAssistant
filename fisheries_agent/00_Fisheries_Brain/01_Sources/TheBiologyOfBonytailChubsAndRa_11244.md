# Biology of Bonytail Chubs and Razorback Suckers in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 technical research proposal, submitted to the Water and Power Resources Service (Lower Colorado River Region) by [[Larry J. Paulson]], [[James E. Deacon]] and John R. Baker of the [[Lake Mead Limnological Research Center]] and the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, argues that the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] and the declining [[Razorback Sucker]] require a two-year field program in [[Lake Mohave]] before proposed power projects at [[Hoover Dam]] proceed. The authors attribute the historic decline of both native fishes to impoundment, altered flows, exotic fish predation, and loss of periphyton food resources. The proposal sets out a work plan covering habitat and population densities, distribution via telemetry, spawning and reproduction, food habits, and periphyton dynamics under changing discharges.

## Background & Description

- **Historic range:** Both species occurred throughout the [[Colorado River]] system (Minckley 1979).
- **Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*):** Classified as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service; now apparently extinct except for a small persisting population in Lake Mohave.
- **Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Still present in most river areas and fairly common in Black Canyon, but Minckley (1980) contended that the Black Canyon population consisted solely of adults with poor reproductive success.
- **Decline timeline:** Declines began in the 1930s. Extensive sightings of about 500 bonytails and numerous razorbacks in Eldorado Canyon in spring 1954 contrast with later gill-netting in Cottonwood Basin that caught only a few individuals of each species.
- **Hoover Dam operations:** Operation from upper intake gates (1045 ft) until 1954 kept some seasonal temperature cycles. Since then, lower intake gates (880 ft) have produced near-constant discharge temperatures of 12–13°C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam effects:** [[Hoover Dam]] (1935) stabilized flows and sharply reduced suspended sediment, leading to armoring of the river bed and dense [[Cladophora]] mats at Ringbolt Rapids and Roaring Rapids.
- **Lake Mohave formation:** [[Davis Dam]] (1951) flooded 28,509 acres and created about 200 miles of new shoreline across a 45-mile reservoir stretch, reducing periphyton and macrophytes in the lower reservoir.
- **Invertebrate loss:** Aquatic insects, including [[Callibaetis]] mayflies, have disappeared from the river since the 1954 Jonez and Sumner study. Oligochaetes, midges and amphipods now dominate Black Canyon invertebrates.
- **Exotic species:** [[Rainbow Trout]] were introduced in 1935 and support a river fishery; [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]] and centrarchids became abundant, with centrarchids implicated in predation on juvenile razorbacks.
- **Parasites and disease:** Up to 15 lernaea-type parasites were found on adult bonytails in summer (Jonez and Sumner 1954). Most Lake Mohave razorback brood stock held at the Willow Beach Hatchery in 1975 died from bacterial infections (*Pseudomonas*, *Aeromonas*).
- **Reservoir zones:** The Black Canyon area (Hoover Dam to Eldorado Canyon) is cold (12–13°C), swift and low in productivity; the transition zone (Eldorado Canyon to Little Basin) is more productive; the area below Little Basin is true reservoir habitat with thermal stratification at 10–15 m.
- **Planned power projects:** Uprating (49,000 cfs) or adding generators (62,000 cfs) at Hoover Dam could increase scouring, alter periphyton production, and displace razorbacks. Proposed Lake Mohave reregulation drawdowns to 600 ft or possibly 570 ft could suppress spawning and desiccate eggs.

## Key Findings

1. **Reproduction:** Both species spawn in shallow upper Lake Mohave from February to May (Jonez and Sumner 1954; Minckley 1973). Hatchery spawning has produced viable offspring, and a juvenile razorback was captured in Black Canyon in March 1979, the first evidence of successful reproduction there.
2. **Recruitment limitation:** Survival of young appears nearly nil, most likely because of predation by exotic centrarchids, though the authors note other factors may contribute.
3. **Food resources:** Historically, Cladophora and associated periphyton invertebrates formed a major part of the diet. Underwater observations indicate Black Canyon razorbacks still rely heavily on periphyton. The loss of these food sources is proposed as a significant factor in decline.
4. **Parasitism:** Parasites have received limited attention as a cause of decline, though they are commonly observed on bonytails and associated with lesions in older razorbacks.
5. **Proposed sampling design:** Six permanent stations across the reservoir, monthly scuba surveys for two years, and quarterly trammel netting (minimum two days per station) using five 100 m nets.
6. **Telemetry plan:** Radio telemetry is preferred for the river and reservoir, subject to testing by consultant Rudy Ringe (University of Idaho) for sonic noise and for conductivity effects (about 1100 µmhos/cm). Initial tagging is planned with 10 razorbacks in summer 1981 and at least 10 razorbacks and 5 bonytails in November.
7. **Spawning studies:** Weekly diver monitoring of redds in Eldorado Canyon and Little Basin from December to March, with a proposed enclosure in Arizona Cove to exclude predators.
8. **Periphyton and substrates:** Quarterly mapping, artificial substrate samplers at 1 m, 3.5 m and channel bottom at six Black Canyon stations, drift sampling, and pan-type sediment traps to quantify effects of higher discharges and reregulation.
9. **Mitigation goal:** Findings are intended to support recommendations for dam operation changes (discharge depth, seasonal patterns, water levels), habitat enhancement, and hatchery stocking if habitat measures prove infeasible.
