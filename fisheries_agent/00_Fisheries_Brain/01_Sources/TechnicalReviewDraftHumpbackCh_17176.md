# Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This technical review draft is the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service [[Recovery Plan]] for the [[Humpback Chub]], a medium-sized minnow endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was prepared by the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team, with final modifications by the Service, and it summarizes what was known in 1987 about the species' description, distribution, abundance, life history and decline. The plan sets the recovery objective of restoring the species to a nonendangered, nonthreatened status. It lays out a stepdown outline of eight major tasks and an implementation schedule with costs in 1987 values. The document is explicitly subject to revision and makes all goals contingent on appropriations.

## Background & Description

- **Discovery and taxonomy:** [[Miller Taxonomic Description|Miller]] (1946) described the species from Grand Canyon specimens. The earliest written record cited by Suttkus and Clemmer (1977) dates to 1914, when the fish was called "bonytail." Remains also turned up at archaeological sites near [[Hoover Dam]].
- **Morphology:** The species is a medium-sized (less than 500 mm total length) silvery minnow with a brown or olivaceous back. It has a flattened head, a long fleshy snout, a ventral mouth, small eyes, and in extreme cases a pronounced nuchal hump. The hump, the ventral mouth and the associated grooves are interpreted as adaptations to turbulent water.
- **Diagnostic counts:** Dorsal rays 8–10 (typically 9) and anal rays 9–11 (typically 10). The species is separated from [[Bonytail Chub]] (slender peduncle) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (deep peduncle) by hump shape, head form and caudal peduncle depth.
- **Identification problem:** Intermediate specimens are common. Multiple analyses (principal components, discriminant function, chromosome staining, electrophoresis) yielded partial or inconclusive separation, and identification of young fish is usually "possible" or "tentative." Taxonomic status therefore remains an open question.
- **Why so little is known:** Discovery came only after World War II, when surplus military rafts made whitewater travel safer. Sampling difficulty in fast, turbid canyon habitat is cited as the main reason biology remains poorly understood (Minckley 1973).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Historically the species occupied mainstem whitewater canyons and tributaries (Green, Yampa, White and [[Little Colorado River]]). Its range has contracted. It was eliminated from the Green River above the Yampa mouth after [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] became operational in 1962. Cold tailwaters below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] reduced its distribution and abundance in Marble and Grand canyons. Current populations are concentrated in two areas:
- **[[Black Rocks-Westwater Canyon]]** (Colorado River), where the highest known upper-basin concentrations occur.
- **[[Little Colorado River]]**, which may hold the largest viable population and is the major reproduction area in the Grand Canyon region.

Other occupied reaches include [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (Yampa and Green rivers, where the species is rare), Desolation and Gray canyons, Cataract Canyon, and the Colorado River above Moab. Tyus et al. (1987) reported that humpback chub made up 28 percent of all Gila collected from the Green River in 1979–86 and 12 percent of those from Yampa Canyon (n=523).

**Abundance.** Pre-1950 abundance is unknown. Desolation Canyon collections in 1979–81 indicated a decline, with few adults collected despite extensive effort.

**Habitat.** The species is generally associated with fast current and deep channels, boulders and steep cliffs. Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon fish were found in water averaging 15.2 m deep (maximum 28 m). Radiotelemetry of adults in 1985 (29 sets of measurements, 10 fish) showed a preference for eddies with sand substrate, a depth mode of 1.5 m (range 0.3–4.6 m), and velocities of 0–15 cm/s. Young-of-year occupy shallow sandy runs and backwaters below 3 m with silt bottoms. In the Little Colorado River, adults use pools adjacent to eddies and areas below travertine dams.

**Reproduction.** Spawning probably occurs in June and July in Grand Canyon, with the Little Colorado River as the main site. Ripe adults were collected there in March and April (water temperature 16–20 °C), and young-of-year in July. Spawning has also been documented in Yampa Canyon (two ripe females in 1986; two ripe females and eight ripe males in 1987).

**Food habits.** Adults and young forage on [[Cladophora Glomerata]] and bottom, midwater and surface material. Young-of-year (around 50 mm) are assumed to feed on epipelagic and epilithic diatoms and small invertebrates. Fish below Glen Canyon Dam fed mainly on planktonic crustaceans from Lake Powell. Kaeding and Zimmerman (1983) found stomachs dominated by immature Chironomidae and Simuliidae.

**Threats.**
- **Habitat alteration:** Dams (Flaming Gorge, Glen Canyon, Hoover) eliminated habitat through impoundment and cold, fluctuating tailwaters. Reduced flows changed river hydraulics.
- **[[Nonnative Fishes]]:** The number of fish species in the basin has tripled. Channel catfish predation was documented in Grand Canyon, and introduced species are described as strong competitors.
- **Parasitism:** The introduced copepod [[Lernaea Infestation]] (Lernaea cyprinacea) infected as many as 50 percent of humpback chub in the Little Colorado River.
- **Pollution:** Over 16 percent of young-of-year Gila robusta from the Yampa and Colorado rivers in 1981 showed spinal deformities (lordosis), possibly related to pollution or pesticides, though no direct data were available.
- **[[Hybridization]]:** Hybrids are suspected, but their extent is unknown. Schwartz (1972) listed 1,945 known fish hybrids, many natural. The plan treats hybridization as a warning sign of habitat change.

## Key Findings

**Status and conclusion.** The humpback chub has declined in both numbers and range. Black Rocks/Westwater and Little Colorado populations appear relatively stable, but trends are not well enough characterized to rule out future decline.

**Recovery objective and criteria.**
- Objective: restore the species and maintain it in a nonendangered, nonthreatened status.
- Downlisting: when five self-sustaining populations are located or reestablished, including the Little Colorado River and Black Rocks/Westwater Canyon.
- Delisting: when five self-sustaining populations and their habitats are protected through secured instream flows, adequate water quality, and removal of major threats.
- Refugia: at least two refugia should be established as insurance against catastrophe. Approximately 50 to 100 fish would be held in each, one for upper-basin stock and one for lower-basin stock. Candidate sites include Page Springs (Arizona), Niland Native Fish Ponds (California) and Ouray National Wildlife Refuge (Utah).

**Stepdown outline (eight major tasks).**
1. Resolve taxonomic problems in Colorado River Basin Gila.
2. Identify Gila cypha populations and define reintroduction sites.
3. Monitor existing populations (genetic composition, adult and juvenile abundance, young-of-year abundance).
4. Conduct research on life history, population dynamics and habitat requirements.
5. Determine threats (hybridization, parasitism, contaminants, development impacts, nonnative fishes) and protect populations, including refugia, regulation enforcement, Section 7 compliance under the [[Endangered Species Act]], water rights and cooperative programs.
6. Assess reintroduction or augmentation sites, establish production facilities (Dexter and Willow Beach National Fish Hatcheries are candidates), and stock fish.
7. Improve communication, public education, publication and coordination workshops.
8. Define self-sustaining populations and quantifiable downlisting and delisting objectives.

**Implementation schedule.** Tasks are ranked by priority: Priority 1 actions prevent extinction, Priority 2 maintain current status, and Priority 3 provide for full recovery. Selected budget entries (1987 values, FY-01 to FY-03) include:
- Taxonomic status determination (Task 1, 3 years): $15,000 / $75,000 / $75,000, done with the bonytail chub plan.
- Population monitoring: $10,000 / $20,000 / $20,000, initially limited to adults.
- Basic biology (Task 4.1): $5,000 / $5,000 / $20,000.
- Population dynamics and habitat requirements (Tasks 4.2 and 4.3): $10,000 / $10,000 / $40,000 each.
- Refugium maintenance (Task 5.1): $3,000 per year.
- Hybridization significance (Task 5.2.1): $15,000 per year.
- Nonnative fish impacts (Task 5.2.6): $15,000 per year.
- Cooperative interagency program (Task 5.3.3): $20,000 per year.
- Public information and education (Task 7.1): $70,000 / $50,000 / $50,000.
- Water rights acquisition (Task 5.3.2): cost unknown, "could be very expensive."
- Hatchery production and reintroduction (Tasks 6.1 and 6.2): costs unknown until production requirements are determined, and deferred until Task 1 and Task 2.2 are complete.

**Research gaps highlighted by the plan.** Spawning requirements in the natural environment, longevity and age at maturity, movements of each life stage, the degree of isolation between populations, the causes of low recruitment, and the effects of nonnative species all remain unresolved and are prioritized as tasks.
