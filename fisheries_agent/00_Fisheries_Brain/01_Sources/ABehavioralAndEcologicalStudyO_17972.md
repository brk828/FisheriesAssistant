# Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*) Behavioral and Ecological Study at Quitobaquito Springs, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This University of Arizona doctoral dissertation (Thomas J. Cox, 1966, Department of Zoology) describes the ecology and behavior of the Quitobaquito form of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), based on fieldwork during 1964 and 1965 at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. The study combines field observation, trapping and marking, stomach analysis, parasitology, and laboratory aquarium experiments. It is primarily descriptive and aims to record natural behavior, which the author contrasts with laboratory behavior and with the previously studied [[Salton Sea]] population described by Barlow (1961).

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *C. macularius* belongs to order Atheriniformes, suborder Cyprinodontoidei, family Cyprinodontidae (per Rosen 1964). The genus includes 23 known allopatric species.
- **Original description:** Baird and Girard (1853), from the Rio San Pedro, Arizona.
- **Range:** Miller (1943) examined about 6,000 specimens and gave a range covering the lower Colorado and Gila River basins, the Salton Sea, and the Sonoyta River of Sonora, Mexico.
- **Arizona populations:** Once present in several Arizona rivers; by the 1960s the only known Arizona populations were at Monkey Spring (Santa Cruz County) and Quitobaquito Springs (Pima County). Declines were attributed to arroyo cutting and water-table lowering.
- **Isolation of the Quitobaquito form:** Probably isolated roughly one million years ago (Pliocene–Pleistocene transition) when the Pinacate lava flow diverted the Sonoyta River, separating it from the Colorado River populations.
- **Study rationale:** Behavioral data gathered in laboratories often differ from natural behavior, so this study emphasized naturalistic, descriptive observation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site description**
- Pond surface area about 0.22 hectares, roughly 300 feet long, mean depth about 39 inches, with a shallow northeast cove and a small eastern cove.
- Spring water is nearly constant at about 23 °C. Surface temperatures ranged from a low of 10 °C (25 January 1964) to a high of 34 °C (August). The largest recorded daily range was 6 °C (July). Surface–bottom difference peaked at 1.0 °C.
- Water source: two springs north of the pond, carried by a stream repeatedly choked with *Scirpus olneyi* (tule) and cleared by the National Park Service.

**Anthropogenic change**
- The National Park Service dredged the pond from January to March 1962, deepening it and adding latrines, picnic tables, a path, and a gravel parking lot. Cattle grazing was excluded earlier.
- Historical accounts (Kino, 1698; Lumholtz, 1912) indicate a higher water table and a surface connection to the Rio Sonoyta, now largely lost.

**Seasonal and interannual dynamics**
- Water level, turbidity, and vegetation changed rapidly. In 1965 the water level dropped 12 inches between 24 April and 6 May, exposing areas used as breeding territories two weeks earlier.
- Rainfall: April–June 1964 totaled 0.09 inches versus 0.96 inches in 1965. January–June 1964 totaled 1.29 inches versus 3.29 inches in 1965.
- Chara sp. covered the shallows in 1964 but was largely absent in 1965. The author suggests that foraging by coots (*Fulica americana*), turbidity, or depth may explain this.

## Key Findings

**Seasonal activity and dormancy**
- Adults in the spring-fed stream were active year-round, but pond adults were dormant from roughly late October until the last two weeks of April, burying in detritus. Seine hauls caught inactive adults during winter.
- Adults reappeared suddenly in late April 1965 once water temperature rose: pond range was 16.6–19.8 °C on 10 April and 23.0–27.2 °C on 24–25 April.
- Breeding behavior peaked in April and waned through the summer; by August most males were not aggressive.

**Spatial distribution**
- Trapping (7 stations) found no fish in March or early April 1965. In May, fish concentrated in shallow peripheral areas (trap station No. 5 caught large numbers on 20 May). By late June they had moved to the deeper central pond.
- A depth-stratified trap experiment (12 July 1965) caught 106 fish in the bottom trap (4 inches off bottom) versus 2 at 31 inches, indicating a strong preference for the bottom.
- Stream census: Area "B" (413 ft) held most fish; Area "A" (112 ft) had high velocity and almost no fish. Females outnumbered males about seven to one in the stream.

**Movement and site fidelity**
- Thirty-three females were marked on 7 May 1965; five were recaptured two weeks later at different stations. Females appeared to move randomly across the pond, which the author suggests reduces inbreeding in a closed system.
- Three territorial males remained in their territories for 13, 20, and 28 days.

**Food habits**
- Pupfish are mainly unselective scavengers that take bites of bottom detritus. Diet included Chara, ostracods, Coleoptera larvae, snails, insect parts, and mollusc eggs.
- Males grab debris in territories; females graze while moving. Fish appear to eat their own eggs. Ten clear mollusc eggs were found in a single female collected 5 June 1965.
- Of 33 fish dissected across eight dates, guts were nearly empty in March.

**Parasitism**
- Of 58 fish examined, 13 (about 22%) were infected with a nematode of the genus *Contracaecum* (identified by Mr. John Ubelaker as resembling *Contracaecum collieri*). Infection sites were the sinus venosus (11 cases) and peritoneal cavity (2 cases). The mean was 1.6 worms per fish, with a maximum of four.
- All fish collected in 1964 were infected, while none collected in 1965 were. The author links this to the possible absence of wading birds (the definitive hosts) after the pond was deepened.

**Laboratory behavior**
- Over 40 matings were observed. The female determines whether spawning occurs: a receptive female nips the substrate after a 180° turn, and the male then clasps her with his anal fin.
- Only about 50% of matings produced an egg.
- Isolated males showed heightened aggression and formed a stable dominance hierarchy in small spaces. Dominant males had very dark irises; subordinate males had silver rings around the pupil.
- Males without competitors did not defend territories.

**Field behavior**
- In the field, territorial males attacked all trespassers, including receptive females. Receptive females often channeled this aggression into spawning, typically averaging three copulations per encounter (range 1 to 13).
- Females never defended territories and were never aggressive toward males in the field.
- Courtship patterns resembling laboratory behavior appeared mainly late in the season, when excitation was lower.
- Territories were concentrated in the large and small coves and the shallow eastern shelf. Territory size increased over the season and varied by male size and vegetation density. One measured territory was 1,728 square inches.

**Comparison with the Salton Sea form (Barlow 1961)**
- The Quitobaquito form showed shorter spawning bouts (5–10 minutes versus 30 minutes to two hours).
- Males here attacked non-receptive females, unlike Barlow's report.
- Overall motor patterns were similar.

**Conclusions**
- The breeding season is triggered by temperature rather than light, consistent with related cyprinodonts.
- Quitobaquito is highly variable in temperature, water level, and turbidity, and these changes strongly affect breeding.
- Habitat selection in the stream favors shallow, open, slow-moving water.
