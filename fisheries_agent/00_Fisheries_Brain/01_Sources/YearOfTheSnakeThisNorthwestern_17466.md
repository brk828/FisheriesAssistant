# Hells Canyon and the Snake River Dam Controversy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Sunset magazine feature by Lora J. Finnegan profiles the [[Snake River]] and [[Hells Canyon]] in the Pacific Northwest as both a recreational destination and a contested dam-management landscape. The article describes the river's recreational value and the conflict between conservationists seeking to bypass four lower [[Snake River Dams]] to aid listed salmon and steelhead and local stakeholders who depend on hydropower, navigation, and agricultural shipping. It closes with a brief survey of dam-removal and dam-operation issues across several Western states.

## Background & Description

- **River:** Born in [[Yellowstone National Park]], the Snake runs 1,056 miles to the [[Columbia River]] and drains an area larger than Colorado.
- **Hells Canyon:** A 71-mile stretch that is one of the federally designated [[Wild and Scenic Rivers]], described as cataract-filled and wild. The river there is noted for large rapids and waves, and for having some of North America's largest rafting-river water volumes.
- **Recreation area:** The 652,488-acre [[Hells Canyon National Recreation Area]] contains more than 900 miles of trails and supports rafting, jet boating, fishing, and hiking. Notable features mentioned include [[Peregrine Falcon]] nesting cliffs, sturgeon in deep holes (reported at up to 8 feet), the rare Snake River biscuit-root, bighorn sheep, and petroglyphs.
- **Cultural context:** Ancient tribes regarded the canyon as sacred.
- **Dam system:** The lower river is managed as the Lower Snake River Project by the [[US Army Corps of Engineers]]. Lower Granite Dam is the first major barrier downstream of Lewiston, Idaho.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salmon and steelhead:** The Snake's lower section is critical habitat for [[Snake River Salmon]] and [[Steelhead]]. The recent Endangered Species Act listing of Snake River salmon is the main impetus for the dam debate, as conservationists argue that rerouting or breaching dams would improve access to upriver spawning grounds.
- **Hydropower and water use:** The dams provide an average of 1,200 megawatts annually of hydropower and support recreation, wildlife habitat, navigation to the Port of Lewiston, and shipment of agricultural products.
- **Regulatory framework:** The 1986 amendment to the [[Federal Power Act]] requires the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] to consider environmental impacts, recreational use, and aesthetics when issuing and renewing licenses. Many dams built between 1928 and 1956 are now facing relicensing.
- **Climate:** Temperatures can reach 100°F in July and August.

## Key Findings

- **Four-dam conflict:** Conservationists want to redirect the lower Snake around four hydroelectric dams; local farmers, businesses, and others oppose this because of power, navigation, and port interests.
- **Corps options:** The Army Corps of Engineers is studying three options: increasing smolt transport around the dams, breaching the dams, and maintaining current operations. A report was planned for release that summer.
- **Economic stakes:** Rerouting would bring fundamental changes to local economies, property, and land use. Supporters, including Katherine Ransel of [[American Rivers]], see it as a model for other Western rivers with dams.
- **Dam removal elsewhere:** The article cites removal of the 85-year-old Condit Dam on the White Salmon River (negotiations underway), the removal of McPherrin Dam on Butte Creek (Upper Sacramento River, California) "just last year," and the Savage Rapids Dam on Oregon's Rogue River as examples of changing attitudes.
- **Other regional status notes:**
  - *California:* Auburn Dam (American River) was halted in 1975 and revived as a possibility in 1996; the Tuolumne River remains a premier rafting and fishing river.
  - *Oregon:* The Rogue River, one of the first eight Wild and Scenic rivers, faces mining, water diversion, farm runoff, and salmon loss.
  - *Utah:* A 210-foot dam is proposed for the Lower Uinta River for agriculture, opposed for fishery and flow concerns.
  - *Washington:* Operational changes at the Ross Dam complex improved survival of Skagit River fish, including pink and chum salmon.
- **Travel planning:** The article provides outfitter, lodging, and access information (May through October raft and jet boat season) for Lewiston and Clarkston gateway towns, but this material is supplementary and not reproduced in full here.
