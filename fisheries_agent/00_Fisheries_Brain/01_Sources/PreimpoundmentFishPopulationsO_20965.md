# Preimpoundment Fish Populations of the Wister Reservoir Area in the Poteau River Basin, Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1951 paper by [[Gordon E. Hall]] (Oklahoma Game and Fish Department) reports a fishery survey of the existing waters of the [[Wister Reservoir]] area, [[Poteau River]] Basin, Oklahoma, conducted from April to September 1949, one month before impoundment on October 4, 1949. The survey documented the relative abundance, species composition, and growth of game, pan, and coarse fishes across four habitats. Rough fishes dominated the preimpoundment community, and the study concluded that stocking of channel catfish and bluegill/crappie was unnecessary, that largemouth bass stocking was advisable, and that a creel census and postimpoundment monitoring were needed.

## Background & Description

The Wister Reservoir was authorized under the Flood Control Act of June 28, 1938, as part of the Arkansas River Basin flood-control plan. The dam lies on the Poteau River, 2 miles south of Wister, Oklahoma, 61 miles upstream from the Arkansas River at Fort Smith, Arkansas. At normal pool elevation (471.6 feet), the reservoir covers 4,000 acres, holds 30,000 acre-feet, and has 75 miles of shoreline.

Because the dam was under construction, the river outlet gates were usually open, and most water remained within the river channel. Survey activity was therefore limited to scattered sites:

- The Poteau River from the dam upstream to the mouth of the Fourche Maline River (4 miles)
- A Poteau River backwater and the [[Stilling Basin]] below the dam
- [[Fanny Creek]], a small tributary half a mile upstream from the dam
- The borrow pit on the upstream side
- Two cutoff lakes, [[Redwine Lake]] and [[Spike Lake]], in the upper reservoir area

Average depths were 11 feet in the river above the dam and 8 feet below it. The stilling basin averaged 15 feet after the rainy season. Substrates ranged from silt and clay upstream to gravel below the dam, with a concrete-floored stilling basin 100 feet long. Redwine Lake was covered by yellow pond lily (*Nuphar advena*), while Spike Lake was ringed by cutgrass (*Zizaniopsis miliacea*). The borrow pit was shallow, turbid, and unvegetated.

**Methods.** Fish were collected with [[Gill Nets]], [[Hoop Nets]], [[Seine Sampling]], [[Rotenone Sampling]] (Fish-tox and powdered derris root), and drawdown of water. Gill nets were 175 feet by 8 feet, and hoop nets had 3.5-foot front diameters. Each net lift, seine haul, or rotenone treatment counted as one collection. Scales were used for age determination of sport fishes, and spines for catfishes. Common names followed the American Fisheries Society (1948) list.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The survey treated each habitat as a separate population, since fishes had been connected to the rivers and sorted by habitat preference as water receded.

Collecting efficiency varied sharply by method (Table 1):

| Method | Collections | Fishes | % of catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gill nets | 50 | 73 | 2 |
| Hoop nets | 69 | 820 | 20 |
| Seines | 17 | 1,345 | 34 |
| Rotenone | 9 | 1,758 | 44 |

Total: 3,996 fishes. Two rotenone collections from Fanny Creek and two seine collections from the stilling basin yielded 52% of all fishes, while netting (119 of 144 collections) captured only 22% of the total catch. The authors judged netting time-consuming, selective, and unsatisfactory for estimating population composition.

Water-level changes shaped the fish community. Upstream fishes were concentrated in a river channel that had been rising and falling during May and June, a period when many species were probably spawning. Spawning-related water fluctuations were considered a possible cause of the weak 1949 hatch for most species. Shad spawning, however, did not appear to be affected.

Dam operations also produced concentrations of fish. Large numbers of [[Carp]], buffaloes, and carpsucker moved upstream in swift water below the dam in April and May. In August, the stilling basin was lowered to allow seining, and the collection of 1,168 fishes weighing 996.8 pounds came mainly from there.

## Key Findings

**Relative abundance and biomass by habitat (Table 2)**

- Total catch: 3,577 fishes weighing 1,662.4 pounds, including stilling-basin collections. An additional 419 fishes were unweighed due to scavenger damage or decomposition after rotenone treatment. Minnows and darters were excluded.
- The stilling basin yielded roughly 1,000 pounds from less than one-seventh of a surface acre.
- Fanny Creek (1,000-yard section) yielded an estimated 106 pounds with Fish-tox, then about 1,585 pounds (98% [[Gizzard Shad]]) with derris root 18 days later.
- Spike Lake yielded 51.2 pounds with Fish-tox from 2 acres.
- Most rough fishes, [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Spotted Bass]] were best represented below the dam. [[Gizzard Shad]], crappies, warmouth, and spotted gar were most abundant in Fanny Creek and above the dam.
- [[Largemouth Black Bass]] had been stocked over 15 years, yet only four specimens were collected.
- Two large specimens of Ohio shad (*Alosa ohiensis*) were taken below the dam, a species newly recorded for Oklahoma.

**Game-to-rough fish ratio**

- Rough fishes made up 80% of weight and 52% of number across all catches. Game fishes made up 15% of weight and 30% of number.
- Rough fish share by site: Poteau River 44.0%, Fanny Creek 66.0%, Redwine Lake 5.5%, Spike Lake 98.0%.
- The authors expected rough-fish dominance to increase after impoundment, following [[Tennessee Valley Authority Reservoirs]] trends.

**Species order of abundance**

- All collections indicated the dominant preimpoundment species were gizzard shad, bluegill, channel catfish, and black buffalo. River carpsucker, freshwater drum, and both crappie species were also abundant.
- Of the 23 most common species, only channel catfish and bluegill ranked among the first 10 in number and weight. Bluegill ranked second in number and seventh in weight. Channel catfish ranked third in number and second in weight.

**Year-classes and recruitment**

- Thousands of young-of-the-year channel catfish were stranded in low-water pools below the dam in July, indicating a large 1949 hatch.
- Few young-of-the-year carp, buffaloes, or carpsucker were collected.
- Young-of-the-year comprised about 80% of the shad killed by rotenone in August, mostly 2 to 5 inches in length.
- The 1945 year-class appeared strong for drum, channel catfish, and black buffalo, but the authors judged sample sizes inadequate to confirm it.

**Growth of key species (Table 4)**

- Channel catfish and spotted bass grew slowly compared with other waters. Channel catfish reached legal length (10 inches) in their fourth summer, while 100 of 432 channel catfish were legal. Only 6 of 111 spotted bass were legal.
- Pan fishes grew faster in the Poteau River than in Redwine Lake. Bluegill averaged 6 inches at age 2 in the river but needed 3 years in Redwine Lake. White crappie reached 6 inches at age 1 in the river but were usually age 2 in Redwine Lake.
- Black crappie and warmouth from both sites averaged 6 inches at age 2.
- Drum and buffalo reached 10 inches in their second or third year. Average weights at age 3 were 10.5 ounces (drum), 2.3 pounds (black buffalo), and 2.1 pounds (smallmouth buffalo).

**Management recommendations (to the [[Oklahoma Game and Fish Council]])**

1. Discontinue stocking of bluegill and crappie. Records showed 66,700 bluegill and 24,500 crappie had been stocked in the Poteau River system over 15 years.
2. Discontinue channel catfish stocking, since the species appeared abundant and reproduced well.
3. Initiate a [[Creel Census]] in the first year of impoundment and continue it for several years.
4. Continue fish population studies after impoundment.
5. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] recommended stocking largemouth bass at 100 fingerlings per acre during the first impoundment year, based on [[Bennett]] (1944) observations of about 3 years of good bass fishing in new lakes.
6. Increase commercial harvest of coarse fishes, which were largely unharvested, to reduce competition with game fishes.

**Implementation**

- In spring 1950, 400,000 fingerling largemouth bass were stocked in Wister Reservoir.
- The Council approved the creel-census and continued-study recommendations and assigned a student to a postimpoundment survey.
- The regional fishery biologist continued periodic sampling.

**Data-quality notes**

- The total fish count differs between Table 1 (3,996) and Table 2 (3,577); the paper does not reconcile the two.
- The discussion from the conference notes that 27,000 bass were stocked over 15 years, which the author placed in context as a small rate relative to the catch.
