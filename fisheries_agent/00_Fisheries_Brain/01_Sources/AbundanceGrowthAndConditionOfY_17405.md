# Abundance, Growth, and Condition of Yellow Bass in Clear Lake, Iowa, 1941–1951

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 study, reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, tracks the [[Yellow Bass]] ([[Morone Interrupta]]) population of [[Clear Lake]], a eutrophic lake in [[Iowa]]. Scale and length data from more than 3,500 fish, collected in 1941–1943 and 1947–1951, were used to assess abundance by [[Year Class]], growth in length and weight, and body condition. The authors found no clear trend in adult abundance from 1945 to 1951. Year-class strength varied widely and was not explained mainly by reproduction. Growth slowed from 1937 to 1949, recovered in 1950–1951, and was linked to June temperatures in the young-of-the-year stage. Condition (the [[Ponderal Index]]) declined over the summer in 1948 and 1949.

## Background & Description

Yellow bass were probably introduced into Clear Lake with fish from the [[Mississippi River]] and were first reported from angler catches around 1932 (Bailey and Harrison, 1945). In some years they supported most of the local angling. [[Creel Census]] data show that yellow bass made up 33% of the May–June catch in 1948, 46% in 1949, 24% in 1950, and 10% in 1951 (Rose, 1951). Their share of the catch usually rose later in the summer.

Clear Lake (Cerro Gordo County, north-central Iowa) is about 5 miles long east to west, slightly over 2 miles wide at its widest point, covers 3,643 acres, and has a maximum depth of about 20 feet. It is heavily used for recreation. Two state parks, public highways, and two docks maintained by the [[Iowa State Conservation Commission]] provide access. Private homes border about 42% of the shoreline, and resorts and cabin camps with access to about 20% of it support tourism.

The study was run as Project No. 39 of the [[Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]] at Iowa State College. Its stated goal was to show how these changes in fish populations might be managed to improve fishing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** From 1947 to 1951 collections were broader than in earlier years. Gear included experimental [[Gill Net]]s (125 ft long, graded mesh from 1¼ to 4 in), hoop nets, wire traps, a 50-ft bag seine, and minnow seines. The Biology Survey Crew made 2–4 hauls with a 500-ft [[Seine Haul]] each year from 1945, usually in late July or August.

**Year-class dynamics.** Most fish collected in 1941–1944 came from the 1939 year class. Scales from 1947–1951 show the population was no longer dominated by a single cohort (Table 2, 3,504 fish combined). The authors concluded that reproduction alone did not control year-class success. Young-of-the-year counts did not match year-class success, and the authors suggested that predation by [[Yellow Perch]] may have removed young yellow bass in fall and winter after poor perch reproduction in 1950 (Carlander, 1951).

**Temperature.** Young fish were larger in early July in years with above-normal June air temperatures. No relationship was found between July–August temperatures and summer growth rates. No correlation was found between summer temperatures or rainfall and annual growth. The authors argued that population density, which they could not measure, probably mattered more.

**Population density.** The authors proposed that growth declined from 1937 to 1949 because of increased numbers of yellow bass and more [[Intraspecific Competition]] as the species became established in the 1930s and 1940s.

**Weather.** May and June 1947 mean temperatures at [[Mason City]] were the lowest of the study period (53.2°F and 63.8°F, 5.1° and 3.1° below normal). Growth was slow that year until after July 1.

## Key Findings

**Abundance**
- Seine, gill net, and angling data showed no clear trend in adult yellow bass abundance from 1945 to 1951. Adult catch per seine haul rose to 15.0 in 1951, and the authors noted a possible increase in adults in 1950–1951.
- Young-of-the-year catches varied widely between years (165 to 2,525 per haul), and no yearlings were taken in 1945 and 1948.
- Year-class success was classed as very successful (1939, 1942, 1943, 1946, 1948, 1949), moderate (1944, 1945, 1947), or unsuccessful (1937, 1938, 1940, 1941, 1950). Note that the 1937 class appears in both the moderate and the "unsuccessful" lists in the source text, and the authors did not resolve this.

**Growth**
- Most length growth occurs in the first three years. The weighted mean annual length increment was 64 mm in year 1, 54 mm in year 2, 39 mm in year 3, and 16 mm in year 4 (Table 5). Weight roughly doubles in the third year.
- The 1948 year class was only 60% as long as the 1937 class at the end of year 2.
- Growth indices were above normal in 1942 (126) and 1943 (144) and below normal from 1945 to 1949, with 1949 the poorest year. Growth recovered in 1950 and 1951.
- Using 1942, 1948, and 1949 as examples, larger fish in early July followed warm Junes. The regression of July 13 length on mean June temperature had a coefficient of 2.416 ± 0.176.
- In 1949 fish of age-groups I and IV showed no growth between July 1 and August 26. The age-group II fish grew slightly.

**Methods and Calculation**
- Back-calculated lengths from scales (using a nomograph with a 16 mm base) matched measured lengths of the same year class. Calculated lengths at each annulus did not drift with later collection year. Example: first-year lengths for the 1946 class were 62–68 mm across 1947–1951.
- Scale-to-body length intercepts were 16 mm (1941–1943), 24 mm (1947), 18 mm (1948), and 21 mm (1949).

**Length-Weight and Condition**
- The average length-weight regression for 1947–1949 was log W = −4.959 + 3.191 log L. Yearly fits differed significantly in slope (F = 8.56), so the authors used the pooled average for estimates.
- In 1948 and 1949 the [[Ponderal Index]] fell steadily through the summer, meaning fish grew thinner for their length. No seasonal change appeared in 1947, 1950, or 1951.
- Condition was best in 1947 and 1951 and poorest in 1949 (Table 9).
- In February 1950, [[Tom Moen]] reported concave abdomens and flesh loss above the lateral line. The mean ponderal index of 35 fish he examined was 2.69.

**Management Implications**
- The authors expected that faster growth in 1950–1951 would improve average size in Clear Lake. They stressed that a long-term study of this one lake could show how populations might be managed for better fishing.
