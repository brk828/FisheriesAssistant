# Black Bullhead Removal from a Headwater Trout Stream in Southern California

#ichthyology #Ictaluridae #invasive_species_management #salmonid_conservation

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*) is a stout-bodied catfish native to central North American drainages but now widely distributed across western states through intentional introductions. Life history attributes facilitating range expansion include high fecundity (2,500–3,000 eggs/female), parental care of offspring, versatile feeding habits, habitat flexibility, temperature tolerance of 0–35°C, and ability to survive dissolved oxygen concentrations as low as 1–2 mg/L. The species exhibits nocturnal feeding behavior. [[Coastal Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss irideus*) represents the last remaining native population in San Diego County, inhabiting a short section of headwater stream with intermittent summer flows bounded by perennial pools.

**Habitat Associations**: Black Bullhead is a demersal species preferring slow-flowing (limnophilic) habitats with soft substrata. In the [[West Fork San Luis Rey River]] (WFSLRR), a second-order stream with bank-full width generally <4 m, the species concentrated in 16 large perennial pools during drought conditions. The stream becomes intermittent in summer in high-gradient sections occupied by Coastal Rainbow Trout. Prolonged drought (2012–2016) reduced water levels and increased the proportion of slow-flowing habitats preferred by Black Bullhead, facilitating concentration in pools.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Black Bullhead exhibited limited movement in the headwater system. A 1.5-m dam upstream likely prevented demersal Black Bullhead from moving downstream from a source stock pond. High-flow events in 2017 and 2019 did not result in discovery of additional fish downstream, confirming limited dispersal capability. The species' demersal nature restricts movement during flood events compared to pelagic species.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Black Bullhead demonstrated benthophagous behavior with opportunistic feeding habits. Previous studies found native fish in 10% of Black Bullhead stomachs (n=255) in the Iberian Peninsula and in 2 of 12 stomachs from Little Colorado River, Arizona. The species possesses an excellent sense of taste for locating food, potentially leading to predation on [[Coastal Rainbow Trout]] redds. Competition for resources and habitat alteration through increased turbidity (reducing feeding efficiency of visual predators like trout) represent additional impact pathways. Snorkel surveys in 2016 observed 33 Black Bullhead and 72 Coastal Rainbow Trout; 2017 surveys detected 0 Black Bullhead and 446 Coastal Rainbow Trout (391 young-of-year), suggesting release from predation/competition pressure.

**Environmental Drivers**: The 2012–2016 California drought, among the worst in over a century, concentrated Black Bullhead in perennial pools by reducing available habitat. Approximately 87% of all Black Bullhead were removed from 16 large perennial pools. A high-flow event during water year 2016–2017 (>20 cm rainfall in 24 hours) likely flushed remaining fish and improved spawning substrate for trout by removing fine particulate organic material accumulated during drought. Drought-induced increases in fine sediments and reductions in flow negatively affected invertebrate communities and likely contributed to reduced trout numbers prior to removal.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Initial Black Bullhead population estimated at 1,361 fish (SE=65.35; 95% CI=1,079–1,641) using Leslie depletion method. Four removal trips in 2016 captured 1,315 Black Bullhead (96.6% of estimated population). Mean CPUE declined rapidly: Trip 1 captured 763 fish (55 traps), Trip 2 captured 362 fish (48 traps), Trip 3 captured 122 fish (72 traps), Trip 4 captured 68 fish (66 traps). Follow-up surveys in 2017 (70 traps) and 2018 (54 traps) captured zero Black Bullhead, indicating apparent eradication. Coastal Rainbow Trout population increased from 72 observed in 2016 to 446 in 2017 (primarily young-of-year) and 178 in 2018.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Invasive species introductions cited as second leading cause of fish extinctions after habitat loss, responsible for 68% of fish extinctions in North America. Southwestern U.S. fish communities characterized by high endemism and low diversity are particularly vulnerable. Black Bullhead threatened the last remaining native Coastal Rainbow Trout population in San Diego County through multiple pathways: direct predation on juveniles and redds, competition for resources, and habitat degradation via increased turbidity. Source population in upstream stock pond continues to pose recolonization risk despite downstream removal success.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Overnight sets of baited minnow traps (Promar collapsible, 60×30 cm, dual 14-cm openings, 1-cm bar mesh) proved highly effective for Black Bullhead removal in small headwater streams. Traps baited with ~15 pieces of tuna-flavored cat treats, set in deepest portions of stream, soaked ~17 hours overnight to target nocturnal adults. Each crew member carried 12 traps plus backpacking gear (maximum 72 traps/trip deployed). Method advantages: low cost (<$30/trap), collapsible for remote deployment, easy setup requiring minimal training, safe operation without electrofishing hazards or chemical treatment risks, low maintenance. Disadvantages: bait attracts turtles which can destroy traps. Timing removal efforts during drought conditions when target species concentrate in pools maximizes efficiency. Address upstream source populations to prevent recolonization. Method likely most effective in small streams; efficacy in larger rivers unknown but doubtful due to reduced ability of fish to locate baited traps in higher water volumes.

**Biological Reference Points**: Complete eradication achieved when 96.6% of estimated population removed over four trips. Depletion method assumptions met: closed population, constant catchability, sufficient catch to reduce CPUE, catches removed >2% of population, equal vulnerability to capture method, independent effort units. Narrowing 95% confidence intervals around CPUE over successive trips indicated effective population reduction. Three consecutive years of zero captures (2017–2018) confirmed eradication success. This represents rare complete eradication of invasive fish species; only 43% of 250 fish control projects reviewed were deemed successful, with physical removal success rates of 33–57%.
