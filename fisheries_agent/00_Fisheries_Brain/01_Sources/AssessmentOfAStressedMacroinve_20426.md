# Assessment of a Stressed Macroinvertebrate Community

#macroinvertebrates #benthic-invertebrates #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This paper tests a simple [[Ordination Method]] for assessing stressed benthic macroinvertebrate communities in the [[New River]] near [[Pearisburg Virginia]], downstream of a [[Synthetic Fibers Plant]]. The method plots total numbers of specimens against total numbers of taxa, treating standing crop as the indicator of community health. It is compared with the widely used diversity index *d* of [[Wilhm and Dorris Index]], which is based on evenness and [[Redundancy]]. Sampling used [[Artificial Substrate Sampler]]s at seven stations across six dates in 1972. The author concludes that the ordination method located stressed areas more consistently, while *d* produced conflicting and often counterintuitive rankings, and so was rejected for this system.

## Background & Description

The study builds on earlier work in biological assessment of water quality. Key precursors include the [[Saprobien System]] of Kolkwitz and Marsson (1908, 1909), the diversity index of [[Margalef Diversity Index]] (1951), and the rapid monitoring approach of Cairns and Dickson (1971). The Wilhm and Dorris (1968) index is described as widely accepted for detecting structural change in macrobenthic communities.

The author argues that diversity indices can mislead. A low *d* can arise when pollution-intolerant forms such as mayflies are abundant, and a high *d* can arise from very few specimens (for example, 8 specimens among 3 taxa). The stated principle is that **[[Standing Crop]]**, the combined number of specimens and taxa, together with [[Niche Partitioning]], defines the healthiest condition for the New River. Health is explicitly defined as a relative term that must be stated for each investigation.

Under this framework, communities are placed in four quadrants on a specimens-versus-taxa plot, centred on reference-station means:

- **Quadrant I** (high specimens, low taxa): tolerant, productive, low-diversity assemblages
- **Quadrant II** (high specimens, high taxa): optimal condition, where standing crop and niche partitioning are maximised
- **Quadrant III** (low specimens, low taxa): least desirable, reflecting a sublethal or toxic stress
- **Quadrant IV** (low specimens, high taxa): a diverse but less productive community, typical of nonselective stresses such as scouring

An "indeterminate area" is also recognised where inconclusive results require station-by-station analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The industrial effluent from the synthetic fibers plant had a localised effect, restricted to the right shoreline near the outfall, according to 1971–1972 water-chemistry data. High discharge temperatures dissipated within about 1 km downstream, and other physicochemical parameters were diluted or assimilated within 1.5 km. Fish populations in the same survey area were reported separately, including by [[Hocutt Et Al Fish Assessment 1974]].

Stressors identified in the study include:

- **[[Thermal Discharge]]:** the heated outfall ditch affected substations immediately adjacent to its mouth, with the effect most pronounced at low flow in summer.
- **[[Siltation]]:** heavy silt deposits from shoreline highway construction and settleable solids affected several downstream and mid-river substations.
- **Scouring:** a shallow (1 m), rubble-bottomed station (5a) was judged possibly affected by scour in March.
- **Seasonal flow:** high spring discharge appeared to channel the thermal plume toward the shoreline, reducing its effect on other substations.

**Study design:** Five principal stations were sampled, along with two stations (HD1 and HD2) near the mouth of the heated discharge ditch, between Stations 2 and 3. Station 1 lay 2.5 km upstream of the plant. Station 2 was at the Shumate Bridge, 1 km above the outfalls. Station 3 was at Stillhouse Branch, downstream of all industrial releases. Station 4 was at the mouth of Piney Creek, 5 km downstream. Station 5 was the "Island Station," 9.5 km downstream. Five samplers (a–e) were set across the river at each station, from the left to the right shoreline, giving substations. Sampling used a combination of [[Bar-B-Q Basket Sampler]] and conservation webbing ([[Conservation Webbing]]) substrates.

## Key Findings

**Method comparison**

- The ordination method and the *d* index gave conflicting interpretations for most substations, with the single point of agreement being that HD1e was the least healthy substation.
- *d* was judged to be unreliable for this system. Substations known to be stressed by industrial loading were often ranked as healthier than reference stations, so the author rejected conclusions based on *d*.
- Example: substation 5d had *d* = 1.29, ranked 13th out of 27 samples, but collected only 3 taxa and 8 specimens. The reference average was 12.25 taxa and 457 specimens.
- Substation 4c (*d* = 1.06) and substation 5e (*d* = 1.07) had nearly equal *d* values, yet differed by 13 taxa and nearly 1,300 specimens.

**Seasonal results (1972, six dates)**

- **11 February:** Ordination identified several stressed substations, including those near the thermal ditch (HD1e). Substation 4c was among the most productive.
- **14 March:** Substations 5d and 5e were critically affected by silt loading. Substations HD1e, HD2c, HD2e, 3e, and 5a were clustered in submarginal conditions. The thermal plume affected only immediately adjacent substations. Substation 4d was the healthiest.
- **12 May:** Substations 2d and HD1e were least healthy, with 2d silted in. HD1d and HD2d were the healthiest, with large numbers of *[[Ephemerella]]* (mayfly) possibly linked to a preferred temperature response.
- **7 August:** The most stressed stations were those affected by siltation (5e) and heat (HD1d, HD1e, HD2b, HD2c, HD2e). *d* values at these stations ranged from 0.22 to 2.01. An adaptive response by [[Chironomidae]] (midges) was noted at HD2d.
- **17 September:** Substations 5d, 5c, HD1d, and HD1e were deleteriously stressed. The largest number of organisms (about 2,500) occurred at 4d, and the most taxa (21) at HD1a.
- **9 November:** The most taxa (22) were recorded at 3a, and the most specimens (about 3,700) at 4e, largely from more than 3,000 chironomids. The least healthy stations were 2c, HD1e, and 5e.

**Overall conclusions**

- Stations most stressed by industrial wastes lay in the immediate vicinity of the thermal discharge (HD1d, HD1e, HD2d, HD2e).
- Stations 3a–e showed no clear deleterious effect, although the author noted this might be masked by inconsistent sampler return.
- The ordination method was sensitive to both natural stress (siltation at 2c and 2d) and man-made stress (5d and 5e).
- Seasonal abundance of both specimens and taxa at reference stations peaked in August.
- The author rejects the [[Indicator Species]] concept in favour of the [[Community Concept]] as a more reliable measure of stress, since taxon presence alone gives little inference.
- Redundancy is expected to be high in many natural macrobenthic collections, even without external stress, because the most successful competitors dominate in numbers or biomass. Evenness of distribution is therefore not a sound criterion for healthy communities.
- The standard 0 to 3+ *d* scale of Wilhm and Dorris (1968) is judged inapplicable to the New River without objective, system-specific calibration.
