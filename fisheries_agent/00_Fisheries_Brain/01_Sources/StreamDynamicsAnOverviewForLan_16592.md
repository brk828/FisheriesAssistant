# Stream Dynamics: An Overview for Land Managers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fluvial-geomorphology #stream-dynamics #sediment-transport

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report by [[Burchard H. Heede]] (Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, 1980) gives land managers a conceptual overview of [[Stream Dynamics]]. It explains how flow, [[Sediment Transport]] and channel form interact, how streams reach and lose [[Dynamic Equilibrium]], and how management actions can trigger channel adjustments. The report contains no fish data. Its aquatic relevance is indirect, through fishery biologists named as one audience and through the physical habitat that streams provide. The author states that current science allows only qualitative prediction of stream behavior, although such predictions show how management actions will affect a stream and its environment.

## Background & Description

The report is written for professionals whose work touches streams: hydrologists, civil engineers, fishery biologists and plant biologists. It is intended to show which information must be collected to characterize a stream and which indicators suggest present and future behavior. Heede notes that stream data are often limited to flow rates and yields, and that a manager should recognize when a situation requires a potamologist (river scientist).

The author stresses that a stream is not a single fixed entity. A mountain brook meanders sluggishly and fills only part of its bed at low flow, but at flood stage it can fill the channel, cover its floodplain and straighten its meanders. Both states are driven by different processes, so a single characterization is inadequate.

The report is structured around:
- Basic fluvial processes (flow regime, flow type and sediment transport)
- Equilibrium and the sequence of adjustment processes
- Channel pattern, shape, banks and bars
- Longitudinal profile changes (aggradation, degradation, armoring)
- The need for monitoring and concluding cautions

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Streams are dynamic systems that change even without human interference. Land management can accelerate this change. Examples cited in the report include:
- Vegetation cover manipulation increasing water yield (Hibbert 1971)
- Streamside forest removal activating bedload transport (Heede 1977)
- Riprap and gabion bank treatments that alter bank material and channel behavior

Fish habitat is affected through the physical template. The report links bed form, bed armor, channel width, bank stability, pool and riffle spacing, and sediment load to the channel conditions that the fishery biologist works within, but it does not present any fish survey, species list or habitat measurements.

Temperature also matters. Most land management actions raise water temperature, which the author links to lower sediment loads and, under extreme conditions, to [[Aggradation]].

Key study sites referenced include the [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[Sycamore Creek]], [[Rio Grande]] (near El Paso, Texas, and at Albuquerque, New Mexico), the Eel River in California, Fool Creek in Colorado, and the White Mountains of Arizona.

## Key Findings

**Flow regime and energy**
- Subcritical (tranquil) flow is the common condition in natural streams. It exerts lower energy on banks and bed, and conserves total head better than supercritical flow.
- The [[Froude Number]] separates the regimes: Fr < 1 is subcritical, Fr > 1 is supercritical, and Fr = 1 (critical flow) is unstable and rarely persists in natural channels.
- Supercritical flow can remove canal linings and concrete diversion structures through uplift and create standing waves. The 1979 Verde River flood displaced a water-diversion structure and a 2-foot-diameter concrete pipeline.
- Stream reaches with supercritical flow should be avoided for structures, and channel narrowing should not create it.
- Turbulent flow is the normal condition. [[Reynolds Number]] values above 2,000 indicate turbulent flow, and values below 2,000 indicate laminar flow.

**Sediment transport**
- At least 30 variables are involved in sedimentation, and no reliable numerical method for bedload transport in alluvial streams is available.
- [[Lane's Balance]] relates sediment load times sediment size to stream slope times discharge. A change on one side forces adjustment on the other, or else the bed will degrade or aggrade.
- Sediment load in natural streams roughly varies as the square of discharge, expressed as Qs = Ks Qw², with a coefficient Ks that changes between streams and seasons.
- Water temperature strongly affects transport. When Colby lowered water from 80°F to 40°F, sediment discharge for 0.125 to 0.250 mm sand increased by 254%. On the Lower Colorado River, sediment loads were 2.5 times greater in winter than in summer for a given discharge. A temperature change of about 35°F (March to August) altered flow hydraulics and bed form on the Rio Grande.
- Management implication: sample both summer and winter, and do not relate winter data from one stream to summer data from another.

**Interdependency of variables**
- Lane identified eight key variables: discharge, longitudinal slope, sediment load, resistance of banks and bed, vegetation, temperature, geology and human works.
- Relationships between variables are mostly empirical and valid only for their area of origin. Brooks (1958) showed that flow with the same depth and slope can occur at two different velocities because of changing bed configuration.

**Adjustment sequence**
- Channel adjustments, from lowest to highest energy and time requirement, are: bed form change, bed armoring, width change, pattern change (meander or braid), and longitudinal profile change.
- Bed form change (for example, dune to antidune) can be enough to restore equilibrium. Antidunes occur in trains of 3 to 20 or more.
- Real streams may skip this orderly sequence, and the most energy-intensive process can operate alone.

**Equilibrium indicators (small mountain streams)**
- Signs of disequilibrium include channel headcuts, underdeveloped drainage nets (channelized water on half or less of the watershed), frequent [[Bedscarp]]s, and the absence of a concave longitudinal profile.
- Equilibrium is recognized by a concave profile, no headcuts, few or no bedscarps, and negligible sediment production.
- Heede (1975) recommends sampling reaches of at least 0.25 mile in the headwater, middle and lower sections of small mountain streams.

**Bars and channel pattern**
- Bar types are point, alternating, transverse, middle and tributary. [[Point Bar]]s form on the convex bank of bends, and their pool depth is inversely related to bend radius of curvature.
- [[Alternating Bars]] create pools and riffles spaced five to seven channel widths apart. These bars are why natural streams rarely remain straight.
- Meanders in a deformable channel tend to have a radius of curvature two to three times the mean channel width (Leopold and Wolman 1960).
- Braiding is associated with steeper slopes, overloading with sediment, cohesionless banks and sparse bank vegetation. [[Braided Channels]] occur where banks are noncohesive, while cohesive banks generally preclude braiding.
- Sinuosity (thalweg length divided by valley length) measures meander intensity. A straight reach has a coefficient of 1.
- Heede found gravel bar spacing in steep mountain streams decreased as gradient increased. In a North Fork Thomas Creek reach, the average gradient of 17% led to several transverse bars.

**Channel shape and banks**
- Semicircular channels convey water most efficiently, but this shape does not occur in nature. The most efficient natural cross section is parabolic.
- [[Width-Depth Ratio]] studies: Fahnestock (1963) found 10 to 71 on Mount Rainier glacial streams. Heede (1972) found 3 to 27 on small streams in the southern Colorado Rockies. Schumm (1960) found wide, shallow channels form in sandy soils and narrow, deep channels in clayey soils.
- [[Shape Factor]] values for geometric sections: triangular 2.0, parabolic 1.5, rectangular 1.0. Measured natural values were 1.1 to 2.7 (Fahnestock) and 1.1 to 2.3 (Heede).
- Banks with high clay content are generally more stable. Heede (1971) found [[Soil Piping]] in gully banks with more than 45% clay, associated with high exchangeable sodium, low gypsum and montmorillonite clay.

**Longitudinal profile and aggradation/degradation**
- Gradients usually decrease downstream. Langbein's concavity ratio (2A'/H) quantifies profile shape.
- Heede (1977) found ephemeral streams in homogeneous mountain watersheds had convex profiles, while perennial streams were concave.
- Road construction that shortens a channel causes [[Degradation]] that propagates upstream. In Sycamore Creek, an 8-foot bedscarp advanced about 300 feet within approximately 25 years.
- [[Aggradation]] raises the bed until a new equilibrium slope is reached. On the Rio Grande at Albuquerque, the streambed aggraded 4 to 6 feet above the floodplain, requiring stream training.
- [[Armoring]] can halt degradation if sufficiently large bed material is available. Removing gravel bars destroys bed armor and often triggers bank instability.
- Dam effects: sediment trapping by reservoirs causes aggradation in the backwater zone and degradation below the dam.

**Management conclusions**
- Gravel mining from bars can harm stream equilibrium, though the Eel River's large sediment supply may buffer it. Redwood root systems over more than 1,000 years show a long-term aggrading floodplain.
- Controlling lateral movement may initiate vertical bed adjustment in the same channel and in tributaries, so a control measure can have effects well beyond its immediate location.
- The general rule is that land managers should work with natural adjustment processes and, where possible, offset losses through land improvements elsewhere.
- Stream restraining measures must be applied with great caution so that one treatment does not simply create another problem.
- Monitoring should focus on essential indicators, and the report concludes that fluvial hydraulics cannot yet predict stream behavior accurately. Judgment and mathematical-statistical analysis are both required, but streamflow records allow prediction of the trend.
