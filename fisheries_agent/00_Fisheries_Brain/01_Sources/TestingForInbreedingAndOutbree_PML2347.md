# Testing for Inbreeding and Outbreeding Depression in the Endangered Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study tests whether [[Inbreeding Depression]] and [[Outbreeding Depression]] affect fitness in the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) across four surviving populations in Arizona. Using replicated sib (inbred), between-population (outbred) and within-population (control) matings over two generations, the authors measured survival, body size, fecundity, [[Fluctuating Asymmetry]] and sex ratio. Most traits showed no depression. The main exceptions were a strongly female-biased sex ratio in the inbred [[Monkey Spring]] population and a decline in brood size in inbred [[Sharp Spring]] lines. The authors reinforce the proposal that Monkey Spring be treated as an [[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The Gila topminnow was once the most abundant fish in the [[Gila River]] basin. It now survives naturally at nine locations in four subdrainages in Arizona. The decline is attributed mainly to habitat loss and the introduction of non-native fishes.
- **Genetic status:** Earlier studies found no mitochondrial DNA variation and low allozyme variation in these populations. Later [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] and [[Microsatellite]] data showed substantial variation within and between populations.
- **Management proposal:** Parker et al. (1999) suggested that Monkey Spring be treated as an evolutionarily significant unit, and that Bylas Spring, Sharp Spring and Cienega Creek be managed as separate management units.
- **Rationale for model:** Gila topminnows can be mated individually and their progeny raised in captivity in large numbers, which makes them well suited to experimental genetics.
- **Background context:** The introduction notes that inbreeding depression is documented in captive populations of many endangered species, while evidence for outbreeding depression is less extensive.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stock origin:** Wild-caught gravid females from each population founded the experimental stocks.
- **Husbandry conditions:** Aquaria were held at 25.5 (±1.5) °C on a 14 h light / 10 h dark photoperiod. Aeration, weekly vacuuming and 1/3 water changes with dechlorinated water controlled dissolved gases, solids and waste.
- **Design:** Each replicate comprised 14 matings and aquaria. Five replicates ran simultaneously, giving 70 matings per generation. Controls were randomized to limit bench effects.
- **Inbreeding coefficients:** F1 sib-mated progeny had coefficients of 0.125 or 0.25, depending on whether parents were half- or full-sibs. F2 progeny from full-sib matings had coefficients of 0.3125 or 0.375.
- **Sperm storage:** Females can store sperm and mate multiply, which is why half-sib matings were possible.

## Key Findings

**Survival**
- Survival to 12 weeks averaged over 90% across populations, generations and mating types. The lowest values were 85% for Bylas Spring and Sharp Spring outbred matings.
- After Bonferroni correction, no survival difference was significant between control and inbred or outbred progeny.

**Body size**
- Size differences were significant in several comparisons before correction, but no consistent trend emerged across sex, population or generation.
- Only one result, F2 male outbred versus control progeny at Bylas Spring, remained significant after correction. Four other pairwise size comparisons also remained significant.
- Controls for females showed unexplained changes between F1 and F2, which complicated interpretation.

**Bilateral asymmetry**
- Pectoral fin rays, pelvic fin rays and lateral line scales were examined.
- No differences were significant after correction.
- Completely symmetrical fish made up 0.82 of inbred, 0.83 of control and 0.88 of outbred matings (pooled).

**Fecundity (brood size)**
- Only one significant difference appeared: inbred Monkey Spring brood size was smaller than control. This result was partly driven by one unusually large control brood (21 young).
- Only one inbred pairing could be made in Monkey Spring because of highly skewed sex ratios.

**Sex ratio**
- Of 20 fish from four Monkey Spring sib matings, only one brood produced males, and that brood had just one male by 12 weeks.
- Inbred Monkey Spring sex ratios remained significantly female-biased after correction, the only significant sex-ratio effect.

**Inbred lines**
- Brood size declined across generations in inbred Sharp Spring lines. Monkey Spring lines reached only two generations, and Sharp Spring lines reached six.
- Overall, the proportion of inbred-line progeny that survived was about 94%. Population values were Bylas Spring 98.4%, Cienega Creek 92.5%, Monkey Spring 90% (N = 20) and Sharp Spring 96.5%.

**Conclusions**
- The authors found no evidence of inbreeding or outbreeding depression for survival, body size, asymmetry or fecundity at the level of the whole design, and only limited evidence for sex-ratio and brood-size effects.
- The Monkey Spring skewed sex ratio may reflect a genetically based sex-determination mechanism, possibly linked to [[Parthenogenesis]] in related unisexual fishes, rather than a generic inbreeding effect. This could not be confirmed.
- Because Monkey Spring is the most distinct population, the results support its designation as a separate evolutionarily significant unit.
- Conservation implication: the authors urge caution in assuming that low genetic variation is harmless. Environmental stress and small population size may expose genetic effects that controlled conditions did not reveal.
