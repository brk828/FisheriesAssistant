# Biological Control of Aquatic Weeds with Tilapia zillii: 1975 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 annual report from the [[University Of California Riverside]] and the [[Biological Control Project]] evaluates the use of [[Tilapia Zillii]] as a biological herbicide for aquatic weed control in irrigation canals of the Lower Colorado River Basin. Fish were stocked in canals and drains operated by the [[Imperial Irrigation District]], the [[Coachella Valley County Water District]], and the [[Palo Verde Irrigation District]]. Results were mixed. Stocking was most effective in canals stocked early, at high density, and in combination with minimal [[Mechanical Weed Control]]. Stocking late in the season, at small sizes, or after heavy weed establishment generally failed. The report also summarizes life-history studies on feeding, fecundity, spawning, movement, predation, and temperature, and it closes with stocking recommendations.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** [[Tilapia Zillii]] is the main species studied. [[Tilapia Mossambica]] was also reared and used, mainly in rice paddies at the University of California Imperial Valley Field Station.
- **Purpose:** The report describes progress in 1975, documents the capabilities and limits of the fish as a herbicide, and describes management techniques for its more effective use.
- **Supporting reports:** Two special reports were issued during the year, on temperature requirements and on salinity tolerance in [[Salton Sea]] water. Four further reports were in preparation for early 1976: food and feeding, spawning, alternate food sources for rearing, and fecundity.
- **Fish sources:** Fish came from district rearing facilities and from a commercial fish grower. The Coachella Valley district and the Imperial Irrigation District each received fish from the commercial grower and expected to do so again in 1976.
- **Study framework:** Work is organized into Study 1 (biological control agent field trials, Experiments 1 to 8), Study 2 (life history and ecology), and Study 3 (environmental factors).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Weed communities.** Weeds in the canals were dominated by [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), which made up 90 to 100% of total cover where it was present. Other species included [[Eurasian Watermilfoil]] (*Myriophyllum spicatum*), [[Southern Naiad]] (*Najas guadalupensis*), [[Hollyleaf Naiad]] (*Najas marina*), and [[Chara]] sp. Tilapia strongly preferred sago pondweed, southern naiad, and chara over watermilfoil. Watermilfoil was the least vulnerable and grew most slowly in spring, but it could become dominant once tilapia reduced the pondweed.

**Water temperature.** Temperature was the main environmental control on the fish.
- Low winter temperatures limited overwintering survival. Survival was possible in only a few drains and canals.
- Tilapia were fairly active and feeding at about 60°F, which was approximately the earliest safe stocking temperature.
- Fish were recommended for stocking at about 68°F in mid-April, once water temperatures were steady.
- Spawning became intensive above about 72 to 73°F.
- Weed control was lost when average canal temperatures fell below about 65°F.

**Predators.** Largemouth bass and channel catfish were described as serious predators of tilapia. Flathead catfish and redear sunfish were also present. In the Rositas Canal, observations suggested that predators limited tilapia reproduction. Mosquitofish and red shiners were also considered potential prey.

**Water flow and barriers.** Siphons and water-level management affected fish movement. A siphon at the lower end of Section 1 in the Ash Canal provided a fairly good barrier to emigration.

## Key Findings

### Experiments 1 to 8: Field Trials

- **Rositas Canal (IID), Experiment 1:** Stocking was heavier than in any previous year. By mid-June, 229.3 lb (5,526 fish) were stocked at an average of 24 fish per pound (3.7 in). On 8 July, 274.4 lb (46,264 fish) were added at a larger size (2.0 in). The equivalent density after the 8 July stocking was 2,049 fish (19.83 lb) per surface acre.
- **Weed biomass:** Estimated wet weight was 7.6 tons per acre on 1 July and 13.6 tons per acre on 25 August in the first section. Weed cover in the upper sections was about 20% at the first stocking. It fell to near zero within six weeks, then rose again in late summer and after mid-October. Some dragline cleaning was required in late August.
- **Stocking location:** The section stocked at the greatest rate, with the largest fish, had the best control. It needed only 14 cuts per 0.1 mile of mechanical work, compared with 37 cuts per 0.1 mile in the next 3.3 miles.
- **Integrated control:** Fish alone could not control heavy infestations, so some mechanical effort was needed before stocking. Late-stocked, small fish had little effect on weeds without mechanical help.
- **Barrier effect:** Section 2, below the siphon, was less well controlled. It required four times more mechanical effort than Section 1.
- **Cost:** Canal weed control cost $12,783 in 1973 and $19,669 in 1974 (OCR-scanned figure, approximate). In 1975, $8,997 was spent by 30 October. Replacing the fish with a commercial stock would have cost about $2,259 at $0.02 per inch.
- **Ash Canal, Experiment 2:** Two lateral sections were stocked (Ash Lat. 15 with 840 fish and Ash Lat. 30 with 588 fish). The canal was later dewatered, and the fish were lost from Lat. 30. Weed cover in Section 1 stayed near 50% all year, with little effect on species composition. Pondweed was largely replaced by watermilfoil.
- **Drainage Ditches, Experiment 3 (CVCWD):** Eight drains were stocked on 4 August 1975 at an average of 45 lb (5,675 fish) per acre. Garfield Street Drain showed a clear inverse relationship between fish and weed distribution. Buchanan Street Drain lost most pondweed within about three months, with no watermilfoil replacement.
- **Coachella Main Canal, Experiment 4:** A total of 255.2 lb (27,162 fish) was stocked. Fish stocked on 4 August were probably in poor condition after a hot-day transfer and were not acclimated. The weed survey showed no measurable effect.
- **Upper Central Main Canal, Experiment 5:** No tilapia were stocked in 1975. Control was satisfactory over 2.65 miles. Weed cover returned between August and November, and sago pondweed reappeared only when tilapia were absent.
- **Other canals, Experiment 6:** Twelve IID canals were stocked with 11,449.6 lb (212,170 fish) in total. Fish ranged from 300 to 105 per pound (1 to 2.5 in). Effects were confounded by mechanical clearing. The Central Main and All American canals had poor results because fish were small, stocked late, and held in the truck for about 36 hours.
- **Stocked Drains, Experiment 7 (IID):** Five drains were stocked on 1 July 1975. Most were nearly weed-free within two months, but tilapia were rarely observed afterward, so the cause of control remains uncertain.
- **Rice Paddies, Experiment 8:** [[Tilapia Mossambica]] was used alone in rice paddies at the Imperial Valley Field Station, working with Dr. W. F. Lehman. Weed control was judged superior, and a combination with [[Tilapia Zillii]] used the previous year was probably better still.

### Study 2: Life History and Ecology

- **Feeding rate versus size:** Feeding rate was tested in 15-gallon aquaria at 81°F with sago pondweed as the only food. Average consumption was 48.0% of body weight per day for 1.77 in fish, 38.9% for 2.95 in, 10.1% for 4.13 in, and about 5.6% for fish longer than 5.34 in. Fish lost weight throughout the trial, so pondweed alone is not an adequate diet.
- **Fecundity:** Potential fecundity of a three-inch fish is about 1,000 eggs. One 3.5-inch female laid 840 eggs, and 753 hatched. Survival from egg to hatch ranged from 47 to 90%. From 1,000 stocked three-inch fish, about 500 broods could produce about 750 fry each, or about 375,000 fry (a 37-fold increase in theory).
- **Spawning:** Digging began at about 67°F. Intensive spawning followed at 72 to 73°F, about one month later in 1975 than in 1974. Spawning declined in mid-summer when food was scarce, independent of temperature. Breeding colonies were more common than solitary nests, and colony nests were reused four or five times.
- **Movement:** Thirteen tagged fish were tracked for 7 to 30 days. Most stayed near the nesting colony, then made abrupt upstream moves of 500 to 2,000 feet, usually after spawning and during food shortages. The report concludes that tilapia are basically sedentary unless habitat is disrupted or food is limited, so small, distributed plantings are preferable.
- **Associated species and predation:** Largemouth bass and channel catfish were the most serious predators. Over half the bass and catfish containing food had tilapia remains in their stomachs. Large bass preferred tilapia over other prey. Small bass were more insectivorous.

### Study 3: Environmental Factors

- **Temperature records:** Continuous records have been kept since summer 1973. Low winter temperatures set the limit on survival, and high summer temperatures had little impact.
- **Stocking timing:** The safest stocking temperature was about 68°F in mid-April, once water stayed steadily warm without sudden drops.
- **Sago pondweed:** It is the most widespread and troublesome weed. It grows rapidly from late spring and peaks by mid-July or early August.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

1. The most important conclusion is to **stock many large tilapia early** in the season.
2. The basic stocking density for weed control should be **about 1,000 to 1,500 three- to four-inch fish per surface acre**. More fish are needed where there is a history of serious weeds, large watermilfoil populations, a large predator population, or late-season stocking. Fewer fish are needed where weeds are less severe, predators are few, or the water is warm enough for overwintering.
3. Fish must be **acclimated** to canal water temperature, ideally for a week or more. They should be stocked only after temperatures exceed 60°F and are not expected to drop again, typically in mid-April.
4. Stocking early prevents extensive weed growth. Heavy existing weed stands should be reduced mechanically before stocking.
5. The effective season for the herbicide runs from about **May through October**.
6. Whole canal systems should be stocked, not isolated sections.
7. Fish should be stocked at several locations within a canal, not all at one site.
8. Sites should be selected so trucks can be emptied quickly to reduce transport time.
9. Field crews need to develop management techniques, especially water manipulation at stocking sites and reduced destructive mechanical methods.
10. Special spawning sites may need to be built in some canals to increase the effective population.
