# Fishes of the Phoenix Metropolitan Area in Central Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-fish #introduced-species

## Executive Summary

This 1982 paper (Marsh and Minckley, Arizona State University) documents how the fish fauna of the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]] has changed as the [[Salt River]] shifted from a desert river with high groundwater to a dry channel with a depressed water table. Surface water is now almost entirely man-made canals. The historic assemblage of 15 native species has declined to 4, while 29 introduced forms have been recorded. The authors argue that the canals are a significant recreational resource holding desirable game fishes, but that their future fish communities will be hard to predict and manage.

## Background & Description

Prehistoric Indigenous peoples (c. 300 B.C. to 1450 A.D.) built at least 500 km of major canals and 1,600 km of laterals in the region. The largest exceeded 10 m in width and 3.5 m in depth and may have diverted up to 50% of the low-flow volume of the then-perennial Salt River. Western European settlement from the 1880s renovated many of these canals and built new ones. Today the canal network exceeds 2,000 km and forms much of the low-elevation lotic habitat in Arizona.

Historically, [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] were abundant. Prehistoric people used them as food, and in the late 1800s wagonloads of native fish were used for human and livestock food and fertilizer.

Data came from published literature, collections at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ) and the ASU Collection of Fishes, and surveys in 1963–1966, 1969, and 1981. Collection methods included seines, dip nets, electrofishing, and hand capture during dewatering. Permits were issued by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], and access was provided by the [[Salt River Project]].

**Habitats**
- *Prehistoric canals* were earthen, with slow currents (cattail pollen in sediments) and vegetation such as cottonwood, willow, and saltbush. Overhanging vegetation, snags, and cattail beds provided cover.
- *Modern canals* are steep-sided, concrete-lined, and uniform in depth, with little riparian vegetation. Algae include [[Cladophora]], and macrophytes include *Potamogeton* and *Zannichellia*. Burrowing invertebrates are dominated by the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] and the introduced [[Procambarus Clarkii]] crayfish, with native oligochaetes, odonates, and chironomids.
- *Water conditions:* temperatures of 15–40 °C and dissolved oxygen from under 5 to nearly 20 mg/L (Wein 1959).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrological decline.** Diversion at [[Granite Reef Diversion Dam]] (1908) and seven dams, ending with Horseshoe Dam (1946), largely stopped continuous flow in the natural channel. Short flowing sections persisted until the early 1950s because of seepage. Concrete lining of canals began in 1925–1930, and a major lining and repair program started in the mid-1950s. Groundwater declines of about 30 to more than 60 m were measured from 1930 to 1973 because of pumping. Before about 1930, groundwater was at or near the surface. By the 1960s, all surface water in the metropolitan area was artificial: wastewater, recreational ponds, and canals. Only the deepest gravel pits still hold water, and [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Green Sunfish]] are the only fishes that consistently persist there.

**Flooding.** Floods above 1,000 m³/s passed through the area several times between 1946 and the early 1980s. A flood of more than 10,000 m³/s in 1978 significantly altered river habitats, but canals were scarcely affected.

**Canal stressors.** Two factors deter fish colonization:
1. Annual dewatering for maintenance lasts 30–50 days each winter, desiccating most of the habitat. Dewatering had no apparent effect on fish distribution in the 1960s. The authors conclude that most canal fishes are annual immigrants from the upstream Salt and Verde rivers and their reservoirs.
2. The herbicide [[Acrolein Herbicide]] has been applied routinely since the mid-1960s to control nuisance vegetation. Its immediate and chronic effects on the aquatic biota are unknown.

**Stocking.** Introduced sport and forage species are stocked on a "put-and-take" basis in urban ponds (Edwards and Okamoto 1980), and new ponds and waterways are planned for the Salt River channel (Anonymous 1979).

## Key Findings

**Species richness has changed sharply**
- Original (1890s) ichthyofauna: 15 native species in the Salt River and canals (Gilbert and Scofield 1898).
- By 1926, native species had fallen to 8, and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) had become established.
- In the following 2–3 decades, four more introduced forms became established, and natives fell to 7.
- Sampling in 1963–1967 yielded 29 species, 5 of them native and widely distributed.
- In 1969, 12–13 species were collected at each of three sites, for a total of 17 species.
- In 1981, 4 native species (all recorded in 1890) and 19 introduced species were found.
- Across the century, 44 species have been recorded (Table 1).

**Fish distribution in 1981**
- Fish were concentrated in the largest canals and essentially restricted to them.
- Greatest abundance and diversity occurred in the first few kilometers below Granite Reef Diversion Dam (Fig. 2).
- Beyond 25 km, only a few individual [[Red Shiner]] or mosquitofish were found, and most sites yielded no fish.

**Native fauna**
- Nine of the original 15 native species have disappeared from the study area. They have also become rare throughout their ranges and are listed as threatened, endangered, or of special concern by federal and/or state agencies (Johnson and Rinne 1982).
- Bonytail Chub and [[Colorado Squawfish]] were extirpated from the Gila River drainage. [[Woundfin]] and [[Spikedace]] are no longer present in the study area.
- Razorback Sucker was reintroduced into the Salt River in 1981–1982.
- The [[Gila Topminnow]] was unsuccessfully reintroduced.

**Introduced fauna**
- Two groups dominate: temperate North American species and tropical Middle American and African species.
- Early introductions (mosquitofish, likely for mosquito control; and sport fishes) came first.
- Tropical forms (poeciliids, cichlids) may reflect aquarium trade or hobbyist releases.
- African tilapias and trouts were introduced for put-and-take or self-sustaining fisheries.
- Common, established species include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Red Shiner]].
- Several upstream species (yellow perch, walleye, buffalofishes) were not recorded downstream, suggesting they do not move there.
- [[Yellow Bass]], introduced before 1931 in Salt River reservoirs, was not recorded downstream until after the 1978–1979 floods.

**Transience and instability**
- Most tropical and reservoir-dependent species (poeciliids, cichlids, black crappie) appear transient. Cold winters, possibly lethal to tropical forms, combined with flooding, limit colonization.
- Several poeciliids (sailfin molly, shortfin molly, guppy, swordtail) were recorded but are now extirpated.
- Golden shiner, fathead minnow, and goldfish do not form large low-elevation populations and may persist through repeated reintroduction as bait.

**Management implications**
- A large pool of actual or potential colonizing species remains available.
- Many introduced fishes are valued for food or sport, but several (carp, green sunfish, goldfish, golden shiner) have caused management problems in Arizona and elsewhere.
- Diversity among introduced species makes future faunal conditions hard to predict, and the authors expect this to hamper the development of recreational fisheries.
