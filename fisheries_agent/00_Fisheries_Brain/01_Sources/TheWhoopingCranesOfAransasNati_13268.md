# The Whooping Cranes of Aransas National Wildlife Refuge

#ornithology #conservation-biology #Gruidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document describes the status and ecology of the world's wild [[Whooping Crane]] (*Grus americana*) population, which winters exclusively at [[Aransas National Wildlife Refuge]] on the [[Texas Gulf Coast]]. As of 1966, the population stood at 43 birds (38 adults, 5 young), representing a gradual recovery from a low of 15 birds in 1941. The species undertakes a 2,500-mile migration between winter grounds in Texas and breeding grounds in [[Wood Buffalo National Park]], Canada. Despite recent population increases, the species remains critically endangered, with survival dependent on protection during migration, successful breeding in remote northern habitats, and continued conservation efforts by multiple agencies and organizations.

## Background & Description

The [[Whooping Crane]] (*Grus americana*) stands approximately five feet tall and weighs close to 20 pounds. Adults display distinctive red-crowned heads and black-tipped wings with a seven-foot wingspan. The species is named for its piercing "Ker-loo, Ker-lee-oo" call, produced by a four-foot-long trachea that forms large loops coiled inside the breast bone, enabling bugle-like vocalizations audible over great distances.

Flight characteristics include a slow downbeat and fast upbeat of wings, with neck and legs fully extended. During migration, birds fly at high altitudes, sometimes out of sight. Juvenile birds are easily recognized by their splotched, rusty brown appearance with black wing tips, which gradually molt to adult white plumage during their first winter.

Wild whoopers are believed to mate for life and demonstrate exceptional parental care, guarding and feeding young for up to eleven months after hatching. Parents keep juveniles between them during flight and close at hand during feeding and preening activities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical Range and Population Decline:**
Whooping cranes formerly nested in Louisiana, Illinois, Iowa, Minnesota, North Dakota, and northward into Canada. The advance of civilization eliminated the southern segment of this nesting range. The species is believed to have been uncommon since the early Pleistocene period (500,000 years ago), though found in larger numbers during early exploration and settlement.

**Current Range:**
- **Winter habitat:** [[Aransas National Wildlife Refuge]], Texas (late October to mid-April)
- **Summer breeding grounds:** [[Wood Buffalo National Park]], Canada, near the Arctic Circle (discovered in 1954 following a fire)
- **Migration corridor:** 2,500-mile flight path over farms, factories, and areas with open waterfowl hunting seasons

**Breeding Biology:**
Each breeding pair lays one or two buff and brown mottled eggs in a mound-like nest on the ground. Eggs are laid two to four days apart, with incubation beginning when the first egg is laid. Both parents participate in incubation, which lasts approximately 34 days. Newly hatched chicks are about half the size of an adult robin and grow rapidly, reaching flight capability in approximately 90 days. Several days of practice flight are required before juveniles can accompany parents on migration.

The short ice-free period of northern nesting areas represents a major hazard and likely accounts for fluctuations in young bird production. If initial nesting attempts fail, second attempts are unlikely to succeed due to the abbreviated summer season, as young must reach strong flight stage before heavy fall freezes.

**Winter Ecology:**
Upon arrival at [[Aransas National Wildlife Refuge]] in mid-October, family groups establish territories of a few hundred acres, guarded against intrusion by other whooping cranes but shared with waterfowl and shore and wading birds. Winter diet includes diverse plant and animal life: blue crabs, shrimp, small fishes, frogs, worms, marine crustaceans, mollusks, acorns, and various planted grains and vegetables from two 100-acre mammal-proof food plots maintained on the refuge.

**Migration Patterns:**
First arrivals typically occur in mid-October, with family groups appearing over several weeks through late November. Spring migration begins in late March and early April, with all cranes normally departing by April 15. Family groups remain together during spring migration, but juveniles are forced to leave parents upon reaching nesting grounds.

**Captive Population:**
As of 1966, six birds were held at [[Audubon Park Zoo]] in New Orleans and one at the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife Research Station]] in Laurel, Maryland. Captive breeding success has been limited. Captive whoopers have lived more than 25 years, while wild lifespan is only 7 to 10 years.

## Key Findings

**Population Trends (1938-1966):**
Annual counts at [[Aransas National Wildlife Refuge]] reveal dramatic fluctuations:
- 1941: Historic low of 15 birds (13 adults, 2 young)
- 1949: High of 34 birds (30 adults, 4 young)
- 1952: Decline to 21 birds (19 adults, 2 young)
- 1961: Recovery to 38 birds (33 adults, 5 young)
- 1962: Decline to 32 birds (32 adults, 0 young)
- 1964: Record 42 birds (32 adults, 10 young—record number of juveniles)
- 1965: 44 birds (36 adults, 8 young)
- 1966: 43 birds (38 adults, 5 young)

**Conservation Status:**
Recent population increases, while encouraging, do not warrant optimism. Historical patterns show similar increases followed by alarming declines. The species faces multiple threats:
- Natural hazards during 2,500-mile migration
- Flight through provinces and states with open waterfowl hunting seasons
- Dependence on hunter cooperation and public awareness
- Limited breeding success due to short northern summer season
- Inability to conduct complete population counts at vast, remote [[Wood Buffalo National Park]]

**Management and Monitoring:**
At [[Aransas National Wildlife Refuge]], whooping cranes receive intensive attention including weekly aerial surveys and frequent boat patrols. Visitors cannot enter crane habitat but may observe birds from a well-concealed observation tower near one family territory.

**Conservation Partnerships:**
Multiple organizations contribute to conservation efforts: [[Canadian Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[National Audubon Society]], [[National Wildlife Federation]], state conservation departments, and numerous individuals and clubs. Success depends on continued cooperation from these entities and private citizens.

**Research Priorities:**
Research with [[Sandhill Crane]]s aims to provide knowledge needed to rear young whooping cranes in captivity, potentially providing means to assist the wild population's survival.
