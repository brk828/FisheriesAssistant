# Aquatic Ecosystems of the American West: Symposium Report on Physical, Riparian, Nutrient, Food-Web and Native-Fish Sustainability

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae #aquatic-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This document is the 1997 report of the Aquatic Ecosystems Symposium, edited by [[Wendell L. Minckley]] for the Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission. It compiles five independent contributions and an introduction and synthesis addressing the sustainability of western US aquatic systems. The unanimous conclusion of all five contributions is that aquatic systems in the American West are broken and must soon be repaired to become sustainable. Degradation stems mainly from dams, diversions, groundwater pumping, grazing, mining, agriculture, urbanization, and non-native species, with climate variability acting as a secondary driver. The report calls for watershed-based, flexible-federalism governance, ecosystem-oriented flow management, expanded protection of riparian and native-fish habitats, and sustained monitoring and research funding.

## Background & Description

The symposium was organized around the question of what critical resources in western aquatic ecosystems are at risk and what their current health and stability are. Five contributions were prepared:

- **Sustainability and Changing Physical Landscapes** ([[William L. Graf]] and coauthors): geomorphology and hydrology of watersheds, rivers, and dams.
- **Sustainability of Western Riparian Ecosystems** ([[Duncan T. Patten]] and coauthors): riparian functions, controlling factors, and human impacts.
- **Sustainability of Western Watersheds: Nutrients and Productivity** ([[Nancy B. Grimm]] and coauthors): nutrient retention, transport, and primary productivity.
- **Sustaining Western Aquatic Food Webs** ([[Mary E. Power]] and coauthors): food-web structure, linkages to terrestrial systems, and degradation.
- **Sustainability of Western Native Fish Resources** ([[James E. Deacon]] and coauthors): fish diversity, non-native species, recovery efforts, and policy.

A synthesis by [[Jack A. Stanford]] closes the volume with policy messages. The report's introduction by Minckley frames sustainability using the Brundtland definition and notes that western water demand is non-sustainable in arid and semi-arid areas. Acronyms cited include [[Endangered Species Act]], [[Clean Water Act]], [[National Environmental Policy Act]], and [[Wild and Scenic Rivers Act]]. The document also contains a long list of scientific names in an appendix; these are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical landscape and hydrology.** Western precipitation is unstable and shifts between El Niño and La Niña patterns, with multidecadal hydroclimatic periods. Human changes, including grazing, logging, road building, and mining, increased runoff, sediment yield and flood frequency in headwater basins. Beaver removal in the 1820–1850 period eliminated hydrologic and sediment-storage functions. Large dams (70 structures with storage over 1 million acre-feet were built, with the construction era ending in 1984) reduced flood peaks, altered seasonality, and deactivated floodplains. The Colorado River fluctuated up to 4.0 m daily in Grand Canyon. Only about 0.3% of US stream miles are permanently protected under the Wild and Scenic Rivers System.

**Riparian ecosystems.** Riparian zones occupy a narrow band but support more than 75% of arid-region animal species at some life stage and are the most productive and diverse communities in the West. Major threats include impoundment and diversion, groundwater pumping, grazing, non-native plants, altered fire regimes, mining, roads, urbanization, and recreation. Saltcedar (*Tamarix ramosissima*) now occupies over 500,000 ha of riparian habitat, and near Yuma, Arizona, it occupies about 90% of the area originally dominated by cottonwood-willow forest.

**Nutrients and productivity.** Most western streams are nitrogen-limited, and rivers are increasingly disconnected from floodplains, hyporheic zones and riparian corridors. Agriculture accounts for about 90% of total water consumption in most western states. The Rio Grande floodplain wetlands shrank from about 52,000 acres in 1918 to 3,671 acres (7%) by 1989. Groundwater pumping in Albuquerque raised withdrawals from about 14,000 to over 118,000 acre-feet between 1960 and 1992, producing a 42 m water-table drop.

**Food webs.** Healthy food webs in western rivers depend on quasi-natural flood regimes and refugia from high flows and drought. Dams disrupt food webs through hydropeaking, altered thermal regimes, loss of floods that flush fine sediments from spawning gravels, and suppression of successional insect communities. Non-native fishes, bullfrogs and crayfish are major threats. At Kesterson Reservoir, California, selenium from irrigation drainage caused embryo deaths (about 20% of nests with deformed chicks and 40% with dead embryos in 1983).

**Native fishes.** At least 40 North American freshwater fish species have gone extinct in the last century, more than half in arid western lands, and 16 since 1964. At least 100 additional native western species are threatened, endangered or of special concern. Of 810 native North American freshwater fishes, 170 (21%) occur west of the Rocky Mountains, and about 40 species (5%) occur on both sides of the Continental Divide. Introduced fish now outnumber natives in the Colorado basin (more than 60 non-native species versus 32 natives).

## Key Findings

**Overarching conclusion.** All five contributions agree that western aquatic systems are degraded and must be repaired to become sustainable. Western watersheds are degraded not only in forested areas but throughout the region.

**Physical systems (Graf et al.)**
- Dams and reservoirs have produced changes greater than any projected from global climate change; the West has segmented, "balkanized" river systems.
- Federal management is fragmented across agencies (Army Corps of Engineers, Bureau of Reclamation, Natural Resources Conservation Service, Fish and Wildlife Service). Recommendations include establishing a commission to reorganize agencies by watershed, "flexible federalism," increasing emphasis on physical integrity under the Clean Water Act, redefining hydropower's primacy, and promoting natural dam release regimes.
- Sediment monitoring is underfunded (about $30,000 per year for one station), and sediment quality standards are lacking.

**Riparian ecosystems (Patten et al.)**
- Dam operations should mimic natural seasonal flows; the 1996 Grand Canyon controlled flood is cited as a promising example.
- Recommendations include reviewing dam utility, managing groundwater to protect streamflow, limiting federally funded highway construction in riparian areas, discouraging floodplain development through insurance policy, prioritizing native plants, and designating a fourth "Natural" class under the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act.
- The authors conclude that riparian systems will likely never return to pre-Columbian condition, but rehabilitation is possible.

**Nutrients and productivity (Grimm et al.)**
- Maintaining the balance between nutrient retention and transport is essential; retention capacity loss transfers water-quality problems to reservoirs, groundwater and estuaries.
- Recommendations include restoring natural or enhanced flood regimes, evaluating dam decommissioning case by case, reducing agricultural water subsidies, treating effluent as a managed point source, and expanding long-term monitoring (NAWQA and LTER networks).
- Adaptive management is highlighted, notably on the Colorado River.

**Food webs (Power et al.)**
- Aquatic food webs are gape-limited and inverted in biomass structure. Vertebrate predators retain nutrients locally and move them upslope, buffering watersheds.
- Recommendations include reconnecting floodplains, flood-proofing rather than building on floodplains, reducing pollution and mining impacts, using digital elevation models for timber harvest decisions, adopting drip irrigation, and training taxonomists to support invertebrate monitoring.
- The Flathead Lake opossum shrimp introduction is cited as a case of food-web collapse with economic costs.

**Native fishes (Deacon et al.)**
- Native fishes are critical indicators of ecosystem health and have been subject to extinction; recovery has been limited. Only about 4% of listed species have been delisted, and Endangered Species Act recovery plans have had limited success.
- Introduced sportfish fisheries are politically powerful and often conflict with native-fish recovery.
- Recommendations include the "user pays" principle, ecosystem-oriented management prioritizing native species, eradication and exclusion of non-natives where feasible, Habitat Conservation Plans with adaptive management and monitoring, and expanded public education.
- Successes cited include the Lake Hebron Spring pupfish restoration and Bruneau Hot Springs snail advocacy.

**Synthesis and policy messages (Stanford)**
- Watersheds are the basic units for water management.
- Recommended policy principles include establishing watershed-based water-quality standards (for example, a sediment standard), protecting riparian zones, recognizing groundwater-surface water interactions, allowing rivers room to migrate, promoting dam operations that create normative flow and temperature regimes, preventing non-native invasions, using best management practices, creating flexible federalism through watershed councils, and linking subsidies and incentives to conservation.
- The Columbia River salmon restoration effort (about $400 million per year) is cited as a failure due to lack of monitoring; fewer than 10 of more than 200 historical salmon and steelhead runs remain reasonably stable.
- Basic research receives "far less than a few tenths of a percent" of the annual water-resources budget; the synthesis calls for investment on the order of 1–2% of annual budgets.

**Cross-cutting themes**
- Non-native species, once established, are essentially impossible to eradicate, and introduced fishes now equal or exceed habitat change as causes of native-fish decline.
- Lack of monitoring, uncertainty, and fragmented governance prevent effective restoration across all five sections.
