# Factors Limiting the Distributions of Native Fishes in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines why four native fish species occur in the lower 14.2 km of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) below [[Chute Falls]], while only one, [[Speckled Dace]], occurs in the upstream reach to [[Blue Spring]]. The authors tested two of four proposed hypotheses: unsuitable water chemistry and limited food resources above Chute Falls. Food resources were found to be adequate or better upstream. A 72-hour caged relocation experiment showed that most native fish survived upstream, although age-0 [[Humpback Chub]] suffered high mortality at the most upstream site. The authors conclude that neither food nor water chemistry alone confines these native fishes to the lower reach. They suggest that translocation above Chute Falls, or modification of the falls, could expand habitat for the endangered humpback chub.

## Background & Description

The LCR, a tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], historically supported most of the eight native fishes of the river. Land and water use from the mid-1800s onward reduced flow, and by the early 1900s the river was intermittent above Blue Spring. Its terminal 21 km then became fed mainly by saline springs, with higher salinity and [[Carbon Dioxide]] (CO2) levels.

Four native species persist in the perennial reach:
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), federally endangered; the largest remaining population is in the LCR and Colorado River.
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), possibly distinct from populations above Grand Falls.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), the only native fish found above Chute Falls.

Three species, [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), were recorded in the early 1900s below [[Grand Falls]] but are now extirpated from the Grand Canyon region. [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is rare and may be extirpated from the canyon.

The four hypotheses for the current distribution were:
1. Water chemistry is unsuitable above Chute Falls.
2. Food resources are limiting above Chute Falls.
3. Chute Falls is a physical barrier to upstream movement.
4. Suitable physical habitat is unavailable above Chute Falls.

The study addressed hypotheses 1 and 2.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and hydrology.** The terminal 21 km of the LCR is sustained by [[Blue Spring]] and smaller springs, giving a near-constant base flow of 6.3 m³/s at the mouth. Blue Spring water is about 20 °C, highly charged with free CO2, and supersaturated with calcite. As water flows downstream, CO2 degasses and calcite precipitates. This process forms [[Tufa Deposition]] dams, which are concentrated between Chute Falls and Salt Trail Canyon. The three largest dams form the [[Atomizer Falls Complex]] (AFC): Lower Atomizer Falls (13.6 km), Upper Atomizer Falls (13.9 km), and Chute Falls (14.2 km). Upstream of Blue Spring, flows range from dry to floods of an estimated 3,396 m³/s.

**Reaches.** The authors divided the study area into:
- Reach 1: 0–14.2 km (mouth to Chute Falls)
- Reach 2: 14.2–21 km (Chute Falls to Blue Spring)

Water chemistry was sampled at six sites across four seasons, from autumn 1991 to summer 1993.

**Water chemistry gradients.** Dissolved oxygen, pH, and conductivity increased, and free CO2 decreased, downstream in Reach 2, with the sharpest changes between Blue Spring and 20 km. Alkalinity decreased through both reaches. Turbidity peaked between 10 and 15 km, consistent with calcite precipitation below the falls. Conductivity rose downstream despite falling alkalinity, which the authors attribute to additional saline spring inputs.

**Food base.** [[Periphyton]] and [[Benthic Invertebrates]] were sampled at five lower sites in June and August 1993. High nitrate, high free CO2, and low turbidity in Reach 2 were proposed to favor algal growth, which supports invertebrate production there.

## Key Findings

### 1. Food resources do not limit native fish distributions
- Chlorophyll *a* was significantly greater in Reach 2 than Reach 1 (Kruskal-Wallis H = 39.71, P < 0.001). It did not differ between sample periods (H = 1.89, P = 0.17) or between substrate types (H = 1.15, P = 0.28).
- More invertebrate taxa were found in Reach 2 than Reach 1: 11 vs. 7 in June and 14 vs. 8 in August. Eight taxa were found only in Reach 2, and none were unique to Reach 1.
- Total invertebrate densities were greater in Reach 2. Invertebrate ash-free dry weight (AFDW) was either significantly greater in Reach 2 or did not differ between reaches.
- Large numbers of speckled dace were observed in Reach 2, supporting the conclusion that food is sufficient there.

### 2. Water chemistry relocation experiment
- In June 1994, 39 age-0 (26–40 mm TL) and 35 age-1 (78–125 mm TL) [[Humpback Chub]], and 12 age-1 (69–140 mm TL) [[Bluehead Sucker]], were captured near 10.5 km.
- Fish were held in cages for 72 h at four sites: 12.5 km (control), 15, 17.5, and 20 km. Sample sizes per site were 10 age-0 and 10 age-1 chub at 12.5 and 20 km, 9 of each age at 17.5 km, 10 age-0 and 6 age-1 chub at 15 km, and 3 suckers per site.
- All age-1 fish survived. Some individuals of both species showed short-term respiratory stress.
- Age-0 humpback chub mortality differed significantly among sites (G = 13.70, P < 0.01). The reported rates were 70% at 20 km and 20% at the 12.5 km control. Some intermediate values are unclear in the scanned text.
- Stress-behavior rates for age-0 humpback chub differed among sites (G = 850.52, P < 0.001). The rate was 93% at 20 km and about 1% at the control. Age-1 stress rates were 88% for humpback chub and 67% for bluehead suckers, observed only at 20 km.

### 3. Free CO2 threshold
- Free CO2 at 20 km (348 mg/L) was at or near the lethal limit for age-0 humpback chub.
- Free CO2 at 15 km (192 mg/L) and 17.5 km (196 mg/L) was well below lethal limits reported for [[Fathead Minnow]] (293 mg/L at 20.4 °C) and [[White Sucker]] (260 mg/L at 17.1 °C). The authors conclude that these fish can acclimate to these concentrations.

### 4. Conclusions and management implications
- The authors conclude that neither food resources nor water chemistry alone prevent humpback chub and bluehead sucker from inhabiting Reach 2.
- Suitable habitat, including deep pools (>4 m) and near-shore slack water, appears available above Chute Falls, and matches habitat suitability curves for humpback chub.
- Possible management actions include:
  - Translocating fish above Chute Falls, where they could use the lower portion of the reach.
  - Modifying [[Chute Falls]] to reduce its vertical drop, which could allow upstream fish movement and might eliminate the need for stocking.
- Upstream passage is most likely during extended high-stage spring floods, when some humpback chub migrate upstream. A January 1993 flood with mean flows of 283–464 m³/s was followed by no further observations of large native fishes above Chute Falls, though sampling there has been sporadic.
