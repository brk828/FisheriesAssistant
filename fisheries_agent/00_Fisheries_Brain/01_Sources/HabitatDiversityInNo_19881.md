## Executive Summary
This 1968 paper by John R. Holsinger surveys habitat diversity among North American subterranean amphipods of the family [[Gammaridae]]. Rather than addressing fish, the study characterizes the ecological distribution of roughly 85 recognized subterranean amphipod species across seven genera, demonstrating that these groundwater crustaceans partition themselves among three broad, geologically-defined habitat categories. The paper emphasizes that habitat occupancy is strongly tied to geology and regional distribution, with most species showing high fidelity to a single habitat type rather than broad habitat generalism.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
North American subterranean [[Gammaridae]] occupy three broad, largely geologically-defined groundwater habitat categories:

1. **Cave systems** — including associated solution channels and fissures in limestone and dolomite (carbonate rock regions).
2. **Interstitial habitats in non-carbonate substrates** — developed in metamorphic and other non-carbonate rocks and overlying mantle deposits.
3. **Interstitial habitats in loosely consolidated sedimentary deposits.**

Key ecological patterns identified:
- Habitat occupancy is influenced by **geographic distribution**; species restricted to limestone regions are rarely found outside them and are most commonly collected from cave waters.
- Habitat specialization is high: only about **20% of recognized subterranean species** occupy as many as two of the three major habitat groups; very few occupy all three.
- Within caves, two main microhabitats are recognized:
  - **Shallow, mud-bottomed pools** fed by seepage/drippage.
  - **Shallow, moderately slow-flowing streams** with silt or gravel substrates.
- **Genus-level habitat partitioning within caves:**
  - [[Apocrangonyx]] and [[Stygobromus]] — primarily inhabit shallow drip/seep pools.
  - [[Stygonectes]] — primarily associated with slow-flowing streams.
  - [[Crangonyx]] — occupies both pool and stream habitats with roughly equal frequency.
- Species inhabiting seep/drip pools are hypothesized to also occupy adjacent **crevices, fissures, and solution channels** that undergo periodic groundwater saturation, suggesting a broader cryptic habitat use beyond directly observable cave waters.
- A small number of species (~5) are known only from **wells**, representing a distinct, less-characterized habitat category.

## Key Findings
- Approximately **85 subterranean amphipod species** (described and undescribed) are recognized across **seven genera** in North America.
- Habitat distribution breakdown among these species:
  - ~50 species known **exclusively from caves**.
  - ~14 species known **exclusively from seeps and springs**.
  - ~16 species known from **caves, seeps, and springs** collectively.
  - ~5 species known only from **wells** (excluded from the above breakdown).
- Habitat specialization is the norm: most species are restricted to one of the three major habitat categories (limestone caves/solution channels, non-carbonate interstitial systems, or unconsolidated sedimentary interstitial systems), with cross-habitat occupancy occurring in only a minority (~20%) of species.
- Geology (particularly the presence of limestone/dolomite karst) is a major determinant of habitat type and amphipod distribution, reinforcing the link between regional lithology and subterranean faunal composition.
- Genus-level ecological differentiation within cave habitats (pool-dwelling vs. stream-dwelling preferences) suggests niche partitioning among co-occurring subterranean amphipod lineages.
