# Bait Fish Production in New York Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by John L. Forney (Department of Conservation, [[Cornell University]]) tested the propagation of bait minnows in privately owned [[Farm Pond]]s in central New York from 1953 to 1955. Six species were stocked individually in 95 ponds. Three were judged suitable for New York pond culture: [[Golden Shiner]], [[White Sucker]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]. Yields were low compared with southern states, and the author concluded that bait propagation is feasible in New York mainly as a supplement to wild bait supplies. The work was a contribution of Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration Project F-4-R.

## Background & Description

A 1952 survey of bait dealers found shortages of pike-size bait (over 4 inches) in spring and limited supplies of smaller bait fish in summer. Small minnows were usually available from lakes and rivers in spring and fall, at low prices. The study was designed to determine which species could be reared successfully in New York and to establish management methods for small impoundments.

Most ponds were privately owned, similar in design to farm ponds, and supplied by runoff or groundwater. They were seldom suited to intensive culture. Oxygen depletion made feeding hazardous, and complete harvests were often impracticable. Pond areas ranged from 0.1 to 4.5 acres, so all data were expressed per acre.

Bait size classes used in the paper:
- Perch size: 2 to 3 inches
- Bass size: 3 to 4 inches
- Pike size: over 4 inches

Species stocked were [[Golden Shiner]], [[White Sucker]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Silvery Minnow]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]] and [[Chub Sucker]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Brood fish were mostly taken from natural waters. Adults were counted individually, while young were estimated from total weight and fish per pound. Sucker eggs were measured by volumetric displacement. Length data came from [[Seining]] with 1/4-inch-mesh nets (Some collections used larger mesh). Population estimates relied on repeated seine hauls, checked against [[Population Estimation]] by draining 13 ponds. Seining caught on average 7% less than the actual weight in six golden shiner ponds and 11% less in five white sucker ponds. The [[DeLury Regression Method]] offered no advantage over total catch.

**Fertilization and feeding.** Cooperators typically applied 200 pounds of commercial fertilizer at 2- to 3-week intervals from April to September. Ponds were to be left unfertilized when [[Secchi Disc Transparency]] fell so low that plankton obscured the bottom at 12 to 18 inches. Manure or soybean meal was used occasionally; bi-weekly or more frequent soybean meal was treated as supplemental feed (see [[Supplemental Feeding with Soybean Meal]]).

**Environmental conditions.** Golden shiner ponds ranged from 400 to 1,900 feet in elevation and from 5 to 200 p.p.m. total alkalinity. Clay turbidity (Secchi transparency 14 to 24 inches) repeatedly depressed standing crops (see [[Clay Turbidity]]). Growth of all species was confined largely to the roughly 6-month period between early May and late October.

## Key Findings

**Golden shiner (Notemigonus crysoleucas)**
- Adults were stocked at 200 to 1,880 per acre. Young were produced in 24 of 27 ponds stocked.
- Survival of yearlings over the 4-month second-season summer averaged 86%, with a weekly instantaneous mortality of 0.01 (about 60% annual survival).
- The number of yearlings surviving to age 1+ appeared controlled by the physical and biological environment rather than by stocking rate.
- Mean standing crop was 167 pounds per acre at the end of the first season and 362 pounds per acre in August of the second season. The two-year yield averaged 420 pounds per acre.
- Fertilized ponds in the second season yielded nearly 20,000 shiners over 3 inches long.
- Reproduction failed in three ponds with Secchi transparency consistently under 14 inches and no submergent vegetation.
- Reproduction was also limited in turbid ponds. Turbid ponds averaged 95 pounds per acre at the end of the first season versus 182 pounds per acre elsewhere.
- Unfertilized ponds averaged 13,730 yearlings per acre versus 48,020 in fertilized ponds. Their standing crop was about one-quarter that of fertilized ponds.
- Golden shiners reached bass size (over 3 inches) by mid-summer of the second year in ponds where the first year's hatch was held over. Only six of 18 ponds produced an appreciable number of bass-size shiners in the first season.
- Pike-size yearling production in fertilized ponds was judged commercially unfeasible.

**White sucker (Catostomus commersonnii)**
- Fry were stocked in 18 ponds at 50,000 to 231,000 per acre. Survival to fall ranged from 1.7% to 71.2% and averaged 24%, close to the 21% reported from Minnesota ponds.
- The coefficient of correlation between stocking number and fall fingerlings was 0.01, so stocking rate had little effect on survival.
- Insect control affected survival: drained and refilled ponds averaged 49.2%, kerosene-treated ponds 29.4%, and untreated ponds 13.1%. This suggested insect predation is an important source of fry mortality.
- Plankton density did not affect early survival in the concrete-tank test. Survival was 70 to 80% in all five tanks, regardless of seston concentration.
- Growth was negatively correlated with fall population density, described by log L = 2.1272 − 0.3762 log P (L in inches, P per acre).
- The first-season yield averaged 285 pounds per acre (17,280 suckers at 2 to 3 inches, 5,240 at 3 to 4 inches, 2,210 over 4 inches).
- Overwintering in seven ponds gave a mean length increment of 0.72 inch and a population weight gain of 92 pounds per acre from fall to May. Mean survival over winter in eight ponds was 79.8%.
- Overwintering more than about 15,000 suckers (2 to 3 inches) per acre was not advised if fish were to reach bass and pike size by May.
- Supplemental feeding of soybean meal in Pond 111 produced 880 pounds per acre in six months, against a maximum of 575 pounds per acre in any fertilized pond without feeding. The estimated food conversion ratio was 1:4 or lower.

**Fathead minnow (Pimephales promelas)**
- Eight fertilized ponds were stocked with adults at 600 to 2,000 per acre. Mean standing crop at the end of the first season was 96 pounds per acre (ranging from 35 to 209).
- The average yield was 12,140 minnows over 1.8 inches per acre from the eight ponds. Seven ponds managed for two years yielded roughly 50,000 salable minnows per acre. Over two years the salable yield was about four times the first-season standing crop.
- Post-spawning mortality was not a management problem. Survival between June and September in two ponds was 92%.
- Spawning began in May and continued through summer. Eggs were observed from May 12 to September 2.
- Lower stocking rates tended to yield more salable young in the first year, possibly because of faster growth at low density.
- The fathead minnow yielded more salable bait than [[Bluntnose Minnow]] ponds.

**Silvery minnow (Hybognathus nuchalis regius)**
- Silvery minnows were stocked in 11 ponds. Disease destroyed the hatch in one pond and the 1955 hatch in another. Adults failed to reproduce in two ponds.
- The average fall young count in eight ponds was 19,430 per acre.
- Standing crops in eight ponds ranged from 56 to 268 pounds per acre. Turbid ponds averaged 58 pounds per acre, versus 186 pounds per acre in clearer ponds.
- Three ponds yielded mostly bass-size fish by September. Ponds 310 and 311 produced over 20,000 bass-size minnows each.

**Bluntnose minnow (Pimephales notatus)**
- Three ponds were stocked at 400 to 1,000 adults per acre in 1953. Bluntnose minnows can be reared and sold as perch-size bait.
- Yields were lower than those of fathead minnow ponds, and the species is much less tolerant of handling and transport.

**Chub sucker (Erimyzon oblongus oblongus)**
- Ripe adults were stocked at 160 and 214 per acre in two ponds. Reproduction failed in Pond 404.
- In Pond 405 the fall population was 4,250 young per acre averaging 2.60 inches, with a standing crop of 47 pounds per acre.

**Conclusions and comparisons**
- Golden shiner, white sucker and fathead minnow are suitable for bait culture in New York.
- New York yields are low compared with southern ponds. [[Alabama Bait Ponds]] (Prather et al., 1953) averaged 500 pounds per acre of golden shiners after one season, while New York ponds needed two seasons to reach similar standing crops.
- Wholesale bait prices in New York are similar to those in Tennessee, so profits are expected to be lower than in southern states. Most cooperating dealers still judged rearing profitable as a supplement to wild supplies.
- The study concluded that bait propagation is feasible in New York, supported by the expansion of small bait-rearing facilities and increased use of farm ponds.
