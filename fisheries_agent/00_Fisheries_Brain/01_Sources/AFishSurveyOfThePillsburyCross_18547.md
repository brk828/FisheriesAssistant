# A Fish Survey of the Pillsbury Crossing Area, Deep Creek, Riley County, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 survey by W. L. Minckley (Kansas State College, Manhattan) documents the fish community of a 900-yard reach of [[Deep Creek]] around [[Pillsbury Crossing]], a tributary of the [[Kansas River]]. Sampling ran from February 21 to April 27, 1956, using nine gear types. Thirteen species and 875 specimens were captured, with [[Red Shiner]] (27.6%) and [[White Sucker]] (20.8%) the most abundant. Three more species were recorded at stations one mile upstream and downstream. The study also describes habitat use, seasonal movement tied to water temperature, and recreational angling success, which was very low.

## Background & Description

Deep Creek is a small, meandering stream draining mostly grassland (estimated 70%) and cultivated land (30%) in Riley and Waubaunsee counties. Cut banks are 3 to 30 feet high (average about five feet). The stream usually flows year-round. The crossing takes its name from a natural limestone ledge that forms a roadbed.

The study area was divided into six sections, numbered from downstream:

- **Section 1:** A roughly 60-foot pool bordered by a limestone bluff, with riffles at each end. It is grazed by cattle, and manure is present.
- **Section 2:** Two pools (a circular upper pool 15 ft in diameter and 11 ft deep, and a lower pool 30 by 15 ft and 2½ ft deep) with a three-foot waterfall and filamentous algae.
- **Section 3:** A riffle three to six feet wide flowing into a shallow pool (under one foot deep) at the road crossing.
- **Section 4:** Transition reach, about 40 ft wide and up to nine feet deep, with a mud bottom and debris.
- **Section 5:** Straight stretch about 40 ft wide, depth six to nine feet, with steep clay banks under large trees.
- **Section 6:** A circular bend 60 to 70 ft across, the deepest part (181/2 ft recorded), used as a local swimming hole.

Current ranged from almost none (Sections 5 and 6) to swift (Sections 2 to 4), returning to slow in Section 1.

### Collection Gear

Nine methods were used: a 15-ft common-sense woven-mesh seine (¼-inch mesh); a 100-ft knotted-mesh seine (1-inch mesh); a 60-ft knotted-mesh haul seine (¼-inch mesh); a six-foot D-mouth [[Hoop Net]]; an experimental [[Gill Net Sampling]] (100 ft, ⅜ to 2-inch bar mesh); eight quart-jar [[Minnow Trap]]s; pole and line; fly and casting rods with artificial lures; and set lines.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species composition.** Of 875 specimens across 13 species in the core area, the community was dominated by minnows (Cyprinidae), with the catostomid, ictalurid, centrarchid and percid taxa contributing smaller shares. The three additional species were [[Suckermouthed Minnow]] and [[Longear Sunfish]] (upstream) and [[Redfin Shiner]] (downstream).

**Habitat associations.** Habitat preferences were noted for several species:

- [[White Sucker]] were mostly caught in gill nets in about six-foot water near mid-stream. Sizes were 7 to 16 inches, apart from one 4-inch fish.
- [[Stoneroller]] occurred only in Sections 1 and 2, at the lower end of riffles, and was very susceptible to seining.
- [[Fathead Minnow]] favored deeper water and cover such as loose rock, algae and brush below the crossing.
- [[Creek Chub]] was found in nearly all sections except Section 3, in diverse habitats, with larger fish (to 8¼ inches) taken by pole and line.
- [[Topeka Shiner]] appeared only in Sections 3 and 4 and was never found below the crossing. It was the most abundant minnow in Section 4.
- [[Common Shiner]] was taken mainly in about three feet of water just above the rock ledge of the crossing, and was absent below it.
- [[Black Bullhead]] occurred only in Sections 4 to 6, near the bottom, and was the only sport-sized panfish present.
- [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] was found in Sections 1 to 4 in water under four feet deep, in or near cover, and no specimen exceeded four inches.
- [[Johnny Darter]] and [[Orangethroated Darter]] moved onto riffles in late March, possibly linked to spawning. Johnny Darters later returned to pool eddies, whereas Orangethroated Darters stayed on riffles.

**Seasonal movement and temperature.** [[Water Temperature]] and season strongly shaped activity (Table 3). Surface temperatures rose from 37°F on February 21 to 64°F on May 3. Minnows first appeared in shallows around 43°F. [[Red Shiner]] moved onto riffles at about 41°F. Topeka Shiner, Bluntnose Minnow and Red Shiner reached Section 3 when temperatures hit about 59°F (April 24). The author inferred that minnows wintered in the deepest pools and moved outward to shallow edges, then riffles, as the water warmed. [[Bluntnose Minnow]] did not appear in seine catches until the last third of the study.

**Anthropogenic pressures.** Cattle access and manure in Section 1, bait-hunters who removed larger Red Shiner and Stoneroller from Sections 1 and 2, and swimming activity at Section 6 are all noted.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness and abundance:** 13 species and 875 specimens. Top species: [[Red Shiner]] (242; 27.6%), [[White Sucker]] (182; 20.8%), [[Creek Chub]] (174; 19.9%), [[Topeka Shiner]] (61; 7.0%) and [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] (76; 8.7%).
- **Adjacent records:** Three further species were taken within one mile of the study area: Suckermouthed Minnow and Longear Sunfish upstream, and Redfin Shiner downstream.
- **Water quality:** Neutral to slightly alkaline (pH 8.2), well-oxygenated (9.0 to 11.35 ppm), with moderate turbidity (25 to 27 ppm) and methyl orange alkalinity of 129.6 ppm in Section 2.
- **Gear efficiency:** The common-sense seine caught most minnows and small sunfish. The gill net was the main method for suckers and bullheads, and pole and line was the only effective method for larger Creek Chub.
- **Seasonal ecology:** Fish activity and distribution followed rising water temperature. Movement from deep pools to riffles and then to upstream sections occurred between 41°F and 59°F.
- **Sport fishing:** About 89.25 hours of pole and line effort yielded 26 fish (0.29 fish per hour, roughly 0.07 lb per hour at ¼ lb per fish). Minnows were the best bait, taking 13 bullheads. Artificial lures (29 hours) produced no strikes, and set lines (about 100 hours) took no fish.
- **Fishery condition:** Local anglers reported that the stream once yielded large bullheads and sunfish. Largemouth bass, channel catfish and crappie had reportedly been stocked into Sections 4 to 6 in recent years, but none were captured, suggesting they did not survive. The author concluded that no fish of worthwhile angling size were present.
