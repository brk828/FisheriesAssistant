# Minnesota Fish Management Policy (1956)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #fisheries-policy

## Executive Summary

This document is the 1956 "Policy Pertaining to Management of Minnesota Waters for Fish and Fishing," written by James W. Kimball, Director of the [[Division Of Game And Fish]]. It sets out the Department's goals, administrative structure, and species-specific rules for managing the state's lakes and streams. The stated primary goal is "the production of the greatest number of satisfactory sport-fishing hours," or "the most fishing for the most people." The policy was prepared by Department fisheries workers and reviewed by experts at the [[University Of Minnesota]] and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]]. It replaces a 1949 policy and is approved by Kimball and George A. Selke, Commissioner of the Department of Conservation, dated April 27, 1956.

## Background & Description

Kimball's introductory article frames fisheries as a public resource. Minnesotans jointly own more than four million acres of surface water. Fishing is about a $100 million industry in the state, and more than one million Minnesotans fish year-round. Fishing also supports the state's vacation and resort industry and supplies roughly 40 to 50 million pounds of protein food per year.

The policy describes 75 years of management history. In the first 25 years, emphasis was on stocking fry, often of non-native species. Brown and rainbow trout were introduced, while [[Carp]] took over many waters to the detriment of game fish. Several salmon species failed to survive. Between 1900 and 1925, stocking of native fishes such as [[Walleyed Pike]], [[Whitefish]], basses, and sunfishes expanded. Experience showed that indiscriminate annual planting often failed, which led to the first biological surveys of Minnesota lakes and streams.

The policy is organized into a general introduction, the basis of modern management, administrative policies (sections IV A–L), and species-specific policies (section V A–L). Its structure is:

- **Administration:** Bureau of Fisheries (operations) and Bureau of Research and Planning (research, surveys, and inventories), organized into District Fisheries Supervisor districts.
- **Habitat and infrastructure:** habitat improvement, dams and fish ladders, farm ponds, and bait minnow production.
- **Species policies:** trout, walleyed pike, northern pike, muskellunge, largemouth and smallmouth bass, panfishes, catfish, whitefish and tullibee, bait and forage fishes, white bass, and grayling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The policy treats a lake's fish community as an interdependent system, not a set of separate crops. For example, young walleyes feed on young perch, while [[Northern Pike]] keep adult perch from becoming overly abundant. The document compares a lake to a forest with many species of different ages rather than a cornfield harvested annually. It holds that intensive "farming" of lakes is practical only in special cases, and that in most lakes the existing fish and habitat must be managed for the best possible fishing.

Habitat is central to the policy. Its environmental measures include:

- pollution abatement and soil erosion control;
- control and adjustment of water levels;
- protection and development of natural spawning areas;
- shoreline fencing, tree planting, and control of aquatic vegetation and nuisance algae;
- caution about dams, which can change fish communities upstream and block migration.

The policy notes that the line between game and rough fish is not sharp. Bullheads, suckers, perch, and sheepshead are valued sport fish in southern Minnesota but are little taken in the north. Some rough fish, such as perch, are valuable forage when young. Where a lake cannot become a game-fish lake, rough fish such as bullheads can be harvested by netting as an "unused fish crop."

Stocking is limited by criteria, and no fish may be stocked in any water not open to the public for fishing, except federal fish for private ponds not connected with public waters.

## Key Findings

**Primary goal and management philosophy**
- Each lake and stream is to be managed for the sport fish best suited to it. Methods must fit local conditions, and the local fish manager has latitude to interpret the policy.
- Laws and regulations should be adjusted to fish abundance so that both a good harvest and healthy stocks are maintained.

**Stocking criteria (seven situations)**
- Newly created or restored waters; lakes subject to periodic winter kill; introducing or re-establishing species; adjusting predator-prey balances; supplying catchable trout to designated streams; supplying panfish for metropolitan put-and-take fishing, especially for children (no fish stocked for contests); and stocking predaceous fingerlings where spawning habitat is lacking.

**Rescue and transfer rules**
- Priority goes to northern pike, walleyed pike, bass, and trout; panfishes get lower priority.
- Fish with serious diseases or parasites may not be transferred. Fish are not moved from unsatisfactory waters to equally unsuitable ones. Panfish are not transferred to waters where the species is already crowded or stunted.
- Green sunfish, orange-spotted sunfish, and white bass are culled from rescue shipments.

**Administrative and facility policy**
- Fish managers make the final stocking decision and must account for all public uses. Experimental waters follow research plans. Accurate records must be kept.
- Commercial fishing in boundary and interstate waters aims for the largest sustained yield compatible with natural population maintenance. Red Lake commercial fishing on the reservation is governed by agreement among the state, the U.S. Indian Bureau, and the Red Lake Fishermen's Association.

**Habitat, dams, and exotics**
- Habitat improvement is emphasized. Aquatic nuisance control is permitted only with Department approval, and the Department accepts no financial or liability responsibility for it. No stream work begins without property owner easements for public access.
- Farm fish ponds are judged "not worth while" in Minnesota because of winter kill and the accessibility of natural waters, so no organized pond program is undertaken. Ponds are better used to raise bait fish.
- [[Fish Ladders]] have been ineffective for game fish and are used mostly by rough fish.
- Introducing exotic fishes is considered dangerous and is allowed only after careful study.

**Species-specific policies**
- **Trout:** Stocking is restricted to suitable cold-water lakes and designated streams. Yearlings are usually stocked in spring. Southeastern streams receive mainly brown and rainbow trout. No trout are stocked below the lowermost falls on North Shore streams. Lake trout are stocked only in larger cold-water lakes.
- **Walleyed pike:** Maintaining habitat, enforcing regulations, and balancing predator-prey relations are the key tools. Stocking is usually unnecessary, and sites must avoid harm to bass and lake trout. State rearing ponds are authorized to raise walleyes from fry to fingerlings.
- **Northern pike:** The policy stresses protection of spawning areas, which are mostly temporary spring sloughs. Reproduction may decline in dry years. Heavy fishing pressure (angling, trolling, and spearing from darkhouses) requires close monitoring and curtailment of the take when needed.
- **Muskellunge:** Only a few Minnesota waters suit this species because of competition with northern pike. Suitable lakes, especially those with few or no northern pike, will be selected and stocked.
- **Largemouth black bass:** The species is underused. The policy supports an earlier opening date than current law allows, protects spawning areas, and limits stocking to waters where natural reproduction is inadequate.
- **Smallmouth black bass:** Fish are reared in ponds and stocked where surveys show inadequate natural reproduction.
- **Panfishes:** These usually need only natural conditions. No panfish are stocked except in new or restored waters, children's ponds, or low-reproduction lakes. Overabundant stunted populations may be thinned.
- **Channel catfish:** Occasionally planted in southern and southwestern lakes with inadequate natural reproduction. No stocking in streams unless confined between barriers.
- **Whitefish and tullibee:** Whitefish may be reared experimentally. No tullibee are raised or stocked except for experimental projects.
- **Bait and forage fishes:** Propagated when needed as food for predators such as muskellunge in ponds.
- **White bass:** Considered an unsatisfactory game fish. No white bass are reared or transplanted to waters where the species is not present.
- **Grayling:** Propagated and stocked to a limited extent in small landlocked reclaimed lakes, if experimental trials prove successful.
