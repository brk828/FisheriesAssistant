# Crayfish Impacts on Wetland Herpetofauna in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This final report (IIPAM Project No. 194054, dated 31 December 1996) by [[Philip J. Fernandez]] and [[Philip C. Rosen]] for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Heritage Program documents the effects of the introduced crayfish [[Orconectes virilis]] on native aquatic herpetofauna and stream ecosystems in Arizona. The report comprises four parts plus an appendix: a field study at Three Forks (White Mountains), a microcosm experiment, a study of Sonoran mud turtles at Sycamore Creek, and a survey of narrow-headed garter snakes in the Black River system. The authors conclude that crayfish reduce aquatic vegetation and invertebrates, prey on native vertebrates, and are a significant threat to Arizona stream ecosystems. The document also includes a letter (January 28, 1998) from Fernandez to a colleague seeking help confirming crayfish identifications and an invoice for report work.

## Background & Description

The report addresses the global amphibian decline and the local decline of leopard frogs in the White Mountains. Two leopard frog species were historically widespread there: [[Northern Leopard Frog]] (*Rana pipiens*), which no longer occurs in the White Mountains, and [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] (*Rana chiricahuensis*), currently known from a healthy population at only one site (Three Forks).

No crayfish are native to Arizona. Two introduced species are documented: *Orconectes virilis* and [[Procambarus Clarkii]] (red swamp crayfish). Crayfish are decapod crustaceans; the report cites 338 recognized taxa occurring on every continent except Africa and Antarctica. In native ranges, crayfish can supply up to two-thirds of the diet of centrarchid fishes (Roell and Orth, 1993), but introductions as bait or for aquaculture have had negative impacts (Holdich, 1987).

Study organisms and sites:
- [[Three Forks]] study site: 8 miles west of Alpine on Forest Route 249, elevation 2506 m (8220 ft), Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest.
- [[Sycamore Creek]] at Sunflower, Maricopa County: a Verde River tributary and site of a [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] population.
- Black River system (East Fork, Apache and Greenlee counties) and Eagle Creek/Blue River: sites for [[Narrow-Headed Garter Snake]] surveys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Crayfish in Arizona streams were linked to several cascading effects:
- Removal of aquatic plants (watercress, *Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*, and water buttercup, *Ranunculus aquatilis*), which provide autochthonous production, stabilize sediments, and offer cover for frogs, turtles and snakes.
- Burrowing into banks, causing erosion, increased turbidity and siltation of gravel beds needed by native fishes for egg development.
- Shifts from living vegetation to woody debris and detritus as the dominant organic matter.
- Reduced native invertebrate biomass (caddisflies, snails, mussels) with crayfish-dominated benthic samples.

Competitors and predators: the authors note that [[Brown Trout]] and other sport fish may have a limited role in regulating crayfish, but that native predators at the study sites could not consume medium to large crayfish. The Arizona native fauna may lack the evolutionary experience to coexist with crayfish.

## Key Findings

**Part I – Three Forks (Chiricahua leopard frog)**
- Ten-year census (1986–1996, except 1989) recorded frog counts ranging from 0 to 13 per survey year, with egg masses ranging from 0 to 13 in some years; in 1992 91 frogs were counted and in 1995 38 and 1996 95 (Table 1 values as reported).
- Crayfish were first observed in 1993 (4 individuals, at site 15); by 1995 they were visibly abundant at sites 7–15, and frogs were largely absent from the upper reach (only 1 frog between sites 10–15 in 1995, 2 in 1996).
- Benthic (Hess) samples: invertebrate count and mass were significantly lower at crayfish sites (mean 0.5 g vs. 3.63 g when crayfish mass was excluded). Caddisflies (Trichoptera), snails (*Physa* spp.) and the mussel *Anodonta californiensis* showed significant declines.
- Minnow traps: native invertebrates averaged 3.8/trap at non-crayfish sites versus 0.25/trap at crayfish sites (t = 3.04, p < 0.01); fish differences were not significant (5.8 vs. 2.1 fish/trap).

**Part II – Microcosm study**
- Ten glass aquaria, five with three crayfish each; 13-day trials.
- Crayfish uprooted and consumed watercress and Ranunculus; Ranunculus was shredded into detritus. Snails declined and *Physa* eggs and aquatic insects were eliminated.
- Crayfish killed and ate newly hatched embryos, tadpoles, a metamorph and an adult frog; a juvenile Sonoran mud turtle and a newborn narrow-headed garter snake were killed and eaten. Vertebrates never harmed a crayfish.
- Authors describe crayfish as a "two-pronged assault": loss of macrophytes and invertebrates, plus direct predation on vertebrates.

**Part III – Sonoran mud turtles at Sycamore Creek**
- Crayfish invaded between 1985/1986 and September 1986, exploded in 1987, and remained highly abundant through 1995.
- The 1995 turtle sample contained only individuals recruited before 1987; recruitment appears to have collapsed after the crayfish explosion.
- Crayfish density in 1995 was estimated at ~4.5 × 10^5 crayfish/ha in pool habitat (~13.6 per sample average); crayfish biomass was ~517 kg/ha.
- Native invertebrate abundance declined and snails were absent from Hess samples in 1995.
- The authors suspect predation on hatchlings and small juveniles is the primary cause of recruitment failure.

**Part IV – Narrow-headed garter snake survey (Black River, August 1995)**
- Four sites sampled on 2–3 August 1995; narrow-headed garter snakes observed at two sites (two individuals each), co-occurring with crayfish.
- The densest crayfish population (Buffalo Crossing) lacked garter snakes, and fishes and invertebrates were at unusually low frequency and diversity there.
- The authors conclude short-term coexistence is possible but longer-term effects are unknown.

**Appendix I – Status of narrow-headed garter snakes (Rosen, 1987)**
- 107 garter snakes recorded during 13–22 July 1987 surveys across Eagle Creek and Blue River.
- Snakes were absent or scarce on upper Eagle Creek; abundance correlated with streamflow and seepwillow cover, and the author raised crayfish as a possible factor.

**Recommendations (Section 4)**
- Research: monitor crayfish spread and effects; sample Sonoran mud turtles at Sycamore Creek; integrate crayfish effects into status surveys of narrow-headed garter snakes; establish a tracking mechanism for introduced species.
- Management: recognize *O. virilis* as a nuisance; no take restrictions; substantial penalties for introduction; possession and live import banned (research exempted); public awareness; control research; eradication where habitat for threatened species can be salvaged (poisoning, trapping, native predators such as river otters, barriers).
