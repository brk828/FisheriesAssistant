# Cytotaxonomy of Acroloxus lacustris: The European Lake Limpet

#malacology #freshwater-biology #acroloxidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by J. B. Burch is the first in a series of cytotaxonomic studies on freshwater limpets (Gastropoda: Basommatophora). It examines the karyotype and spermatogenesis of the European [[Lake Limpet]], *Acroloxus lacustris*, a species long placed in the family [[Ancylidae]]. Burch argues that cytological evidence, supported by earlier egg-capsule and anatomical work, justifies separating *Acroloxus* into its own family, [[Acroloxidae]]. The main evidence is a haploid chromosome number of n=18, which differs from other freshwater limpets, together with large chromosomes, a distinctive karyotype, and highly modified thread-like sperm. The paper also discusses the phylogenetic placement of Acroloxidae and Ancylidae within the Basommatophora and the wider Euthyneura.

## Background & Description

*Acroloxus lacustris* (Linnaeus) is a freshwater limpet found in Europe, northern Asia and the Caucasus. It is often called the "lake limpet" because it prefers lentic (still-water) habitats. The other common freshwater limpet in the same region, [[Ancylus fluviatilis]], lives in rivers.

Freshwater limpet-like mollusks were historically grouped in the single family Ancylidae. Several genera were later moved elsewhere:

- *Latia* (New Zealand) was assigned to Latiidae by Hutton (1882), though this was overlooked for decades.
- *Lanx* was reassigned to the Lymnaeidae by Gwatkin (1914), Pilsbry (1925) and Baker (1925).
- *Protancylus* was placed in the Planorbidae by Pilsbry and Bequaert (1927).
- *Acroloxus* was separated by Bondesen (1950) on the basis of its egg-capsule morphology. Burch (1961b), Zilch (1959) and Hubendick (1962) also treated it as a distinct family.

Burch notes that the anatomy of most freshwater limpets is still poorly known, and that chromosome studies of these groups were rare, with only six prior publications.

### Materials and Methods

- **Specimens:** Ovotestes from 3 specimens taken along the River Thames at Sonning (near Reading) on 13 April 1959, and from 7 specimens collected near East Bergholt, Suffolk, on 26 June 1959. Specimens were supplied by [[Dorothea Franzen]] and Jack Hayworth.
- **Fixation and staining:** [[Newcomer's Fluid]] fixation, acetic-orcein squash for chromosomes, and haematoxylin and eosin for histology (15 µm paraffin sections).
- **Voucher:** Duplicate shells from the Thames specimens are deposited in the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ cat. no. 207600).
- **Microscopy:** [[Oil Immersion Microscopy]] with photographs and camera lucida drawings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses habitat only briefly. *Acroloxus lacustris* is associated with lentic environments, in contrast to the riverine *Ancylus fluviatilis*. The study material came from a river (Thames at Sonning) and from localities in Suffolk, England.

Burch places the cytology of freshwater limpets within a broader framework of chromosome evolution in the Euthyneura:

- Haploid numbers in marine opisthobranchs are all below 18.
- The basic haploid number (x) for freshwater Basommatophora is 18.
- Nearly all land-dwelling [[Stylommatophora]] have haploid numbers above 18.
- Chromosome numbers increase gradually across related taxa, which suggests change by aneuploidy (addition of chromosomes) rather than polyploidy.
- Polyploidy is rare and usually affects species or genera rather than larger groups.

## Key Findings

**1. Chromosome number separates Acroloxus from other freshwater limpets**
- *A. lacustris* has n=18 (2n=36). This is characteristic of the Basommatophora in general, but differs from other freshwater limpets.
- The Ancylinae and Ferrissiinae have a basic number of x=15 (n=15, 30, 60).
- The Laevapecinae have n=17.
- Burch treats this divergent chromosome number as an additional cytological character supporting family-level separation.

**2. Karyotype features**
- Chromosomes are metacentric, as in all Basommatophora.
- Six pairs are medianly constricted: the two largest and the smallest.
- The remaining 12 pairs are submedianly or subterminally constricted.
- One pair has secondary constrictions (the 10th pair).
- This is described as the first accurate determination and illustration of the karyotype of any Euthyneuran snail.

**3. Chromosome size**
- Normally contracted spermatogonial metaphase chromosomes range from 7.0 µm (largest) to 3.5 µm (smallest).
- Other freshwater basommatophorans have been reported at 3.7 µm (largest) and 0.7 µm (smallest).
- Burch estimates *Acroloxus* chromosomes are roughly twice the size of those in other species of the order. No quantitative measurements were made for the chromatin-to-cytoplasm ratio.
- Meiotic chromosomes are also much larger than those of any other basommatophoran studied.

**4. Meiosis**
- Eighteen bivalents were observed at diakinesis and metaphase I.
- Pairing appeared normal, with one or more chiasmata per bivalent.
- The meiotic cycle resembles that of other basommatophorans.

**5. Sperm morphology**
- Sperm of *A. lacustris* have a very long, thread-like nucleus, forming about one quarter of the spermatozoon's length. The acrosome is barely distinguishable from the nucleus.
- All other Basommatophora have bullet- or turnip-shaped sperm heads.
- Using Franzén's (1956) classification, the sperm of *Acroloxus* are "highly modified." Primitive sperm types are not found in Euthyneura.
- Burch notes that the modified sperm of Basommatophora and some opisthobranchs appear to have evolved independently, or that Euthyneuran phylogeny is not yet settled.

**6. Phylogenetic conclusions**
- Acroloxidae may lie close to the base of the Basommatophora, as suggested by Bondesen and Hubendick. Cytology cannot yet confirm this.
- The Ancylidae, based on their chromosome numbers, should also lie near the base of the Basommatophora, though not close to Acroloxidae given the other cytological differences.
- Hubendick's (1962) proposal that *Acroloxus* shares an origin with *Latia* is disputed. Burch finds the radulae of the two genera very different and cites Pelseneer's (1901) anatomical differences.
- Burch suggests that the reevaluation of Euthyneuran phylogeny needs more detailed morphological data.

**7. Taxonomic summary**
- The Ancylidae as currently understood are divided into four subfamilies: Ancylastruminae, Ancylinae, Ferrissiinae and Laevapecinae.
- Burch notes that the divergent chromosome number of the Laevapecinae could suggest their removal from the Ancylidae if anatomy supports it.
- Burnupia (South Africa) is added to the Laevapecinae on the basis of male reproductive structures, dorsal muscle scars and chromosome number (n=17).
