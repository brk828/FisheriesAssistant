# Prevalence of Male Brood Care in Teleosts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei #parental-care #evolutionary-biology

## Executive Summary
This 1978 *Nature* correspondence by Loiselle critically re-examines the [[Dawkins-Carlisle Model]] of male brood care in teleost fishes, which proposed that paternal care arises from females spawning first and deserting males into a "cruel bind" (per [[Trivers' Parental Investment Theory]]). Using comparative data across 38 teleost families, Loiselle demonstrates that the majority of parental teleosts exhibit **simultaneous gamete release** (60.5%), not sequential spawning, undermining the desertion-based explanation. Paternal care is significantly overrepresented among these simultaneous spawners (16 of 23 families), contradicting the null hypothesis of random care allocation. The author proposes an alternative model centered on **spawning site limitation** and **differential gamete production costs** between sexes as the primary drivers of male brood care prevalence. [[Richard Dawkins]] responds, conceding that the comparative evidence does not support the teleost-specific portion of the original 1976 paper, while noting the paper's central purpose (introducing the "Concorde fallacy") remains valid.

## Background & Description
This paper is theoretical/behavioral rather than morphological in focus; no anatomical data on specific species is presented. However, key biological traits relevant to the argument include:

- **Gamete energetics**: Significant asymmetry in unit-cost of production between ova and spermatozoa, cited as a key driver of differential parental investment strategies (Bateman 1948; Rohwer 1978).
- **Spawning mode**: Demersal (bottom-adhering) eggs are specifically implicated as requiring site defense, in contrast to pelagic/broadcast spawning strategies.
- **Reproductive timing**: Species are categorized by degree of temporal separation between oviposition and ejaculation:
  - Simultaneous gamete expulsion (60.5% of families surveyed)
  - Sequential spawning with female-first (e.g., [[Maternal Mouthbrooding Cichlids]])
  - Sequential spawning with male-first (e.g., *Sarotherodon macrochir*, *Bathygobius soporator*)

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Spawning site limitation**: A central ecological constraint proposed as driving male territorial persistence — suitable demersal spawning substrates are a limiting resource, making site defense ("sequestration") advantageous.
- **Predation pressure**: Nest/egg predation is highlighted as a selective force favoring continued parental guarding, since guarded broods show higher survivorship than unguarded ones.
- **Cave-spawning cichlids**: Referenced as an ecological guild exhibiting a full spectrum of care strategies (biparental to fully maternal), despite consistent sequential spawning patterns — used as a counter-example to the desertion hypothesis.
- **Mouthbrooding cichlids**: An extreme ecological strategy where females commit to full parental investment (mouthbrooding) even before eggs are fertilized, representing the most sequential spawning pattern known among teleosts.
- **Marine goby example**: *Bathygobius soporator* retains paternal custodial care despite male-first spawning sequence, demonstrating that spawning order alone does not dictate care-giving sex.

## Key Findings
- **Statistical test of desertion hypothesis**: Among 38 families with known spawning behavior, 23 (60.5%) show simultaneous gamete release. If desertion theory were correct, care should be randomly distributed among paternal/maternal/biparental categories in these cases — but paternal care occurs in 16/23 families, a highly nonrandom result (χ² = 19.00, d.f. = 2, *P* < 0.005).
- **Counter-examples to sequential spawning predictions**:
  - Female-first spawners (cave-spawning and mouthbrooding cichlids) do not consistently show paternal care as predicted.
  - Male-first spawners (*S. macrochir*, *B. soporator*) do not show a shift toward maternal care as predicted.
- **Alternative model proposed** (Loiselle): Two-factor framework replacing the desertion/"cruel bind" hypothesis:
  1. **Limited spawning site availability** — favors territorial sequestration behavior, particularly for demersal egg-layers.
  2. **Differential energetic cost of ova vs. sperm production** — makes site-guarding more reproductively profitable for males than females.
- **Evolutionary trajectory proposed**: Post-spawning guarding behavior is an extension of pre-existing territorial/site-defense behavior (no new motor patterns needed) → can evolve into complex brood care (per [[Barlow's Schema]], 1972) → biparental care emerges as derived state under extreme site scarcity or heavy predation → maternal care can be secondarily derived from biparental precursors via the Dawkins-Carlisle parental investment framework.
- **Author's conclusion**: The prevalence of paternal care in teleosts is better explained by **resource limitation and reproductive bioenergetics** than by the mechanics/sequence of external fertilization.
- **Dawkins' reply**: Concedes the teleost-specific argument is not well supported by comparative data, citing a corroborating independent review (Ridley 1978), but maintains that the broader theoretical contribution of the original paper — the "[[Concorde Fallacy]]" (sunk-cost reasoning in parental investment) — remains unaffected by this critique.
