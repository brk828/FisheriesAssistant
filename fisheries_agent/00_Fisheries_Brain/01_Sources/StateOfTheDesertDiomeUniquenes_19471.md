# State of the Desert Biome: Uniqueness, Biodiversity, Threats and the Adequacy of Protection in the Sonoran Bioregion

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 assessment, sponsored by [[The Wildlands Project]] and authored by Gary Paul Nabhan and Andrew R. Holdsworth, characterizes the biodiversity of the [[Sonoran Desert]] bioregion and evaluates threats and the adequacy of current protection. It compiles surveys of 54 field scientists who average twenty years of field experience in the region. The report identifies the bioregion's high endemism, particularly on the Gulf of California islands and the Baja California peninsula, and documents threats driven by [[In-Migration]], urbanization, water impoundment and diversion, [[Groundwater Overdraft]], livestock grazing and invasive species. It concludes that protection has expanded but that most threats are not adequately addressed.

## Background & Description

The Sonoran bioregion takes its name from the Mexican state of Sonora but spans parts of Arizona and California, USA, and Chihuahua, Baja California Norte and Baja California Sur, Mexico. It covers roughly 310,000 to 330,000 square kilometers of terrestrial habitat, plus riparian, coastal, wetland and oceanic communities. The authors describe it as "the most tropical of the North American deserts," with a bimodal rainfall regime that supports greater structural diversity than neighboring deserts.

The bioregion is delineated after [[Dice]] (1943) and [[Dasmann]] (1974), and nine of the twenty-seven biotic communities on the Brown and Lowe map are analyzed. Survey responses were gathered from over 120 invited field biologists and protected area managers, with 54 responding in whole or part. Responses were tallied for 33 scientists on the threat ranking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Endemism and isolation.** The [[Gulf of California]] and its 21 islands, together with peninsular Baja California, host high levels of endemism. The Baja peninsula is reported at 25% endemic plant species, and 501 of 552 endemic peninsular plant species occur in desertscrub and Cape thornscrub. Gulf islands host 12 endemic plant species, 65 mammal subspecies and more than 30 reptile species or subspecies.

**Freshwater fishes.** Native freshwater fish diversity is described as "rather low" for the region, at perhaps 25 to 30 species. The report stresses that the eleven endemic fishes of springs and creeks, including [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Yaqui Sucker]], [[Sonora Chub]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], show that water is a limiting factor for evolution. At least 250 marine rocky-shore and reef fish species occur in the northern and central Gulf.

**Other taxa.** Amphibians number about 20 species, with 11 endemic to the mainland and none endemic to the islands or peninsula. Reptile diversity is high, with as many as 96 endemic taxa across the Gulf islands, Sonora and Baja California. Mammals total perhaps 130 species, and the San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area holds 86 species, 12 of them at risk. Native bee richness near Tucson is estimated at about 1,200 species.

**Birds and riparian habitat.** At least 500 bird species are reported from the bioregion. Riparian gallery forests may hold 304 to 847 breeding pairs per 40 hectares. Of 82 breeding bird species formerly using riparian woodlands, 36 have declined regionally.

**Cultural diversity.** At least twenty indigenous nations have long tenure in the region, and indigenous communities manage as much as 17.8 million hectares across the ten border states.

## Key Findings

- **Top threats (ranked by 33 scientists):** (1) urbanization and habitat fragmentation; (2) in-migration; (3) water impoundment and diversion; (4) livestock grazing combined with exotic pasture grasses; (5) aquifer mining and salinization. Other threats include lack of growth planning, exotic grass planting, farm conversion, recreation and biological invasions.
- **Population:** the region's population nearly doubled (+98%) between 1970 and 1990, reaching 6.9 million. Between 1940 and 1990, the three states shifted from one-half to two-thirds rural to over three-quarters urban. Maricopa County is projected to reach nearly 2.5 times its 1995 population by 2045.
- **Dams:** 41 major dams were built since 1908 (17 in Arizona, 21 in Sonora, 1 in Baja California, 2 in Baja California Sur).
- **Groundwater:** in 18% of 56 basins, groundwater declined by more than 1 meter per year. Arizona pumping peaked in 1974 at 5.7 million acre-feet and fell to 3.2 million acre-feet in 1990.
- **Colorado River delta:** its wetland area has fluctuated between 5,800 and 63,000 hectares since 1973. The delta supports the world's largest populations of Yuma clapper rails and desert pupfish.
- **Grazing:** stocking rates of 2 to 5 times recommended levels occur regularly on the Sonoran side of the border.
- **Buffelgrass:** "grasslandification" has affected some 600,000 hectares of Sonora. Plant richness reportedly drops fourfold and diversity tenfold in buffelgrass plots.
- **Invasive species:** at least 146 naturalized non-native plant species are documented. Total exotics competing with natives are estimated at over 200 species, including 45 to 50 fish species. Each mature [[Tamarisk]] consumes up to 800 liters of water per day.
- **Protection:** about 1,500,000 hectares (roughly 8% of Sonora's land surface) is under some protection. The Pinacate Biosphere Reserve, Organ Pipe National Monument and Barry M. Goldwater Air Force Range form the largest contiguous unfragmented protected area in the lower 48 states or Mexico.
- **Management survey:** 25 respondents said protected-area managers still allow harmful activities, 9 said no, and 17 said such activities now occur less than before. Twenty-two scientists felt no threat is being adequately addressed.
- **Priority issues for the future:** (1) urban planning and agricultural restoration to maintain wildlife corridors; (2) guaranteed river flow into Gulf lagoons and estuaries, including the Colorado River delta; (3) shifting reserve management from recreation or single species toward whole-habitat biodiversity; (4) planning to reduce coastal and island development impacts in the Gulf, where endemism is highest.
- **Sonoran pronghorn** numbered only 350 in 1993. Of 59 desert bighorn populations in Arizona, only 7 have more than 100 individuals, and in California only 11 of 77 do.
- **Research gaps:** nocturnal fauna, summer-flowering herbs, and marine and island biodiversity are poorly studied relative to their significance.
