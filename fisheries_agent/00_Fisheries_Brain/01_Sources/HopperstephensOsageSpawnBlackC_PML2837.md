# Black Carp Spawning and Zebra Mussel Biocontrol

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Several Arkansas and Missouri fish farms attempted to spawn [[Black Carp]] in 1992 (the year implied by the source's context) to produce sterile [[Triploid Fish]] as a potential biological control for the [[Zebra Mussel]]. [[Hopper-Stephens Hatcheries]] reported success with diploid spawning and a production of 300,000 to 400,000 fry from about 35-pound broodfish. Other farms were unsuccessful or still working toward triploid production. The article also reports the marketing plans for black carp as a foodfish, and a federal fisheries review recommending native predatory fish for zebra mussel control. Industry members agreed that the spawning would prompt research into black carp for zebra mussel control.

## Background & Description

- **Origin and broodstock:** The broodfish were believed to be about 5 years old and were reportedly brought from Europe seven years earlier. Hopper-Stephens produced fry from these 35-pound fish.
- **Participating farms:**
  - [[Hopper-Stephens Hatcheries]] (Arkansas; co-owners Bob Hopper and Mark Stephens)
  - [[Osage Catfisheries]] (Osage Beach, Missouri; Jim Kahrs)
  - [[Keo Fish Farm]] (Keo, Arkansas; Mike Freeze), whose spawning attempts were unsuccessful this season
  - [[J.M. Malone & Son Enterprises]] (Lonoke, Arkansas; Jim Malone), whose spawning attempts were also unsuccessful
- **Triploid production method:** Hormones are given to brood stock to induce spawning. Eggs are then heat-shocked at 28° after fertilization. Twenty-four hours later, the fry are tested to determine the percentages of diploids and triploids. Blood tests on individual fish then identify which are able to reproduce and which are sterile. A final sort isolates the triploids.
- **Zebra mussel background:** The zebra mussel is a bivalve native to Europe, first discovered in [[Lake St. Clair]] in 1988. It attaches to water intake pipes and vessel hulls, choking water flow in industrial and municipal systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spread of zebra mussels:** Zebra mussels have spread through the [[Great Lakes]], the [[Mississippi River]] from Minneapolis to New Orleans, the Erie Canal to the Hudson River, and the Ohio, Arkansas, Cumberland, and Susquehanna rivers. Officials said the widespread midwestern flooding in the summer of the report would likely accelerate their spread.
- **Biological control candidates:** A fisheries biologist (John N. French III, National Fisheries Research Center–Great Lakes) identified six molluscivorous freshwater fishes with pharyngeal teeth and chewing pads suited to eating zebra mussels. These were freshwater drum, copper and river redhorses, [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and [[Common Carp]]. Known natural predators also include certain waterfowl, fish, leeches, and crayfish.
- **Warning on introductions:** The article recommended discouraging purposeful introduction of [[Black Carp]] and roach in North America because introduced biological controllers often spread beyond their intended habitats.
- **Predicted ecosystem effects:** Russian scientists predicted in 1992 that fish biomass in North American waters would likely decline after zebra mussels became established. Mussels would deplete [[Phytoplankton]], an important food source for [[Zooplankton]] that feed fish larvae. Agencies were urged to maintain adequate populations of native molluscivorous fish to suppress zebra mussel explosions, without threatening important sport fish.
- **Predation seasonality:** Waterfowl, especially diving ducks, prey heavily on zebra mussels in Europe and the Great Lakes in fall and winter. Fish predation should peak in summer. In open, ice-free waters, combined fish and waterfowl predation could have a cumulative controlling effect.

## Key Findings

- **Successful spawning:** Hopper-Stephens reported high fertility percentages of diploid black carp eggs and very good pond survival. Mark Stephens estimated a spawn of 300,000 to 400,000 fry from 35-pound broodfish.
- **Partial success elsewhere:** Osage Catfisheries reported spawning black carp and was working to produce triploids through the end of July, with a target date for triploids "sometime this next week." Keo Fish Farm and J.M. Malone & Son Enterprises were unsuccessful in their spawning attempts this season.
- **Research need:** All four farms agreed that the successful spawning would spur needed research into using black carp for zebra mussel control. Freeze said the aquaculture industry had met an important goal by being able to spawn black carp.
- **Conservation conditions:** Before a market can be developed, farmers must convince conservation officials that black carp would be less harmful to other aquatic life and vegetation than zebra mussels. Farmers also agreed to introduce only specially bred, non-reproducing black carp into the environment.
- **Marketing outlook:** Kahrs noted that black carp are an important foodfish in China, where they command the highest prices of all food fish. He said diploids might become popular in U.S. seafood stores. Freeze estimated black carp would sell for roughly the price of [[Grass Carp]] (the price is unclear in the scan, possibly $1.15 per pound), and that the [[Hybrid Striped Bass]] industry had expressed interest in diploids for [[Yellow Grub]] control, since snails are part of that parasite's life cycle.
- **Standards debate:** The four Arkansas hatchery owners informally agreed that black carp should be marketed only as sterile triploids, following the grass carp model. Malone favored an industry-wide standard that only 100 percent triploids be sold. Freeze said the federal, state, and local governments should require a triploid standard, and Kahrs said that triploid technology can produce 100 percent triploids.
- **Restriction on sales:** Malone said that triploid black carp could be established in U.S. aquaculture if research proves they can be established in public lakes and streams to keep zebra mussels in check.
