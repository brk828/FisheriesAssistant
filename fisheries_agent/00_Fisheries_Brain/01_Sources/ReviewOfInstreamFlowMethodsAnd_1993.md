# Review of Instream Flow Methods and Recommendations for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #instream-flow #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This FY93 report (Colorado River Recovery Program, Project #9; PI: Dr. Jack A. Stanford, Flathead Lake Biological Station, University of Montana) is a **policy and methods synthesis**, not a primary morphological or field survey study. Its purpose was to convene an expert panel to critically review existing **instream flow methodologies** used to derive flow recommendations for four [[Endangered Fishes Of The Upper Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. The panel concluded that no mechanistic or deterministic flow-biomass model is currently adequate as a sole predictive tool for managing these species' recovery, and instead recommended a **prudent alternative**: an ecologically-informed interim flow regime supported by adaptive management and expanded monitoring. The final synthesis report (Stanford 1993, 89 pp. + appendices) was formally approved by the Instream Flow Subcommittee and Biology Committee of the Recovery Program.

## Background & Description

No morphological, anatomical, or physiological data on the target species are presented in this document. The report is restricted to programmatic review of flow-quantification methodology, ecological reasoning, and management recommendations. Morphological traits of the four species (e.g., [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] elongate piscivorous body plan, [[Razorback Sucker]] nuchal hump, [[Humpback Chub]] pronounced dorsal hump, [[Bonytail]] streamlined pelagic form) are referenced only implicitly through discussion of habitat and life-history needs (e.g., larval drift), not described directly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System context**: The Upper Colorado River Basin is characterized as an ecologically complex, large-river ecosystem with **N-dimensional, stochastic** structure — explicitly stated to be too complex for simple deterministic flow-biomass models.
- **Key habitat features affecting the endangered fishes**:
  - **Channel and floodplain morphology** — dynamic in space and time due to sediment transport; not a simple stage-area relationship.
  - **Rithron–potamon transition zones** — boundary zones between higher-gradient (rithron) and lower-gradient (potamon) river reaches; their spatial position may shift under different flow regimes, with implications for [[Fish Habitat Suitability]].
  - **Varial (hydropower-fluctuation) zones** — areas subject to daily/weekly flow fluctuation from upstream hydropower operations, affecting food web dynamics in the littoral/riparian margin.
  - **Larval drift dynamics** — spring flow regimes influence larval fish transport and exposure to [[Nonnative Fish Predation]].
- **Threats and limiting factors identified**:
  - Predation and competition from **nonnative fish species**.
  - Potential **contaminant** effects on recovery.
  - **Water scarcity** — tension between fish recovery flow needs and existing water compact entitlements.
- **Flow-ecology linkage**: The panel emphasizes that flow alone may not be a sufficient recovery lever; the endangered fishes' population dynamics are embedded in a broader web of hydrologic, geomorphic, and biotic interactions (see [[Instream Flow Ecology]]).

## Key Findings

1. **No adequate mechanistic model exists** for predicting incremental flow–habitat–biomass relationships for the endangered fishes in large, complex rivers such as the Upper Colorado — simple deterministic or fully dynamic ecosystem models are both deemed unattainable/impractical.
2. **Prudent alternative approach**: Use all available ecological information to derive and implement a **flow regime** for the whole basin ecosystem, paired with monitoring variables that track whether ecosystem trajectory favors fish recovery — an early articulation of [[Adaptive Management]] in river restoration.
3. **Current ecological knowledge is judged sufficient** to justify endorsement of **interim flow recommendations** (i.e., the existing FWS flow recommendations) despite model limitations.
4. **Six Critical Uncertainties identified**:
   - (a) Flow may not be the sole limiting factor — nonnative predation and contaminants may impede recovery.
   - (b) Potential conflict between fish recovery water needs and compact water entitlements.
   - (c) Complex, sediment-driven channel/floodplain morphology undermines simple stage-area flow models.
   - (d) Trade-offs among spring flow regimes (larval drift benefits vs. nonnative predation risk) require evaluation.
   - (e) Effects of hydropower-driven flow fluctuations on varial zone food webs remain undetermined.
   - (f) Potential shifts in rithron–potamon transition zones under recommended flow regimes need study.
5. **Six major recommendations** (approved by Instream Flow Subcommittee & Biology Committee):
   - Implement **interim flows** (FWS recommendations) to reestablish natural seasonality.
   - Foster **common understanding of water availability** among stakeholders.
   - Improve the **standardized monitoring program**, incorporating a community ecology perspective.
   - **Diversify the research program** to resolve the critical uncertainties listed above.
   - Implement a **peer review process** (flagged as highest priority for immediate implementation).
   - Implement **adaptive management** as an ongoing management framework.
6. **Programmatic outcome**: FY93 budget ($120,821) fully expended with no discrepancy; deliverable was the Stanford (1993) final synthesis report, "Instream flows to assist the recovery of endangered fishes of the upper Colorado River basin," submitted to U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Denver, CO.
7. **Mixed stakeholder reception**: while the governing subcommittees formally approved the report's recommendations, some Recovery Program participants disputed whether the project fully met its intended goals and objectives — highlighting ongoing institutional debate over [[Endangered Species Flow Management]] approaches.
