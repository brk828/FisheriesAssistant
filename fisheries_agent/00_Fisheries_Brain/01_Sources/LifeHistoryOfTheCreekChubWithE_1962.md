# Life History of the Creek Chub, with Emphasis on Growth

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This study examines the age, growth, length-weight relationship, and food habits of the [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*) sampled from a seven-mile section of the [[Des Moines River]] in Boone County, Iowa, during the summer of 1961. Based on 151 specimens, researchers used scale annuli and length-frequency methods to reconstruct growth histories. Chubs reached mean total lengths of 58 mm, 95 mm, and 128 mm at the formation of the first, second, and third annuli, respectively, with annulus formation occurring in May. Weight increased approximately as the cube of length (isometric growth). Diet analysis revealed an opportunistic omnivore relying on plant material, aquatic insects, and — in larger individuals — small fish, demonstrating notable dietary adaptability tied to fluctuating river conditions. This work is presented as a regional baseline for [[Creek Chub]] life history, noting that growth in the Des Moines River population was slower than hatchery-reared or southern Illinois stream populations, comparable instead to a cold-water Illinois stream population.

## Background & Description
- **Length-Weight Relationship** (log-transformed):
  `Log W = -3.972 + 2.98 (Log L)`
  where W = weight (g), L = total length (mm); standard error of estimate = 0.0916.
  - A t-test confirmed the slope does not differ significantly from 3.00, indicating **isometric growth** — body shape remains proportionally constant as fish increase in length.
- **Body-Scale Relationship**:
  `L = 8.29 + 2.75 R`
  where L = total length (mm) and R = lateral radius of scale (mm, at 32X magnification). The focal intercept of 8.29 mm was used to construct a growth nomograph.
- **Condition Factor (K, TL-based)**, computed via `K = W(10⁵/L³)`:
  - Age 0: 1.07
  - Age I: 0.90
  - Age II: 1.08
  - Age III: 1.22
  - Overall mean (n=151): 1.02
- **Size distribution**: Fish ranged from 20 mm to 169 mm total length, spanning a weight range of approximately 0.25 g to 58.00 g across sampled size classes (10-mm intervals).
- **Growth at annuli** (calculated total length):
  | Annulus | Mean TL (mm) |
  |---|---|
  | 1st | 58.2 |
  | 2nd | 94.6 |
  | 3rd | 127.6 |
  - Average annual increment: 83.2 mm (annulus 1→2) and 85.9 mm noted as separate increment value in original table.
- Annuli were identified via **anastomosis of circuli in the lateral field**, with closely spaced circuli preceding the annulus and widely spaced circuli immediately following it. Lateral field measurements (rather than anterior field, where circuli were overly crowded) were used for aging.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: The [[Creek Chub]] is characterized as primarily a small-stream fish, preferring channels less than 40 feet wide (per Starrett, 1948). The Des Moines River study site — mainly sand-gravel bottom with silt-bottomed holes and gravel-rubble areas — is considered **marginal habitat** for the species due to its larger size relative to preferred small-stream conditions.
- **Distribution**: Found throughout the eastern United States, ranging as far west as Montana and New Mexico.
- **Collection microhabitats**: Chubs were most extensively collected over gravel and rubble bottoms, particularly above riffle areas; young-of-year individuals were also found in an isolated, receding-water pool cut off from the main river channel.
- **Trophic role**: Functions as an opportunistic omnivore/generalist predator. Diet shifts with fish size — larger chubs (>72 mm) increasingly prey on small fish (e.g., blacksided darter *[[Percina maculata]]*, minnows), while smaller individuals rely more heavily on plant material and aquatic insects.
- **Dietary plasticity**: Demonstrated rapid behavioral response to environmental fluctuation — e.g., following a flood event on July 5–6, 1961, chubs moved into newly flooded rubble areas to consume stranded carabid beetles, illustrating adaptive foraging tied to hydrological dynamics. This adaptability is proposed as a contributing factor to the species' wide geographic range.
- **Parasitism**: Seven chubs were infested with nematodes identified as *Rhabdochona* sp., consistent with prior records of this parasite genus in creek chubs from Ontario ([[Bangham 1941]]).
- **Comparative growth ecology**: Growth in the Des Moines River population was slower than hatchery-raised chubs (up to 3.5 in. by first September) and slower than a spotted-bass stream population in southern Illinois, but comparable to growth rates in a cold-water southern Illinois stream ([[Gunning and Lewis 1956]]).

## Key Findings
- **151 creek chubs** were collected via seining, electric shocking, and hand dip-netting from June–September 1961 (plus isolated March/May samples).
- Mean total lengths at first three annuli: **58 mm, 95 mm, 128 mm**.
- **Annulus formation occurs in May** — confirmed by scale sampling showing only 1 of 3 fish collected May 4 had a new annulus, versus all fish collected after June 1.
- Weight-length relationship exponent (2.98) statistically indistinguishable from cubic (3.00), supporting **isometric growth**.
- **Food habits** (n=123 stomachs examined, 33 empty):
  - Plant material: 21.1% occurrence (Cladophora sp.: 9.8%)
  - Ephemeroptera (mayfly) naiads: 21.1%
  - Adult Coleoptera: 13.8%
  - Fish: 16.3% occurrence overall, concentrated in larger individuals
  - Smallest fish-eating chub: 46 mm (unusual; next smallest was 72 mm)
  - Notable predation events: a 76 mm chub consumed a 45 mm blacksided darter; a 134 mm chub consumed a 65 mm minnow; a 123 mm chub consumed two minnows (24 mm and 35 mm)
- **Parasite load**: 7 chubs infested with *Rhabdochona* sp. nematodes.
- Diet and habitat-use flexibility (e.g., exploiting flood-stranded terrestrial insects) is highlighted as a likely driver of the species' broad ecological success and range.
- Study contributes regional life-history data for [[Semotilus atromaculatus]] where prior comprehensive age/growth work was lacking, despite its established importance as a bait minnow species ([[Harlan and Speaker 1956]]).
