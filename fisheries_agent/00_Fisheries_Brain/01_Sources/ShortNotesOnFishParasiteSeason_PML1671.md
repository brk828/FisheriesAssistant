# Short Notes on Fish Parasite Seasonality, Rainbow Smelt Records, and a New Arizona Topminnow Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae #poeciliidae #parasitology

## Executive Summary

This Southwestern Naturalist notes section (May 1982) contains three short contributions. The first documents the seasonal dynamics of three parasitic worms in [[Largemouth Bass]] and compares them with earlier studies. The second reports the first [[Mississippi River]] records of juvenile [[Rainbow Smelt]] from Arkansas and Tennessee. The third describes a newly discovered population of the endangered [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]] (Topminnow) in a spring system in Santa Cruz County, Arizona, with habitat and water-chemistry data. Parts of the source are degraded by OCR and some passages, especially the closing pages, are unreadable; the summary is restricted to content that could be reliably recovered.

## Background & Description

**Parasite note.** Three parasites are discussed in largemouth bass hosts: [[Camallanus Oxycephalus]], [[Spinitectus Carolini]], and [[Neoechinorhynchus Cylindratus]]. The authors compare their seasonal patterns with earlier work by Hoffman et al. (1974), McDaniel and Bailey (1974), Underwood (1975), and Stromberg and Crites (1975), the last on [[White Bass]] in [[Lake Erie]].

**Rainbow smelt note.** [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) is primarily an anadromous species of the western North Atlantic. Landlocked populations spread through the Great Lakes during the first half of the twentieth century following introductions. Earlier Mississippi River records came from Illinois, Kentucky, and Louisiana.

**Topminnow note.** The [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]] topminnow declined sharply in range over recent decades, mainly due to habitat destruction and introduced exotic fishes. The U.S. Department of the Interior lists it as endangered (Federal Register 45(99):33778, 1980). The exact site location is withheld at the landowner's request.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Parasite seasonality.** Infection by *Camallanus oxycephalus* was relatively constant except for a significant increase in prevalence in June. Stromberg and Crites (1975) linked the August peak in white bass to the coexistence of old and young adult worms in the host population and to recruitment that followed rising [[Copepod]] populations and infection of forage fish. The authors suggest the cycle may occur earlier in Arkansas and Texas because of a milder climate. *Spinitectus carolini* increased from December to June and was scarce from July to December. Its spring-summer recruitment likely reflects a rise in the population of its insect intermediate host, which bass eat. Its later decline is probably due to parasite mortality. *Neoechinorhynchus cylindratus* intensity rose steadily from February to August, with a sharp drop in September, which the authors suggest may reflect a periodicity unrelated to seasonality.

**Smelt habitat.** Most smelt were taken over sandy bottoms in slow current at depths of 0.5 to 1.5 m on overcast days between 1000 and 1500 h. The water temperature at all sites was reported as "approximately 29C" in the scan, which is almost certainly a scan error for approximately 2°C given the December collection dates and the cold-water biology of the species.

**Topminnow habitat.** The site is a spring system tributary to the upper [[Santa Cruz River]] at about 1400 m elevation, in an oak-grassland association. Eighteen elongate pools (5 to 52 m long, 0.5 to 4 m wide, 0.2 to 2 m deep) are connected by a discharge of roughly 0.01 m³/s through saturated cienega marsh. The pools flow over rich organic deposits underlain by gravel. Dominant vegetation includes naiad (*Najas marina*), watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*), duckweed (*Lemna* spp.), and mosquito fern (*Azolla filiculoides*). Marsh sedges and grasses surround the pools, and the riparian zone is dominated by willow (*Salix gooddingii*) with some cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*).

## Key Findings

### Parasite Seasonality in Largemouth Bass
- *Camallanus oxycephalus* prevalence was stable except for a significant increase in June; a similar pattern (with a July peak) was reported by Hoffman et al. (1974).
- *Spinitectus carolini* showed a spring-summer rise and a fall-winter decline. The Feb-Apr mean of 6.7 may be inflated by one heavily infected host.
- *Neoechinorhynchus cylindratus* intensity increased from February to August, then dropped substantially in September.

### Rainbow Smelt in the Mississippi River
- Juvenile smelt (34 to 68 mm SL, mean 51.4 mm) were collected at four sites in December 1980: Crittenden Co., Arkansas (3 specimens); Mississippi Co., Arkansas (1 and 4 specimens at two sites); and Lauderdale Co., Tennessee (a larger preserved series, with the count garbled in the scan).
- These are the first Mississippi River records of *O. mordax* from Arkansas and Tennessee.
- Additional juveniles (46 to 55 mm SL) were collected on 30 January 1981 at Crittenden Co., Arkansas, and Tipton Co., Tennessee.
- Meristic counts (50 Tennessee specimens): dorsal rays 9 to 11 (mean 9.5); anal rays 14 to 16 (mean 14.7); caudal rays 17 to 20 (mean 19.0); pectoral rays 9 to 13 (mean 11.8); pelvic rays 6 to 9 (mean 7.9); lateral scales 56 to 64 (mean 61.2); pored lateral line scales 14 to 19 (mean 16.2).
- Earlier records: 104 and 91 juveniles in December 1978 and 1979 in Illinois and Kentucky (Burr and Mayden), and four juveniles near St. Francisville, Louisiana, during flood conditions (Suttkus and Conner).
- Possible origins discussed: dispersal from Lake Michigan through the Chicago sanitary canal and Illinois River during high water; downstream movement from northern Missouri River introductions; or local reproduction in the Mississippi or its tributaries. The December 1980 collections were made when the river was at a record low for the season, with no high water for the prior 8 months. Because this cold-water species had never been collected in the Mississippi in summer, the authors question whether it can survive there.

### Arizona Topminnow Population
- A new population was discovered in a spring-fed pool system in Santa Cruz County, Arizona, and the habitat was described.
- Water chemistry: saturation 65 to 110% (late summer); pH 8.4 to 8.9; conductivity 290 to 580 µmhos/cm; total alkalinity 3.1 to 5.9 mg/L; surface temperature 6 to 28°C, fairly uniform among pools on a given date.
- Chemical variables showed no clear gradient along the system, though alkalinity and one other measure were higher in winter.
- The first survey (August 1979) found topminnows only in pools 6 through 12, with numbers in pools 13 through 18 later decreasing in absolute abundance over the following winter. The co-occurrence analysis with mosquitofish is in the illegible portion of the scan and cannot be reliably reported.
