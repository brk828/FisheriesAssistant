# History of the Fish Population of Lake LaVerne, Iowa State University Campus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Kenneth D. Carlander]] reconstructs the fish population of Lake LaVerne, a small eutrophic campus lake in Ames, Iowa, from 1937 to 1977. The data come from fishery management class records and other irregularly collected information, gathered as teaching exercises rather than formal research. The fish community shifted from [[Sunfish]] dominance in 1937-1951, to near-total [[Goldfish]] dominance in 1968 after fish kills and dredging, to a renewed mix of sunfish (especially [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Bluegill]]) in 1970-1977. The community is largely a product of haphazard introductions.

## Background & Description

**Lake history.** The site was a marsh until a dam was built on College Creek in 1916, funded by Dr. LaVerne W. Noyes. The [[Civilian Conservation Corps]] removed 19,125 m³ (25,000 yd³) of sediment in 1933 and built a diversion channel. College Creek was diverted around the lake in 1959 to stop pollution from a hog farm and other upstream sources. Dredging then removed 27,210 metric tons (30,000 short tons) of silt. The dam was modified again when Lincoln Way was widened in 1964.

**Watershed and water sources.** After the diversion, the watershed is limited to campus land. Inputs include a tile line from the [[Friley Hall]] complex, which stays warm (24°C) even in winter, and air-conditioning water from the [[Memorial Union]]. Until 1967 all Union cooling water entered the lake; the flow is now limited. The well-sourced water produces an iron precipitate and a sulfur odor when aerated.

**Physical setting.** The lake covers 1.14 ha (2.8 acres), with most of its water less than 2 m deep. It is eutrophic, with algae and aquatic weeds (mostly *Potamogeton* spp.). Copper sulfate and other chemicals have provided fairly adequate control in recent years.

**Sampling methods.** The 1937 and 1938 records are a few seine hauls. Electrofishing caught most fish recorded for 1948-1951 and 1968-1977. Seines, traps and gill nets were used occasionally.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pesticide-driven fish kills.** Several kills followed pesticide spraying:
- **1962:** [[Methoxychlor]], used in a tree-spraying program, killed thousands of minnows and carp.
- **April 1, 1967:** Helicopter spraying caused an extensive kill, mostly of goldfish.
- **1977:** Dead goldfish were seen along the shore after a severe winter when the ice went out.

**Introductions.** Sportsmen stocked [[Channel Catfish]], [[Northern Pike]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] in 1932. The class records starting in 1937 show no evidence of these stockings. The lake has never supported good angling, but it provides many hours of fishing for children. [[Blue Tilapia]] (*Tilapia aurea*) was introduced in 1968 and 1969 to control algae; it does not overwinter.

**Goldfish ecology.** Goldfish were the dominant fish after the 1967 kill. Their fin-length variation suggested genetic contributions from fantail-type strains, so standard length was used. The [[Parasitic Copepod]] *Lernaea* sp. ("anchor worms") was reported by Nasiri (1969) and seen in other years without consistent records. Stomach contents were mainly plankton, detritus and bryozoan statoblasts.

## Key Findings

**Population trajectory**
- 1937-1951: [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] and Green Sunfish were the most abundant fishes.
- After several fish kills and dredging, goldfish dominated in 1968.
- 1970-1977: Green Sunfish, then Bluegill, became more abundant.
- Many other species appear only in small numbers, reflecting haphazard introduction.

**Goldfish decline and mortality**
- Goldfish declined from about 37,700 to 1,720 individuals between 1968 and 1977.
- Recruitment was negligible: one small fish in September 1972, two in May 1976, and one (85 mm, possibly Age II) in May 1977.
- Annual mortality was usually about 20-23%.

**Standing crop**
- With goldfish abundant, standing crop was 1,065-1,115 kg/ha.
- When small sunfish dominated, standing crop was 110-184 kg/ha.

**Goldfish size and age (1968 sample, Nasiri 1969 and this paper)**
- Scale analysis of goldfish from the 1968 sample:
  - 37 Age 0 fish, 40-75 mm (mean 52 mm)
  - 127 Age I fish, 40-115 mm (mean 68 mm)
  - 23 Age II fish, 80-155 mm (mean 117 mm)
  - 4 Age III fish, 200-271 mm (mean 233 mm)
  - 1 Age IV fish, 224 mm
- The largest fish was 271 mm and weighed 680 g (May-June sample).
- Large fish with only two annuli may have been large when released, before time in aquaria.
- By 1977, the surviving goldfish measured 110-200 mm at Ages X and XI. These older scales or otoliths were not examined.

**Other species**
- [[Green Sunfish]]: the only species taken in every year sampled. It made up 5-47% of the catch in all years except 1968, when goldfish predominated. Most were under 125 mm, with a few 181-210 mm (1968) and 184-190 mm (1971).
- [[Common Carp]]: few were taken from 1968 to 1973 and one in 1977, ranging 316-710 mm total length. Large dead carp were reported after the 1962 spraying.
- [[Northern Pike]]: one specimen, 700 mm total length, was shocked on May 12, 1977.
- [[Channel Catfish]]: fingerlings were stocked. Specimens of 307-505 mm total length were recorded in 1968, 1970 and 1974.
- [[Flathead Catfish]]: an 11-lb (5 kg) fish was caught by an angler in the late 1950s.
- [[Black Bullhead]]: a large number was stocked for a "kids' fish day" in the early 1950s. A few have been taken in most sampled years since 1968, but they were never a significant part of the population.
- Minnows: [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Bullhead Minnow]] and [[Bluntnose Minnow]] were collected in seine hauls (1938, 1948-1949, 1968). Three [[Creek Chub]] were taken in June 1951.
- Suckers and carpsuckers: one [[River Carpsucker]] in 1968; one or two [[White Sucker]] in 1973 and 1974.

**Management implications**
- Pesticide use and dredging repeatedly reset the fish community.
- Algae and weed control affected lake quality, and tilapia was introduced as a biological control measure.
- The community's composition has been driven by accidental and deliberate introductions rather than by a managed fishery.
