# Native Fishes of the Upper Gila River System, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 conference paper, presented at a New Mexico Department of Game and Fish meeting, by James F. LaBounty ([[Bureau of Reclamation]]) and W. L. Minckley ([[Arizona State University]]), documents historical change in the fish fauna of the [[Upper Gila River]] in New Mexico. The authors describe a shift from a native-dominated fauna to one increasingly composed of introduced species. Despite this, the upper Gila system still holds one of the least altered native ichthyofaunas in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The paper compares historical and recent collections, lists native and introduced species, and discusses probable causes of local extirpations.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila River]] rises in tributaries of the mountains of southwestern New Mexico. The [[San Francisco River]], a major tributary, originates in the White Mountains of Arizona and re-enters Arizona after flowing through New Mexico. Much of the basin lies within the [[Gila National Forest]], including a designated wilderness area. Vegetation ranges from high-elevation aspen, spruce and fir, through pinon-juniper woodlands, to cottonwood-sycamore gallery forests along watercourses. Lower, broader floodplains have been converted to agriculture.

Early accounts from the mid- to late 1800s describe streamside vegetation similar to that of the present day. Arroyo cutting (channel trenching) has been recorded in the region, but the authors judge its effect on the Gila itself to be smaller than on some streams to the south. Mainstem habitat includes riffles, eddies and large pools. High-elevation tributaries are cold, rocky, tumbling mountain streams.

Methods included seining and, in later years, extensive electrofishing. Specimens are housed at [[Arizona State University]], the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] and the [[University of New Mexico]]. The authors state that these collections were essential to the study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fauna.** The upper Gila basin in New Mexico historically supported at least 9 native fish species (per [[Koster]], 1957), and probably 13. Seven of these remain. One is considered endangered. Another, no longer present in New Mexico, was considered threatened with extinction by Miller (1967). Two of the native species, *Meda fulgida* and *Tiaroga cobitis*, are monotypic endemic genera. Five others are endemic species or subspecies, and several have dispersed from the Gila basin into adjacent drainages.

**Distribution by habitat.**
- High-elevation tributaries hold speckled dace and at least four streams support [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*). The authors suggest the Arizona trout may also have occurred in New Mexico.
- Intermediate-elevation and generally moderate streams hold [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]], which are locally abundant.
- The [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) predominates at the lowest elevations and may have been moving upstream in recent years. It is characteristic of low-desert streams.
- The [[Gila Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) and [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarkii*) occur throughout the larger streams. Other large fishes, including [[Colorado Squawfish]] and possibly flannelmouth sucker, were probably present in the lower New Mexico segment in the past but are treated as hypothetical in current occurrence.

**Non-native fauna.** The authors compiled 19 valid, museum-substantiated introductions of non-native fishes into the upper Gila basin. Of these, 16 persist through natural reproduction or repeated stocking. Introduced species include [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*).

**Historical shift in the native-to-non-native ratio.** The abstract reports that about 1900 there were fewer than four non-native fish species in the Gila River in New Mexico. The ratio of native to non-native species moved to about 1:1 by 1950, to 1:1.5 by 1960, and to a present ratio of about 1:2.3. The shift involved both additions of non-native species and loss of native forms.

**Trout decline.** Both *Salmo* trout species that formerly occupied New Mexico waters have been "decimated principally" by changes in the environment and biota. The taxonomy and distribution of the New Mexico trouts were revised by Miller (1972b).

## Key Findings

1. **Relatively intact fauna:** The upper Gila system contains 7 of the 10 to 13 probable native inhabitants of the basin, making it among the least altered ichthyofaunas in the Colorado River basin.
2. **Persistent introductions:** 16 of 19 verified non-native introductions persist.
3. **Shifting ratio:** The native-to-non-native species ratio has declined from fewer than four non-natives around 1900 to about 1:2.3 at present.
4. **Extirpations:**
   - The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) was restricted to the area of Frisco Hot Spring on the San Francisco River and is considered extirpated from the upper Gila system. Probable causes include human activity, stream incision near the spring, and competition or predation from introduced mosquitofish.
   - The [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta grahami*) was last recorded in 1967, and that record is not substantiated by a specimen. Despite considerable effort, neither author caught it. The species was exceedingly abundant in earlier collections and began declining in the early 1960s, possibly from competition with or predation by smallmouth bass.
5. **Range contraction of native species:** Speckled dace appear to have moved to higher elevations, and longfin dace may be invading upstream, suggesting changing distributions along the elevation gradient.
6. **Trout decline:** Both trout species formerly in New Mexico have been severely reduced.
7. **Conservation context:** The authors frame the Gila fauna within a broader regional pattern of native fish loss in the Colorado River basin. They attribute this to river regulation and development, with replacement by introduced species, and they note that dam sites were proposed on the upper Gila.
8. **Data sources:** Comparisons are based on [[Koster]] collections of the 1940s and 1950s, New Mexico Game and Fish Department surveys of the early 1950s, and the authors' own 1970–72 collections.
