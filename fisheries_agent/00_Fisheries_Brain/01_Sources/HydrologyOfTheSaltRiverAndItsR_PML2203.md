# Hydrology of the Salt River and Its Reservoirs, Central Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1975 paper by [[John N. Rinne]] and [[Patrick C. Marsh]] (Arizona Academy of Science, Vol. 10, No. 2) describes the hydrology of the [[Salt River]] reservoir chain in central Arizona and argues that these hot-desert impoundments are ecologically unusual compared with temperate, more mesic reservoirs. Runoff is episodic and driven by distant snowmelt and rainfall and by isolated intense thunderstorms, delivering large pulses of particulate organic matter. The paper also reviews the effects of the recently installed [[Pumped-Storage Hydroelectric Systems]] and concludes that their effects on the aquatic environment are largely undocumented and should be a priority for research.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context:** The [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (established 1902) built water storage structures in western arid lands; hydroelectric generation was added later. Early reservoirs attracted recreational use before fisheries and wildlife values were recognized, moving management toward a "multiple-use" concept.
- **Study system:** The Salt River chain comprises [[Roosevelt Lake]] (completed 1911) and downstream structures: [[Horse Mesa Dam]] (1925; Apache Lake), Mormon Flat (1927; Canyon Lake), and Stewart Mountain (1930; Saguaro Lake). [[Horseshoe Dam]] and Bartlett Dam on the Verde River were completed in 1939 and 1939/1938 (as given in the source text, 1939 and 1958 or 1936-type OCR ambiguity; see Key Findings).
- **Regional context:** The Salt River drains the White Mountains and the Mogollon area to the north and east. The [[Gila River]] system once contributed substantially to the lower Colorado River.
- **Population:** Human population density in the reservoir region rose from 154 persons per square mile by 1940 to about 772/km² in recent decades, with the author estimating perhaps 1,000/km² at writing.
- **Purpose:** The paper aims to describe the qualitative hydrology of the Salt River reservoirs, the effects of recent modifications, and their probable effects on the hydrology, biology, and fisheries of these environments. It is also intended as an introduction to hot-desert impoundments, which were essentially unstudied at the time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and runoff.** Precipitation over the Salt River watershed averages 25 to 35 cm annually. Mean annual runoff is 7.11 cm (range 1.7 to 29.0 cm; Green and Sellers, 1964). In a given year, runoff ranges from about 2.6 × 10⁶ ha-m to much less, and total storage capacity of the chain is near 2.4 × 10⁶ ha-m. Water is stored during high flows for domestic supply, irrigation, and power.

**Flow patterns.** The Salt River contributes about seven times the volume of [[Tonto Creek]] to the reservoir system. Discharge in both streams displays 3- to 4-year cycles tied to wet and dry periods. Mean river discharge is about 1,200 m³/min.

**Storage and flushing.** Storage-ratio calculations give Canyon and Saguaro lakes a ratio near 0.1 (flushing roughly monthly), Apache Lake about 5 times greater (almost 6 months to flush), and Roosevelt about 3.5 times greater than Apache (about 1.7 years). On average, all four reservoirs flushed in about 40% less time during 1970-71 than in the preceding decade, due to lower lake levels and drawdown for construction rather than increased inflow.

**Organic litter loading.** Litter accumulation is high in the xeric region. Chaparral accumulates about 12.5 metric tons/ha per year, roughly 2.5 times the 5.0 metric tons/ha in ponderosa pine forest. Estimated annual litter accumulation is 3.5 × 10⁶ metric tons in chaparral and ponderosa zones, and up to 7.0 × 10⁶ metric tons per year across the watershed. Drought can suppress decomposition for 5 to 10 years, so litter accumulates and becomes available for flood transport.

**Comparative inputs.** McConnell's estimate for [[Pena Blanca Reservoir]] in southern Arizona was 3.5 × 10⁵ kg annually (about 9 metric tons/km² per year). Scaled to Roosevelt, the author estimates about 2.5 × 10⁶ metric tons from chaparral alone, and about 5.7 × 10⁶ metric tons total per average year, equivalent to roughly 8,150 kg/ha of lake surface (1.2 × 10⁴ kg/ha, per the source's scaled figure). These are annual estimates that may vary by 3 to 4 times with non-annual flow regimes.

**Desert versus mesic input.** In temperate watersheds, organic matter enters mainly as dissolved nutrients through gentler runoff, slow soil processes, and deciduous leaf fall. In desert impoundments, nutrients arrive in brief, massive floods carrying particulate matter with few dissolved substances, and reservoirs must function on that pulse for years.

**Water chemistry.** Total dissolved solids correlate closely with specific conductance (r = 0.99) and show a relationship with discharge on the upstream Salt River.

## Key Findings

1. **Episodic flooding dominates input.** Mean daily discharge exceeded the flood-indicator threshold of 1.7 × 10⁵ m³/min on only 91 days between 1950 and 1965. The 1965-66 flood series was the most important recent event, with three days exceeding 4.9 × 10⁵ m³/min. Afterward, Roosevelt Lake's surface was more than two-thirds covered with debris ranging from fine material to large woody pieces.
2. **Recent low input.** Since 1965, minimal particulate organic input occurred from the highland watershed, in part due to recent scouring, which allowed the studies to proceed.
3. **Nutrient pulses and productivity.** Roosevelt Lake is expected to be the most productive reservoir, consistent with higher relative mean chlorophyll-a concentrations. Post-fire or forest-fire events may stimulate [[Plankton]] blooms through nutrient loading.
4. **Pumped-storage modifications.** Recent construction converted some dams to pumped-storage units. The middle two structures can reverse flow, and the turbine capacity of the chain was increased. These changes drastically altered operating procedures and reservoir water levels.
5. **Knowledge gaps.** Effects of pumping on withdrawal layers, thermal structure, fish spawning and migration, and limnology are poorly documented. The authors state that the effects on hydrology, biology, fisheries, and recreation "are unstudied and should be the primary objective of future research."
6. **Expected effects of destratification.** Destratification by aeration in temperate lakes improves water quality and fisheries, but the authors judge that such results are unlikely under hot-desert conditions and current fisheries management of the Salt River reservoirs.
7. **Conclusion.** The reservoirs are hydrologically and ecologically distinct from temperate systems and require continued monitoring. The authors also emphasize the need for research on the combined effects of pumping, drought, and seasonal dam operation.
