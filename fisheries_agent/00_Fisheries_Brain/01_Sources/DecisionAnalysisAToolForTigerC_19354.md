# Decision Analysis: A Tool for Tiger Conservation and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #felidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter by Lynn A. Maguire, published in a 1987 volume on tigers of the world, introduces [[Decision Analysis]] as a structured method for managing [[Tiger]] (*Panthera tigris*) populations under uncertainty. The author argues that tiger conservation involves multiple, sometimes conflicting objectives and many unresolved biological and sociopolitical uncertainties. The method is illustrated with a [[Decision Tree]] evaluating whether wild tigers in [[Tiger Reserve]]s should be managed more closely and whether reserves should be expanded. Under the illustrative parameters, the analysis favors [[Active Management]] of tiger populations. The chapter stresses that the parameter values are plausible illustrations, not expert-validated estimates.

Note: the subject is terrestrial mammal conservation rather than fish or aquatic ecology, so the taxonomic tags above are retained only because the required format calls for them; the content does not address aquatic taxa.

## Background & Description

The chapter frames tiger management as a difficult decision problem for several reasons:

- **Primary objectives:** maximizing survival probability of tiger populations, maximizing retention of genetic diversity, and maintaining or expanding the wild range.
- **Secondary objectives:** conservation education, international cooperation, minimizing disruption of rural development, minimizing financial cost, and protecting associated species and ecosystems.
- **Measurable criteria:** examples include heterozygosity from enzyme analysis of tissue, inbreeding coefficients from pedigrees, and inbreeding inferred from population records.
- **Open management questions:** allocation of zoo spaces among the five extant subspecies, designation or expansion of reserves, subpopulation structure in captivity, founder additions to [[Captive Breeding]] programs, culling, fertility control, translocation, and reintroduction.
- **Uncertainty:** environmental factors (extreme weather, disease, demographic fluctuation) and sociopolitical factors (resettlement schemes, shifting government support, export restrictions, civil unrest).

The method adapts formal decision analysis from business management (Raiffa 1968), following earlier wildlife applications by Maguire (1986) and Maguire et al. (1986), including the [[Sumatran Rhinoceros]] case study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reserve problem rests on several documented contextual points:

- Designation of reserves in India (*Panthera tigris tigris*) and in eastern Russia and northeastern China (*Panthera tigris altaica*) has been followed by apparent population increases (Srivastava 1979; Bangjie 1983; Panwar 1982).
- Recent gains do not guarantee long-term viability under current management.
- Rising tiger-human conflict around reserve borders suggests some reserves may already be "full" (Srivastava 1979; Hendrichs 1975).
- Tigers are linked to competitor and prey dynamics and social organization, with cited studies on tiger-leopard ecological separation (Seidensticker 1976) and social organization in [[Royal Chitwan National Park]], Nepal (Sunquist 1981).
- Translocation may disturb home-range social structure, increasing mortality and reducing reproduction.

## Key Findings

**Decision framework.** The reserve question is laid out as a decision tree with three options:
1. Status quo management (protecting the reserve and tigers from illegal killing; no added cost).
2. Active management within reserves (culling, fertility control, translocation; cost increase of $20 thousand).
3. Reserve expansion (cost increase of $50 thousand, including land acquisition and foregone development such as logging).

**Key uncertainties.**
- Whether the reserve is "too small" (probability 0.6 in the base case; a biological and conflict-driven uncertainty).
- Whether active management disrupts population processes.
- Whether planned expansion succeeds (sociopolitical).

**Value scale.** The best outcome, a large stable population, is valued at 1; a declining population in conflict with humans at 0; a declining population without conflict at 0.2; and a small, stable population at 0.8. These are illustrative values elicited from a hypothetical manager, not expert-validated data.

**Base-case results.** Expected population values from rollback (right to left):
- Status quo: 0.16
- Active management: 0.44 (cost $20 thousand)
- Expansion: 0.75 (cost $50 thousand)

Expansion has the highest population outcome but also the highest cost, so a [[Tradeoff Analysis]] is required. Indifference judgments lead to choosing active management over the status quo and rejecting expansion. Active management is therefore the selected action.

**Sensitivity analysis.** When the probability that the reserve is too small is lowered to 0.4, status quo expected value rises to 0.64, active management stays at 0.44, and expansion rises to 0.89. Active management is then dominated by the status quo, and expansion is rejected in favor of the status quo because the manager would not pay $50 thousand to raise the outcome from 0.64 to 0.89. Expansion always yields a higher population outcome than the status quo; cost is what makes it undesirable. The breakeven probability for choosing between status quo and active management is identified as the key threshold.

**Value of information.** Perfect information on whether the reserve is too small changes the optimal policy:
- If too small: active management (0.44, $20 thousand).
- If adequate: status quo (0.8, $0).
- Expected result with perfect information: population outcome 0.51, cost $16 thousand.

This implies an average saving of $4 thousand and an outcome gain of 0.07 over the uninformed choice, giving a maximum justified research spend of about $4 thousand plus the value of that outcome gain. The chapter notes that research costs can be shared across several similar reserves, and that sample information (less than perfect) is worth less than perfect information.

**Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Formal methods clarify objectives, make uncertainty explicit, and structure subjective expert judgment.
- Decision analysis helps resolve tradeoffs reflecting local decision-makers' values rather than purely economic cost-benefit results.
- Sensitivity and value-of-information analysis direct research: habitat and reserve-size research, management technique development, or political engagement, depending on which factor drives the decision.
- The tool supports communication among researchers, managers, zoo curators, and officials, but should not be used for automated decisions.
