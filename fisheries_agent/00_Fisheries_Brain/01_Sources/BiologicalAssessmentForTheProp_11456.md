# Biological Assessment for the Proposed Miner Flat Dam Project

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Troglichthyidae #Salmonidae #dam-impacts #White-River-Arizona

## Executive Summary

This Biological Assessment evaluates potential impacts of the proposed [[Miner Flat Dam]] and [[Canyon Day Irrigation Project]] on endangered and threatened species under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. The concrete gravity dam would be constructed on the [[North Fork White River]] at elevation 5,907 feet on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], creating an 8,900 acre-foot reservoir extending 2.4 miles upstream. The project includes expansion of irrigated acreage from 885 to 3,000 acres and hydropower generation (1.8-3.6 megawatts). Primary species of concern include the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), threatened [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*), endangered [[Bald Eagle]], endangered [[Peregrine Falcon]], candidate [[Southern Spotted Owl]], and candidate [[Gooding's Onion]]. Field surveys in 1984, 1986, and February 1988 documented fish populations and habitat characteristics. The assessment addresses impacts from flow regime alterations, water temperature changes, habitat modification, and cumulative effects from logging, gravel extraction, and channelization activities.

## Background & Description

### Project Components

The [[Miner Flat Dam]] would be a concrete gravity structure with crest elevation of 6,062 feet and 260-foot spillway length capable of discharging 115,000 cfs during maximum probable precipitation events. The dam requires 114,500 cubic yards of mass concrete, with 75% of aggregate materials obtained from the reservoir valley floor. A 10-foot diameter outlet works would serve for temporary construction diversion (2,000 cfs capacity), permanent releases, and as penstock for hydropower facilities. The intake structure on the upstream dam face would feature slide gates at 3 levels for selective temperature control of downstream releases.

Normal reservoir pool at elevation 6,062 feet would create 185 acres surface area; minimum pool at 6,000 feet would maintain 62 acres for recreation, fish, and wildlife while providing 2,500 acre-feet sediment storage capacity. Simulation studies (1958-1984 period) indicate the reservoir would maintain full pool 76% of the time and experience drawdown of 20 feet or less 91% of the time. In extremely dry years (e.g., 1959, 1961), full 62-foot drawdown to minimum pool would occur.

### Hydrologic Context

Average annual streamflow of the [[North Fork White River]] near the damsite totals 62,021 acre-feet (approximately 90 cfs mean). The North Fork contributes approximately 42% of flow at the [[Canyon Day]] diversion point, with [[Diamond Creek]] (5.8 miles downstream) and [[East Fork White River]] (18.6 miles downstream) contributing the remaining 58%. Streamflows at the damsite were predicted using multiple linear regression with gaging stations at [[North Fork White River near McNary]], [[East Fork White River near Fort Apache]], and [[White River near Fort Apache]] as independent variables.

The maximum flood of record at McNary gaging station (66 square miles drainage) was 1,290 cfs in 1946; the highest discharge on the White River near Fort Apache was 4,600 cfs in December 1978. Preliminary spillway capacity (115,000 cfs) exceeds historic floods by an order of magnitude.

### Irrigation and Hydropower Operations

During the irrigation season (April-September), releases from [[Miner Flat Dam]] would augment natural streamflow to provide 42 cfs at the [[Canyon Day]] diversion for 3,000 acres of irrigated crops (primarily alfalfa on 3-5 year rotation). Annual irrigation water use would increase from 2,200 to 15,750 acre-feet. Hydropower facilities with design discharge of 200 cfs would generate 5,220 MWH annually; alternative 415 cfs capacity would produce 6,093 MWH annually. Annual electrical energy value ranges from $208,800 to $243,700 at $0.04 per kilowatt hour.

Irrigation return flows (29% of diversions based on 62% irrigation efficiency) would contribute to downstream flow in the [[White River]]. Projected farming operations allow flexibility to plant dryland grain crops (40-50% lower water requirements than alfalfa) during low snowpack years to maintain minimum instream flows and reduce irrigation shortages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Native Fish Communities

Fish sampling in February 1988 documented predominantly native species assemblages in the project area. In the [[North Fork White River]] (3 stations, 21 fish total), [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) comprised 95.2% and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) 4.8%. In the [[East Fork White River]] (3 stations, 64 fish), Sonoran Sucker dominated at 96.9%, with single specimens of Speckled Dace (1.6%) and [[Loach Minnow]] (1.6%). In the [[White River]] (9 stations, 221 fish), Sonoran Sucker comprised 89.1%, [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robustus robustus*) 7.7%, Speckled Dace 1.8%, [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*) 0.9%, and [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila robustus grahami*) 0.5%.

The predominance of native fishes (>99% in riffle habitats) despite presence of non-native species relates to periodic high-magnitude flooding and canyon-bound geomorphology. According to [[Minckley and Meffe]] (1987), floods approaching or exceeding mean discharge by 2 orders of magnitude cause shifts from mixed native/non-native populations to predominantly native species. The [[White River]] drainage's narrow basalt canyons amplify flood energy, as overflow, infiltration, and channel widening cannot attenuate flood forces. Native fishes evolved under extreme flooding regimes possess structural and behavioral adaptations allowing survival that non-native species lack.

### Loach Minnow Status and Habitat

The [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) was listed as threatened in Federal Register Vol. 51, No. 208 (October 1986). Critical habitat proposed for portions of [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Gila River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Tularosa River]], [[Blue River]], [[Campbell Blue Creek]], and [[Dry Blue Creek]]; critical habitat in the White River watershed not yet proposed. The species historically occurred in approximately 1,625 miles of the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, and Gila river systems but currently occupies only 238 miles of former range.

Suitable habitat consists of cobble-rubble bottomed riffles with moderate to rapid current velocity. Fish typically occur singly or in pairs beneath excavated rocks. [[Rinne]] (1984) reported variable habitat parameters: in [[Middle Fork Gila River]], loach minnows inhabit swift water averaging 50 cm/sec, whereas in [[Tularosa River]] they occur in currents less than half this velocity. In [[Aravaipa Creek]], the species occupies riffles averaging ≤10 cm depth; in large rivers, riffles deeper than 25 cm. Recurrent flooding maintains riffle substrates free of embedding sediments and reduces competition with non-native fishes.

[[Minckley]] (1973) documented loach minnow occurrence in the White River between 1963-1972. [[Propst et al.]] (1985) collected specimens near [[Stago Bridge]] on the East Fork and near the confluence of East Fork and North Fork on the mainstem White River. Apparently suitable habitat exists in the North Fork White River although this reach was not sampled by Propst. Minckley (1988, pers. comm.) stated the loach minnow undoubtedly occurs in the North Fork White River in suitable habitats up to approximately 6,700 feet elevation.

Intensive electrofishing surveys in June 1986 concentrated on riffle margins and leading edges with cobble/rubble substrates in the proposed reservoir inundation area and immediately upstream but found no loach minnows. February 1988 surveys sampled 15 potential riffle habitats across North Fork White River, East Fork White River, and White River; one loach minnow was captured in the East Fork at a site with 15-20 m width, 5-75 cm depth, 35 cm average depth, 800 m² area, and water temperature of 42°F.

### Apache Trout Distribution

The endemic threatened [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) occurs in headwater streams of the White, Black, San Francisco, and Little Colorado rivers in the [[White Mountains]]. Populations documented in [[Boggy Creek]], [[Crooked Creek]], [[South Fork Diamond Creek]], [[East Fork White River]], [[Firebox Creek]], [[Little Diamond Creek]], [[Big Bonito Cienega]], [[Flash Creek]], [[Deep Creek]], [[North Fork Diamond Creek]], and [[Paradise Creek]]. Originally distributed throughout most of the White River drainage, the species became restricted to highest, most pristine mountain streams during the 1900s due to interbreeding with introduced non-native trout species.

The [[Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation propagates Apache trout from eggs taken from East Fork White River fish. In 1985, approximately 120 fish from 1983 year class and 700 from 1984 year class were being reared for spring 1986 spawning. By 1988, the hatchery would have 25,000-30,000 one-year-old Apache trout available for stocking on the Reservation and in national forest waters.

Apache trout feed primarily on aquatic invertebrates and do not attain large sizes in streams. [[Harper]] (1976) and [[Robinson]] (1978) documented invertebrate-based diet. [[Rinne et al.]] (1979) and Robinson (1986, pers. comm.) reported Apache trout are relatively easily caught by fishermen. [[Hansen]] (1983, pers. comm.) reported that based on electrofishing and creel census data, 80% of stocked trout in the North Fork White River are caught by fishermen and 20% lost to mortality.

### Wildlife Species

**[[Bald Eagle]]** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*, endangered): Resident population breeds along Salt and Verde rivers (fewer than 15 nests); migratory birds winter statewide near rivers, reservoirs, and lakes. [[Todd]] (no date) concluded most wintering eagles in Arizona frequent waters of the [[Mogollon Plateau]] and White Mountains, usually near lakes with [[Canada Geese]]. [[Grubb and Kennedy]] (1982) found waterfowl (primarily [[American Coot]]) were major winter prey, followed by ungulate carrion and small mammals; fish comprised small percentage. [[Haywood and Ohmart]] (1983) found breeding eagles on Salt and Verde rivers prey extensively on carp and catfish (73%), with mammals (5%), reptiles and amphibians (4%), and birds (1%) comprising remainder.

Nearest active bald eagle nest to project area is near Highway 60 bridge in Salt River canyon. Historic nesting site at confluence of Black and White rivers unused for several years (Caid, 1983). Active nest located in 1986 on [[Canyon Creek]] in northwestern Reservation. Wintering bald eagles regularly observed along White River and North Fork White River in project area.

**[[Peregrine Falcon]]** (*Falco peregrinus*, endangered): Breeds in Arizona at 5,000-9,000 feet elevation on steep rocky cliffs near water; winters statewide. Field investigations at proposed damsite in March, April, August 1984 and spring/summer 1986 revealed no peregrines, potential nests, or whitewash areas associated with nests and perching sites.

**[[Southern Spotted Owl]]** (*Strix occidentalis lucida*, candidate for threatened status): Occurrence on Reservation not documented. [[Ganey]] (1985) studied spotted owls in Arizona excluding Reservation but expects occurrence. [[Ganey and Balda]] (1985) reported concentrations along Mogollon Rim west of Reservation and in White Mountains east of Reservation in narrow canyons and cliffs at elevations similar to proposed damsite. Since August 1984, 90 occupied sites identified in Arizona. Most habitats in steep canyons with mixed-conifer or ponderosa pine vegetation and riparian vegetation along canyon bottoms. Field studies spring/summer 1986 at proposed dam and reservoir site using nocturnal calling surveys following [[Forsman]] (1983) methods failed to reveal spotted owl presence.

**[[Gooding's Onion]]** (*Allium goodingii*, candidate species): Status under review by USFWS; no legal protection under Endangered Species Act but considerable information supports endangered/threatened designation. [[Fletcher]] (1984), [[Wagner and Sabo]] (1977) concurred species qualifies for threatened listing, with primary threat being habitat destruction from recreation, grazing, logging, and possibly beaver removal. Original 1912 collection on White Mountain Apache Reservation near "Bonita Creek"; also collected near Maverick in 1960. Throughout range occurs at 6,500-9,400 feet elevation in shaded canyons on organic soils with little disturbance history. Would not be expected in dam/reservoir site or Canyon Day Irrigation Project (elevation below 6,500 feet, heavily grazed, severe flooding scoured valley floor). Field reconnaissance May and August 1984 found no specimens.

### Streamflow Impacts

Simulation studies using 1958-1984 data quantified monthly streamflow changes. In dry year (1959), reservoir filled October-March with only evaporation replacement (14-85 acre-feet/month) affecting flow. April-June required releases from storage; June 1959 natural flow of 865 acre-feet augmented by 3,115 acre-feet release, reducing reservoir to minimum 2,558 acre-feet. August natural flow of 5,946 acre-feet reduced to 600 acre-feet minimum while refilling reservoir to 7,883 acre-feet.

In near average year (1960), July and September streamflows increased by 1,779 and 1,877 acre-feet respectively; August reduced to 600 acre-feet minimum. In above average year (1965), reservoir at maximum operating level (6,062 feet) throughout year; streamflow differences only due to evaporation replacement.

Annual hydropower production compared with pumping requirements for 3,000 acres irrigation: dry year 1959 production (2,967,119 kWh) inadequate for pumping demands (7,551,016 kWh); average and above average years (1960: 7,901,925 kWh vs. 7,080,094 kWh; 1965: 8,825,120 kWh vs. 6,033,101 kWh) production significantly exceeds demands. Throughout 1958-1984 period, average hydropower production exceeds average pumping demands.

Impact on annual flooding characteristics expected to be minimal. During 1959-1984, reservoir filled before month of peak streamflow in all cases, allowing flood peaks to pass through with minimal reduction. In 1961, reservoir filled 2 months prior to peak flow month; in 1984, peak flow occurred in January but reservoir had filled 52 months earlier in September 1979.

At [[Canyon Day]] diversion (19 miles downstream), impacts diminish as [[Diamond Creek]] and [[East Fork White River]] contribute additional flow. In dry year 1959, April natural flow of 4,590 acre-feet reduced to 603 acre-feet (−3,987 acre-feet) to meet irrigation demands and minimum flows. June natural flow of 606 acre-feet increased to 1,540 acre-feet (+934 acre-feet) through minimum releases and return flows. August natural flow of 13,390 acre-feet reduced to 6,216 acre-feet (−7,174 acre-feet) while storing water at Miner Flat Dam.

At [[Salt River]] above [[Roosevelt Lake]], increased depletion by expanded Canyon Day Irrigation Project (8,460 acre-feet/year additional consumption) would reduce combined Salt and Verde river streamflows (average 1,131,900 acre-feet/year) by less than 0.75%. Natural streamflow losses between Canyon Day and Roosevelt Lake, differences in reservoir evaporation, and unused spills at Roosevelt Dam completely offset upstream depletion. Simulation studies show maintaining historic Roosevelt Dam releases would lower Roosevelt Lake level by average 4.38 feet and reduce Salt River system hydropower potential by 0.36% (Verde River system unchanged).
