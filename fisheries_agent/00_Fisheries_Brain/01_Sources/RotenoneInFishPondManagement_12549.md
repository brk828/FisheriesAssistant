# Rotenone in Fish Pond Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-management

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service leaflet (Leaflet 576, January 1965, reprinted February 1968) by Edward C. Kinney gives operational guidance for using [[Rotenone]], a plant-derived fish toxicant, in fish pond and lake management. It covers legal restrictions, common management purposes, formulation and dosing, volume calculation, application methods, fish response, detoxification with [[Potassium Permanganate]], and toxicity testing before restocking. The leaflet stresses that use requires approval from the appropriate State agency and a fishery biologist's assessment, and that the minimum effective dose should be applied.

## Background & Description

- **Compound:** Rotenone is extracted from the roots of many plant species of the bean family, also called derris or cube. It is a modern insecticide ingredient and has been used by native peoples for centuries to capture food fish.
- **Use history:** Rotenone has been used in U.S. fish management since 1934.
- **Toxicity:** It is lethal to fish but not particularly toxic to mammals. [[Swine]] are more sensitive than other mammals tested.
- **Handling caution:** Powdered rotenone causes respiratory irritation if inhaled. A mask or damp cloth is recommended during mixing, and liquid spray should not drift onto personnel.
- **Legal status:** State laws prohibit use of fish toxicants, including rotenone, in public waters except by authorized personnel. Many States restrict poison application in any waters. Approval is usually required from the State fish and game or conservation department.
- **Formulations:** Commercial products are liquid or powder, with active ingredient content from less than 1% to 20%. Liquids at 5% are commonly used. Some formulations work better in cold water, some in warm surface water, and some penetrate cooler deep water. Water-supply formulations have little effect on taste and odor.
- **Cost:** Treating a 1-acre pond with an average depth of 3 feet costs $5 to $15, depending on type and concentration.

### Management Uses

1. **Complete kill:** Eliminates competitive or undesirable species before stocking, removes overabundant stunted or coarse fish, removes disease carriers from hatchery water supplies, and prevents fish from clogging pump intake screens.
2. **Partial kill:** Thins stunted populations to free space and food for remaining fish, and supports investigative sampling of species, numbers, sizes, and condition.
3. **Selective kill:** Careful application can kill large numbers of [[Gizzard Shad]] (a nuisance fish) with minimal loss of game fish. Selective kills have also been achieved on [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Freshwater Drum]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature:** Some lakes have been treated successfully at water temperatures as low as 44°F. Best results are expected at 60°F or above, and some manufacturers recommend 70°F or higher for surface waters.
- **Concentration drivers:** Dose depends on purpose, fish species, water temperature, and water quality.
- **Dosing guide (ppm):**

| Type of Use | 5% Mixture (ppm) | Active Rotenone (ppm) |
|---|---|---|
| Selective treatment | 0.10 - 0.13 | 0.005 - 0.007 |
| Normal pond use | 0.5 - 1.0 | 0.025 - 0.050 |
| Remove [[Bullheads]] or [[Carp]] | 1.0 - 2.0 | 0.050 - 0.100 |
| Remove bullheads or carp in rich organic ponds | 2.0 - 4.0 | 0.100 - 0.200 |
| Preimpoundment treatment above dam | 3.0 - 5.0 | 0.150 - 0.250 |

- **Volume units:** One [[Acre-Foot]] is 1 acre by 1 foot, equal to 43,560 cubic feet, 326,000 gallons, or 2,718,000 pounds of water.
- **Depth:** Average depth is found by sounding along transects and dividing the sum of soundings by their number.
- **Deep water:** Some formulations do not penetrate pond depths of 20 to 25 feet or more when applied at the surface.
- **Fish response:** Within 10 to 15 minutes of treatment, fish surface and dart about as if disoriented, with gasping. A complete kill is usually obtained in 24 to 36 hours. Fish sink, then rise again as bloating occurs.
- **Detoxification:** Treated water usually detoxifies within a few days to a month. Some colder waters may stay toxic for about a month and a half. Detoxification is faster with lower concentrations, high temperatures, high alkalinity, and abundant light and aeration.
- **Potassium permanganate toxicity (Lawrence, 1955):** At 81°F, it killed [[Bluegill]] at 3 ppm, [[Largemouth Bass]] at 4 ppm, and [[Fathead Minnow]] at 5 ppm. At 58°F, bass and bluegill were killed at about 5 ppm.

## Key Findings

**Dosing and calculation**
- Area and depth example: a pond of 35,700 sq ft (0.82 acre) with an average depth of 32 inches (2.67 ft) from 25 soundings gives 2.19 acre-feet.
- For 5% powdered rotenone, 2.7 pounds equals 1 ppm of mixture per acre-foot.
- General formula: Acre-feet × ppm active rotenone × 2.7 ÷ percent rotenone = pounds to apply.
- Worked example: 2.19 × 0.1 × 2.7 ÷ 0.02 = 29.6 pounds of 2% rotenone for a 0.1 ppm total kill.
- Most manufacturers' data are based on 5% rotenone. Liquids at 5% weigh about 8.3 lb per gallon.

**Application**
- Uniform distribution is essential. Methods include garden sprayers, backpack pumps, boat bailers, garden-hose siphons, gasoline-powered pumps, and hand application.
- Liquids should be pre-mixed with water. Powder must be stirred until lumps dissolve.
- Example: a 1-acre pond with a 9-foot maximum depth treated with 1 gallon of 5% rotenone. One person needs about 30 minutes. Two people (one in the boat, one on shore) need 10 to 15 minutes.
- Deep areas can be treated with a weighted garden hose or perforated sprinkler hose (a 1/4 to 1/2 lb weight is suggested).

**Detoxification and safety**
- [[Potassium Permanganate]] can neutralize rotenone, protect downstream fish if a pond overflows, and reduce damage to non-target species such as bass. Potassium permanganate is itself toxic to fish. A burlap bag of crystals towed behind a boat is a practical method for ponds.
- Rescue of distressed fish into untreated water can work, but most fish are collected too late for revival.
- Test water for toxicity before stocking. A [[Post 1955 Rotenone Test]] (chemical test) is fairly accurate. A live-box test with intended stocking species should include one box in the deep, cool water, because residual toxicity or low oxygen can both cause mortality.

**Conclusions (eight points)**
1. A fishery biologist should determine the need for rotenone before application.
2. Permission should be obtained from proper State authorities.
3. The volume of water to be treated must be determined accurately.
4. Use the minimum rotenone recommended for the treatment type.
5. Distribute rotenone uniformly through the treated volume.
6. Test waters for toxicity before stocking.
7. Follow approved State and Federal stocking recommendations for species, numbers, and timing.
8. Keep non-stocked fish out, using bank and spillway modification and restrictions on bait fish.
