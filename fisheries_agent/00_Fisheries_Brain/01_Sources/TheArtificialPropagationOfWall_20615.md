# Artificial Propagation of Walleyes in Kansas (1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #walleye

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report by [[Frank Schryer]], Fisheries Biologist, to the [[Kansas Forestry, Fish And Game Commission]] documents a spring spawn-taking program for [[Walleye]] at [[Fall River Reservoir]]. Kansas had no in-state source of walleye eggs, and out-of-state supplies were uncertain, so biologists tested whether eggs could be obtained from wild fish in the reservoir. Fish were captured with [[Gill Net]]s from February 20 to April 20, 1956, and the eggs were fertilized and incubated at the [[Pratt Hatchery]]. The program yielded an estimated 25,000 to 30,000 fry, which the author described as insufficient for statewide demand but useful for planning future operations. The report recommends changes to netting methods, avoidance of transporting females, and holding ripe males on site. The author concludes that Kansas walleyes may behave differently from northern populations, so northern spawn-taking methods may not transfer directly.

## Background & Description

- **Species focus:** [[Walleye]], a fish stocked in Kansas reservoirs. The source does not give a scientific name.
- **Stocking history:**
  - [[Fall River Reservoir]] received its first stocking of 695,000 walleye fry in May 1952.
  - A second stocking of 100,000 fry followed in May 1953 (OCR-garbled in source; "1.00,000" and "195" read as 100,000 and 1953).
  - Fish from the 1952 stocking were expected to be sexually mature by 1956.
- **Rationale:** The Commission had no in-state egg source. Out-of-state supply was uncertain, so the agency investigated [[Fall River Reservoir]] as a local spawn source.
- **Spawning timing:** The Kansas spawning period was unknown. Biologists expected it to be earlier than in northern states, where spawning is usually in April or early May.
- **Study site:** [[Speers Cove]] was selected in a visual survey because of its gravel bottom in one to five feet of water.
- **Gear used:**
  - 720 feet of 2¼-inch square-measure [[Gill Net]]s in four sections, set in Speers Cove.
  - One 250-foot experimental gill net with mesh from 1 inch to 2 inches.
  - Later, two 50-yard and one 70-yard gill nets of 2½-inch mesh were used at the dam face.
- **Wild fish characteristics:**
  - The first three fish (two males, one female), taken in water 1½ to 11 feet deep, were all sexually mature, three-year-old fish coming four years old.
  - The female was 22 inches long and weighed 3.9 pounds. The males were 20.5 inches long and weighed 2.5 pounds each.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diet:** Walleyes were feeding heavily on [[Gizzard Shad]] during late February, with estimated pre-digestion lengths of 3 to 6½ inches. Two to three shad of this size were found in each stomach examined.
- **Habitat and bottom type:**
  - Speers Cove had a gravel bottom in 1 to 5 feet of water.
  - The lake bottom at the dam face was clay with many scattered rocks, in 4 to 12 feet of water.
- **Water temperature:**
  - The hatchery water at Pratt was 59 to 60 °F.
  - The author noted that northern-state hatchery water ranged from the lower 40s to the middle 50s °F, which helps explain the shorter incubation time observed at Pratt.
- **Latitude and environment:** The author argues that Kansas walleyes live in a different environment from the northern states, where the species occurs in larger numbers. This may cause different reproductive behavior.

## Key Findings

**Netting and sex ratio timeline (Fall River Reservoir, 1956):**

| Period | Result |
|---|---|
| Feb 21 (first netting) | 3 fish taken: 2 males, 1 female, all sexually mature |
| Feb 29 week | 18 females and 5 males; females averaged 4.6 lb, males 3.2 lb |
| Mar 6–9 | 14 females and 6 males; one 16-inch immature one-year-old female |
| Mar 14 | Gear moved to dam face; 14 walleyes taken |
| Mar 20 | 44 walleyes (37 females, 7 males); cumulative 91 females and 23 males (about 20% males) |
| Mar 27–28 | 70 females and 102 males; females near ripe, males dead ripe |
| Week of Apr 1 | Spawning believed to have begun around April 1 (± 2 days); peak not yet reached |
| Apr 8–14 | 18 fish: 8 ripe females, 1 ripe male, 3 spent females, 6 near-ripe females |
| Apr 15–20 | 23 females: 6 very ripe (taken night of Apr 16 and early Apr 17), 17 spent |

**Key operational results:**

- **Captive ripening:** Nine near-ripe females and three ripe males were held in concrete tanks at the [[Pratt Hatchery]] for about two weeks. One female ripened and was fertilized, but the hatch was low. The author suggested three possible causes: poor milt condition, unnatural captivity, and too much water flow into the hatching jar.
- **Kanopolis males:** Male catch at [[Kanopolis Reservoir]] fell to zero after the March 25–31 peak. Two ripe males were transported from Kanopolis to Pratt.
- **Transport losses:** Four ripe females were taken from Fall River to Pratt, but only one survived. Mechanical problems in transit were blamed for the other three deaths.
- **Best egg lot:** On April 11, eggs from two partly spent females were stripped and fertilized at Fall River Reservoir using one ripe male. Fertilized eggs were washed, hardened in clay water, and placed in a bobbinet-bottomed box in the lake for three days (about 50,000 eggs). About 20,000 fry hatched. The author said this lot looked better than any other.
- **Hatching time:** At Pratt, eggs hatched in 7 to 8 days, which the author described as 10 to 12 days sooner than in northern hatcheries.
- **Total output:** Estimated 25,000 to 30,000 walleye fry for the season. The author called this insufficient for Kansas demand but valuable for future planning.

**Recommendations:**

1. Set [[Gill Net]]s off the dam face and other possible spawning areas during the first or second week of March to assess ripeness, with a single overnight set likely sufficient. Record water temperature at several depths.
2. In the third week of March, intensify netting and add [[Trap Net]]s with wings, which the author expected to cause less harm to fish and prevent egg loss from entangled females.
3. Do not transport female walleyes away from the reservoir for spawn taking, because eggs from transported females had very low hatchability.
4. If ripe males arrive before females, hold a sufficient number in captivity at the upper reaches of [[Speers Cove]] or another low-disturbance site, protected from people and kept as secret as possible.
5. If too few males are caught at Fall River, catch males at [[Kanopolis Reservoir]] and transport them to Fall River for fertilization.
6. Plan for [[Kanopolis Reservoir]] as a possible spawn source in spring 1957, when females from the 1953 stocking will be four years old and capable of reproduction.

**Conclusions:**

- Methods from northern "walleye states" may not apply directly to Kansas, because the environment differs.
- The author speculated that adequate spawn could be taken at regular annual intervals once more local facts about spawning and spawn-taking were established.
- Reproductive potential is high. A four-pound female yields about 100,000 eggs. With an estimated 50% incubation loss, eggs from about 20 females would produce one million fry.
- The Commission should use this high reproductive potential if it wants to extend walleye populations to other suitable Kansas waters.
