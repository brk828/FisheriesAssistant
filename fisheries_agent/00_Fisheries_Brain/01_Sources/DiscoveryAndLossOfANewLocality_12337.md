## Executive Summary
This note documents the discovery and subsequent rapid extirpation of a previously unknown population of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) in the [[San Pedro River]] Basin, Arizona. Found in July 1978 in an artesian well outflow near Mammoth, Arizona, this locality represented a significant range extension for the subspecies, as only one prior record existed from this tributary basin. Within approximately three months, agricultural land clearing, well modification, siltation, and eventual capping of the artesian flow led to the complete loss of the habitat and extirpation of the population, despite notification of federal and state wildlife agencies. The case illustrates the vulnerability of isolated topminnow populations to both habitat destruction and competitive displacement by the introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).

## Background & Description
- No detailed morphological data provided for individual specimens.
- Species identification confirmed via collection and deposition of voucher specimens in the Arizona State University fish collection.
- Population density visually estimated at 500–1,000 individuals in the upper 15 m of outflow habitat during initial survey (July 1978).
- Co-occurring [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) were collected alongside topminnows in a ratio of approximately 10:1 (mosquitofish:topminnow) by late September 1978.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type**: Artesian well outflow forming a marshy area (~15 m long × 2 m wide) draining into a small stream extending several hundred meters before joining the normally dry channel of the [[San Pedro River]].
- **Location**: 111 Ranch, 13 km SE of Mammoth, Arizona (R1E, T9S, Sec. 24), near the east side of the San Pedro River channel.
- **Basin context**: The [[Gila River Basin]] historically supported the Gila topminnow throughout most of its extent in Arizona and New Mexico; by the time of this report, natural populations were restricted to seven known localities, all in Arizona, plus limited experimental reintroductions.
- **Sympatry with invasive species**: Few localities are known where *Poeciliopsis* and *Gambusia affinis* co-occur naturally; their sympatry at this site was interpreted as a transient state, with the hypothesis that *Gambusia* had not yet had sufficient time to competitively exclude *Poeciliopsis*.
- **Threats**: Primary drivers of decline in the broader [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] range include habitat loss from water diversions, impoundments, groundwater pumping, and competitive interactions with introduced [[Mosquitofish]]. Accelerated decline of the subspecies began in the 1930s following mosquitofish introduction.
- **Related taxon**: A related subspecies, *P. o. sonoriensis*, occurs in the Rio Yaqui Basin of extreme SE Arizona and NW Mexico.

## Key Findings
- **New locality discovery (Jul. 1978)**: First confirmed viable population of Gila topminnow within the San Pedro River Basin tributary system, a major tributary of the Gila River, apart from a single prior isolated record.
- **Rapid habitat degradation timeline**:
  - Jul. 1978: Population discovered; habitat intact but adjacent mesquite clearing already underway for agricultural development.
  - Early Sep. 1978: Well connected to irrigation pipe; flow rerouted, causing new channel erosion and siltation reducing marsh depth to ≤5 cm; topminnows became scarce.
  - Late Sep. 1978: Collection confirmed sympatric mosquitofish and topminnow (10:1 ratio).
  - Early Oct. 1978: Well pipe repaired/capped, eliminating artesian flow entirely; habitat completely dried; population extirpated.
- **Conservation gap**: Because the site was on private land, it fell outside protections of the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]]. No attempts were made to negotiate flow retention with landowners or to salvage/relocate a portion of the population, despite its potential to harbor unique genetic components.
- **Competitive dynamics**: The brief period of coexistence between *Gambusia affinis* and *Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis* supports the hypothesis that competitive exclusion by mosquitofish operates over a time-lagged process rather than immediate displacement.
- **Broader implication**: This case exemplifies the fragility of newly discovered or relict populations of endangered [[Poeciliidae]] species in the arid Southwest, where anthropogenic water management and agricultural development can rapidly negate discoveries of conservation significance.
