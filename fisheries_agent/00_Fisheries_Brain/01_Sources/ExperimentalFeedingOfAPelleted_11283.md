# Experimental Feeding of a Pelleted Trout Food to Large Fingerling and Yearling Brook, Brown, and Rainbow Trout (1955-1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #aquaculture #fish-nutrition

## Executive Summary
This study, conducted at the Lanesboro State Fisheries Station in Minnesota, compared a novel pelleted diet (Minnesota Pelleted Diet No. 1, "P-1") against the standard meal-and-beef-spleen diet in [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]] fingerlings/yearlings over seven 31-day feeding periods (~7 months). The trial assessed growth rate, feed conversion, blood chemistry, condition factor, liver health, mortality, and economic cost. Results indicated the pellet diet produced statistically indistinguishable growth rates compared to the standard diet, with superior [[Feed Conversion Ratio]] and lower production costs (16.3–31.6% cost reduction), though it caused mild physiological side effects (lower hemoglobin, liver discoloration in Brown Trout) not present with the traditional diet.

## Background & Description
Data primarily concern body condition and internal organ health rather than external morphology, given the aquacultural focus.

- **Condition Factor (K/C):** Calculated as (Weight in lbs × 100,000) / Length³. Fish fed the pellet diet exhibited **higher condition factors** across all three species compared to those on the regular diet:
  - Brook Trout: Pellet 41.25 vs. Regular 40.21
  - Brown Trout: Pellet 44.04 vs. Regular 43.88
  - Rainbow Trout: Pellet 44.21 vs. Regular 38.22
- **Liver Condition (Gross Examination):**
  - Brook and Rainbow Trout: No visible difference in liver coloration between diets.
  - Brown Trout: Pellet-fed fish showed **slightly yellow livers**, contrasting with the healthy red liver coloration of regular-diet fish, suggesting a diet-related pigmentation/nutritional effect despite normal blood parameters.
- **Size at Start/End of Experiment (Table 1):**
  - Brook Trout: 25 fish/lb → ~147–161 fish/lb (growth from ~625 fish/25 lbs to ~607–614 fish at 3.8–4.1 lbs/fish implied density change)
  - Brown Trout: 40 fish/lb → ~184–196 fish/lb
  - Rainbow Trout: 28.8 fish/lb → ~155–156 fish/lb

## Ecological & Environmental Context
This is a controlled hatchery/aquaculture study rather than a natural ecosystem survey, so "ecological niche" is represented by rearing conditions:

- **Habitat:** Concrete raceways, 40 ft long × 5 ft wide, average water depth 16 inches.
- **Water Supply:** Each raceway had an independent supply; average flow ~50 gallons/minute.
- **Water Temperature:** Constant at 48°F throughout the experiment.
- **Stocking Density:** Each raceway started with exactly 25 lbs of fingerlings (625 [[Brook Trout]], 1,000 [[Brown Trout]], or 720 [[Rainbow Trout]]).
- **Feeding Regimen:**
  - Regular diet: fed twice daily, following the [[Cortland Feeding Level]] (Deuel, Haskell & Tunison 1942).
  - Pellet diet: fed ~4 times daily at 65% of the Cortland rate (adjusted for moisture content differences).
- **Trophic Inputs:**
  - Standard diet: 55% dry meal mixture + 45% freshly ground beef spleen (semimoist, prepared daily, refrigerated at 32°F).
  - Pellet diet (P-1): Dry pellet (1/8 inch diameter), no fresh meat supplement; contained unextracted beef liver meal (12%) in place of higher soy/cottonseed proportions.

## Key Findings

### Growth & Statistical Comparison
- Aggregate percentage weight gain over the full trial:
  - Brook Trout: Pellet 214.3% vs. Regular 203.1%
  - Brown Trout: Pellet 240.5% vs. Regular 224.9%
  - Rainbow Trout: Pellet 207.8% vs. Regular 209.5%
- [[Chi-Square Test]] (6 df) showed no statistically significant difference in growth rate between diets for any species (Brook: 1.388; Brown: 2.332; Rainbow: 2.292).

### Feed Conversion
- Pellet diet produced markedly better **wet-weight conversion ratios**:
  - Brook Trout: 1.69 (pellet) vs. 2.59 (regular)
  - Brown Trout: 1.38 (pellet) vs. 2.36 (regular)
  - Rainbow Trout: 1.86 (pellet) vs. 2.60 (regular)
- On a **dry-weight basis**, differences were smaller and more mixed (e.g., Brook: 1.60 vs. 1.64; Brown: 1.31 vs. 1.49; Rainbow: 1.76 vs. 1.64), indicating much of the apparent conversion advantage of pellets stemmed from lower moisture content rather than intrinsic nutritional superiority.

### Hematology ([[Red Blood Cell Count]] & [[Hemoglobin]])
- Red blood cell counts were statistically similar between diets for all species (no significant difference at 95% probability level), all within/above the normal hatchery range (800,000–1,200,000 cells/mm³ per Tunison et al. 1943).
- Hemoglobin levels:
  - Brown Trout: No significant difference between diets.
  - Brook Trout: Significant difference — lower hemoglobin on pellet diet.
  - Rainbow Trout: Highly significant difference — lower hemoglobin on pellet diet.
- Despite lower hemoglobin in pellet-fed Brook and Rainbow Trout, all values remained above the anemia threshold identified by [[Phillips 1940]] (<710,000 RBC/mm³ associated with mortality).

### Mortality
- Overall mortality was low across all six experimental groups; no statistical analysis was deemed necessary.
- Recorded totals (Table 4): Brook Trout 14 (both diets); Brown Trout 4 (pellet) vs. data incomplete for regular; Rainbow Trout 8 (regular) — mortality broadly comparable between diets.

### Economic Analysis
- Pellet diet cost more per 100 lbs as delivered ($12.56) than the prepared regular diet ($10.74, including $1.40/100 lbs labor cost), but its superior conversion efficiency resulted in **lower overall cost per pound of trout produced**:
  - Brown Trout: 31.6% cost reduction
  - Brook Trout: 23.6% cost reduction
  - Rainbow Trout: 16.3% cost reduction (attributed to unintentional overfeeding/poor conversion in growth periods 1 and 3)
  - Mean cost reduction across species: 23.8%
- Labor savings: Regular diet preparation required 1.09 man-hours per 100 lbs of food (grinding spleen, mixing); pellet diet eliminated this labor and the need for refrigeration/mixing equipment.

- Protein content: Standard diet dry portion 46.8% (30.4% of complete wet diet); Pellet diet 42.1% protein, 5.2% moisture (vs. 36.7% moisture in complete regular diet).

### Conclusion
The [[Minnesota Pelleted Diet No. 1]] was judged a viable, cost-effective substitute for the traditional meal-and-spleen diet in trout hatchery operations, offering equivalent growth performance, improved feed efficiency, and significant labor/cost savings — though minor physiological trade-offs (lower hemoglobin, Brown Trout liver discoloration) suggest potential benefit from periodic supplementation with fresh beef spleen.
