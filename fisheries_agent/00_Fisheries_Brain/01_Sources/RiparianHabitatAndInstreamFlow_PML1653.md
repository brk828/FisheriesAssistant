# Lower Verde River Riparian Habitat and Instream Flow Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) draft report assesses how flow regime controls fish habitat and riparian vegetation on the [[Verde River]] downstream of [[Bartlett Dam]], within the [[Fort McDowell Reservation]] in Arizona. Flows since the dam's 1939–40 completion have been erratic and largely set by irrigation, municipal, and hydropower demands, with near-zero flows and a 1977 near-dewatering. The authors apply the [[Incremental Analysis Method]] and [[Weighted Usable Area]] (WUA) curves to predict habitat for key fishes, evaluate stream temperatures, and examine cottonwood decline linked to groundwater drawdown by the City of Phoenix well field. The report recommends a continuous minimum flow of at least 200 cfs, with no reduction below 100 cfs under drought conditions.

## Background & Description

**Flow history.** Before 1939 the Verde was free-flowing, with a mean monthly flow of 791 cfs and a lowest recorded mean daily flow of 29 cfs. Since 1940 the mean monthly flow has been 397 cfs, but the flows are highly variable. Bartlett Dam has no power generation, so releases mainly supplement flows on the Salt River reservoirs, which produce hydropower.

**Study fish community.** A 1975–76 survey for the [[Orme Dam]] environmental study recorded 16 fish species in the mainstream and backwaters, of which four are native. Total catch was 6,776 fish. The most abundant species was the introduced [[Red Shiner]]. The native [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Longfin Dace]] were common, whereas [[Roundtail Chub]] was uncommon and no young were documented. Introduced sport fishes include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Green Sunfish]].

**Riparian setting.** The floodplain supports mesquite, arrowweed, seepwillow, and saltcedar, with [[Fremont Cottonwood]] and Goodding willow along the channel and old channel sections. Fluctuating flows prevent most tree establishment below the first terrace.

**Methodological framework.** The Incremental Analysis Method, developed by the Instream Flow Service Group (USFWS, Fort Collins, Colorado), predicts habitat available at different discharges. It relies on hydraulic simulation of depth, velocity, and substrate across transects, plus probability-of-use criteria for each species and life stage. Composite probability is the product of the depth, velocity, and substrate probabilities. WUA is the cell area multiplied by that probability, summed across the reach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and water temperature.** Low or intermittent flows, combined with high summer heat, produce conditions unfavorable to cold-water fishes. At 47 cfs, 58°F water released from Bartlett Dam warmed to 84°F about 12 miles downstream, an increase of more than 2°F per mile. Summer temperatures regularly exceeded 80°F. Rainbow trout prefer temperatures below 68–70°F, while [[Moyle]] reports optimal growth between 55–70°F and a preferred range of 55–65°F for brown trout.

**Invertebrates.** Riffles with cobble substrate support the most diverse invertebrate communities, including mayflies, stoneflies, and caddisflies. These taxa are sensitive to low dissolved oxygen, which occurs at low flows and high temperatures. Fluctuating flows limit colonization and standing crops of [[Stream Invertebrates]], which are key prey for most fishes.

**Groundwater linkage.** The USGS (McDonald et al., 1945) found that the Verde recharges the aquifer that supplies the City of Phoenix well field, a 2,960-acre field with 14 wells. Maximum recharge from the river is about 2 feet per day over the wetted area, halved under silt conditions. The USGS concluded that about 200 cfs (possibly as low as 100 cfs) is needed to supply 7–8 MGD without lowering the water table. Well No. 14 goes dry occasionally, and the water table may drop more than 10 feet below the river surface.

**Riparian dynamics.** Riparian plants depend on surface flows and near-surface groundwater. Dams have reduced flood frequency and sediment deposition, limiting the seedbeds needed for cottonwood and willow regeneration. Cottonwood seed dispersal runs from early April to early June, and seedlings are highly drought-intolerant, so the roots must reach the water table.

**Ecosystem values.** The Fort McDowell reach is one of the few remaining mixed cottonwood-mesquite-willow habitats in Arizona. It supports wildlife, hunting, fishing, birding, photography, flood control, groundwater recharge, and bank stabilization. [[Bald Eagle]] diets along the river include native suckers and channel catfish, which together made up nearly 70 percent of fish remains at eagle nests.

## Key Findings

**Flow requirements and habitat**
- Adult rainbow and brown trout reach maximum WUA at 200–300 cfs. Rainbow trout show a 77 percent WUA reduction at 50 cfs, versus 29 percent for brown trout.
- Warmwater species such as [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] decline sharply above about 200 cfs because they tolerate only about 1–2 ft/s velocities. None is expected to support a good sport fishery. Adult smallmouth bass have the greatest WUA of the four, yet still less than 8 percent of gross area up to 1,500 cfs.
- Channel catfish fry show very high WUA (50–60 percent of gross area), but adults peak at only 3.3 percent of gross area at 600–800 cfs. The river likely maintains a small reproducing population, supplemented by reservoir recruitment.
- Carp fry benefit from flows below 200 cfs, with maximum WUA at 50–100 cfs. The red shiner is expected to decline at 200–300 cfs.
- Roundtail chub may benefit from 200–300 cfs, but its recovery is constrained by the lack of young.
- White sucker curves, used as a surrogate for native suckers, peak at 300 cfs with 48.5 percent utilization of total area.
- Example WUA calculation: 1,000 ft² of marginal habitat (composite probability 0.27) equals about 270 ft² of optimum habitat.

**Stocking and trout management**
- About 10,000 eight-inch rainbow trout are stocked annually (November–March) and disappear quickly, probably through fishing pressure, downstream movement, and summer mortality.
- Neither rainbow nor brown trout is expected to reproduce successfully because of high temperatures, erratic flows, and poor spawning substrate, so stocking must continue.
- Brown trout have never been stocked, but sub-catchable introduction was proposed for 1981.

**Cottonwood decline**
- Cottonwood mortality averaged 45 percent after 1977, versus less than 4 percent before. Mortality reached 84 percent in the four-mile reach below Sycamore Creek, and 60–84 percent in areas influenced by the Phoenix well field.
- Very little mortality occurred along Lower Sycamore Creek, where the water table remained high.
- Mortality coincided with the 1977 drought, when Bartlett Dam releases were nearly eliminated during the growing season.
- Mesquite, saltcedar, and Goodding willow were less affected, reflecting their greater drought tolerance.
- The report concludes that the cottonwood community is "on the verge of collapse" without positive intervention.

**Recommendations**
- Maintain a continuous flow of at least 200 cfs below Bartlett Dam at all times.
- If 200 cfs cannot be maintained during drought, the flow should never drop below 100 cfs. This level is sufficient to recharge the aquifer during heavy Phoenix pumping and maintain existing vegetation.
- Consider periodic high releases roughly every 8–10 years, sustained through the spring and summer growing season, to maintain a productive cottonwood community.
- Where natural flooding is inadequate, use replanting. Arizona State University techniques (planting cottonwood and native trees with supplemental water until roots reach the water table) are being applied to the 20-acre Box Bar Cottonwood stand.
- A Riparian Habitat Analysis Group study on the middle [[Rio Grande]] is testing deep cuttings as a low-cost reestablishment method.
