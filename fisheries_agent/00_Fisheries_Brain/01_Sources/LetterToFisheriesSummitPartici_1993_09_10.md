# Letter to Fisheries Summit Participants, Arboretum at Flagstaff

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #native-fish-conservation #cyprinidae #catostomidae #invasive-species #southwestern-us-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1993 letter, submitted by field biologists [[David R. Van Haverbeke]] and [[John K. Cook]] to a Fisheries Summit at the Arboretum in Flagstaff, Arizona, presents a conservation strategy for declining native fish populations across Arizona's watersheds. The authors argue that pervasive establishment of exotic fish species — rather than habitat degradation alone — is the primary driver of native fish decline throughout the state's major river systems (Salt River, Verde River, Gila River, Grand Canyon/Colorado River corridor). They propose designating tributary "sanctuaries" as the most viable conservation strategy, since these smaller, less-invaded streams retain the last strongholds of native fish assemblages. The letter also calls for improved public education, inter-agency coordination, and formal review of exotic fish stocking practices.

## Background & Description

While this document is primarily a policy/conservation letter rather than a morphological study, it identifies and distinguishes several native fish taxa by common ecological/functional roles within the assemblage:

- **[[Lepidomeda vittata]]** (Little Colorado spinedace) — small-bodied cyprinid; documented as vulnerable to displacement by introduced [[rainbow trout]] in Nutrioso Creek.
- **[[Meda fulgida]]** (spikedace) — small minnow, potentially present in West Clear Creek; part of the broader Verde River native fish community.
- **[[Gila robusta]]** (roundtail chub) — larger-bodied cyprinid, occupies a "chub" niche within native assemblages.
- **[[Gila intermedia]]** (Gila chub) — closely related congener to roundtail chub, noted as a potential (but unconfirmed) resident of West Clear Creek.
- **[[Catostomus insignis]]** (Sonora sucker) and **[[Pantosteus clarki]]** (desert sucker) — benthic sucker species (Catostomidae), representing the sucker component of native communities.
- **[[Rhinichthys osculus]]** (speckled dace) — small minnow, widely distributed across surveyed native fish habitats.
- **[[Agosia chrysogaster]]** (longfin dace) — small minnow, potential co-occurring species in West Clear Creek.

Collectively, these species represent a full trophic guild structure — "small minnows, suckers, and chub" — characteristic of intact Verde River-type native fish communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat structure**: Native fish strongholds are consistently located in **headwater and tributary drainages** rather than main-stem rivers, a pattern repeated across multiple Arizona watersheds:
  - **[[Salt River]]** — main stem (Hwy 60 bridge to Lake Roosevelt) is physically pristine (no impoundments/pollution) yet nearly devoid of native fish due to dominance of exotic predators; refuge populations persist only in tributaries like **[[Tonto Creek]]**.
  - **[[Grand Canyon]] / Colorado River** — cold-water releases from Lake Powell have decimated native fish spawning; warm-water tributaries (**[[Paria River]]**, **[[Little Colorado River]]**, **[[Kanab Creek]]**) serve as critical spawning refuges.
  - **[[Gila River]]** — native stronghold persists in **[[Aravaipa Creek]]**, which has escaped heavy mining pollution and exotic introduction.
  - **[[Verde River]]** — native fish decline sharply below the town of Tapco, coinciding with near-total replacement by exotic fish fauna; upper Verde River remains a stronghold but is threatened by proposed water diversion.

- **Exotic species impacts**: The Salt River supports large populations of introduced flathead catfish, channel catfish, smallmouth bass, fathead minnows, red shiners, mosquitofish, and carp, which predate on native fish eggs, larvae, juveniles, and adults — driving near-total extirpation of natives in invaded reaches.

- **Case study — Nutrioso Creek**: Direct literature evidence ([[Blinn et al. 1993]]) documents displacement of **[[Lepidomeda vittata]]** by introduced **[[Oncorhynchus mykiss]]** (rainbow trout), demonstrating a documented predation-driven mechanism of native fish decline.

- **Multi-jurisdictional challenge**: Native fish habitats such as the Little Colorado River span multiple management jurisdictions (Fort Apache Indian Reservation, Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest, private land, state land, Navajo Indian Reservation, Grand Canyon National Park), complicating unified conservation action.

## Key Findings

1. **Tributary refuge pattern**: Across at least four independent Arizona watersheds (Salt, Colorado/Grand Canyon, Gila, Verde), native fish populations persist disproportionately in tributary and headwater streams rather than main-stem rivers, strongly implicating exotic species invasion (rather than habitat degradation alone) as the primary threat in "pristine-looking" main-stem reaches.

2. **Proposed sanctuary tributaries**: The authors recommend prioritizing conservation/management action in **[[Nutrioso Creek]]**, upper **[[Verde River]]**, **[[West Clear Creek]]**, **[[Tonto Creek]]**, and **[[Aravaipa Creek]]** as the strongest remaining native fish systems.

3. **West Clear Creek community composition**: Confirmed to harbor at least four native species (speckled dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker, roundtail chub), with potential additional presence of Gila chub, longfin dace, and spikedace — representing a nearly complete Verde River native fish assemblage in a single tributary, and identified as a priority conservation opportunity due to recent Forest Service acquisition of the Bull Pen area.

4. **Aravaipa Creek significance**: Identified as possibly the most diverse native fish assemblage remaining in Arizona; authors call for reinitiating previously proposed exotic fish control (barrier dam) funding to protect this system.

5. **Documented predation mechanism**: [[Blinn et al. 1993]] (*Trans. Am. Fish. Soc.* 122:139-143) provides rare direct evidence in Arizona literature of rainbow trout predation causally displacing a native cyprinid (Little Colorado spinedace), supporting broader (largely international/Australian) literature on exotic-driven ecosystem collapse.

6. **Policy recommendations**:
   - Establish formal native fish sanctuaries in tributaries.
   - Create multi-agency, watershed-scale management goals centered on native fish enhancement.
   - Institute a review panel/process for evaluating past, current, and future exotic fish stocking programs.
   - Expand public education efforts via wider distribution of existing materials (native fish posters, *Battle Against Extinction* by [[Minckley and Deacon 1991]], USFS technical reports, educational t-shirts).
