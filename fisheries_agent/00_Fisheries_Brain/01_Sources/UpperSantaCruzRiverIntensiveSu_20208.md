# Upper Santa Cruz River Intensive Survey: Water Quality and Biology of an Effluent-Dominated Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #aquatic-ecology #water-quality

## Executive Summary

The Arizona Department of Environmental Quality (ADEQ) report, prepared by Lin Lawson (June 1995), documents a monthly intensive survey of water quality, fish and macroinvertebrates in the upper [[Santa Cruz River]] from November 1992 to October 1993. The study was a cooperative effort between ADEQ staff, volunteers from the Friends of the Santa Cruz River (FOSCR), the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and other agencies. Its main focus was how [[Nogales International Wastewater Treatment Plant]] (NIWTP) effluent changes as it travels 16 miles downstream to Tubac.

Key conclusions: bacterial standard violations were minimal, but the data did not support tightening standards to full body contact, and visitors were strongly advised to minimize water contact. Metals did not appear to threaten aquatic life. Un-ionized ammonia near the plant was severely impairing fish and macroinvertebrate communities, which partially recovered downstream but not to control-site conditions. Volunteer-generated data were judged of high quality, and ADEQ recommended a formal volunteer monitoring program.

## Background & Description

The upper Santa Cruz River is a perennial, effluent-sustained stream in the [[Basin and Range Province]]. Its flow near the study reach is dominated by treated effluent (12 to 15 million gallons per day, about 18.6 to 23.2 cfs), which supports riparian vegetation of [[Fremont Cottonwood]] and [[Gooding Willow]] and attracts wildlife and recreation. Concerns included pathogens, heavy metals, toxic organics, nutrients, and contamination from the Nogales Wash, which carries Mexican wastewater, chlorinated discharges, and reported diesel and DDT residues.

Four sites were sampled:
- **SC19** (control, near Guevavi Ranch): spring-fed, relatively unimpacted, sustains longfin dace.
- **SC2** (Calabasas Road bridge, Rio Rico): 1.5 miles below the NIWTP; most impacted.
- **SC18/20** (replacement site after the January 1993 flood): 7.5 to 9.5 miles downstream.
- **SC17** (near Tubac): 16 miles downstream, the most downstream site.

Study limitations: toxic organics, organochlorines, human pathogens and groundwater were not analyzed, and only four sites were feasible given funding and staffing.

The study used standard EPA and Standard Methods for water chemistry, fecal indicator bacteria, and macroinvertebrate and fish collections. Macroinvertebrates were assessed with an [[Arkansas Biometric Assessment Method]] borrowed from low-gradient Arkansas streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and climate.** The region is arid, with about 16 inches of annual precipitation and bimodal rainfall. Typical border flows range from less than 1 cfs in summer to 30 cfs in winter. A January 1993 flood brought rainfall 430 percent above normal, with a peak of 5,880 cfs at the upstream gage and 14,800 cfs at the downstream gage. The flood scoured aquatic vegetation and macroinvertebrates and disrupted sampling for about five months.

**Channel.** The channel widens from 150 to 250 feet downstream. Water depth rarely exceeds 0.5 feet, the bed is loose sand, and riffles are uncommon.

**Water chemistry.** Specific conductance and hardness increased downstream, with the highest TDS at SC17. Alkalinity was high and bicarbonate-dominated, which buffered pH. The water is hard, and fluoride averaged about 0.54 mg/L, with no detectable NIWTP contribution.

**Nutrients and oxygen.** Nitrogen species and phosphorus were highest at SC2 and fell rapidly downstream, with SC17 approaching control levels. Total nitrogen at SC17 averaged 257 percent above SC19, and total phosphorus 470 percent above. Dissolved oxygen was lowest at SC2, which had summer lows of 3.0 to 4.0 mg/L. The 90 percent saturation standard was consistently exceeded below the NIWTP.

**Bacteria.** Fecal coliform peaks were partly attributed to livestock, since grazing is common. Fecal streptococci peaked in August and were thought to reflect runoff from animal feces.

## Key Findings

**Physical and chemical**
- Water temperatures closely tracked air temperature (Pearson R = 0.83). Air temperatures can swing 30 to 40 °F within 24 hours.
- Unpolluted southeast Arizona waters have mean pH 8.1 (n = 158). All sites stayed within the 6.5 to 9.0 standard, with no exceedances.
- Turbidity exceeded the 50 NTU standard only during receding flood waters.
- Streamflow gained about 31 percent from SC2 to SC18/20, then declined by a similar amount to SC17. This was attributed to possible groundwater inflow, measurement error, evaporation, and groundwater loss.
- Total cyanide was detected in two of 40 samples at the control site (10 and 7 µg/L), below the 41 µg/L acute standard. The source was unknown.
- Total chlorine data were invalid because of contaminated reagent pillows, so chlorine was excluded from analysis.

**Metals**
- Six of eighteen total metals had higher loads below the plant. Barium, boron and manganese were attributed to effluent. Copper, iron and strontium were attributed partly to higher flow and turbidity downstream.
- Of 448 metals observations, one exceedance occurred: total lead at SC19 (68 µg/L, 13 Oct 1993), a 0.2 percent exceedance rate.
- The authors concluded metals were not biologically significant or a threat to aquatic life.

**Bacteria**
- Violations of the 800 CFU/100 ml fecal coliform standard were minimal.
- The authors did not support tightening standards to full body contact. They noted that human pathogens (e.g., Giardia, Cryptosporidium, viruses) are not reliably indicated by bacterial tests and that chlorination does not inactivate them.
- Strong recommendation: visitors should restrict water contact downstream of the NIWTP to a minimum.

**Dissolved oxygen and BOD**
- Highest BOD occurred immediately below the NIWTP and declined rapidly. Black ooze and abundant aquatic plants suggested organic matter was being stored in sediments.
- Pre-sunrise measurements (22–23 June 1993) found SC2 oxygen above anoxic levels (about 4.0 mg/L), though with low saturation.

**Un-ionized ammonia**
- Ammonia was the most likely primary factor affecting fish populations downstream of the NIWTP. Mean longfin dace density declined as mean un-ionized ammonia increased.
- Concentrations at SC2 were consistently above 3.0 mg/L, except during high water in February 1993. Toxicity data suggest acute effects possible at 3.4 mg/L and chronic effects above 0.5 mg/L.

**Macroinvertebrates**
- The community was severely impaired near the plant. Biometric scores showed a bell-shaped recovery trend with distance and peaked in spring, but did not reach control-site conditions.
- SC2 scored excessive or severe impairment. SC18/20 was severely impaired in eight of nine months. SC17 varied from extreme impairment (March 1993) to no impairment (May 1993).

**Fish**
- Longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*) dominated the community. SC2 had the fewest fish in both surveys, and density rose with distance downstream.
- In May 1993, SC2 had one age class, nearly all Age II fish near the end of their lifespan. Age I fish were absent at SC2 but present elsewhere.
- Disease: 14 of 35 randomly inspected fish at SC2 (40 percent) were unhealthy, with none at SC19, two at SC18/20 (7 percent), and two at SC17 (6 percent). A chi-square test (χ² = 26.31, df = 3, critical value 7.82) rejected independence of site and fish health.
- Low dissolved oxygen (lowest 4.10 to 4.85 mg/L at SC2, July to October 1993) was judged within the species' tolerance (it can tolerate 1.0 mg/L briefly), so oxygen alone was considered unlikely to be the primary cause.
- Non-dace species were few: green sunfish (2 at SC2, May), and mosquitofish, Gila sucker and others in December. A [[Gila Topminnow]] (endangered) was observed at SC20 in May and later confirmed there in a 1994 survey.

**Volunteers and program recommendation**
- Volunteer data quality was judged excellent and comparable to staff data. However, the study design lacked a quality control component, which the authors flagged as an error to avoid in future programs.
- ADEQ recommended a Department-supported volunteer stream monitoring program with a full-time coordinator, estimated at a small cost, to supplement the state's 305(b) assessment.
- The supplement proposes trained volunteers conducting habitat, macroinvertebrate and land-use surveys. It suggests partnering with established groups and using EPA guidance, and it stresses training, recognition, and QA/QC.

**Survey conclusions**
- The Santa Cruz River gained water from Rio Rico to Tumacacori and then lost water through evaporation and possibly groundwater losses to Tubac.
- Nitrogen species, total phosphorus and BOD declined rapidly downstream of the NIWTP, and un-ionized ammonia was toxic near the plant but declined with distance.
- Effluent contributed appreciable barium, boron and manganese, with effects on the ecosystem unknown.
