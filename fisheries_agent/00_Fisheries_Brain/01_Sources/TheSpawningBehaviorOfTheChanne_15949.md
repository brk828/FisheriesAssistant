# The Spawning Behavior of the Channel Catfish (Ictalurus punctatus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #catfish #spawning-behavior #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by [[Howard P. Clemens]] and [[Kermit E. Sneed]] documents the spawning behavior of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) induced to spawn in glass aquaria via fish pituitary injections. The research was conducted under the [[Saltonstall-Kennedy Act]] at the [[U.S. Fish Cultural Station]] in [[Tishomingo, Oklahoma]]. The authors conclude that pituitary injections bypassed normal environmental cues but did not fundamentally alter the species' innate spawning pattern. Key findings include detailed descriptions of pre-spawning aggression, pairing behavior, a stereotyped spawning reflex involving tail-wrapping and quivering, egg deposition over approximately 6 hours (~9 releases per hour), and exclusive male parental care post-spawning. The study addresses practical hatchery needs driven by rising demand for [[Channel Catfish]] fingerlings for sport fishing and commercial fish farming in the southern United States.

## Background & Description

[[Channel Catfish]] are economically important as both game and food fish across North America. By the 1950s, demand for fingerlings had surged due to the species' adaptability to diverse habitats, compatibility with [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Crappie]], and superior production rates—[[Brown (1951)]] demonstrated that channel catfish yield more pounds of edible fish per acre than any other species commonly stocked in Texas ponds. Commercial fish farming in rice fields of [[Arkansas]], [[Louisiana]], and [[Texas]] further increased demand, yet hatcheries struggled with inconsistent spawning success due to incomplete knowledge of reproductive biology.

Previous literature on channel catfish spawning was fragmentary. [[Murphree (1940)]] and [[Lenz (1947)]] provided the only direct spawning descriptions; [[Fontaine (1944)]] described [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pilodictis olivaris*) spawning and noted behavioral similarities with channel catfish and [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*). Additional contributions came from [[Shira (1917)]], [[Clapp (1929)]], [[Doze (1925)]], [[Mobley (1931)]], [[Morris (1939)]], [[Rennick (1942)]], [[Toole (1951)]], [[Canfield (1947)]], and [[Davis (1953)]]. Spawning behavior had been documented for related ictalurids: *A. nebulosus* by [[Breder (1935)]], [[Fowler (1917)]], and [[Smith and Harron (1904)]]; *A. catus* and *A. natalis* by Fowler (1917); *A. albidus* by [[Ryder (1883)]]; *Schilbeodes insignis* by Fowler (1917); and *P. olivaris* by Fontaine (1944).

Fish used in this study ranged from 14 to 22 inches total length. Females were selected based on rounded, flaccid abdomens extending past the pelvic fins to the genital orifice, raised and reddish genitals, and overall body condition. Males exhibited secondary sexual characteristics: heads wider than the body, pigmented jaws and bellies, and distinct, resilient genital papillae with dilated urogenital openings. Gross and microscopic examination of testes confirmed that papilla firmness correlated with testicular development. Males were not injected with pituitaries; two received [[Testosterone]] but no effect was discernible. One male spawned with two females and tended eggs from a third spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Spawning occurred from mid-April through late July when water temperatures ranged from 70° to 85°F, with an optimum around 80°F. Federal fish culturists reported that larger, older fish spawn first, while smaller individuals spawn later in the season. The study was conducted in 6-, 10-, and 35-gallon glass aquaria supplied with flowing water and placed on benches in a holding house where workers frequently passed. Tar-paper mats lined aquarium bottoms to facilitate egg removal.

The authors emphasize that pituitary injections bypassed normal environmental cues (visual, physical, chemical stimuli) that would trigger endogenous gonadotropin secretion, but the fundamental spawning pattern remained unaltered. This conclusion is supported by concordance with [[Fontaine (1944)]]'s indirect observations of non-injected channel catfish and the authors' experience with other pituitary-induced species.

## Key Findings

### Pre-Spawning Behavior

When males and females were placed together, males exhibited either aggressive or passive responses. Aggressive males bit and drove females for 20–30 minutes, sometimes to near-death exhaustion. Aggression ceased when the female assumed a stationary position on the aquarium bottom; the pair was then considered "paired." If the female swam away, the male hazed her until she returned. Persistent swimming by the female indicated she was not ready for pairing and was removed.

Passive males suggested several possibilities: neither fish was ready; both were ready and pairing was spontaneous; the male was ready to accept any female; or the female was ready but the male lacked sexual drive. Female aggression toward males or other females indicated readiness to spawn, as paired catfish drove intruders away. Adding a second male often evoked aggression in a previously passive resident male, allowing selection of the more aggressive individual. One 21-inch male that had previously spawned exhibited minimal aggression but maintained an "alert position" (swimming slightly off-bottom with active caudal and pelvic fins), contrasting with non-spawning males that rested on the bottom with drooped fins.

Size disparity could induce excessive aggression. A 22-inch male with poorly developed secondary sexual characteristics and small, soft testes attacked a 16.1-inch female so viciously (biting the caudal peduncle and behind the pectorals) that she could not remain on the bottom. This male was replaced; he may have been capable of spawning with a larger female.

No definitive pre-spawning behavior was noted the day before spawning, though both fish were active, frequently circling and returning to the same bottom position where eggs were later deposited (consistent with [[Fontaine 1944]]). A few hours before spawning, females made short (4–6 inch) "runs" along the bottom in a wiggling motion, pelvic and pectoral fins alternately beating. These runs served to aerate and clean the egg deposition site and likely stimulated the male, as he would attempt to spawn when the female engaged in this behavior.

### Spawning Reflex and Egg Deposition

The **spawning reflex** preceded each emission of sex products. The male and female positioned themselves head-to-tail. The male wrapped his tail around the female's head, covering her eyes with his caudal fin (sometimes reaching the posterior tip of her operculum), and quivered while his pectoral fins beat and pelvics remained motionless, pointed backward or slightly to the side. Reflex duration depended on the female's response: ~5 seconds if she did not react or pulled away; ~8–10 seconds if she was passive; ~12–20 seconds if she participated.

When the female responded (typically within 5 seconds), she wrapped her tail around the male's head and quivered in unison. Both her pelvic and pectoral fins remained motionless. Abdominal muscle contractions moved eggs posteriorly, progressively flattening the area behind her pectoral fins. She then lunged forward 3–5 inches as eggs spurted out; water currents from the lunge caused eggs to swirl upward before settling.

The authors inferred that the male released milt at the reflex's climax, simultaneous with egg release. However, reproductive vents were not opposite each other (separated by the distance between pectoral and pelvic fins plus half the width of each fish). The male's slow pectoral fin beats during the reflex would carry sperm away from eggs, but if milt was released just before the female's lunge, her forward movement would create currents carrying sperm toward the newly released eggs—evidenced by the observed egg swirl.

After the lunge, the female remained motionless for several seconds, head on the bottom, body tipped slightly over the egg mass, caudal peduncle and tail drooped (hump-backed appearance). The male usually became active first. When the female resumed movement, she backed over the eggs and remained above them with pelvic fins beating for several minutes. This sequence repeated until spawning was complete.

### Duration and Frequency

One spawning witnessed from start to finish lasted **6 hours**. Another was estimated at 6 hours based on eggs already deposited when discovered. A 1-pound female discharged ~75% of her eggs in 3 hours before being removed and hand-stripped. A fourth spawning occurred between 12:30 a.m. and 7:30 a.m. From 3:30 p.m. to 5:33 p.m., one pair made **18 egg depositions** (~9 per hour). Extrapolating this rate over 6 hours, a female would lay ~150 eggs per deposition to total 8,000 eggs. Females weighing 1–4 pounds produced ~4,000 eggs per pound; larger females produced ~3,000 eggs per pound.

### Effects of Disturbances

Spawning was not inhibited by:
1. Frequent passage of workers and visitors near aquaria.
2. Moving fish multiple times the previous day to test pairing combinations.
3. Bright lights used for movie photography (two pairs spawned under such conditions).
4. Temporarily moving a spawning male or female to another aquarium and back.

One male ate the first eggs deposited under bright lights but successfully spawned after being fed liver, suggesting hunger rather than disturbance caused the egg-eating. Photography was discontinued.

### Incubation and Development

Embryonic development followed [[Murphree (1940)]]'s sequence (Table 2). At 78°F: <24 hours (no pulsation or pulsation present), 2 days (pulsation), 3 days (bloody streak), 4 days (blood all over), 5 days (eyes), 6 days (eyes, turning in shell), 7 days (complete fish, no bloody streak), 8 days (fish bursting out). Incubation period varied with temperature (70–85°F range): 5–10 days, optimum ~80°F. At mean 80°F, first egg hatched in 6–7 days. One spawn began hatching in 5 days 19 hours; all eggs hatched 6.5 hours later. Eye spots visible by close examination late on day 3 at 82°F, but inconspicuous up to day 5 at 79°F. Eggs laid first hatched last, likely due to oxygen tension gradients between top and bottom eggs.

### Parental Care

During spawning, the female aerated eggs via her "runs." The male assumed care when spawning was complete, hovering over eggs with pelvic fins alternately beating continuously. He occasionally circled away and returned. Most striking was vigorous body wiggling while pressing and packing eggs with the flat sides of his pelvic fins, shaking the entire mass. He moved forward from one end of the pile to the other, appearing to "walk" on his fins—similar to the female's "runs." This aerated eggs (especially deep ones) and possibly moved embryos in shells. Vigorous males worked eggs every 5–10 minutes the first 1–2 days, less frequently later. Less vigorous males worked eggs every 20–30 minutes.

After several workings, egg mass edges sometimes pulled loose from the tar-paper mat, especially when males pulled at eggs with their mouths. [[Murphree (1940)]] claimed males removed dead eggs, but the authors never observed this; dead eggs remained on top throughout incubation. [[Smith and Harron (1904)]] reported male bullheads (*A. nebulosus*) picked up eggs in their mouths and spit them out, sometimes fatally with fry. Channel catfish never took eggs completely in their mouths. One male tried to move a loose egg mass by placing his snout under the edge and carrying it forward.

Nest surface type and silt content may determine egg attachment. Loose egg masses suggested kegs with lips or depressions aid males in retaining eggs—the type found most satisfactory by catfish culturists.

[[Breder (1935)]] reported *A. nebulosus* pairs fanned the nest site for 10 days after egg removal, ceasing when eggs hatched in the lab, suggesting a "wound-up mechanism." Male channel catfish separated from spawn did not exhibit such behavior. Eggs likely stimulate male paternal behavior, confirmed when transferring both eggs and male to another aquarium—the male continued care.

One 15-inch male incubated three spawns (two his own, one additional). He received a severe eye injury during his second courtship and was injected intraperitoneally with ~75,000 units of penicillin in sesame oil on alternate days. He tended the second spawn with less vigor and few egg-working attempts; only ~10% hatched, many dying shortly before or during hatching. He attended a third spawn with similarly poor hatching. Eggs in the center of masses were most affected. A normal, uninjured male can successfully spawn with at least three females, per experienced culturists and these experiments.

### Post-Hatching Activity

When eggs hatched, males were removed. Newly hatched fry accumulated in a mass on the bottom, usually in a corner, remaining ~2 days. They then began surfacing as yolk was greatly reduced and skin pigment became visible. By day 3 they started feeding and moving about; they were transferred to feeding troughs.

### Additional Spawnings

Males can be reused 2–3 days after spawning if in good condition and eggs are artificially incubated, or every 2 weeks if the male attends each spawn. This allows reducing male broodstock to half the number of females.

[[Breder (1935)]] reported second spawnings within a season for bullheads. This may occur irregularly in female channel catfish. One keg raised on day 7 to collect newly hatched fry contained eggs early in development instead. Bottom eggs were dead (believed deposited 7 days prior); the second deposition occurred 3 days later (based on developmental stage), with the male attending. Possible explanations: fish were disturbed during spawning and returned 3 days later; the female deposited eggs without male attendance (believed rare or never); or gonadotropic hormone levels caused delayed or intermittent spawning.

Four pituitary-injected females and two non-injected females could not be stripped post-spawning. One non-injected female spawned 9 days prior yielded ~1,000 eggs when stripped, suggesting incomplete spawning. However, this has no practical significance; in the authors' experience, female catfish usually void all eggs in one spawning.

### Comparison with Previous Reports (Table 1)

**Nest Preparation:** [[Murphree (1940)]] and [[Lenz (1947)]] reported both sexes participate, female more active over egg deposition site. Clemens and Sneed observed this.

**Female Activity Pre-Spawning:** Murphree/Lenz claimed female inhales air to exert pressure during egg passage. Clemens and Sneed watched for this but never observed it, though females sometimes released gas bubbles without surfacing to "inhale" air.

**Male Activity Prior to Egg Passage:** Murphree/Lenz stated male uses teeth to break a gelatinous plug in the reproductive vent. Clemens and Sneed watched for this but never observed it; they believe no such plug exists.

**Egg Laying and Fertilization:** Murphree/Lenz reported female lays eggs, male pushes her aside, then fertilizes. Clemens and Sneed observed egg laying and sperm emission occur within ~15 seconds in mating position (at the climax of the spawning embrace).
