# Genetic Analysis of Constructive and Degenerative Evolutionary Processes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #characidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 paper by [[Curt Kosswig]] uses cross-breeding experiments to examine the genetic basis of two opposing evolutionary processes. The first is **degenerative evolution**, the loss of eyes and body pigment in cave-dwelling fish, studied in the [[Cavernicolous Fish]] *Anoptichthys* and its surface ancestor [[Astyanax Mexicanus]]. The second is **constructive evolution**, the acquisition of a new structure, the male sword of several [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] group swordtails. Because the cave and surface forms interbreed and yield fertile hybrids, the author can infer a polygenic basis for both processes from F2 and backcross generations. The paper also explores whether genetic drift, selection, or hybridization explains the rapid formation of variable populations, and it concludes that the mechanisms behind the high homozygosity of these traits remain unresolved.

## Background & Description

The author begins with the premise that degenerate traits are not inherently inferior. Citing Severtzov (1931), he notes that successful groups such as tunicates and barnacles are degraded relative to their ancestors. Studying the genetics of these changes requires fertile hybrids, which are rare because most species are reproductively isolated or produce sterile offspring.

The author reviews the early work of [[Erwin Baur]] on *Antirrhinum* (snapdragon), which showed that large phenotypic differences arise from the summed effects of many genes of small individual effect, with insect-mediated pollen flow maintaining a shared gene pool.

The central organisms are the following:

- **[[Anoptichthys Antrobius]]** (the Mexican blind cave characin): a cave form described as several species by Alvarez, but shown to be closely related populations that interbreed with each other and with *Astyanax*. Populations include [[Chica Cave]], [[Sabinos Cave]], and [[Pachon Cave]], the last more than 50 miles from the entrance of the Chica system.
- **[[Stygicola Dentatus]]**: a blind Cuban cave brotulid whose pigmentation varies widely (brown, pale, or white).
- **[[Asellus Aquaticus]]**: a cave isopod from Istrian and Slovenian caves, in which the author documented strong variability in eye and pigment traits.
- **[[Poecilia Sphenops]]**: a cavernicolous livebearer described by Gordon and Rosen (1962), with variability similar to the Chica population.
- **Anatolian cyprinodonts**: [[Aphanius]] species, the scale-reduced *Anatolichthys* form, and [[Kosswigichthys]], which is almost scaleless.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Cave fish are a model of degenerative evolution. Eye and pigment loss occur together across unrelated groups, including worms, arthropods, and vertebrates. Pigment reduction in cave animals usually leaves melanin in the eye rudiment, with one exception: true albinism with red eyes was reported in *Anoptichthys* by Sbordoni (1955).

The author argues that cave colonization requires two conditions: (1) a surface ancestor with pre-adaptations for dark environments, and (2) enrichment of many genes for degenerate traits within the gene pool. The first is supported by [[Astyanax Mexicanus]], which is negatively phototactic (Breder and Rasquin, 1947), spawns at night, and shows no hormonal stress when reared in permanent darkness (Franck, 1963).

The author notes that cave populations are small and can be fragmented into microhabitats. In *Asellus*, some pools held at most twelve animals, and some dried out in summer. Such conditions could allow [[Genetic Drift]] to fix degenerate alleles, provided selection does not remove them. Since existing caves are geologically young, the author considers these small populations central to cave differentiation. Competing explanations include food scarcity in caves (Huus, 1951, on *Caecobarbus geertsii*) and compensatory sensory organs, both of which the author finds unconvincing or unproven.

## Key Findings

**1. Cave-fish F2 hybrids show variable eye and pigment phenotypes.** Shadogl u's F2 data, which the author summarizes in Table 1, include:
- Chica × Astyanax F2: n = 732 (eyes: 48.6% normal, 41.8% intermediate, 9.6% blind)
- Sabinos × Astyanax F2: n = 605 (eyes: 54.4% normal, 39.4% intermediate, 6.2% blind)
- Pachon × Astyanax F2: n = 1,065 (eyes: 60.1% normal, 37.6% intermediate, 2.3% blind)

The author states that blind and depigmented F2 fish are more frequent when the cave parent comes from Pachon than from Chica. The author's own F2 cultures comprised several thousand animals and confirmed these findings.

**2. Backcrosses separate the traits.** Backcrosses to the surface *Astyanax* produced about 900 fish, none blind or with reduced vision, all fully pigmented. Backcrosses of F1-derived hybrids to blind Pachon fish (about 500 individuals) produced no F2-type individuals. Pigmentation ranged from dusky to almost melanin-free, and all were blind.

**3. The genetic basis is polygenic.** With a haploid chromosome number of 24 in both *Astyanax* and its cave derivative (per a student, Posch, unpublished), an F1 hybrid would form 2^24 gamete types. The text gives the expected frequency of an F2 phenotype match in the backcross as "one in 16,796,216" (2^24 = 16,777,216, so the printed figure is approximate). This arithmetic supports the view that the traits are controlled by many independently segregating loci.

**4. Hybridization may explain variable cave populations.** The Chica population is highly variable, which the author interprets as the result of secondary hybridization between a true cave fish and surface *Astyanax*, possibly with several introgression events. A similar argument applies to *Poecilia sphenops* in Tabasco and to Anatolian [[Aphanius]] populations in Lakes Burdur and Acı Göl, where the author suggests a "hybrid swarm."

**5. Evolutionary speed is best explained by combination.** For the [[Lake Lanao]] cyprinid radiation (about 10,000 years old, with at least 20 species in three genera), the author proposes that hybridization between genetically distinct *Barbodes binotatus* lineages, entering during glacial periods, could generate many new combinations rapidly. Testing this hypothesis is difficult because the events are geologically remote.

**6. Scale reduction in Anatolian cyprinodonts follows a complementary-gene model.** Crosses of scaled *Aphanius* species with the nearly scaleless *Kosswigichthys* gave F2 offspring with a range of scale-reduction phenotypes, from normal to strongly reduced. The author suggests that a minimum number of scale genes yields normal scalation, but that adding more has little further effect.

**7. Constructive evolution in swordtails.** The male sword of [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] group species is a secondary sexual trait formed by lengthening of the lowest caudal fin rays, and it is hormonally controlled. Testosterone can induce a sword in females of several species. [[Platypoecilus Maculatus]] has no sword and no wiggle display, while [[Xiphophorus Pygmaeus]] and [[Platypoecilus Xiphidium]] show different sword forms. The author concludes that the sword is probably a late acquisition (constructive evolution from a swordless ancestor), in line with Rosen (1960). Kramer's alternative model, in which swordlessness is derived through degenerative evolution, is also discussed. The author notes that the swordless *P. maculatus* produces a small sword when treated with testosterone, which Kramer took as support for his model, but the author says neither view can be decisively proven with genetic evidence.

**8. The sword has little demonstrated function.** Franck's experiments showed that the sword is not essential for successful copulation. Males with swords are rarely courted by other males. Males without swords are courted more often, though less than females. The author suggests that the sword may inhibit homosexual courtship in sexually frustrated males. Possible links to higher metabolic rate or to oxygen-rich, fast-flowing water are raised as hypotheses; the paper cites free amino acid levels (Anders et al., 1962) as a possible indicator.

**9. Hybrid genetics in swordtails.** Gerschler (1914) first described hybrids between *X. helleri* and *P. maculatus*, and the author confirmed Mendelian segregation. The F2 showed extensive recombination and polygenic control of sword length and coloration. Some F2 dwarfs were sterile or matured very slowly, yet displayed strong wiggle display. Zander (1962) crossed seven species to produce 42 F1 hybrids. Hybrids are readily produced in aquaria but are not found in nature, probably because heterospecific sperm are at a disadvantage in competition with conspecific sperm.

**10. Sex determination in swordtail hybrids is complex.** In crosses between *X. helleri* (polygenic sex determination) and *P. xiphidium* (XY males with a dominant colour gene on the Y), the F2 includes grey individuals of both sexes. Males with the Y chromosome had longer swords than their grey brothers. The author notes that this is inconsistent with the testosterone results in *P. xiphidium* females, which develop equal-length swords, so the difference remains unexplained.

**11. Conclusions.** The author concludes that the polygenic basis of both degenerative and constructive traits can be inferred from F2 and backcross generations. How extensive homozygosity arose remains unexplained. Genetic drift alone seems unlikely, but the selective value of the involved genes is unknown. The author suggests that pleiotropic effects on fecundity could give degenerate genes positive selective value, though this is untested. In the swordtails, selection for the sword would need to be explained, but it might act as an indicator of a physiological trait rather than through sexual selection.
