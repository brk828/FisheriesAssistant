## Executive Summary

This FY1993 annual report from the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Program]] (Project #36) details efforts to develop a reintroduction plan for the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) into the Upper Colorado River Basin. The species is considered functionally extirpated from the wild in this basin; remnant lower-basin reservoir populations are believed non-reproductive, and all currently available stock derives from hatchery-reared progeny (notably from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]) with largely unknown native ecological requirements. The study focuses on (1) determining optimum stocking size, (2) evaluating interactions between Bonytail Chub and non-native fishes (particularly predation and competition), and (3) assessing the effect of raceway/pond-rearing conditions on post-release survivorship. Two experimental approaches were underway: a flow/refuge-conditioning experiment examining tail muscle fiber development, and an artificial stream experiment examining feeding behavior and interspecific competition with [[Red Shiner]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila elegans* (Bonytail Chub), family Cyprinidae.
- **Muscle physiology focus:** Study specifically investigates **white/red muscle fiber development** in the **caudal (tail) region**, sampled via muscle plugs from experimental fish.
- **Working hypothesis:** Bonytail reared in static pond environments (e.g., Dexter hatchery ponds) will **fail to develop appropriate tail muscle morphology** (i.e., insufficient red muscle fiber development associated with sustained swimming) necessary for successful reintroduction into large, flowing river systems.
- **Experimental design to test morphology:** Three batches of 15 fish each (n=45 total, part of a larger transported cohort of 300) raised under differing hydrodynamic regimes:
  1. No-flow (static) environment
  2. Medium flow (0.15 m/sec) with **low refuge availability**
  3. Medium flow (0.15 m/sec) with **high refuge availability**
  - Conditions maintained through March 1994, followed by muscle sampling and histological analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical habitat:** Upper Colorado River Basin mainstem/large river systems; specific native habitat and basic ecology are "virtually unknown" due to near-total loss of wild populations.
- **Environmental degradation context:** [[Pearson 1957]] documented significant post-dam changes in **food quality and quantity** in the [[Green River]] following dam construction, which may have historically disadvantaged native chub populations (implicating [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]-type impoundments broadly).
- **Non-native species pressure:** At least **48 exotic fish species** now established in the Upper Colorado Basin (citing [[Tyus et al. 1982]]).
- **Key predator threats identified:**
  - [[Channel Catfish]]
  - [[Green Sunfish]]
  - [[Smallmouth Bass]]
  — all identified as exhibiting high predation pressure on bonytail chubs.
- **Key competitor threat identified:**
  - [[Red Shiner]] — hypothesized as a strong food competitor, especially impactful on **young-of-year/juvenile bonytail chubs**.
- **Feeding ecology under investigation (experimental artificial streams with simulated backwaters):**
  - Drift feeding
  - Surface feeding
  - Benthic feeding
  - Each behavior assessed in both flowing (open channel) and stagnant-flow (backwater) microhabitats.
  - Sequential experimental design: baseline bonytail-only feeding behavior established first, followed by introduction of Red Shiner to measure:
    - Aggressive interactions
    - Scramble competition
    - Exploitation competition

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** Wild Bonytail Chub is functionally extirpated from the Upper Colorado River Basin; remaining lower-basin reservoir individuals show no evidence of reproduction ([[Tyus et al. 1982]]).
- **Data gap:** Recovery planning is fundamentally constrained by lack of basic ecological/feeding data for the species in its native habitat.
- **Threat synthesis:** Three primary biotic factors are hypothesized to limit reintroduction success:
  1. Food limitation (altered post-dam river productivity)
  2. Competitive interactions with introduced species (esp. Red Shiner)
  3. Size-specific vulnerability to exotic predators (Channel Catfish, Green Sunfish, Smallmouth Bass)
- **Study design rationale:** Because the fish is essentially "ecologically blank" (hatchery-only lineage), the project uses **pond and artificial stream experiments** to reconstruct fundamental habitat requirements and interaction dynamics rather than relying on field observation of extant wild populations.
- **Stocking size optimization:** A central applied objective is determining whether a **predictable optimum size/age class** exists for stocking that minimizes both predation vulnerability and competitive disadvantage — to be informed by **gape-limitation predation models** and **temperature-mediated growth rate models**.
- **Rearing method evaluation:** The project explicitly tests whether **raceway/pond-cultured fish** are physiologically (muscularly) and behaviorally suited for release into flowing river systems, directly comparing hatchery-conditioned fish against the demands of riverine habitat.
- **Administrative status (FY93):**
  - Overall project status: On track; reintroduction plan still in development; no major recommendations issued.
- **Experimental cohort:** 300 bonytail transported from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] to Utah State University (USU) for the flow/muscle and feeding-behavior experiments described above.
