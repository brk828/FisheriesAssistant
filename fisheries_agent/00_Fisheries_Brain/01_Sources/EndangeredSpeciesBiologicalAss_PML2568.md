# Endangered Species Biological Assessment for the Dirty Devil River Unit

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This December 1984 biological assessment evaluates the potential effects of the proposed [[Dirty Devil River Unit]] on federally protected species in south-central Utah. The unit is a salinity-control project intended to reduce the roughly 155,000 tons of salt the [[Dirty Devil River]] delivers annually to the [[Colorado River]]. The preferred plan collects saline water from [[Banksville Salt Wash]] and [[Entrada Salt Wash]] and disposes of it by deep-well injection into the [[Coconino Sandstone]]. The assessment concludes that the project is unlikely to affect the [[Colorado Squawfish]] or [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Powell]], that the [[Wright Fishhook Cactus]] will be avoided if present, and that the [[Black-Footed Ferret]] is probably absent from the project area.

## Background & Description

**Project setting.** The unit lies in south-central Utah across parts of Sanpete, Sevier, Emery, Wayne, Piute and Garfield counties and covers about 41,300 square miles. The Bureau of Land Management (BLM) administers roughly 75 percent of the land. Remaining areas include parts of [[Capitol Reef National Park]], [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]], and Fishlake, Dixie and Manti-LaSal National Forests. Elevations range from about 3,600 feet at the mouth of the Dirty Devil River to about 11,500 feet in the mountains at the head of the drainage.

**Purpose.** The unit aims to reduce salinity in the Colorado River. The Dirty Devil drainage contributes about 155,000 tons of salt annually, which raises the river's salinity at Imperial Dam by about 15.5 mg/L. The objective is to recover as much salt as is economically justifiable.

**Plan description.** Of several alternatives studied, only saline water collection from Entrada and Banksville Salt Washes was judged acceptable. The plan would remove about 201,600 tons of salt per year by collecting water at a continuous rate of 1.75 cfs from both washes. Collection would use alluvial wells, with separate facilities for each wash. Disposal would use deep-well injection with standard [[Petroleum Industry]] drilling techniques, after filtration and chemical treatment to limit scaling and algae. All facilities would sit on federal land managed by the BLM, with access roads upgraded and power lines extended along existing routes.

**Banksville Salt Wash.** Collectable water has about 51,900 mg/L total dissolved solids (TDS) and a flow of about 2.5 cfs. Twelve wells, plus three spares, would be needed, each about 50 feet deep. Water would be pumped through about 2,900 feet of buried PVC pipeline to the injection well. Disposal would use the Coconino Sandstone at roughly 3,700 feet depth, about 1,000 feet thick, with permeability of 200 to 650 and porosity of 15 to 25 percent. The 2.5 cfs flow could be injected into a single well over a 50-year operating period. Backwash would go to an on-site evaporation pond. Project area land requirement is about 20 acres, and about 6 miles of existing roads would be reconstructed.

**Entrada Salt Wash.** Collectable water has 25,000 to 35,000 mg/L TDS and a subsurface flow of 0.15 to 0.25 cfs. Ten wells with submersible pumps would be connected to a common pipeline. Water would be pumped through 12,000 feet of buried pipeline. Injection would use the Coconino Sandstone at about 4,400 feet depth, roughly 800 feet thick. The project would need about 10 acres of land and reconstruction of about 9 miles of secondary roads.

**Species assessment scope.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service identified three federally protected endangered species as possibly occurring in the unit: the Colorado Squawfish, the Black-Footed Ferret and the Wright Fishhook Cactus. The [[Razorback Sucker]] was also considered because it is a candidate for listing and is rare in the Upper Colorado River Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado Squawfish.** This species was historically common across the Colorado River basin. Its decline followed water development and the introduction of exotic fish. The closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963 blocked upstream migration and flooded 187 miles of the Colorado River to form Lake Powell. Anglers occasionally catch the species, mostly in the upper reservoir near Bitter Creek.

**Razorback Sucker.** This is considered the largest native fish of the Upper Colorado River. Adults persist, but little evidence of recent successful recruitment has been documented in the upper basin.

**Black-Footed Ferret.** This ferret once ranged from Canada to Texas and from Nebraska to Arizona. It depends on prairie dog colonies (*Cynomys* spp.). Poisoning of prairie dogs with Compound 1080 caused the already small ferret populations to decline.

**Wright Fishhook Cactus.** This cactus occurs only in Emery and Wayne counties, at elevations of 3,000 to 5,000 feet on Entrada sandstone outcrops, slickrock and gravel slopes. Over-collection by plant collectors is the main threat.

**Dirty Devil River and Lake Powell.** The river is highly erosive and carries heavy silt loads. Flows vary from a recorded high of 35,000 cfs to no flow at all. Water temperatures range from over 100°F in summer to 32°F in winter, and water quality is poor. Only the most tolerant fish species live in the river, and no Colorado Squawfish or Razorback Sucker have been collected there. Aquatic macroinvertebrates are nearly absent in the lower river. Near the reservoir, water quality improves and supports a mix of riverine and reservoir fish, including [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], channel catfish, largemouth bass, walleye and [[Threadfin Shad]].

## Key Findings

- **Salt reduction:** The preferred plan would remove about 201,600 tons of salt annually at a combined 1.75 cfs collection rate.
- **Injection capacity:** The Coconino Sandstone can hold the saline water for over 50 years at acceptable pressures, and the Chinle and Moenkopi formations act as confining layers.
- **Land use:** The project needs about 20 acres at Banksville and 10 acres at Entrada, all on federal land. About 6 and 9 miles of roads would be reconstructed, respectively.
- **Squawfish and sucker sampling:** Between December 1983 and September 1984, 19 adult Colorado Squawfish (52 to 70 cm) were captured. One fish moved about 55 miles within the reservoir. Seven Razorback Suckers were collected from January to May 1984 and tagged. Four were radio-tagged, but high water conductivity prevented tracking. No threatened or endangered larval fish were found.
- **Conclusion for fish:** The Dirty Devil inflow is not significant for the squawfish or sucker. Both species probably do not use the river for spawning. The Bureau of Reclamation concluded the project would not jeopardize either species.
- **Wright Fishhook Cactus:** A survey of the Banksville area found scattered individuals and one group of about 25 plants. Positive identification requires flowering, so follow-up surveys were planned for spring 1985. If confirmed, the species would be avoided, and Reclamation believes the unit would not affect it.
- **Black-Footed Ferret:** Only two confirmed sightings occurred between 1981 and 1985, both outside the project area. No prairie dog colonies were found in the immediate project area, so Reclamation concluded the ferret is absent and unaffected.
