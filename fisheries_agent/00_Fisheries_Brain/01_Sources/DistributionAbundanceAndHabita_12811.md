# Distribution, Abundance and Habitat for the Little Colorado Spinedace in East Clear Creek and Its Tributaries

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #threatened-species #stream-ecology #Arizona

## Executive Summary

This report documents a comprehensive survey (May 1990–November 1991) assessing the distribution, abundance, and habitat of the federally threatened [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*) in the [[Coconino National Forest]] and [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] along [[East Clear Creek]] and its tributaries. Approximately 94.7 stream miles were surveyed using electrofishing and seining. Results reveal a drastically reduced distribution compared to historical records: no spinedace were found in the 31 miles of designated Critical Habitat, and the species was detected only in isolated pools within a 2–3 mile segment of [[Leonard Canyon]] and [[West Leonard Canyon]], primarily at [[Dines Tank]]. The population has declined from historical levels, with exotic species (eight nonnative fish species documented) and habitat degradation identified as primary threats. [[Dines Tank]] represents a critical refugium and potential recolonization source.

## Background & Description

The [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] is a federally listed threatened cyprinid fish native to Arizona, originally distributed throughout major north-flowing tributaries of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) including [[East Clear Creek]], [[Chevelon Creek]], [[Silver Creek]], and [[Nutrioso Creek]]. Historical range may have extended into New Mexico's [[Zuni River]]. The species was designated threatened under the Endangered Species Act in 1987, with 31 miles of [[East Clear Creek]] designated as Critical Habitat from headwaters to four miles below 96 Road Crossing (excluding [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] and adjacent private lands).

**Life History**: Spinedace prefer clear, flowing, relatively open pools with fine gravel substrate and cover provided by overhanging banks or large rocks. Young-of-the-year are most abundant on uniformly turbulent riffles. The species is generally solitary but may move in small groups. Spawning occurs primarily in spring (around June), with females ≥50 mm carrying 650–5,600 eggs (size-dependent). Eggs are deposited randomly over substrate or aquatic vegetation. Males mature at ≥50 mm and may develop tubercles and weak orange/yellow coloration at paired fin bases.

**Diet**: Feeds predominantly on aquatic arthropods, especially caddisflies (Trichoptera), and terrestrial insects including grasshoppers (Orthoptera), beetles (Coleoptera), and Hymenoptera. Laboratory studies documented consumption of amphipods (*Hyalella*), mayfly nymphs, chironomid larvae, cladocerans, and filamentous green algae (*Cladophora*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Survey Methods**: Crews of 3–4 people sampled 94.7 stream miles over approximately 132 field-person days. Drainages were divided into "reaches" based on access points, stream miles, or life zones, with reaches subdivided into "sites." Sampling employed Coffelt BP4 backpack electrofishing units (0.35–0.5 amps) and seines. Aerial surveys covered 61 miles of the drainage, identifying 16 miles with virtually no water and 45 miles with permanent water.

**Habitat Conditions**: Surveyed reaches exhibited highly variable conditions. Upper [[East Clear Creek]] (headwaters to [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]]) had only 2% surface water in isolated, stagnant pools with poor clarity and bank erosion. Below the reservoir dam to 96 Crossing, flow was discontinuous with poor water quality and an oil-based film noted near the dam. Downstream reaches showed continuous flow but were dominated by pools (50–99% of habitat) with thick aquatic vegetation (*Myriophyllum* sp.) limiting sampling efficiency. Substrate varied from silt to bedrock, with gravel, cobble, and boulder combinations common.

**Fish Community**: Eight nonnative species were documented: [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus zebrinus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). Native species included [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*), [[Little Colorado Sucker]] (*Catostomus* sp.), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*, collected only at one site). [[Speckled Dace]] dominated most reaches where spinedace were absent.

## Key Findings

**Distribution Collapse**: No spinedace were found in the 31 miles of designated Critical Habitat. The species was detected only in isolated pools within [[Leonard Canyon]] and [[West Leonard Canyon]], specifically at and upstream of [[Dines Tank]]. This represents a dramatic reduction from historical records documenting spinedace at nine separate locations throughout the drainage between 1960 and 1988.

**Population Status at Key Sites**:
- **[[Dines Tank]]** (July 1990): Only 1 adult and several fry collected, representing <10% of total catch. In 1983, spinedace comprised 51% of catch (171 individuals). By November 1991, numbers appeared to increase (25 spinedace in 220 m seine haul), but [[Fathead Minnow]] abundance had risen from <5% (1983) to 12–70% (1990).
- **[[Leonard Canyon]]** above Dines Tank (June 1991): 79 [[Fathead Minnow]] (65.8%), 34 [[Speckled Dace]] (28.3%), 5 spinedace (4.1%), 2 [[Bluehead Sucker]] (1.6%).
- **[[Leonard Canyon]]** below Dines Tank to [[Knoll Lake]] (November 1990): 159 [[Fathead Minnow]] (70.0%), 65 [[Speckled Dace]] (28.6%), 3 spinedace (1.3%).

**Historical Declines**: At [[Jones Crossing]] (upper East Clear Creek), spinedace were documented in nine separate collections from 1960 to 1988. In 1983, 24 spinedace represented 17.9% of total fish (0.02 fish/m²). No spinedace were found in 1990–1991 surveys. At 96 Crossing/[[Barbershop Creek]] confluence, spinedace were collected 11 times between 1963 and 1988 (1–53 individuals per sample). None were found in this survey.

**Exotic Species Impact**: Where [[Rainbow Trout]] were abundant, spinedace were absent. Laboratory studies (Blinn and Runck 1990) demonstrated that rainbow trout and [[Green Sunfish]] readily prey on spinedace. [[Fathead Minnow]], now occupying the entire former spinedace range, represents a major competitive threat due to habitat overlap and similar food habits. [[Golden Shiner]], occupying deep pools, may displace spinedace through competition for space.

**Habitat Degradation**: Severe grazing impacts were documented at [[Dines Tank]], with erosion evident. Upper [[East Clear Creek]] showed extensive habitat degradation with scarcity of water and long dry stretches. Human impacts were evident at [[Jones Crossing]] and 96 Crossing. From [[Hamilton Crossing]] to Coconino Forest boundary, 2.6 miles of stream channel were completely dry.

**Tributary Surveys**: All tributaries with perennial or semi-permanent water were surveyed. No spinedace were found in [[Barbershop Creek]] (historically present 1966, 1981), [[Bear Canyon]], [[Kehl Canyon]], [[Moqui Draw]], [[Dane Canyon]], [[Yaeger Canyon]], or [[Willow Creek]] (historically present at [[Wiggins Crossing]] and [[Mule Crossing]]).

**Tiger Salamander Presence**: Large numbers of [[Tiger Salamanders]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) were present at [[Dines Tank]] in July 1990 (not previously recorded), potentially representing additional predation pressure. No salamanders were taken in November 1991.

**Sample Sizes**: Total efforts across all sites: 61 reaches surveyed, approximately 10,800 shock seconds, plus extensive seining. Voucher specimens deposited at Arizona State University fish collection. Site-specific data entered into Arizona Game and Fish Department Heritage Data Management System for Section 7 consultations.
