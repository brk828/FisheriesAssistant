## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by Kenneth B. Armitage documents the species composition and seasonal distribution of limnetic (open-water) crustacean [[Zooplankton]] across six lakes in northeastern Kansas, based on 17 qualitative plankton hauls collected between September 1956 and June 1960. Sampling used a #25 plankton net towed from an outboard boat or a Clark-Bumpus sampler. The study identifies 14 species of [[Cladocera]] and [[Copepoda]], classifies their seasonal occurrence patterns (perennial, vernal, autumnal, or winter/early-spring), and discusses the ecological implications of frequent co-occurrence of congeneric species — raising questions about [[Interspecific Competition]] and niche partitioning consistent with, or challenging, the [[Competitive Exclusion Principle|Gause's Hypothesis]]. Three species (*Daphnia pulex*, *Daphnia parvula*, *Daphnia ambigua*) are reported from Kansas for the first time.

## Background & Description

While the paper is primarily faunistic/ecological rather than morphometric, it provides limited body-size and biomass data relevant to niche differentiation among congeners:

- **Diaptomus clavipes**: ~0.2 mg dry weight per individual.
- **Diaptomus siciloides**: ~0.14 mg dry weight per individual — smaller than *D. clavipes*, suggested as a possible mechanism of niche separation despite co-occurrence.
- **Tropocyclops prasinus**: Notably smaller in body size than *Cyclops vernalis* and *Mesocyclops edax*, proposed to occupy a distinct "food-niche" reducing direct competitive overlap.
- **Cyclops vernalis** and **Mesocyclops edax**: Described as "quite similar in appearance" and "of similar size" — a morphological similarity that complicates niche-differentiation arguments, since both also share similar seasonal distribution patterns in Nemaha County State Lake.
- **Ceriodaphnia reticulata** and **Ceriodaphnia quadrangula**: Reported as "similar in size, shape, and presumably in feeding habits" in Laboratory Lake, a further case of apparent niche overlap.

Identification of all taxa relied on standard published keys (Wilson, Brooks, and Yeatman in Edmondson 1959; Brooks 1957).

### Seasonal Distribution Patterns
- **Perennial species**: [[Daphnia galeata mendotae]], *Daphnia pulex*, *Daphnia parvula*, [[Bosmina longirostris]], [[Diaptomus pallidus]], [[Diaptomus siciloides]], *Diaptomus clavipes* (perennial in favorable habitats, seasonal in others).
- **Vernal (spring) species**: [[Daphnia ambigua]].
- **Autumnal species**: [[Ceriodaphnia lacustris]], [[Diaphanosoma brachyurum]], [[Tropocyclops prasinus]].
- **Late spring–autumn species**: [[Mesocyclops edax]], [[Cyclops vernalis]].
- **Winter/early spring species**: [[Cyclops bicuspidatus thomasi]] — associated with cold-water preference; seasonal shift toward winter in lakes exceeding 15°C in summer, but perennial where temperatures stay below 15–18°C (cross-referenced with Lake Erie and Colorado reservoir studies).

### Niche Partitioning & Competition
- Co-occurring cyclopoid copepods were always of *different* genera, except *Cyclops vernalis* and *Mesocyclops edax*, which are morphologically and phenologically similar — a potential challenge to [[Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle]].
- Co-occurring calanoid copepods (genus *Diaptomus*) were always the *same* genus (*D. siciloides* paired with either *D. clavipes* or *D. pallidus*), with only slight numerical dominance between species and some evidence of differential vertical stratification (dominance ratios weaker at 2 m depth vs. 1 m).
- *D. clavipes* is typically associated with ponds/roadside ditches; *D. siciloides* with large shallow lakes — habitat overlap in intermediate lakes may permit co-occurrence without full competitive exclusion.
- The Laboratory Lake, being a recently formed habitat (since 1957), showed the most pronounced cases of potential competitive overlap (e.g., *Ceriodaphnia reticulata* vs. *C. quadrangula*), attributed to insufficient time for a stable, characteristic zooplankton community to establish.
- Overlapping population cycles (e.g., *Daphnia galeata mendotae* vs. *D. pulex* in Leavenworth County State Lake) can create a false impression of competition when in fact one species dominates during the other's cyclical decline, with reservoir populations maintained in littoral refugia.

## Key Findings

1. **17 samples across 6 lakes** yielded **11 species of Cladocera** and **9 species of Copepoda** (total across all sites), with an average of **3.0 cladoceran species** (median 3) and **2.3 copepod species** (median 2) present in any single sample — comparable to Pennak's (1957) broader survey of 42 lakes.
2. **Three species reported from Kansas for the first time**: *Daphnia pulex* (Leydig emend. Richard), *Daphnia parvula*, and *Daphnia ambigua*.
3. **Daphnia galeata mendotae** was found only in Leavenworth County State Lake and remains one of only two known Kansas records for this species (with Tash and Armitage 1960).
4. In only **5 of 17 samples** did the "typical" community structure of one calanoid + one cyclopoid + one daphnid occur; the remainder showed **multiple congeneric species co-occurring**, raising recurring questions about niche overlap and competition.
5. **Cyclops bicuspidatus thomasi** exhibits a temperature-dependent seasonal shift: cold-water affinity drives winter/spring dominance in warmer Kansas lakes but year-round persistence in colder lakes (Colorado reservoirs, Lake Erie comparisons cited).
6. The **Laboratory Lake**, as a newly established (post-1957) aquatic habitat, showed the least stable community composition and the strongest apparent cases of interspecific competitive overlap, supporting a hypothesis of ongoing community assembly/succession in new lentic environments.
7. Body-mass differences between co-occurring *Diaptomus* species (~0.2 mg vs. ~0.14 mg dry weight) and vertical stratification patterns are proposed as potential mechanisms allowing coexistence despite congeneric overlap.
8. Findings are cross-referenced against regional literature including [[Pennak 1949, 1955, 1957]] (Colorado lakes), [[Andrews 1953]] (Lake Erie), and [[Prophet, Andrews & Goulden 1959]] (Lyon County, Kansas checklist), situating northeastern Kansas zooplankton assemblages within broader North American limnological context.
