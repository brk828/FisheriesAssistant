# Trout in Farm and Ranch Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This USDA Farmers' Bulletin (No. 2154, issued January 1961) explains how farm and ranch ponds can be planned, stocked, and managed to grow trout for food and recreation, and lists essentials for commercial production. Written by two [[Soil Conservation Service]] biologists, it argues that trout do not require fast streams or mountain lakes. Instead, they grow well in most cool or cold-water ponds, where water temperature and oxygen matter more than location. Key recommendations cover site selection, pond depth and shape, stocking rates, trout species, water quality limits, fertilizing, weed control, and winterkill prevention.

## Background & Description

**Purpose of farm ponds.** Ponds are built mainly for livestock water, irrigation storage, erosion and flood control, or wildlife. Most also provide secondary values such as fishing, duck shooting, fur trapping, swimming, and boating. Ponds in the northern United States may produce one or more broods of ducks. Mink, muskrats, raccoons, doves, pheasants, and quail also use them.

**Kinds of trout.** The bulletin focuses on four species:

- [[Rainbow Trout]]: preferred west of the Mississippi and in parts of the Southeast. It thrives across a wide range of conditions, grows fast, and is the most widely available from western hatcheries.
- [[Brook Trout]]: favored in parts of the East. It is easier to catch and can spawn in springs or trickles unsuitable for rainbows.
- [[Cutthroat Trout]]: little used in ponds and not widely available from hatcheries.
- [[Brown Trout]]: tolerant of wide conditions, but more cannibalistic and harder to catch. The bulletin does not recommend it for ponds.

The bulletin advises against stocking other fish with trout, because they compete for food. It also notes that stocking rainbow and brook trout together adds variety but little harvest weight.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature.** Trout grow best at 55° to 68° F and tolerate a slightly wider range. If water 6 inches below the surface of the deep water stays under 70° F, the pond is probably cool enough, even if surface water reaches 75° to 80° F at midday. Cold water slows growth and reduces food production. Warm water holds less oxygen, which is a hazard for trout.

**Dissolved oxygen.** [[Dissolved Oxygen]] must be 5 ppm or above year-round. Oxygen is usually lowest at daybreak because plants do not produce oxygen at night. Cloudy weather also lowers it. Increasing cool inflow is the easiest way to lower temperature and raise oxygen.

**pH and water quality.** Water should be within pH 6.5 to 9. Trout may survive pH 4.5 to 10 if changes are gradual. Water should be free of heavy silt, pollution, and noxious gases. Spring and well water is often low in oxygen and may hold carbon dioxide. Running it over rocks or baffles helps.

**Weeds and algae.** Dead waterweeds decompose and consume oxygen, contributing to summer dieoffs and winterkill. Some weeds also give trout a musky flavor. Filamentous algae and [[Muskgrass]] can be controlled with [[Copper Sulfate]].

**Winterkill.** Where ice covers a pond for a month or more, fish may die from oxygen loss or trapped gases. Deeper water, reduced vegetation, and winter inflow reduce the risk.

## Key Findings

### Site, Size, and Depth
- Trout can be raised in ponds of almost any size. Ponds under one-fourth surface acre may be too small for a family. One-half to one acre is an ideal family pond, and 1 to 3 acres suits a large family. For clubs, several 1 to 5 acre ponds are preferred.
- Depth: at least one-fourth of the pond should be 10 feet deep in hot-summer or freezing areas, and 12 to 16 feet where winters are long and cold. Where ice is rare and water runs, 6 to 8 feet is usually enough.
- Shore slope should be 3:1 or steeper to a 3-foot depth. Keep as little water as possible less than 3 feet deep.
- Clear trees, brush, and debris for at least 30 feet from the water line.
- A pond near the house is more likely to be fished and managed well.

### Stocking and Restocking
- If other fish are present, drain the pond or use toxicants (with State permission) before stocking.
- Stocking rates in fertile ponds: 500 to 1,000 advanced fry or 300 to 600 fingerlings per surface acre. Use the lower rate for infertile ponds. Advanced fry are 1 to 2 inches long; fingerlings are 2 to 4 inches.
- Trout should grow about 1 inch per month to 9 to 10 inches.
- Eating-size trout can be stocked at 100 to 300 per surface acre. Stocking more than 100 pounds per acre and relying on natural food yields little growth until fish die or are caught.
- Trout rarely reproduce in ponds. Restock at least every second year. Restock in late summer or fall after larger trout are removed.
- Restocking every year yields more fish and pounds each year, but the fish average smaller.
- Stocking in spring or fall is preferred. Avoid stocking when surface water is above 65° F.

### Costs and Sourcing
- Advanced fry cost about $15 per thousand from private hatcheries. Fingerlings up to 4 inches cost 1 to 3 cents per inch.
- At $40 per 1,000 small fingerlings, stocking costs about $20 per surface acre, and the same annually to maintain.
- Fish can be hauled 200 miles or more in milk cans or clean barrels if the water is kept splashing and aerated. Water temperature in transport cans should be within 10° F of the pond. Cover cans with burlap rather than tight lids.
- Sources include private hatcheries, [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] fingerling programs in some states, and State fish and game agencies.

### Supplemental Feeding
- Most ponds stocked at recommended rates need no supplemental feeding.
- Feed daily or two to three times a week. If food remains 15 minutes after feeding, you are overfeeding.
- Do not feed when water is above 65° F. Decomposing food uses oxygen.
- Feeding makes fish tamer and easier to catch.

### Harvest and Fishing
- A good pond should yield 50 pounds of trout per acre per year without supplemental feeding.
- Start fishing when trout reach 6 to 8 inches. About a year after stocking, the population reaches carrying capacity, and growth slows. Numbers and total weight then decline through natural mortality.
- Only a small percentage of stocked trout live more than 2 years.
- Trout feed mostly in mornings and evenings in warm weather, and mid-day on sunny days in early spring or late fall. "Any time you see trout rising is a good time to go fishing."
- Effective baits include angleworms, grasshoppers, crickets, and salmon eggs. Flies and spinners also work.
- Clean trout promptly and freeze them fully encased in ice. They keep fresh taste for several months.

### Water Supply, Inlets, and Outlets
- Water can come from runoff, springs, streams, ditches, wells, or seeps. Continuous heavy flow is rarely desirable.
- Use screened grates or buried intake pipes (under 6 inches or more of gravel) to limit fish entry. Grate bar spacing closer than one-quarter inch is usually impractical.
- Use a [[Trickle Tube]] for normal flow and an emergency spillway for floods. Do not screen the spillway, because screens clog and endanger the dam. A wider spillway reduces trout losses during runoff.
- Three trickle tube designs are described: a pipe-in-pipe type standing in open water, a divided riser set in the dam with flashboards, and a riser with a drain valve (usable at any depth, with no leakage).
- Install a drainpipe so the pond can be fully drained. Some states require it.
- Keep livestock out of the pond with fencing and off-site watering tanks.
- Use the same pipe for inlet and outlet, or place them side by side, to limit water movement.

### Fertilizing
- Fertilize only where at least 80 percent of the pond is over 3 feet deep and flow is minimal in spring and summer.
- Apply 8 pounds of available nitrogen and 8 pounds of available phosphate per surface acre per application. Add 2 to 4 pounds of available potash where potash is low.
- Begin when ice melts (or when crocus blooms in non-ice areas), then repeat weekly until the water turns green or brown, usually two or three applications.
- Use only inorganic fertilizer. Manure uses oxygen as it decomposes.
- Stop fertilizing in early summer where ice covers the pond a month or more, and do not apply after mid-July.
- A white object visible 18 inches below the surface means more fertilizer is needed.

### Weed and Algae Control
- A few weeds may be beneficial. Pull seedling cattails and bulrushes by hand, mow emergent growth, and use sprays for shoreline plants.
- For algae and muskgrass, use 1.5 pounds of copper sulfate per acre-foot (about 0.5 ppm) in neutral or acid water, two to three times that in alkaline water. Treat only a fourth or third of the pond at once.
- Overuse can kill fish and reduce fish food.
- Clear fishing strips or holes by hand, by scythe, or by covering weeds with tar paper or plastic sheets.

### Winterkill Prevention
- Deepen the pond to at least 10 feet if it has no winter inflow. Use 14 to 16 feet where temperatures fall well below zero.
- Keep vegetation out.
- Provide winter inflow. Running a pump a few hours each week can break ice from the shore.
- Cutting holes in ice is of questionable value.
- As a fallback, stock 200 to 300 fingerlings per acre in March or April, then drain and harvest before freeze-up. This gives 6 to 8 months of growth and produces frozen trout, though with little sport fishing. Some states prohibit this practice.

### Commercial Production
Commercial trout farming is difficult, with narrow profit margins and many failure points. Essentials include:
1. A dependable minimum flow of 2 second-feet (about 900 gallons per minute). Producing 50,000 market-size trout (15,000 pounds) annually requires about 31 second-feet (1,500+ gallons per minute).
2. Water temperature of 52° to 62° F. Below 52° F, growth is poor. Above 62° F, disease and oxygen shortage risks rise.
3. Water pH of 7 to 8.5.
4. Spring water is almost essential.
5. A site free of flood hazards, muddy water, and pollution from mines, factories, and cities. Sites below other hatcheries increase disease risk.
6. A dependable year-round road.
7. Individual control of each trough, flume, or pond for draining, harvest, cleaning, and refilling.

The bulletin recommends visiting hatcheries and gaining a few months of hands-on hatchery employment before investing.

### Points to Remember (Summary)
- Build a pond suited to your needs, soil, water supply, and climate. A good small pond beats an inferior large one.
- Keep waterweeds under control, and avoid insecticides on the watershed, since most kill fish.
- Do not overstock. Restock at least every second year.
- If many fish swim near the surface, add water immediately; they likely need oxygen.
- Start fishing once trout are large enough to eat.

### Regulations
Laws for private fishponds vary widely by state. Check with the State fish and game agency for rules on operation, and with the State engineer or water authority for water-storage laws.
