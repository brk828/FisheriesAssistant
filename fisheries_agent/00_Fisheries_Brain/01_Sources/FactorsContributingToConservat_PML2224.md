# Collapse of Native Desert Fishes in the Upper Verde River, USA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the native and non-native fish community of the upper Verde River in central Arizona, a free-flowing reach of a desert river in the American Southwest. Sampling at seven sites from 1993 to 1995 (with a 1986–1996 comparison dataset) tracked how a 50+ year flood and a 7-year flood affected the balance between native and non-native fishes. The native-to-non-native ratio was typically around 85:15, with the proportions shifting only slightly after the 7-year flood and recovering in native species after the 50+ year flood. The authors argue that the natural flow regime, rather than biological interactions alone, is the main control on native fish persistence in this system, and they call for long-term monitoring to refine hypotheses about flood and drought effects.

## Background & Description

The upper Verde River is described as the only major perennial stream in Arizona that is not substantially altered by dams or diversions over its study reach. Eight native species were reported to persist there, including desert sucker, Sonoran sucker, roundtail chub, longfin dace, spikedace, speckled dace, and two large species. [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were reported as historically present, with the text noting they were unlikely to persist in the reach without reintroduction. A group of non-native fishes, including [[Red Shiner]], [[Common Carp]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]] and [[Fathead Minnow]], had become established.

The introduction and background section situates the study within regional declines. Dam construction since the early 1900s and non-native fish introductions, which began before 1900, were identified as the two main combined impacts that reduced native fish range and numbers across the Southwest, often within a few years of their action. Where both occurred, native fish were often eliminated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Precipitation and streamflow in the Southwest are described as unpredictable. Floods above roughly 400 m³/s in the upper Verde were said to occur randomly, and cycles of flood and drought were considered evident.
- **Groundwater:** The headwater spring system (Verde Springs area) is a major source of baseflow, and a proposal to extract water for municipal supply raised concerns about effects on flow and native fish.
- **Flood disturbance:** The 50+ year flood (spring 1993) and the 7-year flood (1995) both reduced fish abundance and altered population structure. Floods are said to scour and rearrange stream substrates, rejuvenating habitat for native fish spawning and recruitment.
- **Competition and predation:** Non-native fishes are described as likely to compete with and prey on natives, which may explain the slower recovery of non-native populations after floods.
- **Study design:** The 60 km reach was sampled at seven sites spaced about 10 km apart, with backpack electrofishing, seines and dip nets across habitat types.

## Key Findings

- **Stable community ratio:** Across the study the native-to-non-native ratio was most consistently about 85:15, varying by river location, species and hydrograph.
- **Flood effects:** The 7-year flood (1995) reduced native fish by 86% and non-native fish by 99%, but the ratio shifted only slightly (from 82:18 to 85:15). The 50+ year flood (1993) was followed by a rise in native dominance, with natives reaching 96% of the community by autumn 1993.
- **Recovery:** By spring 1996, native fish had increased on average by over 700%, while non-native fish increased by about 7% despite marked increases in red shiner and smallmouth bass.
- **Pre-flood baseline:** Data from the 1986 survey recorded 88% native fish, while data from 1984–1992 drought conditions showed non-natives comprised only 12% of the community, suggesting other mechanisms also influence the ratio.
- **Red shiner:** Red shiner increased more than 100-fold between spring and autumn 1994 at Sycamore site, then collapsed by about 99% by 1995 and remained low in 1996. Fathead minnow was essentially absent after autumn 1993.
- **Green sunfish:** The only non-native species to increase in absolute numbers between 1994 and 1995 (about 529 individuals), most of the increase occurring in upper reaches, possibly due to overflowing ponds or a flood magnitude below the threshold for harm.
- **Management implications:** Resource managers need reliable data on flow regimes and need to plan for flows that conserve native fish, particularly as a period of low flow was considered probable after the 1993 and 1995 floods.
- **Research gap:** Long-term (>5 year) studies of native fish in comparable desert rivers were not found, so the Verde River project was described as unique and a valuable opportunity to test hypotheses on flood and drought effects in the Southwest.
