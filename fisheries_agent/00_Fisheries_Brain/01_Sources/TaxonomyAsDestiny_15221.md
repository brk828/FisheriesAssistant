# Nature Vol. 347, 13 September 1990: Lunar Origin, Tuatara Conservation and Tame Tornadoes

#herpetology #sphenodon #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This issue excerpt from [[Nature]] (Vol. 347, 13 September 1990) contains three unrelated pieces. Michael J. Drake questions whether the Moon was derived substantially from the Earth, arguing that the [[Giant-Impact Theory]] does not yet explain all chemical and isotopic evidence. Robert M. May's News and Views article uses the [[Tuatara]] to argue that conservation priorities should weigh [[Taxonomic Distinctness]], not just species counts, and summarises proposed weighting schemes. A short Daedalus column by David Jones proposes a "tame tornado" power-generation concept. The most substantial material concerns tuatara taxonomy and conservation, and the methods for reserve selection.

## Background & Description

**Lunar origin (Drake).** Drake reviews geochemical arguments about the Moon's composition. Key points are:
- The Rb/Cs ratio in the lunar mantle is higher than in the terrestrial mantle. Caesium is more volatile than Rb, so the terrestrial Rb/Cs ratio should be the same as or lower than the Moon's.
- The ⁴¹K/³⁹K ratio in lunar feldspars is lower than in terrestrial feldspars. A substantially Earth-derived Moon would be expected to show an equal or higher ratio, because the lighter isotope would be preferentially lost in any volatile-loss process.
- Abundances of V, Cr and Mn in Earth and Moon would need to reflect megabar metal/silicate partitioning deep inside the Earth. Until this is demonstrated, Drake treats the Earth-derived Moon idea as a theory.

Drake credits Ringwood and co-workers with cautioning against accepting the giant-impact theory without robust support.

**Tuatara (May's commentary on Daugherty et al.).** The tuatara is a large, iguana-like reptile and the sole surviving member of a once-flourishing group whose heyday was the Triassic Period, more than 200 million years ago. It is not closely related to superficially similar lizards. It has a well-developed third eye (a vestigial pineal organ in most vertebrates) and a row of yellow spines down its back, which gives the name from Maori *tua* ("on the back") and *tara* ("spine"). The commentary reports:
- Three tuatara species were recognised in the 19th century. One is now extinct.
- *Sphenodon guntheri* was described in 1877 but has been ignored by legislation. The 1895 Animals Protection Act in New Zealand recognised only [[Sphenodon Punctatus]].
- Daugherty et al. report allozyme variation and morphological differences from 30 islands where tuatara populations are thought to survive. These data support the status of *S. guntheri* as a distinct species.

**Reserve selection (May's commentary).** The article summarises the taxonomic-weighting debate initiated by [[Vane-Wright Et Al.]] and related work by [[Atkinson]] and [[Ackery And Vane-Wright]].

**Tame tornado (Daedalus).** A tornado gathers solar energy from a wide area. Daedalus proposes a tethered, inflated vertical "windsock" over a heated convection cell to drive a generator. Propane burners and fans would start the convection, and the system could run for days or weeks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tuatara conservation status.** Daugherty et al. report the following:
- Of a total of 40 documented populations, 10 have become extinct over the past century.
- Fewer than 300 individuals of *S. guntheri* survive, confined to North Brother Island in [[Cook Strait]]. One other population became extinct earlier this century.
- Intervention for endangered tuatara has been limited, in contrast to successful management of threatened New Zealand birds. On four islands where *S. punctatus* populations are close to extinction, endangered birds have been introduced and intensively managed, possibly to the detriment of the tuatara.

**Taxonomic weighting.** May describes [[Vane-Wright Et Al.]] scheme, which assigns each species an index of distinctness inversely proportional to the number of nodes between its twig tip and the root of a hierarchical classification. Two extremes are considered:
- Equal weighting of all species, which gives the two tuatara species 0.03% of total reptile weight.
- Equal weighting of all [[Sister Group]]s, which gives them 50%.

May estimates that under the Vane-Wright scheme, tuatara represent between 0.3% and 7% of the taxonomic distinctness among the roughly 6,000 reptile species, probably closer to the lower value.

**Reserve design.** Ackery and Vane-Wright studied the [[Milkweed Butterflies]] (158 species) using about 350 sample areas worldwide. A greedy algorithm, which selects the richest area first and then the best area for remaining species, yields 31 reserves covering all species. The first choice is Sulawesi, with 33 species (9 endemic). May notes this approach is efficient, though not guaranteed minimal.

## Key Findings

### Lunar origin
- Rb/Cs and ⁴¹K/³⁹K isotope ratios are inconsistent with a simple volatile-loss model from an Earth-derived Moon.
- V, Cr and Mn partitioning evidence has not yet confirmed a substantial Earth origin. Drake concludes the Earth-derived Moon remains "a theory."
- The giant-impact theory does not yet explain all chemical and isotopic parameters with sufficient robustness.

### Tuatara taxonomy and conservation
- *Sphenodon guntheri* is a distinct species supported by allozyme and morphological evidence, but it has not been recognised in legislation since 1895.
- Legislative monotypy has likely contributed to underestimating the threat, given that 10 of 40 populations have been lost in the last century.
- Fewer than 300 *S. guntheri* individuals survive on a single island, and management effort has been limited.

### Taxonomic distinctness in conservation
- Equal weighting of species, equal weighting of sister groups, and the node-count and branch-sum schemes give very different relative values for distinct taxa.
- The branch-sum refinement changes values in partially resolved trees but makes no difference in fully resolved trees. Relative values under that refinement are 1, 1, 1, 1.75 and 3.5 for species A to E, versus 1, 1, 1, 1.5 and 3 under the node-count scheme.
- In a study of [[Bombus Sibiricus Group]] bumblebees (43 species, 120 grid squares), the top-ranking square changes from Ecuador (10 species, 23% of total) to Gansu, China (23% of the weighted distinctness total, versus 15% for Ecuador) when distinctness is included.
- May argues that a "calculus of biodiversity" must combine taxonomic distinctness with abundance and geographic distribution.

### Reserve selection
- A greedy algorithm on 350 sample areas yields 31 reserves capturing all 158 milkweed butterfly species.

### Tame tornado concept (Daedalus)
- A tethered, inflated windsock over a solar-heated convection cell could drive a generator. Start-up would use fans and propane burners.
- A grid of such devices could theoretically starve wild tornadoes of energy.
- The concept is speculative and presented as a column rather than a tested design.
