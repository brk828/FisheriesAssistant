# Distribution and Taxonomy of Fishes in the Mackenzie Drainage of British Columbia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper reports new and previously published records for 28 freshwater fish species in tributaries of the [[Mackenzie River]] in northern British Columbia, and analyses how fishes crossed the continental divide between the Arctic and Pacific drainages after the last glaciation. The author sampled 22 localities along an 880-mile road traverse of the [[Peace River]] and [[Liard River]] systems in 1953, and drew on earlier collections and literature. The study finds that mountains acted as a partial, not absolute, barrier to fish dispersal, and that several taxonomic names (subspecies in [[Lota lota]] and [[Couesius plumbeus]]) lack adequate support.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** About one third of British Columbia lies in the Mackenzie watershed. The Peace, Liard and Hay rivers drain some 108,000 square miles of the province. The Peace flows east to [[Lake Athabaska]], which drains north through [[Great Slave Lake]] into the Mackenzie and then the Arctic Ocean. The Peace's principal tributaries are the Finlay and Parsnip rivers, which run along the Rocky Mountain Trench. The Liard receives the Dease, Kechika and Fort Nelson rivers. The eastern parts of the Peace and Liard systems lie on the Interior Plains, with gentle profiles and extensive marshes. The western parts lie within the Canadian Cordillera.

**Sampling.** The fish faunas of the [[Fraser River]] and [[Skeena River]] were relatively well sampled, but Mackenzie collections in British Columbia were scant. The 1953 expedition (with Dr. P. A. Larkin) and later collections by the British Columbia Game Commission and the Institute of Fisheries, University of British Columbia, provided most new material. Sites are keyed to a numbered map (Fig. 1) and an Appendix of 27 localities.

**Nomenclature.** The paper adopts several name changes, including *Prosopium* to *Coregonus*, *Cristivomer* to *Salvelinus*, and *Leucichthys* to *Coregonus* (after Eschmeyer and Bailey, 1955). Common names follow the American Fisheries Society recommendations, with older names in brackets where they differ markedly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Glacial dispersal.** After the last glaciation, fishes reached British Columbia by following low points in the [[Continental Divide]]. Some entered from the Mackenzie drainage and spread south into the Fraser. Others moved north from the Fraser or Skeena into the [[Peace River]]. Four source areas are identified: the Arctic and Bering land bridge, the Pacific watershed south of the ice, the Mississippi basin, and the Atlantic coast drainage. Euryhaline species also came from the sea.

**Headwater piracy.** The Peace and Fraser/Skeena headwaters lie at very low elevations at several points, and the terrain is unstable. Glacial till, flooding, log jams, beaver dams and delta deposition can all reverse stream flow. The author cites a diversion of the Driftwood River three miles from its original course "within living memory." Glacial lake basins north and west of Prince George may once have drained north. These factors make headwater exchange easy.

**Dispersal types.** The fauna is grouped into four distribution types:
- **Type A (widespread):** species found on both sides of the divide, including [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Lake Trout]] and [[Dolly Varden]], with probable northern or Mississippi origins.
- **Type B (east of the divide):** species such as [[Trout-Perch]], [[Flathead Chub]], [[Spoonhead Sculpin]] and [[Yellow Walleye]] that are absent from the Yukon and probably derived from the Mississippi centre.
- **Type C (Pacific slope only):** species such as the white sturgeon and the leopard dace, which reach but do not cross the divide.
- **Type D (recent Pacific invaders):** [[Largescale Sucker]], [[Peamouth Chub]], [[Northern Squawfish]], [[Redside Shiner]] and prickly sculpin, which have penetrated the upper Peace only recently.

**Barriers and limits.** The author suggests that ecological limits, such as summer air temperature isotherms for [[Northern Redbelly Dace]] and [[Brook Stickleback]], may restrict northward range as much as geography does.

## Key Findings

**Overall distribution**
- 28 species are listed in the abstract, and the discussion covers 31 species reaching the divide at the Peace headwaters (excluding anadromous Pacific salmon).
- 17 species occur in both the Peace and Fraser, which suggests that mountains are a check but not an insurmountable obstacle to postglacial expansion.
- Five Pacific-slope species (largescale sucker, redside shiner, northern squawfish, peamouth chub and prickly sculpin) have recently penetrated the upper Peace.
- The discussion gives 18 species as failing to cross the divide, with 10 restricted to the east slope and 8 to the west. It states that the remaining 18 species occupy both the Peace and Fraser, of which 7 have limited east-slope distributions (recent crossings) and 4 have limited Pacific-slope distributions. These figures do not sum to 31 as written, so they should be treated with caution.
- Eleven species intolerant of salt water are common to the Skeena and Fraser systems, which points to easy headwater interchange.

**New records for British Columbia**
- [[Flathead Chub]] (*Platygobio gracilis*), [[Trout-Perch]] (*Percopsis omiscomaycus*) and [[Spoonhead Sculpin]] (*Cottus ricei*) are reported from the province for the first time.
- [[Brassy Minnow]] (*Hybognathus hankinsoni*) is new to the Mackenzie drainage. It was found in Summit Lake, a warm creek entering Summit Lake, and in the upper Fraser near Prince George. This extends its known range about 400 miles north and raises doubt about artificial introduction, since the nearest sources in Montana and North Dakota are about 1000 miles away.
- [[Pygmy Whitefish]] (*Coregonus coulteri*) is first recorded from the Mackenzie drainage, from young seine-caught specimens at Dease Lake.
- [[Round Whitefish]] (*Coregonus cylindraceus*) is newly recorded from Liard localities (Liard River, Simmons Lake and Dease Lake), outside the Yukon drainage.
- The report of *Pfrille neogaea* from Charlie Lake is attributed to a transcription error.

**Taxonomic conclusions**
- **[[Lota lota]] (burbot):** The data do not support subspecific names. Morphological clines in peduncle ratio (Table I) are continuous or step-wise across North America and Eurasia, and the author proposes calling all forms *Lota lota*.
- **[[Couesius plumbeus]] (lake chub):** Subspecies are not recognized. British Columbia specimens have 54–65 lateral-line scales (mean 59.4, n = 53), largely outside the published range of 64–72 for *dissimilis*. The earlier equation of *C. p. greeni* and *C. p. dissimilis* is judged erroneous. The author also retains the genus *Couesius* rather than merging it with *Hybopsis*.
- **Other taxa:** Subspecific distinctions are considered invalid in several cases, including *Salmo gairdneri* and *Salvelinus alpinus malma*, where characters vary phenotypically.

**Meristic patterns**
- In [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*), anal fin ray counts in the Peace collections (sample sizes 64, 60 and 46) average 15.45. This is higher than the British Columbia average of 14.69 and the northern United States average of 13.10 (Lindsey, 1953), which suggests a south-to-north increase within comparable temperature zones. The author notes that light may also influence these counts.

**Range extensions**
- [[Largescale Sucker]] (*Catostomus macrocheilus*) now extends to the upper Peace at the Alberta border.
- [[Peamouth Chub]] (*Mylocheilus caurinum*) is recorded from a 50-mile reach of the upper Peace, including Summit and McLeod lakes.
- [[Northern Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus oregonense*) is recorded near the Alberta border, where it coexists with [[Northern Pike]]. The author suggests it could spread into the Mackenzie basin and affect commercial fisheries in large lakes such as Lake Athabaska.
- [[Lake Chub]] and [[Arctic Grayling]] are reported from the upper Yukon and Pacific-slope headwaters through headwater capture.

**Conservation and management notes**
- The [[Redside Shiner]] is known to have harmed the [[Steelhead Trout]] sport fishery at Paul Lake, and its spread may threaten native Mackenzie fishes.
- Hybridization between *Catostomus commersoni* and *C. macrocheilus* is suspected near Prince George and at Summit Lake, and needs further study.

**Limitations.** The author notes that collections in the Mackenzie drainage remain sparse, that many museum burbot specimens are unexamined, and that the *Salvelinus* complex needs taxonomic clarification. Further collections and controlled laboratory rearing are recommended.
