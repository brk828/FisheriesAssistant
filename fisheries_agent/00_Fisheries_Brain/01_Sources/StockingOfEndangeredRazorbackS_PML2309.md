# Stocking of Endangered Razorback Suckers in the Lower Colorado River Basin over Three Decades (1974–2004)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This synthesis by [[Jesse D. Schooley]] and [[Paul C. Marsh]] (School of Life Sciences, Arizona State University) reviews three decades of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) stocking in the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species, federally listed as endangered in 1991, was extirpated from most of its historical range. Millions of hatchery-reared and wild-born fish were repatriated, yet the authors conclude that stocking has been largely unsuccessful: estimated first-year survival of repatriates was under 1%, long-term survival is unknown, and no new self-sustaining populations have been established. The paper argues that survival depends strongly on release size, and that future recovery requires predator-free habitats and larger minimum release sizes.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid that was historically widespread and abundant in the larger streams of the Colorado River basin, ranging from Sonora, Mexico, to Wyoming. Historical densities are poorly quantified, but the species was once used as food and fertilizer and supported a commercial fishery. Its decline is attributed to habitat loss and modification combined with predation by introduced nonnative fishes.

Remaining wild populations occur in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]] (lower basin) and in parts of the Colorado, Duchesne, Green, White, and lower Yampa rivers (upper basin). Adults are long-lived (>40 years), and although they spawn annually, early life stages are rapidly consumed by introduced predators, so recruitment to adulthood is curtailed.

Stocking history began in 1974, when 40 wild adults from Lake Mohave became the first broodstock at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Two repatriation approaches developed:
- **Hatchery broodstock approach:** used for the main-stem Colorado River (including [[Lake Havasu]]) and waters in Arizona, Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico.
- **Lake Mohave wild-progeny program:** only wild-born progeny are stocked, reducing bottleneck effects from limited broodstock parents.

Related big-river natives sharing protected or declining status include [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and [[Desert Pupfish]]. Under the [[Big-River Fishes Management Plan]] and recovery documents, management strategies include augmentation via stocking, use of isolated habitats, and exploiting unique opportunities such as dam closures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat alteration:** Eight dams on the lower main stem and 11 on its tributaries have dramatically altered or eliminated the historical floodplain system.
- **Nonnative predation:** Introduced predators consume early life stages; the authors report that more than 100 nonnative fish species have been released into the lower basin, and about three-fourths have established populations or are still actively stocked.
- **Native-nonnative conflicts:** Management conflicts between native fish recovery and nonnative sport fisheries impede creation of segregated, predator-free habitats.
- **Critical habitat:** The Colorado, Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers of the lower basin were designated critical habitat in 1991, including Pierce Ferry to Davis Dam (with Lakes Mead and Mohave) and Parker Dam to Imperial Dam.
- **Former marine visitors:** Machete, spotted sleeper, and striped mullet once entered the historical Colorado River from the Sea of Cortez, but dams and reduced flows now restrict them to the southernmost reaches.

## Key Findings

**Scope and data**
- Study area: the lower Colorado River below Lees Ferry, Arizona, plus the Gila, Salt, and Verde river watersheds and tributaries.
- Records were compiled from agency receipts, hatchery reports, and databases (USFWS, AGFD, CDFG, USBR, BLM). Stocking receipts were favored over summaries or third-party sources.
- Records covering 15% of repatriated fish lacked sufficient size data and were excluded from survival analysis.

**Scale of stocking**
- A total of **14.6 million** razorback suckers were repatriated in **544 batches** at nearly **200 sites**.
- Distribution by destination: 146 to Lake Mead; 124,942 to Lake Mohave; 514,098 to Lake Havasu; 2.5 million to the lower river below Parker Dam; and 11.4 million to central Arizona rivers and streams (Verde River 837,347; Salt River 9.4 million; Gila River 1 million).
- About **85%** of stocked records were larvae (<27 mm TL).
- Roughly 96% of fish were stocked at an average of 2 million per year during 1982–1988, before listing. Since 1992, an average of 26,227 fish were stocked annually (range 5,775–79,313).

**Survival estimates**
- A size-based first-year survival model derived from Lake Mohave mark-recapture data (1992–2002) was applied to all batches. Its formula used B = 22.782459 and INT = −9.0170896, with TL in millimetres.
- Estimated first-year survivors: **39,149 fish**, an overall rate of **0.27%** (under 1%).
- Lake Mead batches (mean dTL 444 mm) showed an estimated 65% survival, but this reflects large release size. Central Arizona waters (mean dTL 23 mm) showed 0.09%.
- Pre-1997 stockings had estimated survival of 0.07%. Post-1996 stockings had an estimated survival of 14.72% (range 10.57–23.58%), and about three-fourths of overall survivors were stocked during 1997–2004.
- Less than 0.02% of lower-basin stocked fish have been recaptured, and nearly one-fourth of recaptures occurred shortly after stocking.

**Size-length methods**
- Mean total length was derived from text, weight-based inverse regressions (small juveniles N = 47, R² = 0.94; juveniles N = 2,138, R² = 0.93; adult males N = 515, R² = 0.90; adult females N = 278, R² = 0.93), or minimum TL when no mean was provided.

**Population status**
- Lake Mohave's wild population was near 73,000 during 1980–1993. It has since fallen to fewer than 3,000 wild fish in 2001 and fewer than 500 in 2006 (unpublished data).
- Recovery goals require multiple populations of at least 5,800 adults each before downlisting, which the authors consider unlikely in the near term.

**Management conclusions**
- Stocking has been unsuccessful overall; long-term survival is unknown and no new populations have been established.
- Survival increases with release size. Repatriates are now held in nonnative-free environments until they reach at least 300 mm TL, which takes at least a year and yields about 10% first-year survival in the wild. Releasing all fish at 350 mm TL would have produced an estimated more than 100-fold increase in survivors.
- Predator-free habitat creation and dam decommissioning to restore floodplains are needed for self-sustaining populations. The 2005 [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] (50 years, $626 million) is expected to alleviate institutional roadblocks.
- Continued human management, including increasing minimum release size, is the only current means of perpetuating the species.
