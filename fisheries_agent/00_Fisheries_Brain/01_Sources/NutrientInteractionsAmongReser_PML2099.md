# Nutrient Interactions Among Reservoirs on the Colorado River

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This paper examines how the impoundment of [[Lake Powell]] reshaped the nutrient chemistry, productivity and [[Trophic Status]] of [[Lake Mead]], a large reservoir about 450 km downstream. Using [[Grand Canyon Gaging Station]] records and a 1977-1978 [[Nutrient Budget]] for Lake Mead, the authors show that upstream reservoirs can change both the discharge and the nutrient content reaching downstream reservoirs. Biological retention in [[Lake Powell]] lowered nitrate and phosphorus inputs to Lake Mead, shifting the [[Upper Basin]] toward [[Phosphorus Limitation]] and the [[Lower Basin]] toward [[Nitrogen Limitation]]. The authors conclude that multi-reservoir systems must be managed collectively.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] system contains two of the largest reservoirs in the United States:

- **[[Lake Mead]]:** impounded in 1935 by [[Hoover Dam]].
- **[[Lake Powell]]:** impounded in 1953 by [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

The two reservoirs are similar in volume and surface area but differ in morphometry. Lake Powell is highly sinuous. Lake Mead is split by [[Boulder Canyon]] into an Upper Basin (including the [[Virgin Basin]], [[Overton Arm]] and [[Upper Arm]]) and a Lower Basin (including [[Boulder Basin]] and [[Las Vegas Bay]], extending to Hoover Dam).

Inflow to Lake Mead comes from four main sources:

- Powell discharge, providing about 18% of inflow.
- The [[Virgin River]] and [[Muddy River]], entering the Overton Arm.
- [[Las Vegas Wash]], which carries secondary-treated sewage and industrial effluent into Las Vegas Bay.
- The remaining inflow from the Colorado River and tributaries.

Data came from USGS Water Supply Papers (1940-1967), later USGS and state water-resource reports, and the authors' own limnological work on Lake Mead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Discharge and temperature.** Before 1963, spring runoff produced very high flows in May and June. Regulated releases from Lake Powell removed the spring peak and stabilized flow through the year. River temperatures rose by about 10°C in late fall and winter and fell by nearly 10°C during the rest of the year. Discharge temperature at Glen Canyon Dam averages about 8°C. In summer, water warms to 10-11°C at the Grand Canyon gage and 15-18°C at [[Pierce's Ferry]], where the river enters Lake Mead. These summer temperatures are still nearly 10°C colder than before Lake Powell formed.

**Suspended sediment.** The most obvious change is a sharp drop in sediment load. In high-runoff years, up to 140 million metric tons of suspended sediment once flowed into Lake Mead, mostly in spring. Lara and Saunders (1970) estimated that 47% of this material was deposited in the Pierce's Ferry area, with extensive siltation elsewhere in both basins. Sediment loads fell by 70-80% after Lake Powell filled to operating level in 1966. Most sediment now entering Lake Mead comes from tributaries in the Grand Canyon.

**Nitrate and conservative elements.** Nitrate loads fell sharply after Lake Powell formed, then rose during 1966-1969 and have since declined toward an apparent steady state. Conservative elements such as sodium and potassium showed no comparable change, so the nitrate pattern is attributed to biological processes in Lake Powell. Early retention of inflowing nitrate was probably followed by a transient release of nitrogen from sediments. Lake Mead sediments deposited just after impoundment also lost nitrogen briefly.

**Turbid overflow.** Before Lake Powell, the Colorado River formed a turbid overflow spreading across most of the Upper Basin of Lake Mead in spring. Reduced spring discharge, lower sediment load and cooler water now make the river sink deeper, so the overflow no longer forms there. Johnson and Merritt (1979) show that the river now forms a turbid overflow in Lake Powell, similar to the former Lake Mead pattern.

## Key Findings

**1. Upstream reservoir effects reach downstream systems.** Changes caused by Lake Powell's formation altered the nutrient status of Lake Mead. In multi-reservoir systems, biological retention in one reservoir directly reduces inputs to the next.

**2. Nutrient sources are spatially separated.** The 1977-1978 budget found:
- The Colorado River supplied most of the inorganic nitrogen input. The exact percentage is illegible in the scan.
- [[Las Vegas Wash]] contributed 60% of the phosphorus input.

Down-lake flow through [[Boulder Canyon]] confines the Las Vegas Wash phosphorus mostly to the Lower Basin, while the Colorado River supplies most of the nitrogen to that basin. The distant loading, together with unusual morphometry, makes each basin behave like a different lake.

**3. Productivity differs between basins.** Areal phytoplankton productivity and average phosphorus concentrations in Boulder Basin are twice those in Virgin Basin. Nitrate is undetectable in Boulder Basin by June and remains low through September. In Virgin Basin, nitrate drops to about 100 µg/L by mid-summer despite low phosphorus.

**4. Nitrogen limitation develops in the Lower Basin.** Reduced nitrate input from the Upper Basin, combined with high spring productivity, produces nitrogen limitation in summer. Productivity in the Lower Basin falls in June and July.

**5. Late-summer mixing drives productivity.** From July to September, thermocline depth dropped by 10-15 m as epilimnetic temperatures fell. Nitrate stored in the [[Metalimnion]] mixed upward into the epilimnion. In Virgin Basin this produced a slight productivity increase, but because phosphorus is deficient there, most of the nitrate accumulated in the epilimnion. In Boulder Basin, higher phosphorus let the mixed nitrogen trigger a stronger late-summer bloom.

Representative 1978 values (Boulder Basin / Virgin Basin):

| Month | Epilimnion temp (°C) | Metalimnetic nitrate (µg/L) |
|---|---|---|
| June | 23.6 / 23.5 | 305 / 257 |
| July | 26.9 / 26.1 | 193 / 273 |
| August | 25.2 / 24.7 | 162 / 314 |
| September | 22.1 / 21.6 | 158 / 256 |

**6. Lake Powell reduces nitrogen at Hoover Dam.** Nitrate concentrations at [[Hoover Dam]] fell considerably during 1960-1965, rose slightly around 1969-1970, and then declined to very low summer levels. These changes parallel the nitrate loading trends from the Colorado River. Nitrate lost from the [[Hypolimnion]] discharge further reduces concentrations. The Lower Basin became nitrogen-limited in summer in the early 1970s and has remained so.

**7. Lake Powell retains most dissolved phosphorus.** Spring overflow into Lake Powell carries high dissolved phosphorus, which stimulates productivity at the upper end of the reservoir. Phosphorus desorbs from suspended clays and sustains the dissolved pool downstream. About 70% of dissolved phosphorus input is retained in Lake Powell through phytoplankton uptake and possible coprecipitation with calcite. As a result, the Upper Basin of Lake Mead has become more phosphorus-limited since Lake Powell formed. Phosphorus from the Colorado River is now low and fairly constant, at about 5 µg/L as read from the scan.

**8. Productivity gradient has shifted.** Recent measurements show productivity decreasing from the Upper Arm to Virgin Basin and increasing from there to Hoover Dam. Before Lake Powell, the Upper Arm likely had a productivity gradient extending farther down-lake, because Colorado River phosphorus loading was higher. The Lower Basin was probably highly productive in the late 1960s, when phosphorus loading from Las Vegas Wash and higher Colorado River nitrate loading coincided. Nitrogen limitation now restricts summer productivity there.

**9. Management implications.** The authors argue that biological retention in upstream reservoirs can lower downstream productivity. This may be desirable if a downstream reservoir is overly productive, but undesirable if it supports a heavily utilized sport fishery. Because one reservoir's management affects others, the authors conclude that multi-reservoir systems must be managed collectively. Actions in one reservoir should not harm water quality or beneficial uses downstream.
