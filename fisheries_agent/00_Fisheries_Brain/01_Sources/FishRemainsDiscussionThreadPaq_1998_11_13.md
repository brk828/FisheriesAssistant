## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary ichthyological study but a **1998 email listserv thread** (Paquime-L, an archaeology discussion list) in which Southwestern US/Northern Mexico archaeologists exchanged observations on **fish remains recovered from archaeological sites**. The thread was initiated by Robert D. Leonard (University of New Mexico), who questioned whether archaeologists working in the arid desert West were underestimating the role of fish in prehistoric subsistence and settlement patterns. Respondents from multiple excavation projects (Hohokam, Piro Pueblo, Jornada Mogollon, and Casas Grandes-region sites) confirmed recurring but often under-reported fish remains, and raised methodological issues concerning recovery technique (screen/mesh size) as a major driver of detection bias. Notably, one contributor (Cory Breternitz) reported a temporal decline in fish body size at [[Pueblo Grande]], interpreted as possible evidence of prehistoric **overfishing**.

## Background & Description

Limited morphological detail is given, as the source is a correspondence thread rather than a taxonomic study, but several size/taxonomic notes are recorded:

- **Catfish (Ictaluridae)**: Specimens recovered near Roswell, NM (Henderson Village site, excavated by John Speth) included individuals estimated at **~40 lbs**, alongside vertebrae from numerous smaller catfish — indicating a wide size range in the assemblage.
- **[[Longnose Gar]]** (*Lepisosteus osseus*): Mandibles recovered at [[Chaco Canyon]] were interpreted as likely "curiosity" items rather than dietary remains. In contrast, longnose gar recovered at **Qualacu** (a Piro Pueblo site) were part of a subsistence-related faunal assemblage; this species is now **locally extinct in that stretch of the Rio Grande**.
- **Unidentified fish remains** at Galeana and Villa Ahumada (INAH/UNM excavations) ranged from **minnow-sized to medium-sized** fish, recovered primarily via fine-screen fractions and not yet taxonomically identified at time of writing.
- At [[Pueblo Grande]] (Hohokam Classic period, Salt River, AZ), Steve James's analysis achieved identification to **Family, Genus, and in some cases Species level**, and documented a measurable **decrease in fish body size through time** across the occupation sequence (ca. A.D. 1150–1450).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Riverine habitat**: All fish remains discussed derive from freshwater river systems — the **Rio Grande**, **Salt River**, and **Pecos River** drainages — reflecting fish as a resource tied closely to proximity to permanent water in otherwise arid landscapes.
- **Anthropogenic/irrigation habitat**: At Pueblo Grande, fish may have been harvested either directly from the Salt River or from the **adjacent Hohokam canal system**, particularly during cyclical canal-cleaning episodes, blurring the line between "natural" and irrigation-modified fish habitat.
- **Local extinction**: [[Longnose Gar]] is documented as extirpated from the Qualacu-area stretch of the Rio Grande, indicating substantial historical range contraction/habitat alteration in this reach.
- **Freshwater mussels (Unionidae)** co-occur with fish remains in several assemblages (Roswell area, Pecos River sites), and were found even at sites located **~25 miles from the river**, implying transport/trade of aquatic resources inland.
- **Spawning aggregation behavior**: Leonard notes that modern **salmonids** (trout) in regional rivers "gather in great abundance when they spawn," suggesting this seasonal aggregation behavior may have made fish an attractive, concentrated resource for prehistoric exploitation — though this is inferential/analogical rather than directly evidenced in the assemblages discussed.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery bias**: Multiple contributors (Breternitz, Kemrer) emphasize that **screen/mesh size** (e.g., 1/8-inch fine screens, sub-millimeter flotation mesh) strongly determines whether fish remains are detected at all — larger-mesh excavation methods likely undercount fish in the archaeological record across the region.
- **Possible overfishing signal**: At [[Pueblo Grande]], both the **volume** of fish remains increased and the **average size** of fish decreased through the Hohokam Classic period, a pattern interpreted as consistent with sequential depletion of larger/older individuals — i.e., a prehistoric overfishing signature.
- **Nutritional stress context**: Human skeletal analysis at Pueblo Grande indicated the Classic period population was nutritionally stressed and in poor health; charred muskrat remains co-occur with fish remains, raising the question of whether fish and muskrat represent **famine/starvation foods** rather than staple dietary items.
- **Regional distribution pattern**: Most reported subsistence-related fish evidence clusters in **Chihuahuan Desert and semi-arid grassland transition zones** (Roswell area, Rio Grande valley, Casas Grandes region), rather than more forested/well-watered zones — an observation Tim Maxwell flagged as noteworthy.
- **Iconographic evidence**: Fish are depicted in **petroglyphs** in the Las Cruces, NM area and the Bosque del Apache region, supporting the cultural/symbolic (and likely economic) significance of fish beyond the direct faunal record.
- **Historical documentary evidence**: Early Spanish chronicles reportedly note that local Indigenous groups used **nets** to catch fish, which were described as abundant in the Rio Grande at the time of contact.
- **Site examples with confirmed fish remains**: Galeana, Villa Ahumada (Chihuahua, Mexico); [[Pueblo Grande]] (Phoenix, AZ); Henderson Village and other Roswell-area sites; Qualacu (Piro Pueblo, Bosque del Apache NWR, Socorro, NM); Chaco Canyon (limited/non-subsistence use).
- **Site example with fish absence**: Las Huertas (Rio Grande valley) — faunal assemblage included spadefoot toad and box turtle but **no fish remains**, illustrating spatial variability in fish exploitation/preservation even within similar riverine settings.
