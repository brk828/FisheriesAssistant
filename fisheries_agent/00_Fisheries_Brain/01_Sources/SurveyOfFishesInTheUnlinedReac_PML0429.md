# Survey of Fishes in the Unlined Reach of Coachella Canal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #centrarchidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report presents a brief fishery survey of the unlined (earthen) reach of the [[Coachella Canal]] in Imperial and Riverside Counties, California, conducted 1–5 April 2002 by Robert W. Clarkson (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation) and Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University). The [[Coachella Valley Water District]] (CVWD) planned to concrete-line the remaining unlined reach, and the survey aimed to determine whether the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen* sp., OCR-garbled species epithet) was present, since its presence would affect project mitigation.

No razorback suckers were captured. Six fish species were collected by [[Electrofishing]], dominated by [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]]. Because catostomid suckers are known to be poorly sampled by electrofishing, and because the canal provides monotonous habitat, the authors state that the absence of captures does not confirm the species' absence.

## Background & Description

- **Commissioning party:** CVWD, which proposed to concrete-line the remaining earthen portion of the canal. The report was submitted by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation Phoenix Area Office.
- **Target species:** The razorback sucker is known from the [[Colorado River]] near the canal's point of diversion (Minckley 1989) and has been found in receiving habitats of the distributary system (St. Amant et al. 1974). Documented presence in the canal would affect mitigation planning; a negative capture would not confirm absence but would add information on potential project effects.
- **Study area:** The reach to be lined extends from Siphon 7, near Dunlap ("Slab City" area), to Siphon 32, about 3.2 km (2.0 mi) north of the [[Salton Sea]] State Park. The upstream reach was already concrete-lined and was not sampled.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Flow at a Parshall flume at Siphon 7 was low at the start of sampling (the initial value is partly illegible in the scan). It rose abruptly on 2 April and then remained near 1,400 cfs through 5 April, according to CVWD data. The rise followed a canal breach upstream of the study area on 2 April, which reduced water clarity.

**Water quality (2000 hrs, measured at the time of sampling).** Water temperature was 22 °C. A conductance value of approximately 1,060 was recorded, though the units are garbled in the scan (probably µS/cm). Water clarity was poor in downstream reaches.

**Habitat.** The authors describe the canal as monotonous, with limited habitat types sampled. Water clarity varied by reach, which affected electrofishing efficiency: fish could be seen and targeted in clear water (reach 1) but not in turbid downstream reaches, where current was applied to the water column in a more random fashion.

**Sampling sites.** Four reaches were sampled. Reach 1 had clear water. Reaches 2 to 4 were progressively more turbid and downstream.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- **[[Electrofishing]]:** A 14-ft boat with an 18-hp outboard (horsepower partly garbled in the scan) carried a Smith-Root GPP 5.0 electrofisher, operated on pulsed DC at 60 pulses per second. Representative habitats were sampled, including below riffles, along both banks and in the channel. Crane support was provided by CVWD.
- **[[Hoop Net]]s:** Nets were 3 ft in diameter, with 0.5 in mesh, a single throat and an overall length of about 9 ft. They were baited with canned cat food and fished overnight for one night per reach. Three nets were placed side by side along each bank in reaches 1–3, supported by T-posts and ropes. Mid-channel deployment failed in reach 1 because of drag forces. No nets were deployed in reach 4.
- **Effort:** A total of 255 net-hours of hoop-net effort was expended over three nights.

**Results**

- **Species richness:** Electrofishing yielded six fish species (see [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Grass Carp]], [[Flathead Catfish]] in the table) plus a single [[Soft-Shelled Turtle]] in reach 2.
- **Relative abundance:** Channel catfish was consistently the most numerically abundant species on average, followed by common carp, smallmouth bass, largemouth bass and flathead catfish. Grass carp were also collected.
- **Catch per unit effort (CPUE):** Greatest in reach 1 at approximately 2.93 fish/min, where water was clear. In reaches 2, 3 and 4 CPUE was approximately 0.97, 1.03 and 0.51 fish/min respectively. The steep decline downstream is attributed to reduced water clarity rather than the number of electrofishing passes, since the downstream effort was nearly doubled.
- **Razorback sucker:** None captured.

**Discussion and Conclusions**

- The survey was brief and did not capture large numbers of fish. Results suggest the species assemblage is composed mainly of common, non-native fishes.
- Razorback suckers are a rare component of the fish assemblage in the canal. They may occasionally be entrained from the Colorado River, but the canal is not considered suitable for reproduction or for maintaining a population.
- The authors note that a negative result cannot confirm absence because catostomid suckers are highly susceptible to escape from electrofishing, and the monotonous habitat limited sampling effectiveness.
- The authors cite evidence that predation and competition by non-native fishes is the greatest threat to recovery of the razorback sucker (Marsh 1990, reviewed in the report). Non-native fishes dominated the canal catch.
- Where present, the canal does not provide habitat necessary for the sucker's recovery, though it may provide habitat for non-reproducing individuals.
- The report recommends that future assessment consider more suitable distributary habitats for the species.
