# The Hydrobiid Snails of the Cuatro Cienegas Basin: Systematic Relationships and Ecology of a Unique Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Robert Hershler]] (Edwards Aquifer Research and Data Center, Southwest Texas State University) revises the systematics and documents the ecology of the hydrobiid snail fauna of the [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]], Coahuila, Mexico. Earlier work by [[Taylor 1966]] treated three subfamilies, five genera and 12 species as endemic. Anatomical evidence instead places every basin taxon in the Nymphophilinae or Littoridininae, both widespread in North America. The basin holds nine genera (five endemic) and 13 species (nine endemic) across roughly 103 sampled localities. The fauna is partitioned among large springs, groundwater outlets and small streams. The author proposes a local and recent origin for the endemic taxa rather than an ancient lacustrine relict origin.

## Background & Description

The Cuatro Cienegas hydrobiids are small, tiny aquatic snails with high local endemism. Many species have relatively large, sculptured or color-banded shells, unlike most hydrobiids, which have small, smooth, unpigmented shells. The basin's diversity of nine genera and 12 species within a 30 x 40 km valley is described as unparalleled in North and Central America at the time.

Earlier classifications relied on shell, radula, operculum and penis characters. The author argues these characters have converged among non-related rissoacean taxa, so [[Soft-Part Anatomy]] is required for reliable classification. Anatomical groups are separated by female reproductive anatomy: in Nymphophilinae sperm travel along the pallial oviduct, while in Littoridininae they enter a separate spermathecal duct. Nymphophilines have a penis with glandular ridges, and littoridinines have tentacles with ciliary tufts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat partitioning.** Three habitat groups are recognized:
- Species with large, thickened shells (e.g., [[Mexipyrgus churinceanus]], [[Mexithauma quadripaludium]], [[Nymphophilus minckleyi]]) inhabit large springs and their outflows.
- Minute, blind, unpigmented species (e.g., [[Paludiscala caramba]], [[Coahuilix hubbsi]]) are restricted to groundwater outlets at small spring heads.
- Pigmented species ([[Durangonella coahuilae]], "Stiobia" n. sp.) occupy small spring outflows and streams. Dark pigment is suggested to protect against intense insolation.

**Micro-habitat partitioning in large springs.** In Laguna Mojarral East (a limnocrene of about 9,000 m²), each of three species dominates a different substrate: M. churinceanus in soft copropel sediment (0.99 of soft-sediment samples), N. minckleyi on aquatic vegetation, and M. quadripaludium on travertine undersides. Where micro-habitat diversity is reduced, species "switch" to the usual habitat of another, which suggests partly competitive substrate use.

**Downstream decline.** Abundances decline downstream because vegetation thins, sediments become algal-covered, temperature fluctuates (31.0–34.5 °C at the springhead versus 20.6–37.8 °C 77 m downstream over four days) and salts concentrate. Predation by molluscivorous [[Cichlid Fishes]] may also limit large snails, with thick shells providing resistance.

**Groundwater and other habitats.** Blind species are never found in open stream reaches, but they can extend below springheads where riparian cover creates subterranean-like conditions. Only D. coahuilae occurs in playa lakes and pools with large temperature and chemical fluctuations (9.5–35.6 °C), and it does not enter the saline waters of the basin.

**Reproduction.** Water temperatures in large spring pools remain nearly constant (about 26–32 °C through the year). At least one species, N. minckleyi in Laguna Mojarral East, reproduces year-round. Juveniles comprised at least 48% of samples, with a March peak of about 80%. Mexipyrgus churinceanus, M. quadripaludium and Cochliopina milleri are viviparous and carry embryos throughout the year.

## Key Findings

- **No endemic subfamilies.** All basin taxa belong to the Nymphophilinae or Littoridininae. The features used to separate Taylor's three endemic subfamilies (Mexithaumatinae, Paludiscalinae, Nymphophilinae as endemic) are found in non-endemic hydrobiids.
- **Taxonomic reduction.** The six nominal species of Mexipyrgus are reduced to one, M. churinceanus, after morphological data from 33 populations showed intergrading shell features.
- **New taxa and records.** One genus ("Stiobia" n. sp., a new nymphophiline genus) and three species (Coahuilix n. sp., Nymphophilus n. sp., and "Stiobia" n. sp.) are new, to be described elsewhere. Cochliopina riograndensis and Orygoceras (?) sp. are new to the basin.
- **Endemic genera resemble non-endemics.** Three of five endemic genera closely resemble non-endemic taxa in phenetic and cladistic analyses. Mexipyrgus, Durangonella and Mexithauma (endemic) are grouped with non-endemic Coahuilix, Paludiscala and Cochliopina.
- **Three littoridinine clades.** The Littoridininae of the basin fall into three groups: Paludiscala/Coahuilix (oviparous, blind), Mexithauma/Cochliopina (viviparous), and Mexipyrgus/Durangonella (penis with ciliated tip).
- **Origin.** The authors propose a local and recent (late Cenozoic) origin for the endemic fauna, supported by low endemism, close similarity to non-endemics, low intrabasin differentiation and proximity to outside waters. The Rio Grande (Rio Bravo del Norte) has likely had repeated connections with basin waters.
- **Rapid evolution hypothesis.** Rapid shell thickening in large springs may reflect selection by cichlid predation, a pattern also documented in other freshwater faunas.
- **Methodological caveat.** Blind species may be underestimated because standard collecting does not reach groundwater, and Paludiscala and Coahuilix may occur beyond the basin.
