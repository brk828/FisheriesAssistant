# Red River Shiner (*Notropis bairdi*) in Kansas and Depletion of the Arkansas River Shiner (*Notropis girardi*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by Cross, Gorman, and Haslouer, published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science*, documents the spread of [[Notropis Bairdi]] (Red River Shiner) into the [[Cimarron River]] drainage of Kansas and Oklahoma. Over the same period, its cognate species [[Notropis Girardi]] (Arkansas River Shiner) disappeared from much of its former range. The authors attribute the decline mainly to reduced flows in the [[Arkansas River]] caused by irrigation and groundwater depletion. High summer discharge is needed for [[Notropis Girardi]] to spawn and complete its pelagic egg and larval stages.

## Background & Description

*Notropis bairdi* was formerly endemic to the [[Red River Basin]] in Oklahoma and Texas. It was first recorded in the Arkansas system in 1976, in Logan County, Oklahoma. The authors' Kansas material came from a 1979 specimen (KU 18016, 49 mm standard length) and several additional series collected from 1979 to 1981 in the Cimarron River and Big Sandy Creek in Meade and Clark counties. Each series included more than one age group, and adults in at least one series showed pronounced reproductive development.

The two species are structurally and ecologically similar. *N. bairdi* is larger and more robust, with a mean standard length of 62 mm versus 49 mm for *N. girardi*. Diagnostic characters (see [[Morphological Characters]]) include anal fin-ray count (usually 8 in *N. girardi* and 7 in *N. bairdi*), pectoral fin shape, dorsal fin height, eye diameter, and nape scalation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both species occupy shallow, often turbid main channels of High Plains streams. These habitats have uniformly sandy, unshaded beds, widely fluctuating flows, high summer temperatures, high evaporation, and high dissolved solids. One *N. bairdi* series was taken at 38 °C, in a main channel with mean depth 19.8 cm, current 0.5 m/s, and sand substrate. *N. girardi* at a 1964 site was described from depths of 15–70 cm with swift current over sand.

**Reproduction:** *N. girardi* spawns only during high flows in late spring or summer. Its eggs drift pelagically in midstream currents and hatch in about 48 hours. The reproductive mode of *N. bairdi* is unknown, though it is presumed to be similar. Both species appear to spawn once per year over a brief interval. Their fecundities are high and roughly equal, and both exceed the egg complements reported for ten other *Notropis* species in Carlander (1969) (see [[Fecundity Comparison]]).

**Diet:** Stomach contents of five female *N. bairdi* were dominated by terrestrial insects (Orthoptera, Hemiptera, Coleoptera, Mecoptera, Diptera, Hymenoptera). Only one of 16 food items was aquatic.

**Flow decline:** Irrigation has diverted much surface water and lowered groundwater tables across southwestern Kansas. Water tables fell more than 10 ft over much of the region between 1950 and 1975, and more than 100 ft in some areas. The effect has been more severe on the [[Arkansas River]] mainstream than on the [[Cimarron River]]. The authors' main flow evidence comes from the U.S. Geological Survey station at [[Syracuse Gauging Station]] and the [[Kansas Highway 23 Station]] on the Cimarron.

## Key Findings

- **Range shift in the Cimarron:** *N. bairdi* is now common across the Cimarron drainage in Oklahoma and Kansas, largely replacing *N. girardi*. Felley and Cothran (1981) found *N. bairdi* at all 11 Oklahoma sites sampled in June–July 1979.
- **Local replacement:** At the Kansas Highway 23 site, *N. girardi* numbered 174 specimens in 1964. In 1979, *N. bairdi* numbered 45 and *N. girardi* none. At the Big Sandy Creek site, 167 *N. bairdi* were taken in 1979, with no *N. girardi* recorded.
- **Historic abundance:** Ten pre-1964 Cimarron collections held by the University of Oklahoma contained an average of 245 *N. girardi* per collection.
- **Arkansas River depletion:** *N. girardi* was not found at seven Cimarron localities sampled in 1979. Neither species was found at six Arkansas mainstream sites in 1979, at eight sites in 1980 (Clarke, 1980), or in Kansas Fish and Game Commission collections from 1967–1974. The authors suggest *N. girardi* may have disappeared from most of its former Kansas range.
- **Flow changes at Syracuse (Arkansas River, 25,763 sq mi drainage):**
  - Flows above 1000 cfs occurred on only 2 days from 1970 onward.
  - Flows were below 3 cfs more than half the time in 1977–1980.
  - Before 1943, flows exceeded 1000 cfs in 21 of 22 years of record.
  - June flows before 1943 exceeded 200 cfs about 50% of the time. In June 1977–1980 they exceeded 200 cfs about 25% of the time, and 920 cfs on only one day.
- **Cimarron flows:** Discharge at Kansas Highway 23 (8,536 sq mi drainage) declined less than on the Arkansas. Flows exceeded 1000 cfs in 15 of 23 years of record at three gauging stations. In 1980, discharge exceeded 110 cfs on only one day.
- **Reproductive inference:** Recent Syracuse flows are unlikely to support *N. girardi* reproduction, because it depends on abrupt high-flow pulses sustained long enough for egg and larval development.
- **Fecundity and reproduction:** Both species have high, roughly equal fecundity, with ova nearly uniform in size within each gonad. The authors infer annual, brief spawning for both (see [[Fecundity Comparison]]).
- **Size data (after preservation in 70% ethanol):**
  - *N. girardi* mature males: 37–44 mm; mature females: 37–49 mm; average 40 mm in both sexes.
  - *N. bairdi* males: mean 42 mm (range 32–51 mm); females: mean 48 mm (range 39–55 mm).
- **Fecundity ranges (Table 3):**
  - *N. bairdi* (KU 18474, six females, 42–54 mm SL): estimated total ova 1520–3314; GSI 0.313–0.469; egg diameter 0.71–0.76 mm.
  - *N. girardi* (KU 3959, six females, 38–49 mm SL): estimated total ova 1012–3246; GSI 0.286–0.466; egg diameter 0.68–0.70 mm.
- **Key conclusion:** Reduced summer flows in the Arkansas River, driven by irrigation and groundwater decline, most likely caused the depletion of *N. girardi*. *N. bairdi*, which may tolerate the altered Cimarron flow regime, has taken its place there.
