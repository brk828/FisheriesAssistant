# Scale Reduction and Sulphate Tolerance of Anatolian Cyprinodonts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 study by [[Heinz Grimm]] tests whether scale reduction in Anatolian killifish of the genus [[Aphanius]] is linked to physiological tolerance of sulphate-rich water. The author applies a hypothesis from [[Axelray and Villwock]] (1962), which proposed that scale reduction results from pleiotropic gene action tied to salt tolerance. Laboratory experiments tested egg and larval development across sulphate concentrations in four species. The data did not support the pleiotropy hypothesis. Scale reduction is instead interpreted as [[Regressive Evolution]] under extreme, low-selection brackish habitats.

## Background & Description

- **Focal taxon:** [[Aphanius Anatoliae]] shows a polymorphism in scale covering, from fully scaled populations to almost naked forms. Scale reduction mainly affects the ventral region, back and tail stem. In the most extreme case, only the [[Central Lateral Row]] (CLR) scales and a few minute transverse rows remain.
- **Comparative species:** [[Aphanius Dispar]] (Siwa Oasis, Egypt), [[Aphanius Iberus]] (salt-works ponds, southern Spain) and [[Aphanius Mento]] (Ain Fashkha, Israel), all with good scale covering.
- **Naked relative:** *Aphanius (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus* lives in the Hazar Gölü near Elazığ, East Anatolia, and is completely scaleless.
- **Prior work:** Scale reduction was studied morphologically by [[Ermin]] (1945, 1946), who linked it to an increase in CLR scale count through division of the early scale anlage, and genetically by [[Kosswig]], [[Axelray and Villwock]] and [[Villwock]].
- **Methods:** Embryos were reared at 26.5 ± 0.5 °C in MgSO₄ and Na₂SO₄ solutions. Scale reduction was assessed with alizarin staining and CLR counts. Statistics included means, standard deviations, confidence limits (5% significance) and the [[Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney Test]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lakes of Western Anatolia fall into two hydrographic groups (after [[Irton]], 1973):

- **Group A, shallow playa lakes:** These nearly dry out from July to October. The [[Acigöl]] (about 2.5 m deep) is the main example, with very high and fluctuating salinity. Its central region is apparently azoic in summer.
- **Group B, deeper lakes:** Examples are [[Burdur Gölü]] (80 m, 19 g/l salt), [[Salda Gölü]] (100–184 m, 2.1 g/l) and Yarışlı Gölü (5.3 g/l). The brackish Acıgöl inlets also belong here.
- **Group C, freshwater influents:** Springs, brooks and ponds, such as the Acıgöl springs and the Düğer Brook, hold typical limnic fauna and fully scaled *A. anatoliae*.

Across the waters, the flora and fauna are sparse and similar. *A. anatoliae* is usually the only vertebrate present. The exception is Lake Salda, which also holds *Phoxinellus zeregi maeandri*. Water chemistry was measured in Table 1 for 14 water samples. Salinity ranged from about 0.4 g/l (Düğer) to 47 g/l (summer Acıgöl). Sulphate was the dominant anion in most saline waters.

## Key Findings

1. **Hypothesis not supported (egg and larval stages):** Hatching and larval survival showed no difference between scale-reduced and normally scaled *A. anatoliae*. Mortality during gastrulation was also uncorrelated with scale covering.
2. **Species-specific tolerance:** Hatching in control sea salt ranged from 83.3% to 91.2%, with no significant interspecific difference. Egg development stopped above 15 g/l SO₄²⁻ in *A. dispar* and above 20 g/l in *A. iberus* and *A. mento*. *A. anatoliae* embryos still developed above 30 g/l SO₄²⁻.
3. **Unfertilized eggs:** In *A. dispar* and *A. iberus*, unfertilized eggs exceeded 40% in high MgSO₄ (8 g/l SO₄²⁻). *A. iberus* did not spawn at high Na₂SO₄. In *A. anatoliae* (Düğer), unfertilized eggs never exceeded 20%.
4. **Gastrulation mortality:** Controls lost roughly 4–11% of fertilized eggs. *A. iberus* and *A. dispar* exceeded 40% mortality at high sulphate. *A. anatoliae* populations stayed very low even at high concentrations.
5. **Larval survival:** Control survival ranged from 90.3% to 96.0%. Larvae tolerated less sulphate than eggs did. *A. iberus* larvae tolerated more than *A. dispar* and *A. mento*. Acıgöl-rim larvae (Gemiç, Acıpınar, Gelif) were sensitive to Na₂SO₄, unlike Burdur and Düğer larvae. All populations tolerated high MgSO₄.
6. **CLR scale counts:** Scale number rose with greater reduction, as Ermin (1946) found. Sulphate rearing caused only sporadic significant changes: decreases in *A. dispar* at 10 g/l Na₂SO₄, and in the Gemiç population at 5 and 10 g/l MgSO₄. Over three generations, no sulphate-dependent scale covering change was seen. An increase in CLR count was predicted but did not occur.
7. **Regressive evolution:** The author concludes that under extreme brackish conditions, stabilizing selection for scales lapsed, so scales became superfluous and were reduced. Highly variable populations (Burdur, Acıpınar) are interpreted as early stages, Salda as advanced, and the naked *K. asquamatus* as the end point. Comparison is drawn with eye and pigment loss in cave fishes.
8. **Population dynamics:** Secondary gene exchange during high lake levels and isolation during low levels may explain the variability of Burdur and Acıgöl micropopulations.
9. **Timescale:** Geological evidence suggests the lakes became brackish after the Pleistocene. An interval of about 20,000 to 100,000 years is considered enough to produce the observed reduction.
10. **Summary conclusion:** Salt tolerance and scale reduction have no common genetic basis through pleiotropy. The biological function of scales under normal habitat conditions remains unknown and requires further study.
