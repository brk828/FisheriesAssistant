# Fort River Ecosystem: Productivity of the Periphyton Component

#phycology #freshwater-ecology #periphyton

## Executive Summary

This report by [[Stuart G. Fisher]] and [[William T. Sumner]] quantifies the primary production, community respiration, and standing crop of the attached algal community (periphyton) in a 1.6 km reach of the [[Fort River]], a lowland, unpolluted stream in central Massachusetts. The 17-month study combined in situ [[Productivity Chambers]] at multiple sites and temperatures with a field survey of light, temperature, and chlorophyll. The authors built empirical models relating gross production (Pg) and respiration (R) to [[Chlorophyll A]], temperature, and light, then applied them to weekly field data. Periphyton are estimated to produce about 90% of the reach's ecosystem gross production.

## Background & Description

The study site and methods are presented as follows:

- **Setting:** The Fort River rises in the Pelham hills and flows west to the [[Connecticut River]]. The watershed is 155 km², mostly agricultural, residential, and woodland. The study reach lies about 16 km upstream from the Connecticut confluence.
- **Hydrology:** Mean discharge is 1.4 m³/s (50 cfs), ranging from about 0.28 m³/s (summer base flow) to about 28 m³/s (annual maxima). Stream width averages 14 m, summer mean depth is 0.3 m, and mean current velocity is 0.12–0.18 m/s. There is essentially no floodplain.
- **Water quality:** Oxygen stays above 5 ppm, pH ranges 6.9–8.1, and conductivity is 60–120 µmhos/cm. Turbidity is usually below 15 JTU but reaches about 100 JTU during runoff. Water temperature ranges from 0 to 27.5 °C, with summer means near 20 °C.
- **Riparian zone:** Mature alder, oak, elm, and maple trees form a nearly continuous canopy, which strongly shapes stream light.
- **Substrate:** The reach was divided into 50 m segments and classified as cobble (40%), mixed cobble on sand (38%), or sand-silt (22%).
- **Macrophytes:** Up to 20% of the streambed is colonized in summer by macrophytes, including *Potamogeton crispus*, *P. epihydrus*, *Callitriche heterophylla*, *Ranunculus trichophyllus*, *Vallisneria americana*, and *Eleocharis acicularis*.
- **Algal succession:** Filamentous Chlorophyceae were replaced in sequence over the year. *Hydrurus foetidus* dominated in winter, then *Cladophora glomerata* in spring, *Microspora stagnorum* by mid-July, *Spirogyra fluvialatus* in late August, and *Stigeoclonium tenue* in September. Filamentous forms made up at most 20% of periphyton biomass. Microalgae (mainly diatoms) formed the bulk of the community, with *Melosira varians* dominant from mid-summer to early fall.

Methods included six aluminum trays per station filled with natural substrate, chlorophyll extraction in 95% basic acetone with spectrophotometry (after Lorenzen, 1967), anthracene-based light integration (after Dore, 1959), and paired light-dark chambers measuring dissolved oxygen change by Winkler titration over 1–3 hour incubations. Chambers were run at temperatures of 1–23 °C and light intensities below 1 to 60 ly/hr, yielding 59 light-dark pairs in six seasonal test periods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame periphyton as a major autotrophic component of headwater streams that is often overshadowed by allochthonous inputs and, further downstream, by macrophytes and phytoplankton. They note that earlier chamber and tray-based methods had limitations (tray colonization rates, bell jars, and light-dark bottles all distort flow and nutrient conditions), and that no earlier study had combined chamber measurements through a full year at several sites.

Key environmental controls identified:

- **Light:** Light reaching the stream surface was about 35% of open sunlight during leaf-out (May 10 to October 20) and 63% otherwise. About 55% of surface light reached the bed. Shaded sites received as little as 12% of available light in midsummer. Streambed light peaked in early May (weekly means near 175 ly/day) and again after leaf fall.
- **Temperature and photoperiod:** Photoperiods ranged from 16 hours (June) to 9 hours (late December). Stream temperatures ranged from 0 °C in midwinter to 24 °C in midsummer.
- **Discharge and scouring:** Discharge was lowest in August and highest in March. Storm scouring reduced standing crop (for example, from 32 to 10.1 mg/m² after a July 19 storm), but recovery was rapid.
- **Nutrients:** Dissolved PO₄-P averaged 25 µg/L and NO₃-N was usually below 0.2 mg/L. Nutrient levels showed no clear seasonal pattern, but the authors argue that biological demand rises with temperature and that high-density, current-sheltered mats may become nutrient-limited.
- **Grazing:** Macroinvertebrate secondary production was about 3.3 g/m² per year, so grazing was judged to remove less than 10% of net periphyton production.

Comparisons with other systems: Fort River chlorophyll levels were similar to eutrophic lakes (30–120 mg/m²) and lower than in larger, less shaded rivers. Annual ecosystem production was comparable to mesotrophic lakes and below temperate forests.

## Key Findings

**Standing crop**
- Benthic chlorophyll a ranged from 10 to 136 mg/m², with an annual mean of 44 mg/m².
- Chlorophyll peaked in early May (before leaf-out) and in autumn after leaf fall. May and autumn peaks were 3.9 and 1.7 times the mean summer value.
- Midwinter chlorophyll (36.0 mg/m²) exceeded the June–August mean (33.2 mg/m²), despite low light and high discharge.
- Stream bed rock size explained between-site chlorophyll variation better than light or current velocity.
- Light correlated with chlorophyll (r = .41, P < 0.01), but the relationship was significant only at heavily shaded stations (B, C, E).

**Production model**
- The light-saturated photosynthetic rate (PMAX) was fitted with the Smith equation, combined with a chlorophyll-temperature relationship. Predicted and measured PMAX agreed significantly (P < .001).
- Assimilation numbers declined with increasing chlorophyll. Assimilation numbers fell from 17.75 to 4.64 g O₂/g chl·hr at 20 °C as chlorophyll rose from 20 to 100 mg/m².
- The density effect was strongest at high temperature, so dense stands produced relatively less at 20–25 °C than at 10–15 °C.
- Mean photosynthetic Ik was 6.25 ly/hr, and photoinhibition was not significant for stream bed periphyton.

**Respiration**
- Community respiration was modeled as a function of temperature and chlorophyll (R = 0.00124 × T × Chl + 0.00769T + 0.01031 for the simplified form reported), with a highly significant fit (P < .001).
- Respiration reflects bacteria, fungi, and decomposing material as well as algae. Macroinvertebrates contributed only 1.1% of benthic respiration.

**Annual budget (model output, weekly simulation July 1974–December 1975)**

| Metric | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Annual periphyton gross production | 0.58–0.584 kg O₂/m² |
| Annual community respiration | 1.27–1.274 kg O₂/m² |
| Annual P/R | 0.49 (abstract) / 0.46 (model output) |
| Annual photosynthetic efficiency | 1.47% |
| Maximum weekly daily Pg | 6.51 g O₂/m²·day (early May) |
| Winter Pg | < 0.1 g O₂/m²·day |
| Mean daily Pg / R | 1.6 / 3.49 g O₂/m²·day |

- Periphyton production is estimated to be about 90% of ecosystem gross production (0.650 kg O₂/m² by [[Fisher And Carpenter]]).
- Macrophytes had about 9.2% of community primary production. Their summer biomass was about 6.5 times periphyton biomass, but their gross production was only about 15% of periphyton production.
- Summer Pg averaged 3.24 g O₂/m²·day, while summer respiration averaged 5.58 g O₂/m²·day.
- Winter respiration stayed near 1.5 g O₂/m²·day, which drove P/R to an annual low (< 0.06).

**Summer decline and temperature-density hypothesis**
- Light, discharge, and grazing alone did not explain the summer decline in standing crop.
- The authors propose that at high temperatures and high periphyton density, nutrient supply to current-sheltered algae becomes limiting, reducing production.
- Theoretical maximum standing crops (where Pg equals R, assuming R = 30% of Pg) were 0.29, 0.47, 0.99, and 3.74 g chl/m² at 25, 20, 15, and 10 °C, respectively.
- The highest measured chlorophyll (0.492 g/m², early May at 15 °C) was below the 15 °C limit but above the 20 and 25 °C limits.

**Conclusions**
- Fort River periphyton are a fast-growing but vulnerable component of the ecosystem, and their standing crop stays well below their annual production.
- The model is a first step toward predictive stream production models. The authors identify nutrient effects and the replacement of measured chlorophyll with an environmental predictor as needed improvements.
