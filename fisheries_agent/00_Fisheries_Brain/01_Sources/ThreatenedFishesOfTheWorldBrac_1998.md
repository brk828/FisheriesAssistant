# Brachionichthys hirsutus (Lacépède, 1804)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #brachionichthyidae

## Executive Summary

[[Brachionichthys hirsutus]], commonly known as the [[Spotted Handfish]], is a small, critically endangered benthic fish endemic to south eastern Tasmania, Australia. Once considered the most abundant of the inshore [[Handfish]] species, it has undergone a substantial population decline over the past decade (as of publication in 1998). The species is notable for its unique reproductive strategy involving guarded egg masses attached to substrate structures, and its lack of a pelagic larval stage. The cause of decline remains undetermined, though the introduced [[Northern Pacific Seastar]] (*Asterias amurensis*), habitat degradation, and pollution are implicated as likely contributing threats.

## Background & Description

- **Maximum size:** 120 mm total length (TL)
- **Body covering:** Small spinules covering the body; dermal tentacles usually absent
- **Snout-to-gill pore distance:** Approximately 3.1 in total length
- **Dorsal fin:** Longest dorsal fin spine slightly shorter than longest dorsal fin ray
- **Illicium (modified first dorsal spine):** Functions as a lure and probing structure; length of lure is more than 5 in length of illicial stalk
- **Coloration:** Body white or cream, covered in numerous small, closely set orange, brown, or blackish spots (or, less commonly, stripes)
- **Fins:** Modified pectoral and pelvic fins adapted for resting on and moving slowly across the substrate, characteristic of the [[Brachionichthyidae]] family
- **Juvenile size at hatching:** 6–7 mm standard length (SL), emerging as fully formed juveniles

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Benthic species found in coarse to fine sand habitats
- **Depth range:** 5–40 m, most commonly 5–10 m
- **Microhabitat preference:** Shallow, shell-filled depressions or areas near low-relief rocky outcrops
- **Distribution:** Endemic to south eastern Tasmania, specifically the lower [[Derwent River]] estuary, [[Frederick Henry Bay]], [[D'Entrecasteaux Channel]], and northern regions of [[Storm Bay]]
- **Diet:** Small crustaceans and polychaete worms
- **Locomotion:** Uses modified pectoral and pelvic fins to rest on or move slowly across the bottom, rather than typical swimming
- **Reproductive behavior:** Female guards and tends egg masses; no pelagic larval period, a distinguishing ecological trait among fishes
- **Spawning substrate:** Primarily stalked ascidians, but also sponges or seagrasses

## Key Findings

- **Population trend:** Substantial decline in abundance documented over the ten years preceding the 1998 report (Bruce et al. 1997; Barrett et al. 1996), despite historically being the most common inshore handfish species (Last et al. 1983)
- **Reproduction:**
  - Spawning occurs September–October
  - Egg masses contain 80–250 eggs, each approximately 3–4 mm in diameter
  - Eggs are housed in individual flask-shaped envelopes, interconnected by fine tubules of unknown function
  - Eggs hatch after 7–8 weeks into fully formed juveniles (no pelagic larval stage)
  - Illicium is used to probe egg masses, sometimes extended to rest on the substrate
- **Threats (unconfirmed causal links):**
  - Predation by the introduced [[Northern Pacific Seastar]] (*Asterias amurensis*), potentially via direct predation on egg masses or indirect predation on ascidian spawning substrates
  - Habitat modification through increased estuarine siltation, reducing preferred sandy habitat
  - Heavy metal contamination of sediments
  - Urban effluent input into the [[Derwent River]] estuary system
- **Conservation measures:**
  - Protected under State Fisheries legislation (1994) and Australian Federal legislation (1996)
  - Range-wide survey confirmed endangered status (Barrett et al. 1996)
  - Ongoing research into reproductive biology, habitat requirements, and captive husbandry techniques (Bruce et al. 1997)
- **Conservation recommendations:**
  - Urgent need for further studies on basic biology and ecology
  - High priority on identifying specific threatening processes
  - Conservation is linked to broader management of the [[Derwent River]] estuary system
  - Potential need to control introduced marine species and ameliorate habitat changes from rural, urban, and industrial practices
  - Development of captive breeding programs to support potential reintroduction efforts
- **Taxonomic significance:** [[Brachionichthyidae]] is the most speciose marine fish family endemic to Australia; five of the eight recognized species have among the narrowest geographic ranges of any of the 4000+ marine fishes in the region, placing them at elevated risk due to small population sizes
