## Executive Summary
This 1958 field report from *California Fish and Game* documents two suspected cases of mass mortality in wild and hatchery-derived trout populations in Siskiyou County, California, attributed to heavy infestations of an intestinal trematode (fluke) of the genus [[Crepidostomum]], likely *Crepidostomum farionis*. Mass die-offs of [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) and [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) were observed at Lower Gumboot Lake (1956, 1957) and Castle Lake (1957). Affected fish showed severe intestinal inflammation associated with large fluke burdens, suggesting parasitic infection—rather than the commonly assumed causes of poisoning, winterkill, or dynamiting—may be an underappreciated driver of unexplained trout mortality events in lakes.

## Background & Description
**Host fish:**
- Affected trout ranged from **6 to 9 inches** in length, consistent with "catchable" hatchery-planted stock.
- The rainbow trout involved were of the **domesticated, fall-spawning strain**.
- Intestinal walls of heavily parasitized fish were **highly inflamed**.

**Parasite ([[Crepidostomum]] sp., probably *C. farionis*):**
- Preserved (formalin-fixed, contracted) specimens measured:
  - Length: **0.87–1.19 mm**
  - Breadth: **0.42–0.70 mm**
- Living specimens may reach **up to three times** the preserved contracted length.
- Adult worms are **large enough to be visible without magnification**; when placed in water, they exhibit slow movement, aiding field diagnosis.
- A single freshly dead rainbow trout from Castle Lake harbored **446 flukes** in its intestine.

**Co-occurring parasite:**
- Large numbers of the protozoan **[[Hexamita]]** were also found in the intestine of the heavily fluke-infested Castle Lake rainbow trout; its pathogenic role is unclear, though hatchery experience suggests it was likely not the primary cause of death.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** High-elevation freshwater lakes in Siskiyou County, California — specifically **Lower Gumboot Lake** and **[[Castle Lake]]**.
- **Life cycle of Crepidostomum (per Crawford, 1943/1948):**
  1. Eggs are expelled from the trout host via feces.
  2. **Miracidia** hatch and infect the **fingernail clam**, *[[Pisidium]]* (first intermediate host).
  3. **Cercariae** emerge from the clam and infect **mayfly nymphs**, genus *[[Ephemera]]* (second intermediate host).
  4. Trout become infected by **predating on parasitized mayfly nymphs**, completing the cycle.
- The specific mayfly genera present in Castle Lake were not identified by the author, representing a gap in the local life-cycle confirmation.
- Gill-net sampling at Castle Lake (17 eastern brook trout, 4 rainbow trout) showed **10/17 brook trout** and **4/4 rainbow trout** were parasitized, though most carried non-lethal burdens — suggesting a baseline endemic infection level with occasional epizootic spikes causing mortality.

## Key Findings
- Mass trout mortalities in two separate lakes (1956–1957) were linked to heavy intestinal fluke ([[Crepidostomum]]) infestations rather than typical suspected causes like poisoning, winterkill, or dynamiting.
- Fish with **very large fluke burdens** exhibited severe intestinal inflammation, supporting flukes as a **direct or indirect cause of death**.
- Fish with light-to-moderate parasite loads (most of the sampled population) did not appear to suffer serious harm, indicating a **threshold effect** where mortality risk scales with parasite intensity.
- The true scale of population-level losses from these epizootics could not be quantified but was suspected to have an **appreciable negative effect on sport fishing**.
- The paper recommends that **intestinal examination of freshly dead trout** become standard practice during unexplained mass mortality events in lakes, given the ease of detecting adult flukes macroscopically.
- This case implicates a complex **three-host parasitic life cycle** (trout ↔ mayfly nymph ↔ fingernail clam) as ecologically significant in triggering wild trout die-offs, highlighting the interconnectedness of fish health with invertebrate community dynamics in lake ecosystems.
