# Spawning Movements of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 study in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* examines the spawning-season movements of adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), an endangered, large piscivorous cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Using [[Radiotelemetry]] on adults and [[Larval Fish Sampling]] over 1982–1985, the authors found that spawning occurred across widely separated reaches of a 350-km section of the upper Colorado River upstream of [[Lake Powell]]. Adult movements were shorter than those reported in the [[Green River]] basin, and most fish returned close to their capture sites after spawning. The authors conclude that spawning habitat appears widely distributed in the upper Colorado River and recommend against new barriers to fish movement.

## Background & Description

*Ptychocheilus lucius* is a large piscivorous cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River basin. It was presumed to undertake seasonal, often long-distance migrations to discrete, widely separated reaches to spawn, with larvae drifting to distant nursery areas (Tyus 1986). Because migratory fishes are vulnerable to barriers, as shown for Columbia River dams and Pacific salmon (Netboy 1974), the authors sought to test whether the life history proposed for the Green River basin applied to the upper Colorado River population.

The study area was a 350-km reach between Lake Powell, Utah, and Palisade, Colorado, measured in river kilometers from the Green River confluence (RK 0). It was divided into eight reaches defined by tributaries, movement barriers, and changes in river morphometry. Reach 8 is a 4-km segment of the Gunnison River below the [[Redlands Diversion]], a barrier to upstream fish movement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning timing:** Spawning occurred roughly July through early September, beginning when seasonal water temperature at the state-line gauge reached 18–22 °C, water levels were falling, and streamflows were 15–30% of the year's maximum discharge.
- **Habitat:** Each study reach contained cobble substrate similar to spawning habitat used in the [[Yampa River]].
- **Flow alteration:** Upstream dams reduced average peak instantaneous discharge at the Utah–Colorado border by 48% (comparing 1908–1923 with 1966–1989 records). Low-head diversion dams block upstream adult passage.
- **Contrast with Green River:** The [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] cooled Green River summer temperatures, and the authors attribute part of the difference in squawfish movement to this habitat modification.
- **Historical context:** The authors suggest that diversion dams may have blocked upstream populations that formerly migrated downstream to spawn.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Larval sampling used 0.5-mm mesh seines (0.9 × 0.9 m) at about 8-km intervals in reaches 2–7, with sampling roughly every two weeks in July–September 1982–1985.
- Hatching dates were back-calculated from total length using an equation from [[Haynes et al. 1985]]; spawning dates were estimated by subtracting 4 days, based on a median embryo incubation of about 102 h at 20 °C (Marsh 1985).
- Adults were captured by electrofishing, gill nets, and trammel nets. Fish longer than 440 mm received radio transmitters (9–20 g), and were tracked by boat about every two weeks (April–October), weekly during the presumed spawning season, with no tracking November–March.

**Larval Fish**
- 131 larvae were collected in 1982, 37 in 1983, 46 in 1984, and 108 in 1985, representing 0.9, 0.5, 0.4, and 0.9% of all fish larvae collected in those years.
- Larvae occurred in 18%, 6%, 8%, and 14% of collections in those years, respectively.
- Larvae of two size classes (6–10 mm and 11–21 mm TL) occurred in every reach except 7 and 8. No larvae were collected from reach 8.
- Spawning in reaches 1–3 began in late June or early July (1982, 1984, 1985) or late July (1983), and continued into August or September.

**Radio-Tagged Adults**
- 74 adults were tagged (22 in 1982, 21 in 1983, 13 in 1984, 18 in 1985); 50 met the criteria for analysis.
- Contacts per fish ranged from 2 to 28 (mean 10.2).
- Maximum displacement during the monitoring period ranged from 1 to 313 km (mean 38.1 km). The two longest movers (313.0 and 193.4 km) were tagged in reaches 1 and 3.
- Spawning displacement ranged from 0 to 92.7 km (mean 23.2 km); 21 fish moved 0–10 km.
- Of the 50 fish, 30 remained in their release reach during spawning and 20 moved to adjacent reaches. Of those 20, 10 later returned to their original reach.
- At last contact, 72% were in their release reach, and 52% were within 8 km of the release point.
- No significant differences (P > 0.05) were found in displacement among reaches or among 100-mm length classes.
- Seven fish tagged in reaches 6–7 aggregated in a 5-km segment of reach 7 (RK 281–286) in July 1982, including five ripe males in a small pool; this was the only aggregation observed.
- Nine fish tagged in upper reach 6 and reach 7 moved downstream to a 16-km stretch of lower reach 6 during or shortly after spawning, with some apparently following a seasonal pattern.
- No radio-tagged fish were found in the lower Dolores or Green rivers during aerial searches.

**Interpretation and Recommendations**
- Spawning appears widely distributed across the upper Colorado River, with relatively short seasonal adult movements compared with the Green River basin, where 23 of 68 tagged adults (34%) moved 65–160 km to two major spawning sites.
- The authors caution that larval capture does not prove long-distance larval drift, and that additional undiscovered spawning sites in the Green basin could confound previous interpretations.
- Recommendations: (1) no additional barriers to free movement of Colorado squawfish should be permitted within the current range; (2) research should determine the benefits to recovery of providing passage around existing barriers within the historical range.
- Whether adults show fidelity to particular spawning areas is unknown, as is the consequence of blocking access to them.
