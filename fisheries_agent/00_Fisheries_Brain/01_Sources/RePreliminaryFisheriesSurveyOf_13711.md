# Preliminary Fisheries Survey of Aquatic Habitats - Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae #poeciliidae #endangered-species #refuge-management

## Executive Summary

This memorandum documents a preliminary fisheries survey of stock-watering tanks at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] in southern Arizona, conducted in December 1985 and April 1986. The refuge comprises headwaters of the north-flowing [[Altar Valley]], part of the [[Santa Cruz River]] system ([[Gila River]] basin). Native fishes were historically absent from the refuge, including the perennial [[Arivaca Creek]]. The survey identified five nonnative fish species across 45 tanks and evaluated potential use of existing tanks as supplemental growout habitats for endangered fish recovery efforts. The survey also documented amphibian populations including [[Tiger Salamander]] (Ambystoma tigrinum) and various ranid frogs.

## Background & Description

[[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] is located just east of the [[Baboquivari Mountains]] in southern Arizona. The refuge's southern extremes drain southward into Mexico, presumably into the [[Rio Concepcion]] watershed. While native fishes were historically absent from the area, nonnative fishes have been widely introduced in stock-watering tanks throughout Arizona.

Two survey efforts were conducted:
- December 1985: Lief Ahim and Ben Robertson ([[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]) surveyed eight tanks using seine and gillnet
- April 19-20, 1986: W.L. Minckley, J.E. Johnson, Paul C. Marsh, Bruce D. DeMarias, and Arizona State University ichthyology students visited 45 tanks, with only three overlapping with the earlier survey

Sampling methodology employed 6.4-mm-mesh seines of various lengths. The survey did not record detailed habitat conditions; additional work would be required to estimate tank sizes, permanency, and overall desirability for endangered species management.

**Fish Species Documented** (all nonnative):
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
- [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*)
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*)

All specimens were housed in the Collection of Fishes, [[Arizona State University]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The refuge's aquatic habitats consist primarily of artificial stock-watering tanks of varying permanency. Most ponds were experiencing phytoplankton blooms and were evidently rich in nutrients and highly productive. Aquatic invertebrates were abundant and diversified, including principally insects and molluscs.

**Amphibian Fauna:**
[[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*, cf. mavortium) populations were documented, almost certainly representing introduced populations. Specimens included metamorphosed adults, branchiate (aquatic) adults, and juveniles. The presence of branchiate adults indicates sufficient tank permanency for animals to overwinter for at least a year. Immature larvae and ova with developing embryos were noted, indicating reproduction occurred in January or February based on larval size in April.

Ranid frog tadpoles were observed, mostly appearing to be introduced [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*), though native spotted frogs of the *R. pipiens* group were also observed.

**Potential Native Amphibian Species:**
The memorandum lists numerous arid-land amphibians that should be present, some occurring in the United States or Arizona only in the vicinity of Buenos Aires NWR:
- [[Narrow-mouthed Toad]]* (*Gastrophryne olivacea*)
- [[Sonoran Green Toad]]* (*Bufo retiformis*)
- [[Colorado River Toad]] (*B. alvarius*)
- [[Red-spotted Toad]] (*B. punctatus*)
- [[Plains Toad]] (*Bufo cognatus*)
- [[Woodhouse's Toad]] (*B. woodhousei*)
- [[Western Spadefoot Toad]] (*Scaphiophus hammondi*)
- [[Couch's Spadefoot Toad]] (*S. couchi*)
- [[Canyon Tree Frog]] (*Hyla arenicolor*)
- [[Burrowing Tree Frog]]* (*Pternohyla fodiens*)
- [[Western Barking Frog]]* (*Eleutherodactylus augusti*)
- [[Tarahumara Frog]]* (*Rana tarahumara*)
- [[Yellow Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon flavescens*)

*Species marked with asterisk occur in U.S. or Arizona only near Buenos Aires NWR

**Health Concern:**
"Swimmer's itch" was contracted by some personnel, produced by penetration of skin by metacercaria of a bird-specific schistosome. The fluke is not a parasite of humans but can remain viable under skin for a few days, causing substantial discomfort.

## Key Findings

**Management Recommendations for Endangered Species:**

1. **Nonnative Fish Management:** None of the documented nonnative fishes is desirable on the refuge except [[Mosquitofish]], which serves a local mosquito control function. This role can be duplicated by native and endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in smaller tanks.

2. **Endangered Species Stocking Potential:** Larger habitats appear suitable for rearing [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and/or [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). Stocking these species in the upper [[Santa Cruz]] drainage is justified despite being technically outside their native ranges because:
   - They are within the [[Gila River]] drainage from which they were widely taken historically
   - There is need for additional refugium space and research animals
   - Drainage relations preclude escape except into long reaches of dry stream bed

3. **Drainage Considerations:** No tank in the south-flowing drainage should be stocked with fishes indigenous to the [[Colorado River]] basin. However, depending on yet-to-be-confirmed drainage relations, southern habitats might be used for production and growout of other species (e.g., [[Sonoran Chub]], *Gila ditaenia*). The precise divide between north- and south-flowing terrain has not been accurately determined and should be established prior to fish introductions.

4. **Amphibian Management:** A viable management plan for enhancement and maintenance of semiaquatic amphibian species may be to maintain a percentage of smaller tanks free of potential competitors and predators such as fishes. Most arid-land amphibians breed in semipermanent pools filled by infrequent rains (exception: [[Tarahumara Frog]] requires permanent water as in Fresnal Canyon).

**Survey Results Summary:**
Of 45 tanks surveyed in April 1986, many were dry or drying. Tanks with water and notable characteristics were marked as "good tank" in field notes.
