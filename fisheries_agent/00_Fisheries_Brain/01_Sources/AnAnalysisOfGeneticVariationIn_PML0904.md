# AFLP Analysis of Genetic Variation in Little Colorado Spinedace (Lepidomeda vittata)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report uses [[Amplified Fragment Length Polymorphism]] (AFLP) markers to assess genetic diversity and population structure in the federally threatened [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]. Samples came from three natural populations in the [[Little Colorado River]] watershed ([[Rudd Creek]], [[Chevelon Creek]], [[East Clear Creek]]) and from a captive population held at the [[Flagstaff Arboretum]] pond. The study was prepared for the [[Coconino National Forest]] to inform management and captive-breeding decisions.

The authors found substantial population structure among drainages but negligible divergence between the captive population and its source. They recommend keeping stocks from different regions separate and identify Rudd Creek as the most diverse source for captive breeding.

## Background & Description

*Lepidomeda vittata* is a native cyprinid minnow restricted to disjunct populations in north-flowing tributaries of the Little Colorado River in Arizona. It was probably once widespread across the Little Colorado drainage, possibly including the Zuni River. Historical surveys record a decline after 1966, which the authors attribute to human impacts on southwestern ecosystems. The species was listed as threatened in 1987 (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service).

Key threats identified in the source:
- Agricultural and civil water use, which alters flow and degrades habitat.
- Introduction of non-native species that compete with and prey on natives. [[Rainbow Trout]] predation is considered a major factor in spinedace displacement.
- Toxins used to remove non-native fish, and pollutants from agriculture and industry.

A captive population established from Rudd Creek fish at the Flagstaff Arboretum pond failed after three years, possibly because of disease. A new captive population was planned for the same system.

The genetic markers used were dominant AFLP markers, which the authors describe as more sensitive than [[Allozyme Analysis]] for detecting small differences among populations. Earlier work used [[Mitochondrial DNA]] RFLP analysis, which showed divergence among populations (Tibbets et al., 1993).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors link spinedace population structure to the variable hydrology of the arid Southwest. Populations may persist through dry years in isolated pools and rebound when flows return. Repeated bottleneck events followed by expansion could raise overall variation, but small isolated populations face greater risk from [[Genetic Drift]] and reduced [[Gene Flow]].

Trout displace spinedace from optimal mid-water habitat into marginal, more turbid areas. The authors describe spinedace in Nutrioso Creek as inversely related to trout numbers. Competition between trout and spinedace was not tested, but their diets and habitat overlap.

Sampling design also affected results. Rudd Creek samples came from several small pools separated by flowing reaches, whereas Chevelon and East Clear Creek samples came essentially from single pools. This may account for some of the diversity difference among sites.

## Key Findings

### Sampling and Methods

- Sample sizes: Rudd Creek (n = 29), Chevelon Creek (n = 26), East Clear Creek (n = 24), Flagstaff Arboretum captive stock (n = 30). Collections were made between 1995 and 1998.
- Fish were captured by shock or seine, and a small caudal fin clip was preserved in 95% ethanol.
- DNA was extracted by phenol/chloroform, digested with EcoRI and MseI, and amplified in two selective PCR rounds using 15 primer combinations.
- Analyses used [[UPGMA Cluster Analysis]] with Jaccard and Reynolds coefficients, [[Analysis of Molecular Variance]] (AMOVA), [[Weir and Cockerham Theta]] (θ), and [[Fishers Exact Test]], with bootstrap support.

### Marker and Diversity Results

- 70 scoreable polymorphic markers were generated from 15 primer combinations, averaging 5 polymorphic bands per gel (range 2–8).
- Replicate scoring showed a mean difference of 1% between replicates.
- Diversity by population (percent polymorphic loci, P; average heterozygosity, H):

| Population | P (%) | H |
|---|---|---|
| Rudd Creek | 65.71 | 0.2410 |
| Arboretum (captive) | 61.43 | 0.2186 |
| Chevelon Creek | 42.86 | 0.1604 |
| East Clear Creek | 37.14 | 0.1531 |

- The Arboretum and Rudd Creek populations were similar, as were Chevelon and East Clear Creek.
- The authors describe overall diversity as fairly high, and state that all populations had heterozygosity values above those reported for many other fish species.

### Population Structure

- Overall θ for all four populations was 0.5162, and PHIst was 0.555. The authors describe this as substantial structure, comparable to the θ = 0.5542 reported from mtDNA haplotype data.
- AMOVA for all four populations partitioned variation as 44.49% within and 55.51% among populations.
- Captive versus source (Arboretum vs. Rudd Creek): θ = 0.0345, PHIst = 0.023, indicating negligible differentiation.
- AMOVA for Arboretum vs. Rudd Creek: 97.68% of variation within populations and 2.32% among them.
- UPGMA clustering grouped the Arboretum and Rudd Creek populations together, separate from Chevelon and East Clear Creek. Bootstrap support for the Chevelon–East Clear Creek grouping was about 99.2%.
- Fisher's exact tests: all 12 pairwise comparisons were significant (p < 0.0001) except Rudd Creek vs. Arboretum (non-significant, p = 0.9506). Combined test: χ² = 922.652, df = 140, p < 0.0001.

### Conservation Conclusions and Recommendations

- Stocks from different drainages should not be mixed. Each population appears genetically unique, possibly due to local adaptation, and mixing would likely reduce overall diversity and fitness.
- The captive program at the Arboretum was considered a success. Captive-bred fish showed little genetic change from the Rudd Creek source over three years. The authors attribute this partly to rapid growth in the pond, which reached about 9,000 individuals.
- The original collection of 44 fish was considered ideal to start a captive population, and collection should come from several pools within a stream to maximize diversity and limit relatedness.
- If only one captive population is possible, Rudd Creek or upper Little Colorado River samples are the best candidates, due to higher diversity and the greater number of areas to stock.
- The most recent plan, a captive population from East Clear Creek, was supported with the recommendation that fish be collected from several regions of the East Clear Creek system and used only to restock East Clear Creek.
- Management should protect individual populations and their habitats. Mixing populations should be a last resort, used only if a population faces imminent extinction.
- The authors note that [[Inbreeding Depression]] and outbreeding depression are the main genetic concerns. Current heterozygosity values suggest inbreeding depression has not yet occurred.
