# Declining Big-River Fishes of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

The document is a conservation review of native Arizona fishes, focusing on four [[Big-River Fishes]] of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Of the 32 native fish kinds recorded from the state, 26 (80%) are already listed as endangered, threatened, or candidate. All four big-river species are listed as endangered, and the squawfish has disappeared from Arizona. The authors attribute the declines mainly to physical habitat alteration by dams, levees, and water diversion, with [[Non-Native Fishes]] acting as a secondary, biological pressure. Chemical pollution is considered a lesser current threat, though it caused past losses. The document closes with research and management priorities, including removing non-native species and restoring more natural flow regimes.

## Background & Description

**Species and status**
- Native Arizona fishes are losing their competition with humans for water. Of 32 recorded native kinds, 26 are listed or candidate species (the scanned text on the listing of one pupfish is partly illegible).
- The four [[Big-River Fishes]] have suffered most. All are listed as endangered. The squawfish has been extirpated from Arizona. Bonytail persist in some mainstem reservoirs of the Colorado River. Humpback chub are recorded from the [[Gila River]] basin and persist in the [[Grand Canyon]] area.

**Shared adaptations**
These fishes share features that appear adapted to their pre-development habitats:
- **Hydrodynamic specializations** for swift, turbulent currents: streamlined bodies (carried to extremes in some species), small depressed heads, and elongated, slender caudal peduncles. Fins are expansive with strong leading edges.
- **Features that reduce friction and sediment abrasion:** small eyes, especially on the head and anterior body; tiny scales, deeply embedded or essentially absent.
- **Longevity:** Long life suits unpredictable river environments, allowing fish to reproduce over many years rather than a few seasons.
  - Squawfish: historical accounts describe individuals approaching six feet and weights near 100 pounds. Based on the ages of much smaller individuals, the largest were probably over 50 years old.
  - Razorback sucker: reaches about 3 feet and 12 pounds, with at least 45 years of age recorded.
  - Bonytail: largest individual about 25 inches; the oldest aged fish was estimated at roughly 40-plus years (OCR partly illegible).
  - Humpback chub: often reach 20 inches and over 20 years.
- The authors suggest large body size may reflect coevolution with the piscivorous squawfish, whose predation pressure favored larger body sizes in the other three species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical changes (the primary driver)**

- **[[Flood Control Dams]]:** Dams hold floods and release water slowly. This suppresses the flooding required to maintain channels and deposit sediment and nutrients downstream. Channel stabilization with non-erodable levees causes channel incision, isolating floodplain marshes, oxbow lakes, and backwaters. Reduced turbulence simplifies channel shape and reduces habitat diversity.
- **[[Irrigation Dams]]:** Water held longer loses more sediment and nutrients to reservoir sedimentation and biological uptake. Irrigation dams are often built on permanent streams, creating barriers to upstream and downstream fish movement. Demand-driven releases create unpredictable flow variation, and on the lower Colorado, operations produce hourly to seasonal "tidal cycles" that flood and dry shorelines, which native fishes do not tolerate.
- **[[Hydroelectric Dams]]:** These combine impoundment effects with release of cold hypolimnetic water. Temperate native fishes cannot reproduce in cold water. Depending on release volume, thermal effects can suppress reproduction for hundreds of miles downstream. Little or no native reproduction occurs in the [[Grand Canyon]], which is influenced by cold water from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Water-level fluctuations tied to power demand further degrade downstream habitat.

**Chemical changes (secondary, mostly historical)**
- Mine wastes from the Clifton and Morenci area killed big-river fishes.
- Spills near Cananea, Sonora, affected the [[San Pedro River]], but these occurred after native fishes had already disappeared from that stream.
- Pesticide accumulation on the [[Gila River]] below the Phoenix metropolitan area killed many non-native fishes; native fishes were largely absent, so they were not impacted.
- Current threat: native fish remnants are concentrated in single populations, such as humpback chub in the [[Little Colorado River]]. A single accidental spill could eliminate an entire population.

**Biological interactions**
- [[Non-Native Fishes]] are enhanced by disturbance of natural and artificial habitats. They are harmful to native species directly, and indirectly through predation and competition. Reservoirs and canals provide refuges and routes for their dispersal.
- Native fish populations are small relative to their former ranges, which limits their ability to recover.

## Key Findings

**Why these fishes disappear (Question 1)**
- Each species responds differently, so causes must be evaluated individually. The main factors are listed below.
- Physical change: habitat degradation through reduced sediment and nutrient inputs, reduced habitat heterogeneity, cold temperatures below dams, and reduced productivity. These are the dominant causes.
- Chemical change: currently less important than physical change, but historically significant and a persistent future risk given small remnant populations.
- Biological interactions: predation and competition from [[Non-Native Fishes]], with supporting evidence from experiments and field studies.

**Species-specific evidence**
- [[Razorback Sucker]], Lake Mohave: the population originated when the reservoir filled in 1954. No recruitment of young fish has been detected since then. Adults survive well and reproduce annually, but no larvae survive. Growth was initially rapid, but older fish grow very little. Artificial propagation has been perfected.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: population size is larger in areas with less habitat modification. Studies used catch-per-unit-effort, electrofishing, and [[Mark-Recapture Sampling]]. Fish travel great distances to spawn and then return, shown through [[Radiotelemetry]].
- Eggs and larvae of all four species are susceptible to cold water. Laboratory tests using temperature-controlled aquaria were confirmed by field observations of larvae from warm tributaries carried into cold hydroelectric discharge.
- [[Humpback Chub]], Grand Canyon: mark-recapture, population estimates, and movement studies compared fish in the unmodified Little Colorado River with those in the altered mainstem. The mainstem population appears maintained by reproduction in the Little Colorado River, which is the only place in the lower basin where reproducing humpback chub are known.

**Predation and competition experiments**
- Predation by non-native fishes on razorbacks was confirmed by exclusion experiments in artificial and semi-natural habitats.
- Experiments with [[Desert Pupfish]] produced a population that has persisted and reproduced successfully in the presence of non-native fishes.
- Statistical comparison of isolated and co-occurring populations of a native minnow showed habitat displacement by non-native species, probably due to competition.
- Comparisons before and after extreme flooding showed that native fishes resist floods better than non-native fishes, which are more vulnerable. Severe floods therefore selectively reduce non-native predators and competitors, allowing native fish to persist in canyon-bound reaches. In areas with suitable refuges, non-native fishes can recover, and native fishes disappear.
- Laboratory tests with newborn native and non-native fish showed that behavioral avoidance of unfamiliar predators is partly innate, not only learned.

**Research and management priorities**
- Museum collections are being examined to establish historical species composition, abundance, and distribution from the mid-1800s.
- Management goals include protecting biodiversity, determining conditions needed to recover native species, and restoring more natural flow conditions.
- Non-native species removal is a priority, but the authors note that eliminating a reproducing non-native population is often impossible once established.
- Pollutant management: chemical spills or physical barriers can protect native fish, but the authors emphasize that prevention is more practical than remediation for irreversible losses.
