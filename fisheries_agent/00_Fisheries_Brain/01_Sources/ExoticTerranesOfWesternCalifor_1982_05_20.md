## Executive Summary

This source, "Exotic terranes of western California" (McWilliams & Howell, *Nature*, 1982), is a **geological and geophysical study** concerning the tectonic history of the North American Cordillera, specifically the Salinian and Stanley Mountain terranes of western California. The paper uses paleomagnetic inclination data from Jurassic pillow basalts and Upper Cretaceous flysch strata to argue that these terranes were displaced thousands of kilometers northward before accreting to the North American craton. A second short article fragment on "Palaeozoic phosphorites in the northern Sierra Nevada range" (Varga) discusses marine phosphorite deposits as indicators of paleogeography and upwelling zones, but again from a purely geological/sedimentological perspective.

## Key Findings

Since this is not a fisheries/ichthyology source, the "key findings" below summarize the actual (geological) content for completeness and transparency:

- Paleomagnetic data from Stanley Mountain pillow basalts (Jurassic, ~160 Myr) indicate formation at a paleolatitude of **14° ± 7°** (N or S).
- Paleomagnetic data from Upper Cretaceous (Cenomanian–Santonian) flysch strata near Figueroa Mountain indicate a paleolatitude of **6° ± 5°** at time of deposition.
- The Salinian terrane occupied paleolatitudes of **21° ± 7°** (Campanian–Maastrichtian) and **25° ± 3°** (early Paleocene), based on prior studies.
- The Stanley Mountain and Salinian terranes likely collided/sutured in the **late Cretaceous** (~72 Myr BP) at low paleolatitudes far south of their present position, then migrated northward together (~2,500 km) before accreting to North America by the early Eocene.
- This supports a model of large-scale northward terrane translation along the western North American margin during the Cretaceous–early Tertiary, likely driven by relative motion of the Farallon and Kula plates.
- The companion Varga phosphorite note argues that Paleozoic marine phosphorites in the northern Sierra Nevada formed via upwelling along a continental margin, and that this is consistent with (rather than contradicting) an origin near the western margin of North America — i.e., these particular Sierran rocks are **not** interpreted as a far-traveled, trans-oceanic microplate, in contrast to the exotic Salinian/Stanley Mountain terranes discussed in the main article.
