# Comments on the Proposed Nonessential Experimental Population of Colorado Squawfish in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ColoradoRiverBasin #ConservationPolicy

## Executive Summary
This 1987 letter from Dr. Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service is a formal critique of a federal proposal to establish a "nonessential experimental population" of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the lower [[Colorado River]] (Arizona-California reach). While not a primary data-generating field study, the document synthesizes existing knowledge of big-river fish reintroduction ecology in the Colorado River Basin, drawing on unpublished and published monitoring data from the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] systems. The author argues the proposal is scientifically underdeveloped, fails to account for known dispersal behavior of the target species, and philosophically undermines Endangered Species Act protections by designating the population "nonessential" rather than "essential" to species recovery.

## Background & Description
No morphological data are presented in this document; it is a policy/management commentary rather than a taxonomic or anatomical study. The species discussed are large-bodied, big-river cyprinid and catostomid fishes native to the Colorado River Basin:
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) — family [[Cyprinidae]], the largest North American minnow, historically a top piscivorous predator of large Southwestern rivers.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — family [[Catostomidae]], distinguished by a pronounced dorsal keel; also federally imperiled and co-occurring in the same drainage systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historical range**: Colorado squawfish were formerly abundant in the lower Colorado River, the intended reintroduction site, indicating suitable historical habitat conditions existed there.
- **Movement/dispersal ecology**: Upper Colorado River basin squawfish populations exhibit long-distance movement capability over short time periods. Analogous field studies of Colorado squawfish in the [[Verde River]] and razorback sucker in the [[Gila River]] system show strong tendencies for stocked fish to disperse away from release/stocking sites.
- **Habitat connectivity risk**: Both native and exotic fishes in the lower Colorado River system are documented to move between the mainstem river, tributaries, irrigation canals, water delivery channels, lakes, and ponds — raising concern that reintroduced squawfish would similarly disperse into unmonitored or unprotected habitats (e.g., downstream of [[Imperial Dam]]).
- **Predation pressure**: Existing post-stocking studies of fingerling Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker in the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] indicate that small stocked fish are subject to heavy predation from abundant non-native predatory species in the lower Colorado River system, with survivors becoming so widely dispersed that only sporadic recapture is feasible.
- **Regulatory/ecological status conflict**: The "nonessential experimental population" designation would leave dispersing individuals in a legally ambiguous protective status, since they would occupy habitats (canals, backwaters, mainstem reaches) not explicitly covered by the reintroduction proposal.

## Key Findings
- The proposal's classification of the reintroduced population as "nonessential" was argued to structurally exclude it from counting toward the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]]'s official recovery objectives for the species, per contemporaneous Recovery Team discussion.
- Prior experimental stocking and long-term monitoring data (collected by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[California Department of Fish and Game]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]) were not evidently consulted in drafting the proposal, despite being directly relevant to predicting outcomes.
- Based on synthesis of available monitoring data, the author concludes that mass stocking of small (fingerling) Colorado squawfish into the lower Colorado River is likely to result in high mortality from predation and extensive post-stocking dispersal, undermining monitoring and evaluation efforts.
- The proposal lacked specificity regarding: (1) concrete recovery contribution mechanisms, (2) fate of fish dispersing beyond the target zone, (3) applicable state take-law exemptions in Arizona and California, (4) coordination with the [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]] and California wildlife agencies, and (5) any linkage to a parallel Arizona Game and Fish Department initiative to establish a sport fishery for the species.
- The author explicitly opposed using an endangered species as a pilot subject for sport fishery development, citing lack of precedent studies and risk of wasting limited conservation resources.
- The letter concludes with a recommendation to redraft the initiative as an "experimental essential" reintroduction program, explicitly tied to species recovery goals, with broader stakeholder input from state and tribal management authorities.
