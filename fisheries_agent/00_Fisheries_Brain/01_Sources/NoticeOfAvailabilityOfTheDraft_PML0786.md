# Apache Trout (Oncorhynchus apache) Draft Revised Recovery Plan and Public Review Record

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document bundles a 2007 Federal Register notice, a formal comment letter from fishery scientist Paul C. Marsh, a second comment letter from [[Robert W. Clarkson]] (a Recovery Team member), and the full Draft Second Revision of the [[Apache Trout Recovery Plan]] issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) with state and tribal partners. The notice solicited public comment on the draft plan and on new information for a concurrent five-year review of the species under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The plan sets a recovery criterion of 30 self-sustaining populations within historic range, roughly 300 km of secured stream habitat. Both reviewers argue the criterion and its supporting science are inadequate. Marsh judges the draft to need "substantial revision," while Clarkson states that implementation "will not conserve Apache trout" as the ESA defines conservation.

## Background & Description

**Federal notice (July 27, 2007).** The USFWS announced availability of the draft plan and opened a comment period closing September 28, 2007. The Service was also conducting a five-year review, initiated April 27, 2006 (71 FR 20714). The notice cites the [[Endangered Species Act]] section 4(f) requirement for recovery plans and public comment during their development.

**Species status.** [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*) was listed as federally endangered in 1967, reclassified to threatened in 1975 under a 4(d) rule, and has no designated critical habitat. The original plan was finalized in 1979 and revised in 1983. The notice also lists [[Eastern Prairie Fringed Orchid]] and [[Fassett's Locoweed]] as threatened species with pending reviews.

**Taxonomy and genetics.** Apache trout are recognized by the American Fisheries Society as a subspecies of [[Gila Trout]]. Electrophoresis of 36 gene loci gave a genetic similarity index of 0.93 between the two. Carmichael et al. (1993) tested 645 individuals from 31 wild populations and found that hybridization with rainbow and cutthroat trout was common, with backcrossing toward Apache trout and no pure rainbow or cutthroat fish identified.

**Historic range and decline.** Historic range was estimated at about 1,320 km of stream above 1,800 m, down from an earlier estimate of 965 km (Harper 1978). Habitat loss from timber harvest, grazing, reservoir construction, and roads, along with introduced brook, brown, rainbow, and cutthroat trout, drove the decline. Populations were reduced to about 48 km by 1976 (Harper 1976), and the species' range had contracted to roughly 190 km of stream in 25 systems by the 2000s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Juveniles favored shallow water, low velocity, and cover; adults preferred depths over 20 cm. Water temperatures should stay below about 25 °C. Clean coarse gravel is needed for spawning, and healthy riparian corridors are essential.
- **Diet:** Mostly aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates. Fish 6–9 cm fed mainly on mayflies, while fish 15 cm and larger fed mainly on caddisflies (Harper 1978).
- **Reproduction:** Spawning runs from March to mid-June. Fecundity ranged from 72–240 eggs per female in Big Bonito Creek to 646–1,083 in Christmas Tree Lake, and one Ord Creek group rose from 72 to 4,315 eggs per female over five years.
- **Population densities:** Reported values include 0.030–0.310 fish/m² in Flash Creek and 0.005–0.570 fish/m² across nine ASNF streams.
- **Threats:** Non-native salmonids, [[Watershed Alteration]] from grazing and forestry, drought, and wildfire. The 2002 [[Rodeo-Chediski Fire]] burned over 460,000 acres in the White Mountains, and crayfish are established in some streams. [[Whirling Disease]] has been shown to be a risk in lab tests.
- **Community:** Native fishes include speckled dace, desert sucker, bluehead sucker, roundtail chub, and Little Colorado spinedace. Roundtail chub predation has been observed.

## Key Findings

**Marsh comment letter (Arizona State University, September 2007):**

- The 30-population criterion has no documented justification, and it is unclear whether 30 is too few, too many, or appropriate.
- The term "maintain" conflicts with "self-sustaining" and needs clarification.
- "Maximum extent practicable" is undefined, and the definition of "population" as all individuals able to interbreed is problematic.
- The definition of self-sustaining lacks a temporal context.
- Roughly 25% of historic range is reached under the criterion, which is too little protection against fire and other catastrophic events.
- Lack of connectivity among recovery streams means human intervention is needed, which conflicts with the self-sustaining concept, and genetic management is deferred to an unspecified future date.
- Overall: the draft "provides a basic foundation but requires substantial revision."

**Clarkson comment letter (September 12, 2007):**

- Projected habitat is about 300 km (though Table 1 sums to 272.37 km), or 22.7% of the 1,320 km historic range, leaving more than 75% of range extirpated. Clarkson argues this cannot meet the ESA definitions of endangered or threatened.
- Mainstem rivers such as the White and Black, which functioned as metapopulation centers, are excluded.
- Of the proposed streams, only 10 currently support 1,000 or more adults (mean stream length 13.4 km, range 8.25–27.31 km; unpublished Recovery Team data). Only about half the recovery streams meet an 8 km minimum for 1,000 adults.
- The plan cites Reiman and Allendorf (2001) for effective population sizes of about 1,000 spawning adults per year and Allendorf et al. (1997) for total population sizes of about 2,500. Clarkson argues that interconnected networks should be prioritized, citing [[Hilderbrand and Kershner]] (2000).
- He calls the exclusion of interconnected stream networks, mostly for non-biological reasons linked to sportfish management, the single most important failure.

**Plan content (Draft Second Revision):**

- **Recovery goal:** Delist Apache trout by establishing and maintaining 30 self-sustaining populations within historic range.
- **Current status:** About 25 pure populations exist. Of the proposed streams, 12 have replicated populations and 13 are relict populations on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation. Six more streams are identified as potential recovery streams on the ASNF.
- **Five objectives:** (1) assess, protect, and maintain habitat; (2) implement laws and regulations; (3) monitor and control disease; (4) comply with regulatory processes; (5) identify and reduce threats.
- **Management actions:** Barrier construction, non-native fish removal by piscicide, population monitoring, and hatchery stocking. A post-delisting management plan will be developed with the ASNF, AGFD, WMAT, and USFS.
- **Partnership:** A 2000 MOU between AGFD and the ASNF supports recovery of both Apache trout and [[Gila Trout]].
- **Hatchery:** Current hatchery production should be maintained or expanded for sport fishing. A broodstock management plan addresses genetic integrity.
- **Population viability:** The plan states PVA is premature given limited long-term data.

Note: the plan's recovery criterion itself is contested in both comment letters, and the five-year review outcome (retain threatened, reclassify endangered, or delist) remains pending.
