# Recovery Processes in Lotic Ecosystems: Limits of Successional Theory

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #stream-succession

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Stuart G. Fisher]] (Arizona State University, published in *Environmental Management* 14(5):725-736, 1990) evaluates whether general [[Ecological Succession]] theory can organize understanding of how running-water ecosystems recover from disturbance. The author argues that succession does occur in streams, but is overwhelmingly secondary, follows an [[Initial Floristics Model]], is strongly influenced by climate-like variation in [[Nutrient Chemistry]] (especially dissolved inorganic nitrogen), and does not approach a climax state in desert streams. Because succession is difficult to separate from allogenic change and is only one part of [[Ecosystem Stability]], the author suggests the term may be better replaced by the broader stability framework for stream research.

## Background & Description

The paper traces succession from its phytosociological origins ([[Frederic Clements]], [[Henry Cowles]], [[Henry Gleason]]) through aquatic and stream applications, including [[Longitudinal Succession]] and the [[River Continuum Concept]]. Key definitions are set out:

- **Succession:** change in a site after a discrete, unpredictable disturbance causing substantial mortality.
- **Primary vs. secondary succession:** primary succession occurs when no trace of the prior community remains; secondary succession follows when survivors or intact substrata persist.
- **Floristics models:** under the [[Initial Floristics Model]] (Egler 1954) all species of a sere are present shortly after disturbance; under relay floristics, later species colonize only after conditions change.
- **Mechanisms:** facilitation, inhibition and tolerance (Connell and Slatyer 1977), as summarized in [[Connell and Slatyer Models]].
- **Autogenic vs. allogenic change:** internal biotic change versus externally forced (often abiotic) change.

The author notes that most succession theory was derived inductively from forests after moderate disturbance, and that its extrapolation to streams has been contested.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system.** Much of the empirical work is from [[Sycamore Creek]], a Sonoran Desert stream in Arizona that flows into the Verde River. Flash floods scour the channel and cause high mortality among invertebrates and algae, while survivors persist in backwaters, eddies, sediments and the riparian corridor.

**Disturbance types.** Natural disturbances include floods, drying and glacial retreat; human-caused disturbances include flow regulation, channelization, toxic release, dredging and exotic species. Examples cited include the [[Teton Dam Failure]] (Idaho), the [[Mount St. Helens Eruption]] blast zone, and drying of Australian and Arizona streams.

**Hyporheic zone.** Subsurface sediments beneath streams can persist through floods and influence surface recovery through nitrate production by mineralization and nitrification. Scour depth and the nitrogen content of redeposited sediment affect resilience (see Figure 1 in source).

**Fish.** Fish are described as particularly susceptible to drying and absent from many Southwestern drainages that dry periodically. In Sycamore Creek, the sole fish reported in Table 2, *Agosia chrysogaster* (longfin dace), showed 0% mortality across nine flash-flood events. Its role as a detritivorous-omnivore nitrogen cycler is cited from [[Grimm 1988]].

**Recovery chemistry.** Low dissolved inorganic nitrogen flux (below 100 g/day) was associated with invertebrate population declines and favored nitrogen-fixing [[Blue-Green Algae]].

## Key Findings

**1. Succession occurs in streams, but is mostly secondary.** Even severe disturbance rarely eliminates all species; regrowth comes from survivors. Primary succession is restricted to rare, very large disturbances.

**2. The initial floristics model best fits streams.** In Sycamore Creek, algal mortality from 15 floods ranged from 1% to 100% (chlorophyll a basis), yet no species were lost or gained at the patch level. Table 2 mortality for invertebrates ranged from 0% (adult Coleoptera, *Abedus herberti*) to 96% (*Leptohyphes packeri*, *Tricorythodes dimorphus*). Relay floristics applied in a few severe cases, such as the removal of caddisflies by 100-year floods, which recolonized after months.

**3. Mechanisms are many and overlapping.** Facilitation (periphyton mucilage), inhibition (insect predators slowing rare prey establishment) and tolerance (ordered colonization from bacteria to cattails) were all observed or inferred. No single model explains whole successional sequences.

**4. Predictability is limited and variable.** Multiple regression explained 71% of variance in chlorophyll a over 35 floods (N = 352), with days since flood as the strongest predictor. Only 40% of invertebrate density variance was explained (N = 85), with nitrate flux as a key additional variable. Results differed elsewhere: Fort River (Massachusetts) showed no disturbance association, and a Pennsylvania stream showed no temporal succession.

**5. Transition matrices show non-stationary dynamics and no climax.** Across 18 postflood successional sequences, none passed a stationarity test, and no climax state was detected. Succession was flexible and sometimes reversible.

**6. Nitrogen supply is a central driver.** Invertebrate crashes followed declining DIN flux (food-quality limitation via microbial conditioning), while algal chlorophyll a continued rising. Blue-green algae dominated sequences where nitrogen was low, a case the author links to Connell and Slatyer's tolerance model.

**7. Spatial heterogeneity affects resilience.** Pools and hyporheic zones resisted flooding better than riffles and runs. Periphyton recovery was faster at stream margins than in the scoured center, producing communities of very different successional state within centimeters.

**8. The river continuum may be a product of disturbance.** Flooding can reset longitudinal gradients, so that the spatial continuum is generated by temporally discontinuous events.

**9. Recommendation: broaden the framework.** The author concludes that classical succession is "site-specific resilience," a small part of [[Ecosystem Stability]], and that the term may be abandoned in favor of stability theory for streams.

**10. Research priorities (five):**
- Biological interactions (competition, predation, life histories, nutrient cycling) as the core of recovery.
- Mechanisms of resistance and which organisms are most susceptible.
- Interactions among disturbances and with diel, seasonal and secular change, including anthropogenic impacts studied in parallel with natural ones.
- Spatial context of stability and disturbance size.
- The natural geography of disturbance by region, stream type and stream order.
