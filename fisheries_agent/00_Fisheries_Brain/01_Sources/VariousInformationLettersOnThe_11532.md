# Lower Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Team: Information Letters 1 to 24 (1973-1974)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a series of 24 information letters, issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, Parker, Arizona) between March 1973 and September 1974. They document the formation and early work of the [[Lower Colorado River Drainage System Endangered Fishes Recovery Team]]. The team was set up to recover four endangered large river fishes: the [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Colorado Squawfish]], and the [[Gila Chub Complex]] (bonytail, roundtail chub, and humpback chub). The team focused first on the razorback sucker and the squawfish. Its main activities were broodstock collection, artificial propagation and rearing, a lake survey on [[Lake Mohave]], life history research, and planning for stocking and public relations. The letters are administrative and field-operational in nature. They contain no formal quantitative analysis, but they record useful counts, rearing data, and budget figures.

## Background & Description

- **Formation (March 6, 1973):** Representatives from the California Department of Fish and Game, Arizona Game and Fish Department, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Indian Affairs, and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife met in Blythe, California. The Nevada Department of Fish and Game was not represented at that meeting but was later selected to supply a team member. Each agency provided one biologist.
- **Target species:** The team aimed to recover the razorback sucker, the Colorado squawfish, and the bonytail-roundtail-humpback chub complex (the Gila chub complex). The razorback sucker was given priority because it was the only large river form still present in the Lower Colorado River in numbers sufficient for work.
- **Planning outline:** Eight major recovery-plan areas were agreed upon: (1) literature and file search, (2) drainage survey, (3) life history studies, (4) taxonomic studies, (5) artificial propagation, (6) reintroduction into former range, (7) legal protection, and (8) public education.
- **Membership:** The team included state, federal, and university biologists. Consultants included [[Wendell Minckley]] (Arizona State University) and [[James Deacon]] (University of Nevada, Las Vegas). Advisors were added in September 1973, including staff from the Bureau of Land Management (Denver) and the U.S. Forest Service (Albuquerque). A National Park Service representative was requested in January 1974, and a Colorado Division of Game, Fish and Parks liaison was requested in January 1974.
- **Team leadership:** [[Gail C. Kobetich]], Fishery Management Biologist, served as team leader and coordinator. Tom Liles, Tom Robinson, Don Toney, and Al Essbach are also named as active participants in the letters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Survey sections:** The Lower Colorado River was divided into seven survey sections, each assigned to a lead agency biologist:
  1. Lee's Ferry to Diamond Creek (Arizona Game and Fish Department)
  2. Lake Mead (Nevada Department of Fish and Game)
  3. Lake Mohave (Arizona Game and Fish Department)
  4. Davis Dam to Blankenship Bend (California Department of Fish and Game)
  5. Lake Havasu (Bureau of Reclamation)
  6. Parker Dam to Palo Verde Diversion Dam (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife)
  7. Palo Verde Diversion Dam to the International Border (Bureau of Land Management)
- **Lake Mohave razorback population:** Field observations suggested the population was "somewhat larger than we had assumed." The team stated that it needed information on whether the population was stable, increasing, or decreasing. The letters describe the species as "disappearing rapidly from the Upper Basin."
- **Spawning sites:** Razorback suckers were reported spawning in Cottonwood Cove on [[Lake Mohave]]. A transect was also set up in the Colorado River about two miles below Hoover (Boulder) Dam for ongoing population counts.
- **Spawning-habitat limitation:** Don Toney suggested that a lack of suitable spawning grounds was a major factor in the squawfish's disappearance from the Colorado River. The team agreed to consider building an artificial spawning bed on the Lower Colorado River.
- **Feeding:** Young razorback suckers were reported to eat plankton, algae, and invertebrates. Young squawfish needed plankton-rich ponds and trout feed in captivity.

## Key Findings

### Organization and planning
- The team agreed in March 1973 that its first priorities were a literature and file search and a field survey of the Lower Colorado River.
- Cost and time estimates for the literature search were requested from Dr. Minckley and Dr. Deacon.
- Team leadership is rotated each year among the state fish and game agencies, starting with Arizona. California takes over on July 1, 1975, followed by Nevada. A team secretary position was created; Kobetich was named secretary.
- The team agreed that any fish released to the wild would be marked so that stocked fish could be distinguished from wild fish.
- Monitoring would be carried out by the state where fish were released, with team funds passed to that agency.
- A slide show on the razorback sucker program was planned, with a set of slides to be made for each participating agency.

### Funding
- The team's initial allotment was $6,000. In March 1974 about $5,347.26 remained.
- A further $5,000 was added to the Minckley contract for razorback sucker life history studies, bringing that contract to $10,000.
- Early funding priorities were broodstock acquisition and a lake-based life history study on the razorback sucker in [[Lake Mohave]].

### Razorback sucker operations
- **Collection (May 1974):** Operations on Lake Mohave at Cottonwood Cove yielded 40 razorback suckers. Two were taken by electrofishing, and 38 by seining (22 in one haul, then 16 in two more). Six of the 40 were females, four of which had apparently not spawned. No bonytail chubs were observed.
- **Eggs:** About 2,000 to 3,000 eggs were taken from two females. Eggs were hatched at 54°F and 60°F, both on screens and in hatching jars.
- **Hatching:** Eggs began hatching six days after being placed on screens at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Larvae were fed strained liver and egg yolk. Embryonic and larval specimens were preserved at 24-hour intervals for developmental study.
- **Growth:** About 3,500 fry were moved to an outdoor raceway in mid-April 1974. By July about 3,000 remained; the group averaged about 2 inches, with some near 3 inches. In September the fish ranged from 2 to 5 inches, averaging about 2.5 to 3 inches.
- **Diet caution:** Razorback suckers fed on trout food developed swayback deformities. Don Toney suggested that trout food alone may not be a complete diet.
- **Distribution plan:** About 1,500 small suckers were allocated to the [[Page Springs Facility]] of the Arizona Game and Fish Department. About 500 were kept at Willow Beach as a potential broodstock. The remainder were proposed for stocking in the Salt River (no site selected) or in the main stem near Topock Gorge.
- **Adult transfer:** Adult suckers were moved from Willow Beach to the Page Springs facility on May 7, 1974, and all survived. The team did this to avoid holding the entire broodstock in one hatchery.

### Colorado squawfish operations
- **Broodstock source:** The original squawfish broodstock was obtained from the Yampa River in Dinosaur National Monument (July 1973). Six adults were later held at Willow Beach (three males, three females).
- **Spawning:** Females were injected with HCG on June 26, 1974. The fish spawned on a gravel bar in the raceway about one week later. Larvae hatched four to five days after that. Up to about 50,000 larvae were estimated.
- **Fry numbers:** In September 1974, Toney reported roughly 6,000 to 7,000 fry in three groups. One group of about 3,000 averaged under 1 inch and was fed only artificial feed. A second group averaged about 1 inch after a "ich" outbreak. A third group averaged about 2 inches and had access to plankton.
- **Age determination:** A large mounted squawfish was aged at 16 years using otoliths by John Fitch of California Fish and Game.
- **Health:** Bruce Rosenlund examined a squawfish that died in April 1974 for parasites and disease organisms; the report was attached to the letters.
- **Rearing strategy:** The team decided to stock young squawfish into small, plankton-rich ponds with few predators until they reached at least 6 inches before release into the river. Page Springs was offered as a rearing site.
- **Recovery plan:** A Colorado squawfish recovery plan was completed at the Recovery Plan Workshop in July 1974 by Kobetich and Hal Boeker of the Denver Regional Office.

### Research priorities (Minckley contract)
1. Embryology and post-embryonic development, using hatchery juveniles and preserved material.
2. Sex ratios in young fish and changes with age.
3. Sexual dimorphism, with the aim of a reliable non-lethal sexing criterion.
4. Fecundity and fertility of mature fish across age classes.
5. Size and age structure of natural populations, using non-lethal methods where possible (SCUBA, night lights, electrofishing, seines, underwater photography, and minimal netting).

### Regulatory and administrative matters
- Permit requirements under the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]] were discussed in September 1974. No agreement was reached on procedures, and the question was deferred. Kobetich was asked to prepare a report.
- The team name was questioned because some target species were not on the official endangered list. A new name was deferred to the November 6, 1974 meeting.
- The next team meeting was scheduled for November 6, 1974.
