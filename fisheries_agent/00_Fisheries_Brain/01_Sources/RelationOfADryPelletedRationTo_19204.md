# Dry Pelleted Diets and Nutritional Anemia in Brook and Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether a [[Dry Pelleted Diet]] causes [[Nutritional Anemia]] in [[Brook Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], two salmonids raised in hatcheries. Earlier work had shown that trout fed purified or synthetic diets became anemic, and that [[Raw Beef Liver]], fly maggots and some extracts could prevent this. Researchers at Michigan State University and the Michigan Department of Conservation compared red blood cell (erythrocyte) counts in fish fed a dry pellet with or without raw-meat supplementation against fish fed raw beef liver. Neither species developed nutritional anemia under the conditions tested. Small differences in blood counts were judged unimportant.

## Background & Description

Early trout nutrition depended on raw fresh meat supplying essential substances. Diets with adequate vitamins A, C, D, E and the B-complex supported survival and growth but still produced anemia (McCay and Dilley, 1927). A synthetic diet also caused anemia, and fish died when red blood cell counts fell below about 700,000 per cubic millimeter. Liver, raw liver and fly maggots prevented or cured the condition, while liver extracts gave inconsistent results (Tunison et al., 1939, 1941).

Several candidate factors were proposed:

- **[[Xanthopterin]]:** Injection into anemic fingerling salmon raised erythrocytes to about 1,600,000 per cubic millimeter, with a positive dose-response (Simmons and Norris, 1941). Later hatchery work at Cortland did not confirm this.
- **[[Folic Acid]] and biotin:** McLaren, Herman and Elvehjem (1947) reported that anemia did not develop when these were added to an anemia-producing diet.

In 1953 the Michigan Department of Conservation began investigating dry animal and vegetable meals as trout feed. Erythrocyte counts were used to check whether dry rations affected red cell levels.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted in hatchery conditions, not in natural waters. Feeding was adjusted seasonally to match water temperature. Fish were held in groups of 1,000 to 4,000 per species and treatment. Hatchery husbandry, feed composition and fish condition, rather than wild habitat or community interactions, were the main environmental variables considered. The paper contains no data on water chemistry, other aquatic organisms or habitat structure.

## Key Findings

- **Experimental design:** Rainbow and brook trout (3 to 5 inches, 9 months old at the start) were fed either a control ration of 100% raw beef liver or the dry pellet. Feeding rates were 1.5 to 1.9% of body weight per day for the dry diet and twice that amount for the control.
- **Anemia threshold:** Fish with red blood cell counts below about 900,000 per cubic millimeter were considered to be approaching anemia.
- **Two dietary regimes were compared:**
  1. Dry pellet fed 4 days per week, raw beef liver 1 day per week, with no food 2 days per week.
  2. Dry pellet alone for 12 months, without raw-meat supplementation.
- **Long-term supplementation (Table 3):** Fish fed the dry pellet with meat supplementation for nearly 2 years had erythrocyte counts roughly equal to those of liver-fed fish. Values were about 1.1 to 1.4 million per cubic millimeter in both groups. The table caption says "per cubic centimeter," which conflicts with the text and is likely an error.
- **Dry diet without meat (Table 4):** Fish were moved to the dry pellet alone in March 1954. Counts were compared with liver-fed fish a year later.

- **Trend:** Counts rose slightly in dry-fed fish over the year and fell slightly in liver-fed fish. The author did not consider these small differences important.
- **Conclusion:** Under these conditions, neither [[Brook Trout]] nor [[Rainbow Trout]] developed nutritional anemia on the [[Dry Pelleted Diet]], with or without raw-meat supplementation.

### Methods

Blood was drawn from the severed caudal peduncle, with duplicate samples from four fish per group. Samples were diluted in Hayem's solution in a pipette (0.5 mark blood, 101 mark diluent) and shaken for 2 minutes. Red cells were then counted on a [[Hemocytometer]] under the microscope.

### Context

The work was presented in partial fulfillment of a PhD at [[Michigan State University]] and published as a Michigan Agricultural Experiment Station Journal Article (1930). The author attributes the Michigan trials to a 1953 Michigan Department of Conservation program. Other referenced hatchery work includes the [[Cortland Hatchery]] reports from New York.
