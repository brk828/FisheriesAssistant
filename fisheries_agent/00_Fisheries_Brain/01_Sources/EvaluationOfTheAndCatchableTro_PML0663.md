# Evaluation of the 1974 and 1975 Catchable Trout Stockings in the Salt River Below Stewart Mountain Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department report evaluates a two-year program that stocked [[Catchable Trout]] [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) in the [[Salt River]] below [[Stewart Mountain Dam]]. The program aimed to give Phoenix-area anglers trout fishing close to home. About 30,000 fish were planted over 1974 and 1975, each tagged with [[Flag Tag]]s. Harvest was estimated at 50 to 65 percent of planted fish, and catch rates were 0.51 fish per hour in 1974 and 0.35 (or 0.32, see Key Findings) in 1975. The authors judged the program successful and recommended making it a permanent part of the state trout program, with changes to stocking methods and numbers.

## Background & Description

- **Historical stocking:** After Stewart Mountain Dam was completed in 1930, the river below it was seen as a potential put-and-take trout fishery. Trout were first introduced in 1946. Stocking was irregular until 1966, when a [[Fingerling Stocking]] program began. It was discontinued after the 1969 plants because angling success dropped and river flows varied widely.
- **Rationale for catchables:** Renewed interest after the 1973 energy crisis led to a program of planting catchable-size trout. The aim was to offer immediate fishing to people who would otherwise make long trips to northern lakes and streams.
- **Study area:** The lower Salt River lies about 50 km northeast of the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]]. It flows roughly 23 km from Stewart Mountain Dam to [[Granite Reef Dam]], where water is diverted to canals serving agricultural land. The river runs through Sonoran Desert riparian habitat dominated by mesquite, cottonwood, and palo verde, with arrowweed, cattail, and giant reed on sand bars.
- **Flow regime:** Flow follows daily cycles tied to power demand and a strong annual cycle. Winter mean daily flows (October to March) are 25 cfs or less. Summer releases (April to September) are much higher; the exact summer average is garbled in the scan. Flows have increased over the past decade.
- **Climate and recreation:** Annual rainfall is under 25.4 cm. Summer daily temperatures often exceed 40 °C. Tubing and fishing are the main uses, with tubers forming the largest user group. [[Blue Point Bridge]] saw holiday peaks of over 900 tubers per hour.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water temperature and pH.** Continuous-release water temperatures ranged from 17 °C in May to 22 °C in September. Temperatures were recorded in Areas 1, 4 and 5 in 1974 and 1975. pH was measured only in 1974. Both were within acceptable ranges for rainbow trout. Warming between the upper and lower reaches was negligible.

**Downstream movement.** Tag returns showed very little downstream movement, unlike the [[Colorado River]] studies cited. The farthest downstream recovery was 2 km. A temperature rise of only 1 to 2 °C between the dam and Coon Bluff may keep trout in the cooler upper reaches.

**Summer loss.** Tagged 1974 trout were almost absent from creels in 1975. The authors attributed this to fish being unable to survive in the small pools left after water releases ended.

**Temperature driven by reservoir operation.** Warmer 1974 releases are linked to [[Pump-Storage Operation]] at [[Saguaro Lake]]. Pump-back operation at Mormon Flat Dam ran more than 260 days in 1974, versus fewer than 100 days in 1975. This warmed the hypolimnion, and the warmer water was released into the Salt River. Continued pump-generation could make releases too warm for trout.

**Native fish.** Gila mountain suckers quickly clogged gill nets in the 1974 sampling. Other species in the angler catch (largemouth bass, yellow bass, bluegill, green sunfish, channel catfish, bullhead, carp) were mostly incidental catches.

## Key Findings

**Harvest and tag returns**
- Harvest was estimated at 50 to 65 percent of planted trout, based on tagging and angler interviews.
- Tag returns estimated that 2,400 fish (16 percent) of the 1974 plants and 7,500 fish (50 percent) of the 1975 plants were removed by anglers. The authors attributed the gap in part to a 1974 nonreporting rate well above the estimated 40 percent, and to a 58 percent rise in angling pressure in 1975.
- The nonreporting rate of 40 percent was taken from creel inspections at a single-access check station at [[Canyon Lake]]. It was considered acceptable, though the authors noted it may not apply to other waters.

**Catch rates and timing**
- Catch rates were 0.51 fish per hour in 1974. The 1975 figure is given as 0.35 in the abstract and as 0.32 in the results section; the source is inconsistent.
- [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] standard: 0.50 fish per hour is the acceptable threshold. The 1974 catch met it; 1975 fell below it.
- Fishing success was highest right after planting. 57 percent of harvested fish were caught within 10 days in the abstract. The results section gives a figure of 77 percent within 20 days for 1975. Returns in 1974 took about 40 days.
- Average days between plants rose from 15 in 1974 to 29 in 1975. Weekday anglers out-caught weekend anglers, and weekends had 44 percent more anglers.

**Angler use and value**
- May to October 1975 use was estimated at about 10,000 anglers spending 30,000 hours on the water.
- The Salt River attracted about half as many anglers as the state's older summer trout programs. Those higher-elevation waters averaged about 54 percent more angling hours and 38 percent more angler days.
- The Salt River produced 30 to 50 percent more angling hours than neighboring warm-water reservoirs. Shore-angler hours exceeded [[Canyon Lake]] by 33 percent. Total use was 46 percent higher than the [[Saguaro Lake]] fishery.
- In 1975, the program produced about two angling hours for each trout planted, compared with 0.9 hours on [[Oak Creek]].
- Anglers' effort was 13 hours per day in other studies. Because the Salt River is restricted by heat and tuber traffic, 8 hours per day was used. Canyon Lake's winter survey gave 4.2 hours, and 3 hours was selected for the Salt River.

**Growth**
- Only six of about 1,000 weighed and measured recovered fish had been in the river long enough (at least three weeks) to show measurable growth.
- By four weeks, catchables gained about 30 g and 3 cm. One tagged fish at 25 weeks had gained 184 g.
- Fingerlings planted in May 1967 at 11 cm reached 23 cm by August.

**Species composition**
- Rainbow trout made up about 88 percent of the angler catch in both years, based on the table totals. The remainder was mostly incidental species.

**Methodological limits**
- Post-season sampling recovered very few trout. Gill nets were fouled by suckers in 1974. Rotenone sampling in 1975 was limited by interagency conflict.
- The authors concluded that the small recovery reflected sampling failure, not necessarily high fishing mortality. They noted that natural mortality (estimated at about 50 percent before angling) and downstream movement could also explain the gap.

**Recommendations**
1. Carry out a complete [[Rotenone Renovation]] from Stewart Mountain Dam to below Blue Point Bridge in 1976, right after flow ends, to estimate remaining trout and the causes of loss.
2. Stock fingerlings and catchables together in 1977. Plant both immediately after continuous releases begin in March or April. Stop catchable plants once fingerlings reach creel size, about 23 cm.
3. Reduce stocking numbers and frequency in 1977 to 2,000 trout every 15 days. This should give acceptable angler returns and reduce catch-rate fluctuations between plants.
4. Mark catchables (preferably with an adipose clip) to monitor fingerling survival and growth.

**Overall conclusion:** The program produced an acceptable return on the fish planted and provided fishing in a reach of river that was marginal before. The authors described the [[Salt River]] trout fishery as a substantial success that gives anglers fishing close to home at low cost.
