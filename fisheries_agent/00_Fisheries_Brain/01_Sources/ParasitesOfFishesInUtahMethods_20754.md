# Parasites of Fishes in Utah: Methods of Examination and Examples

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-parasitology #protozoa #helminths #myxozoa

## Executive Summary

This paper by Richard Heckmann (Department of Zoology, Brigham Young University) reviews parasites of Utah fishes, a group the author describes as poorly known. It outlines a step-by-step protocol for examining fish for [[Ectoparasites]] and [[Endoparasites]], covering external scrapings, [[Blood Parasites]] sampling, and internal organ examination. It also lists 22 representative parasite examples with taxonomy, host, distribution, habitat, diagnosis, and remarks. The author argues that fish should be given equal consideration with classic parasitology hosts, given the growth of [[Aquaculture]], and calls for surveys, host-parasite studies, immunology and control research in Utah.

## Background & Description

The paper's stated purpose is to report examples of fish parasites in Utah, emphasizing techniques for locating symbiotic organisms. Parasites are grouped by the classic division into protozoa and metazoa; metazoans include helminths (worms), leeches, arthropods and other forms. The author notes that fishes are often omitted from general parasitology courses, although a wide array of parasites can be found on them with careful checking.

Materials listed include dissecting trays, petri dishes, dissecting and compound microscopes, slides and coverslips, pipettes, glass vials, notebooks, and fixatives such as [[Bouin's Fixative]], formalin and [[AFA Fixative]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling priority:** Fish should be examined immediately after death, since some parasites leave a dead host and bacterial breakdown degrades remaining parasites. Moribund fish are considered good prospects.
- **Sampling sites:** Gills, fin bases and body surface are the most heavily colonized external areas. Internal sites include the gut (pyloric ceca and intestine), gall bladder and liver, kidney, gonads, brain, and eyes.
- **Time dependence:** Some blood parasites show daily periodicity in the vascular system, so both peripheral and cardiac samples, plus kidney smears, are recommended.
- **Utah localities named:** [[Deer Creek Reservoir]], [[Provo River]], [[Yellowstone Lake]], [[Strawberry Reservoir]], [[Palisade Reservoir]], [[Nine Mile Reservoir]], [[Fish Lake]] and the [[Colorado River]] (including [[Colorado River Squawfish]] hosts).
- **Host groups:** Salmonids, catfish, suckers, minnows, sculpins, [[Utah Chub]], yellow perch, sticklebacks and bass.

## Key Findings

**Methods summary**

1. **External examination:** Mucus and epithelial scrapings from gills, fin bases and body surfaces are examined unstained at 100X and 430X, then stained with methyl green-pyronine (see [[Methyl Green-Pyronine Stain]]). Silver impregnation is used for ciliate identification.
2. **Blood examination:** Thin blood films are air dried and stained with [[Giemsa Stain]] or [[Wright's Stain]]. Each slide is examined at 430X and 1000X for at least 10 minutes. Packed cell volume, hemoglobin and differential counts are recommended.
3. **Internal examination:** The abdominal cavity is opened along the ventral surface, organs are examined macroscopically and with a dissecting scope, and scrapings and liver squashes are made. Whole mounts of metazoans are stained with [[Semichon's Carmine]] after [[AFA Fixative]].
4. **Histology:** Infected organs are preserved in 10% formalin or Bouin's for histopathology, with [[Hematoxylin and Eosin]] as the usual counterstain.
5. **Virus and bacteria:** Samples should be taken before or at host death.

**Parasite examples (22 listed)**

- **Protozoa (ectoparasitic ciliates and amoebae):** Thecamoeba hoffmani (southern Utah suckers and minnows); [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] ("Ich"), described as the most serious parasitic disease of channel catfish, with no good chemotherapy and common in Utah; Glossatella sp. and Epistylis sp. on sculpins in the Provo River; Trichodina truttae and T. davesi (the latter causing mortalities at high numbers in yellow perch, Deer Creek Reservoir); Trichophrya piscium, heavily infesting fish in Yellowstone Lake.
- **Blood flagellates:** Cryptobia atraria, a new species being described and specific to Utah chub, with leeches as intermediate hosts and reservoirs; Cryptobia salmositica, smaller, in salmonid kidney.
- **Myxozoa:** Henneguya exilis, implicated in high channel catfish mortalities, with no chemical treatment; [[Myxosoma Cerebralis]], the agent of [[Whirling Disease]], which causes permanent bone damage in salmonids. The author reports it has not been found in Utah after screening over 300 fish with the plankton centrifuge-digestion method. Myxobolus sp. is common in Utah chub.
- **Monogenean:** Gyrodactylus elegans (viviparous, questioned taxonomic status).
- **Digenean trematodes:** Crepidostomum farionis (cutthroat trout intestine and bile ducts); Neascus sp. (black spot metacercariae, common in Utah); [[Diplostomum Spathaceum]], causing blindness through lens infection, very common in Utah, with worm burdens in eyes declining. Strawberry, Palisade and Nine Mile reservoirs are heavily infected, and a microsporidan parasite of snail larvae is noted as a possible biological control.
- **Acanthocephalan:** Neoechinorhynchus sp. (warm water fishes, intestine).
- **Nematodes:** Camallanus oxycephalus (viviparous, large intestine and anus).
- **Copepod:** Lernaea cyprinacea (anchor worm, observed in Utah fishes).
- **Cestodes:** Proteocephalus sp. (common bass tapeworm; adults recently found in Colorado River Squawfish); larval cestodes Ligula, Diphyllobothrium and Schistocephalus (plerocercoids, which reduce egg production in sticklebacks); Isoglaridacris sp. (single proglottid tapeworm, common in Southern Utah suckers).

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Many Utah fish parasites remain undescribed, and surveys would extend host ranges and records for known species.
- Parasites should be included in impact studies, biological inventories and fish die-off or condition assessments.
- The effects of parasites on fish are poorly understood, and host-parasite relationships, immune mechanisms and control methods need study.
- Published references cited include works by Amlacher (1970), Hoffman (1967), Hoffman and Meyer (1974), Reichenbach-Klinke (1973), and Snieszko (1970).
