# A New Cyprinodon from Death Valley: *Cyprinodon milleri*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 *Copeia* paper by LaBounty and Deacon describes [[Cyprinodon Milleri]], a new pupfish from [[Cottonball Marsh]] on the floor of [[Death Valley]], California. The authors separate it from its closest relative, [[Cyprinodon Salinus]], and from other Death Valley pupfishes on morphometric and meristic evidence, including shorter pelvic fins, fewer fin rays, and higher scale counts. They also examine the marsh's extreme salinity, the drainage and tectonic history of the valley floor, and the rate at which speciation may have occurred. The authors conclude that the new species arose within the last few thousand years, a very short interval of geologic time. The species is named in honor of [[Robert Rush Miller]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic position:** Described as a distinct species within the Death Valley pupfish system. The authors regard it as a distinct species "in the context of the pupfishes of the Death Valley system, as currently understood."
- **Type series:** Morphometric data are based on a holotype and paratypes from Cottonball Marsh, with comparative samples from Lower Salt Creek (McClean Spring and Upper Salt Creek populations are also tabulated). Meristic counts were taken from 60 specimens, unless otherwise noted.
- **Diagnostic features:**
  - Pelvic fins much shorter than in other Death Valley species, except [[Cyprinodon Diabolis]], which usually lacks pelvic fins.
  - Fin-ray counts generally lower than in other Death Valley species.
  - Scale counts generally higher than in other Death Valley species, most closely resembling those of *C. salinus*.
  - Caudal peduncle longer and body proportionally shallower, producing a distinctive "tail-wagging" swimming movement.
  - Jaw teeth with a different structure from other local pupfishes; the new species lacks the marginal cusps present in *C. salinus*.
  - Preorbital pores frequently reduced in number or absent, a trait shared only with *C. diabolis* among local species.
- **Meristic means (n = 60 unless noted):**

| Lateral-series scales | 29.84 ± 0.21 (range 27–34) |

- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males are larger, with deeper bodies, heads, and caudal peduncles, and have dorsal and pelvic fins positioned farther forward and a larger anal fin. Males show a dark dorsum and broad vertical bars, blackened dorsal fins, and a deep blue body with iridescent turquoise head coloration during breeding. Females have narrower, more distinct vertical bars and a dorsal ocellus. Both sexes have a milky-white venter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** *C. milleri* lives in what the authors describe as possibly the most extreme environment for a fish in North America in terms of temperature and salinity. Cottonball Marsh lies about 80 m below mean sea level in two surface-water areas, about 56.7 ha to the north and 202.5 ha about 1 km to the south. Water covers about 40% of the area in winter and less than 10% in summer. Many shallow pools are only a few centimeters deep, though some reach about 80 cm. As summer evaporation increases, inter-pool connections are cut, trapping large numbers of fish.

**Salinity.** Hunt et al. (1966) reported total dissolved solids of 14 to 160 ppt in the marsh. Salinity generally increases with distance from the inflow toward the dry salt pan. Fish were abundant in all pools, but July 1968 observations showed fewer individuals in the most saline water. The authors note that most fishes tolerate salinities of about 90 ppt at most, with notable exceptions, whereas *C. milleri* occurs even in the saltiest pools.

**Laboratory tolerance.** *C. salinus* survived up to about 67.3 ppt and died at 78.5 ppt after about an hour. Both *C. milleri* and *C. salinus* could be acclimated to distilled water for at least two weeks, and *C. milleri* remained in good condition for at least several days at 78.5 ppt before experiments ended.

**Associated biota.** Aquatic plants include algae on pool edges, a few stalks of *Ruppia maritima*, and a rush (*Juncus* sp.) in less saline areas. Invertebrates include the hydrobiid snail *Tryonia* sp., which D. W. Taylor (San Diego Zoological Society) identified as a genus not previously known from Death Valley, the amphipod *Hyalella azteca* (identified by G. A. Cole), and ostracods. Terrestrial vegetation is limited to pickleweed (*Allenrolfea occidentalis*) and saltgrass (*Distichlis spicata*) near the water source.

**Geologic and hydrologic history.** The authors summarize evidence that a [[Holocene Lake]] about 9 m deep existed in Death Valley before about 2,000 years BP. The evidence includes dune artifacts of Death Valley III and IV occupations and gypsum deposits. Pollen and radiocarbon data from Mehringer suggest connections between aquatic habitats about 3600 BP and 300–400 BP, the latter coinciding with increased precipitation. Tectonic tilting of the valley floor (an uplift of about 100 m northward and about 7 m eastward) likely separated Salt Creek from Cottonball Marsh. Several fishless springs (Tule, Eagle Borax, Nevares, Travertine) remain unexplained.

**Gene flow.** Flood events, including one in spring 1969, have connected populations temporarily. The authors judge that such contact would allow hybridization but that long-term gene flow is unlikely. Allopatric Death Valley pupfishes show little barrier to hybridization, with *C. diabolis* as the exception.

## Key Findings

1. **New species recognized.** *C. milleri* is a distinct species, differentiated from *C. salinus* and other Death Valley pupfishes by fin-ray counts, scale counts, pelvic fin length, and tooth structure.
2. **Morphological patterns with salinity.** Scale counts increase with salinity in the Death Valley series. Fin-ray counts decrease with salinity. Gill rakers, pelvic rays, and pore counts show no clear trend.
3. **Extreme salinity tolerance.** *C. milleri* persists in the saltiest pools of Cottonball Marsh, reported up to 160 ppt TDS, far above the approximate 90 ppt maximum for most fishes.
4. **Rapid speciation.** Because permanent connection with Salt Creek ceased within the last few thousand years, the authors argue that evolution produced a distinct species in a very short period. They cite parallels with rapid speciation in passerine birds, a copepod endemic to the Salton Sea, and a barnacle subspecies.
5. **Environmental drivers.** The authors identify salinity, extreme seasonal temperatures (including high summer temperatures), and isolation as the most likely factors shaping morphological differentiation.
6. **Conservation context.** The marsh is within Death Valley National Monument, and the National Park Service provided cooperation for the study. The authors' data are presented as baseline information on an isolated, highly specialized fish population.

**Etymology:** *milleri* honors Dr. Robert Rush Miller, Curator of Fishes, Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan.
