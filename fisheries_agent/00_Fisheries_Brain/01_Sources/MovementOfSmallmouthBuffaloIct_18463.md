# Movement of Smallmouth Buffalo (*Ictiobus bubalus*) in Elephant Butte Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictiobus-bubalus

## Executive Summary

This 1968 report by Robert R. Patterson (New Mexico State University) examines the movement of [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] in [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]]. The species is a major rough fish targeted by [[Commercial Fishing]] as part of the reservoir's management plan. Between June 1964 and December 1967, 1,518 buffalo were tagged in a [[Mark-Recapture Tagging]] study, yielding 101 returns (a 6.7 percent return rate). The study found no evidence of seasonal migration between shallow and deep water. Instead, the author concluded that buffalo show continuous random movement, with schooling evident among fish tagged together.

## Background & Description

Commercial fishing for rough fish has been used on Elephant Butte Reservoir as part of a lake management plan. From August 1963 through December 1967, commercial fishermen removed approximately 74,000 buffalo weighing 300,000 pounds. Smallmouth buffalo are one of the major rough fish species in the reservoir.

In June 1964, a Dingell-Johnson (D-J) project began to investigate game fish reproduction and rough fish problems. Its goals were to learn about the species' movements, concentrations, and age-growth, and to help increase the effectiveness of commercial fishing. Major game and rough fish species were tagged with Monel butt-end tags fastened to the mandible or Monel strap tags fastened to the opercle.

The reservoir was divided into a grid of half-mile squares (see [[Reservoir Grid System]]). The "deep" area lies downstream (south) of [[Kettle Top Butte]], and the "shallow" area lies upstream (north) of it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal movement hypothesis.** Earlier studies by [[Huntington And Hill 1956]] and [[Rael 1966]] suggested that buffalo move into warm, shallow upstream waters in spring and into deep water in fall, apparently associated with water temperature. Patterson considered their combined basis of 15 tag recoveries too small to support firm conclusions, and his own data did not support seasonal movement.

**Comparison with Watts Bar Reservoir.** [[Martin Auerbach And Nelson 1964]] studied smallmouth buffalo in [[Watts Bar Reservoir]], Tennessee, using 24 recoveries. They described the fish as "sedentary for two years after hatching," then moving "upstream into tributary areas," with most moves in late winter or early spring and no mass migrations recorded. Patterson reported that Martin et al.'s movements (0 to 450 miles over 38 to 577 days) were comparable to his own results.

**Schooling and random movement.** Fish tagged together were often recovered together or at different sites, which the author interpreted as evidence of schooling and of random movement rather than directed migration.

## Key Findings

**Tagging and returns**
- 1,518 smallmouth buffalo were tagged between June 1964 and December 1967.
- 87 tags were returned by commercial fisherman B. C. Sparkman, and 14 were recovered in project nets. The total was 101 returns, a 6.7 percent return rate.
- Table 1 lists 102 tagged fish by month of tagging, with 80 tagged in deep areas and 21 in shallow areas; returns were distributed across deep and shallow areas and across downstream, upstream, and "across" directions. Table 2 summarizes returns by month of recovery. The tables are summarized here, not reproduced.

**Movement patterns**
- No seasonal migration was detected. Analysis of tag returns indicated continuous random movement.
- The apparent upstream movement in Tables 1 and 2 is misleading. Most tagged fish were released in the lower three miles of the lake, and most commercial netting occurs in the upper half, so upstream recapture is more likely by design.
- Catch proportions were consistent with random movement. In January through March 1967, Sparkman caught about 6,500 buffalo (nearly 24,000 pounds) in the upper lake, indicating many fish in shallow water in winter. Nets in deep water caught tagged fish in about the same proportion as shallow-water nets, which suggests random movement into and out of both areas.

**Distance and rate**
- The average minimum distance moved by all recovered buffalo was 5.15 miles.
- The average time between tagging and recovery was 351.0 days.
- The resulting average minimum movement was 77.5 feet per day.
- Notable individual movements:
  - 8.5 miles in 17 days (reported as 2,640 feet per day, or one-half mile per day).
  - 3 miles in 13 days (1,218 feet per day).
  - 11 miles in 71 days (818 feet per day).
  - 13 miles in 138 days (497 feet per day).
  - One fish was recovered in its tagging area 464 days later, with no net movement.
- Five fish were recaptured in the same net the day after tagging, and two more moved to nearby nets. These no-movement, one-day returns lowered the mean distance in the 0 to 3.0 mile group and the grand means.

**Schooling evidence (Figures 2 to 4)**
- Three buffalo tagged together were recovered together 6 miles downstream after 335 days.
- Three others tagged together were recovered at different times and places.
- Five buffalo tagged on the same day were recovered at other sites: two together, two at another site at different times, and one alone at a third site.

**Growth data**
- Six tagged buffalo were recaptured with accurate length and weight measurements. Growth was variable: some fish gained length and weight, one gained 13 mm in length while losing three ounces over 84 days, and one gained 103 mm and 15 ounces over 623 days.

**Overall conclusion**
- Smallmouth buffalo in Elephant Butte Reservoir show random, continuous movement with schooling tendencies, and no evidence of seasonal shallow-deep migration. The author's findings suggest that catch distribution reflects fishing effort rather than fish concentrations in particular zones.
