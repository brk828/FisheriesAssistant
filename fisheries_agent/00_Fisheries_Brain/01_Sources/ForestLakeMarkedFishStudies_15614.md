# Forest Lake Marked Fish Studies

#ichthyology #salmonidae #trout-management #reservoir-ecology #colorado

## Executive Summary

This three-year study (1952-1954) of [[Forest Lake]], a 32.5-acre reservoir on [[Grand Mesa]] in [[Delta County]], [[Colorado]], evaluated marked fish returns, creel census data, food utilization, limnology, and age-growth characteristics of [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], and [[Native Trout]] (Salmo clarki). The primary objective was to assess the effectiveness of stocking two-inch marked fish versus fry. Over the study period, 1,310 fisherman trips yielded 3,793 fish at a catch-per-man-hour (CPMH) of 0.78. Marked two-inch [[Rainbow Trout]] returned 13.2% to the creel over three years with average two-year growth of 8.8 inches, while [[Brook Trout]] returned 10.5% with 7.7-inch growth. Native fry (3/4 inch) planted in 1950 returned only 1.3% by 1954. The aquatic midge larva *Tendipes* (Chironomus) dominated all trout diets. Evidence of winter oxygen depletion suggested periodic winter kills may affect population dynamics. The study recommends annual stocking of 3,000 two- to three-inch [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] each to maintain satisfactory fishing.

## Background & Description

[[Forest Lake]] is a reservoir lake located on [[Grand Mesa]], a lava-capped tableland at approximately 10,000 feet elevation in [[Delta County]], [[Colorado]] (R. 94 W., T. 12 S.). The lake has a maximum surface area of 32.5 acres and minimum of 25.0 acres, with maximum depth of 55 feet. An earth-fill, rock-face dam constructed in 1900 increased storage capacity; a new larger dam was built in 1954. The lake occupies a single basin with approximately 20% shoal area, predominantly near the outlet. Bottom substrate is primarily muck and detritus. The outlet stream, a tributary to [[Surface Creek]] (which flows to the [[Gunnison River]]), is used for irrigation and typically dries by late summer. A bottom-type outlet can drain 7.5 acres of water, with drawdown usually occurring by late August depending on snowfall.

Spawning facilities are extremely limited in both inlet and outlet streams, with no water present in fall. The lake is accessible via State Highway 65 and a 1/4-mile trail. The stored water is released for irrigation of fruit orchards in the valley below the mesa.

Three salmonid species were present: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), and [[Native Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*). Experimental marked fish plants were made June 28, 1951 (3,302 rainbow, 3,280 brook; 1,020 fin-clipped) and July 16, 1952 (same numbers). The 1951 plant averaged 2.4 inches (rainbow) and 2.7 inches (brook); the 1952 plant averaged 2.2 inches for both species. In 1950, 23,600 native fry averaging slightly over 3/4 inch were stocked as an experimental plant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Limnology**: [[Forest Lake]] exhibited thermal stratification throughout summer months, with thermocline typically established between 10-30 feet depth. Surface water temperatures ranged 46-64°F during summer. Dissolved oxygen was generally adequate during summer (surface 6.3-7.7 ppm, bottom variable), but critically low on March 29, 1954 (1.95 ppm), suggesting potential periodic winter kills under ice cover (November-May). pH ranged 6.5-8.5 (average 7.5), reflecting basic chemistry from surrounding basaltic lava formations. Total dissolved solids were 34.8 ppm (calcium carbonate 14.8 ppm, magnesium 2.0 ppm, chlorides 3.9 ppm, sulphates 12.0 ppm, phosphates 2.1 ppm). Secchi disk transparency ranged 119-260 inches.

**Plankton**: The copepod *Cyclops bicuspidatus* was the predominant zooplankter but rarely consumed by trout. *Daphnia longispina* occurred in lower numbers but was extensively utilized by all trout species. Phytoplankton blooms of *Asterionella formosa*, *Anabaena circinallis*, and *Fragilaria crotonensis* occurred, particularly in August. Other zooplankters included *Diaptomus* spp. and copepod nauplii.

**Aquatic Vegetation**: Four species present: *Callitriche* spp., *Ceratophyllum* spp., *Potamogeton* spp., and *Potamogeton alpinus* (dominant). Vegetation most abundant in August in littoral zone bordering outlet.

**Bottom Fauna**: Notably sparse. The midge larva *Tendipes* (Chironomidae) was the only abundant benthic organism, occurring most frequently in samples and trout diets. Other organisms collected infrequently: Gastropoda, Pelecypoda, Hydracarina, and Trichoptera. Benthic populations were higher in inaccessible areas (under logs, in vegetation stands).

**Food Habits**: Analysis of 220 stomach samples (1952-1953) from 123 brook trout (6.3-18.4 inches), 55 rainbow (3.3-13.2 inches), and 42 native trout (7.5-12.2 inches) revealed *Tendipes* larvae as the predominant food item for all species, comprising 50-65.7% of total volume and occurring in 69-94.7% of stomachs. *Daphnia* was the second most important item. Terrestrial insects were extensively utilized by rainbow and native trout when available. Despite *Cyclops* abundance in plankton, it rarely appeared in trout diets.

## Key Findings

**Creel Census Results (1952-1954)**:
- Total: 1,310 fisherman trips, 4,833 hours, 3,793 fish checked (estimated 65-75% of total trips)
- Overall CPMH: 0.78 (declined from 1.25 in 1952 to 0.51 in 1953, 0.57 in 1954)
- 1955 voluntary census: CPMH 0.30 (43.2% as efficient as compulsory census)
- Average fisherman fished 3.7 hours to catch 2.9 fish
- 32.0% of fishermen caught no fish over three years
- Resident men accounted for majority of trips, hours, and catch
- Weekend/holiday fishermen: 81.2% of trips (1952)
- Shore fishermen: ~75-88% of anglers; boat fishermen had higher CPMH

**Species Composition**:
- [[Brook Trout]]: 66.0% of catch, average length 8.4 inches
- [[Rainbow Trout]]: 26.0% of catch, average length 9.9 inches
- [[Native Trout]]: 8.0% of catch, average length 10.5 inches

**Marked Fish Returns**:
- **Rainbow Trout** (two-inch): 9.1% first year, 12.9% second year, 16.0% third year (total three-year return)
- **Brook Trout** (two-inch): 9.1% first year, 10.0% second year, 17.9% total (1951 plant); 2.2% total (1952 plant)
- **Native Trout** (3/4-inch fry, 1950 plant): 0.3% second year, 0.6% third year, 1.3% fourth year (1954)
- Poor 1952 brook trout plant returns possibly due to winter kill, outlet losses (unscreened), or pre-census fishing pressure

**Growth Rates**:
- **Rainbow Trout**: 6.5 inches first year, 8.8 inches second year, 10.3 inches third year
- **Brook Trout**: 5.4 inches first year, 7.7 inches second year
- **Native Trout**: 8.1 inches second year, 10.6 inches third year, 12.2 inches fourth year
- Growth rates comparable or superior to other [[Colorado]] high-elevation lakes

**Age Structure** (216 scale samples analyzed):
- **Brook Trout**: Five age groups present (I-V), indicating successful natural reproduction despite no stocking for 8+ years. Age I: 6.3-10.1 inches; Age II: 9.0-12.3 inches; Age III: 13.5-14.2 inches; Age IV: 15.1-16.7 inches; Age V: 17.0-17.6 inches
- **Rainbow Trout**: Age 0: 3.3-4.7 inches; Age I: 7.3-9.9 inches; Age II: 9.4-13.2 inches; Age III: up to 14.2 inches
- **Native Trout**: Age II: 7.5-10.5 inches; Age III: 10.5-12.2 inches; Age IV: 11.6-13.4 inches

**Management Implications**:
- Two-inch rainbow trout most cost-effective: $54.12 for 13.2% return
- Two-inch brook trout: $53.79 for 10.1% return
- Native fry: $102.00 for 1.3% return
- Evidence of periodic winter oxygen depletion (1.95 ppm March 1954) suggests potential winter kills affecting population dynamics
- Outlet stream census (June 25, 1953) recovered 4 rainbow (all marked) and 112 brook trout (3 marked) in 1/4 mile below lake, indicating unscreened outlet losses
- Natural brook trout reproduction occurs but insufficient to support fishery alone

**Recommendations**:
- Annual stocking of approximately 3,000 two- to three-inch [[Rainbow Trout]] and 3,000 [[Brook Trout]] to supplement natural population
- If brook trout unavailable, double rainbow plant to 6,000
- Stocking rate: 300 two-inch fish per average acreage for fluctuating reservoir lakes
- Plant as early in summer as feasible
- This stocking schedule applicable to similar "type" lakes on [[Grand Mesa]]
- Food supply adequate to support good growth and satisfactory creel returns despite limited species diversity
