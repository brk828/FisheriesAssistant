# Glucose Dehydrogenase in Teleosts: Tissue Distribution and Proposed Function

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1984 research article in *Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology* (Part B) by William J. Berg and Donald G. Buth reports a survey of [[Glucose Dehydrogenase]] (GDH; EC 1.1.1.47) across eight tissues of 21 marine teleost species from the California coast. Using [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] with a histochemical stain, the authors found GDH activity in liver extracts only, in every species tested. Activity varied considerably between orders but was consistent within families. The authors argue, on the basis of prior literature rather than direct tests, that in teleosts GDH probably supplies NADPH to the [[Mixed-Function Oxidase System]], and that its role in glucose oxidation is doubtful.

## Background & Description

Fish biochemistry has been comparatively neglected, and GDH had been studied mainly in mammals for about 50 years before its characterization in "lower" vertebrates. GDH catalyzes the direct oxidation of several substrates using either NAD or NADP as hydrogen acceptor. It has been described in all vertebrate classes, in echinoderms, and possibly in an insect. It is probably a dimer and is associated with the microsomal fraction. In mammals it occurs in nearly all tissues, but its distribution in lower vertebrates was poorly known.

**Study species and collection.** Specimens were obtained by bottom trawl, beach seine and tidepool collection along the California coast. The 21 species span 4 orders (Gadiformes, Atheriniformes, Perciformes, Pleuronectiformes) and 9 families. The Perciformes were the most numerous group, including [[Sciaenidae]], [[Embiotocidae]], [[Clinidae]] and [[Scorpaenidae]]. Specimens were frozen on solid CO₂ at sea, stored at −76 °C, and examined within 4 months of capture.

**Methods.** Skeletal muscle, heart, eye, brain, liver, kidney, gill and stomach were dissected from 2–10 specimens per species. Tissues were homogenized in 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 14,000 g. Supernatants were run on horizontal starch gels in phosphate-citrate buffer at 4 °C (5–6 h, 9 V/cm). Gel slices were incubated at 37 °C for 3–5 h in a nitroblue tetrazolium/phenazine methosulfate stain containing NAD, glucose and buffer. In a subset of cases, substrate concentrations (glucose at 1.00, 0.50, 0.25 and 0.10 M) and incubation times (90 or 180 min) were varied to test effects on staining intensity. Staining was scored subjectively on a five-level scale: no activity (—), faint (+), scorable (+), moderate (++) and strong (+++).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors place GDH within broader metabolic questions. In mammals, GDH is linked to drug metabolism in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, to galactose metabolism (proposed for some galactosemic patients, with equivocal evidence), and possibly to an alternative glucose phosphorylation pathway. Earlier work reported reduced GDH in freshwater fish tissues, and [[Stegeman]] and colleagues reported multiple genetic variants of GDH in [[Brook Trout]]. The authors suggest that this variability may relate to the tetraploid origin of salmonids.

Trophic ecology was also considered. [[Nagayama]] and colleagues reported that carnivorous fish had about twice the GDH activity of herbivores. The authors compared this with trophic data for the local fauna (Cailliet et al., 1978) and found the opposite pattern: the herbivorous atherinids had higher GDH activity than the carnivorous embiotocids. The authors concluded that [[Trophic Level]] does not predict GDH activity.

Stress responses were also relevant. Fish under stress show increased GDH activity and reduced plasma phenylalanine, which the authors interpret as support for a link between GDH and the [[Phenylalanine 4-Monooxygenase]] step of phenylalanine metabolism.

## Key Findings

- **Liver-restricted activity.** Detectable GDH activity was found only in liver extracts across all 21 species. No activity was detected in the other seven tissues, even at substrate concentrations several orders of magnitude above in vivo levels.
- **Single electromorph per species.** Every liver zymogram showed a single electromorph (see Fig. 1, *Atherinopsis californiensis*), suggesting that GDH is the product of a single gene in the species examined. No intraspecific variability was detected, though the authors note that the small sample sizes (2–10 specimens per species) may account for this.
- **Interordinal variation.** GDH expression differed considerably between orders. Within families, variation was low, which the authors suggest may reflect systematic relationships.
- **Trophic level not predictive.** Herbivorous atherinids (*Atherinops affinis*, *Atherinopsis californiensis*) showed higher relative GDH staining than carnivorous embiotocids (*Amphistichus argenteus*, *Phanerodon furcatus*). Activity in the embiotocids was faint or absent under several substrate and incubation conditions (Table 1).
- **Substrate dependence.** Appreciable staining required very high glucose concentrations, consistent with earlier descriptions of GDH as "sluggish" or "virtually inactive" at physiological substrate levels.
- **Thermal stability.** Samples tolerated repeated deep freezing and thawing without appreciable loss of staining intensity, and the staining results were repeatable and not attributable to differential denaturation.
- **Proposed function.** Based on literature review, the authors conclude that in teleosts GDH probably provides NADPH for at least some functions of the mixed-function oxidase system, a role they consider more plausible than a direct contribution to glucose oxidation. This is an inference from prior studies, not a result tested in this paper.
- **Scope and limitations.** The study is a qualitative histochemical survey. Staining intensity was scored subjectively, sample sizes were small, and the only quantitative comparison (interordinal variation) rests on relative staining scores rather than measured enzyme activity.
