# Keeper of the Cogs: Aldo Leopold and the Conservation of Biological Diversity

#conservation-biology #biodiversity #terrestrial-ecology

## Executive Summary

This *Defenders* magazine essay (Nov/Dec 1992) by Curt Meine assesses how far conservation has absorbed the ideas of [[Aldo Leopold]], especially his [[Land Ethic]] and his regard for [[Biological Diversity]]. The essay traces Leopold's intellectual path from forestry and [[Game Management]] to a land-health view of conservation, and argues that modern biodiversity science is only now catching up to what he proposed about 50 years earlier. The document contains no fish, aquatic species, or water-chemistry data; its aquatic references are limited to soils and waters as components of the land.

## Background & Description

- **Author:** [[Curt Meine]], author of *Aldo Leopold: His Life and Work* and a consultant to the National Research Council on conservation.
- **Publication:** Essay in *Defenders*, Nov/Dec 1992, drawing on the [[A Sand County Almanac]] legacy. Leopold died 45 years before publication.
- **Leopold's career arc:**
  - Began forestry in 1909; spent 35 years as a forester, field biologist and wildlife ecologist.
  - Worked with the [[Forest Service]] in Arizona and New Mexico, where he organized sportsmen into "game protective associations."
  - Left the Forest Service in 1928 for full-time game management work under a consortium of sporting arms and ammunition manufacturers.
  - Became the nation's first professor of game management at the University of Wisconsin in 1933, later professor of wildlife management.
  - Bought a worn-out bottomland farm in 1935, the setting for *A Sand County Almanac*.
- **Key writings:** *Game Management* (1933), "The Conservation Ethic," "Threatened Species" (1936), "Thinking Like a Mountain," and the posthumous *A Sand County Almanac*.
- **Terminology:** "Biological diversity" entered common use in the 1970s; "biodiversity" appeared in 1986 at a [[National Forum on BioDiversity]] cosponsored by the National Academy of Sciences and the Smithsonian Institution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Conceptual shift:** Before the 1980s, conservationists rarely treated biodiversity as an object of concern. It was "the medium in which conservation and biology took place." By the late 1980s, diversity loss was recognized as both a cause and effect of environmental decline.
- **Threats cited:** Habitat loss and fragmentation, desertification and deforestation, soil and water degradation in agriculture, exotic species invasions (especially on islands), pollution and overharvesting of aquatic systems, and global warming.
- **Predator and ecosystem balance:** The [[Kaibab Plateau]] case showed that predator eradication, hunting restrictions and vegetation change allowed [[Mule Deer]] to irrupt and then crash in the winters of 1924-25 and 1925-26. Leopold drew on this to argue that predators should not be exterminated over large areas.
- **German forests:** Leopold's 1935 three-month fellowship in Germany showed intensive timber and deer management had reduced plant and predator diversity. He wrote: "We Americans... have not yet experienced a bearless, wolfless, eagleless, catless woods."
- **Sierra Madre contrast:** Hunting trips in 1936 and 1937 to Mexico's Sierra Madre showed an intact biota with mountain lions and wolves, where deer were not overabundant. This shaped his concept of [[Land Health]].
- **Ecology as a field:** Leopold called ecology "a new fusion point for all the natural sciences," influenced by [[Charles Elton]]'s 1927 *Animal Ecology* and their 1931 meeting.

## Key Findings

1. **Leopold anticipated modern biodiversity thinking.** His 1940s line that "the complexity of the land" is "the outstanding discovery of the 20th century" and his principle "to keep every cog and wheel" now underpin biodiversity conservation.

2. **Diversity expanded from game to all living things.** Leopold's circle of concern moved from game species (1915-1919), to nongame and predators (1920s), to "rare plant associations" and "every living thing from bloodroots to Bell's vireo" (1933), and finally to soils and waters as part of "the biota."

3. **Habitat became the center of management.** The 1930s [[American Game Conference]] policy report, which Leopold chaired, stated: "The one and only thing we can do to raise a crop of game... is to make the environment more favorable."

4. **Wilderness and reference landscapes matter for science.** Leopold argued every region should retain "representative samples of its original wilderness condition." The [[Gila Wilderness]] (1924) was the first national designation he helped advance.

5. **Predators are a test of commitment to diversity.** Leopold's regret over extirpating predators in the Southwest drove his later advocacy for their protection.

6. **Land health as a unifying concept.** Conservation was defined as "a state of health in the land," where the land "consists of soil, water, plants and animals."

7. **Genetic and cultural value of diversity.** Leopold noted diversity as a source of future domesticated plants and animals, and as a cultural resource. The essay's list of organisms in *A Sand County Almanac*'s November entries (about 40 species, from [[Sugar Maple]] to prothonotary warbler) illustrates this cultural harvest.

8. **Conclusion:** Meine argues that the human community's future health depends on humility toward and protection of other life-forms, and that the [[Land Ethic]] remains the core of conservation progress.
