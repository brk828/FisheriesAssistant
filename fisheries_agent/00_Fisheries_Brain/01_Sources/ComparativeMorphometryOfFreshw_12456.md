# Comparative Morphometry of Freshwater Drum from Two Midwestern Localities

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Krumholz and Cavanah compares meristic and morphometric characteristics of [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) between two disparate populations: a lotic (riverine) population from the lower [[Ohio River]] and a lentic (lake) population from [[Lake Winnebago]], Wisconsin. Using 117 Ohio River specimens and 70 Lake Winnebago specimens, the authors found that while meristic counts (lateral line scales, dorsal fin rays) were remarkably stable and consistent between the two geographically separated populations, several morphometric proportions differed significantly — most notably body depth, which was consistently greater in the Lake Winnebago population. The study concludes that these differences are likely modificational (environmentally-induced) rather than genetic, underscoring the species' long-term morphological stability since early postglacial times.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Aplodinotus grunniens* Rafinesque, the [[Freshwater Drum]], is the only freshwater representative of the family [[Sciaenidae]] in North America.
- **Common names**: Sheepshead, white perch (regional).
- **Distinguishing features**: Silvery coloration, highly arched back, dorsal fin with both spines and soft rays, and pored lateral-line scales that continue onto the caudal fin.
- **Commercial importance**: Valued as a commercial species in large rivers/lakes of the northeastern U.S. and Canada; high average coefficient of condition (2.16, per Van Oosten 1938) contributes to its stout body form, despite relatively coarse flesh.
- **Natural range**: From Quebec/Ontario and the upper [[St. Lawrence River]] drainage, through the lower [[Great Lakes]], the [[Mississippi River]] system, and along the Gulf Coast to the Río Usumacinta basin in Guatemala.
- **Diet**: Contrary to expectations based on strong molariform teeth, detailed dietary studies (Forbes 1878, 1903; Dendy 1946; Daiber 1952; Moen 1955) show that young feed on aquatic insect larvae and small crustaceans, while adults consume a mix of insect larvae, fishes, crustaceans, and mollusks — not predominantly mollusks as commonly assumed.
- **Evolutionary stability**: Fossil evidence (Hubbs 1940) indicates the species has persisted in essentially its current form since early postglacial times.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Two contrasting habitats compared**:
  - **Lotic environment**: Lower [[Ohio River]] between Louisville, KY, and Cairo, IL — a flowing, continually mixed river system.
  - **Lentic environment**: [[Lake Winnebago]], a large, relatively shallow lake in east-central Wisconsin, presumed to have higher primary productivity and nutrient accumulation due to lack of continuous water turnover.
- **Hypothesized environmental drivers of morphological differences**:
  - Greater body depth and weight in Lake Winnebago fish attributed to a more abundant food supply and reduced physiological stress in the lentic environment, allowing nutrient accumulation within the lake basin.
  - Ohio River fish are more streamlined (shorter tails, shallower bodies), consistent with adaptation to a lotic, current-driven environment.
- **Comparative baseline**: Data compared against Van Oosten's (1938) and Edsall's (1967) findings on Lake Erie sheepshead, showing close agreement in length-weight relationships and standard/total length ratios between Ohio River and Lake Erie fish, but not with the deeper-bodied, longer-tailed Lake Winnebago fish.
- **Preservation effects**: Specimens preserved in 35% isopropyl alcohol exhibited notable dehydration-related weight loss (~4.2–4.3% net loss for whole samples) despite no statistically significant per-individual change, highlighting an important methodological consideration for museum specimen studies.

## Key Findings

### Meristic Stability (Between Populations)
- **Lateral line pored scales**: Highly consistent, ranging 48–53 in both series (Ohio River mean = 50.0; Lake Winnebago mean = 49.5). No significant difference between or within series.
- **Dorsal fin soft rays**: Ohio River range 25–31 (mean 28.8); Lake Winnebago range 28–33 (mean 30.4). No statistically significant differences.
- These counts align closely with historical records (Jordan & Evermann 1898; Forbes & Richardson 1920; Harlan & Speaker 1956).

### Morphometric Differences (Between Populations)
Statistically significant differences (Ohio River vs. Lake Winnebago) were found in:
1. **Standard length as % of total length** — Ohio River fish had consistently shorter tails (74.4–80.0%) than Lake Winnebago fish (74.5–78.0%), though within-series differences were not significant.
2. **Snout-to-anal fin origin distance** — Significant difference, mainly among smaller fish (77–178 mm); Wisconsin fish had proportionally shorter measurements.
3. **Snout-to-pelvic fin origin distance** — Significant between-series difference (Ohio River 24.4–26.0%; Winnebago 23.0–25.2%), no within-series difference.
4. **Snout-to-vent distance** — Significant between-series difference; Ohio River increased 45.3–49.8%, Winnebago 44.4–50.0%.
5. **Snout to vertical line at dorsal fin origin** — Significant between-series difference (Ohio River 25.6–27.6%; Winnebago 24.2–27.2%), no within-series pattern.
6. **Body depth at dorsal fin origin** — The **most pronounced difference**: significant both between and within series. Ohio River fish increased from 22.3% to 26.6% of total length; Lake Winnebago fish increased from 23.7% to 28.7%. Winnebago fish were consistently deeper-bodied at all lengths.

### No Significant Differences Found In:
- Snout-to-dorsal fin origin distance
- Snout-to-opercular margin distance (head length proxy)
- Depth of caudal peduncle
- Horizontal diameter of the bony orbit (decreased proportionally with length in both series, but no between-series difference)

### Interpretation
- All significant differences involving the long axis of the body (except snout-to-dorsal origin) pertain to the region between head and tail, suggesting proportional elongation is concentrated in the **vertebral column** rather than the skull.
- Ohio River fish consistently had **larger proportional values** in body-axis measurements, implying relatively **longer vertebral columns** and **shorter tails**, while Lake Winnebago fish had **relatively longer tails** — potentially to accommodate their greater body depth in a lentic setting.
- The relative "separation" between the anal fin origin and vent with increasing fish length is attributed to allometric growth of the vertebral column, not migration of the vent.
- Preservation-related shrinkage (isopropyl alcohol) was analyzed and ruled out as a confound; length changes were statistically insignificant despite some weight loss.

### Broader Significance
- Despite morphometric plasticity attributable to environmental (modificational) effects, the **meristic stability** across two geographically and ecologically distinct populations supports the conclusion that *Aplodinotus grunniens* has remained taxonomically and morphologically stable since early postglacial times (cf. [[Hubbs 1940]]).
- Findings align with broader ichthyological literature (e.g., [[Coregonidae]] studies by Hile 1937; Svärdson 1949, 1950) showing that body proportion variation in fishes is often environmentally driven rather than indicative of speciation or racial divergence.
