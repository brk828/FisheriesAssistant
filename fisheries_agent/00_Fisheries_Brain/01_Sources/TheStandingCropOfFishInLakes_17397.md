# Standing Crop of Fish in Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-standing-crop

## Executive Summary

This review, by Kenneth D. Carlander and W. L. Minckley (Iowa State College), reanalyzes published estimates of [[Standing Crop]] (fish biomass per acre) from lakes, ponds, reservoirs and river backwaters. Using mainly regression methods, the authors test how lake area, maximum depth, carbonate content (measured as [[Methyl Orange Alkalinity]]) and species number relate to standing crop. They also examine [[Interspecific Competition]] and [[Habitat Segregation]] among species. The authors conclude that area shows no consistent relationship with standing crop, that depth and alkalinity show trends, and that the number of species and trophic level influence crop in ways that depend on context.

## Background & Description

The paper summarizes standing crop estimates obtained by four methods: [[Draining Method]], [[Rotenone Poisoning]] (or copper sulphate), [[Mark and Recovery Estimation]], and intensive netting. The authors note that standing crop does not necessarily match fish production, but it is usually the only available estimate of production. Because annual turnover probably varies less among populations than standing crop does, standing crop was treated as a fairly good proxy.

Earlier work by [[Rounsefell]] had reported a negative relationship between standing crop per acre and lake area. Carlander and Minckley re-examined the underlying data. They found that the relationship was not clearly defined. Four of Rounsefell's five regression groups had positive slopes, and the authors suggested that yield differences may reflect fishing effort per acre rather than production.

Most available estimates came from small ponds stocked with few species, or from lakes with undesirable populations. The authors therefore caution that the data are probably not typical of lakes in general.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Standing crop by water body type (pounds per acre, as reported):**
- River backwaters and oxbows: almost 500
- Midwestern reservoirs: almost 400
- Other reservoirs and ponds: 200 to 300
- Warm-water lakes: 125 to 150
- [[Trout Lakes]]: up to about 50

**Method comparisons:** Draining and poisoning estimates for midwestern reservoirs did not differ significantly (F = 0.2, Table III). Marking and recovery estimates for midwestern warm-water lakes also did not differ from poisoning estimates (F = 0.1, 1 and 20 df). Marking and recovery estimates usually cover only fish over about 5 to 6 inches, whereas draining and poisoning may include smaller fish. Rotenoning coves of large reservoirs gave lower crops than draining or poisoning smaller reservoirs, although the authors could not rule out non-sampling causes.

**Trophic structure:** Species were grouped by food chain length. Species with short chains (gizzard shad, buffalo, carp, suckers) had high standing crops. Small insectivores ([[Bluegill]], other sunfishes, crappies) had intermediate crops. Secondary predators ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Walleye]], [[Northern Pike]]) had low crops. [[Trout]] could fall in either the insectivore or predator group depending on the lake.

**Habitat and temporal segregation:** In [[Norris Reservoir]], Tennessee, [[Dendy]] reported depth stratification linked to temperature. Largemouth bass were mostly in water above 80°F, spotted bass at 70 to 78°F, walleye at 70 to 76°F, and sauger at 60 to 70°F. In Lake of the Woods and Clear Lake, Iowa, walleye foraged mainly at night while northern pike foraged in daytime. [[Svardson]] reported that whitefish species (Coregonus spp.) coexist through different spawning seasons or sites.

## Key Findings

**1. Area.** No significant correlation was found between lake or pond area and standing crop per acre. Regressions were negative in 11 of 17 cases and positive in 6, and only 2 were significant at the 95% level. The midwestern reservoir series showed a significant positive regression, whereas a series of Alabama ponds (1.3 to 29.0 acres) showed a significant negative one.

**2. Maximum depth.**
- Trout lakes: b = -0.5662 (t = 1.89; 2.09 needed for significance at 95%).
- Warm-water lakes: b = -0.4333, significant at 95%.
- Reservoirs: b = +0.0816 with a 20-foot gravel pit containing high buffalo fish numbers; b = -0.1311 without it, not significant.
- Depth explained about 16% of the variation in trout and warm-water lake crops and about 5% in reservoirs.

**3. Carbonate content (methyl orange alkalinity).** Standing crop increased significantly with alkalinity in trout lakes (r = 0.52, about 28% explained), warm-water lakes (r = 0.64, about 41%), and reservoirs (r = 0.83, about 69%).

**4. Trophic level.** Standing crops followed an Eltonian pyramid of numbers. Short-chain species generally had higher crops than predatory species.

**5. Species number.** In midwestern reservoirs, log standing crop regressed on log species number gave P = 2.302 + 0.3243N (n = 38 reservoirs; significant at the 99% level; standard error of b = 0.1187). In pond studies, the pattern was less clear. Species A's average crop was consistently lower in ponds with more than two species than in ponds with only two. In most cases it was also lower in two-species ponds than in one-species ponds. Maximum production of a species often occurred where no other species occupied the same food level.

**6. Competition.** The authors stress that Gause's principle and differences in growth curves (Odum's criterion) are needed to demonstrate competition, and regressions alone cannot prove it. Within [[Midwest Reservoirs]]:
- Largemouth bass: a significant negative regression with [[Carp]] (b = -0.4569, 99% level) and a positive one with sunfishes (b = 0.4928, 99% level). Bass crops were lower with [[Gizzard Shad]] and rough fish.
- Bluegill: crops decreased with green sunfish, bullheads (significant at 95%), and carp.
- Crappies: white and black crappie did better separately than together.
- Carp and buffalo: possible competition.

**7. Summary conclusions (as stated by the authors):**
- No correlation between area and standing crop per acre.
- Negative regression of standing crop on maximum depth.
- Positive relation between carbonate content and standing crop.
- Greater crops for short-food-chain species than for predators.
- Crop rises with species number, but selected species reach maximum crops in lakes or ponds with no more than one other species.
- Some species show lower crops with certain companions (competition) and higher crops with others (protocooperation).

**8. Limits.** The authors state that knowledge of how habitat segregation affects fish production is "extremely limited," and that the number of available niches is generally unknown. They also note that the largemouth bass data could only be analyzed within lake types, because bass populations differed among them.
