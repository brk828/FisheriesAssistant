# Fishery Investigation of the Colorado River from Glen Canyon Dam to the Confluence of the Paria River: Assessment of the Impact of Fluctuating Flows on the Lee's Ferry Fishery

#ichthyology #salmonidae #tailwater-fishery #flow-management #habitat-assessment

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive study (1980-1981, with creel data through 1984) assessed the biological impacts of fluctuating water releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the [[Lee's Ferry]] tailwater trout fishery in Arizona. The 26 km reach below the dam developed into a premier cold-water fishery following dam closure in 1963 and introduction of [[Gammarus lacustris]] (freshwater shrimp) in 1968. The fishery is dominated by hatchery-stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), with lesser numbers of [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii*). Proposed peaking power operations threatened to increase flow fluctuations, potentially impacting spawning success, food base (periphyton and invertebrates), and fish survival through stranding and habitat loss. The study evaluated effects of flow regimes on year-class strength, examined stocking procedures, and assessed sport fishery harvest. Key findings indicate that year-class strength was inversely correlated with number of low-flow days and high daily flow ranges, while the fishery remained heavily dependent on hatchery stocking. Yield increased four-fold from 1977 to 1983 (31 to 120 kg/ha) before declining sharply in 1984, suggesting potential overfishing.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963 on the [[Colorado River]], creating a deep hypolimnetic release reservoir (Lake Powell) that transformed the river from a warm, turbid, highly variable system to a cold, clear, flow-regulated tailwater. Pre-dam flows ranged from 1–28°C with unimodal seasonal peaks in May-June; post-dam temperatures stabilized at 8–14°C (mean ~9°C) year-round. Daily discharge now fluctuates in response to peaking power demand, with highest flows during daylight hours and lowest from midnight to 8:00 AM. Typical patterns include peaking discharges, short-term low flows (weekends, holidays, maintenance), and extended high or low flows (e.g., March 1979 saw 12 consecutive days below 2,000 cfs; July-August 1980 exceeded 25,000 cfs when Lake Powell reached capacity).

The [[Lee's Ferry]] fishery extends 26 km from the dam to the Grand Canyon National Park boundary, accessible by motorized watercraft. The Arizona Game and Fish Department began stocking catchable-sized rainbow trout in 1963, transitioning to fingerling-only stockings in 1976. The fishery has produced trophy rainbow trout exceeding 8.6 kg and a state record 2.3 kg brook trout (1980). Clear water releases increased sediment transport capacity, removing ~9.87 × 10⁶ m³ of bottom sediment by 1975 and creating channel armoring with coarse substrate. Secchi disc readings were clear to bottom (3–5 m) throughout the study. Filamentous algae (*Cladophora glomerata*) established extensive beds extending 98 km downstream to the [[Little Colorado River]], providing critical habitat for invertebrates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The aquatic ecosystem underwent dramatic post-impoundment changes. The benthic invertebrate community developed a low-diversity, high-density pattern typical of isothermal tailwaters with fluctuating flows. Mini-ponar dredge samples contained primarily midges (Chironomidae larvae, 64%) and *Gammarus* (32%), while grab samples of *Cladophora* contained 90% *Gammarus*. Invertebrate densities ranged from 0.4 to 1.8 *Gammarus* per gram wet weight of *Cladophora*. The 1968 introduction of *Gammarus lacustris* and other invertebrates (crayfish, snails, mayflies, caddisflies) was critical; only *Gammarus* and midges became established as important trout food.

Six fish species were collected above Lee's Ferry: [[Rainbow Trout]] (56.8%), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*, 27.5%), [[Brook Trout]] (7.9%), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*, 3.5%), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*, 3.2%), and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (1.1%). Eleven Grand Canyon tributaries (including [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Clear Creek]], [[Crystal Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]]) served as spawning areas for rainbow trout. Stomach contents of rainbow trout showed *Gammarus* (mean 26.9 per stomach, 69% frequency) and chironomid pupae (93.1 per stomach, 61% frequency) as dominant food items. *Cladophora* occurred in 55% of stomachs, though its nutritional value remains unclear—trout may derive benefit from associated diatoms and invertebrates.

Aquatic vegetation distribution was strongly influenced by flow fluctuation zones. *Cladophora* was most abundant at the lower edge of the fluctuation zone; laboratory experiments indicated 12 hours of dewatering would kill *Cladophora*. [[Poolmat]] (*Potamogeton* sp.) grew in complex with *Cladophora* along the extreme lower edge, suggesting lower tolerance to dewatering. Emergent vegetation included sedges (Cyperaceae), with isolated stands of bullrush (*Scirpus*) and cattail (*Typha*).

## Key Findings

**Population Dynamics and Stocking:**
- Exploitation rate increased from 0.26 (1963-1968) to 0.38 (1980-1981), indicating higher fishing pressure.
- Estimated standing stock was 42,320 kg (102 kg/ha) in 1980-81, substantially higher than 22–33 kg/ha in 1965-68.
- Harvest of age-0 fish was significantly correlated with number stocked (r² = 0.7212, p < 0.05), demonstrating fishery dependence on hatchery production.
- Average yield was 444 fish per 1,000 recruits; to sustain harvest rate of 0.15 fish/hour with 350,000 angler hours requires stocking minimum 111,000 fingerlings.
- Hatchery fish released at Nine-mile bar showed higher return rates than other locations; fish released upstream were generally recaptured upstream, while field-marked fish moved downstream.

**Growth and Condition:**
- Rainbow trout growth was excellent compared to other waters: Age I = 378 mm, Age II = 471 mm, Age III = 564 mm (vs. western streams: 160, 254, 343 mm).
- Mean condition factor (K) was 1.41 for rainbow trout at Lee's Ferry vs. 0.81 for Grand Canyon fish.
- Brook trout and cutthroat trout also showed good growth rates.

**Creel Survey Results (1980-1981):**
- Total angler hours: 121,394 (National Park Service estimate) or 112,413 (AGFD estimate).
- Harvest: 11,336 rainbow trout (NPS) or 10,284 (AGFD); 1,019 brook trout; 699 cutthroat trout.
- Mean catch rate: 0.147 fish/hour (6.8 hours per fish); harvest rate: 0.107 fish/hour (9.3 hours per fish).
- Success rate: 46% of anglers caught at least one trout; average fish creeled was 466 mm.
- No significant correlation found between catch rate and flow variables (mean monthly flow, flow range, coefficient of variation) within normal operating ranges.

**Temporal Trends (1977-1984):**
- Angler pressure increased from 174 to 864 hours/ha (1977-1983).
- Yield increased from 31 to 120 kg/ha (1977-1983), then declined sharply to 31.3 kg/ha in 1984.
- Harvest decreased substantially in 1984 (0.08 fish/hr, 52.7 fish/ha) following low stocking in 1979-1980 and high harvest in 1982-1983.
- Mean length decreased from 431 mm (1983) to 370 mm (1984), suggesting overfishing.

**Flow Effects on Year-Class Strength:**
- Spearman rank correlations showed year-class strength inversely related to: days with flow < 3,000 cfs (r = -0.7568), days < 8,000 cfs (r = -0.6847), and days with daily range > 15,000 cfs (r = -0.7388).
- Lack of stocking in 1979 was evident in both 1979 and 1980 harvests (few age-0 in 1979, few age-I in 1980).

**Habitat Assessment (IFIM Analysis):**
- Maximum weighted usable area (WUA) for adult trout occurred at 7,000–10,000 cfs.
- Juvenile trout habitat peaked at 4,000–6,000 cfs; fry habitat peaked sharply at 4,000 cfs.
- Approximately 70% of periphyton habitat loss occurred below 10,000 cfs as cobble bars and benches dewatered.
- Low bar contours composed > 50% of estimated fry habitat at 5,000 cfs and > 50% of fingerling/subadult habitat at 5,000–30,000 cfs.
- Fluctuating flows required fry to move 100–600 ft twice daily to remain in suitable habitat—movements likely too great for post-emergent fry, causing stranding mortality.

**Spawning and Recruitment:**
- Rainbow trout spawned primarily late fall through winter; ripe males September–March, spent females October–April.
- Eighteen cobble bars used for spawning, all within the water fluctuation zone.
- Few fry observed during study; substrate appeared larger than optimal (76–126 mm vs. preferred 13–38 mm).
- Stranding mortalities documented: 23 dead adult trout found in January 1980 backwater pool; additional strandings observed October 1984.
- Natural recruitment appears limited by poor spawning substrate and flow fluctuations, though eleven Grand Canyon tributaries support spawning.

**Management Implications:**
- Theoretical bag limit of 1 fish would reduce harvest by 42.5%; 2-fish limit would reduce harvest by 14.8%.
- Minimum 14-inch length limit would reduce harvest by ~27%.
- Slot limit (15–20 inches protected) would increase catch of fish > 20 inches by 60% while decreasing total harvest by only 14%.
- Artificial-lure regulations recommended to reduce hooking mortality: bait causes 30% mortality vs. 5–10% for lures. In 1983, total fishing mortality (including hooking mortality) was estimated at 66,687 fish vs. 53,780 harvested.
- Comparison with other tailwaters: Lee's Ferry angling pressure (292 hours/ha) similar to Flaming Gorge (254) and Navajo (262), but catch rate (0.11) was ~25% of those fisheries (0.46, 0.50). However, yield (38.8 kg/ha) was comparable due to larger average fish size.

**Recommendations:**
1. Stock minimum 110,000 fingerling rainbow trout annually to maintain catch rates of 0.15 fish/hour.
2. Stock fingerlings March–July to coincide with optimal flow management periods.
3. Implement artificial-lure only regulations to reduce hooking mortality.
4. Consider 15–20 inch slot limit if fish size does not improve with increased stocking.
5. Maintain flows above 8,000 cfs March–July to protect fingerling habitat.
6. Reduce daily flow fluctuations during March–July: minimum 8,000 cfs, maximum 20,000 cfs.
7. Ramp flows gradually over several hours to allow fish to avoid stranding.
8. Mark stocked trout and monitor creel returns for population estimates and growth rates.
9. Continue periodic stockings of brook trout and cutthroat trout for species diversity.
10. Evaluate growth and survival of various trout strains to optimize stocking programs.
