# Palm Lake Native Fish Data Memorandum (February 1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department memorandum (dated February 9, 1994) summarizes the management history of imperiled native big-river fishes at [[Palm Lake]] from 1989 to early 1994. It records stockings from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], removals and relocations to hatcheries and research ponds, and a set of recent collections. The core species are the [[Bonytail]], the [[Razorback Sucker]], and the [[Colorado Squawfish]], which were managed alongside the non-native-status-unclear [[Yellow Bullhead]]. The memo is largely a data record, with a detailed table of 2 to 26 individual bonytail captures.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Internal memorandum with a distribution list, prepared for Arizona Game and Fish Department staff.
- **Subject:** Palm Lake native fish data, covering stocking, removal, and recent sampling records.
- **Species covered:**
  - [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*, abbreviated GIEL in the source): the main focus, with PIT (passive integrated transponder) tags applied to many individuals.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, XYTE).
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, PTLU).
  - [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*, AMNA), handled incidentally.
- **Management tools:** [[Hatchery Stocking]], [[PIT Tagging]], salvage and relocation, and [[Recapture]] monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Palm Lake** served as a stocking and monitoring site for endangered big-river fishes.
- **Lake Havasu** is named as the origin or location for most bonytail in the 1994 collection table.
- **Holding and rearing facilities** included [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Page Springs Hatchery]], the [[Hassayampa River Preserve]] visitor center aquarium, and [[Arizona State University Research Ponds]].
- **Relocation sites:** [[Horsethief Refugia Ponds]] near Grand Junction, Colorado, received bonytail removed from Palm Lake.
- **Partner agency:** the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) carried out tagging, relocation, and sampling in November 1992.

## Key Findings

**1. Stocking history**
- **April 20, 1989:** 688 bonytail (about 203 mm total length) and 35 razorback suckers (about 250 mm TL) were stocked from Dexter National Fish Hatchery.
- **February 3, 1990:** 1,136 Colorado squawfish (about 101.6 mm TL) were stocked from the same hatchery.
- **April 23, 1991:** an additional 250 PIT-tagged bonytail were stocked.

**2. Removals and relocations**
- **April 24, 1992:** 10 bonytail were salvaged and preserved in formalin. A further 22 bonytail were caught and removed. Two went to the Hassayampa River Preserve aquarium, and 20 went to Page Springs Hatchery. Two of those 20 died.
- **July 24, 1992:** the remaining bonytail at Page Springs were moved to ASU research ponds. Sixteen of these fish were PIT-tagged during the procedure. Two had been tagged previously and were assumed to be F1 bonytail (hatchery-reared offspring of stocked fish).
- **November 19–20, 1992:** USFWS PIT-tagged and removed 13 bonytail to the Horsethief refugia ponds near Grand Junction. The same effort caught and released 12 Colorado squawfish and 125 yellow bullheads back into Palm Lake.

**3. Recent collections (January 31, 1993 to February 3, 1994)**
- Sampling produced bonytail (including four F1 recaptures), two razorback suckers, two Colorado squawfish, and approximately 7 yellow bullheads. The bonytail count in the summary text is partly illegible in the scan, but the detailed table lists **26 bonytail**.
- **Bonytail measurements (table):**
  - Total length: 342–424 mm.
  - Weight: 270–605 g (one fish's weight was not recorded).
  - Most fish were from Lake Havasu. The four F1 recaptures were held at the ASU research ponds.
- One bonytail had a fungal lesion on its right side.

**4. Management implications**
- Repeated stocking and removal, combined with PIT tagging, allowed individual fish and hatchery-origin (F1) recaptures to be tracked over time.
- Movement among Palm Lake, hatcheries, research ponds, and refugia reflects an active, multi-site recovery strategy for these endangered native species.
