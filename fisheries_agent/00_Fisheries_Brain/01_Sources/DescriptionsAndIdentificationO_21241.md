# Descriptions and Identification of Razorback, Flannelmouth, White, Utah, Bluehead, and Mountain Sucker Larvae and Early Juveniles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife Technical Publication (No. 38, June 1990), written by Darrel E. Snyder and Robert T. Muth of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University, provides illustrated descriptions, identification keys, and diagnostic criteria for the larval and early-juvenile stages of six catostomid suckers of the Upper Colorado River System. The covered species are the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*C. discobolus*), [[Mountain Sucker]] (*C. platyrhynchus*), [[White Sucker]] (*C. commersoni*), and [[Utah Sucker]] (*C. ardens*). The longnose sucker (*C. catostomus*) is the only Upper Colorado River System catostomid not covered. The manual's stated aim is to remedy the difficulty of identifying early life stages, which has hampered data collection on rare species, particularly the razorback sucker.

## Background & Description

The manual consolidates and extends earlier descriptions and preliminary keys (Snyder 1981, 1983a; Snyder and Muth 1988). Its character set combines morphology, morphometrics, meristics, pigmentation, developmental state, and skeletal characters (cleared-and-stained preparations). Development is described with a [[Combined Developmental Interval Terminology]] that integrates the protolarva/mesolarva/metalarva system of Snyder with the preflexion/flexion/postflexion terms of Ahlstrom et al. and the yolk-sac larva/larva/prejuvenile terms of Hardy et al.

The species fall into two subgenera. [[Subgenus Pantosteus]] ("mountain suckers") includes bluehead and mountain suckers. [[Subgenus Catostomus]] ("valley suckers") includes flannelmouth, white, and Utah suckers. Razorback sucker is the monotypic genus Xyrauchen. The text notes that all Catostomus species here belong to the subfamily Catostominae, tribe Catostomini.

Reproductive classification follows Balon's guilds: all six species are non-guarding, open-substrate lithophils. Razorback sucker nevertheless forms discrete redds in reservoirs (Bozek et al. 1984), suggesting a possible tendency toward a brood-hiding guild.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution in the Upper Colorado River System:** Razorback, flannelmouth, bluehead, and mountain suckers are native. Flannelmouth and bluehead suckers are common to abundant in mainstem rivers. White sucker is less abundant in the Colorado, Gunnison, Yampa, and upper Green rivers. Mountain sucker is largely restricted to headwater tributaries, with rare mainstem records. Utah sucker is largely restricted to headwater tributaries west of the Green River, particularly the Duchesne River. Longnose sucker occurs only above Flaming Gorge Reservoir, in the Blue Mesa area of the Gunnison River, and in the Lake Granby area.
- **Status:** The razorback sucker is endangered in Colorado, protected by Colorado and Utah, and a federal candidate for listing under the Endangered Species Act.
- **Hybridization:** Hybridization among Colorado River System catostomids is well documented. Intermediate characters were recorded in white × bluehead hybrids as small as 25 mm SL and flannelmouth × bluehead hybrids as small as 34 mm SL (Hubbs et al. 1943; Hubbs and Hubbs 1947). Some inconclusive larvae may be hybrids.
- **Larval habitat:** Recently hatched larvae are photophobic and usually hide in the substrate for at least a few days before drifting with the current.
- **Specimen sources:** Cultured series were reared at the Larval Fish Laboratory (1978–1981), at Dexter National Fish Hatchery (razorback, 1982, Lake Mohave stock), and by the Utah Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit (Utah sucker, 1987, Bear Lake stock). Field-collected material came from the Yampa, White, Gunnison, Colorado, Provo, Spanish Fork, and Truckee rivers, plus Lake Mohave and Montana tributaries.
- **Sample size:** 445 specimens were analyzed in detail for morphometrics and meristics, with hundreds more documented for size, developmental state, skeletal characters, and pigmentation.

## Key Findings

**Overall similarity and identification approach**
- Larvae of the seven Upper Colorado River System suckers are broadly similar. Identification relies on size at developmental events, gut form, melanophore patterns, osteology, and, to a limited extent, morphometrics and meristics (especially dorsal fin ray counts for metalarvae and juveniles).
- Razorback sucker is most similar to bluehead sucker as protolarvae and flexion mesolarvae, and to flannelmouth sucker as metalarvae and early juveniles.

**Razorback sucker**
- Early yolk absorption, early scattering of dorsal pigmentation, few or no melanophores on the ventral midline (never more than 6 observed), and generally lighter body pigmentation.
- Later larvae and juveniles have 14 or more dorsal principal fin rays (typical 14–15), a long dorsal fin base, a large fan-shaped first interneural bone, and a large oval frontoparietal fontanelle that stays wide.
- Eggs are 2.5–2.8 mm (Table 1), smaller than flannelmouth eggs. Yolk is completed by about 11–12 mm SL.

**Flannelmouth sucker**
- Largest eggs (3.8–3.9 mm), large size at hatching, and delayed development and gut folding. Yolk is not fully absorbed until about 15 mm SL.
- Limited midventral pigmentation, a distinctive "tractor-tread" pattern on the dorsum of some mesolarvae, and very fine scales (full lateral series not before 39 mm SL).

**Bluehead and mountain suckers (subgenus Pantosteus)**
- Early scattering of dorsal pigmentation, early gut folding, dark peritoneum, a generally smaller dorsal fin, a small blocky first interneural bone, a narrow fontanelle, and a distinctive mouth in early juveniles.
- Midventral pigment line is often complete in mountain sucker larvae and highly variable in bluehead larvae.
- Mountain sucker has the lowest typical principal dorsal fin ray count (10).

**White sucker**
- A complete midventral melanophore line (over 20 melanophores) from heart or branchial region to vent is typical.
- Large scales, often outlined in pigment, and three large lateral spots characterize larger young-of-the-year juveniles.

**Utah sucker**
- Usually larger eyes relative to head length than the other species. Resembles white sucker, but larvae have much less midventral pigment, sometimes none, and juveniles have only two, if any, prominent lateral spots.
- Full lateral scale series by about 24 mm SL, consistent with large scales.

**Developmental size and timing**
- Flannelmouth sucker larvae are much larger at each developmental stage. Other species are nearly equal in size by the metalarval phase.
- White and Utah suckers complete fin development and become juveniles at the smallest sizes (19–20 mm SL). Some razorback sucker transform at 22–23 and 23–24 mm SL, close to flannelmouth sucker.

**Meristics (Table 2 summary)**
- Lateral line scales: white and Utah suckers usually have fewer than 75 rows; bluehead and flannelmouth usually over 85; mountain and razorback typically 75–85.
- Principal dorsal fin rays: razorback highest (14–15 typical); mountain lowest (10); other species 11–13.
- Total myomeres: about 43–49 in all species. Myomeres to the vent are typically 37 or more for bluehead, flannelmouth, and razorback; 37 or fewer for Utah; below 37 for mountain; 35–38 for white.

**Pigmentation and mouth characters**
- Peritoneal pigment is dark in bluehead and mountain sucker by late postflexion stages; uniform pigmentation was rare in other species.
- Oblique rows of three or more preopercular melanophores are typical for white and mountain suckers.
- Mouth becomes subterminal by about 19 mm SL in most metalarvae. Bluehead and mountain suckers have notches at the mouth corners, whereas other species do not.

**Osteology (cleared-and-stained specimens)**
- The first interneural bone separates razorback sucker (large fan-shaped), subgenus Catostomus (moderate to large anvil-shaped), and subgenus Pantosteus (smaller, blocky).
- Anterior-dorsal maxillary projections are very shallow in razorback, long and pointed in Pantosteus, and blunt in subgenus Catostomus.
- Mandible position is diagnostic for Pantosteus (anterior ends near the posterior ends of the maxillae).
- Frontoparietal fontanelle narrows with growth. Adult descriptions indicate closure or reduction in bluehead and mountain suckers. In one 76–81 mm yearling per species, mountain sucker's fontanelle was closed, and razorback's remained wide (width/length about 45%).

**Methods and tools**
- Measurements were taken to 0.1 mm and expressed as percent standard length. Standard length switches from notochord length to hypural length after flexion.
- Keys are built from the [[DELTA System]] and begin with developmental interval and size. Supplemental skeletal keys are provided for postflexion mesolarvae, metalarvae, and juveniles.
- Clearing and staining uses [[Alcian Blue]] (cartilage) and [[Alizarin Red]] (bone), with enzyme (trypsin) or potassium hydroxide clearing.

**Recommendations and cautions**
- Identifications that cannot be resolved should be marked tentative (with "?") or left at the family or genus level.
- Dilute buffered formalin (3–5%) is recommended over alcohol to limit melanin fading and shrinkage.
- Pigment in cultured larvae can differ from field-collected material, so comparisons must account for origin.
- Positively identified larvae can revise assumptions about spawning seasons, larval drift, nursery habitat, and species distribution.
