# Extinction of Most Sandia and Potosí Valleys Endemic Pupfishes, Crayfishes and Snails

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

The authors report the extinction or near-extinction of several recently described or undescribed endemic pupfishes, crayfishes and snails from isolated springs in the arid southwest of [[Nuevo León]], Mexico. Three fish species and their associated fauna disappeared within two years to about a decade of discovery, and the Potosí populations are almost extinct. The main driver is groundwater depletion from agricultural pumping, combined with industrial and urban overexploitation. The authors argue that federal legislation has been poorly enforced and that the region needs regional, basin-based management of geohydrological systems.

## Background & Description

The arid lands of southwestern Nuevo León were thought to lack aquatic fauna until a cambarellid crayfish and a pupfish were collected at the large isolated spring of [[Ojo de Potosí]] in 1948. A dwarf pupfish, later named [[Megupsilon Aporus]], was collected there in 1961. Surveys from 1984 onward (after aerial recognition of a pluvial lake area) found several new endemic pupfishes, each paired with an endemic crayfish, and later a number of new valvatid snails. Several taxa remained undescribed at the time of the study.

Spring-by-spring records:

- **[[Charco La Palma]]**: *Cyprinodon longidorsalis* (described 1993). Population of about 50 to 70 individuals, mostly half-grown or smaller. The spring was discovered in 1984 and was extinct by 1994, after surviving about 10 years.
- **[[Ojo La Trinidad]]**: *C. inmemoriam* (described 1993) and an undescribed crayfish. Known from a single mature male, plus two female crayfish. The spring was dry by 1986, less than two years after discovery.
- **[[Ojo de Agua Charco Azul]]**: *C. aeronicae* (described 1993), an undescribed crayfish, and the valvatid snails *Valvata beltrani* and *Valvata* sp. n. The spring held an estimated 10,000 to 12,000 fish and a similar number of crayfish at first visit. The snails were already extinct when first found. The pupfish was still present in 1994 and is the only local population to survive for some years after discovery.
- **[[Ojo de Agua La Presa]]**: *C. ceciliae* (described 1993) and an undescribed crayfish. Discovered in 1988 and extinct by 1990.
- **[[Ojo de Potosí]]**: *Megupsilon aporus*, *Cyprinodon alvarezi* and *Cambarellus alvarai*. Populations were decimated after the introduction of predatory fishes, and the species were almost extinct by 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat change.** Springs were drying through falling water tables. At Charco La Palma the water dropped 10 cm by 1985 and another 10 cm by 1989. At Ojo La Trinidad the original 10,000 m² spring shrank to about 5,000 m² and then dried. At [[Ojo de Agua La Presa]] the water level fell more than 1.5 m by June 1990. At Charco Azul the water depth fell from several metres in the 1980s to 0.3 m in October 1993 and 0.2 m in November 1994. At Potosí the pool surface area was reduced by about 90% after a drop of nearly 1.5 m in 1984.

**Biological threats.** At Potosí, [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) were introduced before 1961, and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) were recorded in 1974. Both were associated with the decline of the endemic fishes.

**Regional context.** The authors describe northern Mexican waters as broadly overexploited:

- Aquifers were overexploited in 35 of 36 cases and saline in 12 of 36.
- Aquifers were being depleted in 33 of 35 areas by an average of 1 m per year (1981 data, the most recent available).
- Surface water quality was poor in 24 of 37 regions.
- 115 of the 135 endangered or threatened Mexican fishes are from arid and semiarid zones.
- Fifteen North Mexican fish species were documented as extinct by 1994, with the Sandia and Potosí losses adding to earlier extinctions.

**Protected areas.** [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] nearby hosts much of the regional endemism and was classified as protected only in 1993, after 28 years of requests. The authors fear a similar fate under continuing drought and water extraction.

**Legal framework.** The 1988 [[General Environmental Law of Mexico]] did not protect the undescribed Sandia pupfishes or regulate water use. The 1993 official listing covered only the Potosí species. The June 1994 [[Mexican Official Norms]] recognized the Potosí endemics but not the Sandia species. The authors state that these norms were poorly publicized and enforced.

## Key Findings

- Three endemic fish species (*C. inmemoriam*, *C. ceciliae*, *C. longidorsalis*) are extinct and three more (*C. aeronicae*, *M. aporus*, *C. alvarezi*) are under serious threat or nearly extinct. Several associated crayfish and snail populations are also lost or declining.
- The extinctions were rapid. Several species disappeared less than two years after discovery, and the Charco Azul pupfish survived about a decade.
- Agricultural, industrial and urban groundwater pumping is the main cause. The authors describe the pumping as irrational, unsustainable and illegal.
- Bass introduction and goldfish presence contributed to the decline at Potosí. Attempted bass eradication in 1976 removed 286 individuals and was halted for lack of success. Relocation of *M. aporus* to side springs in 1974 was followed by a population increase in the creek by 1980.
- In situ conservation efforts were unsuccessful. Existing laws were poorly enforced and did not cover all species.
- The authors recommend regional management using geohydrological basins as the management unit. They also propose that springs and their aquatic biota be maintained as monitoring systems for environmental quality and water sustainability, with greater involvement of scientists and stakeholders.
