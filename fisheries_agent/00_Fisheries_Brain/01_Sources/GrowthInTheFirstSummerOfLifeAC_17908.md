# Growth in the First Summer of Life: A Comparison of Nine Co-Occurring Fish Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This study compares first-summer growth in nine coexisting freshwater fish species in [[Lake Opinicon]], a 2200-ha eutrophic lake in Leeds County, Ontario, sampled in 1977. The species differ in adult size, age at first breeding, egg size, spawning date and spawning duration. Growth patterns were broadly similar: length increased linearly early in the summer and plateaued by early September, and length–weight relationships were isometric in all cases. Spawning date was the strongest influence on growth, with early spawners reaching greater length and weight. Adult size did not directly determine growth, but it did determine spawning duration, which in turn governed the size variation of young. Diet type showed no clear relationship with growth.

## Background & Description

The authors note that rapid growth in the first months of life is critical to survival and sexual maturity in temperate freshwater fishes. Individuals that fail to reach a minimum size in their first summer may not survive [[Winter Mortality]] at higher latitudes. Body size is also linked to fecundity, survival and dominance hierarchies, so selection should favor effective juvenile growth.

Growth may be constrained by taxonomic group, temperature optima, metabolic efficiency and life history traits. Late or protracted spawners have less time to grow before winter, and species maturing at large adult size must either grow faster or delay spawning. Comparative data on young freshwater fish were scarce at the time. The study had two aims: (i) describe growth patterns of young of nine co-occurring species, and (ii) examine how spawning date, spawning duration, adult size and egg size influence growth. The authors describe the analysis as first-order correlations and a first step toward understanding life history constraints.

**Study species (Table 1 summary):**
- Cyprinidae: [[Golden Shiner]], [[Blackchin Shiner]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]]
- Cyprinodontidae: [[Eastern Banded Killifish]]
- Atherinidae: [[Brook Silverside]]
- Centrarchidae: [[Rock Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]]

Table 1 lists adult size, egg diameter, age at first breeding, and spawning period for each species. Spawning began as early as May (Bluntnose Minnow, Largemouth Bass) and extended as late as June–July for Bluegill and Eastern Banded Killifish. Spawning durations ranged from 16 days (Rock Bass, Largemouth Bass) to 48 days (Eastern Banded Killifish).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and methods.** Fish were collected in 1977 from inshore shallow areas and weed beds in [[Lake Opinicon]] at weekly intervals from late June to the third week of August, with additional collections in early September and early October. A 7 × 2 m bag seine with 0.15-cm bar mesh was used, yielding 60–80 individuals per species per collection. Total lengths were measured throughout; weights were taken only in September and October. Water temperature was recorded continuously by a [[Lambrecht Thermograph]] placed 3 m offshore at 1 m depth.

**Analytical approach.** Length–frequency distributions were compared using [[Kolmogorov–Smirnov Test]]s. Length–weight regressions used [[Model II Regression]] (geometric mean regression), appropriate because both variables carried measurement error. Relationships between variables were assessed with [[Spearman Rank Correlation]].

**Hatch-size caveat.** Hatch lengths were approximations taken from centrarchid larvae on nests and from literature values for other species, so they serve only as a relative starting point. Larval size at hatch was too imprecise to correlate with egg size or adult size.

## Key Findings

**Growth patterns**
- Relative to hatch size, the species grew 6- to 10-fold in length and up to 1000-fold in weight.
- Length increased linearly early in the summer and slowed in August. Most species completed length increase by early September.
- Mean lengths for all species increased significantly between August and September. Only Bluntnose Minnow (early spawner) and Bluegill (late spawner) increased significantly again between September and October.
- Only Blackchin Shiner (late spawner) and Bluntnose Minnow showed significant weight gains between September and October. No other species added weight after early September.
- Summer-long length increments ranged from 26 mm (Bluegill) to 62 mm (Golden Shiner). Weight increments ranged from 0.5 g (Bluegill) to 2.4 g (Largemouth Bass).
- Centrarchids (breeding in their fourth or fifth summer) reached only 15–38% of adult length and 1–6% of adult weight. The smaller, mostly second-summer spawners reached 53–100% of adult length and 12–100% of adult weight.
- Species with the greatest length gains also tended to gain the most weight (r_s = 0.58, p = 0.07, not significant at 0.05). Cyprinids and non-centrarchids gained more length, while centrarchids gained more weight relative to length.

**Length–weight relationships**
- The slope was close to 3.0 for all species, indicating isometric growth.
- Centrarchids were heavier per unit length (gibbose body form), while Labidesthes was lighter per unit length (elongate body form).

**Spawning date**
- Spawning date had the strongest influence on growth. Later spawning was significantly negatively correlated with mean final length (r_s = 0.69, p < 0.05) and weight (r_s = −0.71, p < 0.05).
- Spawning date was unrelated to adult size (r_s = −0.23, p > 0.19), so large species did not gain extra growing time.

**Spawning duration and size variation**
- Small species had longer spawning periods. Spawning duration was negatively correlated with adult size (r_s = −0.67, p < 0.05) and with the coefficient of variation in young size (r_s = −0.86, p < 0.01).
- Adult size was negatively correlated with the coefficient of variation in young length (r_s = −0.60, p = 0.05).

**Adult size and egg size**
- Adult size had no significant relationship with final length (r_s = 0.08) or weight (r_s = 0.53, p > 0.05) of young, though the weight correlation approached significance.
- Large species achieved a smaller proportion of adult length in the first summer (r_s = −0.80, p < 0.01).
- Weight at summer's end was positively correlated with egg size (r_s = 0.72, p < 0.05), but length was not (r_s = 0.08).
- Larger species produced larger eggs (r_s = 0.65, p < 0.05), though the authors caution this is not a general rule (Eastern Banded Killifish has large eggs despite small body size).

**Diet**
- Diet type showed no definitive relationship with first-year growth. Planktivores (Golden Shiner, Blackchin Shiner, Bluntnose Minnow, Brook Silverside), a piscivore (Largemouth Bass) and insect/invertebrate feeders (Eastern Banded Killifish, Lepomis spp., Rock Bass) each included fast-growing species.

**Authors' interpretation and conclusions**
- Early spawners may grow more simply because they have a longer growing season, assuming similar growth rates and a common autumn cessation.
- Small species compensate for limited egg numbers per batch by spawning over extended periods, producing a wider size range of young.
- Rapid growth reduces predation exposure and allows earlier access to larger prey. Slow growers are more vulnerable to overwinter mortality.
- Future work should address environmental variables (temperature, food, vegetation) and physiological constraints on growth in different taxonomic groups.
