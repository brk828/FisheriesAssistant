# Green Lacewings (Chrysopidae) of Canada: Taxonomic Revision and Key

#entomology #neuroptera #chrysopidae

## Executive Summary

This 1932 taxonomic paper by [[Roger C. Smith]] revises the lacewing family [[Chrysopidae]] (Neuroptera) as known from Canada. It is based mainly on specimens from the [[Canadian National Collection]], supplemented by locality records from the [[Museum of Comparative Zoology]] at Harvard. The author provides dichotomous keys to the genera and species, reviews synonymy, and describes one new species, [[Chrysopa Downesi]]. The work also notes that a Canadian species was introduced into New Zealand for [[Aphid Pests]] control on shade trees. The author takes a conservative stance on species and variety recognition because adult colour patterns vary widely and larval life histories are needed for a sound classification.

## Background & Description

Most species descriptions in the family rely on colour patterns and venation. Both traits vary considerably, so the author interprets them cautiously. Size, antennal length, and wing shape are also used. Pronotum proportions change with head position at death, and the genitalia are internal and hard to compare after mounting. The author examined genitalia cleared in KOH and stained, with fair results, but colour pattern remained the most practical character.

Specimens were sent by [[Dr. J. M. McDunnough]] in 1926 and 1931 and by W. Downes in 1926. Many collectors are credited, with the most material from C. B. Garrett, J. McDunnough, and W. B. Anderson. Types were studied at the [[Museum of Comparative Zoology]] in 1927, and [[Nathan Banks]] supplied Canadian records from that collection.

The paper uses a genus-level key that separates:

- [[Nothochrysa]]: the median loop joins M 3+4 rather than M 1+2, and the species is dark brown with salmon or orange markings.
- [[Eremochrysa]]: predominately brown, small (10–14 mm).
- [[Meleoma]]: green, with the basal antennal segments widely separated and male frontal protuberances.
- [[Chrysopa]]: green, medium to large, divided into several sections on antennal and head markings (Nigricornis, Lineaticornis, Oculata, Plorabunda, Rufilabris).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species are terrestrial insects associated with trees, shrubs, and herbaceous plants. The text mentions:

- **Biological control:** [[Chrysopa Downesi]] was introduced into New Zealand at the request of Dr. R. J. Tillyard to control aphids on shade trees.
- **Overwintering:** Most, if not all, [[Plorabunda Section]] members overwinter as adults. Downes reported that *C. downesi* overwinters under loose pine bark. Late-season plorabunda specimens show yellow and brown discoloration.
- **Larval habits:** Some species, including [[Chrysopa Lineaticornis]], [[Chrysopa Cockerelli]], and [[Chrysopa Rufilabris]], have trash-carrying larvae. The author argues that larval biology is essential to resolving adult relationships.
- **Geographic patterns:** Western forms such as [[Chrysopa Coloradensis]] and the *externa* variety of [[Chrysopa Harrisii]] are most common in British Columbia and Alberta. [[Meleoma Emuncta]] and [[Chrysopa Chi]] are more eastern. The author suggests that *C. coloradensis* extends northwest from Colorado.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic decisions**

- *Cintameva* Navas is treated as a synonym of *Chrysopa* Leach. Navas's separation was based on stigmal venation, which the author considers too variable to be a reliable genus-level character. *Chrysopa perla* was designated type of *Chrysopa* by Banks (1903), so it cannot be removed from the genus.
- The author argues that the numbers and arrangement of minor wing veins should not be trusted too heavily, citing the venational changes seen from pupa to adult.
- The author recognizes many synonyms as varieties, mainly to show relationships. Examples include *C. oculata* var. *chlorophana*, *carei*, *xanthocephala*, *bipunctata*, *illepida*, *albicornis*, and *separata*, as well as *C. chi* var. *upsilon*.
- The "red-headed" form (*C. rubicunda* Navas) is rejected as a valid taxon. The author attributes it to over-exposure to cyanide killing jars.
- *Chrysopa canadensis* Banks is accepted as valid, but no specimens were found in the Canadian collection.

**New species and varieties**

- **[[Chrysopa Downesi]]** (new species): a small, very dark green lacewing with a sharply defined ivory dorsal band and wholly green venation. The holotype is a female from Kelowna, B.C., and the paratype series contains 42 specimens from several British Columbia localities and Saskatoon. Length ranges from 12 to 16 mm.
- **[[Chrysopa Oculata]] var. *carei*** (new variety): distinguished by the complete absence of brown vertex spots. The type is from Cranbrook, B.C. (collected by A. A. Dennys, June 6, 1926). Ten Canadian specimens are recorded. The author treats it as a colour form on a gradient from *pleuralis* to *caret*, not as a separate species.

**Notable records**

- **[[Nothochrysa Californica]]**: a single Canadian specimen (Victoria, B.C., May 4, 1918).
- **[[Eremochrysa Fraterna]]**: two specimens from Seton Lake, B.C.
- **[[Meleoma Emuncta]]**: 20 specimens, from B.C., Quebec, and Ontario localities. *M. slossonae* is treated as a synonym.
- **[[Chrysopa Oculata]]** (typical var.): described as the most common Canadian chrysopid, with localities across the country from B.C. to Nova Scotia.
- **[[Chrysopa Coloradensis]]**: 22 specimens from B.C. localities, collected May–August.
- **[[Chrysopa Plorabunda]]**: 47 Canadian specimens, plus 16 from the United States.
- **[[Chrysopa Chi]]**: 24 specimens, plus 13 for var. *upsilon*.
- **[[Chrysopa Pleuralis]]**: 10 specimens from Alberta and B.C., with red head markings that may represent a Canadian variety.
- **[[Chrysopa Gravida]]**: three specimens (16–21 mm), described as very rare.
- **[[Chrysopa Intacta]]**: a Navas species from Toronto. One Canadian specimen (Kazubazua, Quebec, 1927) matches the description reasonably well.

**Abnormalities**

- One *C. oculata* var. *carei* specimen from Creston, B.C., has the M 3+4 branch joining the cross vein as in *Nothochrysa*, in both front wings. The author treats this as a freak and notes that Lacroix had described a similar aberration as *ab. nothochrysiformis* in *C. perla*. Single-wing occurrences are common, so a form name would be of doubtful value.

**Unclassified material**

Two Canadian specimens remain unidentified: a *Chrysopa* near *rufilabris* and a female *Meleoma* near *emuncta*. Both were collected in 1923–1927, and the author refrains from naming them until more material is found.
