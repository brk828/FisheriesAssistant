# Saving Endangered Species: Implementation of the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 1984

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This 1985 report by [[Defenders of Wildlife]] reviews federal performance under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) during 1984. It is the first annual follow-up to the organization's ten-year analysis, and it covers budgets, species listing and recovery, and the roles of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS), the [[U.S. Forest Service]] and the [[Bureau of Land Management]]. The report grades the administration and Congress on an A-to-F scale.

The overall verdict is a mixed picture. Funding rose for FY 1985 but was proposed to be cut for FY 1986. Listing increased but remained far below need, with a backlog of about 4,000 candidate species. Recovery planning expanded, but implementation remained weak. The report describes the situation as "too little too late."

Aquatic and marine species feature prominently, including Colorado River fishes threatened by water projects, a freshwater madtom presumed extinct and later rediscovered, and a marine porpoise listed by NMFS.

## Background & Description

The ESA sets a process for protecting imperiled species. The main steps are:

- **Listing:** official recognition of a species' declining status. The report calls listing "the cornerstone" of the Act (citing a 1979 [[Government Accounting Office]] report).
- **Candidate classes:** Category 1 candidates are biologically well documented as vulnerable. Category 2 candidates may merit protection but need further study.
- **Critical habitat:** areas essential to conservation, where federal actions likely to destroy or adversely modify them are forbidden.
- **Recovery planning and implementation:** plans for listed species, funded by appropriations.
- **[[Section 7 Consultation]]:** federal agencies must ensure their actions do not jeopardize listed species.
- **[[Section 9 Taking Prohibition]]:** prohibits killing, harming or significantly modifying habitat that impairs breeding, feeding or sheltering.
- **[[Section 6 State Grants]]:** federal support for state conservation programs.

Defenders of Wildlife highlighted ten representative species in 1984. Among them were the [[Gray Wolf]] and [[Grizzly Bear]], [[Desert Tortoise]], [[Lynx]], [[Black-Footed Ferret]], [[Western Yellow-Billed Cuckoo]], [[Gulf of California Harbor Porpoise]], [[Pine Barrens Buck Moth]], [[Small Whorled Pogonia]] and [[Red-Cockaded Woodpecker]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat destruction or modification is identified as the main cause of decline for most listed species, so habitat protection is central to recovery. The report stresses several aquatic threats:

- **Colorado River system:** existing projects have reduced flow by 35 percent. Four listed fishes (humpback chub, bonytail chub, Colorado River squawfish and razorback sucker) are threatened by water project impacts. Since 1981, FWS issued 32 biological opinions finding that upper Colorado River basin projects would not jeopardize these fishes, though they would deplete flows by an additional 415,000 acre-feet annually. Approval was pending for 28 more projects.
- **Platte River:** proposed water developments threaten [[Whooping Crane]] habitat used during migration. The river is already reduced to scarcely 40 percent of its pristine flow. The report also notes threats to the [[Piping Plover]] and [[Interior Least Tern]] from water development on the Platte, Missouri and Mississippi rivers.
- **Wetland development:** the report warns that development in Louisiana and Florida could again endanger [[American Alligator]] populations after delisting.
- **Springs and desert waters:** the Texas desert fishes, including the Comanche Springs pupfish, depend on spring flows and the removal of introduced competitors and predators.

## Key Findings

**Funding**
- FY 1985 FWS appropriations rose: Congress provided $23.1 million for Section 15 programs (against a $21.2 million request) and $3.9 million for Section 6 grants. Combined Sections 6 and 15 totaled $27 million, below the $33 million authorized ceiling.
- The FY 1986 proposal essentially froze FWS spending, a $0.9 million real loss after assumed 4 percent inflation.
- Land acquisition for endangered species was proposed at zero for FY 1986 to FY 1988. Congress appropriated $31.6 million for FY 1985, compared with a $14.5 million request.
- NMFS protected species funding was $11.2 million in FY 1985. The FY 1986 request was slightly under $7 million, a 38 percent cut. Defenders recommended $11.2 million plus $2.6 million. The extra funds would support sea turtle trawl-net exclusion devices, recovery plans for Pacific sea turtles, a [[Gulf of California Harbor Porpoise]] recovery plan, and candidate lists for marine fish, invertebrates and plants.
- Defenders recommended raising FWS endangered species funding to at least $53.7 million immediately and at least $72 million by FY 1988, and nearly $50 million from the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund]] for FY 1986 habitat acquisition.

**Listing**
- In the presidential-term year January 1984 to January 1985, 47 species were listed and 60 proposed, the most since 1979.
- The candidate backlog was nearly 4,000. Table 2 lists 3,827 candidates: 1,116 in Category 1 and 2,711 in Category 2. Plants account for 2,588, invertebrates 876 and vertebrates 363.
- At the 1984 listing rate, the report estimates almost 25 years to list Category 1 candidates. Completing the full listing task would take some 60 years.
- Invertebrate listing lagged. Only two invertebrate proposals were published in 1984, one being the bay checkerspot butterfly, nearly four years after its petition.
- NMFS listed the Gulf of California harbor porpoise (the text gives the date as January 1986, which is likely an error given the May 1985 report date). NMFS had not initiated listing for any marine organism beyond this and had rejected three invertebrate petitions. It proposed listing the Guadalupe fur seal in January 1985.
- The smoky madtom, presumed extinct for 23 years, was rediscovered in Citico Creek, Tennessee, in 1980 and listed as endangered in 1984. Reintroduction into Abrams Creek in the Great Smoky Mountains National Park is planned, to redress an inadvertent extirpation there in 1957 caused by a fish toxicant treatment.
- Species losses continued: the Florida sand flax and Texas Henslow's sparrow became extinct while candidates.

**Recovery**
- Recovery plans covered 195 of the 329 listed U.S. species (about 60 percent) in 165 approved plans. In 1984, 55 new plans were approved.
- Among 234 species in the FWS implementation report, 33 were extinct or at crisis levels, 18 declining, 23 increasing and 10 stable. Population status for the remaining 154 species (nearly two-thirds) was unknown.
- The Palos Verdes blue butterfly, listed in 1980, was probably the first species extinct after listing. Its last habitat was rototilled in 1984.
- California condor is declining by about two birds per year in the wild.
- The report notes success stories, including Moapa dace habitat acquisition creating the Moapa National Wildlife Refuge, and Section 7 enforcement protecting the [[Valley Elderberry Longhorn Beetle]] along the Sacramento River. Bunched arrowhead habitat in South Carolina was restored after a Section 7 consultation.
- The report warns that alligator delisting in Louisiana and Texas could allow renewed decline if wetlands are lost.

**Overall Conclusions**
- The report concludes that 1984 was a "mixed" year, with recovery plans increasing on paper but implementation not materially improving.
- Defenders recommended faster listing (including limited protection for Category 1 candidates), greater recovery implementation, more effective Section 7 use, and expanded protection for plants.
