# The Disappearance of Four Leaf Leachates in a Hard and Soft Water Stream in South Western Ontario, Canada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #stream-biogeochemistry #Simuliidae #leaf-litter-decomposition

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Maurice A. Lock]] and [[H. B. Noel Hynes]] investigates the disappearance of leached "dissolved" organic carbon (D.O.C.) from four tree leaf species — sugar maple, trembling aspen, bog cedar, and red pine — in hard-water and soft-water stream systems in South Western Ontario, Canada. Using both water-alone and water-plus-sediment mesocosm experiments, the authors quantified rates and extents of leachate D.O.C. loss over 9 hours and 4 days. Substantial differences emerged between leaf species and between hard vs. soft water chemistries, with sediment-associated microbial uptake accounting for the majority of D.O.C. disappearance within the first 9 hours. The findings are used to explain differences in productivity between hard-water and soft-water (e.g., Canadian Shield) stream ecosystems, with direct implications for detritivore and suspension-feeding invertebrate communities such as [[Simuliidae]] that depend on this organic carbon pathway.

## Background & Description

This work builds directly on [[Lock and Hynes 1976]], which showed that sugar maple leachate introduced into a hard-water stream sediment system was reduced to 15% of its initial level within 9 hours and to 0% within 4 days, with evidence that the leachate carbon was incorporated into stream microflora and thereby made available to detritivores and grazers. Prior studies by [[Madsen 1972]] demonstrated that organic matter with associated microflora accumulates on stone surfaces in streams, while [[Egglishaw 1968, 1972]] showed that streams differing in water chemistry decompose rice grains and cellulose at different rates.

**Methodology:**
- Leachates were prepared from freshly collected autumn leaves of sugar maple (*Acer saccharum*), trembling aspen (*Populus tremuloides*), dead bog cedar (*Tsuga occidentalis*), and dead red pine (*Pinus resinosa*).
- Leaves were ground in a Wiley Mill, water-soluble material extracted in distilled water, concentrated via continuous centrifugation and freeze-concentration, filtered through 0.45 µm Millipore membranes, and freeze-dried into a standardized soluble powder.
- Water and 2 cm sediment cores were collected from:
  - **Hard water:** [[Speed River]] (Ca ~90 mg L⁻¹)
  - **Soft water:** a tributary of the [[South Kashe River]] (Ca <2 mg L⁻¹) and [[Hoc Roe Creek]] (soft water alone)
- Leachate was dosed at 0.25 g leaf L⁻¹ equivalent (per [[Lush & Hynes 1973]]), yielding freeze-dried additions of 0.075 g L⁻¹ (maple, aspen) and 0.020 g L⁻¹ (cedar, pine), based on 30% and 8% water-soluble content, respectively.
- Experiments were run in stirred glass bowls simulating miniature circulating streams, housed in environmental chambers matched to ambient stream temperature and light.
- D.O.C. was measured with a Beckman 915 Total Organic Carbon Analyzer at time 0, 1–2 min, 1, 2, 4, 6, 9 hours, and 1, 2, 3, 4 days, using filtered (0.45 µm glass fiber, pre-combusted) samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry contrast:** Hard water (Speed River, Ca ~90 mg L⁻¹) vs. soft water (South Kashe tributary/Hoc Roe Creek, Ca ≤2 mg L⁻¹) systems represent two extreme stream productivity types typical of Southern Ontario vs. Canadian Shield drainages.
- **Riparian vegetation mismatch:** The dominant riparian tree along the hard-water Speed River was cedar, yet cedar leachate disappeared *slowest* in hard water (last in the sequence pine > maple > aspen > cedar). Along soft-water streams, the dominant trees (red maple *Acer rubrum*, eastern hemlock *Tsuga canadensis*) were not even among the species tested, and taxonomically similar species (maple, pine) did not show enhanced uptake — indicating that a stream's capacity to trap a given leaf leachate is **not** governed by co-evolved relationships between water microflora and local riparian tree species.
- **Productivity implications:** Since the bulk of D.O.C. uptake in sediment-associated systems occurs within the first 9 hours, and previous work suggests this carbon becomes available to detritivores/grazers, seasonal or systemic variability in a stream's leachate-trapping capacity may be a partial explanation for the well-documented lower productivity of soft-water (Canadian Shield) streams relative to hard-water streams.
- **Leaf litter chemistry and evergreen dominance:** Deciduous leaves release ~20–30% of dry weight as leachate, while evergreens release only ~6–10% ([[Lush 1972]]). Because evergreens dominate Canadian Shield watersheds, the *absolute* input of leachate DOC to soft-water streams is inherently lower — compounding the productivity disparity.
- **Invertebrate community linkage:** The greater and more prolonged precipitation of leachate D.O.C. observed in soft water (relative to hard water) is hypothesized to partly explain the abundance of suspension-feeding [[Simuliidae]] (blackflies) in Canadian Shield streams, which exploit fine particulate organic matter formed from leachate precipitation.

## Key Findings

### Water-alone experiments (no sediment)
- No significant difference between filtered and unfiltered D.O.C. levels immediately after leachate introduction — i.e., no immediate precipitation of introduced organic material occurred (Table 2).
- **Hard water:** Leachate D.O.C. remained near initial levels over 4 days for maple, aspen, and pine; aspen showed the greatest decline (to ~90% of initial by day 4). Cedar was an exception, declining rapidly to ~60% of initial by day 3.
- **Soft water:** All leaves except pine showed declines over 4 days — aspen fell to 60% by day 4, maple and cedar declined steadily to 70% and 80% respectively by day 4. Pine remained near initial levels throughout, despite being a genus common on the Canadian Shield.

### Water + sediment experiments — 9-hour and 4-day dynamics
- In **hard water + sediment**, all leachates declined rapidly to 0–15% of initial levels within 1 day, remaining low through day 4 (cedar and pine rose slightly by day 4 to ~10% and ~20%).
- In **soft water + sediment**, first-day declines were more variable: maple (May) fell to 50%, cedar to 30%, pine to 75%. A July repeat of maple and a June test of aspen showed much steeper declines (maple to 25% by day 1, 15% by day 4; aspen to 15% by day 1, rising slightly to 59% by day 3) — indicating **seasonal change in soft-water sediment character** between May and July.

### Interspecific comparison (first 9 hours, water + sediment)
- **Hard water** disappearance rate order (fastest→slowest): **pine > maple > aspen > cedar**. Slopes (b): pine 0.099, maple 0.083, aspen 0.077, cedar 0.050 (Table 4). Pine/maple and maple/aspen slope pairs were not significantly different; all other pairwise comparisons were significant (F 5.12–14.64, p<0.05–0.01).
- **Soft water** disappearance rate order: **aspen > cedar > maple > pine**. Slopes: aspen 0.066, cedar 0.049, maple 0.022, pine 0.012 (Table 4). Only maple/pine and aspen/cedar pairs were statistically indistinguishable; all other pairs differed significantly (F 6.77–53.93, p<0.05–0.01).
- At 9 hours, hard water leachate levels ranged from 0–20% of initial (Table 4); soft water ranged more widely, 23–85% of initial.
- Across both water types, 80–100% of total 4-day disappearance occurred within the **first 9 hours**, with only 10–20% further loss over the subsequent 3.5 days.

### Intraspecific comparison (hard vs. soft water)
- **Maple:** Significantly different slopes between hard (−0.083, July) and soft water (−0.022, May) (F 33, df 1,34, p<0.01). However, a July repeat of the soft-water maple test yielded a much steeper slope (−0.083) statistically indistinguishable from the hard-water value — demonstrating **seasonal (May→July) change in soft-water sediment trapping capacity**.
- **Pine:** Significantly different slopes between hard water (−0.099) and soft water (−0.012) (F 92, df 1,33, p<0.01).
- **Cedar:** No significant difference between hard water (−0.050) and soft water (−0.049) slopes.
- **Aspen:** No significant difference between soft water (−0.066, June) and hard water (−0.077) slopes, though direct comparability is confounded by aspen being tested in a different month (June) than the other species (May).

### Historical/seasonal comparison of hard-water maple uptake
- Hard-water sediment systems reduced maple leachate to: 26% (12 hrs, February, unpublished), 15% (9 hrs, July, this study), 24% (9 hrs, October, [[Lock & Hynes 1976]]) — indicating **relatively stable year-round uptake capacity in hard water**, contrasting with the marked seasonal shift documented in soft water between May and July.

### Synthesis
- Leachate-trapping capacity is not predictable from local riparian tree dominance or taxonomic similarity between test species and dominant trees.
- Soft-water streams show more temporally variable D.O.C.-trapping ability than hard-water streams, potentially contributing to their generally lower biological productivity.
- Lower absolute leachate inputs from evergreen-dominated Shield watersheds compound this effect, providing a plausible mechanistic link between water chemistry, leaf litter inputs, and overall stream productivity — with downstream consequences for detritivore and filter-feeding invertebrate assemblages that ultimately support fish food webs.
