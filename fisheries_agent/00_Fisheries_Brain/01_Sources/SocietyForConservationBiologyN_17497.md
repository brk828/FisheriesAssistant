# Society for Conservation Biology Newsletter, Vol. 1 Issue 3 (August 1994)

#conservation-biology #biodiversity #scientific-society

## Executive Summary

This issue of the [[Society for Conservation Biology]] (SCB) Newsletter covers the 1994 annual meeting in [[Guadalajara]], [[Mexico]], and reports on the journal [[Conservation Biology]]. [[Peter F. Brussard]]'s president's column argues that human population growth and resource consumption are the root causes of biodiversity loss and that conservation biologists must become educators on these issues. The editor's report documents rising submissions, shorter turnaround times, a reduced paper backlog, and the Board's approval of a move to bimonthly publication in 1995. Other items include general membership business, a new half-time Executive Coordinator post, 1994 SCB awards, a [[CONSLINK]] electronic information service, grant and job announcements, and a calendar of meetings. The issue contains no primary research data on fishes or any other taxon.

## Background & Description

The newsletter is a society publication. Its main content falls into five groups:

- **President's column** ("The twin demons"): a commentary on the 1994 meeting and on education for conservation.
- **Editor's report**: statistics on manuscript submissions, turnaround, backlog, decisions, and the editorial board for [[Conservation Biology]], written by [[Reed Noss]] (editor) and [[Ellen Main]] (managing editor).
- **General membership meeting minutes**: election results, finances, future meetings, and resolutions.
- **Announcements**: funding, research positions, job postings, new publications, awards nominations, and conferences.
- **Feature items**: a note on [[CONSLINK]] and the 1994 SCB award winners.

The 1994 annual meeting took place 7–11 June 1994 at the Holiday Inn Crowne Plaza in Guadalajara. The meeting was held jointly with the [[Society for Tropical Biology]]. Brussard reported over 600 participants from more than 25 countries. The minutes give a later figure of 680 individuals from 34 countries. The meeting was organized by [[Eduardo Santana]] and staff at the University of Guadalajara.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The president's column places the meeting in a context of exceptional biodiversity:

- Mexico is estimated to harbor about 10% of the world's species.
- Of Earth's 6,492 recorded reptile species, 717 (about 11%) occur in Mexico, and 55% of these are endemic to the country.
- About a tenth of the world's mammal species occur in Mexico, and 32% of these are endemic.
- A 1992 presidential decree established [[CONABIO]], a high-level Mexican government agency tasked with promoting knowledge and conservation of the nation's biota. Brussard notes that CONABIO predates the United States' [[National Biological Survey]] by two years.

Brussard identifies population growth and resource demand as the central threats. He writes that Mexico's population will double in 30 years, that people there aspire to higher living standards, and that similar pressures exist in almost every less developed country. Cuba is cited as an exception with regard to population growth. He also observes that consumer demand in developed nations is "insatiable." He notes that few papers at the meeting identified population growth and resource consumption as root causes of the conservation problems discussed.

The column urges conservation biologists to educate the public, government officials, environmental groups, and students. Specific suggestions include:

- Insisting that the implications of population growth and resource consumption be discussed publicly.
- Ensuring that introductory biology courses, especially those for non-majors, cover exponential growth and human dependence on natural ecosystems.
- Teaching [[Ecological Economics]] by pairing ecologists with economists.
- Presenting accurate facts to government officials about ecologically damaging projects.
- Helping environmental groups build a stronger biological foundation for their advocacy.
- Setting an example through limited reproduction, lower animal-protein consumption, and reduced personal energy use.

## Key Findings

### Journal Performance (Editor's Report)

- **Submissions:** 320 new manuscripts were received from 1 May 1993 through 31 May 1994, an average of 25 per month. The number submitted in May 1994 (44) was 2.4 times the May 1993 figure (18). Submissions from January through May 1994 (132) were 43% above those for January through May 1993 (92). The editors note they lack enough monthly data for a formal time-series analysis, but the trend is clearly upward.
- **Turnaround to publication:** For 91 manuscripts published in the first three 1994 issues, the average time from submission to publication was 340 days (min 176, max 464, sd 74). The 82 manuscripts published in all four 1993 issues averaged 580 days (min 177, max 194, sd 163, as printed).
- **Acceptance to publication:** 240 days on average in 1994 (min 103, max 401, sd 69), compared with 326 days in 1993 (min 118, max 625, sd 118).
- **Review and decision times:** Review turnaround averaged 67 days (min 0, max 175, sd 40). Submission-to-decision averaged 62 days (min 0, max 185, sd 45). About 10% of papers are rejected without review.
- **Backlog:** Accepted papers awaiting publication fell from 69 (October 1993, after 60 manuscripts were transferred from the Rutgers office) to 44 as of 31 May 1994, excluding papers already scheduled for September.
- **Page budget:** The journal ran more than 100 pages over its previous page budget, at a cost of about US$12,000 to the SCB and publisher [[Blackwell Scientific]]. The March 1994 issue was a record 326 pages, June was 286, and September was estimated at 247. The expanded 1994 page budget of 1050 leaves only 191 pages for December, so a further slight overrun is possible.

### Editorial Decisions

- Of 320 manuscripts received since May 1993, 189 have final decisions and 131 do not.
- Of the 189 final decisions, 109 were rejections (a rejection rate of 58%). Seventy-seven manuscripts were accepted and three were accepted pending minor corrections.
- Of 151 decisions after the first review round (excluding flat rejections), 31 (21%) were rejections without prejudice, 58 (38%) required major revision, 32 (21%) required moderate revision, and 30 (20%) required minor revision.
- The editors describe the rejection rate as high but similar to that of established journals such as [[Ecology]].

### Editorial Board

- The board grew from 31 assigning editors (March 1993 listing) to 39. Six members resigned or left at the end of their terms, and 14 were added.
- The average assigning editor handled 4.3 manuscripts over 13 months (min 0, max 11, sd 3.1).
- About 20% of incoming papers go directly to peer reviewers from the editorial office to avoid overloading assigning editors.
- The editors plan further changes and an increase in the representation of women on the board.

### Move to Bimonthly Publication

- Because submissions rose sharply and biodiversity loss is increasing, [[Blackwell Scientific]] and the editorial office proposed moving [[Conservation Biology]] from quarterly to bimonthly publication in 1995. The editorial office also requested a 48% budget increase.
- The Board of Governors unanimously approved the move, starting with a February 1995 issue. Blackwell agreed to cover some of the added costs. SCB Chief Financial Officer [[Steve Humphrey]] confirmed that the society can afford the expansion.

### General Membership Meeting

- **Election:** 24% of the 4177 ballots mailed were returned. [[Dennis Murphy]] was elected President-Elect. [[Deborah Jensen]] and [[Suzana Padua]] were elected Board Members at Large. [[Malcolm Hunter]] was elected university/college representative, and [[Chris Wemmer]] was elected botanical or zoological garden representative.
- **Finances:** Humphrey projected a US$5000 surplus by the end of 1994. The Board approved a small membership rate increase (printed as US$2–10) to support the additional issues. The SCB has funds for twenty new subscriptions for institutions that cannot otherwise afford the journal.
- **Future meetings:** Fort Collins, Colorado (1995); Providence, Rhode Island (1996, with the [[Ecological Society of America]]); Albuquerque, New Mexico (1997, with the Ecological Society of America and [[The Nature Conservancy]]). The 1998 location is undecided but likely to be outside the United States.
- **Student chapters:** Five new chapters were added, bringing the total to 17.
- **Journal ranking:** The journal's Science Citation Index rating improved to 17th in General Biology and 18th in Ecology. The minutes state that it remains the most frequently cited periodical in applied ecology.
- **Resolutions:** Of 13 Board-approved resolutions, 12 passed unanimously. A resolution on human population growth passed 50 yes to 14 no after floor discussion.
- **Political role:** The SCB filed an amicus brief prepared by [[Neal Lawrence]] and, with the [[Wildlife Society]], planned a review of the Eastside Forest Plan.

### Executive Coordinator Position

- The Board created a half-time paid position, Executive Coordinator, to serve as the society's central contact. Duties include maintaining a computerized membership database, supporting committees, coordinating expert input on scientific issues, handling elections, liaising with other organizations, and answering routine inquiries.
- Applicants should send resumes by 15 November 1994 to Ulla Yandell, Assistant to the President. A search committee chaired by the SCB President hopes to fill the post by 1 January 1995.

### CONSLINK

- The [[Smithsonian Institution]]'s Conservation & Research Center launched CONSLINK in 1989 as a free electronic conference and news service delivered through the [[Internet]].
- CONSLINK has over 700 direct subscribers in about 40 countries, and hundreds more receive its content through redistribution services.
- It distributes electronic versions of eight newsletters, including the Smithsonian's Biological Conservation Newsletter, and several conservation databases.
- Subscription is free, and subscribing requires only one email message to the LISTSERV address.

### 1994 SCB Awards

Eight awards were announced:

- [[Hugh Iltis]]: career in activism, education, and research on temperate and tropical biodiversity.
- [[Instituto Nacional de Biodiversidad]] (Costa Rica): pioneering surveys of biological resources.
- [[Exequiel Ezcurra]]: leadership in protecting Mexican biodiversity as Director General de Aprovechamiento Ecologico de los Recursos Naturales.
- [[Al Gore]]: efforts to focus attention on biodiversity loss, including the book *Earth in the Balance*.
- [[José Sarukhán]]: protection of Mexican biodiversity and ecological studies of tropical plants.
- [[Robert Repetto]]: work showing how policy incentives drive deforestation.
- [[Toshio Kasuya]]: cetacean research that supports their conservation.
- [[Charles Schnell]]: conservation work with the [[Organization for Tropical Studies]] and conservation curricula in Costa Rican schools.

### Announcements

- **Funding:** The [[National Science Foundation]] Directorate for Biological Sciences runs a competition for fundamental research on the conservation and restoration of biological diversity. Awards have ranged from US$40,000 to US$90,000. The deadline is 29 September 1994. [[Primate Conservation, Inc.]] funds field research on endangered primates, with average awards of US$2500 and a maximum of US$5000. Deadlines are 1 March and 1 October.
- **Positions:** Openings were listed at the [[Stanford University]] Center for Conservation Biology (Associate Director for Tropical Conservation Biology), the Organization for Tropical Studies (instructor in Costa Rica), the Maryland Department of Natural Resources (neotropical migrant landbird biologist), and Montana State University (Fish and Wildlife Management and Research professor). Other listings included the [[Sengwa Wildlife Research Institute]] in Zimbabwe and the University of Sierra Leone's Faculty of Environmental Sciences.
- **Publications:** The Center for Wildlife Law's *State Wildlife Laws Handbook* (US$89) covers wildlife codes in all 50 states. The journal *Forest Planning Canada* became the *International Journal of Ecoforestry* with Volume 10.
- **Meetings:** The newsletter lists conferences including the Wildlife Society conference (21–25 September 1994, Albuquerque), the Metapopulations symposium, a Greater Yellowstone brucellosis symposium, a California exotic pest plant symposium, the Forest Canopies symposium (Sarasota, 9–13 November 1994), and the 1995 SCB Ninth Annual Meeting (7–11 June 1995, Fort Collins). The [[Desert Fishes Council]] will hold its twenty-sixth annual symposium on 17–20 November 1994 at Death Valley National Monument. This is the only item in the issue that concerns fishes.
