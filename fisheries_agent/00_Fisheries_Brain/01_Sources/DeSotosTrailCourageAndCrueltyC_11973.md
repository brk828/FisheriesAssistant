# De Soto's Trail: Archeological Evidence of the 1539–1543 Expedition

#archeology #history #de-soto-expedition

## Executive Summary

This 1987 New York Times article (reprinted with science-times framing) reports archeological work tracing the route of [[Hernando De Soto]]'s expedition, the first large European party to penetrate deep into the interior of what is now the United States. Excavations at a site in [[Tallahassee]], Florida, uncovered 16th-century artifacts that archeologists believe mark De Soto's first winter camp (1539–1540) among the [[Apalachee]] people. The article also covers the expedition's northward and westward路 route, its battles, disease impacts on Indigenous populations, and its ultimate failure to meet its goals. The document is a news feature, not a research report, and its conclusions are provisional pending further excavation.

## Background & Description

- **Expedition:** Led by [[Hernando De Soto]] with about 600 men and two women, departing from Florida around 1539 in pursuit of gold and an overland route to New Spain (Mexico).
- **Context:** Took place roughly a generation after Columbus, about 30 years before the founding of St. Augustine, and half a century before [[Roanoke Colony]].
- **Route:** Landed at [[Tampa Bay]] in May 1539; wintered at the Apalachee town of Anaica; moved north through Georgia and the Carolinas, across the [[Smoky Mountains]] to Tennessee; turned southwest through Alabama and Mississippi; reached the [[Mississippi River]] in May 1541; wandered Arkansas, Oklahoma and Texas.
- **Key diagnostic artifact:** [[Chevron Glass Beads]], a bead type with a blue chevron pattern, found so far in Florida only in association with De Soto.
- **Source narratives:** Three firsthand accounts by participants, plus a priest's document recently found in Spain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The expedition moved through swamps, dark rivers (the Withlacoochee, Suwannee and Aucilla), and oak-hickory uplands in Florida, and through the Tennessee River Valley and Coosa lands in Georgia and Alabama.
- The Apalachee were a prosperous farming people growing corn, beans and pumpkins; the Spanish seized these stores during their stay.
- Humid climate made European [[Chain Mail Armor]] a rusting burden and an ineffective defense against Indigenous stone arrowheads.
- Europeans introduced diseases, including possibly [[Smallpox]], against which Indigenous people had no immunity. Mass burials at the Tatham Mound site were attributed to this.
- Two decades later, a Spanish expedition found Coosa societies in collapse, with towns abandoned and populations decimated.

## Key Findings

1. **Tallahassee winter camp:** Archeologist [[B. Calvin Jones]] (Florida state archeologist's office) identified the site after test excavations in March 1987 on six wooded acres. Finds include early 16th-century glass beads, ceramic fragments, iron nails, boot tacks, lead shot, European armor and chain mail links. Jones's realization came about three to four days after the discoveries.
2. **Diagnostic beads:** Blue chevron glass beads are considered the best markers of De Soto's presence. [[Charles Ewen]] (University of Florida) noted that this bead type had been found in Florida only with De Soto.
3. **Discarded armor:** Chain mail links were found in nearly every trench, suggesting the Spanish discarded their armor when they left camp.
4. **Florida survey data:** Of 167 sites examined in Florida over two years, 42 were Spanish, and about half of those bore traces of De Soto's presence (per [[Jerald T. Milanich]]).
5. **Landing site:** [[Jerald T. Milanich]] and [[Charles Hudson]] concluded the party landed in May 1539 at Tampa Bay, near present-day Ruskin, and established a base camp at the mouth of the Little Manatee River.
6. **Tatham Mound (Citrus County):** Excavations yielded chisels, iron spikes, armor and hundreds of beads, including chevron beads. Skeletons of 74 Indians, apparently buried simultaneously, were assumed to be epidemic victims.
7. **Route revisions:** Hudson's research placed Chiaha near Newport, Tennessee, and Mabila near Selma, Alabama (though no relics yet found), and proposed a Mississippi River crossing a few miles south of Memphis.
8. **Mabila battle:** The fiercest battle of the expedition, with Indigenous attackers surprising the Spanish at night; horse stampede alerted the soldiers.
9. **Death and outcome:** De Soto died of fever on June 20, 1542, near the Mississippi River. The survivors (311 by the time they reached a Spanish settlement on the Mexican coast in September 1543) floated down the river and to the Gulf of Mexico, having failed to establish an overland route or settlements.
10. **Disease debate:** Milanich disputes that European diseases spread inland before De Soto arrived, attributing abandoned villages to fear of the expedition.
11. **Site preservation:** Florida Secretary of State George Firestone planned to propose purchase of the land, valued by developers at $1.1 million, to preserve it as a historic site. An office development had been on hold.

**Bottom line:** The article presents the Tallahassee site as very probably De Soto's first winter camp, pending confirmation by further excavation, and frames the expedition as a failure by its contractual goals, with long-term demographic and cultural consequences for Southeastern Indigenous peoples.
