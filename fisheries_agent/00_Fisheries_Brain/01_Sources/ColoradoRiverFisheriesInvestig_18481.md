# Colorado River Fisheries Investigations 1979 Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #river-systems

## Executive Summary

This 1979 progress report documents the first year of the [[Colorado River Fisheries Project]] (CRFP), established through a Memorandum of Understanding between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Water and Power Resources Service]]. The project addresses the biology, habitat requirements, spawning behavior, and cultural technologies for endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), plus threatened [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). Field investigations covered approximately 570 miles of the [[Green River]], [[Colorado River]], and [[Gunnison River]] from July through November 1979, employing systematic stratified sampling and special intensive studies. Hatchery propagation efforts at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] achieved limited success, producing 918 juvenile squawfish and establishing broodstock populations. Research subcontracts with [[Utah State University]] and [[University of Idaho]] initiated tolerance studies, bioassays, and behavioral investigations. The project documented distribution patterns, habitat preferences, and evidence of natural reproduction for target species across multiple river strata.

## Background & Description

The CRFP was initiated in April 1979 to investigate six primary facets of endangered fish biology: spawning requirements, habitat requirements and preferences for young and adults, migration and movement patterns, interspecific competition, cultural technologies, and tolerance to chemo-physical environmental conditions. The study area encompasses the [[Green River]] from [[Split Mountain]] downstream 300 miles to its confluence with the Colorado River; the [[Colorado River]] from Debeque, Colorado downstream 255 miles to [[Lake Powell]]; and the [[Gunnison River]] from Dominquez to Grand Junction (37 miles).

Field stations were established at Vernal, Utah (Green River studies) and Grand Junction, Colorado (Colorado and Gunnison River studies), staffed with permanent and temporary personnel. The rivers were divided into discrete strata based on geophysical parameters (channel configuration, substrate, gradient, depth) and biological characteristics. The Green River was divided into six strata (A through F), the Colorado River into eight strata (I through VIII), and the Gunnison River into one stratum (IX).

Target species include four endangered/threatened fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (adults reaching 350-613 mm total length in 1979 captures), [[Humpback Chub]] (adults 232-360 mm), [[Bonytail Chub]] (one adult captured), and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Twenty-three fish species and four hybrids were documented during the study period. The project employed multiple gear types including trammel nets, gill nets, electrofishing, seines (1/8", 1/4", 3/8", 1/2" mesh), and wire traps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] presents diverse aquatic habitats ranging from high-gradient canyons with rapids and waterfalls to low-gradient braided sections with extensive backwaters. Eight major habitat types were characterized: main channel, backwater, side channel, eddy, pool, riffle, run, and shoreline. Substrate classifications included silt, sand (< 3mm), gravel (3-76mm), rubble (76-305mm), boulder (> 305mm), and bedrock.

Water quality parameters measured at each intensive sample site included dissolved oxygen, turbidity, air and water temperatures, salinity, and conductivity. Physical measurements documented depth profiles using electronic depth recorders at three cross-sections per 0.5-mile intensive section. Depth and velocity measurements accompanied each fish capture to characterize microhabitat preferences.

Sampling was stratified both spatially (by river stratum) and temporally (pre-runoff, during runoff, post-runoff periods; a.m., p.m., and darkness periods within each day). The 1979 field season was limited to post-runoff sampling (July 16 - November 4) due to late project initiation. Sympatric species documented included [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*C. discobolus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*C. latipinnis*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and various centrarchids.

Special study areas included [[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]] (a 440-acre inundated gravel pit near Grand Junction) and [[Black Rocks]] (a deep, swift one-mile section through precambrian gneisses and schists, 19 miles downstream from Loma). Black Rocks supported substantial populations of both humpback chub and Colorado squawfish.

## Key Findings

**Population Distribution and Abundance:**

On the [[Green River]], 77 juvenile and adult Colorado squawfish, 26 adult humpback chub, 1 adult bonytail chub, and 2 adult razorback suckers were collected in systematic sampling. Adult squawfish were captured in all strata, with highest numbers in strata E, F, and B. Young-of-the-year (YOY) squawfish were most abundant in strata A and F. A special backwater survey from Gates of Lodore (RM 399) to Mineral Bottom (RM 52) collected 1,794 young squawfish, with highest catch-per-unit-effort in strata A (2.75 fish/10m² with 1/8" seine; 25.37 fish/10m² with 1/4" seine) and F (1.50 and 8.41 fish/10m² respectively).

On the [[Colorado River]], 36 adult, 30 juvenile, and 256 YOY Colorado squawfish were captured, along with 96 adult, 6 juvenile, and 2 YOY humpback chub. Most adult squawfish and humpback chub were captured at [[Black Rocks]] (RM 125-154). A significant finding was 241 young and 21 juvenile squawfish captured in the 47-mile stratum above the Green River confluence, with one seine haul at RM 34.8 yielding 106 young fish. One juvenile squawfish was caught near RM 138 in Colorado, the furthest upstream capture in several years, indicating successful 1978 spawning.

**Habitat Preferences:**

[[Colorado Squawfish]] adults occupied depths primarily < 10 feet, velocities < 2 ft/s, over boulder substrates in shoreline areas, eddies, and runs. Juveniles preferred depths 0.25-1.2 feet with little or no current. YOY squawfish were found almost exclusively in backwaters over silt-sand substrates with velocities < 1 ft/s and depths < 5 feet.

[[Humpback Chub]] adults were captured in water < 30 feet deep, velocities < 1 ft/s, over silt, sand, boulder, and bedrock substrates in eddies, runs, and shoreline habitats. Young chub were collected only in backwaters and runs over sand-silt substrates. A noteworthy discovery was 10 adult, 1 juvenile, and 2 young humpback chubs in [[Westwater Canyon]] (RM 111-125), about 11 miles downstream from Black Rocks, indicating a previously undocumented population.

**Movement and Behavior:**

Young-of-the-year Colorado squawfish demonstrated diurnal movement patterns between backwaters and main channel, with largest numbers inhabiting backwaters around midnight. Of 54 marked YOY squawfish, 9 were recaptured, confirming movement between backwater areas. Five humpback chub were recaptured; two locally, one 3 miles away, and one 5 miles away from initial capture sites.

Highest numbers of [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) were captured with large numbers of young squawfish, suggesting possible sympatric habitat relationships. Young squawfish were not found in areas preferred by [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*).

**Hatchery Propagation:**

[[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] spawning efforts in 1979 were largely unsuccessful. Humpback chub failed to produce eggs; Colorado squawfish produced 30,000 poorly fertilized eggs resulting in only 918 juveniles. By April 1980, broodstock included Colorado squawfish from multiple sources (Yampa River, Colorado River, Green River strains), humpback chub from Little Colorado River and Colorado River (Black Rocks), and bonytail chub females from [[Lake Mojave]].

In 1980, hybrid crosses were successfully produced: bonytail female × roundtail male (22,430 eggs, ~10,000-15,000 fry); bonytail female × humpback male (22,000 eggs, ~10,000-15,000 fry). Black Rocks strain humpback chub spawning produced 18,000 eggs (13,000-14,000 fry) from field collections and 5,700 eggs (2,000 fry) from hatchery spawning. Approximately 550 future broodstock squawfish (lot 4DYAWB) were distributed among [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] Fisheries Experiment Station (150 fish), [[Dexter Creek National Fish Hatchery]] (270 fish), and Willow Beach (130 fish).

**Research Subcontracts:**

[[Utah State University]] initiated studies on tolerance and preferences of endangered fishes to temperature and total dissolved solids, swimming performance at various velocities, and [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) spawning migrations and feeding habits in the Green and Colorado Rivers. Approximately 200 striped bass were captured below [[Cataract Canyon]], with evidence of spawning between [[Gypsum Canyon]] and upper Cataract Canyon. Notably, 40-45 Colorado squawfish (1.5-6 lb) were found in the same section.

[[University of Idaho]] contracts addressed spawning biology and early life history of [[Northern Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*) with inferences to Colorado squawfish, culture and spawning technique development, histopathological effects of toxicants, and acute toxicity bioassays of 16 selected chemicals (8 organic: DDT, dieldrin, endrin, PCB, parathion, chlordane, lindane, toxaphene; 8 inorganic: selenium, zinc, copper, chlorine, ammonia, mercury, cadmium, lead).

**Tagging and Monitoring:**

All rare fishes > 175 mm total length were tagged with Carlin dangler tags. Radio tagging experiments initiated in April 1980 successfully tagged six squawfish and one razorback sucker, providing data on diurnal movements and habitat selection. Some radio-tagged fish were followed into tributary streams ([[White River]], [[Yampa River]], [[Duchesne River]]), potentially revealing tributary importance for spawning or other life history functions.

**Methodological Developments:**

A computerized data storage and retrieval system (MANAGE program, developed by Western Energy and Land Use Team, Fort Collins) was implemented for organizing physical and biological data. Probability-of-use criteria were developed for different life stages based on depth, velocity, and substrate measurements at capture locations. The sampling design employed stratified random sampling with sequential progression through strata, intensive 0.5-mile study sections sampled for 24 hours, and intervening 4.5-mile reaches surveyed by electrofishing.
