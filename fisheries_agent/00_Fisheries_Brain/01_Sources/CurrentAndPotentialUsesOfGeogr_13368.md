# Current and Potential Uses of Geographical Information Systems: The North American Experience

#ichthyology #marine-biology #not-applicable

*Note: This document is a 1987 review of geographical information systems (GIS) and contains no fish or aquatic species data. The tags above follow the required format; the subject is not aquatic taxonomy.*

## Executive Summary

R. F. Tomlinson of [[Tomlinson Associates Ltd]] (Ottawa, Ontario) reviews two decades of [[Geographical Information Systems]] (GIS) development in North America, emphasizing the last decade. The paper, commissioned by the British Government's Committee of Inquiry into the Handling of Geographic Information (the [[Chorley Report]], 1987), is an acknowledged subjective overview drawn from more than 25 years of company experience. It concludes that GIS adoption has been concentrated in a few sectors, that progress has been uneven, and that management and coordination problems matter as much as technical ones. A successful national programme needs coordinated applications, vendors, trained personnel, government-funded research, and independent expert advice.

## Background & Description

The paper defines GIS as a digital system for analysing and manipulating geographical (spatial) data, with emphasis on analysis and display. It distinguishes GIS from [[Automated Cartography]], whose data structures and functions differ and are not highly compatible, even though maps are often the output of both.

Sector-by-sector review covers:
- **Forestry:** the most rapid recent growth; about 100 installed systems in 1986 (estimate), with a potential market of about 500.
- **Property and land parcel data:** LIS/LRIS systems; a potential market of about 1,000 systems; sector still at a low point on the growth curve.
- **Utilities:** about 200 North American utilities with potential GIS interest; largely served by automated cartography vendors.
- **Transport, facility and distribution planning:** 26 companies listed by Raker (1982); vendors had made little penetration; potential market about 1,000 systems or more.
- **Civil engineering:** about 50 major systems in Canada and perhaps ten times that in the U.S.; not expected to grow strongly.
- **Agriculture and environment:** origin of GIS in Canada ([[Canada Geographic Information System]], CGIS, and [[Canadian Soil Information System]], CanSIS), begun over 20 years earlier.

## Key Findings

- **Inhibiting factors:** lack of digital base mapping, limited and fragmented data, low user awareness, cost and software pricing, management problems, shortage of skilled staff, and political structure.
- **Forestry drivers:** GIS keeps resource inventories current. Updates that once took 10 years or more could be made within weeks or months. Inventory cycles of 20 years were typical for 5,000 map sheets in one agency.
- **Going it alone:** the author states that virtually all agencies attempting in-house or contract GIS development "met with disaster." Coordinated acquisition is recommended instead.
- **Cost trends:** software for about $100,000 had reached hundreds of installations, and the market could be ten times larger at $10,000. Turnkey systems of about $250,000 (VAX or Prime hardware) were preferred over recurring costs of about $100,000 per year.
- **Data standards:** standardization efforts such as the USGS Digital Line Graphs and the Canadian Standard Data Transfer Format had little effect. De facto standards arose from widely installed vendor systems, notably Intergraph's Standard Interchange Format.
- **Data sharing:** coverage is incomplete regionally and thematically, and duplication remains common.
- **Costs and benefits:** formal accounting is rare. Benchmark tests usually check function existence rather than performance speed. Direct quantitative benefit evaluation is considered very difficult; the author expects a case built on qualitative, informed decision-making.
- **Technical developments:** manual digitizing is unlikely to improve much. Raster scanning and automated vectorization are expected to replace vector digitizing for complex documents and bulk data. The author notes that vector systems dominate most sectors, while elevation data and satellite imagery are typically raster.
- **Research priorities:** the author argues GIS is a distinct field needing research in three areas: data structures and algorithms, spatial analysis, and spatial statistics. Uncertainty handling and time-dependent data are identified as gaps.
- **Human factors:** the author notes that GIS is often adopted by individual promoters, after which the rest of the agency may disengage. Advance functional-requirements studies with director involvement are recommended.
- **Qualified personnel:** the supply of qualified Ph.D.s is described as "woefully inadequate," and the gap between agency needs and educational output is growing.
- **Lessons learned:** GIS has been oversold; poor advice produced failures; public-sector in-house development competes with the private sector; and management issues are as important as technical ones. Systems installed on a trial basis "almost invariably fail once the honeymoon is over."
- **Conclusions:** a national GIS programme needs (1) valid applications, (2) active vendors, (3) an educational sector, (4) government-funded R&D, and (5) independent expert advice. Governments are expected to remain the main source of research funding.
- **Notable future developments:** the proposed [[National Center for Geographic Information Analysis]] (National Science Foundation) and the USGS selective digitization of 1:100,000 maps for the 1990 census were identified as likely to influence the field, though the author cautions that forecasting is hazardous.
