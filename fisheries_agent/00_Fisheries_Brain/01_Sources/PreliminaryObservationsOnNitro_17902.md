# Nitrogen Transport in Swift's Brook, a Spring-Fed Ontario Stream

#limnology #stream-ecology #nitrogen-cycle

## Executive Summary

This preliminary study tracked [[Nitrate-Nitrogen]] transport in [[Swift's Brook]], a perennial, 2 km spring-fed stream in Ontario, through weekly sampling from June to October 1973. The spring water carried consistently high nitrate-N, and roughly 60% of the incoming nitrate was lost before the stream reached its outflow. The authors attribute most of this loss to [[Denitrification]] rather than uptake by [[Macrophytes]] or sediment immobilization. The work was framed as a pilot for a larger study of stream nitrogen cycling, motivated by [[Eutrophication]] concerns and the role of diffuse nitrogen sources.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Lake eutrophication research has focused on nutrient inputs. Phosphorus mainly comes from point sources, which are relatively easy to control, while nitrogen comes largely from diffuse sources, which are harder to manage. The authors cite [[Lake Erie]] and [[Lake Ontario]] inputs (70% and 60% of phosphate from point sources; 60–70% of nitrogen from diffuse sources) and the [[River Great Ouse]], where only 17% of nitrate was attributable to defined sewage inputs. Little was known about how nitrogen and phosphorus move and transform during stream transport, even though stream manipulation (damming, bank and bed renovation, vegetation management) might enhance natural nutrient sinks.

**Study site.** Swift's Brook arises from a [[Limnocrene]] (a spring-fed pool) dominated by [[Watercress]] (*Nasturtium officinale*) and mint (*Mentha aquatica*). The stream flows about 2 km before joining [[Blue Spring Creek]], which eventually merges with the [[Eramosa River]]. The channel passes through:
- a cedar stand (about 400 m),
- wooded areas with deep organic sediment deposits,
- an unused pasture with willow and red-osier dogwood riparian vegetation (about 750 m),
- a small oval pond (about 40 × 15 m, mean depth about 1 m),
- a final grassy pasture reach (about 500 m) with meadow grasses and herbaceous vegetation.

The central stream bed is stony. Tributaries were all ephemeral and dry during the study. Stream width averaged 95 cm and depth 10.7 cm during low flow.

**Methods.** Samples were collected about weekly in 1 L polyethylene bottles at four main stations (plus station 3A above the pond) from 22 June to mid-October 1973. Nitrate-N was measured by the phenol-disulphonic acid method, and Kjeldahl N by APHA (1971) standard methods. Ammonium and nitrite were checked by spot tests, which were consistently negative. V-notch weirs were installed in late August to measure discharge. Temperature and pH were recorded, and diurnal sampling (pre-sunrise and late afternoon) was performed on six occasions at stations 2, 3A and 3.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime.** Water at the spring stayed between 8.2 and 9.0°C from June to October and did not drop below 7.8°C in winter. Downstream temperatures approached normal values for the region.
- **Chemistry.** pH was near neutral at the spring and rose slightly downstream, reaching 7.75–8.00 at the confluence with Blue Spring Creek.
- **Nitrogen forms.** Nitrate-N dominated. It was about 7 mg/L in late spring, declining to about 3 mg/L in October. Ammonium and nitrite stayed below about 0.1 mg/L at all stations. Kjeldahl-N was relatively high in early summer, then became negligible from late August.
- **Hydrology.** Discharge at the spring was a steady 3.03 L/s. Downstream discharge was slightly lower (for example 2.41–2.83 L/s at stations 2–3 in September), and the authors found no evidence of dilution by surface water from late June onward.
- **Vegetation.** Watercress was the dominant macrophyte in patches along the whole stream. Woody riparian plants and grasses lined the banks.

## Key Findings

1. **Substantial nitrate loss.** Mean nitrate-N was 5.53 mg/L at the spring and 2.4 mg/L at station 4. Assuming a constant flow of 3.03 L/s, about 171 kg of nitrate-N entered the stream from late June to mid-October, and about 97 kg (roughly 60%) was unaccounted for at station 4.
2. **Loss rates.** The summer loss equals 0.425 g N/m²/day for the whole stream and 0.490 g N/m²/day above station 3. The authors could not construct an annual nitrogen budget because flow could not be monitored from October to June.
3. **Denitrification as the likely mechanism.** The authors "tentatively conclude" that denitrification is the dominant removal pathway. Supporting points:
   - Macrophyte uptake explains only about 1.4 kg N, roughly 1.44% of the deficit. This estimate uses a standing crop of 21.4 g dry weight/m² in July 1974 (40.7 kg total over 2,000 m × 0.95 m) and 3.38% N in whole watercress plants.
   - Immobilization in sediment is judged small because summer organic inputs are low and leaf-fall carbon is rapidly stabilized in autumn (Kaushik & Hynes, 1971).
   - The lower reach (stations 3 to 4) had little removal despite lacking organic deposits, which is consistent with denitrification needing organic sediment.
4. **Reach-specific differences.** Nitrate fell by an average of 1.46 mg/L between stations 2 and 3A (about 710 m of woody riparian vegetation). Only 0.2 mg/L was lost between stations 3 and 4 (500 m of grasses and weeds). The authors read this as evidence that bank vegetation removes only a small share of nitrate.
5. **No clear diurnal effect.** Morning and afternoon decreases did not differ significantly (for example 0.64 vs 0.72 mg/L between stations 2 and 3A; 0.71 vs 0.60 mg/L between 3A and 3). This argues against strong daytime uptake by plants, although water temperatures differed between sampling times.
6. **Comparison with other systems.** Owens et al. (1972) reported large summer nitrogen losses in the Great Ouse (up to about 1,200 kg/day) and the [[River Trent]] (about 25,000 kg/day), equivalent to 0.75 and 1.4 g/m²/day. Brink & Widell (1967) observed similar summer deficits in a Swedish stream that were later recovered. Stake (1967) argued that nitrogen taken up by plants returns to the water within the same growing season.
7. **Management implication.** Streams may remove significant nitrogen in transport, particularly those fed by land drainage, which carries more oxidized nitrogen than sewage outfalls. This suggests that stream manipulation could be a practical tool for limiting downstream nutrient movement.
8. **Limitations and next steps.** The results cover only the low-flow period. The authors planned further work with flow gauging and sampling instruments to confirm the denitrification hypothesis and build a full annual budget.
