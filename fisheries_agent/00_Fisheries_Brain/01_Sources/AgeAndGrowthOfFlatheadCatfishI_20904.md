# Age and Growth of Flathead Catfish in Four Southwestern Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #age-growth #introduced-species

## Executive Summary

This study examined age class structures and growth rates of [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) populations in four southwestern rivers: the [[Lower Colorado River]] (Arizona-California), [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] (all Arizona). Introduced between 1950-1975, these populations now support important recreational fisheries. Age determination via pectoral spine analysis in 1986 revealed that growth rates were similar across all rivers, though fish in the [[Lower Colorado River]] lived longest and attained the largest sizes. Southwestern populations exhibited growth equal to or exceeding that of native populations in the eastern United States. The species' highly predatory nature poses concerns for native fish recovery efforts, particularly for [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*).

## Background & Description

[[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) is a large, piscivorous ictalurid native to lakes Michigan and Erie, and waters of the Mississippi, Missouri, and Ohio basins, Gulf of Mexico coastal plain, and Rio Grande drainage into Mexico. The species supports important recreational and commercial fisheries throughout its native range.

Due to its large size and palatable flesh, [[Flathead Catfish]] were introduced to parts of Arizona, California, Florida, Idaho, New Mexico, Oregon, and South Carolina. The first southwestern introduction occurred in the [[Gila River]] system in New Mexico or Arizona prior to 1950. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] stocked six locations along the [[Lower Colorado River]] in 1962, with subsequent introductions to the [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], and San Francisco rivers from 1968-1975.

The species has spread throughout the lower Colorado, Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers in Arizona. Population density in the lowermost [[Colorado River]] ranged from 155 to 259 flathead catfish per km at maximum discharge in 1986. The species holds the title for largest sport fish taken by angling in Arizona (29.5 kg).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Flathead Catfish]] are highly predatory, feeding primarily on other fish species. This predatory behavior is detrimental to recovery efforts for native [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the [[Gila River]]. The species' successful colonization of all major Arizona drainages, combined with its popularity among anglers and impact on native fish communities, makes understanding its population dynamics critical for management.

Spawning occurs in spring through early summer, as indicated by capture of small fish with no discernible annuli. Annulus formation in spring was coincident with resumption of growth following cooler winter temperatures. Water temperatures reached 28°C in the [[Lower Colorado River]] during mid-summer, but no evidence of false-annulus formation in response to thermal stress was observed among larger fish.

The study areas encompassed:
- **[[Lower Colorado River]]**: 32.5 km reach between Laguna and Morelos dams, Yuma County, Arizona and Imperial County, California
- **[[Salt River]]**: 59 km length upstream from Lake Roosevelt, Gila County, Arizona
- **[[Verde River]]**: Accessible reaches downstream from Childs, Yavapai County to Bartlett Reservoir, Maricopa County, Arizona
- **[[Gila River]]**: 77 km reach from Arizona-New Mexico border, Greenlee County, downstream to Safford, Graham County

## Key Findings

### Sampling and Methods

Collections were made during May through July 1986 using boat-mounted or raft-mounted electroshockers and trammel nets (50 m long, 2.5 cm inner and 20.3 cm outer meshes). Fish were measured to nearest mm total length (TL) and weighed to nearest 20 g. Age determination used pectoral spine cross-sections, with thin sections polished and examined under reflected light with binocular microscope.

Back-calculation of TL at time of annulus formation used the equation: L' = C[S'/S(L-C)], where L' is TL when annulus formed, L is TL at capture, S' is spine radius to annulus, S is total spine radius, and C is intercept value from linear regression of L and S. The regression equation for [[Lower Colorado River]] was: TL = 17.41S + 15.20 (r = 0.989, n = 58). A constant intercept of 15 mm was used for all populations.

### Age Structure and Growth by River

**[[Lower Colorado River]]** (n = 98):
- Age range: 0 to 9 years (1977-1986 year classes)
- Young-of-year: averaged 71 mm TL
- Largest individual: 1,010 mm TL, 9 years old
- Weighted mean TL by age: Year 1 = 106±23 mm, Year 2 = 195±9 mm, Year 3 = 382±42 mm, Year 4 = 455±51 mm, Year 5 = 586±55 mm, Year 6 = 710±56 mm, Year 7 = 798±42 mm, Year 8 = 930 mm, Year 9 = 976 mm
- Mean annual growth: greatest during third year (187 mm)
- Lumen enlargement problematic: >90% of fish ≥3 years missing first year annulus; 23-75% of fish ≥4 years missing second year annulus

**[[Gila River]]** (n = 24):
- Age range: 1 to 5 years (1981-1986 year classes)
- Young-of-year: averaged 103 mm TL
- Largest individual: 520 mm TL, 5 years old
- Weighted mean TL by age: Year 1 = 130±14 mm, Year 2 = 248±28 mm, Year 3 = 327±27 mm, Year 4 = 406±18 mm, Year 5 = 503 mm
- Mean annual growth: greatest during first year (130 mm), relatively consistent thereafter

**[[Salt River]]** (n = 34):
- Age range: 1 to 6 years (1980-1985 year classes)
- No young-of-year collected
- Largest individual: 707 mm TL, 6 years old
- Weighted mean TL by age: Year 1 = 131±8 mm, Year 2 = 254±31 mm, Year 3 = 379±53 mm, Year 4 = 491±40 mm, Year 5 = 581±31 mm, Year 6 = 613 mm
- Mean annual growth: greatest during first year (131 mm), relatively constant through years 2-3, reduced thereafter

**[[Verde River]]** (n = 46):
- Age range: 0 to 8 years (1978-1986 year classes)
- Young-of-year: 145 mm TL (n = 1)
- Largest individual: 920 mm TL, 8 years old
- Weighted mean TL by age: Year 1 = 118±27 mm, Year 2 = 223±21 mm, Year 3 = 362±14 mm, Year 4 = 470±40 mm, Year 5 = 621±43 mm, Year 6 = 747±56 mm, Year 7 = 785±54 mm, Year 8 = 862±28 mm
- Mean annual growth: greatest during third year (139 mm) and fifth year (151 mm)

### Comparative Growth Patterns

No statistical differences in annual growth rates among rivers were detected (t-test, P > 0.10). However, [[Flathead Catfish]] from the [[Gila River]] and [[Salt River]] appeared to grow faster during the first two years of life than those in the [[Verde River]] and [[Lower Colorado River]]. [[Gila River]] fish exhibited slowest growth beyond the third year. [[Lower Colorado River]] fish lived longer and attained larger sizes than fish in other Arizona rivers.

### Regional and Range-wide Comparisons

Among riverine populations, [[Flathead Catfish]] growth rates in Arizona rivers were:
- **Greater than**: Salt River, Missouri; Missouri River, Nebraska
- **Similar to**: Big Blue and Neosho rivers, Kansas
- **Slower than**: Coachella Canal, California

Growth in the [[Lower Colorado River]] was as rapid through 9 years as anywhere reported in the species' range. Reservoir populations show more variable growth rates but generally have similar ranges to river-dwelling stocks, contrary to earlier suggestions that reservoir growth was faster.

### Methodological Considerations

Pectoral spine lumen enlargement due to natural bone formation processes was especially problematic in the [[Lower Colorado River]] population, requiring correction to avoid biases toward younger ages and faster growth. Mean and standard deviation for annuli 1, 2, and 3 were calculated from spine sections with minimal lumen enlargement (n = 15, 27, and 25 respectively for years 1, 2, and 3). If lumen radius exceeded annular mean plus 1 SD, age was increased correspondingly.

Back-calculated lengths agreed well with modes in length-frequency distributions of 884 [[Flathead Catfish]] collected from the [[Lower Colorado River]] in 1982-1986, validating the spine aging method. The method was previously validated from tagged fish returns in the Des Moines River, Iowa, and Lake Carl Blackwell, Oklahoma.
