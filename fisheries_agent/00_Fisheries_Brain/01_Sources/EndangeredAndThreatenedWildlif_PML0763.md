# Spikedace and Loach Minnow: 90-Day and 12-Month Petition Findings (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service notice, published in the *Federal Register* on July 11, 1994, reports 90-day and 12-month findings on a petition to reclassify the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) from threatened to endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The Service found that the petition presents substantial information indicating reclassification may be warranted, and, after review, concluded that reclassification to endangered is warranted but precluded by higher-priority listing actions. The document is a regulatory notice, not a research paper, and draws on published, unpublished, and agency sources.

## Background & Description

Both species are small minnows that inhabit isolated stretches of stream in the upper Gila River system of Arizona and New Mexico. Their habitat has been reduced by [[Dam Construction]] and impoundment, diversion, channelization, and groundwater pumping, along with [[Watershed Degradation]] from livestock grazing, timber harvest, road building, and mining. Both species live in relatively fast-flowing water in large stream environments, and both have declined dramatically since the 1960s. Introduced nonnative fish species have also contributed to their decline.

The spikedace is presently known only from isolated populations in Arizona and New Mexico, including the Gila River, San Francisco River, Eagle Creek, Verde River, and Blue River drainages. The loach minnow is known from isolated stretches of the Gila River and several tributaries, including the East Fork and mainstem White River, San Francisco and Tularosa Rivers, and Dry Blue Creek.

Regulatory history: the spikedace was listed as threatened on July 1, 1986, and the loach minnow was listed as threatened on October 28, 1986. Critical habitat was designated for both species on March 8, 1994. The 1991 five-year review of listed species recommended reclassification to endangered for both.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies the following threats and conditions:

- **Habitat loss and alteration:** impoundment, water diversion, channelization, and groundwater pumping.
- **Water quality and watershed degradation:** livestock grazing, timber harvest, road construction, and mining.
- **Nonnative species:** introduction and spread of nonnative fish, which compete with or prey on native species.

Both species occupy relatively fast-flowing, shallow stream reaches, and the remaining populations are fragmented. Genetic analysis of the four remaining spikedace populations found that each is quite distinct, with the Verde River population considered distinct as well. Genetic information suggests a need to protect each population to maintain genetic diversity in the species.

## Key Findings

- **Petition received:** On September 22, 1993, the Department of the Interior received a petition dated September 17, 1993, from David Hogan, Peter Gelvis, the Greater Gila Biodiversity Project, the Southwest Center for Biological Diversity, and the Biodiversity Legal Foundation. The petitioners requested reclassification from threatened to endangered. The Service accepted it as a valid petition on November 10, 1993.
- **90-day finding:** The petitioners presented substantial information indicating that reclassification may be warranted.
- **12-month finding:** Reclassification to endangered is warranted, but is precluded by work on pending listing actions of higher priority.
- **Status review:** A 1991 status review found that the status of both species had become more precarious, due to substantial declines in numbers since the previous review. The Service found both species met the requirements for endangered status.
- **Expected benefits of reclassification:** Recognition of a more precarious status and increased penalties for prohibited activities under Section 9 of the Act. The Service noted these benefits would be incremental to existing protections, and that protecting species with no current legal protection is a higher priority.
- **Genetic diversity:** Each remaining population should be managed to preserve genetic diversity.
- **Date of findings:** The findings were made on June 30, 1994, and materials are available for public inspection at the Arizona Ecological Services State Office in Phoenix until further notice.
