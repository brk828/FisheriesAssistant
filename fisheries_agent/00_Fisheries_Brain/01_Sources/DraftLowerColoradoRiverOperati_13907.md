# Lower Colorado River Native Fish Protected Habitat Development Project Management Plan

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #habitat-management #conservation

## Executive Summary

This draft management plan implements Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) provision 3 from the April 30, 1997 Biological Opinion on [[Bureau of Reclamation]] operations on the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The plan addresses establishment of at least 300-600 acres of protected habitats for endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). These isolated backwater habitats aim to provide secure environments where native fish can complete their full life history and develop self-sustaining populations. The project represents a first step in restoring these species to the lower Colorado River, with habitats ranging from 3-20 acres plus several larger impoundments. Implementation must occur by April 30, 2002, with partnerships established with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[California Department of Fish and Game]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], and [[National Wildlife Refuges]].

## Background & Description

### Species Biology

The [[Bonytail Chub]] is a highly streamlined fish with a thin, pencil-like caudal peduncle and large, falcate fins, reaching 600 mm in length (300-350 mm more common). Long-lived individuals may reach 49 years of age. Young fish prey on aquatic invertebrates while adults utilize both aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, plankton, and occasionally small fish. Although characterized as a mainstem-dwelling species, bonytail chubs also utilize backwater habitats very successfully.

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a robust fish with a deep caudal peduncle, easily recognized by the large keel behind the head and in front of the dorsal fin. These large fish can reach almost a meter in length, with most individuals in [[Lake Mohave]] between 500 and 750 mm total length. Also long-lived, individuals may reach 45 or more years of age. Young razorback suckers eat mostly plankton while adults utilize plankton and a variety of benthic food sources. Historically, razorback suckers made extensive use of backwater and backwater-type habitats especially during spring and summer months.

### Historical Context

Historically, the [[Colorado River Basin]] supported a fish fauna comprised largely of specialized endemic species. In the last century, populations of most endemic species have precipitously declined and many have been extirpated from much of their former range. Two primary causes for these declines are: (1) alterations of habitats by dams, diversions, channelization, and flood plain development for agriculture or urbanization; and (2) introduction of non-native fish species. Before river harnessing, the Colorado River was characterized by extremely variable flows and water quality, and a mosaic of aquatic, marsh and riparian habitats in every stage of seral succession.

### Project Requirements

The Biological Opinion determined that [[Bureau of Reclamation]] operations and maintenance activities jeopardized the continued existence of endangered bonytail chub and razorback sucker and destroyed or adversely modified designated critical habitat. After review, the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] modified the minimum size of aquatic habitats to be reduced from 100 acres to between 3 and 20 acres, with several larger impoundments included to provide a variety of habitat conditions. This modification does not decrease the total acreage required but increases the number of habitats needed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Selection Criteria

Selected habitats must meet multiple criteria:

**Biological Factors:**
- Ability to support existing fishery (indicating suitable conditions)
- Adequate water supply and quality to support primary production
- Morphology including depths greater than five feet
- Sufficient productivity to provide zooplankton and benthic macro-invertebrates

**Physical Factors:**
- Surface area between 3-20 acres (with some larger impoundments)
- Effective isolation from recreationists and non-native fish
- Not compromised by toxic loads of salts, pesticides or herbicides from agricultural activity
- Reliable water delivery systems (preferably subsurface alluvial connection, permeable dikes, infiltration galleries, or [[Ranney Wells]])

**Institutional Factors:**
- Water rights and property ownership secured
- Focus on [[National Wildlife Refuges]] and other federal/state managed lands
- Resolution of conflicts with sport fishing interests

### Available Habitat Types

Several types of managed backwater habitats exist on the lower Colorado River:
- Completely isolated ponds in historic flood plain with no surface connection to river
- Ponds separated from river by permeable dikes, armored banks or similar structures
- Ponds that were connected embayments of the river and were diked off to isolate them
- Historic ponds, marsh or meander sites currently dry or marginally wet that can be reclaimed

### Potential Sites Under Consideration

**Arizona:**
- [[Achii Hanyo Hatchery]] ponds: 10 acres
- [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]: New ponds 35 acres, existing ponds 83 acres, [[Three Mile Lake]] 20 acres
- [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]: New ponds 40 acres, existing ponds 70 acres

**Nevada:**

## Key Findings

### Management Philosophy

The habitats will be managed with minimal intensive intervention, allowing native fish to establish and maintain populations with minimum human oversight. These are not hatchery facilities requiring daily hands-on management. Monitoring of biological and physical-chemical habitat conditions will assess success or failure, with management actions taken only as necessary to correct identified problems.

### Habitat Preparation and Renovation

All habitats with extant fish populations will be renovated prior to stocking with native fish species. Renovation protocols include:
- Removal and relocation of any native fish and game fish species before treatment
- Application of toxicant (rotenone or antimycin) in compliance with all applicable laws
- Two treatments and monitoring required before non-native fish considered eliminated
- Alternative method: complete drying and refilling of habitat

### Stocking Strategy

**Source of Fish:**
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (New Mexico) - only source available, using broodstock derived from [[Lake Mohave]] individuals
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: Wild-caught larvae from Lake Mohave preferred to maximize genetic variance; alternative is spawning ripe adults at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]

**Stocking Size and Numbers:**
- Fingerling size fish preferred (not subadults) since habitats will be free of non-native fish
- Fingerlings have good survival when fed and reared to fingerling size in captivity
- Initial stocking densities much lower than hatchery ponds (Dexter NFHTC averaged 28,000 fingerlings per acre at >96% survival)
- Stocking rates dependent on physical and biological character of each habitat
- Should not aim to fill available carrying capacity initially to provide space for recruited fish

**Species Distribution:**
- One-third of habitats stocked with both species
- One-third stocked with bonytail chub only
- One-third stocked with razorback sucker only
- This distribution allows assessment of potential deleterious interactions between species

### Genetic Management

Small isolated populations are susceptible to increased rates of genetic drift that can significantly reduce genetic variance. Management plan includes:
- Deliberate relocation of a few individuals between habitats each generation to mimic natural immigration/emigration
- Specific instructions for age/size and numbers of migrants to be developed once habitats selected
- Wild-caught razorback sucker larvae from Lake Mohave preferred to maximize genetic variance in founder populations
- Bonytail chub genetic variance limited due to 1981 F1 generation broodstock derived from at most three paired matings

### Monitoring Requirements

Monitoring will be periodic rather than intensive:
- Basic water quality parameters (temperature, conductivity, dissolved oxygen) recorded 2-4 times per year for first few years
- Fish populations assessed at least annually
- Monitor for: non-native fish contamination, diseases/parasites, recruitment, size structure
- Changes in population ratios between species in mixed habitats
- Monitoring conducted under Cooperative Agreements with State game and fish agencies

### Management of Non-Native Fish Contamination

Physical barriers cannot guarantee non-native fish will not return to habitats over time. Management responses include:
- If contamination occurs within 1-3 years of establishment: renovate habitat and restart
- For established recruiting populations: consider degree of contamination and species involved
- Research opportunities to study interactions between native and non-native species
- Native fish population needs have priority in renovation decisions
- Preferred option: capture and relocate native fish before renovation rather than killing entire population

### Population Structure Management

**Recruitment Issues:**
- If recruitment not occurring at expected levels within five years: examine physical and biological parameters for correctable causes
- Potential actions: creation of additional spawning and nursery areas

**Overpopulation Issues:**
- High recruitment and low natural mortality may result in stunted populations
- Management options: elimination of all or portions of population, physical manipulation to increase productivity, fertilization, reduction of spawning areas, water level manipulations
- Fish removed from habitats should be used appropriately: adjacent river/reservoir, restoration projects elsewhere, research, or broodstock

### Technical Review and Adaptive Management

A Technical Review Group will be established including:
- Federal and State agency personnel
- Academics, consultants, and other interested parties
- Focus on technical rather than social, economic or political issues
- Annual meetings to review monitoring results and determine next year's monitoring needs
- Recommendations on management actions, research proposals, and changes to overall management philosophy

### Implementation Timeline and Compliance

- Project must be implemented by April 30, 2002 (five years from April 1997 Biological Opinion)
- Current plans: stock several habitats in 1999 with others coming online before 2002
- NEPA compliance on project as a whole completed in 1998
- Federal Endangered Species Permits required for all hands-on participants
- State scientific collecting permits likely required
- Additional section 7 consultation may be required for some management activities

### Research Opportunities

While not the primary purpose, habitats can provide research opportunities on:
- Life history and behavior of bonytail chub and razorback sucker
- Interactions with non-native fish species
- Habitat needs for native species
- Management of small impoundments
- Effects of small populations of non-native fish on native fish recruitment
- Specifics of interaction between native and non-native fish species

Research funding not provided directly under RPA 3 but may be available under RPAs 4 and 14.

### Long-Term Context

This Project is only a first step in restoring populations to the lower Colorado River. Establishment of secure habitats is limited in scope. Other RPA provisions address removal of levees, bank stabilization, establishment of new hydrologic regimes, and reconnecting backwater habitats. Additional work anticipated as part of [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP) under development by lower basin States, intended to last 50 years.

### Challenges and Constraints

**Water Availability:**
- Projections include shortage, normal, and surplus years
- In shortage years: factors include seniority of water right, relative priority of native fish habitats, availability of stored/banked water, funds to purchase water
- Strategy for addressing water shortage years needed

**Social Constraints:**
- Concerns about effects to recreational activities, especially fishing
- Converting existing habitats supporting non-native fish to native fish habitats closed to fishing
- Need for public relations/public education strategy
- Reclamation committed to working with States and user groups to address concerns

**Fish Health:**
- Small pond habitats susceptible to disease and parasite outbreaks
- Lower Colorado River has populations of disease organisms and parasites (including introduced parasitic copepod *Lernaea*)
- Limited ability to apply chemicals or alter water quality in Project habitats
- If fish health concerns become significant problem, habitat may have to be removed from Project
