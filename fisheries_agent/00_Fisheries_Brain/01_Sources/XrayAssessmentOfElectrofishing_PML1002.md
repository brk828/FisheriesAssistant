# Radiographic Assessment of Electrofishing Injury in Colorado Pikeminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin, prepared by John A. Hawkins of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at [[Colorado State University]] (November 2002), assesses how often boat-mounted, pulsed-DC [[Electrofishing]] injures endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). Fish captured during 1996 sampling under the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) were examined externally and radiographed in the field.

Of 46 electrofishing-captured fish x-rayed, 12 (26%) had vertebral compressions. Only 3 fish (7%) had compressions judged acute and caused by the capture, while 9 (20%) had congenital or chronic abnormalities. No fractures or misalignments were found. The author concludes that non-vertebral injuries, extreme tetany, and poor handling probably matter more for survival than vertebral compression.

## Background & Description

Colorado Pikeminnow is a federally listed endangered cyprinid that occupies large rivers of the Upper Colorado River Basin. Its populations are tracked through annual abundance estimates and, from 1996 to 2000, through the ISMP, and both programs rely on boat electrofishing to capture adults. Electrofishing is widely used but can reduce performance, injure, or kill fish, which is a concern for rare species. The species is long-lived, reportedly living over 12 years, so cumulative injury is a potential risk.

Fish were captured by Colorado Division of Wildlife and Utah Division of Wildlife Resources crews using aluminum boats with a 5-kW generator and [[Coffelt WP-15]] or CPS control units. Anodes were two stainless-steel spheres (23 or 28 cm diameter) on fiberglass poles; cathodes were two 7.6-m stainless-steel cables. Output was kept under the ISMP limit of 300 V or 12 A, and usually at or below 6 A.

Captured fish were held in saline river water, anesthetized with [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (200 mg/L, buffered with sodium bicarbonate), measured, weighed, examined, PIT-tagged, photographed, and radiographed with a portable veterinary x-ray unit. Fish were released at capture sites, with the goal of completing all procedures within 1 hour. Radiographs were classified by the [[Reynolds Spinal Injury Scale]] (0 to 3), and acute injuries were separated from chronic or congenital ones by vertebral architecture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sampling occurred in May 1996 on the Colorado, White, and Yampa rivers. Water conductivity was 175 to 400 µS/cm in the main river and 350 to 480 µS/cm in flooded tributaries. Secchi depth was 8 cm or less at several sites, reflecting turbidity that limited observation of fish position before capture. Electrofishing settings were pulsed DC at 30 or 60 Hz, with 150 to 390 V and 4 to 15 A.

Electrofishing is effective in turbid rivers, which is why it is hard to replace for this species. The report links injury risk to the electric field gradient near the anode, and notes that boat movement, flowing water, and fish depth made pre-capture position hard to record. Background vertebral abnormalities in wild salmonids were reported at 0 to 16%. Injury from capture in backwater habitats is also linked to water temperature, dissolved oxygen, and rapid temperature change during transfer to live wells.

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 47 Colorado Pikeminnow were x-rayed: 46 captured by electrofishing and 1 found dead of unknown cause. Total length of the 47 averaged 554 mm (SD 89.95), ranging 355 to 774 mm.
- **Vertebral injury:** 34 of 46 (74%) showed no apparent injury; 12 (26%) had Class 1 compressions. No Class 2 (misalignment) or Class 3 (fracture) injuries were seen.
- **Acute vs. chronic:** 3 fish (7%) had acute compressions from the capture; 9 fish (20%) had congenital or chronic compressions.
- **Extent:** Most fish (67%) had compression of only two vertebrae; acute injuries affected 2 to 11 vertebrae, averaging 35% compression of original width.
- **External injury:** 3 of 47 fish had electrofishing-related injuries: one with bruising, puncture wounds, and muscle compaction (and 11 compressed vertebrae); one in respiratory arrest after being trapped under a live anode for 15 to 30 seconds, resuscitated in flowing water for about 10 minutes; and one bleeding from the gills while in electrotaxis toward a cathode.
- **Physiological state at capture (n = 42):** 64% stunned, 24% in electrotaxis, 12% tetanized. Mean distance from anode was 1.3 m (electrotaxis), 0.9 m (stunned), and 0.6 m (tetanized), with overlapping ranges.
- **Recaptures:** Fish with no vertebral injury grew 17.2 mm/year (n = 10). Four fish with congenital or chronic injuries grew 15.6 mm/year. The one fish with acute injuries grew 9.8 mm/year after 2 years at large.
- **Comparison:** The 7% acute rate is far lower than the 43 to 61% reported for rainbow trout with a WP-15 (Sharber and Carothers 1988) and the 18 to 98% reported by Fredenberg (1992). Colorado Pikeminnow also showed a lower rate than razorback sucker in laboratory work (50%).
- **Conclusions:** Vertebral compression was the only spinal injury. The author concludes that non-vertebral injuries, extreme tetany, and poor handling probably affect survival more than vertebral compression. Fish that were injured still swam away normally at release.
- **Recommendations:** Require electrofishing certification for crews in the Upper Colorado River Basin; provide regular formal training; establish protocols for checking equipment (polarity, output); improve handling and resuscitation training; document external injuries with photographs; and use hatchery-reared fish as unshocked controls for before-and-after radiograph studies.
