# Description and Assessment of Operations, Maintenance, and Sensitive Species of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #biological-assessment

## Executive Summary

This Biological Assessment (BA), prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region) for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]], evaluates routine ongoing and projected operations and maintenance of the mainstem lower Colorado River from Pierce Ferry to the U.S./Mexico International Boundary. It serves as the initial documentation for formal section 7 consultation under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and as the baseline for a multi-species conservation plan. The assessment addresses seven endangered, two threatened, one proposed threatened and 32 sensitive species in the United States, plus three Mexican species. Its central theme is that Reclamation's discretion is sharply constrained by the [[Law of the River]], so most operations are non-discretionary, while endangered species conservation is largely discretionary.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River drains about 250,000 square miles across seven U.S. states, with more than 120,000 square miles above [[Hoover Dam]]. Lee Ferry, 15.5 miles below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], marks the boundary between the upper and lower basins. The lower basin is largely arid, with little tributary input except during storms, and about 96 percent of its annual supply enters at Lee Ferry.

The BA has two purposes: (1) to document the ongoing [[Section 7 Consultation]] between Reclamation and FWS, and (2) to initiate development of the [[Multi-Species Conservation Program]], which would cover non-Federal activities under section 10 permits. The Department of the Interior and lower basin stakeholders (Arizona, California, Nevada) signed a memorandum of agreement effective August 2, 1995, with a cost-share agreement signed June 26, 1996. The MSCP was originally scheduled for completion around 1998, and the requested consultation period is up to five years.

The mainstem reach is managed through a series of dams and reservoirs:

- **[[Lake Mead]]** (Hoover Dam): storage of about 31.5 million acre-feet; the principal storage facility.
- **[[Lake Mohave]]** (Davis Dam): re-regulation reservoir, storage about 1.8 million acre-feet.
- **[[Lake Havasu]]** (Parker Dam): forebay for the Colorado River Aqueduct and the [[Central Arizona Project]], storage about 648,000 acre-feet.
- Smaller diversion structures: Headgate Rock, Palo Verde, Senator Wash, [[Imperial Dam]], Laguna Dam and Morelos Dam.

The river is divided into river maintenance divisions, from Mohave Valley to the Limitrophe Division on the Mexican border.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic conditions.** Before development the river had extreme seasonal flows (recorded flows at Yuma ranged from 18 to 250,000 cfs), high sediment loads and extensive riparian forests of [[Cottonwood-Willow Association]], arrowweed, mesquite and saltbush. Marshes were small and few, and backwaters, oxbows and sloughs provided essential slack-water nursery and feeding habitat for native fishes. Grinnell (1914) recorded 66 bird species in the cottonwood-willow association.

**Present conditions.** Tamarisk (saltcedar) has replaced much native riparian vegetation. Surface water totals about 241,500 acres, of which about 212,600 acres are reservoir. In 1986 the floodplain supported over 12,000 acres of marsh habitat, and over 400 backwaters exist between Davis and Laguna Dams. Bankline stabilization and flow regulation have reduced scouring, sediment movement and natural marsh formation, and daily and seasonal fluctuations now replace the historic extremes.

**Native fish community.** Ten native fish species historically occurred in the lower river, including three marine-origin species. Six freshwater natives are of conservation concern: bonytail, roundtail chub, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker and woundfin. Introduced species (common carp, channel catfish, mosquitofish, largemouth bass, threadfin shad, striped bass, tilapia, flathead catfish, red shiner) now dominate. Most natives survive only in reservoir-stocked or hatchery-reared populations, and recruitment of the natives to adulthood is largely blocked by predation of early life stages by non-native fishes.

**Salton Sea.** The 1905–1907 Colorado River flood into the Salton Sink created the Salton Sea. Salinity rose above that of ocean water by about 1920, leaving desert pupfish and striped mullet as dominant fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Legal framework:** Most Reclamation operations (water deliveries, Mexican Treaty obligations, flood control minimums, power generation schedules) are non-discretionary under the Law of the River. Discretion is limited to matters such as surplus declarations, non-consumptive uses, channel maintenance, Yuma Desalting Plant operation and endangered species conservation.
- **Species assessed:** 7 endangered, 2 threatened, 1 proposed threatened (flat-tailed horned lizard), 32 sensitive, and 3 Mexican species (desert pupfish, vaquita, totoaba) under [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] jurisdiction.
- **Key aquatic species:** [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] are endangered with designated critical habitat on the mainstem (designated March 21, 1994). The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is endangered. The [[Desert Pupfish]] is endangered and linked to Salton Sea drains.
- **Conservation program:** A July 1995 workshop by Federal and State biologists set immediate stocking goals of 50,000 adults for [[Lake Mohave]] and 25,000 for [[Lake Havasu]] for razorback sucker and bonytail. Reclamation committed to at least half of these goals. Programs include the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (wild larval collection; over 60,000 wild razorback larvae captured in 1996), [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] warm-water retrofits, the [[HAVFISH Project]] (goal 25,000 razorback suckers and 25,000 bonytail over 10 years), the Boulder City Golf Course rearing ponds, and the Hualapai Native Fish Rearing Facility.
- **Reproductive disparity:** A single female razorback sucker produces roughly 100,000 eggs, Colorado squawfish roughly 100,000 and bonytail roughly 50,000, while common carp, channel catfish, largemouth bass and striped bass produce comparable or greater numbers. Predation by non-native fish on early life stages is identified as the primary limit on native recruitment.
- **Riparian and wetland restoration:** Native riparian nurseries (Imperial, Cibola, Havasu National Wildlife Refuges and Lake Mead NPS), about 200 acres of revegetation planned for 1993–1998, and at least $100,000 per year committed for riparian restoration over five years.
- **Habitat projects:** [[Three-Finger Lake Project]] (about 120 acres dredged, roughly $4 million total cost, completion by spring 1997); [[Boulder City Wetland Project]] (reclaimed wastewater wetland, construction November 1995, completion 1996); [[Lower Imperial Division Wetland Enhancement]] (about 3,000 acres, 30-year project life); [[Las Vegas Wash Wetland Restoration]].
- **Consultation schedule:** Final FWS biological opinion scheduled for April 15, 1997. The March 1996 draft drew 29 written comments, and the submission date for the final BA moved from June 30, 1996, to July 1996.
- **Effects analysis:** Reclamation assumes all existing contractual obligations remain in place and will not act unilaterally to void them. Reasonable and prudent alternatives would be pursued if FWS finds jeopardy or adverse modification of critical habitat.
