# Longevity and Age Validation of Cui-ui (Chasmistes cujus) Using Opercle Bones

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript, transmitted by G. Gary Scoppettone (Research Biologist, Seattle National Fishery Research Center, Reno Substation) to Dr. James E. Deacon (University of Nevada), presents an extended age-validation and growth study of the endangered [[Cui-ui]], a catostomid endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Using the opercle (gill cover) bone rather than scales, the authors validated annulus formation over an eight-year interval, aged fish to 41 years, and argue that longevity is widely underestimated among western North American Cypriniformes. The paper also serves as a cover letter context: the author notes the manuscript was adapted for a chapter and is more engaging than a prior recovery-meeting talk.

## Background & Description

- **Focal species:** [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), an endangered catostomid endemic to Pyramid Lake, Nevada, and a species of conservation concern.
- **Problem addressed:** Accurate age determination is essential for fisheries management, yet many age-validation methods have not been properly validated. Incorrect ageing can lead to resource mismanagement.
- **Prior work:** Earlier aging was based largely on scales, which the authors found unreliable in cui-ui and other catostomids. Earlier studies by [[Koch]] (1972, 1976) and [[Sigler]] et al. (1985) produced much lower age estimates.
- **Method framework:** Validation followed [[Scoppettone]] et al. (1986), who identified and followed two dominant year classes (1969 and 1950) through time.
- **Rationale:** Non-game species often receive less research attention, so their longevity is likely underestimated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat constraints:** Cui-ui and June sucker are potamodromous species of desert environments where drought cycles can deny spawning access for extended periods.
- **Terminal lakes:** Species such as [[Tui Chub]] in [[Pyramid Lake]] and [[Eagle Lake]] inhabit terminal lakes where drought-driven salinity rises can prohibit reproduction. Iteroparity (repeat spawning) may give a strong selective advantage in these systems.
- **Body size and habitat:** The authors observed that larger, older fish occur in larger water volumes, suggesting environmental constraints on body size are reduced in such systems.
- **Historical drought:** The 1930s drought was linked to the extinction of the Pyramid Lake [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], and the authors suggest it likely also affected cui-ui spawning access.
- **Hypothesis on Great Basin lakes:** Longevity should be greatest in lakes most influenced by drought-driven salinity (e.g., [[Eagle Lake]] over [[Walker Lake]] and [[Pyramid Lake]]), and shortest in Sierra Nevada freshwater lakes such as Lake Tahoe.

## Key Findings

### Age Validation and Hidden Annuli
- Annulus formation on cui-ui opercles was validated over an eight-year interval, extending the earlier validation by Scoppettone et al. (1986).
- Fish were aged to 41 years. Hidden annuli (up to three) are covered by the support bone and are counted through the opercle's internal structure.
- Hidden annuli increase with age; generally there is one hidden annulus by age 5 and about one by age 6 (per the study's figure summary). Females had more hidden annuli than males among older juveniles, roughly 50% of females versus 20% of males at 9 years.
- Opercle annuli form in late spring to early summer (June to July), while faint false annuli form in late summer or early fall.

### Operculum vs. Scale Ageing
- Scales were unreliable in older cui-ui; for example, the oldest opercle-aged fish (41 years) was aged only 11 years by scales.
- Scale ageing becomes unreliable around the age of sexual maturity, which was approximately 6 years for the first spawners and 8–12 years for most.

### Growth Patterns
- Three distinct growth periods were identified: rapid growth from 0–10 years, intermediate growth from 10–20 years, and very slow growth after about 20 years.
- Strong correlation between age and fork length for ages 0–10 (r = 0.988 for females, 0.999 for males) weakens among older fish (r = 0.626 females, 0.476 males).
- Separate growth curves were fitted for each sex, with a two-curve approach; sexual dimorphism in size was not readily apparent up to 6 years.

### Sample Composition
- The 1969 and 1950 year classes dominated the adult population; the 1969 class share declined from over 85% of collected fish to about 20%, as new year classes entered the spawning run.
- Opercles from ages 1–41 years were available, with a complete annual series from ages 1–12.

### Longevity in Other Cypriniformes
- Among catostomids, the Lost River Sucker was the oldest at 43 years, followed by cui-ui (41 years) and June Sucker (37 years).
- The oldest cyprinid was a Tui Chub from Eagle Lake, at 32 years, while a Sacramento Blackfish was aged at 30 years.
- Several species had previously published age limits surpassed, including Sacramento Blackfish (by a magnitude of six), Pyramid Lake Tui Chub (by three), and Eagle Lake Tui Chub.

### Management Implications
- The authors urge that ageing methods be varied and validated before assigning definitive ages for suspected long-lived fish. Otolith daily growth ring methods were suggested as one option.
- Preconceptions among fisheries biologists, shaped by work on fish such as [[Carp]] and [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes), may cause the longevity of cypriniforms to be underestimated.
- Scale-based age estimates for cypriniforms are likely systematically too low, producing age bias in management.

### Conclusions
- Cui-ui aging with opercles is a robust tool and may serve as a model to uncover unrecognized longevity in other western North American Cypriniformes.
- Iteroparity may be common among large temperate-zone cypriniforms, a hypothesis requiring further study.
