## Executive Summary

This 1995 pre-proposal outlines a plan to build a Geographic Information System (GIS) database cataloguing indigenous and non-indigenous [[Fishes]] across the [[US-Mexico Borderlands]], spanning from the Rio Tijuana (Baja California Norte) to the Rio Grande/Rio Bravo del Norte estuary — a corridor roughly 100 km on either side of the international boundary. The project, led by [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University), [[Jana Fry]] (ASU GIS facility), and [[Salvador Contreras-B.]] (Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León), builds on prior GIS work covering the lower Colorado River basin and Rio Yaqui (Arizona-Sonora corridor) and seeks to extend coverage to the California/Baja California Norte, New Mexico/Chihuahua, and Texas/Chihuahua-Coahuila-Nuevo León-Tamaulipas border segments. The core scientific premise is that fish distributional data, when spatially compiled, can serve as **biological indicators of ecological change** in arid-land aquatic systems — particularly changes driven by water development, pollution, and species invasion.

## Background & Description

Not applicable in the traditional sense — this document is a database/methodology proposal rather than a species-specific morphological study. However, it emphasizes that regional fishes possess distinctive **physiological/ecological adaptations** relevant to their use as indicator organisms, including:

- Tolerance for **high salinity**
- Tolerance for **elevated temperature**
- Tolerance for other "special" extreme habitat conditions typical of arid-land aquatic systems

These physiological tolerances (vs. intolerances) form the functional basis for using species' presence/absence and range shifts as proxies for environmental change (see [[Bioindicator Species]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat range**: Rivers, streams, and associated aquatic habitats of the arid western/southwestern Borderlands, from the [[Rio Tijuana]] and [[Colorado River Basin]] eastward through the [[Rio Yaqui]], [[Gila River]] system, and ultimately to the [[Rio Grande (Rio Bravo Del Norte)]] estuary on the Gulf of Mexico.
- **Distribution patterns**: Some species are wide-ranging generalists; others are highly restricted endemics, with restricted-range species concentrated in the arid western Borderlands where they are most imperiled by human water development.
- **Indicator-species logic**:
  - Expansion of a "tolerant" species (e.g., salinity-tolerant) into new range signals ecological change, especially when co-occurring with disappearance of "intolerant" species.
  - Community-level shifts (loss of intolerant taxa + gain of a tolerant-species cluster below urban areas) provide stronger evidence of pollution than single-species patterns.
  - At broader geographic scales, contraction/"clustering" of widely distributed species into relatively undisturbed refugia indicates landscape-level disturbance.
  - This pattern was previously observed, but not quantified, in the lowermost [[Rio Grande]] over recent decades.
- **Non-indigenous species**: The project explicitly tracks non-indigenous (introduced) fishes alongside indigenous fishes, recognizing invasion as a component of ecological change in the region.

## Key Findings

- **Three-tiered data reliability framework** proposed for compiling borderlands fish records:
  - **Level I** – Museum specimens (irrefutable voucher evidence of occurrence)
  - **Level II** – Peer-reviewed primary literature from recognized authorities
  - **Level III** – Agency/private data lacking voucher specimens or confirmed identifications
- Two near-complete precursor GIS projects (Heidi B. Blasius's MS thesis on northwestern Mexico inland fish biogeography; Minckley's Gila River AZ/Sonora project) establish methodological precedent and are expected to finish by April–May 1996.
- Extensive existing data infrastructure supports the project:
  - ~10,000-entry computerized literature/research file library (Minckley, ASU)
  - Museum holdings across 14 major US institutions (ASU, Cornell, Field Museum, LACM, MCZ, Oklahoma State, U. Alabama, U. Arizona, U. Kansas, U. Michigan, UNM, U. Oklahoma, UT Austin, Tulane, Smithsonian)
  - Mexican fish collection data from UANL's Laboratorio de Ictiología, Texas Parks & Wildlife Department, and New Mexico Game & Fish Department
- **Proposed GIS data layers**: surface hydrology, political/urban boundaries, transportation routes, and digitized fish sampling sites (from 1:24,000 USGS quadrangles and INEGI maps).
- **Deliverables** (10-month project, 2 months base-mapping + 8 months database development, final 2 months overlapping for reporting):
  1. Level I/II database for indigenous fish distribution/change analysis
  2. Equivalent database for non-indigenous fishes
  3. Reliability/value assessment of Level III data for the region
- **Hardware/software**: HP workstation, CalComp digitizer, HP color printer, Arc/Info and Arc/View GIS software, supplemented by PCs for data formatting.
- **Staffing model**: 2 PIs (Minckley, Fry) plus a Mexican co-PI (Contreras-B.), supported by two half-time (50%) hourly student assistants trained in GIS methods.
