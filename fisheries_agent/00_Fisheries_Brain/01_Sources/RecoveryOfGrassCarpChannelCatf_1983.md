# Recovery of Grass Carp, Channel Catfish, and Centrarchids in Artificial Central Arizona Ponds

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae #Centrarchidae #aquaculture

## Executive Summary
This study reports on the community structure and standing crop of introduced fishes recovered during a 1978 rotenone reclamation of seven artificial ponds at Sun Lakes, a retirement community in central Arizona. The ponds, stocked in 1971–1972 with [[Grass Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] for combined aquatic vegetation control and recreational angling, yielded an exceptionally high mean total fish biomass of 532 kg/ha (range 174–1,159 kg/ha). Biomass was dominated by grass carp (67%) and channel catfish (30%), with centrarchids ([[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]]) contributing minor fractions. A significant positive correlation between grass carp and largemouth bass biomass suggested an indirect trophic linkage — vegetation removal by grass carp likely increased prey (forage fish) vulnerability to bass predation. Neither pond area nor angling regime (open vs. closed to daytime fishing) showed simple, unconfounded relationships with yield, though closed ponds trended toward roughly double the biomass of open ponds across all major species, including the non-harvested grass carp — indicating water quality/nutrient gradients, rather than angling alone, likely drove productivity differences.

## Background & Description
Size and weight data were compiled per pond from rotenone-killed specimens (Day 1 collections):

**[[Grass Carp]]** (Ctenopharyngodon idella)
- Mean total length (TL): 63–93 cm among ponds (overall range 53–102 cm); low variability (CV = 0.07)
- Mean weight (WT): 2.7–11.1 kg among ponds (CV ≤ 0.25)
- High within-pond size uniformity; no evidence of natural recruitment

**[[Channel Catfish]]** (Ictalurus punctatus)
- Mean TL: 33–50 cm among ponds (overall range 13–56 cm)
- Mean WT: 0.310–1.060 kg among ponds (overall range 0.015–2.20 kg)
- CV in TL up to 0.23; CV in WT up to 0.43
- Presence of small individuals in two ponds suggested limited natural recruitment
- Numerically predominant species in 4 of 7 ponds

**[[Largemouth Bass]]** (Micropterus salmoides)
- Mean TL: 19–27 cm among ponds (overall range 17–29 cm; CV = 0.13)
- Mean WT: 0.054–0.226 kg (overall range 0.036–0.266 kg; CV = 0.25)
- Numerically dominant species in 2 of 7 ponds

**[[Green Sunfish]]** (Lepomis cyanellus)
- Mean individual WT: 0.005–0.070 kg
- Numerically dominant in only 1 pond
- Contributed minimal biomass (0.1–4.4 kg/ha; ≤1.4% of total biomass in any pond)

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **System type**: Seven small (0.44–1.02 ha), deep (3.4–4.3 m max depth), kidney-shaped, clay/plastic-lined artificial ponds within a golf course/greenbelt community, interconnected by underground pipes and fed by well water, reclaimed wastewater, and irrigation runoff.
- **Hydrological gradient**: Pond 1 (highest elevation, sole well-water input) showed the highest per-area biomass, with a general downstream decline through ponds 5, 6, 7, and 8 — attributed to possible water quality deterioration or progressive nutrient depletion along the flow path.
- **Trophic dynamics**: Grass carp functioned as an effective herbivore/biological control agent, achieving near-total removal of aquatic macrophytes. This vegetation clearing was hypothesized to increase forage fish vulnerability to [[Largemouth Bass]] predation, explaining the strong positive grass carp–bass biomass correlation (r = +0.87). Absence of both vegetation and forage fishes by the study's end suggested bass may have depleted their own prey base.
- **Anthropogenic inputs**: Nutrient-enriched reclaimed wastewater (Pond 4), golf course fertilizer runoff, and incidental feeding of grass clippings by groundskeepers were identified as confounding productivity variables not fully assessable in this study.
- **Angler access effect**: Ponds closed to daytime angling (Ponds 1–4) had nearly double the total biomass (745 kg/ha) of those open 24 hours (Ponds 5–8; 573 kg/ha), a pattern seen even in non-harvested grass carp, implying factors beyond direct angling pressure (e.g., positional/water-quality gradient) were also at play.
- **Recruitment status**: Grass carp showed no recruitment in any pond; recruitment among channel catfish and centrarchids was minimal, indicating an overall low-reproduction, stocked-and-harvested system typical of intensively managed ornamental/recreational ponds rather than a self-sustaining wild fishery.

## Key Findings
- **Total fish biomass**: Mean 532 kg/ha (range 174–1,159 kg/ha) across 7 ponds — comparable to some commercial aquaculture operations and near the upper range reported for southwestern desert streams and canals.
- **Species composition (mean %)**: Grass carp 67% (range 50–83%), Channel catfish 30% (range 12–46%), Largemouth bass 3% (range 1–5%), Green sunfish <1%.
- **Comparative standing crops**: Sun Lakes channel catfish biomass was at or above published North American lake/reservoir maxima (Carlander 1955); largemouth bass was about average; sunfish biomass was relatively low; grass carp biomass was exceptionally high relative to common carp benchmarks.
- **Significant correlation**: Largemouth bass biomass increased linearly with grass carp biomass (y = 0.0343x + 2.645, r = 0.87, p = 0.025, n = 7) — the only statistically significant species-biomass relationship detected.
- **No significant relationships** were found between pond surface area and biomass of any species (r = -0.04 to -0.60, all NS), nor between channel catfish and grass carp biomass, nor between largemouth bass and channel catfish biomass.
- **Angling regime effect**: Closed (no daytime fishing) ponds averaged 745 kg/ha vs. 573 kg/ha in open ponds; largemouth bass and channel catfish were roughly double in closed ponds, but the non-angled grass carp showed the same trend (500 vs. 252 kg/ha), suggesting confounding by non-angling factors (e.g., water quality, nutrient gradient).
- **Elevational/hydrological gradient**: Biomass declined sequentially from the highest-elevation, well-fed Pond 1 downstream through Ponds 5–8, but this trend was disrupted in Ponds 3 and 4, likely due to nutrient enrichment from reclaimed wastewater inputs to Pond 4.
- **Management implication**: Despite being an illegal introduction in Arizona at the time, grass carp were highly effective at total aquatic vegetation control in this closed pond system, while producing exceptionally high standing biomass; interactive effects with game fish reproduction remain unresolved but plausible given co-occurring biomass trends and absence of recruitment across all species.
- **Related study**: Growth patterns of Sun Lakes grass carp (reported separately in [[Marsh And Dhaenens 1984]]) paralleled standing stock trends among ponds, reinforcing the role of localized water quality/nutrient factors in driving system productivity.
