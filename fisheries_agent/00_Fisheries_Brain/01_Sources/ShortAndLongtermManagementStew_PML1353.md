# Fossil Creek Stewardship Meeting: Short- and Long-Term Management, Stewardship, and Education/Outreach Needs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a stakeholder report from the [[Fossil Creek Stewardship Meeting]], held in October 2005 after the [[Fossil Creek Dam Decommissioning]] in July 2005. The meeting was organized by the [[Northern Arizona University]] (NAU) [[Fossil Creek Ecosystem Studies Group]] and brought together managers, researchers, environmentalists, tribal representatives, and citizens. Participants were asked to identify short- and long-term needs for management, stewardship, and education/outreach at [[Fossil Creek]]. Of 42 people invited, 24 took part in discussion groups, and written responses were collected from absent invitees. The report was compiled by Dr. Marty Lee, Paul Hancock, and Alexis Mullen of the [[School of Forestry]] in December 2005. Its findings are intended as recommendations to land management agencies, and they highlight recreation impacts, native fish protection (especially control of [[Crayfish]] and [[Non-Native Species]]), interagency collaboration, a citizen [[Friends of Fossil Creek]] group, and expanded visitor education.

## Background & Description

Fossil Creek is a spring-fed stream in central Arizona that was the focus of a dam decommissioning in July 2005, which the report describes as the start of "a new chapter" for the creek's ecosystem. Management responsibility is shared among several agencies, including the [[Red Rock Ranger District]], [[Payson Ranger District]], [[Coconino National Forest]], [[Tonto National Forest]], the [[U.S. Forest Service]], the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], and the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]. Non-governmental participants included [[The Nature Conservancy]], the [[Grand Canyon Wildlands Council]], and the [[Yavapai-Apache Tribe]].

The meeting was facilitated and recorded by NAU staff and affiliated facilitators. A cover letter dated 27 January 2006 accompanied the results and noted that the full report would be posted on the NAU Fossil Creek project website. The letter is dated October 25, 2005 for the meeting, while the report itself gives October 26, 2005; the report's date is used in the body of this note.

### Methods

- **Invitations:** 42 people were invited with a reminder, map, and directions. Those unable to attend were asked to submit written responses to the three questions. Two written responses were received and included in the report.
- **Participants:** 24 people joined the discussion groups, which were organized into three mixed groups of researchers, managers, non-profit members, tribal leaders, and citizens. Each group had a facilitator and recorder.
- **Three guiding questions:**
  1. What are the short- and long-term management needs for Fossil Creek?
  2. What are the short- and long-term stewardship needs for Fossil Creek?
  3. What are the short- and long-term education and outreach needs for Fossil Creek?
- **Nominal group process:** Each group followed a [[Nominal Group Technique]] with four steps:
  1. Quiet reflection for about 4 minutes, followed by writing ideas.
  2. Idea generation for about 30 minutes, with no critiquing allowed. Short- and long-term needs were recorded on separate flip charts.
  3. Clarification and combination of ideas by group agreement, then voting with colored dots (3 to 8 per participant, depending on the number of items). Short- and long-term needs were voted on separately, and tallies were recorded.
  4. Groups then discussed "the next step."

The meeting lasted about three hours. Each group worked through the three questions independently. The full list of attendees (Appendix A) is summarized here rather than reproduced: 24 attendees and facilitators from agencies, the tribe, non-profit organizations, and NAU, including biologists, rangers, recreation staff, and facilitators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report identifies several concerns that bear directly on the creek's aquatic community:

- **Native fish:** Protecting and maintaining native fisheries is a stated priority in both the short and long term.
- **Crayfish:** Crayfish control appears in both the short- and long-term management lists.
- **Non-native species:** Preventing the reintroduction of non-native fishes and controlling non-natives are repeated concerns.
- **Recreation impacts:** Visitor use is seen as a source of habitat and resource damage, including litter, campfire impacts, and vegetation loss.
- **Cultural and natural resources:** The written response from a land manager emphasized protection of vegetation, wildlife, cultural resources, and the primitive character of the area.

The report does not provide quantitative data on fish populations, water quality, or species counts. Its focus is on management needs rather than field measurements.

## Key Findings

### Short-Term Management Needs (top-ranked)
- Recreation management to enhance visitor experiences and reduce impacts
- Collaborative, interagency management and monitoring
- Protection of native fish, including crayfish control, enforcing regulations, and developing a monitoring plan

### Long-Term Management Needs
- Maintain native fisheries, including control of crayfish and non-native species
- Manage recreation infrastructure, including parking, trails, and motorized use
- Acquire funding and additional human resources
- Increase law enforcement presence

### Short-Term Stewardship Needs
- Form a stewardship group, [[Friends Of Fossil Creek]]
- Build relationships with existing stewardship groups
- Engage in collaborative planning
- Keep the area clean
- Provide stewardship information to users

### Long-Term Stewardship Needs
- Strengthen interaction between Friends of Fossil Creek and agencies
- Keep the area clean
- Consider user fees
- Strengthen law enforcement
- Increase volunteerism, including with local communities and school groups

### Short-Term Education and Outreach Needs
- On-site information sharing targeting users, including media, kiosks, and on-site hosts
- Promote [[Leave No Trace]] principles and stewardship messaging
- Off-site information sharing with schools and communities
- Recruit volunteers
- Share information within and among groups

### Long-Term Education and Outreach Needs
- Educate visitors and local residents about stewardship, ethics, and Leave No Trace
- Educate the public on native fish to prevent reintroduction of non-native species
- Gather and share information on Fossil Creek science and management with the public through symposia and liaison programs

### Written Response from a Land Manager
The absent land manager's response added several points:
- **Stewardship:** Protect vegetation, wildlife, cultural resources, and the primitive character of the area, and ensure that visitors understand and respect these resources.
- **Management:** Short-term needs include establishing a presence on the ground, enforcing rules on campfires and durable surfaces, requiring visitors to pack out trash, and reducing damage to vegetation and aquatic species. Long-term needs include a stable funding base, drawing on congressional appropriations, grants, and fee revenues.

### Next Steps
- The results are primarily recommendations to the land management agencies responsible for Fossil Creek.
- Many individuals and groups expressed interest in joining future efforts, indicating broad support for collaboration.
- The meeting organizers planned to continue collaboration through NAU's efforts to learn about, protect, and manage Fossil Creek's resources.
- The organizers thanked the [[Nina Mason Pulliam Charitable Trust]] for its support of the meeting.
