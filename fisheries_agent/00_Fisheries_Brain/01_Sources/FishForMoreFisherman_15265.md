# Fish for More Fishermen: Michigan Fish Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Michigan Department of Conservation booklet traces the evolution of [[Fish Management]] in Michigan from the 1860s to the mid-20th century. Its stated goal is "to provide fish for more fishermen." The document argues that fish are a crop whose yield depends on the water that produces them, and that management must balance fish numbers against food supply. It describes the shift from hatchery stocking to habitat improvement, the scientific basis for ending routine warm-water plantings, the regulation of trout and warm-water species, and the emerging watershed-based approach to stream improvement.

## Background & Description

- **Origins:** Management began in the 1860s with net regulations to protect [[Whitefish]] and [[Lake Trout]]. A State Board of Fish Commissioners (1873) founded the hatchery system.
- **Institutional change:** Responsibilities passed to the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]] in 1921. A fisheries research technician was added in 1923, marking the start of scientific research. The [[Fish Division]] now carries this work.
- **Scale:** Nearly two million dollars are spent each year on the program, and more than 300 people work in it.
- **Hatchery era:** Trout were stocked by [[Fish Car]] railway service from 1888 to 1924. Much stocking was wasteful because fish were often released in waters unsuitable for them. The [[Au Sable River]] was restocked with hatchery trout after its grayling were declining; it is described as one of America's top twelve trout streams.
- **Resource base:** Michigan has 36,350 miles of rivers and streams, 11,037 lakes, and 38,504 square miles of Great Lakes water (two-thirds of the American-owned total). Its waters hold nearly 150 fish species.
- **Angler trends:** Only 15 to 17 percent of licensed anglers buy trout stamps. Fishing pressure has grown sharply since World War II.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population regulation.** Fish numbers are limited mainly by food supply and shelter. Abundant food produces large, fast-growing fish, while scarce food stunts fish. Overcrowded populations are stunted, and reduced populations recover quickly through natural reproduction.

**Reproductive capacity.** Maximum egg counts reported include walleye 300,000, northern pike 272,160, largemouth bass 109,314, yellow perch 48,000, bluegill 39,950, smallmouth bass 20,825 and pumpkinseed 2,923. In a four-year study on a small lake, each 100,000 fry produced in the first year yielded only 3 bluegills, 5 pumpkinseed, 30 rock bass and 13 largemouth bass surviving to adulthood.

**Spawning requirements.** Largemouth bass, bluegill and pumpkinseed spawn over gravel or plant roots. Bluegills spawn from mid-May to mid-August. [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]] and [[Walleye]] have more specialized needs, which limits their distribution.

**Warm-water rivers.** Many southern Michigan warm-water rivers are polluted by sewage, industrial waste and soil erosion. A [[Huron River]] survey found bass concentrated in good pools, with shallow stretches holding few bass and stunted [[Rock Bass]].

**Watersheds.** Erosion, siltation, and warming reduce trout habitat. Watershed-scale work reduces flood crests, maintains summer flows, raises groundwater, and lowers stream temperatures.

**Beaver dams.** Beaver dams raise stream temperatures and can cause severe summer oxygen depletion in ponds. They can also create short-term trout fishing and may be an asset on some streams, depending on local conditions.

## Key Findings

### Warm-water fish management

- **End of routine stocking:** The Conservation Commission abandoned routine plantings of bass, bluegills, walleyes and other non-trout species in 1946. The document also says routine plantings were abandoned "in 1945." Planting was limited to waters lacking fish, new species introductions, and post-catastrophe restocking.
- **Evidence:** Fishing success did not decline after the policy change. Catch-per-hour before and after differed by only a statistical fraction of one fish.
- **Test plantings:** Experimental plantings in 1945 onward added little to lake populations and nothing to angler catch. Disease risk was another argument against routine stocking.
- **Core conclusion:** "Planting more fish in lakes already too crowded with fish is wasteful, and wrong." Lakes produce more fish only when they produce more food.
- **Habitat improvement:** The document calls habitat improvement "the key" to warm-water fish. Measures include winter drawdowns for [[Northern Pike]], gravel nesting piles for [[Smallmouth Bass]], spawning slabs for minnows, [[Brush Shelters]] (persisting at least ten years), and impoundments.
- **Rough fish:** Gars and dogfish are considered harmless and often beneficial. Carp are scarce in Michigan, so their threat is less serious than in neighboring states.
- **Predators:** Fish-eating birds, reptiles and mammals mostly eat suckers, carp and minnows. They take pan fish, usually small ones three to five inches long.

### Regulations

- **Pan fish liberalization:** A 1946 test on 12 lakes led to removal of pan fish size limits, year-round seasons for many species, and closed-season elimination for bluegills and sunfish by 1951.
- **Predatory fish:** Restrictions on [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Muskellunge]] and [[Walleye]] were retained because Michigan has a shorter growing season and less productive soils than some states.
- **Experiments from 1954:** A five-year test on nine lakes compared no closed season, no size limits, and a 16-inch bass or 24-inch pike limit.
- **Winter spearing:** Creel census found an average of twelve hours to spear one pike. Spearing did not take more females or larger fish than spring open-water fishing. Research points to spawning-habitat destruction through draining swamps as the main cause of pike decline.

### Trout management

- **Catch decline:** The catch per hour for trout is falling season after season despite planting, regulation, habitat work and research.
- **Planting:** Trout planting established most of Michigan's great trout waters. Emphasis has shifted to legal-size fish, which are expensive and limited in number.
- **Planting timing:** Spring and open-season plantings outperform fall plantings. Sublegal fish (4 to 6.9 inches) stocked in spring are better than fall fingerlings but cost almost as much as spring legals.
- **Species differences:** Brook and rainbow trout give better immediate returns than brown trout. Rainbow plantings contribute for as long as browns.
- **Season:** Brook and brown trout lose condition in late summer and regain it by mid-April to late June. Proposed changes include a season opening on the second Saturday in May and closing on Labor Day.
- **Daily limit:** Only six percent of anglers caught more than eight trout. The limit could be reduced to five without loss of satisfaction, partly because of a psychological "par for the course" effect.
- **Size limits:** Female brook trout in the larger streams mature at about 8.5 inches, implying a 9- to 10-inch limit to protect first spawning. Brown trout need about 11 inches. Rainbows, which need four growing seasons to mature, may need 13 to 14 inches on Great Lakes run fisheries.
- **Hooking mortality:** The [[Artificial Flies]] killed 2.7 percent of trout hooked, while worm baits killed an average of 36.8 percent. The worm-baited hook was 13.6 times as deadly. Only about five percent of trout stream mileage can be fished effectively with flies.

### Predators of trout

- **Fish:** [[Northern Pike]] eat many planted trout in lakes, so pike lakes are not stocked.
- **Birds:** Kingfishers had trout in about 30 percent of stomach contents. Nine blue herons yielded 64 percent trout. American mergansers contained 88 percent trout.
- **Mammals:** Of 297 mink stomachs, 3.6 percent of fish eaten were trout. Of about 500 raccoon fecal samples, four showed fish remains.
- **Biologists' view:** Food supply matters more than predators in controlling the final yield to anglers. Predators are most damaging at concentrated rearing stations.

### Watershed program and access

- **Watershed program:** Stream improvement moved from channel devices on public land to entire watersheds after the Rifle River work began in 1950. Partners include federal agencies, the Soil Conservation Service, Soil Conservation Districts, the U.S. Geological Survey and the State Water Resources Commission. Federal Dingell-Johnson funds help finance the work.
- **Public access:** Since 1939, part of each fishing license dollar has been earmarked for land acquisition for public fishing sites.

### Research needs

The booklet calls expanded warm-water research "the greatest single need." Open questions include species interrelationships, whether [[Yellow Perch]] overabundance affects bluegills and bass, whether walleyes and pike compete, and what limits [[Muskellunge]] numbers.
