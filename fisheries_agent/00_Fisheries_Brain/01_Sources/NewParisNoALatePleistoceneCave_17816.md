# Late Pleistocene Cave Deposit at New Paris Sinkhole No. 4, Bedford County, Pennsylvania

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mammalogy #paleoecology

## Executive Summary

This 1964 *Bulletin of the [[National Speleological Society]]* paper (Guilday, Martin & McCrady, with a reptile and amphibian chapter by Richmond) reports the excavation of a roughly 9.1 m column of surface-derived mud and rubble from [[Sinkhole No. 4]] ("Lloyd's Rock Hole") near New Paris, Pennsylvania. The deposit yielded at least 2,769 vertebrates, a pollen profile, and a [[Carbon-14 Dating|radiocarbon date]] of about 11,300 years B.P. The authors interpret the late-glacial environment as cool boreal parkland early in infill, progressively reforesting during a later warming that remained boreal.

Note: the source concerns terrestrial and cave-dwelling vertebrates; no fish are reported, so the ichthyology tags reflect the template and are not supported by the text.

## Background & Description

The paper's central aim is to document late-glacial range adjustments of boreal birds and mammals in eastern North America, and to separate [[Boreal Forest]] from [[Boreal Woodland]], which pollen alone cannot reliably do. Small vertebrates are treated as sensitive indicators of tundra, woodland and forest.

The sinkhole lies on the west flank of [[Chestnut Ridge]], an anticline of Silurian-Devonian limestone and sandstone, at about 465 m elevation. Dome-pit sinkholes fill slowly with surface-derived debris. Earlier work at [[New Paris Sinkhole No. 2]] (Elk Hole) yielded a Recent fauna of 26 mammal species and a wolf date of 1,875 ± 100 yr B.P.

Excavation (1956–1963) was carried out by the [[Pittsburgh Grotto]] with [[Carnegie Museum]], using 0.3 m levels, 6 mm and 1.5 mm screens, and at least 125 tons of matrix processed. Bone identifications and catalogue data are held at Carnegie Museum.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate and setting:** The valley lies in the rain shadow of [[Allegheny Mountain]]; present precipitation is about 89–130 cm/yr depending on location. The authors suggest a prehistoric rain shadow was likely, with jack pine favoured by relative aridity.
- **Biotic zonation:** Several life zones (tundra, Hudsonian, Canadian, Carolinian) were probably juxtaposed under a critical climate regime, a pattern only weakly seen today.
- **Infill rates:** A Recent wolf burial gave roughly 1 m per 1,000 years. The 11,300 yr date implies a minimum of 0.5 m per thousand years for the upper fill, with faster infill during the colder initial phase.
- **Taphonomy:** Many skeletons were articulated and uncrushed, though a few rodent-gnawed remains and modern pollen in rodent scats indicate some contamination from above.
- **Pollen:** Pollen is poorly preserved but abundant enough in 12 of 15 samples. Unit B (6.4–8.5 m) is dominated by small pine pollen, sedge and spruce. Unit A (3.8–5.8 m) has more oak, alder, birch and fern. Two radiocarbon dates conflict: Y-727 (charcoal, 11,300 ± 1,000 yr B.P.) and M-1067 (bone, 9,340 ± 500 or 9,540 ± 500 yr B.P., sources differ).

## Key Findings

1. **Fauna:** Over 2,700 vertebrates were recovered (2,769 is given as the minimum). Mammals dominate, with [[Microtus xanthognathus]] (yellow-cheeked vole) the most abundant terrestrial mammal, exceeding 40% of mammals at the 8.8 m level. The collared lemming [[Dicrostonyx hudsonius]] (three individuals) is the first North American fossil record outside Alaska and indicates nearby tundra.
2. **Birds and herps:** Birds were scarce (7 species, 13 individuals), including sharp-tailed grouse and wild turkey. Amphibians included the Hudson Bay toad, and reptiles included garter snakes, rattlesnakes and copperheads. Lizard and turtle remains were absent.
3. **Vertical succession:** Boreal woodland species dominate the lower units (9–6 m). Forest and temperate species, including [[Clethrionomys gapperi]] and [[Glaucomys sabrinus]], increase upward. The authors interpret this as a shift from boreal parkland to boreal forest.
4. **Bergmann's response:** Of 13 species analysed, nine showed a positive response (larger than modern central Appalachian populations) and four a negative response, interpreted as adaptation to boreal conditions rather than time-driven evolution.
5. **Prairie elements:** Thirteen-lined ground squirrel and sharp-tailed grouse indicate a late-glacial steppe invasion predating the mid-post-glacial prairie peninsula, supporting [[Schmidt]]'s (1938) earlier hypothesis.
6. **Extinction and humans:** The long-nosed peccary [[Mylohyus nasutus]] occurred in a boreal context, which the authors use to argue against climatic causes for its extinction, consistent with Paleo-Indian hunting as a contributing factor.
7. **Refugia:** The authors conclude the boreal fauna and pollen give little support for an Appalachian ice-age refugium for temperate forest; conditions were boreal rather than hemlock-hardwood forest.
8. **Chronology caveat:** The date conflict (11,300 vs. ~9,500 yr B.P.) is discussed at length. The authors favour the charcoal date, and Binford's statistical analysis suggests the true date is closer to 11,300.
9. **Chemical analysis:** Fossil hare bone had higher aluminium and silicon from clay infiltration, with fluorine rising from 0.25% to 1.69%.
