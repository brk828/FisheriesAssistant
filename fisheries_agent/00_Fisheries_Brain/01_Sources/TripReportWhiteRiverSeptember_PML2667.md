# Loach Minnow Distribution Survey, White River Drainage, Arizona (September 1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Ecological Services trip report, dated September 1988, documents a two-day field survey of the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) in the [[White River]] drainage on the [[White Mountain Apache Reservation]], Arizona. The visit supported a [[Section 7 Consultation]] with the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]] on the proposed [[Miner Flat Dam and Reservoir]]. The primary goal was to map the extent of loach minnow distribution in the North Fork and mainstem White River, because the East Fork population had last been documented in February 1988 and was not resampled. Sampling at five sites used backpack-style pulsed DC electrofishing with seines. Loach minnow were not captured at the first site, and the report identifies a deep canyon near the North Fork mouth as a possible barrier to upstream movement.

## Background & Description

- **Species of concern:** The loach minnow is a small, bottom-dwelling cyprinid of the desert Southwest. Its status and distribution in the project area were the central questions of the consultation.
- **Project context:** The Miner Flat project was the subject of a Section 7 consultation under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The proposed diversion for irrigation of benchlands was of particular concern because it lay near the sampled reaches.
- **Survey team:** Sampling was led by the author (Sally Stefferud, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) with assistance from Jim Hanson of the [[Fisheries Assistance Project]], Ron Kenney, and Jim Smith. Lodging and access were arranged through [[Alchesay National Fish Hatchery]], where the crew staged.
- **Travel and timing:** The author left Phoenix at 1:30 p.m. on Monday, 19 September 1988, and sampling began at 8:30 a.m. on Tuesday, 20 September 1988. The author left the White River at about 3:30 p.m. and returned to Phoenix at about 8:00 p.m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow and habitat:** Flows in both the North Fork and the mainstem were described as relatively low and clear. Riparian vegetation was in good condition, with extensive stands of large willow and cottonwood.
- **Sampling method:** Fish were collected with a generator-powered pulsed DC shocker mounted on two four-wheel ATVs. A single anode probe on a 200-foot cable was used with a cable cathode laid in the river. Stunned fish were collected with a 9-foot seine at the first site. Because that seine was unwieldy in the current, a 4-foot kick seine was used at later sites. Sampling targeted habitats judged suitable for loach minnow.
- **Sampling strategy:** The priority was to sample as many sites as possible rather than intensively sampling one reach, to clarify the species' distribution.
- **East Fork:** The East Fork was not sampled. It holds the largest known loach minnow population, and the report notes that native fishes there could be affected by increased numbers of exotic predators if the project proceeds. The project is not expected to directly affect the East Fork.
- **Non-target species:** Associated native fishes captured included [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) and [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*). Speckled dace and rainbow trout were also reported, with some specimens preserved for identification confirmation.
- **Canyon barrier:** A narrow, vertical-walled canyon near the mouth of the North Fork, roughly 100 feet deep, was inspected from the bridge. Its deep pools and sheer cliffs appear impassable to backpack electrofishing gear, and the report suggests it may be the barrier that prevents loach minnow from moving upstream into the North Fork. No suitable habitat was observed there, but the author recommended checking for riffles within the canyon.

## Key Findings

1. **Sampling coverage:** Five sites were sampled on the North Fork and mainstem White River, but the East Fork was excluded.
2. **Loach minnow absence at site 1:** No loach minnow were taken at the confluence of the East and North Forks (the location of previous collections), although riffle habitat there appeared suitable.
3. **Mainstem record:** A loach minnow collection in the mainstem was noted at a site about one river mile below the proposed diversion for the irrigation project, which the author identified as the area of greatest project concern.
4. **Upstream barrier hypothesis:** The absence of loach minnow in the North Fork may be due to the canyon at its mouth rather than to a lack of suitable habitat.
5. **Recommendations:** Future work should prioritize (a) resampling the East Fork population, which was last documented in February 1988; (b) checking riffles within the North Fork canyon; and (c) assessing the risk that the project's reservoir will increase exotic predator numbers, which could harm native fishes.
6. **Documentation:** Voucher specimens of unidentified fish were preserved for later confirmation, and a site-by-site record was kept of species captured.
