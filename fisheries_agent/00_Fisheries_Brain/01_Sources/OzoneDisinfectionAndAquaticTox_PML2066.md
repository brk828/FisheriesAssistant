# Ozone Disinfection and Aquatic Toxicity: EPA Guidance, Vendor Data and Correspondence (2001)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ozone-toxicology

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled research file assembled by Carol Pacey (Arizona State University, Department of Biology) in early 2001. It brings together the EPA *Alternative Disinfectants and Oxidants* guidance chapter on ozone (April 1999), manufacturer and vendor material, email correspondence with water utilities and ozone suppliers, toxicity database extracts, and EPA and scientific literature on ozone's effects on fish and aquatic organisms. The purpose was to judge whether applying ozone to kill non-native aquatic species in a flowing canal, the [[Central Arizona Project]] near Lake Havasu, is technically and economically feasible. The correspondence indicates that vendors considered the cost high and a pilot study necessary. The toxicity literature shows that ozone is lethal to fish and invertebrates at low doses, which supports the concept but raises ecological and cost concerns.

## Background & Description

**Ozone chemistry and history.** The EPA guidance describes ozone as a gas that is unstable, highly oxidizing and toxic. It is generated on site, usually by [[Corona Discharge]] from oxygen or air. Ozone was first used for drinking water treatment in 1893 in the Netherlands. The [[Los Angeles Aqueduct Filtration Plant]] began service in 1987 (the vendor email gives 1987; the guidance text states 1997 for ozone adoption in the US context). As of April 1998, the guidance reports 29 operating plants in the United States. Ozone's aqueous solubility is low (about 570 mg/L at 20 °C for 100% ozone), and ozone decays rapidly, especially at high pH and high temperature.

**Treatment system components.** The guidance describes four basic components: a gas feed system (air, high-purity oxygen or both), an ozone generator, an ozone contactor, and off-gas destruction. The contactor types discussed are [[Bubble Diffuser Contactor]], injector and turbine mixer designs. Off-gas must be destroyed because ozone is toxic. The off-gas destruct unit reduces concentrations to 0.1 ppm, the [[Occupational Safety and Health Administration]] (OSHA) limit for an eight-hour shift.

**Facility profile (Los Angeles Aqueduct Filtration Plant).** Per the plant's process engineer, Don Christie, the plant treats 600 MGD (930 cfs). Its ozone system has a 50 ton/day cryogenic oxygen plant and 19,000 lb/day installed ozone capacity, with about 550 tons of liquid oxygen storage. The original capital cost was $13.4 million, with about $1.5 million in upgrades. Ozone production costs about 2 kWh per lb (roughly $0.50/lb). Maintenance is about $500,000 per year and power about $750,000 per year. The typical ozone dose is about 1.2 mg/L, and the stated goal is 99% inactivation under the EPA surface water treatment rule.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ozone toxicity to aquatic organisms.** The file includes several toxicity records:

- Ozone-produced oxidants (OPO) were lethal to striped bass and other estuarine species. Early life stages were most sensitive; juveniles and adults were more resistant. Fish died of hypoxia after gill damage, and moderate gill damage repaired within days in clean water.
- Rainbow trout studies (Fukunaga et al., 1999) showed ozone causes red blood cell hemolysis and methemoglobin formation through oxidative mechanisms rather than through extracellular ozone.
- White perch (*Morone americana*) and bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*) exposures showed lethal and sublethal responses. Reported bluegill and white perch LC50 values in the ECOTOX extract range from about 0.0002 to 4.0 mg/L depending on exposure duration, life stage and water quality. The ECOTOX extract covers 105 aquatic records, including [[Bluegill]], [[White Perch]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Zebra Mussel]] and white sucker.
- A vendor-supplied observation reported that 0.4 ppm ozone for 4 minutes kills bacteria, viruses, mold and fungi, but this is promotional material and was not peer reviewed.

**Ozone in indoor air.** The EPA public information document *Ozone Generators That Are Sold as Air Cleaners* concludes that ozone generators do not effectively remove indoor pollutants at safe concentrations and can exceed health limits when used as directed. Health effects include lung damage, chest pain and coughing, and exposure limits are summarized in a table of standards.

**Ozone depletion context.** A 2001 EPA/NOAA summary and a related web page describe stratospheric ozone depletion by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), the 1987 Montreal Protocol, and increased UV-B exposure at ground level.

**Non-native species control context.** The Central Arizona Project canal (about 3,000 cfs, 60 ft wide, 16 ft deep) hosts exotic aquatic species in numbers that make manual removal ineffective. Chemical control (for example rotenone) was also considered.

## Key Findings

- **Feasibility inquiry for canal treatment.** Carol Pacey sought information on ozone to kill aquatic species in a 3,000 cfs canal. Vendors responded differently:
  - TriO3 Industries (Clarence Marsden) said cost would be "extremely high." He reported pilot studies showing juvenile shrimp tolerated up to 5 ppm, while white crabs died within 12 minutes at 1 ppm.
  - L. Joseph Bollyky (BAI) said an ozone system "could very likely be designed" for aquatic species control, but recommended a pilot study. He estimated an order-of-magnitude requirement of about 10,000 lb/day, explicitly as a rough guideline.
  - Praxair stated it had no experience with this application and referred Pacey to ozone consultants.
  - A French contact (Jean-Yves Penot) noted that seawater ozonation is for killing bacteria and viruses, not fish.
- **Utility response.** The Tonsina ozone project (Alaska) is a BP project. The FWS RCAC project manager stated that information should come from BP, and an ozone-based system for the Tonsina is described as a trial project.
- **Plant data.** The Los Angeles plant data came from Don Christie, with a referral from Jim Yannotta.
- **Dose and CT concepts.** The EPA guidance emphasizes CT requirements (concentration × contact time), that ozone decay reduces CT credit, and that Giardia and Cryptosporidium inactivation requires substantial CT values.
- **Byproducts.** Ozone does not form halogenated DBPs directly, but bromide in source water produces brominated byproducts, including bromate ion, which is a probable human carcinogen. Bromate concentrations up to 60 µg/L have been reported in ozonated water.
- **Safety requirements.** Ozone systems require ventilation, detectors, interlocks and a two-stage alarm (0.1 mg/L and 0.3 mg/L), plus isolation of ozone equipment from the rest of the plant.
- **Pilot study recommendation.** Both the vendor and Marsh's team concluded that a pilot study would be needed to define ozone dose, contact time and generator capacity before a canal-scale decision.
- **Cost reference points.** LA plant capital cost of $13.4 million; operating cost roughly $1.25 million per year (maintenance plus power).
- **Open questions.** Marsh's email (March 2001) asked for clarifications on a citation (Burton et al. 1981), Marsden's affiliation, and the wording on delayed symptoms and bromide/bromate production in the draft.
