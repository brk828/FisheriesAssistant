# Species Relationships Among Fishes of the Genus Gila in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Robert Rush Miller]] and [[W. Daniel Sable]] examines the taxonomy of the chubs of the [[Gila Robusta Complex]] (family Cyprinidae) inhabiting the [[Colorado River]] basin and certain rivers in northwestern Mexico. The authors argue that the group has been confused by subjective, coarsely coded morphological characters. Using [[Principal Components Analysis]] of 34 meristic and morphometric characters from 140 specimens, they show that large-river specimens segregate into three distinct clusters, which they treat as the species [[Gila Robusta]] (roundtail chub), [[Gila Elegans]] (bonytail), and [[Gila Cypha]] (humpback chub). They also present diagnostic identification keys, juvenile identification criteria, and a corrected type locality for the species. The authors conclude that the fishes are threatened by dams, reservoir conditions, and introduced competitors, and that [[Hybridization]] following the creation of [[Lake Powell]] may produce a single generalized species.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic problem:** Earlier work by [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970) used subjective codes for nuchal hump form and converted continuous morphometric variables into discontinuous states, obscuring intermediacy. The nuchal convexity had never been measured precisely or quantitatively related to other characters.
- **Objectives:** (1) Determine the validity and relationships of the roundtail, bonytail, and humpback chubs in big-river habitat; (2) determine their relationships to other middle and upper Colorado River populations of Gila; (3) find characters for identifying young and adult G. robusta, G. elegans, G. cypha, and possible hybrids.
- **Scope:** The Gila River basin complex was not treated, except to note that [[Gila Intermedia]] may be a separate evolutionary line secondarily related to G. robusta.
- **Diagnostic traits used:** Fin-ray counts, vertebral numbers, gill rakers, caudal peduncle depth and length, snout shape, fin position, and nuchal hump form.
- **Nomenclature:** The holotype (USNM 251) and three syntypes of G. robusta (USNM 246) were described from the "Zuni River, New Mexico" by [[Baird and Girard]] (1853, 1854). The authors argue that the type locality is actually Grand Falls on the [[Little Colorado River]] (see [[Sitgreaves Expedition]]). G. gracilis is a synonym of G. robusta.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat specialization:** The three species are the most extreme members of the genus in their specialization for the big-river habitat of the Colorado River. They appear to be omnivorous carnivores with specializations related to habitat. Food-processing characters (jaws, teeth) are similar, except that G. robusta has fewer gill rakers and G. elegans has more.
- **Diversity and habitat size:** The three species do not coexist in smaller tributaries. In small tributaries such as the [[Virgin River]] and possibly the [[San Juan River]], a single intermediate-morphology species seems to be selected for. In the larger Gila River tributary systems, a mosaic of generalized forms occurs.
- **Dam effects:** Dams destroy swift-water habitat above the obstruction and drastically decrease temperature and turbidity below it. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] destroyed main-river habitat and created what the authors describe as a natural experiment. Early indications are that the two more specialized species will not persist in the [[Lake Powell]] environment.
- **Threats:** Introduced exotic competitors and predators (especially on young) are a threat, particularly in modified areas. The reservoir is silting in rapidly, which the authors suggest prevents the stability needed for re-oriented diversification.
- **Historical disturbance:** A 1962 poisoning of the upper [[Green River]] (Miller 1963b) was followed by erroneous public statements about these fishes. The paper cites a claim by [[Stroud]] (1963) that [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] made available large numbers of humpback chubs, all supposedly male. Subsequent surveys by [[Vanicek]] (1967) and [[Vanicek et al.]] (1970) found G. cypha to be very rare below the Yampa River mouth: three specimens were taken in 1963, and none in 1964–1966.
- **Colorado Squawfish records:** Holden and Stalnaker (1975) reported [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius) in Grand Canyon, but the two specimens came from between Glen Canyon Dam and Lees Ferry. The only valid Grand Canyon record is an adult of about 320 mm S.L., caught in 1975 at the mouth of Havasu Creek (ASU 7087). Squawfish formerly moved up the Little Colorado River to the base of [[Grand Falls]].
- **Research needs:** The authors call for research on habitat, food, reproduction, recruitment, hydrodynamics, and energetics, which they consider essential for management.

## Key Findings

1. **Three distinct populations:** PCA of 34 characters across 140 specimens showed morphological segregation of large-river specimens into three clusters with little or no overlap. The segregation is based on body proportions, fin-ray counts, and vertebral numbers, not the nuchal hump.
2. **Few intermediates:** In the Green River at least, very few intermediates occur, and most specimens (including the "intergrades" of Holden and Stalnaker 1970: Figure 4) can be assigned to G. cypha or G. elegans.
3. **Species status:** G. robusta, G. elegans, and G. cypha have diverged in fin-ray counts, vertebrae, gill rakers, caudal peduncle, snout shape, fin position, and nuchal hump form. G. cypha is intermediate in general but extreme in several respects.
4. **Evolutionary relationships:** G. cypha and G. elegans may have been derived from G. robusta by separate speciation events. G. robusta retains the most primitive characters, and neither G. cypha nor G. elegans is likely ancestral to the others.
5. **Peripheral populations uncertain:** Populations in tributaries (including [[Gila Robusta Seminuda]] of the Virgin River and a similar San Juan population) were not fully resolved. The authors use the term "Gila robusta superspecies" for the entire complex, while acknowledging that species isolating mechanisms may break down under reservoir conditions.
6. **Hybridization at Lake Powell:** The three species appear to be breaking down locally by hybridization. The authors predict that a single species of mixed origin and generalized characteristics will appear.
7. **Identification key:** Diagnostic couplets separate G. robusta (no nuchal hump in adults; distance from anal origin to caudal base much less than to opercle), G. cypha (abrupt nuchal hump; snout overhangs upper lip; in young under 150 mm, eye diameter less than 2/3 caudal peduncle depth), and G. elegans (nuchal hump not abrupt; snout not overhanging; in young under 150 mm, eye diameter greater than 2/3 caudal peduncle depth).
8. **Juvenile identification:** Using the new criteria, the authors identified G. robusta robusta (UMMZ 162818) to 20 mm, G. cypha (UMMZ 182415) to 54 mm, and G. elegans (UMMZ 162846) to 22 mm in total length. Earlier University of Michigan collections contained young as small as 22–40 mm S.L. identified as early as 1926, and humpback chubs as small as 43 mm S.L. had been determined by 1968. Earlier work by [[Vanicek and Kramer]] (1969) had combined all specimens under 200 mm total length as "Colorado chub."
9. **Type locality correction:** Based on Sitgreaves' (1854) report and map, the authors conclude that Grand Falls, not the Zuni River, is the correct type locality for G. elegans and G. robusta. The Zuni River was described as a mere rivulet, and G. elegans is unknown from the Little Colorado basin above Grand Falls, a barrier 56 meters high (Dryer 1965).
10. **Conservation:** The perpetuation of these species requires large unspoiled main-channel sections. Dams, altered temperature and turbidity, and exotic predators and competitors are the primary threats. Clear taxonomic characters are needed to support management of these endangered or threatened fishes.
