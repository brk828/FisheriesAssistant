# Bonytail Roundup Report: Lake Mohave, AZ-NV, 2006

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Arizona Fishery Resources Office, Parker; June 2006) documents a May 2006 netting effort on [[Lake Mohave]] to collect wild [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) for the [[Broodstock Management|broodstock]] at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Wild bonytail are nearly extinct in the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. Sampling with [[Trammel Net Sampling|trammel nets]] over four nights captured 199 fish, but only two bonytail, both non-recaptures collected on separate days. The authors argue that wild fish are needed to refresh a genetically narrow hatchery stock.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Bonytail (*Gila elegans*), a cyprinid native to the Colorado River system. Historically recorded from Grand Canyon, [[Lake Mead]], Lake Mohave, the river from Parker Dam to Yuma, the historic delta in Sonora, Mexico, and the Salton Sea.
- **Conservation status:** Listed as endangered in 1980 under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The [[Recovery Plan]] was completed in 1991, and [[Critical Habitat]] was designated in 1993.
- **Decline context:** Bonytail taken after 1980 were largely recaptured or collected by researchers seeking wild fish for broodstock. The report describes part of that effort.

### Genetic Concerns

- In 1981, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service produced progeny from a very small number of wild bonytail (described as "fewer" than the original count in the OCR text).
- The current broodstock numbers **110 fish** (R. Hamman, pers. comm., 22 May 2006). It includes adults captured in Lake Mohave between 1980 and the present.
- The authors state that these fish are genetically sound and are the best broodstock available. They also carry representation of each original cross.
- Because the broodstock is aging, the authors stress the need to recover additional wild individuals. Hedrick et al. (2000) estimated that **one wild fish would raise genetic diversity by about 20%**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Study Area and Methods

- **Survey site:** Lake Mohave, from the Bailey Cove area to Arizona Cove near Katherine Landing. The site was chosen because bonytail had been collected there in the previous five years.
- **Gear:** Six [[Trammel Net Sampling|trammel nets]] (100 m long) were fished for four nights. Nets were tied to shore and run perpendicular or angled to the shoreline, mostly in depths under 5 m. Nets were fished for 12 hours, set in early evening, and run in early morning.
- **Handling:** Native fish were measured (total length, mm) and weighed (g), then tagged with 400 MHz tags, except bonytail over 400 mm total length. Non-native fish were counted and released. Any bonytail judged to be wild were to be transported to the hatchery, with a fin clip taken for [[Genetic Analysis]].
- **Dates:** Sampling took place 16–19 May 2006.

### Catch Overview

- **Effort:** 24 trammel net sets in total (6 nets × 4 nights).
- **Total catch:** 199 fish (reported in the Results section). Species included [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], and [[Green Sunfish]], among others.
- **Bonytail:** Two wild bonytail were captured on separate days at the same location. Neither was a recapture. One was a female, and the other had breeding tubercles. Neither was scanned for wire tags. Both were processed and released, since the hatchery could not accept them that season.

## Key Findings

1. **Wild bonytail remain extremely scarce.** Only two were captured among 199 fish in four nights of effort. Non-native species dominated the catch.
2. **Broodstock genetic diversity is limited.** The 110-fish hatchery stock descends from a small founder group. Adding wild fish could substantially raise diversity.
3. **Ongoing capture is a conservation priority.** Lake Mohave remains a key site for collecting wild broodstock, and the authors recommend continued monitoring.
4. **Past management results matter.** The authors note that the outcomes of earlier recovery actions, including those for [[Razorback Sucker]], should guide future decisions.
5. **Information needs.** The report calls for continued monitoring to maintain wild bonytail populations in the lower Colorado River and to inform future recovery and management, citing USFWS (2004).
