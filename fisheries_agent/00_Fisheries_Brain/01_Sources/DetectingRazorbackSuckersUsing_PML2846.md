# Detecting Razorback Suckers Using PIT Tag Antennas in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Webber and Beers (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Colorado River Fish Project) tested whether passive instream flat-plate antennas (PIAs) could detect [[Razorback Sucker]]s tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags) at a spawning site, [[Razorback Bar]], in the [[Green River]], Utah. Two PIAs were deployed in spring 2012 (29 days of operation) and 2013 (about 90 days). Together they detected 612 tagged fish, including 569 razorback suckers and 19 [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. Most of the razorback suckers had not been detected by researchers since stocking. The authors conclude that passive detection can substantially improve the detectability, precision, and accuracy of survival and population estimates for endangered Colorado River basin fishes.

## Background & Description

**Species of concern.** The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid endemic to the Colorado River basin and is listed as endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. Declines are attributed to dams (population fragmentation, altered hydrography and thermal regimes) and predation by nonnative fishes. In the early 1990s the upper Colorado River basin (UCRB) population was estimated at as low as 500 individuals (Modde et al. 1996). The [[Stocking Program]] began in 1995 and has released more than 200,000 razorback suckers into the UCRB. Increased larval captures in the last decade suggest that stocked fish are spawning successfully (Bestgen et al. 2011).

**Monitoring gap.** Capture and recapture data from non-target projects were insufficient for precise [[Mark-Recapture Models]] of razorback sucker population size and survival. Low sample sizes reduce precision and accuracy (Otis et al. 1978). The authors cite the 1998 [[Recovery Plan]] and Bestgen et al. (2012) as calling for more recaptures.

**Study rationale.** Razorback Bar, known historically as the Escalante or Jensen spawning site, concentrates spawning razorback suckers in spring. Earlier radiotelemetry work there (Modde et al. 2005) required [[Electrofishing]], which may discourage spawning. PIAs detect tagged fish within a set distance without handling them. Webber et al. (2012) had previously used PIAs successfully on [[Bluehead Sucker]]s in the Weber River, documenting 59 of 98 tagged fish over six months.

**Tag and handling context.** Researchers handled 863 adult razorback suckers in 2012–2013 at Razorback Bar. Of these, 63 were untagged (7.3%). This total is inferred from the reported percentage because the scanned count is unclear. Tag loss in related species is reported as below 3% (Ward and David 2006; Ward et al. 2008). Colorado pikeminnow are not stocked. They are wild-produced and tagged only when captured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site and hydrology.** Razorback Bar lies along the eastern channel of the Green River, where the river flows around a large island. The spawning area covered about 2 ha. Razorback suckers spawn on the ascending limb of the hydrograph, usually from late April to early June. 2012 was a drought year with lower-than-average peak flows, so the PIAs were inundated only twice by a few centimeters in April, and suspected spawning began by early May.

**Equipment and setup.**
- Two PIAs (33 × 68 cm), about 100 m apart, each connected to a Biomark FS200l F-ISO reader.
- Detection range was about 30 cm from the antenna. Reader cables were 15 m long and buried about 15 cm deep.
- The antennas were anchored with rebar stakes and placed where spawning was expected once water rose about 15 cm.
- Readers logged time and date, and suppressed repeat reads of the same tag within 10 s.
- 2012: batteries (12 V deep-cycle) were replaced every 7 days; readers were removed during high flows (21–25 May).
- 2013: solar panels powered the system, and a 2-m platform kept the battery, panel, and reader above high water.

**Seasonal timing.**
- 2012: PIAs were in the water from 7 May. Detections ran until 1 June, with none from 2 to 7 June. The equipment was removed on 7 June.
- 2013: PIAs ran continuously from 3 April to 1 July, with the last detection on 15 June.

**Other species.** Non-target detections included flannelmouth and bluehead suckers, showing the antennas also detected other tagged catostomids.

## Key Findings

**Detection totals.**
- 2012 (29 days): 59 unique tags detected.
- 2013 (about 90 days): 553 unique tags detected.
- Species, from the database query: 569 razorback suckers, 19 Colorado pikeminnow, 15 flannelmouth suckers, 1 bluehead sucker, and 8 tags with no species record.
- The abstract reports 16 flannelmouth suckers. This differs from the Results count of 15 flannelmouth plus 1 bluehead (16 combined), so the split between the two species should be checked against the supplementary data.

**Razorback sucker results.**
- Of the 59 fish detected in 2012, only 3 razorback suckers were detected again in 2013.
- 40 razorback suckers had been recaptured once by electrofishing between stocking and PIA detection.
- For the remaining 529 razorback suckers (93%), PIA detection was the first detection since stocking, up to 13 years earlier.
- Most razorback suckers (468) were detected on only one day. One fish was detected on 6 days spanning a 13-day period.
- The oldest detected fish was 15 years old. The youngest were 3-year-olds stocked in 2011 and detected in 2013.
- Females mature at 3–4 years. Most detected fish were older than 4 years and were present during the spawning season.
- The farthest stocking site was river kilometer 193, an upstream movement of 311 km to Razorback Bar.

**Colorado pikeminnow results.**
- The 19 detected fish were tagged between 1996 and 2013.
- 15 were tagged in the Green River. One moved 169.3 km upstream (the scanned value is unclear).
- 3 were tagged in the [[Yampa River]], and one moved 202.3 km downstream.
- 1 was tagged 22 km up the [[White River]] and traveled 130 km to Razorback Bar.

**Interpretation.**
- PIAs substantially increased detections compared with active sampling alone.
- Detection may have been incomplete. Only one small area was covered, and fish that visited but did not pass the antennas went undetected. Modde et al. (2005) reported fish staying for weeks at a time.
- Spawning was not confirmed, because no fish were captured.
- Survival estimates may have been underestimated in recent years because passive data were not included. Zelasko et al. (2010) reported first-year survival for stocked razorback suckers. The scanned value ("5o/o") is ambiguous and may be 5% or 50%. Schooley et al. (2008) reported lower-Colorado River survival rates that rose after PIA recapture data were added. The scanned values are also ambiguous.
- Adding passive detections to active sampling reduces handling-related effects, avoids learned trap avoidance, and can detect fish that are hard to capture. These benefits are also reported by Hewitt et al. (2010) and Webber et al. (2012).

**Recommendations.**
- Use a more robust antenna system, with longer cords or a sturdier platform, and more PIAs, to cover more of the site.
- Note that longer cords extend above high water but reduce read range.
- Deploy PIAs at other known high-density sites, including tributary confluences and the recently found spawning location in the lower [[White River]] (Webber et al. 2013).
- Develop and use passive recapture techniques for recovery of razorback sucker and Colorado pikeminnow.
