# Tree Leaf Control on Low Flow Water Quality in a Small Virginia Stream

#freshwater-ecology #limnology #water-quality #leaf-litter

## Executive Summary

This 1968 study by [[Keith V. Slack]] and [[Herman R. Feltz]] (U.S. Geological Survey) examines how autumn leaf fall from [[Riparian Vegetation]] degrades the chemistry of a small, otherwise unpolluted stream, the [[North Fork Quantico Creek]]. Field measurements in 1963 were paired with [[Laboratory Leaf Culture]] experiments on five tree species. As leaf fall increased, [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and pH fell, while [[Water Color]], [[Specific Conductance]], [[Iron]], [[Manganese]], and [[Bicarbonate]] rose. Leaf species differed widely in their effects. Water quality recovered quickly after [[Storm Flushing]]. The authors conclude that [[Organic Loading]] by tree litter can strongly control stream water composition, especially at [[Low Flow]].

## Background & Description

The study site is a first-order tributary in the [[Piedmont Province]], about 30 miles south of Washington, D.C. Nearly all of its watershed lies within [[Prince William Forest Park]], managed by the [[National Park Service]].

- **Drainage area:** 17.9 sq. km (6.9 sq. miles), 68% of it upstream of the study reach; the stream is ungaged.
- **Dimensions:** width about 4 m (12 ft); depth about 0.2 m (9 in) at normal flow.
- **Gradient:** 4.7 m per km (25 ft per mile) in the study reach.
- **Land use:** more than 90% forested. Farming and logging began in the late 17th century, and abandoned land has reverted to forest over roughly the last 50 years.
- **Geology:** the channel cuts through thin Coastal Plain sediments into Piedmont schist, quartzite, and granite. Pyrite was mined near the North and South Fork confluence before 1920.
- **Habitat:** riffles were formed by bedrock, rubble, or boulders. Pools held gravel, sand, silt, waterlogged trunks, and abundant branches and leaves. [[Beaver]] colonies and at least two dams lay upstream. Buried beaver-dam deposits released soluble ferrous iron, which oxidized to orange ferric hydroxide where it emerged. Riffle rocks were coated with iron- and manganese-rich black deposits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame leaf litter as a seasonal stressor. In most U.S. streams, autumn is the annual low-flow period, when [[Ground Water]] supplies most of the flow and dissolved solids are normally at their yearly maximum. Streams also have the least capacity to assimilate organic material at this time. Decomposing litter can deplete [[Dissolved Oxygen]], which may cause [[Fish Kill]]s, reduce [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]], produce anaerobic odors, clog water-treatment filters, and degrade aesthetics.

Leaf litter also affects streams beyond forests. Tree litter from urban storm drains and tree-lined banks in agricultural or arid lands can add organic loading. The authors note that leaf material is part of a continuing detrital load that streams carry to pools, reservoirs, and other receiving waters.

In the field, [[Leaf Fall]] was measured with 1 sq. m wire-mesh trays, emptied every 1 to 2 weeks, oven-dried at 80 °C, and weighed. Water chemistry followed standard methods, including a modified [[Winkler Method]] for dissolved oxygen and filtration through 0.45-micron membranes for iron and manganese.

## Key Findings

**Field results (1963)**

- Leaf fall began in the latter half of September and peaked about one month later. Over the first six weeks it averaged 5.3 g/m²/day in the trays, probably an underestimate of deposition to the stream surface.
- In some pools, more than 90% of the water surface was covered by floating leaves at peak fall. The authors suggest this may reduce light and oxygen transfer at the air-water interface.
- Water quality changed significantly for nearly all 17 measured parameters. The seven most striking, discussed in the paper, were dissolved oxygen, water color, pH, specific conductance, iron, manganese, and bicarbonate.
- Quality changes tracked leaf fall closely at low flows. Leached solutes and decomposition products drove pH down by nearly one unit, overriding stream buffering.
- Field stream flow fell to 0.1 to 0.3 L/s in October. A storm produced bankful flow of 280 L/s on November 7, and quality recovered by November 15 and 27.
- On October 16 a few dead fish were seen. By October 25 more were dead and surviving fish were gulping air at the surface. Dissolved oxygen in affected pools was below 1 mg/L.

**Laboratory cultures**

- Ten grams (air dry) of whole leaves were incubated in 16 L of filtered North Fork water in 19-L polyethylene tanks at 12–13 °C, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Duplicates were run for each species except maple, against a control.
- Within 24 hours, color and specific conductance rose by as much as 100%, depending on species.
- By day 6, most cultures showed higher manganese, color, and conductance than the control. Over the same period, pH and dissolved oxygen fell.
- Manganese reached about twice the iron concentration early on. Both metals fell significantly between days 75 and 180. Oxygen later recovered, which the authors attribute partly to algal growth.
- Beech leaves caused the smallest oxygen demand and least water-quality impairment. Tulip tree and dogwood (more alkaline leaves) impaired water more than oak (more acidic). Maple, oak, and tulip tree lowered oxygen more than dogwood.
- Leaf-bag tests in the creek showed that leaves in winter and spring could contain up to 100 times the iron and manganese of freshly collected leaves, suggesting stream-bed sources as well.
- Chase and Ferullo (1958) reported maple leaves consuming oxygen equal to 75% of their initial dry weight after 396 days.

**Interpretation and conclusions**

- Highest iron and manganese in the stream during leaf fall far exceeded peak values in laboratory cultures. Litter composition data for the stream were not available, so the leaf contribution cannot be quantified.
- Water color was inversely related to dissolved oxygen and directly related to iron and manganese. The authors link this to colloidal colored polymers that complex or adsorb cations.
- Poorest water occurred in the most stagnant pools. Barely perceptible flow reduced impairment.
- Degradation was linked to the weight of litter per unit water volume, litter species composition, and flushing of affected pools.
- The authors recommend accounting for litter effects when relating ground-water composition to low-flow stream quality, and note relevance to fisheries management, areal budgets, and stream assimilative capacity.
