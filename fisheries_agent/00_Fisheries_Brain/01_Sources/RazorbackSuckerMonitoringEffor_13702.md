# Razorback Sucker Monitoring Effort, Arizona Reservoirs, March 1985

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #native-fish-conservation #reintroduction

## Executive Summary

This memorandum documents a 14-day cooperative monitoring effort in March 1985 to detect reintroduced [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in three Arizona reservoirs: [[Bartlett Reservoir]] (Verde River), [[Roosevelt Reservoir]] (Salt River), and [[San Carlos Reservoir]] (Gila River). The reintroduction program, ongoing since 1981, uses fish propagated at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] from [[Lake Mohave]] brood stocks. Despite intensive gill netting, trammel netting, and electrofishing during the expected spawning season, no razorback suckers were captured. The only native species detected was [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) in San Carlos Reservoir. Sampling documented abundant non-native fish assemblages dominated by [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and [[Black Crappie]].

## Background & Description

[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a native catostomid being reintroduced into selected Arizona watersheds within the [[Gila River]] basin under a Memorandum of Understanding between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Arizona Game and Fish Department. The reintroduction effort began in 1981 using fry and fingerlings produced at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] from wild-caught and hatchery-reared brood stocks originating in [[Lake Mohave]], AZ-NV.

Fish from the 1981 and 1982 year classes have reached sexual maturity under hatchery conditions at Dexter NFH, though maturation rates under natural riverine or reservoir conditions remain unknown. In [[Lake Mohave]], adult razorback suckers spawn from January through April or early May along gravelly shorelines. Males move inshore while females remain in somewhat deeper offshore waters until spawning. During this period, they are highly susceptible to gill nets, trammel nets, and nighttime electrofishing.

The March 1985 monitoring effort targeted prebreeding aggregations in reservoirs downstream from reintroduction sites, hypothesizing that reintroduced individuals might be abundant and large enough to appear in spawning aggregations by spring 1985.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sampling encompassed three major reservoir systems in the [[Gila River]] basin:

**[[Bartlett Reservoir]]** (Verde River): Sampling covered the river channel above the reservoir downstream to near Bartlett Dam in all discernible habitats except the deepest central portions. [[Horseshoe Reservoir]] immediately upstream was unworkable due to flood debris.

**[[Roosevelt Reservoir]]** (Salt River): Sampling included Salt River mainstream, both sides of the Salt River Delta at the reservoir head, and downstream on both north and south sides to Windy Point.

**[[San Carlos Reservoir]]** (Gila River): Sampling covered both sides of the Gila River Delta at the reservoir head, San Carlos River Arm (an essentially cutoff bay), and downstream to near Coolidge Dam.

The fish assemblages documented were dominated by non-native species. [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) was the most abundant species by biomass across all three reservoirs, comprising 22.9-35.7% of gill/trammel net catches. [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) dominated electrofishing catches (56.3-66.1% of catch per hour). Other abundant non-natives included [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker Detection**: The 14-day cooperative monitoring effort involving Arizona Game and Fish Department, Arizona State University, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and Bureau of Reclamation personnel was unsuccessful in capturing any razorback suckers from the three target reservoirs.

**Native Fish Presence**: The only native species captured was [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) from the San Carlos River arm of [[San Carlos Reservoir]], with catch rates of 0.09 fish per 100 m² per night (gill/trammel nets) and 1.29 fish per hour (electrofishing).

**Sampling Effort and Methods**: 
- Gill and trammel nets: 30-100 m multi- and monofilament units with mesh sizes (bar measure) from 1.2 to 7.5 cm, fished near bottom
- Total net units fished: 29.84 (Bartlett), 28.06 (Roosevelt), 32.76 (San Carlos)
- Electrofishing: mostly nighttime with boat-mounted, variable voltage, D.C. pulsating units
- Total electrofishing hours: 8.94 (Bartlett), 5.72 (Roosevelt), 2.23 (San Carlos)

**Fish Community Composition by Reservoir**:

*Bartlett Reservoir* (11-15 March 1985): Total catch 7.93 fish/100 m²/night (nets) and 134.34 fish/hour (electrofishing). Dominant species: [[Threadfin Shad]] (25.0% net catch), [[Common Carp]] (22.9%), [[Largemouth Bass]] (19.9% nets, 66.1% electrofishing), [[Black Crappie]] (17.8% nets, 9.8% electrofishing).

*Roosevelt Reservoir* (dates not specified): Total catch 11.41 fish/100 m²/night (nets) and 166.42 fish/hour (electrofishing). Dominant species: [[Largemouth Bass]] (25.9% nets, 56.3% electrofishing), [[Common Carp]] (23.4% nets, 37.3% electrofishing), [[Black Crappie]] (15.6% nets, 3.8% electrofishing), [[Channel Catfish]] (11.6% nets).

*San Carlos Reservoir* (25-29 March 1985): Total catch 16.81 fish/100 m²/night (nets) and 251.51 fish/hour (electrofishing). Dominant species: [[Common Carp]] (35.7% nets, 27.5% electrofishing), [[Largemouth Bass]] (13.3% nets, 35.7% electrofishing), [[Channel Catfish]] (15.4% nets), [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) (11.7% nets, 13.4% electrofishing).

**Daytime vs. Nighttime Electrofishing**: Limited daytime electrofishing in Bartlett Reservoir (1.16 hours) yielded 51.78 fish/hour: [[Common Carp]] 28.5 (55.0%), [[Largemouth Bass]] 20.69 (40.0%), [[Black Crappie]] 2.59 (5.0%).

**Data Archival**: Field notes and detailed sampling procedures are on file with Arizona State University Center for Environmental Studies, Arizona Game and Fish Department Nongame Branch, and Dexter National Fish Hatchery.
