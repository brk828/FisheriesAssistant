# Status of the Sonoran Topminnow and Desert Pupfish in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department special report, based on field surveys from June 1989 to April 1990 and a review of historic data, assesses the status of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in Arizona. Both species were Federally listed as endangered (topminnow in 1967, pupfish in 1986). The report evaluates introduced and natural populations against the criteria of the [[Gila and Yaqui Topminnow Recovery Plan]] (USFWS 1983), discusses the causes of decline (habitat loss and predation by [[Mosquitofish]]), and recommends priority actions. Of 192 introductions into wild habitats of Gila topminnow, 32 were successful as of September 1989 (17 percent). The authors conclude that the recovery criteria for downlisting have been met for Gila topminnow, but that natural populations continue to decline.

## Background & Description

**Species and subspecies**
- The Sonoran topminnow and desert pupfish are each represented in Arizona by two subspecies.
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): historically found in the Gila River drainage of southern Arizona.
- [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): native to the Yaqui River drainage near Douglas, Arizona.
- [[Lower Colorado River Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*): historically in the Gila River drainage; eliminated from Arizona prior to the 1950s.
- [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*): the only historic locality is Quitobaquito Springs, in the Sonoyta River drainage near Lukeville, Arizona.

**Historical context**
- Both fishes were once widespread in southern Arizona and considered among the most abundant fishes there (Hubbs and Miller 1941; Minckley 1973).
- Causes of decline include habitat modification and loss, and predation by non-native fishes, particularly mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) (Meffe et al. 1983; Schoenherr 1988).
- Reestablishment attempts date to 1936; large-scale recovery for the topminnow began in the early 1980s, and recovery for the desert pupfish had not yet begun at the time of the report.

**Terminology used in the report**
- "Captive" populations are those at a site with a mailing address (universities, museums, zoos, hatcheries, backyards). All others are "in the wild."
- "Introduced population": a population established by transplanting fish within historic range to a site with no record of natural populations of that species.
- "Natural population": a site that held fish before human transplants and where fish have not been artificially mixed with other populations.
- Population quality categories (after Simons 1987):
  - Category 1: moderate to high numbers, natural surface water expected to persist indefinitely without human support.
  - Category 2: moderate to high numbers, persistence only with anthropogenic support.
  - Category 3: very low numbers or limited habitat; not expected to persist many years.
  - Category 4: mosquitofish present, regardless of population size or habitat quality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and methods**
- Introduced populations were monitored June to September 1989, a period chosen to assess desiccation, identified as the most serious threat to many introduced populations.
- Sampling used dipnets (1/8 inch mesh, 1.5 foot opening) and seines (3 ft x 3 ft and 10 ft x 4 ft, 1/8 inch mesh), depending on habitat size.
- Voucher specimens were deposited at the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes.
- Natural populations were sampled with the same methods, with systematic samples at sites where native and non-native fish coexisted (e.g., [[Sharp Spring]], [[Sonoita Creek]], [[Redrock Canyon]]).

**Habitat and threats**
- Habitats range from large springs and creeks to small stock tanks, troughs, and artificial ponds.
- Desiccation was the primary cause of loss for introduced populations. Precipitation in 1989 was more than two inches below normal (NOAA 1989), identified as the major contributing factor in the failure of at least seven introduced and one natural Gila topminnow population.
- Vegetation encroachment (notably [[Cattail]], *Typha*) and siltation were noted at several sites.
- Mosquitofish invasion has replaced topminnows at several natural sites.
- Genetic differentiation among Gila topminnow drainages has been documented (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985; Quattro and Vrijenhoek 1989), and a watershed-based approach to introductions is recommended.

**Natural population groupings (Gila topminnow)**
- Bylas Spring group: Bylas Spring (Site #7), Middle Spring (Site #6), North Fork of Ash Creek (Site #126).
- San Rafael group: Sheehy Spring (Site #3), Sharp Spring (Site #4), Santa Cruz River (Site #10).
- Sonoita Creek group: Cottonwood Spring (Site #1), Monkey Spring (Site #2), Sonoita Creek (Site #9), Redrock Canyon (Site #11).
- Cienega Creek group: Cienega Creek (Site #5).

**Associated fauna noted in surveys**
- [[Longfin Dace]], [[Gila Chub]], [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Leopard Frog]], [[Mexican Garter Snake]], and [[Mud Turtle]] were among species recorded at various sites.

## Key Findings

**Recovery criteria and status**
- Gila topminnow downlisting criterion (20 populations reestablished in the wild, within historic range, surviving at least three years): stated by the authors as met, with 32 successful introduced wild populations as of September 1989.
- Gila topminnow delisting criteria (50 populations surviving three years, or 30 surviving five years) were not met.
- Yaqui topminnow: one of three delisting criteria met (acquisition of San Bernardino Ranch by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as a National Wildlife Refuge). Mosquitofish removal and long-term persistence criteria were not met.
- Desert pupfish: draft Recovery Plan under review; downlisting and delisting criteria not discussed.

**Introduced Gila topminnow populations**
- 192 introductions into wild habitats; more than 102 captive sites; more than 340 total introductions (Appendix A).
- 32 introduced wild populations successful as of September 1989 (17 percent of 192 introductions).
- 24 populations persisted three years or more (introduced before 1987); 19 for five or more years (introduced before 1985); 5 since before 1982.
- 15 wild populations failed between 1987 and 1989 (Table 2), mostly due to desiccation.
- Only five new introduced populations were established between April 1988 and August 1989: Buffalo Corral Pond Spring (Site #99), Hassayampa River Preserve (Site #140), Little Nogales Spring (Site #125), Red Rock Wildlife Area (Site #139), and Roper Lake State Park Lower Headquarters Pond (Site #123b).
- Of the 32 successful sites, 7 are Category 1, 10 are Category 2, 9 are Category 3, and 6 are Category 4.
- Only seven populations are expected to persist more than 10 years without human intervention.
- Eleven populations successful for at least four years before 1987 were eliminated by 1989.

**Introduced Yaqui topminnow**
- More than 26 stockings into 18 wild and 4 captive habitats.
- Eight wild populations successful in 1989: five Category 2, two Category 3, one Category 4.
- Three introduced populations were eliminated in 1989 due to mosquitofish.

**Introduced desert pupfish**
- More than 65 introductions of Lower Colorado River pupfish to 14 wild and 31 captive habitats.
- 31 introductions of Quitobaquito pupfish documented for 11 wild and 12 captive sites.
- In 1989, four wild populations were successful and four failed.
- Bog Hole (Site #130) and Finley Tank (Site #131) contain fish believed to be hybrids of uncertain genetic integrity; renovation is proposed, and an Environmental Assessment is being written.

**Natural populations**
- Gila topminnow: eight of eleven natural populations successful in 1987 persisted in 1989. Three (Sites #1, 2, 5) are considered secure.
- Three of 11 natural populations failed in 1989: Sheehy Spring (Site #3), Middle Spring (Site #6), and North Fork of Ash Creek (Site #126).
- Sheehy Spring: more than 1000 fish collected in 1988-1989, none were topminnows.
- North Fork of Ash Creek: more than 2600 poeciliids identified, all mosquitofish.
- Bylas Spring (Site #7): only one topminnow collected in 1989; mosquitofish comprised 98% of fish in 1987. In 1990, 60 individuals were removed and are held at Arizona State University as a refugium population.
- Cienega Creek (Site #5): the largest natural Gila topminnow population in Arizona; mosquitofish have never been collected there. Acquisition by the Bureau of Land Management in 1989 was described as probably the single most important action for topminnow conservation since recovery began.
- Quitobaquito Springs (Site #128): the only natural population of Quitobaquito pupfish; a large population was present in 1989 and no exotic fish were collected.
- Yaqui topminnow: only Tule Pond (Site #142) is considered to still harbor a natural population.
- Lower Colorado River pupfish eliminated from Arizona before the 1950s.

**Causes of failure cited**
- Desiccation and drought (1989 precipitation more than two inches below normal).
- Mosquitofish invasion and predation.
- Flooding (e.g., Tule Creek, Site #75; Big Spring, Site #84).
- Vegetation encroachment and siltation.
- Unauthorized stockings and illegal drainage or water diversion.

**Recommendations (from the report)**
1. Secure natural populations as the main emphasis of recovery: remove mosquitofish, construct fish barriers, and manage habitat. Site-specific recommendations are in Appendix C.
2. Conduct genetic studies on all natural Gila topminnow populations to guide management of diversity.
3. Introduce fish into quality habitats with long-term persistence potential, renovating sites where necessary. Surveys for additional sites should continue (Appendix D).
4. Recognize that these recommendations have been made since the early 1980s without being fully implemented, and that the Sonoran topminnow and desert pupfish have high recovery potential.

**Limitations noted by the authors**
- Current delisting criteria ignore natural populations after 1987, and do not account for long-term persistence.
- Some stocking records are incomplete, so the persistence of many sites is uncertain.
- Status for some introduced sites remains unknown (e.g., sites not sampled since introduction).
