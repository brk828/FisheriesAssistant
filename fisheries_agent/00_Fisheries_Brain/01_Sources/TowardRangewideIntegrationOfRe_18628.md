# Integration of Science-Based Recovery Programs for Native Fishes of the Colorado River Basin Under the Endangered Species Act

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology #river-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document examines regulatory accommodations and recovery programs for four endangered native fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). These "big river fish" were historically abundant throughout the basin but now face extinction due to habitat loss from dam construction (at least 20 dams), flow alteration, blocked migrations, and competition/predation from non-native species. The paper analyzes the legal framework of the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), particularly Sections 4, 7, and 10, and describes three major recovery initiatives: the [[Upper Basin Recovery Program]] (initiated 1988), the [[San Juan Recovery Program]], and the emerging [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]]. The Upper Basin Program represents a cooperative federal-state effort combining flow protection, habitat restoration, genetic conservation, and non-native fish management, funded at approximately $11 million annually (FY 96). The Lower Basin is pursuing an unprecedented multi-species [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] (HCP) covering 40+ species with estimated costs of $142.5-185 million over 50 years.

## Background & Description

### Species Status and Distribution

The four native fish species were listed under the ESA between 1967-1992. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] were first listed in 1967, the [[Bonytail Chub]] in 1980, and the [[Razorback Sucker]] in 1992 (following petition by [[Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund]]). Critical habitat for all four species was finally designated in March 1994, covering 1,980 river and reservoir miles and associated 100-year floodplains throughout the basin.

**Current Distribution:**
- **Colorado Squawfish**: Found only in Upper Basin; extirpated from Lower Basin
- **Bonytail Chub**: Rarest species; only a few old fish exist in wild with virtually nonexistent recruitment
- **Humpback Chub**: Most numerous in Lower Basin, particularly at confluence of Colorado and [[Little Colorado Rivers]] in [[Grand Canyon]]; not described as separate species until 1946
- **Razorback Sucker**: Mostly old adult fish concentrated in Lower Basin lakes, especially [[Lake Mohave]]; Upper Basin populations in lower [[Yampa River|Yampa]] and [[Green River]], mainstream Colorado, and lower [[San Juan River]] show limited recruitment and decreasing numbers

### Causes of Decline

Major factors contributing to population declines are well understood:

1. **Habitat Loss and Fragmentation**: At least 20 dams built over past century fragmented habitat and altered flows
2. **Migration Barriers**: Dams blocked natural spawning and rearing migrations
3. **Thermal Regime Changes**: Lower water temperatures from reservoir releases limited recruitment
4. **Flow Regulation**: Impoundments flattened hydrographs, reducing flood events and spring flows to which native fishes adapted over thousands of years
5. **Non-Native Species**: Introduction and proliferation of non-native fishes in reservoirs and warm water habitats; non-natives compete for habitat and prey on young native fish

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Critical Habitat Designation

The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) identified the following physical and biological attributes as essential constituent elements for recovery:

- **Water**: Sufficient quantity and quality delivered to specific locations in appropriate hydrologic regime for all life stages
- **Physical Habitat**: River corridors, bottomlands, side channels, secondary channels, oxbows, backwaters, and other habitat within 100-year floodplain needed for spawning, nursery, feeding, and rearing
- **Biological Environment**: Food and nutrient supply; competition and predation dynamics with other fishes

The designation generally described critical habitat wherever such elements occurred within the 1,980 river miles and associated floodplains, but did not prescribe specific flow regimes or identify precise locations of constituent elements. Several reaches were dropped for biological reasons; none were eliminated based on economic impacts. The FWS argued most economic impacts stemmed from listing itself rather than critical habitat designation.

**Key Limitations**: No critical habitat for [[Colorado Squawfish]] was designated in Lower Basin. The designation does not require continuous maintenance of reservoirs at full pool elevations. Of 41 aquatically-related critical habitat designations as of 1994, only one included specific instream flow prescription.

### Habitat Requirements and Ecosystem Function

Dr. Jack Stanford's 1993 review endorsed FWS flow recommendations and recommended a broader ecosystem approach, emphasizing:

- Experimenting with higher peak flows and more stable base flows in regulated reaches
- Developing understanding of ecosystem functions in relatively undeveloped reaches
- Maintaining those ecosystem functions
- Reconnecting and restoring highly productive side channel and floodplain habitat
- Testing hypotheses about natural function of floodplain habitat in riverine ecosystems

## Key Findings

### Legal Framework: Endangered Species Act

**Section 4 - Listing and Critical Habitat:**
- Complex process for listing species as endangered triggers ESA protections
- Critical habitat generally designated concurrently (within 2 years) of listing since 1978
- Critical habitat defined as geographic area containing physical and biological features "essential" to conservation (recovery to point of de-listing)
- FWS may exclude areas if economic impacts outweigh benefits, but only where exclusion won't undermine survival
- Recovery plans required with site-specific actions, objective measurable criteria for de-listing, and cost/timeframe estimates
- No set timeframe for recovery plan development or direct connection between recovery plans and critical habitat designation

**Section 7 - Federal Agency Consultation:**
- Section 7(a)(1): Affirmative duty for all federal agencies to utilize authorities to conserve listed species; may mandate committing uncommitted water supply or flow regulation to listed fishes
- Section 7(a)(2): Federal agencies must consult with FWS on whether actions will jeopardize species, adversely modify critical habitat, or appreciably reduce likelihood of recovery
- If jeopardy/adverse modification found, FWS must suggest "reasonable and prudent alternatives" that avoid jeopardy, stay within scope of federal action and agency authority, and are economically/technologically feasible
- "God Squad" exemption possible but extremely rare in practice
- Applies to continuing operations of pre-1973 federal water projects
- Set timeframe for consultations, though extensions common
- Incidental take authorization required when action takes species but doesn't jeopardize; must specify impact, minimization measures, and implementation terms
- "Taking" includes significant habitat modification that injures species by impairing essential behaviors (upheld in [[Sweet Home Case]])

**Section 10 - Habitat Conservation Plans:**
- Allows FWS to issue permits authorizing taking by private/state action without federal nexus
- Requires applicant-submitted plan specifying: impact of taking, minimization steps, funding, alternatives considered and rejected
- FWS must issue permit if: taking is incidental, applicant will minimize/mitigate impacts and fund plan, taking won't appreciably reduce survival/recovery likelihood
- No set timeframe for development/approval
- Broader basis for habitat planning than Section 7 reasonable and prudent alternatives
- More costly and time-consuming for applicants than Section 7 process
- Cannot authorize taking by significant habitat modification if it adversely modifies designated critical habitat essential to recovery

### Upper Basin Recovery Program

**Program Development (1983-1988):**
- Initial draft conservation plan (June 1983) prescribed instream flows but lacked precise substantiation; vigorously attacked by states and water developers
- FWS withdrew plan (1984) and formed [[Upper Colorado River Coordinating Committee]] with [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR) and Colorado, Wyoming, New Mexico representatives
- Core concept: Program serves as reasonable and prudent alternative in Section 7 consultations AND provides for recovery by comprehensively addressing habitat needs and limiting factors
- Instream flow protection balanced with: bottomland/backwater habitat restoration, fish passage construction, non-native fish control, genetic conservation/restocking, coordinated monitoring/research
- States to apply their laws to legally protect instream flows
- Water developers and conservation groups invited to participate
- Program formalized in "Blue Book" (November 1987) after 3 years of debate
- NEPA compliance completed; program initiated January 1988

**Flow Quantification and Protection:**
- FWS flow recommendations repeatedly challenged by states and water developers
- Dr. Jack Stanford's 1993 external review largely endorsed FWS recommendations, offered broader ecosystem approach
- FWS revised recommendations for priority reaches: lower [[Yampa River]] and [[15 Mile Reach]] of Colorado River
- Specialists developed "modifiable instream flow water rights" policy for [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] (CWCB)
- CWCB has exclusive authority to appropriate instream flow rights under Colorado law; cannot establish rights depriving Colorado of compact entitlements
- Innovation: "Converse instream flow rights" protecting flows by describing allowable depletion levels, combined with conventional baseflow protection
- Water development allowance "sandwiched" between top-end and baseflow protections
- CWCB filed water court applications December 1994 for this configuration
- Utah State Engineer adopted policy (November 1994) for [[Green River]] from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] to [[Duchesne River]] confluence, determining new permits not in public interest if violating FWS flow recommendations
- Wyoming pursuing similar "permit state" approach

**Reservoir and River Operations:**
- 1992 biological opinion on [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] recommended dampening unnaturally high late summer/fall flows, 5-year research program on operations to stabilize winter flows and approximate natural spring peaks
- Stanford criticized failure to limit hourly flow fluctuations disrupting food webs
- Similar research program for [[Aspinall Unit]] reservoirs on [[Gunnison River]] in 4th year
- Studies near completion for saving/operationally sharing canal seepage and administrative spills in [[Grand Valley]]
- Strategy: Recovery Program funds capital improvements allowing same farm deliveries with lower river diversions, enabling "rule curve" storage at [[Green Mountain Reservoir]] for release to improve habitat and generate hydropower
- [[Ruedi Reservoir]] biological opinion: BOR delivers storage water to Grand Valley habitat; short and long-term lease agreements with CWCB protect instream use
- Similar lease agreement for [[Steamboat Lake]] releases to [[Yampa River]] habitat
- Conceptual agreement on Recovery Program contribution to enlarge off-mainstem reservoir as centerpiece of water rights/instream flow management plan for Yampa River
- [[Colorado River Water Conservation District]] to voluntarily retire senior conditional water rights in Yampa Basin, improving priority/legal strength of CWCB instream filings

**Floodplain Habitat Restoration:**
- Working group reached agreement on framework following Stanford recommendations
- Hypotheses about natural function of floodplain habitat being developed
- Testing approach: Select sites from comprehensive Upper Basin inventory, acquire access/ownership, reconnect some to river by removing levees, monitor response of natives and non-natives
- Sequential, temporary reconnection of selected sites compared to naturally occurring habitats
- Some hypotheses tested at controlled sites with existing program access
- Distinguishes from population augmentation in grow-out ponds (separate program element)

**Fish Refugia and Population Augmentation:**
- Genetic guidelines approved; "hatchery" facilities plan developed
- Process established for periodically reviewing fish needs for research, broodstock, population stabilization
- Construction/expansion underway: Two primary refugia (one for Colorado River subbasin, one for Green River subbasin), backup refugia, low-cost grow-out ponds
- Adaptive plans approved for: Razorback population augmentation on Green River, razorback restocking on upper Colorado and Gunnison rivers
- Banking based on "presumptive stocks" (geographically separated populations with potentially different breeding grounds) pending completion of genetic studies

**Non-Native Fish Management:**
- Cooperative federal-state review procedure for non-native stocking proposals overhauled/strengthened end of 1993, then withdrawn and revised again
- Studies underway on mitigating impacts of already-introduced non-natives
- [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] (CDOW) proposing to eradicate non-natives in ponds connected to listed fish habitat above Colorado-Gunnison confluence, then reintroduce three non-native sportfish
- CDOW maintains historic escapement/survival of these three species minuscule
- CDOW claims state action not subject to Section 7 consultation or NEPA compliance

**Section 7 Agreement and Recovery Action Plan:**
- Key premise: Agreement on Section 7 consultations not reached until 1993, substantially amended since
- Water developers initially sought no-jeopardy assurance if: $10 million Congressional appropriation for water rights purchase, $10/acre-foot one-time charge per new project, Program in place on paper
- Conservation groups and FWS declined; groups qualified endorsement pending adequate assurance of water rights acquisition/enforcement
- FWS determined opinions couldn't rely solely on Program being on paper/funded; must guard against large new depletions until reasonable assurance of flow protection
- FWS judgment: Projects depleting >3,000 acre-feet/year average assured no-jeopardy only if "progress to legally protect instream flow needs has been sufficient to offset impacts"
- 1990 [[Muddy Creek Reservoir]] opinion (15,000 acre-feet/year depletion): Progress insufficient; conditioned on 3,000 acre-foot set-aside of non-firm yield for fish
- FWS broadened "sufficient progress" criteria to consider progress on other recovery elements and in other drainages
- Federal reclamation projects exempted from depletion charge; BOR funding ~$2 million/year from Upper Basin power fund, committed to reoperate projects for instream flows
- Section 7 Agreement consolidated/reformed FWS consultation policy; conditioned on development of detailed [[Recovery Implementation Program Recovery Action Plan]] (RIPRAP)
- Agreement requires FWS judge whether progress (measured by RIPRAP) keeps pace with depletion impacts
- For larger depletions before flow protection: Opinion may be conditioned on timely RIPRAP action completion; if infeasible, project yield set-aside contemplated as last resort
- For smaller depletions or after flow protection: All progress to consultation point sufficient; no project yield set-aside; consultations cannot be revisited even if Program fails recovery

**Program Organization and Funding:**
- [[Implementation Committee]]: Regional directors (FWS, BOR, [[Western Area Power Administration]]), state natural resource/water development directors (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), conservation group/water development/hydropower representatives
- [[Management Committee]]: Representatives appointed by Implementation Committee members
- Three technical committees support Management Committee: Biology, Water Acquisition, Information and Education
- Membership corresponds to Implementation Committee across all levels
- Objective: Reach consensus at every level
- Program Director directly accountable for operations, work planning, budgeting
- Staff coordinator for each major element reports to Program Director
- Annual process: Project reporting → annual report to Management Committee → RIPRAP revisions/annual guidance → technical committee review → Management Committee recommendations → Implementation Committee approval → project proposals → annual work plan → committee review → fall Implementation Committee approval

**FY 96 Budget (~$11 million total):**
- ~$6.4 million: BOR appropriation for capital projects
- ~$2 million: Upper Basin power fund expense for annual projects (charged as non-reimbursable since 1993, removing from power rates)
- ~$1.1 million: FWS appropriations for annual projects
- ~$0.85 million: Colorado contribution
- Balance: Water depletion charges, Wyoming/Utah contributions, water user contributions, carryover from earlier BOR water rights purchase appropriation

**Long-term Funding Challenges:**
- FY 96 represents high mark; year-to-year appropriations may not continue at this level
- BOR's non-reimbursable expense decision may not be authorized
- Annual costs expected to inflate modestly
- Capital costs over program life controversial: projected $60-100 million
- Working group developing federal legislation to stabilize long-term funding
- Concepts under consideration: Redirect hydropower revenues by removing "lumpiness" of current apportionment formula, or authorize both annual and capital costs as non-reimbursable power fund expenses
- Would also cover San Juan recovery program long-term funding

### Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program

**Background and Context:**
- No official recovery plan in place for four big river fish in Lower Basin
- Upper Basin recovery/management tied to regulatory relief and jeopardy avoidance only in Upper Basin
- Ongoing Lower Basin efforts: [[Lake Mohave]] razorback sucker program, [[Lake Havasu]] bonytail program, humpback chub studies in Grand Canyon
- Larger populations of razorback sucker, bonytail, and humpback chub in Lower Basin
- Less free-flowing river due to extensive dam/reservoir development
- More stable planning basis than Upper Basin: relatively little new water development expected

**Management Prospectus (February 1995):**
- Preliminary draft released by FWS emphasizing need for separate Lower Basin plan
- Goals: "Establish and maintain genetically diverse populations of big river fishes throughout lower Colorado River basin; stabilize and maintain self-perpetuation of populations of all four big river fish"
- Objectives: Prevent extinction, perpetuate genetic variability, stabilize populations, expand populations, achieve self-sustaining populations, down-list or de-list
- Called for [[Lower Basin Management Plan Coordination Team]] establishment and coordinator appointment
- Team to include federal/state agency personnel, academia, tribes, NGOs

**Biological Assessment by BOR:**
- September 27, 1994: FWS informed BOR that existing project operations would trigger formal consultation if affecting listed species or adversely modifying critical habitat
- BOR initiated biological assessment (BA) preparation to determine if mainstream lower Colorado federal activities likely to affect listed/candidate species (not just 4 fish) or critical habitat
