# Recent Bonytail Chub Mortality at Willow Beach

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #fish-management

## Executive Summary
This is a 1995 internal U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum addressing an incident of [[Bonytail Chub]] mortality following a translocation/tagging operation at Willow Beach on the Colorado River. Rather than a controlled scientific study, the document is a defensive incident report from the Parker Fisheries Resource Office (Parker FRO) rebutting an implied attribution of the death to "excessive handling" by field personnel. The memo reconstructs a detailed capture-to-release timeline, proposes alternative mortality hypotheses (PIT-tag injection injury, inadequate thermal tempering, natural mortality), and requests a formal necropsy and fish health review. It also documents field protocols for an interagency (ASU, NBS, USBR, FWS) bonytail sampling effort using boat and truck-based live-car transport.

## Background & Description
No morphometric or anatomical data (length, weight values, meristics) are reported numerically in this memo, though the standard field protocol implies:
- **Handling procedure**: Individual fish were weighed, measured, and PIT-tagged during processing — standard practice for tracking [[Bonytail Chub]] in recovery programs.
- **Marking method**: PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tag injection, raised as a possible source of internal injury ("inadvertently injected the PIT tag into vital tissues").
- **Species identity**: [[Gila elegans]] (Bonytail Chub), a federally endangered [[Cyprinidae]] species native to the Colorado River Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: The fish was captured via netting operations in the Colorado River system near [[Willow Beach]] and [[Lake Mohave]], with transport logistics between capture site and Cottonwood Landing.
- **Thermal considerations**: The memo highlights the ecological/physiological importance of **temperature tempering** when moving fish "from warm to cold water," implying the source and destination waters differed thermally — a critical stressor for [[Coldwater Fish Transport]] and reintroduction efforts.
- **Interagency recovery effort**: The operation was part of a multi-agency (Arizona State University, National Biological Survey, U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) endangered species monitoring/translocation program, reflecting the cooperative management structure typical of [[Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] activities.
- **Sampling protocol**: Nets were run on a 6-hour cycle, with captured bonytail transported by boat or truck depending on feasibility — indicative of standard field methodology for monitoring rare cyprinids in large-river habitats.

## Key Findings
- **Timeline reconstruction**: From capture to hatchery delivery spanned roughly 6–7 hours; total direct fish handling time was estimated at only 15–20 minutes, primarily by trained Service personnel (contradicting the "excessive handling" hypothesis).
- **Handling sequence**:
  1. Fish netted and immediately cut free (~instant).
  2. Transported to live car (~10 min).
  3. Removed for weighing, measuring, and PIT-tagging (~5 min).
  4. Transferred to transport tank via dip net (~1 min).
  5. Transported in covered, oxygenated container.
  6. Removed for photographs, placed in garbage can for hatch house transfer (~1–2 min) — the only point non-Service personnel contacted the fish.
- **Alternative mortality hypotheses proposed**:
  - Accidental PIT tag injection into vital tissue causing internal injury.
  - Insufficient thermal tempering duration compared to a 1993 precedent case.
  - Natural/incidental mortality unrelated to handling.
- **Protocol gaps identified**: No salt or polyaqua stress-reduction additives were used during transport (author cites uncertainty about proper salt dosage and lack of polyaqua supply) — a potential contributing stressor for [[Fish Transport Stress]].
- **Contingency planning**: A secondary live car/truck was staged at Cottonwood Landing in case water transport became infeasible, indicating field-level redundancy planning.
- **Institutional response requested**: Formal necropsy by Ron Major and Fish Health Program recommendations on warm-to-cold water tempering protocols were requested to resolve causal uncertainty.
- **Interagency coordination note**: No single agency held formal authority over the joint operation; protocols were established verbally among cooperating scientists (George Divine, Paul Marsh) rather than through a written study plan — highlighting a procedural gap relevant to future [[Endangered Fish Handling Protocols]].
- **Follow-up decision**: The Parker FRO explicitly declined further participation in a subsequent Arizona Game and Fish Department bonytail collection effort (30 May–1 June 1995), citing lack of jurisdiction over AGFD's independent research plans.
