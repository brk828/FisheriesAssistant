# A 22,000-Year Record of Vegetation Change in the North-Central Sonoran Desert

#botany #paleoecology #paleobotany

## Executive Summary

This paleoecological study reconstructs about 22,000 years of vegetation change at [[Eagle Eye Mountain]], near the northern border of the [[Sonoran Desert]] in Arizona's [[Arizona Upland]] subdivision. Plant macrofossils from eleven radiocarbon-dated [[Packrat Middens]] (*Neotoma* sp.) were analysed alongside measurements of modern vegetation at eighteen shelters. The record moves from [[Late Pleistocene]] pinyon-juniper-oak woodland to a middle Holocene desertscrub community that differed from today's, and finally to modern-type desertscrub after about 4000 years ago. The authors use species distributions to infer that the middle Holocene was warmer in winter and substantially wetter in warm-season precipitation.

## Background & Description

The study was published in *Palaeogeography, Palaeoclimatology, Palaeoecology* (1998) by Joseph R. McAuliffe (Desert Botanical Garden, Phoenix) and Thomas R. Van Devender (Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum, Tucson).

- **Midden method:** Packrats collect plant material and preserve it in urine-impregnated midden deposits in dry rock shelters, giving a radiocarbon-dated record of local flora.
- **Sample set:** Eleven middens were collected from rock shelters at 800–825 m elevation on 27–30 May 1989. Of 31 subsamples, 11 were selected for radiocarbon dating and detailed macrofossil analysis. Pre-washed sample weights were roughly 300–900 g.
- **Study site:** Eagle Eye Mountain is a small andesitic volcanic breccia hill, about 120 m above the basin floor and 6 km south of Aguila. The nearby town receives about 190 mm of annual precipitation, and the authors estimate the mountain receives slightly more than 200 mm.
- **Gap addressed:** Long midden series in the Arizona Upland (Puerto Blanco and Waterman mountains) lie near 32°N. The Eagle Eye record extends coverage northward, where the Arizona Upland had few dated middens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Modern vegetation (18 shelter sites):**
- Plots covered a 30 m radius hemicircle for presence, with a 12.6 m radius (about 250 m²) for density and canopy cover, using the log-series method of McAuliffe (1991).
- [[Cercidium Microphyllum]] was dominant, usually 25 to more than 50% of perennial cover, with no systematic aspect pattern.
- [[Encelia Farinosa]] co-dominated on southerly exposures but was absent from north and northwest aspects. Its presence or absence was the main variable separating sites in the [[Twinspan Classification]] analysis.
- [[Acacia Greggii]], *Aloysia wrightii* and *Viguiera deltoidea* were restricted to northerly aspects, and [[Carnegiea Gigantea]] was rare there.
- Single old *Prosopis velutina* and *Quercus turbinella* individuals survived on northerly cliff bases, where runoff and shade create mesic microsites.

**Key climatic contrasts:**
- Eagle Eye Mountain receives about 200 mm annual precipitation, with about 60 mm (30%) falling June–August.
- Summer monsoon precipitation is the dominant warm-season moisture source. [[Tropical Cyclones]] tracking in from the eastern Pacific during El Niño years may also add warm-season rain.

## Key Findings

### Vegetation succession

- **Late Pleistocene (22,140 ± 350 yr BP, one midden):** Woodland of *Juniperus osteosperma*, [[Pinus Monophylla]] and [[Quercus Turbinella]], with *Ericameria* spp., *Artemisia tridentata*-type, *Ephedra nevadensis* and *Yucca baccata*. Only nine perennial species were recorded, fewer than in Holocene middens, which may partly reflect the smaller sample size (292 g).
- **Early Holocene (10,440 ± 70 yr BP):** *Pinus monophylla* disappeared. Juniper, oak and several succulents persisted, and *Acacia greggii* first appeared with *Carnegiea gigantea*, *Ferocactus cylindraceus*, *Opuntia chlorotica* and *Agave deserti*. *Artemisia ludoviciana* appears only in this midden; it is documented today about 3 km southwest on the Harquahala Mountains.
- **Middle Holocene (8.9–4.0 ka; five middens):** A large gap of about 4000 years separates the 10.4 ka and 6.4 ka samples. Juniper and oak disappeared while many desertscrub species arrived, including *Cercidium floridum*, *Encelia farinosa*, *Galium stellatum*, *Hyptis albida*, *Larrea tridentata*, *Trixis californica*, *Viguiera deltoidea* and *Janusia gracilis*.
- **Late Holocene (4.0 ka–present; four middens):** [[Cercidium Microphyllum]], *Encelia farinosa* and *Larrea tridentata* dominated. *Cercidium floridum* was absent, and cacti declined in abundance and number of species.

### Timing of Cercidium microphyllum

- *Cercidium microphyllum* first appeared at 4540 yr BP, late in the middle Holocene. It was absent from three older middle Holocene middens (5.1, 5.3 and 6.4 ka).
- The two species coexisted briefly, at about 4.0–4.5 ka, during the middle to late Holocene transition.
- The tree arrived about 6000 years after *Carnegiea gigantea*. The authors attribute the lag to dispersal limitation: [[Seed Dispersal]] by seed-eating rodents ([[Pocket Mice]] and [[Kangaroo Rats]]) confines seeds to short distances, whereas bird dispersal of saguaro seeds covers many kilometres.
- Comparison sites support this. *C. microphyllum* was present about 10,000–9370 yr BP at the Homaday Mountains (southern Sonora), 5240 yr BP at Puerto Blanco and 3880 yr BP at Waterman Mountains.

### Paleoclimate

- **Temperature:** *Encelia farinosa*, which is frost-intolerant, occurred at 4540 and 6425 yr BP on a northeasterly exposure where it is absent today (the most northerly modern occurrence is 60° NE). This contradicts an earlier suggestion of more frequent freezes during the middle Holocene and indicates slightly warmer, particularly winter, conditions. The study cites independent isotope and packrat body-size evidence for warmer middle Holocene temperatures (the value in the scanned text is illegible).
- **Precipitation:** Middle Holocene [[Cercidium Floridum]] and [[Setaria Leucopila]] on exposed slopes indicate moister conditions. Modern analogues for *C. floridum* receive 300–400 mm annually, roughly 50–100% more than Eagle Eye Mountain.
- *Setaria leucopila* (a C-4 grass) is absent today from the site and occurs where summer precipitation is 100–120 mm or more. The authors estimate this threshold is 67–100% above present summer rainfall, pointing to a substantial increase in warm-season precipitation during the middle Holocene.
- Cacti were also more abundant in the middle Holocene, including *Opuntia acanthocarpa*, which is absent from the modern shelter plots but abundant on alluvial fans to the southwest.

### Statistical notes

- Mean species richness per Holocene midden was 15.4 (range 13–21), compared with 9 species in the single Late Pleistocene midden.
- Holocene herbaceous species per midden ranged from 5 to 13 (mean 8.8). The Late Pleistocene midden had one herbaceous species.
- The number of winter-active and summer-active ephemerals was not significantly negatively correlated across Holocene samples (Spearman ρ = −0.45, p = 0.19).

### Limitations and recommendations

- Radiocarbon-dated middens from the early Holocene and first half of the middle Holocene are scarce. Timing of arrival for many desertscrub species (including [[Larrea Tridentata]]) is poorly constrained.
- Lower Colorado River sites, such as Picacho Peak, Whipple Mountains and Tinajas Altas, have denser early–middle Holocene series and offer better resolution.
- The authors call for more middens and for paleohydrological and climate-modelling work on the sources of warm-season precipitation.
