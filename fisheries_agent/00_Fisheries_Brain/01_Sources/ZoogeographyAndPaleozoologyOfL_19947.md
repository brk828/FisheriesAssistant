# Zoogeography and Paleozoology of Leeches, Molluscs, and Amphibians in Western Bonneville Basin, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ranidae #Hirudinea #Mollusca

## Executive Summary

This 1993 paleolimnology study by Peter Hovingh compares the distribution of amphibians, leeches, molluscs and fish among the artesian spring systems of [[Tule Valley]], [[Snake Valley]] and [[Fish Springs Flat]] in the western [[Bonneville Basin]], Utah. Tule Valley springs lack fish and molluscs entirely, while the adjacent valleys support both. The author attributes this contrast to limited dispersal after [[Lake Bonneville]] desiccated about 13,000 years ago, and proposes paleozoological models for the present fauna. Passive aerial dispersal is judged unsuccessful as an explanation for the observed patterns.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The [[Great Basin]] contains 68 hydrologically isolated basins in Nevada and Utah. Earlier fish work by [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1948) and [[Hubbs et al.]] (1974) proposed Pleistocene hydrological links among these basins and rejected passive aerial dispersal of fish.
- **Paleolake:** During the Pleistocene, Lake Bonneville was about 300 m deep with a surface area of 52,000 km², similar to present-day [[Lake Michigan]] (58,016 km²).
- **Tule Valley:** A topographically closed depression in the southwestern Bonneville basin, separated from Snake Valley by the Confusion Range and from the Sevier basin and Fish Springs Flat by the House Range.
- **Focal taxa:** Amphibians (*Rana pretiosa*, the [[Spotted Frog]]), leeches (Hirudinea) and molluscs (Mollusca), with fish used as comparative data.
- **Methods:** Field conductivity and temperature were measured in 1981 with a YSI-33 meter; oxygen with a Jenway 9070 meter. Over two dozen visits to Tule Valley were made over 12 years, plus one to four visits per spring in adjacent valleys. Substrates were sieved (1 mm mesh), and specimens were preserved in ethanol and archived at the Utah Museum of Natural History.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Paleolake chronology (per source):**
- Pluvial cycles earlier than 600,000 yr BP, 200,000 yr BP, 140,000 yr BP, 60,000 yr BP, and 30,000–11,000 yr BP.
- Lake Tule in Tule Valley reached 1393 m, covered about 660 km², and was 75 m deep.
- Lake Bonneville overflowed at Sand Pass into northern Tule Valley about 19,500 yr BP, and remained connected to Tule Valley until about 14,200 yr BP.
- Tule Valley regressed to 1369 m by about 13,790 yr BP, at an average rate of 0.6 m per year.

**Spring hydrology:**
- Tule Valley has six high-elevation springs above the Lake Bonneville level and 26 artesian springs on the basin floor, probably linked to faults and fractures.
- Over 80% of ground water inflow comes from the Sevier, Wah Wah and Snake basins.
- Spring water is predominantly sodium-sulfate-chloride. Flows range from none (impounded wetlands) up to 251 s⁻¹ at Coyote Springs.
- Spring temperatures are 15–31 °C; conductivity is 1500–2700 µmhos/cm.
- Spring-associated wetlands cover about 195,000 m² and show conductivity from 1200 to over 93,000 µmhos/cm.

**Climate and vegetation:**
- A 50% increase in precipitation between 1980 and 1984 increased spring flow and wetland extent, which peaked in 1986–1987.
- Increased flow killed greasewood, and cattails and bulrushes expanded. The author reports a roughly three-year lag between wetter conditions and spring response, and a three- to six-year lag after drier conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Fish:** Snake Valley has six native fish species (including [[Least Chub]], *Lotichthys phlegethontis*); Fish Springs Flat has two; Tule Valley has none.
- **Molluscs:** Snake Valley supports 18 species and Fish Springs Flat 12. Tule Valley springs contain none, although subfossil shells of *Physa*, *Lymnaea*, *Helisoma subcrenata* and *Pisidium* occur on the saline mud flats.
- **Leeches:** Four species occur in Tule Valley, confined to the South Tule, North Tule and South Central complexes. *Theromyzon rude* and *Helobdella triserialis* were recorded only in Tule Valley, and *Haemopis marmorata* was found in Tule Valley and not Snake Valley. *Erpobdella punctata* occurs in all three valleys.
- **Amphibians:** The [[Spotted Frog]] (*Rana pretiosa*) occurs in Snake and Tule Valleys. Introduced [[Leopard Frog]] and [[Bullfrog]] populations are restricted to Snake Valley and Fish Springs. The [[Spadefoot Toad]] (*Scaphiopus intermontanus*) is largely terrestrial and breeds in ephemeral waters.
- **Genetic evidence:** Tule Valley and Snake Valley spotted frogs were genetically indistinguishable across 32 enzyme loci (Nei distance = 0). The Provo River population was distinct (Nei distance = 0.113).
- **Snail distributions:** *Catinella* persists above the highest Lake Bonneville shoreline in Tule Valley and southern Snake Valley, but has not reached valley floors. *Oxyloma* has colonized Snake Valley floor habitats after the lake receded. *Pyrgulopsis* occurs above the shoreline in Tule Valley.
- **Dispersal:** The author judges that no successful passive aerial dispersal of molluscs, fish or *Helobdella stagnalis* has reached Tule Valley since about 14,000 years ago. *Theromyzon rude* is a bird parasite that could disperse passively, but it is restricted to Tule Valley. Waterfowl are the most likely carriers, but none were seen at the high Tule Valley springs.
- **Paleozoological models:** Two explanations are proposed for the missing fauna: (1) the Tule Valley arm may have been a net evaporative, more saline arm during regression; (2) rising salinity may have removed permanent freshwater refugia. Tule Valley may have acted as a refugium for some taxa, and subsurface flows may have preserved leeches (*Haemopis marmorata*, *Erpobdella punctata*).
- **Recent human impacts:** Spring excavation, introduction of exotic fish and amphibians, and channelization of outflows are noted as impacts in neighbouring valleys. Tule Valley was not affected by these practices.
- **Conclusions:** Post-Bonneville movement is inhibited by the aridity and salinity of the basin. Species differ in their ability to move laterally along the paleolake shoreline and vertically during transgression and regression.
