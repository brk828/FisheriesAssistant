# Fisheries Report of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 report, prepared by the Fisheries Task Group of the [[Colorado River Great Basin Field Committee]], describes the present and potential sport fisheries of the Lower Colorado River from [[Hoover Dam]] to the Mexican border. The river is divided into seven sections, each assessed individually for physical setting, aquatic habitat, fish and other aquatic life, public access, fishing use, and recommended management. The authors conclude that the fishery is a valuable and growing recreational resource that is not being used to its fullest extent, and they recommend continued research, controlled stocking, habitat improvement, public access protection, and interstate coordination among [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]], Nevada, and California.

## Background & Description

The report was requested by the Great Basin Field Committee and compiled by biologists from the fish and game departments of Nevada, Arizona, and California, with a representative of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Field work was carried out over the preceding year, and some results were still pending at the time of writing. Drought and the drying of lakes and reservoirs across the Southwest had made the lower river the last frontier for freshwater anglers.

The report covers the following fish species:
- **Game fishes:** Rainbow Trout, Largemouth Bass, Smallmouth Bass, Black and White Crappie, Bluegill, Green Sunfish, Brown and Yellow Bullhead, Channel Catfish
- **Rough fishes:** Common Carp, Bonytail, Razorback Sucker, Colorado Squawfish, Speckled Dace, and Tenpounder
- **Other species noted:** Striped Mullet, Utah Chub (as a threat), and Golden Shiner (as a recommended forage fish)

Aquatic plants and invertebrates mentioned include Sago Pondweed, Spiny Naiad, Widgeon Grass, Horned Pondweed, Cattail, Rush, Tule, Freshwater Shrimp (*Gammarus*), and snails. Also noted are the Green Sea Turtle-type [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] and [[American Bullfrog]] as minor recreational elements, and a freshwater sea-turtle species (*Emydoidea*-type soft-shelled turtle).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Section I – Lake Mohave (Hoover Dam to Davis Dam).** A reservoir in Black and Eldorado canyons, operated with a four-foot fluctuation target. Rough water from strong winds is a major limit to fishing. Rainbow Trout and Largemouth Bass coexist, with trout favouring the cool upper lake and bass the warmer, rocky lower shoreline. Water released from Hoover Dam is about 53°F from the lower outlet, which keeps the upper ten miles cool. A summer thermal barrier was expected between Cave Rapids and Emery's Landing.

**Section II – Davis Dam to Needles.** A 36-mile reach through Mohave Valley, with lush riparian vegetation (willow, cottonwood, mesquite, arrowweed, saltcedar). Water temperatures are being modified by the Davis Dam releases. Substrate is unstable sand, limiting trout habitat until scouring creates a rubble bottom.

**Section III – Needles to Parker Dam.** Includes the newly dredged Needles-Topock channel and [[Havasu Lake]], a roughly 25,000-acre reservoir behind [[Parker Dam]]. Lake level fluctuations are generally limited to six feet or less. The dredged channel was expected to reduce habitat productivity, while the Topock Swamp opening was expected to create good warmwater fish habitat.

**Section IV – Parker Dam to Headgate Rock Dam.** A river section with a stable bottom, rooted aquatic vegetation along the shore, and clearer, more stable water than upstream. It is subject to large flow fluctuations from Parker Dam power releases.

**Section V – Headgate Rock Dam to Palo Verde Drain.** A sandy, shifting-channel reach with several lentic waters, including Lost Lake, Alligator Slough, Lake Mohave-adjacent backwaters, and Palo Verde Drain, which is turbid because of irrigation return flow.

**Section VI – Palo Verde Drain to Imperial Dam.** A reach with extensive backwaters such as Davis Lake, Three-Finger Lake, and Cibola Lake. Sedimentation and planned dredging were expected to drain Cibola Lake and related waters.

**Section VII – Imperial Dam to the International Border.** A warmwater zone with shifting sand, high salinity from agricultural drainage, and a turbid, sediment-laden main channel. Lakes include Laguna Lake, Martinez Lake, and Mittry Lake, the last with a dissolved-salt concentration rising toward levels unsafe for fish.

**Key environmental drivers:** water temperature regimes set by dam releases, reservoir level fluctuations, turbidity, aquatic vegetation, shoreline cover, and irrigation drainage.

## Key Findings

- **Fishery status:** The fishery is largely under-utilised, with the exception of some heavily used sections. Fishing pressure is growing with population in the Southwest.
- **Lake Mohave trout:** Rainbow Trout spawn in the upper lake and are reported to exceed the size of 1950-catch records. The 1950 record fish weighed 18 pounds.
- **Lake Mohave bass:** A large bass population had a successful spawn in April and May 1951, promising strong bass fishing in the lower lake.
- **Havasu Lake creel census:** Catch per angler-day was 0.91 pounds of fish per hour, with an average weight of about one pound. Overall productivity was considered low, with a declining bass catch since the post-impoundment peak.
- **Lake Mohave use:** Fishing use for 1951 (first five months) was 51 percent above the same period in 1950.
- **Creel census (Section I):** Of 825 fishermen over six months, over 95 percent of catch was Rainbow Trout, averaging 3.25 fish per fisherman.
- **Out-of-state anglers:** An estimated 80 percent of boat anglers at Lake Mohave came from California, with 15 percent from Nevada and 5 percent from other states.
- **Use permit:** Arizona and Nevada require a Colorado River Special Use Permit ($5.00, valid one year) for fishing from a boat on shared waters.
- **Section IV (Parker Dam to Headgate Rock Dam):** Estimated fishing use of 150,000 to 200,000 angler-days in 1950; the fishery is dominated by Bluegill and Channel Catfish.
- **Section V:** Channel Catfish is the mainstay of the fishery, with Bluegill and Largemouth Bass of secondary importance. The Palo Verde Drain bass fishery had declined because of turbidity from irrigation.
- **Section VI (Imperial Dam to Castle Dome Landing):** About 95 percent of fishing was from boats, with total effort of roughly 3,500 angler-days. The creel census recorded 1.52 fish per angler-day, and Largemouth Bass were the most popular species.
- **Section VII:** The fishery is warmwater, dominated by Channel Catfish; Imperial Dam operations cause fish losses through spillway passage.
- **Management recommendations (summary):**
  - Manage the upper Lake Mohave section as a trout fishery with continued fry and fingerling stocking.
  - Protect spawning areas and restrict use of game fish as live bait; prohibit Utah Chub introduction, which can destroy sport fisheries.
  - Plant rooted aquatic vegetation to increase cover and food.
  - Keep public access lands along the river and improve roads, especially Arizona-side access.
  - Limit water level fluctuations during spawning seasons (for example, under two feet for bass in March to May).
  - Continue research, including studies of channel catfish and bass, and evaluate introduction of forage or game species such as Golden Shiner and Smallmouth Bass.
  - Coordinate regulation among all three states and form a permanent interstate fisheries body, modelled on other interstate fisheries.
- **Conclusions:** Dams have generally benefited river sport fisheries, but channel dredging and extreme water fluctuations may prove harmful. Stocking native warmwater fishes already present in the river is not recommended.
