# Management of Discharge, Temperature, and Sediment for Native Fishes in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1993 position paper, prepared by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona State University]] and Bio/West, Inc., recommends how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] should be operated to restore native fishes of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. The authors argue that the 1963 closure of the dam and its subsequent operation altered flow, thermal and sediment regimes, severely harming the native [[Ichthyofauna]]. They contend that conservation and restoration depend on returning conditions close to those of the fishes' evolutionary history, a process that will take generations of these long-lived fish.

The core recommendations are to:
- eliminate daily flow fluctuations;
- adjust seasonal hydrology to parallel natural discharge;
- deliver controlled spring floods to suppress [[Non-Native Fishes]];
- add water temperature modification (via a [[Multi-Level Intake Structure]]) and sediment augmentation to complete restoration.

The authors state that the opinions are based on their collective field experience and do not address political or economic considerations, and do not necessarily reflect the views of their agencies.

## Background & Description

**Authors and purpose.** The document presents recommendations for operating Glen Canyon Dam to optimize management, enhancement and reestablishment of native fish populations in the Colorado River and its tributaries. It is written so that high-level managers with limited time can grasp the argument, with a longer technical section supporting it.

**Management objectives.** The stated objectives for Grand Canyon are to:
1. promote successful reproduction of native fishes in the Colorado River and its tributaries;
2. increase numbers of early life stages;
3. increase recruitment to sustain reproducing segments of populations;
4. reduce the competitive and predatory effects of non-native fishes.

Complete restoration would also require reintroducing species extirpated after the dam closure.

**Species of concern.** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was extirpated from Grand Canyon within a decade of the dam's closure. The [[Humpback Sucker]] also declined, and its mainstem reproduction was reduced or eliminated. Non-native species such as the [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]] are discussed as competitors and predators whose populations are favored by altered conditions.

**Pre-dam information.** The paper notes a scarcity of detailed pre-dam habitat descriptions for the mainstem Colorado River and Grand Canyon, so reconstruction of historic habitats relies on the physical processes that formed them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow changes.** Before the dam, a pronounced, predictable seasonal flow variation existed. After closure, daily discharge fluctuations (driven by [[Hydropower Peaking]]) replaced it. Lake Powell's sediment trapping greatly reduced the sediment transported through Glen and Grand canyons, and hypolimnetic releases altered downstream temperatures, reducing their seasonal range and mean.

**Habitat loss.** The authors state that these changes eliminated nearshore backwater and [[Mainstem Rearing Habitat]] and shifted the balance toward non-native fishes. Physical barriers (such as the Little Colorado River mouth environment) and cold-water temperatures further restricted native reproduction.

**Non-native introductions.** Most non-native species in Grand Canyon were introduced after the dam closure, including sportfish stocked above and below the dam. The paper notes that these introductions were carried out with little regard for their impact on native fishes.

**Thermal and sediment context.** Cold hypolimnetic water inhibits mainstem spawning by native fish. Silts and clays in sediment contribute turbidity (which provides cover for native fishes and limits predators) and supply phosphorus, which appears to limit primary production in Grand Canyon.

## Key Findings

**Flow recommendations**
- Replace daily fluctuations with seasonal discharge patterns, with base flows during autumn and winter kept low.
- Spring peak flows should be increased when water is available beyond legally mandated volumes (the assumed mode of 8.23 million acre-feet annual delivery). Spring-summer needs are met first, with a minimum of 5,000 cfs; the peak should approach about 30,000 cfs in May, though not necessarily as a maximum.
- Spring flows should serve four purposes: a spawning cue for mainstem fishes; backing up tributary mouths to form lentic rearing habitat; maintaining nearshore rearing habitat; and suppressing non-native fishes through scouring, displacement, disruption of reproduction and reduced survival of early life stages.
- The descending limb should decline in monthly steps, return to base flows by September, and hold base flows through February. Base flows should be between 5,000 and 10,000 cfs.
- Descent in June and early July should be slow to support mainstem spawning, then rapid once the spawning period ends. Maintaining nearshore habitat within the 5,000–10,000 cfs range is recommended.

**Temperature recommendations**
- A Multi-Level Intake Structure is considered the only practical way to remove cold-water restrictions on mainstem reproduction.
- Release temperatures should warm gradually from March or the earliest feasible spring month to a high of approximately 15°C from epilimnetic releases in June through September or October, then return rapidly to hypolimnetic temperatures in November.
- Estimated effects: about 2°C warming at the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence and nearly 7°C at [[Diamond Creek]] (356 km downstream). Temperatures would reach about 19–20°C in the upper reaches, supporting optimum spawning and growth in a reach beginning about 200 km below the dam.
- The paper flags risks of warming, including altered productivity, changes in species composition, invasion by additional non-native fishes, and spread of non-native diseases and parasites. The authors judge that periodic floods would remain a major control on non-native populations.

**Sediment recommendation**
- Sediment augmentation during the highest spring flows is recommended to restore habitat-forming processes and maintain nearshore rearing habitat.
- The quantity, grain size and delivery method remain unknown. The authors consider an elaborate pipeline unnecessary, pending hydrological and sediment transport models.

**Integrated management**
- The authors advance a working hypothesis that combining flow, temperature and sediment manipulation can destabilize the balance in favor of native fishes and provide a flexible tool for management.
- They also note that winter flow fluctuations could disadvantage small non-native species such as the fathead minnow and red shiner, and that these fluctuations would benefit hydroelectric production.

**Conclusion.** Failure to incorporate these hydrologic, thermal and sediment processes into dam operations is identified as a major contributor to the stalemate over a preferred alternative. A protracted, multi-generational recovery period is expected.
