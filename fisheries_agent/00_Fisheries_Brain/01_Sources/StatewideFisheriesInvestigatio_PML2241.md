# Statewide Fisheries Investigation: Fossil Creek Fish Management Report, 1994–1996

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report summarizes three years (1994–1996) of fish surveys on [[Fossil Creek]], a 14-mile perennial stream in north-central Arizona and a tributary of the [[Verde River]]. Surveys were run by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Region II Fisheries) to determine the status of [[Roundtail Chub]], a native species, in the presence of increasing non-native fish. Surveys found that native chub were being displaced in parts of the system by [[Smallmouth Bass]] and other non-native species. The authors present four management options and recommend further studies before any action is taken. They also caution that proposed flow increases could worsen the problem.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Fossil Creek is a perennial stream with bedrock pools, boulder runs and riffles in a well-defined channel. Channel gradient is fairly constant, ranging from about 0.2% to 2.8%.
- **Water source:** Large natural springs near the Mogollon Rim supply the creek, which carries high calcium carbonate and has historically built travertine dams. Over 36 years of record, mean discharge averages 43.3 cfs, making it one of the largest spring-fed streams in Arizona.
- **Human use:** Two hydropower plants (the Childs and Irving plants, operated under [[FERC Relicensing]] conditions) divert most of the flow. Only a small residual flow returns to the creek.
- **Disturbance:** Steep banks and steep gradient make the creek prone to floods. The largest flood recorded by plant personnel occurred in 1995. It briefly flowed about 10 feet deep at high velocity and displaced fish downstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Survey design.** Five permanent electrofishing stations (each about 200 m long) were set up in July 1994 following the [[Roundtail Chub Survey Protocol]]. They were spread along the full 14-mile reach, from the confluence (station 5, the lowest) upstream to the dam area. Surveys used [[Electrofishing]] effort, reported as electrofishing units (EFU, one EFU = 600 seconds of effort). Habitat was recorded by subhabitat type (pool, run, riffle) with depth, velocity and cover.

**Species present.** The fish community at the stations included:
- Native: Roundtail Chub, [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Pantosteus Clarki]] (suckers).
- Non-native: [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]].

**Habitat preference.** Using the [[Habitat Usage]] tables:
- Roundtail chub were more often found in faster, shallower water (mean 1.24 m/s, mean depth 344 mm) than smallmouth bass (mean 1.56 m/s, mean depth 455 mm).
- Smallmouth bass were associated with cover, with high cover values recorded in their pools.
- Chub used runs and riffles more, while smallmouth bass were more common in pools and, by 1995, in runs.

## Key Findings

**1. Native chub were dominant in the upper stations in 1994.** Roundtail chub were the most abundant native fish at stations 1–4 in 1994. Non-native species dominated station 5, and smallmouth bass were present at station 3.

**2. Chub declined where non-native species increased.** By 1996, native chub were absent from stations 3 and 4, and smallmouth bass were present throughout the system. Smallmouth bass also appeared to be entering at station 5. Catch per EFU was low or zero for chub at stations 2 and 5 across all three years, which the report uses as evidence of reduced chub numbers.

**3. Possible negative relationship between chub and non-native species.** The data showed a general inverse pattern: where non-native species increased, chub numbers tended to fall. The authors state that the three years of data are not enough to prove a causal link. They also point out that natural flood events can cause large short-term changes in fish distribution.

**4. Non-native removal by anglers.** A December 1995 visit (not part of the formal survey) to find a barrier site recorded 21 smallmouth bass taken on hook and line. A June 1996 trip and angler reports documented 2–3 lb smallmouth bass being caught in the lower reach.

**5. Management options.** The report outlines four options:
- **Option 1 (most costly, highest chance of success):** Install a fish barrier near the Fossil Creek–Verde River confluence, chemically renovate the lower reach from the Irving plant to the confluence (about 10 miles of creek), and reintroduce native fish after confirming success.
- **Option 2:** Install a fish barrier without chemical renovation. This is less costly but has a high chance of non-native fish moving back into the creek.
- **Option 3:** Remove the barrier(s) that currently block fish movement in the creek and the Verde River, and monitor the native population.
- **Option 4 (status quo management):** Increase bag limits on smallmouth bass to encourage angler harvest, promote Fossil Creek as a smallmouth and roundtail fishery, and improve angler access (parking, camping, trails).

**6. Flow increase concerns.** A future FERC relicensing plan proposes releasing an additional 5–15 cfs into Fossil Creek to improve habitat and riparian areas. The report warns that higher flows could help non-native species spread and could threaten chub populations. If flows are increased, Option 1 should be implemented at the same time.

**7. Research needs.** The authors recommend studies on smallmouth bass–chub interactions, including predation, competition and habitat overlap, before any action is taken. Any barriers, structures or flow changes should be documented and photographed for future reference.

**Conclusion.** Other studies have concluded that non-native species can reduce or eliminate native fish populations. The authors therefore conclude that non-native fish are a serious threat to Roundtail Chub in Fossil Creek and that prompt management is needed.
