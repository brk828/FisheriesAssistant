# Age and Growth of the Brown Trout in the Logan River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study by William F. Sigler of the [[Utah State Agricultural College]] examines the life history of the [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta fario*) in the [[Logan River]], a cold, steep, high-velocity stream in [[Cache County]], [[Utah]]. Of 1,053 brown trout examined, 286 were retained for age and growth analysis. Growth was estimated from [[Scale Analysis]] and validated against standard methods. The study found that Logan River brown trout grow at roughly the same length rate as many other populations, with a relative weight somewhat higher than those populations. The work was part of a broader fishery investigation begun in 1948 by the [[Department of Wildlife Management]] and the [[Utah Cooperative Wildlife Research Unit]].

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The investigation ran from June 1948 through February 1951. Its aim was to document the life history of brown trout, a species for which detailed wild-growth data were limited. The author notes that [[Carlander]] (1950) lists more than 100 publications referencing brown trout, yet few describe the growth of wild populations.

**Fish community.** Native fishes above the lowest impoundment dam include:
- [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*)
- [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*)
- [[Utah Sculpin]] (*Cottus bairdi semiscaber*)
- [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus hydrophlox*), rarely encountered

Introduced fishes include the [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis fontinalis*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri irideus*), and brown trout. A rainbow x cutthroat hybrid has also appeared since rainbow trout were introduced. No upstream fish passage exists over the lowest impoundment dam.

**Study area.** The Logan River heads in Franklin County, southern Idaho, and flows southwest to the [[Bear River]]. Key characteristics:
- Watershed of 225 square miles of weathered Paleozoic limestone, dolomite, and shale
- Main branch enters Utah at 8,500 ft and drops to 4,500 ft at [[Cache Valley]], about 36 stream miles
- Average gradient of about 70 feet per mile
- Brown trout most abundant below 6,800 ft
- Three artificial impoundments above 4,500 ft, none exceeding 22 ft in depth or 4 acres, supplying power and irrigation water

**Sampling methods.** Fish were collected mainly by [[Electrofishing]], with some specimens taken by experimental gill nets and hook and line. Scales and length data followed standard procedures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Substrate:** Dominated by coarse gravel, rubble, and boulders. Sand and gravel are scarce because of steep gradient and high velocity. Silt is slight except during spring runoff.
- **Turbidity:** Ranged from 25 to 32 parts per million (silicon dioxide equivalents) in spring and dropped to 6 ppm in September and October. Pollution is minimal apart from soil erosion.
- **Velocity:** Averaged 2.6 feet per second across stations sampled from April to October in 1948 and 1949.
- **Temperature:** Water is cold because springs supply most summer and fall flow, and high velocity and bank shade limit warming. Temperatures from the Idaho line to the upper (third) dam averaged about 48°F and did not exceed 60°F.
- **Discharge:** Average monthly mean discharge (1946–1949 water seasons) was 247 cubic feet per second, peaking in May (717 cfs) and lowest in February (108 cfs).
- **Habitat overlap:** The mountain whitefish occupies habitat similar to that of the brown trout.

The abstract describes the stream as having a steep gradient, high velocity, and relatively few pools.

## Key Findings

**1. Body-scale relationship.** Standard length can be predicted from scale radius:
- L = 40.46 mm + 0.44185R + 0.01908R², where L is standard length (mm) and R is scale radius (mm) × 80.
- The model was fit to 286 fish. Standard length was measured from the snout tip to the posterior edge of the hypural plate.
- Growth was back-calculated with a nomograph after the scale method was validated by the methods of [[Van Oosten]] (1929) and [[Hile]] (1941).

**2. Growth rate.**
- Brown trout grow about 3 inches per year after their first year.
- They reach the 7-inch legal length early in their third year.
- Older year-classes do not grow faster than younger ones. They simply live longer.
- Growth in length is comparable to that of several other populations, including those reported by [[Eddy and Carlander]] (1939) and [[Bean]] (1902).
- Calculated mean lengths at the end of years 1 through 8 were roughly 87, 148, 211, 262, 337, 399, 552, and 600 mm, based on the sampled age classes.

**3. Length conversions** (from Table 1 fish):
- Standard length × 1.173 = total length
- Total length × 0.852 = standard length
- Fork length × 0.887 = standard length

**4. Length-frequency distribution and sampling bias.**
- Fish under 4 inches made up only 8% of the total. This did not follow the expected pattern, and the authors attributed it to electrofishing selectivity. Smaller fish receive less shock.
- Counts dropped steadily from 7 to 20 inches.
- Of the legal-sized fish, 47% were under 10 inches and 61% were under 15 inches.
- Fish between 4 and 9.7 inches made up 95% of the age sample and 76% of the total population studied.

**5. Length-weight relationship.** Weight in grams relates to standard length in millimeters as W = 0.000022 L^2.96 (see [[Length-Weight Relationship]]). Actual and calculated weights agree well for groups containing more than six fish.

**6. Condition factor.**
- Condition factor K = W × 10⁵ / L³ (see [[Condition Factor]]).
- The highest K (1.938) occurred in the smallest fish, and K declines somewhat with length.
- Logan River fish have a higher condition factor than several other populations, citing [[Schuck]] (1945) and Carlander (unpublished).

**7. Maximum size.**
- Few brown trout over 5 pounds are taken from streams, according to [[Needham]] (1938).
- [[Bean]] (1902) lists a maximum of 22 pounds and 35 inches, but notes that 5 or 6 pounds is a good average.
- The Logan River has produced very large fish, including a 39-inch individual taken from the lowest impoundment in 1937. The weight in the scanned text is garbled and cannot be reliably reported. Fish of 12 to 18 pounds are caught most years in the lower river.

**Conclusion.** The Logan River brown trout population grows at a typical rate for wild populations, with above-average relative weight, in a cold, fast, gravel-bottomed stream whose springs stabilize temperatures.
