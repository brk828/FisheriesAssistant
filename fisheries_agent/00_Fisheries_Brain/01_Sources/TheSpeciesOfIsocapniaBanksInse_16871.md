# Taxonomic Revision of the Stonefly Genus Isocapnia (Insecta, Plecoptera, Nemouridae)

#entomology #plecoptera #nemouridae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic revision treats the stonefly genus [[Isocapnia]] (family [[Nemouridae]], order [[Plecoptera]]). The genus includes 10 known species in the North American cordillera, 1 in Japan and 1 in coastal Siberia. Four new American species (*fraseri*, *hyalita*, *missourii*, *mogila*) and one new Siberian species (*kudia*) are described. The paper's most notable finding is the occurrence of dwarf males with rudimentary wings alongside normal long-winged males, a condition that the author considers unusual or possibly unique among stoneflies. The work provides a key to the American species, species groupings based on structural characters, and distribution and collection records.

## Background & Description

The genus was established by Banks (1938) with *Arsapnia grandis* Banks (1908) as genotype. It has a short history as a named genus but has attracted interest because of its rarity and because several species are large compared with most capniines. Adult external anatomy was documented in detail by [[Hanson]] (1946), and the thoracic sterna are distinctive enough to separate the genus from other capniines. The known nymphs are unusual among stoneflies in having long swimming hairs along the cerci.

Specimens came from a number of institutional collections, including the Canadian National Collection (CNC), Cornell University Collection (CUC), United States National Museum (USNM), Illinois Natural History Survey (INHS), Harvard MCZ, Montana State College (MSC) and the private collection of S. G. Jewett, Jr. (SGJ). Much of the material was collected by the author, [[William E. Ricker]], and by R. A. Hays and S. G. Jewett, Jr.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dwarf males and brachyptery.** Brachyptery (reduced wings) is not rare in Plecoptera, but it usually affects males more severely than females. In *Isocapnia*, short-winged and fully long-winged individuals occur in the same population without intergradation, and the short-winged males are also generally smaller in body size. The author suggests the dwarfs may be precocious year-old maturing males in populations where most males and almost all females mature at 2 years. This is compared with precocious "jack" males in salmon of the genus *Oncorhynchus* (*O. nerka*, *O. kisutch*, *O. tshawytscha*).

**Habitat and distribution.** The habitats recorded include clear mountain streams and lakes (e.g., *integra* at Banff, Alberta), cold creeks on Vancouver Island (e.g., Cameron Creek and Haslam Creek), and the large, muddy Fraser River near sea level (*fraseri* and *agassizi* at Agassiz, British Columbia). The upper Spray River at Banff had recently been dammed and regulated, so its temperature regime was likely different from earlier conditions. Emergence is seasonal and often narrow: *hyalita* was collected only in a limited window in May, and adults of several species emerged from March to June. Some specimens were taken from cobwebs on a water-gauging station, which made it unclear whether they had emerged in the same season or the previous one.

**Co-occurring fauna.** Hyalite Creek, the type locality of *hyalita*, supports a rich stonefly fauna, including the rarities *Utaperla sopladora* and *Alloperla albertensis*.

## Key Findings

**1. Dwarf male occurrence.** Dwarf males are known in the following species:
- Only dwarf males known: *missourii*.
- Both dwarf and normal males: *agassizi*, *hyalita*, *fraseri*, *integra*, *japonica*, *spenceri*.
- Only normal males known: *abbreviata*, *crinita*, *grandis*, *mogila*.

Dwarfing is not uniform across a species' range. Dwarf *agassizi* were abundant on the Fraser River but absent from several large Cameron Creek collections, although dwarf *spenceri* occurred there. Repeated searches failed to find dwarf *grandis* even where long-winged males were numerous.

**2. Species groups.** The American species fall into three groups:
- **grandis group** (*agassizi*, *crinita*, *grandis*): numerous costal cross veins (four to eight before the end of Sc), a long, slender male supra-anal process, and nymphs with short body hair.
- **hyalita group** (*fraseri*, *hyalita*, *integra*, *missourii*, *spenceri*): fewer costal cross veins, a raised bilobed or cleft male 9th tergite process, and nymphs densely covered with long hair.
- **abbreviata group** (*abbreviata*, *mogila*): small species with irregular forewing venation in *mogila*. Nymphs are unknown.

The Japanese *japonica* fits the hyalita group. The Siberian *kudia* is reminiscent of *grandis* but has fewer costal cross veins and a female 8th sternite unlike any American species.

**3. Taxonomic changes and new species.** Four American species are described as new:
- ***Isocapnia fraseri*** (Fraser River, Agassiz, British Columbia): the material was previously included in the type series of *agassizi*. Its distinguishing features include blackish-brown wing membranes and a male 9th tergite process with a broad black band.
- ***Isocapnia hyalita*** (Hyalite Creek, Montana): males have a small chisel-like terminal process on the 9th tergite and a stout, curved supra-anal process. The male is known from a single holotype and allotype with additional paratypes.
- ***Isocapnia missourii*** (Missouri River, Toston, Montana): very close to *fraseri* but much larger, with a longer, more slender supra-anal process. The known dwarf male is the only male form recorded for the species.
- ***Isocapnia mogila*** (Cow Creek, Douglas County, Oregon): the forewing venation is irregular, and the male supra-anal process is short and recurved forward.

The Siberian ***Isocapnia kudia*** was collected at the Kudia River, Amagu (the modern Amgu), in July 1923, and is recorded from the holotype female. It extends the genus's known range onto the Asian mainland.

Other nomenclatural actions include:
- *Isocapnia spenceri* variety *thujae* Ricker (1943), a dwarf male form, is placed in synonymy with *spenceri* because it occurs repeatedly with long-winged spenceri in the same populations.
- *Capnia crinita* Needham and Claassen (1925) had long been an "orphan" because the type was poor. The author studied the cleared abdomen of the type and concluded that the species belongs to the grandis group. The author also notes that Hanson (1943) had suggested synonymy with *grandis*, but the author treats *crinita* as distinct.

**4. Identification key.** A dichotomous key separates the American species using wing length, the male supra-anal process (slender, stout, S-shaped or hooked), the female subgenital plate (entire, recessed or with a central sclerotized tongue), wing membrane colour and costal cross vein counts.

**5. Distribution.** The American species occur from coastal Alaska and central British Columbia south to Colorado, Utah and central California. Several are restricted to particular regions, for example *spenceri* on the coastal plain and valleys from California to British Columbia, and *grandis* from southern Alaska to northern California and Montana. The author notes that *agassizi* is abundant as an emerger from the Fraser River and occurs in smaller coastal streams from southern British Columbia to the Willamette Valley of Oregon.

**6. Specimen basis.** Records cited in the paper include the following counts. At Agassiz, British Columbia, collections on April 17, April 24 and May 1, 1957 yielded hundreds of specimens, including 48 dwarf males, 15 long-winged males and 11 females on May 1, 1957. Most other localities yielded only a few individuals. The large series supporting the dwarf-male analysis came mainly from the Fraser River and Vancouver Island streams.
