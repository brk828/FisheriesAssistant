# Spawning and Hatching of the Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus), Dexter National Fish Hatchery, 1981

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This 1981 hatchery report by Theophilus D. Inslee, of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, documents trials to spawn and hatch the [[Razorback Sucker]] from wild broodstock taken from [[Lake Mohave]]. Methods tested in 1981 included [[Hormone-Induced Spawning]] with [[Human Chorionic Gonadotropin]] (HCG) and [[Carp Pituitary]], stripping, natural [[Pond Spawning]], and several incubation systems. The 1982 follow-up aimed to refine hormone dosage, incubation, fungus control and fry handling. The central comparison was between wild-caught brood fish and fish held in culture ponds for one year. The report's conclusions were that hormone-injected stripping and the [[Heath Incubator]] worked best, that broodstock held for a year performed as well or better than wild fish, and that pond spawning was limited by silt.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid fish, and the hatchery was trying to produce large numbers of fry for a proposed stocking program. The species had to be captured, transported and held in captivity, and its reproductive biology in culture was poorly known.

- **Source and arrival:** Fish were transferred from Lake Mohave and arrived at the hatchery on January 15, 1981, in overall fair to good condition. Of 136 fish, 5 were in bad condition and 28 had thick white mucous on the body and caudal peduncle.
- **Initial treatment:** Fish were treated with 1:6000 [[Formalin]] for one hour to remove external parasites. Most males were fluid, and several females released a few eggs during unloading.
- **Holding:** Twenty fish were held in the holding house for spawning, and 15 were held separately for treatment of the mucous spots.
- **Handling:** Adults were docile and easily seined. Grasping by the tail caused immediate struggling, so cradling the fish under the head and peduncle was the preferred method.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The trials were carried out under hatchery conditions, and the report repeatedly identifies environmental factors that limited success.

- **Water temperature:** Ponds ranged from 44°F to 61°F over the first 68 days, mostly in the 50s°F. Temperatures in the low 50s°F were not conducive to plankton production. Temperatures for wild fish differed by as much as 10°F to 12°F from the lake water, which the author thought may have harmed spawning.
- **Sediment and substrate:** Wind-driven erosion from newly built, unvegetated dikes made water turbid and deposited silt on rock spawning beds. The author concluded that silt was the limiting factor for reproduction in the ponds.
- **Algae and plankton:** A [[Phytoplankton Bloom]] limited visibility for the last 30 days of the pond spawning period. Slime algae grew on rocks and pond bottoms in the flow-through pond. A [[Zooplankton Bloom]] was needed to feed swim-up fry.
- **Fungus and water quality:** Fungal infestations (Saprolegnia-type growth, described as fungus) developed on eggs and substrate. Water exchange through the rock beds was low, and the water in one rock area became sour.
- **Seasonality:** Wild fish were captured when ripe in late winter and early spring. The report suggests that the species may be an intermittent spawner during late winter and early spring.

## Key Findings

### Spawning methods

- **Pond spawning:** Two 0.1 surface acre ponds (C5 and C6) were stocked with 26 females and 24 males (C6) and 26 females and 23 males (C5). Spawning activity was first noted on day 12. Three cleaned nest areas were found in C5 and five in C6. Eggs were seen on rocks on three occasions, with at least four spawning events in total. The author concluded that pond spawning would not provide enough fry for the proposed stocking program unless specialized spawning ponds were built.
- **Flow-through pond (Pond D):** Five females and five males were stocked over a fiberglass liner with rock. Nest preparation was seen, but no eggs were observed before the fish were moved in mid-April. The author recommended retrying the method with riper fish.
- **Circular tank spawning:** Females injected with 2 mg carp pituitary per pound spawned in 6-foot tanks. Nest preparation was visible after 24 hours, and eggs were found after eight hours. Spawning occurred in darkness. In tank #1, an estimated 50,000 eggs were present, about 30% of which water-hardened. A total of 838 fry were harvested from tank #1, and none from the control tank. Low fry numbers were attributed to suspected egg loss from low oxygen in the rocks. A second trial with one female yielded about 3,000 eggs by stripping, and a total of 11,000 fry were obtained from that female.
- **Holding house tank spawning:** No eggs were deposited on the rocks in any holding-house test. The technique was not recommended for future years.

### Hormone dosage

- **1981 results:** Daily HCG injections followed by stripping showed the most promise. Carp pituitary at 2 mg per pound was used in several trials. The report noted that 800 IU HCG per female every other day was too strong, since over half the eggs were immature and would not water-harden.
- **1982 recommendation:** Daily intramuscular HCG injections at 100 IU per pound of female weight were the most effective dosage.
- **Males:** Fluidity was maintained with 300 to 500 IU HCG every third to fifth day. Males that were dry at first injection were brought to fluidity with daily injections for three to four days.
- **Females held 14 days before injection:** These gave a very poor response, and the eggs were nonviable, with signs of reabsorption.

### Egg handling and enumeration

- **Stripping and water hardening:** Eggs were stripped and fertilized, then moved to incubator trays once water-hardened, 45 to 60 minutes after fertilization. Egg viability at 96 hours increased from roughly 20% to roughly 60% when eggs were stripped at 12-hour intervals after the start of ovulation.
- **Enumeration:** The [[Gravimetric Egg Enumeration]] method was judged more accurate than the [[Water Displacement Method]] or the [[Von Bayer Method]]. The mean was 130 eggs per gram. Fry enumeration by water displacement on 92 samples gave 233 fry per mL.
- **Egg size:** Mean egg diameters ranged from .088" to .091" among four females. Counts per quart varied from about 89,800 to 99,297.

### Brood fish held one year

- **Performance:** Fish held one year in a culture pond spawned as well as or better than wild-caught fish. They ripened about one month later, which the author considered a major advantage. They were more predictable, although secondary sexual characteristics were less developed.
- **Survival:** Lake Mohave females suffered a 49% loss. Every fish held for one year survived.
- **Egg yield:** Sixty-seven females produced a combined egg total, averaging 109,948 eggs per female. Mean egg viability at 96 hours was reported as 27.37% of 2,013,910 expected fry.

### Egg incubation

- **[[McDonald Hatching Jar]]:** Eggs developed normally until about the 48-hour stage, after which the proportion with developing embryos declined. A high proportion of prematurely hatched embryos died or were malformed. Sampled larvae showed body-curvature abnormalities in 62% of cases.
- **[[Heath Incubator]]:** This was the best method. Fungal infestation on days 3 to 5 and degradation of non-fertile eggs around day 4 were the main problems. Suggested starting density was 10,000 to 20,000 eggs per tray. Eggs were separated from bad and fungused eggs in a McDonald jar, with more than 90% hatching during the four-hour separation. The Heath incubator produced 28,500 swim-up fry, averaging 14,250 per female.
- **Circular tank hatching:** This was less satisfactory, with putrid areas, fungal growth and limited control. It was still recommended, but fungus would be hard to control.
- **Suspended trays in tanks:** Low water exchange and eggs being washed out made this method unsatisfactory, though it was considered worth further testing with air stones.

### Fungus control

- **Egg bioassays:** Eggs tested at 1:50 formalin for 10 minutes showed 54% mortality for three-day eggs and 33% for four-day eggs. Five minutes at 1:50 caused 21% loss in three-day eggs and 72% in four-day eggs. Treatments at 1:75 for 5 and 10 minutes caused no egg mortality.
- **Fungus bioassays:** Fungus was killed at 1:75 and 1:100 for 5 and 10 minutes, and at 1:200 for 10 and 15 minutes. Treatment at 1:300 delayed growth but did not kill the fungus. Concentrations above 1:300 showed growth.
- **Recommendation:** A constant-flow treatment with 1:100 formalin for 10 minutes on the second and early on the fourth day after hatching or fertilization.
- **Stack of eight trays:** Flow-through treatment was unsuccessful because the chemical was diluted before it reached the lower trays.

### Fry rearing and feeding

- **Fry in tanks:** About 1,000 fry were fed trout starter diet for two weeks, with at least six feedings daily. The survival rate in one test was 60.6%, with most early losses attributed to abnormal fry from jar hatching.
- **Larger trial:** Losses to the drain were estimated at 5,000 to 10,000 fry, and about 8,500 fry were missing when counted. Plugged screens were the main problem.
- **Rearing ponds:** A 0.1-acre pond was fertilized with 100 pounds of manure and 80 pounds of 18-46-0 before filling, plus weekly treatments. Stomach analysis showed that rotifers were the main food for the first three weeks.
- **Adult feeding:** Adults were fed five pounds of catfish ration on work days, with the rate planned to rise to 2 to 2.5% of body weight as temperatures increased.

### Recommendations for 1982

The report recommended: (a) testing the flow-through pond with a gravel bed on a liner and ripe females; (b) injecting HCG at 300, 400 or 500 IU per pound with hand stripping and fertilization; (c) circular tank spawning with daily HCG injections at 300 to 500 IU per pound; (d) incubating eggs in Heath incubators, circular tanks and/or suspended trays; (e) controlling fungus with ten-minute 1:100 formalin flow treatments; (f) preparing rearing ponds two weeks to one month before expected spawning to have zooplankton available; (g) closer monitoring of viable versus nonviable eggs; and (h) starting a year class in 1981 for future brood fish.
