# Recovery of Imperiled Species Under the Endangered Species Act: The Need for a New Approach

#conservation-biology #endangered-species-act #wildlife-management

> Note on scope: this document is a policy and conservation-science essay, not fish research. Fish (chiefly salmon) appear only as examples. The note therefore uses general conservation tags in place of the usual ichthyology taxon tags.

## Executive Summary

Scott et al. argue that the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) needs a more realistic definition of "recovery." Many listed species cannot be delisted unless they keep receiving active management, so the authors propose treating recovery as a continuum rather than a simple recovered/not-recovered split. They define species that depend on ongoing intervention as "conservation-reliant" and propose [[Recovery Management Agreements]] (RMAs): enforceable, science-based contracts that formalize long-term management after delisting or downlisting. The paper is a perspective piece in *Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment*, and it draws on case studies, delisting records, and cost estimates to support its case.

## Background & Description

- **Legal framework.** The ESA's stated goal is to "recover" listed species to the point where its protections are no longer needed. Following listing, the responsible agency (the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] or [[NOAA Fisheries]]) prepares a recovery plan. Until then, species receive protection through prohibitions on "take" (section 9) and on jeopardizing the species (section 7), along with incentives such as federal funding to states (section 6(d)).
- **Main threats.** The most common threats to imperiled US species are habitat degradation and invasive species ([[Wilcove et al. 1998]]). Human impacts have accelerated species loss through habitat alteration, altered natural disturbance regimes (such as fire), introduced species, and the globalization of economies.
- **Current status.** The paper cites a total of 1260 listed species in the US as of October 2004 (Status data as of 31 October 2000 in one graphic label; the text reports 1260 species). Of these, 13 had been delisted because they recovered, and 22 had made enough progress to be downlisted from endangered to threatened. The text also mentions 72 species reclassified from endangered to threatened, a figure that is partly garbled in the scan.
- **Recovery progress is slow.** The authors argue that few species could have recovered within the ESA's roughly 30-year history, given the complexity of threats, limited recovery funding ([[Miller et al. 2002]]), and the decades or centuries many species need to reach a point of acute vulnerability ([[Wilcove et al. 1993]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dynamic environments.** Species and their environments change over time. Droughts, wet years, or the reestablishment of fire can increase a population, while human land-use changes, such as [[Conservation Reserve Program]] enrollment, timber shifts, and hedgerow loss, continually reshape the conditions in which recovery plays out.
- **Management-dependent threats.** Many listing causes stem from ongoing human alterations, such as habitat destruction and non-native species, which cannot be eliminated. Under those conditions, continuing management is part of the recovery strategy itself.
- **Examples of ongoing management:**
  - Control of invasive species.
  - Prescribed fire to maintain suitable habitat, such as jack-pine stands for [[Kirtland's Warbler]].
  - Grazing or public-access restrictions.
  - Predator control.
  - Regulated water releases from dams.
  - Limited translocations to maintain genetic diversity.
  - Control of [[Brown-headed Cowbird]] parasitism for least Bell's vireo.
- **Salmon and dams.** Salmon examples show how management can be artificial. Hatchery-reared salmon and the trucking of smolts around dams to overcome migration barriers are cited as dependencies that do not qualify a species as conservation-reliant. Dam effects on salmon dynamics are described in [[Levin and Tolimieri 2001]] and hatchery impacts in [[Myers et al. 2004]].
- **Captive and reintroduced populations.** The California condor and Attwater's greater prairie chicken survive only through captive breeding and periodic releases, so the authors consider them unrecovered under any standard that matches the ESA's ecosystem goals. The Guam Micronesian kingfisher, Hawaiian crow, and several Hawaiian plants survive only in captivity.
- **Financial cost.** Managing currently occupied habitat for roughly 60% of listed species threatened by invasive species or disrupted fire regimes was estimated at a figure of several hundred million dollars per year in 1997 terms ([[Wilcove and Chen 1998]]). The scan garbles the exact number, so it is not reproduced here.

## Key Findings

**1. Recovery should be a continuum, not a binary.**
Recovery goals are usually framed as abundance thresholds, but recovery actually sits along a gradient of human intervention. Species can be considered recovered at several points along it. The key question is whether there is "reasonable certainty" that the intervention will continue.

**2. Conservation-reliant species are the norm, not the exception.**
The authors suspect that only a few species can be "walked away" from after delisting. These are species threatened by clear, remediable threats that are unlikely to recur, such as the Aleutian Canada goose after fox removal from nesting islands. Most listed species need continuous management to maintain recovered status.

**3. Definition of conservation-reliant species.**
A conservation-reliant species can sustain a self-sustaining wild population only if ongoing management actions of proven effectiveness are implemented. It is not conservation-reliant if it depends on captive-reared releases or on manipulation of large portions of a population instead of habitat.

**4. Delisting precedent: Robbins' cinquefoil.**
The [[Robbins' Cinquefoil]] was delisted in 2002 after management agreements were put in place. These agreements included fencing and onsite personnel to prevent trampling and illegal collection. The case is the model the authors propose generalizing.

**5. Reclassifying to threatened.**
Species that have not reached recovery goals but can be sustained with continuing management should be reclassified as threatened. This gives the threatened category its intended role as an intermediate step. For these species, rigid statutory restrictions could be replaced with more flexible rules as long as the needed management continues.

**6. Recovery Management Agreements (RMAs).**
The authors propose a new class of ESA section 10 agreements with two parts:
- *Biological standards*, set by the threats the species faces and the management needed to counter them, and developed from the recovery plan.
- *Legal requirements*, formalized as an enforceable contract between the federal wildlife agency and a management entity with the authority and funding to provide long-term management.

RMAs should be operational well before downlisting or delisting, so that a track record demonstrates that the management works.

**7. Managing entities can include a range of partners.**
The management entity could be a federal land-management agency, a state, tribal, county, or municipal government, or a nongovernmental organization with the resources and a track record for long-term obligations. Private landowners and local governments with zoning powers may also be involved. All agencies and organizations involved in management should participate in drafting the RMA.

**8. Precedent exists.**
Section 4(b)(1)(A) of the ESA already recognizes state and local conservation actions in delisting decisions. Interagency agreements, such as the [[Safe Harbor Agreements]] (USFWS 1998, 1999), provide further precedent. The RMA proposal formalizes these practices.

**9. Societal decision.**
Determining how much risk must be reduced to count as "recovery" is a societal decision. Societal values determine how much effort and resources should go to preventing extinction. Whatever targets are chosen, the costs of continuing management must be included in the accounting.

**10. Expected gains.**
The authors argue that while the costs are real, adopting the concepts of a recovery continuum and conservation-reliant species could move many listed species into a relatively secure state. They consider the gains worth the costs.

**Specific examples cited:**
- The [[Grizzly Bear]] recovery goal would occupy a much smaller share of its pre-Columbian range than the historic distribution (a specific fraction is garbled in the scan).
- Bald eagle recovery plans call for ranges approximating pre-listing status.
- Puerto Rico: [[Hurricane Hugo]] killed nearly half of the wild Puerto Rican parrot population in 1989, showing how natural events can reverse progress ([[Wiley 1991]]).
