# Notes on Habitats, Systematic Characters and Life Histories of Texas Salt Water Cyprinodontes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Tulane Studies in Zoology paper (Vol. 4, No. 4, December 31, 1956) by [[Don G. Simpson]] and [[Gordon Gunter]] reports a field survey of the killifishes (family Cyprinodontidae, called Cyprinodontes in the text) inhabiting salt and brackish waters along the Texas coast. Sampling covered 60 stations from the Rio Grande to the Sabine River, with 84 station visits between September 1950 and March 1951. Nine species were taken. [[Cyprinodon Variegatus]] was by far the most abundant, and the authors conclude that the group is mainly an inhabitant of sloughs, tidal ponds and protected shallows rather than open bay or Gulf waters. The study also documents an unusually hypersaline period caused by drought, with the highest salinity at which live fish had been reported to that time, and gives notes on color, meristic characters, reproduction and feeding.

## Background & Description

The study began in September 1950 at the suggestion of Gunter, and was planned for quarterly visits over one year. Simpson was called into the United States Air Force after about one and a half coverages of the area, and the data were turned over to Gunter for writing. The authors describe Texas salt water cyprinodonts as abundant and vigorous fishes that tolerate great diurnal and seasonal temperature swings, abnormally high salinities, freshets and rapid salinity changes. They are regarded as the most resistant fishes in these waters to physical variation. The fishes serve as ecological dominants and have some value as bait, though no commercial statistics are kept.

Sampling used a jeep with four-wheel drive and a fifty-foot minnow seine of quarter-inch stretched mesh, with one haul per station and no standardized haul length. Water temperature was measured with a thermometer, salinity was determined by sea water hydrometer and checked by [[Titration]] when extreme, and voucher specimens were deposited in the [[Institute Of Marine Science]] collections. Commercial shrimp (Penaeidae) presence was also noted.

Species characteristics were examined using a table of body proportions and scale counts (standard length, depth, head, snout, eye and scales), along with dorsal and anal ray counts and dorsal insertion. The authors note that external characters are hard to separate and that field workers rely on color and subtle shape differences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preference.** The cyprinodonts are predominantly fishes of tidal ponds, sloughs, salt water creeks and bayous rather than bays or open waters. They were rare on the Gulf beach, occurring occasionally near pass mouths. The authors suggest that cover such as shells, plant debris or a rugose or muddy bottom restricts them to bay areas. When disturbed, the fish settle to the bottom and hide, and [[Kilby 1955]] reported that *C. variegatus* buries itself in mud or plant debris as the tide falls.

**Salinity regime.** In 1950–51 the region was in severe drought. Over 30 percent of salinities recorded were well above normal sea water, all from south Texas, including several records from the hypersaline [[Laguna Madre]]. Drying ponds reached about 80 ‰, and no such salinities had been found in earlier wetter-year work by Gunter.

**Cold events.** Cold waves kill large quantities of fish along the Texas coast. Dead cyprinodonts were not found on bay shores after these events. Instead they were found offshore in water three feet deep or more, and many fish in shallow isolated ponds were wiped out by the freeze of January 28, 1951.

**Comparative data.** Table 2 compares catches with earlier studies by Gunter (1945, 1950) and [[Kilby 1955]], who worked in Florida brackish marshes. In Kilby's study, of 54,687 fishes caught, over 40,000 were cyprinodonts.

## Key Findings

**Species composition and abundance**
- Nine species were taken, listed in order of abundance: *Cyprinodon variegatus* (8,859 specimens, 63 station occurrences), *Fundulus grandis* (1,099; 49), *Mollienesia latipinna* (780; 19), *Fundulus similis* (505; 32), *Adinia multifasciata* (323; 21), *Gambusia affinis* (173; 8), *Lucania parva* (160; 21), *Fundulus pulvereus* (60; 16) and *Fundulus jenkinsi* (24; 6).
- *C. variegatus* was almost three times as abundant as all other species combined. The authors consider the Table 2 figures the most representative for Texas waters because hauls covered every habitat type along the coast.
- *Gambusia affinis* was never taken in open waters. *F. jenkinsi* was taken once in six shore hauls, *F. pulvereus* four times in 16, and *M. latipinna* once in 19 on bay shores.
- *F. jenkinsi* was found only at Dickinson Bayou, its type locality. Its distribution west of Dickinson Bayou was not confirmed, so the range limit given by Miller (1955) was accepted.

**Salinity tolerance**
- *Gambusia affinis* is a freshwater species that enters moderate salinities. Eleven fish (7.2 percent of its catch) were taken between 11.3 and 20.6 ‰; the rest were at 3.5 ‰ or below.
- *Adinia multifasciata* is a true brackish species, most common at medium salinities, with over nine percent of its fish at hypersalinities.
- *Lucania parva* showed a very wide range (1–48.2 ‰). Only 39.4 percent of catches were below 10 ‰, versus 81 percent in Kilby's Florida work, and over 17 percent were saltier than sea water.
- *Fundulus grandis*, *F. similis* and *C. variegatus* have high salinity preferences and tolerances. The authors classify these three as fully marine.
- The highest salinity recorded was 142.4 ‰ (hydrometer, calculated as sea water) in a drying pond, with titration giving 130.0 ‰. A Laguna Madre haul at 147.0 ‰ contained only dead *C. variegatus*. The authors regard this as near the toleration limit.
- Abundance peaked for all species except *G. affinis* between 20 and 36.9 ‰. High catches of *C. variegatus* above 80 ‰ are attributed to fish concentrated in drying ponds, not to selected habitat.

**Morphology and meristics**
- *C. variegatus*, *Adinia multifasciata*, *Lucania parva* and *M. latipinna* have lower scale counts. *F. grandis*, *F. similis* and *F. pulvereus* have higher counts.
- *C. variegatus*, *A. multifasciata* and *M. latipinna* are deep-bodied; *F. jenkinsi*, *F. similis* and *F. pulvereus* are slim.
- *Gambusia affinis* and *Lucania parva* have the largest eyes and are the smallest species. *F. grandis* and *F. similis* have the smallest eyes.

**Reproduction and life history**
- *Cyprinodon variegatus*: males 35–58 mm (mean 45.0) and females 31–55 mm (mean 46.5). Small fish were added from June to December, and monthly catches peaked in October (3,215). The species is pugnacious and males defend territories during breeding, as described by [[Raney Et Al 1953]] in Florida and seen here at Aransas Pass.
- *Fundulus similis* was observed spawning in July in small shore potholes. Ripe fish were present from April to August. Smallest fish increased from 20 mm in September to 43 mm in March, and females averaged slightly larger than males.
- *Fundulus pulvereus* appears to spawn in fall and winter. Males were 30–46 mm and females 16–45 mm.
- *Fundulus grandis* is predaceous; three specimens had partially swallowed mollies. Ripe females were taken in October, and monthly catches peaked in October (832).
- *Fundulus jenkinsi*: nine males (26–45 mm) and 15 females (25–60 mm). Females averaged several millimetres longer, and developing eggs were found in September and January.
- *Mollienesia latipinna*: 533 fish in October (13–56 mm). Galveston-area samples included 64 males to 196 females, and the largest male was 53 mm versus a 71 mm female.
- *Gambusia affinis*: specimens were 20–48 mm. Stomachs held small fish, crustaceans and annelids.
- *Lucania parva*: 17–48 mm. Eggs were clear in November and yellow in January. Stomachs held larval crustaceans and annelids.
- *Adinia multifasciata*: 16–38 mm among 360 fish. Ripe females with yellow eggs were taken in October 1951.

**Methodological limitations and conclusions**
- Haul length was not standardized and the bottom varied in time and place, so a standard drag was impossible. The authors state that catches are nonetheless comparable across stations.
- The authors conclude that the cyprinodonts are primarily inhabitants of protected waters, that the three fully marine species tolerate very high salinities, and that drought and cold-wave kills strongly affect local abundance.
