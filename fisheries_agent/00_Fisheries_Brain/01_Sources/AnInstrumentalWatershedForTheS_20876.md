## Executive Summary

This 1978 methodological paper by [[Aaron Yair]], [[David Sharon]] and [[Hanoch Lavee]] (Hebrew University of Jerusalem) describes the design, instrumentation and procedure of an experimental study of spatial variation in slope runoff over an 11,325 m² arid limestone watershed at Sde Boker, Northern Negev. The authors argue that slope runoff in arid areas, like humid areas, is generated by a partial-area contribution mechanism, driven by rainfall non-uniformity and surface properties rather than soil moisture. The site is equipped with a dense rainfall network, tilted raingages, 16 runoff plots and nine water-level recorders. A single low-frequency storm of 18 mm in 8 hours (12 March 1976) is briefly analysed to illustrate the data.

## Background & Description

**Problem.** Geomorphologists since the [[International Hydrological Decade]] (1964–1973) had concentrated on channel rather than slope processes. Slope data from small plots were assumed to extrapolate to larger areas, but studies in humid and arid areas showed runoff is not uniform across slopes. The authors state that a systematic study covering an entire watershed slope area was still missing.

**Rainfall measurement approach.** Conventional horizontal-orifice raingages do not represent rain actually intercepted by sloping ground. The authors therefore use "tilted" raingages whose orifice is parallel to the local surface, following [[Tilted Raingage Method]] precedents (Levert 1959; Hamrcvon 1954; Grunow 1953). Rain inclinations up to 40° from the zenith were cited for flat areas and above 50° for mountain areas.

**Study site.** The site lies in the Northern Negev. Mean annual rainfall is 95 mm (extremes 34 and 167 mm over 30 years), with 15 to 42 rain-days per year. Strata are Turonian limestones of the Drorim, Shivta and Netser formations ([[Turonian Stratigraphy]]), which create three contrasting surface environments:
- **Drorim formation:** thin, densely jointed strata weathering into cobbles and boulders; thick, stony, relatively extensive soil cover; sparse but uniform vegetation.
- **Shivta formation:** massive limestone forming stepped terraces; bedrock exposed over about 50% of the surface; soil confined to strips with relatively rich vegetation.
- **Netser formation:** thin, very densely jointed strata; shattered outcrops cover about 30% of the surface; shallow, patchy soil and very poor vegetation.

Site geometry: relative relief about 50 m (under 22 m for any single slope); slope lengths 55 to 76 m; mean gradients 11.5% to 29.5%. Only one half of a first-order basin, on one side of the channel and facing north, is drained, for technical and financial reasons.

**Instrumentation.** Meteorological: 21 twin raingage stations (tilted and horizontal orifice at 30 cm), two Hellman-type recorders, a Woelfle wind recorder at 3.5 m, micrometeorological experiments at 10 stations, and an evaporation pan near the channel. Hydrological: 16 runoff plots (ten contiguous slope-parallel plots, with plots 2, 4 and 9 subdivided into 9 subplots), nine water-level recorders with automatic suspended sediment samplers, volumetric water meters on the remaining plots, and upper-layer soil moisture sampled at nine stations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Rainfall non-uniformity:** earlier studies (Caprus 1958; Sharon 1970, 1976; Shanan 1976) found more rain in channels and adjoining slopes than on ridges, implying higher runoff frequency and magnitude at slope bases.
- **Runoff thresholds:** minimum rainfall generating overland flow is 1–2 mm on massive rock and 3–5 mm on stony soil cover.
- **Flow velocities:** shallow overland flows move at roughly 3–6 cm/s at peak discharge, seldom above 10 cm/s, so runoff from slopes beyond about 50 m often cannot reach the base during short rainfall. Contributing areas during frequent small-to-medium storms are therefore likely limited to the lower slope belt.
- **Storm duration:** showers are short; storms over 20 mm usually consist of several separate showers, each generating a shallow, separate runoff event.
- **Surface controls:** bare bedrock at upper slopes yields high runoff, but downslope coarse colluvium with high infiltration capacity can absorb it before it reaches the channel.
- **Soil moisture:** the authors judge soil-moisture-driven partial areas unlikely in hot, dry deserts, and assume the arid mechanism arises from rainfall regime and surface properties.

## Key Findings

1. **Rainfall geometry matters.** For a 20° slope and 40° rain inclination, a windward slope may receive almost twice the rainfall of an adjacent leeward slope; for 15° slopes the ratio is about 1:1.5.
2. **Partial area hypothesis for arid zones.** Runoff generation in arid areas is assumed to follow a partial-area pattern similar to humid areas, driven by rainfall regime and surface properties.
3. **Rainstorm of 12 March 1976 (plot 2):** 18 mm in eight hours, estimated as a 1-in-10 to 1-in-15-year event.
   - Incident rainfall was highest on northwest-facing slope portions facing the wind, and increased near the channel.
   - The storm produced several separate flow events.
   - Subplot 2C (upper slope, bare bedrock) generated more individual flows and more runoff per unit area than subplots 2B and 2A combined.
   - The contributing slope length to the base of plot 2 during the major flow was estimated at about 35 m out of a total length of 63 m.
4. **Interpretation:** high specific discharge at 2C is attributed to bedrock runoff being absorbed by colluvium downslope, a process that may be reinforced by local surface-property differences.
5. **Status:** the instrumentation had only been operating briefly, so no firm conclusions were drawn. Planned work includes:
   - establishing a relationship between standard and inclined raingage catches, allowing fewer gages to be operated;
   - sprinkling experiments on plots 2, 4 and 9 to map flow velocities and contributing-belt width with tracing methods.
