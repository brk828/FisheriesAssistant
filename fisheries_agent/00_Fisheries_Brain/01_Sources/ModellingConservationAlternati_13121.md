# Modelling Conservation Alternatives with ILWIS: A Case Study of the Volcano Rabbit

#ichthyology #marine-biology #leporidae

*Note: The source concerns a terrestrial mammal, not fish or aquatic taxa. The tag line above follows the mandated template; the taxon tag is set to the rabbit's family.*

## Executive Summary

This ITC Journal study models how two alternative agricultural policies could affect the habitat of the [[Mexican Volcano Rabbit]] (*Romerolagus diazi*), an endangered lagomorph of central Mexico. Agricultural encroachment, fuelled by the growth of [[Mexico City]], has fragmented its range. The authors built a [[Geographic Information System]] in [[ILWIS]] to compare a current-distribution model, a pessimistic model and an optimistic model that includes a conservation corridor. They argue that the gap between scientists and government or farming stakeholders is a central obstacle to conservation. The models are presented as a starting point for planning, pending more data.

## Background & Description

The volcano rabbit is a small, social lagomorph restricted to high volcanic slopes in the Mexican highlands. Its populations form groups of roughly five to seven individuals in open areas, retreating to dense [[Bunchgrass Communities]] when threatened.

The paper frames conservation as a three-part process: (1) research on species ecology, (2) planning, and (3) implementation and monitoring. The authors note that scientific input is usually weakly linked to planning in developing countries, and that Mexico's unique ecosystems mean that solutions from elsewhere often cannot be transferred directly. Human activity, rather than ecological constraints alone, now strongly shapes the distribution of many endangered species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Urban and agricultural pressure.** Mexico City grew from 15 km² in 1930 to about 120 km² by 1980. Its population rose from 1,670,314 to 13,455,000. Expansion displaced agriculture south and southeast into the rabbit's range, with associated deforestation and road construction.

**Habitat fragmentation.** The rabbit's unfragmented range was estimated at 150,000 ha in 1979. By 1991 it had fallen to about 38,650 ha across 16 fragments.

**Study area.** The work covered 680 km² on the Tlaloc and Pelado volcanoes, where 177 sampling units were investigated between 1987 and 1990. Two of the four core distribution areas were examined.

**Vegetation.** Using the [[Braun-Blanquet Approach]] and TWINSPAN classification, researchers identified 16 plant communities: 13 natural and three cultural. Habitat categories were assigned by rabbit pellet-count abundance, using ANOVA and canonical correspondence analysis (CCA).

**Habitat categories.** Communities were grouped as optimum, suboptimum and unsuitable. Unsuitable habitat included cultivated fields and natural vegetation where the rabbit is ecologically excluded.

## Key Findings

- **Landscape-unit approach:** The authors used discrete landscape units rather than interpolation. Social species occupy discrete groups, and rabbit abundance varied significantly between plant communities.
- **Current distribution (Model 1):** The 22 landscape units were classed into five suitability classes. The rabbit persists in all classes except the unsuitable one. Its presence in suboptimum class III is shaped by both human and ecological factors. Classes I and II are more widely distributed on Tlaloc, suggesting more rabbits there.
- **Pessimistic model (Model 2):** Agriculture expanding between 3100 and 3300 m on terrain under 10% gradient, within 1 km of roads and 5 km of towns. This shrinks the range, mainly class III, and isolates the optimum and suboptimum 1 classes. Intermountain valleys are identified as priority conservation areas.
- **Optimistic model (Model 3):** Agriculture is restricted to below 3000 m, on terrain under 5% gradient, within 0.5 km of roads and 3 km of towns. A corridor between Tlaloc and Pelado is added to support genetic exchange. The model shows a substantial reduction in cultivated area, with a corridor that would need reforestation and monitoring.
- **Corridor obstacles:** Two highways, the town of Parres and a railway may block animal movement. Intensive agriculture further widens these barriers.
- **Data limitations:** The models rely on assumptions about farmers' acceptable distances from roads and towns, which are undocumented. Grazing, burning, logging, competition and predation are not yet included. The authors state that missing data should not delay communication among stakeholders.
- **Recommendations:** The pessimistic and optimistic maps are proposed as base documents for monitoring and as tools linking research to planning. Implementation should involve decision-makers and farmers, and corridor management could employ local farmers to compensate for lost profits.
