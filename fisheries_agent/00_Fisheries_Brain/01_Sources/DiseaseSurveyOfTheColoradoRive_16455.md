# Disease Survey of the Colorado River Fishes (1980)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 report summarizes a disease survey of endangered and other fishes of the Colorado River system, conducted by the [[Fish Disease Control Center]] (FDCC) in Fort Morgan, Colorado, in cooperation with the [[Colorado River Fishes Project]] (CRFP). The survey examined 28 endangered fishes (23 of them dead) and 97 exotic and other endemic specimens. No virus was detected in the endangered fishes. The only virus found in the study was [[Lymphocystis]], recovered from a single host species, [[Lepomis Cyanellus]]. Most bacteria isolated were saprophytic and ubiquitous in aquatic environments. The most notable parasites were undescribed myxosporeans, which occurred widely without detectable pathology. The authors concluded that disease agents associated with exotic fishes did not adversely affect endemic fishes and were unlikely to have contributed to the decline of the endangered populations. They recommended focusing future effort on field-collected samples and hatchery-reared fish mortalities, because the greatest risks to hatchery propagation come from [[Saprolegnia]], [[Motile Aeromonads]], and ciliate protozoans.

## Background & Description

The survey had three stated objectives:

1. Assess the impact of disease agents on populations of endangered fishes.
2. Determine whether disease agents associated with exotic fish species have adversely affected endangered fishes.
3. Identify potential disease problems for hatchery propagation of endangered fishes.

**Sample collection.** Sample kits were distributed to all field crews and to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Crews collected virus, bacteria, and parasite samples from endangered fishes that died during capture or holding. Samples were chilled and shipped to the FDCC, and formalin-fixed whole fish were also submitted for parasite examination. FDCC personnel made several on-site collections of exotic and other endemic species.

**Laboratory analysis.**
- Virus samples were inoculated onto the cell line phylogenetically closest to each host species, following standard Fish Health Section procedures of the American Fisheries Society.
- Bacteria were cultured from kidney and/or lesions on Tryptic Soy Agar, with isolates identified by morphological and biochemical characters. Kidney and spleen smears were Gram stained.
- Skin scrapings, gill arches, kidney, and intestine were examined grossly and microscopically for parasites and abnormalities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Endangered fishes examined.** The endangered group included *Gila robusta*, *Gila elegans*, *Gila cypha*, and *Ptychocheilus lucius* (Northern Squawfish). Several specimens were hatchery-held, including *G. cypha* and *G. elegans*, and fish from Willow Beach were sampled.

**Exotic and other endemic fishes.** This group included [[Ictalurus Punctatus]], [[Micropterus Dolomieui]], [[Lepomis Cyanellus]], *Catostomus latipinnis*, *Notropis lutrensis*, and others. The authors noted that these fishes harbored a greater variety of disease agents than the endangered fishes, possibly because of the larger sample (97 versus 28 specimens).

**Host specificity.** The central ecological interpretation is that the endemic and exotic fishes carry distinctly different pathogen groups. The authors described a high degree of loose host specificity. Several parasites, such as the myxosporeans, appear to be species- or group-specific, suggesting long-term association with their hosts.

**Environmental conditions.** Opportunistic pathogens were linked to stress and water quality. Facultative bacterial pathogens were most common in hatchery-held *Gila* and in fish held in live boxes. Saprolegnia infections were associated with stress from capture, handling, and tagging, and with osmotic imbalances. Ciliates such as *Apiosoma* and *Ambiphrya* caused problems mainly under poor water quality. Warm, sluggish waters were thought to favor [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] multiplication and attachment.

## Key Findings

**Viruses**
- No virus was detected in the endangered fishes.
- The only virus found in the study was Lymphocystis, recovered from *L. cyanellus*. The authors judged it neither highly pathogenic nor highly contagious.
- The absence of detectable virus was not taken to rule out an enzootic virus, but the authors concluded that virus is not a limiting factor in endemic fish populations.

**Bacteria**
- Isolates included *Pseudomonas fluorescens*, *Ps. putrifaciens*, *Enterobacter agglomerans*, *En. liquefaciens*, *Aeromonas hydrophila*, *A. punctata*, *Escherichia coli*, *Proteus morganii*, and *Plesiomonas shigelloides*.
- Most were saprophytic and ubiquitous. The facultative pathogens were common in hatchery fish but posed no threat to the general health of the endemic fishes.

**Fungi**
- [[Saprolegnia]] was found on several specimens. It was considered a likely limiting factor in rearing endangered fishes.

**Protozoa**
- An undescribed [[Myxobolus Sp.]] was found in all species of endangered fishes, in gill, liver, spleen, kidney, intestine, and skin, and in nearly every endemic specimen. No pathology was associated with it. The authors judged it unlikely to affect survival or propagation.
- An undescribed [[Myxidium Sp.]] was found only in the gall bladders of *G. robusta* and *G. elegans*. Identification of the *G. elegans* host was questioned, so it may be only *G. robusta* that harbors it.
- [[Tetrahymena Sp.]] was found on the skin and gills of hatchery-reared *G. cypha*. Apiosoma and Ambiphrya were also considered hatchery-related commensals.
- [[Chilodonella]] has the potential to cause significant hatchery mortality but rarely does so in feral populations.
- An amoeba was found in the gills of one hatchery-held *G. elegans*.

**Trematodes**
- Larval trematodes of the genus [[Neascus]] (identified as *Neascus* of *Ornithodiplostomum* in *P. lucius*) were found. Other larval trematodes, *Posthodiplostomum minimum* and *Uvulifer* sp., occurred in *G. robusta*. Their final hosts are birds, so the authors judged it unlikely that stocking exotic fishes introduced them.

**Cestodes**
- *Ophiotaenia* sp. was recovered from six *P. lucius* and was sometimes abundant in the intestine. It was the most host-specific parasite found. Another investigator tentatively identified it as *O. fragilis*, but the authors hesitated to confirm this.
- Other cestodes in the exotic and endemic group included larval *Ligula* sp. (in *M. dolomieui* and *L. cyanellus*), adult *Corallobothrium* in *I. punctatus*, and four unidentified Caryophyllaeidae in *C. latipinnis*.

**Nematodes and crustaceans**
- [[Rhabdochona Pellucida]] was found in *G. robusta*, and *Philometra cylindracea* in the body cavity of *N. lutrensis*. *Capillaria* sp. was found in *M. dolomieui*.
- *Lernaea cyprinacea* was common across nearly all species sampled. *P. lucius* had heavier infestations, possibly due to habitat preference. Direct mortality from it was rare, and the authors concluded it was not limiting for the endangered fishes.

**Reproductive observations (incidental)**
- Reproductive status was outside the study's scope but was checked on request. Vesicles containing clear fluid on the ovary surface of one *G. elegans* from Willow Beach could not be explained. Virus and bacteria sampling were not possible because the ovaries were formalin-fixed.
- One *P. lucius* had an immature ovary and a degenerative one. Another female was in developing condition and would not have spawned until 1981. A third fish was confirmed male.

**Impact assessment**
- Disease agents associated with exotic species did not appear to adversely affect endemic fishes. The endemic fishes harbored a distinctly different pathogen group.
- The authors concluded that disease was likely not a factor in the decline of the endangered populations.

**Hatchery implications**
- Extrapolating feral disease data to hatcheries is difficult. Culture methods and management quality were judged more important to success than infectious agents.
- Motile aeromonads and Saprolegnia were expected to threaten culture. Ciliates may also cause mortality.
- Environmental control and understanding the biology of the fishes were emphasized as keys to healthy stocks.

**Recommendations**
- Shift sampling effort from all fish species to endemic species only.
- Laboratory analysis of specimens collected in the field was the most productive approach. On-site sampling was more expensive and yielded fewer results.
- Continue disease work mainly through field-crew sample collection, laboratory examination, and formalin-fixed whole fish.
- Place greater emphasis on diagnosing [[Hatchery Propagation]] mortalities and advising on hatchery practices.
- Use multiple research purposes for the few specimens available, since the organisms associated with a rare species are part of its biology.
