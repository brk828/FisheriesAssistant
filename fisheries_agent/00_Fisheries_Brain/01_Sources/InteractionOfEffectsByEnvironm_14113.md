# Interaction of Effects by Environmental Factors on Primary Productivity in Ponds and Microecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1964 Oklahoma State University doctoral dissertation in Zoology by [[John Lawrence Butler]] examines how water exchange, turbidity, dissolved solids, temperature and light control primary productivity in farm ponds and in laboratory [[Microecosystem]] studies. Field work on [[Myers Ponds]] and [[Bassler Ponds]] showed productivity that varied inversely with water exchange and turbidity and directly with dissolved solids. A 2×2×4×4 factorial experiment in 64 microcosms then tested how these factors interact. Interactions were significant, and the optimal dissolved solids level was about 300 ppm. The work is primarily ecological and limnological. It does not address fish species, and no fish are named in the source.

## Background & Description

- **Author and setting:** John Lawrence Butler, Ph.D. (degree dated May 31, 1964), Department of Zoology, [[Oklahoma State University]], Stillwater. The study is 89 pages long.
- **Funding:** Oklahoma Oil Refiners' Waste Control Council, the [[National Science Foundation]] Research Participation Program for College Teachers, and a Public Health Service Research Traineeship.
- **Central problem:** Pond productivity in central Oklahoma is limited by colloidal clay turbidity and by mineral content. Both are governed by water exchange rate. Field results could not separate the relative influence of turbidity from that of ionic content.
- **Conceptual framework:** The ecosystem is treated as a [[Black Box]] (Ashby, 1958). Inputs are environmental factors, and outputs are productivity, community respiration, standing crop and species diversity. Microcosms are defined as "miniatures" of natural ecosystems that keep much of their complexity.
- **Methods used:**
  - [[Oxygen Method]] (diurnal dissolved oxygen, Winkler/azide) in ponds.
  - [[Carbon Dioxide Titration]] (pH–CO₂ titration curves, computer integration) in microcosms.
  - Chlorophyll extraction in 90% acetone, with carotenoid estimates.
  - [[Factorial Experimental Design]] analyzed by ANOVA, [[Half-Normal Plot]] and a [[Response Surface]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond conditions.** Euphotic zones deeper than 2 m are uncommon in central Oklahoma farm ponds. Dissolved solids usually range from 60 to 300 mg/L. Clay particles approach colloidal size (0.5–5 µm) and are mainly montmorillonite. High water exchange gives runoff-like water with no mineral buildup. Low exchange allows evaporative concentration and deeper light penetration.

**Clearing mechanisms.** Flocculation of clay is favored by hydrogen ions, by calcium and magnesium, and by gypsum or oil-field brine addition. Daily pH swings driven by producer and decomposer activity also promote clearing.

**Watersheds.** Grassed watersheds gave more stable water chemistry. Cultivated watersheds gave more variable and harder water. The ratio of watershed area to pond storage capacity was more important for turbidity than watershed size alone.

**Microecosystem community.** The stabilized communities contained algae, bacteria, protozoans, rotifers and small crustaceans. Principal algal genera were listed in Table IV (about 25 genera; the most important were [[Scenedesmus]], [[Chlorella]], [[Mougeotia]], [[Spirogyra]], [[Tetrahedron]] and [[Euglena]]). Copepods were abundant in the 600 ppm microcosms.

**Literature context.** Earlier work on pond productivity, turbidity, oxygen and pH diel cycles is reviewed in Chapter II. The author cites [[Lindeman]] (1942) on energy flow and [[Odum]] on primary production methods. The note on Oklahoma's farm pond number is an estimate of over 300,000 ponds (Anon, 1960), with a lower figure of over 100,000 from Wallen (1955).

## Key Findings

**Field studies (farm ponds, summers 1960–1962)**
- Summer gross productivity was 0.3–5.0 g O₂/m²/day in turbid ponds and 3.0–17.0 g O₂/m²/day in clear ponds.
- In the summary, the clear pond with low exchange had gross productivity two to three times that of the adjacent turbid pond with high exchange.
- P/R ratios ranged from 0.7 to 2.3. They exceeded one in the clear lower Myers pond and were below one in the turbid upper pond, which received allochthonous organic matter.
- Efficiency ranged from 0.03 to 2.0%, and was directly related to productivity. Efficiency in the lower Myers pond averaged about 1.3%.
- Turbidity in Bassler Pond A was 200–300 ppm, against 20–30 ppm in downstream Pond C, a gradient of about 10:1.
- Community respiration exceeded productivity in turbid ponds, but diffusion prevented anaerobiosis.

**Microcosm experiment (64 combinations)**
- Design: four turbidity levels (0, 25, 50, 75 ppm), four TDIS levels (75, 150, 300, 600 ppm), two temperatures (about 11 and 23 °C), two light intensities (about 400 and 800 ft-c). The source text also lists 100 ft-c in its scope statement, a discrepancy in the original.
- Gross primary productivity ranged from about 0.1 to 1.0 mM CO₂/L/day. P/R ratios were close to 1.0.
- Significant interactions: light × TDIS, light × temperature, temperature × TDIS, and turbidity × TDIS.
- Light, temperature and TDIS had linear effects on productivity. The effect of TDIS was parabolic, with optimum near 300 ppm.
- Temperature had a smaller overall effect than light. Gross productivity rose by about 50% over a 12 °C increase.
- Turbidity had less effect at low than at high temperature.
- Biomass (ash-free dry weight) depended only on TDIS: about 100 mg/L at 75 ppm, 200 mg/L at 150 ppm, 300 mg/L at 300 ppm and 550 mg/L at 600 ppm (values as printed in the source).
- Species diversity depended only on TDIS: about seven species per cycle at 300 ppm, five at 150 ppm, four at 75 ppm and two at 600 ppm.
- Assimilation numbers ranged from 0.4 to 4.0. Productivity per unit biomass was highest at the lowest TDIS.
- Dark CO₂ fixation was 10–21% of light fixation in four microcosms.
- Photosynthetic quotients ranged from 0.6 to 1.9.

**Main conclusion:** Water exchange rate indirectly controls productivity by setting turbidity and dissolved mineral levels. The two effects could not be separated in the field, but the factorial experiment shows that dissolved solids are the dominant control in microcosms, with light and temperature modifying the response.
