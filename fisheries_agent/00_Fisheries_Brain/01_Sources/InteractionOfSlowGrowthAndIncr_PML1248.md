# Slow Growth and Increased Early-Life Mortality: A Hypothesis on the Decline of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper (Kaeding & Osmundson, *Environmental Biology of Fishes*, vol. 22, no. 4, 1988) develops a hypothesis that the interaction of slow growth and increased early-life mortality is an important cause of the decline of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species, once the principal native piscivore of the basin, now survives only in upstream reaches and is threatened with extinction. The authors compare a temperature-based growth suitability index for historic and present habitats, relate first-year growth to that index, and use population simulations to show that increased early-life mortality hurts slow-growing populations far more than fast-growing ones. They conclude that water management to enhance growth temperatures and control of [[Introduced Fishes]] could aid recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Ptychocheilus lucius* (Cyprinidae), the Colorado squawfish, a large native piscivore of the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Historic range:** Large rivers and major tributaries across the basin, now occupied only in upstream regions (Fig. 1 contrasts historic and remaining habitat).
- **Status:** Threatened with extinction; the authors describe it as an endangered species requiring recovery programs.
- **Historic size:** Behnke & Benson (1983) report that the largest fish found today weigh about 7 kg, whereas squawfish over 20 kg were apparently not uncommon early in the century.
- **Comparative growth:** Among piscivores, rapid early-life growth is typical (e.g., [[Northern Pike]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]), reducing the period when fish are vulnerable as prey and widening prey choice. Colorado squawfish growth in upper-basin rivers is conspicuously slow by comparison (Fig. 2).
- **Growth controls:** Growth depends largely on the interaction of water temperature and food availability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature and growth:** Black & Bulkley (1985a) found growth optimal at 25 °C, with growth at 15, 20 and 30 °C at 19%, 54% and 51% of optimum. Growth appears to cease below about 13 °C. The authors built a suitability index from this relation.
- **Temperature data:** USGS records were used for the Green River (near Green River, Utah) and the upper Colorado River (near the Colorado–Utah border), both still inhabited. Historic data came from the lower Colorado River near Yuma, Arizona (1917–1924) and the middle Colorado River near Grand Canyon, Arizona (1947–1957 reports).
- **Climate correction:** Historic lower- and middle-river data were not concurrent with present data. Climate analysis suggested that adjusting for climate would raise the historic lower-river averages and lower the others. The authors note the adjustment is not quantifiable.
- **Habitat:** Age-0 fish are most often captured in river backwaters, which have larger diel temperature variation than main channels.
- **Spawning:** Spawning begins when temperatures reach 20–22 °C, normally in July or August in the upper basin, with embryos hatching 4–5 days later. In the historic lower river, spawning temperatures were reached in early May, giving young fish a much longer first growing season.
- **Dam effects:** Dam operation did not reduce temperatures in most reaches still inhabited, so temperature alteration alone does not explain the decline. Cold hypolimnetic releases do reduce embryo survival (Marsh 1985).
- **Introduced fishes:** Channel catfish, [[Green Sunfish]] and Largemouth Bass have colonized squawfish reaches. Green sunfish can greatly suppress native cyprinid populations (Lemly 1985).

## Key Findings

1. **Growth comparison:** A pond-reared cohort (Osmundson 1987) stocked at 50–75 mm grew to an average of 226 mm within one year (largest 304 mm), far faster than wild upper-basin fish, indicating that slow growth reflects suboptimal conditions rather than an inherent limit.
2. **Temperature suitability:** Suitability indices were 3.2 (Green River), 2.1 (upper Colorado), 3.9 (middle Colorado) and 4.9 (lower Colorado). The growing season in upper-basin reaches was under 6 months, against 7–9 months in historic reaches.
3. **Bias check:** Reducing Green River data by a worst-case 1 °C still gave a suitability of 2.7, about 29% higher than the upper Colorado.
4. **Growth–suitability relation:** Mean total length of age-0 squawfish in fall was strongly and positively related to the suitability index (p < 0.01, r = 0.95; r² = 0.91; 91% of length variation explained).
5. **Simulation results:** Under 95% and 99% early-life mortality, fast-growing fish (maturing at 3 years, 410 mm) produced far more mature females than slow-growing fish (maturing at 6 years). Fast-growing stocks exceeded slow-growing ones by large margins, and the gap widened as mortality rose.
6. **Population growth:** Present upper-basin squawfish have a net reproductive rate (R₀) of no more than 1, a condition worse than even the simulated slow-growing stock at 99% mortality. The slow-growing 99% mortality population had very low capacity to sustain additional mortality.
7. **Causal reasoning:** The authors argue that slow growth in upper-basin rivers is largely a result of suboptimal temperatures, and that historic downstream reaches probably supported fast growth. They attribute the current decline to the combination of slow growth with predation by introduced fishes and other human-caused increases in early-life mortality.
8. **Recruitment:** Small age-0 fish entering winter may limit recruitment, because overwinter survival in related species depends on size (e.g., [[Smallmouth Bass]] and largemouth bass studies cited in the paper).
9. **Management recommendations:**
   - Operate dams and reservoirs to lengthen the growing season and increase available growth temperatures, while recognizing that this may also benefit undesirable species.
   - Avoid water-development programs that reduce available temperatures.
   - Investigate the relation between age-0 size and overwinter survival as a basis for growth-enhancement objectives.
   - Prevent further introductions of non-native fishes; eliminating existing introduced species is considered impracticable in a river system this large.
