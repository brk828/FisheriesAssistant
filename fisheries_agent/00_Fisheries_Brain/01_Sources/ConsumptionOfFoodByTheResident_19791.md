# Food Consumption of Resident Pike (Esox lucius) in Lake Windermere

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This study estimates the annual food consumption, production and yield of [[Northern Pike]] in [[Lake Windermere]], England, using the [[Beverton-Holt Model]] and the experimentally derived [[Consumption Of Food]] values. It is the second of two parts of the work, with the companion paper on experimental food consumption. The analysis compares two years of a netting program: 1944–45, the first full year of exploitation, and 1954–55, when the population appears to have stabilised at a lower level. Biomass fell from 8182 kg to 4349 kg, and annual consumption fell from 22,124 kg to 16,563 kg. The author concludes that pike consume a large share of lake production while forming only a small fraction of total biomass.

## Background & Description

Windermere is the largest lake in England, with a total area of 14.82 km² (1482 ha) and a maximum depth of 65 m. A chain of islands divides it into north and south basins with mean depths of 26 m and 17.7 m. Over the past century the lake became more [[Eutrophication]], and [[European Perch]] and pike replaced [[Arctic Char]] and [[Brown Trout]] as dominant species.

A large-scale removal experiment began in 1943. Pike were gillnetted and perch trapped. A tagging program started in 1950, which allowed population numbers to be estimated. Every pike removed was weighed and measured, and [[Frost and Kipling]] (1959) determined age and growth from these data. Earlier dietary work by [[Frost]] (1954) showed that pike switch from invertebrates to fish at around 40–100 mm length.

The population model uses:
- The von Bertalanffy growth curve and [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Constant]] (K)
- Natural and fishing mortality
- The length–weight relationship W = cL^n
- A maintenance energy coefficient (Q) derived from experimental work

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Pike are confined to the lake margin, mostly in water shallower than 6.5 m, with no evident migration in or out of the area. Their age at recruitment is therefore zero.
- **Prey switching:** Pike at 40 mm start supplementing invertebrates with small fish. By 100 mm, fish are the staple diet.
- **Seasonal prey:** Char enter shallows to spawn in winter and are heavily preyed upon. Perch use shallow water in summer and are consumed in quantity, but are less important in winter, when they live in deeper water.
- **Production context:** Mean production in English waters is estimated at about 23.6 kg/ha (Mortimer, 1954). Perch production in Windermere is about 15.5 kg/ha (Le Cren, 1962). Pike consumption within their occupied area therefore greatly exceeds local production, implying that pike draw on prey from the whole lake.

## Key Findings

### Population and Biomass

| Parameter | 1944 | 1954 |
|---|---|---|
| Population (vulnerable fish) | 2049 (1009 M, 1040 F) | 858 (345 M, 513 F) |
| Recruits | 2049 | 1319 |
| Biomass (kg) | 8182 | 4349 |
| Basic food consumption (kg) | — | — |
| Total annual consumption (kg) | 22,124 | 16,563 |

- Biomass fell by about 47% (abstract) or 50% (results text), with consumption down 25%. The two sections give different figures for the biomass reduction.
- Males made up 34% of biomass in 1944 and 44% in 1954. The author attributes this to heavier fishing on the larger, faster-growing females.
- The 1954 population came from tagging data with a Petersen estimate, adjusted for tagged fish returned by anglers. The 1944 estimate used the assumed constant effectiveness of fishing, because no tagging records exist for that season.
- Recruitment was assumed constant between the two years, supported by Le Cren (1959), who showed that young pike numbers stabilised after an early rise.

### Age at Entry and Growth

- Age at 50% vulnerability to gill nets fell from 5.7 to 4.7 years for males, and from 6.5 to 3.9 years for females (1944 to 1954).
- Maximum known ages were 16 years for males and 14 for females.
- Asymptotic lengths were 75 cm for males and 100 cm for females.
- Von Bertalanffy K was 0.22 for males and 0.24 for females. Theoretical age at birth (t₀) was −2.2 years for males and −0.18 years for females.

### Food Consumption and Production

- Annual production per unit biomass rose from 0.26 kg (1944) to 0.39 kg (1954), a 47% increase.
- Consumption per kilogram of biomass rose from 2.70 kg to 3.81 kg.
- Consumption per kilogram of production was 10.28 (1944) and 9.86 (1954).
- Cropping rate fell from 14.93 to 11.17 kg/ha across the whole lake. Over the 420 ha occupied by pike, it fell from 52.67 to 39.44 kg/ha.
- Pike consumed about 63% of total lake production before netting began, falling to 47% afterwards. In 1944 the upper confidence limit for consumption (22.98 kg/ha) was nearly equal to estimated production.
- A mean pike is estimated to consume 3–4 times its mean annual weight each year, depending on fishing intensity.

### Energetics

- Gonad production was estimated at 84% of flesh production for both sexes combined. It was assumed that female egg production equals annual flesh production and male testes production is half of that.
- Maintenance accounted for 30% of intake in small experimental pike but rises to 78% across the whole population.
- The "gross conversion" figure for small experimental pike was 3.42.

### Model Validation

The yield for 1954 was calculated from the model and compared with the actual catch. The text and Table VII report the calculated and actual yields in opposite columns, so the figures below are the table values:

| Sex | Calculated yield (kg) | Actual catch (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| Males | 219.0 | 237.6 |
| Females | 545.3 | 468.3 |
| Total | 764.3 | 705.9 |

The author acknowledges some circularity, because the calculations start from catch statistics, but argues that the procedure generated substantial information with little accumulated error. Results are described as reasonable approximations, not precise to the nearest kilogram.

### Conclusions

- Reducing pike predation by removing adults is difficult. Production per unit biomass rose sharply, while consumption fell only modestly.
- Pike depend on the whole lake's production, not only the inshore zone they occupy.
- Large predators face a high maintenance cost, which leaves a small share of food for growth, so efficient conversion is advantageous.
