# Modifications of Models Predicting Trophic State of Lakes

#phytoplankton #limnology #eutrophication

## Executive Summary

This EPA Project Summary, prepared by the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] Environmental Monitoring Systems Laboratory in Las Vegas, Nevada (EPA-600/S3-81-001, February 1981), argues that lake trophic classifications based only on [[Total Phosphorus]] are unreliable. Using data from 757 U.S. lakes, the authors found that the relationship between [[Phytoplankton]] biomass, measured as [[Chlorophyll A]], and total phosphorus is much weaker across the country than earlier studies reported. The authors propose adjusting [[Nutrient Loading Models]] and ambient models of [[Trophic State]] so that they account for lake-specific differences in the chlorophyll a produced per unit of phosphorus.

## Background & Description

Phosphorus supply is considered the primary determinant of algal biomass and production in most temperate lakes. Existing ambient and loading models estimate lake total phosphorus from tributary loading and are widely used to classify lakes and guide management. These models use ambient phosphorus thresholds of 10 and 20 micrograms per liter to divide lakes into three standard classes: [[Oligotrophic Lakes]], [[Mesotrophic Lakes]], and [[Eutrophic Lakes]]. The models assume that every lake responds to phosphorus in the same way.

The authors note that phosphorus use by phytoplankton varies from lake to lake. Efficiency depends on light availability, the supply of other nutrients, and the biological availability of different phosphorus species. The analysis drew on data from the [[National Eutrophication Survey]], collected in spring, summer, and fall from 1972 to 1975, covering 757 lakes across the 48 conterminous states.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame the problem in terms of the [[Eutrophication]] process. Prior researchers reported log-log product-moment correlations between chlorophyll a and total phosphorus of 0.85 to 0.98. The authors argue these results reflect near-ideal conditions without major interferences. Their own correlation for the 757 U.S. lakes was 0.60, and the response ratio (chlorophyll a produced per unit of total phosphorus) varied widely.

The interference factors identified as limiting phytoplankton chlorophyll a include:

- Light availability, especially [[Light Attenuation]] by materials not related to chlorophyll a
- Limitation by growth factors other than total phosphorus
- Domination of the aquatic flora by vascular plants instead of phytoplankton
- Short hydraulic retention time
- Toxic substances

The ratio of biologically available phosphorus to [[Nitrogen]] was an important factor in some lakes. Temperature was not found to be an important factor.

## Key Findings

- **Weak national correlation:** Chlorophyll a and total phosphorus showed a log-log correlation of only 0.60 across 757 U.S. lakes, compared with 0.85 to 0.98 in earlier studies.
- **Light is a major control:** Light attenuation from non-chlorophyll a interferences can dramatically reduce phytoplankton biomass in many U.S. lakes.
- **Nutrient ratio matters in some lakes:** The biologically available phosphorus-to-nitrogen ratio sometimes determines how much chlorophyll a is produced per unit of phosphorus.
- **Temperature was not important:** The study found temperature did not play a major role in this relationship.
- **Classification changes:** When lakes were classified by chlorophyll a instead of total phosphorus, 25 percent of the 757 lakes were classified as less eutrophic.
- **Management implications:** The authors suggest that if these lakes are representative of the U.S., some communities could avoid costly nutrient-removal programs that phosphorus-based classifications would require.

**Recommendations and methods:** The authors recommend adjusting ambient and loading models to account for chlorophyll a produced per unit of total phosphorus. The methods are designed to:

1. Change trophic classification from an ambient total phosphorus level to one based on the chlorophyll a response.
2. Determine the critical total phosphorus levels that would produce unacceptable chlorophyll a levels, so phosphorus can be managed for a desired water use.
3. Account for lake-specific characteristics that affect chlorophyll a produced per unit of phosphorus.

The authors argue that the manifestations of nutrients, such as excessive algal growth, matter more for water quality than an arbitrary total phosphorus classification.
