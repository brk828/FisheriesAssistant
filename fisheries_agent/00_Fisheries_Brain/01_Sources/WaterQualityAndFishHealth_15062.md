# Water Quality and Fish Health

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #water-quality #fish-toxicology

## Executive Summary

This FAO/EIFAC Technical Paper (No. 54, 1993, 59 pp.) is a textbook edited from lectures given at the FAO International Training Course on Freshwater Fish Diseases and Intoxications, held in Vodnany, Czechoslovakia (June 15 - July 3, 1992). Written by [[Zdenka Svobodova]], [[Richard Lloyd]], [[Jana Machova]] and [[Blanka Vykusova]], it reviews how natural and man-made changes in water chemistry and physical habitat affect fish, how fish adapt physiologically, which pollutants cause poisoning and at what concentrations, how to diagnose fish kills, how to control pollution through prevention and legislation, and how pollution interacts with viral, bacterial, fungal and parasitic disease.

## Background & Description

The publication is intended as a training tool for courses of broader character and for fisheries managers. It is structured in six chapters: an introduction on natural and anthropogenic inputs; a brief review of adaptive mechanisms (respiration, osmoregulation, detoxification); causes and effects of pollution, covering natural water-quality variables and about twenty groups of man-made chemicals; diagnosis of fish poisoning; control of water quality; and pollution in relation to disease.

The introduction notes that no water has a "normal" chemistry. Rainwater carries acidic ions, volatile chemicals and particulates, and atrazine in rain can reach 1 µg in areas of heavy herbicide use. Soft, acidic runoff from granite, peat bogs and coniferous forests contrasts with alkaline, hard water from chalk and limestone. Man-made inputs are divided into point sources (pipe discharges) and diffuse sources (agricultural runoff, aerial deposition, road runoff), summarised in Fig. 1. Physical alterations such as canalisation, dams and diversions also change flow, depth and suspended solids.

Mechanistic background relevant to all later chapters is given in the adaptation section. Gill structure (secondary lamellae, counter-current exchange, [[Carbonic Anhydrase]] activity) and osmoregulation (sodium uptake in exchange for hydrogen ions, chloride for bicarbonate) explain why fish are vulnerable to water chemistry changes, since the gills are both the respiratory surface and the main route of toxicant entry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature:** Fish are poikilothermic. Temperature shock is noted when transfers differ by about 12°C (8°C for salmonids), and by as little as 1.5-3°C for young fry. Immune function in most species is optimal near 15°C.
- **pH:** The optimal range is 6.5-8.5. Salmonids are damaged above pH 9.2 or below 4.8; cyprinids are rapidly killed above 10.8 or below 5.0. The [[American Char]] tolerates pH 4.5-5.0.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Salmonids need 8-10 mg/l and show suffocation signs below 3 mg/l; cyprinids tolerate 6-8 mg/l and suffocate at 1.5-2.0 mg/l. Diel oxygen cycles in eutrophic ponds often produce pre-dawn minima, and the text warns that a satisfactory daytime reading does not guarantee night-time levels.
- **Organic loading:** Oxygen demand is characterised by [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] (BOD5) and [[Chemical Oxygen Demand]] (COD), with cyprinid limits of 8-15 mg O2/l (BOD5) and 20-30 mg O2/l (COD), and salmonid limits of 5 mg O2/l (BOD5) and 10 mg O2/l (COD).
- **Nitrogen compounds:** [[Un-ionised Ammonia]] is the toxic form, and its proportion rises with pH and temperature. Nitrite toxicity is strongly modified by [[Chloride]] concentration. Nitrate toxicity is low.
- **Dissolved gases and related factors:** [[Gas Supersaturation]] causes gas bubble disease, [[Hydrogen Sulphide]] is toxic in organically polluted and anoxic conditions, and free CO2 deficiency or excess affects acid-base balance.
- **Pollutant classes covered:** [[Heavy Metals]] (aluminium, chromium, iron, nickel, copper, zinc, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, lead), [[Chlorine]], [[Cyanides]], [[Phenols]], [[Polychlorinated Biphenyls]], [[Surfactants]], [[Pesticides]], [[Oils and Refined Products]], [[Dyes]] and [[Phytoplankton Toxins]].
- **Diagnosis tools:** [[Bioassay]] using [[Daphnia Magna]] and [[Poecilia Reticulata]], periphyton analysis, sediment analysis, and [[Toxic Gill Necrosis]] diagnosis in carp.

## Key Findings

**1. Adaptation has limits, and rate of change matters.** Fish can detoxify or excrete some substances and can raise resistance to a toxicant up to about four-fold after sub-lethal exposure. Gradual change is tolerated, but rapid change, or change beyond natural limits, produces stress and increases disease susceptibility.

**2. Ammonia toxic gill necrosis in carp.** In a field study of the Dřemliny (Diemliny) pond, diagnosed on 2 May 1984, about 50% of two- and three-year-old carp died. Blood serum ammonia-N in affected carp was 2000-4800 µg/100 ml, against a normal 350-800 µg/100 ml. Prevention centres on stocking at the correct spring time, avoiding oxygen deficits, and a biological test: an oral ammonia dose of 350 mg/100 ml in starch gel, with stocking allowed if serum ammonia returns to baseline within 6 hours.

**3. Pesticide Soldep.** Application at 200 ml/ha (1 m mean depth) was recommended to control daphnid overproduction and prevent oxygen deficiency. Its 48-h LC50 for common carp is 545 mg/l, but for *Daphnia magna* it is 0.0002-0.001 mg/l, illustrating non-target effects.

**4. Toxicity categories.** Czech standard ON 46 6807 classes substances by 48-h LC50 from category (e), almost no toxicity (>10 000 mg/l), down to category 6, extreme toxicity (<0.1 mg/l). Classification uses the most sensitive organism tested.

**5. Maximum admissible concentrations (MACs) are given for many substances.** Examples include ammonia (un-ionised) 0.05 mg/l for cyprinids and 0.0125 mg/l for salmonids; nitrate 80 mg/l for carp and 20 mg/l for rainbow trout; copper 0.001-0.01 mg/l; cadmium 0.0002 mg/l for salmonids and 0.001 mg/l for cyprinids; inorganic mercury about 0.001 mg/l (salmonids) and 0.002 mg/l (cyprinids); lead 0.004-0.008 mg/l (salmonids) and 0.07 mg/l (cyprinids).

**6. Bioaccumulation.** Mercury, selenium and cadmium bioaccumulate strongly. In the Berounka River drainage, fish flesh mercury was about 10 times that of their food, and leeches (*Helobdella stagnalis*) were proposed as mercury indicators.

**7. Diagnosis is difficult and must be systematic.** Delayed discovery of kills means pollutants may have dispersed and post-mortem changes may obscure lesions. The text gives a 16-item field checklist, sampling protocols with preservation requirements, and advice to examine water, sediments, periphyton, invertebrates and fish. It notes that tissue residues alone do not prove causation.

**8. Pollution weakens disease resistance.** Sub-lethal zinc lowered serum total protein, globulins and lysozyme in carp. Pollutants reduce zooplankton and zoobenthos, which removes natural food. Organic pollution and low oxygen favour furunculosis, branchiomycosis and columnaris. Low oxygen increased *Gyrodactylus* reproduction about three- to four-fold at a 50% decrease in oxygen concentration.

**9. Prevention and legislation.** The authors argue that producer discipline is the cheapest method of pollution prevention. They recommend treatment of waste waters, leak-proof containment at industrial sites, and grassy buffer strips and spray restrictions on farmland. Legislation is of two types: liability-based and regulation-based (MACs and monitoring), and each has trade-offs.

**10. Chronic and alternative testing.** Standard chronic tests use rainbow trout or common carp for 90-100 days (Svobodová and Vykusová, 1991), and *Daphnia magna* for reproduction. Cell-culture and embryo-larval tests are being developed, mainly for screening, and cannot yet replace chronic tests for persistent, bioaccumulative substances.

**Overall conclusion.** Fish health depends on a stable and balanced water environment. Diagnosis and prevention require combined chemical, biological, and health examinations, and the authors identify the link between pollution and fish disease as an under-researched area in natural populations.
