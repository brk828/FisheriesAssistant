# Physical Habitat Utilization of Fish in a Sonoran Desert Stream, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by J. N. Rinne (USDA Forest Service and Arizona State University, published in *Ecology of Freshwater Fish*, 1992) describes the physical habitat use of seven native cypriniform fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a low-gradient, hydrologically stable stream in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of southeastern Arizona. Habitat use was measured in terms of water depth, velocity and substrate. The seven species occupied significantly different depths and velocities, and they fell into groups that partly reflect niche overlap. The paper also reviews threats to the stream, including reduced flow, aquifer overappropriation and invasion by [[Red Shiner]].

## Background & Description

Desert streams support a depauperate but highly specialized fish fauna adapted to flood-and-drought cycles (Soltz & Naiman 1978; Naiman & Soltz 1981). Human alteration of hydrology through dams, diversions and groundwater pumping, along with introduced fishes, has caused a sharp decline in native fish resources. Quantitative habitat requirements for many desert fishes were lacking when the study began in spring 1983.

The study species are:

- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), federally threatened
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), federally threatened
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)

Five are minnows (Cyprinidae) and two are suckers (Catostomidae). These are seven of the 17 native fishes that historically inhabited the [[Gila River Basin]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Aravaipa Creek is a low-gradient (<1%), narrow (3–5 m), often braided stream with a gravel-pebble substrate. Modal discharge is 0.5–1.0 m³·s⁻¹, with the highest flows in winter. Late-summer convectional storms can cause sudden flash floods. A canyon-bound central reach separates open upper and lower reaches, and the perennial section lies within riparian deciduous forest and woodland. The stream has a database spanning 30 years.

Sampling used randomly located habitat transects (maximum of 10 per reach) in the upper reach. Fish were immobilized with direct-current electrofishing and captured with a 1-m-wide block seine. Habitat was measured at each 1–2 m² sample area, whether or not fish were present, using a meter-transect method and a habitat sampler. Juvenile (>1 year) and adult fish were analyzed. Data were combined across seasons (winter n = 90, spring n = 94, summer n = 142; sampling April 1983–July 1984) because no seasonal difference in habitat use was detected.

Substrate classes were: sand (<2 mm), gravel (3–16 mm), pebble (17–64 mm), cobble (65–256 mm) and boulder (>256 mm).

## Key Findings

**Overall differences**
- Depth differed among the seven species (ANOVA, P < 0.0001, F = 32.5, df = 273).
- Velocity also differed (ANOVA, P < 0.0001, F = 10.6, df = 273).

**Groupings**
- Loach minnow, speckled dace and longfin dace used similar shallow depths (12–16 cm).
- Loach minnow and speckled dace occupied the shallowest riffles (14–15 cm) with faster velocity (about 40 cm·s⁻¹) over pebble/cobble substrate.
- Desert sucker shared the velocity and substrate use of loach minnow and speckled dace but used slightly deeper riffles.
- Longfin dace and spikedace were most often found in shallow (<20 cm) riffles with moderate velocity (~35 cm·s⁻¹) over pebble/gravel substrate.
- [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] occupied the deepest pools (30–34 cm) with the lowest velocity (15–25 cm·s⁻¹) over sand/gravel substrate.

**Habitat guilds**
- Riffle, swift turbulent flow over gravel-pebble: loach minnow, speckled dace, desert sucker.
- Riffle, smoother laminar flow at moderate velocity over gravel-sand: spikedace, longfin dace.
- Pool, slow to zero velocity over sand: Sonora sucker, roundtail chub.

**Niche and competition**
- Speckled dace, loach minnow and desert sucker occupy essentially the same physical habitat. Niche partitioning may occur through food habits and microhabitat (loach minnow uses substrate interstices; speckled dace uses the surface to open water).
- Desert sucker feeds mainly by scraping algae and diatoms. Speckled dace feeds mainly on invertebrates.
- Food habits of speckled dace overlapped widely (80%) with longfin dace, spikedace and loach minnow (Schreiber & Minckley 1981).
- Roundtail chub, the top carnivore, overlapped less than 80% with loach minnow in diet.
- Rinne (1991) reported similar habitat use by [[Red Shiner]] and spikedace, suggesting competition.

**Conservation implications**
- Over 95% of historic riparian-stream habitat in the Southwest has been lost. Aravaipa Creek retains the greatest percentage of the Gila Basin's historic native fish fauna intact.
- Protection comes from Bureau of Land Management primitive-area status, Defenders of Wildlife land holdings and instream flow designations.
- Five of the seven species depend on shallow riffles, so flow maintenance is critical. Under reduced flow, only the roundtail chub and Sonora sucker would be likely to survive.
- Upstream aquifer protection, particularly against overappropriation of the Sulphur Springs Valley aquifer, is essential.
- Non-native fish invasion from stock tanks or the Gila River is an equal or greater threat. Red shiner was recorded in the lower reaches of Aravaipa Creek in 1990.
