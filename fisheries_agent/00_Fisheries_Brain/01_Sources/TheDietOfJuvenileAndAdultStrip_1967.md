# The Diet of Juvenile and Adult Striped Bass (Roccus saxatilis) in the Sacramento-San Joaquin River System

## Executive Summary
This 1967 study by John L. Thomas (California Department of Fish and Game) analyzed the stomach contents of more than 4,500 [[Striped Bass]] collected between 1957 and 1961 across six distinct food communities of the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] and [[San Francisco Bay Estuary]]. Roughly half of the stomachs examined contained identifiable natural food. The study found that diet composition varied strongly by **season**, **geographic area**, and **fish size**, driven primarily by the migratory behavior of both the bass and their prey. Dominant prey included [[Northern Anchovy]], [[Shiner Perch]], juvenile [[Striped Bass]] (cannibalism), [[King Salmon]] smolts, [[Carp]], crayfish, bay shrimp, [[Mysid Shrimp]] (*Neomysis awatschensis*), isopods, scuds (*Corophium* spp.), and aquatic insect larvae. A key ecological finding was that turbidity in the Delta appears to suppress piscivory in juvenile bass, which instead rely heavily on mysid shrimp.

## Background & Description
This paper is a dietary/ecological study rather than a morphological one; however, it defines size classes used for comparative feeding analysis:

| Age Class | Total Length | Legal Status |
|---|---|---|
| Age I (juvenile) | 6–10 inches | Sub-legal |
| Age II (juvenile) | 11–15 inches | Sub-legal |
| Age III+ (adult) | ≥16 inches | Legal (California minimum) |

- Size-class segregation was used to correlate **predator body size** with **prey item size**, revealing a clear ontogenetic dietary shift (see [[Key Findings]]).
- Small bass (6–10 in) preferentially consumed small crustaceans (e.g., [[Mysid Shrimp]]), while large bass (>16 in) shifted almost exclusively to fish prey (up to 100% frequency of occurrence of fish in largest size class from Crockett-to-Pittsburg samples).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### Study Area Structure
The bass habitat was divided into six food communities along a freshwater-to-marine gradient:
1. **San Francisco Bay** – marine-influenced, anchovy/shiner perch dominated
2. **San Pablo Bay** – transitional, anchovy/bay shrimp dominated
3. **Crockett to Pittsburg** – anchovy, king salmon, striped bass cannibalism
4. **Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta** – freshwater, [[Mysid Shrimp]]-dominated, low piscivory
5. **Lower Sacramento River** – carp, king salmon smolts, crayfish
6. **Upper Sacramento River** – king salmon smolts, carp, insect larvae dominated

### Trophic Relationships
- **Adult bass** in bay/marine areas are primarily piscivorous, targeting [[Northern Anchovy]], [[Shiner Perch]], [[Pacific Herring]], and [[Staghorn Sculpin]].
- **Juvenile bass** in the Delta feed almost exclusively on invertebrates, especially [[Mysid Shrimp]] (*Neomysis awatschensis*), rather than fish — contrasting with juveniles elsewhere in the system that consume substantial fish prey.
- **Cannibalism**: Small [[Striped Bass]] were a significant prey item for larger conspecifics, particularly in the Crockett-to-Pittsburg reach (up to 50% of diet volume in fall) and the Delta.
- **Predation on salmonids**: [[King Salmon]] fry/smolts made up the majority of bass diet volume (60–73%) in the Sacramento River during their downstream spring/summer migration, raising concerns about predation impacts on salmon populations.
- **Turbidity hypothesis**: The near-total absence of piscivory among Delta juveniles, despite abundant prey fish species there, is hypothesized to result from high water turbidity limiting visual predation efficiency — a niche-defining environmental constraint.
- **Seasonal prey migrations** structuring bass diet:
  - Northern anchovy upstream migration peaks June–August in Carquinez Strait.
  - Young-of-year striped bass distribution tied to freshwater runoff.
  - Downstream king salmon smolt migration concentrated in spring/early summer.
- **Emerging prey shift**: [[Threadfin Shad]], not abundant until after the study period (post-1962), later became a major Delta food source, indicating the diet is dynamic relative to prey community shifts.

## Key Findings

### Major Food Items by Volume/Frequency (across all size classes and seasons)
- Vertebrate prey: [[Northern Anchovy]], [[Shiner Perch]], [[Striped Bass]] (juveniles), [[King Salmon]], [[Carp]], [[Staghorn Sculpin]], [[Pacific Herring]], [[Threadfin Shad]] (post-study)
- Invertebrate prey: Bay shrimp (*Crago* spp., *Palaemon macrodactylus*), [[Mysid Shrimp]] (*Neomysis awatschensis*), isopods (*Synidotea* sp.), scuds (*Corophium* spp.), crayfish (*Pacifastacus leniusculus*), aquatic insect larvae (caddisflies, midges, true flies)

### Seasonal Patterns
- **Spring**: Anchovies dominate bay areas; mysid shrimp dominate Delta (66% frequency, 42% volume); king salmon dominate upper Sacramento River (65% volume, 62% frequency).
- **Summer**: Anchovies dominate San Francisco/San Pablo Bays; mysid shrimp peak in Delta (91% frequency, 80% volume); king salmon/carp dominate upper river (73% combined volume).
- **Fall**: Bay shrimp important in San Pablo Bay (25% volume); juvenile striped bass cannibalism peaks at Crockett-to-Pittsburg (50% volume) and Delta; carp dominate upper river (68% volume).
- **Winter**: Bay shrimp dominate San Pablo Bay (47% volume, 70% frequency); mysid shrimp dominate Delta (74% frequency); white seaperch and herring important in San Francisco Bay (64% volume combined).

### Size-Related Food Selection (Crockett-to-Pittsburg Comparison, Table 6)
- Fish 6–10 in: Mean frequency of occurrence — Mysid shrimp 42%, isopods 25%, shrimps 19%, fishes 55%
- Fish 11–15 in: Mysid shrimp 30%, isopods 26%, shrimps 79%, fishes 0% (anomalous low fish value noted in original data)
- Fish >16 in: isopods 8%, shrimps 27%, fishes 95%
- Confirms a strong **positive correlation between predator size and prey size**, consistent with earlier findings by Shapovalov (1936) at Waddell Creek lagoon.

### Notable Anomalies
- Two of 443 San Pablo Bay stomachs contained large numbers of [[Northern Midshipman]] (9 and 19 individuals respectively) with no other food present, suggesting deliberate, selective feeding behavior on locally aggregated prey rather than random opportunistic feeding.

### Comparison to Historical Data
- Findings are broadly consistent with [[Johnson and Calhoun 1952]] dietary study (1947–1948 samples): both identify shrimp and anchovy as dominant summer/fall prey in San Pablo Bay and mysid shrimp as key winter/spring prey for small bass in the Delta, though shrimp appear to have declined in relative importance over time.

### Management/Ecological Implications
- Predation by striped bass on juvenile [[King Salmon]] and on conspecific juvenile [[Striped Bass]] may influence recruitment and year-class strength, though the magnitude of population-level impact could not be determined from this dataset.
- Diet patterns support the hypothesis that habitat turbidity is an important, under-recognized regulator of predator-prey dynamics in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]].
