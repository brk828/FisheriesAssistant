# Status of the Habitat Evaluation Procedures

#wildlife-ecology #habitat-evaluation #mammalogy

## Executive Summary

This paper, prepared for the 47th North American Wildlife and Natural Resources Conference (March 1982), reports on the status of the [[Habitat Evaluation Procedures]] (HEP), a habitat-based method developed by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) with the [[Army Corps Of Engineers]] (COE), [[Soil Conservation Service]] (SCS), and [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (BR). HEP combines habitat quantity (area) with habitat quality (a [[Habitat Suitability Index]], HSI) to produce [[Habitat Units]] (HU's), which can be used to compare baseline conditions and predict the effects of development alternatives. The system also includes a [[Human Use And Economic Evaluation]] (HUEE) component that converts habitat data into economic values.

The authors report that HEP was used on four COE and three SCS demonstration projects, that over 1,300 people had been trained through a nationwide program, and that the overall consensus so far is that HEP provides useful planning information. The paper stresses that any model must be documented and verified, and it identifies data shortages for habitat models as the main continuing limitation.

## Background & Description

**Origins.** The 1970s saw growing demand for predictive impact assessment, driven by environmental legislation requiring noneconomic project evaluations and by recognition that traditional inventories documented only existing conditions. A 1971 task force report (White) recommended that the FWS develop a nonmonetary evaluation procedure. A system by Daniel and Lamaire (1974) was selected for development, and the procedure was published as HEP in 1976. HEP was revised as HEP-80 and issued as three components in the FWS Division of Ecological Services manual series (ESM 101–104).

**Purpose.** A standardized habitat-based method was intended to (1) improve communication among agencies and professions, since biologists often lacked evaluation methods comparable to engineering and economic ones, and (2) focus limited fish and wildlife research and resources on a common framework.

**Core equation.**
- Habitat area × Habitat quality = Habitat Units (HU's), or Area × HSI = HU's.
- HSI is scaled from 0.0 to 1.0, where 1.0 is maximum habitat quality and is assumed to correlate positively with carrying capacity.
- HU's are calculated separately for each species, life requisite, life stage, or guild in the evaluation.

**Changes from HEP-76 to HEP-80.** HEP-76 used subjective habitat suitability estimates averaged into a single value per cover type. HEP-80 tracks individual species, life stages, requisites, or guilds and uses documented models based on measurable environmental variables. Studies at the University of Missouri found that methods measuring environmental variables were more repeatable than subjective estimates. HSI models focus on physical and chemical habitat variables and usually exclude competition, disease, and contaminants, although these can be included when appropriate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design and uses.** HEP data support baseline comparisons (different areas at one time) and impact assessments (the same area at different times or under alternative management or development options). Outputs describe the amount of habitat involved, its quality for species of concern, and an index combining both.

**Limiting factors.** Because HSI models include individual variables, managers can identify which habitat factors are limiting. The authors note that management should target the most limiting factors, assuming all variables are equally manageable.

**Known gaps.** The relative importance of biological versus physical factors in determining carrying capacity remains unclear. Few studies quantify relationships between habitat variables (canopy cover, ground cover, tree size, distance to water) and animal numbers. The authors illustrate this with the difficulty of predicting [[Gray Squirrel]] population responses if half the mast trees were removed.

**Field demands.** Quantitative models require careful sampling design, especially regarding accuracy and precision. An inventory techniques manual (Hays et al. 1981) guides selection of measurement methods for terrestrial habitat variables.

## Key Findings

**1. Baseline example (hypothetical data, Table 1).** Four sites were compared:

| Site | Area (acres) | HSI | HU |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 1.0 | 1,000 |
| 2 | 1,000 | 0.2 | 200 |
| 3 | 10,000 | 0.9 | 9,000 |
| 4 | 10,000 | 0.4 | 4,000 |

Sites 1 and 3 had the highest habitat quality. The authors note that a development decision might favor sites 2 or 4 because they have the lowest wildlife habitat value, whereas conservation goals would protect sites 1 and 3.

**2. Impact assessment example (hypothetical pine marten data, Table 2).**

| Scenario | Area (acres) | HSI | HU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 10,000 | 0.4 | 4,000 |
| Alternative A (habitat management) | 10,000 | 0.8 | 8,000 |
| Alternative B (development with compensating management) | 1,000 | 0.2 | 200 |
| Alternative C (development) | 1,000 | 0.0 | 0 |

Alternative C eliminates all suitable [[American Marten]] habitat.

**3. Marten HSI model demonstration (Table 3).** Model variables were: percent tree canopy closure, percent of overstory canopy of fir or spruce, successional stage of stand, and percent ground surface covered by downfall (≥7.6 cm diameter). Field values were converted to suitability indices using standard graphs (Figure 1). The model aggregates them as a geometric mean, HSI = (V1 × V2 × V3 × V4)^1/2.
- Baseline: canopy closure 85%, fir/spruce 60%, old growth, downfall 20%. Suitability indices were 1.0 for all variables, giving HSI 1.0 and 2,000 HU's on 2,000 acres.
- Alternative A: canopy closure 30%, fir/spruce 10%, pole/sapling stage, downfall 40%. Indices were 0.1, 0.3, 0.3, and 1.0, giving HSI 0.1 and 200 HU's on 2,000 acres.

**4. Demonstration projects.** HEP-80 was tested on four COE and three SCS projects to evaluate institutional effectiveness and technical credibility. The final evaluation was not complete, but the early consensus was that HEP provides useful planning information.
- Strengths: habitat-based focus, documented models that create a record and support recommendations, and value as a diagnostic tool for identifying limiting factors.
- Weaknesses: mechanistic models need many measurements and calculations, HSI models must be solved repeatedly, and early studies lacked automated software. A shortage of literature data for building models is a persistent problem.

**5. Adoption and training.**
- HEP was the most widely used FWS evaluation technique, with 112 applications in 1981.
- A one-week training course was offered at over 25 U.S. locations, reaching over 1,300 people from agencies including the COE, SCS, Bureau of Land Management, Forest Service, and over 40 State fish and game agencies, as well as consultants, universities, and several foreign governments.

**6. Efficiency guidance.** The paper lists seven adaptations to reduce time and cost:
1. Define study objectives narrowly, focusing on featured species.
2. Evaluate only critical cover types.
3. Choose species that use a single cover type, since multicover species are harder to model.
4. Use models with few variables, or aerial photography (infrared photos can estimate some variables).
5. Select target years at key change points, such as pre- and post-project conditions.
6. Limit alternatives by re-evaluating only the parts that change.
7. Match baseline sampling reliability to the resolution of the projection; 100-year projections do not justify highly precise baselines.

**7. Future direction.** Priorities are testing and improving species habitat models, exploring guild-based community models, applying multivariate statistics to define habitats more quantitatively, and developing HSI software. The authors anticipate about 15 terrestrial, 15 inland aquatic, and 5 estuarine HSI models scheduled for publication in 1982, and expect expanded HEP use once software and models are readily available. Training would continue at a reduced level.

**8. Economic link.** HUEE converts HU's into estimated animal populations and sustainable use, using biological supply as the limiting factor. It supplements cost-benefit analyses of changes in wildlife availability for human use.
