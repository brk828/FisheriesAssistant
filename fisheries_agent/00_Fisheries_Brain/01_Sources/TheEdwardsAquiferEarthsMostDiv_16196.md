# The Edwards Aquifer Ecosystem: Earth's Most Diverse Groundwater Ecosystem?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

Studies of the [[Edwards Aquifer]], a karstic, cavernous limestone system in central Texas, describe an extremely diverse community of aquatic troglobites. Sampling of wells, springs and caves suggests the system may be the most diverse subterranean aquatic ecosystem known, with at least 35 to 40 troglobitic species, many of them endemic. The note also proposes a food-web hypothesis in which fungi and bacteria feeding on fossil organic matter support the invertebrate base of the food chain, with [[Blind Catfish]] at the top in the San Antonio area. The aquifer faces long-term threats from pumping, which the cited projections suggest could eventually exceed recharge and dry the springs.

## Background & Description

The aquifer lies along the [[Balcones Escarpment]] and Fault Zone, the dominant structural feature of the area. It is a porous, honeycombed, cavernous limestone of fine-grained carbonate rock lying between the base of the Del Rio Clay and the top of the Glen Rose Formation. The porous Edwards limestone is 122 to 152 m thick (Puente, 1976). The aquifer is about 282 km long and 8 to 64 km wide.

Water moves from the Edwards Plateau recharge area southward, then eastward and northeastward to natural outlets including [[Comal Springs]] (New Braunfels), [[San Marcos Springs]] (San Marcos), Leona Springs, San Antonio and San Pedro Springs, and Hueco Springs. Many wells in the artesian zone, particularly around San Antonio, flow at ground level.

Hydrologically the aquifer is divided into three pools: Uvalde, San Antonio (Central) and San Marcos. These connect when piezometric levels are high and separate during severe drought.

The troglobitic invertebrate fauna (Table 1 in the source) comprises roughly 25 named or described taxa across Platyhelminthes, Mollusca, Annelida, and Arthropoda (ostracods, copepods, isopods, thermosbaenaceans, amphipods, shrimp and a beetle), with many records from Hays, Bexar, Uvalde, Comal and Medina counties. Notable entries include [[Sphalloplana mohri]], [[Monodella texana]], [[Stygobromus]] amphipods and [[Palaemonetes antrorum]]. The table also notes "plus 8 other amphipod species" including one new family and four new genera.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Recharge and water budget.** Recharge averages more than 616 hm³. Recorded extremes between 1934 and 1975 were a minimum of 54.25 hm³ (1956) and a maximum of 2,096 hm³ (1958). The highest pumping on record was 501.58 hm³ in 1971 (Edwards Underground Water District, 1977). Projections cited from the U.S. Department of the Interior (1973) state that future pumping may exceed recharge and eventually dry all springs.

**Energy input.** In the San Marcos area the recharge zone sits close to the artesian zone, so allochthonous organic matter from recharge-zone caves and streams is considered the main energy source. In the San Antonio area, some wells yielding troglobites in southern Bexar County lie 32 km from the nearest recharge area. These wells are often within 1.5 km of a "bad water zone" with high total solids, sulfur compounds, high temperature and anaerobic conditions. The author judges that recharge-zone organic matter is unlikely to reach these deep wells (up to 609 m deep). Oil and peat deposits in overlying strata are noted, and oil is pumped locally.

**Biogeographic barriers.** Piezometric highs at the base of the aquifer may limit distribution. [[Texas Blind Salamander]] (*Typhlomolge rathbuni* and *T. robusta*) occurs in the San Marcos Pool but is absent from the San Antonio Pool.

**Hypothesized food web.** Saprophytic fungi, whose mycelia were found in well samples, are proposed to grow on fossil organic matter or on bacteria using it. Fungi feed the smallest invertebrates, which feed larger ones (amphipods, isopods, decapods, snails). These are then eaten by blind catfishes in the San Antonio area.

## Key Findings

- **Diversity:** The Edwards Aquifer holds at least 35 to 40 troglobitic species, many endemic. Most organisms studied were larger than 500 micrometers. The author states it is "either the most diverse or one of the most diverse" subterranean aquatic ecosystems known.
- **Vertebrate troglobites:** Four vertebrates occur in the artesian region: the Texas blind salamander, the [[Widemouth Blindcat]] (*Satan eurystomus*) and the [[Toothless Blindcat]] (*Trogloglanis pattersoni*). The blindcats are the top predators in the San Antonio Pool.
- **Salamanders:** Other *Eurycea* species occur in recharge-zone caves, including *E. tridentifera*, *E. troglodytes* (possibly a hybrid swarm of *E. neotenes* and *E. tridentifera*, per Sweet, 1978), *E. latitans* and *E. neotenes*.
- **Feeding and predation:** Texas blind salamanders eat nearly all available invertebrates in the San Marcos Pool. In captivity, 33 preadults and adults were held at the Southwest Texas State University Aquatic Station, and seven juveniles were kept at 20-22°C. The dytiscid beetle *Haideoporus texanus* (4-5 mm, eyeless, unpigmented) may be a predator; captive individuals fed on dead shrimp.
- **Catfish abundance:** In San Antonio wells, toothless blindcats were twice as abundant as widemouth blindcats. The author describes the toothless form as a bottom-sediment feeder and the widemouth form as opportunistic, including eating young toothless blindcats.
- **Pumping threat:** Projected pumping may exceed recharge, a risk to all the springs. The total volume of water stored in the aquifer is unknown.
- **Research status:** The author's conclusions rest on sampling of wells and springs, and the food-web model is an explicit hypothesis, not a tested result.
