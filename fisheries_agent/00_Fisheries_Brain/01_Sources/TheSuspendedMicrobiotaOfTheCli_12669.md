# The Suspended Microbiota of the Clinch River and Adjacent Waters in Relation to Radioactivity in the Summer of 1956

#aquatic-ecology #microbiota #radioactivity #plankton #algae #protozoa

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study by J.B. Lackey documented the microscopic aquatic biota of the [[Clinch River]] system and adjacent waters in the [[Oak Ridge]] area, focusing on the relationship between suspended microorganisms and radioactivity from [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]] (ORNL) operations. The survey identified 435 species of algae and protozoa across 80+ samples collected between June 15 and September 15, 1956. Key findings include: (1) microscopic organisms accumulate radioactivity at concentrations 10,000+ times greater than surrounding water; (2) the [[Clinch River]] enters the Oak Ridge area virtually plankton-free from [[Norris Dam]] but develops considerable plankton downstream due to fertilization and warming; (3) certain algal-protozoan associations thrive in highly radioactive environments (250-400 mr/hr) in seepage areas; and (4) radioactivity in both plankton and water diminishes downstream, becoming undetectable by standard methods within a short distance below Oak Ridge.

## Background & Description

The [[Clinch River]] receives radioactive wastes primarily from the ORNL settling basin effluent via [[White Oak Creek]], along with nutrient materials and mixed microorganism populations. The river enters the Oak Ridge area cold (12°C) and plankton-poor after emerging from [[Norris Dam]] at 100 feet depth. Three factors explain this initial barrenness: (1) plankton concentrates near the surface in [[Norris Lake]], so deep discharge contains minimal organisms; (2) cold temperature slows metabolism and division rates; (3) steep gradient and short residence time (few hours) from dam to [[Gallaher Bridge]].

The river warms approximately 5°C and develops rich plankton from Gallaher Bridge (mile 14) downstream to [[Watts Bar Dam]]. This contrasts sharply with the [[Columbia River]], which remains plankton-poor. The study documented 435 species across major groups: Myxophyceae (20 species), Chlorophyceae (82), Bacillarieae (31), Euglenophyceae (56), Volvocales (38), Rhizopoda (38), Mastigophora (37), and Ciliata (69). Blooms were frequent, sometimes exceeding 500 individuals/ml of single species, with combined standing crops typically much greater.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Clinch River]] system represents a fertilized freshwater environment with elevated nitrogen (trace to 5.0 ppm NO₃) and phosphorus (0.020-0.042 ppm PO₄) levels. The N:P ratio approaches 90:1, higher than the optimal 20-30:1, suggesting nitrogen may be limiting for some species. Water clarity differs markedly from the Columbia River—the Clinch frequently carries silt that "traps" radioactivity and sweeps organisms to bottom sediments as flowing water enters stilled areas.

Specific ecological associations were identified in radioactive environments. The seepage from Disposal Pit IV (250-400 mr/hr radiation) supported a characteristic community dominated by [[Chlorella pyrenoidosa]] (3,532,000/ml), [[Oscillatoria]] and [[Lyngbya]] strands (403,200/ml), [[Pleurococcus]] sp. (180,000/ml), and colorless flagellates (288,000/ml). This assemblage, common in organic waste degradation, appears resistant to both high radiation and high salt concentrations. Laboratory experiments exposing these organisms to Co⁶⁰ radiation at 80,000 r/hr for 32 minutes (total dose ~42,700 r) produced no visible immediate effects, and cultures remained viable after one year.

The ORNL settling basin contained rich growths of filamentous algae ([[Oedogonium]], [[Spirogyra]]) and diverse plankton. [[White Oak Lake]] featured vast mats of blue-green algae covering mud when water levels were low. Temperature, nutrient availability, and water residence time were primary factors controlling plankton development, with radioactivity showing no apparent restrictive effects on species diversity or abundance.

## Key Findings

**Radioactivity Accumulation by Algae:**
- Filamentous green alga [[Oedogonium]] from ORNL settling basin accumulated radioactivity at concentration factors exceeding 10,000× the surrounding water (37 × 10⁴ counts/min/gram dry weight vs. 35 counts/min/ml water on August 20, 1956)
- Older [[Oedogonium]] mat (September 2) showed 15.5 × 10⁵ counts/min/gram ash, approximately 21,000× the water concentration (74.7 counts/min/ml)
- [[Vaucheria]] mat from seepage stream accumulated 22,000 counts/min/gram dry weight, maintaining activity for 14+ days with virtually no decay
- [[Ulothrix]] from Pit IV seepage (water: 3,103 counts/min/ml) concentrated activity 45× in ash (14 × 10⁴ counts/min/gram from 2.9 mg sample)
- Ash represented only 17% of dry algal weight, indicating most radioactivity retained in non-volatile cellular components

**Rapid Uptake During Blooms:**
- [[Chlorella pyrenoidosa]] bloom developed in ORNL settling basin from near-zero to 20,000 cells/ml in 3 days (August 31-September 2, 1956)
- This represented ~14 cell divisions, but radioactivity accumulation (1.13 × 10⁵ counts/min/gram) far exceeded what division rate alone would predict
- Mechanism involves rapid water exchange across cell membranes during synthesis, with retention of carried-in nuclides
- Different bloom species showed varying radioactivity concentrations: [[Chlorella]] (1.13 × 10⁵), [[Golenkinia radiata]] (5.8 × 10³ at 8 miles downstream), mixed diatoms (3.9 × 10³ at 17 miles downstream)

**Downstream Diminution:**
- Radioactivity in both plankton and water decreased with distance from [[White Oak Creek]]
- Water showed no detectable radioactivity by standard methods within short distance below Oak Ridge
- Only traces detectable at river mouth, contrasting with [[Columbia River]] where activity persisted 362 miles below Hanford
- Silt in Clinch water may trap radioactivity; centrifuged silt from [[White Oak Embayment]] showed 2 × 10⁴ counts/min/gram while supernatant water was near background (August 31, 1956)

**Mud-Water Interface:**
- Surface mud in ORNL settling basin showed surprisingly low radioactivity: 15 × 10³ counts/min/gram
- Much lower than expected given years of receiving radioactive material and high algal accumulation
- Suggests recycling through bacterial degradation and downstream loss of soluble salts and organisms
- Biological degradation processes may prevent long-term accumulation in sediments

**Species Composition and Resistance:**
- No morphologically aberrant, malformed, or apparent mutant organisms observed despite multi-generational exposure to radiation
- Seepage area species diversity limited by high salt concentration rather than radioactivity
- Characteristic waste-treatment association ([[Oscillatoria]], [[Lyngbya]], [[Chlorella]], [[Cyclidium]], ciliates) thrived in 250-400 mr/hr environment
- 435 total species identified, comparable to [[Ohio Valley]] streams but far exceeding [[Columbia River]] (fewer species documented)

**Ecological Implications:**
- Filamentous algae like [[Oedogonium]] and [[Spirogyra]] may concentrate radioactivity sufficiently for controlled harvesting, drying, and ashing as decontamination strategy
- Plankton-rich character of Clinch below Gallaher Bridge due to: warming from 12°C to ~17°C, stilling in embayments, nutrient enrichment, seeding from [[White Oak Lake]]
- Attached algal blooms (e.g., [[Spirogyra]] mat in [[White Oak Lake]] estimated >1 ton, >100 millicuries) retain radioactivity in place; planktonic forms transport it downstream
- Winter die-off of attached algae likely increases downstream radioactivity transport when only planktonic forms and thin blue-green bottom cover remain

**Comparative Context:**
- [[Clinch River]] system much more plankton-rich than [[Columbia River]]
- Varied plankton composition (not predominantly diatoms as in Columbia)
- Smaller water volume and greater anthropogenic fertilization contribute to productivity
- Phosphorus values (0.020-0.042 ppm PO₄) and nitrate (trace to 5.0 ppm) indicate high fertility despite unfavorable N:P ratio
