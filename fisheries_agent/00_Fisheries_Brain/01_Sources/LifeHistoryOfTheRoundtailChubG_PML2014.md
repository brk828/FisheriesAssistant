# Ecology of the Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta grahami) in Fossil Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This M.S. thesis (August 1976) describes the life history of an isolated population of the [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Gila Robusta Grahami]], in the headwaters of [[Fossil Creek]], a spring-fed tributary of the [[Verde River]] in central Arizona. The study combined monthly collections (540 specimens over one year), stomach analysis of 453 adults, [[Gonadosomatic Index]] and fecundity estimates, length-frequency and [[Scale Analysis]] for age and growth, [[Condition Factor]] and [[Length-Weight Relationship]] calculations, and parasite records. The author concludes that the chub is an opportunistic omnivore, that it is in good condition in this stable habitat, and that it spawns in spring in pool-riffle areas.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The roundtail chub was long placed in several genera (e.g., *Richardsonius*, *Siphateles*, *Snyderichthys*) before being assigned to *Gila*. Three subspecies of [[Gila Robusta]] are recognized in Arizona: [[Gila Robusta Robusta]], [[Gila Robusta Seminuda]] (Virgin River system, northwestern Arizona), and *G. r. grahami*. In this thesis, "roundtail chub" refers only to *G. r. grahami*.
- **Distribution:** *G. r. grahami* is restricted to headwater areas of tributaries of the Verde, Salt, and Gila rivers, including Tonto, Fossil, and Aravaipa creeks and the Black and East Verde rivers. Barrier falls isolate it from other subspecies.
- **Ecological context of the genus:** Species of *Gila* occupy lakes and streams. [[Tui Chub]] were studied in Eagle Lake, California, and [[Utah Chub]] occur in both lentic and lotic systems of the Bonneville Basin and upper Snake River, where they are more sluggish. *G. robusta* is a fast-water species that usually occupies pool and pool-riffle habitats in Arizona rivers.
- **Prior work:** A single earlier study (Vanicek and Kramer, 1969) had described the life history of the Fossil Creek population, so this thesis extends that baseline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Fossil Creek rises at about 1,690 m, roughly 12 km west of Strawberry, Arizona. It emanates from a series of warm springs that flow year-round at 19–23 °C. Arizona Public Service built a dam roughly 500 m downstream in the early 1900s and diverted water by flume to the Irving and Childs powerplants. Below the dam the creek is intermittent, so the study was restricted to the permanent reach above it.

- The main spring supplies most of the flow, with a discharge of 0.9886 m³/s (Mason, 1910). Smaller springs contribute less, and upstream flow above the springs is runoff.
- Four pool-riffle areas were sampled. Habitats were 0.5–6.0 m deep and 7–15 m wide, with dense narrow-leaf *Potamogeton* growth for much of the study period.
- Riparian vegetation includes cottonwood, sycamore, walnut and fruit trees, grading to junipers at the desert margin.
- Air temperatures ranged widely; the minimum reached 4 °C, and freezing occurred infrequently. The spring-fed water buffers the stream against such extremes.

**Habitat use.** Roundtail chubs use all available niches, including pools, backwaters, and riffles with and without macrophytes. Large adults are common in pools, while larval fish occur along stream banks. Gila mountain suckers and speckled dace are more secretive, occurring under banks and in vegetation.

## Key Findings

### Materials and Methods
- **Collections:** 540 *G. r. grahami* were collected monthly over one year. Fish of 140–360 mm were taken with experimental gill nets (set overnight) and other methods. Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin and later preserved in 50% isopropyl alcohol.
- **Measurements:** Total lengths were taken to the nearest mm and body weights to the nearest gram. Gonads were weighed to the nearest 0.0001 g. Ovaries were preserved for fecundity work.
- **Diet:** 453 stomachs from a 12-month period were examined. Stomach contents are expressed as percent frequency of occurrence. Fish under 50 mm were analyzed with a compound microscope.
- **Age:** Length-frequency distributions were supported by [[Scale Analysis]] using a projector at 12× magnification, following Hubbs and Lagler (1964).
- **Condition and growth:** Condition factor K(TL) = W/L³ and the log-linear length-weight relationship (log W = log A + n log L) were computed with a custom program.
- **Taxonomy check:** Subspecies were verified with [[Rinne (1969)]] criteria: lateral line scale counts, anal fin ray counts, and peduncle-to-head ratios.

### Food Habits
- Stomach analysis showed that the Fossil Creek chub is an **opportunistic omnivore** with food habits similar to *G. r. robusta*.
- **Dominant adult food:** Aquatic insects were the most consumed group. [[Chironomidae]], [[Trichoptera]], [[Odonata]], and [[Ephemeroptera]] averaged 17.3, 18.1, 7.7, and 13.6% frequency of occurrence, respectively. Other items included [[Diatoms]], filamentous algae, macrophytes (*Potamogeton*), and ostracods.
- **Seasonal shifts:** Spring stomachs held the most diverse prey (19 groups). Macrophytes dominated September stomachs (63%), while diatoms dominated November (68%) and December (43%). Winter diets had the fewest food types.
- **Juveniles:** Fish under 50 mm ate only filamentous algae and diatoms.
- **Empty stomachs:** Empty stomachs were very rare, with the percentage elevated in February, June, and July when few adults were collected.

### Reproduction
- **Gonadal cycle:** Gonadosomatic indices rose in spring 1975 to peak values in both sexes. They dropped from May, then rose again from January through March 1976, in step with increasing ova diameters (0.5 mm to 1.5 mm).
- **Fecundity:** In a least-squares regression of total eggs against weight for 30 females, the relationship was not strictly linear. A 321 g female held 36,959 mature ova. Females of 100–260 mm carried 1,000–4,300 maturing eggs of 1.2–2.1 mm diameter. Ova were adhesive and yellow.
- **Spawning:** Spawning occurred in spring 1975 and 1976, in pool-riffle areas with sandy-rocky substrate. Groups of 20–25 fish were seen, mostly males (8–10), which escorted females. Males released milt while in close contact with females. Egg development was assumed to occur in the sediments, but incubation times were not established.
- **Secondary sexual traits:** Males developed breeding coloration and head tubercles. Females showed an enlarged genital papilla and minor head tuberculation.
- **Maturity:** Chubs reached maturity in their third year.
- **Post-emergence:** Fry use shallow stream-bank water and backwaters, feeding on diatoms and filamentous algae and sheltering in macrophytes.

### Age and Growth
- **Length-frequency:** Young-of-the-year appeared in May but were not collected until June (fry 12–28 mm). The 1975 year class appeared as the first peak from June 1975 to January 1976, reaching 70–110 mm.
- **Scales:** Five year classes were identified by scale analysis, but annulus formation could not be dated. Annuli formed throughout the year, indicating extended growth periods that made aging difficult. Fish under 36 mm had no scales.
- **Mean lengths by age class:** 0 = 68 mm, 1 = 127 mm, 2 = 174 mm, 3 = 217 mm, 5 = 321 mm (one fish).
- **Scale-length:** Scale size increased with fish length (r = 0.9180). Annuli spacing decreased with age, indicating slower growth in older fish.
- **Annuli triggers:** The author suggests flooding and spawning as possible causes of annulus formation.

### Condition and Length-Weight
- **Length-weight:** n = 2.739, indicating nearly isometric growth, with length increasing slightly faster than weight. This matches *G. r. robusta* (n = 2.860, Vanicek and Kramer, 1969).
- **Condition factor:** Population K ranged from 0.9703 to 1.6728, indicating healthy fish. K rose with size until chubs reached 150–180 mm, then declined, probably because of caudal peduncle elongation or a marked decrease in length growth relative to weight gain.

### Predators and Parasites
- **Nematodes:** The most abundant parasite was a nematode in the digestive tract, found in the stomach contents or mucous lining but never embedded in the wall. Frequency was 43% (January), 50% (February), 40% (March), 65% (April), and 55% (May).
- **Metacercariae:** A rare black grub, the metacercaria of a trematode, was found in the epidermis of under 1% of fish.
- **Absent parasites:** No *Lernaea* were found, despite heavy infestation of other *G. robusta* in the Verde mainstem.
- **Predation:** Predation was not observed directly, but piscivorous birds (great blue heron, kingfisher, Mexican black hawk) and humans indicate that limited predation occurs.

### Conclusions
- The Fossil Creek roundtail chub feeds opportunistically on whatever prey is available in a stable spring-fed habitat.
- It is healthy, has near-isometric growth, spawns in spring in pool-riffle areas, and matures in its third year.
- Parasites appear to have little influence on the population, and predation is minor.
