# Role of Tributary Streams in the Recovery of Endangered Big River Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, transmitted on 2 April 2001 by Harold M. Tyus of the [[Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences]] to the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], evaluates how tributary streams contribute to the recovery of four endangered "Big River" fishes: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. It is a review and synthesis of existing information, not new field sampling. Tributaries contribute **directly** by providing occupied habitat, and **indirectly** by supplying flow, sediment, and solutes that shape mainstream habitat. The authors deliberately assign no relative weight to the two types of contribution, leaving that to the Recovery Program.

The Yampa River, including the [[Little Snake River]], is identified as uniquely important: it is largely unregulated and supports three of the four listed species. The report also highlights barriers, nonnative fishes, [[Flow Regulation]] by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], selenium and metals, and sediment management as the main obstacles to recovery.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River basin (about 98,000 mi²) has been reshaped by more than 50 dams and major diversions by the 1960s. Impoundments altered flood timing and magnitude, reduced sediment loads (in some reaches by 90%), and introduced cold-water releases. Nonnative fishes now dominate most native fish habitat. The four species are federally listed as endangered, and recovery goals require natural recruitment in the wild.

The study's goals were to:
1. Determine each tributary's direct support of life history needs.
2. Evaluate potential to support expanded populations.
3. Determine indirect abiotic contributions (water, sediment, other factors).
4. Identify obstacles preventing tributaries from reaching their potential.
5. Recommend research and management actions.

Methods combined published and unpublished reports, USFWS capture records (1978–1997), USGS flow and sediment data, and EPA hazardous-materials inventories. The basin was divided into six geographic reaches, and tributaries were ranked on an ordinal (high/medium/low) scale. Habitat volume was estimated from topographic maps using Manning's equation with a roughness coefficient of 0.035. Bonytail could not be evaluated adequately due to insufficient data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history and flow linkage.** Flow is the main cue for most life history events. Adult [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] overwinter in mainstem reaches, migrate during peak runoff (usually May) to spawning sites in tributary mouths and bars, and spawn at 22–25°C after peak runoff. Larvae drift to alluvial nursery backwaters, which are created by declining post-runoff flows. [[Razorback Sucker]] stage in off-channel habitat and spawn over gravel and cobble, and their young depend on flooded bottomlands. The [[Humpback Chub]] is relatively sedentary and spawns shortly after peak runoff (mid-June to late July).

**Hydrology.** Most water originates as snowmelt above 10,000 ft. Smaller tributaries in arid basins produce storm-driven flows that supply sediment and nutrients during base flow. Key hydrological facts:
- Flaming Gorge Reservoir (closed 1962) can store roughly three median-flow years; it attenuated peaks, elevated base flows, and reduced sediment transport.
- The Yampa River supplies about 49.8% of the Green River's median annual flow at its mouth (Table 7) and virtually all of the peak flow observed downstream at Jensen and Green River.
- The Gunnison River supplies about 47.4% of the Colorado River's flow above the Gunnison's confluence (median) and dominates base flow in the lower Colorado.

**Sediment.** The Little Snake River supplies about 70% of the sediment in the lower Yampa, despite contributing about 27% of its flow. The White River contributes sediment similar to the Yampa, and Taylor Draw Dam and Kenney Reservoir now trap much of it.

**Water quality.** Concerns include selenium (linked to razorback sucker reproductive failure), heavy metals released from seasonally anoxic reservoirs in Utah, suspended sediment, and oil spills. Temperature was excluded from the detailed analysis because it would require a large modeling effort.

**Nonnative fishes.** The report describes three nonnative assemblages: small cyprinids that prey on backwater larvae, centrarchids (largemouth bass, crappies, sunfish) in deeper pools, and larger riverine species such as channel catfish, carp, walleye, and northern pike. Nonnatives comprise over 90% of fishes in backwaters and flooded wetlands in some reaches.

## Key Findings

**Direct contributions by tributary (Table 15 summary):**
- **[[Yampa River]]**: The only tributary in Category 5, meaning it can complete the full life cycle of at least one species. It supports adult and spawning Colorado pikeminnow (435 captures in the lower 138 miles, 1981–97) and humpback chub (172 captures in the lower 44 miles), and razorback sucker (31 captures in the lower 37 miles). It is designated critical habitat for all four species from Craig to the mouth.
- **[[Gunnison River]]**: Category 4. Supports Colorado pikeminnow adults and spawning, with 127 captures from RM 36.4 to the mouth (1978–97), of which 115 came below Redlands Diversion. Razorback sucker stocking is underway.
- **[[White River]]**: Category 2. Adult Colorado pikeminnow are heavily used. Taylor Draw Dam (1984) blocks about 32% (about 50 mi) of historic range. Kenney Reservoir is filling with sediment, with about 4,400 AF accumulated by 1999 (about 34% of storage).
- **[[Little Snake River]]**: Category 3. Supports humpback chub and Colorado pikeminnow.
- **[[Duchesne River]]**: Category 3. Used by both Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker in its lower reaches; 41 razorback suckers captured by the Recovery Program in the lower 2.5 miles.
- **[[Price River]]** and **[[San Rafael River]]**: Adult Colorado pikeminnow (Price) and larval razorback sucker aggregating at the San Rafael mouth (73 larvae and 2 adults captured by Chart et al. 1999).
- **[[Dolores River]]**: Category 2 but with degraded water quality and flow depletion; 70–80% of fish captured were nonnative.
- **[[Plateau Creek]], [[Dirty Devil River]], [[Escalante River]]**: Category 1, with no significant present use.

**Indirect contributions:**
- The Green River below the Yampa carries about one-third of its sediment from the Little Snake and Yampa sub-basins, with the White River supplying a similar share.
- Tributary storm flows and sediment deliveries in small arid basins are important for nursery habitat but are poorly quantified.
- The Dirty Devil and Escalante rivers are of negligible importance because they enter Lake Powell.

**Obstacles to recovery:**
- Barriers: Taylor Draw Dam, Redlands Diversion (fish ladder operating), Hartland Dam, Grand Valley Diversion, Price-Stubb Dam, Government Highline Dam.
- Nonnative fishes: northern pike in the Yampa; centrarchids and cyprinids in tributary shorelines; Kenney Reservoir as a source of nonnatives to the White River.
- Water quality: selenium in Ashley Creek and Stewart Lake; metals from anoxic Utah reservoirs (25 of 26 Green River basin lakes listed for dissolved oxygen).
- Flow depletion: Duchesne River depletions of about 676 million m³ annually (USFWS 1998 biological opinion); Price River flows reduced by about 50%.

**Recommendations (abridged):**
1. Expand efforts to circumvent or eliminate barriers, especially in high-priority tributaries.
2. Reevaluate fish access to upstream areas and develop a monitoring system for range expansion.
3. Develop plans to remove nonnative fishes from tributaries and control their movement into mainstem rivers.
4. Determine environmental attributes of tributary mouths used by larval razorback sucker.
5. Identify primary sediment sources for Colorado pikeminnow nursery habitat.
6. Study metal release from Utah reservoirs with seasonal dissolved oxygen depletion.
7. Participate actively in TMDL development for suspended sediment.
8. Develop management plans for tributaries according to their importance to recovery.

**Overall conclusion:** The Yampa River is the most important tributary for recovery at the system scale, and no other tributary offers comparable assets. Because the Yampa offsets much of Flaming Gorge's effect on the Green River, its water development should be approached with caution. Reintroduction is the most promising route to increasing population numbers in the Yampa, the upper Colorado (tributary), and the upper Green, but it must be paired with barrier removal and nonnative control.
