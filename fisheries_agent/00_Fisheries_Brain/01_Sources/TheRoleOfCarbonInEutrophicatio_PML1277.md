# The Role of Carbon in Eutrophication

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1970 journal article, authored by Darrell L. King (Associate Professor of Civil Engineering, University of Missouri-Columbia), argues that carbon availability, rather than only nitrogen and phosphorus, is a major and under-recognized limit on algal photosynthesis and therefore on [[Eutrophication]] in natural waters. The author contends that aquatic plants face a carbon supply problem that terrestrial agricultural models ignore, because atmospheric [[Carbon Dioxide]] exchanges with water slowly. The main carbon reserve for aquatic algae is the [[Carbonate Alkalinity]] system, which algae draw down during the day and which is replenished by night respiration. The paper concludes that eutrophication is ultimately a carbon-accumulation phenomenon and that the carbonate-bicarbonate buffer controls which algal types dominate.

## Background & Description

The article reviews the definition of eutrophication as increased plant production in lakes linked to nutrient enrichment. Authors differ on whether nitrogen and phosphorus or organic matter drive the process, but all agree that human activity in a lake basin accelerates it. A lake is generally considered eutrophic when it supports summer [[Blue-Green Algae]] blooms and shows [[Hypolimnion]] oxygen depletion.

Historical work from 1910 to 1938, including studies by Birge and Juday, Burr, Emerson and Green, and Warburg, indicated that free carbon dioxide limits algal photosynthesis in nutrient-enriched laboratory waters. The author argues that the role of carbon has been overlooked in favor of nutrient studies.

The central chemistry involves the carbonic acid equilibria. Inorganic carbon exists as free carbon dioxide, carbonic acid, bicarbonate ion, and carbonate ion, with the distribution set by pH. The first and second dissociations of carbonic acid occur at approximately pH 6.3 and pH 10.2 at 25°C. Atmospheric carbon dioxide is about 400 µg/l, and equilibrium with air would give about 0.7 mg/l (about 16 µmoles) of free carbon dioxide per liter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Carbon supply in water versus land.** On land, wind keeps carbon dioxide concentrations near constant. Aquatic plants lie beneath a foot or more of water and have little access to atmospheric carbon dioxide except under extreme wind. Exchange between water and air is slow.

**Diel carbon cycling.** During the day, algae remove carbon dioxide and produce oxygen, borrowing carbon from bicarbonate alkalinity. Night respiration repays this "loan" by returning carbon dioxide to the system. Respiratory oxygen demand absorbs most photosynthetic oxygen, so dissolved oxygen often rises little.

**Routes of uptake.** Ruttner considered two routes: direct uptake of bicarbonate ion, or reliance on free carbon dioxide released by bicarbonate dissociation. The author concludes that enriched-water algae most likely depend on free carbon dioxide.

**Noncarbonate buffers.** Ammonia uptake by algae releases hydrogen ions that shift the equilibrium toward more carbon dioxide release. Humates, which are derived from bacterial breakdown of organic matter, also act as noncarbonate buffers and can supply extra free carbon dioxide.

**Algal succession.** Carbon dioxide depletion drives succession. As pH rises, dominance shifts from flagellated green algae (for example, Chlamydomonas) to Actinastrum, Scenedesmus, Chlorella, and finally blue-green algae, which tolerate the lowest carbon dioxide concentrations. Dominance changes after a high-wind mixing event in a lagoon may also involve inhibitory substances released from the anaerobic layer.

**Acid and humic waters.** Humic acids in highly colored dystrophic lakes can destroy bicarbonate alkalinity, leaving little carbon for algae. Acid strip-mine lakes have low productivity for a similar reason: no bicarbonate reserve exists.

## Key Findings

- **Carbon limitation is widespread.** Multiple studies cited (Shamieh, Forester, Wright and Mills, Welch) show carbon limiting photosynthesis at various carbon dioxide levels.
- **Threshold concentrations vary by algal type.** Reported carbon-limitation thresholds include about 2.5 µmoles/l for a mixed blue-green community (Shamieh), about 7.5 µmoles/l for Chlorella pyrenoidosa (Emerson and Green), about 10 µmoles/l for mixed green algae (Oswald et al.), and about 30 µmoles/l for Chlamydomonas sp. (Forester).
- **Blue-greens tolerate low carbon dioxide.** Blue-green algae continue photosynthesis at lower carbon dioxide concentrations than other algae, so less nitrogen and phosphorus is needed for blue-green dominance in low-alkalinity lakes.
- **Nutrient limitation may be misdiagnosed.** Many suspected phosphorus limits may actually be carbon limits. Gerloff found phosphorus limitation in fewer than 13 percent of 189 lake-water samples, and nitrogen limitation was not detected in any of them.
- **Lime treatment can worsen eutrophication.** Lime precipitation of phosphorus raises carbonate alkalinity and carbon availability. The author recommends two-stage recarbonation with intermediate flocculation and settling to yield low-alkalinity effluent.
- **Alkalinity sets productivity.** Carbonate-bicarbonate alkalinity is the primary buffer and the main reserve carbon source, which makes it a central governing factor for algal activity.
- **Elemental ratios.** Algae require far more carbon than nitrogen or phosphorus. Blue-green algae showed a carbon:nitrogen:phosphorus atomic ratio near 106:16:1 in cited data.
- **Management implication.** Controlling carbon dioxide availability is required, alongside adequate nutrients, to maximize algal productivity. The author argues that terrestrial agricultural theories cannot be applied to aquatic systems unchanged.

The paper's principal thesis is that eutrophication is ultimately a carbon-accumulation phenomenon, governed by the carbonate-bicarbonate buffer system rather than nutrients alone.
