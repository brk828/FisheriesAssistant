# At the Crossroads: Status of California's Endangered and Rare Fish and Wildlife (1980)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species #california-wildlife

## Executive Summary

This is the fifth biennial report of the California Department of Fish and Game on the status of state-listed endangered and rare fish and wildlife, prepared under the California Species Preservation Act. It covers 63 forms of fish and wildlife (the list was 43 in 1971), describes their current status and summarizes conservation actions. The report's central argument is that endangered species survival depends on habitat protection, and that protection on private land is difficult and dependent on acquisition funding that is becoming costlier as wildland prices escalate. The document is a general status compendium rather than a single-study research paper, and most species accounts are brief summaries drawn from Department surveys and cited literature.

## Background & Description

- **Legal framework:** California was the first U.S. state to prohibit the importation, taking, possession and sale of endangered and rare species (Endangered Species Act of 1970, Assemblymen Warren and Sieroty). The California Species Preservation Act (1970, Assemblyman Karabian) mandated biennial reporting.
- **Federal context:** The federal [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 extended protection to invertebrates and plants and defined "endangered" and "threatened" species. A cooperative agreement signed in June 1976 made California eligible for federal grant-in-aid funds at a 2/3 federal to 1/3 state match.
- **Funding and advisory bodies:** The General Fund reimbursed the Department for nongame programs, rising from $1 million (1974-75) to $9.? million budgeted for 1981-82 (figure as garbled in the source). A seven-member Citizens Nongame Advisory Committee was appointed in 1975.
- **Species list changes (October 1980):** Ten species were added to the endangered list, five to the rare list, the Modoc Sucker and Fresno Kangaroo Rat were reclassified from rare to endangered, and the Thicktail Chub was deleted as probably extinct.
- **Organization:** Accounts are arranged by phylogenetic order: gastropods, crustaceans, fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals, followed by extinct and extirpated species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report repeatedly identifies habitat loss as the primary threat. Recurring stressors across taxa include:

- **Water diversion and impoundment:** Dams such as Shasta Dam (blocking salmon migration that once fed [[Bull Trout]]) and McCloud Dam (diverting flow to the Pit River drainage in 1965) altered river habitat.
- **Exotic species:** Introduced predators, competitors and hybridizing species, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], tilapia, mollies, mosquitofish, exotic crayfish, and Sacramento Sucker hybridization with the Modoc Sucker.
- **Agricultural and urban development:** Conversion of [[Coachella Valley]] dunes and San Joaquin Valley alkali sinks reduced habitat for [[Coachella Fringe-toed Lizard]] and [[San Joaquin Kit Fox]].
- **Pollutants:** DDT contamination drove the decline of the [[American Peregrine Falcon]] and affected the [[California Brown Pelican]].
- **Groundwater pumping and flow reduction:** Threats to the [[Desert Pupfish]] at San Felipe Creek and to [[Owens Pupfish]] habitats.

The report also notes the broader ecosystem linkage: preserving the [[Salt Marsh Harvest Mouse]] habitat in San Francisco Bay tidal marshes would protect a wide diversity of dependent wildlife.

## Key Findings

**Policy and management conclusions**
- "In short, endangered species preservation is habitat preservation."
- Private-land habitat protection remains the main difficulty; acquisition by state and federal agencies has been the most successful method, but rising land prices reduce its effectiveness.
- Local governments should be encouraged or required to protect habitat on private land, or state appropriations should increase substantially.
- Public surveys and citizen reports are needed for species not on the list that may be in decline.

**Notable species accounts (status and actions)**
- **[[Trinity Bristle Snail]] (Monadenia setosa), State Rare:** known from only four locations in Trinity County; vulnerable to shell collectors. Interim management plan by the U.S. Forest Service.
- **[[California Freshwater Shrimp]] (Syncaris pacifica), State Endangered:** endemic to Marin, Napa and Sonoma counties; occurred in at least 10 streams as recently as 1964, extirpated from about five by 1975.
- **[[Shasta Crayfish]] (Pacifastacus fortis), State Rare:** restricted to Fall River, Hat Creek and the Pit River section connecting them. Crayfish bait harvest was prohibited in December 1980.
- **[[Bull Trout]] (Salvelinus confluentus), State Endangered:** native to the McCloud River only in California; approaching extirpation, with no confirmed report since 1975. Take and possession made illegal.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon macularius), State Endangered:** remaining natural habitat estimated at about 5,000 fish in lower San Felipe Creek and San Sebastian Marsh; several refugia established since 1970.
- **[[Owens Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon radiosus), Endangered:** confined to small areas of Fish Slough and near Big Pine; Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary established in 1968.
- **[[Lost River Sucker]], [[Modoc Sucker]], [[Shortnose Sucker]] and [[Humpback Sucker]]:** all state endangered; management plans in preparation, with threats from habitat alteration, hybridization and impoundment.
- **[[California Condor]]:** population estimated at fewer than 30 birds; Condor Advisory Committee established and cooperative agreement signed in 1979.
- **[[Least Bell's Vireo]]:** California population possibly under 200 pairs; cowbird removal program initiated.
- **[[Morro Bay Kangaroo Rat]]:** range fell from 1,243 ha (1957) to under 130 ha (1978).
- **[[Fresno Kangaroo Rat]]:** range fell from about 6,070 ha (15,000 acres) in 1975 to under 2,023 ha (5,000 acres) in 1980.
- **[[San Joaquin Kit Fox]]:** habitat loss has largely eliminated it from valley floor areas of Kings, Kern and Tulare counties; night hunting prohibited.

**Species list and status summary**
- Total forms on the list: 63 (up from 43 in 1971), including fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.
- Ten species added to the endangered list in October 1980, including California Freshwater Shrimp, Bull Trout, Desert Pupfish and Great Gray Owl.
- Several taxa are listed as extinct or extirpated (e.g., Xerces Blue, Gray Wolf subspecies, Thicktail Chub).

**Recent population data cited**
- California Least Tern: statewide breeding estimate of 800 pairs in 1979 and 600 pairs in 1980, with about 50 percent nesting in San Diego County.
- California Brown Pelican: 980 young from 1,218 nesting attempts on Anacapa Island in 1979; 1980 productivity remained below normal for the species.
- Bald Eagle: 52 known breeding territories in 1980; 43 active, 33 producing 58 fledglings; 855 wintering birds counted statewide in January 1979.
- Inyo Brown Towhee: estimated population of 72 to 138 individuals.
- Island Fox: no population totals given, but threats from feral cats and goats are documented.

**Recovery and future management priorities**
- Continued habitat acquisition, ecological reserves and cooperative management agreements with federal agencies and private owners.
- Recovery plans under federal and state programs for condor, bald eagle, peregrine, clapper rails, least tern and kit fox.
- Captive propagation and reintroduction for [[California Condor]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[California Bighorn Sheep]].
- Removal of exotic species, including African clawed frog, feral goats and cats, and Black Rats on San Miguel Island.
