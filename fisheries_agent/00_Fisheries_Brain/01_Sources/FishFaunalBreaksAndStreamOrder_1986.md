## Executive Summary

This study by [[William J. Matthews]] (1986) tests whether **[[Stream Order]]** (Horton-Strahler classification) serves as a meaningful biological unit for organizing longitudinal fish community structure in North American streams. Analyzing presence-absence and abundance data across **23 streams** in the eastern and central United States, the author found that distinct qualitative faunal "breaks" occurred in only **8 of 23 streams**, and quantitative breaks occurred in **11 of 14 streams** with abundance data. Critically, the location of these breaks did **not correspond to transitions between stream orders** in either dataset. The results contradict earlier claims (e.g., [[Kuehne 1962]], [[Lotrich 1973]]) that stream order represents a discrete biological unit for fish assemblages, instead supporting a model where local **microhabitat conditions**, not stream order per se, structure fish distributions.

## Background & Description

- **Central question**: Do fish faunal assemblages change abruptly at consistent longitudinal points in streams (i.e., "zonation"), and do these breaks align with stream order transitions?
- **Theoretical context**:
  - [[Balon & Stewart 1983]] proposed fish distribution occurs in overlapping longitudinal zones ("taxocenes").
  - [[Hawkes 1975]] summarized literature suggesting sharp zonal borders are typical worldwide.
  - Contrasting North American evidence (e.g., [[Sheldon 1968]], [[Harrel et al. 1967]], [[Whiteside & McNatt 1972]]) generally shows **gradual species addition** downstream rather than abrupt replacement.
  - [[Lotrich 1973]] postulated stream order represents a discrete biological unit for fish, with species addition proceeding in discrete stream-order steps.
- **Alternative model** proposed by Matthews: fish faunal breaks may instead track a **physicochemical harshness gradient** — e.g., transitions from intermittent, harsh headwater conditions to permanently watered, more benign downstream reaches — rather than formal stream order boundaries.

### Methods
- Data drawn from **11 published quantitative studies** + Matthews' own unpublished collections from 3 systems: **[[Roanoke River]] (VA)**, **[[Brier Creek]] (OK)**, and **[[Kiamichi River]] (OK)**.
- An additional **9 streams** provided only qualitative (presence-absence) data, for a total of 23 streams (Table 1).
- **Similarity indices**:
  - **[[Morisita's Index]]** (Iλ) — used for quantitative (abundance) data; relatively insensitive to sample size differences.
  - **[[Sorensen's Index]]** (QS) — used for qualitative (presence-absence) data.
  - Both indices range 0 (no similarity) to ~1.0 (identical composition); values **<0.5 arbitrarily defined as a "faunal break."**
- Comparisons made between **adjacent collecting stations**, moving from headwaters to lowermost mainstream station, across all 23 streams.
- Sampling primarily via **seining**, supplemented by electrofishing/gill netting depending on original study.
- Original raw collection data provided for three systems studied directly by the author:
  - **[[Roanoke River]]**: 6 stations, April–October 1978, 4.6 m seines, 29 species recorded (Table 2).
  - **[[Brier Creek]]**: 5 stations, June–July 1981, 21 species recorded (Table 3); includes sites from [[Smith & Powell 1971]].
  - **[[Kiamichi River]]**: 7 stations, July 1981, 30 species recorded (Table 4).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Streams surveyed ranged from **high-gradient upland creeks** to **low-gradient prairie streams**, spanning stream orders 1 through 6 (Table 1).
- **Brier Creek** (Marshall Co., OK) exemplifies a harsh-to-benign gradient: intermittent pasture-runoff headwaters (drying to isolated pools in summer) transitioning to a spring-fed, continuously flowing midreach, then a lower reach with deep pools and Lake Texoma-influenced fish fauna.
- **Kiamichi River** (OK) transitions from high-gradient Ouachita Mountain riffle-pool habitat over rubble/shale bedrock upstream, to low-gradient muddy-bottom conditions in the valley floor near Antlers — this physical transition partially corresponds with one of two detected faunal breaks.
- Several streams showed confounding factors:
  - **[[Burton & Odum 1945]]** streams (Sinking, Craig, Johns, Spruce Run Creeks, VA) showed the most pronounced qualitative breaks, but these are suspected to be **sampling artifacts** (inadequate techniques, per R.E. Jenkins' review) rather than true ecological breaks.
  - **East River (WV-VA)** and **Paddy's Run (OH)** faunal breaks are associated with **point-source pollution** influence.
- No significant relationship was found between **inter-site geographic distance** and similarity index values, ruling out sampling-distance artifacts as an explanation for detected breaks.
- The physicochemical harshness gradient model (harsh intermittent headwaters vs. benign permanent flow) is proposed as ecologically more relevant than discrete stream-order categories, consistent with prior findings on [[Headwater Fish|headwater vs. mainstream fish tolerance]] (Matthews & Styron 1981).

## Key Findings

1. **Qualitative (presence-absence) data**: No faunal breaks (QS ≥ 0.5 throughout) in **15 of 23 streams**; distinct breaks (QS < 0.5) found in only **8 streams**.
   - 4 of these 8 breaks occur in the Burton & Odum (1945) Virginia streams, likely due to inadequate historical sampling methods rather than true biological zonation.
   - The remaining breaks are associated with pollution (East River, Paddy's Run) or natural physiographic transitions (Dry Fork Whitewater River, Kiamichi River).

2. **Quantitative (abundance) data**: Faunal breaks (Morisita's Iλ < 0.5) found in **11 of 14 streams**; only Big Creek (AR), Roanoke River (VA), and Big Sandy Creek (TX) showed no breaks.
   - Breaks were roughly evenly distributed longitudinally: 7 in upper third, 9 in middle third, 8 in lower third of streams.

3. **No relationship to stream order**:
   - Qualitative breaks in East River, Whitewater River, and Paddy's Run did **not** correspond to stream order transitions; only 1 of 2 breaks in the Kiamichi River aligned with an order change.
   - Quantitative breaks occurred at **22%** of station-pairs where stream order changed vs. **24%** of station-pairs within the same stream order — statistically indistinguishable.
   - Mean Iλ for station-pairs differing in stream order (0.70) vs. same stream order (0.68) — not significantly different (ANOVA, F₁ = 0.039).
   - Mean Iλ for upstream vs. downstream watershed halves (0.69 vs. 0.65) also not significantly different (ANOVA, F₁ = 0.58).

4. **No effect of inter-site distance**: No significant correlation between distance between adjacent sampling sites and Iλ similarity values across 8 tested streams, ruling out a distance-driven artifact explanation for faunal breaks.

5. **Longitudinal position trend**: Faunal breaks trended slightly toward middle/lower reaches rather than headwaters, though not statistically significant (χ² = 3.08, df = 2).

6. **Conclusion**: Local fish faunas differ **as much within as between adjacent stream orders**, refuting the hypothesis (Kuehne 1962; Lotrich 1973) that [[Stream Order]] alone is a strong, general organizer of fish community structure in small-to-medium streams of the eastern/central US. Findings align with [[Evans & Noble 1979]]'s single-stream Texas study reaching the same conclusion.

7. **Practical implication**: Stream order remains useful as a general predictive tool (correlating with stream size, gradient, habitat complexity) but should **not be assumed a priori** to determine fish assemblage similarity between two locations; local microhabitat structure is the more likely proximate driver of fish distribution (cf. [[Gorman & Karr 1978]]).
