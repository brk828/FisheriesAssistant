# Macroinvertebrate Assemblage Change in Doe Run Creek, Kentucky: 1960 and 1990

#macroinvertebrates #benthos #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study re-examines the benthic macroinvertebrate assemblage of [[Doe Run Creek]], a spring-fed karst stream in Meade County, Kentucky, by repeating the 1960 sampling design of [[Wendell Minckley]] (1963). It compares 1960 and 1990 data, and also 1989 and 1990 data, to place the 30-year change in context. The authors report that total invertebrate density and taxonomic richness did not change significantly between 1960 and 1990. Differences fell within the range seen between years or seasons. Physical and chemical variables were largely unchanged, except that dissolved iron rose and light reaching the stream surface fell, most likely because riparian cover increased. The authors conclude that the spring-fed system is fairly stable at the system level, although individual taxon-habitat associations changed.

## Background & Description

Long-term comparisons of stream macroinvertebrate assemblages are rare. Few studies have quantitative early data sets from specific stream communities to compare against (Ward 1975; Ladle 1990). The authors used Minckley's (1963) quantitative data set to address three questions:

- How did the distribution, taxonomic richness and abundance of benthic macroinvertebrates change between 1960 and 1990?
- How much did these assemblages vary seasonally and annually (1989 vs. 1990)?
- Did physical and chemical variables differ between the two studies?

**Study site.** Doe Run Creek is a 2nd-order spring-fed stream at approximately 37°56'N, 86°07'W. It flows north-northeast for 15.5 km, receives input from several additional springs, and empties into the [[Ohio River]]. The average gradient is 0.005%, and the watershed has typical [[Karst Topography]]. The headwaters (upper 5.6 km) averaged 10 m wide in 1990. In July 1961 the downstream section (km 5.6–12.2) was impounded to create the 147-ha [[Doe Valley Lake]] (mean depth 8.8 m). The lowest section (km 12.2–15.5) is now a backwater of the Ohio River. Upstream of the reservoir, the stream runs through riparian forest of sycamore (*Platanus occidentalis*), elm (*Ulmus* spp.) and maples (*Acer* spp.).

**Stations.** Minckley sampled five stations (I–V). The current study re-sampled three of them (I, II and III), all within the first 5.5 km, because the other two were lost to the 1961 impoundment. Station I is at the main spring source. Station II is at km 3.3, and station III at km 5. Station I has agricultural land on the western bank. Stations II and III are in heavily forested areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The spring-fed nature of the system gives it a stable annual water-temperature cycle, with marked seasonal variation only in downstream waters (more than 5 km from the source). Most of the water comes from springs rather than surface runoff, so the system is buffered against spates and other disturbances that alter more typical creeks (Hynes 1970). Oxygen is supplied in part by numerous "sinkhole" cavern openings typical of karst aquifers (Gunn 1986).

Land use changed between the two studies. Heavy [[Lumbering]] near the site ceased in the mid-1950s. The forest around station III was clear-cut in 1954 or 1955. Much of the land around station I was logged in the late 1940s and farmed until the early 1970s. Farming has since declined, and a substantial understory has developed. Together these changes increased riparian shading relative to 1960.

Macrophyte beds (habitats of *Fissidens*, *Nasturtium*, *Myriophyllum* and *Myosotis*) were key habitats. Invertebrate abundance was correlated with macrophyte biomass. The authors note that decreased light at station I has nearly eliminated the once-dense macroalga *Nitella capillata*, while the low-light species *Batrachospermum moniliforme* continues to thrive.

## Key Findings

### Methods in Brief

- **Physical and chemical sampling (1989–1990):** Temperature was measured biweekly from May 1989 to April 1990. Light was measured biweekly from March to August 1990 at stations I and III, using a different meter from 1960. Discharge and current velocity were measured with the cork-float method (Lagler 1952). Dissolved oxygen was measured by the [[Winkler Titration]] method. pH and dissolved iron were measured with Hach kits.
- **Macroinvertebrate sampling:** Monthly samples were taken from April 1989 over a 16-month period. Samples were collected with a Surber sampler (0.09 m², 0.5 mm mesh) or a 15-cm² Ekman grab, depending on habitat. A total of 144 samples were collected. Taxa were sorted to coarse categories (usually Order or Family), so "taxonomic richness" here means the number of groups per sample.
- **Statistics:** Three-way ANOVAs (investigator × date × habitat) were used, with the three-way interaction as the error term because there was no within-date replication. Tukey's a posteriori tests were applied. Chi-square tests compared relative taxon composition. Taxa under 5% of abundance were pooled.

### Physical and Chemical Variables

- Discharge and current velocity at station I were nearly identical between studies. Station III had consistently higher discharge in both studies because of additional spring input.
- Mean water temperature was similar between studies at both stations. Station I ranged 11.7–14.5 °C in 1990, while station III ranged 6.1–18.3 °C.
- **Light:** Light penetration to the stream surface decreased. The 1960 values ranged from 140 µmol quanta m⁻² s⁻¹ (station I) to 740 µmol quanta m⁻² s⁻¹ (station III). In 1990, light levels were similar across stations and seldom exceeded 140 µmol quanta m⁻² s⁻¹. The authors could not compare light statistically because the meters differed.
- **Oxygen saturation:** Mean saturation at station I was 71.5% (1960, SE 2.7) and 71.6% (1990, SE 7.0), so the spring source was stable. At station III, saturation fell from 97.0% (1960) to 87.3% (1990). The ANOVA found no significant investigator effect, but a significant station effect (p = 0.004).
- **pH:** Values were nearly identical to Minckley's. Station I was near neutral (7.01 in 1960, 7.05 in 1990), and station III was slightly alkaline (7.71 in 1960, 7.93 in 1990). The station effect was significant (p = 0.0001).
- **Dissolved iron:** Concentrations rose significantly, from 0.17 to 0.27 mg/L at station I and from 0.14 to 0.25 mg/L at station III (ANOVA investigator effect p = 0.0001). The increase is about 33%, but levels remain within EPA drinking-water standards (Mull et al. 1989).
- **Nutrients and bacteria:** Nitrate, nitrite and phosphate were within the ranges recorded by Minckley. Phosphate was 0.05–0.65 mg/L at station I and 0.02–0.50 mg/L at station III. Fecal coliform tests were negative at both stations on all trials.

### Invertebrate Density and Richness

- **Density, 1960 vs. 1990:** The overall difference was not significant. Mean abundance fell by 46%, from 23,504 ± 4,525/m² in 1960 to 12,568 ± 2,149/m² in 1990. The decline was driven mainly by two *Fissidens* habitats (habitats 1 and 5), as shown by the significant habitat and investigator × habitat effects. Density varied significantly among dates in both studies, with highest densities in late fall and lowest in early summer.
- **Density, 1989 vs. 1990:** Density was slightly higher in 1990 (11,042 ± 2,595/m²) than in 1989 (7,432 ± 1,069/m²), but the difference was not significant. The authors suggest the four-month window may have been too short to detect it.
- **Richness, 1960 vs. 1990:** Richness did not change. It did vary significantly by date and habitat.
- **Richness, 1989 vs. 1990:** Richness fell significantly, from 7.37 to 6.85 (p = 0.04).
- **Longitudinal trend:** Mean richness rose downstream, from 6.7 at station I to 8.0 at station II and 8.2 at station III. Abundance was usually highest at station II, followed by stations I and III. High abundances at stations I and II were attributed to their macrophyte habitats.
- **Replicate samples:** Replicated samples from unvegetated riffles (36 samples, 4 per station per date) showed significant habitat and date effects on density, and a significant habitat × date interaction. The richness data showed a habitat effect but no seasonal effect. The authors argue that the treatment effects seen in the unreplicated comparison are therefore real.

### Changes in Assemblage Composition

- Chi-square tests found significant shifts in relative taxon abundance in most habitats between 1960 and 1990. Nine habitats changed at p < 0.01, and three at p < 0.05 (Table 8).
- Between 1989 and 1990, nine habitats also showed highly significant compositional change. Only riffles at station I and *Fissidens* beds at station II were described as stable in relative frequency.
- Overall, the authors describe community structure in most habitats as highly variable, even though richness values changed little.
- Appendix 1 lists the taxa from both investigations. Many 1960 names were revised in the 1989–1990 survey, and several identifications were flagged as misidentifications or amended names. Oligochaetes and turbellarians could not be identified to species because of fixation artifacts. Appendix 2 gives relative frequencies and mean densities for each habitat.

### Conclusions

- The authors conclude that there was no detectable change in macroinvertebrate density or richness at the system level between 1960 and 1990. Observed variation fell within seasonal ranges and within differences among habitats on a single date.
- Physical and chemical stability, a consequence of the spring-fed setting, is proposed as the reason for the biological stability. The only physical variable to change was light, because of increased riparian cover.
- Changes in light may have altered the energy budget through reduced periphyton biomass or changed allochthonous inputs. Primary productivity was not measured, but the authors suggest it may have declined in some habitats in 1990.
- The authors acknowledge that the broad taxonomic levels used may hide species-level changes.
