## Executive Summary

This draft policy (April 12, 1994), jointly developed by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], establishes unified guidance for the **controlled propagation** of species listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973. It aims to (1) standardize agency practices during propagation, introduction, and monitoring phases of species recovery, (2) ensure smooth transitions between conservation program stages, and (3) promote prudent use of limited conservation funding. The policy explicitly subordinates captive propagation to in-situ recovery, stating that propagation is a supportive tool—not a substitute—for addressing the root causes of species decline. It references the NMFS's existing interim policy on [[Pacific Salmon]] artificial propagation (58 FR 17573, April 3, 1993) as a precedent framework.

## Background & Description

Not applicable — this document is a policy framework and contains no species-specific morphological, anatomical, or physical description data. Where propagation programs are implemented, the policy calls for propagated individuals to be **genetically and phenotypically comparable to wild/free-ranging populations**, including consideration of:
- Phenotypic characteristics
- Genetic similarity/genetic makeup
- Behavioral and physiological suitability for release
- Life-history and demographic traits

These criteria are to be detailed in required [[Genetics Management Plan|genetics management plans]] preceding any propagation effort.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While no specific habitat or niche data for a taxon is provided, the policy defines the **ecological contexts** in which controlled propagation is applied:

- **Refugia populations**: Temporary holding of nearly extinct species/populations until habitat threats are resolved or catastrophic events (chemical spills, storms, fires) subside.
- **Reintroduction habitat**: Historical range habitat targeted for establishing new, self-sustaining populations (explicitly excludes [[Experimental Population|experimental populations]], governed by separate ESA policy).
- **Supplementation/enhancement**: Augmenting extant wild populations to facilitate recovery.
- **Controlled environments**: Defined as human-manipulated settings (labs, hatcheries, zoos, aquaria, botanical facilities) designed to prevent unplanned escape or entry of organisms/reproductive material.

### Identified Ecological & Genetic Risks
The policy catalogs risks associated with propagation and reintroduction relevant to population ecology and genetics:
- Broodstock removal reducing wild abundance and genetic variability
- Catastrophic loss risk (disease, equipment failure, human error) in captivity
- Inbreeding/adverse genetic effects from partial gene pool propagation
- Genetic homogenization via mixed-stock transfers ([[Genetic Introgression]])
- Domestication selection reducing wild survival/reproductive fitness
- Post-release impacts: introgression, predation, competition (food/space/mates), disease transfer, and behavioral displacement
- Elevated take risk via commercial/recreational harvest when listed species co-occur with unlisted target species

## Key Findings

- **Propagation is a last-resort recovery tool**: To be employed only when other in-situ recovery measures have failed, are likely to fail, or are insufficient alone (Policy Section E.1).
- **Recovery Plan Primacy**: Controlled propagation must be grounded in specific recommendations from approved [[Recovery Plan|recovery plans]], including identification of lead agency, cooperators (e.g., [[AAZPA]], [[Center for Plant Conservation]]), costs, and duration.
- **Mandatory Risk Assessment**: Programs must evaluate ecological/genetic effects of broodstock removal and reintroduction impacts on receiving populations (invoking ESA §7, §10, and [[National Environmental Policy Act|NEPA]]).
- **Genetics Management Plan required**: Must precede propagation; addresses genetic similarity to wild populations, disposal protocols for unfit/surplus individuals, and broodstock suitability. Exempt only for pure research not intended for reintroduction (e.g., spawning success studies in fish/mussels).
- **Intercross restrictions**: Use of intercross individuals (hybrids/interspecific crosses) in propagation is restricted unless necessary to preserve unique genetic material, and requires Director/Assistant Administrator approval.
- **Multi-site redundancy**: Propagation should occur at more than one facility where feasible to mitigate catastrophic loss risk.
- **Disease and range containment**: Programs must minimize disease/parasite spread and prevent escape or introduction of captive stock outside historic range.
- **Annual Reporting Mandate**: Regional authorities must submit annual reports (due October 31) covering recovery priority number, policy compliance gaps, program description, cooperators, expenditures, and prospects/obstacles.
- **Cooperator framework**: Establishes formal mechanisms for partnering with States, Tribes, zoos, aquaria, academia, and other qualified entities, contingent on scientific merit, technical capacity, and cost-sharing willingness.
- **Transitional/grandfather clause**: Pre-existing candidate/proposed species propagation programs that become listed are granted temporary exception but must conform to this policy within 12 months of listing.
- **Superseding authority**: This policy supersedes all prior agency policy statements on controlled propagation (Section K).
