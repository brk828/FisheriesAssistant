# Comparative Sensitivity of Larval Razorback Sucker and Flannelmouth Sucker to Selenium Exposure

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (Project CAP-6 SE-NF), prepared by Daniel W. Beyers and Cris Sodergren of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University (January 2001), tests whether larval [[Razorback Sucker]] are more sensitive to [[Selenium]] than the closely related, co-occurring [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Two experiments were run: a 28-day early life-stage (ELS) food-chain and dissolved-exposure test, and a separate 96-hour renewal acute test that also included [[Fathead Minnow]] as a comparison species.

No negative effects on survival or growth were detected in the ELS test at dissolved concentrations up to 190 µg/L and dietary concentrations up to 8.24 µg/g. Median lethal concentrations (LC50s) from the acute test were similar for the two sucker species. The data do not support the hypothesis that larval razorback sucker are more sensitive than flannelmouth sucker. The authors stress that the absence of detected effects does not mean razorback sucker populations are unaffected, because other life stages may be more sensitive.

## Background & Description

Selenium is a metalloid present in geologic formations and surface waters of the Colorado River Basin, and human activity has increased its mobilization into surface waters. It is an essential micronutrient, but at elevated levels it replaces sulfur in amino acids, altering protein structure and function. Organic forms such as selenoamino acids are more bioavailable and more toxic than inorganic forms.

Razorback sucker populations are presumed to have declined in the Upper Colorado River Basin because of habitat loss, altered temperature regimes, interactions with non-native fishes, and degraded water quality. Some razorback sucker nursery habitats have selenium concentrations above the [[USEPA Selenium Criterion]] for freshwater aquatic life (5 µg/L; USEPA 1987). Elevated selenium has been measured in larval and adult razorback sucker, yet co-occurring native fishes remain relatively abundant. This paradox motivated the hypothesis that razorback sucker are more sensitive to selenium than related natives.

Flannelmouth sucker was chosen as the comparison species because it is closely related, co-occurs with razorback sucker, and shares life-history traits. The work used a three-trophic food chain (algae, [[Brachionus calyciflorus]] rotifer, and fish) cultured in selenium-contaminated water, following [[ASTM Early Life-Stage Guide]] and [[ASTM Acute Toxicity Guide]] methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dilution water:** Tap water was aerated, heated to 18 °C, and adjusted to "very hard" water approximating the Colorado River near the Colorado–Utah state line. Well water (average selenium 6.16 µg/L) was used for ELS dissolved exposures at 20 ± 1 °C.
- **Water chemistry:** Dilution-water hardness was about 400 mg/L as CaCO3 in the ELS test and 354 mg/L in acute tests; pH was about 8.0 to 8.5.
- **Food chain:** Algae (*Chlorella vulgaris*) and rotifers were cultured across five selenium levels (0, 25, 50, 100, 200 µg/L), so dietary and dissolved exposure were linked.
- **Dominant selenium form:** Selenate, the form tested, is the dominant form in natural waters of the Grand Valley, Colorado (Butler et al. 1994). It is generally considered the least toxic of the common selenium forms.
- **Nursery context:** Razorback sucker larvae are thought to arrive in nursery habitats at first feeding (about 10–12 days after hatching). Fathead minnow complete their life cycle in these habitats, which is why the authors consider them a potential surrogate.

## Key Findings

**Study design and ages**
- ELS test: 5 dissolved/dietary treatments (control, 25, 50, 100, 200 µg/L nominal) × 2 species, 4 replicate beakers, 10 larvae per beaker, 28 days.
- Razorback sucker were 41 days old (mesolarvae) at ELS start and 16 days old in the acute test. Flannelmouth sucker were 11 days old in both tests. Fathead minnow were about 3 days old in the acute test.

**Selenium bioaccumulation**
- Algae ranged from <0.183 to 5.61 µg/g dry weight; rotifers from <0.702 to 8.24 µg/g.
- Whole-body concentrations ranged from 3.33 to 12.9 µg/g in razorback sucker and from 3.43 to 10.2 µg/g in flannelmouth sucker. Flannelmouth sucker values were consistently lower, probably due to tissue dilution from their larger size.

**ELS survival and growth**
- Survival ranged from 95 to 100% in razorback sucker and 82.5 to 100% in flannelmouth sucker. No selenium effect was detected (p = 0.4028), but species differed (p < 0.0001), reflecting lower overall survival of flannelmouth sucker unrelated to selenium.
- Wet mass increased with selenium concentration (p = 0.0019) in both species. The authors interpret this as a slight positive effect, not edema, because total length did not decrease (p = 0.2665 for the concentration effect in total length).
- Razorback sucker were consistently smaller than flannelmouth sucker in mass and length (p < 0.0001), a natural size difference.

**Threshold comparison**
- Guidelines predict adverse effects above 2 µg/L in water and 3 µg/g in diet (Lemly 1993a; NIWQP 1998). These were exceeded in the two highest treatments without observed effects.
- The authors conclude the dietary threshold lies between 8.24 µg/g (highest tested) and 20.3 µg/g, the lowest observed effect concentration in [[Ogle and Knight 1989]].

**Acute toxicity (96-hour LC50s, 95% CI)**
- Razorback sucker: 35.5 mg/L (32.3–38.8)
- Flannelmouth sucker: 32.5 mg/L (28.9–35.1)
- Fathead minnow: 21.8 mg/L (18.6–25.2)
- Razorback/flannelmouth ratio of about 1.09 falls within the normal experimental range (0.5–1.5; Mayer and Ellersieck 1986). The fathead minnow ratio of about 1.6 indicates the minnow was more sensitive.
- Survival dose–response curves crossed, so relative sensitivity depends on concentration. The fathead minnow control had six of 60 deaths (about 10%), so the fathead data should be interpreted cautiously.

**Interpretation and surrogate concept**
- The authors propose fathead minnow as a possible surrogate for field evaluation. It is ubiquitous in the Upper Basin and made up about 30% of fish in fall seine collections (Miller and Snyder 2000).
- Recruitment of age-0 fathead minnow, rather than adult presence, is suggested as a more useful indicator, since selenium may impair adult reproduction.

**Other sensitive life stages**
- Young-of-year overwinter survival, where selenium may raise metabolic rate while winter limits food intake (Lemly 1993b).
- Reproductively active females, since selenium transfers from adults to eggs and may affect eggs and larvae.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Razorback and flannelmouth sucker responded similarly. Neither species was affected by dietary selenium up to 8.24 µg/g, so a dietary sensitivity ratio could not be estimated.
- Recommended future work: overwinter survival of young-of-year fish under selenium exposure; bioaccumulation in wild adults linked to movement by radio telemetry; mesocosm studies under realistic conditions; and reproductive effects, depending on results from ongoing work ([[Hamilton et al. 1999]]).
