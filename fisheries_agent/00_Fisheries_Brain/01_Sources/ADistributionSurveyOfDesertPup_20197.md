# Distribution Survey of Desert Pupfish Around the Salton Sea, California, 1991

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

A qualitative survey by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (CDFG), Inland Fisheries Division, measured the distribution of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in non-refuge habitats around the [[Salton Sea]] during April, May and June 1991. Baited [[Minnow Trap]] sets and visual surveys covered [[Irrigation Drains]], [[Shoreline Pools]], [[Salt Creek]] and [[San Felipe Creek]]. Pupfish occurred in 72% of drains, 64% of shoreline pools, and both natural tributaries. The report recommends repeated surveys, formal drain-maintenance agreements, and research on exotic-species interactions and human impacts.

## Background & Description

The desert pupfish is a member of the family Cyprinodontidae. It was historically widespread in Arizona, southeastern California and northern Mexico. In California it occurred in [[Colorado River]] backwaters and in springs, streams and seeps of the Salton Sink (Miller 1943).

Current California range is limited to:
- shoreline pools of the Salton Sea
- two natural streams
- eight artificial refugium ponds
- several irrigation drains flowing to the sea (Bolster 1990)

Habitat alteration and exotic species introductions caused its decline. The California Fish and Game Commission listed the species as endangered in 1980, and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) followed in 1986. The draft USFWS recovery plan calls for habitat protection, creation of refugia for specific genetic stocks, and periodic population monitoring.

Project funding came from USFWS Endangered Species Act Section 6 grant-in-aid monies (Project EF9OXII-1).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Wire-mesh minnow traps baited with cat food were placed in perforated sealed bags and left for about 20 hours. Traps were set in clear, slow-moving water 2 in. to 2 ft deep, preferentially near algae or vegetation. Densely vegetated or stagnant sites were not sampled. Substrates ranged from firm sand to fine silt and soft mud. Habitat data were recorded for each set, including depth, substrate, clarity and vegetation.

**Sampling coverage.**
- Of 66 drains on [[Imperial Irrigation District]] (IID) and [[Coachella Valley Water District]] (CVWD) maps, 27 of 27 possible north-end drains and 30 of 39 possible south-end drains were trapped.
- Five of the nine unsurveyed drains lacked suitable habitat; the other four had been replaced by sump pumps.
- Of 14 shoreline pools trapped, 11 were reachable only by airboat.
- The [[Alamo River]] and [[New River]] lacked favorable habitat and were not trapped.
- Beach seining in the Salton Sea proper was not performed.

**Species labels.** Sailfin and shortfin mollies were lumped as *Poecilia* sp. ("molly"). The two African cichlids, [[Mozambique Tilapia]] and [[Zill's Cichlid]], were lumped as "tilapia".

**Drain maintenance.** IID and CVWD remove vegetation periodically. CVWD maintains its north-end drains more often than IID does its south-end drains.

## Key Findings

**Overall distribution**
- Desert pupfish were found in 72% of surveyed drains overall.
- North-end drains: 24 of 27 (89%) held pupfish; some were confirmed by netting or visual identification.
- South-end drains: 17 of 30 (57%) held pupfish.
- Catches per north-end drain ranged from 0 to 155 (Avenue 78 drain). South-end catches ranged from 0 to 44 per drain.

**Shoreline pools**
- 9 of 14 pools (64%) contained pupfish. Catches ranged from 0 to 30 per pool.
- All pool catches came from trapping except one pool between Trifolium 23 and Trifolium 23N, where fish were netted.
- The authors state more sampling is needed to judge whether this distribution represents shoreline pools as a whole.

**Natural tributaries**
- Pupfish were caught at the mouth of Salt Creek. The upper creek is trapped quarterly by Dr. Allen Schoenherr of Fullerton College, so it was not surveyed.
- Visual surveys showed pupfish throughout San Felipe Creek, with many young and a few adults. Flow was intermittent, confined to shallow disjunct pools between the sea and Highway 86.
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) was the only exotic species observed in San Felipe Creek. It appeared to prefer larger, deeper pools.

**Associated fauna**
- Besides pupfish, 12 fish species and one crustacean were captured: two molly species, two tilapia species, [[Longjaw Mudsucker]], mosquitofish, [[Red Shiner]], [[Porthole Livebearer]], [[Swordtail]], [[Orangemouth Corvina]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Crayfish]].
- Unidentified organisms included tadpoles, water beetles, water scavengers, pileworms and snails.

**Factors affecting trap results**
- Trap placement is critical. Pupfish often appeared in only one of five or six traps set in apparently homogeneous habitat.
- Drains that initially yielded no pupfish produced them on later visits.
- Larger trap numbers can saturate an area and raise capture chances.
- Bait type and brand may alter efficiency; the survey used an inexpensive fish-flavored bait.
- Matsui (1981) reported breeding from late March through late September. Surveys should coincide with this period.
- Barlow (1958) documented movement changes tied to daily temperature or light, which may have affected shoreline-pool results.
- Irregular irrigation-water supply and wind-driven level changes altered drain conditions and may have disrupted bait scent trails.

**Impact of drain maintenance**
- Three drains that held pupfish and underwent partial vegetation removal still contained pupfish afterward. A portion of each drain was left undisturbed.
- The north/south difference (89% vs. 57%) may reflect more frequent CVWD maintenance, which can prevent overgrowth and stagnation.

**Recommendations**
1. Expand surveys to all potential shoreline pool habitat and other unsampled sites, repeating at least every two years.
2. Study the effects of continued drain maintenance, and develop formal agreements among USFWS, CDFG and the irrigation districts to regulate its frequency and extent.
3. Investigate other human impacts in drains, including water diversion, bait fishing, agriculture and water quality.
4. Examine interactions with introduced species. Schoenherr (1981) reported that mosquitofish reduced five *Cyprinodon* species. Matsui (1981) found that Zill's cichlids and sailfin mollies interfered behaviorally with pupfish reproduction.
5. Study pupfish tolerance of human impacts and exotic-species interactions to support management of this endangered species. Schoenherr (1988) summarized known life-history information.
