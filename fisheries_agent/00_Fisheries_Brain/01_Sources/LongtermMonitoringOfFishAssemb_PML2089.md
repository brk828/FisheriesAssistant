# Long-Term Monitoring of Fish Assemblages in the Gila River Drainage, New Mexico (1988–2005)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Gila-River-Drainage

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] by the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] and a retired [[U.S. Forest Service]] biologist, summarizes annual October–November sampling of warmwater fish assemblages at six permanent sites in the Gila River drainage and two in the San Francisco River drainage, covering 1988–2005. The stated objective was to document long-term population trends of native and nonnative fishes at sites representative of drainage lotic habitats.

Eight native and fifteen nonnative species were collected. Native fish abundance declined at most sites, most noticeably in the last six years. Nonnative abundance did not appreciably increase anywhere except the [[Middle Fork Gila River]]. [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] declined at the Gila forks sites, and neither has been collected at the Middle Fork since 1998. Only one [[Roundtail Chub]] specimen was recorded during the study.

## Background & Description

The historical native fauna of the Gila River drainage in New Mexico comprised eleven, perhaps thirteen, species. [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were not documented by specimens in New Mexico but likely entered seasonally from Arizona. [[Gila Topminnow]] was extirpated in the 1950s and repatriated in 2005. Ten native species persist, mostly at reduced abundance and range.

Taxonomy of the Gila chubs has been revised. For consistency with the state recovery plan, chubs from the forks were classified as [[Headwater Chub]] (*Gila nigra*) and those downstream of the forks as [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). The only known New Mexico population of [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) is in Turkey Creek.

Conservation status is significant. [[Gila Trout]], Gila chub, spikedace, loach minnow, and Gila topminnow are federally listed as threatened or endangered. Roundtail and headwater chub have been petitioned for federal listing, and headwater chub has been recommended for state listing as endangered. [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Sonora Sucker]] are widespread in New Mexico.

Several nonnative warmwater fishes are established, including channel catfish, flathead catfish, smallmouth bass, red shiner, fathead minnow, and western mosquitofish, with other species such as yellow bullhead and green sunfish occurring irregularly.

Eight sites were sampled. Gila sites comprised the East, Middle, and West Forks, Riverside, Middle Box, and Fisherman's Point. San Francisco sites comprised Glenwood and Tularosa (Eagle Peak Road). Sampling began in 1988 at five sites, 1989 at West Fork, and 1997 at Middle Box and Fisherman's Point. The East Fork was not sampled in 1996, and the San Francisco and mainstem sites were not sampled in 2000 due to high flows. Channel gradients were typically about 0.5%. Sites ranged from small (Tularosa) to comparatively large ([[Gila River]] at Riverside, Middle Box, and Fisherman's Point).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Discharge and precipitation strongly structured the record. At the Gila gauge near Gila, NM, the mean daily discharge averaged 224 cfs from 1988–1996 and 142 cfs from 1997–2005. Annual precipitation averaged over 21 inches before 1997 and under 18 inches afterward. On the San Francisco River, mean discharge averaged 28 cfs (1988–1996) and 14 cfs (1997–2005). Discharge was highest in 1993 in both basins.

Wildfire was a major disturbance. Between 1989 and 2004, fires burned much of the upper watershed. Ash flows and elevated sediment loads followed, especially at the West Fork, where floods in 2001–2004 were common.

Human land use included dispersed livestock grazing, recreation, mineral extraction, irrigated agriculture, and wild elk grazing. Livestock were excluded from Fisherman's Point in 1997, and vegetation increased on its banks afterward. Levees, channel dewatering, and [[Nonnative Predators]] were also identified as pressures.

Habitats were classified into mesohabitat types (e.g., pool, run, riffle, backwater, chute) with characteristic depth, velocity, and substrate. Fish were sampled with drag seines, backpack electrofishers, and dip nets, and density was expressed as fish per square meter.

## Key Findings

**Assemblage trends**
- Native fish density at most sites declined over the study. Average density from 1988–1992 was nearly twice that of 2001–2005 at all sites except West and East Forks.
- Nonnative density more than doubled between the same periods at all sites except West Fork.
- Nonnative species numerically dominated the Middle Fork, Middle Box, and Fisherman's Point sites in recent years.
- No native fish has been collected at Fisherman's Point since 2002 (or in the final two years, per the text's internal inconsistency).
- Collections totaled 23 species and 42,094 individuals, with 8 native and 15 nonnative species.

**Native and rare species**
- [[Loach Minnow]] was never abundant at forks sites and was not collected at any forks site for four years. It remained relatively abundant at Riverside and the San Francisco River, but has not been collected at Tularosa since 2002.
- [[Spikedace]] density showed significant negative trends at all Gila forks sites.
- [[Headwater Chub]] declined significantly at the East Fork, though it increased slightly at West Fork.
- [[Longfin Dace]] was the only native species with a significant positive trend, at Riverside.
- Sonora sucker and desert sucker were the most common and widely distributed natives.

**Regression findings**
- Native density was positively correlated with discharge at Middle Fork, East Fork, and Fisherman's Point.
- Nonnative density was generally negatively correlated with discharge, except at West Fork.
- Over the full 1988–2005 period, native densities declined at most sites, significantly at West Fork.

**Habitat associations**
- Native fishes were most abundant in run and riffle habitats; nonnatives were most often collected in backwaters and shore runs.
- Headwater chub occupied slower, deeper habitats with finer substrate than the suckers.
- Loach minnow occurred where substrates were larger and velocities faster; longfin dace and spikedace shared similar velocities, with longfin dace on finer substrate.

**Overall conclusion**
The authors attribute native declines to drought, nonnative fishes (especially large, long-lived predators), wildfire and ash flows, scouring floods, flood control structures, channel dewatering, and sediment. They conclude the trends "do not bode well" for native species, which are also increasingly rare across their ranges.
