# Evaluation of the Introduction of *Tilapia sparrmanii* into California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 California Department of Fish and Game Inland Fisheries Administrative Report, by Ronald J. Pelzman, evaluates whether [[Tilapia Sparrmanii]] (the Banded Tilapia) should be introduced into California. The species was proposed for aquatic weed control and for [[Mosquito and Chironomid Midge Control]]. The review concludes that the species would likely establish, spread into game fish waters, and harm [[California Game Fish]] through competition. The report recommends against introduction and recommends that possession be prohibited except for display in public aquariums.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** A member of the family [[Cichlidae]]; the smallest of the African tilapias, with a long continuous dorsal fin and two incomplete lateral lines.
- **Morphology:** Deep, strongly compressed body; back dark olive-brown, sides bright sea green, undersurface yellowish; large orange or red spots on scales, especially on the middle of the sides.
- **Size:** Attains 7 to 9 inches. One specimen 7.9 inches long weighed 5.2 ounces (Scheffer, 1960). Captive fish in California grew from about 1 inch to about 6 inches in 17 months at room temperature (Kennedy, pers. comm.).
- **Native range:** The most widely distributed African tilapia, found in streams, lakes and ponds of southern Africa from Natal to the Congo Basin.
- **Prior introductions:** Introduced to Japan for experimentation and as live bait for skipjack fishing. Under investigation by George Kennedy at the University of California, Los Angeles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and salinity tolerance**
- Most cold-tolerant tilapia; survives water temperatures as low as 45 °F and survived in outdoor artificial ponds in California, occasionally under a thin ice layer.
- Upper temperature tolerance is undocumented; tilapia generally have optimal growth at 65–80 °F and can tolerate 95 °F.
- Survived 50% seawater; fish placed in 100% seawater died within 2.5 hours (Lowe, 1959, as reported).
- Has greater salinity tolerance than most freshwater species, supporting the report's concern for the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning begins when water temperatures exceed about 61 °F; in Rhodesia the season runs November to March, and breeding can occur year-round in favourable conditions.
- Substratum spawner (not a mouthbrooder). Nests are usually on stones, though other substrates are used.
- Clutch sizes: up to 1,000 eggs per spawning (Sterba, 1963 reports as many as 1,000; the text also cites 11,000 in one passage, which appears to be a scan error). Kennedy observed at least 150 eggs in one spawning.
- Both parents build the nest, guard eggs and young, and excavate several pits; fry are moved between pits to reduce predation.
- Individuals in aquaria spawned at least six times in 17 months and reportedly at about 11 inches in length.

**Feeding and habitat**
- Largely herbivorous; consumes zooplankton and insect larvae while bottom feeding. Juveniles under 5 cm TL prefer zooplankton; 5–8 cm TL consume nearly all available food; larger fish favour bottom foods.
- Restricted to shallow, vegetated areas for food and shelter.
- Tends to overpopulate and stunt in ponds.

**Competition risks**
- Would compete directly with [[Largemouth Bass]] and bluegill for cover and for small invertebrates in vegetation, especially young game fish.
- California fisheries already contain abundant bluegill and carp (Cyprinus carpio); adding this species would increase competition with largemouth bass.
- Chinits (1957) considered it a better forage fish for largemouth bass than [[Bluegill]], but Hey (1952) judged it not superior.

**Disease:** No information on diseases or parasites was available.

## Key Findings

1. **Recommendation:** Do not introduce *T. sparrmanii* into California; list it among prohibited animals and permit possession only for display in public aquariums.
2. **Pathways of escape:** If stocked in irrigation systems for weed control or for mosquito and midge abatement, it would most likely enter game fish waters.
3. **Establishment:** Likely to survive and reproduce in many California waters, with a high risk of overpopulation and stunting.
4. **Ecological impacts:** Would control aquatic weeds and midges, but would compete directly with game fishes for cover and invertebrate food.
5. **River and Delta risk:** Could establish wild populations in larger California rivers, with high probability of establishment in the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta due to salinity tolerance.
6. **Fishery impacts:** Detrimental to game fish through direct and indirect competition, particularly in waters already dominated by bluegill and carp.
