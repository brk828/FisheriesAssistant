# Observations on the Spawning of Sacramento Hitch in a Lacustrine Environment

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #spawning-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by J. B. Kimsey documents spawning behavior of [[Sacramento Hitch]] (*Lavinia exilicauda exilicauda*) in [[Clear Lake]], California, challenging the prevailing assumption that this cyprinid species requires streams with gravel bottoms for successful reproduction. Direct observations and egg collection in May 1956-1957 confirmed that hitch can spawn successfully along lake shores and in isolated farm ponds without stream access. The findings have significant implications for fisheries management, as hitch populations cannot be controlled simply by blocking access to tributary streams.

## Background & Description

The [[Sacramento Hitch]] is a large Californian minnow native to the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Drainage Basin]] and related watersheds. The species serves as forage for warmwater game fishes during their first year but grows too large for this purpose in the second year. Prior to 1959, hitch were commonly sold as bait fish in central California, seined from wild stocks rather than raised domestically.

Unauthorized introductions into trout lakes resulted in competition severe enough to require eradication before trout fisheries could be maintained. Murphy (1948b) previously concluded from extensive biological observations at [[Clear Lake]] that hitch required streams with gravel bottoms for successful spawning, which would have simplified management by allowing control through blocking spawning access to tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Clear Lake]] is located in Lake County, California. The study focused on two primary locations: the Lakeport city park beach and the [[Clear Lake Oaks]] shoreline. A two-acre farm pond in the Schindler Creek drainage (N.W. ¼ of Section 30, T. 14 N., R. 7 W., M.D.B. & M.) was also investigated.

Shore spawning occurred in shallow water (1-5 inches depth) over gravel ranging from one-quarter to three inches in diameter. Wave action cleaned a band of gravel approximately three feet wide near shore, removing silt and debris from interstices. Water temperature during observed spawning was 64°F. The farm pond had no gravel areas, only mud bottom with heavy marginal plant growth, and received water from natural pasture runoff rather than established streams.

Evidence suggests predation by introduced species ([[Carp]], [[Bluegill]], [[Catfish]]) may significantly impact shore-spawned eggs and larvae. Schools of carp were observed feeding over spawning areas. During dry years with short or low tributary flow, eggbound hitch are regularly found in the lake long after normal stream spawning season, suggesting population segments may be obligatory stream spawners despite the presence of suitable shore spawning habitat.

## Key Findings

**Direct Spawning Observations:**
- On May 21, 1956, approximately 50 hitch were observed spawning at Lakeport city park beach in 1-5 inches of water over cleaned gravel
- Detailed observations on May 3, 1957 at [[Clear Lake Oaks]] documented spawning beginning at 7 p.m. (water temperature 64°F)
- Spawning behavior included pairs or groups forcing themselves into shallows until backs were exposed, pressing bodies together, and vigorous wriggling and splashing
- Some fish became stranded on the bank and died due to spawning excitement
- One seine haul captured 15 hitch, all with flowing eggs or milt

**Egg Collection and Rearing:**
- Three eggs were collected on May 3, 1957 using a stream-type bottom sampler in the wave backwash (depth 0.5-4 inches)
- No eggs found in water one foot deep with less wave action influence
- Eggs hatched in laboratory aquaria at 62°F in two days
- Fry were reared for 54 additional days and identified as hitch; average length 11.3 mm
- On May 9, 1957, several hundred eggs in various developmental stages were collected and successfully hatched
- Three newly hatched hitch were captured in plankton net towed near shore on May 9

**Farm Pond Population:**
- A self-sustaining hitch population existed in a two-acre farm pond with no stream access
- Population reportedly started from a [[Clear Lake]] plant in 1948
- Pond had mud bottom with no gravelly areas and no established creek connection
- A minnow dealer harvested an estimated 60,000 hitch (1.5-5.0 inches fork length) in 1956
- Operation had continued for several seasons, demonstrating sustained reproduction

**Larval Development:**
- Newly emerged fish had very small yolk amounts and undeveloped mouths
- Larvae exhibited characteristic swimming behavior: rapid upward movement with wriggling, then falling head-down to bottom
- After three days, larvae could maintain position in middle water layers and appeared to seek food

**Management Implications:**
- Hitch are not obligatory stream spawners; populations can establish and maintain themselves in lakes and ponds without tributary streams
- The two spawning types (stream vs. shore/pond) cannot be differentiated morphologically
- Control strategies based on blocking stream access are insufficient
- Appropriate precautions must be taken against introducing hitch into new waters even when tributary streams are absent
- During dry years (e.g., 1946, 1947), virtually no recruitment may occur despite available shore spawning habitat, possibly due to predation by introduced species on unguarded eggs
