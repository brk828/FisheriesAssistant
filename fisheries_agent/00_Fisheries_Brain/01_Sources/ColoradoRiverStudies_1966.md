## Executive Summary

This 1966 completion report by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] documents fish and wildlife impact assessments along the [[Lower Colorado River]] between [[Davis Dam]] and the Mexican boundary. The study evaluates three major Bureau of Reclamation/Army Corps programs: the **Lower Colorado River Front Work and Levee System** (channelization), the **Water Salvage and Phreatophyte Control Project** (removal of 140,000 acres of salt cedar and mesquite), and **Navigation Permits** for river-front development. While the report centers on avian impacts (dove and quail populations), it contains substantial data on aquatic habitat loss driven by channel straightening — specifically the elimination of oxbows, backwaters, marshes, and sloughs that constitute critical fish habitat. No individual fish species are named, but the report is foundational to understanding anthropogenic habitat alteration pressures on the Lower Colorado River fish community during the mid-1960s.

## Background & Description

The [[Bureau Of Reclamation]]'s Front Work and Levee System Project sought to straighten and confine the Colorado River channel between Davis Dam and the Mexican boundary into a narrow, deep, engineered channel. This "channelization" was substantially complete in the **Mohave** and **Palo Verde Divisions**, ongoing in the **Cibola Division**, beginning in the **Parker Division**, and still in planning for the **Topock Gorge** and **Yuma Divisions**.

A companion program, the Lower Colorado River Water Salvage and Phreatophyte Control Project, proposed clearing 140,000 acres of salt cedar (*Tamarix* spp.) and mesquite (*Prosopis* spp.) riparian vegetation to reduce evapotranspirative water loss — vegetation that, while non-native/water-consumptive, also served as critical nesting habitat for [[Mourning Dove]] and [[White-Winged Dove]].

A parallel administrative process required review of U.S. Army Corps of Engineers **Navigation Permits** for boat docks, ramps, dredging, and other water-oriented construction, providing a secondary check on habitat-altering development.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types threatened by channelization:**
- Oxbow lakes
- Backwater sloughs and marshes
- Sandbars
- Cutoff river bends isolated by channel straightening

These habitats were explicitly identified as "extremely important fish and wildlife habitat," and their loss was described as likely under channelization "if protective measures are not taken."

**Successional threat to mitigated habitat:** Even where oxbow lakes and backwaters were retained via inlet/outlet structures (a 1959 mitigation recommendation by Arizona, California, and the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]]), agencies warned that without **periodic maintenance dredging**, these cutoff water bodies would be destroyed through **biotic succession** (i.e., natural infilling/vegetative encroachment converting open water to terrestrial habitat). This is a key limnological/ecological finding: mitigation structures alone are insufficient without ongoing management.

**Topock Marsh protection:** A dike was constructed by the Bureau of Reclamation in **Topock Marsh** (part of the Mohave Division) during winter 1965–66 specifically to prevent marsh drainage that would otherwise result from downstream channelization in Topock Gorge — an early example of engineered habitat compensation.

**Mittry Lake Wildlife Area** (Yuma Division) was proposed as a mitigation site for fish and wildlife losses from channelization, though a portion of the proposed development was rejected by the Bureau of Reclamation on cost grounds despite Arizona Game and Fish Department data showing benefits outweighing costs.

**Urbanization threat to mitigation lakes:** In the Parker Division, the [[Colorado River Indian Tribe]] indicated plans for urban development of river-front areas that would include five backwater lakes constructed specifically as fish and wildlife mitigation — prompting the Department to recommend a ¼–½ mile no-development buffer zone around these mitigation waters.

**Aerial survey monitoring program:** Beginning March 1966, Arizona and California conducted joint aerial surveys along the entire study reach to quantify **boat and shore fishermen** and wildlife numbers, providing baseline data on recreational fishing pressure and use patterns. Originally planned as 72 flights, budget/logistics reduced this to 48 flights (24 per state) through January 1967, stratified across weekdays and weekends.

## Key Findings

- **Dove population loss from phreatophyte clearing (proxy for habitat-wide impact):** Clearing 140,000 acres of salt cedar/mesquite would eliminate an estimated **1,795,000 mourning and white-winged doves annually** under current habitat conditions; under optimum (fully matured) habitat conditions, the loss potential rises to **1,993,000 doves annually** (see Table I, reach-by-reach breakdown from Davis Dam to Mexican boundary).
- **Navigation permits reviewed:** 30 total applications; 29 approved with no anticipated detrimental effect on fish/wildlife. One dredging permit (10-mile low-flow channel south of Bullhead City) was denied — notably for hydropower operational reasons (interference with Davis Dam Power Plant) rather than purely ecological ones, though the Department had independently flagged the application over habitat concerns.
- **Channelization status by division (1966):** Complete — Mohave, Palo Verde; In progress — Cibola; Just beginning — Parker; Planning stage only — Topock Gorge, Yuma.
- **Mitigation infrastructure planned:** Inlet/outlet structures for oxbow lakes and backwaters in Palo Verde and Cibola Divisions, with Bureau of Reclamation construction slated for fall 1966 — an 8-year delay from the original 1959 mitigation recommendation.
- **Institutional response to controversy:** Persistent disagreement among state and federal agencies over channelization/phreatophyte impacts led Secretary of the Interior [[Stewart Udall]] to convene a 21-member **Colorado River Advisory Board** (June 1966) comprising Interior, Arizona, California, and water-user association representatives to review the Topock Gorge, Parker, and Yuma Division plans before further action.
- **Policy statement of record:** The Arizona Game and Fish Department formally invoked the **[[Fish And Wildlife Coordination Act]]** (as amended 1958) before the Advisory Board, asserting that wildlife conservation — including water for fish and wildlife as a recognized "beneficial use" under Arizona and California law — must receive equal consideration with other water-resource development features.
- **No species-level fish data (e.g., abundance, biomass, catch-per-unit-effort) is reported** in this segment; the report is primarily programmatic/administrative with quantitative data limited to avian (dove) nesting productivity and permit/flight-survey counts. Fish impacts are treated qualitatively via habitat-loss framing (oxbow/backwater/marsh elimination).
