# Vanishing Fishes of the Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from *Vanishing Fishes of North America* (Ono, Williams and Wagner, 1983) traces how the [[Colorado River]] system produced a highly endemic fish fauna and how human alteration has pushed that fauna toward extinction. It covers the river's geological origin, its extreme natural conditions, and the cumulative damage from dams, water depletion, [[Non-Native Species]] and [[Rotenone]] poisoning. Five endemic species are profiled: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Woundfin]]. The authors conclude that the native fauna is among the most threatened in North America.

## Background & Description

**Geological origin.** The Colorado River formed in the Oligocene Epoch, about 25 to 40 million years ago, when lakes that had filled with sediment on the Colorado Plateau overflowed. By the end of the Miocene Epoch, about 10 million years ago, the river and its major tributary, the [[Green River]], had cut a 1,700-mile course to the Gulf of California. The river drops about two miles in elevation from the Rocky Mountains to the Gulf and carved canyons such as the [[Grand Canyon]].

**Natural conditions.** Flows ranged from extreme summer lows to winter and spring floods. At peak flow the river carried over 175 million cubic yards of silt per day. Despite this, sixteen fish species evolved in the system.

**Endemism.** The authors estimate that endemic species make up 70 percent of all fishes in the Colorado River system. The endemics show adaptations to swift, turbid water:
- Streamlined bodies and wing-like fins for holding station in currents.
- Humped backs with enlarged muscles that stabilize swimming in turbulence.
- Flat, sloping heads that let fish hold near the bottom while facing upstream.
- Small eyes, with reliance on smell, taste and touch in silty water.

**Species profiles.** Each species is described in the source:

- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*):** Named for the hump behind the head, with a keel and grooves that channel water over the gills. Females of a fourteen-inch fish lay about 2,500 eggs, each 2.7 mm in diameter.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*):** A pencil-thin tail with a deep fork reduces drag. It grows to about twelve inches and one pound. Females of this species carry up to 10,000 eggs.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):** Described as the "Colorado River salmon," it is a minnow, not a salmon. It can reach five feet and 50 to 80 pounds, making it the largest minnow in North America.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Named for the dorsal keel. It is the only species in its genus, grows to three feet and 10 to 14 pounds, and may live up to thirty years.
- **[[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** A small, silvery minnow with sharp spines in its dorsal and pelvic fins, which likely deter predators. It has barbels for detecting food on the river bottom.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dams.** More than twenty dams were built on the Colorado River and its principal tributaries. The most significant were:
- [[Hoover Dam]], closed in the 1930s (the squawfish decline followed its 1935 closure). It created [[Lake Mead]].
- [[Glen Canyon Dam]], the last mainstem dam, closed in 1963. It created [[Lake Powell]].
- [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] on the Green River, which transformed warm river habitat into cold, clear reservoir and tailwaters.

Dams trapped sediment, chilled the bottom waters, and produced artificial flow patterns. Summer releases replaced the natural winter and spring floods, which disrupted spawning cues. The authors describe the river as "a series of mill ponds, bordered by clear, cold trout streams," leaving the endemic species "strangely out of place."

**Non-native species.** State and federal agencies stocked reservoirs with game fish. These competed with natives for food and space, preyed on eggs and fry, and introduced parasites and diseases to which natives had no resistance. Accidentally released baitfish, such as the [[Red Shiner]], also competed with juvenile endemics. Habitat changes allowed species to cross into one another's breeding areas, creating a threat of [[Hybridization]].

**Rotenone poisoning.** Before Flaming Gorge Dam closed, authorities tried to clear "trash fish" from the river. In September 1962 they released the fish poison [[Rotenone]] along 445 miles of the Green River and its tributaries upstream of the dam. The treatment nearly wiped out several endemic species in the Green River system.

**Water depletion.** Irrigation, urban use and reservoir evaporation are the second most severe human impact. Key figures from the source:
- Virgin water supply in the upper basin averaged 24.6 billion cubic yards per year (1914 to 1971).
- In 1961, 5.8 billion cubic yards per year were consumed, 3.2 billion of it for irrigation.
- Some reservoirs lose more than five feet of depth each year to evaporation.
- Scientists projected upper-basin consumption would reach 10.7 billion cubic yards per year by 2020.
- In some years no water reaches the Gulf of California.

Depletion reduces habitat, lowers dissolved oxygen (harming egg development) and changes temperature.

**Other changes.** Pollution from mining, sewage and fertilizers, bank erosion from overgrazing, and the long-term effects of non-native introductions are harder to document. A 1976 U.S. Department of the Interior document warned that the native fauna "may have the unique distinction of being the first major fauna to undergo mass extinction" if habitat degradation continues.

**Habitat context.** The [[Little Colorado River]] is the main spawning area for the Grand Canyon humpback chub population. Its cold water, released from Glen Canyon Dam, reduces spawning habitat. The [[Gila River]] basin, once home to many of these species, lost populations to diversions that dried its tributaries.

## Key Findings

- **Endemic richness:** Sixteen species evolved in the Colorado River system, and endemics make up 70 percent of its fishes. Their morphology reflects adaptation to extreme flow and silt.
- **Dams are the most damaging change.** More than twenty dams altered temperature, flow and sediment, and the endemic fishes could not adapt.
- **Non-native fishes and parasites** compete with, prey on and infect the natives, and they drive hybridization.
- **Rotenone** treatment of the Green River in 1962 severely reduced endemic populations.
- **Water depletion** is rising. Consumption was projected to nearly double between 1961 and 2020.
- **Species status:**
  - *Humpback Chub:* Only restricted populations remain, and it is absent from the Colorado River below the Grand Canyon. The recovery goal is at least five self-sustaining populations by 1990.
  - *Bonytail Chub:* Survey effort in the 1960s collected only 67 fish, and no reproduction was found in one later study. It was proposed for endangered listing in 1978 and was not formally listed until 1980. The authors consider it the most critically endangered of the four large-river endemics.
  - *Colorado Squawfish:* Once abundant, it is now confined to the Colorado and upper Green rivers. It was listed as endangered in 1967. In November 1980 thieves stole 14 of 27 adult broodstock fish from the Willow Beach hatchery.
  - *Razorback Sucker:* Survives in isolated segments. A single 1949 fisherman caught 7,000 pounds from Saguaro Lake, which was drained in 1966 with no razorbacks left.
  - *Woundfin:* Its last recorded occurrences were in the lower basin, and drought, agricultural diversion and non-native fishes eliminated it from much of its range.
- **Recovery efforts** include a Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team (see [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]]), life-history studies at Lake Mohave, and hatchery propagation at Willow Beach and Dexter national fish hatcheries. The authors note that restoring the system to its original state would require reversing the conditions that harmed these fishes, so protecting remaining habitat is the priority.
- **Broader extinction context:** Over the last 100 years, fourteen species and two subspecies of North American fish have become extinct.
