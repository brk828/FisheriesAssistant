# Bonytail Reintroduction Plan for the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #reintroduction

## Executive Summary

This draft plan outlines a comprehensive strategy for reintroducing hatchery-reared [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) into the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] (UCRB). Listed as federally endangered in 1980, the bonytail has virtually disappeared from its native range due to habitat loss from dams and interactions with exotic species. Reintroduction appears to be the only option for recovery; without it, the species should be considered extirpated from the UCRB. The plan proposes a six-year, phased approach combining laboratory experiments, field studies, and experimental stocking to address critical knowledge gaps in bonytail life history, minimize hybridization risks with congeners ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]]), reduce nonnative fish impacts, and optimize conditioning protocols for captive-reared fish. Initial stocking would release 2,000 pit-tagged, flow-trained juveniles (100-150 mm TL) at two locations during Year 1, with intensive monitoring to evaluate survival and inform adaptive management.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) is an endemic cyprinid of the [[Colorado River]] system. Taxonomic uncertainty persists regarding its relationship to [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*); references to "bonytail" before 1970 are tentative, and hybridization complicates field identification. Morphometric and genetic studies suggest discrete species status, though debate continues.

**Morphology**: The bonytail exhibits specialized features including a narrow, elongate caudal peduncle, falcate fins, raised nuchal hump, and embedded scales—adaptations suggesting specialization for swift-water environments. Small eyes and a terminal mouth may have protected against high sediment loads characteristic of the historic Colorado River system.

**Historical Distribution**: Historical accounts describe bonytails throughout the Colorado River system over 100 years ago, primarily in open mainstem sections. The last significant concentration occurred in [[Split Mountain Canyon]] of the [[Green River]] through [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (Vanicek 1967). Very few fish have been captured and positively identified since 1980. Valdez (1985) identified 14 possible bonytails (including juveniles) in [[Cataract Canyon]], Utah. Single captures occurred in [[Black Rocks]] section and [[Gray Canyon]]. Wild bonytail are considered extirpated from the Lower Colorado River Basin.

**Current Status**: Relict populations inhabit [[Lake Havasu]] and [[Lake Mohave]] in the Lower Colorado River Basin. A hatchery broodstock developed from Lake Mohave specimens (1974-1989) is maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico, providing the source for reintroduction efforts.

**Life History**: Adult bonytails historically occupied eddies and pools near swift currents over silt and boulder substrates. Chart and Cranney (1993) found adults in zones of mixing water between eddies and runs. Diel movement patterns show peak activity during crepuscular periods, with seasonal variation (reduced winter activity). Most gross movement occurred downstream, though surviving fish eventually remained localized. High discharge correlated with movement.

Feeding observations are contradictory: Vanicek and Kramer (1969) reported surface feeding on terrestrial insects, plant debris, and algae, while Crowl and Lentsch (in preparation) observed benthic feeding behavior similar to trout in laboratory flumes. Young Gila feed on chironomids and ephemeroptera larvae.

Spawning occurs after water temperatures reach 18°C on the descending limb of spring peak flows. Successful spawns correlate with high discharge years. Broadcast spawning over gravel has been observed in Lake Mohave. Marsh (1985) found highest larval survival (32%) at 15-20°C, with no survival at 5, 10, or 30°C. After hatching, larvae swim upstream before adhering to substrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bonytail's decline is attributed to habitat loss from dams that altered the natural physical environment and interactions with exotic species. Catastrophic loss resulted from active elimination programs replacing native fish with sport fish in dam tailwaters. The [[Endangered Species Act]] provides priority protection, and dam operations (e.g., [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]) now incorporate seasonality into annual flow regimes to benefit endangered fish.

**Hybridization Risk**: Evidence suggests hybridization occurs between bonytail, humpback chub, and roundtail chub. Hamman (1982) artificially produced viable offspring from bonytail × roundtail and bonytail × humpback crosses, though these did not occur naturally in hatcheries, suggesting ethological isolating mechanisms. Spatial and temporal overlap during spawning has been documented, but exact mechanisms remain unclear. Hybridization may result from breakdown of natural systems due to rapid population declines or represent natural hybrid swarm dynamics.

**Nonnative Interactions**: Nonnative fish impact native populations through predation and competition. Minckley and Meffe (1987) found nonnatives gradually overcome natives absent flood discharges; flooding regimes allow natives to maintain dominance. Crowl and Lentsch (in preparation) found feeding efficiency of age-1 bonytail decreased by presence of [[Red Shiner]], though sympatric bonytails learned aggressive feeding over time.

Predation vulnerability modeling based on gape limitation theory indicates bonytail reach safety from most predators (pike to 710 mm TL, channel catfish to 480 mm TL) at 150 mm TL, approximately age 3 under normal growth.

**Domestication Effects**: The 1988 experimental stocking documented 74.5% mortality (likely higher), attributed to domestication and stress. Sixty-seven fish died in holding tanks. Conditioning experiments are proposed to minimize domestication effects through: (1) flow training for muscle/aerobic fitness, (2) exposure to nonnatives to elicit learned feeding aggression, (3) natural/live food introduction, and (4) acclimation to natural river water.

## Key Findings

**Goal**: Establish a stable, self-sustaining population of bonytail in the Upper Colorado River Basin through a stocking program.

**Four Primary Objectives**:

1. **Minimize hybridization risk** with roundtail and humpback chub through:
   - Laboratory experiments determining habitat segregation across flow/substrate conditions
   - Field studies comparing capture site habitats and radio-telemetry monitoring
   - Investigation of spawning segregation (spatial, temporal, ethological)

2. **Minimize nonnative impacts** through:
   - Determining optimal release size to minimize predation (target: 150 mm TL based on gape limitation modeling)
   - Quantifying competition impacts on fitness across life stages
   - Field diet analysis and electivity studies

3. **Condition bonytail for maximum survival** through:
   - Flow training experiments (completed for age-1; needed for ages 2-4)
   - Feeding efficiency and swimming ability assessments
   - Optimization of training regimes (timing, duration, intensity)

4. **Experimental stocking** to determine optimal protocols:
   - **Year 1 Plan**: Release 2,000 pit-tagged, flow-trained juveniles (target 150 mm TL) at two locations during late summer base flows
   - **Source**: Lake Mohave broodstock from Dexter National Fish Hatchery
   - **Conditioning**: Flow training, natural food exposure, 2-week river water acclimation in holding pens
   - **Locations**: (1) Green River valley below [[Desolation Canyon]] (Swaze to Green River, UT), (2) [[Moab Canyon]] or [[Professor Valley]] (below [[Westwater Canyon]], UT)—selected to minimize hybridization risk
   - **Monitoring**: Bi-monthly sampling for 2-3 months post-release, monthly sampling March-April Year 2 for overwinter survival assessment

**Six-Year Implementation Schedule**:
- Years 1-3: Laboratory studies (habitat segregation, nonnative interactions, conditioning optimization)
- Years 4-6: Field studies (habitat/spawning segregation, predation/competition assessment)
- Years 1-6: Progressive experimental stocking with adaptive management

**Survival Estimates**: Using razorback sucker as proxy (agreed by Biology Committee, May 1995), 300 bonytails expected to survive to Year 2 from 2,000 released, with progressive mortality reducing numbers to 23 by Year 7.

**Adaptive Management**: The plan emphasizes iterative evaluation, incorporating results from each year's monitoring and experiments into subsequent stocking efforts. Numbers and locations will increase in out-years as information accumulates. Power analysis from trial release-recapture experiments will establish minimum sample sizes for robust statistical evaluation (70% power to detect treatment differences).

**Critical Knowledge Gaps**: Life-history requirements, evolutionary history, spawning ecology, isolating mechanisms, optimal conditioning protocols, and size/age-specific vulnerability to predation and competition remain poorly understood, necessitating the experimental approach outlined in this plan.
