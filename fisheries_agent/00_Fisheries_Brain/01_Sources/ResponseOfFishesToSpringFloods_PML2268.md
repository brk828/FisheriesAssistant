# Fish Response to Floods in Black Creek, Mississippi

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a partial journal article (The American Midland Naturalist, vol. 109, no. 1; the running head identifies it as Ross & Baker, 1983) on how a stream fish assemblage responds to flooding. Fish were sampled by seining and trapping in the channel and floodplain of [[Black Creek]], Mississippi, under normal flow and flood conditions. The central result concerns [[Notropis texanus]] (Texas shiner), whose abundance tracked spring discharge across years. The authors conclude that fish assemblage structure reflects both habitat suitability and stochastic flood events, and that each species responds differently to these drivers. The OCR text is heavily degraded, so the species tables are only partly legible and are summarized here rather than transcribed.

## Background & Description

The study belongs to a baseline data collection programme on Black Creek, supported by grants from the South Mississippi Electric Power Association (SMEPA). The authors examine two questions:

- Whether repeated collecting (for example, by bait collectors, a concern raised by [[Markus (1934)]]) depletes minnow populations.
- Whether flood conditions, especially spring flooding, shape population success in stream fishes.

Sampling used seines and traps across the channel and [[Floodplain Habitat]] during normal flow and flood periods, with catches expressed as percentage composition and catch per unit effort (catch/trap-hour ×10³ in the figures). The stream is narrow; one cited width is 6.7 m. The fish assemblage is multi-family, including lampreys, [[Amiidae]] and [[Anguillidae]] representatives, pikes (Esox), minnows (Cyprinidae), [[Cyprinodontidae]] (killifishes), [[Poeciliidae]] (livebearers), [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes) and [[Percidae]] (perches and darters). The OCR table is too degraded to give a reliable species count.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Spring discharge (January–May) is the key environmental variable. High-discharge years exceeded 35 m³/s and low-discharge years fell below 25 m³/s.
- **Floodplain role:** The authors argue that fringing floodplains are important to stream fishes, and that the assemblage is shaped by structural habitat suitability (citing [[Baker & Ross (1981)]]) and by stochastic flood intensity.
- **Species-specific responses:** Species do not respond uniformly. A key contrast is between the shiner, which tracked flow, and the darter, which did not decline with collecting effort.
- **Prior literature:** The study draws on flood-impact work on other streams, including [[Hurricane Agnes Flood Effects]] on brook trout, flood effects on Devil's River fishes in Texas, and drought and flood effects on intermittent-stream minnows.

## Key Findings

1. **Declining shiner abundance with repeated collecting.** [[Notropis texanus]] abundance declined over the study as a function of collection number (Y = 218 − 14.7X; F = 7.8; P < .05). The total count of this species across collections is given as N = 1457.

2. **No effort-related decline in the darter.** [[Percina nigrofasciata]] did not decline with collecting effort (F = 1.0; not significant). Its total count is given as N = 205.

3. **Flow-linked population success for the shiner.** Adjusted means were used to remove the collection-effort effect (Ȳadj = Ȳ + d, where d = (Ȳi − Ŷi), after Sokal & Rohlf, 1969). High-abundance years (1975, 1979, 1980) coincided with spring discharge above 35 m³/s. Low-abundance years (1976–1978) coincided with spring discharge below 25 m³/s.

4. **Significant correlation.** The change in shiner abundance between years was significantly correlated with the change in spring flow (r = .91; P < .05).

5. **Hypothesis test.** For *N. texanus*, the data reject the null hypothesis of no relationship and support an alternative hypothesis linking population success to spring flow. The authors also note that three years of repeated collecting did not alter minnow population abundance in this small stream, although [[Markus (1934)]] had linked minnow declines to bait collecting elsewhere in Illinois. Effects of repeated collecting on Black Creek fishes are deferred to a subsequent paper.

6. **Floodplain importance.** The fish assemblage is controlled partly by habitat structure and partly by stochastic flooding, and each species responds differently to these factors.
