# Thyroxine Concentrations in Developing Flannelmouth Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Eastern Washington University technical report (Final Report, 1991, Part 2) measures [[Thyroxine]] (T4) in developing [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] eggs and larvae from the [[Green River]], Utah. Eggs were collected from wild fish, fertilized, and reared at the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]]. Thyroxine was quantified by [[Radioimmunoassay]] in 540 eggs and larvae across two spawning groups. The study reports two thyroid hormone surges, one at hatching and one at swim-up, that the authors compare with surges documented in other fishes and link to metamorphosis and possible [[Imprinting]]. The work was funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and is framed as a baseline for managing a species whose populations in the [[Colorado River Basin]] are poorly understood.

## Background & Description

The flannelmouth sucker is a member of the endemic fish community of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its range is restricted to larger streams and rivers of the middle and upper basin in Arizona, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming. Populations are recorded from the Colorado mainstem and major tributaries, including the Green, Yampa, Gunnison, Uncompahgre, Dolores, San Juan, Little Colorado and Gila rivers.

The report places the flannelmouth alongside other basin natives facing decline, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. Irrigation impoundments and introduced exotic species are cited as the main drivers. Minckley (1973) reported that the species does not maintain viable populations in impoundments. Woodling (1985) reported that introduced white and longnose suckers had replaced native flannelmouth and bluehead suckers in the Gunnison River above Blue Mesa Reservoir.

Unlike its congeners, the flannelmouth is relatively abundant. In a 1987–1989 survey of the Green and Yampa rivers in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (Karp and Tyus 1990), it was the most abundant species captured, at 2,159 of 7,885 fish. It is not protected by any state, although Wyoming lists it as a species of special concern. Life history, behavior and ecology remain poorly documented, which motivated the early-development study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regulated flows and temperature.** The report identifies altered flow and temperature regimes caused by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] as a suspected cause of razorback sucker decline in the Green River. After the dam became operational, water temperatures below it were colder later in the year than before. The authors note that flannelmouths have been less severely affected, even though the two species co-occur and spawn at about the same time. The exact spawning locations of the two species are unknown.

**Energetics of delayed hatching.** Larvae live on stored yolk energy between hatching and swim-up. The authors hypothesize that larger eggs store more energy, so species with larger eggs may tolerate the delayed hatching and swim-up expected in cooler water. The proposed test uses bomb calorimetry for egg energy stores, metabolic rates at different temperatures, and time to hatch and swim-up.

**Hybridization.** Flannelmouths are reported to hybridize with razorback suckers in the wild (Hubbs and Miller 1953; Sigler and Miller 1963). This is cited as a reason their developmental rates would be expected to be similar.

**Rearing conditions.** Eggs were incubated in vertical stacked Heath Tray incubators at a constant 21.1 ± 1.0 °C until swim-up, then moved to rearing troughs.

## Key Findings

### Study design and methods

- **Sample size:** 540 flannelmouth sucker eggs or larvae were assayed in total.
- **Spawning groups:** Eggs were fertilized on May 28, 1991 (Group 1) and June 10, 1991 (Group 2) and collected from the Green River.
- **Sampling:** Group 1 had 25 individuals sampled daily to day 10 post-fertilization. Group 2 was sampled daily to day 18, then at 7-day intervals.
- **Sampling discrepancy:** The Abstract gives the Group 2 endpoint as 38 days and the Methods give 45 days. The Methods list 15 Group 2 individuals sampled per day, while the Abstract and Figure captions give 25.
- **Standardization:** Fish were sampled between 0830 and 0930 h to control for diurnal variation in T4. Samples were weighed to 0.1 mg, flash frozen, and stored at −80 °C.
- **Extraction:** Tissue was extracted in ice-cold ethanol with 1.0 mM 6-n-propyl-2-thiouracil, adapted from Parker (1988). The assay used a commercial coat-a-count T4 kit from Diagnostic Products, Inc., with a standard curve of 0–24 ng/dl. Each sample was assayed in duplicate.
- **Units:** Concentrations are reported in ng T4 per g body weight.

### Development timing

- **Group 1 hatching:** Hatching began on day 5–6 and was complete by day 7 post-fertilization.
- **Group 1 swim-up:** Swim-up began on day 10.
- **Group 2 hatching:** Hatching began on day 6 and was complete by day 7.
- **Group 2 swim-up:** Swim-up occurred on days 9–10.
- **Discussion summary:** Hatching occurred at 5.5–7.0 days and swim-up at 9.0–10.0 days post-fertilization at 21.0 ± 1.0 °C. Alevins had absorbed their yolk sac and started gaining weight by this time.
- **Weight:** Weight loss occurred at hatch. Fish began gaining weight by day 9–10.

### Thyroxine concentrations

- **Group 1 basal levels:** T4 rose from 1.7 ± 0.2 to 4.2 ± 0.8 ng/g over days 1–5. Prehatch eggs ranged from 1.6 ± 0.4 to 2.5 ± 0.4 ng/g.
- **Group 1 hatch peak:** T4 peaked at 18.6 ng/g on day 6 (Abstract gives ± 8.0 SEM) at hatch.
- **Group 1 dip:** T4 fell to 5.9 ± 0.7 ng/g on days 7–9.
- **Group 1 swim-up peak:** T4 peaked again at 14.9 ± 0.7 ng/g on day 10 at swim-up.
- **Group 2 hatch peak:** T4 peaked at 8.5 ng/g on day 5 at hatch. The Abstract gives ± 5.1 SEM and the Results give ± 5.6.
- **Group 2 dip:** T4 fell to 3.4–4.1 ng/g on days 7–9.
- **Group 2 swim-up peak:** T4 peaked at 11.8 ± 5.4 ng/g on day 9 at swim-up.
- **Post-swim-up fry:** T4 ranged from 2.6 to 10.6 ng/g on days 10–18, then fell to 5.8 ± 0.2 ng/g on day 24 and 5.1 ± 1.4 ng/g on day 38.

### Surge magnitude

- **Hatch surge:** Concentrations of 8.5–18.6 ng/g at hatch were about 2× to 5× (200–500%) basal prehatch levels of 1.6–4.2 ng/g.
- **Swim-up surge:** Concentrations of 11.8–14.9 ng/g at swim-up were about 2× to 4× (200–400%) the level of recently hatched yolk-sac larvae (3.4–6.4 ng/g).
- **Comparison:** The authors state the surges are similar to those in razorback suckers, [[Kokanee Salmon]], pink and chum salmon, and Cyprinidae, Centrarchidae and Pleuronectidae. In those taxa, surges were tied to metamorphosis and imprinting.

### Quality assurance

- **Blind controls:** Samples were run at low, medium and high concentrations against values supplied by Diagnostic Products, Inc.
- **Low control:** Actual 2.3 ng/dl; measured 2.1 ± 0.1 ng/dl (n = 6). Acceptable range 1.9–2.7.
- **Medium control:** Actual 7.3 ng/dl; measured 8.0 ± 0.6 ng/dl (n = 6). Acceptable range 6.2–8.4.
- **High control:** Actual 11.5 ng/dl (per text); measured 12.5 ± 1.2 ng/dl (n = 6). Acceptable range 10.0–13.0.
- **Nonspecific binding:** 341 ± 22 cpm, compared with 56,427 ± 306 cpm in total count tubes.
- **Pipetting error:** Error was 8.8% for 25 µL aliquots, with a coefficient of variation of 9.43. For 1 mL aliquots of tracer, error was about 2% with a coefficient of variation of 0.9.
- **Duplicate error:** Mean percent error was 8.2 ± 1.5% (range 0.1–35.3%). The Results state n = 270 duplicate samples, but the Figure 5 caption gives n = 136.

### Development compared with razorback sucker

- **Egg mass:** Flannelmouth eggs weighed 25.0–30.0 mg, versus 10.8–11.0 mg for razorback suckers.
- **Alevin mass:** Flannelmouth alevins weighed 10.0–15.0 mg, versus 4.1–4.9 mg for razorback suckers.
- **Rate:** Flannelmouth development proceeded at about the same rate as razorback development under uniform conditions (Scholz et al. 1992b).

### Conclusions and recommendations

- The authors conclude the T4 surges represent a real increase rather than assay noise, because surge magnitudes (200–500%) far exceed the measured error.
- They suggest the hormone surges at hatch and swim-up may serve a function similar to that in other species.
- They recommend energetic and temperature experiments to test whether larger-egg species better tolerate the cooler water and delayed hatching that may follow dam operation.
