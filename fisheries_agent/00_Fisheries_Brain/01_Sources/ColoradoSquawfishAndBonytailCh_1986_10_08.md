## Executive Summary
This 1986 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum documents a coordination effort between the Service, Arizona State University (ASU) researchers, and Dexter National Fish Hatchery to stock endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] (along with [[Razorback Sucker]]) into experimental ponds at the ASU Research Park in Tempe, Arizona. The stocking, approximately 2,000 fingerlings each of Colorado squawfish and bonytail chub, was intended to support university-led research on interactions between native Colorado River fishes and non-native ("exotic") species. The document is administrative rather than data-driven, but it provides valuable context on early recovery-program methodology for these federally listed species.

## Background & Description
The source memo does not provide direct morphological data (e.g., length, weight, meristics) for the fishes in question. However, contextual taxonomic notes relevant to these species include:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): The largest North American cyprinid, historically reaching lengths over 1.8 m; an elongate, streamlined piscivorous minnow adapted to large, turbid river systems.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): A slender-bodied cyprinid with a pronounced nuchal hump reduction relative to congeners, adapted to swift, turbid mainstem river currents; considered one of the most endangered fishes in North America.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Recognizable by a distinctive sharp-edged dorsal keel behind the head, a catostomid adaptation possibly related to hydrodynamic stability in swift currents.

The fish being transferred were **fingerlings**, i.e., juvenile life stage, likely hatchery-reared at Dexter National Fish Hatchery (Dexter NFH), New Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native habitat**: All three species are endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], historically occupying large, warm, turbid, free-flowing river reaches.
- **Experimental habitat**: The ponds at [[ASU Research Park]] (Tempe, Arizona) total approximately 20 surface acres across three ponds, functioning as semi-natural mesocosms for controlled ecological study.
- **Research objective**: The stocking was explicitly designed to study **interactions between native and exotic (non-native) fishes**, a critical issue given that introduced species (e.g., centrarchids, ictalurids, cyprinids from other basins) are considered a major driver of decline for native Colorado River fishes through predation and competition.
- **Conservation context**: This work directly supports **species recovery efforts** for federally listed endangered fish, tying applied ecological research to management and recovery planning under the Endangered Species Act.

## Key Findings
- The USFWS **concurred** with ASU's proposal (led by Dr. Paul Marsh and Dr. W.L. Minckley) to use Colorado squawfish and bonytail chub (and separately, razorback sucker) for controlled pond studies.
- Approximately **2,000 Colorado squawfish fingerlings** and **2,000 bonytail chub fingerlings** were requested for fall 1986 stocking.
- Fish were to be supplied via coordination with the **Dexter National Fish Hatchery**, a key facility for propagation of endangered Colorado River fishes.
- The study represents an early example of **applied conservation aquaculture** research, using semi-controlled pond environments to investigate native-exotic fish interactions — data considered essential for informing basin-wide recovery strategies for the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- Permissions for pond use were secured through university researchers, reflecting a collaborative model between federal agencies and academic institutions in endangered species recovery programs.
