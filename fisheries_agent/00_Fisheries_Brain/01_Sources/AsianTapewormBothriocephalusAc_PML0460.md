# Asian Tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) in Native Fishes from the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1997 paper in the *Great Basin Naturalist* documents infection of native cyprinids in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), Grand Canyon, Arizona, by the nonindigenous cestode [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*). Gastrointestinal tracts were examined from fish collected in 1991–1994. Infection was found in the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and in [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), with lower infection in several nonnative species. The authors argue that the parasite's rapid spread through the [[Colorado River Basin]] since its 1979 detection, and its pathogenic effects on definitive hosts, make it one of the most serious threats to the conservation and recovery of native fishes. The study was funded through a Cooperative Agreement with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

## Background & Description

**Problem context.** Extinctions, extirpations, and declines of native fishes in the American Southwest have been attributed to nonindigenous fishes and physical habitat alteration. Fish translocations can also move parasites and pathogens with them. Asian tapeworm is an example of this effect in the Colorado River Basin.

**Parasite introduction and spread.**
- First detected in the basin in minnows from the Virgin River, Arizona, Nevada, and Utah, in 1979 (Minckley et al. 1986 cited in source).
- Later reported from Virgin River tributaries (Beaver Dam Wash, Arizona; Muddy River, Nevada) by [[Heckmann et al. 1987]] and [[Heckmann et al. 1993]].
- Recently reported from the Colorado River and tributaries in Grand Canyon, the San Juan River (Utah and New Mexico), and the Green River (Utah).

**Study site.** The LCR is the major spawning and rearing habitat of the largest remaining population of humpback chub. Sampling was concentrated near the confluence with the Colorado River, and the authors note the LCR is the species' only major spawning site in Grand Canyon.

**Methods (summary).**
- Fish were collected mainly by seine and dip net, with supplemental trammel, gill, and hoop net captures of larger fish.
- Fish were measured for total length (mm) and preserved in 10% formalin immediately after capture.
- Larvae and early juveniles were identified using keys by [[Snyder 1981]] and [[Snyder and Muth 1990]].
- Guts were excised, contents identified, and relative volumes visually estimated by category.
- Tapeworms were identified visually, then confirmed with [[Mitchell 1994]]'s key.

**Host species examined.**
- Humpback chub, in larval, postlarval, age-0, and age-1+ stages.
- Speckled dace, in larval, juvenile, and adult stages.
- Fathead minnow, plains killifish, and common carp (nonnative) were also examined for infection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Parasite biology.** Asian tapeworm prevalence and infrapopulation (number of worms per infected fish) are likely shaped by temperature, temperature-dependent rejection responses, immune responses, and host age, along with density-dependent factors in both the intermediate host (cyclopoid copepods) and the definitive fish hosts (Granath and Esch 1983a, 1983b; Riggs and Esch 1987; Marcogliese and Esch 1989). The authors suggest the observed patterns may arise from (1) seasonal or annual variation in infection, (2) density-dependent parasite mortality, and (3) acquired host resistance to reinfection (Anderson 1982).

**Pathological effects.** Documented or suspected effects on fish hosts include:
- Intestinal abrasion, loss or separation of gut microvilli/enterocytes, and perforation of the GI tract (Hoole and Nisan 1994; Mitchell 1994).
- Chronic effects, which were less studied but may include emaciation, anemia, reduced intestinal, liver, and pancreatic enzymes, reduced reproductive capacity, muscle fatigue, and secondary bacterial infections.
- Laboratory work by [[Granath and Esch 1983c]] showed significantly reduced survivorship of [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) infected with the parasite. The authors caution that these effects may not occur in the wild.

**Host range and vulnerability.** Roughly half of the geographic range of Asian tapeworm includes cyprinids, its most common hosts. Most of these are already endangered, threatened, or of special concern. The authors name the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) as a vulnerable species within the Gila River drainage.

**Broader threats.** The authors argue that biotic changes to the Colorado River Basin, including fish and pathogen translocations, are effectively ubiquitous and seemingly irreversible. They present these as among the most serious threats to native fish populations.

## Key Findings

**Infection rates and prevalence**
- Mean prevalence was reported as roughly 28% (range 0–78%) in humpback chub. The scanned abstract is partly garbled, so exact values should be verified against the original.
- Speckled dace had the highest reported prevalence in the abstract, around 87% (range 4–66%), with infrapopulations reported as high as values that could not be reliably read from the scan.
- Fathead minnow: 6 of 75 specimens (8%) infected, collected in 1991 and 1994.
- Single infected specimens of plains killifish and common carp were found. Their sample sizes are reported in the source table.
- Speckled dace prevalence was greatest in 1991–1993, and the cestode was not detected in some host groups in 1994 samples (per source table).

**Infrapopulations and maximum counts**
- Humpback chub: the first LCR observation was made in May 1990 (Minckley 1990), from a larva and then from older fish. Infrapopulations were absent in some 1990–1993 samples. Maximum counts were reported as up to 46 worms per fish in age-1+ specimens (value per scan).
- Speckled dace: mean infrapopulations of approximately 0.5 (<19 mm), 5.3 (19–50 mm), and 3.5 (>50 mm), with maximum counts of 1, 18, and 28 respectively (values per scan).

**Diet and gut contents**
- Tapeworms accounted for a substantial mean relative volume of humpback chub gut contents, reported as a maximum of 100% of gut contents in infected fish. The exact mean value is unclear in the scanned text.

**Epidemiological conclusions**
- The authors conclude that the rapid spread of Asian tapeworm across Colorado River drainages, first detected in 1979, likely portends an eventual cosmopolitan distribution in lower-elevation habitats suited to its thermophilic life history.
- Because the LCR is the major spawning site for the Grand Canyon humpback chub population, the parasite poses a particular concern for that population's status.
- The authors call for more research to determine the exact effect of the parasite on native fish populations in Grand Canyon and elsewhere, while stating that the parasite clearly has the potential to regulate fish populations.

**Conservation recommendation**
- Asian tapeworm and similar biotic changes should be treated as among the most serious threats to the conservation and recovery of native fish populations in the Colorado River Basin.
