# Arizona Game and Fish Department Comments on Captive Propagation of Native Herps for Commercial Purposes

#herpetology #wildlife-management #policy #reptiles #amphibians #law-enforcement

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of inter-office memoranda from multiple [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] regional supervisors, law enforcement officers, and wildlife managers responding to a petition for rule change that would allow captive propagation and commercial sale of native [[Arizona]] reptiles and amphibians. Written in July 1994, the memos unanimously oppose the proposed "Captive Propagation Permit" on grounds of wildlife commercialization, enforcement difficulties, biological concerns, and inconsistency with departmental mission. Staff cite [[California]]'s captive propagation program as creating a "law enforcement nightmare" and express concern that the permit would be used to launder wild-caught specimens. Key species mentioned in the proposal include [[Gila Monster]], [[Rosy Boa]], [[Arizona Mountain Kingsnake]], and [[Green Ratsnake]].

## Background & Description

The petition under review proposed establishing a new permit system allowing [[Arizona]] residents to captively breed and sell native reptiles and amphibians for commercial gain. The proposal included specific provisions for:

- Unlimited possession of breeding stock individuals (contradicting existing bag limits of 2-4 per year)
- Sale of "juvenile" specimens below specified size thresholds (e.g., 12 inches for [[Rosy Boa]], 14 inches for [[Arizona Mountain Kingsnake]])
- Exchange of breeding stock between permittees
- A lottery system for distributing [[Gila Monster|Gila Monsters]] to breeders
- PIT tag identification for individual specimens
- Annual veterinary certification of breeding stock numbers and identification
- Record-keeping requirements for all progeny

The proposal argued that captive propagation would reduce collecting pressure on wild populations, noting that [[Arizona]] species were already being propagated commercially out-of-state (particularly in [[California]]) and that market prices drop when captive-bred juveniles flood the market.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Department staff raised multiple biological and ecological concerns:

**Disease Introduction**: Captive-reared or wild-caught individuals released back into the wild could introduce exotic diseases contracted from other animals in the pet trade, potentially devastating wild populations. The international scope of the reptile pet trade increases contamination risk.

**Genetic Concerns**: Staff noted risks of releasing genetically inferior captive-bred individuals into wild populations, cross-breeding geographically or topographically isolated populations, and subsequent release of hybrid progeny. These scenarios would have "serious biological ramifications in terms of genotypic or phenotypic changes of population characteristics."

**Habitat and Collection Pressure**: Multiple respondents disputed the petition's claim that captive propagation would reduce wild collection pressure. Staff observed that [[California]]'s program had not reduced collecting pressure and that [[Arizona]] was already experiencing increased collection activity from individuals holding [[California]] captive breeder permits. Officers noted that "it's still cheaper to get your own animals from the wild" and that recreational collecting would continue regardless of commercial availability.

**Population Protection**: Current bag limits (2-4 per year) and possession limits were described as "biologically based to protect wild populations." The proposal's unlimited possession provision would undermine these protections.

## Key Findings

**Unanimous Opposition**: All regional supervisors and law enforcement personnel who submitted comments opposed the petition. Region I (Dave Cagle, Ron Thompson), Region III (Steve Ferrell), Region IV (Dave West), Region V (Dan Romero), and Region VI (Ray Kohls) all recommended denial.

**California Program Failures**: Multiple officers cited [[California]]'s captive propagation permit as creating severe enforcement problems. A [[California]] Fish and Game warden described it as a "law enforcement nightmare" with increased collecting pressure, permit holders circumventing laws by having non-permit holders collect for them, and animals being hidden under breeding licenses. A large-scale sting operation by [[California]] Fish and Game "did not deter the unlawful collecting of reptiles."

**Laundering Concerns**: Staff identified the permit as providing "paper cover" for illegal reptile trade. Once captive-bred animals enter retail markets, enforcement becomes impossible because individuals can claim any specimen was purchased or gifted. The burden of proof falls on the state to prove otherwise, effectively rendering possession and take laws "useless."

**Gila Monster Inclusion Opposed**: All commenters specifically opposed including [[Gila Monster|Gila Monsters]] (a species with closed season and zero bag limit) in the program. Concerns included: current closed season status, Department burden of administering a lottery system, liability issues (Department policy prohibits employees from handling venomous reptiles), and that few [[Gila Monster|Gila Monsters]] obtained by the Department are easily placed with educational or research facilities.

**Administrative Burden**: Staff emphasized that effective monitoring would require tracking permittee records, individual identification of each specimen, facility inspections, and verification of veterinary certifications. Region III noted this would be an "impossible feat" and that "special license administration consumes significant time of regional staff." Cost concerns included PIT tag readers at "in excess of $2,000 for each unit" per region.

**Contradictory Provisions**: Multiple respondents identified internal contradictions in the proposal:
- Unlimited breeding stock possession versus adherence to bag limits (2-4 per year)
- Breeders can sell juveniles, but buyers can only dispose of adults by gift or as directed by Department
- Exchange of breeding stock contradicts existing bartering prohibitions (R12-4-404B and ARS 17-309)

**Size Limit Inconsistencies**: [[Arizona]]'s proposed 12-inch maximum for [[Rosy Boa]] differed from [[California]]'s 14-inch limit, creating enforcement problems at reptile conventions. Staff requested biological justification for all size limits and noted many wild-caught [[Arizona Mountain Kingsnake|Arizona Mountain Kingsnakes]] are less than the proposed 14-inch threshold.

**Commercialization Philosophy**: Staff repeatedly emphasized the proposal contradicts Department mission and philosophy prohibiting commercialization of native wildlife. Rod Lucas stated: "History undeniably provides a consistent and reoccurring message—when a commercial value is placed on any species, abuses dominate and detrimental repercussions affect the population."

**Comparison to Game Birds Invalid**: The petition compared reptile breeding to native game bird propagation (e.g., [[Gambel's Quail]]), but staff clarified that game farm licenses only permit imported birds—"no native wildlife or wild-caught birds can be possessed live, bred or sold." R12-4-413 explicitly protects native populations by prohibiting licenses when the same species exists in Arizona.

**Enforcement Experience**: Dan Romero (Region V) noted that executive officers of the Arizona Herpetological Association (AHA), which submitted the petition, "have been cited in the past for illegally possessing [[Gila Monster|Gila Monsters]] and other protected reptile species" within the previous 18 months. During undercover work, he found "a profound lack of concern for the resource and a strong push in favor of the monetary gains from native reptiles."

**PIT Tag Technology Concerns**: Staff noted that implant technology and equipment are readily available to hobbyists at reptile expos, enabling self-implanting and switching of tags between wild-caught and breeding stock animals, bypassing the requirement for licensed veterinarian implantation.

**Veterinary Oversight Questioned**: Staff questioned what legal recourse the Department would have with veterinarians and whether vets would be concerned about "voracity" of paperwork. Concerns included that added bureaucracy might decrease the number of vets treating reptiles.

**Data Collection Purpose Unclear**: The extensive record-keeping requirements (described as resembling "a Ph.D. dissertation") lacked clear purpose. Staff questioned whether data would be used for performance reports or long-term planning, warning that dormant data would invite public criticism.

**Recommendation**: All responding staff recommended the petition be denied, stating denial "will not further restrict individuals in regards to Live Wildlife Rules, and is consistent with the Department Mission Statement." If the Commission were receptive, staff recommended the Department—not petitioners—author any rule.
