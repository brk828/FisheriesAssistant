# Native Fish Work Group 1992 Annual Report: On-Site Razorback Sucker Augmentation in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 annual report from the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) documents an effort to rebuild the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] population in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. The adult population, estimated at about 60,000 fish, is old (40+ years) and has shown no detectable recruitment to adulthood in nearly four decades. The NFWG, a multi-agency coalition formed in 1990, reared young suckers in two isolated backwaters, [[Yuma Cove]] and [[Davis Cove]], before tagging and releasing them to the lake.

In 1992, on-site spawning in Yuma Cove produced fast-growing juveniles, and some males matured at about nine months of age, earlier than any previous record. However, production (296 juveniles harvested) was far below the roughly 6,000 to 12,000 sub-adults per year the authors consider necessary. Key constraints were limited backwater space, high early mortality, poor water quality, dense aquatic vegetation, and predation by odonate naiads.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an endangered [[Catostomidae]] endemic to the Colorado River basin. The Lake Mohave population is the largest remaining wild stock and is considered a focal point for recovery. Previous augmentation attempts relied on hatchery production, but stocking young fish elsewhere has proven largely unsuccessful because of predation losses. Growing fish to a sub-adult size of about 30 cm total length (TL) before release is considered more promising.

Earlier on-site attempts at Yuma Cove (1985, 1986, 1987) were compromised when the berm separating the backwater from the lake was breached, allowing non-native predators to invade. The berm was rebuilt and compacted in winter 1990-91, and the 1992 effort was the first to succeed.

The NFWG is a cooperative effort of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State University]], the National Park Service, the Nevada Division of Wildlife, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (which provided substantial resources), and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The 1992 report was prepared by Thomas Burke and Gordon Mueller and includes four appendices (A-D) on production, growth, light trapping, and odonate predation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Yuma Cove.** This naturally isolated backwater adjacent to Arizona Bay was treated with [[Rotenone]] (ichthyocide) to remove non-native fishes before stocking. Water quality deteriorated in summer. Water temperatures exceeded 30 °C in the shallow cove, and [[Dissolved Oxygen]] in the bottom 18 inches fell below 1 mg/L by late July, caused by thermal stratification and biological oxygen demand from decaying plant material. No fish kill occurred, but suckers were visibly stressed. Operational depth was about 1 foot.

**Aquatic vegetation.** [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and spiny naiad (*Najas marina*) grew densely. By the end of July, pondweed covered the cove surface, blocking seining and netting, and it also contributed to low oxygen.

**Flash flood.** A flash flood on 10 August 1992 carried litter, debris, sediment and feral burro (*Equus asinus*) faeces into Yuma Cove. The authors report it had net positive effects: cooler temperatures, higher oxygen, increased turbidity that reduced algae, and flattened weed beds that improved netting. The authors suggest the nutrient input may have stimulated food production and contributed to exceptional growth.

**Davis Cove.** This backwater between Katherine's Landing and Davis Dam covers about 3 acres, with a minimum operational depth of 12 feet. A barrier net was installed across its mouth to exclude reservoir fish. Non-native fishes were removed, but predation by [[Largemouth Bass]] was suspected to be high. After most bass were removed, a large crayfish population developed; by October, 5 to 10 crayfish were commonly caught per 150-foot trammel net set overnight.

**Predators.** Odonate naiads (dragonflies and damselflies, [[Odonata]]) were very abundant in light traps, at times exceeding 5,000 per trap set. Turtles, crayfish and catfish were also identified as likely predators on young suckers hiding in daytime cover.

**Reservoir level.** Lake Mohave's declining elevation and monthly fluctuations contributed to poor backwater water quality. The effects of these fluctuations on the backwater fish remain unknown.

## Key Findings

**Adult brood stock and spawning (Yuma Cove)**
- Adult suckers were collected by trammel nets in late January and [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging|PIT tagged]] before release into Yuma Cove. Spawning was observed from mid-February through April.
- Of 88 brood fish, 82 were recaptured and returned to the reservoir, 5 were found dead, and 1 was unaccounted for. Only two fish needed retagging. (Appendix A gives 87 recovered adults, 83 alive, 4 natural mortalities and 1 unaccounted; Table 3 lists 28 females and 60 males stocked.)
- At stocking, 8 of 28 females (29%) and 53 of 60 males (88%) were ripe.

**Growth**
- The first larvae appeared on 26 February at about 11 mm TL. Fish reached about 26 mm by late April, about 100 mm by early June, 200 mm by late July, and were over 300 mm by October.
- Average TL after the 9-month post-hatch period was 35.4 cm, with the largest individual at 39.1 cm. Growth averaged about 4 cm per month (0.028 cm/day).
- Growth slowed for about two weeks in mid-July, probably from falling lake level, high temperature and low oxygen.
- Yuma Cove growth substantially exceeded hatchery growth and was about twice that in central Arizona urban lakes.

**Early maturation**
- At least five ripe males (tuberculate and expressing milt) were found among the fish removed in November, when they were about nine months old. The previous earliest recorded age at male maturity was two years.

**Harvest and survival**
- Fourteen NFWG members removed 109 juveniles (mean 354 mm) during the week of 23 November using trammel nets and electrofishing. All were PIT tagged and released into Lake Mohave.
- Rotenone treatment on 25 November yielded five more suckers; three were revived and released and two died.
- Total harvest of juveniles from Yuma Cove was 296 (298 including two dead fish found after poisoning).
- Genetic analysis indicated that at least 10 of the 29 females selected produced young in Yuma Cove.

**Mark-recapture accuracy**
- Three multiple-census estimates from 16 June to 29 July gave 306 (original Schnable), 276 (modified Schnable) and 329 (Schumacher-Eschmeyer, 95% CI 251-472).
- These predicted 93 to 111% of the actual harvest, according to the authors.
- Only 64% (34 of 53) marked fish were recovered after 22 July, which the authors attributed to mortality during poor water quality. Adjusting for this gave a predicted harvest of about 211 fish, within the extrapolated range of 161 to 302.

**Light trapping (Appendix C)**
- A total of 226 trap sets (1,327 trap hours) were completed from 19 February to 30 April, 758 hours in Yuma Cove and 569 in Arizona Bay.
- Dog food and cyalume light sticks (about 1.0 lux) gave maximum capture rates of 0.1 and 0.2 larvae per hour.
- White light traps were effective. 12-W halogen (190 lux) gave 2.8 larvae/h in Yuma Cove and 29.8 larvae/h in Arizona Bay. 55-W halogen (460 lux) gave 6.0 and 14.5 larvae/h.
- The authors suggest the 55-W bulbs may have exceeded the sensitivity threshold in clear water.
- Light also attracted zooplankton, odonate larvae, Hemiptera and toads.

**Odonate predation (Appendix D, laboratory)**
- In Experiment 1, 12-day-old larvae (11.8 ± 0.7 mm TL) had 24% survival with dragonfly naiads and 19% with damselfly naiads, compared with 88% in controls.
- In Experiment 2, 35-day-old larvae (estimated 14-15 mm) had 47% survival with pooled odonates versus 82% in controls. Larger larvae were less susceptible.
- Dense pondweed may increase predation by giving odonates access to more of the water column.

**Davis Cove grow-out**
- About 25 volunteers installed the barrier net on 4-5 February. Divers inspect the net bimonthly.
- Electrofishing, gill netting, fyke netting, seining and angling removed about 800 pounds of fish during the week of 16 June. Complete removal was not achieved.
- About 10,000 juveniles from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] were stocked on 24 June, averaging 68.1 mm TL. Mean length reached 127.9 mm by 10 September (Table 2).
- Suckers were nocturnal and associated with sandy substrate near dense vegetation after dark.
- Preliminary data suggest Davis Cove growth was slower than Yuma Cove growth. The authors could not determine the cause (habitat, stocking size, non-native fish, or flood effects).

**Constraints identified by the authors**
1. Yuma Cove space is insufficient for target production; additional habitats are needed.
2. Mortality between hatching and juvenile size is the largest source of loss. Juvenile mortality during poor water quality reduced numbers by about one third.
3. Aquatic vegetation hampers sampling and recovery and needs control.
4. The growth difference between Davis and Yuma Cove needs investigation.
5. The effect of lake level fluctuations on backwater fish is unknown.

**Production goal**
- The authors state that 6,000 to 12,000 sub-adults must be produced annually to replace the adult population within 5 to 10 years, which is 20 to 40 times the 1992 output.

**1993 outlook**
- Continue on-site spawning and rearing at Yuma Cove, focusing on production and genetic diversity.
- Control aquatic weeds, with a liner installed in early 1993 and herbicide as a backup. A herbicide permit from the National Park Service was still pending in 1992.
- Continue stocking Davis Cove with fish from Yuma Cove and Dexter NFH, plus several hundred subadult (12-inch) bonytail chub and razorback suckers, depending on predation.

**Public relations**
- A Tucson public television station filmed the project for a February broadcast. Reclamation produced a 13-minute video, a poster and a brochure. The National Park Service added the project to its interpretive program, with an information box at Davis Cove.
