# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies: NRC Supplemental Advisory Report and Review Correspondence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This item is a bundle of [[National Research Council]] (NRC) correspondence and a supplemental letter report from the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Oversight Committee]], issued to the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] in 1988. It follows up on the committee's 1987 report, [[River And Dam Management]], which reviewed the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES, conducted 1982–1986/1987). The Bureau asked for immediate advice on (1) interim research for summer and fall 1988, (2) the highest-priority 1987 recommendations, and (3) future research in Glen Canyon over the next decade or so.

The committee issued four recommendations: establish an independent [[Science Research Advisory Group]] at the Department of the Interior level; continue sediment and fish monitoring; perform an [[Economic Analysis]] of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations; and secure and manage GCES data through a [[Data Base Management]] system. The letter also covers the [[NRC Review Process]] that governs the report.

## Background & Description

The item contains several documents:

- **Memorandum (May 18, 1988):** Sheila D. David, Water Science and Technology Board (WSTB) staff officer, asked four reviewers (John Boland, Robert Sievers, Wendell Minckley, Charles Howe) to review the draft supplemental letter report. Comments were due May 27 for a June delivery deadline.
- **Supplemental Letter Report (third draft, May 1988; final version July 1988):** Responds to the Bureau's three requests and sets out four recommendations. Page references in parentheses point to the 1987 NRC review.
- **Statement of Task (February 3, 1988):** Expands the committee's scope to (1) set priorities among the 1987 recommendations and (2) provide scientific advisory review of GCES work plans and products. The previous statement was dated June 26, 1986.
- **Guidelines for Review of Reports (NAS, June 15, 1979):** Lists eight key review questions covering responsiveness, evidence, uncertainty, clarity, completeness, relation to other literature, fairness, and policy handling.
- **Cover letter (July 11, 1988):** Transmits five copies of the report to David L. Wegner, GCES Program Manager, under Contract Number 6-FC-40-04240. The next meeting was set for October 26–28, 1988, in Page, Arizona.

The GCES was a four-year program of roughly 30 environmental studies. The report notes that the Executive Review Committee was about to recommend the future direction of GCES research and of Glen Canyon Dam operations to the Secretary of the Interior.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Grand Canyon/Colorado River ecosystem is shaped by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and the reservoir it created, [[Lake Powell]]. The report identifies several key processes and concerns:

- **Sediment disequilibrium:** Closure of the dam, the filling of Lake Powell, and subsequent operations since the early 1980s have left sediment transport and storage out of balance. Sediment from the Colorado River is now interrupted by the reservoir, while tributary sediment still reaches the canyon.
- **Beach dynamics:** Some [[Grand Canyon Beaches]] may be degrading, others stabilizing through vegetation that did not establish under pre-dam annual floods, and others growing with tributary sediment inputs. These beaches are the public's most visible concern.
- **Native fishes:** The [[Humpback Chub]] is the central endangered species. Its survival may be affected by dam operations. The [[Little Colorado River]] population appears to be the largest remaining anywhere.
- **Exotic trout:** Trout are non-native and arrived after dam construction. The cold water released from the dam, which contributed to the loss of native species, supports the trout fishery that anglers value. Trout populations are influenced by the recent hydrologic regime and by changes in angling regulations.
- **Downstream connections:** The sediment budget links Lee's Ferry, the Paria River, the Little Colorado River, Phantom Ranch, and Diamond Creek to [[Lake Mead]].
- **Legal framework:** The [[Endangered Species Act]] and the [[Law Of The River]] constrain allowable operations, and the [[Western Area Power Administration]] network is part of the power system affected by them.

## Key Findings

**1. Establish a Science Research Advisory Group (Recommendation 1)**
- The committee recommends a single advisory group of three to five senior scientists not currently employed by the Department of the Interior, reporting above the four DOI agencies: the Bureau of Reclamation, the National Park Service, the Fish and Wildlife Service, and the U.S. Geological Survey.
- The chief scientist should be retained full- or part-time for long-term continuity. Other members might serve without pay or on consulting fees, with rotating or staggered terms.
- Required disciplines: sediment transport/hydrology/geomorphology; fishery science/aquatic ecology; ecosystem science/arid lands ecology; resource economics/water resource operations; and conservation biology.
- Diagnosis of the 1982–1987 GCES: planning was weak. Agencies recommended tasks they could perform rather than high-priority tasks, which left many subtasks marginal and omitted others, such as beach-building hydrodynamics. The debris-flow work was expert but not connected to system-wide sediment movement.
- Early priorities: work plan for an ecosystem investigation of the Colorado River in Grand Canyon, centered on (1) Glen Canyon Dam operation, endangered native species, and the trout fishery, and (2) the fate of riverside beaches. Both were judged "centrally critical" by the GCES Executive Review Committee.
- Procurement: agency scientists should be considered first for tasks they are best suited to perform. Remaining tasks should go to competitive requests for proposals, with rules changed so that the best private-sector and academic scientists can compete.

**2. Continue Monitoring (Recommendation 2)**
- **Sediment gages:** Six sites should continue or be reinstated: Colorado River at Lee's Ferry; Paria River near Lee's Ferry; Colorado River above the Little Colorado confluence; Little Colorado River at Cameron; Colorado River at Phantom Ranch; and Colorado River at Diamond Creek. The long-term sites at Lee's Ferry, Phantom Ranch, and Little Colorado were discontinued when Lake Powell was full. Removing any one site would weaken the system-wide sediment budget.
- **Analyses required before modeling:** assess water-discharge error (which magnifies sediment-load error); uncertainty in the sediment-discharge relationship; time-sampling uncertainty, since major flow events carry most sediment and may go unmeasured; and bias from the empirical discharge–sediment relationship and from most sediment moving at high discharges.
- **Native fishes:** Much more intensive research is needed on humpback chub. Basic life-history data are missing: reproduction timing, fecundity, survival of young, maturation, longevity, age structure, and environmental controls. Open questions include the genetic uniformity of the Little Colorado population, possible hybridization with other Gila species, the reasons for its high survival, and its relationship to mainstem fish. Population records cover only a few years, so each reproductive season matters.
- **Trout:** Records are short, so data from each season are important.
- **Beaches:** Tracking the condition of extant beaches is a simple, low-cost activity that can be tied to monitoring. Results could inform whether sediment deposition can be managed through discharge manipulation.

**3. Perform an Economic Analysis (Recommendation 3)**
- The Bureau should evaluate lost hydroelectric revenues, the cost of buying peak-period energy from other sources, and potential benefits to recreation and the environment.
- The Bureau must define the maximum legal and physical range of Glen Canyon Dam operating options, covering diurnal fluctuation (timing and amplitude), spill control and planned maximum flows, and temperature management through multiple-outlet withdrawals from Lake Powell.
- Models should state the operating rule explicitly and formally, including how monthly release targets depend on storage and snowpack, so that sensitivity (spill frequency, release shortages) can be tested by changing the rule. Models must follow the Law of the River but also simulate deviations from it.
- The Bureau should identify costs and benefits of each option and their system-wide effects on sport fishery, endangered species habitat, recreation, and flora and fauna, especially within National Park Service responsibilities.
- The dam should be used for controlled release experiments that test hypotheses about flood, drought, and volume effects on sandbars, aquatic invertebrates, fishes, and riparian vegetation.

**4. Ensure Data Preservation and Establish a Data Base Management System (Recommendation 4)**
- The DOI should make physical preservation of all GCES data, records, and results a priority, and should fund documentation to strengthen the record.
- A data base management system should archive and catalog results and support retrieval for new analyses.
- Data protocols should cover quality assurance and quality control, explicit documentation of data sets, and documentation of methods, sample sites, schedules, units, and data-use history.
- Publication in peer-reviewed journals is encouraged as the surest route to scientific acceptance.
- A dedicated data base facility is needed, with adequate personnel, space, and equipment.

**5. Review Process and Administrative Status**
- The committee will review further work plans and meet again with the GCES program manager and Bureau/DOI personnel.
- The NAS review guidelines require that the report be responsive to its charge, be supported by evidence, state uncertainties, be clear, be complete, relate to existing literature, be fair, and handle policy questions with care. Reviewers are not to discuss the report publicly until it is released.
- Contract: 6-FC-40-04240. Cover letter signed by G. Richard Marzolf, Chairman.
