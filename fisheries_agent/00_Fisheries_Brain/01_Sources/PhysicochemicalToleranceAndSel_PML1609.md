# Physicochemical Tolerance and Selectivity of Stream Fishes as Related to Their Geographic Ranges and Issues of Distributional Ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter (Matthews, in *Community and Evolutionary Ecology of North American Stream Fishes*, edited by Matthews and Heins, 1987) tests whether the geographic and local distributions of North American stream fishes reflect physicochemical tolerance and "selectivity" in combination. Tolerance is defined as the ability to survive acute stress; selectivity is the relative breadth of a population's distribution along an environmental gradient. The study compares [[Notropis]] species from physically harsh [[Prairie Stream Fishes|prairie-margin]] streams with congeners from benign [[Upland Streams]] in Oklahoma and Arkansas, and examines intraspecific variation in two widespread species. The author concludes that physicochemical harshness is an important, though not exclusive, factor shaping stream-fish distributions and assemblage composition.

## Background & Description

The chapter addresses a gap in the literature. Many studies have measured temperature or oxygen tolerance, or habitat preference, in fishes, but few tested whether these traits, singly or together, explain distributions across harsh-benign environmental gradients. The author proposes three hypothetical tolerance-selectivity strategies for fishes in harsh versus benign habitats:

- **Type 1:** Broad tolerance and low selectivity, suited to fluctuating habitats.
- **Type 2:** Narrow tolerance but acute selectivity, allowing fish to track optimal conditions.
- **Type 3:** Narrow tolerance and low selectivity, typical of benign habitats.

Fish are used as a model because ectotherms' body temperatures track water temperature, so physiology is closely linked to the physicochemical regime. The chapter notes that many Great Plains streams exhibit much greater thermal and oxygen extremes than upland streams.

**Study species.** Eleven [[Notropis]] species were tested, grouped a priori into a "prairie stream" group (including [[Notropis lutrensis]], *N. girardi*, *N. stramineus*, *N. venustus*, and *N. atherinoides*) and an "upland" group (including *N. boops*, *N. nubilus*, *N. chrysocephalus*, *N. rubellus*, and *N. pilsbryi*). *Notropis ambraesilis* was classified as upland but occupies low-gradient streams in parts of its range. Additional work addressed a [[Brier Creek]] fish assemblage and intraspecific variation in *Etheostoma spectabile* and *N. lutrensis*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Upland streams** (Ozark, Ouachita, and Arbuckle uplands) have permanent flow, relatively constant conditions, and few physicochemical gradients. Diel temperature variation is minimal because of spring inputs and riparian shading.
- **Great Plains streams** in southwestern Oklahoma and Texas are wide, shallow, and variable in discharge. Diel and annual fluctuations approach or exceed tolerance limits for many species. Daily thermal fluctuations of 10–13 °C are common in summer.
- **Headwaters** that are not spring-fed are often intermittent, dewatering in dry summers. In [[Brier Creek]], August 1982 headwater pools showed diel fluctuations of 9–10 °C, a mean daily maximum of 32.6 °C, and early-morning oxygen of 0.4–2 ppm in 10 of 11 pools. A mid-reach site, fed by seeps and shaded, fluctuated only about 1.2 °C.
- **Zonation:** Fish faunas in Brier Creek show a distinct, depauperate headwater fauna and a more speciose fauna in the mid-reach and lower watershed near Lake Texoma.
- **Historical stability:** The author assumes species have occupied their present contrasting habitats since the end of the Wisconsin glaciation, long enough for adaptation.

## Key Findings

**1. Thermal tolerance tracks prairie versus upland distribution.**
- Critical thermal maximum (CTM) means differed significantly among all Notropis species. Values ranged from a low for *N. pilsbryi* to 36.4 °C for *N. lutrensis* (the scanned low value is partly illegible).
- Prairie species had significantly higher thermal tolerance than upland species (a priori test).
- Among the 11 species, a direct relationship existed between thermal tolerance and geographic distribution. Four of five prairie species showed little avoidance of hot conditions, so their thermal strategy relies more on tolerance than on acute avoidance.

**2. Oxygen tolerance differs sharply.**
- In tests lasting 8.5–10 h at 0.8 ppm, four of five prairie species showed 33–44% survival. No upland species exceeded 5% survival, and all individuals of four upland species died.
- Prairie species also showed significantly sharper avoidance of low-oxygen zones. The low end of the oxygen gradient averaged 1.35 ppm in 1982 and 2.34 ppm in 1981.

**3. Strategy classification.**
- Prairie species generally fit Type 1 for temperature (high tolerance). Several also showed acute oxygen selectivity, so the author classifies their adaptation as a combination of Types 1 and 2.
- Upland species were more consistent with Types 2 or 3.
- No species showed a pure Type 2 strategy for harsh environments, so the author rejects that pure form for these Notropis.

**4. Within-watershed patterns (Brier Creek).**
- Thermal tolerance, thermal selectivity, and oxygen selectivity were not significantly correlated with longitudinal position.
- Oxygen survival (below 1 ppm, 8.5–10 h) was significantly and positively correlated with longitudinal position of 12 species (r_s = 0.64; P < 0.05). Only fish capable of surviving hypoxia persist in headwater pools.
- Ten of 11 common Brier Creek species showed high thermal tolerance, implying that thermal stress is frequent enough in ecological time to select for tolerance throughout the watershed.
- A 1980 event with pool temperatures of about 39 °C caused fish deaths.

**5. Recolonization of a dewatered reach.**
- A 1.5-km reach of Brier Creek dewatered in the 1982 drought and remained dry until March 1983. Monthly seine collections from March to September 1983 documented recolonization.
- Successful colonists tended to arrive from elsewhere in substantial numbers, spawn successfully, survive late-summer drought, or combine these traits.
- Successful colonists showed markedly higher hypoxia survival than nonsuccessful colonists (the scanned percentages are not reliably recoverable), supporting the link between oxygen tolerance and colonization.

**6. Intraspecific variation.**
- *Etheostoma spectabile* (Orangethroat Darter) populations from four distinct thermal habitats differed significantly in CTM. The population from a cool, constant spring run had the lowest mean CTM, and one from a sharply fluctuating environment had the highest. The author suggests this plasticity may explain the species' wide occupation of thermally different environments.
- *Notropis lutrensis* (Red Shiner) populations across 18 sites from north Kansas to south Texas (May–June 1983) showed no significant difference in mean CTM. All means fell within 0.35 °C of 36 °C, which the author considers "static" thermal tolerance.
- Thermal selectivity of 14 *N. lutrensis* populations did differ significantly (F = 6.74; p < 0.01). Populations from six adjacent north-south drainages in Kansas and Oklahoma showed more acute selectivity. Selectivity was not correlated with CTM (r = −0.047), so no tradeoff between the two traits was detected.

**7. Overall conclusions.**
- Among the Notropis species, tolerance is more closely linked to distribution than selectivity is. Good correspondence exists between laboratory tolerance and distribution in naturally harsh environments.
- The findings support the idea that physicochemical harshness helps sort species into assemblages, and that assemblage composition is not solely the product of biotic interactions. Biotic interactions probably fine-tune composition in stable periods.
- The author notes that the extent to which species-level physicochemical responses regulate assemblage composition remains largely unresolved.

**Methods note.** Fish were acclimated for at least two weeks before thermal tests and one week before oxygen tests. CTM tests heated fish at 1 °C/min and used loss of equilibrium as the endpoint. Gradient chambers (180 cm × 9 cm, 3.5 cm water depth) produced thermal gradients from about 15 °C to 37 °C. Selectivity was defined as the location holding 83% of the fish, a conservative measure.
