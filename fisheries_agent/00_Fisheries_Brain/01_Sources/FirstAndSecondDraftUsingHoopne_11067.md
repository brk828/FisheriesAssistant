# Using Hoopnets and Other Sampling Methods to Assess Microhabitat Use by Fishes in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #sampling-methods

## Executive Summary

This document is a research proposal and methodological framework developed by Owen T. Gorman (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Pinetop Fishery Assistance Office) for assessing microhabitat use by fishes in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), with special emphasis on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The proposal, drafted in August-September 1991, addresses the biases and limitations of hoopnet/fykenet sampling for studying habitat use patterns in stream fish assemblages. It outlines low-impact sampling protocols designed to minimize stress on the resident [[Humpback Chub]] population while providing data on seasonal habitat use, spawning locations, and the effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. The study is part of the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Phase II and involves coordination with Arizona Game and Fish Department and Arizona State University. The LCR fish assemblage is relatively simple, dominated by [[Humpback Chub]], with subdominant species including [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]].

## Background & Description

Hoopnets and fykenets are widely used in fishery research to assess relative abundance, distribution, and movement of fishes in lakes, reservoirs, and larger streams. However, their biases are well documented: capture frequencies vary with season, net location, and exhibit strong species-specific and size-specific differences. While hoopnets can provide reasonable population parameter estimates, location biases and species-specific capture efficiencies make them less than ideal for studying habitat use patterns in diverse stream fish assemblages. The relative catchability of each species must be evaluated in pilot studies, and results should be compared with other methodologies (trammel nets, seines, electroshocking).

The [[Little Colorado River]] assemblage is relatively simple and depauperate, dominated by endemic native fishes. The [[Humpback Chub]] is the dominant species, with common subdominants including [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Less common species include common carp and channel catfish. Only adult common carp appear to avoid hoopnets, making this sampling method potentially suitable for the LCR assemblage.

Hoopnets do not provide ideal individual point data on microhabitat use (as observational studies do); instead, they provide data on habitat patch use. The area sampled by a hoopnet is time-dependent: initial captures recruit from the local neighborhood, while longer soak times recruit fish from greater distances based on home range behavior. Escape rates also increase with time and vary by species; studies show that all individuals of some species escape within 3 days. Therefore, nets should have soak times of no more than 1 day to minimize time-dependent bias.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study focuses on the lower 21 km of the [[Little Colorado River]] within the [[Grand Canyon]], a turbid stream containing a relatively depauperate fish fauna dominated by endemic native species. The [[Humpback Chub]] is federally listed as endangered, and while the LCR population may number in the thousands, it has limited distribution and is subjected to year-round sampling by multiple research teams. The resident population occupies approximately 14 km of river, and adults from the mainstream [[Colorado River]] population migrate into the LCR to spawn during spring months.

Five study areas are proposed: the Confluence (0-1 km), Lower Base Camp/Powell Canyon area (2.5-3.5 km), Upper Base Camp/Salt Trail Canyon area (10-12 km), Atomizer Falls reach (14-15.5 km), and Blue Springs reach (20-21 km). The Atomizer Falls are viewed as the terminus of [[Humpback Chub]] distribution in the LCR.

The study aims to evaluate the impact of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on [[Humpback Chub]] populations, particularly how dam releases affect physicochemical conditions and microhabitat in the confluence area. Discharge-frequency and flow duration curves will be developed to understand how various flow regimes affect stream habitat, particularly during seasonal high flows in spring (spawning period), summer flood events, and low base flows when young-of-year chub are present.

## Key Findings

**Recommended Hoopnet Sampling Protocols:**

1. **Suitability Assessment**: Efficacy of hoopnets in sampling all assemblage members must be evaluated through comparison with other methods (seining, trammel nets, electroshocking).

2. **Net Design**: Nets should be physically as small as possible with mesh size allowing capture of all assemblage members. Smaller nets (20" x 4' "mini-hoopnets" with 1/4" mesh) can sample more habitats than larger ones.

3. **No Baiting**: Nets should never be baited, as this grossly increases capture bias and confounds trophic versus spatial utilization patterns.

4. **Soak Times**: Nets should be run to sample diurnal and nocturnal periods (12-hour day and night soak times). To reduce time-dependent bias, nets should not be left in place more than one 24-hour period.

5. **Habitat Availability**: Must be determined in areas sampled by hoopnets. Nets should be set to sample habitats in proportion to habitat availability.

6. **Grid Deployment**: Nets should be run in grids to reduce sampling biases related to presence/absence of hoopnets. Within 500-750 m study areas, 15 hoopnets will be deployed in regular grids with 10-20 m spacing.

7. **Pre-Net-Set Conditions**: Habitat measurements should reflect conditions before net placement, centered on the physical center of the net (not the mouth).

8. **Sampling Frequency**: Nets should not sample study areas more than once per month to reduce learned avoidance behaviors.

9. **Water Column Sampling**: Smaller hoops can be rigidized and suspended to sample different strata (bottom, mid-water, upper).

**Low-Impact Sampling Protocols:**

- Nets/traps set and run every 12 hours to reduce predation by catfish and decrease stress on captive chubs
- Not set in same locations for more than three consecutive days
- Not run in same locations more often than every four weeks
- Hoopnet/trap grids within 100 m considered same location
- Running nets every 12 hours will detect diel patterns of habitat use

**Complementary Methods:**

- Standard minnow traps (Gee's 1/4" mesh) complement hoopnet sampling in shallow edge habitats, with at least 8 traps per grid at 1-2 m spacing
- Seining limited to narrow array of edge and shallow habitats due to travertine and boulders; most effective for small and young-of-year fishes
- Trammel nets provide point-of-capture habitat data but are size-selective, cannot be placed in all habitats, require frequent running (every 6 hours), cause physical damage through entanglement, and occasionally kill endangered [[Humpback Chub]]
- Electroshocking ineffective in LCR due to high water conductivity
- Radio telemetry has high signal extinction rates in high-conductivity LCR waters
- Ultrasonic telemetry more suitable, with 1 km range; successfully used in Arizona Project canals where high conductivity rendered radio tags useless

**Proposed Collection Limits:**

- Unlimited capture, handle, and release of all fish species
- Maximum 25 young-of-year individuals up to 100 mm TL per native species due to incidental mortality
- 100 [[Speckled Dace]] (all size classes)
- 100 per introduced exotic species (all size classes)
- Up to 10 incidental mortalities of adult native fishes (>100 mm TL) for [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Razorback Sucker]]

**Study Objectives:**

- Quantify habitat use by juvenile and adult [[Humpback Chub]] in lower 21 km of LCR and other [[Colorado River]] tributaries
- Evaluate potential for establishing new spawning aggregations
- Evaluate impact of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on populations
- Assess habitat use by other native and introduced species
- Develop discharge-frequency and flow duration curves to predict habitat characteristics during various flow regimes
- Identify spawning and nursery habitats for recovery efforts

**Experimental Stream Research:**

Proposal to construct portable streamside experimental stream to determine habitat preferences of various age classes of [[Humpback Chub]] and how preferences are modified by depth, current, substrate, cover, turbidity, time of day, intraspecifics, interspecifics, predation threat, temperature, and season. Ideal location at dam below Big Canyon. Research to be developed and executed by PhD student under direction of Drs. O. Eugene Maughan (Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit), Owen T. Gorman (USFWS), and Stuart C. Leon (USFWS).

**Coordination:**

Research closely coordinated with Arizona Game and Fish Department (Dennis Kubley, Rob Clarkson) and Arizona State University-Navajo Fish and Wildlife (Drs. Michael Douglas, Paul Marsh) to avoid duplication and minimize impact on fish populations. Exclusive study areas designated for USFWS sampling maximum one week per month; other teams may sample during other months following low-impact protocols.
