# Life History of the German Carp, *Cyprinus carpio*

## Executive Summary
This 1967 literature review, compiled by Jerry L. Stegman at Kansas State College, synthesizes life history data on the [[German Carp]] (*[[Cyprinus carpio]]*), a member of the family [[Cyprinidae]]. The report covers taxonomy, geographic distribution, morphology, feeding ecology, growth, reproduction, behavior, population dynamics, disease susceptibility, and human utilization. Key conclusions highlight the species' broad environmental tolerance, omnivorous feeding strategy, slow post-maturity growth, high fecundity, general disease resistance, and its controversial status as both a food resource and an ecological nuisance impacting game fish and aquatic vegetation.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomy**: The genus *Cyprinus* is represented in Kansas by a single species, *Cyprinus carpio*, one of the larger members of the family [[Cyprinidae]] (Breukelman, 1950).
- **Races in Kansas**: Three morphological races occur:
  - **Completely scaled form** — most common
  - **[[Mirror Carp]]** — large scales restricted to irregular rows along back and sides
  - **[[Leather Carp]]** — nearly scaleless
- **Morphological Description** (Beckman, 1952):
  - Body robust, compressed; back considerably elevated
  - Head conical, tapering rapidly to a bluntly pointed snout
  - Mouth small, anterior, oblique; maxillary does not extend past anterior nostrils
  - Two pairs of maxillary barbels
  - Teeth broad and truncate with molar surfaces (formula 1,1,3–3,1,1)
  - Intestine longer than body; peritoneum gray, speckled
  - Dorsal and anal fins bear a strong toothed spine
  - Dorsal fin rays: 17–21; anal rays: 5 or 6
  - Large scales, lateral line count ~35–38
- **Coloration**: Olivaceous dorsally, bluish to dusky upper body; sides and belly yellowish. No apparent external sexual dimorphism.
- **Note**: Because three distinct races exist in Kansas, not all described characteristics apply uniformly to every individual.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Range and Distribution
- Native to **Asia**; introduced into **Europe** ~1800 A.D.
- Introduced into the **United States** in 1872 (Simon, 1951); now widespread across the country.
- First introduced into **Kansas** waters in 1880; subsequently became one of the state's most abundant and widespread fish species (Breukelman).
- Associated with warm, shallow, rich lakes and streams (Ward, 1954; Oklahoma); tolerant of cooler waters in Wyoming (Simon).
- In Kansas, fluctuating water levels are believed to be the primary factor restricting carp to larger, more permanent streams and lakes, as smaller streams/ponds dry out seasonally.

### Food and Feeding
- Carp are **omnivorous**, typically feeding on the bottom by "rooting" in the mud.
- **Ontogenetic shift**: Young carp consume a higher percentage of plant material than adults (Struthers, 1929, 1930, 1932).
- **Seasonal diet variation** (Moen, 1953, Iowa lakes):
  - Winter food: 100% animal matter
  - Summer food: plant material rarely exceeded 12%
  - Higher proportion of inorganic "debris" (mud, sand) found in summer stomachs, likely due to lack of winter plant foods
- Food abundance in the environment correlated with quantity ingested by carp (Moen).
- **Consumption rates**: 100 lbs of carp required an average of 370 cc of food per acre per day in summer (~5 lbs food/acre/week).
- **Digestion**: Stomach contents fully digested within 24 hours at water temperatures ≥49°F (Moen).
- **Feeding activity patterns**: Continuous feeding during cool weather; early morning/evening feeding peaks during summer heat (Harlan and Speaker, 1956).

### Behavior and Species Interactions
- Carp in large reservoirs (Oklahoma) concentrate over favorable bottom types (Ward, 1954).
- Observed in the Platte River (Wyoming): large schools moved into moderate current but dispersed or relocated when encountering swift water (author's observation; corroborated by Dyche).
- **Effects on other species**:
  - Accused of eating spawn of other fish species and disturbing spawning grounds (Simon).
  - Charged with uprooting near-shore vegetation.
  - However, Dyche's examination of 1,200 carp stomachs found the few fish eggs present were carp's own eggs — not those of other species.
  - Centrarchids (sunfish family) guarding nests were observed capable of repelling much larger carp or even carp schools (Dyche).
- **Vegetation destruction mechanism**: Experiments (Threinen and Helm, 1954; Tryon, 1954) indicated aquatic plants were destroyed not by direct "rooting" but by resulting **turbidity** — still considered a detrimental ecological impact on game fish habitat (Mraz and Cooper, 1957).

### Diseases and Parasites
- Carp are largely **resistant** to parasites common to many other freshwater fish, especially flesh-dwelling parasitic worms (Dyche).
- **Leech infestation**: *Piscicola punctata* epidemic reported in the Rock River, Illinois (Thompson, 1927); carp among infested species.
- **Popeye disease**: Reported in carp from the Mississippi River near Keokuk, Iowa (Barnickol and Starrett); caused by eye grub *Diplostomum* sp. (Fischthal, 1949).
- **Dropsy**: Reported in European carp (Wonder, 1955), caused by bacterium *Pseudomonas punctata*.
- Overall, carp showed general resistance to most other fish diseases/parasites reviewed.

- Growth rate slows notably after the **5th year**; weight gain slows after the **6th year**.
- Mississippi River commercial carp averaged **2.65 lbs**, suggesting a mean commercial catch age of **3–4 years** (Barnickol and Starrett, 1951).
- **Temperature effects**: Cold water slows growth; warm water accelerates growth and metabolism/digestion (Watts and Harvey, 1946; Durham, 1955 — analogy drawn from smallmouth bass studies).
- **Aquaculture findings (Japan)**: Agitation/crowding in rearing ponds reduced growth rate by ~5% over a 210-day season; ammonia-nitrogen and phosphate accumulation from excreta decomposition negatively affected growth ratio (Kawamoto, 1957).

### Reproduction
- **Maturity size**: Males mature at 14–15 in.; females at 12.5–14.5 in. (Barnickol and Starrett).
- **Maturity age**: Males ~2 years; females ~3 years (Carlander).
- **Spawning behavior**: Both sexes "splash a lot" during spawning; eggs abandoned immediately after deposition (Ward, 1954).
- **Direct observation** (Minckley, Blue River near Manhattan, Kansas, May 1956):
  - Occurred in ~12 inches of water along the bank in moderately swift current
  - Female led, male swam alongside with head level to her dorsal fin
  - Eggs and milt released simultaneously; eggs adhered to vegetation and substrate
- **Fecundity**: 200,000 to 2,000,000 eggs per female (multiple sources).
- **Early mortality**: No direct survival studies found; adhesive egg quality suggests low pre-hatch mortality, with most losses occurring in the first month of life.
- **Predation on young**: Documented predators include [[Bass]] (*Micropterus* spp.), [[Crappie]] (*Pomoxis* spp.), [[Catfish]] (*Ictalurus* spp. and *Pylodictis olivaris*), and [[Sunfish]] (*Lepomis* spp.) — predation likely the primary cause of juvenile mortality (Dyche, 1914).

### Populations
- **Standing crop** (Carlander, 1955):
  - Ponds (unfed): maximum **2,100 lbs/acre**
  - Lakes/reservoirs: maximum **600 lbs/acre**; mean **95 lbs/acre**
- **Natural mortality**: 4.6% of known adult population over a 3-month period in Wisconsin ponds (Mraz and Cooper, 1957).
- **Density-dependent recruitment**: Number of young-of-year carp varied inversely with adult stocking rate in controlled pond experiments (Mraz and Cooper).
- Population composition attributed to two general factor categories per Lagler (1956): (1) genetic/physiological makeup of the species, and (2) total environmental context.

### Relations to Man
- **Japan**: Carp is a prized food fish; intensive carp farms reportedly yield up to **1.33 million lbs/acre** (Kawamoto, 1957).
- **Europe**: Cultivated since the 1200s; considered excellent food fish.
- **United States**: Carp farming found **not economically profitable** due to high costs of fertilization/supplemental feeding relative to low market price (Swingle, 1954).
- **Commercial harvest data**:
  - U.S. total: ~15–20 million lbs/year (Carson, 1943)
  - Missouri (1950): >360,000 lbs, sold at $0.20–$0.25/lb (Keefe, 1952)
  - Mississippi River (Illinois): harvested via trammel nets, haul seines, hoop nets, wing nets, trap nets, basket traps, and trotlines (Barnickol and Starrett, 1951)
- **Management stance**: Reviewed literature uniformly emphasized **control and eradication** of carp populations rather than conservation, reflecting the species' status as an invasive/nuisance fish relative to native [[Game Fish]] and aquatic vegetation health.
