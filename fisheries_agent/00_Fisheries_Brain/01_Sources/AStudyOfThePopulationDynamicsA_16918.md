# Population Dynamics and Trophic Ecology of Brook Trout in Two Subalpine Lakes, Southeastern Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salvelinus

## Executive Summary

This 1964 final report (Part VI of the [[Studies On The Ecology Of The Brook Trout In The Rocky Mountain Region]] series, by Thomas R. Garofalo) examines the [[Brook Trout]] fisheries of [[Towner Lake]] and [[Little Brooklyn Lake]] in southeastern Wyoming during summer 1962. It was a joint product of the [[University Of Wyoming]] and the [[Wyoming Game And Fish Commission]], funded under Dingell-Johnson project F-23-R-4. The study estimated fish stock, recruitment, production, harvest, and diet, and tested the effect of removing yearling trout from Towner Lake.

Key results: Towner Lake held an estimated 4,016 brook trout and Little Brooklyn Lake 2,672. Midge larvae (Tendipedidae) and pupae, with cladocerans, were the main food items. The 1962 creel harvest was about 582 trout from Towner Lake and 325 from Little Brooklyn Lake. Roughly 930 yearlings were removed from Towner Lake to test the effect on older fish.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** This segment was the sixth in the Rocky Mountain brook trout project. Earlier segments covered age determination from scales, population dynamics in several lakes, and beaver-trout relationships on Pole Mountain. Those studies called for better information on how efficiently brook trout use available food and on production and yield in these fisheries.

**Objectives (1962):**
1. Evaluate stock, recruitment, production, and crop.
2. Evaluate the food of these fisheries.
3. Remove a known segment of age-group 0 and I trout from Towner Lake to measure later effects on growth and the crop of older fish.
4. Continue spawning observations if weather allowed.

**Methods overview:**
- **Physical-chemical:** Dissolved oxygen (Winkler method), pH, alkalinity, free CO₂, and temperature, measured twice monthly at surface and bottom with a [[Kemmerer Water Sampler]].
- **Plankton:** Vertical samples from three stations, four times per summer, with a Wisconsin net. Counts used the Sedgwick-Rafter method.
- **Benthos:** [[Ekman Dredge]] samples at twelve stations per month (transect design, 6 sq ft per month per lake). Midge larvae were identified to genus where possible.
- **Emergence:** Screen tubes trapped adult midges at inlets.
- **Aufwuchs:** Organisms on [[Elodea Canadensis]] at five stations, expressed per 100 g wet Elodea.
- **Fish populations:** Three-group [[Mark And Recapture]] using the [[Peterson-Lincoln Index]] with Schnabel and Bailey modifications. Group A was young-of-year (under 2.0 in), B was 2–5.5 in (age I), and C was over 5.5 in (age II and older).
- **Growth and diet:** Samples of about 25 fish per age-group at roughly 10-day intervals, with instantaneous growth rates. Stomachs were examined by fullness, composition, and weight ([[Stomach Content Analysis]]).
- **Creel census:** Stratified by season, time of day, and day type, with fishing pressure counts and interviews ([[Creel Census]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Physical conditions were similar to 1959, and both lakes were thermally and chemically similar to earlier years.
- Surface temperature in Towner Lake ranged from 40°F (June 17) to 56°F (August 16). Little Brooklyn Lake ranged from 43°F (June 17) to 59°F (July 10).
- [[Dissolved Oxygen]] in Towner Lake rose from 7.3 to 8.4 ppm at the surface. The pH rose from 7.2 to 8.9, and total alkalinity rose from 27 to 47 ppm.
- In Little Brooklyn Lake, pH rose from 7.7 to 7.9 at the surface and from 7.5 to 8.1 at the bottom. Alkalinity rose from 35 to 37 ppm at the bottom.
- Dissolved oxygen in Towner Lake was lower in July–August than in 1958, possibly because [[Elodea Canadensis]] grew less densely in 1962.

**Plankton.** Quantitative estimates were not obtained. Many samples were fouled with mud and Elodea, and open-water plankters were scarce. Diatoms (Fragilaria, Tabellaria, Asterionella), green algae, and cladocerans (Daphnia longispina) were present. Cyclops and Diaptomus nauplii were also observed.

**Benthos and aufwuchs.** Midge larvae dominated.
- Towner Lake benthos standing crop was 153.6 lb/acre, of which midge larvae were 115.6 lb/acre.
- Little Brooklyn Lake benthos standing crop was 3.7 lb/acre, with midge larvae at 12.8 lb/acre (as reported in the abstract; the body text has OCR-garbled figures for this lake).
- In Towner Lake, [[Tendipes Attenuatus]] made up about 88% of the midge larvae.
- In Little Brooklyn Lake, [[Anatopynia Dyari]] (about 36%) and [[Calopsectra Sp]] (about 41%) were dominant. The difference was attributed partly to bottom type, since Little Brooklyn Lake has a rockier substrate.
- Mean midge larvae standing crop in Towner Lake declined over the years: 468 lb/acre (1958), 214 (1959), and 116 (1962). The author attributed the lower 1962 values partly to the larger sample size and transect design.
- Oligochaetes and [[Physa]] snails were common. Leeches were more abundant in Little Brooklyn Lake.
- Screen tubes showed midge emergence only from open water, with a peak in mid-to-late July (about 15 adults per tube).

**Fish population and mortality.**
- Towner Lake estimate: 4,016 trout. Age-groups 0, I, II, III, and IV+ made up 36, 53, 7, 3, and 1 percent.
- Little Brooklyn Lake estimate: 2,672 trout. Age-groups 0, I, II, III, and IV+ made up 24, 52, 18, 5, and 1 percent.
- The Little Brooklyn Lake estimate (2,672) was close to Royer's 1959 estimate (2,789).
- Towner Lake's age-group 0 declined since 1958, possibly from lower spawning success. Age-group I rose, possibly from downstream drift from Telephone Creek.
- Calculated standing crops were 16.4 lb/acre (Towner) and 16.9 lb/acre (Little Brooklyn), by wet weight. The author noted that natural and fishing mortality estimates had little meaning because of unmeasured migration between lakes and beaver ponds.

**Growth and diet.**
- Maximum growth occurred from mid-June to late August in both lakes. Age-group I growth curves levelled off as water temperature declined, which supports a temperature link to growth. Baldwin (1956) found maximum growth near 55.4°F, close to the temperatures at which age-group I fish were nearing their maximum.
- Towner Lake age-group 0 fish grew from 0.7 in (0.03 g) in early July to 1.89 in (1.13 g) in late August. Instantaneous growth rate for Little Brooklyn Lake age-group 0 in August was 0.0421/day, the highest of 1962.
- Diet was dominated by midge larvae and pupae and by cladocerans, with mollusks important for age-group II. In Towner Lake, age-group 0 fish fed mainly on Calopsectra sp. (rare in the benthos), possibly because it is more available in open water.
- Of 190 stomachs examined (98 Towner, 92 Little Brooklyn), regurgitation was rare.
- Physa snails appeared mainly in females: about 75% of mollusks in Towner Lake stomachs and about 92% in Little Brooklyn Lake. The author suggested a link to spawning nutrition, but this was speculative.

**Harvest and fishing pressure.** The creel census ran June 11 to September 8, 1962. The daily limit was 12 fish or 10 lb and one fish, with no minimum size.
- Towner Lake harvest: 582 ± 517 trout (Table XXXIX, based on an estimated 507 resident-caught fish). Little Brooklyn Lake harvest: 325 ± 304 trout.
- Harvest per acre was 66 fish (Towner Lake) and 41 fish (Little Brooklyn Lake). Estimated harvest biomass was 13.0 lb/acre and 5.8 lb/acre.
- Average harvested trout were 9.1 in (Towner Lake) and 8.8 in (Little Brooklyn Lake).
- Boat fishermen had higher catch rates than bank fishermen. Resident anglers had higher catch rates than non-residents, except in the first period on Little Brooklyn Lake.
- The authors judged the harvest estimates high and the standard deviations very large, so true harvest could vary widely.

## Key Findings

- **Stock:** Towner Lake had an estimated 4,016 brook trout and Little Brooklyn Lake 2,672. Recapture rates were high, which gave reliable estimates except for Towner Lake group C fish, which were probably undercounted.
- **Yearling removal:** Approximately 930 yearling trout were removed from Towner Lake after the population estimate, to test effects on older age-groups. The body text gives 287 seined plus 673 gill-netted, but these figures are OCR-garbled and do not match the 930 total. The population response was not yet measurable in this report.
- **Benthos:** Midge larvae dominated the bottom fauna. Standing crop was 153.6 lb/acre (Towner Lake) versus 3.7 lb/acre (Little Brooklyn Lake). The two lakes differed in their dominant midge species.
- **Diet and competition:** Midge larvae, pupae, and cladocerans were the main food. Competition for midges and cladocerans probably exists among age-groups. Mollusks were eaten mostly by females.
- **Growth:** Maximum growth ran from mid-June to late August. Growth was closely tied to water temperature, with age-group I fish nearing maximum growth at about 55.4°F.
- **Harvest:** Estimated harvest was 582 trout (Towner Lake) and 325 (Little Brooklyn Lake), with 13.0 and 5.8 lb/acre. Abstract values (523 and 325) differ from the body text (582), a discrepancy in the source.
- **Methods caveats:** Benthic sampling variance was high (for Tendipes attenuatus in July, mean 117 ± 112 per station). Taxonomy of Tendipedidae was described as unstable, and some identifications were tentative.
- **Recommendations:** Future work should quantify midge pupae and cladocerans, improve benthic sampling, and monitor the effects of yearling removal over several years.
- **Spawning:** No spawning observations were made because of early fall snow.
