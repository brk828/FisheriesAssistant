# Bonytail May Enhance Survival of Razorback Suckers by Preying on Juvenile Red Swamp Crayfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) could reduce [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*) numbers in [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) rearing ponds. Introduced crayfish prey on larval and juvenile razorback suckers, and the standard removal method, a 6.34-mm mesh trap, cannot catch small crayfish. Laboratory feeding trials showed that juvenile bonytail readily ate crayfish from about 3 mm to 15 mm carapace length (CPL), and that crayfish consumption was not reduced by the presence of pellets. Escape cover did reduce bonytail predation. The authors conclude that juvenile bonytail may help control crayfish in rearing ponds and suggest that integrating bonytail and razorback sucker rearing programs could benefit both species.

## Background & Description

The [[Lower Colorado River]] supports a highly differentiated fish fauna with a high proportion of endemic species. Of 13 native fish species, only bonytail and razorback sucker persist in the river as originally known, and both are federally listed as endangered. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) has been reestablished in the mainstream immediately below Davis Dam.

Recovery efforts involve federal and state agencies, water-user groups, and private businesses, including the [[LCR MSCP]] multi-species conservation program. The [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] runs its own independent recovery program, under which this study was conducted.

Razorback suckers were common in [[Lake Mohave]] until the 1950s, when the lake filled. Since then recruitment has been extremely low. Adults in Lake Mohave and across the lower river are now old, and the population is declining. A much smaller bonytail population also persists in Lake Mohave. Recruitment failure in both species was attributed mainly to introduced sport fishes. Both species spawn annually in Lake Mohave, and eggs and larvae have been collected since 1980, but the larvae do not survive.

Bureau biologists have captured wild razorback suckers from Lake Mohave, reared them to about 150 mm total length (TL) at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], and moved them to rearing ponds containing no predatory fish species. Once they reach about 400 mm TL, the sub-adults are repatriated to the lake, where they have formed breeding aggregations. A similar program for bonytail was being developed.

Predation is a major threat in rearing ponds. Odonate nymphs can limit survival of larval razorback suckers. Red swamp crayfish, recently introduced into the ponds, are a further predator. Adult crayfish have been removed with modified 6.34-mm mesh barrel traps (Morgan et al. 1999), but reproduction by the remaining population still sustains predation pressure. The bonytail diet includes aquatic and terrestrial insects, plant material, and filamentous algae. No crayfish are native to the Colorado River system, and no prior study had shown that bonytail eat them.

The study asked four questions: (1) what crayfish sizes escape traps; (2) whether juvenile bonytail eat crayfish and which sizes are most vulnerable; (3) whether bonytail eat crayfish when other food is available; and (4) whether escape cover reduces bonytail predation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and husbandry.** Experiments were run at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, Arizona, about 17 km (11 mi) downriver from [[Hoover Dam]]. Bonytail of 100-200 mm TL from hatchery stock were anesthetized with MS-222, measured, weighed (0.1 g), and held for seven days in 38-L aquaria. Weekly prophylactic treatments of 25 mg/L formalin and 0.05 mg/L malachite green were given to reduce infection. Fish were fed trout pellets daily, except on trial days.

**Crayfish source and culture.** Adult red swamp crayfish were collected from backwater ponds at Lake Mohave in late September 1999 and held in a hatchery raceway at 22-29 °C. Mating was allowed until early October, after which males were removed. Ovigerous females were held in 38-L nursery aquaria in a warm-water (27-29 °C) circulation system. Brood size ranged from about 150 to 500. Juvenile growth was highly variable, averaging about 1-2 mm per week.

**Size classes.** The smallest crayfish used were stage 2 juveniles (about 3 mm CPL), the first stage able to separate from the mother. Earlier stages (eggs and instar 1) remain attached to the mother's abdomen and are likely protected from bonytail.

**Trap limitation.** Crayfish under about 17-20 mm CPL are not captured by 6.34-mm mesh traps. Trapping preferentially removes males and non-reproductive females. Berried (egg-carrying) females and young tend to stay hidden and are less vulnerable to traps. Red swamp crayfish in warm climates can breed year-round and produce up to three generations per year.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design.** Four experiments were conducted in 38-L aquaria, with a 24-hour photoperiod. Crayfish counts were taken at 1, 2, 4, 16 and 20 hours. Pellet counts were taken at 1 and 2 hours, after which disintegration prevented reliable counts.

- **Experiment 1 (trap escape):** Crayfish of four size classes were offered in aquaria containing a 6.34-mm mesh horizontal sheet, which separated them from the bonytail. Bonytail (mean 131 mm TL) ate most of the 7-mm and 10-mm crayfish. Crayfish of about 3 mm CPL and smaller than about 6 mm were rarely lost to the bonytail, which means that the smallest escape the trap while remaining vulnerable to predation.
- **Experiment 2 (size selectivity):** Single bonytail of the smallest size class (mean TL about 131 mm) were offered crayfish of 3 mm and larger in different size classes. Crayfish of 4-10 mm CPL were consumed most rapidly, with the 4-10 mm group the most vulnerable. Consumption of larger crayfish (11-15 mm) was lower.
- **Experiment 3 (food choice):** Over a 2-hour period, bonytail consumed significantly more crayfish than trout pellets when both were offered. Crayfish consumption was not reduced in the presence of pellets: mean crayfish consumed was M = 34 (SD = 7.0) with pellets versus M = 27 (SD = 10.2) without, t(2 df) = 0.7, p = 0.29. When given a choice, bonytail preferred crayfish to pellets, even though they had been habituated to pellets. Pellet consumption was reduced when crayfish were present (M = 9.3, SD = 2.5; t(2 df) = 5.0, p = 0.02).
- **Experiment 4 (cover):** Crayfish were offered in tanks with no cover, horizontal cover, or vertical cover. After 16 hours, none of 50 crayfish remained in the uncovered tanks, 74% remained in tanks with horizontal cover, and 26% remained in tanks with vertical cover. Cover reduces the short-term effectiveness of bonytail predation, and vertical cover gives more protection than horizontal cover.

**Consumption rates.** The average consumption rate was about 15 crayfish per hour. If sustained for part of each day in rearing ponds, this could remove very large numbers of young crayfish before they become large enough to prey on young razorback suckers. The authors suggest that bonytail could become an effective biological control agent.

**Conclusions.**

- Juvenile bonytail can reduce young crayfish that traps cannot remove (3-15 mm CPL).
- Crayfish sheltering in cover are less vulnerable, and cover reduces predation, but crayfish that leave cover are likely to be eaten.
- Integrating bonytail and razorback sucker rearing could increase survival of young razorback suckers by reducing crayfish predation.
- Johnson and Hines (1999) noted a possible risk that bonytail might prey on razorback larvae in clear rearing ponds. The authors consider this unlikely because razorback larvae are reared to about 150 mm TL before release and are nocturnal, and because the two species evolved together, so bonytail pose no greater threat than the exotic crayfish.

**Limitations.** Trials were short and in small aquaria, while ponds are more complex and contain more food types. Bonytail were hatchery-raised on pellets and may have behaved differently in the wild. Sample sizes were small (for example, three bonytail per treatment in Table 2).
