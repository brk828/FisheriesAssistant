# Texas Cave Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #siluriformes

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by [[Clark Hubbs]] and reprinted from the [[Natural History of Texas Caves]] (edited by Ernest L. Lundelius), reviews the limited knowledge of fishes living in Texas caves. Of the nine fish species recorded by [[Reddell and Mitchell 1969]], two are troglobites, namely [[Trogloglanis Pattersoni]] and [[Satan Eurystomus]], and seven are well-known surface fishes. Only six specimens of the two troglobites had been studied. The author argues that catfishes, particularly ictalurids, have the ecological and sensory traits that make them the most likely fishes to evolve into cave inhabitants in Texas, and that this pattern also holds globally.

## Background & Description

**Troglobitic blind catfishes.** The two Texas troglobites have an unusually high specimen-to-publication ratio:

- [[Trogloglanis Pattersoni]] was described by [[Eigenmann 1919]].
- [[Satan Eurystomus]] was described by [[Hubbs and Bailey 1947]].
- Each species has three studied specimens. Earlier reports mentioned more blindfish than were examined: [[Eigenmann 1919]] reported one specimen, [[Hubbs and Bailey 1947]] reported two, and [[Suttkus 1961]] reported three. Those authors alluded to "several," "about twenty," and "a dozen" individuals, respectively.
- All known specimens came from deep or [[Artesian Wells]] in [[Bexar County]], Texas.

**Collection localities.**

- *Trogloglanis pattersoni:* an artesian well in [[Brackenridge Park]]; a 1000-foot artesian well on the Jusef Boecke Ranch near the Alamo, close to the I-35 bridge over Salado Creek; and a 2000-foot artesian well on the O. R. Mitchell Ranch near [[Von Ormy]], 14 miles southwest of [[San Antonio]].
- *Satan eurystomus:* a 1250-foot artesian well near San Antonio; a 1400-foot artesian well near the Loop 13 and I-35 intersection in southwest San Antonio; and the O. R. Mitchell Ranch well.

**Taxonomic status.** The restricted and overlapping ranges of the two blind catfishes suggest they may belong to the same taxon. However, both are known only from immature males, so this remains unresolved. The related [[Prietella Phreatophila]] (described by [[Carranza 1954]]) is represented by 65 specimens, presumably of both sexes. Variation among these 65 is less than the variation between the two Von Ormy specimens. The author calls for additional material, preferably living, to study variation and ecology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish diversity in Texas.** More than 200 fishes inhabit Texas freshwaters. Of these, two (1%) are cave fishes. Eleven catfishes are known from the state, and two (18%) of them are cave fishes. Two of the seven surface fishes recorded in Texas caves (29%) are catfishes, which the author takes as evidence of catfishes' tendency to occupy cave-like environments.

**Traits favoring catfishes.** As a group, catfishes are:

- Bottom-inhabiting ([[Trautman 1957]]) and nocturnal ([[Harlan and Speaker 1951]]).
- Egg-layers that breed in holes in the substrate ([[Davis 1953]]; [[Breder and Rosen 1966]]).
- Equipped with poorly developed eyes adapted to low light ([[Polyak 1957]]). Their sensory systems rely on olfactory and tactile cues rather than vision.
- Low in metabolic demand. Resting oxygen consumption in bullheads is considerably lower than in most fishes except carp ([[Beamish 1964]]). Low metabolism is also beneficial in caves, where food is usually scarce ([[Heuts 1953]]).
- Capable of sound production, which influences behavior ([[Tavolga 1962]]).

**Comparison with other freshwater groups.** The author contrasts catfishes with other Texas freshwater groups:

- Clupeids and serranids are midwater fishes, and poeciliids, cyprinodonts, and atherinids are surface fishes.
- Catostomids are bottom-dwelling but mostly migrate to spawn, which is difficult in caves.
- Centrarchids and percids have large eyes, bright breeding colors, and carnivorous habits, which may hinder survival in caves.
- Darters face mate-discrimination problems, and most fishes depend on [[Photoperiodism]] to trigger reproduction. Midwater and surface fishes appear more light-dependent than bottom fishes.

**Global pattern.** [[Hubbs 1938]] reported that 12 or 13 of 35 to 37 known subterranean fishes worldwide were catfishes. Cave catfishes are typically reduced in pigmentation and are not of marine origin. Benthic fishes tend to be melanistic, whereas troglobites tend to be albinistic. Other groups with cave inhabitants include:

- Cyprinids, which have many surface species and habits that could be preadaptive for cave life.
- Gobiids and brotulids, whose marine habits make cave entry predictable.
- Amblyopsids, which all live in cave or cave-like habitats.

The author notes that a cyprinodont-like ancestor would make troglobitic evolution difficult, while an aphredoderid-like ancestor may have been preadapted to cave life ([[Greenwood et al. 1966]]).

**Mexican cave fish.** [[Astyanax Jordani]] is the most surprising troglobite in the discussion. Its ancestor, [[Astyanax Mexicanus]], is a carnivorous midwater fish. The author suggests it entered caves in San Luis Potosi because no more likely taxon existed in the depauperate Mexican fauna when surface streams were captured by underground drainages.

## Key Findings

- **Low known diversity:** Only two of nine Texas cave fish species are troglobites, and only six specimens had been studied at the time of writing.
- **Catfish dominance:** Catfishes are overrepresented in Texas caves (2 of 11 catfish species, or 18%) and make up 29% of surface fishes found in caves.
- **Adaptive profile:** Catfishes combine nocturnal and bottom habits, low metabolism, reduced vision, and strong chemosensory and tactile senses. The author concludes these traits make catfishes the most likely source of future Texas troglobites.
- **Global consistency:** Catfishes were 12 or 13 of 35 to 37 known subterranean fishes in 1938, so the pattern is not limited to Texas.
- **Taxonomic uncertainty:** The two Texas blind catfishes may be conspecific, but this cannot be confirmed without more material, especially living specimens.
- **Research gap:** The author stresses that ecological information on these fishes is minimal and that additional collections are needed.
