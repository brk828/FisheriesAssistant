# Fish Species Diversity in Relation to Stream Order and Physicochemical Conditions in the Plum Creek Drainage Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-fish-assemblages

## Executive Summary

This 1972 study by B. G. Whiteside and Randy M. McNatt, published in *The American Midland Naturalist* (Vol. 88, No. 1, pp. 90–101), examines how fish species diversity varies with [[Stream Order]] and physicochemical conditions across the [[Plum Creek]] drainage basin in south central Texas. Samples were collected at 202 stations between January and April 1968. The authors classified streams using [[Horton Stream Order Classification]], which was chosen because it reduces the number of environmental variables that must be considered. Twenty-seven fish species were collected. Fish species diversity was measured with a [[Species Diversity Index]] (D) derived from information theory. Fluctuations in physicochemical conditions declined as stream order increased, and fish diversity and species numbers peaked in the middle orders before falling in the fifth-order stream.

## Background & Description

**Stream classification.** The authors note that streams have been classified by slope and velocity, water source, substrate, drainage size, algal associations, habitat, climate, and branching pattern. Horton's branching-based method was adopted here. In this scheme, the smallest unbranched tributary is a first-order stream, the confluence of two first-order streams forms a second-order stream, and so on. Adding a smaller tributary does not change the order of the receiving stream.

**Diversity indices.** Several indices were reviewed, including those of Fisher et al. (1943), Preston (1948), Simpson (1949), Margalef (1951), Menhinick (1964), and Patten (1962). Only [[Harrel and Dorris 1968]] and [[Kuehne 1962]] had applied mathematical diversity expressions to fish communities. Earlier work by Wilhm (1962; 1967) and Patten (1962) on benthic invertebrates showed that D approached an asymptote at or before the fifth sample. The authors used this to justify their sampling effort.

**Study area.** Plum Creek is a tributary of the San Marcos River. Its main channel begins northwest of Lockhart, flows for 51.6 km, and joins the San Marcos River 7.25 km southeast of Luling. Five oil fields lie within the watershed. Elevations range from about 214 m at the headwaters to about 92.5 m at the mouth. The northern half of the basin is underlain by limestone and shale, and the southern portion by sandstone and sandy loams. The predominant soils are clays. Climax vegetation includes little bluestem, Indiangrass, and pecan, with American elm along stream banks. Gradients for first- and second-order streams were estimated from a random sample of 50 first-order and 50 second-order streams. Higher-order gradients were based on all streams in each order.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Fish were collected by seining, with effort varying by stream size, pool size, accessibility, and stream length. First- and second-order streams were sampled twice, third-order streams at least three times, and fourth-order streams at least four times. The fifth-order stream was sampled 12 times.

**Measured variables.** Water and air temperature were measured with a mercury thermometer. Conductivity was measured with a [[Beckman Conductivity Meter]]. Alkalinity (phenolphthalein and total) was determined by titration, and pH was estimated with a Hellige color comparator. Stream width and depth were measured, and [[Dissolved Oxygen]] was determined by the Winkler method.

**Diversity metrics.** Community diversity (D) was calculated from total individuals (N), individuals per species (ni), and number of species (s). Per-individual diversity, minimum and maximum values (Dmin and Dmax), and redundancy were also derived. Redundancy is the difference between Dmax and D, expressed relative to Dmax.

**Physicochemical patterns.** Fluctuations in physicochemical conditions decreased with increasing stream order. Mean dissolved oxygen declined regularly from first- to fifth-order streams, starting at 9.9 ppm in first-order streams. Mean [[Alkalinity]] usually increased with stream order, and most alkalinity was bicarbonate-based, with carbonate alkalinity present only in the northern part of the basin. [[Conductivity]] increased downstream. Mean pH and turbidity showed no consistent trends. Local land use, such as cultivation, and trash in the water affected these variables more than stream order did.

**Seasonal and disturbance context.** An unusually heavy rainfall period occurred from late 1967 into early 1968, with high water in the fifth-order stream. Oil-field runoff was observed at one second-order site, which had an oily sheen and reddish coloration. Lower-order streams were sampled mainly in February and March, while higher-order streams were sampled throughout the period.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** Twenty-seven fish species were collected. Minimum, maximum, and mean numbers of species per stream rose through the first four orders and dropped in the fifth-order stream.
- **Mean D:** Declined from first- to second-order streams, rose through fourth-order streams, and declined again in the fifth-order stream.
- **Cumulative D:** Rose through third-order streams and fell in fourth- and fifth-order streams.
- **Adventitious streams:** D values for tributaries generally tracked the trends of the higher-order stream they entered. Second-order streams that joined the fifth-order stream directly showed a decrease in D, while fourth-order streams entering the fifth-order stream directly had higher mean D than the two fourth-order streams that formed it.
- **Redundancy:** Highest in first-order streams and lowest in second-order streams.
- **Species distribution:** Species numbers were 19 in both third- and fourth-order streams and 12 in fifth-order streams. These patterns are similar to those reported by [[Harrel and Dorris 1968]] in Oklahoma and [[Kuehne 1962]] in Kentucky.
- **Proposed causes of fifth-order decline:**
  - Migration of fishes into lower-order streams for spawning.
  - Escape from abnormally high water in the fifth-order stream.
  - Reduced [[Seining]] efficiency caused by greater depth and obstacles such as logs and barbed wire.
- **Temperature:** Water temperature in the fifth-order stream may have been lower because the water was substantially deeper.
- **Conductivity and alkalinity:** Both increased with stream order, with a decrease in mean conductivity in the fourth-order streams that the authors could not explain.
- **Stability:** The general decline in the range of physicochemical variables with increasing stream order indicates greater stability in higher-order streams. This finding agrees with Harrel and Dorris (1968).
- **Broader implications:** Smaller streams that dry seasonally may host fewer species and depend on fish recolonization. Fish movement among stream orders therefore matters for management of these assemblages.
