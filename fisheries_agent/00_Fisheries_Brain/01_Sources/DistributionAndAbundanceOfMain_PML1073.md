# Distribution and Abundance of Mainstream Fishes of the Middle and Upper Colorado River Basins, 1967–1973

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 survey by [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit) documents the distribution and relative abundance of fishes in the middle and upper [[Colorado River Basin]] above [[Lake Mead]], sampled from 1967 to 1973. Twenty-nine species were collected, of which 19 were introduced and 10 native. Native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] dominated the study area. Four endemic large-river species, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Sucker]], were rare and considered endangered. The authors attribute the decline of native fishes mainly to habitat alteration by high dams and to introductions of exotic species.

## Background & Description

The introduction places the basin's fauna in context. [[Miller]] (1959) determined that 74% of native Colorado River basin fishes are endemic, a pattern linked to long periods of geographic isolation. In its natural state the river was warm, turbid, and prone to sudden, drastic changes in flow and turbidity. Endemic fishes fall into two groups: small-stream forms, found mainly in the lower basin, and large-river forms, found in the main stream and larger tributaries. The endemic large-river fishes are:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

Earlier work had documented declines in the lower basin. [[Minckley]] (1973) reported large-river fishes as extremely rare or extinct below [[Lake Mohave]], which made the upper and middle basins the last extensive natural strongholds.

The study covered the Green River system from Craig, Colorado, the Yampa River, and the Colorado River from Rifle, Colorado, to Lake Mead, excluding [[Lake Powell]]. Major tributaries included the Gunnison, Dolores, Price, White, and Little Snake Rivers. The main system was divided into 12 sampling areas. Most of the study area is canyon country, with low-gradient sandy reaches in areas 5, 6, and the upper part of area 4, and high-gradient gravelly reaches elsewhere.

Two major reservoirs were built in the 1960s: [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] on the upper Green River and [[Glen Canyon Dam]], which created Lake Powell just upriver from Marble and Grand Canyons. A fish eradication program in September 1962 removed most native species above and for many kilometers below Flaming Gorge Dam before closure in November 1962.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Sampling from 1967 to 1973 used gill nets, seines, electrofishing, and hook and line. Intensive sampling took place near [[Echo Park]], at the confluence of the Green and Yampa Rivers. Other areas included the lower Green River, the upper Colorado River, Glen Canyon below the dam, and Marble and Grand Canyons (three float trips, 1970–72). Marble and Grand Canyons were the least intensively studied because of high cost, floating algae, daily 120–150 cm river-level fluctuations, and deep, swift channels. Seining shallow areas for juveniles was emphasized as an indicator of recent reproduction. Most fish were released alive; rare or dead specimens were preserved at the Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit collection, Utah State University. Abundance was recorded in relative terms:

- **Abundant:** collected readily with little effort, several age groups present, juveniles taken by seine.
- **Common:** readily collected, usually more than one age group.
- **Rare:** collected occasionally, with no certainty regardless of effort.
- **Occasional:** present due to stocking or seasonal movement, usually in low numbers.

**Habitat and thermal regime.** Rainbow trout in the cold Green River (10–15 °C) moved down from the warm Yampa River (16–22 °C) in summer. In spring and fall 1970, when the Yampa was cool, they invaded areas 2 and 3. Dams and reservoirs have changed the thermal regime. Cold tailwaters below Flaming Gorge Dam showed no native fish reproduction for 104 km downstream, until the Yampa River joins and moderates the cold water. Lowered temperatures below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], little solar warming in deep canyons, and the daily fluctuation in river level all reduced habitat quality. Secondary production in the main river appeared very limited.

**Competition and introductions.** Most introduced species apparently entered the upper basin as released baitfish. The channel catfish became well established in the early 1900s and added an abundant carnivore. More recently, the [[Red Shiner]] spread rapidly and competed with juvenile fish for space and food.

## Key Findings

**Species composition and abundance**

- 29 species were collected: 10 native and 19 introduced. Introduced species outnumbered natives 19 to 10.
- Native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (well over 6,000 adults collected) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] were the dominant large fishes. Flannelmouth suckers were the most abundant large native species, found in all study sections and habitats.
- The [[Roundtail Chub]] was the historically dominant native carnivore of tributaries, abundant in areas 1, 2, 3, 9, and 10, and it diminished downstream. Little change in its historical distribution was noted.
- Four endemic species were considered endangered: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Sucker]].

**Endangered endemic fishes**

- [[Bonytail Chub]]: very rare; 36 taken, from areas 2, 3, 4, and 5. Strong year classes were calculated for 1959–1961 from adults, but only three possible bonytails were found among several hundred young chubs, suggesting poor recent reproduction. The decline coincided with the closure of Flaming Gorge Dam.
- [[Humpback Chub]] complex: 61 taken, mostly from eddies near fast currents. 26 came from Echo Park (areas 2 and 3) in 1969, and 15 from area 11 in 1967, but only one from area 11 in 1970 despite increased effort, suggesting a decline. Juveniles with nuchal humps were found only in area 4.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: 300 adults collected, 261 of them from area 2. Juveniles were abundant at Echo Park in 1968, very few in 1969, and none in 1970 or 1971, probably reflecting poor spawning success. Congregations of ripe males were collected in area 2 each July and early August, 1968–70. The largest specimen seen was estimated at 7 kg, against a recorded maximum of 36 kg and 150 cm (Jordan and Evermann 1896).
- [[Humpback Sucker]]: 53 taken, almost exclusively in stagnant or quiet water. Abundance has declined markedly this century, from abundant to extremely rare.

**Introduced species**

- The [[Rainbow Trout]] population in Tapeats Creek (Grand Canyon) was self-sustaining. Other records came from planted or wandering fish in areas 1, 2, 3, 9, 11, and 12.
- Only eight [[Cutthroat Trout]] were taken, in areas 2 and 3, considered wanderers from cold-water stocking.
- The [[Channel Catfish]] is one of the most common fishes of the middle and upper basin. The largest specimen collected weighed 5.9 kg, and it supports a small fishery.
- The [[Red Shiner]] extended its range up the Green River during the study. It was abundant in the Colorado and upper Green Rivers, and became the most abundant fish in seine hauls from the middle Colorado and lower Green Rivers. Its introduction was most probably near Grand Junction, Colorado, in the late 1950s or early 1960s.
- Carp were introduced into Colorado in 1882 and were abundant only in area 8 (near Grand Junction, enriched by sewage), and in a few quiet-water areas.
- The [[Creek Chub]] was found only near Echo Park. Vanicek et al. (1970) collected just 12 specimens below Flaming Gorge Dam over four years.

**Hybrids**

- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] × [[Humpback Sucker]] hybrids: 40 collected, in quiet backwaters with humpback suckers. No fertile hybrids have been reported, though hybrids have been known for over 75 years.
- Bluehead × white sucker hybrids occurred only in the Yampa River.
- Flannelmouth × white sucker hybrids were recorded in areas 1, 2, and 10.

**Conclusions and conservation outlook**

- The authors attribute the decline of native fishes primarily to high dams, their reservoirs, and cold tailwaters, which provide poor reproductive habitat, and to the competition and predation from introduced species.
- Reproduction of rare fishes was evident only in the Green River in areas 4 and 5 and the Yampa River, so any alteration there, especially below Flaming Gorge Dam, is likely to be harmful.
- Only native species adapted to tributary streams (speckled dace, bluehead, and flannelmouth suckers) are likely to survive if conditions continue to decline.
- The authors conclude that little additional alteration may be enough to push the endemic species to extinction, and that the Colorado River environment itself "should be considered 'Endangered.'"
