# Draft Biological Opinion: Ongoing and Long-term Grazing on the Coronado National Forest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft biological opinion (US Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona Ecological Services Field Office, dated April 14, 1999; consultation AESO/SE 2-21-98-F-399) evaluates the effects of ongoing and long-term livestock grazing on five [[Coronado National Forest]] allotments (Seibold, Kunde, Crittenden, Papago, and San Rafael) on two federally endangered fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Chub]]. The consultation was requested by the Forest Supervisor on November 5, 1998, and the consultation period was extended by 60 days.

The Service concludes that the proposed grazing is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of either species. For Gila Topminnow, the opinion identifies serious concern about cumulative and baseline degradation in [[Redrock Canyon]], [[Sonoita Creek]], and [[Cienega Creek]], and sets an incidental take statement with explicit take triggers, reasonable and prudent measures, and mandatory terms. For Yaqui Chub, take is limited to no more than five individuals annually.

## Background & Description

**Gila Topminnow** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is a small live-bearing poeciliid. Males rarely exceed 25 mm (1 inch) and females reach 51 mm (2 inches). Fertilization is internal, brood development takes 24 to 28 days, and superfetation produces broods at different developmental stages. Litters range from 1 to 31 young, with breeding largely from spring to August. The species is an omnivorous opportunistic feeder. It was listed as endangered in 1967 without critical habitat. The taxon is divided into the Gila subspecies and the Yaqui subspecies (*P. o. sonoriensis*), with both protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Yaqui Chub** (*Gila purpurea*) is a medium-sized cyprinid listed as endangered on August 31, 1984. Critical habitat was designated only for "all aquatic habitat on the San Bernardino NWR," and does not cover Leslie Canyon. Adults favor the deepest, most permanent pools and undercut banks; young prefer marginal habitat. Diet is mostly algae, insects, and detritus. Reproduction is protracted through warm months, with high reproductive potential.

**Historical context:** Gila Topminnow was once among the most common fishes of the Colorado basin, particularly in the Santa Cruz system. It has been reduced to 15 natural populations, of which 12 are considered extant. The document reports that the Yaqui Chub was once known from several basins in Arizona and Sonora, but the Rio Sonora and Matape chub has since been reassigned to a separate species, *Gila eremica*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and water chemistry:** Gila Topminnow tolerates temperatures of 6 to 37 °C (51 to 99 °F), pH 6.6 to 8.9, dissolved oxygen of 2.2 to 11 mg/L (22 to 110 ppm?), and salinities approaching seawater. The species lives at pool and backwater edges with abundant aquatic vegetation and can persist in springs with high dissolved carbonates and high pH.

**Threats:** Major drivers of decline include dewatering, impoundment, channelization, flow regulation, poor land management producing arroyo formation, and introduction of predatory or competitive nonnative fishes, crayfish, and bullfrogs. The species is described as predator- and competitor-naive, having evolved in a naturally depauperate fish fauna. Western mosquitofish and largemouth bass are particularly damaging.

**Redrock Canyon:** A tributary of middle Sonoita Creek, with elevation of 1,240 to 1,524 m (4,070 to 5,000 ft) and largely intermittent flow with perennial springs. Land use history includes livestock grazing since the 1700s, mining, fuelwood cutting, and downcutting of channels beginning around 1890. Persistent grazing, roads in the stream channel, and water development continue to degrade riparian and channel conditions. Nonnative species recorded include mosquitofish, largemouth bass, green sunfish, bluegill, red shiner, and yellow bullhead; nonnative plants include salt cedar and fountain grass.

**Cienega Creek:** A Santa Cruz River tributary largely within the BLM Empire-Cienega Resource Conservation Area. It holds the largest existing Gila Topminnow population and is one of only three populations uncontaminated by nonnative fish. Fish fauna consists of Gila Topminnow, longfin dace, and Gila chub.

**Sonoita Creek:** A major tributary to the Santa Cruz River. Gila Topminnow is rare and apparently declining above Patagonia Lake, which serves as a source of nonnative fish. Ranchette development in the Sonoita area draws groundwater that may threaten Cienega Creek's supply.

**West Turkey Creek (Yaqui Chub):** A perennial-intermittent stream on the western side of the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] draining toward the Sulphur Springs Valley. About 4.0 miles of potential Yaqui Chub habitat exist, with about 2.5 miles on National Forest land within the Turkey Creek Allotment. Ponds on the private [[El Coronado Ranch]] serve as refugia during drought under a Section 10 Habitat Conservation Plan issued in 1998.

## Key Findings

**Gila Topminnow status and distribution**
- Of 15 historically recorded natural populations, 12 remain extant; the document notes 12 natural and 18 stocked localities currently exist.
- Cienega Creek supports the largest population; about 10.5 km of perennial habitat plus 1.7 km (Mattie Canyon) and 1.5 km (Empire Gulch). Densities up to 566 per square meter were recorded in warm groundwater areas (Simms and Simms 1991).
- In Redrock Canyon, the Cott Tank drainage population is the largest of three centers (about 2.75 km of interrupted perennial water). The Gate Spring population has been absent since November 1995. Mosquitofish rose from 1 to 4 percent of poeciliids in the late 1980s and early 1990s to an average of 38 percent (range 0 to 99 percent) since 1991.
- Redrock Canyon forms a metapopulation of at least seven subpopulations. Genetic work (Hedrick and Parker 1999) indicates the falls population has significantly higher genetic diversity than the Cott Tank population.
- Gila Topminnow has not been found at the Patagonia Preserve since 1990; the Sonoita population is considered tenuous.

**Grazing effects and monitoring**
- Four exclosures in Redrock Canyon (Cott Tank, Gate Spring, Falls, Pig Camp Spring) produced riparian recovery where grazing was excluded, but breaches and grazing incursions occurred.
- Range condition in Redrock Canyon remains poor to fair. Trends are static on Seibold, Crittenden, and Kunde and downward on San Rafael and Papago/Triangle. Soil conditions are mostly unsatisfactory or impaired.
- On the Papago allotment, 99 percent of acreage shows downward trend and 82 percent unsatisfactory soil condition.
- Forest-wide utilization guidelines (for example, 20 to 35 percent in some management areas) are exceeded by the proposed 45 percent utilization level; the Service is concerned this uses "site specific information" never furnished.

**Biological opinion conclusions (Gila Topminnow)**
- The proposed action is not likely to jeopardize the species, but cumulative effects are of serious concern given the small, isolated populations.
- No take is anticipated in Cienega Creek. Take is expected in Redrock Canyon and Sonoita Creek.
- Take exceeded if: an exclosure fence is open more than 2 months per year; grazing in exclosures exceeds 5 percent woody utilization; allotment trends do not reverse within 5 years (Papago/Triangle, San Rafael) or 3 years (Seibold, Kunde, Crittenden); 45 percent utilization is exceeded; or rangeland conditions decline.
- Key required terms include extending the Cott Tank exclosure about 0.5 km downstream, constructing new exclosures on Redrock Canyon and Oak Grove Spring, removing nonnative fish from Downunder Tank and an Oak Grove stock tank, removing Lampshire Canyon dams, closing roads, establishing a drought policy, and conducting cumulative water-use analysis.
- Conservation recommendations include considering the four allotments as a unit in future NEPA analysis and removing the entire Redrock Canyon watershed (excluding Harshaw Canyon) from livestock grazing.

**Yaqui Chub status and distribution**
- Surveys in 1996, 1997, and 1998 found Yaqui Chub in low numbers (2 to 19 individuals) in the upper reaches of West Turkey Creek, including fry and young-of-year, indicating suitable conditions.
- Nonnative rainbow trout, fathead minnow, and longfin dace were found in some years; none were found in 1998.
- Cattle access occupied habitat; the Turkey Creek Allotment is permitted for 66 cow-calf pairs yearlong plus 25 cows September through December. In 1998, only 25 percent of permitted use was applied for.

**Yaqui Chub conclusions**
- Take is anticipated at no more than five Yaqui Chub annually. The Service concludes the proposed grazing is not likely to jeopardize the species, and critical habitat at San Bernardino NWR is not affected.
- Conservation recommendations include analyzing recreation impacts, developing a watershed plan, and working toward closing West Turkey Creek to fishing.

**Santa Cruz River and the broader Forest (concurrence)**
- For the 34 allotments in the Santa Cruz River area, the Service concurred with the Forest's "may affect, but is not likely to adversely affect" determination for Gila Topminnow. Ten of these allotments are rated unsatisfactory for watershed condition.
- The Service identifies lack of current environmental baseline data as the greatest single hindrance to effect determinations.
