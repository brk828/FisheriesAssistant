# Canyon Creek Aquatic Habitat Improvement Project: Operational Plan (1986)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 operational plan, compiled by [[Sue Morgensen]] for an interagency and citizen team, sets management direction for Canyon Creek from its spring source to the Fort Apache Indian Reservation Boundary. The goal is to build a "Blue Ribbon" brown and rainbow trout fishery, emphasizing [[Brown Trout]] and a native fish forage base, through riparian management, modified stocking, and restrictive fishing regulations. The team was drawn from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), the [[Tonto National Forest]] (TNF) and [[Trout Unlimited]] / [[Anglers United]]. Activities ran from October 1985 to May 1986, with a five-year implementation schedule of 16 prioritized actions.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Canyon Creek is a coldwater stream originating from springs beneath the [[Mogollon Rim]] and flowing south into the [[Salt River]]. Its total drainage area is approximately 325 square miles (the plan's watershed table gives 32.5 square miles for the study watershed). [[Mule Creek]] is a tributary.
- **Climate:** Mean annual precipitation of about 24 to 32 inches; peak flows occur primarily during February through April snowmelt and runoff.
- **Vegetation:** Ponderosa pine dominates uplands; riparian zones contain narrow-leaf cottonwood, willows and alder, now seriously depleted by past land use.
- **Fishery history:** Described in 1963 as one of the better brown and rainbow trout fisheries in Arizona. Access was improved in the late 1970s. Fly-fishing-only regulation was enacted in 1963 and artificial lures were added in 1986.
- **Current management:** A put-and-take rainbow fishery with a basic-yield brown trout fishery. Catchable rainbow trout have been stocked since 1949 (average 5,780 per year), with about 5,000 fingerling brown trout (last stocked 1983; 26,000 since 1979).
- **Native fish:** Speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*) and desert sucker (*Catostomus clarkii*).
- **Public use:** Recreation visitor days rose from 14,000 in 1970 to 65,500 in 1985. In 1981, about 6,600 angler days were recorded, with a catch of 1.5 trout per angler per day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Watershed and land use.** Timber harvest is ongoing on the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] and TNF, with sales planned for 1986–1988 totaling about 10 million board feet. Sedimentation from haul roads and skid trails is the main timber impact. Grazing has been a long-standing pressure: the [[OW Ranch]] allotment was historically yearlong and caused overgrazing of riparian vegetation, shade loss and sedimentation. Since 1979 it has been summer seasonal yearling use under a four-pasture deferred rotation. Off-road vehicle (ORV) use and recreation add further impacts, with [[Forest Road 188]] identified as a major sediment source.

**Soils and hydrology.** Soils exceed one meter in depth, are highly erodible, and have unusually high silt content. Springs are fed by unknown sources, suspected to be limestone sinkholes above the rim. Average spring flow is approximately 10 cfs. A USGS gauge (established 1976, abandoned 1981) provided the only published streamflow data.

**Water quality.** Chemistry generally met Arizona standards for wildlife and fish. Two concerns were identified: water temperature sometimes exceeded the 68°F maximum for cold water fisheries, and high silt and clay loads were evident, though no sediment samples had been collected in the study area.

**Habitat suitability.** A February 1986 mid-winter survey used modified suitability index models for [[Rainbow Trout]] (Raleigh et al. 1984a) and Brown Trout (Raleigh et al. 1984b). Surveys covered reaches 1–7 on Canyon Creek and 7 transects on Mule Creek, using a 500-foot transect grid. Summer temperatures, base flow and dissolved oxygen could not be measured. Mule Creek pool and bank data were lost to snowpack.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Benthic samples support a Biological Condition Index, sampled bi-annually at a cost of $600 per set. The stream has a moderate aquatic invertebrate assemblage despite high silt and low allochthonous input.

## Key Findings

**Reach-based limiting factors (Table 4, priority 1–6):**
- Fine sediment (fines) too high across all reaches, a major problem in Reach 1 and all others.
- Lack of spawning substrate and of pools of sufficient size, in all reaches.
- Lack of instream cover for adult trout, a problem in Reaches 2–7.
- Lack of shade and high maximum temperature, the major problem in Reaches 3–7.
- Bank instability, linked to lack of riparian vegetation.

**Strategy by reach:**
- Reach 1 (springhead to low-water crossing) is considered as good as it can be given watershed conditions; large-fish habitat is currently unavailable. The team judged natural reproduction would likely produce stunted trout.
- Efforts concentrate on Reaches 2–6 (sheep driveway/OW boundary fence to the box canyon).
- Reach 7 (box canyon to the reservation boundary) is expected to improve if upstream solutions succeed, since its high summer temperatures stem from upstream thermal loading.

**Slope:** Canyon Creek averages 4.3 percent from springhead to the Mule Creek confluence; Mule Creek averages 4.7 percent; the reach below Valentine Canyon averages 1.8 percent, and the lowest reach is about 1 percent.

**Operational plan (16 actions, Table 5 and implementation table):**
1. Finish implementation plan and Environmental Assessment (10 person-days).
2. Fence riparian pasture ($20,000).
3. Beaver trapping ($3,000/yr).
4. Monitoring: thermographs, crest stage gauges and automatic sediment samplers.
5. Riparian planting: cottonwood poles, modified cottonwood poles, experimental summer planting, and experimental alder/sycamore.
6. Cutbank modification and seeding at 12 locations ($5,000 per location).
7. Structural enhancement of pools ($7,000 per mile).
8. Additional monitoring: macroinvertebrates, summer plantings, cutbank modifications, fisheries surveys, and cover/photo points.
9. Finish Mule Creek inventory and manage Mule Creek under the same restrictions as Canyon Creek.
10. Close FR 188 and build a new road to the OW Ranch.
11. Maintain and expand pole fence to exclude ORV use.
12. Install headgate at OW Ranch field irrigation.
13. Study sinkholes on the rim.
14. Modify fisheries regulations toward a catch-and-release [[Blue Ribbon Fishery]].
15. Evaluate success and revise plan.

**Monitoring priorities:** Maximum stream temperature is the primary success criterion. A permanent gaging station was judged too costly ($20,000–30,000 to build, $10,000–15,000 per year), but crest stage gauges were judged affordable. Fisheries surveys should move to a bi-annual schedule using electrofishing and seining to test a catch-and-release "Blue Ribbon" fishery.

**Information gaps:** Coordinate with the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest on upper-watershed effects. Conduct macroinvertebrate studies throughout implementation. Collect Mule Creek bank and pool data in summer 1986. Evaluate creel, size-class distribution and stocking rate and method.

**Compliance:** The Pleasant Valley Ranger District is preparing an Environmental Assessment tiered to the Tonto National Forest Plan's Environmental Impact Statement, to satisfy the [[National Environmental Policy Act]].
