# Operation of Glen Canyon Dam: Draft EIS Appendices (Long-Term Monitoring, Hydrology, Water Quality, Sediment, Hydropower)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystem-monitoring

## Executive Summary

This draft Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) appendix volume supports the [[Operation Of Glen Canyon Dam]] and covers five technical areas: long-term monitoring and research (Appendix A), hydrology (Appendix B), water quality (Appendix C), sediment (Appendix D), and hydropower (within the monitoring framework). The central theme is that the [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]] is now the most heavily regulated large river in North America, and that dam operations have substantially altered its flows, sediment load, and water chemistry since closure in 1963. The appendices propose a monitoring program that tracks ecological and economic responses to dam operations, with fish, aquatic food base, and riparian vegetation as priority components. The document gives no cost projections for monitoring.

## Background & Description

Appendix A was prepared by Duncan T. Patten, PhD, Senior Scientist, [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]], and is dated May 1993. It was prepared under the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992]], which directs the Secretary of the Interior to establish long-term monitoring that protects and improves the values for which Grand Canyon National Park and Glen Canyon National Recreation Area were established.

The volume distinguishes monitoring (repeated measurement over time to detect change, after [[MacDonald et Al]] 1991) from inventory (a snapshot at one time) and from research (testing relationships among system attributes). The monitored area is the mainstem corridor from Glen Canyon Dam to [[Separation Canyon]] (RM 240), roughly 255 miles long, with [[Lake Powell]] forebay as the upstream limit. Thirteen GIS reaches of 2 to 12 miles each have detailed 1:2400 topographic data.

Appendices B through D are technical companions to the main EIS. Appendix B presents frequency distributions of flows and reservoir levels, Appendix C summarizes water quality including [[Selective Withdrawal]] considerations, and Appendix D analyzes [[Riverbed Sand]] and sandbar dynamics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Management agencies and interests** (Appendix A) include the [[National Park Service]] (restore and simulate the pre-dam ecosystem), the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (balance Law of the River releases, hydropower, and downstream protection), the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (threatened and endangered species, native and sport fish), [[Western Area Power Administration]] (marketing and transmission), the [[Hualapai Tribe]], other Indian Tribes, the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and the Bureau of Indian Affairs (no direct management role).

**Critical attributes** monitored include water quantity and quality, sediment dynamics, fish, vegetation, wildlife and habitat, endangered species (including [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Belted Kingfisher]], and [[Kanab Ambersnail]]), cultural resources, recreation, hydropower, and non-use values.

**Water chemistry** (Appendix C): Lake Powell gets roughly 95 percent of its inflow from the Colorado, Green, and San Juan Rivers. Releases come from an intake at elevation 3470 ft, about 230 ft below full pool, in the hypolimnion. Dam releases are relatively clear and low in nutrients, with a mean temperature near 46 °F and salinity averaging about 600 mg/L. Phosphorus and nitrogen are largely trapped in reservoir sediments, and [[Chironomid Larvae]] made up about 95 percent of macroinvertebrates in Lake Powell studies.

**Sediment and hydrology** (Appendices B and D): Sand supply and transport capacity have fallen sharply since dam closure, and sand loss from the Glen Canyon reach is described as irreversible. About 70 percent of postdam sand load comes from the [[Paria River]] and [[Little Colorado River]]. Fluctuating releases produce waves that transform downstream into triangular, pointed forms, and minimum flows increase with distance.

## Key Findings

**Monitoring design (Appendix A)**
- Monitoring emphasizes attributes that might change over time and be linked to dam operations. A "conservative" approach measures attributes that may be affected and lack surrogates, favoring non-invasive methods.
- Fish monitoring: adult long-lived species sampled annually at first, moving to a four-year cycle as information improves; short-lived species and young-of-year sampled twice yearly.
- Riparian vegetation: marshes and low bars sampled twice a year for five years, then annually; old high-water zone sampled about every five years.
- Cultural sites: directly impacted sites monitored quarterly, about 20 percent of other sites annually, and high-risk sites weekly by time-lapse photography.
- Recreation and tribal values are to be surveyed annually or on a five-year cycle. Hydropower output is to be monitored hourly.
- Gage priorities: keep stations at Lees Ferry, above the Little Colorado River, above Bright Angel Creek, and above Diamond Creek. The National Canyon gage is the least important and the candidate for removal.
- Information management requires pilot projects, QA/QC, a centralized data base stored in at least two locations, an Adaptive Management Working Group, and no dependence on any single individual.

**Hydrology (Appendix B)**
- Historic annual flow at Lees Ferry (1922-1990) had a median of 9,894,699 acre-feet and an average of 11,145,854 acre-feet. The predam (1922-1962) median was about 12,035,000 acre-feet. The postdam (1963-1989) median was 8,353,662 acre-feet.
- Postdam daily median flows in the April-July period exceed predam values, and monthly releases are more consistent. Appendix B gives frequency curves only and offers no interpretation beyond the main EIS.

**Water quality (Appendix C)**
- Selective withdrawal would draw warmer surface water in late spring and summer to warm the river, which may aid native fish spawning. Warmer releases may be needed roughly once in five years.
- Lake Powell dissolved oxygen averages 8.0 mg/L in the epilimnion. The metalimnetic oxygen minimum may fall to 2 mg/L. Releases range from about 6 mg/L in winter to 9 mg/L in summer.
- Lower-reservoir selective withdrawal would likely lower downstream nutrient and salinity inputs, while raising river temperatures. Effects on [[Humpback Chub]] thermal requirements remain unresolved.
- Study needs: thermal tolerances by life stage, release temperature estimates, downstream warming rates, structural feasibility, and overall effects.

**Sediment (Appendix D)**
- Tributary sand supply (Table D-1) totals about 2,713 thousand tons per year gauged, plus 723 thousand tons ungauged.
- Fluctuating flows deliver about 65 to 70 percent more sand than steady flows of the same volume (Table D-2).
- Sandbar monitoring at 29 sites across 16 research flows: 3 eroded, 11 unchanged, 15 aggraded. Measured volume rose 2.9 percent overall, but total sand volume fell 1.2 percent.
- Rapidly falling stage is the main cause of fluctuating-flow sandbar erosion; ramp rate has not been linked to erosion.
- Sand storage depends on alternative and release year; net gain probability for 1989 under the No Action alternative is 53 percent, versus under 1 percent for the 21.1 maf year (1984).

**Hydropower and economics**
- Hydropower and economic review would use Western's annual revenue, WSCC load and supply reports, and [[Power Resources Committee]] Phase II assumptions.
- Non-use values of the canyon would also be surveyed.
