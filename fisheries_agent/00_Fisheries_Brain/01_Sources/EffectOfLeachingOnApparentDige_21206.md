# Effect of Leaching on Apparent Digestion Coefficients of Feedstuffs for Salmonids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service study (Smith, Peterson and Allred, Tunison Laboratory of Fish Nutrition) tested how [[Leaching]] of feces in water affects [[Digestion Coefficient]] values for [[Rainbow Trout]]. Fecal matter was collected using the [[Metabolism Chamber Method]], then separated into solid and liquid fractions by settling, centrifugation or filtration. More than half of the fecal nitrogen was in the liquid fraction. Protein digestion coefficients calculated from solids alone were about 10 percentage points higher than those calculated from the total suspension, which the authors attribute to leaching. The paper also tabulates digestibility and [[Metabolizable Energy]] for 55 diet ingredients determined by the chamber method.

## Background & Description

**Study organism:** [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), reared intensively. The document notes that intensive culture of rainbow trout and other salmonids had grown rapidly in recent years.

**Motivation:** [[Fish Meal]] has been the main protein source in fish diets. Its high price and short supply pushed nutritionists and feed manufacturers to seek cheaper alternatives, many of which had little or no known value for fish. Reliable digestibility data were therefore needed for proteins from plant and animal sources.

**Methodological problem:** Digestion trials with fish are harder than with land animals because fecal and metabolic excretions are suspended or dissolved in large volumes of water. Earlier methods (Cho and Slinger 1978; Choubert et al. 1979; Inaba et al. 1962; Nose 1960) assumed that insoluble matter settled or filtered from the water was fecal and that soluble excretions were metabolic. These methods can expose feces to considerable leaching. [[Windell et al. 1978]] used anal suction to remove feces from the lower large intestine. [[Smith 1971]] developed a metabolism chamber that collects urine, feces and gill excretions separately and quantitatively.

**Stated purpose:** To compare digestion coefficients based on settleable or filterable solids with the coefficient based on total fecal collection.

### Materials and Methods

- **Fish:** Sound, healthy male rainbow trout weighing 200–250 g, individually weighed and marked.
- **Feeding:** Fish were force-fed daily at an amount giving a nitrogen balance near zero, equal to 1.88% of metabolic body weight (daily food = 0.0188W^0.72, with W in grams). Fish swam freely in a tank and were force-fed once daily for 4 days.
- **Chamber:** On day 5, fish were fitted with a catheter and rubber diaphragm and placed in a [[Metabolism Chamber Method]] chamber. Urine was collected through a catheter in the ureter. Feces stayed in static water around the posterior body. [[Gill Excretion]] remained in the headwater, separated from the body water by the diaphragm. Waste was pooled over the collection days and stored at 4°C.
- **Analyses:** Gross energy was measured with a Parr adiabatic calorimeter on lyophilized urine and on concentrated, lyophilized feces. Nitrogen was measured on a Technicon Autoanalyzer II after sulphuric acid digestion.
- **Headwater energy:** Derived from nitrogen, assuming about 85% of gill nitrogen is ammonia and the rest mainly urea. Heats of combustion were 68.9 kcal/mol (ammonia) and 151 kcal/mol (urea), giving a weighted average of 4.99 kcal/g N, rounded to 5.0.
- **Solid–liquid separation (three methods):** (1) settling for 24 h and decanting; (2) centrifugation for 30 min at approximately 3500 g; (3) suction filtration through Whatman No. 41 paper in a Buchner funnel.
- **Calculations:** Protein digestion coefficient = (N fed − N in feces) / N fed. Energy digestion coefficient uses energy fed and energy in feces. ME (kcal/g) = [Energy fed − (Energy in feces + Energy in urine + Energy in gill excretions)] / dry weight of feed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat/water:** Feces and excretions are suspended or dissolved in roughly 1 L of water per daily collection. The authors identify this water as the main source of leaching.
- **Stress and confinement:** Chamber methods were criticized because confinement may stress fish and reduce digestion. Gabaudan (1979), using blood lactate, found that fish in chambers were not significantly different from undisturbed fish swimming freely. Blood lactate rose when chambers were aerated with air rather than oxygen, and was higher in chambers without the rubber diaphragm and catheter. Fish were not stressed when partly restrained and oxygen was kept above air saturation.
- **Temperature:** Ingredient values in Table 3 were determined at 15°C.
- **Feed variability:** Feed handling and milling have changed over recent years, and recent samples show digestion coefficients different from earlier tests, especially for by-product feeds. Different lots of one feed may vary in nutrient content and in nutrient availability to fish.

## Key Findings

1. **Leaching effect:** More than 50% of total fecal nitrogen was in the liquid portion. Solids alone did not indicate the true amount of fecal nitrogen. Protein digestibility based on solids was about 10 percentage points higher than digestibility based on the total homogenized fecal suspension.
2. **Method agreement:** The sum of solid and liquid nitrogen closely matched the whole-suspension total, and the three separation methods agreed relatively closely.
3. **Protein digestion coefficients (solids, Table 2, Diet A, 10 tests):** Settling 94.6%, centrifugation 93.1%, filtration 95.2%. Values for the unseparated suspension were not legible in the scan.
4. **Protein digestion coefficients (solids, Table 2, Diet B, 11 tests):** Settling 92.7%, centrifugation 93.3%, filtration 93.2%.
5. **Diet composition:** Diet A contained fish meal 35%, soybean meal 20%, wheat middlings 33%, vitamin premix 2%, mineral premix 1%, carboxymethylcellulose 3% and fish oil 6%. Diet B was 70% Diet A and 30% soybean meal.
6. **Ingredient database:** Digestibility and [[Metabolizable Energy]] were tabulated for 55 diet ingredients (Table 3, values on a dry basis, at 15°C). Selected examples:
   - Anchovy fish meal (mechanically extracted): protein digestion 85.2 ± 1.7%, energy digestion 91.3 ± 1.5%, ME 4020 ± 72 kcal/kg (8 trials).
   - Herring fish meal: protein digestion 86.7 ± 0.9%, energy digestion 91.5 ± 1.1%, ME 4133 ± 59 kcal/kg (5 trials).
   - Textured vegetable protein: protein digestion 85.3 ± 1.6%, energy digestion 84.7 ± 2.3%, ME 3601 ± 127 kcal/kg (9 trials).
   - Crab, whole, dehydrated, ground: protein digestion 71.9 ± 2.6%, energy digestion 85.1 ± 1.6%, ME 3214 ± 106 kcal/kg (6 trials).
   - Wheat standard middlings: protein digestion 65.0 ± 2.5%, energy digestion 39.6 ± 1.8%, ME 1509 ± 84 kcal/kg (7 trials).
7. **Ammonia energy correction:** Ammonia has no nutritional energy value for vertebrates, but its heat of combustion must be considered. Using zero for ammonia energy would make the ME value about 5% too high. The earlier [[Smith 1976]] values were revised for this reason and because some ingredients were retested.
8. **Processing effects:** Soybean processing (roasting, microwave, extrusion, steam cooking) produced widely varying digestion coefficients in Table 3. Examples include full-fat roasted soybeans at 127°C for 10 min (protein digestion 40.3 ± 3.9%) versus 232°C for 8 min (protein digestion 77.5 ± 1.2%).
9. **Recommendation:** Variability between feed lots, and changes in processing over time, should be allowed for in fish feed formulation.
