# California Condor Reintroduction to the Vermilion Cliffs, Arizona

#ornithology #conservation-biology #cathartidae

## Executive Summary

This Canyon Journal feature (a magazine essay by several contributors, not a research paper) describes the planned reintroduction of [[California Condor]] to the [[Vermilion Cliffs]] of northern Arizona. It traces the species' deep history in North America, its decline under human pressure, and its near extinction, with only 21 birds left in 1982. It then covers the captive breeding recovery program, the legal framework for the release, and the uncertain future of the population. The authors argue that the condor's fate reflects human choices, not inevitable evolutionary failure.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and evolutionary history.** Condor-like vultures have existed in North America for about 13 million years. The genus *Gymnogyps* appeared around 1.6 million years ago. Condors were never abundant, with perhaps only a few thousand birds across their Pleistocene range. They scavenged the remains of [[Pleistocene Megafauna]] such as giant sloth, mastodon, saber-toothed cat, and mammoth. Fossil remains in the Grand Canyon caves include nests, eggshells, feathers, and bones up to 22,000 years old.

**Physical description.** Condors are among the largest flying birds in North America, with a wingspan of about nine and a half feet. They belong to the [[New World Vultures]] family. Their heads are bare, and the sexes look alike. Adults show a white wing pattern visible only when the wings are extended. The head skin turns bright pink during breeding and is dull orange otherwise. Immature birds have grey heads and dark or mottled wings. The white wing patch appears in the third year, and full adult plumage is reached in the seventh or eighth year. Condors can soar at up to 80 miles per hour, travel up to 100 miles per day, and reach altitudes of 15,000 feet using thermal updrafts.

**Life history.** Captive individuals have lived to 80 years. Condors become sexually mature at about six years, mate for life, and lay one egg every other year on average. Young depend on their parents for at least two years and learn foraging and group behavior during that time. The authors note that a species with this life history needs adult annual mortality of no more than 5–7%.

**Historical timeline.**
- Arrival of modern humans in North America (30,000–12,000 years ago), followed by the decline of large mammals and the birds that depended on them.
- Sightings in Arizona from about 1865 to 1924. The last known sighting was at Williams in 1924.
- The last condor in the Grand Canyon was shot by a prospector in 1881.
- Captive breeding began in 1983. The last wild condor was caught in 1987.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline.** Egg and specimen collecting and shooting caused the first major collapse. The authors call this the first time in 13 million years that condors faced a predator. During the 1980s, wild mortality ranged from 23% to 40% per year. Causes included collisions with human structures, shooting, lead poisoning from bullets, predator-control chemicals, and habitat fragmentation. Dr. Steven Emslie suggests that 1800s sightings may reflect range expansion driven by European livestock introductions. In 1889 there were 200,000 sheep grazing the Kaibab Plateau.

**Site selection.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service analyzed the Vermilion Cliffs before choosing them. Reported advantages include:
- Safe release, roosting, and nesting sites
- Favorable winds for soaring and takeoff
- Eighteen water sources within three miles
- The nearest power lines lie about 21 miles north, reducing collision risk
- Public viewing opportunities and logistical access

The site lies about 12 miles west of Lees Ferry and 30 miles north of [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Released birds are expected to disperse into the Colorado River system, including [[Glen Canyon]], [[Lake Powell]], the Grand Canyon, and [[Lake Mead]].

**Food availability.** The Arizona Department of Game and Fish reported:
- 2,100 mule deer and 225 bighorn sheep on the Paria Plateau
- 9,500 mule deer on the Kaibab Plateau, with a 10% annual mortality rate
- About 225 bison and 100 pronghorn in the area

Wallace's assessment was that "there is enough food for some; only time will tell how many." Condors can scavenge any carrion, including hunters' gut piles, and may also feed on trout at Nankoweap Creek during spawning runs. Supplemental feeding may be needed for up to ten years, because the released birds have no parents to teach them.

**Legal framework.** The birds were released under Section 10j of the [[Endangered Species Act]], which allows "non-essential experimental populations." Under this designation, losing the population would not appreciably reduce the species' survival chances. Intentionally harming, killing, or capturing a condor remains illegal. Section 10j was added in 1982 after local opposition to earlier reintroductions. Opposition also arose in Kanab, Utah, where residents objected to federal involvement more than to the birds themselves.

**Broader context.** From 1973 to 1986, 93 endangered bird and mammal species were in recovery programs in the United States. The authors cite other recoveries, including the [[Mauritius Kestrel]] (fewer than ten birds in 1974, more than 300 today, with about 70 breeding pairs) and peregrine falcons (39 U.S. breeding pairs in 1975, about 1,000 now).

## Key Findings

- **Population crisis:** The world population of California condors was 21 in 1982. Wild birds were brought into captivity in 1987, when no free-flying condors remained.
- **Captive breeding:** Wild eggs were hatched at the [[San Diego Zoo]], and wild birds were bred at the [[Los Angeles Zoo]]. Removing eggs from nests increased clutch output from one to two or three eggs. Hand puppets were used to prevent imprinting on humans.
- **Genetic bottleneck:** All living condors descend from 14 individuals representing three genetic groups. Young birds are paired to maximize genetic diversity.
- **Current status:** There are 107 condors in the world. Of 31 birds returned to the wild in California, 17 remain. Four died in power line collisions, one from antifreeze ingestion, and nine were returned to captivity for inappropriate behavior. Fifteen captive breeding pairs produce about 20 young per year.
- **Aversion training:** All released birds now undergo aversion training to avoid people and power lines.
- **Monitoring:** Released birds wear numbered and colored wing tags and radio transmitters. The [[Peregrine Fund]] tracks them 24 hours a day, year-round, and sightings are reported to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The Peregrine Fund implements the program using private donations rather than tax dollars.
- **Recovery criteria:** The federal recovery plan requires at least two non-captive populations and one captive population. Each must number at least 150 birds, include at least 15 breeding pairs, be reproductively self-sustaining, and have positive growth. Wild populations must be geographically separate and descend from all 14 founders. Only then could the species be reclassified from endangered to threatened.
- **Uncertain outlook:** Yearly evaluations will decide whether releases continue. The contributors agree that "it will be many decades before we really know the success or failure of these efforts."
- **Ethical conclusion:** The authors argue that human actions, not evolutionary obsolescence, caused the decline. They frame the question as "what will we do with that power?"
