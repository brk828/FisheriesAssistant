## Executive Summary
This BLM (Bureau of Land Management) memorandum documents a multi-year effort (1983–1991) to establish and re-establish a population of the federally endangered **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) in **Mesquite Spring**, Arizona. An initial 1983 stocking of 200 individuals reportedly survived and reproduced but went undetected by 1989 due to habitat degradation from livestock use, duckweed overgrowth, and organic sludge accumulation. Following pond renovation (fencing, dredging, substrate addition) in 1991, a second stocking attempt of ~200 pupfish resulted in acute mass mortality (~25 individuals within 10 minutes) attributed to critically low **dissolved oxygen** levels, likely caused by a dense surface mat of duckweed (*Lemna* sp.) blocking atmospheric gas exchange, compounded by bacterial decomposition of residual organic matter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Small, shallow desert spring pond (final renovated dimensions ~7 x 5 x 1 m)
- **Substrate:** Historically thick (~0.30 m) organic ooze/sludge; renovated with ~0.15 m layer of sand and gravel to create potential spawning substrate
- **Vegetation cover:** Dense surface mat of duckweed (*[[Lemna]]* sp.) covering entire pond, restricting atmospheric oxygen exchange
- **Anthropogenic pressures:**
  - Livestock grazing/trampling degrading pond margins and fencing infrastructure
  - Replacement of original fencing (railroad tie/barbwire) with "hog-wire," later removed and replaced with smooth four-strand barbwire cattle exclusion fence (16 x 27 m enclosure)
  - Sediment/organic matter accumulation from lack of maintenance
- **Water Quality Parameters (4 October 1991, Hach Water Test Kit Model FF-1A):**

## Key Findings
1. **1983 Stocking Outcome:** 200 desert pupfish stocked into Mesquite Spring were confirmed to survive and reproduce (per FY84 HMP summary), but no population data was collected; by July 1989 the population could not be located via seining.
2. **Habitat Degradation (1989):** Site inspection revealed increased livestock impact, deteriorated fencing, complete duckweed coverage, and a thick (~0.30 m) layer of organic ooze substrate — likely contributing to the apparent extirpation of the population.
3. **Renovation Efforts (1991):** Two-phase dredging operation removed railroad ties and ~0.75 m of benthic sludge; cattle-exclusion fencing was rebuilt; sand/gravel substrate was added to support future spawning.
4. **Water Chemistry Post-Renovation:** Despite renovation, dissolved oxygen remained critically low (0.60–1.0 mg/l), still well above lethal thresholds cited in literature but insufficient to prevent mortality upon fish introduction — likely due to acute physiological shock from a steep DO gradient relative to source pond conditions.
5. **Mass Mortality Event (10 October 1991):** Of ~200 pupfish transported from Boyce Thompson Arboretum, approximately 25 died within ~10 minutes of release, observed gasping at the duckweed-covered surface. Remaining fish in the second transport cooler were not released and were returned to the source pond as a precaution.
6. **Probable Cause of Mortality:** Combination of (a) duckweed mat blocking atmosphere–water oxygen exchange, and (b) bacterial decomposition of residual organic sludge depleting available oxygen — creating an oxygen "shock" environment beyond what the translocated fish could physiologically tolerate, despite the species' known hypoxia tolerance ([[Barlow 1958]]).
7. **Management Recommendation:** Current biological conditions at Mesquite Spring were deemed unsuitable for sustaining a desert pupfish population; continued monitoring was recommended to reassess habitat suitability and confirm presence/absence of surviving individuals.
