# Rediscovery of the Shoshone Pupfish at Shoshone Springs, Inyo County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Shoshone Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis shoshone*) was regarded as extinct since before 1970. In July 1986, pupfish were found to be common to abundant throughout the outflow of [[Shoshone Springs]] and along a permanent segment of the [[Amargosa River]] near Shoshone, California. Meristic comparison with a [[Tecopa Bore]] population and with [[Miller 1948]] data indicates that the Shoshone fish most closely match *C. n. shoshone*, supporting the hypothesis that the population persisted undetected in a marshy outflow. The modal number of caudal fin-rays shifted from 18 in all earlier samples to 16 in 1986, which the authors attribute to a genetic bottleneck.

## Background & Description

The study reviews the decline of [[Death Valley Pupfishes]], which have been under stress since World War II because of habitat loss and predation or competition from exotic species. Two Amargosa pupfish subspecies, the [[Tecopa Pupfish]] (*C. n. calidae*) and the Shoshone Pupfish, were reported extinct (Pister 1974). The Tecopa Pupfish was the first organism officially declared extinct under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The Shoshone Pupfish was never formally listed, because it was considered gone before the Act was passed.

Historical records trace the population's decline:

- Common in the Shoshone outflow in 1938 (Miller).
- No specimens found in January 1966 or July 1967 (Miller 1967). [[Robert Ornduff]] reported the fish fairly common in the lower outlet stream during 1966.
- Considered probably extinct by November 1969 (Miller 1970).
- None found in 1977 collections (Selby 1977), despite abundant [[Mosquitofish]].

Three hypotheses were proposed to explain the 1986 occurrence: (1) natural dispersal from another area during a flood; (2) human introduction; and (3) a small, undetected persistent population. Geographic proximity suggested hypotheses 1 or 2 would yield fish resembling *C. n. amargosae*, while hypothesis 3 would yield fish resembling *C. n. shoshone*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Shoshone Springs lies at the base of a volcanic hill on the northern edge of Shoshone, at roughly 518 m elevation. Concrete boxes have enclosed the source for more than 48 years, diverting most of the flow to the town and swimming pool. The outlet creek runs 400–500 m to the Amargosa River, where elevation is below 487 m.

**Conditions on 31 July 1986:**
- Channel width about 8 cm to 1.5 m; depth 8 cm to 0.5 m.
- Conductivity about 2959 µmhos/cm; pH 8.2.
- Chlorine detectable but below 0.4 ppm at the Old State Highway.
- Water temperature 34°C at an air temperature of 39°C; winter temperatures may fall to about 28°C (Miller 1948).

**Channel sections.** The channel is a concrete ditch between the Old State Highway and State Highway 127. Below Highway 127 it passes through a dense [[Cattail Marsh]] and a nearly impenetrable [[Tamarisk Thicket]] before reaching the Amargosa River. In summer 1986 water filled the river bed for about 1.5 km north to 3–4 km south of Shoshone.

**Habitat disruption.** The authors document a concrete channel, chlorine from pool outflow, concrete spring boxes, heavy equipment use in fall 1986, and an deeply eroded Amargosa channel. Disruption has been common over the past 40 years, yet the subspecies has shown considerable resiliency.

## Key Findings

**Rediscovery and status**
- Pupfish were found in large numbers throughout the Shoshone Springs system in summer 1986 and most closely resemble *C. n. shoshone* as described by Miller (1948).
- The authors conclude the population probably persisted continuously, and earlier failures to detect it likely reflected small population sizes.
- Pupfish were not found in the Shoshone area in 1966–1977 collections, despite earlier documentation of the subspecies.

**Meristic comparison**
- Sixty specimens from each population were analyzed for dorsal, anal, caudal, pectoral, and pelvic fin-rays, and for body and caudal peduncle scale counts (following [[Miller 1948]]).
- The Shoshone population has lower mean counts (±2 SE) of pelvic, dorsal, and caudal fin-rays than the Tecopa Bore population.
- Body scale circumference means from 1986 were 22.78 ± 0.29 (Shoshone), compared with 23.25 ± 0.17 in 1939 data, and 24.02 ± 0.26 and 26.26 ± 0.15 for the two 1986 Tecopa Bore and Amargosa samples.

**Caudal fin-ray shift**
- Earlier samples (1891–1969) showed a strong modal count of 18 caudal rays; the 1986 collection showed 16.
- Only the Bradford's Spring race of the [[Ash Meadows Pupfish]] (*C. n. mionectes*) had a modal count of 16 in the 1929-specimen dataset of Miller (1948), and small specimen size partly explained that result.
- Caudal-ray counts from 29 adults at Bradford's Spring (16–20 rays, modal 18) rule out introduction from that site.
- The authors propose a [[Genetic Bottleneck]] released dominance and epistasis variance, allowing selection to favor 16-ray individuals.

**Conservation outlook**
- The authors suggest careful planning and protection of remaining habitat could allow the Shoshone Pupfish to persist as one of the oldest, most characteristic inhabitants of Shoshone, California.
- Comparable bottleneck-driven morphological change has been documented in the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] refugium population (Williams 1977).

**Literature**
