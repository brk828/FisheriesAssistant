## Executive Summary

This study documents the ichthyofauna of the lower [[Rio Yaqui]] basin, Sonora, Mexico, focusing on the [[Rio Sahuaripa]] and [[Rio Bacanora]] sub-basins (draining from the Rios Papigochic, Sirupa, and Aros). Based on 24 collections conducted in August 1988, researchers recorded 20 fish species: 50% native, 45% exotic, and 5% euryhaline. The study reveals a clear spatial partitioning between native and introduced species, with natives concentrated in upper tributaries (78.5%) and exotics dominating the main river channel. The paper raises early conservation concerns regarding the displacement of native fishes by invasive species such as [[Gambusia affinis]] and [[Tilapia]], driven by competition, predation, and hybridization pressures, despite the apparent lack of physical habitat degradation.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide detailed morphometric or meristic data for the species surveyed. Species are referenced primarily by taxonomic identity and relative abundance rather than physical description. Noted taxa include:

- **[[Ictalurus pricei]]** (Yaqui catfish) – native Ictaluridae, recorded in very low numbers.
- **[[Cichlasoma beani]]** – native cichlid, recorded in very low numbers.
- **[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]** – native poeciliid, recorded in very low numbers.
- **[[Dorosoma smithi]]** – native gizzard shad, previously recorded in the basin but **not detected** in this survey.
- **[[Dorosoma petenense]]** – euryhaline/exotic shad, recorded in the main river bed near its mouth close to Ciudad Obregón.
- **[[Gambusia affinis]]** – exotic poeciliid, widely distributed across the basin.
- **[[Tilapia]]** sp. – exotic cichlid, widely distributed; shares habitat extensively with native species in both river bed and small streams.
- **[[Campostoma ornatum]]**, **[[Catostomus bernardini]]**, **[[Agosia chrysogaster]]** – native cyprinids/catostomids noted specifically at Rio Sahuaripa due to high parasite loads.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat partitioning**: Native species predominantly occupy the uppermost effluents/tributary streams (78.5% of native occurrences), while exotic species preferentially occupy the main channel of the [[Rio Yaqui]].
- **Habitat overlap**: [[Tilapia]] is notable for sharing habitat with most native species in both the main river bed and smaller tributary streams, representing the greatest zone of native-exotic overlap.
- **Range restriction concerns**: [[Ictalurus pricei]], [[Cichlasoma beani]], and [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] were found in very small numbers, suggesting population stress or restricted range.
- **Species loss**: [[Dorosoma smithi]], previously documented in the basin, was absent from the 1988 survey collections.
- **Distribution of euryhaline species**: [[Dorosoma petenense]] was localized to the main river bed near the river mouth, close to Ciudad Obregón, consistent with its euryhaline physiology and proximity to estuarine/coastal influence.
- **Parasite ecology**: [[Lernaea]] sp. (anchor worm, Copepoda) and digenetic trematode cercariae were documented, with infestation levels highest on native species — particularly *Poeciliopsis* spp., [[Campostoma ornatum]], [[Catostomus bernardini]], and [[Agosia chrysogaster]] at Rio Sahuaripa.
- **Habitat condition**: No physical alteration of the river or stream habitats was detected by the survey team, indicating that observed native fish declines are likely driven by biotic (exotic species interactions) rather than abiotic/habitat degradation factors at the time of study.

## Key Findings

1. A total of **20 fish species** were recorded from 24 collections across the lower Rio Yaqui basin in August 1988: **50% native, 45% exotic, 5% euryhaline**.
2. **78.5% of native species** occurrences were concentrated in the uppermost effluents/tributaries, contrasting with exotic species which preferred the **main Rio Yaqui channel**.
3. Three native species — **[[Ictalurus pricei]]**, **[[Cichlasoma beani]]**, and **[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]** — were found in critically low numbers, suggesting potential population decline.
4. **[[Dorosoma smithi]]**, previously documented in the region, was **not recorded** in this survey, raising concern about local extirpation.
5. **[[Gambusia affinis]]** and **[[Tilapia]]** (both exotic) were the most widely distributed species in the basin, with Tilapia notably overlapping heavily with native species habitat in both river beds and small streams.
6. **[[Dorosoma petenense]]** was restricted to the main river channel near its mouth, close to Ciudad Obregón.
7. Parasitism — **[[Lernaea]]** sp. and digenetic trematode cercariae — was observed predominantly on native species, with the highest infestation intensities recorded at Rio Sahuaripa on *Poeciliopsis* spp., [[Campostoma ornatum]], [[Catostomus bernardini]], and [[Agosia chrysogaster]].
8. No detectable physical alteration of river/stream habitats was found, implying that native fish decline is primarily attributable to **biotic pressure from exotic species** — specifically competition for habitat and food resources, predation, and hybridization — rather than habitat destruction.
9. The study underscores an early conservation warning for native Rio Yaqui ichthyofauna due to the ongoing invasion and establishment of exotic species within the basin's core river habitats.
