# Brown Trout Eradication and Golden Trout Restoration, South Fork Kern River (1976)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 project report from the [[California Department Of Fish And Game]] (DFG) and the [[Inyo National Forest]] Service describes the eradication of invasive [[Brown Trout]] from the upper [[South Fork Kern River]] on the [[Kern Plateau]]. The goal was to restore a pure [[Golden Trout]] stream and protect nearby [[Golden Trout Creek]] from illegal transplants. Earlier attempts using electrofishing and [[Calcium Hypochlorite]] (HTH) had failed. The project used [[Antimycin]] A, applied through a chain of drip stations and sand broadcasts over about 10 stream miles, from September 8 to 16, 1976. Golden trout were temporarily held and then restocked. The authors judged the treatment a likely 100% kill, pending later monitoring.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Kern Plateau has rolling hills, flat topography, meandering streams and large meadows. The South Fork Kern emerges from springs above [[South Fork Meadows]], flows through [[Tunnel Meadow]] and [[Ramshaw Meadow]], and reaches [[Templeton Meadow]], where barriers had been built in 1973.
- **Discovery:** On May 22, 1969, a brown trout was caught in Tunnel Meadow. In September 1969, shocking yielded browns of year classes 0, I and IV, suggesting they had jumped a falls barrier during high water in 1967–1969.
- **Origin hypotheses:** (1) Northward migration from [[Kennedy Meadows]], where browns had been planted since 1940, including 21,000 fingerlings in 1962; (2) an illegal angler plant. The first report from Ramshaw Meadow was in 1966.
- **Concerns:** Browns were seen as aggressive and cannibalistic, capable of decimating the golden trout, which the report calls the State Fish with a "20,000 year homeland." Proximity to Golden Trout Creek raised the risk of a "coffee can transplant."
- **Failed earlier efforts:**
  - 1969: Electrofishing and 96 lb HTH in the lower Tunnel Meadow.
  - 1970: A barrier between Ramshaw and Tunnel Meadows (built with the Forest Service), followed by electrofishing and 75 lb HTH; browns were still found.
  - 1971–1975: Annual shocking and HTH use. The team concluded these methods could not control the population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Chemical selection.** Antimycin was chosen because it kills eggs in gravel as well as free-swimming fish, fish cannot detect it, and its toxic effect is irreversible. It is also far more concentrated than [[Rotenone]], so less material must be carried into the backcountry. The main constraint was supply: antimycin was no longer manufactured, and the search for enough material remained unresolved.

**Dispersal hardware.** Ten usable 55-gallon drums (six sound drums from the Bishop dump, with a second side bung welded onto four) were steam-cleaned and fitted with plumbing modeled on a prototype in *The Progressive Fish Culturist* (January 1963). Five-gallon plastic bottles with drilled bottoms drained in about hours and served springs and stringers. More than 300 feet of hardware cloth made live cages and barriers. A Forest Service helicopter flew the gear in.

**Pre-treatment reconnaissance.** In August 1976, a survey crew electroshocked stringers in Ramshaw and Templeton meadows to compare brown and golden trout numbers, checked for barriers, and mapped every stringer, spring and marshy area needing treatment. The work took three days.

**Holding area.** A channel diverted from Golden Trout Creek was fenced with 1/4-inch hardware cloth at four points. The holding area was about 40 yards long with a flow of roughly ½ cfs. The site was chosen because a lava dam and silt deposits historically separated the two drainages by a ridge only about 175 yards wide.

**Water conditions.** Rain on September 10 raised water levels. About 2,000 fish escaped from live cages in the South Fork before they could be moved. The team limited treatment to the headwaters through Tunnel Meadow and deferred the Ramshaw and Templeton section to 1977.

## Key Findings

**Treatment design**
- Ten drip stations were placed on [[South Fork Meadows]], Tunnel Meadow and the rapids below. Stations 1–7 and the rapids-area drums were set between 10 and 20 ppb, and springs and stringers received 30 cc concentrate per 5-gallon jug (about 55 ppb).
- Each 55-gallon drum drained in about 3½ hours at about 1 quart per minute.
- Concentrations were raised through rapids, where oxidation was expected to be faster.
- Six additional drums were placed below the hardware cloth fence at roughly ½-mile intervals at 20 ppb. Sand was broadcast over marshes, undercut banks and slow water.
- Potassium permanganate neutralization was not used. The team judged it hard to obtain and assumed most of the chemical would oxidize in the rapids above Ramshaw Meadow.

**Bioassay**
- The first jug dose of 3 cc Fintrol-5 (5.5 ppb) was insufficient. Refilling with 10 cc left fish alive after several hours.
- By September 10, six of the bioassay fish at Lewis Stringer were dead, and the last caged fish was in distress.
- On September 15, no living fish were seen in the stream. On September 16, all bioassay fish were dead.

**Mortality**
- **September 16:** Several large dead brown trout were seen. The kill extended down into Ramshaw Meadow through the first couple of beaver dams.
- **September 17:** 212 dead golden trout were measured in Tunnel Meadow, averaging 3.4 inches. Two brown trout measured 7.0 and 4.0 inches. Sixteen brown trout from Ramshaw Meadow averaged 9.4 inches, the largest 19.0 inches.
- **September 19:** No living fish were seen or shocked below Tunnel Guard Station. A species check found 140 golden and 181 brown trout, but 100 golden trout had been shocked from this section before treatment. From the Ramshaw barrier to the first beaver dam, 56 suckers (*Catostomus occidentalis*), 75 browns and 1 golden trout were found.
- **September 21:** Livecars holding fish from Tunnel Meadow showed no mortality, suggesting the chemical had detoxified.

**Aquatic insects**
- Sally Stefferud, an aquatic entomologist, sampled insects before, during and after treatment.
- She found that antimycin did affect the aquatic insect fauna. This contradicted the Salmon Falls River project report of "no bad effects."

**Restocking and recapture**
- On September 22, 750 golden trout were removed from the Golden Trout Creek holding area and carried by mule to South Fork Meadow.
- On September 23, full recapture began. Fish under logs and roots were hard to reach, and water was cold in the mornings.
- Restocking totaled 1,930 fish (1,000 below the lower rock outcropping, 200 below Tunnel Guard Station, 730 from McConnel Meadow to East Meadow fence), plus about 70 in South Fork Meadow.
- More than 2,000 fewer fish were recovered than were placed in the holding area. Proposed causes were escape, miscounting, incomplete recapture, predation by raccoons or birds, and predation or competition among size classes. The authors thought a combination was most likely.
- Golden trout from Golden Trout Creek and the South Fork were compared, but many fish were not distinctive enough to separate them.

**Costs**
- Total project expenses were $2,980.81. Air fares were the largest item at $1,861.00. Equipment and materials came to $856.32, and rentals and wages to $263.50.
- Personnel data list 195 man-days across pretreatment, treatment and posttreatment phases.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors concluded that antimycin suited backcountry work because of its high potency. They recommended future use of antimycin bars to replace the 55-gallon drums and 5-gallon jugs.
- The project appeared to achieve a 100% kill, to the best of the authors' knowledge, pending later monitoring by fishing and shocking.
- Treatment in summer 1977, during a second drought year, was recommended. Lower flow would reduce chemical needs and limit marshy, hard-to-treat terrain.
- Ramshaw and Templeton meadows were scheduled for treatment in summer 1977. Barriers at Templeton were improved in October 1976.
- The authors cautioned against illegal plants of trout by anglers that could undo the restoration.
