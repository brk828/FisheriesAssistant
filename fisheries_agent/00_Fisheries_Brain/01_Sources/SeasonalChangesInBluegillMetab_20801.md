# Seasonal Changes in Bluegill Metabolism

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by Donald E. Wohlschlag and Rogelio O. Juliano measured [[Oxygen Consumption]] in wild [[Lepomis Macrochirus]] (bluegill) in [[Felt Lake]], a small California reservoir, from March 1956 to February 1957. Using a field [[Respiration Chamber]], the authors fitted [[Multiple Regression Analysis]] models relating log oxygen consumption to log body weight, swimming velocity and water temperature, separately for four limnologically defined seasons. Weight coefficients were close to the classical [[Krogh Constant]] (~0.85) except in summer. The authors conclude that bluegill in western U.S. reservoirs of this type have poorer [[Biological Productivity]] than eastern populations, because of a long growing season with inadequate food and no evident mechanism for lowering [[Metabolic Rate]] during lean periods.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Lepomis Macrochirus]] (Centrarchidae), the dominant fish in Felt Lake.
- **Other fish present:** [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), brown bullhead (*Ictalurus nebulosus*) and sculpin (*Cottus asper*), all in very small populations.
- **Rationale:** Most published fish metabolism data come from acclimated laboratory fish. Little was known about how metabolism varies with seasonal limnological conditions in natural populations.
- **Factors affecting respiration** (reviewed from Fry 1957): temperature, acclimation, oxygen and carbon dioxide content, body weight, body fat, activity, feeding state, [[Social Facilitation]], sex, spawning, stress, parasitism and disease.
- **Rate types:** "standard" (near basal), "routine" (spontaneous activity other than swimming) and "active" (sustained maximum swimming).

### Study Site

Felt Lake is a reservoir behind an earth-fill dam, fed by a half-mile diversion ditch from Los Trancos Creek.

- Drainage basin above diversion: about 5.4 sq mi; immediate tributary basin: about 110 acres.
- Rainfall: about 15 inches per year, mostly November to March.
- Capacity: about 300 million gallons at 46 acres surface area, held until about May.
- Drawdown: roughly 10–12 ft by late summer, when volume was about 125 million gallons.
- Surface temperatures in 1956–57 ranged from 8.0 to 26.5 °C. Surface oxygen stayed near saturation.

### Field Apparatus

- **Chamber:** a rotating, fully enclosed transparent plastic chamber, 25 cm inside diameter, 10 cm deep, volume 10,400 ml, suspended from a phonograph-motor drive. One revolution equals about 1 m of fish swimming.
- **Oxygen analysis:** modified Winkler method, with samples taken at 10–15 minute intervals over 30–60 minutes.
- **Fish handling:** bluegills were seined or trapped, held in a live car for at least one hour, then run singly or in groups of 2 to 18.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water level and littoral zone:** Dense weed growth followed the falling water level, so the littoral fauna was poorly developed. Lake volume fell by about 50% from spring to autumn.
- **Food supply:** Bottom fauna declined sharply through the year. For bluegill of 125 mm fork length and over, mean stomach volume fell to 0.20 cc in autumn and 0.17 cc in early winter, when mollusks and plant remains dominated. By late March insects returned, and mean volume rose to 0.52 cc.
- **Condition decline:** Mean weight at fixed length fell from spring to autumn. Fish of 125–149 mm fell from 54.8 g to 50.5 g, and fish of 150 mm and over fell from 90.1 g to 75.5 g.
- **Population:** Fish of 125 mm and over numbered about 20,000–25,000 in spring and early summer. Autumn mortality, poor growth and parasitism reduced this by as much as one-half.
- **Spawning:** Smaller fish began to grow and spawn in April–May. Larger fish spawned from May to June, continuing into August.
- **Spawning-bed behavior:** Spawning fish stayed on shallow beds despite diurnal temperature swings of an estimated 5 °C. A May–June 1955 oxygen-depletion kill affected only spawning bluegill.

## Key Findings

### Seasonal Regression Models

The model used was: Y = constant + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + b₃X₃, where Y is expected log mg O₂ per hour per kg, X₁ is log weight (g), X₂ is swimming velocity (m/min) and X₃ is temperature (°C). The "average weights" equations (per fish basis) were selected as best representing seasonal relationships:

| Season | Equation | Constant | b₁ (log weight) | b₂ (activity) | b₃ (temperature) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apr–June | Eq. 6 | −0.6954 | 0.8036 | 0.0250 | 0.0069 (not significant) |
| July–Aug | Eq. 7 | −2.1951 | 1.0621 | 0.0138 | 0.0567 |
| Sept–Dec | Eq. 8 | −0.9242 | 0.8494 | 0.0142 | 0.0198 |
| Jan–Feb (active fish) | Eq. 10 | −1.9310 | 0.8624 | 0.0427 | 0.1153 |

- Multiple correlations were highly significant for all equations.
- All partial coefficients were significant except the low spring b₃ = 0.0069.
- Equations 6, 8 and 10 gave weight coefficients close to Krogh's ~0.85. The summer value (1.06) is higher, possibly because larger fish were spawning while smaller fish had finished.
- Winter temperature coefficients were the highest, which the authors suggest reflects spontaneous non-swimming activity at temperature extremes.

### Activity, Temperature and Sex

- Swimming-velocity relationships were similar across spring, summer and autumn. Winter required relatively more oxygen for comparable swimming, possibly due to [[Cold Adaptation]].
- Temperature effects were similar in spring and autumn, lower than summer and much lower than winter.
- Females had higher metabolic rates than males in every season. None of the differences was statistically significant.

### Effect of Grouping

- All ten April–June group runs had observed rates above expected values.
- Six of nine September–December group runs were above expected values. The authors judged this not a significant indication of difference.
- Groups containing spawning fish showed higher-than-average rates. Post-spawning or immature groups in summer showed lower rates, consistent with [[Social Facilitation]] studies on goldfish.
- Pritchard (personal communication) reported lower per-weight oxygen use in groups than in single fish in late summer, with no difference in winter.

### Metabolic Adaptation and Productivity

- Predicted spring and autumn rates at 17 °C were of the same order. Winter predictions at 10 °C were also comparable, so the data show no clear metabolic depression beyond what temperature and activity would predict.
- Growth rates at Felt Lake were of the same order as northern Indiana bluegill (≥90 mm, [[Gerking Protein Turnover]] data), despite a growing season estimated at about twice as long.
- **Protein-turnover comparison:** For a Gordy Lake (Indiana) summer population of 8,979 fish weighing 420.0 kg (mean 46.78 g), the seasonal oxygen requirement was about 71 L. Felt Lake estimates for a 46.78 g fish over 150 days were 28 L at zero velocity and 38 L at the average summer swimming velocity of 9.06 m/min. The authors read this as lower production per unit weight at Felt Lake.
- **Conclusion:** Without a mechanism to suppress maintenance metabolism or secure food during the long growing season, bluegill productivity in western U.S. waters of the Felt Lake type is expected to be poorer than in native eastern habitats.

### Methodological Notes

- Oxygen in the chamber fell linearly, usually to no less than about 70% saturation, so the rates were not biased by declining oxygen tension.
- March 1956 data were excluded because shallow-water temperatures varied by 2–3 °C during the day, which made them unusually variable.
- The authors preferred equations based on average weights over total weights, because they gave better statistical significance for the parameters, though the statistical advantage was acknowledged as possibly illusory.
