# Fishes and Aquatic Habitats of the San Pedro River System, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) and written by Paul C. Marsh, Professor of Zoology at Arizona State University, documents the fish fauna of the upper San Pedro River basin and assesses the causes of native fish decline. Of 19 native fish species known from the Gila River system, at least 13 occurred historically in the San Pedro, and eight persist as remnant populations. The report reconstructs pre-disturbance aquatic habitats, primarily [[Cienega]] marshlands, and argues that the 1890 [[Arroyo Cutting]] event fundamentally changed the river's hydrology and fish communities. It concludes with recommendations for habitat restoration, native fish reintroduction and management within the proposed San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area.

## Background & Description

The San Pedro River originates in southern Sonora, Mexico, and flows roughly 240 km north to join the [[Gila River]] near Winkelman, Arizona. Its watershed of about 6,635 km² spans a wide range of vegetation zones, from coniferous forest above 2,300 m to Sonoran desertscrub near 730 m at the mouth. The mainstream averages about 4.0 m/km gradient and flows through structural basins in a channel now largely incised.

Fish records for the basin begin with collections by the U.S. and Mexican Boundary Survey (1850s) and the [[U.S. Fish Commission]], followed by [[Carl Hubbs]] (1938), [[James Simon]] (1943), [[Robert Miller]] (1950s–60s), and extensive collections by [[Ulysses Minckley]] at Arizona State University from 1964 onward. Historic specimens are housed at the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] and ASU.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic hydrology and habitat.** Before about 1890 the mainstream consisted of marshy [[Cienega]] habitats alternating with short incised reaches, supported by perennial springs and a balance of aggradation and degradation. Historical accounts describe dense riparian marshes, beaver dams, and perennial surface flow. Around 1890 the river cut a steep-walled trench, after which flow became intermittent and the floodplain water table dropped.

**Current conditions.** Most of the mainstream remains incised. Substrate is mostly sand with shifting bedload, offering little pool or cover habitat. Discharge near the mouth has averaged 1.67 m³/s over 13 years, with a median of 1.08 m³/s and a range from no flow to 476 m³/s. Flow is bimodal, with floods in summer and winter separated by spring and autumn droughts. Dense riparian vegetation covers only a fraction of former extent, and cultivated land and cattle grazing have expanded since the 1930s.

**Land use history.** Indigenous irrigation, Spanish and Mexican settlement, cattle ranching (with heavy stocking from the 1870s), mining at Tombstone and Charleston, and groundwater pumping for agriculture all contributed to habitat change. Apache raids limited settlement until the late 1800s.

**Upstream mining.** Open-pit copper mining at Cananea, Sonora, in the headwaters poses a continuing pollution threat. A 1979 spill turned water rust-red, with pH as low as 3.1 and dissolved oxygen as low as 2.0 mg/L, killing aquatic life for at least 100 km downstream.

**Introduced species.** Of 14 recorded introduced fishes, the most significant include [[Common Carp]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Red Shiner]]. Mosquitofish predation is considered the main factor in the decline of the Sonoran topminnow.

## Key Findings

- **Historical richness:** The San Pedro supported at least 13 native fish species; eight persist as remnant populations. Of the total native fauna of 19 species, one is extinct.
- **Faunal decline:** Native species declined gradually after 1890. Large-river species (Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker) disappeared first, followed by cienega-associated species (Gila chub) and species of pool-riffle streams (roundtail chub). Gravel-bottomed stream species (loach minnow, speckled dace, spikedace, Sonoran sucker) persisted about 50 years after incision.
- **Persistence:** Longfin dace and desert sucker remain in the mainstream. Longfin dace made up 70 to 80% of mainstream fish collected since 1964 (figure as given in the source) and desert sucker less than 30%.
- **Refugia:** [[Aravaipa Creek]] supports seven native species, including spikedace, loach minnow, roundtail chub, and Sonoran sucker. [[Redfield Canyon]] and the [[Babocomari River]] also retain native fauna. Of 13 native fishes, nine are under some form of physical protection in the basin, and four are extirpated from the mainstream.
- **Hydrologic reconstruction:** Pre-disturbance flow regimes were more stable, with greater base flow, fewer and smaller floods, and extensive cienega storage. Habitat stability and heterogeneity supported large populations of fishes in categories requiring permanent water.
- **Federal and state status:** Spikedace and loach minnow are federally listed as Threatened. Colorado squawfish, Sonoran topminnow, and desert pupfish are Endangered. Gila chub and razorback sucker are candidates for listing.
- **Restoration recommendations:** The author recommends watershed erosion control, reduction of grazing, and construction of off-river semi-natural channels to re-create cienega habitat and water spreading. Non-native fish invasion must be managed, and stocking should use genetically appropriate source populations.
- **Prioritisation:** Top priority is protection of extant populations in Aravaipa Creek, Redfield Canyon, and the Babocomari River basin. Minimum flows of at least 0.28 m³/s are recommended to maintain persistent species.
- **Management philosophy:** The author argues against designating reintroduced populations as "nonessential experimental," arguing this is an abrogation of federal responsibility, and advocates a multi-species approach.
