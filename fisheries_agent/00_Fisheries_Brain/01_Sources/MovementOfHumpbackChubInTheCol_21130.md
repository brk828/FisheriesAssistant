# Movement of Humpback Chub in the Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study describes the movements of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona, from October 1990 through November 1993, and evaluates how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations influence those movements. Adults showed strong spatial fidelity and limited long-range movement within the mainstem. The largest movements were seasonal spawning migrations between the mainstem and the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), which the authors attribute to cold, suboptimal mainstem temperatures released from the dam. Daily movement rates were higher under high-fluctuating flows than under [[Interim Flows]], but long-range displacement did not differ between the two flow regimes. The manuscript was submitted to the *North American Journal of Fisheries Management* by R.J. Ryel and R.A. Valdez of BIO/WEST, Inc., with data collected under contract to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a relatively large endemic cyprinid of the Colorado River system. It has declined in numbers and distribution since dam construction. The Grand Canyon population is one of six remaining populations, and the species was listed as endangered. Earlier work in [[Black Rocks]] and [[Westwater Canyon]] in the upper basin indicated that adults were sedentary and did not make sizable spawning or inter-population movements. By contrast, related Colorado River fishes such as [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] showed much larger movements. Prior Grand Canyon work presumed that adults moved from the mainstem into the LCR to spawn, but mainstem movement had not been studied in depth.

The study used two main tools:

- **[[Radiotelemetry]]:** 75 adults were implanted with radio transmitters in the LCR inflow aggregation (LCRI), of which 69 were analyzed. Three more adults were tracked in [[Middle Granite Gorge]] (MGG).
- **[[PIT Tagging]] and recapture:** Humpback chub of 150 mm TL or larger were tagged with passive integrated transponders, and recaptures were used to measure long-range movement, including movement between the mainstem and LCR.

Juvenile movement (<150 mm TL) was tracked with fin clips and punches. Movement was related to season, time of day, [[Turbidity]], flow regime, flow level, [[Ramping Rate]], and magnitude of daily flow change.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The study covered about 225 river miles (Lees Ferry to Diamond Creek). Humpback chub occurred in nine aggregations across roughly 295 km of mainstem. The largest mainstem aggregation was at the LCR inflow (RM 61.3). Flows were regulated by Glen Canyon Dam at the upper end of the canyon, with daily hydropower fluctuations.

**Flow regimes.** The [[Research Flows]] (high-fluctuating releases) ran from October 1990 through July 1991. Interim Flows began August 1, 1991.

| Parameter | Research Flows (high-fluctuating) | Interim Flows |
|---|---|---|
| Flow range | 3,000–31,500 ft³/s | 5,000–20,000 ft³/s |
| Mean ramping rate | 886 ft³·s⁻¹·h⁻¹ (SD=1,230) | 378 ft³·s⁻¹·h⁻¹ (SD=379) |
| Mean daily flow change | 5,643 ft³/s (SD=5,144) | 4,014 ft³/s (SD=1,991) |

**Temperature.** Dam releases ranged from 7–11 °C, warming downstream to about 15 °C at RM 213. LCR temperatures ranged from 2 °C in winter to 25 °C in summer, close to pre-dam mainstem conditions. The LCR is cut off upstream of 44.9 km by a waterfall.

**Predators and cover.** Large brown trout, rainbow trout, and channel catfish prey on humpback chub up to about 340 mm TL. Osprey and bald eagles also take larger subadults and adults.

## Key Findings

**Long-range movement within the mainstem was limited.**
- Radio-tagged adults in the LCRI aggregation had a mean net displacement of 1.49 km (range 0.00–6.11 km) and mean gross displacement of 5.13 km. Mean displacement between contacts was 0.26 km.
- Net displacement was under 1 km for 51% of these adults and under 3 km for 84%.
- Adults in MGG (n=3) had a mean net displacement of 1.88 km and gross displacement of 3.38 km. This did not differ significantly from LCRI fish (t=0.38, P=0.704).
- The LCRI aggregation's estimated home range was about 28.4 km (13.5 km mainstem plus 14.9 km LCR).
- PIT-tagged fish (238 fish, 285 movements) averaged 1.64 km net displacement (range 0.0–99.8 km). Adults and subadults were 150 mm TL or larger, per the text.

**Movement between aggregations was rare.**
- Only 7 of 356 PIT-tagged fish recaptured in the mainstem (2.0%) moved between aggregations. The abstract reports 2.9% of observed movements between aggregations. Both figures are given as reported.
- The longest movement was an adult (333 mm TL) that travelled 99.8 km to the MGG aggregation.
- Four of the seven inter-aggregation movers were subadults (<200 mm TL) that dispersed downstream from the LCRI aggregation.

**Strong site fidelity was maintained.**
- Adults spent one to several days in an area before moving and tended to reoccupy distinct sites, often large recirculating [[Eddy Complex]] habitats.
- Of 63 PIT-tagged fish captured in the mainstem, LCR, and mainstem again, 54 (86%) returned within 2 km of their original locale.

**Spawning migrations into the LCR were the largest movements.**
- Mean net displacement for PIT-tagged fish moving between the mainstem and LCR was 6.72 km (820 fish, 846 movements), significantly greater than within-mainstem movement (1.64 km; t=9.96, P<0.00005).
- 35 of 69 radio-tagged adults (51%) were contacted in the lower LCR or inflow at least once.
- Spawning movement occurred in four phases:
  1. February aggregation in mainstem eddies.
  2. March–May staging near the LCR inflow.
  3. Ascent into the LCR, mainly February–May, linked to declining or steady low flows, decreased turbidity, and rising water temperatures.
  4. Post-spawning descent, spread across May–September.
- Of 20 PIT-tagged fish that moved into the LCR, 17 (74%) did so during rising water temperatures (9.6–22.7 °C).
- Of 23 PIT-tagged fish moving mainstem to LCR within 30 days, 20 coincided with the spawning period.

**Juveniles dispersed from the LCR during floods.**
- Eleven fin-clipped juveniles from the LCR were recaptured in the mainstem in 1993. They coincided with floods in January and February 1993.
- Only 10 of 1,228 fin-punched fish marked in the mainstem were recaptured, all within the LCRI aggregation.
- The authors hypothesize that LCR-to-mainstem juvenile dispersal may be the main source of fish for downstream aggregations, since summer mainstem temperatures (10–15 °C) probably preclude successful mainstem spawning.

**Flow regime did not change long-range movement.**
- No significant differences in mean net displacement (t=0.777, P=0.440) or gross displacement (t=0.253, P=0.802) were found between Research Flows and Interim Flows.
- Net movement did not differ significantly by season, except that fall displacement was lower (ANOVA, F=3.15, P=0.027).

**Near-surface activity responded to turbidity and season.**
- Turbidity strongly affected near-surface activity (ANOVA, F=99.41, P<0.00001), with higher activity during high turbidity.
- Near-surface activity was also higher during spawning season (F=19.97, P<0.00001). Day–night differences were not significant.
- Proportion of movement during spawning season was 0.21 (N=705), vs. 0.13 (N=1126) in non-spawning season (χ²=22.25, P<0.00001) in the LCRI aggregation.

**Daily movement rose with flow change and ramping.**
- Movement was higher during high-fluctuating flows (proportion 0.21) than during Interim Flows (0.16) (χ²=5.18, P=0.023).
- At flows above 10,000 ft³/s, movement was roughly three times higher (χ²=39.31, P<0.00001).
- Ramping rates above 300 ft³/s/h increased movement under both regimes, but this effect was significant only above 10,000 ft³/s.
- Total distance travelled increased with daily flow change, especially when the high–low difference exceeded 4,000 ft³/s.
- Higher daily movement did not translate into greater long-range displacement, suggesting fish moved to more favourable microhabitats after flow changes.

**Energetic and predation implications.**
- High condition factors for adults indicated no negative energetic effects from increased movement.
- The authors note that higher movement may increase energy costs and predation risk, and that low turbidity (which reduces cover) may increase predation potential.

**Low temperatures limit juvenile swimming.**
- In laboratory tests (Bulkley et al. 1982), sustained swimming at 0.51 m/s lasted 90 minutes at 20 °C but only 2.22 minutes at 14 °C. This suggests that cold mainstem water may hinder juveniles' ability to hold position in high flows.

**Management implications.** The authors conclude that Interim Flows did not change adult long-range movement or basic habitat. However, flow-driven daily movement may carry energetic and predation costs. Key unanswered questions include which flow levels, ramping rates, and flow changes passively transport large numbers of young fish, and at what point transported juveniles are lost from the reproducing population.
