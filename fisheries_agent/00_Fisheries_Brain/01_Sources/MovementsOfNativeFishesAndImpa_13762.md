# Movements of Native Fishes and Impacts of Non-native Fishes in Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary
This is a research proposal/grant application to The Nature Conservancy (submitted 15 December 1988) by Principal Investigator W. L. Minckley of Arizona State University. It requests **$3150.00** (matching funds $1050.00; total cost $4200.00) for work running 1 February 1989 to July 1990 at the Aravaipa Canyon preserve. Aravaipa Creek holds one of the last intact native fish assemblages in the American Southwest, including the federally listed [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. A fish barrier has been proposed near the lower end of the stream to exclude non-native fishes, especially [[Red Shiner]]. The project would measure whether the barrier could deplete native fishes through loss of adults moving downstream and of drifting larvae, and would assess predation and competition by non-native species.

## Background & Description
- **Objectives:** (A) delineate fish movements relative to physical features; (B) determine downstream drift of larval fishes; (C) assess likely impacts of the proposed barrier on native fishes; (D) collect non-native fishes and analyze their food, feeding habits, distribution and other ecology.
- **Movement hypothesis:** Siebert (1980) gave preliminary data that [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]] move into the central gorge in summer and out of it (upstream and downstream) in winter, presumably in response to temperature. Minckley (1981) found from standing crops and relative abundances that smaller species migrate seasonally too. Neither study was definitive.
- **Barrier rationale:** The barrier is meant to block [[Red Shiner]] invasion from the [[San Pedro River]], where Minckley recorded it about a mile from the mouth of Aravaipa Creek in the 1960s. Red shiner has repeatedly been implicated in the decline of native minnows, including spikedace, loach minnow and [[Woundfin]]. Aravaipa populations of the first two are among the most viable remaining anywhere.
- **Larval drift:** Larvae of most Southwestern stream fishes drift downstream, then move to shoreline shallows, then into the channel and back upstream. The magnitude, distance and upstream repopulation patterns are unknown. A barrier could cause losses far larger than those of adult movement.
- **Other non-natives present:** [[Green Sunfish]], [[Western Mosquitofish]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Natural flooding tends to keep non-native populations in check by washing them downstream (Minckley and Meffe 1987).
- Fish sampling is essentially impossible during floods, and high turbidity reduces electrofishing efficacy. Timing is therefore set by flow and turbidity.
- Monitoring of Aravaipa Creek fishes has been ongoing since 1965 as part of a broader ASU research program on southwestern fishes. Food habits and habitat relations of the native fishes are known from earlier studies.
- Recent years of relatively high runoff are expected to have raised the population of fish >125 mm TL above that in Siebert's study.

## Key Findings
**Planned methods**
- **Electrofishing safety pre-test:** Concern about electrofishing injury (Sharber 1986; Sharber and Carothers 1988, who documented vertebral damage in [[Rainbow Trout]] under high-voltage DC) leads to a controlled test in [[Bonita Creek]]. Samples of [[Longfin Dace]], desert sucker and Sonora sucker would be seined (control) or electrofished (AC gasoline, DC battery, and Sharber/Carothers equipment), frozen, then X-rayed and autopsied by Carothers. Results are wanted before 1 February 1989. Minckley reports no evidence of damage or population change from Aravaipa electrofishing since 1965 (AC and DC, 110 V and higher).
- **Contingency:** If damage is unacceptable, the mark-recapture and non-native impact components would be dropped. Larval drift studies would continue regardless.
- **Mark-recapture:** Fish >125 mm TL (roundtail chub, desert sucker, Sonora sucker) would be marked with individually numbered anchor tags (nylon crossbar anchor, 60-mm shaft, 1.75-mm outside diameter, 45-mm colored vinyl tube) inserted through the dorsal musculature. Roundtail chub, increasingly rare in Arizona, would be tagged only if abundant. Electrofishing would avoid tetany and midday heat. Siebert (1980) recovered 245 of almost 2097 tagged fish; at a rough 10% recovery this implies a population of ~21,000 fish. The goal is a comparable marked percentage.
- **Larval drift:** Drift nets at three sites (near the mouth of Turkey Creek, the lower end of Aravaipa Gorge, and the proposed barrier site), with midstream and shoreline samples, for 24 hours at least four times in the main reproductive season (March through June). Drift is computed as fish per unit water volume. Subsamples would be preserved for lab identification.
- **Non-native fishes:** All would be removed and preserved. Habitat notes, stomach contents (identified, counted and measured by volume) and reproductive condition would be recorded. Electrofishing removal will also be evaluated.
- **Analysis and timing:** Statistics run on ASU mainframe and PC systems. Fieldwork runs February 1989 to early summer 1990, with at least one summer overlap with Siebert's data.

**Permits, impact and products**
- Federal permit PRT-676811 and Arizona Management Research Permit No. 00032 are already held.
- Based on over 20 years of experience in the creek, the work is expected to have no discernible impact on native fishes despite unavoidable mortality from capture, tagging and larval preservation.
- Interim reports are quarterly or semi-annual; the final report is due in 1990, likely identical to a Master of Science thesis by Caryl M. Williams (ASU).

**Budget**
- Requested from The Nature Conservancy: assistants $1200.00, per diem $1800.00 and another item $150.00, totaling $3150.00. Matching funds total $1050.00.
- Travel costs $1600 (20 trips, about 7000 miles at $0.225/mile) and per diem $2400 (240 person-days at $10.00/day) are listed in total cost. Equipment is provided at no cost.
- If $1050.00 is not obtained from the U.S. Bureau of Land Management (Safford District), fieldwork would drop to 15 trips.
