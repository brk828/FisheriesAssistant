# Recovery Plan for the Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Rio Yaqui

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #ictaluridae #recovery-plan #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This draft recovery plan addresses four federally listed fish species endemic to the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]]: [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*, endangered 1984), [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*, endangered 1967), [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*, threatened 1984), and [[Yaqui Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*, threatened 1984). All four species historically occurred throughout the Rio Yaqui Basin in the USA and Mexico. Current USA populations are limited primarily to [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] in southeastern Arizona. Distribution in Mexico is uncertain.

The species are heavily dependent on artesian wells and spring flows. Water development and pumping of underground aquifers constitute the greatest threat, followed by introduction of non-native fishes (particularly [[Mosquitofish]] for the topminnow). Recovery objectives include stabilizing existing populations and downlisting *G. purpurea* and *P. o. sonoriensis*. Due to limited habitat in the USA (only 2% of historic range), full delisting cannot occur without sufficient protection in Mexico. The plan emphasizes ecosystem-level management and international cooperation with the Republic of Mexico.

## Background & Description

### Rio Yaqui Basin

The [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] of northwestern Mexico comprises approximately 73,000 km² of mountainous terrain between 27-32°N and 107-110°40'W. The system begins as the Rio Papigochic in western Chihuahua, enters eastern Sonora to receive the Rio de Bavispe, then flows southward entering the [[Gulf of California]] near Ciudad Obregon, Sonora. Average discharge is approximately 2,800 ha per year, making it one of the largest streams in the region.

The USA portion includes only 1,500 km² (less than 2% of total watershed) in [[Cochise County]] Arizona and [[Hidalgo County]] New Mexico. Historic habitat included streams, springs, and cienegas tributary to San Bernardino and Douglas valleys. Three of the four species did not occur elsewhere in the United States.

### Species Descriptions

**[[Yaqui Chub]]** (*Gila purpurea*): A robust cyprinid first described by C. Girard in 1856. Head and anterior body thickened, dorsal fin origin slightly behind pelvic fins. Vertically elongated, diffuse triangular caudal spot usually present. Prefers deep pool habitat, able to withstand extreme changes in water flow. Feeding appears opportunistic, with preference for algae and invertebrates. Nearly extirpated in 1969 when drought dried [[Astin Spring]]; 200 individuals rescued and moved to [[Leslie Creek]] by Dr. W.L. Minckley.

**[[Yaqui Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): Small livebearer (family [[Poeciliidae]]). Males usually ≤25 mm, females 30-40 mm. Olive colored with breeding males almost black. Scales of dorsum darkly outlined, fin rays outlined with melanophores but lacking dark spots. Prefers shallow, warm, fairly quiet waters with dense algae mats and debris, sandy substrates sometimes covered with organic muds. Food habits generalized: bottom debris, vegetation, amphipod crustaceans, insect larvae including mosquito larvae.

**[[Yaqui Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus pricei*): Dark gray to black dorsally, white to grayish beneath; darker overall than [[Channel Catfish]] or [[Blue Catfish]]. Barbels jet-black except on chin (gray to whitened). Caudal fin shallowly forked. Historic occurrence in USA likely limited to [[San Bernardino Creek]]. Collected in Rio Sirupa and Rio Aros, Mexico in 1987 and 1990. Currently held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Extirpated in wild in USA. Ecology probably similar to channel catfish. Decline due to habitat loss and hybridization with introduced channel catfish.

**[[Yaqui Beautiful Shiner]]** (*Cyprinella formosa*): Small cyprinid with silvery coloration and orange to yellow-orange fins. Compressed body, relatively pointed snout, oblique mouth. Breeding males have bluish body often masked by orange, pink, or yellow wash. Historically occurred in southeastern Arizona and [[Mimbres River Basin]] in southern New Mexico. Re-introduced to USA in 1990: fish collected in Arroyo Moctezuma, Chihuahua, Mexico and stocked into Twin Pond on San Bernardino NWR in May 1990. Reported to occur on riffles of small streams and intermittent pools with high riffle habitat percentage. On San Bernardino NWR, adapted to pond habitats where reproducing and thriving.

### Historical Context

Evidence of human use dates to [[Clovis Culture]]. Spanish presence in San Bernardino valley dates to 1694 (Father Eusebio Francisco Kino). Cattle introduced 1822 with San Bernardino Land Grant acquired by Ignacio Perez. John Slaughter purchased grant in 1884, operating large cattle operation. [[The Nature Conservancy]] purchased 2,400 acres of San Bernardino Ranch in 1980 and 1,240 acres in Leslie Canyon in 1988, selling to [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1982 and 1988 respectively to establish refuges.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Requirements

Diversity of aquatic habitats is high in the basin. Mountain creeks are cold and clear, supporting trouts (*Onchorhynchus* sp.). Most fish species, including all four treated here, live at intermediate to lower elevations in springs, cienegas, creeks, and moderate-sized rivers. [[Cienegas]] (stream-associated marshlands characterized by low emergent aquatic plants and hydric-adapted trees such as willows) were of marked importance historically. Streams typically have alternating riffles and pools, heterogeneity with undercut banks, cover from boulders and woody debris, clean gravelly bottoms in swifter areas, abundant algae growths.

### Current Distribution (Table 2 data)

- **Yaqui chub**: Leslie Creek, House Pond, Twin Pond, North Pond, Mesquite Pond, Black Draw, El Coronado Ranch (Turkey Creek and ponds). Habitat: wide variety including deep pools, ponds, streams.
- **Yaqui topminnow**: Leslie Creek, Robertson cienega, Tule Spring, Twin Pond, North Pond, Bunting Spring, Cottonwood well, Middle Well, Cienega Well, Mesquite Pond. Habitat: well outflows, ponds, pool margins, shallow areas.
- **Yaqui beautiful shiner**: Twin Pond; [[Guzman Beautiful Shiner]] at Dexter NFH. Habitat: streams, ponds, deep pools.
- **Yaqui catfish**: Dexter NFH only. Habitat: ponds (captive).
- **Mexican Stoneroller** (candidate species): Rucker Creek, Black Draw. Habitat: pools and riffle areas.

### Threats and Reasons for Decline

Water scarcity in southeast Arizona led to large-scale cattle grazing and farming in late 1800s. Physical impacts from watershed use/misuse, stream channel diversion and modification led to decline and disappearance of Yaqui fauna in USA. Excessive pumping of underground aquifers reduced spring flows. Lowering of water tables due to stream channel incision led to desiccation of cienega habitat. Introduction of exotic species (mosquitofish, [[Largemouth Bass]], channel catfish) contributed significantly to decline. Mosquitofish introduction for mosquito control particularly devastating to topminnow populations.

### Conservation Efforts to Date

Major effort: purchase of San Bernardino Ranch and Leslie Creek to establish refuges. In 1969, Dr. W.L. Minckley moved approximately 200 Yaqui chub and 200 Yaqui topminnow from rapidly drying Astin Spring to Leslie Creek. These fish formed basis for broodstock taken to Dexter NFH. Descendants stocked into San Bernardino Ranch in 1980. House Pond on Johnson Historical Museum property drained and treated with toxicant to remove exotic fish; now contains large populations of Yaqui chub and topminnow. Yaqui beautiful shiners collected in Mexico 1989, Yaqui catfish collected 1990 and 1987. Shiners re-introduced to San Bernardino NWR May 1990. Yaqui catfish currently held at Dexter NFH. Yaqui chub stocked into Turkey Creek and El Coronado Ranch in 1986, additional ponds stocked 1991.

## Key Findings

### Recovery Objectives and Criteria

**Primary Objective**: Restore Yaqui chub, topminnow, catfish, and beautiful shiner as secure and stable members of native fish fauna in river systems where they once occurred. San Bernardino/Leslie Canyon NWR will serve as refugium and source stock. Limited USA habitat (northern limit of species range) means recovery cannot be accomplished only in USA. **Republic of Mexico must be full partner in recovery efforts for delisting to occur.**

**Downlisting Criteria** (all conditions must be met for 5 years):
1. All non-native fish species eradicated from critical habitat
2. Secure and protect San Bernardino aquifer so all artesian flows maintain themselves year-round; secure and protect Leslie Creek watershed for adequate flows
3. Protect critical habitat from detrimental human disturbance (mining, non-native fish introduction, water diversion/removal)

**Species-Specific Criteria**:

- **Yaqui Chub** - Downlist to threatened when: (a) surface/underground water protected, (b) viable self-sustaining populations established on refuges, (c) viable self-sustaining population in West Turkey Creek established and secure. Delisting not considered an option in foreseeable future due to limited historic distribution.

- **Yaqui Topminnow** - Downlist when: (a) artesian flows maintained and protected, (b) non-native fishes controlled/managed from San Bernardino NWR with protection against future invasion established and topminnow populations stable/secure, (c) stable populations survived ≥5 years in all suitable existing and reclaimed SBNWR habitats.

- **Beautiful Shiner** - Delist not determinable until: (a) Arizona populations established and secure, (b) Guzman beautiful shiner reestablished as secure, viable, self-sustaining population in Mimbres River and other available habitat within historic range in New Mexico, (c) viable self-sustaining populations demonstrably secure within historic range in Mexico.

- **Yaqui Catfish** - Delist not determinable due to lack of information on status in Mexico. Recovery must occur in Republic of Mexico before action can be taken. Maintain populations at San Bernardino NWR for refugium and gene pool maintenance.

### Recovery Actions (Stepdown Outline)

**1. Cooperate with Republic of Mexico**: Pursue agreement and develop management plan for long-term survival of species in Mexico.

**2. Protect historic habitat**: Maintain subsurface waters to levels sufficient for artesian well flows and protect surface waters. Obtain instream flow rights for Leslie Creek and Black Draw. Acquire and protect essential habitats and waters. Work with water users and public agencies to prevent aquifer overuse.

**3. Manage existing habitat and populations**: Determine water requirements to maintain listed species at refuges. Determine aquifer configuration and capacity. Revise and continue implementation of Refuge Master Plan. Develop water use plan for SBNWR, fish management plan for each species, genetic monitoring plan and schedule. Enhance and monitor existing habitats. Prevent introduction of non-native species.

**4. Determine habitat and biological requirements**: Examine life histories (spawning, growth, food requirements). Determine impacts of intra- and inter-specific competition. Determine habitat requirements and utilization. Determine genetic composition of existing populations (baseline genetic information needed due to habitat fragmentation and population isolation).

**5. Assess habitat for re-introduction and re-introduce fishes**: Identify areas for possible re-introductions. Develop culture techniques for Yaqui catfish (breeding protocol, determine optimal fish size/time of year/stocking density). Stock species into suitable historic habitat.

**6. Develop information and education programs**: Develop programs at local, state, and national levels to bring attention to plight of Yaqui fishes and their value as part of natural fauna of USA and Mexico.

### Critical Management Issues

International cooperation essential: Only 2% of historic habitat in USA at northernmost portion of range. USA habitat provides refugium to protect from extinction, but full recovery and delisting cannot occur without habitat and species protection in Republic of Mexico. Current farming operations in Mexico withdraw water from same aquifer supplying artesian system providing USA refugium habitat. Negotiations needed with Mexican landowners to maintain artesian well pressures.

Genetic monitoring critical due to habitat fragmentation and population isolation. Baseline genetic information needed to identify changes among isolated populations and establish criteria for future management decisions. Future investigations needed to document possible genetic changes over time.

Non-native fish management essential: Success requires restoring and restocking historic habitats. Non-natives compete with, prey upon, and sometimes hybridize with native fishes. Invasion prevention and habitat renovation (before re-introduction) required.

Human disturbance control: Extremely limited distribution and small ecosystems make habitat protection critical. Limited water availability subject to diversion, pumping, and damaging land practices. Areas for possible acquisition should be identified and protected as available.
