# Investigations of Razorback Sucker Distribution, Movements and Habitats Used During Spring in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (December 1996), prepared for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin by Timothy Modde (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Vernal, Utah) and Edmund J. Wick (National Park Service, Fort Collins, Colorado), examines adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) movements and habitat use, early life-stage growth and survival in [[Floodplain Wetlands]] relative to main-channel habitats, and the flows needed to inundate [[Old Charley Wash]] on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]. The study is organized into three largely independent chapters.

Movement and spawning data from capture records (1975–1992) and radio telemetry (three spawning events, 1993–1995) indicate that spring discharge is the primary cue for adults moving to spawning bars, and that the middle Green River supports a single reproductive population using multiple spawning sites. Growth and survival of juveniles were generally higher in wetlands than in backwaters, but red shiner predation nearly eliminated larvae in a competition trial. The authors conclude that a natural hydrograph and the duration of out-of-bank flows are central to recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Razorback sucker, an endangered catostomid endemic to the Colorado River basin. Adults were studied primarily as radio-tagged males.
- **Study area:** The Green River from RK 391 (lower end of West Branch) to its confluence with the Yampa River (RK 555), the upstream portion of the longest low-gradient reach below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. It holds the largest known population of the species in riverine habitat (Modde et al. 1996).
- **Hydrologic context:** Historic bankfull flows at the refuge averaged 575 m³/s and were exceeded in about two of every three years before the dam; after closure they were exceeded only one year in four. Reduced peaks have altered channel morphology and reduced floodplain connectivity.
- **Nonnative fauna:** Introduction of over 30 nonnative fish species has altered the fish assemblage, including the predatory [[Red Shiner]].
- **Wetlands:** Old Charley Wash (Wood's Bottom), Leota Bottom, and Sheppard Bottom are natural Class IV wetlands (Cowardin et al. 1979) on the refuge between RK 402 and 418, with artificial dikes and water supplied by gravity flow, pumping, or over-dike flooding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow-driven migration:** Adults concentrated on the spawning bar between RK 491 and 507 (Escalante spawning area, also called the Jensen site) as flows rose. Fish in 1994 were not present until the hydrograph rose, even when water temperature was already high.
- **Postspawning dispersal:** After mid-May, 62% of captured fish were downstream of the primary spawning area, with concentrations at tributary mouths such as [[Ashley Creek]], Stewart Lake outlet, the Duchesne River, and the Old Charley Wash outlet.
- **Habitat use:** Telemetry fish during spring and summer 1993 occupied main-channel eddies, runs, and eddy-fence transition zones, mostly at 1–3 m depth. In the summer low-flow period they used sites behind mid-channel sand bars; many moved into [[Split Mountain Canyon]] by July.
- **Floodplain productivity:** Inundated floodplains hold higher invertebrate standing stocks than main-channel habitats. Mabey (1993) reported invertebrate production in Old Charley Wash roughly an order of magnitude greater than in backwater or main-channel habitats.
- **Wetland types:** Irving and Burdick (1995) distinguished terrace wetlands (drain as river levels fall) from depression wetlands (retain water), a distinction with uncertain importance for razorback sucker.

## Key Findings

**1. Adult distribution and movement (Chapter One)**
- Capture data included 771 adult observations, nearly all between RK 80 and 564; only 20 occurred below RK 370.
- Of 10 fish captured in early April, 9 were near the Duchesne River mouth (RK 399).
- In mid-April to early May, 73% of fish were between RK 491 and 507; only 4% were at the lower Yampa River spawning site.
- Seven male adults were radio-implanted (30 April–11 May 1993; 464–520 mm TL; 1,120–1,600 g). Five came from the Escalante area, one from the Yampa spawning bar, and one from Island Park.
- Over the 12 months after implantation, maximum downstream movement averaged 22.1 km (SD 21.7; range 0.0–60.4 km) and maximum upstream movement averaged 18.5 km (SD 14.0; range 0.0–32.2 km).
- Postspawning movement in 1994 averaged 61.8 km downstream (SD 59.9; range 4.8–112.7 km), with three fish moving 93.3–112.7 km.
- Of the seven fish, one died about two months after implantation, so six survived.
- By March 1995 all tracked fish were within a 19.3 km reach between Island Park (RK 534) and the Escalante area (RK 492–501).
- Fish were not necessarily loyal to one site: one fish moved from the Yampa bar to the Escalante area, and one fish occupied two spawning sites in the same year.

**2. Spawning cues**
- Movement to spawning sites was associated primarily with discharge and, to a lesser extent, temperature. In 1994 the greatest fish counts on the bar coincided with peak flows and daily maximum temperatures near 16 °C.
- In 1995 the greatest count (five fish) coincided with a sharp rise in discharge on the ascending limb of the hydrograph.
- The authors interpret these results as evidence of a single reproductive population using several spawning sites.

**3. Wetland growth and survival (Chapter Two)**
- A 1991 pilot study placed 20 juveniles (90–180 mm TL) per cage in three wetlands and three backwaters.
- Mean survival was 67% in wetlands and 40% in backwaters. Gains in weight occurred in two of three wetlands, while all backwater cages lost weight. Growth and survival were low in Old Charley Wash.
- Mortality in Old Charley Wash was attributed to bacterial septicemia, and that site had the heaviest infestation of *Lernaea* sp. among replicates.
- The 1993 competition trial found 100% larval consumption by red shiners in mixed cages. Only one of 300 juveniles survived (0.33%) in the juvenile trial, from 29 July to 26 August 1993. Red shiner survival averaged 66.7% in two of three cages.
- Authors note that adult razorback sucker also preyed on larvae, suggesting nonnative and native predation risks in wetlands.

**4. Inundation of Old Charley Wash (Chapter Three)**
- Surface elevation data from the 1993 outlet gage showed overbank connection between the Green River and Old Charley Wash at discharges between 405 and 455 m³/s.
- The lowest point on the dikes lies at about 1418.8–1419.1 m above mean sea level.
- The inlet channel (elevation 1417.6 m) would require about 240 m³/s to deliver water, and the wetland floor at 1417.0 m could be lowered by dredging to reach it at slightly lower discharge.
- In 1995 flows began inundating the levee on 23 May, when Ouray flows were estimated at 454 m³/s. The flood stage is 524 m³/s.

**5. Management implications and recommendations**
- A natural hydrograph, including both magnitude and duration of high flows, benefits spawning success, growth, survival, and recruitment.
- Flaming Gorge releases that mimic a natural hydrograph and coordinate with [[Yampa River]] flows to maximize floodplain connection would benefit recovery.
- Duration of out-of-bank flows is especially important for early life and adult stages.
- Key unknowns include the mechanisms by which wetlands benefit razorback sucker, the relative value of terrace versus depression wetlands, and the effect of wetland management on nonnative fish production.
- Recruitment evidence: 11 wild yearling razorback sucker (250–370 mm) were collected in Leota Pond in 1994, and 73 age-0 juveniles were collected in Old Charley Wash in 1995–1996. These fish were spawned during high-water years (peak Jensen flows above 566 m³/s).
