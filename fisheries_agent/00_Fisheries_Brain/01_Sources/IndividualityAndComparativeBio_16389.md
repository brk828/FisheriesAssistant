# Categories, Life, and Thinking: Commentaries on Ghiselin's Species-as-Individuals Thesis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Amniota

## Executive Summary

This note covers a multi-part commentary from *The Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (1981, vol. 4) on [[Michael Ghiselin]]'s target article "Categories, Life, and Thinking." The document contains three parts: Ghiselin's closing remarks on natural selection and behavior, a systematist's commentary by William L. Fink (Museum of Comparative Zoology, Harvard University), and a philosopher's commentary by Helen Heise (San Francisco State University). The central debate is whether [[Species Concept]]s should be treated as individuals rather than classes. The text contains no new field data, specimen counts, or habitat observations. Fish appear only as a worked example of classification history. The note is therefore a conceptual summary, and the species, habitat, and location fields are marked N/A.

## Background & Description

Ghiselin's thesis is that "species are not classes: they are individuals." Its main supporting argument is the idea of a composite whole, a group held together by genealogical continuity rather than shared features. The commentaries address three linked topics:

- **Causation in evolution.** Ghiselin argues that genetic change and behavioral change are both causes within [[Natural Selection]] and should not be separated from it.
- **Individuality of higher taxa.** Fink extends the individuality argument to monophyletic groups ([[Monophyly]]).
- **Philosophical foundations.** Heise examines the [[Problem of Universals]], nominalism versus realism, and the logic of concepts.

The text cites a number of foundational authors, including [[Hennig]] (1966), [[Darwin]], [[Weismann]] (1904), [[Lamarck]], and [[Monod]] (1971).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is not an ecological study. Its environmental relevance is indirect and comes mainly from examples:

- **Genetic causation example.** The recessive *raspberry* mutation in [[Drosophila Melanogaster]] is cited because, when homozygous, it causes both raspberry-colored eyes and a sharp decline in fecundity. Ghiselin uses it to show that a genetic change can be a direct, deterministic cause of differential survival.
- **Behavior and organism-environment relationships.** Ghiselin argues that behavioral change can initiate changes in organism-environment relationships. He proposes that much biological change may be a second-order effect of teleonomic (cybernetically controlled) selection.
- **Fish classification.** Fink cites [[Colin Patterson]] (1977), who used the history of "fish" classification to show a consistent trend toward monophyletic groups.
- **Crocodile and reptile behavior.** Fink notes that crocodiles share behavioral traits with birds and primitive mammals, including guarded egg clutches, peeping young, vocalizations, imprinting, and parental care.

## Key Findings

**Ghiselin's closing argument (natural selection and behavior)**

1. Genetic changes are not a separate "raw material" from natural selection. They can be direct causes of differential survival and so are part of selection.
2. Behavioral change is not a subsidiary "pacemaker." It has been one of the most important causes of evolution and forms a major subset of the causal influences that constitute natural selection.
3. Ghiselin suggests that a synthesis of Darwinian and [[Lamarckism]] may be appropriate. Lamarck was the first to propose that changes in learned behaviors ("habits") were a major cause of evolutionary change. Transmission mechanisms remain Weismannian.
4. Evolution is better described as an interactional process combining chance, necessity, and teleonomy, in place of Monod's "Chance and Necessity."

**Fink's systematic commentary (individuality and higher taxa)**

5. Monophyletic groups, which include a single ancestral species and all its known descendants, can be treated as individuals or "historical entities" ([[Wiley]] 1980). They have one origin in time and space and end at the extinction of their last member.
6. A "natural group" is a monophyletic group that exists in nature. It is natural because its members are joined by genealogical descent, not because of shared features. The search for natural groups thus becomes the search for monophyletic groups.
7. The history of systematics, including pre-Darwinian work, shows an implicit search for such individuals. Systematists often found monophyletic groups without being able to name the defining characters.
8. Class-based groupings such as "Reptilia" or "fishes" can obscure evolutionary history. For example, crocodiles and dinosaurs share a unique history with birds, while other "reptiles" such as *Dimetrodon* share history with mammals, and turtles share history with all of them.
9. Fink argues that the [[Triune Brain]] theory (MacLean 1973) is undermined by this point. Because there is no natural group "Reptilia," there can be no "reptilian brain." Brain and behavioral complexity should be studied within a phylogenetic framework.

**Heise's philosophical commentary (universals and concepts)**

10. Heise and Starr (1968) raised the problem that treating a species as an individual requires either making the species "complete," which precludes new members, or revising the concept of individual so that it need not be complete. Heise judges such revisions to be serious.
11. Ghiselin's argument falls under the classic problem of universals. Because he treats properties as real, he is a realist in the ontological sense.
12. Heise argues that no single "technical sense" of "individual" is obvious or accepted. Peirce is cited as listing forty kinds of unities. Concepts must be defined, shown to cohere with each other and with the facts, and then persuasively adopted.
13. Heise questions Ghiselin's view of the relation between reality and concepts. Properties must be discerned and labeled by observers, and the existence of error shows that concepts are not identical to their objects.
14. Heise concludes that no proposition that matters should be accepted on authority alone, whether the authority is Plato, Aristotle, Darwin, or oneself.

**Overall assessment**

The document presents a live, unresolved debate. Fink supports the individuality of monophyletic groups and sees it as heuristically valuable. Heise remains unconvinced that the species-as-individual view can be accommodated without major conceptual revision. Ghiselin's closing remarks broaden natural selection to include behavior and teleonomy.
