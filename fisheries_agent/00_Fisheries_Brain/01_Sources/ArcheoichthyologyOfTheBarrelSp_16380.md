# Archeoichthyology of the Barrel Springs Site, Colorado Desert, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Mugilidae #Elopidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript by Lloyd T. Findley identifies fish remains recovered from the [[Barrel Springs Site]] (CA-SDi-4443), an archaeological site on the western slope of the [[Salton Basin]] in San Diego County, California. Dated by radiocarbon and trade-bead evidence to roughly 1540–1816 A.D., the site yielded fish bones dominated by two former Lower Colorado River freshwater species, [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]], with a small number of elements from the euryhaline marine species [[Striped Mullet]] and [[Machete]]. The study infers capture season, likely fishing methods, and the historical distribution of these fishes in the region, which includes the ancient [[Lake Cahuilla]] and the [[Salton Sea]].

## Background & Description

The fish material was recovered by routine 1/8-inch screening of sand substrate from eight surface sample units and five subsurface levels in four excavation units, during a field excavation directed by Joan Oxendine of the California Department of Parks and Recreation (May–July 1977). The site sits beside [[San Felipe Creek]], a normally dry wash, and near a small seep (Barrel Springs) too small to hold standing water today.

Identifications relied on comparative skeletal material and literature. The author notes a general paucity of comparative material for species of the characteristic [[Lower Colorado River]] fish fauna, which constrained identifications; several elements remain tentative. Seasonality was estimated from terminal annuli on vertebrae. Readings were difficult, so only "summer" and "winter" were usually recorded.

The manuscript was completed in 1977 and reformatted with minor emendations in 1992, without a review of recent literature. A 1977 note adds a comment on Casteel (1976), who reported slower growth of a humpback sucker from ancient Lake Cahuilla.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lower Colorado River:** Historically supported abundant native fishes, but impoundment, channelization, and exotic species caused major declines in the twentieth century ([[Habitat Alteration]]).
- **Ancient Lake Cahuilla:** Formed when the Colorado River discharged into the Salton Basin, creating lakes whose shorelines held large fish populations and archaeological sites.
- **Salton Sea:** Formed 1905–1907 by Colorado River floodwaters. Early records show native freshwater fishes declining as salinity rose, while mullet and machete entered through canals and distributaries.
- **Seasonal flow:** Colorado River flow peaked in June, fell sharply in August, and was very low from September to February (Miller 1961, as cited). This coincided with the spawning season of the two main species.
- **Regional fishing methods:** Ethnographic sources record seines, arrowweed and willow traps, weirs, and cactus-spine hooks among regional groups. Spears were not recorded. The Cahuilla are not recorded using nets.

## Key Findings

**1. Dominant taxa.** Most of the fish bones represent [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Both were formerly abundant in the Lower Colorado River and in the ancient lakes, and they likely formed a major part of the local aboriginal fish diet.

**2. Marine-origin taxa.** Only single individuals of [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*) and [[Machete]] (*Elops affinis*) were found. The machete record is apparently the first archaeological report of this species from North America. Its early depth of occurrence (90–110 cm) argues against recent introduction as the sole explanation for its presence.

**3. Absent species.** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large predatory minnow, is absent from Barrel Springs. The author suggests it was scarce in this area of lower flow.

**4. Seasonality.** Most readable annuli indicate summer capture, matching the spawning aggregations of bonytail chub and humpback sucker. Some elements suggest fall/early winter or spring capture.

**5. Fishing methods.** Spawning aggregations and schooling behavior made the two main species vulnerable to seines and traps (including arrowweed traps). Snag-lining and bow-and-arrow fishing suit the schooling species, while the fast-swimming machete was probably harder to catch.

**6. Source lake.** The author argues that the Barrel Springs fish most likely came from [[Lake Cahuilla]] shorelines, or later from New and Alamo River marshes during high Colorado River flows.

**7. Cooking.** A few charred or fire-scorched bones suggest cooking of fresh, locally caught fish rather than trade in dried fish, though trade was not fully excluded.

**8. Conservation status.** Both dominant species are now extirpated across much of their former range and are listed as endangered and fully protected in California.
