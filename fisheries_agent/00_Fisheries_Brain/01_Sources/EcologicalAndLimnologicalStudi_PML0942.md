# Ecological and Limnological Studies of the Green River in Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 report, prepared under a contract between the [[National Park Service]] and [[Colorado State University]], documents a summer-fall 1962 study of the fishes, habitat, water chemistry and invertebrates of the [[Green River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Its main aim was to understand the distribution and life history of rare fishes endemic to the Green River. Fieldwork was led by graduate student [[Joseph E. Banks]], with a 14-student crew under [[Harold K. Hagen]] in October 1962. A September 1962 pollution event, most likely a rotenone-type kill, killed large numbers of fish, especially at [[Gates of Lodore]] and [[Echo Park]]. The October comparison with 1961 showed sharp declines in both fish and invertebrates.

## Background & Description

The study was a follow-up to 1961 surveys and was planned as the first of repeated October samplings to track population dynamics. Its scope covered fishes, fish stomach contents, parasites and sampling gear, while aquatic insects, plankton, algae and plants were handled mainly by Utah scientists. Physical and temperature records held by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Geological Survey and state agencies were largely left out for brevity.

The fish of concern are rare, river-adapted species, including the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Round-tailed Chub]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]]. Non-native [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were also common. The Green River Spring dace was taken in quantity.

Stations included [[Split Mountain]], [[Island Park]], [[Echo Park]] (where the Green and [[Yampa River]] meet), Castle Park on the Yampa, and [[Gates of Lodore]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat.** Canyon walls deflect currents and create pools, eddies and backwaters that serve as fish refuges. Sediment deposits on inner bends, while outer bends are scoured to bedrock. Stream grade varies strongly along the river:

- Near Lodore: about 2.5 ft/mile
- Lodore Canyon average: 18.2 ft/mile
- Echo Park: about 4 ft of drop
- Whirlpool Canyon: more than 12 ft/mile
- Island and Rainbow Parks: 3.3 ft/mile
- Split Mountain Canyon: nearly 22 ft/mile

**Temperature.** The Green River ran 4–5 °F cooler than the Yampa. The authors suggested this may limit squawfish, humpback sucker and humpback chub from moving far up the Yampa. Air and water temperatures were strongly correlated at Island Park (Sept 23–Oct 28; r reported as ~0.85, scan-garbled). Water temperature and dissolved oxygen were even more strongly correlated (r = 0.95). Dissolved oxygen ranged from 8.1 ppm at 62 °F to 10.6 ppm at 48 °F.

**Turbidity.** Flows and turbidity changed daily. Depth rose and fell 5–7 inches or more per day. Secchi disc visibility at Island Park ranged from about 1/4 inch to 13½ inches ([[Secchi Disc]] data, September–October 1962). Turbidity made [[Gill Netting]] impossible in May, June and July unless nets ran parallel to shore. Turbidity was also seen as a key pressure shaping species adaptations.

**Water chemistry.**

- Gates of Lodore: pH 7.4–7.6; dissolved oxygen 6.5–7.3 ppm; total alkalinity values (M.O.) 157–1,690 ppm (the author suspected large errors at the high end).
- Echo Park, Green River: pH steady at 7.5; dissolved oxygen 6.8–8.0 ppm; M.O. 121–151 ppm.
- Echo Park, Yampa River: pH 7.5; dissolved oxygen 6.0–8.1 ppm.

**Aquatic plants and algae.** Periphyton increased sharply after the water cleared; areas bare in July and early August were thickly coated by late September. Filamentous algae washed downstream in September and clogged throw lines.

**Invertebrates.** Mayflies were most numerous, and [[Stonefly]] larvae were scarce. One July 29 collection at Echo Park held eight mayfly species from the families Baetidae, Heptageniidae and Ephemeridae. The mayfly *Lachlania powelli* was noted for clinging to debris in turbid flow. Table 4 gave average October numbers per square foot, 1961 vs. 1962: Lodore 22.9 vs. 6.0; Echo Park 12.6 vs. 1.8; Split Mountain 18.5 vs. 12.0.

**Stomach contents.** Insects, plant matter, and in some cases fish remains occurred in adult squawfish, chubs, carp, catfish and one flannelmouth sucker. Fish remains appeared only in the largest chubs. The authors judged feeding to be opportunistic rather than selective.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling conditions.** Crews needed to be large and fully equipped (electrofishing, seines, and rotenone for side channels). Throw lines caught fish only within about 10–15 feet of shore (up to the third hook), suggesting most fish stayed out of fast, turbulent water. Hoop nets and fyke nets performed poorly. A push net was effective for one-person work.
- **Fish distribution.** Bonytail chubs were found in fast narrow channels, while round-tailed chubs were found in slow areas near sandbars. Carp occurred throughout the Monument, including [[Jones Creek]], the Monument's only real trout habitat. Squawfish were rare at all sites. One adult squawfish of 20.6 inches (1,213.7 g, female) was caught near Echo Park on August 4.
- **Humpback sucker and pollution.** Humpback suckers were not seen until the September pollution event, after which many were found dead from Lodore to Split Mountain. Hybrids between humpback sucker and flannelmouth sucker were also found after the event.
- **Carp deformities.** Carp with a pronounced nuchal hump were noticed in 1961 and again in 1962, including a 3-inch carp from the lower Yampa and a dead carp near Pot Creek. The authors described this as significant.
- **Parasites.** Three primary parasites were recorded: [[Anchor Worm]] (*Lernaea cyprinacea*, a copepod), a leech identified as "…dellea moorei," and a sporozoan causing a white-spot-like disease. The copepod was found on all fish species except channel catfish, black bullhead, sculpin and carp. Parasite incidence was highest in the Yampa River (41.5%), then Echo Park (31.3%) and Monument Headquarters at Split Mountain (22.2%).
- **Fish counts (October 19–21, 1962, Table 2).** Gates of Lodore: 0; Echo Park: 26; Island Park: 35; Castle Park: 133; Split Mountain: 75. Recovery at Split Mountain was estimated at about half or less. At Echo Park, nearly all stunned fish were collected.
- **Species loss, 1961–1962 (Table 3).** Lodore had no fish in 1962. Echo Park retained only three of the eleven species recorded in 1961. Island Park lost many species; Split Mountain lost fewer. Pimephales (fathead minnow) appeared at Echo Park in 1962 but not 1961.
- **Invertebrate decline.** Invertebrate numbers fell by about 74% at Lodore, 86% at Echo Park and 35% at Split Mountain between 1961 and 1962. The authors noted this was counter to expectations for lower water levels.
- **Pollution event (September 1962).** Fish mortality was extensive. Rotenone-based poison was used in a side channel at more than 2 ppm, and rotenone was detected at about 0.25 ppm in the river the following morning (chloroform tests). Caged fish showed about 80% mortality. Dead fish, including humpback suckers, bonytail chubs, squawfish and channel catfish, were counted along the river. Echo Park channel catfish losses were estimated to exceed 200 fish at one point.
- **Data gaps.** The authors acknowledged that the sampling could not guarantee representativeness and that 1961 fish counts were not made, so direct numerical comparison was limited. Stomach analysis of several hundred fish was left for later work. Invertebrate work was incomplete.
- **Management recommendations.** The authors urged continued sampling in summer 1963 and before [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] filled, and continued October comparisons. They noted that the river fell about 7 inches in early November, probably due to closure of the Flaming Gorge dam.
