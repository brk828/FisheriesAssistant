# Ecological and Limnological Studies of the Green River, Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 report documents a field survey of the [[Green River]] and its tributary the [[Yampa River]] within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. It was prepared by [[Harold K. Hagen]] (Associate Professor, Fisheries Science) and [[Joe L. Banks]] (Graduate Student) at [[Colorado State University]] under a contract with the National Park Service's Midwest Regional Office. The study aimed to describe the fish fauna, aquatic invertebrates, algae, plankton, and physical-chemical conditions of the river, and to build a multi-year record for future comparison. Collections were made from June 25 to November 6, 1952, with comparison to October 1951 collections. The report emphasizes that water volume, temperature, and turbidity fluctuate greatly, and that these fluctuations likely shape the fish fauna and the life-history adaptations of its rarer species.

## Background & Description

**Origin and purpose.** The contract was signed in June 1952. Its stated goals were to study ecological and limnological conditions in the monument, to provide information on the distribution of aquatic organisms (fishes, invertebrates, algae, plankton, and bryophytes), and to support the interpretation of the fishes that occur there. A companion inventory of the terrestrial flora and fauna had been completed in 1951 by Dr. Harold Bergen of Utah State University.

**Daily log.** A daily field log by Joe L. Banks is included as the core of the report. It records river stage, temperature, turbidity, and collection details at each station. Physical data (stream flow, temperature, turbidity) were also drawn from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, the U.S. Geological Survey, and the state game and fish agencies of Wyoming, Utah, and Colorado.

**Sampling design.** The plan was to place a single graduate student in the field for the entire open-water period to collect fishes under varying water conditions, and to record behavior, habitat, and capture methods. Field crews worked from [[Echo Park]], [[Lodore Canyon]], and [[Island Park]], with an October 1952 survey (led by Bergen and Banks) covering sites on both rivers.

**Previous work.** A 1951 survey (Bergen 1951) and a 1951 Utah State University Job Completion Report (Utah Fish and Game) are cited as prior baseline studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** Substrates range from fine sand and silt in the lower reaches to boulder and rubble in canyon sections, with bedrock exposed where scour is strong. Stream gradient changes markedly: about 7.5 ft/mi near Lodore and about 18.2 ft/mi in part of the canyon, with a drop of about 12 ft/mi in one reach. Such variation alters current velocity and habitat, and fish distribution followed these changes.

**Temperature.** Water temperature rises and falls with air temperature and time of day. In one 24-hour record at Lodore, water ranged from 68.0°F to 72.0°F. The Green River was usually several degrees cooler than the Yampa. Cool-water inputs from the Green River were thought to be important for fish that disappear in warm tributaries. A correlation of about 0.95 was found between air and water temperature.

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen at Echo Park ranged from 6.8 to 8.0 ppm, and at another site from 6.5 to 7.3 ppm. Recorded pH values were 7.5 to 7.6, and alkalinity measurements were noted but are partly uncertain because of a suspected error in pH and alkalinity determinations. Dissolved oxygen and temperature showed a strong inverse relationship in the monthly record.

**Turbidity and flow.** Turbidity and volume changed greatly in response to rain and runoff. The Green River was often very turbid in June and July, and river depth fell by up to a foot during dry periods. Turbidity limited visibility, and the field crew noted that the river was the highest the boatman had ever seen during a June high-water event.

**Algae and plankton.** Blue-green algae and diatom blooms were common in late summer and clogged plankton nets. Algal growth increased in September, and the thick green coverage in some areas reduced visibility to about 2 to 3 feet.

**Invertebrates.** Aquatic insects were sampled with square-foot bottom samplers and plankton nets. Collected groups included Coleoptera, Diptera, Ephemeroptera, Odonata, Trichoptera, and Hemiptera. Quantitative comparisons between 1951 and 1952 showed reduced bottom-fauna averages in 1952, which the authors attributed to the year's high water.

## Key Findings

- **Survey effort and scope.** Fish, invertebrate, plankton, and water-quality collections were made from June 25 to November 6, 1952, with a supplementary October 1952 survey. Crews worked on the Green and Yampa Rivers within the monument.
- **Rare fish records.** The [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was the main rare-species focus. Adults were captured in the Lodore area, and a large humpback-sucker collection was made for the University of Colorado. Bluehead sucker (*Pantosteus* sp.) was searched for but not recorded in any collection from the Green River. Thirty-two humpback and other specimens in Table 2 are reported, although exact counts are partly illegible in the scan.
- **Fish community.** The fauna included [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], Channel Catfish, Common Carp, Black Bullhead, Green Sunfish, Utah Chub, and Colorado River fish species. Common carp and channel catfish were widespread, and large carp were often seen but rarely caught.
- **Sampling bias.** The authors noted that high water in 1952 made gill nets, seines, and electrofishing gear less effective. Most large fish escaped capture in high water, so the 1952 totals are considered a minimum estimate. Hook-and-line and seining were the most effective methods for the Green River under these conditions.
- **Fish kill.** A mass mortality of fishes occurred in late August 1952 after a flood event, attributed to high turbidity and stress. Many dead fish, including humpback suckers, were recovered floating downstream.
- **Parasitism.** A survey of parasites found infection rates of about 11.5 percent of fish examined in the Green River, 31.3 percent at Echo Park, and 2.2 percent at Monument Headquarters (Split Mountain). Copepod parasites caused visible ulcerations on some fish.
- **Invertebrate decline.** Bottom-fauna counts were lower in 1952 than in 1951. The authors suggested that the high-water year and flood scouring caused the decline. A full taxonomic analysis was not complete at the time of writing.
- **Plans and recommendations.** The authors recommended continued annual surveys for at least several years, consistent water-quality monitoring, and a larger field crew to capture a broader range of fish under all flow conditions. They emphasized that the 1952 results would provide a base for future comparison.
