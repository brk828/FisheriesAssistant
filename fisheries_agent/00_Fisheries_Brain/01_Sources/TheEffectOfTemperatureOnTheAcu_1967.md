# The Effect of Temperature on the Acute Toxicity of Phenol to Rainbow Trout in Hard Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #ecotoxicology

## Executive Summary
This 1967 study by Brown, Jordan, and Tiller (Water Pollution Research Laboratory, UK) investigated how water temperature modulates the acute toxicity of phenol and "gas-liquor phenols" (a mixture of monohydric phenols from coal carbonization) to [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) in hard water. Contrary to the prevailing literature consensus (e.g. [[Marchetti 1966]]) that increasing temperature generally increases toxicant potency in aquatic animals, this study found the **opposite** relationship for phenol: resistance to phenol poisoning **increased** with temperature up to at least 18°C, with the 48-hr LC50 at 18°C being almost double that at 6°C. This finding held for both pure phenol and gas-liquor phenol mixtures, and was independent of season, fish size/age, and acclimatization duration. The variability of population response (slope function) was lowest at 12°C, suggesting an optimal thermal window for consistent toxicological response. The authors propose that decreased rates of hepatic conjugation/detoxification at low temperature outpace decreases in absorption rate, leading to greater effective internal phenol concentrations at colder temperatures — a mechanism with significant implications for pollution management in cold-water conditions.

## Background & Description
- **Test organism:** Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdnerii*, Richardson), now commonly referenced as [[Oncorhynchus mykiss]]
- **Fork length range:** 5.2–11.3 cm (general increase in size occurred across the 6-month test period, June–November 1965)
- **Weight range:** approx. 5.7–16.2 g (average per test, see Table 2)
- **Condition factor (K):** 1.32–1.43, calculated as K = 100w/l³ (w = weight in g, l = length in cm); values >1.2 indicated fish were comparatively "overweight," partly attributable to post-mortem water absorption (only ~5–6% weight increase even after 7 hr submersion post-death)
- **Post-mortem physiology:** Fish weighed within 1 hr of death to minimize water-absorption artifacts affecting condition factor calculations
- **Gill pathology:** Sub-lethal and lethal phenol exposure associated with gill inflammation, necrosis, and blood congestion; internally, poisoning linked to nerve toxicity, paralysis, coagulation in the heart cavity, and suffocation via gill congestion (per [[Linhardt 1951]], [[Havelka And Effenberger 1957]], [[Vishnevetsky 1962]])

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat medium:** Hard, untreated borehole water (total hardness 320 mg/l as CaCO3; total alkalinity 240 mg/l as CaCO3), pH controlled at 7.8 ± 0.3, dissolved oxygen maintained near saturation
- **Thermal range tested:** 5.6°C to 18.1°C, spanning conditions relevant to temperate freshwater systems subject to seasonal variation and thermal pollution
- **Pollutant source context:** Phenolic contamination in natural waters typically originates from coal carbonization waste discharges (gasworks/coking effluents), producing complex mixtures of monohydric phenols termed "[[Gas-Liquor Phenols]]"
- **Toxicant exposure regime:** Static-renewal bioassay; solutions renewed every 6 hr (>10°C) or 12 hr (<10°C) to counter degradation via oxidation/microbial action
- **Relevance to receiving waters:** Findings imply that cold-water fisheries (e.g., winter conditions, upland/spring-fed streams) — often assumed to be more environmentally "favorable" — may in fact be at **greater risk** of phenol-related toxic damage than warmer waters, with important implications for effluent discharge standards and [[Water Pollution]] regulation.

## Key Findings
- **Inverse temperature-toxicity relationship:** The 48-hr LC50 for pure phenol increased with temperature; at 18°C the LC50 was almost double the value at 6°C — a factor of ~1.8 increase in LC50 for a 10°C rise (from 6.3°C to 16.3°C).
- **48-hr LC50 values (pure phenol):**
  - 6.3°C → 5.4 mg/l
  - 18.4°C → 9.8 mg/l (approx., per Fig. 3 data)
- **Gas-liquor phenols** showed a parallel relationship, with toxicity likewise decreasing (LC50 increasing) as temperature rose, despite tests being conducted within a compressed 10-day window (ruling out seasonal or fish-growth confounds).
- **Slope function (population variability):** Ranged from 1.08 to 1.32 across temperatures, defined per [[Litchfield And Wilcoxon 1949]] as ¼(LC84/LC50 + LC50/LC16); minimum variability (most consistent population response) occurred at **12°C**.
- **Acclimatization independence:** At 6°C, acclimatization periods of 1, 5, and 9 days produced statistically similar 48-hr LC50 values (5.4, 5.0, 5.4 mg/l respectively), indicating the temperature-toxicity effect is not an artifact of acclimatization duration.
- **Time-to-death dynamics:** At ≥9°C, all mortality occurred within 25 hr, making the 24-hr LC50 equivalent to the 48-hr LC50; at 6°C, deaths continued for up to 36 hr, indicating slower toxicokinetics at low temperature.
- **Low-mortality threshold shift:** To limit mortality to 1% (rather than 50%) within 48 hr, the safe concentration had to be reduced by ~25% at 12°C (8.0 → 6.1 mg/l) versus ~40% at 6°C (5.4 → 3.2 mg/l), indicating steeper safety margins needed at lower temperatures.
- **Comparison with literature:** Results contradict [[Gersdorff 1943]] (goldfish, high-concentration survival time decreasing with temperature) and general reviews by [[Wuhrmann 1952]] and [[Marchetti 1966]], but align with data from [[Herbert 1962]] (rainbow trout) and [[Wuhrmann 1952]] (brown trout) when plotted on the same temperature-LC50 curve (Fig. 2).
- **Proposed mechanism:** Detoxification of phenol via conjugation (glucuronic acid and sulphuric acid pathways, per [[Williams 1959]]) may slow disproportionately more than absorption rate as temperature decreases, resulting in higher **effective internal phenol concentration** at low temperature despite lower metabolic/absorption rates — consistent with general poikilotherm thermal physiology as discussed by [[Prosser 1964]].
- **Analogous invertebrate evidence:** [[Crisp And Marr 1957]] reported a similar inverse temperature-toxicity relationship for n-butyl acetate narcosis in barnacle nauplii larvae (3–20°C), supporting the idea that this pattern is not unique to fish or phenol.
- **Specificity of phenol toxicity:** Unlike many non-specific narcotic organic poisons whose toxicity follows thermodynamic activity ([[Ferguson 1939]]), phenols were argued by [[Lindenberg And Gary-Bodo 1952]] to act as specific poisons independent of thermodynamic activity, consistent with this study's unusual temperature-response pattern.
