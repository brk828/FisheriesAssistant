# Edible Land Snails in the United States

#malacology #gastropoda #helicidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service publication (Resource Publication 91, by Lola T. Dees) reviews nine species of [[Edible Land Snails]] introduced to the United States for food. The author concludes that most of these introductions have been problematic. Several species damage gardens and crops, and some have required costly eradication. Federal law, administered by the [[United States Department of Agriculture]], restricts the importation and interstate movement of edible snails. The paper summarizes each species' distribution, shell features, reproduction, and control methods.

## Background & Description

**Scope and regulation.** Edible snails cannot be bred or raised in interstate commerce without USDA approval, and USDA permits for import are limited to certain species entering through the ports of Boston and New York for consumption in those metropolitan areas. Movement into rural areas is not permitted. State agricultural officials must also be consulted, and the [[United States Department of the Interior]] may regulate snails that threaten wildlife. Knowing violations of these laws can result in fines, imprisonment, or both.

**Classification.** Edible land snails belong to the phylum Mollusca, class Gastropoda ("stomach-footed"), order Pulmonata ("lunglike organs"). The key food genera are *Achatina* (family Achatinidae) and *Helix*, *Cepaea*, *Otala*, and *Theba* (family Helicidae).

**Anatomy.** A snail has a head, a broad retractile foot, and a shell. The head bears two pairs of retractable tentacles. The longer pair ends in eyes that detect only light and dark, and the shorter pair is thought to sense smell or touch. The mouth holds a horny jaw and a [[Radula]], a rasping organ with many rows of teeth. The shell usually has three to six whorls, normally coiled to the right, and is often banded. Snails lack a permanent operculum, but they can build a temporary one from lime. Snails may live about 5 years or more, and they can regenerate lost eyes, tentacles, parts of the foot, teeth, and shell.

**Reproduction.** The account focuses on [[Helix Pomatia]]. Snails are hermaphrodites but do not self-fertilize. Mating occurs in early spring and sometimes again in July. Clutches typically contain 50 or more eggs, which are laid in a hole in loose soil and covered with slime. Eggs hatch in 25 to 40 days depending on temperature. Because there is no larval stage, hatchlings resemble adults. Adults do not tend their eggs or young.

**Food and feeding.** Adults eat tender flowers, vegetables, decaying plant matter, and some fruit. They feed only at night, or on dark or damp days, and locate food with the shorter tentacles.

**Locomotion and behavior.** Snails move by waves of foot contraction at roughly 2 1/2 inches per minute for a large *Helix*, and they leave protective slime trails. With the first frosts they hibernate, sealing their shells with an operculum and burying themselves in dead leaves and slime. They estivate (enter a torpid state) during hot or dry weather.

**Enemies.** Birds and small mammals are the most important predators. Snails defend themselves with slime, nocturnal habits, drab color, small size, and withdrawal into the shell.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Snails occupy a wide range of habitats that provide food, lime, moisture, and shelter. They are more common on calcareous soils and rare on acid soils. Dense, low vegetation, piles of boards, bricks, and stones, and damp spaces beneath porches all shelter them.

The author emphasizes that native U.S. snails are seldom numerous enough to be noticed as pests, whereas introduced species can become abundant in limited areas and damage cultivated plants. Several species are noted for their ability to survive adverse conditions. [[Otala Lactea]] estivates high on trees and buildings and forms a double operculum, which helps it tolerate extreme heat and cold.

**Control methods.** Poisoning is the principal method. [[Metaldehyde]] alone kills snails under dry conditions, but under moist conditions it must be combined with [[Calcium Arsenate]]. Baits come as granules or pellets. Granules are broadcast at 1 pound per 1,000 square feet, and pellets at 5 to 10 pounds per acre. Dusts with 10 percent metaldehyde can be applied to greenhouse ornamentals at 1 pound per 1,000 square feet. Sprays should not be used near vegetable plants. Other measures include keeping infested materials out of greenhouses and basements, removing hiding places, keeping spaces dry, sealing wall chinks, and hand-picking snails at night.

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory status.** Federal law bars USDA approval of edible snail importation or interstate movement for breeding or raising, and permits are restricted to the Boston and New York ports for metropolitan consumption.
- **Nine species** are covered. Each is known to be edible in its native country and was introduced to the United States for food.
- **[[Achatina Fulica]] (giant African snail)** has a maximum body length of nearly 9 inches and up to 600 offspring per year. It became established only in [[Hawaii]], where taxpayers spent some $200,000 on control. A 1963 introduction in north Miami, [[Florida]], had grown by 1966 to an estimated 20,000 snails across a 13-square-block area. Ground baiting was used because aerial poisoning was not possible in the populated area. Eradication was unsuccessful against the estivating snails.
- **[[Cepaea Hortensis]] (garden snail)** is widely distributed in central and northern Europe, Iceland, and Newfoundland. It was introduced into Maine, Massachusetts, and New Hampshire in Colonial days but has not established itself there.
- **[[Cepaea Nemoralis]] (wood snail)** was introduced into Ontario, Canada, and has been recorded in several U.S. states, including Massachusetts, New York, and California. It has not proved to be a destructive pest in the United States.
- **[[Helix Aperta]] (green or burrowing snail)** damages flower and vegetable gardens in California and Louisiana. Eradication efforts in California were discontinued except for local control. An October 1941 California survey found an average of 75 eggs per nest (minimum 36, maximum 129).
- **[[Helix Aspersa]] (European brown snail)** is the most widely eaten European snail. It was introduced to many U.S. locations, including Charleston, New Orleans, Oregon, California, Arizona, Utah, Florida, and Dallas. It may lay about 400 eggs a year. California did not attempt eradication because the cost would be prohibitive.
- **[[Helix Pomatia]] (Burgundy or Roman snail)** is distributed from eastern France to the Balkans and from Denmark to the Balkan Peninsula. In the United States it was established at New Orleans, Louisiana, and Jackson, Michigan, and it has also been found in Florida. It damages gardens. Its natural life span is about 6 to 8 years.
- **[[Otala Lactea]] (milk or Spanish snail)** was locally introduced into California, Texas, Mississippi, Florida, Georgia, and Sullivans Island, South Carolina. It has not become an important agricultural pest. Winter 1940 counts in California found 40 to 100 eggs per nest, averaging 66.
- **[[Otala Vermiculata]]** was introduced locally into New Orleans, where it was first seen in 1918. It has not been collected there since, and its present status is unclear.
- **[[Theba Pisana]] (white snail)** is a very injurious garden pest in its native and introduced ranges. It was eliminated from California by flaming, poison baiting, and handpicking, and it is not known to occur anywhere in the United States.
- **Management emphasis.** The author notes that controlling established snails is difficult because of estivation and burrowing. Prevention, such as restricting imports and removing hiding places, is treated as the most practical approach.
