# Lower Colorado River Ecoregion Ecosystem Plan

#ecosystem-management #conservation-planning #endangered-species #riparian-restoration #water-management

## Executive Summary

This Final Draft Ecosystem Plan for the [[Lower Colorado River Ecoregion]] (LCRE) represents one of 52 watershed-based units defined by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] through an ecosystem approach to natural resource conservation. The LCRE encompasses approximately 160 million acres across portions of Arizona, California, Nevada, Utah, Baja California del Norte, and Sonora, Mexico, including roughly 700 miles of the [[Colorado River]] from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to the [[Gulf of California]].

The plan addresses critical conservation challenges in a region where extensive habitat alteration has occurred, particularly in riparian and aquatic ecosystems. Currently, the LCRE supports 24 Federally-listed Endangered and Threatened species, one proposed species, and 69 Candidate species. Native riparian habitats, including [[Fremont Cottonwood]]-[[Goodding's Willow]] gallery forests, have declined to less than 5% of historical extent. Most native fish populations are imperiled, with [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Southwestern River Otter]] already extirpated.

The plan establishes eight interconnected goals: (1) fostering partnerships across jurisdictions; (2) ensuring adequate water management and quality; (3) recovering threatened and endangered species; (4) restoring migratory bird populations; (5) protecting native riparian habitats; (6) restoring wetlands; (7) protecting other biotic communities; and (8) providing sustainable human uses while increasing public awareness. Success requires extensive coordination among Federal, State, Tribal, and Mexican agencies, plus private sector cooperation, particularly regarding water management operations that consider wildlife and habitat needs alongside human demands.

## Background & Description

The [[Lower Colorado River Ecoregion]] encompasses three major North American deserts: the [[Great Basin Desert]], [[Mojave Desert]], and [[Sonoran Desert]]. Upland habitats vary along elevational and latitudinal gradients, including desertlands, grasslands, shrublands, and montane woodlands and forests. The watershed includes major tributaries: the [[Paria River]], [[Little Colorado River]], [[Virgin River]], [[Bill Williams River]], and [[Gila River]].

Regional biological diversity is enhanced by extensive marsh and riparian habitats associated with the watershed, including the [[Salton Sea]], [[Ciénega de Santa Clara]] in Mexico, and the [[Gulf of California]]. The region has experienced dramatic ecological alterations, most evident in riparian areas and aquatic habitats. Major wetland losses have occurred, and stands of native [[Fremont Cottonwood]]-[[Goodding's Willow]] and associated faunal communities face local extirpation.

Water development projects for storage, diversion, flood control, and power generation have profoundly impacted habitats through: elimination of seasonal flood regimes, reservoir formation, channelization, blockage of fish migration corridors, decreased water quality, introduction of non-native species, intensified recreational use, agricultural development, and human-caused fire regimes. The exotic [[Salt Cedar]] (tamarisk) has replaced native riparian vegetation, providing relatively few wildlife benefits.

The multi-jurisdictional nature of water management and land ownership, combined with intense water demands, means achieving biological diversity goals requires extensive intergovernmental, interagency, and private sector-governmental coordination. Water use and allocation among communities, counties, States, Tribal governments, and countries may become the most volatile regional issue of the 21st century.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The LCRE's aquatic ecosystems have undergone drastic and rapid change. Native fishes represent a fauna in serious trouble—the majority are Federally-listed as Endangered, Threatened, or Candidate species. Wild populations of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]] have been extirpated, while [[Bonytail Chub]] persists in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. The [[Razorback Sucker]] remains in these reservoirs and portions of the lower Colorado River. The [[Humpback Chub]] occurs only in the [[Grand Canyon]]. [[Virgin River]] fishes remain in that system, while other native species like [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Desert Sucker]] occur sporadically.

Construction of dams along the Colorado River and tributaries eliminated historic spawning runs and altered water temperature, turbidity, and flow regimes. Dewatering of historic habitats and introduction of non-native fishes—many competitors and predators of native species—have had significant negative impacts. Remnant populations of [[Desert Pupfish]] persist in isolated ciénega habitats in northern Mexico, southern Arizona, and California.

The near extirpation of native riparian habitats has caused precipitous population declines in many avian species. Neotropical migrants such as the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], [[Arizona Bell's Vireo]], and [[Vermillion Flycatcher]] have become scarce and concentrated in remaining sizable stands like those on [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]. Less than 100 years ago, the LCRE contained over 100,000 acres of native riparian habitats; today less than 5% remains.

Loss of vast estuarine marshes of the [[Colorado River Delta]] in Mexico has significantly reduced habitat for waterfowl, marsh and waterbirds, and shorebirds. The LCRE constitutes important wintering habitat for many migratory bird species, with major wetland habitats including [[Ciénega de Santa Clara]], [[Salton Sea]], and the Colorado River with associated tributaries and backwaters. The Lower Colorado River serves as a major migration corridor for neotropical migratory bird species.

Water quality concerns, particularly elevated selenium levels in LCRE aquatic habitats, must be understood and managed. The effects of selenium loading and contamination on fish and wildlife require research to develop water management strategies that decrease environmental selenium levels.

## Key Findings

**Conservation Status and Priorities:**
- 24 Federally-listed Endangered and Threatened species currently occur in the LCRE
- 1 species proposed for listing
- 69 Candidate species under assessment for possible listing
- 2 species extirpated: [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Southwestern River Otter]]
- Native riparian habitats reduced to <5% of historical extent (from >100,000 acres)
- Most native fish species are listed or candidates for listing

**Eight Primary Goals Established:**

1. **Partnerships**: Strengthen existing and foster new international, intergovernmental, interagency, and private sector-governmental partnerships through coordination with Mexico via NAFTA-created task forces, improved federal-state agency communication, increased Tribal government coordination, and active partnerships with hydroelectric power and water management agencies.

2. **Water Management and Quality**: Ensure water management operations consistent with ecosystem needs by developing recommendations for [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Colorado River operations, quantifying minimum instream flows, maximizing Service water entitlements through "pooling" rights for Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuges, conducting hydrological studies, and implementing water conservation strategies. Address selenium contamination through research on impacts to nesting waterbirds and neotropical migrants, determine effects of flushing rates on selenium levels, and identify human-caused selenium inputs in the Upper Basin.

3. **Endangered Species Recovery**: Prevent extinction and maintain populations while moving toward recovery of all Federally-listed species. For native fishes, establish self-perpetuating populations of [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Chub]] through artificial propagation, grow-out facilities, genetic management, habitat restoration, and research. Recover [[Endangered Desert Pupfish]], implement [[Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan]], and initiate management actions to preclude listing of Candidate species. For wetland species, recover [[Endangered Yuma Clapper Rail]] through habitat management and population assessment. For riparian species, prevent extinction of [[Endangered Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]. Address upland species including [[Threatened Coachella Valley Fringe-toed Lizard]] and [[Desert Tortoise]].

4. **Migratory Bird Resource**: Protect, restore, and enhance Trust migratory bird populations through monitoring programs, scientific studies, land acquisition, and habitat management. Continue monitoring of waterfowl, shorebirds, sandhill cranes, and white-faced ibis populations. Conduct studies on brown-headed cowbird parasitism, gull-billed tern nesting success, grebe die-offs, and burrowing owl dispersal. Prioritize lands for acquisition including Cibola Valley Irrigation District lands, Planet Ranch, and mitigation lands adjacent to [[Salton Sea National Wildlife Refuge]].

5. **Native Riparian Habitat**: Establish long-term (20+ year) restoration initiative for [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Goodding's Willow]], honey and screwbean mesquite habitats. Conduct GIS analyses to identify core habitats, buffer zones, and linkages. Develop partnerships for restoration on public, Tribal, State, and private lands. Continue ongoing restoration on Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuges with goal of restoring 2,000 acres over 20 years. Develop integrated pest management for exotic species like [[Salt Cedar]] and common reed. Maintain active fire prevention and suppression programs to protect remnant and restored stands.

6. **Wetland Habitats**: Protect, restore, and enhance wetland habitats through GIS analysis, partnership development, and specific projects including: Conservation Plan for [[Ciénega de Santa Clara]] in Mexico; restoration of [[Topock Marsh]] on [[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]]; enhancement of Three Fingers Lake and Hart Mine Marsh on [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]; restoration of Martinez Marsh wetland complex on [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]; and protection/management of over 7,000 acres of wetlands on Lower Colorado River refuges and 800 acres on Salton Sea refuge.

7. **Other Biotic Communities**: Protect, restore, and enhance desertlands, grasslands, scrublands, and montane woodlands/forests through cross-ecoregion initiatives, GIS analysis of key upland habitats, and participation in ecosystem management planning efforts including [[Bureau of Land Management]] California Desert District planning and [[International Sonoran Desert Biosphere Reserve]] establishment.

8. **Sustainable Human Uses**: Provide sustainable uses on public lands consistent with ecosystem goals by expanding public outreach, environmental education, and interpretation programs. Construct multiagency office/visitor center complex for [[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]]. Develop watchable wildlife projects, observation platforms, trails, and interpreted auto tours. Develop zonal management plan for waterborne recreation emphasizing protection of sensitive resources. Increase law enforcement capabilities on river refuges.

**Critical Implementation Elements:**
- Establishment of grow-out facilities for native fish stocking (3-year project, $100,000)
- Development of additional native fish rearing sites ($1,500,000, 5 FTE)
- Restoration of free-flowing river sections for native fish recovery (5-year project, $1,000,000, 3.0 FTE)
- Native riparian habitat restoration projects totaling $3,000,000 over 20 years (4.0 FTE)
- Wetland restoration projects: [[Topock Marsh]] ($750,000), Three Fingers Lake and Hart Mine Marsh ($1,500,000), Martinez Marsh complex ($1,000,000)
- Fire management: 3-person fire crews with dedicated equipment ($275,000 annually, 4.5 FTE)
- GIS analysis and mapping capabilities ($150,000, 1.0 FTE)
- Multiagency visitor center construction ($9,000,000)

**Partnership Requirements:**
The plan emphasizes that success depends on dynamic partnerships at international, Federal, Tribal, State, and local governmental levels, plus cooperation with public and private organizations, industry, private landowners, and other interests. Key partners identified include: [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[California Department of Fish and Game]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[National Park Service]], [[Corps of Engineers]], Tribal agencies, [[Lower Colorado River Habitat Conservation Plan Steering Committee]], irrigation districts, conservation organizations, and Mexican agencies.

**Legal and Policy Context:**
All actions must address legal questions within the context of the "Law of the River" (interstate compacts, court decrees, international treaties, federal and state legislation, contracts, and allotments governing Colorado River management) or other appropriate legislative mandates. The plan operates under the [[Endangered Species Act]] as amended (16 U.S.C. FR 1531, et seq.) and National Policy Issuance No. 94-07 establishing the ecosystem approach.
