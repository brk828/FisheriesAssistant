# Endangered Fishes of the Colorado River Basin

## Executive Summary
This report documents the precipitous decline of three native Arizona fishes -- the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] -- once so abundant in the Colorado and Gila River systems that early settlers reportedly harvested them with pitchforks from drained irrigation canals. All three species are now listed as federally and state endangered. ASU zoologists [[W.L. Minckley]] and [[Paul Marsh]] have initiated a conservation stocking program, introducing 1,000 individuals of each species into lakes at ASU's Research Park, funded by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in cooperation with the Arizona Game and Fish Department. The goal is to establish observable populations to study poorly understood aspects of their biology and decline, and ultimately to support re-establishment efforts in the wild.

## Background & Description

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) -- a large native cyprinid; historically the top predatory minnow of the Colorado River system.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) -- a native cyprinid; described as "the most critically endangered fish in the Southwest."
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) -- a native catostomid, named for its distinctive raised dorsal keel (not explicitly described in text, but implied by common name).

All three species are characterized as adapted to swift-water riverine conditions, having evolved over "millions of years" in free-flowing river habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historical range**: The three species ranged throughout the [[Colorado River]] Valley, from Wyoming to Mexico, and into the [[Gila River]] as far as the New Mexico border.
- **Habitat preference**: Swift-flowing, free-flowing river systems (e.g., the historical Salt and Gila rivers prior to damming).
- **Habitat alteration**: Construction of dams such as [[Roosevelt Dam]] converted these rivers into slow-moving ponds and streams, eliminating the swift-water habitat required by these species.
- **Current distribution**:
  - [[Bonytail Chub]]: found rarely, only in [[Lake Mohave]]; only 24 individuals documented in Minckley's last 10 years of research there.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: also restricted to [[Lake Mohave]]; population estimated in the low thousands.
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]]: possibly "many thousands" remaining in the wild, but restricted in distribution to the Colorado River.
- **Threats**:
  - Habitat loss from damming and river regulation.
  - Introduction of non-native fishes (e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]]) that may prey on juveniles of native species.
- **Broader context**: 16 of Arizona's 25 native fish species are listed as endangered, indicating a systemic ecological crisis in the region's freshwater fish fauna, not isolated to these three species.

## Key Findings
- All three focal species were historically abundant enough to be harvested incidentally by settlers from irrigation canals, indicating a dramatic ~100-year population collapse.
- Population decline occurred primarily within the last ~40 years, coinciding with dam construction and introduction of non-native sport fishes.
- The [[Bonytail Chub]] is the most critically endangered, with a wild population possibly numbering only in the low hundreds.
- Minckley draws a direct historical parallel between the fishes' decline and the near-extinction of the American bison, emphasizing that this represents a shift from "abundant to endangered," not merely "rare to rarer."
- A captive/refuge population strategy (stocking 1,000 individuals of each species at ASU Research Park lakes) was implemented to prevent extinction and enable closer scientific study, since these species became rare before adequate scientific data could be collected.
- The research project is a collaborative conservation effort between ASU, the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].
- Minckley frames the loss of native fish species as an early warning indicator of broader ecological instability, drawing an analogy to potential agricultural crop failures (e.g., wheat, rice) to underscore the human stakes of biodiversity loss.
