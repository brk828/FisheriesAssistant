# Endangered and Threatened Fish and Wildlife of Arizona and New Mexico Forests and Rangelands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1983 handbook from the USDA Forest Service, Southwestern Region, profiles endangered and threatened fish and wildlife of Arizona and New Mexico forests and rangelands. It has two editions, one for each state, that share most of their text. The handbook is an identification and awareness guide, not a research study. It covers 20 listed taxa: nine fishes, ten birds and mammals, and one reptile and one crustacean. Each profile gives the species description, historic and existing distribution, reasons for decline, habitat, food, and a "Did You Know?" note. The handbook's central message is that recovery of rare species depends on specialized habitat knowledge and on public commitment.

## Background & Description

The handbook is framed by the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, which the document calls a national milestone. It notes that states have passed their own protective laws and that the Southwest has some of the most diverse wildlife and habitats in the country. Its information was compiled from [[Recovery Plans]] issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (for example, Colorado River Squawfish 1978, Woundfin 1979, Humpback Chub 1979), the state handbooks of Arizona and New Mexico, and Minckley's *Fishes of Arizona* (1973).

Fish profiles include:

- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*), Endangered: a "big river" fish with a prominent dorsal hump and thick body, reaching about 18 inches.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*), Endangered: streamlined, dark above, reaching about 24 inches.
- **[[Colorado River Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), Endangered: the largest native minnow of North America, nearly six feet long and up to 80 pounds.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), Endangered: a silvery minnow about 2.5 inches long, named for a sharp dorsal fin ray.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) and **[[Yaqui Topminnow]]** (*P. o. sonoriensis*), Endangered: guppy-like live-bearers about 2½ inches long.
- **[[Arizona Trout]]** (*Salmo apache*), Threatened: a spotted trout that is also an Arizona game fish.
- **[[Gila Trout]]** (*Salmo gilae*), Endangered: a deep-bodied, fine-spotted trout.
- **[[Pecos Gambusia]]** (*Gambusia nobilis*), Endangered, under consideration for downlisting to threatened: a small live-bearer of the Pecos drainage.

Non-fish profiles include the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[American Peregrine Falcon]], [[Brown Pelican]], [[Whooping Crane]], [[Masked Bobwhite]], [[Black-Footed Ferret]], [[Sonoran Pronghorn]], [[Mexican Gray Wolf]], [[Ridge-Nosed Rattlesnake]] (Threatened), and [[Socorro Isopod]] (*Exosphaeroma thermophilus*, Endangered). The New Mexico edition omits the Arizona-only taxa and adds Socorro Isopod, Pecos Gambusia, and the rattlesnake.

Each profile has a range map, and many are marked "Historic" and "Existing" with the State and Private Forestry and Forest Service office locations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The handbook repeatedly identifies habitat change as the main driver of decline. Its examples include:

- **River alteration**: dam construction, water diversion, channelization, and altered temperature and flow. These affect the big-river fishes ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Woundfin]]). Dams also created new marsh and reservoir habitat that expanded the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and wintering [[Bald Eagle]] populations.
- **Hybridization and introduced species**: hybridization with [[Bonytail Chub]] threatens humpback chub, and hybridization with rainbow trout and competition with exotic trout threaten the native trouts. Introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) displaced topminnows and Pecos gambusia. The document reports that Gila topminnows were eliminated locally within 1 to 3 years after mosquitofish introductions.
- **Pesticides**: accumulation caused reproductive failure in [[Brown Pelican]] and [[American Peregrine Falcon]] populations through egg shell thinning, and it is the main factor for the decline of the Bald Eagle continental population.
- **Grassland and desert change**: overgrazing, grassland conversion, and fencing affected the [[Masked Bobwhite]] and [[Sonoran Pronghorn]]. Prairie dog control removed the food base of the [[Black-Footed Ferret]], which depends almost entirely on [[Prairie Dogs]].
- **Spring and thermal habitat loss**: spring capping and diversion are the only known cause of decline for the [[Socorro Isopod]], which lives only in thermal springs at 88–92°F.
- **Overcollection and persecution**: intensive predator control brought the [[Mexican Gray Wolf]] near extinction. Over-collection for zoo and pet trades threatens the [[Ridge-Nosed Rattlesnake]].

Habitat requirements show how narrow many of these taxa are. Native trout need small, cool, clear mountain streams. Pecos gambusia is restricted to sinkholes and shallow, slow streams with high carbonate levels. The Mexican gray wolf used elevations from 3,000 to 12,000 feet, and the ridge-nosed rattlesnake lives at 5,700 to 9,000 feet.

## Key Findings

- **Decline causes are consistent across taxa.** Dams, diversions, habitat loss, hybridization, exotic competitors, and pesticides recur throughout the profiles.
- **Fish counts.** Native trout have low reproductive output: Gila trout lay 75 to 150 eggs per spawning, far fewer than the hundreds laid by other trout.
- **Population status:**
  - Yuma clapper rail: estimated at 1,000 in 1973 and over 2,000 at the time of the handbook.
  - Gila topminnow: nine natural populations in Arizona; presumed extinct in New Mexico after a 1977 survey found none.
  - Yaqui topminnow: ten natural populations in Arizona.
  - Pecos gambusia: five natural populations, two in New Mexico and three in Texas.
  - Bonytail chub: presently found in Lake Mohave.
  - Woundfin: the Virgin River is its only supporting habitat.
  - Mexican gray wolf: about 50 remaining in Mexico; the last known U.S. den was found in 1938.
  - Whooping crane: the wild flock at Wood Buffalo National Park numbered 78, and an experimental flock had 16 birds wintering in the Rio Grande valley of New Mexico.
  - Masked bobwhite: naturally occurring populations are limited to Sonora, Mexico; the species was reintroduced in Altar Valley, Arizona.
- **Recovery actions** include hatchery propagation at Dexter National Fish Hatchery (topminnows, Pecos gambusia), reintroduction of captive-raised birds (peregrine falcon, masked bobwhite, whooping crane), and captive breeding of the Mexican gray wolf.
- **Public role.** The handbook's closing argument is that recovery success depends on public commitment and awareness.
- **Notable biology from "Did You Know?" entries:** the humpback chub's hump deflects water to keep it in swift currents; the bald eagle reaches pure white head and tail at 4 to 5 years; the brown pelican lived over 31 years in the wild; the American peregrine falcon can reach 165 to 180 miles per hour; the pronghorn can run 40 miles per hour over short distances; Gila topminnow females give birth to 1 to 15 live young every 24 to 28 days in warm months.
