# Why All Cave Animals Do Not Look Alike: Adaptive Value of Reduction Processes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #speleobiology #plethodontidae #characidae

## Executive Summary

This theoretical review by [[Boris Sket]] (published in the [[Nss Bulletin]], 1985) re-examines why eyes, pigment and other integumental structures are reduced in cave animals. It weighs the [[Neutral Mutation Hypothesis]] against the [[Energy Economy Hypothesis]] (direct selection) and considers the [[Pleiotropy Hypothesis]] as a compromise. The author argues that several arguments favouring neutral mutation are unreliable, and presents new evidence (clinal variation, relative variability, eye-appendage trade-offs in amphipods) that supports an adaptive role for reduction. The paper concludes that the question is not settled and that controlled competition experiments are needed.

## Background & Description

- **Historical framing:** The paper begins with [[Charles Darwin]]'s statements that unused structures would be reduced by natural selection and that rudiments would be highly variable. The author notes Darwin did not recognise the tension between these two ideas.
- **Three genetic hypotheses:** Structural reduction is explained by (1) energy economy (direct selection), (2) neutral mutation (mutation pressure), or (3) pleiotropy. The author avoids terms such as "regression" or "degeneration" as prejudiced.
- **Key taxa discussed:**
  - [[Typhlotriton Spelaeus]], a cave salamander whose eyes reduce mainly after metamorphosis, when it moves into a more rigorous terrestrial cave habitat.
  - [[Astyanax Mexicanus]], a cave characin whose eye degenerates during ontogeny, with a sudden change near sexual maturity.
  - [[Asellus Aquaticus]] (cave and surface subspecies), studied along the [[Postojna-Planina Cave System]].
  - [[Gammarus]] (troglobitic and epigean amphipods) and [[Niphargus]].
  - [[Troglocaris Anophthalmus]] (cave shrimp) and [[Synurella Ambulans]] (amphipod-like crustacean in the Pivka system).
  - [[Ancylus Fluviatilis]] (freshwater limpet) and [[Poecilia Sphenops]] (cave population of a poeciliid fish).
- **Scope:** The paper is mainly about invertebrates and cave fishes, with fish cases (Astyanax, Typhlichthys-related Yucatán cave fishes, Poecilia) forming a minority of the examples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Energy-poor caves:** Cave habitats are food-poor and lack seasonal temperature fluctuations, so reduced metabolic demand is likely advantageous. Eye tissue is energetically costly: in the fly *Calliphora vicina*, complete eyes consume a share of body O2 in darkness (figure garbled in the scan, ending at 6.4%) while accounting for about 2% of body weight.
- **Caves change over time:** The author argues that cave ecological conditions are ephemeral, shaped by Pleistocene climate change and speleogenesis, so cave age is a poor predictor of reduction grade.
- **Pollution gradient:** Along the Pivka underground river, the non-troglomorphic phenotype of *Asellus* increases with energy input (pollution), and the troglomorphic phenotype persists only in the poorest, cleanest downstream waters.
- **Osmotic stress:** Marine ancestors entering fresh water face hypotonic problems, which the author proposes may explain stronger reduction in some Yucatán fishes.
- **Freshwater energetics:** Amphipods increase oxygen consumption when moving from sea to low-ionic freshwater, which may raise energy costs for eyes.

## Key Findings

1. **Energy economy is plausible:** Although exact energy data are lacking, the author sees strong indications that eye reduction saves energy, particularly in developmental histories such as that of [[Typhlotriton Spelaeus]].
2. **Reduction is not simply degeneration:** Mutations in structural genes are only one possible basis. Changes in morphogenetic centre dominance, competitive differentiation timing, and active ontogenetic destruction are also possible. The author suggests eye destruction may sometimes be an active process invoked by genetic novelties.
3. **Integumental linkage:** Eye, pigment and cuticle are integumental structures in arthropods, possibly linked genetically or morphogenetically. This would make a single destructive mutation more costly and increase the economic effect of reduction.
4. **Variability argument rejected:** Reduced structures were not found to be more variable than selected, well-developed ones. In the *Poecilia* case, the apparent increase in eye variability was small (about twofold in relative terms) and disappeared in absolute or volume measures. In *Synurella*, low phenotypic variability in small character states likely reflects fewer active genes.
5. **Regional and age patterns:** Under the neutral hypothesis, reduction should be similar across populations of similar age. Reported differences in reduction grade among *Troglocaris* and *Neohoratia* populations in the Dinaric karst, which have similar ages, argue for selection with varying ecological severity.
6. **Clinal variation:**
   - *Asellus aquaticus cavernicolus* shows graded eye, pigment and cuticle reduction along the Krka and Postojna-Planina river courses, with an admixture of the surface subspecies.
   - *Synurella ambulans* shows ommatidia counts of about 20 upstream, 6–12 downstream, and 1–11 in the Rak tributary.
   - *Ancylus fluviatilis* shows variable pigmentation and eye reduction along the cave course.
7. **Amphipod eye-appendage trade-off:** Hypogean palearctic *Gammarus* species with completely lost eyes have unchanged appendages, whereas those with partly reduced eyes have elongated antennae and legs. Marine or mixohaline *Gammarus* have eyes at 35–50% of head length, while freshwater epigean species have 20–30% (exceptionally 40%). The completely blind *Niphargus valachicus* is described as one of the most euryvalent European amphipods.
8. **Cricket example:** In Venezuelan cave crickets, the microphthalmic type (about 60 ommatidia) occurs in energy-poor caves and the macrophthalmic type (about 200 ommatidia) in energy-rich ones, supporting an energy-based interpretation.
9. **Future research:** The author calls for:
   - exact data on metabolic costs of structures that are usually reduced;
   - competition experiments in population cages mixing epigean-type fish or animals with mutants having reduced eyes and/or pigment, but no progressive troglomorphic traits;
   - control of light and food quantity and quality.

   The author describes these experiments as the only reliable route to direct evidence of adaptive value.
