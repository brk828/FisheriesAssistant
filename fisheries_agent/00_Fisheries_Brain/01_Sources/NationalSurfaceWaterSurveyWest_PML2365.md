# Western Lake Survey Phase I: Quality Assurance Report on Lake Water Chemistry

#limnology #water-quality #freshwater-lakes

## Executive Summary

This EPA Project Summary describes the [[Quality Assurance Program]] for the Phase I synoptic chemistry component of the [[Western Lake Survey]] (WLS-I), part of the [[National Surface Water Survey]]. The survey was built to assess the effects of acidic deposition on lake water chemistry in the Western United States. The quality assurance (QA) program checked whether the data were of known and acceptable quality for estimating population characteristics. Most data quality objectives (DQOs) were met. The final data set is considered high quality and usable for subregional population estimates. The report also identifies gaps for future surveys, mainly in accuracy estimation for [[Acid Neutralizing Capacity]] and [[Closed-System pH]], and in blank-based contamination control.

## Background & Description

**Origin and purpose.** The [[National Acid Precipitation Assessment Program]] (NAPAP) was started at the request of the U.S. Congress to evaluate the effects of acidic deposition on aquatic resources. The National Surface Water Survey was created under this program, and the WLS-I was its western component.

**Survey design.** The WLS-I was a synoptic (single-snapshot) survey of lakes. Field crews collected lake water samples and recorded field measurements. Field laboratories handled on-site analyses, and contract analytical laboratories performed selected chemical and physical tests. Samples were preserved with HgCl2 at the lake site or in the field laboratory. The survey measured 28 chemical and physical characteristics, with six primary variables discussed in detail.

**Quality assurance activities.** QA covered:
- Selection of analytical methods and contract laboratories
- Training of field sampling and field laboratory crews
- On-site evaluation of field operations and analytical laboratories
- Daily communication with participants
- Verification and validation of collected data

QA samples (blanks, duplicates, audit samples, and spikes) were used to identify, quantify, and partition sources of variability into precision, accuracy, bias, and detectability components.

**Data management.** Data management and review were coordinated by the [[Environmental Monitoring Systems Laboratory]] in Las Vegas, Nevada, and the [[Environmental Research Laboratory]] in Corvallis, Oregon. The data base management system was run by Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. An independent contractor audited data consistency.

**Special studies.**
- **[[Calibration Study]]:** A subset of 50 wilderness-area lakes was sampled to compare the established sampling method with the new method. Linear regression was used to evaluate the calibration. No significant effects were found from sampling protocol or from lake location on analyte concentration.
- **[[Nitrate-Sulfate Stability Study]]:** Split samples were collected with a Van Dorn sampler, preserved with HgCl2, and analyzed at two laboratories to test whether preservation method affected results. Differences between the two preservation approaches were found to be minor and not expected to affect population estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The survey addressed lake chemistry as an indicator of acidification risk. Key water-quality parameters were acid-base status (ANC and base neutralizing capacity), major ions (calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, sulfate), nutrients (nitrate, ammonium, phosphorus), trace metals (aluminum, iron, manganese), dissolved organic carbon, pH, and conductance. These variables are central to understanding how acidic deposition affects lake biota and buffering capacity. Low-concentration analytes, such as [[Extractable Aluminum]] and nitrate, were near detection limits in many lakes, which made precision and accuracy harder to evaluate. Lake waters were often low in ionic strength and near circumneutral pH.

## Key Findings

**Overall data quality**
- The final WLS-I data set is high quality and suitable for calculating population estimates.
- Precision, accuracy, and detectability estimates generally met the DQOs, with a few exceptions.
- The QA program confirmed the overall soundness of survey design, execution, and data generation.
- Data from the West can be compared with data from other regions of the National Surface Water Survey. No calibration was needed for procedural or analytical method differences.

**Acid Neutralizing Capacity (ANC)**
- All QA estimates indicated acceptable quality, suitable for subregional population estimates.
- Field blank data met the required detection limit.
- System precision met DQOs across the routine concentration range.
- No reliable accuracy estimation method was included in the QA program. One should be developed for future surveys.

**[[Calcium]]**
- Routine sample data are of acceptable quality.
- Background contamination reached as much as 0.07 mg/L. This is likely related to high routine concentrations (mean 3.7 mg/L) and carryover. The contamination is considered too slight to affect population estimates.
- Relative analytical bias was 4 to 8 percent between the two analytical laboratories. Field audit samples showed an 8 percent bias. One laboratory consistently read higher, so the analyzing laboratory should be tracked by subregion.
- Accuracy at a theoretical concentration of 0.19 mg/L favored one laboratory. This was based on a single concentration level and should not be extrapolated across the full range.

**[[Dissolved Organic Carbon]]**
- Data are of acceptable quality.
- Background concentrations were generally 0.05 to 0.35 mg/L, against a required detection limit of 0.1 mg/L.
- Precision for field duplicate pairs above the quantitation limit met the DQO, except for two pairs. Many QA samples exceeded the DQO. Routine concentrations were generally low, so precision may still indicate high-quality data.
- Accuracy was within acceptable limits.

**[[Extractable Aluminum]]**
- All detectability data met DQOs, and contamination was not a significant factor.
- Of 210 field duplicate pairs, 2 had mean concentrations above 0.04 mg/L. One of six audit sample lots had a mean above 0.01 mg/L.
- Accuracy could not be estimated because the extractable Al species were unstable in the synthetic audit solution. Alternative methods are recommended.

**[[Closed-System pH]]**
- Field laboratory (closed-system) pH data are of high quality and can be used confidently.
- Field blanks were not analyzed, so background contamination could not be assessed.
- Trailer duplicate precision was 0.03 pH unit, meeting the DQO. Pooled precision across five field laboratories was 0.12 pH unit. Field audit samples showed precision near the DQO.
- No mechanism for estimating closed-system pH accuracy was provided. One should be developed.

**[[Sulfate]]**
- Routine data are of acceptable quality for population estimates.
- Background contamination was 0.02 mg/L above the required detection limit.
- Precision and accuracy met DQOs.
- Relative interlaboratory bias was 2 percent from field audit samples and 5.5 percent from the calibration study. Because the biases are relative, results should be assessed by the subregions each laboratory analyzed.

**Contamination and blanks**
- Background contamination from analytical laboratories was negligible based on calibration and reagent blanks.
- Helicopter and ground crews had similar success in limiting contamination of lake samples.
- DQOs were not set for field and trailer blanks before the survey. These should be developed for future surveys.
- Matrix spike results showed minimal, if any, matrix interference with routine analysis.

**Recommendations for future surveys**
- Refine data quality objectives and sampling design to account for lake-water variability, analyte concentration differences, and ionic strength differences.
- Develop accuracy estimation methods for ANC and closed-system pH.
- Set DQOs for field and trailer blanks.
- Consider alternative methods for extractable aluminum.
- Track which analytical laboratory processed samples from each subregion.
