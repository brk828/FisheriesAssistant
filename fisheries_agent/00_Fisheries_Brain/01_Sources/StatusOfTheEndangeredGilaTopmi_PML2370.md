# Status of the Endangered Gila Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) in the United States in 1987

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report by Lee H. Simons (Arizona Game and Fish Department) to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] reviews the status of the [[Gila Topminnow]], one of two Sonoran topminnow subspecies in the United States, in 1987, about six years after intensive recovery actions began. The subspecies was listed as endangered in 1967. Its decline is attributed to habitat loss and predation by [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and other exotic fishes. The report documents 35 surviving introduced populations within historic range and 10 natural populations, and concludes that the subspecies meets the 1983 Recovery Plan criteria for downlisting to threatened status. It cautions, however, that many populations are fragile, depend on human support, and that genetic diversity is critically low.

## Background & Description

- **Subspecies in the US:** The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) includes the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*P. o. sonoriensis*), native to the Yaqui River drainage, and the Gila Topminnow (*P. o. occidentalis*), native to the southern Colorado River drainage. Mexican populations were excluded.
- **Listing and decline:** Listed as endangered in 1967. Two causes are generally cited: (1) habitat modification and loss, and (2) predation by exotic fishes, especially mosquitofish.
- **Regulatory framework:** A 1981 memorandum of understanding among the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Forest Service, and Arizona Game and Fish Department set guidelines for introductions on Forest Service lands and for monitoring and downlisting/delisting. The 1983 [[Recovery Plan]] formalized these criteria.
- **Recovery criteria (1983 Plan):**
  - Downlist when 20 populations are successfully reestablished in the wild within historic range, persisting for at least 3 years.
  - Delist (before 1987 framework) when 50% of existing natural populations are secured, or 50 populations are established for at least 3 years, or 30 populations for at least 5 years.
  - After 1987, delist when 50 populations persist 3 years, or 30 populations persist 5 years.
- **Key definitions:** "Introduction" means stocking into free-living habitat, excluding aquaria and temporary hatchery facilities. "Natural population" means one existing in its historic location, free of known anthropogenic mixing. Terms such as "successfully reestablished" and "in the wild" were undefined in the Recovery Plan, which the report identifies as a management problem.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats:** Mosquitofish predation is the dominant threat to natural populations. Drought, flooding, drying of stock tanks, low water, cattle trampling, and erosion of artificial barriers threaten introduced populations. Some introduced sites are also affected by [[Red Shiner]] invasion, goldfish, and [[Largemouth Bass]] and sunfish.
- **Habitat dependence:** Many introduced populations occur in cement watering troughs, artesian wells, stock tanks, and pools that require human maintenance or persist only while artificial barriers last.
- **Study area groupings:** Natural populations are grouped into four geographic areas: Bylas, San Rafael Valley, Sonoita Creek, and Cienega Creek. Redrock Canyon is discussed separately due to its mosquitofish invasion.
- **Notable sites:** [[Monkey Spring]], [[Sharp Spring]], [[Sheehy Spring]], [[Bylas Spring]], [[Middle Spring]], [[Patagonia Lake]] (Sonoita Creek), [[Redrock Canyon]], and [[Salt Creek]], which was lost to red shiner and mosquitofish invasion between 1980 and 1984 and later restocked.

## Key Findings

**Introduced populations**
- 99 documented stockings have occurred within historic range since 1982 (Appendix A). Before 1982, 92 documented introductions were made, plus an unknown number of undocumented ones.
- 191 known introductions have been attempted overall, and 99 since 1982; 28 of the 99 introductions since 1982 persisted in 1987 (Appendix A, B).
- In fall 1987, **35 introduced populations** existed within historic range. Of these, 30 persisted at least 3 years and 23 persisted at least 5 years.
- Of the 35, **10 are Category 1**, 17 are Category 2, 6 are Category 3, and 2 are Category 4. Thus 27 populations (Categories 1 and 2) are free of mosquitofish and immediate threats, and only 10 (Category 1) should persist without human support.
- Seventeen Category 2 populations depend on pumped groundwater, impounded water, or other anthropogenic support.
- Table 2 lists 15 introduced populations that failed between 1985 and 1987, mainly due to drying or low water.
- Six additional "dispersal" populations exist, five from introduction sites and one from an unknown source. These are not counted toward Recovery Plan goals.

**Natural populations**
- Ten natural populations persist in 1987. Four are free of exotic fishes, while six are threatened by mosquitofish. Mosquitofish comprised 92% of poeciliids at one site, and Sheehy and Sharp Springs were nearly extinct.
- Natural populations at Sharp Spring and Sheehy Spring have coexisted with mosquitofish since at least 1979.
- In Redrock Canyon, mosquitofish and topminnow coexisted across about a 2 km zone in 1982, with mosquitofish above and topminnow below. Isolated topminnow sites in the canyon lacked mosquitofish in 1987.
- Six of the remaining natural populations exist on private land with no formal protection.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The 1983 downlisting criteria were met in 1985 and continue to be met in 1987, so the report supports a proposal to downlist the subspecies to threatened status.
- Long-term viability of many introduced populations is "tenuous," and persistence is not assured even in natural systems. Drought-related losses (for example, after a mild drought in 1982) demonstrate this risk.
- Genetic diversity is critically low: all but two introductions since 1981 derive from a single lineage (Monkey Spring). Future introductions should use other sources, such as the more heterogeneous [[Sharp Spring]] stock now held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Recommended actions include continued monitoring, further introductions from natural sources into mosquitofish-free habitat (at least one population per geographic area), mosquitofish control, protection of private lands through purchase or conservation easements, and renovation of sites.
- The report warns against losing progress after downlisting, citing the [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Woundfin]] as species in greater peril that may receive higher priority.
