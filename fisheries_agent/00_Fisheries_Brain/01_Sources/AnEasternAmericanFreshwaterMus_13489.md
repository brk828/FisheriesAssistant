# Eastern American Freshwater Mussel *Anodonta* Introduced Into Arizona

#malacology #freshwater-biology #unionidae

## Executive Summary

This short note by [[Dwight W Taylor]] reports an eastern North American freshwater mussel of the genus [[Anodonta]] found in Arizona, far outside its native range. Three recently dead, juvenile paired valves were collected from [[Lake Mary]] near [[Flagstaff]] in 1955. Their features place them in the eastern subgenus [[Pyganodon]], and [[Henry Van Der Schalie]] tentatively identified them as [[Anodonta Corpulenta]] or a close relative. The author argues that the most plausible route of introduction is larval glochidia carried on transplanted fishes, making this the only known case of a mussel introduced alongside fishes. The occurrence also shows that mussel distribution depends on both present biological factors and the long-term continuity of stream drainages.

## Background & Description

- **Material examined:** Three conspicuously different juvenile specimens from the western American *Anodonta* collection of the [[California Academy Of Sciences]], San Francisco. All are paired valves, collected recently dead, catalogued as CAS 38846.
- **Diagnostic features:** Inflated beaks and double-looped beak sculpture with nodes on the postero-ventral angles of the loops. These match the subgenus *Pyganodon*, which is native only to North America east of the continental divide. The few western forms, in subgenus *Anodonta* (s.s.), have flatter beaks and more subdued sculpture.

- **Taxonomic caution:** The author notes that specimens this young are hard to identify in such a variable genus. The determination is therefore tentative.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Mary:** A long, narrow, shallow lake, partly created by artificial dams. Seasonal fluctuation had left the *Anodonta* stranded on the shore.
- **Native range of the candidate species:** According to [[E C Baker]] (1928), *A. corpulenta* is widespread in the [[Missouri River]] and [[Mississippi River]] drainages. A known natural host of its [[Glochidia]] (larval stage) is the [[Skipjack Herring]] (*[[Alosa Chrysochloris]]*). Host specificity for most mussels is poorly known, so the author cannot conclude that this *Anodonta* uses only that fish.
- **Pathway of introduction:** The author considers larval transport on fishes the only plausible explanation. Many eastern North American fishes have been introduced into Arizona, including around Flagstaff ([[Miller And Lowe 1964]]). No single introduction can be identified, because the host range is unknown and the established host is unlikely to be the responsible fish.
- **Passive dispersal:** Despite thousands of intentional fish transplants from the east to the west, this is the only recorded case of a mussel introduced along with fishes. The author takes this as evidence that [[Passive Dispersal]] alone cannot explain much of mussel distribution.
- **Biogeographic link:** Mussel distribution correlates with present or former [[Stream Drainage]] patterns. The complex life cycle of mussels, which depends on fish hosts, adds to this pattern.

## Key Findings

1. **Range extension:** A subgenus *Pyganodon* (eastern) *Anodonta* is recorded from Arizona, extending the known Pacific Coast drainage fauna of mussels.
2. **Probable identity:** Specimens are referable to *Anodonta corpulenta* or a closely related species, based on [[Henry Van Der Schalie]]'s determination, which the author considers provisional for juveniles.
3. **Introduction mechanism:** Introduction on fish hosts, as glochidia, is the most plausible explanation. The specific fish vector cannot be identified.
4. **Uniqueness:** This is the only documented instance of a mussel introduced with transplanted fishes, among thousands of fish transplants.
5. **Current status:** Only three dead valves were found. Whether a living population persists is unknown. Further survey is needed.
6. **General principle:** Mussel distribution reflects both present-day biological factors and the historical continuity of drainage systems over geologic time. The author says this view, previously supported by [[Henry Van Der Schalie]] (1945, 1963) from eastern U.S. studies, applies more broadly.
