# Letter from Peter B. Moyle to Walter R. Courtenay, Jr. on Exotic Fishes of Southern California

## Executive Summary
This 1991 letter from ichthyologist [[Peter B. Moyle]] to [[Walter R. Courtenay Jr]] documents an effort to catalog exotic (non-native) fish species established in Southern California for an updated edition of *Inland Fishes of California*. Moyle lists nine candidate exotic species — spanning [[Poeciliidae]] livebearers, [[Cichlidae]] tilapias, and the [[Oriental Weatherfish]] — compiled from Courtenay's records, and requests confirmation of established status, distributional records, life history data, and illustrations. The letter also notes a significant ecological development: the rapid freshwater invasion of the [[Chameleon Goby]] into the Sacramento–San Joaquin Estuary and southward through the California aqueduct/canal system to Pyramid Reservoir.

## Background & Description
The correspondence is a professional exchange between two leading authorities on introduced fishes in the United States — [[Peter B. Moyle]] (University of California, Davis) and [[Walter R. Courtenay Jr]] (Florida Atlantic University) — occurring in the context of ongoing documentation of non-native fish establishment in California. Moyle references Courtenay's data compilations as the primary source for a list of exotic species believed to occur in Southern California drainages, including irrigation systems in the Coachella, Imperial, and Palo Verde Valleys, Orange County flood control channels, and the Colorado River.

Species discussed:

1. **Shortfin Molly** (*Poecilia mexicana*) — drainage canal south of Mecca, Riverside Co.
2. **Blue Tilapia** (*Tilapia aurea*, now *Oreochromis aureus*) — Colorado River, inferred from Arizona collection records.
3. **Mozambique Tilapia** (*Oreochromis mossambicus*) — widespread; notably introduced into High Rock Spring, Lassen Co.
4. **Redbelly Tilapia** (*Tilapia zillii*) — irrigation ditches of Coachella, Imperial, and Palo Verde Valleys.
5. **Oriental Weatherfish** (*Misgurnus anguillicaudatus*) — Orange County flood control channels.
6. **Porthole Livebearer** (*Poeciliopsis gracilis*) — same distribution as *P. mexicana*.
7. **Green Swordtail** (*Xiphophorus helleri*) — establishment status uncertain.
8. **Guppy** (*Poecilia reticulata*) — establishment in Southern California unconfirmed.
9. **Wami Tilapia** (*Tilapia hornorum*) — possibly limited to Bolsa Chica; historically used in mosquito abatement programs.

Moyle also raises a taxonomic question regarding the use of tilapia generic names (*Tilapia* vs. *Oreochromis*) and requests the official American Fisheries Society ([[AFS]]) position on nomenclature, as well as a proposed identification key to distinguish the four tilapia species present in California.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The species discussed occupy a range of anthropogenically modified freshwater habitats characteristic of introduced fish establishment: agricultural drainage canals, irrigation ditches, urban flood control channels, and isolated spring systems. These habitats in the arid Southern California landscape (Coachella, Imperial, Palo Verde Valleys) and the Colorado River corridor provide thermally stable, low-competition environments favorable to warm-water exotic species such as tilapiine cichlids and poeciliids.

A key ecological concern raised is the documented negative impact of introduced [[Mozambique Tilapia]] on native fish communities: its introduction into **High Rock Spring** (Lassen Co.) is associated with the **extinction of the native tui chub** population there — an example of exotic species-driven displacement of native California fishes.

Separately, the letter documents a major invasion event: the [[Chameleon Goby]] (*Tridentiger trigonocephalus*), originally a brackish/estuarine species, made a rapid transition into freshwater habitats within a five-year period preceding 1991. It became one of the most abundant fish species in the **Sacramento–San Joaquin Estuary** and dispersed through the interconnected canal/aqueduct system as far south as **Pyramid Reservoir** in Southern California — illustrating the potential for estuarine exotics to colonize freshwater conveyance systems over broad geographic ranges.

## Key Findings
- Nine exotic fish species (spanning [[Poeciliidae]] and [[Cichlidae]]) were flagged as likely established or present in Southern California drainages as of 1991, based on compiled field records.
- **Mozambique Tilapia** introduction is directly linked to the extirpation of the native **tui chub** population in High Rock Spring, Lassen Co. — a documented case of exotic-driven native extinction.
- Distribution of **Redbelly Tilapia** is confirmed in irrigation systems of the Coachella, Imperial, and Palo Verde Valleys, with uncertainty about additional extant populations.
- Establishment status of **Green Swordtail** and **Guppy** in Southern California remained unconfirmed at time of writing, highlighting gaps in exotic species monitoring.
- **Wami Tilapia** presence may be limited to a single population (Bolsa Chica), potentially maintained through historical mosquito abatement stocking practices.
- Taxonomic ambiguity existed regarding tilapia genus assignments (*Tilapia* vs. *Oreochromis*), reflecting a period of active systematic revision in cichlid nomenclature.
- The **Chameleon Goby**, an estuarine/brackish-water species, underwent a rapid (≤5-year) range expansion into freshwater habitat, becoming dominant in the Sacramento–San Joaquin Estuary and spreading via canal systems to Pyramid Reservoir — demonstrating high invasive potential and rapid habitat-shifting capability in interconnected water conveyance infrastructure.
- The letter underscores reliance on inter-institutional data sharing (Courtenay, St. Amant, Horn, Minckley) for compiling accurate exotic species distribution records in the absence of centralized monitoring.
