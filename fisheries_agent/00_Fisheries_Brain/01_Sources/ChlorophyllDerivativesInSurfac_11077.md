## Executive Summary

This study by Eville Gorham investigates the abundance and character of chlorophyll degradation products (chlorophyll derivatives/pheophytin) in surface muds from five English lakes of contrasting trophic status. Alongside chlorophyll derivatives, total carbon and total sulfur were measured in mud samples to test whether sediment organic chemistry tracks lake fertility. All three variables — chlorophyll derivatives, total carbon, and total sulfur — increased systematically from oligotrophic, deep, lime-poor lakes (e.g., [[Wastwater]], [[Ennerdale Water]]) to small, shallow, nutrient-rich, phytoplankton-dense water bodies (e.g., [[Esthwaite Water]], [[Priest Pot]]). Chlorophyll derivatives showed the most dramatic (>30-fold) increase across this trophic gradient, suggesting they may serve as a more sensitive proxy for lake fertility than bulk carbon or sulfur content. Absorption spectroscopy of acetone extracts further revealed differences attributable to the relative contributions of allochthonous (terrestrial) versus autochthonous (planktonic) organic matter, and to the duration of exposure to oxidizing conditions before burial.

## Background & Description

While this is not a fish-focused study, the paper does report on the pigment/spectral "morphology" of chlorophyll degradation products derived from a key primary producer:

- **Diatom studied**: *Melosira italica subarctica* (planktonic diatom), used as a reference for living vs. dead algal chlorophyll content.
  - **Living cells**: absorption spectrum showed a major peak near **435 nm**, with a minor hump at **620 nm**.
  - **Dead cells** (after centrifuging and standing 3–4 days): major peak shifted back to **~410 nm**; minor hump shifted to **605 nm**; two new minor humps appeared at **530 nm** and **505 nm**; marked decrease in absorption between 490–530 nm. This shifted spectrum closely resembled that of **pheophytin a**, identified by Orr and Grady (1957) as the dominant chlorophyll derivative in marine sediments.
  - Chlorophyll content of living *Melosira*: ~250 units/g carbon, declining to roughly half that value after death and short-term degradation.

- **Terrestrial plant leaves** (from three land plant species) yielded 19–55 units of chlorophyll per gram of carbon; this declined further (e.g., to 21 units) after grinding and moist storage for eight days, indicating rapid post-mortem pigment degradation analogous to processes in aquatic systems.

- **[[Asterionella]]** populations (observed by J. F. Talling, cited as personal communication): populations collected below the thermocline after stratification showed a shift of the major absorption peak (~435 nm) to lower wavelengths, indicating in-situ chlorophyll degradation prior to sedimentation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study frames chlorophyll derivatives as tracers of the trophic and biogeochemical "niche" of each lake system:

- **Oligotrophic lakes** ([[Wastwater]], [[Ennerdale Water]]): deep, well-oxygenated hypolimnia throughout stratification; low lime and potash; sparse phytoplankton. Sediment organic matter here appears to be substantially **allochthonous**, resembling the spectral signature of aerobic woodland mull/mor humus — implying significant terrestrial (catchment-derived) organic input.

- **Mesotrophic lake** ([[Windermere]], North Basin): large, deep lake with oxygenated hypolimnion; moderate lime and phytoplankton levels — intermediate in all measured sediment parameters.

- **Eutrophic lake** ([[Esthwaite Water]]): shallower, with hypolimnetic deoxygenation near the mud surface; higher lime/potash and larger phytoplankton crops. Sediment traps at 1 m and 11 m depth captured settling debris with much higher chlorophyll-derivative concentrations (75–80 units/g carbon) than surface mud (12 units/g carbon), implying substantial pigment degradation occurs in the water column and/or at the sediment-water interface before permanent burial.

- **Hypereutrophic pond** ([[Priest Pot]]): very small and shallow, with the richest water chemistry (lime, potash) and densest phytoplankton (necessitating Buchner filtration for water clarification). This site showed the highest concentrations of all three sediment variables (carbon, sulfur, chlorophyll derivatives), consistent with abundant **autochthonous** primary production and a thin oxidized microzone at the mud surface, limiting oxidative pigment breakdown before burial.

- **Redox and burial dynamics**: Thicker oxidized surface microzones in infertile lakes prolong exposure of settling organic material to oxidation (per [[Mortimer 1942]]), promoting greater chlorophyll degradation before burial — a key ecological control layered onto the trophic-status gradient.

## Key Findings

1. **Trophic gradient in sediment chemistry**: Across five lakes ranging from oligotrophic to hypereutrophic, total carbon (%), total sulfur (%), and chlorophyll derivatives (units/g dry weight) in surface muds all increase in parallel with lake fertility (phytoplankton abundance, lime/potash concentration).

2. **Relative sensitivity of proxies**: From the least to most fertile lake, total carbon increased ~3-fold, total sulfur increased ~7-fold, but chlorophyll derivatives increased **>30-fold** — suggesting chlorophyll derivatives are the most sensitive sedimentary indicator of lake trophic status among those tested.

3. **Sulfur:Carbon and Chlorophyll:Carbon ratios**: Sulfur content rose from <3% to >6% of carbon between infertile and fertile lake muds; chlorophyll derivatives rose from ~3 units/g carbon (Wastwater, Ennerdale) to 36 units/g carbon (Priest Pot).

4. **Living vs. sedimented vs. buried pigment comparison**: Chlorophyll content declines progressively from living algae (~250 units/g C) → dead algae (~half that) → sediment trap debris (75–80 units/g C in Esthwaite) → surface mud (12 units/g C in Esthwaite), documenting a degradation cascade from production to permanent burial.

5. **Spectral signature of degradation**: Conversion of chlorophyll to pheophytin a is marked by a shift of the primary absorption peak from ~435 nm to ~410 nm and altered minor peak structure; this transformation reduces overall light absorption by ~30% for the chlorophyll→pheophytin conversion alone, but empirically observed declines (50% in *Melosira*, >60% in oak leaves) imply additional degradation processes beyond simple pheophytinization.

6. **Optical density ratio (410:350 nm) as a fertility index**: This ratio rose steadily from 0.7 in Wastwater (infertile) to 1.8 in Priest Pot (hypereutrophic), with even higher ratios in living/dead *Melosira* (2.9/2.3) and sediment trap material (2.0–2.1), reflecting differing proportions of allochthonous vs. autochthonous organic matter and degradation states.

7. **Allochthonous vs. autochthonous organic matter inference**: The spectral resemblance between Wastwater mud extracts and aerobic woodland humus extracts supports a substantial terrestrial (catchment) contribution to sediment organic matter in oligotrophic lakes, contrasting with the predominantly autochthonous (phytoplankton-derived) inputs in eutrophic systems like Esthwaite Water and Priest Pot.

8. **Proposed controlling factors** for sedimentary chlorophyll/carbon/sulfur concentrations, identified as priorities for future research:
   - Initial pigment content of source organisms
   - Rate of sedimentation and burial
   - Concurrent mineral sedimentation rates and composition
   - Degree of bacterial activity and associated oxygen dynamics in the lake and its muds
