# Intestinal Cestode of the Colorado River Squawfish, Ptychocheilus lucius

#ichthyology #parasitology #cyprinidae #cestoda #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1972 Master's thesis reports the first identification of the tapeworm *Ophiotaenia fragilus* from the [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a native cyprinid that has experienced dramatic population decline over 30 years. Thirty-nine cestode specimens were recovered from the hindgut of one immature male squawfish (160 mm standard length) collected from [[Lake Powell]], Arizona in 1967. The parasite was previously known only from the introduced [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). The study provides detailed morphological description of the cestode and reviews the life history of the host fish, raising questions about whether non-native parasites introduced with exotic fish species may contribute to the squawfish's decline.

## Background & Description

### Host Species: Colorado River Squawfish

[[Ptychocheilus lucius]] is the largest native cyprinid in North America, historically reaching lengths of five feet and weights exceeding 80 pounds, though recent specimens typically weigh 4-15 pounds and measure 2-3 feet. The species is endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system of western North America and is locally known as "Colorado River salmon" or "white salmon." The genus *Ptychocheilus* includes three other species: *P. grandis* (Sacramento River), *P. oregonensis* (Columbia River), and *P. umpqua* (Oregon coastal streams).

Morphologically, the squawfish has an elongate body with a large, nearly horizontal mouth, long cone-shaped head, and narrow elongate lower limb of the pharyngeal arch. Dorsal and anal fin rays typically number 9, with 80-95 scales in the lateral line. Pharyngeal teeth are arranged 2,5-4,2 and are frail, elongate, and hooked. Coloration is olive-green dorsally, yellowish on lower sides, and nearly white ventrally in adults. Young possess a dark wedge-shaped spot at the caudal fin base.

### Parasite: Ophiotaenia fragilus

The cestode belongs to class [[Cestoda]], order [[Proteocephalidea]], family [[Proteocephalidae]], genus *Ophiotaenia*. Specimens ranged 40-120 mm in length with distinct segmentation. Only one of 39 recovered specimens was mature. The scolex is globose (0.8-1.3 mm) with four round suckers, each situated in a fleshy prominence, with no rostellum or hooks. The neck measures 6-8 mm in length and 0.5-0.7 mm in width.

Mature proglottids are nearly square (approximately 1 mm²) with prominent lateral bulges at genital pore openings. Testes number 120-210, arranged in two lateral medullary fields that coalesce anteriorly. The cirrus pouch measures 0.2-0.3 mm × 0.1-0.17 mm. The bilobed ovary is M-shaped in the posterior segment region. Follicular vitellaria extend in two lateral columns. The uterus in mature proglottids occupies most of the medullary parenchyma with 6-10 lateral diverticula (compared to 12-20 reported by Essex 1929).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat and Distribution

[[Colorado River Squawfish]] inhabit large rivers with strong currents, muddy waters, and bottoms of stone, mud, and sand. Adults typically occupy pools and eddies, though some are found in runs with slow to moderate current. Young squawfish inhabit shallow pools near shore with slight or absent current, over gravel or small rubble bottoms. Adults generally lie in deep holes below riffles but move closer to shore when water levels rise.

Vanicek and Kramer (1969) collected 1,469 squawfish from the [[Green River]] (1964-1966), ranging 15-610 mm in length, with most between 15-100 mm and only 27 exceeding 400 mm, indicating severe population decline from historical sizes.

### Feeding Ecology

The squawfish is carnivorous, indicated by its large size, mouth, and head. Despite weak pharyngeal teeth for a piscivorous minnow, sharp jaw edges compensate. Diet varies ontogenetically:
- Fish <50 mm: [[Cladocerans]], [[Copepods]], and [[Chironomid]] larvae
- Fish 51-100 mm: Insects become important
- Fish >100 mm: Fish increasingly important
- Fish >200 mm: Fish exclusively

Stomach content analysis of 198 specimens confirmed this dietary progression.

### Reproduction

Historical accounts describe spawning migrations up major rivers and tributaries in spring. Vanicek and Kramer documented gravid females and ripe males from late June through early August (1964-1965). Water temperature of 65°F appears to initiate spawning, with receding water levels as an additional stimulus. In 1966, earlier warming resulted in spawning occurring one month earlier than previous years. Young-of-the-year length-frequency analyses confirmed spawning timing.

### Population Decline

The species has suffered dramatic population decline over 30 years (as of 1972), now considered a zoological rarity. Environmental modifications to the [[Colorado River]] system and introduction of exotic species are implicated factors.

## Key Findings

### Parasite Identification and Morphology

1. **First Report**: *Ophiotaenia fragilus* identified from [[Colorado River Squawfish]] for the first time; previously known only from [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), an introduced species.

2. **Collection Data**: 39 tapeworms recovered from hindgut of one immature male squawfish (160 mm standard length) from [[Lake Powell]], Arizona (November 1, 1967).

3. **Morphological Comparison**: Measurements differ somewhat from Essex's (1929) original description from channel catfish:
   - Length: 40-120 mm (vs. 45-80 mm)
   - Scolex: 0.8-1.3 mm (vs. 0.50 mm)
   - Testes: 120-210 (vs. 150-230)
   - Uterine diverticula: 12-20 (vs. 14-24)

4. **Taxonomic Placement**: Identified using keys of Hoffman (1967), Wardle and McLeod (1952), and LaRue (1911). Placed in species-group VII, one of only two *Ophiotaenia* species reported from fishes.

### Life History Inference

Based on related proteocephalid species, the presumed life cycle involves:

1. **First Intermediate Host**: Crustacean (likely [[Copepods]] such as *Cyclops* spp.) ingests eggs; oncosphere develops into procercoid larva in haemocoel (8-15 days).

2. **Second Intermediate Host**: Aquatic vertebrate (fish) consumes infected crustacean; procercoid penetrates gut wall, encysts in body cavity or tissues, develops into plerocercoid larva.

3. **Definitive Host**: Piscivorous fish (squawfish or catfish) consumes infected fish; plerocercoid released during digestion, develops to adult in intestine.

Supporting evidence from related species:
- *Ophiotaenia perspicua*: Procercoids in *Cyclops viridis* (Herde 1928), plerocercoids in fish tissues
- *Proteocephalus ambloplites*: Two intermediate hosts required (Hunter and Hunter 1929)
- *Corallobothrium parvum*: Procercoids in *Cyclops prasinus*, plerocercoids in small fish (Larsh 1941)

### Conservation and Parasitology Implications

1. **Introduced Parasite Hypothesis**: *O. fragilus* may have been introduced to the [[Colorado River]] system with [[Channel Catfish]] in early 1900s. The bass tapeworm (*Proteocephalus ambloplites*) was also found in 65% of squawfish >200 mm from [[Green River]], Utah (Vanicek and Kramer 1969), likely introduced with centrarchids.

2. **Potential Impact on Decline**: Heavy non-native parasite infections may contribute to squawfish population decline. Newly introduced parasites established with exotic fish may be more virulent in the vulnerable native host.

3. **Environmental Stress**: Modified river conditions may have reduced squawfish ability to cope with parasitic infections.

4. **Research Priority**: Urgent need to define native parasite fauna before further species introductions and environmental changes occur. Only one previous cestode report existed for this host species prior to this study.

5. **Methodological Limitations**: Insufficient material (only one host specimen, one mature parasite) prevented detailed anatomical study and optimal preparation technique determination. Variations in measurements attributed to: age of parasite, host species differences, geographical separation, environmental conditions, and preparation methods.

6. **Comparative Parasitology**: Other *Ptychocheilus* species harbor different cestodes: *P. grandis* hosts *Proteocephalus cobraeformis*; *P. oregonensis* hosts *Eubothrium salvelini*, *Ligula intestinalis* (larvae), and *Proteocephalus ptychocheilus* (Hoffman 1967).
