# Reintroduction of Native Fish into the Bill Williams River

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary
This 1993 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum documents a native fish translocation effort from [[Burro Creek]] to the [[Bill Williams River]] (Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge, Arizona) as part of a native fish reintroduction/recovery program. Field crews seined fish from Burro Creek during early morning hours (0630–0800 hrs) on 22 July 1993, collecting both native and introduced species. A total of 1,041 native fish — [[Longfin Dace]] (563), [[Roundtail Chub]] (384), and [[Sonora Sucker]] (94) — were transported in salinized water (5 ppt) via Gott coolers and released into an isolated, pre-cleared side channel of the Bill Williams River, approximately three miles upstream of the river bridge. The operation illustrates a practical desert-fish conservation methodology combining donor-population sampling, exotic species exclusion, and staged habitat reconnection.

## Background & Description
Size-class data were recorded as total length (TL) ranges, indicating the collected natives were predominantly young-of-year (YOY) or one-year-old cohorts:

- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*): TL 40–80 mm; most abundant native species; comprised current-year spawn plus one-year-old fish.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*): TL up to 90 mm; entirely 1993-cohort (young-of-year) fish; second most abundant native.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*): TL 40–110 mm; also entirely 1993-cohort fish; third most abundant native, smallest overall sample size.

Introduced species were present but not described morphologically in detail; relative abundance ranking (most to least abundant) was: [[Red Shiner]] > yellow bullhead (recorded as *Ictalurus punctatus*) > [[Common Carp]] > [[Green Sunfish]] > [[Bluegill]] > [[Fathead Minnow]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Donor site — [[Burro Creek]]**: A desert stream supporting a mixed assemblage of native Sonoran Desert fishes (longfin dace, roundtail chub, Sonora sucker) alongside multiple non-native species (red shiner, carp, fathead minnow, green sunfish, bluegill, and an ictalurid catfish), reflecting the widespread invasion pressure on southwestern U.S. native fish communities.
- **Compositional structure of donor community**: Red shiner dominated the total catch (~50%), followed by longfin dace (~30%) and roundtail chub (~19%); all other exotics combined made up <1%, indicating a native community still persisting but heavily co-occurring with a dominant invasive cyprinid.
- **Recipient site — [[Bill Williams River]]**: An isolated side channel on refuge lands was selectively seined beforehand to remove introduced fish, creating a temporary "native-only" refugial habitat. This side channel was to remain physically disconnected from the main river channel for two weeks post-release, presumably to allow acclimation and reduce immediate re-invasion or downstream drift before reconnection.
- **Translocation methodology**: Fish were held/transported in Gott coolers with river water salinized to 5 ppt (rock salt), a technique likely used to reduce osmotic/handling stress and disease transmission during transport.
- **Voucher specimens**: A subsample of collected fish was preserved and deposited in the fish collection at [[Arizona State University]] for reference/taxonomic verification.

## Key Findings
- **1,041 total native fish** were translocated from Burro Creek to the Bill Williams River in a single operation on 22 July 1993.
- Species and counts transplanted: **563 Longfin Dace**, **384 Roundtail Chub**, **94 Sonora Sucker**.
- All roundtail chub and Sonora sucker collected were young-of-year (1993 cohort), suggesting a recent successful spawning event at Burro Creek and a source population capable of supplying juveniles for reintroduction.
- The donor stream (Burro Creek) hosts a heavily invaded fish community, with the [[Red Shiner]] alone representing half of total catch — underscoring the invasive threat to native desert fish and the rationale for active reintroduction efforts elsewhere.
- Managers employed active exotic-species exclusion (pre-seining) at the release site and a staged (two-week delayed) reconnection strategy to improve establishment odds for translocated natives before merging with the main river channel.
- This effort exemplifies a broader regional strategy for [[Native Fish Conservation]] in the Lower Colorado River Basin, using non-native-free refugial habitats within larger rivers as a tool against the ongoing decline of Sonoran Desert native fish assemblages.
