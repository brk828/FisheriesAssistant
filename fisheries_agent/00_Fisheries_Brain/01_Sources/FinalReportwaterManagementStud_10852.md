# Water Management Study: Upper Rio Grande Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission]] (July 1997), prepared by Ernie Niemi (ECONorthwest) and Tom McGuckin (New Mexico State University), assesses competition for scarce water and related resources across the Upper Rio Grande Basin, from the Colorado headwaters to Ft. Quitman, Texas. The authors define a "problem" as a failure to use the Basin's water and related resources optimally against three criteria: highest net value, highest standard of living, and perceived fairness. The study makes recommendations only for federal policies, agencies and activities; it explicitly makes none regarding the [[Rio Grande Compact]], state and local law, resource-owner rights, the merits of disputants' claims, or specific resource uses.

The authors identify two "bottom-line" problems: resources are finite while demands are not, and resources are persistently allocated in a less-than-ideal manner. Seven contributory problems are described, including the lack of ecosystem-based management, widespread hydrologic and ecological uncertainty, and pervasive distrust among stakeholders. Three recommendations follow: federal policy should treat water as part of an ecosystem in a complex economy; federal agencies should mitigate anticompetitive factors; and federal interests in the Basin should be clarified.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The Basin spans about 1,000 miles and has a semi-arid to arid climate. Most of the Basin receives 7–15 inches of precipitation annually, and about half arrives as high-mountain snowfall with the rest from intense summer thunderstorms. Major population centers are [[Albuquerque]] (about 650,000) and [[El Paso]] (about 650,000), with [[Ciudad Juarez]] (more than 1.5 million) across the border.

**Water budget.** The Colorado portion produces about 975,000 acre-feet (af) per year, and only 325,000 af reach the Colorado–New Mexico border. New Mexico streamflows add about 650,000 af, and about 100,000 af are imported from the San Juan Basin via the [[San Juan-Chama Project]]. About 700,000 af/yr reaches [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]], and the U.S. must deliver 60,000 af/yr to Mexico under the 1906 treaty. Agriculture accounts for about 89% of major water uses (the report also cites about 80% in other sections).

**History.** Pueblo irrigation predates Spanish colonization. The [[Follett Report]] (1896) documented demand exceeding supply, leading to the 1896 embargo. Prior appropriation was adopted in New Mexico (1898) and Colorado (1872 law). The [[Reclamation Act]] (1902) enabled federal projects, including Elephant Butte Dam (completed 1916). The [[Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District]] (formed 1925) and the 1938 Rio Grande Compact shaped current allocations, which reflect the economy of the late 1920s.

**Legal and institutional framework.** Each state applies prior appropriation differently. Colorado recognizes instream flow rights, but these are generally junior and offer little protection in dry years. New Mexico does not recognize instream flows as a beneficial use. Texas has several exceptions to prior appropriation. Pueblo water rights, based on the [[Reserved Rights Doctrine]], remain largely unadjudicated, and federal rights to Rio Grande Project water are ambiguous.

**Physical modification.** Dams, levees, channelization and diversions have transformed the river. The reach below Elephant Butte Dam is largely a network of canals, and 71% of native fish species there no longer occur. The [[Middle Rio Grande Bosque]], the longest continuous cottonwood forest in the American Southwest, is now largely disconnected from the river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish and aquatic fauna.** The [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] was listed as endangered in 1994 by the [[U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service]]. It is the sole survivor of a guild of five similar species once present in the Rio Grande, and half the original Middle Rio Grande fish fauna has disappeared. A 1996 drought-period diversion that dried the river bed killed about 40% of the minnow population, according to Fish & Wildlife Service estimates.

**Riparian and upland systems.** Between 1918 and the 1980s the Middle Rio Grande lost all saltgrass meadow (48,603 acres), nearly half its cottonwood timber and brush (17,498 acres), and saw 17,833 acres become dominated by exotic species. Crawford et al. (1996) conclude the bosque will undergo irreversible change without a new management paradigm. Upland pinyon-juniper ecosystems covering 23 million acres in New Mexico are also degraded.

**Hydrology and climate.** Snowpack in 1995–96 was extremely low. The area experiences a major drought every 20–25 years, and the authors note that a large share of the current population has no memory of earlier droughts. Global warming is expected to add stress; a warming of 7°F, even with an 8% precipitation increase, could reduce Basin farmland prices by $200–450 per acre, and possibly up to $1,000.

**Groundwater.** Albuquerque's aquifer holds far less readily extractable water than once thought; water tables have dropped more than 150 feet at some wells over 30 years. Pumping in the Albuquerque area was about double recharge in recent studies. El Paso drew about 40% of its supply from the aquifer.

**Water quality.** Sediment is high for most of the river's length. Septic systems and hazardous spills affect shallow aquifers, and naturally occurring arsenic exceeds proposed EPA standards in some Albuquerque wells. Downstream of El Paso–Ciudad Juarez, the 1994 binational study identified high potential for toxic chemical impacts on aquatic health.

## Key Findings

**Bottom-Line Problem #1 (finite resources, unlimited demand).** Within the past decade the ecosystem's carrying capacity has become clearer. The minnow listing, the 1995–96 low snowpack, groundwater limits in Albuquerque and El Paso, and projected population growth (New Mexico up about 55% between 1995 and 2025) all signal stress. Albuquerque projects demand of about 300,000 af/yr by 2060 unless per-capita use falls 30%, and demand would still exceed 200,000 af/yr even if that target is met.

**Bottom-Line Problem #2 (persistent misallocation).** Prevailing prices do not reflect scarcity. Federal subsidies mean farm businesses pay perhaps less than 20% of the cost of BuRec water. The study finds the marginal value of irrigation water is zero or near zero: the 1990 USDA analysis by [[Hansen and Hallam]] estimated zero marginal value for irrigation, versus about $100 per af for angling. Booker and Young (1994) found consumptive and nonconsumptive values roughly equal in comparable Colorado analysis.

**Public value of instream flows.** Berrens et al. (1995) found New Mexicans willing to pay $30 per household per year for five years to protect minimum instream flows for the minnow, and $79 to provide minimum flows in all major state rivers. Scaled across 580,000 households, the authors estimate about $17.4 million per year for the Middle Rio Grande area.

**Commodity bias.** Laws and agency operating rules, including BuRec, CoE and MRGCD operations, were largely set in an era of commodity-driven development. Facilities such as [[Cochiti Reservoir]] operate under 1960s authorizations.

**Contributory problems.** The report identifies seven: (1) the Basin has not been managed as an ecosystem; (2) past practices have made water unsuitable for some uses without corrective action; (3) monetized industrial demands are hard to reconcile with non-monetized ones; (4) many groups cannot participate effectively in decisions; (5) hydrologic and ecological uncertainty is widespread; (6) the resource–economy relationship is poorly understood; (7) distrust among stakeholders is pervasive.

**Economic change.** Agriculture remains about 1% of study-area income and 1.7% of employment (2% in 1993 per the table data as presented). Amenities increasingly drive location decisions: [[Greenwood et al. (1991)]] estimated an amenity-related wage differential of 8–13% for New Mexico, roughly $1.3–2.1 billion annually. El Paso's average earnings are about 79% of the U.S. average, with 34.2% of adults lacking a high school diploma in 1990.

**Cooperative efforts.** The report describes the [[New Mexico/Texas Water Commission]] (formed 1991 from settlement of litigation), the [[Rio Grande Alliance]] (1996), New Mexico's regional water planning, the Rio Grande Silvery Minnow "White Paper" strategy (1996), and the Jemez River watershed rotational agreement (1996), which the authors note is the first such priority-call process for affected users in New Mexico history.

**Recommendation #1: Ecosystem and economy.** Federal agencies should (1) promote institutions with a broad view of economy and environment; (2) initiate an integrated scientific assessment of ecological and economic conditions; (3) describe tradeoffs more clearly and set priorities; and (4) communicate ecological and economic issues more clearly.

**Recommendation #2: Mitigate anticompetitive factors.** Agencies should reduce transaction costs by identifying "hotspots," curtail externalities through multi-agency cooperation, and support institutional innovations, including devolution of authority to state and local bodies where federal agencies first specify desired outcomes and accountability mechanisms. The report says Congress should specify economic and ecosystem goals for the Basin. The authors judge the Rio Grande Compact an impediment to interstate competition, but make no recommendation regarding it.

**Recommendation #3: Clarify federal interests.** Five categories are identified: stewardship, corporate, Pueblo trust responsibilities, economic welfare, and public participation. The distribution of risk itself constitutes a federal interest, and each agency should issue a statement of its interest.

**Limitations.** The authors caution that the report's figures should not be used without accounting for more recent data. Several estimates (for example, flow and diversion volumes, and the subsidy figure of more than $50 per af) are general or derived from other BuRec projects, not Basin-specific measurements.
