# Analysis of Factors Associated with Intraspecific Variation in Propagule Size of a Stream-Dwelling Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Heins and Baker examined egg size in the [[Blacktail Shiner]] (*Notropis venustus*) from 16 populations in river systems draining to the northern [[Gulf of Mexico]], from eastern Texas to northwestern Florida. Egg size was unrelated to female size within populations but correlated with female size among populations and among pooled females. No clutch size–egg size trade-off was detected with observed clutch sizes, though a weak trade-off appeared among populations using predicted clutch size. Mean egg size varied significantly among females and among populations, yet individual females produced eggs of consistent size. Egg size was most strongly associated with [[Mean Annual Runoff]], with larger eggs in higher-runoff regions. The authors interpret these results as support for a "no optimum egg size" view and call for genetic and mechanistic follow-up.

## Background & Description

The study belongs to the [[Propagule Size Theory]] literature, which began with [[Smith and Fretwell Model]] (1974), in which offspring fitness rises with per-offspring investment but at a diminishing rate, implying an optimum offspring size. Later work challenged this idea:

- **Capinera (1979)** proposed that the fitness value of propagule size depends on the environment, so the optimum shifts with conditions.
- **Amphibian studies** (Kaplan, 1980; Crump, 1981, 1984; Kaplan and Cooper, 1984) found within- and among-female variation hard to reconcile with a single optimum egg size. Kaplan and Cooper proposed "adaptive coin-flipping," a developmental mechanism producing random egg-size variation.
- **Life-history models**: [[R-K Selection]] theory predicts small propagules when environments are variable or unpredictable; bet-hedging theory predicts small eggs when adult mortality is variable (Stearns, 1976, 1977).
- **Competition and food models** (Svardson, 1949; Williams, 1966; Brockelman, 1975; Wilbur, 1977) link egg size to food supply, predation, and competition.

The species was chosen because it is widespread, eurytopic, and shows some taxonomic differentiation, with three recognized subspecies (Gibbs, 1957). Subspecies were not found to explain any observed variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Streams offer a wide range of physical and chemical conditions, making them suitable for testing how environment shapes propagule size. The study measured drainage area, mean annual discharge, coefficient of variation in discharge, gradient, stream width, runoff, pH, alkalinity, specific conductance, and [[Total Dissolved Solids]] (TDS). Specific conductance and TDS were highly correlated (r = 0.982, N = 16, P < 0.001), so only TDS was retained. Streamflow showed little marked seasonality, so annual values were treated as representative of conditions experienced by eggs, larvae, and fry. The species has a prolonged breeding season (March–October; Heins and Dorsett, 1986).

Sampling sites spanned drainages including the [[Pearl River]], [[Choctawhatchee River]], [[Escatawpa River]], [[Blackwater River]], [[Sabine River]], and [[Trinity River]]. Collections were made within a short window (2–18 June 1984) to limit seasonal effects on egg size. Environmental data came mainly from [[United States Geological Survey]] records, supplemented by Mississippi Bureau of Pollution Control data for the Pearl River. The Blackwater River values were estimated from the similar Styx River watershed.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Female *N. venustus* were collected from 16 populations; up to 10 mature females per site were analyzed (154 females in total, with 7–10 per population).
- Egg diameter was measured to 0.05 mm on 10 ova per female. Clutch size, mean egg weight, and [[Gonadosomatic Index]] (GSI) were also recorded.
- Analyses combined correlation, nested ANOVA, [[Principal Components Analysis]] with varimax rotation, and stepwise and all-possible multiple regression.

**Egg size and life-history traits**
- Mean egg diameter was not correlated with female SL or weight within any population. Egg weight correlated with female weight in only two populations (Choctawhatchee River near Pittman, r = 0.639; Catahoula Creek, r = −0.658).
- Among populations, mean egg size and mean female size were significantly correlated (r = 0.591–0.784, N = 16, P < 0.05). Among pooled females, egg diameter correlated with SL (r = 0.428–0.592, N = 154, P < 0.0001).

**Clutch size**
- Observed clutch size showed no correlation with egg size within populations, among populations, or among pooled females.
- Clutch size regressions on SL were highly significant in 15 of 16 populations (r = 0.857–0.980). Common-slope ANCOVA showed no significant slope differences (F = 0.92; P > 0.05).
- Predicted clutch size at 60 mm SL was significantly correlated with mean egg weight among populations (r = −0.502, N = 16, P < 0.05) but not with egg diameter (r = −0.469, P > 0.05). The authors describe this as a weak trade-off.

**Gonadosomatic index**
- GSI correlated with egg diameter, egg weight, or both in 11 of 16 populations (for example, population 15: r = 0.921 with diameter).
- Among populations, mean egg size was not correlated with mean GSI. Among pooled females, correlations were significant but weak (r = 0.318 and 0.299; r² ≈ 0.10 and 0.09).

**Variation in egg size**
- Egg diameter and weight were highly correlated within 15 of 16 populations (r = 0.925–0.989). The exception was the Pearl River near Coal Bluff (r = −0.024).
- Mean egg diameter differed significantly among females within every population (P < 0.0001) and among populations (F = 19.21; df = 15,138; P < 0.0001).
- Individual females' egg diameter CVs (0.036–0.053) were consistently lower than population CVs (0.060–0.131).
- The smallest and largest population means differed by 38% in diameter, which corresponds to a 292% increase in egg weight because weight scales exponentially with diameter.

**Environmental correlates**
- In the first PCA, runoff loaded strongly on the egg-size component. Runoff correlated with egg diameter (r = 0.824, N = 16, P < 0.05) and egg weight (r = 0.844).
- Stepwise regression identified runoff as the only significant predictor of egg diameter (F = 29.79; df = 1,14; P < 0.0001), explaining 68% of variation.
- Runoff plus mean annual discharge explained only about 4% more. A four-variable model (runoff, discharge, alkalinity, drainage area) explained nearly 78%.
- pH and TDS were negatively correlated with egg size (diameter r = −0.761 and −0.609, respectively).

**Conclusions and interpretation**
- Egg size is most closely tied to runoff, with larger eggs in higher-runoff streams. Higher pH and TDS may slightly offset this effect.
- Larger eggs may be advantageous where streamflow causes density-independent mortality of eggs, larvae, or fry.
- The authors support a "no optimum egg size" hypothesis and predict a genetic basis with "grandfather effects," but they note that the mechanisms remain unverified and that correlation does not establish causation.
