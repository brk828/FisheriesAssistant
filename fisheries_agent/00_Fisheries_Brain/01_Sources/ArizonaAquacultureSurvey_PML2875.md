# Arizona Aquaculture Survey and Market Research (1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #ictaluridae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

A 1987 market research study by [[West Group Marketing Research]] surveyed Arizona fish farms (sixteen interviews, licensed by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]) and Arizona and Southern California seafood brokers. It assessed supply, production capacity, market demand, pricing, transport and consumption. The study concludes that Arizona aquaculture is a real and growing industry, dominated by [[Tilapia]] and [[Channel Catfish]] production, but that producers are not yet marketing effectively. Most Arizona output was unprocessed and had no local buyers, and brokers were largely unaware of Arizona producers. The report recommends coordinated marketing, central processing and a shared marketing plan.

## Background & Description

The survey was commissioned to establish a picture of the current aquaculture market in Arizona: what fish were raised, in what quantities, at what prices, and where they could be sold. It covered five species groups: [[Channel Catfish]], [[Tilapia]], [[Carp]], [[Buffalo Fish]], and [[Crayfish]], with [[Largemouth Bass]] added for Arizona producers.

Of 16 supplier interviews, 13 farms had or were anticipating commercial-size production and 10 were capable of selling in commercial markets. Three were planning to start in 1988, three were non-commercial growers, and one declined to be interviewed. Demand-side work covered 10 brokers in Phoenix, 3 in Tucson and 9 in Southern California. Coverage of institutional food-service brokers was limited.

Production is concentrated in three regions:
- **Hyder Valley** (southwest of Gila Bend): the largest region, with about 35% of state production, 39% of tilapia and 75% of catfish.
- **Southern Arizona** (Safford/Duncan, Benson, Marana): mainly catfish on ponds.
- **Central and Northern Arizona**: mostly recreational or lake-stocking operations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Tilapia cannot survive in water below 55°F, which restricts commercial culture to warm, geothermally heated water or to a few warm locations such as Arizona. Hyder Valley's geothermal or warm groundwater allows intensive culture. The report notes that a producer can raise 100,000 pounds of tilapia per acre of water compared with 5,000 pounds of catfish. Hyder Valley producers were using tanks and raceways for more intensive culture.

Monoculture dominated overall, but catfish and tilapia farms were often polyculture: tilapia were stocked with catfish to provide live food for the catfish and to control tilapia populations.

Carp and buffalo fish are mostly wild-caught in the US. Crayfish were produced both wild and farm-raised, with Louisiana the largest producer.

## Key Findings

**Supply and facilities**
- Thirteen of the sixteen farms interviewed had, or were anticipating, commercial-size production; ten were actively selling.
- Existing facilities covered about 180 ponds, tanks, raceways and irrigation ditches, with approximately 151 acres of water surface as of August 1987.
- Planned expansion of 431 facilities and 101 acres of water surface within the next 12 months; roughly half of producers planned to expand.
- Hyder Valley held about 73% of facilities but only 39% of water surface; Southern Arizona held about 20% of facilities but 45% of surface area.

**Inventory and production**
- Current stocks exceeded 5 million fish: about 770,000 catfish, 1.55 million tilapia, 10,000 crayfish and roughly 50,000 largemouth bass. Much was not yet marketable size.
- 1987 marketable production was forecast at 1.5 million catfish, 2 million tilapia, 10,000 crayfish and 4,000 largemouth bass.
- By 1994, producers forecast a minimum of 7.5 million catfish and 13.5 million tilapia (1988 forecasts from existing producers).
- Ten commercial farms reported profitability; most expected to become profitable in 1988 or later.

**Imports and national context**
- Imports of catfish were about 1% of US production in 1986 (US production 213.7 million pounds round weight).
- Imported tilapia from Taiwan and Mexico sold at roughly $0.65 to $1.00 per pound.
- National fish consumption in 1985 was 15.4 pounds per capita (5.6 lb finfish, 3.7 lb shellfish).

**Brokers and demand**
- Two Southern California brokers were buying Arizona tilapia (about 8,000 pounds per month combined). No Phoenix or Tucson broker was buying Arizona product as of September 1987.
- Reasons given for not buying were lack of familiarity with Arizona producers, need for processed fillets, and uncertain supply.
- Arizona brokers were selling about 73,060 pounds per month of freshwater fish in 1987 (about 11% of total seafood sales); Southern California brokers were selling about 839,000 pounds per month.
- Forecast: Arizona freshwater fish sales to grow about 36% per year to 188,700 pounds per month by 1994; Southern California to grow about 53% per year to over 3 million pounds per month.
- Fresh fish made up about 69% of freshwater sales; frozen about 31%; live sales were minimal.

**Pricing and transport**
- Delta Pride catfish from Mississippi, the largest supplier, sold for about $1.60 per pound wholesale.
- Arizona producers' farm-gate prices were about $1.00 per pound, while Arizona tilapia sold for about $1.00 to $1.50.
- Refrigerated truck freight from Arizona to Los Angeles: about $0.4 per pound (roughly 4.1 cents) versus about 9 to 10 cents from Mississippi or Arkansas; freshness advantage of one to two days.
- Potential savings of $0.05 to $0.12 per pound versus Southeast producers.

**Consumption and restaurants**
- Phoenix and Tucson restaurants served about 111 million meals annually in 1986 (estimated), with about 11.4 million pounds of seafood consumed in restaurants.
- Restaurant patrons in Phoenix showed higher restaurant use than Tucson.

**Main production problems**
1. Lack of financing for start-up costs.
2. Need for local processing facilities.
3. Support needed from utilities, consultants and government.
4. Natural factors and production mistakes.
5. Lack of a capable, cost-effective local feed supplier.

**Recommendations**
- Form a cooperative marketing effort and central clearinghouse for inventory, expansion and sales information.
- Establish a processing plant (fillets and pan-ready products, fresh and frozen), possibly through a cooperative or a [[Master Broker]].
- Maintain a steady supply, competitive pricing, consistent quality and proper packaging.
- Limit the number of brokers per market to avoid price wars.
- Brand and label Arizona products, and fund advertising and sales promotion.
- Develop a local source of low-cost, high-quality feed.

**Overall outlook**
- Seventy-seven percent of Arizona producers rated the industry outlook as excellent or good; none rated it poor.
