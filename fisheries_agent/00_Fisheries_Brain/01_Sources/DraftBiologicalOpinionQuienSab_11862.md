# Quien Sabe Prescribed Burn: Biological Opinion on the Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) biological opinion, dated October 3, 1991, issued under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] to the [[U.S. Forest Service]] (USFS). It assesses the proposed [[Quien Sabe Prescribed Burn]], a 4,000-acre controlled burn on the Cave Creek Ranger District of the [[Tonto National Forest]], for its effects on the [[Gila Topminnow]], a federally endangered livebearing fish. The FWS concluded that the burn is "not likely to jeopardize the continued existence" of the species. It did, however, anticipate incidental take in the form of a major reduction or loss of the [[Cave Creek]] population and possible reduction or loss of the [[Seven Springs Wash]] population. The opinion attaches reasonable and prudent measures, mandatory terms and conditions, eight conservation recommendations, and monitoring requirements.

## Background & Description

**Species.** The Gila topminnow was listed as endangered on March 11, 1967. No critical habitat has been designated. It is a small livebearing fish of the Gila, Sonora and de la Concepcion river drainages in Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora, Mexico. It was once among the most common fishes of the Gila River and its tributaries, but it has been extirpated from most of its range. Causes include water diversion, stream downcutting, channelization, impoundment, vegetation clearing, and competition with and predation by nonnative [[Mosquitofish]].

**Local history.** The Cave Creek-Seven Springs system, a tributary of the Salt River, lies within the historic range of the species. It was stocked in 1965, 1975 and 1980. The first two stockings are thought to have failed because of floods in 1965-66 and 1978. The Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) found topminnow surviving at Seven Springs in 1987, 1989 and 1991. Fish were reported in Cave Creek in 1989, and the USFS found them in Cave Creek below the Ashdale Administrative Site in December 1990.

**Project description.** The burn targets 4,000 acres, with an objective of 60-80 percent burned. A larger allowable area of 10,000 acres is the zone that may burn if the fire escapes the target boundary. Buffer zones of 350 feet are required along Bronco Creek, Skunk Tank Canyon and Matty's Fork. An unquantified buffer is to be left along Cave Creek, along with some steep slopes. The burn was planned for June 1992. The stated USFS purposes are:

- Improve livestock forage on the [[Cartwright Allotment]] (+100 lb/acre herbaceous forage expected)
- Increase water yield from the watershed (about 200 acre-feet in year one)
- Improve wildlife habitat through edge effects and browse rejuvenation
- Reduce hazardous old-growth [[Chaparral]] fuel buildup (60-80 percent removal of brush overstory)

The project area is bounded by Cave Creek (north and west), Seven Springs Wash (east) and Cottonwood Creek (south). Upland vegetation is dominated by manzanita, turbinella oak, ceanothus and mountain mahogany. The riparian corridor is dominated by cottonwood, sycamore and walnut.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The opinion explains that native Gila River fishes are adapted to frequent, severe floods. Topminnow in small, confined and degraded streams are more vulnerable, however. Degraded channels offer few refuges, have low habitat complexity, and erode more during floods. Those conditions make the species sensitive to disturbance.

Fire is a natural process in chaparral. The FWS noted that the topminnow now survives in small, isolated remnant habitats, and that degraded habitat, unstable channels and nonnative predators exert continuous pressure on the population. Under these conditions the species may no longer be able to withstand natural disturbances, so its conservation may depend on managing those events.

The Cave Creek/Seven Springs population is one of about 30 reintroduced wild populations. It has persisted for 11 years, making it second in longevity among reintroduced populations. It has remained small and fluctuating, and it ranks in the middle for recovery importance. Because the species is short-lived with high fecundity, its numbers change rapidly, and nearby expansion habitat (Cave Creek, for the Seven Springs population) is important for long-term survival.

Key environmental factors analyzed:

- **Rainfall:** 12-18 inches per year, below the 18 inches that [[Hibbert]] (1983) recommended as the minimum for chaparral water-yield gains from burning. Rainfall is also clumped.
- **Slope:** average 50 percent (range 25-80 percent). Hibbert (1983) recommended restricting prescribed fire in chaparral to slopes under 60 percent.
- **Watershed flow:** median yearly discharge of 1,660 acre-feet at the Cottonwood Creek gauge (9 years of record).

## Key Findings

**Effects of the action**

1. **Increased flood severity.** Pre-burn annual water yield from the burn area is about 200-250 acre-feet. The burn would add about 200 acre-feet in year one, a 100 percent increase from the project area and about 12 percent of the watershed median. Because the increase would be concentrated in a few storm events, peak increases would be much greater than 12 percent. Flood effects are expected to last about three years.
2. **Sediment and water chemistry.** Ash-laden sediment from burned slopes can kill fish directly (suffocation, abrasion, toxicity). It also reduces habitat complexity, smothers invertebrates and plants, and lowers surface flow. Minor channels without buffers supply much of the sediment, so buffers on major channels alone will not capture it all.
3. **Timing.** June burning is likely to produce a hotter, more complete burn that removes litter, which is the key control on sediment yield. A burn just before the July monsoon onset would raise sediment and flood risk, so the FWS favored burning when rain risk is lowest and before June 15.
4. **Project size.** The 4,000-acre burn is within the 3,000-7,000-acre range typical for Tonto chaparral fires, but water and sediment yield increase with burned area, so the effects would be substantially larger than for a smaller burn.
5. **Livestock grazing.** Grazing soon after the burn would reduce litter and prolong flood and erosion effects beyond the expected three-year recovery. The Ranger District hoped to return cattle "shortly" after the burn.

**Conclusions**

- The FWS concluded the burn is "not likely to jeopardize" the species.
- Short-term effects (flood intensity, sedimentation, water chemistry) would be adverse for several years but should decline. Buffers and steep-slope protection would partly reduce them.
- If the watershed above Seven Springs Wash burns, the population in that wash could be lost as well, and the Cave Creek population could be lost entirely.
- Loss of the Cave Creek/Seven Springs population would not endanger the species overall, but it would "significantly retard recovery."

**Incidental take and required measures**

- Take cannot be quantified because population estimates are unreliable. The FWS used a worst-case scenario, given the project's all-or-nothing design.
- **Reasonable and prudent measures:** minimize direct mortality, minimize habitat destruction, and keep complete records.
- **Mandatory terms:** implement the burn with buffers of at least 350 feet; exclude slopes over 40 percent adjacent to the stream; keep a delineated exclusion map on site; protect buffers on Seven Springs Wash as a priority after human life and homes; do not divert flowing or standing water; restrict motorized vehicles; monitor for two years (before the burn, immediately after, and at one and two years, with results due within 60 days); and submit a final report within 90 days.

**Conservation recommendations (eight)**

1. Revise the burn plan with a multi-agency team, including [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] and USFS hydrologists.
2. Move the allowable-area boundary west to the ridge between Seven Springs Wash and East Fork.
3. Leave at least 40 percent of the area unburned as a mosaic, and do not burn slopes over 60 percent.
4. Do not burn after June 15.
5. Incorporate the burn into ongoing USFS fire-sediment research.
6. Do not graze the burned area for at least three years, or until team criteria are met.
7. Prevent pollutants other than ash and sediment from entering streams.
8. Minimize erosion and sediment transport before and after the project.

**Reinitiation triggers.** Consultation must be reinitiated if the take limit is reached, new information reveals unconsidered effects, the action is modified in unconsidered ways, or a new species is listed. Under 50 CFR 402.16, these are the conditions for reopening the consultation.
