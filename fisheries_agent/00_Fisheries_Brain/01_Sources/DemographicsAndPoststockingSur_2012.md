# Demographics and Post-Stocking Survival of Repatriated Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #catostomidae #endangered-species #population-ecology

## Executive Summary

This three-year study (2008-2011) examined demographics and post-stocking survival of repatriated [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a mainstem [[Colorado River]] reservoir. The wild population has declined to fewer than 50 individuals, making the repatriation program essential for species persistence. Multiple research approaches were employed: acoustic telemetry (two rounds), routine mark-recapture monitoring, creel census collaboration, ecological modeling, and novel remote PIT scanning technology. The repatriate population was estimated at 2,966 fish (1,509-6,063 95% CI) in 2010, representing a 100% increase from 2009. Post-stocking survival varied significantly with release size, with fish released at 45+ cm total length showing 4-8 times higher survival than those at 30 cm. Remote PIT scanning revealed distinct riverine and basin subpopulations with limited exchange. [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) predation was confirmed as a major mortality factor, with one transmitter recovered from a 13.6 kg striped bass stomach. The study demonstrates that continued repatriation at maximum feasible size is critical for maintaining this critically imperiled population.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Mohave]] is a 107 km² reservoir created by [[Davis Dam]], extending from [[Hoover Dam]] downstream. It once supported the largest known population of wild [[Razorback Sucker]], historically exceeding 100,000 individuals, but has declined dramatically to fewer than 50 wild fish by 2010. The reservoir exhibits distinct habitat zones: a cold, riverine section with gravel bars between Hoover Dam and [[Willow Beach]] (perennial 12.8°C releases), transitioning to reservoir-like conditions downstream through [[Black Canyon]] to [[Davis Dam]].

The repatriation program, established in the early 1990s by the [[Lake Mohave Native Fish Workgroup]], collects wild-produced larvae, rears them in protective captivity to nominal 30+ cm TL, then stocks them into the reservoir. From 1992-2010, stocking totals varied dramatically by year and size class, with 153 fish in 1992 increasing to peaks of 22,750 in 2003 and 18,538 in 2009. Target release sizes evolved from 30 cm (2002) to 35 cm (2005) to 50 cm (2008), though actual size distributions varied considerably.

The reservoir supports abundant nonnative fishes including [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*, 120,000+ stocked annually), [[Striped Bass]] (established 1981), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*M. dolomieu*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave's hydrological regime is dominated by cold hypolimnetic releases from Hoover Dam that vary dramatically (42.5 to 991 m³/sec) over hours due to power demands. This creates a unique thermal environment with year-round cold temperatures in the upper riverine reach, contrasting with warmer basin conditions downstream. Sediment from side canyons creates gravel bars, some with hot spring discharge, that serve as spawning habitat for razorback sucker.

The nonnative fish community, particularly striped bass, represents the primary ecological threat to repatriated razorback sucker. Striped bass populations surged following 1983 spillway releases and feed on stocked rainbow trout as well as native fishes. Direct evidence of predation came from recovery of an acoustic transmitter from a 13.6 kg striped bass that had consumed a 50 cm razorback sucker only days after stocking. This demonstrates vulnerability across all size classes, though larger fish show better survival odds.

Spatial structure within the reservoir appears significant. Remote PIT scanning and telemetry revealed distinct subpopulations in riverine (upstream of Willow Beach) and basin zones, with only 7 of 1,044 unique fish detected in both zones during 2011. Fish released in the riverine section tended to remain there for 6+ months post-release, while basin-released fish showed different dispersal patterns. Thirty-five percent of telemetered adults released at [[Fortune Cove]] dispersed upstream to shallow gravel bars between Willow Beach and Hoover Dam, where large aggregations were observed during spawning season.

## Key Findings

**Post-Stocking Survival and Dispersal:**
- 2008-09 telemetry: 67% of subadults (mean 38 cm TL) and 80% of adults (mean 54 cm TL) survived six months post-release
- 2009-10 telemetry: 100% of both river-caught adults (mean 61 cm TL) and hatchery-reared adults (mean 53 cm TL) remained active through six months
- All subadult mortality (3 of 10 fish) occurred within 35 days post-stocking; adult mortality (4 of 20) within 96 days
- Mean total distance traveled: 44 km for subadults, 116 km for 2008 adults, 71 km for river adults, 128 km for hatchery adults
- Five transmitters recovered by SCUBA showed no associated fish remains, suggesting rapid consumption or scavenging

**Population Estimates and Demographics:**
- Wild population declined from 24 fish (9-480 95% CI) in 2009 to 13 fish (4-250 95% CI) in 2010
- Repatriate population increased from 1,439 (753-2,805 95% CI) in 2009 to 2,966 (1,509-6,063 95% CI) in 2010
- Total 2010 population estimate: 2,979 razorback sucker
- Routine monitoring (Dec 2008-Mar 2011) captured 50 razorback sucker: 49 repatriates, 1 wild
- Mean repatriate time at large: 32 months; mean growth rate: 1 cm/month
- 61% of captured repatriates reared at off-site facilities, 39% in lakeside backwaters

**Size-Survival Relationships and Modeling:**
- Updated mark-recapture model (1992-2011 data) showed lower survival estimates across all sizes compared to 2005 relationship
- Survival for 50 cm fish estimated at 61% (updated model) vs. 87% (2005 model)
- Stocking simulations: 45 cm target size yields 4-8× higher post-stocking survival than 30 cm target
- Equilibrium population modeling: stocking 6,000 fish at 45 cm target produces stable population of ~15,000-20,000 (depending on adult survival rate)
- Model results sensitive to adult survival assumptions (70-80% annually) but relative benefits of larger release size remained consistent

**Remote PIT Scanning Results:**
- 1,044 unique razorback sucker contacted in 2011: 730 in riverine zone, 321 in basin, 7 in both
- Basin population estimate: 1,531 fish (based on 134 kHz tagged proportion)
- Riverine population estimate: 1,880 fish (regression-based removal estimate)
- Only 0.7% of contacts shared between zones, suggesting largely segregated subpopulations
- 16% of contacted fish detected at 2-4 different spawning sites (1-12 km apart)
- Seven fish moved between river and basin zones during 2011 spawning season

**Predation and Mortality:**
- Striped bass predation confirmed via transmitter recovery from 13.6 kg fish
- Creel census (2006-2009): 18 large striped bass (>80 cm TL) and 2 channel catfish scanned, none contained PIT tags
- Novel web-based forum (LakeMohaveStripers.com) launched April 2011 to track striped bass catches and stomach contents
- 15 striped bass reported through forum, 8 with trout in stomachs (no large-scale native fish stockings occurred during monitoring period)

**Stocking History and Patterns:**
- 1992-2010: 185,664 razorback sucker stocked (Table 1 totals)
- Size class distribution shifted over time: 1990s dominated by <30 cm fish; 2000s increased proportion of larger fish
- 2008: 56.5% of 978 stocked fish >45 cm (closest to 50 cm target achieved)
- Stocking locations evolved from basin sites (e.g., [[Yuma Cove]]) to riverine areas near Willow Beach
- Growth data: fish released at 30-35 cm grew to 45+ cm within one year; minimal growth for fish >40 cm at release

**Movement and Habitat Use:**
- Adults showed strong upstream movement: 35% of Fortune Cove releases reached areas above Willow Beach
- Subadults rarely moved upstream: only 7% reached above Willow Beach
- 50% of river-released adults and 54% of Bubbling Ponds adults remained in release zone throughout study
- Telemetry fish from 2008 releases detected in all three zones 4-27 months post-release
- 2009 river releases remained in river zone up to 19 months post-stocking

**Recommendations Implemented:**
- Continue bi-annual netting for growth, health, census, and genetic data collection
- Stock at largest size possible in greatest numbers feasible
- Distribute stockings equally among three subpopulation centers (River, Liberty, Basin)
- Deploy remote PIT scanners with minimum 200 scanning hours per zone
- Maintain concurrent stocking timing across zones with similar mean sizes
- Target minimum 500 fish per location and stocking event for adequate future contact analysis
