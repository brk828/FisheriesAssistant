# San Pedro River Basin Water Quality Status Report, 1973–1979

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Department of Health Services report compiles water quality data for the Upper [[San Pedro River]] for 1973–1979. It covers the 1973 intensive survey, the 1977 University of Arizona monitoring, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department and BWQC spill investigations. Its main focus is the repeated acidic, metal-laden discharges from the [[Cananea Mine]] in Sonora, Mexico (December 1977 to April 1979). The report also documents recovery after the spills, as measured by the July 17–18, 1979 Joint Survey. That survey found no surface water chemical violations and only one fish species, but aquatic life was still recovering slowly.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The San Pedro rises in Sonora, about 25 miles (40.2 km) south of the border, and flows north about 125 miles through Cochise, Pima, and Pinal Counties to join the [[Gila River]] near Winkelman. The Upper San Pedro runs from its origin to the Narrows, 11 miles (17.71 km) north of Benson. The Lower Basin includes the largest tributary, [[Aravaipa Creek]]. Most of the basin is ephemeral. Only three reaches are perennial: about 25 miles from Hereford to Charleston, about 5 miles at The Narrows, and about 1 mile below Peppersauce Creek.

**Ecological significance.** The river and Aravaipa Creek provide an exceptional wildlife habitat. The report states that the area has the highest bird species diversity in the United States and is listed in the nationwide rivers inventory as a potentially nationally significant ecosystem.

**Pollution sources.** Point sources include municipal treatment plants at Bisbee, Benson, Sierra Vista, and Mammoth. Septic seepage, cattle grazing (sediment and fecal material), mining, and airborne pollutants are listed as additional sources.

**Survey programs summarized:**
- [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Winkelman gauge chemistry (part of the BWQC Fixed Station Monitoring Network from 1976)
- 1973 BWQC intensive survey of the full river length
- June–August 1977 [[Water Resources Research Center]] (University of Arizona) survey
- BWQC spill investigations, December 1977–April 1979
- [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] statewide fishery work since July 1977

Key sampling sites (Table 1) include the International Boundary (site SP000.1), Palominas, Hereford, Charleston, Fairbank, St. David, Benson, Redington, and the mouth at the Gila River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**The Cananea Mine spills.** The [[Cananea Mine]] (Compañía Minera de Cananea) sits at the headwaters of the San Pedro. Two factors caused the spills. Leaching ponds were badly sited in a runoff-prone headwater area. Unusually heavy rains then overflowed the ponds, weakened the earthen dams, and sent polluted runoff into the river. The discharge was bright orange-brown to brick-red, acidic, and high in iron, copper, manganese, zinc, and suspended solids.

**Spill timeline:**
- Mid-December 1977 to April 1978: first episode, with BWQC monitoring from March 13 to April 6, 1978
- December 26, 1978 to March 1979: second episode, after a reported nine-inch rain in 30 hours overflowed the dam spillway

**Regulatory framework.** Surface water standards for 1973–1979 were set for partial body contact, warm-water fishery, aquatic and wildlife use, and agricultural irrigation. Drinking water recommendations followed [[U.S. Public Health Service]] standards (1962) and the Arizona Drinking Water Regulations (May 26, 1978).

**Ecological and social impacts.** During the spills, the acidity and metals were "devastating to fish and aquatic life." Residents and farmers worried about livestock, crops, and drinking water wells. The Cochise County Superior Court temporarily enjoined the St. David Irrigation District from diverting the river on April 3, 1978.

**Remediation.** Mining company plans in June 1979 included breaking down the old tailings ponds and building new ponds and a dam in the Sonora River watershed, outside the San Pedro basin. Diversion ditches and pipelines were also built.

## Key Findings

### Pre-spill data (1973 and 1977)
- **1973 intensive survey:** Surface water turbidity was higher than state standards at some sites. The site below the Mammoth wastewater pond exceeded fecal coliform limits. Groundwater had elevated sulfates and total dissolved solids above U.S. Public Health Service recommendations.
- **1977 WRRC survey:** Surface water violations were recorded for pH, barium, lead, and copper. Copper violations were not found in the 1973 survey, which the report notes as a contrast.

### Spill-period conditions (1977–1979)
- **Lowest pH:** 3.1
- **Lowest dissolved oxygen:** 2 mg/l
- **Elevated metals:** iron, copper, manganese, and zinc
- **Source interpretation:** leach solution, copper precipitation process liquor, concentrator tailings, and storm runoff. The red color came from oxidized iron.
- **Biological impact:** all aquatic life was killed over a 60-mile reach north of the International Boundary.
- **Other effects:** deformed, listless birds were found along the river. Extensive fish kills occurred before the 1977–78 episode.

### Joint Survey (July 17–18, 1979)
Conducted by [[Arizona Department Of Health Services]] (BWQC), Arizona Game and Fish Department, and Anaconda Copper Company. Sampling covered seven surface water sites and sediment from the border to St. David, plus seven wells within half a mile of the river.

- **Surface water chemistry:** no parameter exceeded state standards. Iron and magnesium were higher than pre-spill values, while copper, manganese, and zinc were lower. The report notes statistical significance was not determined.
- **Downstream pattern:** iron, copper, manganese, and zinc dropped sharply at St. David. Filterable residue, hardness, sulfate, and calcium rose, which the report attributes to tributaries such as Babocomari Creek.
- **Sediment:** pH was above 8 at all sites, reflecting alkaline, buffered southwestern soils. Copper, zinc, and iron decreased with distance from the source. A special iron precipitate sample from Charleston was about 13.9 percent iron and 0.1 percent copper.
- **Biota:** one fish species, six riparian and aquatic plant species, and eight aquatic invertebrate species. Invertebrate numbers rose with distance from the source, except at Fairbank.
- **Fish:** the only species found was the [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), present at all four biological sites. Its reputation for tolerating harsh, mine-impacted conditions is highlighted. The report states that five fish families had been recorded previously (Table 15): Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Poeciliidae, Centrarchidae, and Ictaluridae. These include [[Mosquito Fish]], green sunfish, bluegill, and black bullhead catfish. The Joint Survey was not a complete fish survey.
- **Invertebrate counts (Table 14):** Palominas 5, Lewis Springs 22, Charleston 37, Fairbank 24 individuals. Baetidae (mayflies, two species) were the most numerous family.
- **Temperature and algae:** filamentous algae were present at all sites except Palominas. Palominas also had the highest water temperature, 34°C, versus 29°C at Lewis Springs, 28°C at Charleston, and 27°C at Fairbank.
- **Groundwater:** the six drinking wells met drinking water standards for all parameters tested. Abandoned irrigation well 7, ten yards from the river, had excessive iron, lead, and manganese. The report cannot confirm whether spill infiltration or casing leaching caused this, because no pre-spill data exist.

### Recovery assessment and recommendations
The report calls the ecological recovery "surprisingly rapid," four months after the last spill subsided. Long-term effects, such as heavy-metal transfer through the food chain, may not be known for years. The report recommends:
1. Ongoing surveillance of surface and groundwater through the BWQC program with SEAGO, to provide early warning and long-term baselines.
2. Inspection of the Cananea diversions and dams to confirm they can withstand heavy runoff.
3. Contingency plans for future spills, including lime addition to reduce acidity and metal levels, and settling ponds to slow releases.
4. Continued international communication and cooperation with Mexico through the [[International Boundary And Water Commission]].
