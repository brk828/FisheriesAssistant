# Proposed Listing of the Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) as Threatened with Critical Habitat Designation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register proposed rule (Vol. 50, No. 117, June 18, 1985), issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under the Department of the Interior, proposes to list the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) as a threatened species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended, and to designate critical habitat in Arizona and New Mexico. The species' range has declined by about 85 percent, with roughly 380 km of its historic approximately 2,500 km of habitat still occupied. The proposal also includes a special rule permitting take for educational, scientific, propagation and conservation purposes under State collecting permits. Public comments were due by August 19, 1985, and requests for a public hearing by August 2, 1985.

## Background & Description

*Tiaroga cobitis* was first collected in 1851 from the [[Rio San Pedro]] in Arizona and described from those specimens by Girard in 1856. It is a small (less than 80 mm), slender, elongated fish, olivaceous in colour, with dirty-white spots at the base of the dorsal and caudal fins. It has a highly oblique terminal mouth and markedly upward-directed eyes. Breeding males develop vivid red-orange markings.

The species inhabits small to large perennial streams, using shallow-water runs and riffles with predominantly cobble substrates, swift currents, and growths of filamentous algae. Moderate flooding is important to its biology: it keeps the substrate free of fine sediments, maintains spawning habitat, and helps the species maintain a competitive edge over invading exotic fish species.

Historically the species was locally common throughout much of the [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[San Francisco River]] and [[Gila River]] systems, from the Phoenix area upstream, including the mainstem and perennial tributaries up to about 2,200 m elevation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Current distribution.** The range has been reduced to approximately 271 km, and the document notes that the 380 km presently occupied represents about 15 percent of its former range. Occupied areas include:

- **Aravaipa Creek** (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona): about 27 km of the perennial stream, including the [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]].
- **Gila River** (Arizona and New Mexico): upper Gila River above the Middle Box canyon, the Cliff-Gila Valley, and the confluence of the East, West and Middle Forks.
- **San Francisco and Tularosa Rivers** (Catron and Grant Counties, New Mexico), and the lower reaches of Whitewater Creek.
- **Blue River** (Greenlee County, Arizona), about 50 km.

**Land ownership.** Occupied habitat is mixed. Aravaipa Creek is largely managed by the [[Bureau of Land Management]], with a portion owned by Defenders of Wildlife (George Whittell Wildlife Preserve). The Gila River mixes private land, [[Nature Conservancy]] holdings, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish property, and large sections of the [[Gila National Forest]]. The Blue River is almost entirely within the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]].

**Threats.** Habitat degradation, channel downcutting, substrate sedimentation, water diversion, groundwater pumping, and predation and competition from exotic species have reduced the species. Proposed dam construction, water loss, habitat alteration and exotic species continue to threaten it. Of the approximately 2,000 km of stream historically occupied, about 2,220 km (as stated in the text) no longer supports the species. Exotic predators and competitors such as [[Red Shiner]] are identified as a major factor, and the document notes that channel modification and grazing also degrade habitat.

**Proposed critical habitat.** The proposal designates stream segments in Arizona (Aravaipa Creek, Blue River, Campbell Blue Creek, Tularosa River) and New Mexico (East, West and Middle Forks of the Gila, Gila River, San Francisco River, Blue River, Campbell Blue Creek, Tularosa River and Whitewater Creek). Primary constituent elements include unpolluted water, a depth of at least 3 cm in low-velocity areas, and cobble and gravel substrate.

**Federal activities.** Consultation under Section 7 may be needed for U.S. Forest Service activities such as grazing, mining, timbering and recreation. Possible effects include federal flood control projects on the Gila River (including a proposed Upper Gila dam and reservoir under the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] and [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Upper Gila Water Supply Study), federally funded highway and bridge work, and federally funded agricultural land-leveling and pipeline projects in the Cliff-Gila Valley and along the Tularosa and San Francisco Rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed status:** Threatened under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended, with critical habitat proposed.
- **Range reduction:** Occupied habitat (about 380 km) represents approximately 15 percent of the former range.
- **Primary threats:** Habitat disturbance and alteration rather than direct taking or commercial exploitation; exotic species competition and predation; proposed dams; water diversion and groundwater pumping.
- **Special rule (Section 4(d)):** Take is permitted under State collecting permits for educational, scientific, propagation, zoological exhibition and other conservation purposes, and incidental take by licensed recreational anglers is not a violation if the fish is immediately returned to habitat. Habitat-disturbance take remains prohibited.
- **Environmental assessment:** Not required under NEPA, per a notice published in the Federal Register on October 25, 1983.
- **Public input:** The Service solicited comments on biological data, additional populations, range, current and planned activities, and economic impacts. Public comments were due August 19, 1985.
