## Executive Summary

This report details a proposed renovation plan for **Palm Lake**, a 3–4 acre spring- and well-fed artificial pond near Wickenburg, Arizona, adjacent to the [[Hassayampa River]]. Recently acquired by The Arizona Nature Conservancy (TANC), the pond has historically been used for exotic fish production (largemouth bass, green sunfish, mosquitofish). The Arizona Game and Fish Department, in collaboration with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) and TANC, proposes eradicating these exotics and converting Palm Lake into a secure **refugium and grow-out facility** for five critically endangered or sensitive native fish species: [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and [[Gila Topminnow]]. The plan addresses urgent conservation needs, including high predation-driven mortality of reintroduced native fry/juveniles, the near-extinction of wild bonytail chub populations, and vulnerabilities in existing broodstock security (e.g., at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]). The document outlines a two-phase chemical renovation strategy (spring-head isolation followed by main lake treatment) using piscicides such as Antimycin-A, Rotenone, and Sodium Hypochlorite.

## Background & Description

This source is a habitat management and conservation planning document; it does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of the target species. Limited size-related data is noted in the context of predation vulnerability:

- Reintroduced [[Colorado River Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are typically stocked as **fry or 2–3 inch juveniles**, a size range demonstrated to be highly susceptible to predation.
- Current native fish hatchery infrastructure is limited in its capacity to produce **larger, predation-resistant specimens** at scale, underscoring the need for grow-out ponds like Palm Lake to raise fish to less vulnerable sizes prior to river stocking.
- No morphometric, meristic, or coloration data for any of the five target native species are included in this text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Context:**
- Palm Lake is a spring- and well-fed artificial pond within the **Gila River basin**, a historic range for all five target species, though no confirmed historic collection records exist specifically from the Hassayampa River near the site.
- The pond exhibits habitat complexity, including potential **subterranean/spring inflows**, complicating full eradication of resident exotic fishes.
- Existing exotic fish community: **largemouth bass** (*Micropterus salmoides*), **green sunfish** (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and **mosquitofish** (*Gambusia affinis*).

**Predator-Prey Dynamics:**
- In riverine habitats historically occupied by native cyprinids and catostomids, invasive [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]] have become the **primary predators** on reintroduced Colorado River squawfish and razorback sucker fry/juveniles.
- Secondary predation pressure comes from centrarchids (e.g., smallmouth bass) and other ictalurids (e.g., yellow bullhead).
- This predation dynamic is a central driver for the grow-out strategy: raising native fish in a predator-free, controlled pond environment (Palm Lake) before release to increase post-stocking survival.

**Population Status Context:**
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: The only remaining wild Arizona population, in **Lake Mohave** (Colorado River), is described as a senescent, non-reproducing adult population since reservoir closure. The Upper Colorado River basin population is considered "essentially extinct." A captive broodstock is maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Lake Mohave-derived).
- [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]]: Prior broodstock/donor habitat became compromised by **fathead minnow** contamination, and had suffered repeated illicit exotic introductions, prompting the search for a more secure alternative site.

## Key Findings

- **Predation bottleneck**: Only stocking programs using larger native fish specimens are likely to succeed in re-establishing [[Colorado River Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] populations in Arizona rivers, necessitating dedicated grow-out ponds.
- **Broodstock security rationale**: Geographic isolation of a portion of the [[Bonytail Chub]] captive broodstock (held at Dexter) to a second facility (Palm Lake) would reduce catastrophic risk (e.g., crop-dusting accidents, contamination events) to the species' genetic reserve.
- **Site security advantages**: Palm Lake's proximity to Department offices, planned fencing/posting of the TANC preserve, and on-site caretaker housing offer improved protection against illicit/exotic fish introductions compared to the prior desert pupfish/Gila topminnow source habitat.
- **Multi-use value**: Beyond production/refugium functions, a multi-species native fish pond would support behavioral/ecological research (via proximity to Phoenix-area universities) and provide public relations/conservation outreach benefits through the TANC partnership.
- **Renovation methodology**: A phased approach is recommended — (1) isolate and treat the spring-head area repeatedly with piscicides (Antimycin-A/Fintrol, Rotenone, and/or Sodium Hypochlorite, in combination) before reconnecting it to the lake; (2) drain/lower the main lake body via either (A) breaching the impoundment dike (preferred, allows full drying and habitat improvements) or (B) pumping/siphoning without breaching (less likely to fully dry the lake, requiring repeated toxin treatments with lower probability of success).
- **Downstream safety**: Draining to the [[Hassayampa River]] is not expected to introduce new exotic species (all lake species already occur in the drainage) nor to produce toxin concentrations sufficient to cause fish kills in the river.
- **Detoxification preparedness**: Potassium permanganate and Sodium thiosulfate will be kept on hand to neutralize piscicides in the unlikely event of an emergency requiring detoxification.
- **Stakeholder consensus**: Department, FWS/Dexter, and San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge personnel, along with TANC's Land Steward, have concurred on both the site's suitability and the proposed renovation methodology.
