# Ecological Studies of the Flora and Fauna in Glen Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #aquatic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This multi-author University of Utah publication (Anthropological Papers No. 40, Glen Canyon Series No. 7, June 1959) documents pre-impoundment ecological baseline studies in [[Glen Canyon]], the canyon of the [[Colorado River]] that was to be flooded by the [[Glen Canyon Reservoir]], a unit of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]]. The work was a salvage effort conducted under agreements with the [[National Park Service]], and it covers vegetation, mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians, endoparasites, insects, algae, and an aquatic survey of the river and its tributaries. The aquatic component, the focus of this note, records physical, chemical and biological data from the 1958 expedition, a 17-species fish fauna dominated by introduced species, and a low-productivity main channel contrasted with biologically richer tributaries.

## Background & Description

The project began after Congress authorized water development in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] (Public Law 485, 84th Congress, 1956). The [[Working Plan for Ecological Studies]], written by [[Angus M. Woodbury]] in October 1957, set out a 20-year program in four phases: a preliminary planning study, a pre-filling inventory, changes during filling (estimated 10 years), and effects of the artificial lake. The planned dam was a concrete arch structure 580 feet high, with a crest about 1,500 feet long and closure planned for late summer 1962. At full pool (elevation 3700 ft) the reservoir was estimated at 28,010,000 acre-feet over 162,700 acres, extending about 186 miles up the Colorado River and 71 miles up the [[San Juan River]].

Field work included a 1957 reconnaissance flight, a boat trip from Hite to Lees Ferry (September 8 to 15, 1957), and a 19-man expedition from Hite to Lees Ferry (June 30 to August 9, 1958). The aquatic survey of 1958 was carried out by [[Gerald R. Smith]], [[Guy G. Musser]] and [[Donald B. McDonald]], with McDonald representing the [[Utah Department of Fish and Game]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**River character.** The river drops about 341 feet over 170 miles through Glen Canyon, averaging about two feet per mile, against roughly nine feet per mile in [[Cataract Canyon]]. Canyon depths range from 500 to 2,000 feet. Flows ranged from more than 100,000 second-feet during high water to as low as 4,000 second-feet in late summer, and the water is heavily sediment-laden. The river habitat is described as an "aquatic desert" with swift current and moving sand that scours the streambed.

**Climate and sediment.** Lees Ferry averages a July temperature of 87°F and a January temperature of 35°F. Annual precipitation averages 6.2 inches, and evaporation is about 60 inches a year. The total silt load passing Lees Ferry is estimated at 100,000,000 tons annually. The reservoir was projected to trap about 100,000 acre-feet of sediment per year, which would fill it in about 280 years.

**Water chemistry.** Surveyed waters were alkaline and bicarbonate-rich, reflecting calcium-bearing rock. Water in most brooks was not very hard, at 110 to 160 ppm (extremes 80 to 274), and pH values were generally 7.4 to 9.0. Dissolved oxygen was well saturated in the river, while some tributary samples dropped to 4.8 ppm.

**Habitat contrast.** The tributaries are overwhelmingly more productive per unit volume than the river. Tributaries offer clear water, rocky riffles and pools, and refuge from scouring floods, whereas the river is seldom clear and its sand is largely sterile of macroscopic life.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and yield**
- Studies were made at 46 stations, with 15 sites in or along the river and 47 sampling areas in tributaries and side canyons. Some stations were subdivided, and sampling in the main current was very difficult, yielding only 16 river samplings.
- Totals collected: 14 algal samples, 984 fish specimens and 2,315 insects. The river yielded 116 fish (5 species) and 247 insects (16 species). The tributaries yielded 868 fish (17 species) and 2,068 insects (77 species, 6 orders at that level).
- Gear used included dynamite, seines, fyke nets, rotenone, hook and line, and a hand screen. No effective method was found for the deep, swift river channel.

**Fishes**
- The annotated check list in Appendix B includes 17 species in seven families and three orders. Six are native to the Colorado River system, and 11 are introduced.
- Introduced [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]] dominated the river sampling: 72 channel catfish at five stations and 34 carp at two, against eight native flannelmouth suckers at four stations.
- In tributaries, native [[Upper Colorado Speckled Dace]] and introduced [[Fathead Minnow]] were closely matched for dominance.
- Native big-river species were scarce. Only one immature [[Colorado Squawfish]] was collected, and only two immature [[Humpback Sucker]] were taken, both from tributaries.
- Adult [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Colorado River Mountain Sucker]] were rare. The mountain sucker was mostly 2 to 4 inches long, which the author linked to poor conditions in the canyon.
- Carp were abundant but grew slowly. The largest carp, at 8 to 9 inches, were in the four-to-five-year age group, while carp of that age commonly exceed 20 inches under favorable conditions. The author attributed this to poor food supply.
- The author noted that the 1958 surveys were the only careful study of this section before the introductions, so pre-introduction conditions could only be conjectured.

**Fish diets**
- Stomachs from the river were mostly empty: 21 of 44 were empty or nearly so. Only one of 16 tributary stomachs was empty. The author took this as evidence that tributaries provide better feeding for small fish.
- Carp and flannelmouth sucker stomachs held mainly amorphous material from bottom ooze, which suggested possible competition between the two.
- Channel catfish were omnivorous, consuming camp refuse, insects, plant matter and small fish.

**Insects**
- Eight aquatic orders were collected. Stoneflies (Plecoptera) and the expected Orthoptera were missing from the aquatic samples.
- Caddisflies were among the most abundant insects, especially the Helicopsyche genus. Beetles were the richest order, with 22 species.
- The author recorded the rare sand-dwelling mayfly genus Homoeoneuria, possibly a new species, from the Escalante River.
- The Glen Canyon reservoir was expected to replace these aquatic communities, so the author stressed that the 1958 insect list might be the last record.

**Algae**
- Of 26 algal samples, 53 species were identified: 28 green algae (Chlorophyta), 5 blue-green algae (Cyanophyta), and 20 diatoms (Chrysophyta). Dominant filamentous greens included Spirogyra, Zygnema and Cladophora.

**Limnology and reservoir outlook**
- The authors expected that reservoir filling would trap cold water and sediment, and that the river below the dam would shift from muddy and temperature-variable to clear and cold.
- Changes in the aquatic biota were expected to take many years to stabilize.
- The report recommended long-term quantitative sampling, with attention to population cycles, interrelationships, and correlations with environmental conditions.

**Management concerns**
- Silt from the [[Paria River]] could raise the delta high enough to back water up to the power plants, which the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] was considering countering with a sediment dam.
- Range management on tributary drainages was identified as a [[Forest Service]] and [[Bureau of Land Management]] responsibility.
