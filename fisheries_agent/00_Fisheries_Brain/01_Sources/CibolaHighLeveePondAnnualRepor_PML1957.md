# Cibola High Levee Pond Annual Report 2003: Early Life Ecology of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from the [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] (CHLP) covers the second year of a four-year field effort on the early life ecology of [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Both native species spawned and produced swim-up larvae in 2003. Bonytail recruitment continued annually, while razorback sucker recruitment remained intermittent. Work combined [[Radio Telemetry]] of adult bonytail, [[Underwater Videography]] of spawning behavior, physical and water-quality monitoring, [[Light Trapping]], [[Trammel Netting]], and [[Predator Prey Tank Tests]]. The central finding is that [[Non-Native Species]], including [[Bullfrog]] tadpoles, [[Red Swamp Crayfish]], and small non-native fish, are effective predators of eggs and early larvae, which the authors describe as a potentially overwhelming obstacle to recovery.

## Background & Description

The authors are from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Fort Collins Science Center, [[Arizona State University]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (AZFRO-PA). The study is a continuation of work that began in 2002, when surveys estimated a standing crop of about 1,100 razorback suckers and 6,000 bonytail (>15 cm). The pond's standing crop was 4,350 fish/ha with a biomass of 635 kg/ha. No measurable razorback recruitment had been detected in the two preceding years.

The 2003 work was organized into four categories:

- **Telemetry:** Adult bonytail were collected by trammel net on 18 March 2003 (11 fish, 358–514 mm). Ten fish (404–514 mm, mean 453 mm) were fitted with 8 × 35 mm sonic micro-transmitters with a 60-day battery life. Transmitters were attached externally with a wire harness secured around the caudal peduncle, a method the authors believe may be a first for freshwater fishes. Fish were tracked for 8 weeks by directional hydrophone from two fixed stations.
- **Videography:** A black-and-white underwater camera with an infrared spotlight (bonytail avoided visible light) recorded spawning on VHS tape. More than 124 hours were recorded, nearly all at spawning sites.
- **Physical and chemical monitoring:** Hydrolab profiles, light trapping, trammel netting, bathymetric mapping (by BioSonics DT-4000 echosounder and GPS, July 2003), and acoustic tests.
- **Predator/prey tank experiments:** Conducted at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Achii Havasu Fish Facility]].

Bonytail were recaptured and gut samples were taken by a nonlethal stomach and intestinal flushing method adapted from work on chub species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

CHLP is described as the only oxbow community where the early life stages of these two species can be studied. Its habitat is dominated by riprap on the [[Riprap]] high levee, gravel and cobble along the river levee toe, and beaver-disturbed substrate. Historically, woody debris, snags, root wads, and drift piles provided cover in the lower basin, but these were lost with riparian deforestation. The authors suggest that cavities in riprap now substitute for that lost cover.

Water quality was described as normal and well mixed. Temperatures ranged from 16 to 30 °C, dissolved oxygen from 7.7 to 10.8 mg/L, conductance from 1,207 to 1,234 µS/cm, and pH from 7.1 to 7.6. Spawning water temperatures were 15.3–20.1 °C for razorback suckers and 20.4–21.6 °C for bonytail.

Non-native species are a major concern. Largemouth bass and bluegill were removed in November 2002 but others continue to occur. The authors note that removal of large predators has often increased smaller and intermediate-sized predators (Smith and Brooks 2000; Demers et al. 2001; Davis 2003), so standing crop may be unchanged while size structure shifts. Under optimal foraging theory, small predators are expected to feed on the small larvae that are being lost.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and site selection**

- Razorback suckers spawned from late January to mid-March over gravel and large cobble at the levee toe (2–3 m depth). Spawning began before field work started on 18 February. Disturbed substrate covered about 90 m² (3 m wide, roughly 30 m long). Surrounding substrate was covered by about 10 cm of fine sediment.
- Razorback spawning density averaged 4.76 fish/m² (range 1.19–9.0; n = 286). With the 2002 population estimate, the authors suggest nearly one third of the sucker population was on the spawning area at a given time.
- Bonytail spawned along the levee shoreline in mid-April, in water shallower than 50 cm, in small areas (<3 m²) where beaver had disturbed the riprap. Bonytail density over spawning sites averaged 17.44 fish/m² (3.6–55.4; n = 222). The authors estimate that only about 5% of the community spawned at any one time.
- Bonytail spawning was brief (hours rather than days). Only two definite spawning acts were recorded, each lasting 1–2 seconds (razorback-type acts lasted about 2–4 seconds).
- Razorback suckers fin vigorously during spawning, flushing fines and leaving the gravel relatively clean. Substrate disturbance (scour or beaver activity) appears important for site selection.

**Spawning behavior and egg predation**

- Spawning was accompanied by feeding frenzies. Razorback suckers fed on their own eggs, and bonytail fed on razorback eggs or concentrated at the edge of spawning sites to feed on emerging larvae. Bonytail were never seen scavenging razorback eggs.
- Razorback males made up 80–90% of the spawning group. Razorbacks displayed a distinctive eye-rolling "winking" behavior exposing the sclera (observed in more than 50 instances). Its function is unknown.

**Bonytail telemetry and behavior**

- Adult bonytail were strictly nocturnal and cover-oriented. All ten tracked fish remained in riprap cavities during daylight and did not enter open water until about 30 minutes after sunset. They returned no later than about 30 minutes before sunrise.
- Tracked fish showed high fidelity to specific zones and cavities. Weak and changing signals suggested fish moved several meters into the riprap.
- Fish retreated into riprap when disturbed by outboard motors or rowing.
- No mortalities were detected during the telemetry period.
- Schooling occurred at all life stages. Small schools (up to about 50 fish under 10 cm) and larger aggregations (>100 larvae and fry) were seen under overhead cover. Older juveniles (10–15 cm) were fewer and more dispersed. Adults over 20 cm were rare on camera (about 5 per hour), and fish over 30 cm were seen only at night.

**Diet**

- Adult bonytail gut contents included algae, vegetation, small fish, and crayfish. Small bonytail fed at the surface on large zooplankton and invertebrates.
- Gut samples from 30 fish were collected: 20 just before dawn (most material) and 10 just after dusk (very little). Tapeworm was found in 4 of 30 fish (13%). The summary reports at least 5 of 27 adults (18%) infested with an unidentified intestinal tapeworm.

**Larval recruitment**

- Ten razorback larvae were collected by light trap during the week of 24 March 2003 (8.5–9.6 mm, mean 9.0 mm). One had consumed 25 [[Daphnia]].
- Four bonytail-type larvae (7.8–15.8 mm, mean 11.1 mm) were collected in May. Identification was pending.

- The bonytail catch rate in May was nearly twice that of the other efforts. Only three bonytail were caught in July, possibly because of reduced summer activity.

**Mortality and tag effects**

- Five dead adults (one bonytail, four razorbacks) were found in the week of 22 July. None showed external signs of disease, parasites, or wounds. The authors suggest a common event such as lightning or poisoning may have caused the deaths.
- Recaptured tagged bonytail showed chafing around the caudal peduncle, where cable-tie teeth cut into the skin. The authors recommend a wider banding material or electrician's tape. Otherwise, they consider the method promising for short-term studies.

**Non-native predation**

- Bullfrog tadpoles and red swamp crayfish were frequent on spawning sites. Video densities were 0.89 tadpoles/m² at the razorback site, 3.66 at the bonytail site, and 2.84 at controls. Based on control densities, the authors estimate about 57,500 tadpoles in CHLP. Using a published rate of 17 catfish larvae per day, they estimate tadpoles could eat nearly one million fish larvae per day.
- Tadpoles ate all 300 razorback eggs within about 90 hours (100% egg predation, from an initial 75 tadpoles).
- Crayfish (two per tank) killed 96.7% of 16.2 mm razorback fry within 72 hours without cover. Cover results were inconclusive: deep cover yielded 70% loss and shallow cover yielded 83% loss, but the shallow-cover fry were larger (20.5 mm).
- Among tested predators on 10–16 mm razorback larvae, mortality was 100% for rainbow trout, above 85% for bonytail, red shiner, and yellow bullhead, and low for bullfrog tadpoles and [[Bluegill]]. The highest instantaneous predation rate was 0.455 larvae per predator per hour for adult rainbow trout. Bullfrog tadpoles had the lowest, 0.015.
- Mixed-prey tests with [[Fathead Minnow]] showed no significant preference by [[Bluegill]], [[Channel Catfish]], or [[Largemouth Bass]] for razorback suckers over fathead minnows. Prey were similar in size and surviving prey were not size-selected.
- Control mortality averaged 1.03% (SE 0.46) across 29 control tanks (580 larvae total).

**Conclusions and planned work for 2004**

- Adult bonytail use the entire pond, but spawning has occurred only on disturbed rock and gravel along the river levee in the past two years. Spawning elsewhere, especially during high water, cannot be ruled out.
- Dark cover in riprap cavities is probably essential habitat. Cavity use may have reduced avian predation historically but may now increase risk from channel and flathead catfish.
- The authors describe small non-native predators as "an overwhelming obstacle to recovery that we may never fully resolve."
- Planned work includes tracking juveniles (15–20 cm) with smaller transmitters, large winged hoop netting, expanded gut sampling, at least four seasonal fish acoustic surveys, repeat tank tests on tadpoles and crayfish, and tests of large and medium predators.
