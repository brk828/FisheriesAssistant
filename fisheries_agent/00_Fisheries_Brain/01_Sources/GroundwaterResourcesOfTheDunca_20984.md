# Ground-Water Resources of the Duncan Basin, Arizona

#hydrogeology #groundwater #aquifer

## Executive Summary

This 1946 [[United States Geological Survey]] report, prepared in cooperation with the [[Arizona State Land Department]], evaluates the ground-water resources of the [[Duncan Basin]] on the [[Gila River]] in Greenlee County, Arizona. The study was undertaken because the Arizona State Legislature (1945) funded investigations of every ground-water basin in the state, and state regulation of ground-water withdrawals requires data on quantity, quality, use, source, and movement. The principal proven reservoir is the sand and gravel fill of the [[Inner Valley Alluvium]], which supplies all irrigation wells in the basin. Recharge is dominated by canal seepage, irrigation return, underflow and rainfall, and the aquifer is closely linked to river flow, so withdrawals are expected to reduce streamflow. Water quality is generally moderate, but sodium-rich well water and high [[Fluoride]] levels limit some uses.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and cooperation**
- Ground-water regulation in Arizona requires adequate information on quantity, quality, use, source, and movement of ground water.
- Investigation conducted by the [[United States Geological Survey]] under a cooperative agreement with the [[Arizona State Land Department]] (O. C. Williams, Commissioner).
- Field data were collected in 1939–1941 in cooperation with the State of Arizona and the United States Engineer Department; periodic water-level measurements and pumpage inventories have been made each year since 1941.
- Field personnel included H. M. Babcock, D. E. Bratton, R. L. Cushman, J. M. Cushing, H. G. Fernandez, L. C. Halpenny (engineers), R. B. Morrison (geologist), and J. D. Hem (chemist), under Samuel F. Turner, district engineer (Ground Water).

**Location and physical setting**
- The Duncan Basin is part of a structural trough extending from near Clifton, Arizona, southeastward toward Lordsburg, New Mexico.
- The basin lies entirely within Greenlee County, between the confluence of the San Francisco and Gila Rivers and the point where the Gila River crosses the Arizona-New Mexico state line.
- Southeast of the state line the trough is called [[Virden Valley]]; the remaining interior-drainage area is the [[Animas Basin]]. The Duncan/Virden division is administrative, with no geologic or hydrologic basis, and the report treats the trough as interrelated.
- The lower end is bounded by the [[Peloncillo Mountains]], through which the Gila River has cut a narrow gorge. The Steeple Rock Mountains lie along the northeast side and the Peloncillo Mountains along the southwest side.
- The basin is 2 to 15 miles wide and about 23 miles long, comprising uncultivated upland slopes and an inner valley 1/6 mile to 2½ miles wide that contains all irrigated land.
- Inner-valley elevation ranges from 3,235 feet at the gaging station at the lower end to 3,736 feet at the state line.

**Climate**
- Precipitation is about 10 inches per year at Duncan (five-year U.S. Weather Bureau record).
- No temperature records exist for the Duncan Basin. At [[Safford Basin]] (nearest comparable area, elevation 2,900 feet) the mean annual temperature is 62.5°F.
- The frost-free season at Safford lasts about six months. (The 1944 Safford temperature range is garbled in the scanned text.)

**Development history**
- The basin was first settled by farmers around the mid-1870s (the scan reads "1275"; the exact year is uncertain).
- Farmers originally irrigated with diverted Gila River water. In dry years the river supply proved inadequate, and beginning about 1935 (scan reads "19%5") ground water supplemented surface supply.
- In 1945 there were 27 irrigation wells in operation; the number increased to 40 by January 1, 1946 (scanned value uncertain), with further drilling in progress.
- The 1940 census lists 151 irrigated farms with a total farm population of 1,156 in Greenlee County. A field survey by Geological Survey engineers showed 4,669 acres under irrigation in 1941.
- Principal crops: alfalfa, grain, cotton and vegetables.

**Previous investigations** (cited in the report)
- Surface water: [[United States Geological Survey]] annual reports (from 1899 onward); J. B. Lippincott, *Storage of Water on Gila River, Ariz.* (Water-Supply Paper 33, 1900).
- Ground water: S. F. Turner and others, *Water Resources of Safford and Duncan-Virden Valleys* (1941); R. B. Morrison and H. M. Babcock, well and spring records (1942); R. B. Morrison, *Geology and Ground-Water Conditions in the Duncan Basin* (1942, unpublished); annual water-level publications for 1940–1945; A. T. Schwennesen, *Ground Water in the Animas, Playas, Hachita and San Luis Basins* (Water-Supply Paper 422, 1918).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and aquifer framework**
- The basin is filled with unconsolidated gravel, sand, silt and clay derived from hard volcanic lava, granite, gneiss and porphyry in the surrounding mountain blocks. Older sedimentary rocks are exposed about six miles upstream along Apache Creek.
- Mountain blocks serve two functions: (1) higher elevations receive more rainfall and supply a large share of basin water; (2) relatively impermeable rocks confine ground water within the basin.
- Ground water occurs mainly in the basin fill rather than in the mountain rocks, which may yield springs from fractures and weathered zones.

**Older alluvial fill**
- Deposited in an enclosed basin, forming a playa or semi-playa lake along the basin axis.
- Thickness may be 1,090 feet or more (scanned value). No deep wells exist in the Duncan Basin; the estimate is based on the [[Safford Basin]], where the deepest well reached 3,767 feet without reaching bedrock and encountered several deep water-bearing beds. Deep aquifers may therefore exist in the Duncan Basin.
- Near the mountains, the "gravel zone" (1 to 2 miles wide on each side) is partly consolidated and moderately permeable. Calcrete (caliche) layers originally capped the upper gravel zone but have been cut through by streams.
- Grades inward from interbedded sand and silt, to silt with sand, to the impermeable "lake-bed zone" of silt and clay with local sand stringers. Traces of salt and gypsum occur in places, but no rock salt or gypsum beds are known.

**Inner valley alluvium and tributary washes**
- After deposition of the older fill, the Gila River cut a valley through it, then cut a second, narrower valley about 200 feet deep, which was refilled to 50 to 100 feet with river and tributary-wash sediments.
- An intermediate erosion surface on the older fill lies about 50 to 100 feet below the original depositional level and forms the main "mesa" level above the river plain near Duncan (scan reads "59").
- Inner-valley deposits (gravel, sand and silt) are discontinuous; wells very close together may encounter water-bearing beds at very different depths. Water percolates downward along circuitous routes to recharge the underlying reservoir.
- Tributary-wash alluvium is often only a foot or two thick and cannot supply wells, though some larger washes support domestic and stock wells.

**Sources and movement of ground water**
- Older fill: recharged by rainfall and stream seepage where streams cross sand and gravel outcrops near the mountains. Direct rainfall recharge is small because caliche acts as a partial barrier, except where tributary channels cut through it. Water moves toward the basin axis, where it may become artesian in sand and gravel fingers within the lake beds and becomes mineralized through contact with salty clays and silts.
- Inner valley: derived from (1) upward seepage and springs from the older fill; (2) underflow of the Gila River and tributary washes; (3) rainfall and irrigation infiltration (canals and fields). If the water table falls below the river, river recharge also occurs.
- Per Schwennesen, ground water from the Animas Basin may enter the Duncan Basin from the south near Franklin; this is also suggested by changes in Gila River chemistry near Franklin.

**Discharge pathways**
- Artesian springs along faults, e.g., in Rainville Wash east of Duncan and along the Gila River. Some faults are sealed with clay and do not transmit water.
- Upward movement through the most permeable parts of the lake-bed zone into inner-valley alluvium, detected by its higher mineralization relative to river-alluvium water.
- Phreatophytes (plants drawing moisture directly from the water table), evaporation from wet river-bottom land, underflow out of the basin, and seepage into the river.

**Surface water relations**
- Between the state line and the Duncan highway bridge, the river usually lost flow to heavy pumping, though it gained during periods of little pumping. Mineralization increased, suggesting highly mineralized seepage (high in sodium sulfate and chloride).
- Between Duncan and Cosper's Crossing (one mile north of Sheldon), the river lost flow and was frequently dry at Sheldon, with the dry reach extending about five miles at low flow.
- At low flow, the river gained at least 8 cubic feet per second in the two miles immediately above York Canyon, with water of very good quality, partly from large tributary washes and partly from forced underflow where the inner valley fill narrows.

**Relationship between pumping, river and water table**
- The water table is usually controlled by river level, so declines are slower than where no perennial stream exists.
- Pumping lowers the water level in proportion to volume pumped, forming a cone of depression that deepens and expands over time.
- Phreatophyte water use peaks in July and August and can lower river-bottom water tables considerably; some species use up to 5 acre-feet per acre per year at average density.
- Heavy irrigation with river water raises water levels through percolation and reduced pumping.

## Key Findings

**Recharge and discharge (October 1, 1939 – October 1, 1940)**
- Total recharge to the inner valley from all sources: about 28,000 acre-feet.
- Recharge from older alluvial fill: about 2,500 acre-feet (equal to the discharge from the older fill).
- Underflow of the Gila River through the inner-valley fill at the state line: about 10 second-feet, or 7,200 acre-feet per year (calculated from a 6-mile-wide fill, average saturated thickness, a water-table slope, and laboratory permeability tests).
- Recharge from canal and irrigation sources in the Duncan-Virden Valley: about 17,000 acre-feet. The report states that about one-half of surface water diverted and one-fourth of water pumped enters the ground-water reservoir. Using the 60 percent share for the Arizona portion (4,669 of 7,769 irrigated acres), recharge in the Duncan Basin was about 10,200 acre-feet.
- Rainfall recharge to the inner valley: between 350 and 700 acre-feet.
- Phreatophyte use in the Duncan-Virden Valley: about 12,500 acre-feet, of which about 75 percent (9,400 acre-feet) occurred in the Duncan Basin.
- Evaporation from wetted lands in the Duncan Basin: about 1,200 acre-feet.
- Combined evaporation and transpiration in the river-bottom area: about 10,600 acre-feet.
- Underflow out of the basin at the Clifton gaging station: less than 0.5 cubic foot per second, about 350 acre-feet per year.
- Ground-water discharge to the river measured in an earlier investigation: 4 to 25 cubic feet per second; the river may lose water to the ground during heavy pumping.

**Pumpage**
- Irrigation-well pumpage ranged from a minimum of 1,348 acre-feet in 1941 to a maximum of 8,600 acre-feet in 1944.
- Pumpage in 1944 was reported as 34 times that of 1940 (scanned value; the multiplier may be misread).
- Wells yield 170 to 2,250 gallons per minute; depths 29 to 150 feet; pumping lift 10 to 45 feet; diameter 6 to 24 inches (scanned value).
- Most wells supplement surface diversions; only a few serve as the sole irrigation supply.

**Safe yield**
- Defined (from Meinzer, 1931) as "the practicable rate of withdrawing water from it perennially for human use."
- In the Duncan Basin the water table slopes toward the Gila River, so the aquifer predominantly feeds the river. Evaporated and transpired pumped water is therefore an eventual loss of river flow.
- During heavy pumping, parts of the basin reverse, with the river recharging the aquifer; pumping then increases the water-table slope away from the river. Large withdrawals reduce streamflow in either case, so safe yield requires appraising both surface and ground water.

**Water quality**
- Samples from 24 wells varied greatly; dissolved solids ranged from 250 to 2,500 ppm, with most between 300 and 900 ppm.
- Older-fill water has higher total mineral content, relatively more sodium salts and fluoride, and lower total hardness than recent alluvium.
- Gila River at the state line (low flow, 1940–1941): dissolved solids 180 to 406 ppm, calcium bicarbonate type, suitable for irrigation.
- Irrigation wells: 9 of the 24 samples; dissolved solids 300 to about 2,500 ppm; 6 of the 9 contained more sodium than calcium plus magnesium.
- Domestic wells: most moderately mineralized (under 1,000 ppm dissolved solids).
- Fluoride: 15 wells sampled; 14 contained 1.1 to 9.6 ppm; one well at Sheldon had none measurable. The highest value (9.6 ppm) was in the deeper of two Duncan town wells.
- Hardness: 52 to 578 ppm; most samples classed as relatively hard. The softest waters occurred near Duncan where sodium was high.

**Conclusions and regulatory considerations**
- The principal proven ground-water reservoir is the sand and gravel fill of the inner valley along the Gila River, and all adequate-capacity irrigation wells draw from it.
- Ground-water withdrawals are intimately linked to surface-water rights; regulation must consider the interrelation of the two sources.
- Four source types are discussed: (1) ground water from canal and irrigation seepage (wells could intercept part of it or canals could be lined); (2) ground water from tributary-wash inflow and outcrop recharge that augments flow above York (interception reduces river flow); (3) water saved by clearing river-bottom phreatophytes or lowering the water table, which could return to the river for later diversion; (4) surface water that moves downstream to meet prior claims, much of which is used by phreatophytes or lost to evaporation.
- Water from the Gila River is usually suitable for irrigation; some well water is too high in sodium for safe irrigation without occasional Gila River water.
- Fluoride content in all but one domestic well sampled exceeds levels considered desirable for small children.
- Dissolved solids in Gila River water below Guthrie appear slightly higher and hardness appreciably lower than at the state line. Data are too limited to determine whether total dissolved load leaving the basin exceeds the load entering it.
