# Site-Specific Water Quality Studies of the Straight River, Minnesota: Complex Effluent Toxicity, Zinc Toxicity, and Biological Survey Relationships

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. EPA Project Summary (EPA/600/S3-85/005, March 1985) reports a study by [[Anthony R. Carlson]] and [[Thomas H. Roush]] of the Environmental Research Laboratory, Duluth, Minnesota. The study tested whether laboratory toxicity results could predict effects on resident river biota downstream of a [[Sewage Treatment Plant Effluent]] (STP) that carries high concentrations of [[Zinc]]. Laboratory toxicity tests used [[Ceriodaphnia Reticulata]] and the [[Fathead Minnow]]. The authors also related effluent and zinc toxicity to biological survey data from the [[Straight River]].

A strong correlation was found between effluent toxicity to daphnids and zinc toxicity. A correlation between toxicity and adverse impact on river biota could not be established. Benthic macroinvertebrate and fish communities at a station 2.7 miles (3.2 km) downstream did not appear to be adversely affected. On this basis, the authors concluded that a [[Site-Specific Water Quality Criteria]] average zinc concentration of 145 µg/L, about three times the national average, would protect Straight River biota.

## Background & Description

The bioavailability and toxicity of a metal such as zinc can change with water chemistry and physical conditions. The [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] published guidelines for deriving site-specific criteria that account for these factors (U.S. EPA, 1983a). The report describes two derivation approaches:

- **Resident species testing:** A prescribed number of resident species are tested in site water to meet minimum data requirements, and a site criterion is calculated.
- **Indicator/surrogate species testing:** Sensitive species from the same population are tested in both site water and [[Laboratory Water]] under similar conditions. The ratio of site-water toxicity to laboratory-water toxicity adjusts the national criterion to a site-specific value.

Both approaches assume that (1) differences in toxicity between laboratory and site water result from chemical factors (for example, complexing ligands) or physical factors (for example, adsorption) that alter [[Biological Availability]] and toxicity, and (2) the selected test species integrate these differences and directly measure the capacity of site water to raise or lower toxicity. The authors note that single-chemical criteria assume no other pollutants are present, which rarely occurs in real effluents. Zinc is one of many components in the effluent that may affect its bioavailability and toxicity.

The study had two objectives:

1. Evaluate sublethal [[Toxicity Testing]] endpoints (survival, growth, and reproduction) from effluent dilution and receiving-water tests for predicting impacts on resident biota.
2. Determine whether site-specific criteria are protective under the complex ambient conditions created by a point-source effluent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study setting:** The Straight River receives effluent from an STP with high zinc concentrations. Water and biota were sampled under summer (three times), fall (once), and winter (once) conditions over a one-year period. Sampling covered the following components:

- Effluent toxicity tests
- Effluent toxicity in receiving water
- Zinc toxicity in receiving water and in reference (laboratory) water
- Benthic macroinvertebrate, fish, and plankton survey data

**Flow conditions:** The authors reported that river flow was often higher before sampling. Susceptible biota may therefore not have been exposed long enough to react to adverse conditions. Higher flow may also have allowed recolonization that masked effluent effects.

**Exposure limitations:** The authors suggest that the lack of correlation between daphnid toxicity and in-river biological impact, measured about 1 mile (1.6 km) below the discharge, may reflect insufficient numbers of sampling and testing days. Effluent and river water were not tested over enough days to characterize the duration of exposure experienced by river biota.

**Plankton:** The total number of plankton taxa differed markedly from the reference station on one of three sampling dates. The authors suggest this could result from predation, or from upstream effluent effects that caused plankton death or sedimentation.

## Key Findings

- **Effluent toxicity:** A strong correlation was established between the effluent's toxicity to daphnids and the toxicity of zinc.
- **Biological impact:** Toxicity could not be correlated with adverse impact on river biota.
- **Benthic macroinvertebrates:** At the station 2.7 miles (3.2 km) downstream, the total number of taxa was similar to or greater than at the reference station on all four sampling dates.
- **Fish:** Total species numbers were similar between the downstream and reference stations on the two dates sampled.
- **Plankton:** Taxa totals differed markedly on one of three dates. The cause was not confirmed.
- **Zinc concentrations:** At the 2.7-mile station, zinc ranged from 100 to 154 µg/L across four sampling dates and averaged 144 µg/L on three of four dates.
- **Proposed criterion:** The authors concluded that a site-specific average zinc concentration of 145 µg/L, about three times the national average concentration, would be protective of Straight River biota. This conclusion assumes that aquatic life can tolerate some stress and occasional adverse effects, as stated in the national and site-specific guidelines.
- **Limitations:** The study's sampling frequency was limited, and river flow and recolonization may have affected results.
