# Impact of the Desert Pupfish and Western Mosquitofish on Pond Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 *Hilgardia* study compared the effects of the native [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) and the introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis affinis*) on aquatic insects and zooplankton in 12 experimental ponds in Riverside, California, during 1974 and 1975. Both fish reduced beneficial insect predators, [[Culex Tarsalis|mosquito larvae]] and zooplankton to comparable degrees. The authors concluded that *C. macularius* is an acceptable substitute for *G. affinis* in [[Mosquito Control]], and may be preferred where other native fishes occur because it shows little piscivory.

## Background & Description

The [[Western Mosquitofish]] has long been used in [[Mosquito Control]] worldwide. Its impacts on native fauna have raised concern, including predation on juvenile native mosquito-eating fishes, cyprinid fishes and juvenile black bass in hatcheries. Earlier work suggested it may also displace native fish through competition for food and space, and disrupt ecosystems by preying on [[Biological Control|beneficial insect predators]] and zooplankton.

The [[Desert Pupfish]] cohabits with a wide range of fishes and aquatic organisms in its native [[Lower Sonoran Desert]] habitats, with apparently minimal ecosystem disruption. It had been proposed as a native alternative to *G. affinis* for mosquito control. This study aimed to:

- Compare the effects of introduced *G. affinis* and a southern California strain of *C. macularius* on principal biotic elements of natural pond and rice ecosystems.
- Determine the basis of food selection in these habitats.

**Study design:** Twelve earthen ponds (4 x 7 m) were supplied with well water. Three treatments (*C. macularius* only, *G. affinis* only, and no fish as control) had four replicates each, assigned at random in 1974 and reassigned in 1975. Water depth was uniform apart from a 0.5 m shallow periphery. In 1974, two replicates per treatment were planted with rice (*Colusa 1600*) and maintained at 0.25 m depth. In 1975, no rice was planted and depth was 0.36 m. Study periods were June 8 to October 24, 1974, and June 17 to September 5, 1975.

**Stocking:** Each replicate received 10 mature males and 15 mature females of each species, equivalent to 3,625 fish per acre (8,957 per hectare).

**Methods:**
- Aquatic insects were sampled with a [[Water Column Sampler]]. In 1974, immature [[Chironomidae|chironomid]] densities were also measured with benthic trays.
- [[Zooplankton]] were collected with a qualitative zooplankton sampler and counted under microscopes.
- Fish were trapped with minnow traps, fixed in formalin, and their stomach contents analysed ([[Stomach Analysis]]).
- A preliminary study established [[Diel Feeding Patterns]]. *C. macularius* stomachs were fullest in the 3 hours beginning 2 hours before sunset, and *G. affinis* peaked 1 to 5 hours after sunrise.
- Density differences were tested with Duncan's New Multiple Range Test on transformed data. Differences at the 10 percent level were treated as significant. Fish stomach contents were compared with pond densities using bivariate correlation at the 5 percent level.

In 1975, 105 *C. macularius* (47 mature females, 28 mature males, 30 juveniles) and 108 *G. affinis* (42 mature females, 38 mature males, 28 juveniles) were examined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions:** Pond temperatures ranged from 18 to 36°C in 1974 and 20 to 33°C in 1975. By mid-July, filamentous Chlorophyta (mainly *Chara*, *Cladophora* and *Spirogyra*) covered 60 to 90 percent of pond bottoms. By September, Chlorophyta and [[Zannichellia Palustris|horned pondweed]] (*Zannichellia palustris*) covered more than 80 percent of bottoms, with weed mass filling 30 to 90 percent of the water volume. In 1974, rice reached 1 m and headed out by early August, then declined and lodged in October.

**Reproduction:** Both species reproduced within the study period. In 1975, *G. affinis* fry appeared 10 days after introduction, and *C. macularius* fry 10 days later. *G. affinis* young-of-the-year females developed about one month earlier than those of *C. macularius*.

**Dominant organisms:**
- Predatory beetles were mainly Dytiscidae, notably *Laccophilus terminalis* and the hydrophilid *Berosus punctatissimus*.
- Odonata were mostly Anisoptera, with *Libellula pulchella* dominant in 1975, along with the zygopteran *Anomalagrion hastatum*.
- Hemipterans were sparse. The notonectid *Buenoa scimitra* occurred in 1974, and corixids were recorded in 1975.
- Benthic [[Chironomidae|chironomid midge larvae]], chiefly *Chironomus* spp., declined steadily from June to September in both years, peaking in control ponds at about 270 to 360 larvae per square metre.
- [[Culex Tarsalis]] larvae and [[Ephemeroptera]] (*Callibaetis* sp.) occurred at low densities.
- Zooplankton in 1975 were four to eight times more abundant than in 1974, possibly due to heavier nutrient supplementation. [[Cladocera]] outnumbered [[Copepoda]] and [[Ostracoda]]. Dominant forms included *Ceriodaphnia pulchella*, *Alona* sp., *Diaptomus pallidus* and *Eucyclops agilis*.

## Key Findings

**Insect predators and mosquitoes**
- Ponds with fish held fewer total insect predators in both years (P < .10), especially from August to October. Reductions were also seen in early July.
- Both fish fed mainly on coleopteran larvae. The effect was density-dependent and strongest in July.
- *G. affinis* had more effect on aquatic Coleoptera than *C. macularius*, but significant evidence was found only in 1974.
- Stomach contents correlated significantly with pond coleopteran larval density (r = 0.975).
- Both fish reduced mosquito larvae. In 1974, reductions (P < .05) occurred in all fish-treated ponds throughout the season, and *G. affinis* controlled low mosquito densities as effectively as *C. macularius* in rice.
- Odonata naiads were significantly reduced (P = .05) late in the season in both years, but stomach analysis showed little intensive foraging on them.
- Corixids were little affected, yet both fish consumed them. *C. macularius* feeding on corixids had not been reported before.

**Chironomid midges**
- Chironomid populations were not notably reduced by either fish. Both fish ate chironomids, but *C. macularius* took them at all sampling intervals, while *G. affinis* did so mainly in July.
- Bay and Anderson (1966) found larvae averaged 21 percent higher in ponds with *Gambusia*. A similar trend appeared in some months of this study.

**Zooplankton**
- Zooplankton as a group were reduced in all fish ponds in both years. In 1974, reductions occurred only in late July, while in 1975 they appeared in August and September.
- Rice shelter in 1974 appeared to protect *Alona* sp. and *Ceriodaphnia pulchella* from *C. macularius* predation, possibly because *Simocephalus vetulus*, being larger, was more often taken.
- *G. affinis* grazed more on accessible planktonic forms such as *Diaptomus pallidus*, whereas *C. macularius* foraged on littoral and benthic zooplankton.
- Ostracods were not significantly reduced, yet they appeared in 90 percent of juvenile *C. macularius* stomachs in August at 6.4 per stomach.
- Both fish had a greater impact on total zooplankton biomass and community diversity than [[Bluegill Sunfish]] reported in earlier work.

**Other food sources**
- The snail *Physa virgata* was a major food item, present in 58 percent of *G. affinis* and 33 percent of *C. macularius* stomachs in August.
- Vegetation made up most of the *C. macularius* stomach contents in August and September (65 to 85 percent frequency), with greater consumption as algae accumulated.
- *G. affinis* ate many terrestrial insects from the pond surface, such as aphid nymphs, thrips, springtails, ants and adult Diptera.
- *G. affinis* was piscivorous: 6 percent of stomachs contained fry. No fry were found in *C. macularius* stomachs.

**Conclusions**
- Both fish showed comparable consumption of beneficial insect predators and produced no mosquito upsets or phytoplankton blooms at the stocking rate used.
- Although *G. affinis* reproduced faster, its ecosystem effects were similar to those of the lower numbers of *C. macularius*.
- *C. macularius* is an acceptable substitute for *G. affinis* in southwestern mosquito control. It may be preferred where native fishes occur, given its minimal piscivory.
- *C. macularius* is more [[Salinity Tolerance|euryhaline]], tolerating 4.6 to 6.8 percent salinity in its natural habitat. *G. affinis* shows high laboratory mortality at these levels and is normally found below 2.5 percent. *C. macularius* may therefore suit diverse saline environments.
