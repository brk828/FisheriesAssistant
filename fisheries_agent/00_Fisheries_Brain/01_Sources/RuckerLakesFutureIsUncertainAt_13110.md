# Rucker Lake's Future Is Uncertain: Sediment, Wildfire and the Push for an Urban Fishery in Douglas, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonid

## Executive Summary

This 1998 newspaper feature from *The Daily Dispatch* (Douglas, Arizona) examines the future of [[Rucker Lake]], a small, human-made trout fishing lake in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] that has been degraded by sediment since a major wildfire in 1994. The [[Coronado National Forest]] has deferred restoration until the upper [[Rucker Canyon]] watershed recovers. Frustrated local residents have turned to a proposed urban lake along the Mexican border, which [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] biologists and Forest Service staff have said they would support as a partner, though neither agency has the personnel or funding to lead it.

## Background & Description

Rucker Lake is not a natural lake. During the 1930s the [[Civilian Conservation Corps]] excavated an area of about four acres and dammed [[Rucker Creek]]. The dam stores water but also traps gravel and sediment that would normally move downstream. Left alone, the lake filled naturally every seven to 10 years and had to be dredged to remain usable for recreational fishing. According to [[Bob Lefevre]], the Coronado National Forest hydrologist, the lake has been dredged three times since 1983.

For about 60 years the lake provided trout fishing and lakeside camping. Long-time Douglas resident [[Gary Clark]] described it as one of the "special places" he hoped to keep using throughout his life, and recalled where his son caught his first trout there.

The article also covers the [[Douglas Border Patrol Checkpoint]] on Highway 191, which the Border Patrol describes as a second line of defense. Spokesman Daniels said the Douglas area had the highest immigrant apprehension numbers in the Tucson Sector, and that the checkpoint was responsible for nearly 2,000 apprehensions. He also stated that the Douglas area had 18,076 apprehensions for the month of January. This material is only tangentially related to the lake and is included here for completeness.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Wildfire and erosion.** On June 28, 1994, a lightning strike on [[Rattlesnake Peak]] started what was then the largest known wildfire on the Douglas Ranger District. Within 28 days it had burned 27,500 acres in the northern half of the Chiricahua Mountains, including upper Rucker Canyon. The fire destroyed vegetation that had stabilized the watershed. Rain, wind and storms that followed caused significant erosion, sending more sediment and gravel downstream than before the fire. The dam held this material, and gravel buried the [[Rucker Lake Campground]] so that only the tops of picnic tables were visible.

**Watershed recovery.** Forest Service hydrologists judged that dredging would not be cost effective until vegetation was re-established in upper Rucker Canyon. Lefevre later found that parts of the watershed were recovering well, while other areas remained badly eroded. He concluded the watershed had not returned to its pre-fire condition and was unstable at best. Even with pre-fire conditions restored, the lake would remain a high-maintenance facility requiring periodic dredging, because the watershed historically carried heavy sediment loads.

**Fisheries management.** [[Will Hayes]], the Arizona Game and Fish Department fisheries biologist, said it is increasingly difficult to establish non-native fish fisheries in forest and wilderness areas. He cited heightened scrutiny from environmental groups and funding limitations. Arizona Game and Fish's mission includes creating more fishing opportunities while balancing the needs of native species.

**Urban lake concept.** Local proponents are considering a recreational lake in the floodplain known as "the jungle," a tangle of shrubs, weeds and small trees between the Wal-Mart Shopping Center and Agua Prieta, Mexico. Water from Douglas and Mexico collects there during monsoon and heavy-rain periods. The concept includes recreational fishing, an RV park, a riparian area for wildlife viewing and birding, and an effluent treatment system to supply water to the lake. Proponents argue that the lake could reduce flooding in Mexico, which would give the project international scope and make it eligible for more grant funding. A riparian area within a wastewater complex could also create urban wildlife habitat, giving residents wildlife viewing close to town.

## Key Findings

- **Rucker Lake is structurally unstable as a fishery.** It was created by damming Rucker Creek in the 1930s, and sediment fills it every seven to 10 years. It has been dredged three times since 1983.
- **The 1994 wildfire changed the watershed.** The fire burned 27,500 acres within 28 days, and post-fire erosion has pushed far more sediment into the lake than before.
- **Restoration is on hold.** The Coronado National Forest will not act until the upper Rucker Canyon watershed has stabilized. Lefevre said recovery could take "another several years."
- **Community frustration is high.** Residents have written to senators and congressmen, and the City of Douglas passed a resolution asking the Forest Service to restore the lake. Local economic losses are also cited.
- **The Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Forest Service see limits.** Hayes raised questions about whether a restored lake would be cost effective if it silted in again, and about whether non-native stocking would remain permitted.
- **An urban lake is the preferred alternative.** The city's proposed border lake, with fishing, an RV park, a riparian area and effluent treatment, is being considered as a partnership project. The Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Forest Service have offered technical help and grant assistance but are not in a position to lead it.
- **Comparable project cited.** The [[Picacho Reservoir Project]] in Pinal County raised $5 million over four years to build an approximately 600-acre water recreation area with a picnic area, campground, boat ramp and designated swimming and boating zones.
- **Institutional perception is a problem.** Forest District Ranger [[Bill Wilcox]] said residents perceive the Forest Service as unresponsive, partly because earlier small fishing areas at Herb Martyr and John Hands were not replaced after they filled with sediment. He stated that a decision on Rucker Lake would follow watershed stabilization.
