# Life History and Ecology of the Sonoran Mud Turtle (*Kinosternon sonoriense*)

#herpetology #freshwater-ecology #kinosternidae #turtle-ecology #life-history

> **Note:** This source paper concerns a freshwater/terrestrial reptile (Sonoran Mud Turtle), not a fish species. Content has been structured according to the requested template but reflects chelonian (turtle) biology and its associated aquatic ecosystem rather than ichthyological data.

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report presents five years (1990–1994) of mark-recapture data on an isolated population of the [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense*) in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] of southeastern Arizona, focused on the West Turkey Creek (WTC) drainage and associated stock tanks. Researchers marked 580 individuals and recorded 913 recaptures, documenting growth rates, sexual maturity, clutch size, egg morphometrics, and reproductive allocation (lipids, shell mass). Key findings include a female-biased adult sex ratio (1:1.29), rapid juvenile growth (~17.5 mm carapace length/year), minimum female maturity at 5 years, average clutch size of 6.7 eggs, and evidence that at least 23% of females produce multiple clutches per season. The study also highlights a potential ecological threat: introduced [[Bullfrog|Bullfrogs]] (*Lithobates catesbeianus*), which may prey on hatchlings and reduce juvenile recruitment, threatening long-term population stability of this long-lived species.

## Background & Description

*Kinosternon sonoriense* is the only native freshwater turtle occurring in the Chiricahua Mountains and ranges across northwestern Mexico and the southwestern USA (western New Mexico through central/southern Arizona). It is a member of the family [[Kinosternidae]] (mud and musk turtles). As with most chelonians, *K. sonoriense* is long-lived, making it a valuable model organism for testing life-history theory contrasting long-lived versus short-lived taxa (per Williams 1966; Tinkle et al. 1970; Congdon & Gibbons 1990a).

Prior ecological studies of the species were limited to two prior works (Hulse 1982; Rosen 1987), leaving major gaps in knowledge regarding nesting ecology and age-specific biology. This study aimed to:
1. Establish long-term life-history trait data,
2. Identify sources of variation in these traits,
3. Provide baseline data to inform conservation/management of long-lived aquatic/riparian species.

### Study System & Methods
- **Study Area:** ~3 km stretch of West Turkey Creek (WTC, ~1675 m elevation) plus associated stock tanks (farm ponds) on tributaries; Rock Creek site added in 1994 (~5 km from WTC, isolated except via overland movement).
- **Capture Methods:** Baited hoop traps (float-equipped), hand capture in shallow pools, dip nets, and incidental land captures.
- **Marking:** Unique notch codes filed into marginal carapace scutes.
- **Morphometrics:** Carapace length (CL), plastron length (anterior + posterior), shell height/width, body mass (0.1 g precision).
- **Reproductive Assessment:** Radiography used to determine clutch size (CS), egg widths (XREW), and pelvic aperture width (indexing potential pelvic constraints on egg size), following Congdon & Gibbons (1987) methodology.
- **Hormonal Induction:** Oxytocin injections (1.5 ml/kg body mass) used on 15 Rock Creek females to induce oviposition for egg collection (yolk lipid analysis via Soxtec extraction with petroleum ether).
- **Age Determination:** Plastral growth ring counts (annuli) used for turtles with <9 rings; age class 0 defined as the embryonic/nest period; age class 1 = post-hatching yearlings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat Use:** ~90% of turtles were captured in stock tanks (artificial lentic ponds), only 5% in natural stream pools and 5% on land — suggesting a strong preference for lentic (still-water) habitats over the lotic (flowing) stream environment of WTC.
- **Paleohydrological Context:** The regional population is hypothesized to be a relict of ancient [[Lake Cochise]], a pluvial lake that dried ~10,000 years ago (Long 1966; Cameron & Lundin 1977), isolating turtle populations in permanent Chiricahua Mountain streams. Preference for lentic stock-tank habitats may reflect ancestral adaptation to lacustrine (lake-like) conditions.
- **Reproductive Phenology:** Nesting appears concentrated during the North American monsoon season (July–August). Evidence suggests embryonic diapause through fall/winter, with development resuming in spring (~80-day incubation; Ewert 1991), leading to hatchling emergence coinciding with subsequent summer rains — meaning total time from egg-laying to emergence may exceed 11 months.
- **Predation Threat — Introduced Bullfrogs:** Bullfrogs were introduced into the WTC drainage circa 1983 and have since reached high densities. They are known predators of turtle hatchlings (Schwalbe & Rosen 1988) and may suppress juvenile survivorship — a critical demographic parameter for maintaining stable populations in long-lived species — potentially driving population decline.
- **Isolation & Connectivity:** The Rock Creek subpopulation is geographically isolated from WTC by ~5 km, with turtle exchange requiring overland (terrestrial) dispersal, underscoring the ecological importance of terrestrial habitat corridors for this semi-aquatic species.

## Key Findings

### Population Structure & Demographics
- **Sample size:** 580 marked individuals; 913 recaptures (1990–1994); 573 captured alive + 7 found dead unmarked.
- **Age class composition (of 573 live captures):** 18.5% juveniles, 35.8% adult males, 45.7% adult females.
- **Sex ratio (adults):** 1 male : 1.29 females — significantly different from 1:1 (χ² = 6.93, p < 0.01).
- **Juvenile proportion** was roughly half that reported for a Tule Creek population (Hulse 1974), suggesting differing recruitment dynamics across populations.

### Growth & Body Size
- **Hatchlings (n=4):** averaged 3.0 g body mass, 22.2 mm CL (captured with egg teeth, early August).
- **Juvenile growth rate:** ~17.5 mm CL/year from hatching to age 6 (linear growth phase).
- **Adult females:** mean 130 mm CL (max 160 mm), mean 340 g body mass (max 610 g).
- **Adult males:** mean 128 mm CL (max 168 mm), mean 298 g body mass (max 660 g).
- **No significant sexual size dimorphism** between ages 5–10 yr (F₁,₃₅₁ = 0.88, p = 0.35).
- WTC turtles reached **larger maximum body sizes** than most other studied *K. sonoriense* populations in Arizona (Hulse 1974, 1982; Rosen 1987) and grew faster (17.5 mm/yr) than Sharp Spring population (~10 mm/yr) or *K. hirtipes* in Mexico (~14 mm/yr).

### Sexual Maturity
- **Minimum age at maturity (females):** 5 years.
- **Minimum size at maturity:** smallest gravid female = 106 mm CL, 150 g body mass.
- Minimum maturity size (105–110 mm CL) was **larger** than reported for Sharp Spring *K. sonoriense* and *K. hirtipes* (~90 mm CL both).
- Comparable minimum maturity age to *K. hirtipes* females (6 yr) in Mexico (Iverson et al. 1991).

### Reproductive Output
- **Clutch size:** mean 6.7 eggs (range 2–11; n = 231 clutches), significantly positively correlated with female body size (Spearman RHO = 0.62, p<0.05; linear model CS = –0.846 + 0.112·CL, r² = 0.42).
- **Multiple clutching:** minimum 23% (9/39) of females captured twice in a season produced ≥2 clutches; minimum inter-clutch interval = 20 days. Other populations reportedly produce up to 4 clutches/year (Rosen 1987), though estimates are inconsistent across studies.
- **Egg dimensions:**
  - Mean length: 28.93 mm (range 27.0–31.8)
  - Mean width (actual): 16.85 mm (range 15.8–18.2)
  - Mean width (radiographic, XREW): 16.94 mm (range 16.0–18.2) — not significantly different from actual measurement (paired t-test, t=–0.797, p>0.469)
  - Mean wet mass: 5.03 g (range 4.7–5.5)
  - Egg length:width ratio: 1.72
- **Egg width vs. body size:** significantly positively related to CL (slope XREW on CL = 0.041); average XREW increased by ~3 mm across 75 mm range of adult female CL.
- **Pelvic aperture constraint:** No evidence of pelvic constraint on egg width — slope of pelvic aperture width vs. CL (0.144) was **3.5× steeper** than slope of egg width vs. CL (0.041) (Wilks' Lambda = 0.7822, F₂,₁₇₂ = 69.88, p=0.0001). This contrasts with species like *Chrysemys picta marginata* and *Deirochelys reticularia*, where similar slopes suggest pelvic constraint does operate.
- **Clutch size vs. egg width trade-off:** Negative relationship between XREW and residuals of clutch size~CL regression (Spearman RHO = –0.2104, p=0.0008), suggesting a resource allocation trade-off between egg size and number within a given body size.

### Egg Composition & Yolk Biochemistry
- **Yolk dry mass:** mean 0.96 g (range 0.73–1.25 g)
- **Non-polar lipids (% of yolk dry mass):** mean 28.75% (range 26.47–29.87%)
- **Egg shell proportion:** 32.75% of total egg dry mass (2SE=1.3) — more similar to turtle species with **brittle-shelled eggs** (~40.7%) than **parchment-shelled eggs** (~19.25%; per Congdon & Gibbons 1985).
- **Yolk water content:** 60.2% by mass (2SE=3.2).
- Lipid proportion was **lower** than in *[[Kinosternon flavescens]]* but similar to *[[Kinosternon subrubrum]]* in South Carolina — species-level lipid allocation patterns may relate to whether hatchlings delay nest emergence (overwintering strategy).

### Proposed Reproductive Phenology Model
1. Ovulation/shelling begins in June.
2. Eggs retained in oviducts until monsoon rains (July–August).
3. Oviposition in terrestrial nests away from water.
4. Embryonic diapause through fall/winter.
5. Development resumes in spring (~80 days total development; Ewert 1991).
6. Hatchling emergence coincides with onset of summer monsoon rains — potentially >11 months from laying to emergence.
- This pattern (egg/embryo overwintering, not post-hatch overwintering) parallels *[[Kinosternon baurii]]* and *[[Deirochelys reticularia]]*, contrasting with some northern *Chrysemys picta* populations where hatchlings overwinter in the nest.

### Conservation Implication
- Long-lived turtle populations require **high juvenile survivorship** to remain stable (Congdon et al. 1993, 1994).
- Introduced **bullfrog predation** on hatchlings represents a plausible mechanism for reduced recruitment and potential population decline in the WTC *K. sonoriense* population — an important consideration for conservation management of isolated mountain aquatic ecosystems in the [[Sky Islands]] region of the Madrean Archipelago.
