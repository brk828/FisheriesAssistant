# Life History of Three Redhorse Species (Moxostoma) in the Des Moines River, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined the life history of three [[Moxostoma]] redhorse suckers in a 6.5-mile reach of the [[Des Moines River]] in central Iowa, from June 1960 through May 1961. The species were [[Golden Redhorse]], [[Northern Redhorse]], and [[Silver Redhorse]]. The work covered habitat use, movement (via fin-clip mark-recapture), length-weight relationships, condition, age and growth from [[Scale Analysis]], food habits, maturity, and fecundity. The author, William H. Meyer of the [[Iowa Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]], Iowa State University, framed the study as management-oriented, since redhorse numbers had been rising in the river and made up an estimated over 25 percent of river fish biomass.

## Background & Description

The three species belong to [[Moxostoma]], a genus of North American suckers in the family [[Catostomidae]]. The author notes that the genus has long been poorly understood. Before this work, the only comprehensive life history study of a redhorse was on the [[Black Redhorse]] in Missouri (Bowman, 1959).

Routine stream surveys by the Iowa Conservation Commission showed the redhorse population increasing in the Des Moines River since 1946. The author estimated that redhorses made up over 25 percent of the total river population by weight, which motivated the need for biological data to support fishery management.

The study area ran from the Fraser low-head dam upstream to the Boone waterworks dam (also low-head) downstream. Fish were sampled mainly by [[Electrofishing]] from a boat. Only three small redhorses were caught in more than 100 drag-seine hauls.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting:**
- The river generally floods in spring, causing greatly increased turbidity.
- Secchi disc readings ranged from 2.25 inches (spring 1960) to 17 inches (fall).
- Silt was the only pollution problem in this section.
- The bottom is sand with silt or gravel, with scattered rubble and boulder areas.
- Aquatic vegetation and bottom fauna are limited by fluctuating water levels and shifting sand.

**Associated fauna:** The most abundant fishes, in order, were carpsuckers (*Carpiodes* spp.), [[Carp]], redhorse, [[Channel Catfish]], [[Walleye]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Flathead Catfish]].

**Habitat partitioning:**
- Young golden and silver redhorses occupy slow-moving waters over soft bottoms, near overhanging banks, which likely offer protection from predators.
- Young northern redhorses use fast-water habitat.
- Adult golden and silver redhorses are widely distributed and show no strong affinity for hard or soft bottoms. Silver redhorse are somewhat more common where current is slower and long, deep pools occur.
- Adult northern redhorses have the strictest habitat requirements, usually found in fast water over rock, gravel, and rubble. They are occasionally found over thick silt behind eroded bank vegetation.

The author notes that reservoir formation on rivers could adversely affect redhorse populations and might serve as a control method.

## Key Findings

### Movement and Population Structure

- Of 1,642 redhorses fin-clipped and released (June through October 1960), 501 were recaptured (30.5%). The author notes that recaptures do not equal 501 distinct fish, since some were caught several times.
- Recapture rates by species: golden 340 of 875 (38.9%), northern 106 of 470 (22.6%), silver 55 of 297 (18.5%).
- Of all recaptures, 22.0% had moved upstream, 45.1% showed no movement, and 32.9% had moved downstream.
- Chi-square tests for downstream bias (χ² = 3.84 critical, 1 d.f., p = 0.05) gave 9.99 for golden redhorse (significant downstream movement), 0.27 for northern, 2.13 for silver, and 11.00 for all combined.
- Summer populations were judged "sedentary" by Funk's (1957) classification. Some stations had up to 55 percent recaptures of fish marked there in late summer 1960, indicating a stable population.
- Low-head dams at each end of the study area restricted upstream movement and limited downstream movement except during high water. The electric shocker was selective for larger fish, so results apply to fish over about 6 inches total length.

### Length-Weight Relationships and Condition

- Weight was regressed on total length using logarithmic, first-, second-, and third-degree equations. Logarithmic equations gave the best fit for each species (r² of 99.6 to 99.9%).
- Log-transformed exponents: northern 3.0207, golden 3.0980, silver 3.1243.
- Mean coefficients of condition (C_TL): northern 36.0, golden 38.9, silver 40.4.
- Condition showed seasonal trends, with lows following spawning and increases through summer and fall.
- Condition factors were highest before spawning, then dropped sharply. Silver and golden redhorse peaked in May, while northern redhorse peaked in April.
- The TL/FL ratio was the most stable length conversion factor. The declining TL/SL and FL/SL ratios with size indicate allometric growth, meaning the caudal fin grows more slowly than the rest of the body.

### Age and Growth

- Scales were read from 1,034 samples; only seven were unreadable due to regeneration.
- Ages were determined for 487 golden, 319 northern, and 196 silver redhorses. The author found no reason to doubt the validity of annuli as year marks.
- Young redhorses generally form annuli in June, while older fish complete annulus formation as late as August.
- Scale-length regressions: golden L = 1.00 + 1.4143S; northern L = 0.44 + 1.6505S; silver L = 1.25 + 1.4564S.
- Golden redhorse grew slowest and northern redhorse fastest.
- Northern and silver redhorses had their greatest length increments in the first year. Golden redhorse grew slightly better as yearlings, which the author attributes to their later spawning, leaving less time for young-of-year growth before the first annulus.
- Most growth occurred in late July, August, and early September.
- Growth did not differ noticeably between sexes in April and May 1961 data.
- Comparisons with other studies: Des Moines River golden redhorse grew faster than in Illinois and Missouri but slower than in several Oklahoma, Tennessee, Kansas, and Ohio waters. Northern redhorse grew faster than in Minnesota and Missouri and about the same as in South Dakota. The only silver redhorse comparison, from Missouri, showed similar growth.

### Food Habits

- Stomach contents from 49 golden, 28 northern, and 42 silver redhorses showed the same food items across seasons. The three species used the same foods in roughly equal amounts.
- For redhorses over 4 inches, the main foods by frequency of occurrence were immature [[Chironomidae]] (91%), immature [[Ephemeroptera]] (62%), and immature [[Trichoptera]] (18%).

### Reproduction and Maturity

- Silver redhorse do not mature until age V. Most northern and some golden redhorses mature at age III.
- Of 20 age-III northern redhorses, 17 were mature males and 1 was a mature female. All 23 older northern redhorses were mature.
- Of 7 age-III golden redhorses, 4 were immature and 3 were mature males. Of 30 age-IV golden redhorses, 17 were mature males and 10 mature females.
- Spawning temperatures differed by species:
  - Northern redhorse: 52°F, late April (earliest spawners).
  - Silver redhorse: 56°F, peak condition in the first week of May.
  - Golden redhorse: above 60°F, mid-May.
- Males reached spawning readiness before females and congregated to defend territories. Sex ratio on spawning grounds was 75 males to 37 females, indicating the female's brief stay. Ratios at other times were 1:1.
- Spawning was observed on a gravel-rubble area (1 to 3 feet deep) in the main channel, but turbidity prevented direct observation. None of the species was seen spawning in the small tributary.
- Breeding tubercles were well developed on males (anal fin and ventral caudal fin). At least one female of each species had barely visible tubercles.

### Fecundity

Total length and egg number were linearly related:
- Golden: (−33.1 + 3.46 TL) × 1000
- Northern: (−19.7 + 2.55 TL) × 1000
- Silver: (−18.8 + 2.62 TL) × 1000

Observed ranges:
- Golden (19 fish): 6,100 eggs (11.5 in, age IV) to 25,350 (15.7 in, age VI)
- Northern (9 fish): 13,500 (12.8 in, age III) to 27,150 (18.1 in, age VI)
- Silver (3 females): 14,910 (13.3 in, age V) to 36,340 (19.3 in, age unknown)

### Methodological Notes

Fins were clipped close to the body to prevent regeneration. The author accepted [[Gerking]]'s (1953) conclusion that mortality from electroshocking and fin-clipping was negligible. Scale impressions were read at 42x using a projector, measuring the dorsoventral axis following [[Spoor]] (1938). Stomach contents were examined under a dissecting microscope, usually identified only to order. Egg counts used the volumetric method.
