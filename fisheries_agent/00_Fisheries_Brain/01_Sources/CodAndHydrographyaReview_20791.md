# Cod and Hydrography: A Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae

## Executive Summary

This literature review, issued by the [[Bureau Of Commercial Fisheries]] as Special Scientific Report–Fisheries No. 245 (May 1958, Washington, D.C.), by fishery research biologist [[John P Wise]], collates scattered studies on how [[Atlantic Cod]] ([[Gadus Morhua]]) life stages relate to [[Hydrography]]. The author notes the review is incomplete but intends it as a starting point for experiments and as an aid to interpreting observations. Temperatures are standardized to Centigrade; salinities are given in parts per thousand (‰).

The review covers spawning, eggs and larvae, feeding and growth, distribution of mature fish, and mortality. Its central conclusion is that cod are strongly influenced by hydrographic conditions. Temperature dominates most life stages, salinity matters chiefly for egg and larval buoyancy and survival, and oxygen affects distribution in the Baltic. Cod occupy a remarkably wide thermal range but have a narrow margin near the freezing point.

## Background & Description

The document is a compiled review rather than primary research. Its author read most of the sources cited, and a few unavailable works are noted with the secondary source from which their data came. The bibliography lists more than 100 references, mostly from 1880–1956, drawn from Scandinavian, German, British, Canadian, American, Newfoundland and Spanish fishery literature.

The review follows the cod life history in sequence:

- **Spawning:** temperature, salinity, oxygen and pH.
- **Eggs and larvae:** temperature, salinity, and miscellaneous factors such as currents, storms, oxygen and pH.
- **Feeding and growth:** temperature effects on feeding and metabolism.
- **Distribution and abundance of mature fish:** temperature, salinity and oxygen.
- **Mortality:** temperature and other lethal factors.
- **Recapitulation:** a synthesis of the above.

Much of the reported evidence is anecdotal or based on single experiments, which the author identifies as a central limitation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiology.** Cod are poikilotherms with little internal temperature regulation. Britton (1924) found cod body temperatures less than 1° above ambient at ambient temperatures around 10°.

**Hydrographic setting.** Surface and intermediate layers matter for pelagic eggs and larvae. Runoff, wind-driven surface water, and the [[Thermocline]] (or intermediate cold layer) influence where eggs float, suspend or sink. [[Water Temperature]] and [[Salinity]] are described as interacting, with a physical link in that seawater density falls as temperature rises above 0°. Below 10° and 35‰, however, temperature alone changes density by no more than 0.001 relative to 0°, so buoyancy effects are minor across the natural range.

**Baltic and marginal seas.** Baltic salinities usually range from 4‰ to 16‰, against an oceanic average of about 35‰. Baltic cod recruitment has been linked to inflows of Atlantic water, which raise salinity, and to low [[Dissolved Oxygen]] levels that may cause deformities and reduce numbers of mature fish.

**Regional thermal regimes.** Reported optima and fishing ranges vary by region, which the author attributes to different stocks or "races" and to differing food distribution.

## Key Findings

### Spawning
- **Temperature:** Captive cod have spawned at temperatures from −1.1° to 5.5°. Wild spawning on the American side was reported at 0.6° to 8.9°, 0.546° to 10.1°, 8° to 12°, and 7° to 12°. Spawning on the European side occurred at about 0° to 6.5°.
- **Overall range:** Combined evidence indicates a spawning range of 0.6° to 12° in the Northwest Atlantic and about 0° to 6.5° in the Northeast Atlantic, with all observations spanning −1.1° to 12°.
- **Pelagic spawning:** Some cod leave the bottom and spawn pelagically, and the thermocline may be a preferred habitat in some areas. Scandinavian workers suggested that temperature variation within the water column may matter more than temperature itself.
- **Salinity:** European cod spawn at about 34–35‰; American cod at about 32‰ (Georges Bank), and about 35.16‰ on the Grand Bank per Damas. Baltic cod spawn at about half the usual oceanic salinity. Whether salinity induces spawning is unresolved.
- **Oxygen and pH:** Sund (1935) observed pelagic spawning in layers with lower oxygen and pH, but gave no values.

### Eggs and Larvae
- **Year-class strength:** Warmer water during the pelagic egg and larval stages is associated with better year classes in several regions (Danish waters, West Greenland, Newfoundland). The Fylla Bank relationship (Hermann 1953; Hachey, Hermann and Bailey 1954) is shown in Figure 1. A. Dannevig (1947) reported that the successful 1938 year class in the Transition Area followed cooler-than-usual water, attributed to delayed spawning and a better match to the zooplankton cycle.
- **Egg development:** Development from spawning to hatching occurs from below 0° to 14° in the [[Development Time Series]] data. Time-temperature data from Earll (1880) and H. Dannevig (1895) agree well. For example, eggs took 42 days at −1°, 20 days at 3°, 13 days at 7.2°, and 8.5 days at 14°.
- **Egg survival:** Fertilization occurs as low as −2° (A. Dannevig 1919). Eggs are unlikely to survive at the low extremes. Full development lies between −1° and 0°, according to Johansen and Krogh (1914).
- **Optima:** Eggs have an optimum of about 5°, within a few degrees on either side (Bigelow and Schroeder place it at 5°–8.3°). Larvae survive from about 2° to 16°, with an optimum of about 7°.
- **Lethal limits for fry:** Taning (1953) reported lethal temperatures below about 1.8°. Fry survived up to 16° in laboratory jars (A. Dannevig and Sivertsen 1933).
- **Salinity and buoyancy (Table 2):** Eggs and larvae are damaged below about 13‰. Eggs survive from about 19‰ upward, while larvae need more than 25‰ to survive more than a few days. Below 23‰ buoyancy is reduced for both. Above 26‰ all eggs and larvae float. Cod larvae are buoyant at about 27.39‰ (Henly 1952), consistent with Dahl (1909) and A. Dannevig (1919).
- **Critical salinity:** The review estimates a critical point for survival in the neighborhood of 15‰, with no clear natural upper limit.
- **Oxygen:** Poulsen (1931) found cod larvae avoid oxygen below 4.9 ml per liter.
- **pH:** A pH of 4.1 was fatal to larvae in laboratory experiments; larvae survived between pH 5.7 and 8.5.
- **Other factors:** Storms and surface waves can damage eggs mechanically (Rollefsen 1930). Transport by currents can carry eggs and fry to unsuitable areas. Sund (1924) linked low spring runoff from snow to good year classes in Norway.

### Feeding and Growth
- **Growth:** Captive cod grew about twice as much in July–August as in March–April. Fry grew fastest between 7° and 10° (Dahl 1909).
- **Feeding range:** McKenzie (1934–1935) found most feeding between 2.2° and 15.5°. Cod of all sizes fed rarely below 0°, and cod won't feed below 1.1° (Grenfell 1910). Smaller cod fed at higher temperatures, up to 20°.
- **Feeding summary:** The review concludes that cod feed regularly from about 2° to 15°, with optima varying by size.
- **Implication:** Hook-and-line catches may underestimate abundance at temperatures outside this range.

### Distribution and Abundance of Mature Fish
- **"Cod water":** No hard definition exists. Proposed ranges include 2°–4° on the Grand Bank (Rodriguez and Rojo 1955) and 4.4°–7.2° (Huntsman 1925). Hjort's suggested isotherm range (6°–8° at 100 m) was judged too restrictive, with Bigelow and Welsh giving 1.7° to 10.0°.
- **Grand Bank:** Spring optimum is 0° to 4° (lower half favored); summer optimum is 3° to 5°.
- **Nova Scotian Banks:** Spring fishing is best at −0.5° to 1.5°, with 3° as the upper limit. Summer optimum is 2.5° to 5.5°.
- **Gulf of St. Lawrence:** Limiting value of 12°; Thompson gives 0.5° to 6° with 2° to 5° optimal; Tremblay found 8° most productive on longlines.
- **West Greenland:** Holsteinborg Deep longline catches occurred at 2.20° to 2.33° (Rasmussen) or 3.1° to 4.0° (Anonymous 1953). Cod avoid temperatures below 1°.
- **Labrador:** Preferred range 1.7° to 5.6° (Grenfell 1910).
- **Spitsbergen-Bear Island:** Fishing unprofitable below about 2°; summer range 3° to 5°, winter 2° to 3°.
- **Long-term changes:** Taning and Lundbeck linked shifts in cod distribution, such as West Greenland's recent resurgence and Barents Sea depth changes, to warming trends. Templeman and Fleming found no clear link between temperature and abundance across 81 years of catch statistics, likely due to confounding variables.
- **Salinity:** Cod occur from nearly fresh water (reports of straying into rivers) to full oceanic salinity, about 35‰. Baltic cod are found around 6‰.
- **Oxygen:** Critical oxygen for mature cod is 2.7 ml per liter; asphyxia occurs at 0.80 ml per liter (Sundnes 1957). Low oxygen may have caused deformed young cod and scarce mature cod in the Baltic.

### Mortality
- **Cold:** Freezing is fatal. Lethal points reported include −1.1° (Earll, Carswell, Nielsen), about −2° (Thompson), and below −1.0° (Templeman and Fleming). Leim's experiment showed discomfort at −1.2°, reduced respiration near −1.5°, and cessation of heartbeat at −1.9°.
- **Heat:** Most large cod died at 15.0° in tanks, and 20.0° was fatal to all large fish (McKenzie 1935).
- **Variability:** Lethal temperatures depend on prior conditioning and rate of change. Within 0.7° near freezing, cod moved from apparently comfortable to dead.
- **Sudden cold:** Lumby and Atkinson (1929) attributed a North Sea die-off in 1929 to a sudden temperature drop.

### Recapitulation and Conclusions
- Cod are substantially influenced by hydrography, but the relationships are not simple.
- Salinity has little influence on spawning. It matters greatly for egg and larval buoyancy and survival.
- Cod growth and feeding increase with temperature, while very cold water may prevent feeding through metabolic limits.
- Sensitivity of cod to changes is high: bull (1936, 1938, 1952) showed cod can detect temperature changes of 0.05° and salinity changes of 0.17‰. The author extrapolates pH detection of about 0.05.
- Optima vary by region and size class, and different cod groups may adapt to different temperatures.
- The author concludes that considerable work has been done, but much remains, particularly on the eggs and larvae of autumn-spawning groups and on the effects of salinity and oxygen.
