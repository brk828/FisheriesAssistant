# Trip Report Upper Colorado River Endangered Fishes Researchers Meeting February 1987

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #conservation

## Executive Summary

This memorandum documents a trip report from the Upper Colorado River endangered fishes researchers meeting held February 24-25, 1987 in Grand Junction, Colorado. The meeting covered standardized monitoring programs, habitat research, captive spawning efforts, and recovery planning for four endangered native fish species: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (referred to as squawfish), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Key discussions included unsuccessful captive spawning attempts at [[Grand Junction]], population estimates for razorback sucker (ca. 1000 adults), tetracycline marking techniques for larval fish, and controversial recovery implementation plans that prioritize research despite anticipated flow depletions and habitat alterations. The meeting highlighted tensions between upper and lower basin recovery efforts and concerns about the functional extinction of bonytail chub.

## Background & Description

The meeting brought together state biologists from Colorado and Utah, federal personnel from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), and various researchers working on endangered fish recovery in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The standardized monitoring program utilized electrofishing and seining to collect adult and young-of-year (yoy) squawfish and humpback chub. Data management employed dBASE III software for storage and retrieval, with individual principal investigators responsible for their own analyses and interpretations.

Four endangered cyprinid and catostomid species were the focus: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (Ptychocheilus lucius), [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha), [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (Gila elegans). These species face threats from habitat alteration, flow modifications, and competition/predation from introduced species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat research presentations addressed instream flow modeling using [[PHABSIM]], temperature and hydrology modeling under varying flow regimens, and aerial surveys quantifying available backwater habitats. The goal was to determine habitat availability at various flow levels, with recommendations based on focal point data from instream flow studies, runoff patterns, proposed water projects, and current consumptive water use.

Winter habitat studies by [[Colorado State University]] (CSU) and Bio West revealed that both [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] prefer slower velocities and greater depths during winter compared to other seasons. Razorback suckers exhibited very sedentary behavior while squawfish showed limited movement.

[[Cataract Canyon]] surveys by Rich Valdez (Bio West) indicated that squawfish spawning likely occurs in upstream areas over a 1-2 month period. All life stages of humpback chub were collected but in low numbers, representing a remnant population from the period during Lake Powell filling.

Tom Nesler presented evidence that short-term spikes in declining flow rates after high spring flows may trigger squawfish spawning, independent of temperature and photoperiod effects. Back-calculation of larval ages revealed bimodal spawning with a second peak during increased flows in mid to late July, presumably coinciding with summer rains.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Monitoring:**
- [[Harold Tyus]] reported a mark-recapture population estimate of ca. 1000 adult [[Razorback Sucker]] in the [[Green River]]
- Collections showed lack of young, subadult, or young adult razorback suckers, indicating recruitment failure
- [[Cataract Canyon]] humpback chub collections were low, representing a remnant population

**Captive Propagation Efforts:**
- Attempted captive spawning of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] by Grand Junction personnel was unsuccessful; likely due to stress from collecting and handling wild adults in their first year
- [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] constructed three 1/10 acre ponds, drilled a separate well, and set up circular tanks to support razorback sucker spawning efforts
- Harold Tyus planned to collect and spawn razorback sucker from the [[Green River]] for aquaria studies, pond studies, and potential restocking
- [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] spawning efforts might provide larvae to DNFH

**Marking Techniques:**
- Bob Muth summarized tetracycline marking experiments on larval squawfish from DNFH
- Most successful treatment: immersion in 300 mg/l tetracycline bath for 4-12 hours
- Mark retention after 77 days was 100% for survivors
- Marks most notable on otoliths but also occurred on opercle bones
- Long-term retention believed acceptable based on literature review

**Fish Passage and Infrastructure:**
- Bob Burdick presented alternatives for fish passageways at [[Redlands Diversion]]
- Preferred alternative cost approximately $800,000
- Final recommendation: do not construct due to expense, uncertainty about squawfish use, and low priority relative to other research

**Taxonomic Tools:**
- [[Lynn Kaeding]] presented preliminary work using fin ray counts to distinguish yoy humpback chub from other chubs (based on 1982-1985 collections)
- Intended as field tool for personnel to quantify humpback yoy in seine samples

**Genetic Concerns:**
- Mark Rosenfeld's electrophoretic and morphometric work on chubs
- Analysis of DNFH bonytail broodstock revealed a "killer gene" causing production of only females

**Recovery Planning and Policy:**
- [[John Hamill]] outlined upper basin recovery plan
- [[Bonytail Chub]] considered "functionally extinct" and will not be considered when reviewing proposed water projects
- Room was "strangely silent" after this statement with minimal apparent support
- Recovery plans for squawfish and chubs being revised by Dick Wydoski for mid-April 1987 review
- Plans being updated to fit upper basin implementation plan; may become appendices to that plan
- Experimental listing package for bonytail chub now considering [[Gila River]] below [[San Carlos Lake]] as proposed introduction area

**Research Priorities:**
- Tom Pitts specified that research topics should assist recovery despite flow depletions and habitat alterations
- "Feeling of helplessness" among biologists after presentation
- Lower basin recovery efforts characterized as taking a "shotgun approach" (unsystematic)
- Upper basin researchers expressed little support for this approach
- Questions raised about "experimental, non-essential" population designation and inability to protect stocked fish and habitats

**Broodstock Needs:**
- Roger requested additional [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] for DNFH: 50 adults ideal, 30 minimum, or 500 subadult and yoy
- Harold Tyus stated that many adults may not be available
- [[Region 6]] committed Grand Junction and Vernal field stations to collectively provide six adults to DNFH
- Lynn Kaeding suggested frozen milt from males could be provided for spawning

**Communication and Coordination:**
- Report author recommended continued attendance at future meetings
- Suggested multiple presentations from lower basin personnel to provide comprehensive, accurate view of recovery efforts and remaining fish communities and habitats
- Need for enhanced communication between upper and lower basins identified
