# Desert Fishes Council Symposium Proceedings: Desert Fishes, Native-Nonnative Management, and Colorado River Tailwater Sucker Ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This page set from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] symposium proceedings (1998 symposium, published November 15, 1999) contains three items: a Spanish-language abstract on desert fishes of the Dominican Republic, a paper on resource use by [[Colorado River]] fishes and the management of native versus non-native species, and a study of the abundance, distribution and movement of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] in the [[Lee's Ferry]] tailwater below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The papers address how altered habitats, introduced species and small isolated populations affect the persistence of native fishes.

## Background & Description

The Dominican paper (Spanish abstract, author surname partly garbled in the scan, presumably Marsh) describes desert fish communities in the [[Dominican Republic]]. The [[Colorado River]] paper is by Marsh, Pacey and Minckley (Arizona State University, Department of Biology, Tempe). The tailwater study is by McKinney, Persons and Rogers (Arizona Game and Fish Department, Research Branch, Phoenix). Native species discussed include [[Cyprinodon Bondi]], the endemic cichlid [[Cichlasoma Haitiensis]], and Flannelmouth Sucker. Relevant genera include [[Limia]] and [[Gambusia]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dominican Republic:** Saline lakes contain at least three pupfish (*Cyprinodon*) species. Two occur in lakes in the southwestern part of the country and two in lakes in the southeastern sector; some species names are garbled in the scan. Warm springs linked to the lakes hold [[Limia]] and [[Gambusia]] species and the endemic [[Cichlasoma Haitiensis]]. *Cyprinodon bondi* co-exists with an isolated population of the [[American Crocodile]] (*Crocodylus acutus*).

**Colorado River:** The river is physically altered and hydrologically controlled. Dams create impoundments and cold-water tailwaters that differ strongly from the pre-dam stream. Non-native fishes now dominate the lower river, and native fishes survive as a few imperiled species in isolated reaches. The authors attribute this displacement to the feeding, behavioral and reproductive traits of non-natives, which allow them to displace, replace or exclude natives regardless of habitat features. The [[Tailwater Habitat]] below Glen Canyon Dam is the setting for the sucker study.

## Key Findings

- **Dominican desert fishes:** Several pupfish species inhabit saline lakes, with warm springs supporting livebearers (*Limia*, *Gambusia*) and an endemic cichlid. The pupfish share habitat with an isolated American crocodile population, which is a notable conservation pairing.
- **Colorado River management:** Non-native fishes have overwhelmingly displaced natives, so the authors recommend [[Segregated Management]] of native and non-native fishes as the only way to maintain self-sustaining native populations. Their argument is that the displacement reflects biological traits, not only habitat condition.
- **Flannelmouth Sucker abundance and movement:** Researchers studied a 26-km reach of the [[Colorado River]] tailwater below Glen Canyon Dam during the early 1990s (study years partly garbled in the scan). They captured a total of 212 fish and recaptured 21 that had been tagged previously by others, which supports the population analysis through [[Mark-Recapture Sampling]].
