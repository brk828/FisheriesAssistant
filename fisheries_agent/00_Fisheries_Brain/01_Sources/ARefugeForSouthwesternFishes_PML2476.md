# Endangered Fishes of the Southwest and the Dexter National Fish Hatchery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This popular-science article, written by [[Hans Stuart]] and J.E. (Jim) Johnson for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), describes how the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in eastern New Mexico is used to hold, propagate, and study rare Southwestern fishes. Dexter's mission changed in 1974 from raising warmwater sport fish to保护 endangered species. The article profiles recovery efforts for the [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]], and frames habitat loss, dam construction, and introduced species as the main threats. It concludes that the survival of Southwestern fishes depends on biological knowledge balanced against water development decisions.

## Background & Description

Dexter is described as the first and only facility of its type in the world, designed to hold and propagate endangered fishes while supporting research into their biology. The FWS acquired the 6.10-acre site from the [[New Mexico Game and Fish Department]] in 1981. Its original ponds and buildings were built by 1935. Until 1974 the site raised warmwater fish, mostly bass and catfish, for stocking in federal waters across the Southwest.

Dexter's facilities include:
- 55 ponds
- 16 indoor holding tanks
- an artificial stream

The hatchery holds 13 federally listed fish species and five others that may be listed in the future.

Species profiles from the article:
- **[[Chihuahua Chub]]:** A dark brown minnow with whitish undersides, deep blue fins, and an average length of 5-6 inches. It was once abundant in the [[Mimbres River]] and streams of the [[Guzman Basin]].
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]:** The largest minnow in North America. It can reach six feet and 80 pounds, and was once a commercial and sport fish.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]:** Named for a bony keel on its back, an adaptation to turbulent water.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]:** Among the most endangered Colorado River fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Native fish decline across the region is driven mainly by habitat destruction. On the Mimbres River, irrigation diversions and floods that filled pools with gravel gradually removed the deep pools, undercut banks, and cover the chub needed. On the Colorado River, the [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1935) and more than 20 other dams turned much of the system into reservoirs and cold tailwaters, which squawfish cannot survive in. Competition from more than 100 introduced fish species and reduced river flows have also depleted native populations.

Regional and national status:
- New Mexico has 50 native fish species.
- Since 1900, five fish species have been lost from the state: beautiful shiner, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, Gila topminnow, and Gila chub. The Chihuahua chub is barely surviving.
- 15 native New Mexico fishes are federally listed as threatened or endangered or are candidates for listing.
- Nationally, 45 species are listed as threatened or endangered, and 150 more are candidates.

The article also notes broader biodiversity loss: biologists estimate that 10 percent of the world's life forms (one million of ten million species) may become extinct within 20 years, mostly plants, invertebrates, and fish. Only about one percent of Earth's plant and animal species have been investigated for economic value.

## Key Findings

**Dexter's role and results**
- In 1974, six endangered fishes were brought to Dexter: Goodenough gambusia (now extinct in the wild), fountain darter, Pecos gambusia, Comanche Springs pupfish, Big Bend gambusia, and Leon Springs pupfish. All six reproduced in their first year.
- The Big Bend gambusia population had been reduced to three individuals (a pregnant female and two immature males) shortly before transfer.
- All fish taken to Dexter, except a single Yaqui catfish caught in 1978, have spawned there.

**Chihuahua chub**
- First identified as a separate species in 1880. Declared extinct by 1934.
- Rediscovered in 1975 near the village of Mimbres. The surviving population was estimated at about 30 individuals.
- Flooding in December 1978 eliminated at least half of that population.
- In 1978, [[Robert Miller]] found that of 19 healthy streams in the Guzman Basin documented about 20 years earlier, only eight remained healthy, and only four were considered stable.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing the U.S. and Mexican populations as endangered.
- Chubs were moved from the Mimbres River into a tributary spring. They have spawned there for two years and now number over 100.
- Proposed recovery actions include replacing temporary bulldozed dams that periodically disturb the channel, prohibiting introduction of exotic fish, and restricting bait fish movement from the Mimbres River.

**Colorado squawfish**
- Once found throughout the Colorado River basin and tributaries such as the San Juan River. It now survives mainly in the upper Colorado River basin.
- More than 200,000 young squawfish were produced at Dexter in one year for reintroduction into remaining suitable rivers.
- Woody noted the species has "a good potential as a sport fish."
- Proposals to restore squawfish have often been opposed, and the species has been the subject of a lawsuit over dam construction.

**Razorback sucker**
- In January 1981, more than 130 adults were captured in Lake Mohave and moved to Dexter. They spawned within days, producing 20,000 young.
- On June 23, 1981, the first 7,000 five-inch suckers were stocked into the Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers, with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].
- Stocking is planned to continue for 10 more years, aiming to establish natural reproduction.

**Bonytail chub**
- Biologists captured eight adult fish from Lake Mohave, including five males, the first found in more than seven years.
- These eight produced 70,000 young, which will be grown at Dexter and restocked into Lake Mohave once large enough to avoid predators.

**Management and policy**
- Colorado River fishes depend on undisturbed riverine habitat, which conflicts with water development. 50 additional dams have been proposed for the Colorado River basin.
- The [[Endangered Species Act]] calls for habitat protection and restoration of species to parts of their original range, not just captive propagation.
- [[Jack Woody]], FWS endangered species coordinator in Albuquerque, argued that "Dexter can never completely replace the natural habitats," and that captive fish risk adapting to hatchery conditions and losing their wild character.
- Woody also stated that "economics and politics should be balanced with biological information," and that decision-makers "should first know at least what we are losing."
