# North Pond Stream-Type Habitat Proposal for Yaqui Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #yaqui-fishes

## Executive Summary

This internal memorandum from the Refuge Manager of the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (dated December 17, 1996) proposes changing Phase I of the [[Asian Tapeworm Eradication Project]] at North Pond from earthen ponds to an artificial stream-type habitat. The design uses concrete septic tanks as deep pools, connected by lined troughs with riffles and runs, and is intended to reduce parasites, water use, vegetation problems and bullfrog pressure while better resembling the historic habitat of [[Yaqui Fishes]]. The author requests critical review from interested parties.

## Background & Description

The memo follows earlier meetings on eradicating Asian tapeworm from refuge fish populations. The proposal is framed as a revision of earlier recommendations, and the author states that it addresses problems associated with earthen ponds.

The proposed system includes:

- **Shallow pools**, **large deep pools**, and **shallow backwaters** with slight current.
- **Deep pools** made from concrete [[Septic Tank Pools]] of various sizes up to 2,000-gallon capacity. A 2,000-gallon elliptical tank measures 12 x 8 x 6 ft deep (576 cu ft) and costs about $1,100 each installed on the refuge.
- **Estimated stocking of tanks:** about 10 tanks per system, described by the author as a guess, sufficient for healthy numbers of Yaqui fishes. Tanks could be added yearly as funding allowed.
- **Smaller pools** similar to those at Bylas Springs for [[Gila Topminnow]] habitat, and shallow concrete backwaters under 8 inches deep for frogs and topminnows.
- **Stream sections** of lined troughs (hypalon or plastic) to limit infiltration, with gravel and cobble in riffle or run areas, and boulders, logs and gravel in tank bottoms for cover.
- **Water routing:** flow through the system, with excess piped to Black Draw to help restore habitat there.
- **Margin design:** tanks set into the ground so soil along their edges stays moist and supports riparian vegetation, which would camouflage the tanks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author argues the stream system would:

- Use considerably less water than a pond system, and lose less water to infiltration and evapotranspiration.
- More closely imitate the historic habitat of Yaqui fishes.
- Be less conducive to parasites, likely producing healthier fish populations.
- Reduce aquatic vegetation problems and improve control of non-native vegetation, which would benefit [[Lilaeopsis]].
- Allow more effective [[Bullfrog Control]], since the whole area could be hunted more easily.
- Provide better habitat for [[Leopard Frogs]] and [[Mexican Garter Snake]], because Black Draw was their last stronghold.
- Be easier to manage, monitor and study, with lower labor and maintenance demands (vegetation, disease, water, exotics).

The author acknowledges that fish numbers may be lower than in a pond, but argues that healthier fish in more natural habitat would be better in the long run.

## Key Findings

- **Filling time and water loss:** A pond would take up to about one month to fill at 20 gal/min. The stream system could fill in about 4 days at 5 gal/min (the scan reads "Sgal/min," interpreted here as 5), with very little water lost during initial filling.
- **Cost and labor:** Tanks cost about $1,100 each installed. Labor for setup and maintenance is described as substantially less than for an earthen pond.
- **Design rationale:** The author reports no identified problems with the concept and asks reviewers to identify serious flaws or suggest improvements.
- **Connected benefits:** Excess water would support habitat restoration in Black Draw, and the design is expected to benefit leopard frogs, Mexican garter snakes and native vegetation.
- **Trade-off:** Overall fish population densities may be lower than in ponds, which the author considers acceptable given expected health and habitat gains.
- **Document status:** The memo is a draft for comment. The handwritten page 3 is largely illegible in the scan and contains only partial notes on tank installation, stream configuration and compensation, so it adds no reliable data.
