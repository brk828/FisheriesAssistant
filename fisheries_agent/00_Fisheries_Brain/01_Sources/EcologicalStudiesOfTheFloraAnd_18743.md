# Aquatic Survey of Green River and Tributaries within the Flaming Gorge Reservoir Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report is a 1959 aquatic survey of the Green River and selected tributaries in the [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] Basin, Utah and Wyoming. It was published as University of Utah Anthropological Papers No. 49 (Upper Colorado Series No. 3, November 1960), a by-product of a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] contract to document the flora and fauna that would be inundated by the reservoir. The survey sampled 47 stations (36 in the Green River, 11 in tributaries or side canyons) and collected 4,831 fish representing 15 species. It combined physical, chemical and limnological measurements with fish, insect and algae collections, plus a 24-hour diel water-quality study and notes on fish diseases, parasites, food and condition.

## Background & Description

The volume is one of several Flaming Gorge studies in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] development program, authorized by Federal Public Law 485 (84th Congress, 1956). Earlier biological work in [[Glen Canyon]] appeared in Anthropological Papers Nos. 31, 36 and 40. The vegetation survey was issued separately as No. 45. The field data derive mainly from two expeditions down the Green River in 1959, which began at river mile 383.1 near Green River, Wyoming, and ended at Hideout Forest Camp, Utah (mile 306.8). The fauna survey was extended with support from the University Research Fund and the [[Utah State Department of Fish and Game]].

The aquatic survey was led by Arden R. Gaufin, Gerald R. Smith, and Phil Dotson, with Guy G. Musser and Jean Musser contributing to the work. Smith served as crew leader and usually recorded physical and chemical data. Specimens were collected and preserved mainly by Musser and Smith.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions**
- River water temperatures ranged from 60°F to 79°F and were generally several degrees warmer than tributary waters.
- Cold tributaries such as Carter Creek and Sheep Creek were more suitable for trout.
- River pH ranged from 7.5 in the polluted reach below Green River, Wyoming, to 8.4 across most of its length. Most tributaries were alkaline, except Carter Creek, which had low carbonate content owing to its granitic drainage basin.
- Turbidity varied widely, with extremes attributed to floods. Sheep Creek ran heavily silted and reddish brown.
- Dissolved oxygen in 24-hour runs ranged widely, with values such as 6.0 to 30.0 p.p.m. at Mile 356 and up to 42 p.p.m. at other sites.

**Habitat types**
- **Primary river channel** above and in the canyon was 200 to 300 ft wide, with velocities measured up to about 7 ft/s.
- **Littoral habitat** was dominated by redside shiners and dace, with sculpins and young flannelmouth and bluehead suckers.
- **Island channels** (2 to 30 ft wide) appeared to act as refugia for immature fishes.
- **Large pool below Hideout Forest Camp** (about 25 × 70 yd, over 15 ft deep, silt and organic bottom) was the most productive site, yielding ten species.
- **Tributaries above the canyon**, such as [[Blacks Fork]] (average width about 45 ft), were intermittent except for Blacks Fork, Henrys Fork and possibly Currant Creek.
- **Canyon tributaries** such as Sheep, Carter, Eagle and Skull creeks were clear, cold and permanent, and supported trout populations.
- **Springs** were sampled, but no fish were collected from them.

**Sampling methods:** primacord (explosive) blasts, rotenone, seines, gill nets, hoop nets, dip nets, angling, and hand screens. Primacord was most effective in slow pools. Seines worked well in shallow water.

## Key Findings

**Fish community**
- A total of 4,831 specimens representing 15 species were collected. The check list includes 17 taxa, covering the families Salmonidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Cottidae and Ictaluridae.
- The **flannelmouth sucker** ([[Catostomus Latipinnis]]) was the most common species by frequency of occurrence (87 collections, the count as printed), and it included individuals over 400 mm (largest 556 mm).
- The **bluehead sucker** (*Pantosteus delphinus*) occurred in 28 of the 46 collections and was absent from the colder, faster trout streams.
- The **speckled dace** and **silver-side minnow** were the most abundant species, and the **redside shiner** dominated the littoral zone above the canyon.

**Endangered and rare natives**
- The [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endemic of the Colorado River system, was rare in the upper Green River. One adult, 488 mm long, was taken near Hideout Forest Camp. Six hybrids with flannelmouth suckers were reported from the same location in 1950.
- The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest North American minnow, was uncommon. Two specimens (544 and 400 mm) were taken from the same pool.
- Three forms of genus *Gila* were recorded: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. robusta*, including the subspecies *G. r. elegans*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*). The authors noted that their taxonomy needs further study.

**Trout and whitefish**
- Brown trout (*Salmo trutta*, introduced) were found in four tributaries, mostly at lower ends of cooler creeks. One specimen weighing about 14 lb was reported from Sheep Creek.
- Cutthroat trout (*S. clarki pleuriticus*, native) and rainbow trout (*S. gairdneri*, introduced) hybridized extensively, making pure specimens hard to identify.
- Angling in Carter Creek yielded 46 trout in 3½ hours.
- Mountain whitefish (*Prosopium williamsoni*) was sporadically distributed and native.

**Introduced and other species**
- Fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*) was not native, and this survey was believed to be the first record in the Green River drainage. Thirty carp were collected near river mile 339.
- Channel catfish were found only in the canyon area. The black bullhead was not collected by the expedition but was taken later just below the study area.

**Water quality and food**
- Bonytail chub stomach analysis (17 fish, August 1959) showed omnivorous feeding. Terrestrial insects, especially ants (58.7% of items), dominated animal matter, and woody plant fragments were a large part of the diet.
- The Green River was polluted by sewage from the city of Green River, Wyoming. Diseased fish were more prevalent about seven miles below the town.

**Diseases and parasites**
- Bacterial ulcers were observed on flannelmouth and bluehead suckers. These were not found on other species.
- Leeches were common on suckers, chubs, trout and whitefish. A plerocercoid of *Ligula intestinalis* was found in a bluehead sucker.

**Condition factor**
- Condition factors (K) were calculated for representative specimens. Examples include flannelmouth suckers (mostly around 1.3 to 1.6), bonytail chubs (about 1.1 to 2.1), and cutthroat trout (about 1.0 to 2.1).

**Conservation implications**
- The authors highlighted the scarcity of native Colorado River fishes (humpback sucker, Colorado squawfish, humpback chub) and the spread of introduced species, including trout and fathead minnows. They also noted the undesirable effects of transfers of fishes between drainages.
