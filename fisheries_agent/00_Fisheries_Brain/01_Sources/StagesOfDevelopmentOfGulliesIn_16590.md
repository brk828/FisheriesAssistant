# Stages of Development of Gullies in the West

#geomorphology #erosion #watershed-management #sediment-yield

## Executive Summary

This document examines the development and morphology of ephemeral gully systems in western mountain lands, proposing a classification based on stages of development from youthful to old-age. The research, conducted primarily in the [[Colorado Rocky Mountains]] with observations from [[New Mexico]], [[Arizona]], [[Nevada]], and [[California]], focuses on understanding gully formation processes, historical development patterns, and the transformation of discontinuous gullies into continuous systems. The author argues that current gully-classification systems provide insufficient information for land managers addressing erosion control priorities and sediment yield predictions.

## Background & Description

Ephemeral gullies in western mountain lands carry flows only during severe storms or spring snowmelt. They develop across major vegetation zones including desert, grasslands, brushlands, and open forests such as [[Pinon-Juniper]] and [[Ponderosa Pine]] types. Research was based on studies from the eastern and western flanks of the [[Colorado Rocky Mountains]], particularly at the [[Manitou Experimental Forest]].

Two primary gully types are recognized:

**Continuous gullies**: Begin downstream with many small rills that coalesce, rapidly attaining great depth maintained throughout most of their length. Depth decreases only in the lowest reach above the gully mouth along a concave profile.

**Discontinuous gullies**: Start with an abrupt headcut that may be located at any position on a hillside slope. They rapidly decrease in depth downstream, developing a gentler gradient than the original valley floor. Where gradients intersect, sediment fans build, and new gullies may begin. These gullies generally occur in series along drainageways.

Historical evidence indicates many present gully systems began development or were reactivated in the last quarter of the 19th century, coinciding with population increases and intensive grazing in the West. Gully formation accelerated during the 1930s drought and economic depression.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Gullies rarely occur on sites supporting healthy, dense vegetation cover. They are uncommon in well-forested subalpine regions of eastern [[Colorado]] or oak brushlands of western [[Colorado]], where ground surfaces are covered by humus, litter, and herbaceous vegetation such as [[Thurber Fescue]]. Infiltration rates in these protected areas are nearly 5 times higher than nearby sagebrush sites where gullies are abundant.

The [[Silverhill-Montosa Watershed]] on the [[Cibola National Forest]] near [[Magdalena]], New Mexico (26.6 mi² area) demonstrated active gully expansion: the system increased from 16 mi of gullies in 1936 to 84 mi in 1968 (425% increase over 27 years), with no new gullies forming but existing ones expanding. In contrast, the [[Monica Watershed]] on the same forest showed all 7.6 mi of gullies developed after 1936, illustrating ongoing gullying activity.

The [[Nursery Gully System]] at [[Manitou Experimental Forest]] exemplifies both gully types: headwater portions contain several discontinuous channels separated by ungullied stretches and alluvial fans, while a continuous channel system forms the downstream section. Coalescence is visible through rapid succession of pronounced channel nick points (0.5 to 1.5 ft deep) representing fused discontinuous gullies.

## Key Findings

**Gully Formation Processes**: Discontinuous gullies form when locally lowered resistance to erosion (from grazing, trampling, fire, or other agents) creates initial furrows, scarplets, or small basins. Subsequent storms cause headward progression while toes develop small fans. Channel formation creates vertical headcuts, and water concentration in trenches reduces channel storage, increasing peak flows with greater velocities and cutting power.

**Gully Transformation**: Discontinuous gullies develop into continuous gullies through fusion. The [[Nursery Gully]] system demonstrates this process, with upper reach coalescence indicated by rapid succession of nick points. One studied discontinuous gully was 286 ft long with average gradient of 0.07, marked by a 2.4 ft deep headcut at the upper end and terminating in an alluvial fan.

**Developmental Stages**: Gully development should be recognized as landform evolution proceeding from youthful to old-age stages. Youthful and early mature stages were defined in this study, with early mature stage coinciding with transformation of discontinuous into continuous gullies. Comparison with river hydraulic geometry suggests mature stage should be characterized by dynamic equilibrium, though this could not be verified in study gullies.

**Vegetation Influence**: In ephemeral gullies, vegetation grown during dry channel periods exerts major influences on development. Criteria for mature gully stage may include channel vegetation stability aspects beyond stream equilibrium alone.

**Management Implications**: Present gully-classification systems do not yield sufficient information for land managers confronting problems of priorities in gully control, erosion rates, and sediment yields. Quantifying gullies in terms of development stages, erosion rates, and sediment yields would provide watershed managers with a useful tool for decision-making in land and water management.

**Data Limitations**: Insufficient data on gully mechanics and morphology prevents complete quantification of gully stages. Scarcity of data does not provide thorough understanding of gully processes basic to defining gully-development stages.
