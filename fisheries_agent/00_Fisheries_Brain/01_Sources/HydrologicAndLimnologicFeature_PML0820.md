# Hydrologic and Limnologic Features of Quitobaquito Pond and Springs, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report (Cooperative National Park Resources Studies Unit, Technical Report No. 22; Quitobaquito Science Series No. 8, June 1989) by [[Stuart G. Fisher]] characterizes the hydrology and limnology of [[Quitobaquito Pond]] and its springs in the Sonoran Desert. It develops a midsummer [[Water Budget]] balanced at about 6.1 cm/day, examines light, temperature, water chemistry, oxygen and sediments, and compares results with [[Cole and Whiteside Study (1965)]]. The pond is a spring-fed, shallow, polymictic system in good condition, with a near-balanced water budget that is sensitive to modest reductions in spring delivery.

## Background & Description

Quitobaquito Pond is a man-made impoundment (about 0.22 ha, 1 m mean depth) fed by two springs, [[North Spring]] and [[South Spring]], which flow about 100 m through ditches to the pond. The springs are among the few surface-water sources in one of the harshest deserts of North America and support songbirds, waterfowl, and the endangered [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon macularius). The site is managed by the [[National Park Service]].

The study had two objectives: (1) generate an approximate water budget typical of a midsummer day, to judge how critical the water supply is and how much reduction the pond could tolerate; and (2) describe the limnologic dynamics of the pond across seasons and within a single day, to define the range of states the system may occupy. Field methods followed standard limnological techniques (for example [[Lind Methods]] and [[Wetzel and Likens Methods]]), with laboratory chemistry based on [[Golterman and Clymo Methods]] and [[Hem Methods]], modified for southwestern waters. Samples were collected on five dates from July 1984 to July 1986.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Spring discharge, measured at a [[USGS Gauge]], showed no seasonal trend or response to precipitation. Discharge ranged from 0.03 to 0.10 cfs, with a mean monthly flow of 0.072 cfs (about 2 L/s). From October 1982 to February 1986 there was no long-term trend overall, but a significant decline of about 4% per year occurred from October 1983 to February 1986 (p < 0.001). The author recommends longer monitoring.

**Evapotranspiration and riparian vegetation.** Riparian trees and shrubs (for example [[Saltcedar]] and [[Seepwillow]]) draw water from bank storage. Plants do not consume more water than an open surface would lose under the same conditions; they mainly shift where loss occurs, from open-water evaporation toward transpiration.

**Light and stratification.** Water clarity varied from crystal clear to highly turbid. The extinction coefficient (k) ranged from 0.68 to nearly 4.0, with the clearest water in March and April. In July 1984 the bottom was not visible at 0.9 m, and the study suggests light is probably not limiting primary production except during turbid periods.

**Temperature.** Spring temperatures were relatively constant (South Spring about 24.4 °C in March to 28.3 °C in July). The pond had a much wider range and showed daily [[Polymixis]] (thermal stratification that breaks down nightly), which is unusual for a shallow pond but common in desert waters because high temperatures create strong density gradients.

**Water chemistry.** Conductivity was high (about 1000 µS/cm, pond 812 to over 1200 µS/cm), typical of alkaline western waters. Nitrogen was dominated by nitrate in springs, and nitrate was rapidly removed in the pond (0.25 to 1.2 mg/L in summer, 10 to 35% of input concentrations). Phosphorus was low but detectable, likely replenished by weathering of parent calcium-phosphate minerals. Calcium carbonate precipitation (marl and travertine) was indicated by higher calcium in spring water than in pond water.

**Oxygen.** Water was well oxygenated throughout. Springs were typically anoxic or very low in oxygen, rising as water flowed toward the pond. Pond oxygen was supersaturated in the afternoon and dropped sharply at night, indicating largely benthic, autotrophic production. Gross primary production estimates were 0.35 and 0.46 mg O₂/cm²/d on two dates, consistent with [[Cole and Whiteside Study (1965)]].

**Sediments.** Pond sediments were carbonaceous, compacted, and reactive to acid. Shoaling was estimated at only 1 to 3 cm per decade, and the pond bottom retained its original dimensions within about 10%.

## Key Findings

- **Water budget (midsummer, cm/day):** Inputs total 6.12 (precipitation 0.122 + spring flow 6.0). Outputs total 6.16 (pond evaporation 0.84 + evapotranspiration 5.32). Surface outflow, subsurface seepage and storm overland flow were treated as zero in the conservative model. The budget closes within about 0.04 cm/day.
- **Precipitation:** Summer mean 0.122 cm/day (summer records 1944–1970). Spring flow was corrected for about 21% lost to the old stream channel, giving 6.00 cm/day.
- **Evapotranspiration:** Estimated from a riparian zone 40 m wide around the pond (sector A40), rate 5.32 cm/day over pond area. An independent empirical check gave 6.01 cm/day, which the author considered in good agreement.
- **Sensitivity:** The budget is near balance, so a small reduction in spring delivery, or a leak in the delivery pipe, could shrink the pond. Pond outflow is considered "excess" water; problems arise only when outflow is zero.
- **Light:** Light penetration is highly variable; the bottom was visible in March and April but not in July 1984. Turbidity in July 1984 was 112 units, compared with 140 units in 1964.
- **Stratification:** The pond is polymictic, stratifying and mixing daily in summer, and is unusual for its shallow depth.
- **Nutrients:** Nitrate is the probable limiting nutrient in the pond, with rapid removal likely due to uptake by biota or denitrification. Phosphorus is maintained by mineral weathering.
- **Comparison with 1964–65 data:** Water quality is good and typical of the region. Little evidence of significant change over 20 years, and biological productivity is similar to rates reported then.
- **Management recommendations:** Monitor long-term spring flow trends; protect the water supply to keep the pond full; dredging of the pond risks penetrating the low-permeability basin substrate; watch for lateral macrophyte encroachment (estimated about 0.25 cm/year).
