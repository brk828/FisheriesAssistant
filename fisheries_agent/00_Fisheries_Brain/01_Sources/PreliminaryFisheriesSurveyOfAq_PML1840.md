# Buenos Aires NWR Preliminary Fisheries Survey of Aquatic Habitats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum reports a preliminary survey of stock-watering tanks on the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (BANWR) in southern Arizona. The survey asked whether existing tanks could serve as supplemental habitat for recovering endangered fishes. Two sampling trips were made, one in December (1985, as read from the table) by Ahlm and Robertson and one in April (1986) by a team from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and [[Arizona State University]]. Across both trips, five fish species were collected, all nonnative. The memo concludes that most tanks are unsuitable for native fishes without further habitat work, recommends limiting fish stocking, and identifies a few native species that could be propagated in small or isolated tanks.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The refuge lies just east of the Baboquivari Mountains and comprises much of the headwaters of the north-flowing Altar Valley, part of the [[Santa Cruz River]] system in the [[Gila River]] basin. Its southern extremes drain toward Mexico, probably into the Rio Concepción watershed.
- **Native fish status:** Native fishes were historically absent from the refuge. Even the perennial Arivaca Creek was fishless.
- **Introductions:** Nonnative fishes have been widely introduced into stock tanks throughout Arizona and are common in the area.
- **Survey purpose:** Assess whether tanks could support recovery efforts for endangered fishes.
- **Methods:** Tanks were sampled with 6.4-mm-mesh seines of various lengths. Ahlm and Robertson also used gill nets. Eight tanks were sampled in the December survey. In April, 45 tanks were visited, and only three had been sampled previously. Habitat details (size, permanence, water quality) were not systematically recorded, so further work is needed to estimate these.
- **Scope:** Numerous roads and smaller tanks not on the base map were encountered, so more ground survey is needed to map available habitats and access.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishes collected (five species, all nonnative):**
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
- [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*)
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), the only species judged useful on the refuge, providing local mosquito control

Specimens were deposited in the [[Arizona State University Fish Collection]].

**Other vertebrates and invertebrates:**
- [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*, possibly *A. mavortium*) was the most widespread vertebrate and was probably an introduced population. Specimens included metamorphosed adults, branchiate (aquatic) adults, and juveniles. Branchiate adults indicate tanks are permanent enough for overwintering. Larvae and eggs with developing embryos suggest breeding in January or February.
- Ranid frog tadpoles were found in several tanks, including introduced [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) and spotted frogs of the *Rana pipiens* group.
- Aquatic invertebrates were abundant and diverse, mainly insects and mollusks. Phytoplankton blooms were evident, and several ponds were nutrient-rich and highly productive.

**Health concern:** Some personnel developed [[Swimmer's Itch]], a skin reaction caused by a bird-specific schistosome. The parasite cannot mature in humans but can survive under the skin for a few days. The source tanks could not be identified.

**Potential native amphibians and reptiles:** The survey noted that arid-land amphibians likely occur in the area, several of which are rare in the U.S. and found in Arizona only near BANWR. These include the narrow-mouthed toad, Sonoran green toad, Colorado River toad, red-spotted toad, Plains and possibly Woodhouse's toads, Western and Couch's spadefoot toads, Canyon treefrog, Lowland leopard frog, Western barking frog, and possibly the Tarahumara frog. Tarahumara frogs require permanent water, whereas the others breed in semipermanent pools filled by heavy rain. The [[Yellow Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon flavescens*) may also occur as an isolated population.

## Key Findings

1. **Only one nonnative species is useful:** Mosquitofish provide local mosquito control. That function can be duplicated with native fishes in smaller tanks, so there is no justification for keeping the other introduced species.
2. **Native fish candidates:** Small tanks could hold [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*). Larger habitats are needed for [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*).
3. **Stocking restrictions:** Stocking the three large-river species in the upper Santa Cruz drainage is outside their native ranges but within the Gila drainage they were previously taken from. This is justified by the need for additional refugium space and research animals, since the drainage barriers prevent escape except into long stretches of dry streambed.
4. **Southern tanks:** No tank in the south-flowing drainage (only a few of which were sampled) should receive fishes native to the Colorado River basin. Depending on drainage relationships yet to be confirmed, southern habitats might be used for propagating Gila topminnow and Gila chub. The divide between north- and south-flowing terrain must be determined before any fish introductions in the southern refuge.
5. **Management of competitors:** Maintaining a percentage of tanks free of fish predators and competitors is recommended for the enhancement of semiaquatic amphibian and reptile species.
6. **Data gaps:** Habitat characteristics, tank size and permanence, and the full tank inventory remain incomplete. Additional ground surveys are required before tanks can be selected for endangered-species management projects.
