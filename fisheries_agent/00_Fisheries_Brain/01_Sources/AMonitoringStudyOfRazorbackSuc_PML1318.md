# Razorback Sucker Reintroduction Monitoring: October 1987 Monthly Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This monthly report from the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], prepared by consultant [[Dan Langhorst]], documents October 1987 stocking and monitoring of hatchery-reared juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the lower [[Colorado River]]. Activities covered stocking at [[Goose Flats Backwater]], sampling at [[Parker Lake]], and sampling at two training-structure backwaters about 10 river miles south of Parker, Arizona. Stocked fish were not recovered in any sampling effort. The report attributes poor capture success to possible predation by non-native piscivores, emigration from backwaters, and a disease outbreak in rearing ponds. It also notes that 2 weeks after release no stocked razorbacks were captured at Goose Flats.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large, endemic catostomid of the Colorado River basin. In this program, fish were reared in [[Native Fish Ponds]] and released as juveniles. Earlier work used [[Trammel Net]] sampling and large [[Minnow Trap]] sets, which produced no razorbacks. Shoreline [[Seining]] and an [[Oneida Net]] were tested in October 1987 as alternative capture methods.

Key stocking and size data from the report:

- Juvenile razorbacks reared at Native Fish Ponds were stocked at Goose Flats Backwater in October 1987. Table 1 total is given as 2,200 fish; the scanned table is partly illegible, so exact pond-by-pond numbers should be checked against the original.
- About 125 razorbacks were collected during seining at the time of stocking, with mean total length 92 mm (range 67–129 mm). An additional 150 were tallied upon release.
- Ponds were infested with [[Anchorworm]] (*Lernaea* sp.). Source is unclear; the report suggests transport in hatchery water or contamination from [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which occur in all rearing ponds. Fish were removed from infested ponds, and mortality may have been complete there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Goose Flats Backwater.** Five trammel nets (40 m long, 2 m deep, 15.5 mm outer mesh, 6.5 mm inner mesh) were set overnight on October 13 to assess post-stocking use. Table 2 records about 1,300 individuals across species, with [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) dominant. Other taxa included [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Mosquitofish]]. No razorbacks were captured.

**Predation.** The report identifies [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], and [[Flathead Catfish]] as possible contributors to poor recapture. Low capture numbers of these predators suggest their impact may be minor. Stomachs of predators large enough to eat stocked razorbacks contained no razorbacks.

**Parker Lake.** On October 21, Parker Lake was seined and an Oneida net was set in an attempt to detect a razorback stocked there on March 31, 1987. Earlier trammel net and large minnow trap sets had also failed to capture razorbacks. Shoreline seining caught mosquitofish, young-of-the-year carp, and young-of-the-year bluegill. One Oneida net set captured approximately 10,000 young-of-the-year shad and 10 young-of-the-year fish of another species. No razorbacks were captured.

**Parker backwaters.** On October 26, two trammel nets and one large minnow trap were set overnight in each of two training-structure backwaters, located about 25 river miles downstream of Parker Dam. Both sites were previous larval and juvenile stocking sites. Trammel nets were set parallel to shore near [[Myriophyllum]] beds, and minnow traps were placed mid-backwater. The backwaters experience daily water-level fluctuation. Depth ranged from about 2 m at high water to a shallower depth at low water; the low-water value is illegible in the scan. Trammel nets caught no razorbacks. Minnow traps caught 4 young-of-the-year largemouth bass, 4 adult redear sunfish, and 1 juvenile bluegill.

**Parker area, October 27.** Trammel nets sampled the California and Arizona sides of Parker backwaters (Tables 4 and 5). Species captured included threadfin shad, bluegill, largemouth bass, common carp, redear sunfish, [[Tilapia]] spp., and channel catfish. No razorbacks were captured on either side.

**Blythe pond.** Razorbacks stocked as larvae in the Blythe Municipal Golf Course pond in 1987 were monitored. Eight large minnow traps set overnight caught very few fish. Snorkel surveys estimated about 1,000 juvenile razorbacks in the pond. The report notes that the Oneida net has proven effective there and will be set later to collect sufficient numbers.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking outcome:** No stocked razorbacks were captured at Goose Flats Backwater 2 weeks after release. The report suggests the fish had emigrated from the backwater.
- **Predation:** Non-native piscivores may have contributed to poor capture success, but their numbers were low, and no stocked razorbacks were found in predator stomachs.
- **Disease:** Anchorworm infestation forced the removal of razorbacks from rearing ponds and may have caused complete mortality in those ponds.
- **Parker backwaters:** No razorbacks were captured in either backwater by trammel net or minnow trap. Backwater use after release was assessed as intermittent, with stocked fish entering the main channel within days of release in earlier tests.
- **Parker Lake and Parker area:** Multiple gear types, including trammel nets, minnow traps, seining, and Oneida nets, failed to capture razorbacks.
- **Blythe pond:** Snorkel estimates of about 1,000 juvenile razorbacks indicate survival in that pond. The Oneida net was identified as the most effective capture method there.
- **Fieldwork:** Fieldwork included completing California Native Species Survey forms. Hours worked were at least 40 per week for the work weeks of 1–2, 5–9, 12–16, 19–23, and 26–30 (the report's dates).
