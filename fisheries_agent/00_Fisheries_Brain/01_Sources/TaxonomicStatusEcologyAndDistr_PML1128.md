# Taxonomic Status, Ecology, and Distribution of the Headwater Catfish (*Ictalurus lupus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 final report by Julian M. Humphries and Robert Rush Miller (Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan) examines the [[Headwater Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lupus*), one of the least known freshwater fishes of North America. The study tests whether *I. lupus* is a valid taxon separate from the [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), maps its historical and present distribution, assesses population status, and recommends conservation measures. The authors conclude that *I. lupus* is a valid species, that its range has contracted during the 20th century, and that it does not currently warrant listing as endangered or threatened, provided [[Stocking]] of *I. punctatus* into its habitat is avoided.

## Background & Description

*Ictalurus lupus* was described by Charles Girard in a cursory report on fishes of the U.S.–Mexican Boundary region. Girard's description was insufficient for diagnosis apart from a low anal-ray count. Later authors (including Günther, Jordan, Evermann, and Cope) added few characters or localities. [[Seth Meek]] first documented the species from the San Fernando and Soto la Marina systems in Mexico, and most later Mexican records derive from his material. Several 20th-century workers, including [[Hubbs]] and [[Robinson]], treated *lupus* as an ecological variant of *punctatus*. The only substantive morphological work was an unpublished dissertation by [[Lundberg]] (1979), based on skeletal material from [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]] that the authors judged possibly atypical.

Methods followed a [[Morphometric Measurement System]] designed to cover body shape more completely than traditional measures, with most distances taken between homologous points. Three meristic characters were used: gill rakers, anal-fin rays, and vertebrae (total counts, including five Weberian apparatus vertebrae, from radiographs). Analysis used [[Principal Components Analysis]] (PCA), with morphometric and meristic variables analyzed separately.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The species occurs in spring-fed tributaries and swift-flowing riffles, and was also collected from main-channel habitats of the [[Rio Grande]], which the authors found unexpected. In the Rio Grande, specimens were collected in daytime and appear to occupy a niche similar to that of [[Madtoms]] (*Noturus*) in eastern North America.

**Distribution.** Historically the range is extensive. In the United States it includes all but the extreme headwaters of the [[Pecos River]] and [[Devils River]], plus scattered Rio Grande populations. Earlier reports from the [[Guadalupe River]], [[San Marcos River]], and [[Nueces River]] were poorly supported, and only a few valid records exist for these drainages. In Mexico the species occurs in the [[Rio Conchos]], [[Rio Salado]], [[San Juan River]], [[San Fernando River]], and [[Soto La Marina River]] systems. The authors examined specimens from 17 drainage units.

**Population status.** The species is extremely rare in the [[San Antonio River]] and Nueces drainages. The population in [[San Solomon Springs]] (Toyah Creek) numbers in the thousands, but the spring is periodically drained for cleaning, injuring or killing many fish in a shallow outlet canal. [[Delaware Creek]] populations are healthy, with a headwater site on private land that is considered a relatively safe location. Mexican populations were judged more stable than US populations and not susceptible to extirpation in the near term.

**Threats.** The authors identify:
- [[Reservoir Construction]], which removes free-flowing habitat, although reservoirs also favor *I. punctatus*.
- [[Low-Water Bridges]], which form pools above concrete berms and may hinder upstream migration.
- [[Water Diversion for Irrigation]], which has reduced free-running water.
- [[Introgression]] with *I. punctatus*, including stocking and spread from [[Amistad Reservoir]].
- Conflicts between landowners and state and federal officials, which blocked sampling on private land along the Devils River.

## Key Findings

1. **Valid taxon.** The authors concluded that the morphological, meristic, and osteological differences provide persuasive evidence that *I. lupus* is a valid species. Subsequent [[Electrophoresis]] abstracts (Kelsch et al.; Yates et al., 1982) reported genetic differences between the species.

2. **Diagnostic characters (Table 2).**
   - Anal-fin rays: *lupus* 22–27 (modal 24–25); *punctatus* 26–31 (modal 28–29).
   - Vertebrae: *lupus* 44–48 (modal 46–47); *punctatus* 48–59 (modal 49–50).
   - Osteological traits, including clavicle process ornamentation, supraneural shape, and supraoccipital spine length, also differ.
   - Vertebral counts of 46–49 overlap between species, so individuals in this range require additional characters for identification.

3. **Hybridization.** Intergradation was detected in the [[Devils River]], the Pecos drainage, and the [[Toyah Creek]] population. Individuals with 49 vertebrae were considered possible hybrids.

4. **Geographic variation.** Populations from [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]] and the [[Upper Rio Conchos]] were consistently distinctive. Cuatro Cienegas specimens initially resembled *punctatus* morphometrically, and the authors could not determine whether they represent an independent derivation or divergence from *lupus*. Vertebral counts were lower in upstream Rio Grande samples and higher in lower tributaries such as the San Fernando and Soto La Marina, suggesting a geographic pattern. The Gila River population resembled *punctatus* and may be an introduced stock.

5. **Expanded range.** Museum records and 1982 fieldwork showed the distribution is far more extensive than published records indicated, although the Lower Conchos likely holds unsurveyed populations.

6. **Conservation recommendation.** The authors recommend that *I. lupus* not be listed as threatened or endangered, but that its populations be maintained. Recommended measures include:
   - Notifying state fish and game departments of the species' presence.
   - Preventing transplantation of *I. punctatus* into *lupus* habitat.
   - Further study of the Conchos population, which if distinct would have a much smaller range.
   - Monitoring the Devils River introgression, which the authors attribute to *punctatus* from Amistad Reservoir.
