# Muleshoe Cooperative Management Area: Planning Correspondence and Native Fish Conservation Scoping

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of planning correspondence for the [[Muleshoe Cooperative Management Area]] (CMA) in southeastern Arizona. It contains three parts: a December 10, 1993 letter from the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) reinitiating planning for the [[Muleshoe Ecosystem Management Plan]] (EMP); the BLM's November 1990 scoping issues list; and a January 17, 1998 comment letter from aquatic ecologist [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University). Minckley's letter is the only part that addresses fishes and aquatic systems directly. It argues that the Gila River basin has lost most of its native fish fauna, that five native fish taxa persist on the CMA, and that conservation and reintroduction of native fishes should be explicit goals of the plan.

## Background & Description

**Planning context (BLM letter, December 10, 1993):**
- The Muleshoe CMA is cooperatively managed by the BLM, the [[U.S. Forest Service]], and [[The Nature Conservancy]].
- Planning was delayed pending completion of the [[Safford Resource Management Plan]].
- The planning area was expanded to the larger Muleshoe Ecosystem, which includes major portions of the Redfield, Hot Springs, and Cherry Springs watersheds.
- The EMP is to cover the [[Redfield Canyon Wilderness]], the proposed [[Hot Springs-Swamp Springs ACEC]], and the [[Soza Mesa Allotment]] in one interdisciplinary plan, using an [[Ecosystem Management]] approach.
- Written comments were requested by January 24, 1994, addressed to Karen Simms, Tucson Resource Area Office.

**Scoping issues (BLM, November 1990 Open House, reexamined in 1993):** Issues were grouped into Cultural, Economy, Fire, Other/General Planning, Access/Roads, Wildlife, Wilderness/Recreation, Watershed (including riparian vegetation), and Livestock Grazing/Maintenance of Improvements. Watershed and wildlife items mention riparian communities, threatened and endangered (T&E) species, and water sources for [[Bighorn Sheep]] and deer. Several items call for protecting [[Riparian Communities]] and their associated species.

**Minckley's comment letter (January 17, 1998):**
- Minckley states that the initial issues list dealt with aquatic systems only in a general way, mainly as water sources for wildlife and domestic stock.
- He notes that public interest in riparian, aquatic, and native fisheries conservation appears to be increasing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish fauna status (Gila River basin):**
- Of 17 native fish taxa known from the [[Gila River Basin]], one is extinct and all remaining taxa are imperiled.
- Nine taxa are federally listed as threatened or endangered.
- The remaining seven are recognized or proposed as Category 1 or 2 (eligible for or under consideration for listing).
- Six of the remaining 16 taxa are extirpated from the Gila River basin but persist elsewhere in the [[Colorado River System]].

**Fishes on the CMA:**
- Only five native taxa are known to persist on the CMA: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*).
- None of the five is federally listed. Minckley believes only one is listed by the State of Arizona.
- These five are generally the most abundant members of the original fauna in relatively natural aquatic habitats of the Gila basin.
- At least eight native taxa originally occurred in the CMA area but are now absent. Minckley describes this as evidence of environmental degradation already incurred by the area.

**Non-native organisms:** The letter identifies [[Bullfrog]] (*Lithobates catesbeianus*) and game fishes as non-native organisms of concern. It also mentions [[Crayfish]] as game fish forage and as a possible concern. Predatory and competitive fishes are noted as present in livestock-watering ponds.

**Habitat fragmentation:** Minckley describes fragmentation of aquatic habitats and native biotas as natural in the American Southwest. Complete loss of emigration and immigration potential is not natural. He therefore argues that each refugium ecosystem depends on others of its kind in the region.

## Key Findings

1. **Native fish loss:** Of 17 native fish taxa in the Gila River basin, one is extinct and the other 16 are imperiled. Nine are federally listed as threatened or endangered, and seven are Category 1 or 2 candidates.
2. **Extirpation:** Six of the remaining 16 taxa are extirpated from the Gila River basin but persist in the Colorado River system.
3. **Current CMA fauna:** Five native taxa persist on the CMA: speckled dace, longfin dace, Gila chub, Sonora sucker, and desert sucker. None is federally listed.
4. **Historic loss:** At least eight native taxa originally occurring in the CMA area are now absent.
5. **Conservation priority:** Minckley argues that conservation of extinction-resistant common taxa is a major goal, and that species reintroduction should also be part of the overall plan.
6. **Planning gap:** The 1990 scoping issues addressed aquatic systems only in general terms, such as water sources for wildlife and domestic stock.
7. **Recommended questions for scoping (Minckley's eight items):**
   - Who will manage existing aquatic habitats for existing aquatic wildlife?
   - Will management plans enhance and expand native populations into existing and potential habitats?
   - Will native fishes and other aquatic organisms be considered relative to non-native introductions and enhancement (e.g., game fishes, bullfrogs, crayfish)?
   - Will detrimental non-native organisms (predatory and competitive fishes, bullfrogs) be managed against or removed, including from livestock-watering ponds?
   - Will habitats be evaluated, enhanced, or re-created so that extirpated native species may be considered for reintroduction?
   - Will native species be reintroduced to restore original or near-original aquatic ecosystems?
   - Will management be watershed-based (ecosystem level) or based on land ownership?
   - Will aquatic biologists trained in non-native species management be appointed to the interdisciplinary planning group?
8. **Vision for the area:** Minckley's vision has two parts. First, enhance aquatic habitats to sustain as diverse an aquatic and riparian community as develops naturally, with assistance such as reintroduction. Second, integrate efforts to maintain naturalness in biota and ecology with efforts elsewhere in the region, through networks such as [[Riparian Conservation Areas]], [[Nature Conservancy Refuges]], and [[National Wildlife Refuges]].
9. **Document status:** The 1993 BLM letter requests public comment and states that public input will shape the EMP. The 1998 letter is Minckley's formal comment submission and requests a place on the mailing list.
