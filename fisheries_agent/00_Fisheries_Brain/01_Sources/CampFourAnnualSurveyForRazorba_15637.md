# Camp Four 1991 Annual Spring Survey: Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub in Lake Mohave and Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) report, dated September 25, 1991, summarizes the "Camp Four" multi-agency field survey for [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The survey was run from the Phoenix Field Office and took place in March 1991 at [[Arrowhead Cove]] on [[Lake Mohave]] (March 16-18) and north of [[South Cove]] on [[Lake Mead]] (March 18-22). Only two razorback suckers were captured, both in Lake Mohave, and none were found in Lake Mead. The report recommends a coordinated recovery approach for Lake Mead, with trammel or hoop nets in preference to electrofishing in the turbid eastern reaches.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a long-lived, rare catostomid that was proposed for endangered listing when this survey was conducted. The bonytail chub is a rare cyprinid of the Colorado River system. The fourth in a series of multi-agency sampling efforts, the survey aimed to capture bonytail and survey for razorback suckers. Because few razorback suckers had been found in the lower end of Lake Mohave in earlier years, the crew added a reconnaissance trip to eastern Lake Mead. The stated purposes were to support the listing and consultation process, assess recovery potential in Lake Mead similar to Lake Mohave, and contribute data to the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]].

Coordination involved the [[Arizona State University]] Center for Environmental Studies (Drs. W.L. Minckley and Paul Marsh, who coordinated the survey and will tabulate final results), the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (Kingman), the Hualapai Fish and Wildlife Department, Mohave Community College, the National Park Service (NPS), and the Nevada Department of Wildlife. Six participants are listed in Appendix A. The report also includes a distribution list of about 20 agency and university recipients.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods (Lake Mohave).** The survey used passive integrated transponder (PIT) tags in place of the floy tags used in earlier years. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] chose PIT tags, which are 1.2 x 10 mm, internally placed, biologically inert, and read by an external reader. One multifilament [[Trammel Net]] (150 ft x 6 ft) was fished at [[Burro Cove]] (Cove #32), where a wild bonytail had been captured in 1988. It was set for 38 hours, equal to 2 net-nights. [[Electrofishing]] was done from a 20-ft Kann boat with a Coffelt VVP-15 unit, a 6.5-kW generator, and 12-inch spherical electrodes, for a total of 70 minutes at five coves.

**Methods (Lake Mead).** The team had planned to launch at Pearce Ferry, but heavy sediment in the [[Colorado River]] made navigation hazardous, so it launched at South Cove and camped in a protected cove north of the ramp. The team used the NDW 20-ft Whaler for scouting and the FWS 20-ft Kann for electrofishing. Weather and lake conditions were calm on the first evening and deteriorated during the week. The survey focused on last known razorback capture sites in Gregg Basin, drawn from a 1978-79 AGFD baseline study that listed [[Hualapai Bay]], [[Smith Bay]], Wagon Trail Cove, and South Cove as 1977-1979 collection sites. All except Hualapai Bay were investigated.

**Local observations.** NPS Meadview Ranger Don McBee knew of no razorback sucker sightings by anglers or others in the previous 10 years. He reported a lake elevation of 1179.2 feet above sea level.

**Fauna observed.** Species seen or recorded included [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], a small centrarchid, red-necked grebes (*Podiceps grisegena*), horned grebes (*P. auritus*), and double-crested cormorants (*Phalacrocorax auritus*).

**Habitat notes.** In Lake Mead, the eastern habitat that looks most promising for razorbacks also tends to have poor water clarity during much of the survey season. Silt was noted near the Arizona-Nevada State Line and at Bradley Bay. Many coves had rocky reefs, pondweed, or gravel bottoms, and some had woody debris or tamarisk and arrowweed along the shoreline.

## Key Findings

**Lake Mohave results**
- Five species were captured, for a stated total of 31 individuals. The table entries add up to 34, so the discrepancy may be a transcription or table error.
- Only **two razorback suckers** were captured, both by electrofishing at Burro Cove (Cove #32, 17 March). Their recorded values were 590 mm TL / 2246 g (PIT 7F7D177121) and 589 mm TL / 2033 g (PIT 7F7D154368).
- One razorback was seen at Forked Cove (#24) but not captured.
- No razorback suckers were found at Arrowhead Cove, which was historically a traditional site for the species in the lower lake.
- Catch by trammel net:
  - Striped bass: 19 fish, mean TL 262 mm (243-299).
  - Largemouth bass: 3 fish, mean TL 421 mm.
  - Carp: 5 fish, mean TL 438 mm (369-624).
  - Rainbow trout: 5 fish, mean TL 234 mm (223-261).
- Scales from a largemouth bass were sent to the AGFD Regional Office in Kingman.

**Lake Mead results**
- **No razorback suckers were caught or observed** in Lake Mead during the March 18-21 electrofishing survey.
- The most notable catch was a very large **striped bass (1265 mm TL)** electrofished near the Nevada shoreline. Its estimated weight was about 20 kg, scales were collected, and the fish was released.
- Coves surveyed included those at Smith Bay (Cove #156, with a reported "good" habitat for razorbacks), South Cove and the North Bay area, Bradley Bay, Devil's Cove, and Hell's Kitchen. Carp were the most common fish observed.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The absence of razorbacks in 1991 does not prove they are absent from eastern Lake Mead. Hualapai Bay, the areas above Iceberg Canyon, and the Colorado River delta were not surveyed.
- Rain, cold wind, and turbidity may have reduced razorback use of shallow coves and limited detection by electrofishing.
- Future effort up-lake from the Arizona-Nevada State Line should use trammel nets or [[Hoop Net]]s, either alone or alongside electrofishing.
- The Native Fish Work Group should review potential Lake Mead recovery actions and set a coordinated approach. The report views the growing inter-agency interest within the Department of the Interior as a positive basis for continuing the native fish recovery program.
