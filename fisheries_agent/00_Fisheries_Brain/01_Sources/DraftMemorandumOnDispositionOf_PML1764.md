# Draft Memorandum on Disposition of Bonytail Tissue and Otoliths

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 internal memorandum from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in Parker, Arizona, comments on a draft plan for handling tissue, [[Otolith Aging]] structures, and related specimens from [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a rare fish. The bonytail specimens come from recent mortalities at a lake. The reviewer agrees that the cause of these deaths must be investigated but disagrees with the proposed methodology. The main objections are the risk of contamination of a rare species, the proposed transfer of samples to a laboratory without relevant experience, and the inclusion of known-age fish for comparison. The memo recommends that trained experts at [[Arizona State University]] remove tissue and read otoliths, with multiple researchers confirming the results.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Draft memorandum, two pages, dated 22 June 1992, from the Parker Fish and Wildlife Office.
- **Subject:** Disposition of bonytail tissue and otoliths, and processing of specimens of old and bonytail.
- **Correspondents:** The memo responds to a draft prepared by Gary Carnical, with a copy to the Associate Manager, Fisheries. It is written by Chuck Minckley, Project Coordinator at the Parker office. Other named recipients include [[Paul Marsh]], [[W.L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University), and staff at Dexter.
- **Scan quality:** The text is heavily OCR-corrupted. Some passages, including a lake name, the upper age limit for known-age fish, and parts of the sample-handling steps, could not be recovered with confidence.
- **Species focus:** Bonytail is described as a rare species, and the memo stresses the need to avoid contaminating its tissue.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document focuses on specimen handling and aging rather than on the habitat. It indicates that bonytail were dying at a lake, prompting a study of the cause. Key methods and tools referenced include:

- **Genetic analysis:** tissue for [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) and [[Allozyme Analysis]] (described as "ploy" and "allozyme" samples). Replicate samples are to be stored at -80°C.
- **Morphological documentation:** photographs of each fish for a [[Morphology Study]].
- **Age determination:** [[Otolith Aging]] as the primary method, with [[Scale Aging]] and [[Vertebrae Aging]] as backups if necessary.
- **Reference material:** known-age bonytail held by Paul Marsh and W.L. Minckley, and fish known to be younger than about 15 years or older than an illegible upper limit.

The memo also describes the [[Tissue Contamination Risk]] that drives the reviewer's concerns. Because bonytail are rare, the reviewer wants handling kept under the control of experienced personnel.

## Key Findings

1. **Methodology dispute.** The reviewer agrees that the recent bonytail mortalities should be investigated but rejects the plan as drafted. They state they do not object to the research goal.

2. **Sample transfer.** The reviewer proposes sending all tissue samples to the appropriate research laboratories rather than having them processed at Dexter. Tissue for [[Genetic Analysis]] should be removed, sent to the relevant researchers, and replicate samples stored at -80°C.

3. **Contamination concern.** The reviewer says that personnel involved in the taxonomy project should remove tissue for analysis. They describe a general fear of contamination among staff, which they say has been voiced repeatedly.

4. **Timing of removal.** Otoliths should be removed from the fish at the time of death, with the fish then frozen. Once thawed, the fish can be examined for morphology. The memo states that otoliths should then be sent to Arizona State University for [[Otolith Aging]].

5. **Expertise at Arizona State University.** Researchers there have been removing, mounting, and examining bonytail otoliths for at least ten years. Staff at Dexter lack comparable experience and are not recommended for these tasks.

6. **Known-age fish are unnecessary.** Including known-age individuals in the sample is called an unnecessary cost, since Paul Marsh and W.L. Minckley already hold aged bonytail and known-age reference fish for comparison.

7. **Wild versus hatchery origin.** The memo says that fish older than hatchery stock should be easy to distinguish, so the aging question should be straightforward for older animals.

8. **Involve Arizona State University staff.** The reviewer recommends contacting Arizona State University personnel directly. If they are unavailable, other knowledgeable individuals should be identified to make the best use of the material.

9. **Recommended workflow (summary):**
   - Experts photograph the fish for the morphology study.
   - Experts remove tissue and otoliths.
   - Arizona State University reads the otoliths.
   - Otoliths, and if necessary scales and vertebrae, are sent to multiple researchers to confirm results.
   - Inexperienced persons should not handle the material at Dexter, especially if the fish are old.
