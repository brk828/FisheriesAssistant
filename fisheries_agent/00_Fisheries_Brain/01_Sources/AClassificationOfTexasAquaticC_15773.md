# A Classification of Texas Aquatic Communities and Conservation of Endangered/Threatened Taxa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #biogeography #conservation-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 study (Edwards, Longley, Moss, Ward, Matthews, and Stewart) develops a hierarchical classification of [[Texas Aquatic Habitats]] and cross-references it against the distribution of endangered and threatened aquatic vertebrates across the state. The authors define **ten primary aquatic habitat types** (with two subdivided into intermittent/permanent categories, yielding 11 total) and **seven biotic provinces** derived from and expanding upon the classic biogeographic work of [[Blair 1950 Biotic Provinces]] and [[Hubbs 1954 1957 Texas Fish Distribution]]. Key findings show that spring-associated habitats (aquifers, springs, spring-runs) harbor a disproportionate share of endangered fauna in central/western Texas, while large rivers and perennial streams dominate the endangered species pool in eastern Texas. The **Chihuahuan Biotic Province** shows the highest percentage of endangered native fishes (56.4%), more than four times any other province, reflecting the extreme vulnerability of desert spring systems.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose**: To create a classification framework for [[Texas Aquatic Communities]] that facilitates conservation planning for taxa listed by the Texas Organization for Endangered Species ([[TOES]]), Texas Parks and Wildlife Department, and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Scale of Texas water resources**: >3,500 streams/tributaries totaling ~128,000 km (80,000 mi) of streambed; seven major and 17 minor aquifers supplying ~70% of human water use (Texas Dept. of Water Resources 1982).
- **Spring decline**: Texas originally had >280 major springs; only two of four largest historically remain as large springs, and more than 60 have failed completely (Brune 1975, 1981) — a critical data point for [[Spring System Decline in Texas]].
- **Classification methodology**: Combines (1) a longitudinal/qualitative habitat-type descriptor (analogous to but distinct from stream-order concepts) with (2) the biogeographic concept of **biotic provinces** (per Blair 1950; Hubbs 1954, 1957; refined by Conner & Suttkus 1986; Smith & Miller 1986; Diamond et al. 1987).
- **Data source for endangered taxa**: TOES (1988) vertebrate list, restricted to taxa dependent on aquatic environments for at least part of their life cycle; predatory birds/mammals reliant on aquatic prey were also included. Two parallel analyses were run — one with all vertebrates, one restricted to fishes, amphibians, and reptiles only — to control for taxonomic bias.
- Coastal/estuarine organisms were included only as a catch-all category and largely excluded from detailed biotic-province analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Primary Aquatic Habitat Types (Table 1)
1. **Headwaters/Creeks**
   - 1a. Ephemeral/Intermittent — small creeks flowing seasonally (autumn/spring).
   - 1b. Permanent — small headwater creeks with continuous flow; often forested (east) or spring-fed (central/west).
2. **Springs** — aquifer outflows.
3. **Spring-run systems** — permanent flowing reaches immediately downstream of headsprings.
4. **Streams**
   - 4a. Intermittent Plains streams — larger order, highly variable flow (often dry in summer).
   - 4b. Perennial streams — permanent minimal flow year-round; major tributaries.
5. **Rivers** — largest lotic systems (middle-lower reaches of major rivers).
6. **Sinkholes** — karst/limestone depressions connecting to aquifers or water table.
7. **Playa Lakes** — includes Llano Estacado playas, prairie potholes, oxbow/resaca lakes.
8. **Aquifers** — 7 major + 17 minor underground reservoirs.
9. **Lakes** — primarily [[Caddo Lake]] (natural; reservoirs excluded).
10. **Swamps** — forested/shrub wetlands with bayous, restricted to eastern Texas.
11. **Coastal aquatic systems** — estuaries, beaches, coastal waters (catch-all, minimally analyzed).

### Biotic Provinces of Texas (Fig. 1)
1. **[[Austroriparian Province]]** — Piney Woods, Mixed Pine-Hardwood Forests, part of Oak Woods/Prairies and Gulf Coast Prairies.
2. **[[Texan Province]]** — East/West Cross Timbers, Blackland Prairies (Grand Prairie).
3. **[[Kansan Province]]** — Rolling Plains (Mesquite Plains, Escarpment/Canadian Breaks), High Plains.
4. **[[Balconian Province]]** — Edwards Plateau (Live Oak-Mesquite Savannah, Balcones Canyonlands, Lampasas Cut Plains), Llano Uplift.
5. **[[Tamaulipan Province]]** — South Texas Brush Country, Bordas Escarpment, Subtropical Zone, Coastal Sand Plains.
6. **[[Chihuahuan Province]]** — Trans-Pecos (excluding Guadalupe Mountains): Mountain Ranges, Desert Grassland, Desert Scrub, Salt Basin, Sand Hills, Stockton Plateau.
7. **[[Navahonian Province]]** — Guadalupe Mountains streams only (Trans-Pecos).

### Habitat Vulnerability Patterns
- Habitats tied to **spring systems** dominate endangerment in central/western Texas due to aquifer depletion and spring failure.
- **Large rivers/perennial streams** dominate endangerment in eastern Texas, largely from river modification (damming, channelization).
- **No endangered vertebrates** were recorded as primary inhabitants of sinkholes or intermittent streams.
- Prairie potholes and permanent creeks also carry significant numbers of threatened species.
- Swamps, aquifers, lakes, and ephemeral creeks show comparatively fewer endangered species.

## Key Findings

### Distribution of Endangerment by Province (Table 2)
- **All species analysis**: Chihuahuan (28.7%) and Austroriparian (19%) provinces contain the largest shares of endangered aquatic vertebrates; Balconian (16.0%) and Tamaulipan (18%) also significant. Texan, Kansan, and Navahonian provinces show the fewest threatened taxa.
- **Fish/amphibian/reptile-only analysis**: Similar pattern — Chihuahuan (34.2%) and Austroriparian (27.4%) lead, followed by Tamaulipan (17.8%) and Balconian (19.2%).
- Difference between "all vertebrates" and "fish/amphibian/reptile only" analyses was generally small (~4% or less), suggesting habitat degradation impacts diverse taxonomic groups similarly.

### Percent of Native Fishes Endangered by Province (Table 3)
| Biotic Province | Native Fish Species | TOES-listed | % Endangered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Austroriparian | 105 | 13 | 12.4% |
| Texan | 80 | 0 | 0% |
| Kansan | 46 | 0 | 0% |
| Balconian | 75 | 9 | 12.0% |
| Tamaulipan | 59 | 3 | 5.1% |
| **Chihuahuan** | 39 | 22 | **56.4%** |
| Navahonian | ~10 | 0 | 0% |

- The **[[Chihuahuan Desert Fish Fauna]]** shows extreme vulnerability: >50% of native species threatened, driven by dependence on small, declining springs and runs feeding the Pecos River and Rio Grande.
- **Austroriparian** endangerment is tied to modification of large river systems (damming, flow alteration).
- **Balconian** endangerment parallels the Chihuahuan pattern — heavy dependence on aquifers, springs, and spring-runs (e.g., Edwards Aquifer-associated taxa) rather than large rivers.
- **Texan, Kansan, Navahonian** provinces show no endangered fishes, attributed to their fauna being wide-ranging, flow-tolerant plains species.

### Notable Listed Fish Taxa (Appendix A, selected)
- **[[Etheostoma Fonticola]]** (Fountain Darter) — Balconian Province, springs/spring-runs (limited distribution).
- **[[Cyprinodon Elegans]]** (Comanche Springs Pupfish), **[[Cyprinodon Bovinus]]**, **[[Cyprinodon Eximius]]**, **[[Cyprinodon Pecosensis]]** — Chihuahuan pupfish complex, spring/perennial stream dependent.
- **[[Gambusia Nobilis]]**, **[[Gambusia Amistadensis]]** (Amistad Gambusia — noted extinct), **[[Gambusia Gaigei]]**, **[[Gambusia Heterochir]]**, **[[Gambusia Senilis]]**, **[[Gambusia Georgei]]** (San Marcos Gambusia) — Gambusia species heavily tied to isolated Chihuahuan and Balconian spring systems.
- **[[Notropis Simus Simus]]**, **[[Notropis Orca]]**, **[[Notropis Buccula]]**, **[[Notropis Chihuahua]]** — Rio Grande/Pecos basin shiners.
- **[[Percina Maculata]]**, **[[Ammocrypta Clara]]**, **[[Etheostoma Grahami]]**, **[[Etheostoma Radiosum]]** — darters associated with perennial streams and rivers.
- **[[Satan Eurystomus]]** and **[[Trogloglanis Pattersoni]]** — troglobitic catfishes restricted to aquifer habitats (habitat type 8), illustrating extreme habitat specialization.
- **[[Micropterus Treculii]]** (Guadalupe Bass) — Balconian perennial streams.

### Amphibians of Note
- **[[Eurycea Nana]]** (San Marcos Salamander), **[[Eurycea Latitans]]**, **[[Eurycea Tridentifera]]**, **[[Typhlomolge Robusta]]** (Blanco Blind Salamander) — Balconian Province, spring/aquifer-dependent troglobites.

### Conservation Implications
- No single threatened habitat type applies uniformly across Texas; each biotic province has unique hydrographic vulnerabilities.
- Long-term trend of decreasing and modified water sources (TDWR 1981) predicts increasing endangerment across aquatic communities.
- Several taxa are already extinct (e.g., **Gambusia amistadensis**); the authors stress that conservation of diverse habitat types — not just large rivers — is essential to prevent further losses, particularly for spring- and aquifer-dependent endemics in the [[Edwards Aquifer]] and Trans-Pecos regions.
