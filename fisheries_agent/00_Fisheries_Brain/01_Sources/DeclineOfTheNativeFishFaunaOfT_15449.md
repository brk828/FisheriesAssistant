# Decline of the Native Fish Fauna of the Sierra Nevada Foothills, Central California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Moyle and Nichols surveyed fishes in warm, intermittent streams of the [[Sierra Nevada Foothills]] east of California's San Joaquin Valley, part of the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Drainage]]. They collected 24 species, 12 native and 12 introduced, at 167 localities. Comparing these with pre-1900 records, they found that introduced species and [[Rainbow Trout]] have expanded their ranges while native species, especially [[California Roach]], [[Hardhead]], [[Sacramento Squawfish]] and [[Sacramento Sucker]], have contracted. Healthy native assemblages survive only in a narrow middle-elevation band of relatively undisturbed stream sections, and these populations are now isolated from one another. The authors argue that native stream fish faunas should be protected even when they contain no endangered species.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a gap in conservation attention. Much effort on U.S. freshwater fishes focuses on endangered species (Miller, 1972), which can leave declining faunas without listed species neglected. The foothill streams are among the harshest aquatic habitats in the system. In summer many stop flowing, and organisms are crowded into stagnant pools where water temperatures may approach air temperature (30-35 °C). The authors note that these streams are the last waters in the San Joaquin system still holding mostly native fauna. The native fish fauna of the Sacramento-San Joaquin system is reported as 75% endemic (Miller, 1958).

Sampling covered streams between 90 and 1100 m (mean 401 m) in Tulare, Fresno, Madera, Mariposa and Tuolumne counties, with a few sites in Stanislaus and Calaveras counties. Kern County was not sampled. Of the 167 localities, 130 were sampled between 27 July and 4 September 1970, and 37 during summer and autumn 1969, 1970 and 1971. Most streams were not flowing during the main study period, so fish were confined to isolated pools. Collections used 6 mm mesh [[Seine Sampling]] nets 1 m deep and 3-9 m long. Snorkel checks suggested seine catches reflected relative abundance, though lampreys, sculpins and speckled dace may have been undersampled because they burrow. Locality records were mapped against pre-1900 records from Rutter (1908) and Evermann and Clark (1931).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study streams drain rocky, heavily grazed dry grasslands with scattered oaks (*Quercus* spp.) and, at higher elevations, digger pine (*Pinus sabiniana*). Habitat change was the main driver of decline. The authors link differences between the introduced and native associations mainly to human-caused changes: impoundment, reduced stream flow and siltation (Moyle and Nichols, 1973). Stream management for game fish further restricts habitat for native minnows and suckers.

Fish assemblages were grouped into four associations, each tied to a habitat zone (Moyle and Nichols, 1973):

- [[Introduced Fishes Association]]: low-elevation, disturbed waters, usually with two or more centrarchids, [[Mosquitofish]], and a catfish or cyprinid.
- [[Native Cyprinid-Catostomid Association]]: larger streams at intermediate elevations, dominated by Sacramento squawfish, hardhead and Sacramento sucker, confined to a narrow altitudinal band.
- [[California Roach Association]]: small tributaries at intermediate elevations, numerically dominated by California roach.
- [[Rainbow Trout Association]]: high-elevation streams, maintained in some lower streams by poisoning and stocking.

Two of the authors' conclusions concern threats in the wider system. Isolation of native populations by altered lowland reaches can cause local extinction during drought, with no way for populations to recolonize once flow returns.

## Key Findings

**Overall species status and composition**

- 24 species were collected: 12 native and 12 introduced (Table 1 in the source).
- Six native species recorded by Rutter (1908) were not found in this study. Five (Sacramento blackfish, splittail, thicktail chub, tule perch, Sacramento perch) are mainly lowland forms that reached the foothills only in large rivers. The sixth, speckled dace, was recorded at only one locality by Rutter.
- Pre-1900 and modern records were compared with the aim of documenting range changes.

**Species-level results**

- [[Rainbow Trout]] occurred in 20% of samples, mostly in cool, clear, permanent high-elevation streams (average 748 m). Populations in many streams are maintained by stocking.
- [[California Roach]] occurred in 32% of samples, mainly in small intermittent tributaries (average 458 m). It is absent from the upper San Joaquin and Fresno river systems, where [[Green Sunfish]] has replaced it, and rare in the Chowchilla system. Roach were once present in all three systems, since Girard described the species in 1854 from Fort Miller on the San Joaquin River.
- [[Hardhead]] occurred at only 9% of localities but were abundant where present, dominating the Chowchilla River system. They occurred in large undisturbed streams at an average of 320 m.
- [[Sacramento Squawfish]] were widespread but uncommon at low elevations, most abundant at 200-500 m.
- [[Sacramento Sucker]] was the most widely distributed native fish (42% of samples). Most individuals were juveniles, suggesting foothill streams act as nursery areas. Average elevation was 371 m.
- [[Hitch]] survive as scattered populations and are most abundant in the middle Fresno River.
- [[Mosquitofish]] occurred at 34% of sites, mostly in human-disturbed reaches. They were introduced for mosquito control.
- [[Green Sunfish]] was the most widely distributed fish in the study area. It was abundant mainly in impounded or disturbed streams, and roach were usually absent where it was abundant.
- [[Chinook Salmon]]: three small fish (10.5-11 cm TL) were taken in Mill Creek in April 1970, and one adult was caught below [[Friant Dam]] the previous summer. Salmon now reach the Kings and San Joaquin rivers only in years of exceptionally high runoff.
- [[Pacific Lamprey]] spawning was observed below Friant Dam in spring 1969, and ammocoetes were collected near Fresno. A single [[Pacific Brook Lamprey]] was also recorded at Friant.
- Sculpins were probably undersampled, since seines miss them easily.

**Historical change in the San Joaquin River at Friant**

- Four collections were compared: Rutter (1898), Needham and Hanson (1934), Dill (1940-41) and the present study (1970) (Table 2 in the source).
- The 1898 fauna was transitional between the Valley floor and foothill faunas. By 1934, introduced species had appeared alongside natives. By 1940-41 introduced species had increased, but many natives remained. By 1970, the reach below the dam was dominated by planted rainbow trout, prickly sculpins, threespine sticklebacks and mosquitofish, and further downstream by carp, centrarchids and catfishes.
- The source gives Friant Dam's completion as 1946 in one passage and 1942 in another. Both dates appear in the text as written.

**Conservation conclusions and recommendations**

- Healthy native fish populations are restricted to comparatively undisturbed foothill stream sections, and this range is likely to shrink further as development proceeds.
- Native populations in each stream are isolated from one another, raising the risk of local extinction during severe drought.
- Native fish populations that seem widespread should be monitored closely, especially after severe natural conditions stress populations already affected by human activity.
- If decline continues, suitable stream sections should be set aside and managed for native fishes.
- Native fish fauna should be considered in environmental impact statements for any development, whether or not endangered species are present.
- Further studies are needed on native fish status across river systems throughout California.
