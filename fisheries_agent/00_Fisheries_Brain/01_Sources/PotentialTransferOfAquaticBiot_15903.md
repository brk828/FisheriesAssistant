## Ecological & Environmental Context

The compiled literature collectively describes several interconnected ecological processes governing lotic (stream/river) ecosystems:

- **[[Invertebrate Drift]]**: Waters (1964, 1965, 1972) established foundational concepts of downstream drift as a colonization and recolonization mechanism in stream benthos; Brusven (1970) examined drift periodicity and upstream dispersion of insects.
- **Upstream Recolonization**: Bishop and Hynes (1969) and Elliott (1971) documented upstream movements of benthic invertebrates counterbalancing downstream drift losses.
- **[[Passive Dispersal]] via Vertebrate Vectors**: Multiple studies (Malone 1965a-c; Proctor 1959, 1964; Proctor and Malone 1965; Rees 1952; Roscoe 1955; Dundee et al. 1967) document dispersal of aquatic snails, plankton, algae, and crustacean eggs via waterfowl and shorebird intestinal tracts, feathers, and migratory behavior — a key mechanism for colonizing isolated freshwater habitats.
- **[[Passive Dispersal]] via Insects**: Stewart and Schlichting (1966) and Revill et al. (1967) show craneflies and midges also transport viable algae and protozoa.
- **Aerial/Atmospheric Dispersal**: Schlichting (1961, 1964) and McAtee (1917) investigated airborne transport of algae/protozoa and unusual "showers of organic matter," including Gudger's (1929) inquiry into fish reportedly falling with rain.
- **Colonization of Novel/Isolated Habitats**: Fernando (1958, 1959, 1963) and Maguire (1963) provide foundational work on aquatic insect colonization of small or isolated freshwater bodies — directly relevant to assessing invasion risk in newly connected systems like a diversion channel.
- **Fish–Invertebrate Trophic Link**: Elliott (1967) and Hoopes (1959) tie invertebrate (mayfly/caddisfly) prey availability to fish diet in trout streams and the Mississippi River, respectively.
- **Invasive Species Case Study**: The extensive Corbicula literature (Sinclair 1963, 1971; Sinclair and Isom 1963; Eckblad 1975; Thompson and Sparks 1977) exemplifies a well-documented freshwater bivalve invasion, including debate over waterfowl-mediated dispersal as an invasion vector.
