# Draft Environmental Assessment: Completed Segments of the Rio Grande Boundary Preservation Project, Fort Quitman to Hacienda, Texas (April 1983)

#riparian-ecology #river-restoration #environmental-assessment

## Executive Summary

This draft environmental assessment (EA), prepared by the [[United States Section of the International Boundary and Water Commission]] (US Section, IBWC) in April 1983, evaluates the completed segments of the US part of the [[Rio Grande Boundary Preservation Project]]. The 199-river-mile reach runs from near Fort Quitman to Hacienda, Texas. The project restores the river channel so the middle of the Rio Grande keeps its legal character as the [[International Boundary]], as set by the 1848 and 1853 treaties and confirmed by the [[1970 Boundary Treaty]]. As of March 1983 about 25 percent of the US length requiring restoration was complete, covering 42.7 miles. The EA reviews the need for the project, the completed work, observed benefits, and responses to wildlife and conservation concerns raised by agencies and associations. It concludes that the completed work is consistent with the treaty and with the findings of the original Environmental Impact Statement (EIS), and it sets out improved practices and a schedule for the remaining work.

## Background & Description

- **Authority and process:** The joint project followed [[Minute No. 262]] of the IBWC, approved by both governments. The US part was authorized by Public Law on October 25, 1972. A Draft EIS was circulated with a public meeting, and a Final EIS (FEIS) was adopted after comments were addressed. Construction began in April 1980.
- **Need:** Since the 1940s, flows in the 199-mile reach have fallen so far that there is little or no water in the riverbed for long periods. Flash-flood sediment from tributary arroyos is filling the channel, and salt cedar ([[Salt Cedar]]) is overgrowing it. Sections of the channel are becoming hard to locate, so the international boundary is hard to identify and moves frequently. Floodwaters pond across the flood plain, become saline, and leave an alkali-encrusted monoculture of salt cedar, which has reduced native plant and animal life and taken farmland out of production.
- **Basic plan features (per the FEIS):**
  1. Channel restoration to a depth of six feet with a top width of at least 38 feet, with excavation spoils placed in mounds at least 100 feet from the centerline.
  2. A 25-foot vegetative fringe on each bank, with 23-foot gaps at intervals of about 230 feet to pass floodwater (the fringe was not achieved in all sections).
  3. Clearing of an overbank passageway parallel to the channel.
  4. Disposal of clearing vegetation at least 100 feet from the centerline, covered with excavation spoil.
  5. Prohibition of obstructions within 100 feet of the centerline, with relocation of private levees.
  6. Preservation of diversion weirs still in use, and removal of abandoned weirs.
- **Division of work:** Each government performs overbank work on its own territory and shares channel excavation equally. The US has the upstream half and Mexico the downstream half, each covering about 95 river miles, excluding canyon sections that need no restoration. The dividing point is Ruidosa.
- **Mitigation:** Measures include retaining selected trees, seeding overbank areas and spoil mounds, constructing ten wildlife ponds (two one-acre and four-acre ponds in the US), and acquiring about 295 acres of ephemeral wetlands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Endangered species and cultural resources:** The [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] advised that restoring and preserving the channel would not adversely affect any known endangered species or modify habitat essential to their survival. The EIS conclusions on endangered species and cultural resources were judged unchanged. Archeological work and consultation with the [[State Historic Preservation Officer]] continue.
- **Wetlands and floodplains:** The project was assessed for compliance with [[Executive Order 11988]] (Floodplain Management) and [[Executive Order 11990]] (Protection of Wetlands).
- **Benefits observed through March 1983:**
  - Restored channel is improving drainage of adjoining lands and halting their trend toward sterile alkali conditions.
  - Cleared, seeded overbank areas provide better habitat for white-winged and mourning doves, non-game birds, and other wildlife. Hunters and livestock grazers now use the cleared passageways.
  - Spoil mounds provide habitat for small burrowing mammals and reptiles, and high ground for wildlife and livestock during overflows.
  - Clearing, fringe, and mounds have added edge and diversity to the former monotypic salt cedar community.
  - Ponds provide permanent (one-acre) and ephemeral (four-acre) water for migratory and resident waterfowl, deer, and other wildlife.
- **Wildlife concerns and responses:** Concerns raised at a June 29-30, 1982 field inspection, a July 1982 meeting, and later letters from the US Fish and Wildlife Service and the [[National Wildlife Federation]] were addressed in the EA (Appendix B holds the letters). Technical details are in Appendices C through G.

## Key Findings

**1. Ecological health units (EHUs).** Using the [[Computerized Ecological Impact Evaluation Method]] (CEIEM), the reevaluation of the completed 23-mile Capote Creek to Ruidosa Reach and the partly completed 51-mile Ruidosa to Hacienda Reach showed an initial-year EHU increase of 4 percent over the FEIS's preferred alternative. The reevaluation also reported an initial-year composition increase of 0.2 percent for the Hacienda Reach, an edge increase of 10.7 percent for the Capote Creek Reach, and a diversity change of -7.2 percent for the Hacienda Reach, as printed in the source.

**2. Spoil mound concerns.**
- *Steep slopes:* The slopes have since been flattened.
- *Lack of seeding:* The mounds have since been seeded with grasses and forbs.
- *Orientation:* Mounds were not built obliquely to the river. Mexico objected that oblique mounds would deflect floodwater onto its side, contrary to Article IV B(f) of the 1970 Treaty. Mounds were built roughly parallel to the river or in near-round or oblong shapes. Aligning them obliquely would have required acquiring land out to about one-eighth mile, an unnecessary taking of private land.
- *Proximity and levee use:* Mounds are spaced about 200 to 291 feet apart. The EA judges their use as levees by landowners "not reasonably foreseeable," because the levees were built long ago and no one has used the mounds as levees since.
- *Easement adequacy:* No future spoil problem is foreseen. Future maintenance spoils will go on the Mexican side in the Capote Creek and Ruidosa-Hacienda reaches, and additional easements will be acquired as needed.

**3. Farm development.** The critical factor for farm development is the lack of a dependable water supply, not spoil mounds. Most developed land is cattle pasture, and pasture provides more wildlife food than salt cedar.

**4. Dragline use.** Draglines were used in the 23.2-mile reach because water in the channel made bulldozers impractical. Restoration was urgent to prevent a channel change and an international boundary dispute. Some temporary adverse impacts to aquatic organisms may have occurred, but the EA does not consider them significant. The fringe showed no significant loss of trees.

**5. Construction in the bird breeding season.** Work in the 23.2-mile reach during breeding season was necessary to restore the channel and avoid an international problem. Future work will avoid breeding season unless urgent, and then only to the minimum required.

**6. 23-foot gaps.** Leaving gaps uncleared for five years was rejected because the gaps are needed for floodwater flow, and later reclearing would be costlier and more damaging to habitat. The concern about incomplete fringe width was found valid in part. Fringe width was below 25 feet in places, mostly because of cultivation near the bank and inadequate contractor control. The shortfall is attributed to US Section operations and is limited to under 2 percent of the fringe along the 169-mile US bank.

**7. Mitigation delays.** Seeding was delayed in the Ruidosa to Capote Creek Reach, but natural reseeding has covered 85 percent of the area, with 60 percent coverage in one year. Grass and forb seed has been added where cover is below 60 percent. Wetland and pond acquisition has been slower than planned: 183 of the 295 acres of wetlands have rights-of-way, and seven of ten ponds have rights-of-way, with four completed. Right-of-way agreements prohibit pumping water from mitigation ponds and wetlands for purposes other than stock watering.

**8. Watershed management.** A task force found that over 97 percent of the estimated benefits of a watershed management program would accrue to livestock and wildlife production. The US Section supports such a program but considers it the responsibility of the [[Soil Conservation Service]], US Department of Agriculture.

**9. Improved practices for remaining work.**
- Flatter spoil mound slopes, with mounds at least 100 feet apart and cleared passageways seeded as soon as possible.
- Channel excavation by bulldozers and scrapers where practical. Draglines will be used where water is present and restoration is required to meet the 1970 Treaty.
- Fringe protection, with cottonwood and willow planted to compensate for fringe lost during construction.
- Mitigation measures built alongside other project features.
- The US Commissioner decided to do essentially all remaining work with US Section forces and equipment instead of by contract. Biologists and wildlife authorities will conduct more frequent, detailed inspections.

**10. Schedule.**
- The 125-mile reach upstream of Capote Creek will receive channel excavation and overbank work in the fall and winter of 1983-84 and 1984-85, subject to weather.
- The 32-mile reach from Hacienda to Ruidosa, which requires only US-side overbank work, is tentatively scheduled for fall and winter 1983-84, after Mexico completes its channel excavation there.
