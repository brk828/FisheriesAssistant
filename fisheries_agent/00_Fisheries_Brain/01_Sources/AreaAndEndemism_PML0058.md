# Area and Endemism

#biogeography #endemism #vertebrates

## Executive Summary

This review article examines how the size of a geographic area relates to the percentage of [[Endemism]] found within it. Anderson argues that the correlation between area and endemism must be positive, and that larger areas generally show higher degrees of endemism. The paper first addresses a [[Major Semantic Problem]] in the term "endemic," which has been used with at least four meanings, then builds a [[Conceptual Model]] of the processes that change endemism and [[Species Density]]. Empirical patterns are drawn from published data on mammals, birds, anurans, plants and fish from many regions. The paper concludes that area, group characteristics, and geological and climatic history jointly shape endemism, and that these factors interact in a network rather than a linear sequence.

## Background & Description

The article opens by framing endemism across scales. At one extreme, a species confined to a tiny area is endemic to it. At the other, the entire [[Biosphere]] contains 100 percent endemic species. The central question is what happens between these extremes:

- Do areas of different sizes have different degrees of endemism?
- Do areas of the same size have the same degree of endemism wherever and whenever they occur?
- Do different taxonomic or ecologically defined groups differ in endemism within areas of the same size?

Anderson concludes the answers are "yes," "no," and "yes," respectively.

The author reviews the literature, principally recent work scanned through Biological Abstracts from 1969 to 1992, to illustrate definitional problems and to examine the factors thought to influence endemism. Data are compiled in Tables 1 and 2 and summarized in Figure 1. Tables and figures are selected examples, not an exhaustive survey.

Key concepts include:

- **Centers of endemism:** clusters of endemic species with restricted, largely congruent ranges (Haffer, 1981).
- **Centers of coincidence:** areas where environmental conditions suit all members of a faunal element (Armstrong et al., 1986).
- **Areas of endemism:** organised hierarchically, with smaller areas nested in larger ones (Cracraft, 1985, 1991).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Anderson develops a conceptual model in which the percentage of endemism and [[Species Density]] change through a set of processes:

1. Speciation within the area, which adds endemic species.
2. Expansion of an endemic species' range beyond the area, which reduces endemism.
3. Contraction of a range within the area, which reduces endemism.
4. Immigration of a non-endemic species, which lowers endemism but raises species density.
5. Extinction of an endemic species, which reduces both endemism and species density.
6. Expansion of a non-endemic species into the area, which increases species density.
7. Combinations of these, such as a range shift that moves an endemic species from one place to another.

Factors influencing endemism include:

- **Distance from sources of immigrants** (factor A): greater distance tends to raise endemism.
- **Area** (factor B): larger areas tend to have more endemism.
- **Geological age** (factor C): older areas accumulate more endemics.
- **Slower rate of extinction** (factor E), often expressed as a lower [[Turnover Rate]].
- **Environmental stability** and reduced seasonality (factors F and G).
- **Topographic variation** (factors K and L), which creates more habitats.
- **Vagility** (factor N): groups with lower dispersal ability show more endemism. [[Vagility]] also governs whether a taxon is endemic at all.
- **Ecological tolerance** (factor P): groups with narrow environmental tolerances show more endemism.
- **Speciation rate** (factor Q): faster speciation raises endemism.

The author notes that these factors interact and are not independent.

## Key Findings

**Semantic and conceptual findings**

- The term "endemic" has been used in medical, national, area-restricted, and small-area senses. Anderson argues that endemism must always be specified with respect to an area, and that "endemic" without an area is meaningless.
- De Candolle (1820) used endemic to describe taxa confined to a single country, not necessarily a small one. The "small area" meaning emerged later.
- Good (1947, 1974) proposed restricting "endemic" to taxa whose ranges are smaller than average for their rank. Anderson considers this limitation unfortunate.

**Area-endemism patterns**

- Larger areas tend to have higher degrees of endemism. Within Australian anurans, endemism rises with area even though species diversity increases less rapidly.
- A single species confined to a tiny range can be 100 percent endemic. The Devils Hole pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) lives in a single limestone pool in southern Nevada, and fish endemism is 100 percent for areas under 1.0 x 10⁻² km².
- The Amazonian area is slightly more diverse in proportion to its area than the drylands. Anderson suggests that the western montane forest holds the greatest concentration of diversity (Pimm and Gittleman, 1992).

**Island and archipelago data**

- Mayr (1965) found that the proportion of endemic birds increases with island area in several island groups.
- Heaney (1986) estimated extinction rates of 50 percent in 10,000 years for islands of 10,000 km² and suggested that insular mammalian faunas are in "dynamic disequilibrium."
- Heaney (1986) also reported that geologically old oceanic islands have many endemics, while young islands have few.
- In the Galápagos, Johnson and Raven (1973) found that plant endemism was greater in the arid transition zone than in littoral or mesic zones.

**Specific regional figures**

- Madagascar: the 101 native terrestrial mammals show a high proportion of endemism (the scanned percentage is partly garbled). Volant mammals (bats) are only 32 percent endemic (7 of 22 species), while nonvolant terrestrial mammals are 94 percent endemic (Honacki et al., 1982). Bird endemism is 85 percent (Mayr, 1965), or 75 percent for breeding birds.
- Baffin Island (roughly Madagascar's area) has nine terrestrial mammals and no endemics. The Yukon Territory has 64 terrestrial mammal species, none endemic.
- Andean altiplano: 23 of 54 mammal species (about 42 percent) are endemic. For birds in the Páramo, Puna, and Andean Alpine biome, 48 of 318 species (12 percent) are endemic.
- Plains states (North Dakota to Oklahoma, 9.7 x 10⁵ km²): 146 freely ranging mammal species, none endemic.
- Argentina: 257 terrestrial mammal species, 11 percent endemic; 863 bird species, 14 endemic (1.6 percent).
- Europe: 362 bird species, one endemic (the finch *Serinus citrinella*).
- Palearctic: 554 terrestrial mammal species, 67 percent endemic.
- New Caledonia: 5 bat species, one endemic (20 percent). Birds are about 36 percent endemic (Mayr, 1965), with other counts between 24 and 18 species.
- Philippines: endemism ranges from about 30 percent (birds) to 70 percent (vascular plants). Amphibians are 56 percent endemic.

**Group comparisons**

- Mammals generally show higher endemism than birds in the same areas, but exceptions exist. In South America, bird endemism is greater than mammal endemism in most macrohabitats (Vuilleumier, 1988; Mares, 1992).
- At the family level, Lenglet (1977) found birds had lower endemism (29 percent in the Neotropics) than mammals (41 percent). Mammalian endemism peaked in the Australian realm at 48 percent.
- Schwartz (1969) found endemism at the subspecies level was higher among reptiles than birds on satellite islands near Hispaniola.

**Taxonomic-level results**

- Endemism declines from species to genera to families. For world mammals, Krzanowski (1986) data show that endemism is lower at higher taxonomic levels.
- Marine mammals show lower endemism than terrestrial mammals because the Old and New Worlds are not clearly separated habitats for them.

**Methodological cautions**

- Tallies must specify which records are included: breeding versus migrant birds, accidentals, introduced populations, and oceanic species. Including oceanic species would add about 66 nonendemic species to a North American total of 1719.
- Some apparent endemism values are artefacts of sample area. Random circular areas in Australia would rarely produce the extreme endemism of single-species ranges.

**Overall conclusions**

- Endemism is intimately tied to area, geological history, and the biology of the organisms.
- Anderson recommends using individual cases to generate hypotheses and to identify places, times, and groups for critical tests. He warns against unique and untestable explanations.
