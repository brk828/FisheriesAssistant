# Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans) Recovery Plan Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1988 agency review draft of the Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan, prepared by the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team for U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 6), together with a cover letter of 5 September 1988 from [[Paul C. Marsh]], Arizona State University Center for Environmental Studies, commenting on the draft. The bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) was listed as endangered on 23 April 1980. It is now extremely rare in the wild, with no confirmed self-sustaining population and no recent successful reproduction documented. The plan's immediate goal is to prevent extinction by securing broodstock in refugia and reintroducing hatchery-reared fish, with downlisting or delisting deferred until the threat of extinction is removed.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and naming.** The species was first described by Baird and Girard (1853). The name "bonytail chub" is used in the plan although "bonytail" is the accepted common name, because the longer form was used when the species was listed. Bonytail chub, [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*) are close relatives adapted to swift, turbulent mainstem flows. Their identification is difficult, especially for juveniles, and hybridization among the three species has been suggested but not resolved.

**Morphology.** Adults reach 30–35 cm total length, with specimens up to 60 cm recorded from Lakes Mohave and Havasu. Key features include an elongated, laterally compressed body, a narrow caudal peduncle (head length divided by peduncle depth greater than 5, versus less than 5 in roundtail and humpback chubs), a small terminal oblique mouth, and a smooth nuchal hump rising from a concave skull. Dorsal and anal fin ray counts are typically 10–10 in bonytail, 9–9 in roundtail and 9–10 in humpback chub. Breeding males show bright red-orange lateral slashes and tubercles.

**Life history.** Riverine adults occupy pools and eddies. Spawning in the Green River was extrapolated to late June to early July at about 15°C. Growth back-calculated from scales (Vanicek and Kramer 1969) gave 55 mm, 100 mm and 158 mm at the end of the first three growing seasons. The largest fish was 399 mm and 7 years old. Three Lake Mohave specimens were estimated at 34 to 49 years old from otoliths (Hinckley 1985). Diet changes with size: young fish eat chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs, while adults in reservoirs feed mainly on plankton and algae. In the Gila River, adult food was almost entirely gastropods and caddisfly larvae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Historically, bonytail chub were recorded throughout the mainstem Colorado River from near Grand Junction, Colorado, to the Gulf of California, and in tributaries including the Gila, Salt, Verde, San Juan and Gunnison rivers. They were reported abundant in the late 1800s, but few records exist from the early 1900s. Declines are documented from the Salt and upper Gila by 1926 and from the lower Colorado by 1950.

**Present status.** In the lower basin, a few large, old adults persist in [[Lake Mohave]] and occasionally [[Lake Havasu]]. A total of 32 adults were collected from Lake Mohave between 1974 and 1987, and anglers reported several more. Ten fish were collected from Lake Mohave in spring 1988. In the upper basin, only sporadic records exist, mostly from Dinosaur National Monument (Green and Yampa rivers), Desolation and Gray canyons, and Cataract Canyon. Recruitment is apparently nonexistent or extremely low.

**Reproduction in captivity.** Hatchery work at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] has produced substantial year classes under pond conditions. Hatching success varied strongly with temperature: 35% at 15°C, 32% at 20°C, 0.5% at 25°C, with no survival at 5, 10 or 30°C (Marsh 1985). Average fecundity in 2-year-old females was 4,000-plus eggs (range 1,015 to 10,58x, per the OCR), and egg viability averaged 67.5%.

**Threats and causes of decline.** The plan identifies four main factors:
- **Dams and altered flow/temperature:** [[Hoover Dam]] (1935), Parker Dam (1938), Davis Dam (1954), and dams on the Salt and Verde rivers converted riverine habitat to lacustrine. Cold reservoir releases reduced hatching success.
- **Water depletion:** Irrigation and mining reduced flows, and groundwater depletion and overgrazing degraded riparian vegetation in the Gila system.
- **Nonnative fishes:** Competition and predation are considered major threats. The plan notes that [[Mosquitofish]] and African cichlids coexisted with young bonytail in a California pond.
- **Hybridization:** The very small remaining population raises the risk of hybridization with more abundant Gila species. F1 hybrids have been produced in captivity.

## Key Findings

- **Extreme rarity:** The species is near extinction in the wild. The plan's immediate objective is to prevent extinction, not to meet downlisting or delisting criteria.
- **Recovery strategy (Part II):** The short-term approach involves (1) establishing and maintaining two refugia, (2) obtaining wild fish and verifying their taxonomic status, (3) protecting populations and habitats, and (4) reintroducing hatchery-reared fish into priority reaches.
- **Refugia:** The existing refugium at Dexter NFH (New Mexico) should be maintained. A second refugium is proposed for protection against catastrophic loss, with candidate sites including the Arizona Game and Fish Department's Page Springs Hatchery and Niland Native Fish Ponds, California.
- **Priority recovery reaches:** (1) Green and Yampa rivers at Dinosaur National Monument; (2) Green River at Desolation/Gray canyons; (3) Colorado River at Cataract Canyon; (4) San Juan River. Lake Mohave is to be maintained as a wild refugium.
- **Research priorities:** Spawning requirements, predation and competition, movement patterns, essential habitat, food habits, age and growth, hybridization, and taxonomic resolution remain unknown.
- **Regulatory actions:** Proposed measures include Section 7 consultation under the [[Endangered Species Act]], a cooperative agreement among seven states to prohibit introduction of nonnative fishes, and a restriction on stocking warmwater nonnative fishes.
- **Stocking history:** Augmentation releases from Dexter and Willow Beach hatcheries included about 40,000 fingerlings (1981), 13,320 fingerlings to Lake Mohave (1982), 45,000 swim-up fry into an isolated embayment (1983; later surveys recovered none), and 13,971 fish to Lake Mohave (November 1987). Success is unknown.
- **Review comments (Marsh letter):** The reviewer argued that the plan's recommendations were being discounted, that the distribution and abundance section was redundant and confusing, that the role of nonnative predators was overstated relative to the quantitative evidence, and that captive-handling protocols needed to be stricter. He also recommended removing references to a "non-essential experimental population" designation, which he considered controversial, and proposed that the plan be revised to avoid unnecessary repetition.
- **Plan scope:** The authors state that the plan is the most appropriate one that can be drafted at present, and that a more detailed recovery plan should follow once the species is more secure.
