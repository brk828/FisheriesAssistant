## Executive Summary
This study presents a multi-year (1980–1984) field observation of a dense population of [[Dollar Sunfish]] (*Lepomis marginatus*) inhabiting a manmade pond in Hoke County, North Carolina. The research, conducted by Lee and Burr, documents detailed reproductive behavior, nesting ecology, growth rates, and diet of this understudied member of the [[Centrarchidae]] family. The absence of large predators in the study pond appears to be the primary driver of the exceptionally high population density observed.

## Background & Description
- **Standard Length (SL) at hatching/early growth:** Fry reach approximately **10 mm SL** within one month of hatching.
- **Minimum Breeding Size:** ~**60 mm SL**, typically not attained until the second year of life.
- **Sexual Dimorphism/Signaling:** Ripe females entering spawning territories display pronounced, dark **vertical stripes**, a temporary coloration pattern hypothesized to function as an appeasement signal to reduce male aggression.
- **Physical Condition of Males:** Nesting males exhibited visible physical damage (presumably fin/body injuries) within a few weeks of the breeding season due to constant territorial combat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Use (Seasonal):**
  - **Winter:** Adults remain in deep water refugia.
  - **Spring/Summer (breeding season):** Adults migrate to shallow water to spawn, beginning mid-May.
  - Females and juveniles largely avoid spawning beds, remaining in deeper water during the breeding season to avoid male aggression.
- **Spawning Substrate:** Males construct nests on **hard sandy bottoms** with minimal aquatic vegetation.
- **Nesting Density:** Extremely high, at **3–5 nests/m²**, indicative of a crowded, competitive breeding environment.
- **Diet:** Primarily **immature aquatic insects** and **small crustaceans**, consistent with a generalist invertivore niche typical of small [[Lepomis]] species.
- **Predation Pressure:** The study pond is notably free of large predatory fish, which the authors suggest is the key ecological factor permitting the unusually large, dense population of *L. marginatus*.
- **Population Structure:** Up to **six age classes** were documented within the pond, suggesting a stable, self-sustaining population over the multi-year study.

## Key Findings
1. **Extended Spawning Season:** Breeding began in mid-May, peaked in late May/early June, but continued intermittently through early August — a relatively prolonged spawning window for a centrarchid.
2. **Male Reproductive Strategy & Competition:** Nesting males engaged in frequent territorial behavior, including lateral displays, aggressive advances, and physical combat (5–10 aggressive acts/min/individual). Small males were largely unsuccessful at long-term nest maintenance due to constant harassment from neighboring males, suggesting a size-dependent reproductive success hierarchy.
3. **Multiple Brood Care:** Successful (typically larger) males spawned continuously throughout the season, and by mid-summer many were simultaneously guarding a current clutch of eggs alongside two previous broods of fry — indicating asynchronous, overlapping brood care.
4. **Fecundity:** Each spawning event yielded approximately **150–200 fry**.
5. **Female Behavioral Adaptation:** The appearance of dark vertical stripes in ripe females entering nesting territories is interpreted as a behavioral/visual adaptation to mitigate male aggression during courtship and spawning.
6. **Delayed Sexual Maturity:** Despite rapid initial growth (10 mm SL within a month), most or all young did not reach the minimum breeding size of 60 mm SL until their **second year**, indicating a multi-year juvenile growth phase before reproductive maturity.
7. **Predator-Free Population Boom:** The exceptionally large population size documented in this pond is attributed largely to the absence of large predatory fish, highlighting the strong top-down regulatory role predators typically play in centrarchid population dynamics.
