# Behavior of Pupfish (Cyprinodon atrorus) in Natural, Semi-Natural, and Laboratory Settings

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodon

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study by Murray Itzkowitz and W. L. Minckley in the *Great Basin Naturalist* describes the courtship, spawning, and territorial behavior of the [[Cyprinodon Atrorus]] pupfish. The fish came from [[Cuatro Ciénegas]], Coahuila, Mexico, and were observed in a [[Semi-Natural Pond]] in [[Tempe Arizona]] and in [[Laboratory Aquaria]]. The authors catalogued a set of discrete [[Motor Patterns]], compared behavior across the three settings, and asked how far laboratory observations represent natural behavior. Their central conclusion is that aquarium behavior is more stereotyped and complete than field behavior, and that the field sequence is constrained by shallow water and by the pond's physical setting.

## Background & Description

*Cyprinodon atrorus* inhabits saline marshes and lakes on the floor of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]]. The authors note that such habitats are unstable, with rapidly changing salinity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen, so their biotic residents tend to be resistant species. Fishes have long been used in [[Ethology]] studies, but the authors observe that few studies compare a fish's behavior across natural and laboratory conditions.

Fish for the study came from a stock collected in December 1963 near Cuatro Ciénegas. Individuals were moved to an artificial pool, and the work was later done in a semi-natural pond on a patio at [[Arizona State University]], Tempe. The pond had a several-year accumulation of flocculent *Chara*-type sediment and was supplemented with feeding. The authors chose this site because the Tempe climate and seasonal cycle resemble those of the Cuatro Ciénegas area.

The observers recorded behavior with the naked eye from a point about two meters above the water and used underwater observation where possible. They described behavior using named motor patterns, following [[Barlow 1961]], and recorded quantitative observations such as pattern sequences and territory size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonality:** Breeding activity in the pond began in late January, intensified through early July, declined, and often showed a second period of activity in early autumn.
- **Habitat constraints:** The pond was shallow. All natural and pond observations occurred in water shallower than the study's depth threshold, which the authors suggest limited the use of some complex displays, especially those performed from above the substrate.
- **Territory:** In the pond, permanent territories formed by late mid-February. Three adjacent territories were studied intensively. Their depths ranged across shallow water, and their boundaries were ill-defined in early February but became fixed later. Territory sizes in nature were estimated from 23 observations, with a mean of about 1,950 cm².
- **Interspecific interactions:** Intruding [[Poeciliidae]] (live-bearers) were chased from the spawning area, and young *C. atrorus* were often ignored by defending males. Fighting was not confined to one area and was associated with feeding periods.

## Key Findings

### 1. Motor pattern repertoire
The authors describe a set of discrete motor patterns that combine into courtship and spawning sequences:
- **Contacting:** The male stays beside the female as she moves slowly over the bottom, with his head near or touching hers.
- **Tilting and nipping:** The female tilts her body downward (about 30°) and nips the substrate, taking up sediment and spitting it out. Nipping by the female signals receptivity.
- **Following:** Defined in three forms (below, above, and in line), in which the male follows the female at varying depths and positions.
- **S-shaping, wrapping, and jerking:** The male forms an S-shape with the female, wraps his anal fin around hers, and jerks his head, which marks the moment of sperm release.
- **Slow rising:** Both fish rise from the substrate after spawning, which the authors treat as the signal that spawning has ended.
- **Patrolling, chasing, lateral display, tailbeat:** Territorial and aggressive behaviors, including straight spurts of 30 to 50 cm with the median fins raised.
- **Looping:** Five variations in which the male swims a circular "figure-eight" loop in front of, below, or beside the female, or in a zigzag pattern.
- **Nuzzling:** Three forms in which the male touches the female with the anterior part of his body.

### 2. Natural and pond spawning
- In the pond, the male approached the female and the sequence proceeded to contacting, tilting and nipping, S-shaping, wrapping, and spawning. A female that was not receptive often nipped repeatedly until the male approached.
- The authors recorded 116 observed sequences in nature. The repertoire was invariable in sequence.
- In one unusual, confined observation in very shallow water (under 1.5 cm deep), a pair produced repeated nuzzling and nipping, with a spawning-like response occurring 12 or 33 times in a 31-minute period. No such hyper-repetition was seen in other field or semi-natural observations.
- Females in the pond left the male's territory quickly after spawning, which may have prevented the complex post-spawning displays seen in aquaria.

### 3. Aquarium spawning
- Pairs were kept isolated for a period, then combined in 28-liter tanks (35 × 16 × 22 cm).
- Typical aquarium behavior was contact, followed by a sequence of tilt and/or nip, S-shape, wrap, jerk, or rise. Across 668 observed spawns, the sequence of contact, tilt/nip, S-shape, wrap, jerk, or rise predominated.
- Forty-one percent of random courtship patterns led directly to a second spawn, suggesting that courtship behavior stimulates the female to continue spawning rather than initiating spawning.
- Aquarium females were more receptive and remained near the male after spawning, which differs from natural behavior.

### 4. Territoriality and social hierarchy
- Males became territorial as breeding season began, with intensity changing with reproductive activity.
- In the pond, three males held adjacent territories by mid-March. Newly arriving males were forced into smaller, compressed areas and were displaced within a week.
- Territorial defense included lateral displays, short charges, and occasional contacts with "matched" rivals of similar size and color, usually near the water's surface. Some contests lasted up to 30 seconds.
- In a 160-liter tank with seven adults, a dominance hierarchy formed within two weeks and lasted for a month, with one male defending a 10 × 38 cm area and the others subordinate. The authors note that similar hierarchies were found in *Cyprinodon macularius* and other pupfishes.

### 5. Comparison of settings and conclusions
- The authors argue that field observation is difficult, and that new artificial factors introduced by aquaria may also produce misleading results.
- Some behaviors were recorded only in aquaria (e.g., the jerk pattern, and the lack of a slow rise after spawning in the field). The authors suggest that shallow water, the difficulty of observing from above, and the escape of females from the male's territory may explain these differences.
- Males unable to hold territories in nature remained aggressive and were often limited to areas near stones or vertical banks, which suggests that territorial success depends on habitat structure.
- The authors conclude that aquarium behavior, although more complete, reflects stimulation that is absent from nature, and that natural and laboratory observations should be treated as complementary rather than interchangeable.
