# Bonytail Ecology and Recovery in the Upper Colorado River Basin: 1998 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 draft annual report from the [[Utah State University]] Ecology Center, prepared for the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], presents partial results of a study of [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) basic ecology. The overall goal is to manage genetic integrity and develop a basin-wide restoration plan for this endangered cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The report contains four chapters: juvenile bonytail interactions with [[Red Shiner]] (substrate and velocity selection); the role of introduced predators in the [[Green River]] using a [[Predation Risk Model]]; a laboratory temperature-versus-growth experiment; and a study of [[Flow Training]] for successful stocking. Key conclusions are that juvenile bonytail strongly prefer cobble substrate and deep, low-velocity positions, and that introduced predators pose extreme predation risk to young fish under 100 mm.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Basin]] has a highly endemic fish fauna, reportedly the highest percentage of endemic fish in North America (Hickman 1983). Historic native fishes include [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], roundtail chub, bluehead sucker, and flannelmouth sucker. Impoundments under the Colorado River Storage Act, water development, and the introduction of nonnative sport fish, including a rotenone poisoning of the upper Green River, fragmented habitat, altered flow and temperature regimes, and increased competition and predation from nonnatives.

Bonytail are the rarest endemic Colorado River fish and were listed as endangered on 23 April 1990. The USFWS ranked their recovery priority as 5C (high priority). No recruitment into remaining upper-basin wild populations has been documented; recruitment has been recorded only in [[Lake Mohave]] (Lower Basin), a lentic habitat, so long-term population stability is uncertain. Early records are unreliable because bonytail were confused with humpback and roundtail chubs.

Biology summarized from the USFWS recovery plan:
- Elongated body, small head, oblique mouth, and a predorsal hump; 9-11 (typically 10) dorsal and anal fin rays.
- Adults commonly reach 300-350 mm; specimens up to 600 mm have been taken from Lakes Mohave and Havasu. Ages up to 49 years were recorded in the Lower Basin; Upper Basin specimens were seven years old.
- Adults prefer eddies and pools beside deep, swift, semi-rocky main-channel areas; juveniles use backwaters and sheltered shorelines.
- Spawning occurs in mid-June to early July, at about 20 °C, over gravel bars. Females produce 1,000-17,000 eggs; larval survival was highest (32%) at 15 and 20 °C (Marsh 1985). Juvenile survival is 17-38%.
- Diet: adults eat terrestrial insects, plant debris, and filamentous algae; juveniles eat chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs.

The 1995 [[Bonytail Reintroduction Plan]] (Lentsch et al.) was partly based on this work and addresses stocking, facilities, and essential studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Competition and habitat use.** Adult bonytail were previously reported in eddies near swift currents over silt and boulder substrates, and in mixing zones between eddies and runs (Chart and Cranney 1993).

**Predation.** Direct predation on young-of-the-year (YOY) and juvenile fish is considered potentially the most severe factor limiting recruitment. Cage experiments suggested that [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Green Sunfish]] consume pikeminnow. Nonnative fish dominate the food web in the Upper Colorado River Basin by density and biomass.

**Hypotheses on decline.** The report cites flow regulation by dams, blocked migration and spawning habitat, water withdrawals, reduced summer temperatures, and introduced-fish impacts. Minckley and Meffe (1987) found nonnatives dominated at low discharges, while natives dominated in number during high flows.

**Nursery habitat.** The 60-river-mile Green River reach (RM 200-305) is low-gradient (<0.003 m/m) and is one of only three remaining pikeminnow nursery habitats in the basin. It is downstream of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and the Yampa River confluence.

**Feeding.** Juvenile Gila feed mainly on chironomids and mayfly nymphs; ontogenetic shifts in pikeminnow run from planktivory to piscivory over the summer.

## Key Findings

**Chapter 1: Bonytail-Red Shiner Interactions (substrate and velocity)**
- One-year-old bonytail in substrate trials selected cobble 100% of the time, with no variance; sand, gravel and sand/gravel were avoided.
- Bonytail were near the bottom 93% of the time during the day and 84% at night in non-cobble substrate trials; they were never observed at the surface.
- In low-velocity flow trials (mean available 0.0336 m/s), bonytail selected lower velocities (mean 0.0177 m/s; T=2.36, p=0.0249, df=31). Medium and high velocity trials showed no significant velocity preference.
- Depth selection changed with velocity: deep depths were used 71% (low), 82% (medium), and 85% (high) of the time.
- Red shiners did not displace bonytail from cobble or deep positions. Bonytail showed schooling-like following of red shiners and moved into faster water near pumps when shiners were present.
- Recommendations: raise juveniles with boulder substrate and flow, and with nonnative fish such as red shiners where possible.

**Chapter 2: Introduced Fishes in the Green River (predation risk model)**
- Step 1 regression (prey height on total length): prey ht = 5.095 + 0.253 (TL), r² = 0.8181.
- Step 2 regression (predator gape on total length): pred gape = -1.292 + 0.073 (TL), r² = 0.7899.
- Model #3, the best-supported model, estimated that a 25 mm fish faces predation from eight of nine predator size classes. Two classes may exert nearly 100% risk.
- Figure 2.3 suggests bonytail are largely beyond predation risk at about 150 mm TL, which is roughly 2-3 years of age under average conditions, or one year under captive optimal rearing.
- YOY pikeminnow average 25 mm on arrival in June, reach about 60 mm at 12 months and 125 mm at 16 months, and experience YOY mortality of 75-90% within 12 months.
- Density of introduced predators was highest for red shiners, but green sunfish and channel catfish are considered more influential predators.
- The authors conclude that introduced predation and competition are likely accelerating the decline of native fish recruitment in the Green River.

**Chapter 3: Temperature vs. Growth Experiment**
- 1996 trial (four temperatures: 12, 17, 22, 27 °C; two size groups) showed the best growth at 22 °C. All fish were lost to *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* infection on 6 December 1996.
- 1997 trial (12, 17, 22, 25 °C, because fish died at 27 °C during acclimation) showed similar growth across treatments over about five months.
- The authors note that hatchery pond rearing could raise survival above the 5% age-0 estimate for razorback sucker.

**Chapter 4: Flow Training for Stocking**
- Prior work on age-1 bonytail raised in 0.03-0.06 m/s flow showed changes in mitochondrial volume, muscle fibre diameter, and fibre number.
- The experiment compared three flow regimes (no flow, 12 h on/12 h off, constant flow) across six tanks with two size groups, running July 1996 to December 1997.
- Growth was measured on 19 July and 19 September 1996; fat content was analyzed from one fish per tank in January 1997.
- Results are reported as tables and figures; numeric outcomes are not given in the text provided.

**Overall recommendations**
- Reintroduction should include flow and boulder substrate in rearing, and exposure to nonnative species.
- Predator management should focus on nursery habitats; captive rearing until bonytail exceed about 150 mm TL reduces predation risk.
