# Geography of Western North American Freshwater Fishes: Description and Relationships to Intracontinental Tectonism

#ichthyology #marine-biology #zoogeography

## Executive Summary

This chapter by [[W. L. Minckley]], [[Dean A. Hendrickson]] and [[Carl E. Bond]] reviews the zoogeography of freshwater fishes across western North America, from the [[Continental Divide]] to the Pacific and from the [[Columbia River Basin]] south to the Rio Grande de Santiago Basin in Mexico. The authors argue that the modern fauna's distribution is largely the product of [[Cenozoic Tectonism]]: plate-boundary interactions, [[Basin and Range Extension]], [[San Andreas Transform]] displacements and [[Paleohydrography|shifting drainage patterns]], overlaid by aridification and Pleistocene lake cycles. The fauna is characterized by endemic subfaunas in major basins and sparse, unique taxa in minor systems. The authors propose that many present disjunctions reflect vicariance and microplate transport rather than dispersal alone, and they stress that the fauna is threatened by recent human activity.

## Background & Description

The study area is bounded east by the Continental Divide, west by the Pacific Ocean, north by the Columbia River system and south by a faunal break north of the Rio Grande de Santiago Basin. Most of the region lies within the [[Basin and Range Province]], much of it endorheic (internally draining) for much of the Cenozoic.

The authors define dispersal narrowly as movement between or within drainages via direct surface hydrographic connections. They assume allopatric speciation has prevailed in western fishes, while acknowledging sympatric or parapatric divergence may occur. They apply the principle of minimum age of origin, treating fossil dates as minimum ages of occupation. Taxonomy generally follows [[Robins et al. 1980]], with noted exceptions: retention of the catostomid genus Pantosteus, specific status for Gila intermedia, and placement of Crenichthys and Empetrichthys in the family Goodeidae.

The fish record shows that Cretaceous ichthyofaunas were archaic. Teleost-dominated freshwater faunas appear in the Late Paleocene and Eocene (about 55 to 37 mya). The modern fauna appeared during the Oligocene and Miocene (23 to 5 mya), while many archaic groups, including hiodontids, umbrids, esocids, ictalurids and centrarchids, declined to or near extinction by the Pliocene (5 to 1.8 mya).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic framework.** Western North America west of the Basin and Range is a heterogeneous array of allochthonous terranes accreted from the Paleozoic to the Late Miocene. The Laramide Orogeny (Late Cretaceous to Eocene) uplifted broad areas, followed by an Late Eocene to Early Oligocene erosional surface. Collision of the East Pacific Rise with the continent about 29 mya created the right-lateral San Andreas Transform. Extension of the Basin and Range began around 27 mya, with block faulting intensifying from 13 to 10 mya.

**Subareas.** The Oregon Coast Ranges, Klamath Mountains and Cascades behaved as a coherent subplate that rotated and was displaced up to 340 km since about 20 mya. The Sierra Nevada uplifted from about 18 mya, mostly accomplished since 5 mya. The Great Valley is an ancient forearc basin. The Salinian Block and Transverse Ranges moved northward along the transform. The Gulf of California opened, and the Baja California Peninsula separated from the continent after 5 mya, with 300 to 400 km of displacement at the gulf entrance.

**Paleohydrography.** Drainage was originally largely east and north from the western cordillera. Eocene lake basins existed across parts of the Great Basin, Arizona and Wyoming, but most disappeared by the Eocene or Oligocene. The ancestral Colorado River integrated with the Gulf of California around 10.6 to 3.3 mya, and the Gila River became through-flowing between 10.5 and 6.0 mya. Lake Lahontan, Lake Bonneville and Death Valley's Lake Manly represented large Pleistocene habitats, with overflow connections repeatedly altering faunal exchange.

**Paleoclimatology.** Cenozoic trends moved from subtropical and mesophytic floras in the Paleocene and Eocene toward cooler, drier conditions from the Oligocene onward. Glaciation in the Pleistocene produced wetter episodes, but aridification was the dominant long-term trend.

**Modern human impacts.** The authors note that extinction rates are rapidly increasing and that a substantial share of the western ichthyofauna is in imminent danger of disappearing. Dams, diversions, introductions and hybridization with introduced trout are repeatedly cited as causes.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna composition.** [[Table 15.1]] lists freshwater fishes recorded from the study region, organized by taxon and drainage. [[Table 15.2]] lists fishes of marine derivation recorded from continental waters of Oregon, California, Baja California and northwestern mainland Mexico, with more than 90 species recorded from peninsular Baja California alone and at least 48 from the Sacramento-San Joaquin estuary region. Entries are too numerous to reproduce here.
- **Oregon coastal streams.** The coastal fauna consists largely of Holarctic families. Native marine-related freshwater species include three petromyzontids, two acipenserids, five common and two uncommon salmonids, two osmerids, one gasterosteid and five cottids. Several fishes, including [[Catostomus macrocheilus]] and [[Cottus rhotheus]], reached coastal drainages probably via headwater transfers or stream capture from the Columbia Basin.
- **Klamath River Basin.** This basin supports unique fishes, mainly above Klamath Falls, including [[Lampetra minima]] (possibly extinct), [[Chasmistes brevirostris]], [[Catostomus snyderi]] and [[Gila coerulea]]. Its relationships are with the Snake River Plain, Great Basin and Pit River faunas.
- **Great Basin and Oregon Lakes.** Harney, Alvord, Warner, Summer Lake and Alkali basins each show distinct fish assemblages reflecting local lake connections and stream captures. [[Gila bicolor]] forms are distributed across many basins, and two extinction-resistant species, Gila bicolor and Rhinichthys osculus, are widely shared.
- **Lahontan system.** The modern fauna includes an amalgamation of recent and relict elements. Endemics include [[Eremichthys acros]], [[Richardsonius egregius]], [[Catostomus tahoensis]] and [[Chasmistes cujus]]. Fossil evidence (Pliocene Honey Lake beds) shows older links with the Snake River Plain.
- **Death Valley system.** Fishes of disrupted drainages are unique, including four families with cyprinodontoids (Cyprinodontidae and Goodeidae) represented by two genera, six species and 13 subspecies. The fauna is interpreted as an ancient lowland assemblage later fragmented by faulting and desiccation.
- **Bonneville Basin.** With 20 species, this is the largest interior fish fauna in western North America. Twelve of these species are shared with the upper Snake River. Endemics include [[Prosopium gemmiferum]] (known only from Bear Lake), [[Cottus extensus]] and [[Cottus echinatus]].
- **Sacramento-San Joaquin system.** This is the most species-rich western fauna, largely due to marine-derived and anadromous forms. Relict taxa, including [[Orthodon microlepidotus]], [[Mylopharodon conocephalus]] and [[Archoplites interruptus]], share Miocene and Pliocene congeners in the Snake River Plain.
- **Colorado River Basin.** Fishes are grouped into big-river fishes (large, modified skins, large fins), endemic small-stream forms and intermediate-elevation components. [[Ptychocheilus lucius]] is the largest North American cyprinid, approaching 2.0 m total length. Plagopterine cyprinids (Lepidomeda, Meda, Plagopterus) form a distinctive tribe.
- **Mexican drainages.** The Mesa del Norte and Sierra Madre Occidental contributed fishes to western Mexican rivers through stream captures and drainage integration. Poeciliopsis includes several unisexual (all-female) hybrid strains; the authors note these arose from interspecific hybridization, with [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] and [[Poeciliopsis monacha]] implicated in the Rio Mayo.
- **Fossil record.** Most knowledge of Early Cenozoic freshwater fishes derives from the Eocene [[Green River Formation]]. Miocene to Pleistocene faunas show fishes now restricted to eastern North America, such as Hiodon, Esox and Umbra relatives, indicating a broader former distribution.
- **Tectonism and distribution.** Major faunal areas correspond with aseismic subplates, and seismic boundaries often form faunal barriers. Systems crossing seismic zones, such as the Columbia and Colorado rivers, have composite faunas. Vicariance through stream capture, drainage reversal and faulting is repeatedly invoked.
- **Fish hook pattern.** A zoogeographic track linking the Snake River Plain westward through the Sacramento and Klamath rivers, and southward on the east side of the Sierra Nevada to the Mohave Desert, is supported by fossil and extant distributions. Its timing is estimated via area cladograms against geologic events (nodes 1 to 25, [[Figure 15.4]]).
- **Microplate transport.** The authors propose that some southern California fishes, including [[Gila orcutti]] and [[Pantosteus santaanae]], were transported northward on displaced crustal blocks along the San Andreas system, and that goodeids and cyprinodontoids may reflect older displacements from central Mexico.
- **Conclusion on drought and persistence.** Western fishes persisted through repeated drought and tectonism by generalist traits, refugia and hybridization. Specialists dependent on constant, scarce habitats are most vulnerable. The authors conclude the fauna is an assemblage sorted by tectonic and climatic cycles and now heavily threatened by human activity.
