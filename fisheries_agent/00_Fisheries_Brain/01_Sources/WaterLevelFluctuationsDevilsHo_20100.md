# Water Level Fluctuations at Devils Hole, Nevada

#hydrogeology #speleology #carbonate-geology #devils-hole

## Executive Summary

This 1972 field report, prepared for the [[Desert Research Institute]] by [[Peter J. Mehringer Jr.]] and [[C. Vance Haynes Jr.]], examines [[Devils Hole]] (Nevada) for evidence of past water-level fluctuations, with particular interest in whether levels were ever lower than the present stand. The authors used surface observation, leveling and [[SCUBA Diving]] survey to a depth of 30 feet below the water surface. They documented four types of [[Carbonate Deposits]] and numerous [[Tufa]] strand lines and solution notches recording former higher stands, including one at about 9.5 feet above the local bench mark. They found no unequivocal evidence of a lower-than-present stand; only a few weak features within two feet below the water surface were possibly related to lower levels.

## Background & Description

Devils Hole is a structurally controlled grotto occupying a northeasterly striking fault fissure dipping 70° E and transecting the Bonanza King Formation, a dark gray to black Cambrian limestone. The roof, partly collapsed in the past, is a keystone-like limestone wedge. The bedding strikes N40W and dips 40 to 45 degrees southwest, which the authors note makes water-level features unlikely to be confused with bedding or jointing.

Elevations were referenced to a [[USGS Benchmark]] about 1000 feet east of the hole (stamped 2377 feet) and to a brass washer benchmark inside Devils Hole, established by [[Richard Naff]] at 2357.2 ± 0.1 feet. Fieldwork took place on June 27 and 28, 1972, with a team including representatives of the [[Atomic Energy Commission]], the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and [[Death Valley National Monument]] staff. Measurements below water level were made when the pool stood about 3.6 feet below the brass washer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report is primarily geological and hydrological. It describes the hole as a "natural standpipe" in which former water levels can be read from strand-line tufas and solution notches. Carbonate chemistry reflects the interplay of dissolution and precipitation: rainwater picks up CO₂ from soil air, dissolving limestone, and carbonate is redeposited where water evaporates, either in soil calcic horizons or at the air-water interface. Present-day precipitation is described as inadequate to carry dissolved carbonates to the water table. The report does not provide biological, fish, or invertebrate data.

## Key Findings

- **Four carbonate types identified:**
  - *Type A:* coarse, white, translucent calcite of subaqueous origin, still forming at depth; occurs as 2 mm to 1 cm coatings cementing fallen rock 30 feet below the surface.
  - *Type B:* pale brown, laminated carbonate typical of pedogenic (soil) formation, now being dissolved at the surface.
  - *Type C:* rippled flowstone on the southeast wall, ending at a former ground-water level about 19 feet above the brass washer; indicates a climate with enough precipitation to carry vadose water to the water table, which the authors state has not recurred.
  - *Type D:* white to pale brown, porous [[Tufa]] strand deposits at the air-water interface marking former water levels, forming since roof collapse.
- **Former higher stands:** At least eleven separate tufa rims were measured above the pool, the highest about 9.5 feet above the local USGS benchmark. Solution benches at about 9.2 and 21.5 feet are interpreted as former still stands.
- **Spring tufas** about ¼ mile southwest of the hole occur in desert pavement at 2372 feet and continuous exposures at 2369 feet (about 12 feet above the brass washer), independently confirming higher past water levels.
- **Possible former overflow:** Carbonate similar to Type A lies about five feet below the present overflow elevation, beneath fan gravels, suggesting past surface discharge. No firm evidence was seen above the 21-foot ledge.
- **Lower-than-present levels:** No single feature unequivocally indicated a lower stand across the 30 feet surveyed. Possible candidates lay within two feet below the water surface (−3.6 feet reference):
  1. A thin Type D deposit extending to 18 inches depth in the north corner, the only such deposit observed below water.
  2. A nearly horizontal solution groove about seven feet long at 10.5 to 16 inches depth on the northwest wall.
  3. A shelf about 23 inches below the surface, with a smaller ledge at about 14 inches.
  4. Lighter-colored wall rock for 5 to 6 feet along the southeast wall to a depth of 9 inches, possibly from a lower stand, biochemical activity, or another cause.
- **Age and preservation:** Carbonate-cemented alluvial gravel 5 feet below the lip contains large vertebrate bone fragments; if diagnostic, these could date the roof collapse. Submersion by a rising water level would likely dissolve any tufa, so brief low stands may leave little record; the 18-inch Type D relict is the best candidate.
- **Conclusion:** Strong evidence of past fluctuations and possible overflow exists, but none confirms a lower-than-present water level.
