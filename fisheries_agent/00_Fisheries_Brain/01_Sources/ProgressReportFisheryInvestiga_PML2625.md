# Fishery Survey of the Black and Salt Rivers, Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife progress report summarizes a preliminary fishery survey of the [[Black River]] and [[Salt River]] on the [[Ft. Apache Reservation]] and [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]] in Arizona. The survey ran from June 1963 to December 1965 (one passage gives June 1953, which appears to be a typo). It documents a fair-to-good [[Rainbow Trout]] population in the upper 16 miles of the Black River, a good [[Channel Catfish]] population in the lower Black and parts of the Salt River, and a significant gap in game fish between these reaches. To fill that gap, [[Smallmouth Bass]] were stocked in 1964 and 1965. The report concludes that the upper Black River has high recreational potential, which depends on road and campground development and on agreement between the two tribal councils.

## Background & Description

The Black and Salt Rivers flow through some of the most spectacular canyons in the Southwest. The Salt River canyon crossing at [[Highway 60]] is often called the "Grand Canyon of Arizona." Canyon walls rise vertically in places, and the canyon depth ranges from about 300 to roughly 2,000 feet on the reservations. Vegetation changes with elevation, from spruce and ponderosa pine on the rims to juniper, yucca, and desert scrub in the lower canyons.

The Black River begins in the White Mountains at about 10,500 feet, and the Salt River forms at the confluence of the Black and Salt Rivers, which together form the common boundary between the two reservations. Access is difficult. The survey used known access points, including Ten of Diamonds Ranch, Old Military Crossing, and the Black River pumping station, and relied on four-wheel-drive vehicles, trolleys, and hiking.

The fish community was sampled at every known access point using seining, gill netting, electrofishing, hook-and-line fishing, and rotenone. Species recorded include:

- Bonytail (*Gila robusta*)
- Flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus* sp.)
- Gila sucker (*Pantosteus clarkii*)
- Rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus* sp.)
- Smallmouth bass (*Micropterus dolomieu*, introduced)
- Green sunfish (*Lepomis* sp.)
- Black bullhead (*Ictalurus* or related genus)
- Channel catfish (*Ictalurus* sp.)

Genus and species names are partly illegible in the scanned report, so the identifications above are the most likely readings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and water chemistry.** Flows are lowest in the winter and in June and July, and highest in spring from snowmelt (March through April). The average discharge of the Black River was 153 cfs over the period of record ending in September 1964. Monthly mean flows ranged from 36.6 cfs in January to a high in the 400 cfs range. The maximum daily flow was 960 cfs. Creek water is clear at low flow. During high flow, the Black River near the pumping station becomes moderately turbid, and the Salt River becomes very turbid.

**Water temperature.** Temperatures were recorded at the pumping station from May 21 to September. Daily temperatures fluctuated by as much as 15°F. Maxima occurred around 5:00 p.m. and minima around 8:00 a.m. Temperatures peaked in the first week of July and then declined. Temperatures rose gradually downstream and fell gradually upstream of the pumping station. Excessive summer temperatures downstream are the reason the trout population is limited to the upper 16 miles.

**Water diversion.** Water is diverted at the Black River pumping station for mining, ore processing, and domestic supply near Morenci, Arizona. Remaining flow is impounded on the Salt River below the reservations by the [[Salt River Project]]. Diversion rose from about 1,500 acre-feet per year (1963) to about 5,000 acre-feet per year in the years reported. The report warns that excessive pumping under low-flow conditions could endanger downstream fish populations.

**Habitat and vegetation.** Bottomlands on the upper Black River are dominated by ponderosa pine. Downstream, pine gives way to scrub oak, juniper, and oak, and the lower Salt River is desert scrub.

## Key Findings

**Fish distribution**
- Rainbow trout: fair to good population in the upper 16 miles of the Black River. Trout are distributed widely in winter.
- Bonytail, Gila sucker, flannelmouth sucker, and green sunfish: distributed throughout the river system. Suckers and bonytail were the most numerous fishes.
- Black bullhead: found only in the Salt River.
- Channel catfish: good population in the lower Black River and parts of the Salt River.
- The Black River section between the trout and catfish zones has very few game fish, which was identified as the main management gap.

**Smallmouth bass stocking program**
- Smallmouth bass were chosen as the best species to fill the gap and were recommended for stocking over three consecutive years, followed by a period of evaluation.
- First plant: August 27, 1964, with fish (about four inches long) released at three locations.
- Second plant: July 16, 1965, with fingerlings released at two locations.
- Evidence so far suggests the plants were at least partly successful. Two smallmouth bass (7 and 6 inches) were caught by hook and line near the double crossing area. Three were seen at the Military Crossing in October 1965, and one was caught above the [[Highway 60 Bridge]]. The bass appear to have spread over a long section of both rivers.

**Recreational potential**
- Angling pressure on the reservations is very low, which contrasts with heavy use on other Arizona streams. Inaccessibility is the main reason.
- The upper Black River has high recreational potential and should be a development priority.
- With road and campground development, the section above the pumping station could support about 15,000 angler-days per year. Trout anglers in Arizona spend $13.68 per day, which would benefit the tribal economies.

**Access and infrastructure recommendations**
- A new highway under construction across the San Carlos Reservation (from Highway 70 to Barlow Pass, then to Highway 666) is the main access option.
- Two route options were proposed from Barlow Pass. The first, an easterly route across Eagle Creek, would not open the Black River to fishing. The second, a northerly route past Point of Pines to Maverick, would cross the Black River near Paddy Creek and open access to the canyon bottom. The report favors the second route and recommends an all-weather road from Maverick to Big Lake in the Apache National Forest.

**Management and governance**
- The two tribal councils must agree on common recreation rules and regulations for the Black River.
- They also need an orderly plan for road and campground development on the river bottom.

**Survey conclusion**
- The report is a preliminary progress report, approved by the Division of Fishery Services supervisor and written by Fishery Management Biologist Richard J. Navarre. Water temperature data were provided by Mr. Randall Lunt at the pumping station, and field assistance came from Mr. John Anderson.
