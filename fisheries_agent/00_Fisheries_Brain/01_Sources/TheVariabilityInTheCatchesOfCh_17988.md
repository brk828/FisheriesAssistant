# Variability in Multi-Mesh Gill Net Catches of Charr and Perch in Windermere

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[J. F. Craig]] and [[J. M. Fletcher]] of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] examines how catches of [[Arctic Charr]] and [[European Perch]] in [[Multi-Mesh Gill Nets]] vary with setting position, month and mesh size. Nets were fished monthly at [[Rawlinson's Nab]] in the south basin of [[Windermere]] from July 1979 to June 1981. The authors used [[Variance Analysis]] on log-transformed catches and calculated [[Confidence Limits]] for the [[Geometric Mean]] catch at different numbers of net settings. Their conclusion is that multi-mesh gill nets are of little value for detecting changes in stock abundance, although they remain useful for other population statistics.

## Background & Description

The work was motivated by the need to monitor the [[European Perch]] population in Windermere after an [[Perch Disease Outbreak]] first recorded in 1976. The authors place their study within earlier gill net reviews by [[Hamley]] (1975, 1980) and variance studies on [[Northern Pike]] and [[Perch Traps]] by [[Bagenal]] (1972a, b). A companion paper by Craig & Fletcher (1981) addressed the use of multi-mesh gill nets in freshwater fish surveys.

**Gear specification**

- Nets were supplied by Lundgrens of Sweden, weighted to sink, and made of multifilament nylon.
- Each net was 60 m long and 1.5 m deep, with twelve 5 m panels.
- Bar mesh sizes were 8, 10, 12.5, 16, 19, 22, 25, 30, 33, 38, 45 and 55 mm.
- The hanging coefficient was 60/120, minus 10% on the floatline and plus 10% on the bottom line.

**Sampling design**

- Three nets were set simultaneously for 24 h each month. Net A ran parallel to the shore in about 4 m of water. Nets B and C ran perpendicular to the shore, with mesh sequences reversed between them. Net B was not set in July 1979.
- December 1979 was not sampled because of rough weather.
- On lifting, species, net and mesh were recorded. Fish were measured, weighed, sexed and staged for maturity, and scales and bones were taken for ageing.
- Because variance exceeded the mean, catches were transformed by y = log(x + 1) before analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Only [[Arctic Charr]] and [[European Perch]] were caught in sufficient numbers for statistical analysis. The authors reported that:

- Charr were caught mainly from November to February and were predominantly mature. The high lower size limit (about 15 cm) was attributed to spatial behaviour differing among size groups rather than net selectivity.
- Perch were sampled from about 5 cm upward. They were never caught in the 45 or 55 mm meshes, so analysis used ten mesh sizes.
- Variance in perch catches rose from winter through spring and autumn to summer. This coincided with larger catches, which the authors suggested may be linked to rising [[Water Temperature]] (citing Craig, 1977).
- Catch variance for perch in winter nets (0.0633) was close to that reported for pike in single-mesh winter nets (0.065), so the authors judged that solitary or shoaling behaviour could not clearly explain the differences.

## Key Findings

**Setting position and mesh effects**

- Nets set perpendicular to the shore (B and C) consistently caught more fish than the parallel net A.
- No significant difference was found between nets B and C.
- Mesh size effects were highly significant for both species, as expected for a heterogeneous population.
- For charr, a second-order month × net × mesh interaction was highly significant, so the analysis was split by month.

**Charr (November–February, nets B and C)**

- The total variance per single net was 0.2281.
- From 14 net settings, the arithmetic mean catch was 11.1 and the geometric mean was 9.4, with 95% confidence limits of 4.5 to 18.8.
- Twelve nets would be needed for the limits to fall within half or twice the geometric mean.

**Perch: winter (December–April)**

- Total variance per single net was 0.0633.
- From 18 net settings, the arithmetic mean catch was 1.1 and the geometric mean was 0.8 (95% CL 0.4–1.3).
- Six nets would be needed for the stated precision.

**Perch: summer (July–October)**

- Total variance per single net was 0.9121.
- From 15 net settings, the arithmetic mean catch was 60.7 and the geometric mean was 43.3 (95% CL 12.0–149.7).
- Forty nets would be needed.

**Perch: spring and autumn (May, June and November)**

- Total variance per single net was 0.4176.
- From 12 net settings, the arithmetic mean catch was 39.1 and the geometric mean was 23.4 (95% CL 8.4–62.4).
- Twenty nets would be needed.

**Comparison with other gear**

- The variance of perch catches in gill nets during spring and autumn (0.42) was higher than the variance of spring perch trap catches (0.25) reported by Bagenal (1972b).

**Conclusions**

- Confidence limits for charr in winter, and for perch in each season, overlapped between years, so the data could not reliably show changes in abundance.
- Only one area was sampled. The authors noted that comparing areas would probably add further variance.
- The authors concluded that multi-mesh gill nets are not a good sampling gear for comparing stock abundance, but they remain useful for other population statistics.
