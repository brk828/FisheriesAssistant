# Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Yampa and Green River Drainages

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This partial literature review, prepared by the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] in 1977 and updated by an addendum in December 1978, covers the status, natural history, and life history of four fish species endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. All four are listed as endangered or threatened by the U.S. Department of the Interior and the State of Colorado.

Dams, water diversions, and other human activities are identified as the main causes of population decline, and in some reaches the outright elimination of these fishes. The authors conclude that the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] are among the last natural strongholds for these species, and that little additional alteration may be enough to push them toward extinction.

## Background & Description

The review was undertaken because these species had received little fisheries attention while they were not recognized as game fish. Early regional publications typically noted only that the fishes occurred in the area and that little was known of their life or natural history. Interest grew after the fishes were listed, and key studies include work on the Green River after the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Vanicek and Kramer, 1969; Vanicek, Kramer and Franklin, 1970), a survey of the upper basin from 1968 to 1971 (Holden, 1973), and a broader survey of the upper and middle basins from 1967 to 1973 (Holden and Stalnaker, 1975).

**Taxonomy and morphology**

- The squawfish, bonytail, and humpback chub are minnows of the family [[Cyprinidae]]. The razorback sucker is a member of the family [[Catostomidae]] and the only representative of its genus.
- The three chubs of the genus *Gila* (roundtail, bonytail, humpback) are very similar as young fish and hard to separate. Holden and Stalnaker (1970) found good separation between *G. elegans* and *G. robusta*, but some confusion between *G. elegans* and *G. cypha*. A computer comparison by Robert Miller (1976) showed good separation between the latter two.
- The razorback sucker hybridizes with *Catostomus latipinnis* (flannelmouth sucker). Holden found 40 hybrids among the 53 razorbacks he collected, a sharp increase over the historical rate as razorbacks became rarer.

**Size and growth**

- Colorado squawfish is the largest minnow in North America. Large lower-basin specimens reportedly reach 5 feet (153 cm) and 80 pounds (36 kg). One specimen from the Gunnison River measured 35 inches (91 cm) and weighed 19.75 pounds (9.95 kg). Squawfish maximum size and growth declined after the closure of Flaming Gorge Dam.
- Bonytail chub may reach 18 inches (46 cm). Wyoming specimens typically measured 8 to 10 inches (20 to 25 cm).
- Humpback chub growth is poorly known because of its scarcity. Specimens from the Colorado River measured 11 to 15 inches (30 to 38 cm).
- Razorback sucker weights of up to 8 to 10 pounds (3.6 to 4.5 kg) and lengths up to 30 inches (76 cm) were confirmed. Larger fish of up to 14 pounds (6 kg) were reported from Salt River impoundments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food habits**

- Young squawfish eat insects, larvae, and nymphs, along with cladocerans and copepods. Fish become the main food of squawfish over about 8 to 15 inches (200 to 390 mm).
- Bonytail over 8 inches (200 mm) mainly ate terrestrial insects, with some plant debris and algae. Before impoundment, bonytails in the Flaming Gorge area were omnivorous.
- Humpback chub feed on hatching mayflies and planktonic crustaceans and algae.
- Razorback sucker is a benthic feeder that eats detritus, algae, mayflies, caddisflies, diptera, and beetles.

**Habitat requirements**

- Colorado squawfish is associated with silty canyon waters. It was most abundant in slower water, including eddies, backwaters, and flooded canyon mouths.
- Bonytail occurs in torrential canyon waters over varied substrates. In the Flaming Gorge basin it was restricted to the main river channel and absent from colder tributaries. Young bonytails use shallow, quiet water along the main channel margins.
- Humpback chub were mostly collected in eddies next to fast water, though a specimen-based report found the species in deep, slow currents of the Colorado River. Experts describe it as specialized for swift water.
- Razorback sucker prefers quieter, shallow to medium-depth water over sand, mud, or rock. It concentrates behind obstructions, deep holes, cut banks, or fallen trees. During spawning it moves into faster currents.

**Economic value**

Squawfish, bonytail, chubs, and razorback suckers were so abundant in Arizona until about 1911 that they were removed from irrigation ditches with pitchforks for fertilizer. Squawfish were locally known as "white salmon" and were eaten, and some anglers treat them as game fish. Razorback suckers were formerly an important food fish, easily taken in seines.

**Threats and human impacts**

- Dams, water diversions, and impoundments have altered habitat and driven declines. Dams and related alterations are linked to the loss of native reproduction.
- The 1962 fish eradication program on the Green River, followed by the closure of Flaming Gorge Dam, led to the probable extirpation of squawfish, bonytail, and razorback sucker in Wyoming.
- The proposed [[Lake Gatamount]] reservoir (500 acres, 203 ha) on the Yampa River was judged likely to have a substantial impact on the species. The authors note that 30 or more impoundments were proposed for the Yampa drainage, and their cumulative effects could extirpate all four species.
- Other threats include lowering water tables, eutrophication and pollution, and the introduction of exotic species (Minckley and Deacon, 1968).
- Portions of the Yampa and Green Rivers were recommended as critical habitat under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Key Findings

**Status and abundance**

- **Colorado Squawfish:** Vanicek et al. reported 1,477 squawfish in the Green River below Echo Park. Holden and Stalnaker collected 300 adults, and 261 (about 87%) came from lower Yampa Canyon in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Juveniles were abundant near [[Echo Park]] in 1968, declined sharply in 1969, and were not found in 1970 or 1971. Holden and Stalnaker concluded that populations had declined greatly within 30 to 40 years.
- **Bonytail Chub:** Holden found it in only 4 of 12 river segments, and collected 36 specimens, rating the species very rare. Vanicek and Kramer reported good reproduction in 1959, 1960, and 1961, but after the dam closure Holden and Stalnaker found only three bonytails in the same section. A live bonytail taken in 1975 was moved to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], but no mate could be found.
- **Humpback Chub:** Vanicek et al. found only three specimens. Holden collected 36 (25 from near Echo Park in 1969), and later work raised the total to 61. An inventory of the Yampa River in 1974 found none.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Jordan (1891) described it as abundant in 1889. By 1953 it was uncommon. Vanicek et al. located 73 razorbacks, all downstream of Echo Park. Holden collected 53 specimens and rated the species rare. Post-impoundment work found only large adults and no reproduction. An inventory in 1974 found none in the Yampa River.

**Spawning and reproduction**

- Spawning in all four species appears triggered by rising water temperatures. Holden determined that squawfish spawn at a minimum of 70°F (21°C).
- Vanicek and Kramer found ripe squawfish about one month after water reached 65°F (18°C), between June 29 and August 4. Squawfish spawn in shallow water and their adhesive eggs were laid on gravel.
- Bonytails did not spawn until water reached 65°F (18°C), usually from late June to early July in Dinosaur National Monument. No spawning was found upstream of the confluence with the Yampa River.
- Chubs of the genus *Gila* spawn in late spring at higher elevations. Several males attend one female, and adhesive eggs are broadcast near shore.
- Razorback sucker spawning temperatures range from 58 to 68°F (14 to 20°C). Douglas observed spawning at Lake Havasu from March 15 to 17, but the authors argue such dates do not apply to Colorado. Spawning there probably does not occur before early June. Eggs measured 0.08 to 0.13 inches (2.0 to 3.2 mm).

**Spawning success**

- Spawning success for all four species declined markedly in the Yampa and Green Rivers.
- Successful spawning was found below Echo Park for all native species except humpback chub. There was no evidence of success above Echo Park to Flaming Gorge.
- Holden and Stalnaker concluded that successful reproduction occurred only in the Yampa and lower Green Rivers, and that any alteration of the Green River system would be harmful, especially to the Yampa populations.
- Razorback sucker spawning has been observed in several reservoirs, but there is little evidence that it has produced offspring. Artificially taken eggs hatched at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery at 58°F (14°C).

**Conclusions**

The authors conclude that the four species are in severe decline and that the remaining habitat is fragile. Future impoundments and water development could push them to extinction. The addendum notes that several more recent studies, including a habitat and movement study of rare fishes in the Green River (Holden, 1977) and a status evaluation of endangered upper-basin fishes (Joseph et al., 1977), had been published after the review was written.
