# Native Fish Needs and Watershed Fisheries Management in the Little Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) materials for a 1998 expert meeting and a draft watershed-scale fisheries management plan for the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) basin. The plan aims to reconcile native fish conservation with sportfish recreation. It asks experts to define how many populations of seven native fish, and at which localities, the basin must hold to ensure long-term sustainability. The package includes the meeting memo and agenda, the plan's rationale and methods, basin statistics, and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] biological summaries for each target species. The source notes that data on these native fishes are scarce.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and meeting.** Kirk Young, the Nongame Native Fish Program Manager, invited specialists to a November 17, 1998 meeting in Phoenix. The goal was to set population numbers and localities for seven native fish: [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Zuni Bluehead Sucker]], [[Little Colorado River Sucker]] and [[Apache Trout]]. The agenda defined a population as a group of individuals occupying a particular area, or a unit that functions together. Results were to feed a watershed management report.

**Management context.** The Department's mandates cover nongame and endangered wildlife protection and sportfish management. The source notes that the two mandates often conflict within the same water body. Past stocking and renovation projects had little regard for native fish. A stocking review under [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 caused negative impacts on several sportfisheries. The document argues for proactive, integrated management, with [[Hydrologic Watershed]] units as the planning scale.

**Species profiles (summarized from AGFD biological summaries, draft February 1998):**

- **Little Colorado spinedace** (*Lepidomeda vittata*, Cyprinidae): Small, typically under 100 mm. Federally threatened, with critical habitat designated in 1987 (31 mi of East Clear Creek, 8 mi of Chevelon Creek, 5 mi of Nutrioso Creek). Six extant populations exist in Arizona. Eggs range from 650 to 5,000 per female. Diet is chiefly chironomid larvae and ephemeropteran nymphs. Expected lifespan is about four years.
- **Speckled dace** (*Rhinichthys osculus*, Cyprinidae): The most widespread western fish, up to 110 mm. The summary calls it stable and not endangered, though four Nevada and Wyoming subspecies are protected. Sexual maturity is reached at age 2, with up to 1,000 demersal eggs per spawn. Non-native predatory fish reduce its survival.
- **Roundtail chub** (*Gila robusta*, Cyprinidae): Typically 250–350 mm, occasionally 500–600 mm. Federally a "Species of Concern" (1991). Two subspecies are endangered. Fecundity ranges from about 600 to 45,125 eggs. Spawning occurs at 14–24 °C. Lifespan averages 8–10 years.
- **Bluehead sucker** (*Catostomus discobolus*, Catostomidae): Widely distributed and not considered at risk. Arizona populations include the LCR, Clear, Chevelon, Nutrioso and Silver creeks. Typical length is 250–350 mm. Fecundity reaches 5,000–8,500 eggs. The LCR form has a thickened body and short fins, attributed to ancient stream capture and hybridization.
- **Zuni bluehead sucker** (*C. d. yarrowi*): Endemic to the Zuni River drainage in New Mexico. Arizona records are limited to Kin Li Chee Creek, and the subspecies is believed possibly extirpated from Arizona. It is a New Mexico state endangered species and a federal Species of Concern.
- **Little Colorado River sucker** (*Catostomus* sp.): Possibly of hybrid origin between *C. latipinnis* and *C. insignis*, genetically distinct from both. Federal status moved from C2 candidate (1995) to Species of Concern (1996). Spawns in early to mid-spring in riffles.
- **Apache trout** (*Oncorhynchus apache*, Salmonidae): Arizona's state fish, native to White Mountain headwaters. It was listed as endangered in 1973 and downlisted to threatened in 1975. The recovery goal is 30 self-sustaining populations. Growth is slow, about 50 mm per year. Females mature at three years or 130 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin characteristics.** The [[Little Colorado River]] and its tributaries drain more than 27,000 square miles of northeastern Arizona and northwestern New Mexico. The mean annual discharge is 179,000 acre-feet. The river rises near Greer in the White Mountains at 8,200 ft and joins the [[Colorado River]] in Grand Canyon National Park at 2,800 ft. The basin has nearly 650 miles of perennial habitat across the mainstem and more than 50 tributaries.

**Fish assemblage.** The basin supports nine native species, four of them federally listed and four former federal candidates. At least 24 introduced species occur, 18 of them sportfish. Sensitive amphibians and reptiles, including northern and Chiricahua leopard frogs, painted turtle and narrow-headed garter snake, are present or historically recorded.

**Stressors.** The source identifies dams, diversions, groundwater pumping, flow modification, habitat loss, and erosion as causes of decline. Introduced predators and competitors, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Crayfish]], are also significant. Some species, including the LCR spinedace and Apache trout, are threatened by hybridization or competition with these introduced fish.

**Sportfishery and economics.** Active sportfish management occurs at about 40 reservoirs and streams covering over 10,500 acres. The 1992 angler survey (AGFD Fisheries Branch) reported:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Angler days (excluding Indian lands) | 849,801 |
| Average spend per day | $54.50 |
| Total dollars spent | $46,215,406 |
| Total economic benefit (multiplier 1.8) | $83,187,730 |

The plan's sportfish goals include avoiding losses at [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]], [[Knoll Lake]], [[Nelson Reservoir]] and [[Lyman Lake]]. Stocked rainbow trout and channel catfish threaten these fisheries through their potential effects on LCR spinedace.

**Methods.** The plan divides perennial and intermittent waters into [[Management Unit]]s. The criteria include changes in species occurrence, land ownership, barriers, stream order, temperature, habitat condition and flow regime. Each unit is scored on native fish value and sportfish value using the [[Native Fish Flowchart]] and a sportfish flowchart. Outcomes are HIGH, MODERATE, LOW or UNKNOWN. The higher score sets the management emphasis. A six-member Core Management Planning Group carried out the work. Occurrence data came from museum collections, agency surveys, and databases such as HDMS and the stocking database. The source notes that the process is partly subjective, a deliberate choice intended to keep the watershed from being forced into a poorly fitting model.

## Key Findings

- **Plan rationale:** A watershed-based approach is needed to address conflicts between native fish conservation and sportfish management. The LCR basin was selected as a pilot because of its sportfishery importance and diverse native fish assemblage.
- **Core goals:** Recover listed species, conserve non-listed natives, avoid future federal listings, and maintain angler opportunity. The plan's stated objectives are delisting, maintenance and enhancement of native populations, sustained angler opportunity, and basin-wide conservation.
- **Data gap:** The memo states that data on the native fishes are scarce. Population numbers and localities were not yet established, and angler needs were assumed to be met based on expert opinion rather than survey data.
- **Population targets:** The expert group was to define minimum and ideal population numbers for each of the seven native species, at sub-basin level. The source does not report the final numbers.
- **Listed-species status:** Several species have shifted status over time, from federal candidates to species of concern to threatened. Their ranges are often reduced to a few headwater reaches.
- **Economic stakes:** Angling in the basin supports about 850,000 angler days and an estimated $83 million in economic benefit (1992 data).
- **Integrated management:** The source concludes that native and sportfish management should be proactive and integrated, rather than reactive, to avoid repeating past conflicts.
