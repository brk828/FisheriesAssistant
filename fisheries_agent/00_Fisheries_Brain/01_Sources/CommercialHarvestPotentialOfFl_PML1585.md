# Commercial Harvest Potential of Flathead Catfish in the Colorado, Gila, Salt and Verde Rivers, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by [[Arizona State University]] for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Contract Number 0017 per the body text), assesses the status and commercial harvest potential of introduced [[Flathead Catfish]] in Arizona's flowing waters. Of the rivers examined, only the [[Lower Colorado River]] population was judged suitable for commercial harvest. Based on a 1987 mark-recapture estimate of about 5,053 fish of 330 mm total length or more in a 33.5-km reach near Yuma, anglers removed an estimated 3,387 kg in 1987, leaving roughly 6,669 kg available for other harvest. Potential gross value of the unexploited resource was estimated at $50,000 to $180,000, of which about $3,000 to $8,000 might reach the fisherman. A subsequent flow reduction after 1987 caused a marked decline in the population, so these estimates are no longer considered valid.

## Background & Description

Flathead catfish (*Pylodictis olivaris*) is a large, piscivorous ictalurid native to the Mississippi, Missouri and Ohio basins, the Gulf of Mexico coastal plain, and the Rio Grande drainage. It was first introduced into the Gila River system of Arizona or New Mexico before 1954, and stocked in the lower Colorado River in 1963. Later introductions from 1968 to 1975 occurred in the Salt, Verde, and San Francisco rivers.

Morphologically, the species has a naked skin, an adipose dorsal fin, and a strongly depressed head with a conspicuous lower jaw. Coloration ranges from yellow to dark brown with mottling. Juveniles feed on aquatic insect larvae and crayfish; above about 250 mm total length adults are almost exclusively piscivorous. Males mature at 3–5 years and females at 3–7 years depending on habitat, and spawning occurs in secluded cover from June through August. The world angling record cited is 41 kg (91 lb 4 oz), from [[Lake Lewisville]], Texas.

Target study areas were chosen for flowing water, presence of flathead, and accessibility. These included the [[Yuma Division]] of the lower Colorado River, the Salt River above [[Roosevelt Lake]], the Verde River between Childs and Horseshoe reservoirs and Bartlett Reservoir, and the Gila River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Flathead catfish occupy major flowing rivers and reservoirs across the lower Colorado, Gila, Salt and Verde basins, and support important local recreational fisheries. Growth rates in the lower Colorado were rapid, at least as fast as anywhere in the species' range, and fish there lived longer and reached larger sizes than in other Arizona streams. Immigration from upstream sources was thought to contribute to growth variation in the lower Colorado.

Water chemistry and flow strongly shape habitat. Flows in the lower Colorado below about 38 m³/s make navigation perilous. Post-1987 flow reduction removed undercut banks, deep pools and debris accumulations, replacing them with steeply sloping, clean sandy banks and uniform channels.

Native fish assemblages are rare and at risk. Native species made up a small share of catches, including [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarkii*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and [[Sonora Sucker]]. Incidental capture of endangered species, including the [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], in commercial gear was identified as a concern.

Contaminants were the second environmental focus. Organochlorine pesticides were measured in marketable-size flathead from each river.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- Flathead catfish has expanded steadily in Arizona since its introduction and now occurs in the mainstem Colorado River from Parker Dam to the Gulf of California, and in the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers.
- Only the lower Colorado River population was judged suitable for commercial exploitation, based on population density and accessibility.

**Angler use**
- A 1986 creel survey of the lower Colorado covered 14 days, with 847 anglers fishing 3,078 hours. Flathead catfish made up 70% of the catch by number, and largemouth bass made up 14%.
- Angler pressure on the Gila, Verde, and most of the Salt River was low.

**Contaminant analysis**
- Samples from 33 flathead catfish of marketable size (400–500 mm total length) were analyzed. Pesticide residues in fish from the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers were below FDA tolerance levels.
- Two lower Colorado samples exceeded the FDA tolerance for heptachlor (0.32 and 0.39 ppm in the original reporting, against a 0.3 ppm limit). Heptachlor epoxide and other compounds were measured, and lower Colorado samples had notably high DDT and BHC metabolites, but all were below tolerance.

**Gear efficiency**
- Electroshocking was clearly the most efficient capture method, with catch per unit effort averaging 33.3 fish/hour on the lower Colorado and 34.7 fish/hour on the Verde.
- Trammel and gill nets, hoop nets and set-lines yielded poor catches of flathead, and debris and seasonal alga caused net problems.

**Age and growth**
- Lower Colorado specimens (n = 78) ranged from age 1 to 9. Young-of-year averaged 71 mm at capture, and the largest fish was 1,010 mm long and about 9 years old.
- Gila River samples (n = 14) ranged from 1 to 5 years; Salt River samples (n = 54) from 1 to 6 years; Verde River samples (n = 46) from 1 to 8 years.
- Growth in the lower Colorado was faster than in most other Arizona streams during the first years of life.

**Population estimates (1987, lower Colorado, Yuma Division)**
- Petersen, Chapman-adjusted Petersen, Schumacher–Eschmeyer and Chapman-modified Schnabel methods were compared. The best-supported estimates fell in the range of about 5,000 fish (95% confidence limits roughly 3,700–13,600 fish for the Petersen estimate).
- Density in the lower Colorado was estimated at about 53 fish/ha; the Gila River was estimated at about 41 fish/ha.

**Yield and value**
- Using the Thompson and Bell approach with instantaneous rates of total mortality (Z = 1.0070), fishing mortality (F = 0.6017) and natural mortality (M = 0.4043), total potential yield was estimated at 5,946 kg per year.
- Anglers were estimated to remove about 3,387 kg (about 1,461 fish), leaving about 6,669 kg (about 2,559 kg in a lower estimate) for commercial or other harvest.
- Yield was more sensitive to natural mortality than to fishing mortality, and maximum yield was estimated near F = 0.4 under the assumptions used.
- Estimated retail value of the unexploited resource ranged from about $50,000 to $180,000 per year, with an estimated $3,000 to $8,000 to the commercial fisherman.

**Post-1987 collapse**
- After river flow fell below about 14–17 m³/s in 1987, habitat was largely lost. Electrofishing in 1988 caught only 1 fish in May, 9 fish in June, and 49 fish in September, with fish of marketable size almost absent.
- Authors attribute the collapse to habitat reduction combined with angler harvest, and conclude that a sustained commercial fishery is unlikely.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Short-term commercial ventures during periods of abundance could be profitable, but a sustained fishery is unlikely under present conditions.
- Commercial harvest would substantially remove large fish and reduce the value of the resource.
- Any future commercial fishery should be accompanied by monitoring of abundance and population structure, and removal programs could be considered as an alternative to commercialization.
