# Economic Study of Montana Commercial Fisheries Products

#ichthyology #commercial-fisheries #economic-analysis #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 economic study examined the processing and marketing potential of commercial fishery products from [[Montana]] reservoirs, focusing on [[Carp]], [[Buffalofish]], [[Sucker]], and [[Goldeye]]. The research found that Montana's commercial fisheries face significant challenges including irregular demand, low market prices, unethical middlemen practices, negative consumer perceptions of "rough fish," and seasonal harvesting limitations. While [[Fort Peck Reservoir]] alone could sustain annual yields of 6-9 million pounds, actual 1967 harvest was only 1,275,000 pounds valued at $90,000. The study concluded that Montana fish products face limited market potential for direct human consumption but identified possible alternative uses including institutional feeding, pet food, and fish meal production.

## Background & Description

Montana's commercial fisheries operate exclusively on four man-made reservoirs east of the Continental Divide: [[Fort Peck Reservoir]] (245,000 acres), [[Canyon Ferry]] (35,200 acres), [[Lake Helena]] (2,100 acres), and [[Nelson Reservoir]] (4,560 acres). These waters contain approximately 20 fish species, with commercial value concentrated in [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Buffalofish]] (*Ictiobus* spp.), [[Goldeye]] (*Hiodon alosoides*), and various [[Sucker]] species (*Catostomus* spp.).

Montana Fish and Game Department biologists estimated sustained annual yield potential from [[Fort Peck Reservoir]] at: [[Buffalofish]] 1.25-2.5 million pounds, [[Carp]] 3.75-5.0 million pounds, [[Goldeye]] 0.5-1.0 million pounds, and [[Sucker]]s 0.5 million pounds, totaling 6-9 million pounds annually. These species are classified as "rough fish" or "trash fish" that compete with sport fish for food resources.

Commercial fishing in Montana began in 1954. By 1967, three enterprises operated in the state, though not exclusively in Montana waters. Fishermen used seining equipment and fish traps, with operations limited to April-November due to ice conditions. Montana law imposed minimal entry restrictions: free licenses for qualified applicants, with fees of 5% of net receipts for [[Goldeye]] and [[Catfish]], $2.00 per ton for [[Carp]] and [[Sucker]]s, and $5.00 per ton for [[Buffalofish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study characterized Montana's commercial fish species as competitors with game fish (family Salmonidae, genus *Stizostedion*, genus *Esox*, genus *Micropterus*, and genus *Polyodon*) for food resources. Montana Fish and Game Department chose to control rough fish populations through commercial fishing rather than expensive state-funded removal programs. Nebraska data indicated state removal costs of 3.1 cents per pound net cost, compared to Montana receiving fees while fishermen removed 675,663 pounds in 1966.

The reservoirs' productivity appeared lower than comparable waters in the upper Midwest. Buyers consistently reported Montana [[Buffalofish]] as "too lean, not as 'meaty,' 'rich,' or 'plump'" compared to fish from [[Louisiana]], [[Minnesota]], [[Iowa]], [[Wisconsin]], and [[Illinois]]. This difference was attributed to feeding conditions and water characteristics, though specific limnological data were not provided.

Seasonal limitations severely constrained operations. Winter ice conditions prevented year-round harvesting, unlike [[Wisconsin]] fisheries that operated continuously. Water level management practices at [[Fort Peck Reservoir]] eliminated spawning seasons for extended periods, creating boom-and-bust cycles in fish availability.

## Key Findings

**Market Demand Analysis**: United States per capita consumption of commercially caught fish remained stable at 10.7 pounds annually from 1920-1965, with income elasticity of demand at 0.42 and price elasticity at -0.65. Population growth represented the only significant driver of increased total demand. Regional demand (Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, Utah, North Dakota, South Dakota) was projected to reach 52 million pounds by 1980, a 30% increase over 1960 consumption of 40 million pounds. However, a pilot study in [[Missoula]] found residents consumed fish less than one-third as frequently as residents of major seaport cities.

**Marketing Channels**: Montana fish moved through three primary channels: (1) [[Buffalofish]] shipped to [[San Francisco Bay]] area retailers at 20¢/pound to fishermen; (2) [[Carp]] and [[Sucker]]s sold on west coast or to [[Idaho]] trout farms at 2¢/pound; (3) [[Goldeye]] shipped to [[Winnipeg]] processors at 25¢/pound (1966 prices). Transportation costs to [[Chicago]] (8¢/pound from Montana vs. 2.5¢/pound from [[Iowa]]) placed Montana at competitive disadvantage in Midwest markets.

**Buyer Attitudes Survey**: Depth interviews with 14 major buyers in [[Chicago]] and [[Los Angeles]] (July-August 1968) revealed overwhelmingly negative perceptions. Nine of 14 buyers had never purchased Montana fish; most were unaware Montana had commercial fisheries. Buyers characterized [[Carp]], [[Buffalofish]], and [[Sucker]]s as "inferior," "cheap," "rough," "scavenger" fish for "low-class" or "ethnic markets" (primarily African American and Jewish consumers). Not one buyer reported personally consuming these species.

Specific complaints about Montana fish included: inferior quality compared to [[Louisiana]] [[Buffalofish]]; irregular supply; lack of proper dressing/grading; inadequate icing during transport; higher prices than Midwest sources; and seasonal unavailability. One [[Los Angeles]] wholesaler noted: "That fish out of Montana is not as good as the fish out of L.A....the public...prefer a L.A. fish. Therefore, if it comes in from Montana, you just can't get the money out of it."

**Goldeye Market**: [[Winnipeg]] represented the primary market for [[Goldeye]], where the species commanded premium prices ($1.50-$1.95/pound retail) as a smoked delicacy served in restaurants and railroad dining cars. However, by September 1967, 400,000 pounds sat in cold storage, causing prices to fishermen to collapse from 45-50¢/pound to as low as 17¢/pound. The market demonstrated extreme price inelasticity—small supply changes caused large price fluctuations. A [[Calgary]] survey found annual sales of only 4,000-4,500 pounds despite favorable consumer attitudes, limited by high wholesale prices (80¢/pound), "woody" flesh quality, and unreliable supply.

**Business Conditions**: Montana commercial fishing operations faced profitability challenges similar to those documented in Great Lakes fisheries, where expenses reached 80% of gross sales excluding owner labor and capital returns. Unethical practices pervaded the industry—fishermen reported being cheated on weight and price by wholesalers in [[Winnipeg]] and [[Chicago]], while wholesalers alleged fishermen understated sales to reduce state fees. One study noted "the low standard of ethics that becomes manifest all too often in the trade."

**Historical Performance**: Commercial sales fluctuated dramatically: 268,000 pounds ($32,000) in 1963; 237,000 pounds ($16,000) in 1964; 354,000 pounds ($32,000) in 1965; 675,663 pounds ($50,780) in 1966; and 1,275,244 pounds ($90,000) in 1967. These fluctuations reflected irregular fishing effort driven by supply conditions in competing regions rather than stable market development.

**Alternative Uses**: The study identified several potential alternative markets: (1) Fish protein concentrate for human nutrition; (2) Institutional feeding (Montana custodial institutions held 2,500+ inmates/employees in 1967; university system enrollment projected at 15,000+ for 1968); (3) Pet food production; (4) Fish meal and oil for animal feed; (5) Liquid fish fertilizer. However, detailed feasibility analysis of these alternatives was not included in the available document pages.

**Competitive Position**: Montana fisheries faced structural disadvantages: greater distance from major markets than Midwest competitors; lower fish quality (leaner flesh) than southern sources; seasonal operation vs. year-round Midwest production; lack of processing infrastructure; small scale preventing economies of scale; and negative "rough fish" image. The only competitive advantage was proximity to western markets for species not readily available from marine sources.
