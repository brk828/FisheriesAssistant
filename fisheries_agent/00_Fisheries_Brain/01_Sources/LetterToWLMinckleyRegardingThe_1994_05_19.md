# Letter to W.L. Minckley Regarding the National Fish Hatchery System

## Executive Summary
This 1994 letter from Edwin P. "Phil" Pister (Executive Secretary, [[Desert Fishes Council]]) to Dr. W.L. Minckley ([[Arizona State University]]) addresses the role of the [[National Fish Hatchery System]] (NFHS) in managing [[Threatened and Endangered Species|threatened and endangered fishes]] (TES). Rather than presenting primary morphological or ecological field data, the letter is a policy/advisory document raising two central concerns: (1) the ecological harm caused by NFHS-facilitated stocking of nonnative gamefishes into native fish habitats, and (2) insufficient hatchery infrastructure dedicated to rearing and conserving imperiled native fish species. The letter uses the invasive spread of [[Sacramento Perch]] (*Archoplites interruptus*) in the [[Owens River]] drainage as a case study, and cites [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as the sole (and thus inadequate) NFHS facility engaged in TES propagation.

## Background & Description
The letter contains minimal direct morphological data, as its focus is policy-oriented. The only species explicitly named is:

- **[[Sacramento Perch]]** (*Archoplites interruptus*)
  - Family: [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes)
  - Native range: Central California waters (specifically noted as originating from a population >100 miles from the Owens River introduction site)
  - No quantitative morphometric data (length, weight, meristics) is provided in the source text
  - Notable for being a native California centrarchid capable of establishing self-sustaining populations far outside its native range when introduced

No morphological descriptions are given for salmonid species or other threatened/endangered fishes mentioned generally in the text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: The letter addresses aquatic habitats broadly degraded by water development projects, particularly in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] and [[Owens River]] drainage (near Bishop, California).
- **Invasion ecology case study**: *Archoplites interruptus* (Sacramento perch) appeared "suddenly and unexplainedly" in 1985 in a major reservoir on the Owens River, likely via unauthorized angler introduction. Over the subsequent decade or more (per the letter's 1994 vantage point), the species spread throughout the drainage.
  - This spread occurred both **upstream and downstream** of the introduction point and into **adjacent and distant waters**, illustrating a general principle: once a nonnative fish is established, containment is effectively impossible.
  - Impact: Detrimental to both native fish species and more "desirable" (i.e., managed/game) fish populations.
  - Risk correlation: Likelihood of illegal/unauthorized transplant introductions is described as being in **direct proportion to proximity of the source population**.
- **Anadromous salmonids**: The NFHS's primary historical involvement in California has been with hatcheries tied to water development projects, serving almost exclusively [[Anadromous Salmonids]]. This use is under "intense scrutiny" due to concerns over genetic integrity of wild salmonid stocks — a concept linked to [[Hatchery-Wild Genetic Introgression]].
- **Endangered species refugia**: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Roswell, New Mexico) is identified as a critical but singular refugium for threatened/endangered fish rearing, lacking capability for cold-water/salmonid TES — creating a geographic and thermal gap in the national conservation infrastructure.

## Key Findings
1. **Nonnative stocking as a driver of native fish decline**: The letter asserts that NFHS-linked introduction of gamefishes into native fish habitats "adds measurably" to the documented decline and endangerment of native fish species, referencing the draft USFWS document *Procedures for Stocking Nonnative Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin*.
2. **Uncontrollable spread post-introduction**: Once a nonnative species is stocked and establishes, it will inevitably spread beyond intended management boundaries — demonstrated by the *Archoplites interruptus* Owens River case.
3. **Policy recommendation #1**: Units of the NFHS should discontinue rearing and distribution of nonnative species, given the "voluminous documentation" supporting caution in nonnative stocking.
4. **Insufficient TES hatchery capacity**: Only one NFHS facility, [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], is meaningfully engaged in threatened/endangered fish propagation — an "all eggs in one basket" vulnerability, especially given its lack of capacity for salmonid TES.
5. **Policy recommendation #2**: Additional NFHS units across diverse locations and water types should be developed to cover the full diversity of imperiled fishes, supported by dedicated fish geneticists at each installation to preserve genetic integrity.
6. **Supporting literature cited**: [[Williams et al. 1989]] (*Fisheries* 14(6):2-21) and [[Miller et al. 1989]] (*Fisheries* 14(6):22-38) are referenced as key documentation for the status of threatened/endangered/extinct North American fishes.
7. **Underlying philosophy**: Hatchery programs reflect the "social values of their time" and must be re-evaluated to prioritize conservation of native aquatic biodiversity over historical gamefish-stocking mandates, amid continued global aquatic habitat degradation.
