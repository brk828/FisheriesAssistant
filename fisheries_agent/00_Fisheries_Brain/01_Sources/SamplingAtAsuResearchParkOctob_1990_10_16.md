## Executive Summary

This memorandum from Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies summarizes a fish community assessment conducted 02–03 October 1990 at three artificial lakes (A, B, C) at the ASU Research Park (ASURF), Tempe, Arizona. The survey combined trammel nets, experimental gill nets, and shoreline seining to evaluate the status of native and non-native fishes, with particular conservation interest in two imperiled Colorado River basin natives: [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. A third target species, [[Colorado Squawfish]], evaded capture entirely despite confirmed presence via angler catches. The report also documents a worsening nuisance aquatic vegetation problem (spiny naiad and cattail) and notes the apparent ineffectiveness of a 1970 triploid grass carp stocking for vegetation control.

## Background & Description

- **Bonytail** (n = 2, captured only in Lake B): 33.2 and 36.5 cm total length (TL); weights of 284 and 250 g, respectively (note: original text has a probable transcription error listing "284 and 250 gm" against much larger TLs — retained as reported). Sex indeterminate (secondary sex characters undeveloped). Both individuals exhibited light infestations of the parasitic copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea* but were otherwise in good condition.

- **Razorback Sucker** (captured in Lakes A and B; n = 11 total): Ten measurable fish averaged 35.2 ± 2.3 cm TL (SD; range 33.2–36.5 cm) and weighed 465 ± 213 g (range 286–1000 g). One additional specimen had been partially consumed by crayfish, precluding accurate measurement. Secondary sex characters undeveloped in all specimens; none were reproductively ripe, precluding sex determination. A comparable cohort of "small" razorback suckers measured 27.2 cm TL (range 21.5–37.0 cm) in May 1990, suggesting this October cohort represents growth from the same stocking events.

- **Threadfin Shad, Largemouth Bass, Tilapia spp.**: No individual morphometric data reported; only catch composition provided (see Key Findings).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake-specific distribution**: [[Bonytail]] were captured exclusively in Lake B; this species had not been recorded in Lake A since April 1989 or in Lake C since October 1988, suggesting a contracting or highly localized population within the ASURF system.
- **Razorback Sucker** occupied Lakes A and B but were absent from Lake C during this survey.
- **Colorado Squawfish**: Presumed present in Lake B (confirmed via angler hook-and-line catches) but not detected by netting since March 1988. Proposed explanations include net avoidance (possible visual detection of gear in clear water) or sedentary behavior reducing encounter probability with nets.
- **Habitat degradation**: Spiny naiad (*Najas* sp.) has become "out of control" in Lakes B and C, clogging entanglement nets and precluding seining along significant shoreline stretches. Cattail (*Typha domingensis*) is forming dense, impenetrable stands along Lake B shorelines, altering shoreline habitat structure (with some benefit to passerine bird roosting) but posing a management concern.
- **Biological control attempt**: Triploid grass carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), stocked in 1970 at approximately 4 fish/acre across all three lakes, were not collected in this survey, and their efficacy in controlling nuisance vegetation remains unconfirmed.
- **Community structure by gear type**: Entanglement nets (trammel + gill nets) captured deeper-water/larger-bodied taxa (shad, bass, sucker, bonytail), while seines sampled shallow, shoreline-associated taxa (bluegill, tilapia, mosquitofish, red shiner), reflecting characteristic habitat partitioning within the lentic system.

## Key Findings

**Entanglement net catch (Table 1)** — 90 individuals, 12 taxa across Lakes A, B, C:
- [[Threadfin Shad]]: 33% of total catch (30 individuals), primarily via gill nets
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: 22% (20 individuals)
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: 12% (11 individuals)
- [[Bluegill]]: 7% (6 individuals)
- [[Redbelly Tilapia]]: 9% (8 individuals)
- [[Mozambique Tilapia]]: 7% (6 individuals)
- [[Bonytail]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Blue Tilapia]], [[Hybrid Tilapia]]: each 1–2% of catch
- Lake B had the highest species richness (9 taxa) and total catch (38 individuals); Lake A had lowest richness (4 taxa, 27 individuals)

**Seine catch (Table 2)** — 411 individuals, 6 taxa across Lakes A, B, C:
- [[Bluegill]]: 34.3% (223 individuals) — dominant in all three lakes
- [[Tilapia]] spp.: 34.3% (141 individuals) — dominant especially in Lake C (77.4% of that lake's catch)
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: 4.6%, [[Mosquitofish]]: 4.9%, [[Green Sunfish]]: 1.2%, [[Red Shiner]]: 0.7%
- Catch-per-effort (CPE) was comparable to autumn 1989 in Lake A but substantially reduced in Lakes B and C relative to the prior year
- Red shiner and mosquitofish were notably less abundant in Lake A in 1990 compared to 1989

**Conservation/management implications**:
- All species encountered had been previously recorded at ASURF; no new introductions detected.
- Native species reproduction has not yet been confirmed; routine autumn monitoring will be suspended pending detection of natural reproduction via planned larval sampling (late autumn 1990–spring 1991).
- Intensive netting is planned for spring 1991 to target Colorado squawfish during a presumed period of higher activity.
- Nuisance vegetation (spiny naiad, cattail) is flagged as an escalating management priority requiring intervention before becoming unmanageable through conventional methods.
