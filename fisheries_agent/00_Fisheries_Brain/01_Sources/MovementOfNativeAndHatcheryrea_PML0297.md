# Movement of Native and Hatchery-Reared Game Fish in a Small Ohio Headwater Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950s study by [[Edward H. Brown Jr.]] of the [[Ohio Department of Natural Resources]], Division of Wildlife, tracked the movement of tagged [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Rock Bass]] in [[Massie Creek]], a small headwater tributary of the [[Little Miami River]] in southwestern Ohio, during 1953 through 1957. It compares native fish with [[Hatchery-Reared Smallmouth Bass]] released into the stream. Native fish remained largely within limited areas, while a considerable proportion of stocked bass moved long distances, predominantly downstream. The author concludes that these findings place substantial doubt on the value of stocking yearling or older smallmouth bass in streams as a management tool.

## Background & Description

- **Study scope:** Data from 248 recaptures of 2,270 game fish tagged and released in Massie Creek.
- **Tagged fish:** 637 native smallmouth bass, 240 native rock bass, and 893 hatchery-reared smallmouth bass.
- **Tagging method:** Band-type jaw tags (Style 4F.1242M-Monel, manufactured by the National Band and Tag Company) were placed on the lower mandible. Tag retention was not tested directly, though other investigators reported such tags to be among the most durable available.
- **Purpose:** Native fish were tagged alongside studies of distribution, age and growth, reproduction and angler harvest. Two hatchery groups were tested for stocking success in an area with a sizable native population and in a submarginal area where the species had apparently become extirpated.
- **Study stream:** Massie Creek flows southwesterly for about 22 miles through Clark and Greene Counties to join the Little Miami River. A gorge at Cedarville divides it into an upper section of open farmland and a lower section of wooded pastures and bedrock channel. A mill dam about 8.5 miles from the mouth isolates headwater fish from downstream populations.
- **Literature context:** The paper reviews prior work by [[Scott]] (1949), [[Gerking]] (1950, 1953, 1959), [[Larimore]] (1952, 1954), [[Funk]] (1957), and [[Wickliff]] (1933), the last of whom reported on stocked fish dispersal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat conditions:** Stream width and maximum depth of survey pools in the lower 8.5-mile section averaged 34 and 2.6 feet during low flow in summer 1956. Mean daily discharge 4 miles above the mouth (October 1952 to September 1957) was 38 cubic feet per second. Gradient was under 10 feet per mile in the upper section and about 18 feet per mile in the lower section.
- **Population distribution:** Native smallmouth bass had the highest population densities in the Cedarville gorge area within the Little Miami headwaters. Rock bass were more widely distributed and were taken from both upper and lower sections.
- **Spawning:** Smallmouth bass nested as early as April 13 and remained at nests into late June. Rock bass nests appeared a week or two after the first smallmouth nests, and rock bass were spawning heavily in mid-June.
- **Stocking sites:** Hatchery smallmouth bass were released in the lower area on March 11–12, 1953, and in the upper area on October 18, 1955.

## Key Findings

**Native smallmouth bass**
- 70 tags were returned by anglers, 11.0 percent of the 637 native fish marked 1953–1957. Returns ranged from 6.4 percent of the large 1952 group to 25.0 percent of the small 1954 group.
- 64 fish (91.4 percent) reported by anglers were recaptured within 1/2 mile of release points.
- 34 additional tagged native fish were recaptured by electric shocker in summers 1956 and 1957.
- Of 35 fish recaptured by shocker that had been tagged in 1955–1956, 22 were retaken from the same or adjacent pools, indicating limited areal movement.

**Native rock bass**
- 27 tagged fish (3.6 percent of those tagged in 1953–1957) were returned by anglers. Returns ranged from 1.4 percent (1955) to 10.2 percent (1953).
- 21 of 23 angler-reported fish (91.3 percent) were recaptured within 1/2 mile of release points.
- Nineteen fish were rerecaptured in hoop nets from the same pool during the first year, and 14 were retaken by electrofishing within two to four pools.

**Hatchery-reared smallmouth bass**
- 57 tags (9.5 percent) from the March 1953 stocking below Cedarville were returned by anglers in 1953–1955. Returns from the October 1955 upper-area stocking were 13 (4.4 percent) in 1956–1957.
- None of the stocked fish were taken by electric shocker.
- 21 fish (35.7 percent of the 1953-stocked bass reported) moved more than 1/2 mile, compared with less than 9 percent for either native species.
- Nearly all recorded movements from the 1953 stocking were downstream, with the longest reaching about 70 miles (captured in the Ohio River at Cincinnati).
- The proportion of recaptured fish moving 11–50 miles downstream increased significantly from 1953 to 1954, suggesting progressive downstream dispersal. A chi-square test gave a value of 10.96, a highly significant difference.
- All fish stocked in the submarginal upper area and recovered by anglers moved more than 1/2 mile, and all returns were from downstream, with none recaptured within 2 miles of release points except one hoop-net capture.

**Size and stocking effects**
- The 1953 stocking had a length range of about 6 inches. Length had no significant influence on recapture rate in 1953 for the main length groups (after excluding the smallest fish), although a significant effect was reported for the 1955 stocking.
- The 1955 hatchery fish averaged 2.4 inches, compared with 5.1 inches for the 1953 stocking.
- Stocked fish contributed relatively little to the creel. Only 1.5 percent of stocked age-group II fish were reported in mid-summer 1954, compared with 8.0 percent of native fish.

**Management implications**
- Native smallmouth bass and rock bass showed a high degree of areal stability. The author supports the view of [[Gerking]] that stream fish populations act as discrete natural units rather than a single freely mixing group.
- Movement of hatchery-reared smallmouth bass away from release sites suggests stocking may be of limited practicality as a management tool.
- Higher natural mortality of dispersing fish, and reporting bias (anglers far downstream less likely to report tags), may partly explain the rapid disappearance of 1955-stocked fish.
- The author cautions against generalizing from a single stream, noting differences between Massie Creek and the [[Jordan Creek]] study in Illinois.
