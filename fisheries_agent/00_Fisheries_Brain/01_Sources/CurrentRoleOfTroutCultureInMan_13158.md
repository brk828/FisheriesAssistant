# Current Role of Trout Culture in Management of Trout Fisheries: A U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Perspective

## Executive Summary
This paper, authored by Larry Visscher of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 6), reviews the 123-year history of federal fish hatchery programs and their evolving role in trout fisheries management in the Intermountain West and beyond. It traces the shift from historic mass-stocking practices (measured by quantity) toward a modern, genetics-based, quality-focused [[National Broodstock Program]]. The document addresses two distinct hatchery roles: (1) supporting recreational trout fisheries in reservoirs created by federal water development projects, and (2) restoring and recovering native salmonids, using the [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] as a successful case study. The paper emphasizes that hatchery propagation is not a substitute for habitat protection and that future stocking programs require rigorous management planning, monitoring, and evaluation.

## Background & Description
This source is a fisheries management policy document, not a morphological study; it does not provide anatomical, meristic, or morphometric data for any species. Species mentioned are referenced only by common name and their role in stocking/culture programs:
- **Brown Trout** (*Salmo trutta*) — introduced from Germany; historically stocked nationwide.
- **Common Carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*) — introduced from Germany alongside Brown Trout.
- **Brook Trout** (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) — native to the Northeast U.S.; historically translocated westward.
- **Rainbow Trout** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) — native to the West; historically translocated eastward.
- **Steelhead** and **Pacific Salmon** (*Oncorhynchus* spp.) — more recently stocked into the [[Great Lakes]] and large Missouri River reservoirs.
- **[[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus clarkii stomias*) — native salmonid subject of a recovery/restoration program; no morphological description provided in text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historic habitat alteration**: Federal water development projects (dams, diversions) converted riverine habitats into reservoirs throughout the West, blocking spawning runs, inundating spawning habitat, and altering water quality/quantity — rendering native riverine fish assemblages unsuitable for these new lentic systems ([[Reservoir Ecology]]).
- **Recreational fishery niche**: Reservoirs created by mitigation projects can only be managed for recreational value via continuous hatchery-supported stocking rather than natural reproduction.
- **Native trout stocking guidelines** (Region 6, established late 1970s):
  - Trout will **not** be stocked into rivers/streams containing native trout species (exceptions only with full management consensus).
  - Trout **may** be stocked into natural lakes with a documented history of no natural reproduction.
  - Trout **may** be stocked into reservoirs specifically for recreational fisheries development.
- **Genetic/ecological risk**: Hatchery-origin fish can hybridize with native populations (destroying local adaptations), compete for food resources, and prey upon native fish — key concerns driving the [[Native Trout Restoration]] debate in the Intermountain West.
- **Case study niche restoration**: The [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] recovery involved habitat restoration, non-native trout removal, and broodstock-based reintroduction, resulting in 21 self-sustaining populations re-established within native range, leading to downlisting from endangered to threatened status.

## Key Findings
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Fisheries program began 123 years ago (per publication date) with the first U.S. Commissioner of Fish and Fisheries, tasked with investigating fishery declines and initiating propagation/restocking programs.
- **Hatchery history milestones**:
  - Bucksport Station, Maine (1871) — first federal hatchery.
  - McCloud River Hatchery, California (1872) — first freshwater station.
  - Leadville National Fish Hatchery, Colorado (1889); Bozeman Fish Technology Center, Montana (1893); Spearfish (D.C. Booth) Hatchery, South Dakota (1898) — still operating.
  - A total of 285 hatcheries/facilities built historically; 83 operated by the Service at time of writing.
- **Historic stocking practices** relied on nationwide species transplantation (Brown Trout/Carp from Germany, Brook Trout east→west, Rainbow Trout/Salmon west→east, Steelhead/Pacific Salmon into Great Lakes/Missouri reservoirs), using trains, wagons, horses — later tanker trucks, boats, aircraft. Success was measured purely by numbers stocked, producing both quality recreational fisheries and significant long-term ecological damage.
- **Trout culture peak**: 1960s–mid-1980s, coinciding with major federal water development and habitat conversion.
- **Consensus view**: Hatcheries are broadly recognized by fishery managers as a poor substitute for habitat protection, though accepted as a necessary compromise when reservoirs replace lost riverine fisheries.
- **Modern reform - National Broodstock Program**:
  - Guided by the "Inland Salmonid Broodstock Management Handbook (712 FW 1)."
  - Developing species/strain-specific plans covering genetics, baseline genetic surveys, broodstock selection, holding, feeding, spawning, incubation, and shipping.
  - Emphasizes feedback loops from receiving hatcheries on egg/fish quality.
  - New **Trout Strain Registry** created to help managers select appropriate species/strains (domestic vs. wild) for specific water bodies.
- **Management-driven production model**: Fish production must follow comprehensive management plans specifying stocking objectives, quality control at time of stocking, and mandatory post-stocking monitoring/evaluation to assess survival, fish quality correlation, and native species impacts.
- **Restoration policy shift**: Region 6 established formal stocking guidelines (late 1970s) restricting trout stocking in native trout waters, reflecting growing recognition of genetic and ecological risks of hatchery introgression.
- **Success case**: [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] recovery program — combining recovery planning, broodstock development, habitat restoration, and non-native trout removal — achieved 21 re-established self-sustaining populations, resulting in downlisting from endangered to threatened, with eventual delisting anticipated.
- **Future direction**: The Service commits to a cooperative, management-led hatchery model for both recreational stocking and native salmonid recovery, requiring improved planning, stricter fish quality standards, and systematic monitoring/evaluation across all programs.
