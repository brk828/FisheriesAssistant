# Effect of Water Hardness on Thermal Resistance of Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmo-gairdneri

## Executive Summary

This study by David E. Craigie (Department of Zoology, [[University Of Toronto]]) tested whether the mineral hardness of rearing water changes the thermal resistance of [[Rainbow Trout]] (*[[Salmo Gairdneri]]*) yearlings. Fish were reared from fertilization in [[Hard Water]] or [[Soft Water]], then exposed to lethal temperatures of 27, 28 and 29 °C in soft and saline test media. Trout reared in soft water survived longer than hard-reared trout in every test condition, and increased salinity of the test medium raised thermal resistance. The author concluded that the water hardness experienced shortly before the lethal exposure matters, while hardness during embryonic development and early growth does not have a significant effect.

## Background & Description

The paper is a reprint from the *Canadian Journal of Zoology* (Vol. 41, 1963), published as No. GLI 18 by the [[Great Lakes Institute]]. It is based on a Master of Arts thesis and was supported by National Research Council grants to Dr. F. E. J. Fry, who suggested the problem.

The study builds on two lines of earlier work:

- **Podoliak (via Phillips):** Trout from high-calcium waters showed a marked increase in metabolic rate when moved to low-calcium water. Phillips suggested this could mean poor survival of hatchery trout from hard water when planted in soft water.
- **Bailey (1959, 1960):** Trout from two hard-water hatcheries planted in soft water showed no significant survival differences, although trout from the softer water survived in slightly greater numbers.

Craigie's stated aims were to compare the thermal resistance of yearling rainbow trout reared in hard and soft water under otherwise similar conditions, and to identify which life stage the ionic environment influences.

The author divided the life history into two arbitrary periods:
- **Development:** fertilization until final absorption of the yolk sac.
- **Growth:** the remainder of the life history.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Source stock and rearing.** Forty-two rainbow trout were collected in May 1959 from the Sand, Sable, Pancake and Harmony rivers at the eastern end of [[Lake Superior]]. Fish were held briefly to ripen in a pond at the [[Tarentorus Provincial Trout Rearing Station]], Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario, then stripped. Eggs and milt were pooled separately and fertilized dry, without added water.

**Water media.** The Ontario Water Resources Commission analyzed four media (Table I of the source):

| Medium | Description | Hardness (p.p.m. as CaCO₃) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Soft: Tarentorus hatchery supply (Coldwater Creek) | 30.5 |
| 2 | Hard: Laboratory well water, [[Laboratory For Experimental Limnology]] | 229.1 |
| 3 | Artificial soft: 90% zeolite-softened medium 2 + 10% medium 2 | 29.8 |
| 4 | Saline: 87.5% medium 2 + 12.5% [[Atlantic Seawater]] | 1053.4 |

Medium 3 was used as the artificial soft test water. Medium 4 reached 2375 p.p.m. chloride and 1325 p.p.m. sodium, which gave the saline test condition. Calcium was 7.8 p.p.m. in soft media versus 54.2 p.p.m. in hard medium 2, and 97 p.p.m. (calculated) in medium 4.

**Egg transfer and cross-rearing.** Hard-water and soft-water eggs were shipped in water ice (about 8 hours) and placed in temperature-controlled hatching troughs at both locations. After yolk-sac absorption, half the fry from each group were moved to the other location and water for growth. This design separated the effects of development-period water from growth-period water. Diet was identical at both sites, no prophylactic treatments were used, and no disease was encountered.

**Acclimation.** In June 1960, the yearlings were moved to [[Maple, Ontario]] and placed in medium 3, made by mixing [[Zeolite Softening]] water with Maple tap water in a 9:1 ratio, following Miller (1944). Fish were acclimated at 1 °C per day to 20 °C, held at 20 ± 0.5 °C for at least 28 days, then held at 20 ± 0.1 °C for 2 days before testing. A subset (one quarter) was hatched and reared at 7.5 °C while the rest were reared at 15 °C; the author reported this thermal history had no effect on thermal resistance.

**Lethal temperature tests.** Three fish from each of the four groups were taken at random and placed in a large, aerated lethal bath controlled to within 0.1 °C. Tests were run at 27, 28 and 29 °C in medium 3 (soft) and medium 4 (saline), with two replicates of each test. The response measured was time to death, analyzed as [[Probit Analysis]] of [[Median Resistance Time]] (LD₅₀), with significance assessed by [[Analysis Of Variance]].

## Key Findings

**1. Growth-period water hardness determines thermal resistance.**
- Soft-reared fish survived longer than hard-reared fish in all tests, with the difference significant at the 95% level or better (ANOVA) in every instance.
- When data were regrouped by development-period water, no significant difference was found. Fish were therefore classed as soft- or hard-reared by growth medium only.

**2. The soft-water advantage varies with temperature and medium.**
- In soft medium, the advantage of soft-rearing increased with lethal temperature.
- In saline medium, the advantage increased as lethal temperature decreased.

**3. Salinity of the test medium raises thermal resistance.**
- Increased salinity increased resistance time in general, consistent with earlier workers cited through [[Kinne]] (1957).
- For hard-reared fish, the salinity benefit grew with temperature. At 27 °C, salinity did not significantly change hard-reared resistance.
- For soft-reared fish, the salinity benefit was significant at all three temperatures and largest at 28 °C.

**4. Median resistance times (Table II, minutes).**

| Lethal temp. | Group | Medium 3 (soft) | Medium 4 (saline) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27 °C | Soft-reared | 423 | 572 |
| 27 °C | Hard-reared | 363 | 357 |
| 28 °C | Soft-reared | 104 | 158 |
| 28 °C | Hard-reared | 83 | 112 |
| 29 °C | Soft-reared | 34 | 70 |
| 29 °C | Hard-reared | 42 | 61 |

*Note on the source data:* the text states soft-reared fish survived longer "in all cases," but at 29 °C in soft medium the hard-reared median (42 min) exceeds the soft-reared median (34 min) in Table II. This discrepancy is in the original and should be checked against the raw data before the table is quoted.

**5. Conclusions as stated by the author.**
- Hardness during embryonic development and early growth does not significantly influence yearling thermal resistance.
- Hardness experienced immediately before lethal exposure does.
- Soft-water trout tolerate thermal stress better than hard-water trout regardless of the salinity of the comparison medium. The size of this advantage depends on the hardness and temperature of the test medium.
- Salinity of the test medium affects thermal resistance, and the size of that effect depends on rearing hardness and lethal temperature.

**6. Practical implication.** The work supports Phillips' concern that hatchery trout reared in hard water may have reduced thermal tolerance after planting in soft water, although the paper itself reports no direct field survival test. Bailey's natural-condition studies, which found no significant survival differences, are cited as a counterpoint.
