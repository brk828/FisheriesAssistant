# Key Findings from a Post-Election Voter Survey Conducted for the National Wildlife Federation

#environmental-policy #public-opinion #survey-research

## Executive Summary

This document reports a post-election telephone poll of voters, conducted for the [[National Wildlife Federation]] by [[Peter D. Hart Research Associates]] from December 1 to 4, 1994. The survey covered 1,201 registered voters who said they voted in the November 8, 1994 U.S. Congressional election. The poll finds that environmental issues were a minor factor in voting choices, but that voters oppose rolling back environmental protections. Majorities favored stronger safe drinking water rules, fair market fees for public lands use, pesticide reduction incentives, and strong Endangered Species Act requirements. Voters were more evenly divided on property rights compensation (takings). The document contains no fish or aquatic ecology data, so the taxonomic fields are marked N/A.

## Background & Description

**Survey design**
- Sponsor: [[National Wildlife Federation]]
- Pollster: [[Peter D. Hart Research Associates]]
- Fieldwork: December 1 to 4, 1994, by telephone
- Sample: 1,201 registered voters who reported voting in the November 8, 1994 general election for U.S. Congress
- Oversamples: 100 interviews each in the [[Agricultural Midwest]] and the [[Rocky Mountain West]], weighted back into the main cross section of 1,000 in their proper population proportion
- Margin of error: ±3.2%

**Voter profile**
- Party registration: 35% Republican, 29% Democrat, 26% registered with another party or unaffiliated
- Reported House vote: 61% Republican candidate, 42% Democratic candidate (as reported; these figures sum to more than 100%)

**Context**
The document is framed around the newly elected members of the [[104th Congress]] and the policy debate over rolling back environmental laws.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses environmental policy and public opinion, not ecosystems. Its subject matter includes:
- Safe drinking water regulation, and the cost burden on smaller communities
- Pesticide pollution of food and water, and subsidies to farmers who reduce chemical use
- Grazing, logging, and mining fees on [[Public Lands]]
- The [[Endangered Species Act]] and the protection of plants and animals
- [[Takings Legislation]] and property owner compensation
- Overall environmental quality ratings given by voters, and their views on the [[Clinton Administration]]'s environmental record

No aquatic species, habitats, or water chemistry measurements are reported.

## Key Findings

**Voting priorities**
- Issues that most affected voters' decisions: economy 26%, crime 24%, health care 21%, taxes 15%
- 11% said they were sending a message about change and the need for better government
- Only 7% mentioned the environment or a specific environmental problem
- Reasons given for the low environmental emphasis: other issues seemed more urgent (85%); environmental issues were not really discussed by the candidates (23%); enough has been done for the environment (8%); the Clinton Administration is doing a good job protecting the environment (10%)

**Views on environmental quality and regulation**
- Rating of local environmental quality: 62% excellent or good (excellent 15%, good 47%)
- Current laws and regulations do not go far enough: 41%; go too far: 18%
- Among Republican candidate voters, 34% say laws do not go far enough versus 25% who say they go too far
- 50% of Democratic candidate voters say regulations do not go far enough
- Groups saying laws do not go far enough: women 48%, ages 18 to 34 65%, minorities 67%, urban dwellers 47%
- About one in five voters, across parties, say existing regulations strike the right balance

**Performance of the Clinton Administration**
- Poor or fair: 46% (fair 31%, poor 15%)
- Positive: 26% (excellent 3%, good 23%)
- Insufficient information to rate: 25%

**Business obligations**
- 46% say environmental laws do not require businesses to do enough to protect the environment
- 20% say the laws place an unfair burden on businesses
- 21% say the laws balance the needs of businesses and the public
- Among employed voters in high- or middle-level professional and executive positions, 49% say the laws do not ask enough of businesses

**Civic engagement**
- 23% personally belong to or contribute to at least one environmental or conservation organization
- 12% support two or more groups

**Policy initiatives (pro-environment position support)**
Support for the pro-environment position ranged from 76% to 56% across five legislative goals. The document reports the following:

- **Safe drinking water:** 76% favor strengthening safe drinking water laws, versus 15% opposed. Support held at two-thirds or more in every demographic group, including suburban (76%), small town (77%), and rural/farming (72%) voters. Among voters who cited the economy (77%) or taxes (76%) as key issues, support was similarly high. About seven in ten Republican candidate voters favor raising standards.
- **Pesticide reduction incentives:** Using existing subsidies to reward farmers who reduce chemical pesticide pollution wins 64% support versus 24% opposed. Rural/farming community residents split 57% to 61% in favor, Agricultural Midwest residents 59% to 80%, and 50% of Republican candidate voters support the argument for pesticide reduction.
- **Public lands use fees:** 63% say Congress should charge fair market fees for mining, logging, and livestock grazing, versus 25% opposed. Mountain West voters favor this 53%, and West Coast voters 59%. Republican voters (60%) and Democratic voters (64%) are similarly supportive.
- **Endangered Species Act:** Voters favor maintaining strong ESA requirements over relaxing them. The document reports the margin as "57% to 82%," which appears to be an OCR or typographical error, so verify against the original. Democratic voters favor strong protections 68% to 21%. Republican voters split at 47% in favor and 41% opposed. Regional support: Northeast 62%, Midwest 63%, West 54%.
- **Takings (property owner compensation):** This is the most closely divided issue. 56% oppose government and taxpayer compensation for property owners whose use of land is restricted by environmental laws, and 36% say owners deserve compensation when constitutional property rights are compromised. Among voters who own their homes, 58% oppose compensation. Republican candidate voters oppose it 53% to 35%, and Democratic candidate voters 60% to 32%.

**Overall conclusion**
The poll concludes that voters do not want environmental protections rolled back, even though economic concerns dominated their vote choices. Voters who said environmental issues were not discussed by candidates were even more likely than average to support the pro-environment position on all five initiatives.
