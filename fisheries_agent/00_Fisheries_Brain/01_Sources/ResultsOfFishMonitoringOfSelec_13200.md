# Results of Fish Monitoring of Selected Waters of the Gila River Basin, 1995-1996

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This report presents results of fish monitoring conducted in the [[Gila River Basin]] during 1995-1996 as required by a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The monitoring program was established to detect transfers of non-native fishes from the [[Lower Colorado River]] via the CAP canal system that could negatively impact four threatened or endangered native species: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Target monitoring reaches included the CAP aqueduct, [[Salt River Project]] canals, [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]], and portions of the [[Salt River]], [[Gila River]], and [[San Pedro River]]. Monitoring was conducted cooperatively by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and Arizona State University using multiple gear types including electrofishing, gill nets, seines, and traps.

## Background & Description

The [[Central Arizona Project]] is a series of aqueducts and pipelines transporting Colorado River water from Lake Havasu to central and southern Arizona. Construction was largely completed by 1993. A 1994 FWS Biological Opinion determined the project would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fish species through potential transfers of non-indigenous aquatic organisms from the Lower Colorado River to Gila River Basin drainages. The Reasonable and Prudent Alternative directed Reclamation to develop and implement baseline studies and long-term monitoring of non-native fish presence and distribution in the CAP aqueduct and selected river and canal reaches.

Target monitoring reaches were stratified by geomorphology or flow characteristics with replicate quantitative sampling stations established. Electrofishing served as the primary gear in streams, supplemented by gill nets, trammel nets, drift nets, hoop nets, minnow traps, seines, dip nets, trot lines, and angling. Canal sampling was more opportunistic, conducted during low-flow or dry-up conditions. The monitoring plan underwent revisions through October 1996, with methodologies evolving as experience accumulated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Gila River Basin contains critical habitat for multiple federally-listed fish species. Native fish assemblages have been severely impacted by habitat modification, flow alteration, and non-native species introductions. The CAP represents a potential vector for additional non-native species transfers, creating ongoing conservation concerns. Natural flow regimes, particularly flood events, appear to play critical roles in native fish persistence and non-native species control.

Monitoring revealed diverse aquatic habitats across target reaches. The [[San Pedro River]] featured sandy-bottomed, braided channels in lower reaches with shallow riffles and pools upstream. The [[Gila River]] between [[Coolidge Dam]] and [[Ashurst-Hayden Dam]] exhibited steep riffles and deep rocky pools in upper stations, transitioning to finer substrates and increased flows downstream. The [[Salt River]] between [[Stewart Mountain Dam]] and [[Granite Reef Dam]] ranged from shallow riffles to deep pools and reservoir-like habitats. Water conductivities reached approximately 3000 µS/cm in uppermost Gila River reaches due to warm spring inputs below Coolidge Dam.

## Key Findings

**Species Richness and New Records:** Monitoring documented 19 total species across all target reaches during 1995-1996. New species records included: [[Black Bullhead]] and [[White Bass]] in the CAP canal; [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Threadfin Shad]] in the Florence-Casa Grande Canal; and [[Black Crappie]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] in the Salt River. [[Grass Carp]], intentionally stocked for weed control since 1990, was documented in the CAP canal for the first time.

**CAP Canal Assemblage Structure:** The upper CAP canal (Hayden-Rhodes and Fannin-McFarland aqueducts) showed relatively low species richness (6-8 species) with [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Striped Bass]] most common. The lower reach (Tucson Aqueduct) contained 13 species total with *Lepomis* species and [[Largemouth Bass]] dominating. [[Threadfin Shad]], previously common, was virtually absent from 1995 collections in the lower canal. Notable absences compared to 1986-1989 surveys included [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Mosquitofish]].

**Gila River Patterns:** Ten species were collected from the Gila River in 1995, with maximum site richness of seven species. [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) numerically dominated the assemblage throughout most of the study reach, but native [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and suckers (*Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*) followed closely, with suckers accounting for majority of biomass. Collections from 1991-1994 showed similar patterns with 13 total species recorded. Only one station (Cochran) was sampled in 1996, yielding eight species including [[Green Sunfish]] not encountered in 1995.

**San Pedro River Dynamics:** Nine species were collected from eight San Pedro River stations in 1995, with maximum site richness of seven. [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Mosquitofish]] were the only species captured at all eight stations. [[Largemouth Bass]] was restricted to the upper reach. Eleven total species were documented in 1996, including range expansion of [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]] in the lower reach and invasion of [[Red Shiner]] into lower reaches. [[Longfin Dace]] was by far numerically dominant, reaching phenomenal densities at lower stations (2184-5505 individuals in 1995).

**Red Shiner-Flood Correlation:** Critical pattern emerged linking [[Red Shiner]] occurrence in the lower San Pedro River with absence of major flood events. Red shiner was absent in 1993 following an 11,000+ cfs flood but reappeared in 1996 after years without significant floods. This pattern correlated with 1990 and 1997 red shiner invasions into [[Aravaipa Creek]]. USGS discharge records showed periods between 1986-1991 and 1992-1996 without flood magnitudes exceeding approximately 1500-3000 cfs, corresponding to red shiner persistence and invasion events.

**Florence-Casa Grande Canal:** Five species were collected in 1995, increasing to 12 species in 1996 (greater richness than upstream Gila River). The 1996 collections included three new canal records: [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Threadfin Shad]]. Seven species were taken above the China Wash electrical fish barrier, 11 below. Collections from 1991-1994 found nine total species. [[Red Shiner]] and [[Mosquitofish]] nearly consistently dominated numerically, with [[Channel Catfish]] comprising large component above the electrical barrier.

**Salt River Assemblage:** The Salt River between Stewart Mountain and Granite Reef dams was among the most species-rich reaches monitored, with 13 species captured in 1995 and 11 in 1996. Native suckers, especially [[Desert Sucker]], were most abundant overall. Age-0 desert sucker was captured only in riffles below Stewart Mountain Dam with impressively high 1995 cohort density. [[Largemouth Bass]] was next most numerous, with sporadic captures of most other species.

**Salt River Project Canals:** Considerably more species were routinely collected from SRP canals during dry-up than from the upstream Salt River source. Electrical barrier reaches contained 13-15 species from the Arizona Canal and 10-13 from the South Canal across both years. Nineteen total species were recorded from 1990-1996 sampling. Native suckers, [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Red Shiner]] were most numerous. [[Rainbow Trout]] was numerically conspicuous above electrical barriers, especially 1991-1994. Native [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) retained important presence in canal system despite rarity in upstream Salt River.

**Electrical Barrier Effectiveness:** [[Grass Carp]] was detected above the South Canal electrical barrier in 1995 and above the Arizona Canal barrier in 1996, indicating barrier failures. Smith-Root reports documented power failure on December 23, 1993 (backup generator failed) and lightning damage September 2-3, 1994 on South Canal. Discussions with Smith-Root personnel revealed fishes can potentially ascend weirs during shallow water conditions (<8 cm depth) present during dry-up operations. The author observed several thousand [[Tilapia]] descend over South Canal weir following electrical cutoff in 1997 due to incomplete headgate closure, and observed a small [[Desert Sucker]] transgress the barrier upstream.

**Assemblage Structure Variability:** Considerable instability in assemblage structure across years was evident, especially in canals. South Canal "censuses" above electrical barriers showed shifts in numerical dominance from red shiner and tilapia (1990), to suckers/rainbow trout/channel catfish (1991), to suckers/red shiner/channel catfish (1992), to common carp/desert sucker (1993), to red shiner/suckers/channel catfish (1995), to desert sucker/channel catfish/tilapia (1996). Similar dramatic shifts occurred in Arizona Canal assemblages.

**Monitoring Implementation Issues:** Several deficiencies in fully implementing the monitoring plan were noted, including: occasional omissions of electrofishing settings, conductance and temperature measurements; incomplete field notes and station maps; inadequate voucher specimen preservation; lack of complete standardization of sampling methodologies; delays in quality control/quality assurance procedures; and absence of a management action plan. Right-of-entry issues prevented sampling of some stations. Equipment failures (pulsator burnout in high conductivity water, shocker unavailability) limited some collections. The 1996 sampling missed several target reaches due to scheduling conflicts and resource limitations.

**Conservation Implications:** The most serious deficiency identified was failure to develop a management action plan defining threshold criteria for species richness, distribution, and assemblage structure indices, and establishing management action contingencies. The report emphasizes that monitoring serves as an early warning system, but has limited utility without plans to remedy new species incursions or detrimental community changes. Management agencies must confront this issue directly if conservation and recovery of native fishes in the Gila River Basin is to proceed, as native fish populations will continue to diminish in the face of new non-indigenous forms without active intervention.
