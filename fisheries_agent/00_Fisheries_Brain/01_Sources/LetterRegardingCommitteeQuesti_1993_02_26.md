## Executive Summary

This document is a formal letter from the National Research Council's **Committee to Review the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]]** (chaired by William M. Lewis, Jr.) to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, raising detailed scientific and technical questions on the preliminary draft Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) for **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]** operations (January 1993). While the letter spans hydrology, sediment transport, power economics, and recreation, it contains substantive critiques of the EIS's treatment of fish and aquatic ecosystem science in the **[[Colorado River]]** below Glen Canyon Dam and within **[[Lake Powell]]**. Key concerns include an overemphasis on **[[Rainbow Trout]]** at the expense of native and endangered fish species, inconsistent species lists, unclear or contradictory temperature data, unexplained fish-kill mechanisms, and insufficiently supported claims linking flow regimes to fish and backwater habitat quality.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphological descriptions or measurements of fish. However, it references taxonomic and nomenclatural issues relevant to species identity:

- The Committee requests review of nomenclature in Chapter III (p. 82) against the **[[American Fisheries Society]]** 1990 naming guidelines, implying inconsistencies in scientific names used in the draft EIS.
- Specific mention of **[[Flathead Catfish]]** (*Pylodictis olivaris*) as a species meriting inclusion — flagged as a "potentially important predator in this system," but seemingly omitted or underrepresented in the draft's species treatment.
- Figure FSH-2b (p. III-71) is noted as containing 5 plotted lines but legends for only 3, indicating unidentified taxa or data series in the fish figures.
- The species list on p. III-82 is flagged as inconsistent with species mentioned in the body text — several fish species discussed are not included in the formal list.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key habitat and niche-related observations raised by the Committee:

- **Backwater habitats**: The Committee questions the ecological role of low-velocity areas formed by low-elevation sandbars (p. III-61) as important fish habitat, and requests clarification on how [[Backwaters]] are affected by fluctuating discharge (p. IV-75). This is a recurring theme — backwaters are treated as critical nursery/rearing habitat potentially sensitive to flow fluctuation.
- **Temperature regimes**: Figure FSH-3 (pp. III-74–75) reports a temperature range of 58–70°F, which the Committee finds inconsistent with the expectation that main-channel waters remain near 50°F — raising the question of whether this range reflects backwater rather than main-stem conditions (relevant to [[Thermal Habitat Suitability]] for trout and native fish).
- **Predator-prey structure**: Flagged omission of flathead catfish as a "potentially important predator" suggests the EIS underrepresents the trophic complexity of the post-dam fish community.
- **Lake Powell limnology**: The Committee explicitly challenges the claim that the limnological features of [[Lake Powell]] are "well described," suggesting the reservoir ecology (relevant to downstream fish energetics and sediment/nutrient inputs) is inadequately characterized.
- **Sediment dynamics and habitat**: Extensive sediment budget discussion (pp. III-42, III-46, III-61–66) ties directly to physical habitat structure (sandbars, backwaters) that underlies fish habitat availability, though the Committee notes these conclusions rest heavily on modeling rather than empirical, long-term data.
- **Flow regime vs. native fish adaptation**: In the section on the Preferred Alternative, the Committee argues that steady flows would provide "a hydrologic regime that is much closer to the hydrologic regime to which the **[[Native Fishes]]** are adapted" — a direct ecological niche argument favoring restoration of natural flow variability patterns over dam-induced fluctuation patterns.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomic bias in reporting**: The EIS disproportionately emphasizes **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (a non-native sport fish) over **[[Endangered Fishes]]** and native fish species; the Committee explicitly requests expanded coverage of native and endangered fishes, including unpublished data from Arizona State University and Northern Arizona University working groups.
- **Unexplained fish mortality mechanism**: The Committee questions a claim on p. III-79 that "very high dissolved oxygen" can kill stranded adult fish — a physiologically counterintuitive statement (typically low dissolved oxygen, not high, causes stranding mortality) that the EIS does not adequately explain.
- **Trout life history gaps**: The Committee notes the absence of good life-history studies for trout in the Glen Canyon/Grand Canyon system (p. III-81) and asks for incorporation of general trout literature regarding thermal optima, life-stage-specific habitat/food requirements, and population dynamics regulation.
- **Confounded causality on fishery decline**: The Committee challenges the EIS's implied link between declining trout fishery quality and the research flows, noting that fishery decline reportedly began *before* the research flows were implemented — a potential confound in attributing cause.
- **Fluctuating flow vs. backwater/fish benefit claims unsupported**: The draft EIS's assertions that fluctuating flows benefit backwaters (p. IV-32) and that certain flow changes benefit trout (p. IV-63) are flagged as insufficiently defended with evidence.
- **Manipulability of trout resource**: The Committee suggests the EIS should stress that trout abundance (particularly large, catchable trout) is a resource that can be largely controlled via stocking, reframing trout management as a management variable rather than a strictly flow-dependent outcome.
- **Support for steady, natural-flow-like regimes**: In critiquing the tentatively preferred "low fluctuating flows" alternative, the Committee lists ecological advantages of steady/seasonally-adjusted flows over fluctuating flows, including: steadier backwater temperatures and hydrology, reduced stranding of trout, reduced desiccation/freezing of aquatic vegetation, and closer alignment with the natural hydrologic regime to which native fishes are adapted — directly linking flow management policy to [[Native Fish Conservation]] outcomes.
- **Sediment-habitat linkage uncertainty**: Conclusions on sediment dynamics (which underlie physical fish habitat such as sandbars and backwaters) are based on modeling with a short empirical record, raising concern about the validity of long-term predictions relevant to habitat persistence.
