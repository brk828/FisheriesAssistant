# Possible Effects of Water Diversions on Fish Distribution in British Columbia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by C. C. Lindsey, of the [[British Columbia Game Commission]] and the [[Institute of Fisheries]] at the University of British Columbia, summarizes the known distributions of freshwater fishes across British Columbia mainland drainages and assesses how proposed hydroelectric water diversions could alter them. The [[Continental Divide]] marks a sharp faunal break. Diversions that cross the divide, particularly the [[Liard-Stikine Diversion]], the [[Yukon-Taku Diversion]] and the [[Peace-Fraser Diversion]], could introduce [[Northern Pike]], which would threaten [[Pacific Salmon]] and trout and could spread the [[Triaenophorus Tapeworm]]. Diversions between Pacific watersheds are judged unlikely to change fish distribution much. The author concludes that changes resulting from transbasin diversions are likely to be economically harmful.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses hydroelectric schemes in which interior headwaters would be diverted toward the coast. One scheme near Kitimat had already been completed, and waters had so far been moved only between Pacific slope systems. Newer proposals would move water from Mackenzie (Arctic Ocean) or Yukon (Bering Sea) headwaters into Pacific watersheds. The scope is limited to fish introductions caused by diversions; direct dam effects such as blocked spawning runs are excluded.

Distribution data came from published records and recent collections by [[British Columbia Game Commission]] personnel, deposited at the [[Institute of Fisheries]]. Table I lists 68 species across principal drainages, and the Appendix lists locality-specific records by river system, with sampling coordinates and species numbers. Five species were recorded in the province for the first time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage structure.** Major Pacific slope rivers run from south to north: [[Columbia River]], [[Fraser River]], [[Skeena River]], [[Nass River]], [[Stikine River]] and [[Taku River]]. Interior drainages include the [[Mackenzie River]] system (Peace and Liard) and the [[Yukon River]].

**Faunal break.** Of the 57 species on the Pacific slope in B.C., 35 do not occur in the Mackenzie or Yukon drainages within the province. Of the 33 species in those interior areas, 11 occur nowhere on the Pacific slope. Eighteen species occur in every major river system from the Columbia north to the Taku. Most Pacific slope species appear to have spread north from the Columbia after the last ice sheet.

**Headwater capture.** Some Mackenzie and Yukon species, including [[Arctic Grayling]] and [[Round Whitefish]], reached northern coastal drainages through headwater capture. The same process probably explains [[Slimy Sculpin]] in the Stikine.

**Distribution of key taxa.** Five Pacific salmon species occur in all coastal drainages but are absent from the Mackenzie system in B.C. [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Chum Salmon]] also reach the Yukon headwaters. Northern pike are found in the Yukon and Liard systems and up the Peace River to Hudson Hope. On the Pacific slope they occur only in the Taku headwaters. [[Northern Squawfish]], a piscivorous minnow, is absent from Pacific drainages north of the Skeena, so the Nass and Stikine currently lack both pike and squawfish.

**Parasite cycle.** Pike act as the definitive host of *Triaenophorus crassus*. Other fish act as intermediate hosts, and cysts in their flesh make the fish unmarketable. In the Bristol Bay area of Alaska, 37% of 26 sockeye smolts from Lake Aleknagik carried encysted larvae, while 291 smolts from Skeena, Fraser and Columbia lakes had none. Pike or muskellunge were present in every infested area.

## Key Findings

- **Liard-Stikine Diversion:** This would probably introduce several species, including trout, into the upper Liard, and several others, including northern pike, into the Stikine. Only 7 of the 33 species listed for the Stikine or Liard occur in both systems. Pike are already abundant in [[Dease Lake]]. Rainbow trout occupy the Tanzilla River and would probably be transferred. Pacific salmon in the Stikine could be harmed by pike and infected with *Triaenophorus*.
- **Yukon-Taku Diversion (Frobisher Scheme):** This one-way diversion would allow fish to pass from the Yukon into the Taku. Eight Yukon species are absent from the Taku, including inconnu and burbot, which are piscivores. Six species are shared. Pike already occupy the Paddys Lake–Taysen Lake system, but impassable falls on the Nakina River separate them from salmon. The diversion could spread pike more widely through the Taku system.
- **Peace-Fraser Diversion:** This would probably introduce [[Arctic Grayling]] into the Fraser. Pike might also enter, depending on where the dam sits relative to their upstream limit at Hudson Hope. Pike and [[Yellow Walleye]] could harm the Fraser's major rainbow trout sport fishery and its salmon. Some sport fisheries could be created by the new species.
- **Skeena-Fraser Diversion (Babine–Stuart):** Only 3 species (bridgelip sucker, leopard dace, slimy sculpin) could move from the Fraser into the Skeena, and the author considers this movement unlikely. Of 29 species common to both systems, none would be new to the Fraser.
- **Columbia-Fraser Diversion:** A one-way transfer from the Columbia into the Fraser would probably not change distribution much. [[Torrent Sculpin]] and [[Pygmy Whitefish]] would probably spread further into the Fraser system.
- **Chilko Lake Diversion:** This westward diversion into the Southgate or Homathko rivers would probably cause little change, since all 23 recorded coastal species also occur in the Fraser and Skeena.
- **Downstream spread:** Fish that live above a turbine are likely to spread downstream. Some survive turbine passage, especially the very young. Modifying turbines to kill all fish, eggs and larvae would be difficult.
- **Overall conclusion:** Transbasin diversions across the Continental Divide are virtually certain to change fish distribution. The net effect is likely to be an economic loss rather than a gain. Pacific-slope exchanges are unlikely to cause marked changes.
