# Vital Statistics of the Fish Population of Gordy Lake, Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 study (published 1953 in the [[Transactions of the American Fisheries Society]]) by Shelby D. Gerking of Indiana University quantifies the fishing yield, population size, standing crop, and mortality of the fish community of [[Gordy Lake]], a 28.5-acre lake in northern Indiana. The work was conducted in summer 1950 using [[Creel Census]] records and [[Mark-And-Recapture]] trapping, with several independent [[Population Estimation]] methods used for cross-checking. [[Bluegill]] dominated the catch and standing crop. The lake was judged about average for Indiana lakes in yield and standing crop, but above average in cane-pole catch rate. Exploitation rates for bluegill, [[Redear Sunfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] were high relative to earlier Indiana records. The paper argues that carefully designed fish population studies can yield reliable estimates for management, and it supports the conclusion that sport fishing had not depleted panfish populations.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The work was part of the [[Indiana Lake And Stream Survey]], sponsored by Indiana University and the Indiana Department of Conservation, Division of Fish and Game. It extended earlier studies of northern Indiana lakes by Ricker (1942, 1945a) and Gerking (1950). The study was made two years after Indiana had removed seasonal limits on panfish fishing, and the author found no evidence that panfish populations had declined as fishing pressure increased.

**Lake description.** Gordy Lake is oval, with a small inlet on the northeastern shore and outlet on the northwestern shore. Both are heavily vegetated and carry little flow except after exceptional rain. The lake covers 28.5 acres with a maximum depth of 35 feet. In 1950 a [[Thermocline]] formed between 13 and 26 feet. The narrow, heavily vegetated [[Littoral Zone]] drops off steeply within about 10–30 feet of shore. The lake is one of nine in the [[Indian Village Chain]], which drains northwestward into [[Lake Wawasee]]. Access was limited to two points (a Scout Camp and a farmer's boat rental), so fewer than 2 percent of anglers were missed during the summer.

**Earlier records.** [[Hile]] (1931) sampled Gordy Lake fish for growth study and took ciscoes in gill nets, which was unusual for so shallow a lake. Gill netting by the survey in 1951 took 10 ciscoes, and one was taken in 1952.

**Methodological stance.** The author emphasizes that fish population studies must be conducted with laboratory-like care, because capture, marking, handling, and post-release behavior are uncontrolled. He argues that estimates can be accurate enough for present fishery problems, but that a short lake visit is not sufficient for reliable data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishing regime.** Panfish could be fished year-round in Indiana, so angling overlapped with the marking period. Cane-pole fishing was the principal method, with fly fishing and plug casting contributing less. Winter fishing was reported as very light, and bass were rarely caught in winter.

**Fish community.** Bluegills and redear sunfish were the most abundant species in the catch. Minor species included warmouth, green sunfish, and pumpkinseed (Centrarchidae), plus largemouth bass, yellow perch, black crappie, bullheads, bowfin, golden shiners, and ciscoes. The redear sunfish had been introduced through hatchery stocking beginning in 1928 and was established in most northern lakes, where it occupied a subordinate position to bluegill.

**Population dynamics.** Length distributions of successive age groups overlapped widely. Recruitment of younger fish into the legal size range over the summer was a significant source of potential bias. The redear sunfish showed evidence of variable recruitment, with almost no age-V fish present.

**Habitat shifts.** Trap data indicated that larger redear sunfish moved out of shallow water during July, which reduced their catchability. The author therefore excluded late-trapping data from some analyses.

## Key Findings

**Creel census and yield (June 2 to September 4, 1950)**
- Total catch by all methods: **4,012 fish weighing 722.5 lb**.
- Total yield: **25.4 lb per acre** of water surface, of which **81.5%** was bluegill. The author describes this yield as about average for Indiana lakes.
- Cane-pole catch rate: **15.2 fish (2.72 lb) per 10 pole-hours**, roughly 4 fish per 10 pole-hours better than the average for Indiana lakes reported to that time. Fly fishing (5.7 fish per 10 pole-hours) and plug casting (1.7 fish per 10 pole-hours) were poor.
- Bluegill catch was about 13 times the redear sunfish catch in numbers and about 12 times in weight for equal effort. Cane-pole effort totaled 2,538 pole-hours.
- Bluegill catch per unit effort peaked between mid-July and August 1 (20.2 per 10 pole-hours), and otherwise ranged from 8.7 to 12.9.
- Fifty-five largemouth bass (48.9 lb) were caught, mostly by plug casters.

**Population estimates (legal size: 5 in. total length for panfish, 10 in. for bass)**
- **Bluegill:** about **7,900** legal fish, totaling **1,320 lb**. Estimates used Petersen, Schnabel (fisherman-based), and age-group methods, which agreed well with one another. Trap-based Schnabel estimates could not be made for unexplained reasons.
- **Redear sunfish:** about **600** legal fish, weighing **105 lb**. Age-III legal fish were estimated at about 250, age IV at about 280, and age V+ at about 70.
- **Age-III redear (all sizes):** Schnabel estimate **435** from 143 recaptures (95% confidence limits 390–500), and DeLury estimate **566**. The DeLury estimate was 30% higher, though the two methods agreed reasonably well. The author concluded that the age-III population was about 450–500 fish.
- **Largemouth bass:** legal-sized population estimated at **100–300** fish (88–270 lb). The author stated that the population "probably did not exceed 300 and may have been as low as 100." Only 20 bass were marked, and 12 of them were legal.
- **Warmouth, green sunfish, pumpkinseed:** Schnabel estimates of 583, 295, and 162 fish, respectively. Legal-sized percentages were 59%, 69%, and 14%. Pumpkinseed had the smallest share of legal-sized fish.
- **Total standing crop:** about **78 lb per acre** for all species. Legal-sized fish of six species accounted for about 56–61 lb per acre, with bluegill contributing about 79%.

**Mortality and exploitation (annual rates)**
- **Bluegill:** total annual mortality **82%**, exploitation **35%**, and natural death expectation **47%**. This total was calculated from age data for ages IV and older using the [[Jackson Method]].
- **Redear sunfish:** total annual mortality **about 69%** (average range 60–70%), exploitation **29%**, and natural death expectation **40%**. The author notes the redear sample showed variable recruitment. He also confirms Ricker's (1945a) finding that redears survive better than bluegills.
- **Largemouth bass:** total annual mortality **60%**, exploitation **36%** (about 35.7% summer rate), and natural death expectation **24%**. The exploitation rate was much higher than the 12% reported for Shoe Lake (Ricker 1945a) and 17% for Oliver Lake (Gerking 1950). The author notes that local residents consider Gordy Lake a good bass lake, which may support the estimate.
- **Minor species:** exploitation was estimated at 2.2% for green sunfish and 11.0% for pumpkinseed. No marked warmouth were found in the fishermen's catch, so their exploitation rate was judged very low.

**Appraisal and conclusions**
- Gordy Lake is about average among studied Indiana lakes for fishing success, yield, and productivity, and also in comparison with the [[Rounsefell Summary]] of standing crop statistics. The standing crop (about 78 lb per acre) and yield (25.4 lb per acre) both matched Rounsefell's predicted values.
- Bluegill standing crop of 46 lb per acre sits about midway in the range of six earlier estimates from three Indiana lakes (1 to 102 lb per acre).
- Exploitation rates of 29% for redear and 35% for bluegill are approaching the highest recorded in the author's lakes. The author attributes this partly to increased fishing pressure nationwide since World War II.
- The author concludes that the Schnabel and Petersen methods agree well in Indiana studies, and that DeLury's method can work where whole-population trapping is achieved.
