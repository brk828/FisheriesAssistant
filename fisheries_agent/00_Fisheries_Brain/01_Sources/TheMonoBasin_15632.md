# The Mono Basin: Pleistocene Geology and Ecology

#geology #paleoecology #Pleistocene #Great-Basin #Lake-Russell

## Executive Summary

This 1967 report by George L. Batchelder provides a comprehensive synthesis of the Pleistocene geology and ecology of the [[Mono Basin]], California. The basin offers a unique opportunity to study synchronous glaciation and lacustrine fluctuations during the Quaternary. The document describes multiple glacial advances (McGee, Sherwin, Tahoe, Tioga) and the expansion of pluvial [[Lake Russell]] to an estimated maximum surface of 267 square miles. The fortuitous latitude (38°00'N) and relief (6,300–12,000 feet elevation) create an ecotone between the semi-arid [[Nevadan Province]] and the humid montane [[Sierra Nevada Province]], supporting diverse flora and fauna. Absolute radiometric dates establish chronology: McGee Till <2.6 million years, Sherwin Till >700,000 years, Tahoe Till 60,000–90,000 BP, and Tioga Till 9,800±800 BP. Recent rhyolite domes (6,000–10,000 years) occur on the lake's south shore. Climatic variations involving decreased evaporation and increased precipitation, driven by southward displacement of polar easterlies, caused pluvial lake expansions coincident with glacial maxima.

## Background & Description

The [[Mono Basin]] lies at the western edge of the [[Great Basin]], between the [[Sierra Nevada]] and [[Rocky Mountains]]. Unlike most of the 150+ closed basins in the Basin and Range Province, the Mono Basin's proximity to Sierra peaks allowed valley glaciers to reach the shore of pluvial [[Lake Russell]], leaving traceable moraines that were periodically modified by lacustrine advances and retreats.

**Historical Context**: The first scientific investigation occurred in 1863–1864 when the California Geological Survey explored the area. [[I.C. Russell]]'s definitive 1889 monograph described Pleistocene glaciation and the synchronous expansion of the water body during pluvial times. [[Blackwelder]] (1931) identified and named four distinct glaciations: McGee, Sherwin, Tahoe, and Tioga, correlating them with midwestern glacial episodes (Nebraskan, Kansan, Iowan, and late Wisconsin). [[Sharp and Birman]] (1963) added evidence for Mono Basin glaciation (possibly Illinoian) and Tenaya glaciation (intermediate Wisconsin).

**Physical Setting**: [[Putnam]] (1950) described four sub-provinces: the Sierra Nevada, morainal belt, lake plains, and [[Mono-Inyo Craters]]. The Sierra flanks rise 6,000 feet above the lake plain without intervening foothills. The lake plains, most extensively developed at the west end of [[Mono Lake]], preserve concentric gravel ridges 75–100 feet wide marking major lake level fluctuations. The Mono-Inyo Craters complex consists of a 19-mile series of rhyolitic domes and obsidian flows extending south from Lake Russell's shore.

**Glacial Chronology**:
- **McGee Till** (Nebraskan equivalent): <2.6 million years; scattered on ridges 3,000–3,330 feet above the plain at [[Mt. Morrison]]
- **Sherwin Till** (Kansan equivalent): >700,000 years; type area at Sherwin Hill northwest of Bishop; overlain by [[Bishop Tuff]] dated at 700,000 years
- **Mono Basin Till** (Illinoian equivalent): poorly established; laterals at oblique angles to younger Tahoe forms in [[Sawmill Canyon]]
- **Tahoe Till** (early Wisconsin): 60,000–90,000 BP; massive ridges several hundred feet high; represented greatest extent of glaciers into basin and highest evident lake level
- **Tenaya Till** (middle Wisconsin): small laterals behind Tahoe moraines but in front of Tioga terminals at Leevining, Rush, and Pine Creeks
- **Tioga Till** (late Wisconsin): 9,800±800 BP; average 2,000 boulders per acre (vs. 70 for Tahoe)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biotic Provinces**: The basin straddles two major biotic provinces, creating a rich ecotone. The [[Nevadan Province]] (semi-arid Great Basin) is represented by 8 genera in four families of low shrubs and small trees. The [[Sierra Nevada Province]] (relatively humid montane) contains 20 genera in ten families.

**Plant Communities** (from high to low elevation):
1. **Alpine** (>10,500 feet): Above timberline; [[Timberline Bluegrass]] (*Poa rupicola*), [[Lupine]] (*Lupinus*), [[Alpine Willow]] (*Salix petrophylia*), [[Whitebark Pine]] (*Pinus albicaulis*), [[Lodgepole Pine]] (*P. murrayana*), [[Mountain Hemlock]] (*Tsuga mertensiana*)
2. **Sub-Alpine** (9,500–10,500 feet): Patches of conifers with open meadows; [[Wild Buckwheat]] (*Eriogonum incanum*), [[Rothrock Sagebrush]] (*Artemisia rothrockii*)
3. **Jeffrey Pine Forest** (7,000–9,500 feet): Dominated by [[Jeffrey Pine]] (*Pinus jeffreyi*); [[Mountain Big Sagebrush]] (*Artemisia tridentata*), [[Antelope Bitterbrush]] (*Purshia tridentata*), [[White Fir]] (*Abies concolor*), [[Quaking Aspen]] (*Populus tremuloides*)
4. **Pinyon-Juniper Woodland** (variable, 4,000–8,000+ feet): [[Big Sagebrush]], [[Black Sagebrush]] (*A. nova*), [[Single-leaf Pinyon]] (*Pinus monophylla*), [[Utah Juniper]] (*Juniperus osteosperma*)
5. **Sagebrush Scrub** (lowest): Covers >250,000 square miles across western states; isolated Big Sagebrush bushes and bunch grasses

**Vertebrate Fauna**: The document provides extensive lists (Table 2) including:
- **Fish**: 11 species including [[Cut-throat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*), [[Lahontan Tui Chub]] (*Siphateles bicolor*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- **Amphibians**: 4 species including [[Great Basin Spade Foot Toad]] (*Scaphiopus intermontanus*), [[Western Toad]] (*Bufo boreas*)
- **Reptiles**: 18 species including [[Long-nosed Leopard Lizard]] (*Crotaphytus wislizenii*), [[Great Basin Rattlesnake]] (*Crotalus viridis lutosus*), [[California King Snake]] (*Lampropeltis getulus californiae*)
- **Birds**: 50+ species including [[Golden Eagle]] (*Aquila chrysaetos*), [[California Gull]] (*Larus californicus*), [[Mountain Quail]] (*Oreortyx picta*), [[Pinyon Jay]] (*Gymnorhinus cyanocephalus*)
- **Mammals**: 40+ species including [[Yellow-bellied Marmot]] (*Marmota flaviventris*), [[Belding Ground Squirrel]] (*Cytellus beldingi*), [[Mule Deer]] (*Odocoileus hemionus*), [[Mountain Lion]] (*Felis concolor*)

**Anthropological Significance**: The [[Northern Paiutes]] of the region were nomadic hunters and gatherers highly sensitive to biotic variation. They utilized over 300 plants for medicinal use, gathered brine shrimp larvae (*Artemia*) from [[Mono Lake]] (calling themselves "Kutsavidukada" or "Kutsavi eaters"), and depended on pinyon nut harvests. The ecotone provided diverse resources but also required adaptation to variable conditions.

## Key Findings

**Evidence for Lake Fluctuations**:

1. **Lake Sediments**: [[Paoha Island]] is covered with 200–300 feet of white lacustrine sediments with a wave-cut terrace 12 feet below the 1889 lake level. [[Rush Creek]] cut through its pluvial delta 160 feet to canyon floor, exposing 60% gray silt from granitic rocks and 40% iron-stained sand and dark gravel. [[Negit Island]] lacks lacustrine veneer, indicating post-Tahoe extrusion.

2. **Shorelines and Deltas**: [[Putnam]] (1949) identified an indistinct terrace at 7,170–7,180 feet elevation representing maximum Lake Russell at height of Tahoe glaciation. A more distinct bench at 7,070 feet correlates with Tioga glaciation. About 30 recessional benches below Tioga maximum occur in three distinct groups:
   - Older group (7,070–6,875 feet): Close-spaced narrow benches in Sierra bedrock
   - Middle group (6,830–6,750 feet): Broader terraces on delta surfaces indicating longer stability periods
   - Youngest group (6,730–6,415 feet): Close-treaded benches of intermediate width

3. **Overflow**: [[Russell]] (1889) initially suggested pre-pluvial discharge through [[Aurora Valley]] into [[East Walker River]] of the [[Lahontan]] drainage, but calculated the highest shoreline to be 100 feet below the divide. [[Putnam]] (1949) saw evidence of overflow through the divide north of [[Cowtrack Mountain]] through [[Adobe Meadows]] into [[Adobe Valley]] at 7,150 feet during Tahoe maximum. This would connect Lake Russell to a chain of pluvial lakes extending to [[Lake Manly]] in [[Death Valley]]. The presence of the minnow *Siphateles* throughout this system but absent from the [[Colorado River]] system supports this connection.

4. **Tufa Towers**: Masses of precipitated calcium carbonate bordering the south shore represent biochemical deposition by algal cells around formerly submerged lake springs, indicating continuing lake fluctuation. Since [[Los Angeles]] began diverting tributaries, the lake level has gradually dropped; estimates suggest the lake could disappear within 14 years (as of 1967).

**Tectonic Activity**: Evidence of continuing activity includes:
- Fresh fault scarps (one at ancient beach 680 feet above lake; 50-foot scarp across Tahoe moraine at [[Lundy Canyon]])
- High-temperature springs associated with tufa towers and on Paoha Island
- Disturbances in lake clays caused by slippage along bedding planes
- Possible tilting of shorelines (though [[Lajoie]]'s 1967 measurements were frustrated by inability to confidently correlate shorelines over long distances)

**Volcanic Activity**: The [[Mono-Inyo Craters]] complex shows Recent activity (6,000–10,000 years, [[Dalrymple]] 1967). [[Panum Crater]] extends below highest beach but shows no wave marks or lacustrine deposits, indicating post-pluvial formation. At least 10–15 craters formed in or around the lake since its last expansion. [[Paoha Island]] has a Recent lava flow on its northern shore without pumice veneer. Black [[Negit Island]] also developed post-Tahoe.

**Climatic Causes of Pluvial Fluctuations**: The document argues against glacial meltwater as the primary cause, noting that during Tahoe maximum, [[Lake Lahontan]] drainage contained 70 cubic miles of ice (equivalent to 64 cubic miles of water) while the lake contained up to 300 cubic miles of water. Various estimates of necessary climatic changes include:
- [[Russell]] (1885): 20-inch rainfall increase with same temperature
- [[Antevs]] (1952): 7–9°F (2.5–3.0°C) temperature depression plus 29-inch precipitation increase
- [[Broeker and Orr]] (1958): 8-inch rainfall increase with 9°F temperature drop
- [[Snyder and Langbein]] (1962): Decrease of evaporation from 44 to 31 inches with modern temperatures

**Global Atmospheric Mechanism**: During pluvial periods, higher pressures developed over continental ice caps, forcing the polar front southward from 60° to 45° latitude. This brought the belt of eastward-moving cyclonic storms (Polar Easterlies) over the [[Great Basin]], causing increased cloudiness, precipitation, and decreased temperature and evaporation. This formed pluvial lakes throughout closed basins and caused coincidental glacier expansion. When the polar front shifted northward, temperature increased and precipitation decreased, returning the area to semi-arid/arid climate. [[Galloway]] (1965) controversially suggested 20% reduction in evaporation and precipitation during last glacial period, meaning pluvial period occurred during post-pluvial temperature optimum.

**Absolute Dates** (Table 3):
- Mono Craters rhyolite: 6,000–10,000 years
- Tioga Till: 9,800±800 BP
- Tahoe Till: 60,000–90,000 BP (overrides basalt at Sawmill Canyon)
- Mammoth Mountain till: <370,000 years (possibly early Tahoe)
- Sherwin Till: >700,000 years (overlain by Bishop Tuff)
- McGee Till: <2.6 million years (rests on basalt)

**Future Research**: Pending work by [[Al-Rawi]] and [[Lajoie]] on definitive evaluation of basin history, plus C-14 dates of organic lake sediments below Mono or Inyo Craters ash layers in [[Adobe Valley]], will add useful data to the pattern of Quaternary events.

**Conservation Concern**: The document notes that water diversions by Los Angeles threaten to create an "artificial playa as a momento to 'progress'" within 14 years, highlighting early recognition of anthropogenic impacts on this unique ecosystem.
