# Grazing Fishes as Components of North American Stream Ecosystems: Effects of Campostoma anomalum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, drawn from a 1987 volume on stream fish ecology, argues that algivorous (algae-grazing) fishes have been largely overlooked by stream ecologists. The authors focus on the widespread grazing minnow [[Campostoma Anomalum]] (Central Stoneroller), which they show can strongly control the distribution and standing crop of attached algae ([[Periphyton]]) in North American streams. Studies in [[Brier Creek]], Oklahoma, show that grazing minnows dominate pools lacked predators, while [[Largemouth Bass]] suppress minnow use of habitat and thereby allow algae to accumulate. Surveys of [[Ozark Uplift]] and [[Ouachita Mountains]] streams revealed a different pattern, in which predators and grazers were not spatially complementary. The authors conclude that grazing fishes may drive basic stream processes, including nutrient cycling and primary production, and call for further study.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** [[Campostoma Anomalum]] is a large minnow reaching about 230 mm standard length (SL). It is the most abundant and widespread algivorous minnow in eastern and central North America, and it can dominate fish communities of small and medium streams by number and biomass.
- **Congeners:** [[Campostoma Oligolepis]] occurs in Midwestern uplands, the Tennessee River and Mobile Bay drainages. [[Campostoma Pauciradii]] occurs in eastern Gulf Coast drainages.
- **Other grazers:** At least 42 North American stream fish species are largely herbivorous, and 24 feed mainly by grazing attached algae (Lee et al., 1980). Others mentioned include several Notropis and Phoxinus minnows, and the catostomid Catostomus santannae of southern California.
- **Feeding modes documented for Campostoma:**
  - *Nipping:* small, rapid bites, used mainly in Brier Creek where detritus and epiphytes are common.
  - *Swiping:* vigorous sideways head strikes that detach blue-green and diatom felts, leaving paired grazing scars; the dominant mode in Ozark streams.
  - *Shoveling:* pushing the lower jaw along smooth rock while swimming, leaving trails several cm long.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Brier Creek (south-central Oklahoma):** A small prairie-margin stream of the Red River drainage. A 1-km reach contains 14 pools. Four "Campostoma pools" held schools of 50-400 minnows and lacked large predators, and were almost devoid of algae. Nine "bass pools" held 3-8 large bass (>70 mm SL) and had prominent filamentous green algae (Spirogyra, Rhizoclonium).
- **Ozark and Ouachita streams:** Campostoma were larger (often 125-150 mm TL, with some 160-175 mm), more abundant, and foraged more freely than in Brier Creek, where individuals over 150 mm TL are very rare and the largest collected was about 125 mm TL.
- **Hydrology:** Riffles between Brier Creek pools are shallow (2-4 cm), restricting bass movement at normal flow. Ozark riffles are deeper (10-15 cm), allowing both bass and minnows to move between pools.
- **Canopy effects:** In Tyner Creek, Campostoma were absent from the four most densely shaded pools, where substrates were nearly devoid of algae.
- **Thermal tolerance:** Schools feed at temperatures as low as 7°C.

## Key Findings

1. **Grazing controls algae in Brier Creek.** Substrates moved into a Campostoma pool were rapidly grazed, and algal ash-free dry weight fell to under 25% of its initial value within 24 h. Campostoma pools had markedly lower standing crops than pools without minnows during normal low flow. Flood scour, however, can decouple algal biomass from grazer distribution.
2. **Predators structure grazer distribution.** Pool-to-pool occurrence of bass and Campostoma was inversely related in all but one of five 1983 censuses. Within a shared pool, bass occupied deep water and minnows shallow water, and algae grew only where bass patrolled. A tethered bass (300 mm SL) "guarded" algae-covered cobbles, which were then little grazed. Adding bass to a minnow pool caused emigration and shallow-water use, reduced feeding, and increased hiding; regrowth of algae followed within 1-2 weeks.
3. **Grazer removal experiment.** Stocking minnows on one side of a bass-free pool (after electroshocking bass and large sunfish) caused algae to decline rapidly over five weeks, while the unstocked side bloomed with Spirogyra.
4. **No complementarity in the Ozark-Ouachita streams.** Across 86 pools outside Brier Creek, Campostoma occurred without bass in 51 pools and with bass (>150 mm TL) in 29. The bass-minnow distributions were independent (χ² = 0.942; P = 0.33; 2×2 contingency analysis). Proposed causes include pool size, riffle permeability, and predator identity and prey size (mostly smallmouth bass, which eat more crayfish than fish).
5. **Grazing scar density.** In a Tyner Creek bedrock pool, grazing scars averaged 1,800 per m² across depths of 10-59 cm, and over 5,000 per m² below 40 cm. Scars averaged 0.57 cm² (N = 90). About 10% of submersed substrate showed grazing overall, rising to as much as 28.5% in the deepest, most heavily grazed zones.
6. **Baron Fork grazing lawns.** Campostoma are very abundant in Baron Fork, and algal standing crop is consistently low (108-325 g dry weight/m²) despite high productivity of thin felts (0.6 g O₂/m²/h). The authors infer that grazing removes biomass rather than limiting growth. Campostoma appear to be the main herbivores there.
7. **Grazing shifts algal composition.** Tiles in troughs excluding grazers developed diatom turfs (Melosira, Cymbella, Synedra). When moved to the stream bed, they were grazed and were replaced by blue-green felts within weeks. Natural felt-covered slates overgrown by diatoms within 4-10 days in troughs. Grazing scars also reflect faster turnover of blue-green felts dominated by Calothrix.
8. **Nutrient and ecosystem effects (speculative).** Calothrix fixes nitrogen, unlike the diatoms that dominate ungrazed areas, so grazing may alter nitrogen cycling and the food quality of other algivores. Feces accumulate in deeper pools, and grazing may either speed or slow nutrient turnover depending on nutrient limitation. The authors draw an analogy with "grazing lawns" of ungulates on the Serengeti.
9. **Possible effects on other biota.** Invertebrate numbers were similar in pools with few or many minnows, but snorkel observations suggested lower diversity of small fishes in minnow-dominated reaches. Notropis pilsbryi may benefit from minnow-disturbed invertebrate drift.
10. **Research priorities.** The authors stress that the transfer of ecological theory from terrestrial and lake grazers to flowing waters is unresolved, and that field studies at the whole-reach scale (km) are needed to test these hypotheses.
