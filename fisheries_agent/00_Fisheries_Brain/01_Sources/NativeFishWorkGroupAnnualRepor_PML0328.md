# Native Fish Work Group 1992 Annual Report: On-Site Propagation of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

The 1992 annual report of the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) documents an on-site effort to rear endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in isolated backwaters of [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. The aim is to replace an aging adult population (estimated at about 60,000 fish, roughly 40+ years old) that shows essentially no recruitment to adulthood. Adults were stocked into [[Yuma Cove]], where spawning and larval production occurred, and juveniles were grown out at [[Davis Cove]] before being tagged and released into the reservoir. The report also includes four appendices on larval growth, light-trap capture rates, and odonate predation.

Production was phenomenal in growth terms but low in numbers: 296 juveniles were harvested from Yuma Cove, and the authors conclude that output must rise roughly 20 to 40 times to meet replacement goals.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an endangered catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining population, which has been studied since the lake's impoundment in the 1950s. Larvae are produced each year, but they disappear before reaching juvenile size, and no detectable recruitment has occurred in nearly four decades. Predation by non-native fishes on early life stages is considered a leading cause.

The NFWG was formed in 1990 as a multi-agency coalition of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State University]], [[National Park Service]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Its plan has three phases: on-site spawning in Yuma Cove, grow-out in Davis Cove (isolated by netting and cleared of non-native fishes), and stocking of roughly 30 cm (TL) sub-adults into the lake. Hatchery production at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] supplemented the program.

The 1985 to 1987 attempts in Yuma Cove showed that the berm separating the backwater from the lake was prone to breaching, which allowed fish to escape and non-natives to invade. The berm was rebuilt in 1990-91.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Yuma Cove (shallow backwater, about 1 ft operational depth):** Water temperatures exceeded 30 °C in July and August. Thermal stratification and decomposition of plant material drove dissolved oxygen in the bottom 18 inches below 1 mg/L by late July. Dense [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and [[Spiny Naiad]] (*Najas marina*) covered the surface, impeded netting and degraded water quality. A flood on 10 August 2092 [sic: 1992] cooled the water, raised oxygen, increased turbidity, reduced algae and flattened weed beds, which aided capture.

**Davis Cove (about 3 acres, operational depth about 2 feet):** Located between Katherine's Landing and Davis Dam. A barrier net was installed in February 1992. Roughly 800 pounds of non-native fish were removed in June. Largemouth bass predation was suspected to be high, and a crayfish population then expanded, with 5 to 10 crayfish captured per 150-foot trammel net overnight by October.

**Predators and behaviour:** Odonate naiads (dragonfly and damselfly larvae) reached densities sometimes exceeding 5,000 per trap set. Young suckers were nocturnal, hid in cover during the day, and were preyed upon by turtles, crayfish and catfish.

## Key Findings

**Yuma Cove production (Table 1 and Appendix A):**
- 88 brood fish (29 females and 88 males were noted as ripe at stocking, with 8 females and 53 males recorded in one sub-count) were stocked in late January. Spawning ran from mid-February through April. 82 were recaptured and returned to the lake, 5 were found dead, and 1 was unaccounted for.
- The first larvae were collected on 25 or 26 February at 11.0 mm TL. Fish reached about 26 mm by late April, 100 mm by early June, 200 mm by late July and more than 300 mm by October.
- Genetic analysis indicated that at least 10 of 29 females selected produced young in the cove.
- Growth was about 4 cm per month (0.028 cm/day), and the largest individual reached 6 g.

**Harvest:**
- 109 juveniles averaging 354 mm were removed in late November, PIT-tagged and stocked into Lake Mohave. Total juvenile harvest from Yuma Cove was 296.
- Six ripe males were found among these juveniles, the earliest recorded sexual maturity for razorback sucker (about nine months, compared with two years previously reported).

**Population estimates:**
- Three mark-recapture estimates gave 305, 276 and 329 juveniles (95% CI 251 to 472 for the Schnabel-type Schumacher-Eschmeyer estimate). These predicted 93 to 111% of the actual harvest.

**Davis Cove grow-out:**
- About 10,000 juveniles from Dexter NFH were stocked on 24 June 1992 at an average of 68 mm, reaching 127.9 mm by 10 September.
- Growth was slower than in Yuma Cove, with possible causes including habitat, the flood, initial size and predatory fish.

**Light trapping (Appendix C):**
- 226 trap sets totalled 1,327 trap hours. White 12 W lights attracted 2.8 larvae/h (Yuma Cove) and 29.8 larvae/h (Arizona Bay). 55 W halogen lights gave 6.0 and 14.5 larvae/h respectively. Dog food and cyalume sticks gave 0.2 larvae/h or less.

**Odonate predation (Appendix D):**
- In a 7-day aquarium trial, survival of 11.8 mm larvae was 24% with dragonfly naiads and 19% with damselfly naiads, compared with 88% in controls. Survival of larger (14 to 15 mm) larvae was 47% with predators versus 82% in controls.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- Augmentation was about 2.5 times (females) to 5 times (males) the number of adults stocked, but production must rise to 6,000 to 12,000 sub-adults per year.
- Key constraints: limited space, high larval and juvenile mortality (about one-third lost during poor water quality), dense aquatic vegetation, and unresolved growth differences between Yuma and Davis coves.

**Outlook for 1993:** Continue on-site spawning at Yuma Cove, with an herbicide permit (pending) and a liner for part of the cove; stock Davis Cove with fish from Dexter NFH and Yuma Cove; and test subadult survival in the reservoir.
