# Biological and Conference Opinion on Lower Colorado River Operations and Maintenance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) Biological and Conference Opinion (BO), transmitted to the Bureau of Reclamation (Reclamation) in 1997, assesses the effects of Reclamation's continuing operations and maintenance of the [[Lower Colorado River]] from [[Lake Mead]] to the Southerly International Boundary (SIB) with Mexico, for up to five years under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The opinion covers the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]], endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (both with critical habitat), endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], endangered [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], and the proposed threatened [[Flat-tailed Horned Lizard]].

The Service concluded that the proposed action is likely to jeopardize the bonytail chub, razorback sucker, and southwestern willow flycatcher, and likely to destroy or adversely modify critical habitat for the two fish. It concluded the action is not likely to jeopardize the Yuma clapper rail or the flat-tailed horned lizard. A Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) with seventeen provisions and fourteen terms and conditions for the three jeopardized species was identified.

## Background & Description

**Document context.** The package includes a cover letter from Reclamation's Lower Colorado Regional Office (inviting a 45-day comment period ending July 11, 1997, and three public meetings in Las Vegas, Phoenix and Yuma in June 1997), a transmittal memorandum from the Service's Albuquerque regional office, and the full BO (consultation no. 2-21-95-F-216).

**Proposed action.** Reclamation's discretionary program covers dam operations, river control (banklines, levees, dredging), water deliveries, hydropower, the Yuma Desalting Plant (operated at one-third capacity), the 5-Mile Zone well field, and endangered species conservation. The action area spans about 412 river miles from Pierce Ferry to the SIB, generally within the 100-year floodplain. The action period runs five years from issuance (projected to May 15, 2002) or until the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (MSCP) is developed, whichever comes first.

**Legal framework.** Operations are governed by the [[Law of the River]], including the 1964 Supreme Court decree in *Arizona v. California*, the Boulder Canyon Project Act, interstate compacts and the 1944 treaty with Mexico (1.5 maf per year to Mexico). Reclamation is obligated to deliver at least 9.0 maf annually to the lower basin states and Mexico. Reclamation's priorities as Watermaster are river regulation and flood control, irrigation and domestic uses, then power generation.

**Species.**
- *Bonytail chub* (*Gila elegans*): a streamlined fish up to about 600 mm, with individuals reaching at least 49 years. Historically occupied about 3,500 river miles.
- *Razorback sucker* (*Xyrauchen texanus*): the sole member of its genus, with a sharp bony keel and lifespan of at least the mid-40s.
- *Southwestern willow flycatcher* (*Empidonax traillii extimus*): a small insectivorous riparian bird of about 11 g, nesting in dense willow, saltcedar or mixed riparian stands.
- *Yuma clapper rail* (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*): a marsh bird dependent on freshwater marsh and cattail/bulrush habitat, feeding largely on crayfish.
- *Flat-tailed horned lizard* (*Phrynosoma mcallii*): a cryptic lizard of sandy Sonoran desert flats, feeding mainly on ants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic conditions.** Before major development, the river had seasonal spring floods, meandering channels, beaver-created backwaters and extensive cottonwood-willow and mesquite bosques. Native fish used backwaters, sloughs and inundated bottomlands for feeding, spawning and nursery habitat. Historical accounts describe catches of 37 lb/hr in lagoons near Yuma in the 1700s, and fish clogging irrigation canals as late as 1911.

**Development.** Key changes, as summarized in the environmental baseline, include:
- 1823 onward: trapping, steamboat fuel harvest of cottonwood and mesquite, and agricultural diversions.
- 1905–1907: the Colorado River was diverted into the Salton Sink.
- 1909: [[Laguna Dam]] built.
- 1935: [[Hoover Dam]] closed, ending natural flow patterns; [[Lake Mead]] formed.
- 1938–1954: Parker, Imperial, Headgate Rock and Davis dams built, creating reservoirs and fragmenting the river.
- 1954–1996: channelization and bank stabilization. By 1996, 167.46 miles of bankline stabilization and 113.8 miles of levees lined the river from Davis Dam to the SIB.

**Non-native species.** Largemouth bass, bluegill, green sunfish, black crappie, carp, channel catfish, rainbow trout and red shiner were introduced. The river holds 44 recorded fish species, of which nine are native and 33 non-native (13 of the non-natives are "hypothetical"). Non-native fish are described as competitors and predators of native fish, and crayfish are a major food source for the rail.

**Habitat loss.** Cottonwood-willow habitat declined about 80% between 1938 and 1960 in the Parker II Division. The 1996 vegetation survey recorded 43,623 ha of riparian, marsh and desert vegetation between the border and Davis Dam, of which 42% was saltcedar and 3% cottonwood-willow. Adding Lake Mead inflow, cottonwood-willow totals about 1,841 ha over the 412-mile reach.

**Contaminants.** Selenium was found at high levels in backwater fish, in Yuma clapper rail livers and eggs, and in a crossed-bill flycatcher fledgling. Organochlorines were linked to altered male carp in razorback sucker spawning areas of Lake Mead.

## Key Findings

### Jeopardy and critical habitat determinations
- **Jeopardy (bonytail chub, razorback sucker, southwestern willow flycatcher):** Reclamation's operations are likely to jeopardize these species, and are likely to destroy or adversely modify critical habitat for the bonytail chub and razorback sucker.
- **No jeopardy (Yuma clapper rail, flat-tailed horned lizard):** Not likely to jeopardize; no critical habitat proposed or designated for these species.

### Species status
- **Bonytail chub:** Eliminated from at least 85% of its historic range. Recruitment is virtually non-existent. Lake Mohave held few young adults and very few old adults; about 217,000 fingerlings were reintroduced to Lake Mohave since 1981. Projected 2002 population of 500–1,000 fish if reintroductions fail, versus 50,000 adults if they succeed.
- **Razorback sucker:** Lake Mohave population estimated at about 20,000 fish, less than 50% of the population a decade earlier. Lack of swim-up fry recruitment is the major threat. About 60,000 larvae were collected in 1996 and about 40,000 fingerlings were ready for grow-out. About 5,000 advanced fingerlings had been reintroduced by then.
- **Southwestern willow flycatcher:** Rangewide population of about 454 territories, with a range of 300–500 likely. More than 75% of occupied locations hold five or fewer territorial birds. Nest predation runs about 30–50%. Cowbird parasitism is a major threat; at the South Fork Kern River, a cowbird trapping program raised nest success from 26% to 48%.
- **Yuma clapper rail:** The 1994 river-wide count estimated 1,145 birds on the U.S. Colorado River, and the population appears stable. About one-half of the total population lives in Mexico's delta.
- **Flat-tailed horned lizard:** About 39% of the species' remaining important habitat in the United States lies in Arizona, estimated at 550 km² (212 mi²). About 40 records exist in the 5-Mile Zone.

### Key threats identified
- Lake Mead inflow willows (about 465 ha, 1,148 ac) are inundated above root crowns, and the Service expects significant, if not complete, mortality between the 1997 and 1998 growing seasons. Three flycatcher nests were lost to treefall in 1996.
- Fire, saltcedar invasion and recreation are major threats to remaining riparian habitat.
- Entrainment into canals and passage through powerplants cause fish losses, especially as augmented populations grow.

### Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA)
- **Short-term provisions** include expanding grow-out facilities to produce at least 50,000 razorback suckers over a five-year period, reviewing backwater and wetland programs (with no new backwaters designed for sport fishing), and building at least three native fish impoundments of about 600 acres (not fewer than 300 acres accepted).
- **Long-term provisions** are to be integrated into the MSCP.

### Incidental take
- Take of Yuma clapper rails and flat-tailed horned lizards is expected to be small. The lizard take estimate is 1–14 lizards annually in the Arizona population.

### Reclamation's programs
- Reclamation committed to at least $100,000 per year for native riparian plant restoration.
- The Three-Finger Lake project (about $4 million total) was restored partly in 1995 and due for completion in spring 1997.
- The Lower Imperial Division wetland enhancement project (about 6,100 acres, 150 backwaters) received a $590,000 Arizona Water Protection Fund grant, matched by Reclamation.
- Reclamation proposed preserving about 16,000 acres within the 5-Mile Zone as a flat-tailed horned lizard preserve, the Yuma Desert Management Area.

### Consultation timeline
- Informal consultation began February 6, 1995; a public review draft BA was published in March 1996; the final BA was completed in August 1996; the Service sent the BO to Reclamation on May 7, 1997 (signed April 30, 1997).
