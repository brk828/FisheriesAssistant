# River Renewal: Restoring Rivers Through Hydropower Dam Relicensing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

*River Renewal* is a May 1996 report by [[American Rivers]] that describes how the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] (FERC) relicensing process can be used to reduce the ecological and recreational damage caused by hydropower dams. It explains the relicensing procedure, the legal role of public intervenors, and the advantages of negotiated [[Settlement Agreements]] over traditional licensing. It then catalogs typical mitigation measures in two parts: Part I covers recreation and conservation mitigation options, and Part II summarizes nine settlement packages from rivers across the United States. The report argues that relicensing, which occurs every 30 to 50 years, is a rare opportunity to restore flows, fish passage, reservoir operations, and public access.

## Background & Description

**Scale of the problem.** The report states that the United States has nearly 75,000 dams. These include approximately 750 [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] or Bureau of Reclamation dams, 1,750 dams on Bureau of Land Management land, 300 on Native American lands, 430 in National Wildlife Refuges or the National Park System, and at least 50,000 general purpose dams (EPA estimates). Hydropower dams are the focus of the report.

**Impacts of dams.** Dams alter chemical, physical and biological river processes by reducing river levels, altering flow timing and water temperature, decreasing oxygen levels, fluctuating reservoir levels, trapping silt and nutrients, killing fish in turbines, and restricting public access. "Peaking" operations release water in large surges to meet power demand, which erodes banks and disrupts fishing and boating. The report notes that hundreds of thousands of fish are killed annually in turbines.

**Regulatory framework.** FERC licenses non-federal hydropower dams under the [[Federal Power Act]]. Licenses last 30 to 50 years and stipulate operations, minimum flows, fish passage and, in some cases, watershed land management. A 1986 amendment requires FERC to give equal consideration to power generation, energy conservation, fish and wildlife, recreation, and environmental quality. Environmental reviews take the form of an [[Environmental Impact Statement]] (EIS) or Environmental Assessment (EA).

**Relicensing timeline.** The process begins five years before expiration with a notice of intent, followed by an initial consultation package. A relicensing application is filed two years before expiration. FERC then issues a "ready for environmental analysis" notice, after which resource agencies and intervenors propose license terms.

**Intervention.** Under the Federal Power Act, individuals and groups may file a motion to intervene, becoming formal parties with standing to seek judicial review. Without intervention, parties may comment but cannot appeal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam-related habitat loss:** Dams can dewater stream reaches. The report notes bypassed reaches range from a few hundred yards to 15 miles long.
- **Flow regime:** Peaking operations cause fluctuating flows that erode soils, strand or flood wildlife, and disrupt seasonal cues for spawning and growth.
- **Reservoir fluctuation:** Drawdowns endanger loon nests and fish eggs in shallow areas.
- **Water quality:** Reservoirs stratify, producing cold, oxygen-deficient bottom water that alters downstream temperature and dissolved oxygen. The report notes that over half the nation's drinking water originates in river systems.
- **Contaminated sediments:** Dams trap pollutants, which can be resuspended during drawdowns or repairs.
- **Migratory fish:** Dams block upstream and downstream passage of anadromous species such as [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[American Shad]], sturgeon and river herring.
- **Listed species:** The report cites the federally endangered [[Indiana Bat]] and [[Bald Eagle]] management plans on the Au Sable River.

## Key Findings

**1. Relicensing is a major but time-limited opportunity.** In 1993, 160 licenses expired, affecting 262 dams on 105 rivers. Only about 51% of those relicensings had been completed, and 550 more dams are due in the next 15 years.

**2. Settlements are preferred over traditional relicensing.** Settlements negotiated outside FERC have generally yielded faster and more creative river improvements, and they establish long-term cooperation. Because FERC may omit non-conventional terms, parties often make settlement terms legally binding independently of the license.

**3. Mitigation categories are broad.**
- *Recreation:* access (boat launches, portages, disabled access), facilities, whitewater releases, aesthetics.
- *Conservation:* instream and minimum flows, bypassed reach restoration, reservoir fluctuation limits, fish passage and protection, watershed land protection, erosion control, water quality, wildlife habitat, cultural resources.
- *Additional:* trust funds, decommissioning funds, management committees.

**4. Fish protection and passage require tailored solutions.** Trashracks with narrow bar spacing (e.g., 1.0 inch replacing 4.5 inches on the Chippewa Falls project) reduce turbine entrainment. Upstream passage technologies (ladders, lifts, locks, trap-and-truck) work for only some species and cause stress. The report notes that little data exist on their success in passing viable numbers. Monetary compensation and hatcheries are weaker alternatives.

**5. Settlement packages delivered measurable benefits.** Examples include:
- [[Beaver River]] (New York): Niagara Mohawk fund of $590,000 for restoration; reservoir fluctuation limits of 1.0 to 1.5 feet; trashrack replacement at eight dams.
- [[Black River]] (New York): Black River Fund started at $30,000 with $10,000 added annually; minimum flow of 250 cfs; scheduled kayak releases.
- [[Deerfield River]] (Massachusetts and Vermont): settlement valued at $25 to 30 million; restored flows over 12 river miles and increased flows over 33 miles; 18,335 acres under conservation easements; Atlantic salmon spawning and cold-water fishery objectives.
- [[Gauley River]] (West Virginia): scheduled fall whitewater releases; annual whitewater industry estimated at $33 million.
- [[Manistee, Muskegon and Au Sable Rivers]] (Michigan): about $50 million over the license terms; $575,000 annual fish-loss contribution; water quality protections with liquidated damages.
- [[Pigeon River]] (North Carolina and Tennessee): 1,200 cfs flow at Brown's Bridge; Canton Mill discharge of color reduced by two-thirds; dioxin monitoring.
- [[Saco River]] (Maine and New Hampshire): basin-wide fish passage plan for Atlantic salmon, American shad and river herring; four-year assessment cycles.
- [[Salmon River]] (New York): base flows of 285 cfs (January to April), 185 cfs (May to August), and 335 cfs (September to December); 11-plus miles of river corridor protected.
- [[Skagit River]] (Washington): $845,000 for erosion control over nine years; anadromous fish flow plan with spawning flow limits of 4,500 cfs for chinook; $6.32 million non-flow fish program.

**6. Economic and social benefits.** The report documents benefits including the Black River attracting about 10,000 boaters and generating about $700,000 in direct sales annually; the Deerfield generating $1.2 million in outfitter revenue; and the Pigeon's potential whitewater industry adding roughly $20 million annually.

**7. Conclusions and recommendations.** Settlements should be creative and flexible, with environmental and recreational groups speaking with one voice. Parties should learn the science of river systems and dam operations, and start cooperative relicensing years before expiration. The report expects the focus of relicensing to shift west toward larger projects.

[[Beaver River]], [[Black River]], [[Deerfield River]], [[Gauley River]], [[Manistee, Muskegon and Au Sable Rivers]], [[Pigeon River]], [[Saco River]], [[Salmon River]] and [[Skagit River]] are the nine settlement case studies.
