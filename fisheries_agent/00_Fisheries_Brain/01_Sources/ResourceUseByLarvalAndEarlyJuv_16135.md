# Resource Use by Larval and Early Juvenile Native Fishes in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 study by [[Michael R. Childs]], [[Robert W. Clarkson]] and [[Anthony T. Robinson]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department, published in *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*) examines how early life stages (30 mm total length or less) of four native fishes use the [[Little Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The authors assessed microhabitat use, behavioral time budgets and diet across species and across larval stages within species. Their central conclusion is that these fishes partition resources, with differences in habitat use, feeding behavior and diet, and that the river's unregulated temperature and flow regime makes it a useful reference for managing larval native fish habitat in the regulated [[Colorado River]] mainstem below [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

## Background & Description

**Study species**

- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), an endangered cyprinid.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), a cyprinid.
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), a catostomid.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), a catostomid.

**Life stages**

Larvae were classed as protolarval, mesolarval or metalarval, with juveniles also included, following [[Snyder 1981]] and [[Snyder and Muth 1990]]. Average lengths of the sampled fish were 15.6 mm (bluehead sucker, n = 444), 18.2 mm (flannelmouth sucker, n = 124), 20.2 mm (humpback chub, n = 251) and 18.2 mm (speckled dace, n = 522).

**Context and motivation**

Native fishes of the Colorado River basin have declined since the late 1800s. Adult populations often persist, but recruitment of early life stages is rare or absent. Cold hypolimnetic releases from dams can retard embryonic development and reduce swimming performance, and shallow nearshore nursery areas are sensitive to discharge fluctuations. Little was known about the ecology of larval native fishes, and few diet studies existed outside the upper basin (see [[Colorado Squawfish]]).

**Research questions**

1. Does microhabitat use differ among species?
2. Do ontogenetic habitat shifts occur within species?
3. Do behavioral time budgets or use of vertical zones differ among species?
4. Do diets differ among species?

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area**

The study covered the terminal 14 km of the canyon-bound Little Colorado River. Perennial flow of about 6.3 m³/s originates at Blue Spring, 21 km above the mouth, and from lesser downstream springs. Sampling took place during clear-water base flows in 1993 and 1994. Nearshore habitat was defined as low-velocity water (<0.2 m/s) bounded by shoreline, including vegetated and nonvegetated margins and peripheral pools.

**Contrast with the mainstem**

Mainstem temperatures below Glen Canyon Dam range from 7°C to 14°C because water is drawn from the hypolimnion of [[Lake Powell]]. Reproduction by native fishes there is limited. The Little Colorado River, by contrast, has essentially unaltered temperature and flow regimes.

**Habitat variables**

Measured variables were depth, distance from shore, current velocity, cover (boulder, cobble, depth >0.5 m, instream vegetation, ledge, overhanging vegetation, travertine dam, undercut bank, woody debris) and substrate (detritus, calcium carbonate precipitate, silt, sand, pebble, cobble, boulder, travertine, bedrock). Substrate classes followed [[Lane 1947]].

**Ecological framing**

The authors note that the nearshore habitats used by these fish closely resemble the nursery habitats of many larval warmwater stream fishes described by [[Scheidegger and Bain 1995]]. Differential resource use may reflect competition, predator avoidance, morphology or a combination of these, and does not necessarily indicate competition ([[Ross 1986]]).

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**

- **Habitat use:** Fish were collected by dip net during daylight from April 30 to August 5, 1993. Habitat was measured at points 10 cm apart along one to three transects per site. Analysis used forward stepwise [[Logistic Regression]] with likelihood ratio tests (α = 0.05) and a Holm correction for multiple tests.
- **Behavior:** Focal-animal observations of fish 30 mm TL or less were made during daylight in 1991, 1993 and 1994. Each fish was watched for 5 min and its vertical position recorded in five zones, from benthic to surface. Time budgets were compared with G-tests and Kruskal–Wallis tests.
- **Diet:** Fish were collected in May and June 1993 for diet overlap analysis. A diel sampling set was collected on May 14–15, 1994, with 62–78 fish taken every 6 h over 24 h. Diet was measured as relative gut volume (point system) and frequency of occurrence. Overlap was calculated with [[Schoener's Index]].

**Habitat use results**

- Habitat was measured at 1,199 sites representing 1,341 fish (444 bluehead suckers, 124 flannelmouth suckers, 251 humpback chub, 522 speckled dace).
- Fish occupied nearshore sites with mean current velocities of 0–19 cm/s, mean depths of 2–78 cm and mean distances from shore of 1–435 cm.
- Logistic models classified species correctly 54.4–80.9% of the time and life stages within species 59.7–94.7% of the time.
- Significant models could not be built for flannelmouth sucker versus humpback chub, or flannelmouth sucker versus speckled dace. Current velocity was not a significant component of any species comparison.
- **Bluehead suckers** used shallower water than any other species and were the most distinctive in habitat use, with more interspecific differences than any other species.
- **Flannelmouth suckers** were found over calcium carbonate precipitate and silt more than bluehead suckers and appeared to be habitat generalists relative to the cyprinids.
- **Ontogenetic shifts** occurred in every species. Older bluehead sucker larvae occupied deeper water, farther from shore and with less woody cover. Older flannelmouth sucker larvae used deeper, slower water farther from shore. Humpback chub metalarvae used lower velocities than mesolarvae. Older speckled dace used deeper water over substrates other than calcium carbonate.

**Behavioral results**

- Time budgets and feeding behaviors differed across species (G-test, P < 0.005).
- Percent time spent feeding (total) was 26.0% for bluehead suckers (n = 68), 47.8% for flannelmouth suckers (n = 39), 26.8% for humpback chub (n = 23) and 12.7% for speckled dace (n = 48).
- Bluehead suckers fed less than flannelmouth suckers and speckled dace fed less than both flannelmouth suckers and humpback chub. The catostomids fed less in the water column than the cyprinids.
- Vertical water-column use differed among species (G-test, P < 0.025). Bluehead suckers used the upper third of the water column less than the other species and the surface less than flannelmouth suckers or humpback chub. All four species spent most time on the bottom.

**Diet results**

- Immature [[Chironomidae]] (midges) were the major identifiable food of all fish and occurred in more than 60% of guts. They made up about one-third to one-half of total gut volume in all species.
- Relative volume of adult chironomids was higher in humpback chub.
- Organic and inorganic matter made up more of the gut volume of catostomids than cyprinids. Diatoms appeared only in catostomid stomachs.
- Unidentified seeds were a larger share of humpback chub gut volume than in any other species.
- Cyprinids ate more food types than catostomids, and humpback chub ate more taxa than any other species.
- Diet overlap was highest between the two catostomids (0.91) and between the two cyprinids (0.82). Lower overlap occurred between the families: humpback chub with bluehead sucker (0.58) and with flannelmouth sucker (0.56), and speckled dace with bluehead sucker (0.60) and with flannelmouth sucker (0.61).
- Diel sampling in 1994 showed that bluehead sucker was the only species whose diet changed across diel periods. Its viscera held more immature chironomids at 1100 and 1700 hours than at 2300 hours. Its gut fullness was greater in daylight than at night (K-W test, P < 0.001), while speckled dace and humpback chub gut fullness did not vary. No flannelmouth suckers were collected in the diel sample.

**Conclusions**

- Some resource partitioning occurs among larval fishes in the Little Colorado River, shown by habitat use, behavioral time budgets and diet.
- Feeding location differences (benthic detritus and inorganic material for catostomids versus adult dipterans and surface feeding for cyprinids) suggest that foraging strategies differ at the family level.
- Diet overlap was much greater within families than between them.
- Nearshore habitats (shallow water, fine substrates, varied cover, slow current) should be conserved in the Little Colorado River and the mainstem through Grand Canyon. Future operation of Glen Canyon Dam should consider impacts on these areas.
- The authors caution that sampling scale and taxonomic resolution may hide partitioning. Chironomids identified only to family may conceal partitioning, since [[Gray 1981]] recorded 15 chironomid species from [[Sycamore Creek]], Arizona.
- Laboratory studies are recommended to test whether the observed differences reflect competition, predator avoidance or morphology, and how habitat complexity and dietary diversity affect the coexistence of young native fishes with introduced species.
