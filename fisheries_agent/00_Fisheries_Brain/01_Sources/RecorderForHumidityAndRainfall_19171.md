# Recorder for Humidity and Rainfall

#hydrology #meteorology #environmental-instrumentation

## Executive Summary

This 1956 technical paper, reprinted from the *Journal of the Institution of Water Engineers* (Vol. 10, No. 2, March 1956), describes an automatic instrument that records relative humidity and rainfall together on photographic film. The apparatus was developed at the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] in Stevenage by G. V. Dyke and G. Knowles. It combines a forced-ventilation [[Wet-and-Dry Bulb Hygrometer]] with an electrode-based [[Tipping-Free Rainfall Recorder]] and a timed camera.

The document does not address fish, invertebrates, aquatic habitats or water chemistry. It is an instrumentation paper on atmospheric monitoring, included here because weather data is needed in water-quality and hydrological investigations.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The authors note that many investigations affected by the weather need continuous records of rainfall and humidity. The design uses photographs of a hygrometer and a rain gauge taken at regular intervals. This allows a simple, reasonably accurate forced-ventilation wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer to be used.

**Humidity methods reviewed.** The paper compares measurement approaches:
- Weighing a hygroscopic chemical before and after it absorbs moisture from a sample is the most accurate method.
- The [[Dewpoint Method]] is considered next in accuracy but requires elaborate apparatus.
- The wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer, with forced ventilation and other precautions, is accurate to within about 1 per cent relative humidity across the normal range of humidities and temperatures.
- Less reliable methods include hair hygrometers, resistive and capacitive films, and anodized aluminium wire.

Mercury-in-glass wet-and-dry bulb thermometers were chosen because they gave the required accuracy and could be photographed easily.

**Humidity hardware.** The thermometer bulbs sit in a copper tube (3.5 cm diameter) fed by a motor-driven blower. Air is drawn through the tube for 10 minutes before each photograph. The wick of the wet bulb dips into distilled water in a constant-level reservoir. A Perspex tube shields the wick from evaporation, and a Perspex rod magnifier on each thermometer makes the readings legible on film.

**Rainfall hardware.** Float-gauge recorders are considered the most accurate common type, but they are subject to siphon dribble, evaporation and friction errors. The Water Pollution Research instrument instead uses:
- A 5 in. diameter funnel draining into a Perspex cell (about 2.5 cm internal diameter, 7 cm deep).
- Two brass electrodes, with the upper, horseshoe-shaped electrode adjustable by a screw thread.
- An electromagnetic inlet valve that closes when the electrodes are bridged by water.
- An electromagnetic outlet valve that opens only after water stays in contact with the upper electrode for 15 seconds. This prevents false counts from splash or drips.
- A counter unit that registers each discharge.

Because rain collected while the inlet valve is closed passes through the cell later, all rainfall is recorded. The paper notes that siphon-based designs lose rain collected during siphoning, though probably only a very small amount.

**Camera.** A recording camera (by Shackman Ltd.) carries a Wray f/3.5 lens of 36 mm focal length, placed about 14 in. from the subject. It uses 35 mm panchromatic film and is lit by four 40-watt pearl bulbs. The camera holds 200 exposures, each about 1 in. square. Each photograph shows both thermometers, an hour dial on a synchronous clock, the rain counter (lower figure) and the exposure number (upper figure).

## Key Findings

**1. Humidity performance**
- Occasional checks against an [[Assmann Hygrometer]] agreed within 1 to 2 per cent relative humidity.
- A comparison of about a dozen paired readings, taken over a few weeks at about 19 °C, showed close agreement. Examples include 71% (Assmann) vs 69% (recorder), 66% vs 66%, and 53% vs 51%. Some OCR values are garbled and were not reproduced.
- Extensive performance testing was not carried out. No checks were made below 0 °C.

**2. Rainfall performance**
- Laboratory tests used rainfall rates of 0.15 in/h and 0.8 in/h.
- For a 30-unit test (30 × 0.0243 in = 0.729 in), the recorder reading was compared with measuring-cylinder readings.
- The maximum error in both cases was 2.0 per cent.
- Reported "true" readings ranged from about 0.714 to 0.744 in. The OCR of this table is partly illegible.

**3. Design advantages**
- Forced-ventilation wet-and-dry bulb thermometry offers a simple, accurate humidity method suited to automatic recording.
- The electrode-and-valve rain cell avoids siphon losses and the mechanical complexity of float-gauge recorders.
- Rainfall is recorded in fixed 0.0243 in units, with the counter photographed every 4 hours. This gives the total rainfall for each 4-hour period, which the authors say was all the information the investigation required.

**4. Control sequence**
- A synchronous clock advances a stepping switch every 10 minutes.
- Positions 1 to 20 produce no action. From position 21 onward, the sequence charges a condenser, records a unit on the exposure counter, starts the lights and blower, fires the camera, and then resets.
- Relays hold the blower and lights on for a few seconds while the photograph is taken.

**5. Adaptability**
- The rainfall setting can be adjusted using the screw thread.
- The camera could be replaced with a cheaper recorder that marks the time of each rain unit on a scale, if combined humidity recording is not needed.
