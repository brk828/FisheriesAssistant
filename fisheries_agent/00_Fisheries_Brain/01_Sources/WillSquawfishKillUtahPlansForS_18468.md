# Colorado Squawfish and the White River Synfuels Dam Dispute

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Wall Street Journal clipping is a multi-article collection. Three of its four items concern business and finance: tax-exempt industrial-revenue bonds that exclude small borrowers, shopping-center tenants resisting holiday opening requirements, and proposed IRS rules on debt versus equity. None of these addresses aquatic life. The aquatic article, by Bill Paul, covers a conflict between a proposed dam on the [[White River]] in Utah and the federally protected [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Tracking showed a squawfish stopping near the planned dam site. Federal officials indicated they will likely withhold the permit under the [[Endangered Species Act]], which Utah officials say blocks the state's [[Synthetic Fuels]] development.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is described as silvery-gray and unattractive, with poor taste. State officials in Utah once regarded it as a "trash fish" and killed many individuals to make room in the [[White River]] for game fish such as trout and bass. That history is cited as a contributing factor in its listing on the federal endangered species list.

The article draws a parallel with the [[Snail Darter]] case. In 1974, officials found that completing the [[Tellico Dam]] in Tennessee would threaten the snail darter. Environmental groups sued, and the dispute took five years of litigation, an act of Congress, and transplantation of the fish before the dam could be completed.

Utah is pursuing [[Synthetic Fuels]] development in its northeastern region, which is rich in [[Oil Shale]] and [[Tar Sands]]. According to the governor's office, Utah holds 40% of all tar-sands deposits in the U.S. The state also has some of the richest seams of oil shale and coal. The proposed dam is considered essential to supply water for this industry, and the [[Colorado River System]] is central to the water-diversion plans.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

In spring, [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] officials fitted a squawfish in the White River with an electronic tracking device. Biologists followed its upstream movement using a raft, a jet-powered speedboat, and a slow-flying plane. The fish appeared to stop, presumably to [[Spawning]], at the location where the dam is proposed.

The Fish and Wildlife Service's main concerns are:

- **[[Fish Migration]] blockage:** The dam could prevent squawfish from completing their upstream migration.
- **[[Water Temperature]] change:** The service expects the dam to cause a "disastrous" drop in water temperature below the dam.
- **Reduced downstream flow:** The dam could seriously deplete water flowing below the structure, where the fish has been found.

Utah's natural resources coordinator, Jim Butler, argues that although the tagged fish reached the dam area, it is the first squawfish sighted there in several years. Robert Shields, area manager for the Fish and Wildlife Service, does not dispute this but says his agency needs more work on the species' migration patterns.

## Key Findings

- **Permit at risk:** Fish and Wildlife officials have indicated, based on preliminary information, that they will not issue the permit needed to build the White River dam. Shields states that "construction of the dam could jeopardize the survival of the squawfish."
- **Legal constraint:** Shields says the Endangered Species Act "says that if we err, it must be in favor of the fish" and that there is "absolutely no wiggle room" under it. He also says the agency is looking for an alternative.
- **Policy conflict:** Utah officials describe the situation as "crazy," noting that Congress passed an Energy Security Act calling for synthetic-fuel development while the Endangered Species Act constrains the required infrastructure.
- **Precedent for industry:** Butler states that the dam decision will set the precedent for whether a synthetic-fuels industry can develop in the Far West, and that without the dam "we might as well pack our bags."
- **Data gaps:** Shields concedes that the agency needs more research on squawfish migration patterns, and he predicts that finding a solution "acceptable to everyone" will be difficult.
- **Other articles (non-aquatic):** The remaining items report that federal agencies may deny loans to borrowers whose projects use tax-exempt industrial-revenue bonds, that shopping-center tenants are resisting mandatory holiday opening under leases, and that the IRS has revised proposed rules on classifying debt and equity.
