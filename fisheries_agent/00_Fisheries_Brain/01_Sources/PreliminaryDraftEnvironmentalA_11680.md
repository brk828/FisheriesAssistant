# Red Shiner Eradication Project Environmental Assessment - Virgin River, Utah

#fisheries-management #invasive-species #rotenone #endangered-species #Virgin-River

## Executive Summary

This preliminary draft Environmental Assessment, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in June 1996, evaluates alternatives for eradicating the invasive [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) from the [[Virgin River]] in Utah. The red shiner, a small minnow native to the North Central States and northeastern Mexico, was introduced to the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] as bait fish in the early 1950s and first discovered in the Virgin River system in 1961. By the 1980s, it had become the dominant fish species in the 21-mile reach between the [[Virgin River Gorge]] and [[Washington Fields Diversion]], threatening federally endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*), as well as the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*). The document proposes chemical treatment with rotenone to eliminate red shiner populations and protect native fish communities, building on previous eradication efforts conducted in 1988, 1989, 1991, and March 1996.

## Background & Description

The red shiner invasion represents a critical threat to the Virgin River's native fish fauna. Prior to red shiner establishment, the fish community consisted almost exclusively of native species including woundfin, Virgin River chub, Virgin spinedace, [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*). In July-August 1984, native species comprised 99.9% of the catch above the Virgin River Gorge. By October 1987, woundfin numbers had dropped to only 15% while red shiner numbers climbed to 59% in the reach between the Gorge and Washington Fields Diversion.

The red shiner has been implicated in declines of native fishes throughout the Southwest, including [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) in Arizona's [[Verde River]], [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitus*) in the [[Gila River]], and [[Desert Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) in the [[Bill Williams]] drainage. Competition for food resources and space, predation on larval and young-of-the-year life stages, and potential introduction of parasites like the [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) are primary mechanisms of impact.

Previous eradication attempts in 1988 and 1989 used rotenone (Noxfish® at 2-5 ppm concentrations) with potassium permanganate detoxification. While these treatments greatly reduced red shiner numbers, complete eradication was not achieved. The 1988 treatment covered a 21-mile reach; detoxification failures resulted in dead and distressed fish downstream to Mesquite, Nevada. The 1989 supplemental treatment targeted a 14-mile reach and included intensive treatment of marshes (Seegmiller Marsh - 15 acres, duck pond - 5 acres), Fort Pierce Wash, irrigation return ditches, potholes, springs, and seeps. A 1991 treatment focused on the buffer zone between Johnson Diversion Dam and Washington Fields Diversion Dam (6.4 miles), and another retreatment of this buffer zone occurred in March 1996.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology**: The Virgin River is a tributary to the [[Colorado River]], flowing approximately 320 km (200 miles) through southwestern Utah (59% of basin), northwestern Arizona (33%), and southeastern Nevada (8%) before entering [[Lake Mead]]. The basin encompasses approximately 5,100 square miles. The river is characterized by steep-walled, narrow canyons including [[Zion Canyon]], [[Parunuweap Canyon]], [[Timpoweap Canyon]], and the Virgin River Gorge. Elevations range from 10,000 feet near headwaters to below 1,900 feet at Littlefield, Arizona.

The Virgin River exhibits extreme flow fluctuations typical of desert streams, with high salinity, temperature, and turbidity. Mean annual flow at Littlefield, Arizona (water years 1930-1992) is 170,600 acre-feet. Mean daily flows ranged from 100 cfs (1991) to 697 cfs (1983), with instantaneous flows varying from 38 cfs to 61,000 cfs. Mineral springs like [[Littlefield Spring]] and [[La Verkin Springs]] significantly alter water quality; La Verkin Springs contributes 12-18 cfs at 100-109°F (35-39°C) with salinity of 9,650 mg/l.

**Vegetation**: Four distinct communities characterize the basin: fir-pine at headwaters, juniper-pinyon zone, blackbrush zone, and desert community dominated by creosote bush. Riparian vegetation includes abundant exotic species such as [[Russian Olive]] and [[Tamarisk]]. Aquatic vegetation is uncommon due to high sediment loads.

**Native Fish Community**: Six native species inhabit the system. Two are federally endangered: woundfin and Virgin River chub. Critical habitat has been proposed encompassing the mainstem Virgin River and riparian zone within the 100-year floodplain from the confluence of Ash-La Verkin Creeks to the Arizona border. Virgin spinedace was proposed for threatened status (59 FR 25875, May 18, 1994) but the proposal was withdrawn February 6, 1996 (61 FR 4401) following implementation of a Conservation Agreement signed in April 1995 by USFWS, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, Nevada Division of Wildlife, Washington County Water Conservancy District, and Arizona Game and Fish Department.

**Nonnative Species**: Fourteen introduced fish species occur in the system: red shiner, [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*). Red shiner and black bullhead are most abundant. Crayfish (Astacidae sp.) have also been introduced.

**Other Species of Concern**: [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) - federally endangered; [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*) - federally endangered; [[Desert Tortoise]] (*Gopherus agassizii*) - federally threatened; [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] (*Strix occidentalis lucida*) - federally threatened; [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) - federally threatened; [[Arizona Southwestern Toad]] (*Bufo microscaphus microscaphus*) and [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] (*Rana yavapaiensis*) - species of special concern.

**Invertebrate Fauna**: Sparse overall but inflated populations occur in the Washington Fields Diversion to Bloomington reach due to organic inputs from irrigation return flows, feedlot runoff, and wastewater discharges. Macroinvertebrates include chironomids, oligochaetes, simuliid blackfly larvae, caddisflies, and mayflies. Zooplankton include cladocerans, ostracods, and copepods. All are detrital-algal feeders and active drifters capable of rapid reestablishment after flooding.

**Land Use**: The treatment area spans three segments with varying ownership. Washington Fields Diversion to Johnson's Diversion Dam (6 miles): mostly private with 3/4 mile public land. Johnson's Diversion Dam to St. George sewer plant (8 miles): virtually all private with 1/2 mile State Trust land. St. George sewer plant to Arizona border (7 miles): 1/4 mile State Trust, 1 mile private, 5 3/4 miles BLM public lands. Washington County population grew from 13,669 (1970) to 26,065 (1980) to 48,560 (1990), averaging over 6% annual growth. In 1990, the county utilized approximately 87,740 acre-feet/year for agricultural irrigation and 33,751 acre-feet/year for municipal and industrial demands from Virgin River water.

## Key Findings

**Purpose and Need**: The Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan identifies red shiner as a limiting factor in recovery of woundfin and Virgin River chub. The Conservation Agreement for Virgin spinedace identifies removal of red shiner below Washington Fields and Johnson Diversion dams as a required conservation action. Red shiner competes with native fishes for food and space, may prey on larval and young-of-the-year stages (reducing survival and recruitment per Addley and Hardy 1993), and may introduce parasitic organisms. Gregor and Deacon (1988) documented significant dietary overlap between woundfin and red shiners.

**Alternatives Evaluated**:

1. **No Action Alternative**: Continued monitoring with no eradication efforts. Would result in continued competition and displacement of native fishes, eventual invasion above Washington Fields Diversion, severe hindrance to recovery efforts, and potential need to list Virgin spinedace.

2. **Utah Treatment (Preferred) Alternative**: Chemical treatment with rotenone of progressive downstream reaches from Washington Fields Diversion Dam (River Mile 118.8) downstream approximately 21 miles to Virgin River Gorge at Utah-Arizona border. Long-term project lasting several years beginning late summer/fall 1996. Rotenone concentration: 1.5 ppm. Detoxification: potassium permanganate at minimum 2.35 ppm for each 0.05 ppm active rotenone. Rhodamine dye added for monitoring. Four to six times required potassium permanganate available on-site.

3. **Buffer Zone Treatment Alternative**: Treatment limited to 6.4-mile reach between Washington Fields Diversion Dam and Johnson Diversion Dam. Protects refugium above Washington Fields where red shiner have never been detected and native fishes thrive. Same methodology as preferred alternative.

**Treatment Protocol**: Pre-treatment activities include locating all inflow sources and sampling for red shiner presence/absence, reducing flows to minimize treatment area and rotenone quantity, determining appropriate rotenone concentrations, obtaining flow data, contacting irrigators and water users, public notification, and dye studies to determine travel time and dilution. Extensive native fish salvage (estimated 40-60% capture rate) using seines, backpack electroshockers, block nets, and dip nets. Native species transported and released downstream of treatment area. Live traps strategically placed to monitor treatment effectiveness and detoxification success.

**Environmental Consequences - Preferred Alternative**:

*Biological Resources*: Approximately 40-60% of native species salvaged pre-treatment; remaining individuals eliminated during treatment. Intra-Service Section 7 consultation required prior to implementation. Impacts to native species expected to be minimal because: (1) native species comprise relatively small proportion of current fish community in treatment zone, (2) they are relatively abundant upstream, and (3) rapid repopulation expected. Previous treatments demonstrated fish reestablishment within one month, with full community recovery within one year/one breeding season. Community structure would shift with red shiner elimination; native species populations would increase and dominate in absence of competition. Short-term adverse impacts to endangered species offset by greater long-term beneficial effects.

Amphibians: Adult frogs and amphibians can avoid rotenone by leaving water. Larval amphibians (tadpoles, juvenile salamanders) highly susceptible per Fotenot et al. (1994); one year's recruitment lost. Prior treatments showed amphibian population boom post-treatment, possibly due to reduced fish predation.

Aquatic Invertebrates: Temporarily adversely affected; sensitivity varies by species. Rotenone highly toxic to aquatic insects and zooplankton. Rapid recolonization expected due to: (1) upstream and downstream refugia, (2) active drifter life history, (3) short life-cycles, (4) high organic inputs in treatment area. No long-term effects on population abundance, dominant species, or species diversity documented (Sousa et al. 1987). Crayfish not negatively impacted; rotenone residues in crayfish peak at 35% of treatment concentration six hours post-treatment, decline to detection limit by day seven (Gilderhus et al. 1988). Snails and clams not affected.

Birds and Mammals: Not directly affected; rotenone toxicity to birds and mammals very low. No bioaccumulation. Indirect effects possible through temporary displacement of insectivorous and piscivorous species. Of 19 bat species in Washington County, only five rely on aquatic insects as more than occasional diet component: [[Big Brown Bat]] (*Eptesicus fuscus*), [[Silver-haired Bat]] (*Lasionycteris noctivagans*), [[Long-eared Bat]] (*Myotis evotis*), [[Long-legged Bat]] (*Myotis volans*), and [[Yuma Bat]] (*Myotis yumanensis*). None feed entirely on aquatic insects; no adverse effects expected. Southwestern willow flycatcher may be temporarily affected by reduced aquatic insect availability, though proportion of aquatic insects in diet unknown. Riparian disturbance from treatment crews may temporarily disturb flycatchers; surveys prior to treatment will identify presence and allow avoidance. Peregrine falcon may experience indirect effects through reduced prey (wrens and swifts) availability. No effects expected on bald eagle (winter residents only; treatments in spring/late summer-fall), Mexican spotted owl (does not feed on aquatic species), or desert tortoise (does not occur in river or floodplain).

*Hydrology*: Water diversions may be necessary to minimize flows; coordinated with Washington County Water Conservancy District, Army Corps of Engineers, and affected landowners. Short-term impacts to water users/rights possible. Seegmiller Marsh will be drained for approximately two weeks; no long-term impacts anticipated. Rotenone neutralized with potassium permanganate; any unneutralized rotenone decomposes rapidly. Half-life in warm water ponds: 0.94 days (Gilderhus et al. 1988). At 80°F, natural detoxification in less than four days. Rotenone non-persistent; does not accumulate in water, soil, plants, or surviving animals.

*Land and Water Resources*: Agricultural and municipal water use not adversely impacted. EPA determined no basis for concern using rotenone-treated water for irrigation. Rotenone rapidly breaks down; does not accumulate in plant materials. Concentrations used pose no health hazards to people or livestock. Municipal water sources not affected; normal treatment processes would remove/destroy traces of rotenone. Oral toxicity estimates: 300-500 mg rotenone per kg body weight. A 132-pound person would need to consume over 60,000 liters (15,790 gallons) of treated water at one sitting for lethal dose. With 1,000x safety factor, could drink 14 liters treated water per day below conservative safe intake level. Dead fish accumulation along banks minimal due to low fish density, remote treatment areas, and downstream transport. Fish small and decompose rapidly. If objectionable accumulation occurs near habitation, crews will remove and dispose in landfill.

*Recreation*: Mildly affected due to low use level. Local notices posted. EPA suggests swimmers wait four hours before entering treated water as precaution. Rafting, kayaking, tubing discouraged during treatment. Light swimming at Washington Fields Diversion affected for few days; swimmers can move upstream. All-terrain vehicle users may temporarily refrain from channel use. Fishing and boating (restricted to reservoirs above treatment areas) not affected. Golf course and park irrigation not affected. Downstream camping and hiking in Virgin River Gorge not affected.

*Cultural Resources*: No cultural resources affected by preferred alternative or buffer zone alternative. Native American sites from Archaic period and Anasazi and Basketmaker cultures occur in area, as do historical relics from Mormon colonization.

**Rotenone Properties** (from Appendix C): Natural substance from tropical plant roots (*Derris* spp., *Lonchocarpus* spp., *Tephrosia* spp.). Inhibits biochemical process at cellular level, preventing oxygen use in energy release. Selective to fish at fishery management concentrations. Trout and salmon most sensitive; sunfish less sensitive; catfish most resistant. Fish highly susceptible because rotenone readily absorbed through gills and cannot escape exposure. Toxicity reversible with potassium permanganate or chlorine (1:1 ratio plus additional to satisfy chemical oxidation demand). Breaks down when exposed to light, heat, oxygen, and alkaline water. Approximately 20 degradation products identified; most spontaneously break down to non-toxic substances. Ultimately breaks down to carbon dioxide and water. Mobility in soil low to slight; expected leaching distance only 2 cm in most soils (8 cm in sandy soils). Strongly bound to organic matter; unlikely to enter groundwater. Maximum residue in fish flesh from 5.0 ppm treatment at 74°F: approximately 0.15 ppm. No evidence of carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, teratogenicity, or reproductive effects in extensive EPA-reviewed testing.
