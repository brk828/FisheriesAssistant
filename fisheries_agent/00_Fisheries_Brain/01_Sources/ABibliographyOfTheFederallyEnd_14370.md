# A Bibliography of the Federally Endangered Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans) in the Colorado River of the American Southwest

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River #bonytail-chub

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive bibliography, prepared by Terry C. Inman, [[Paul C. Marsh]], and [[W.L. Minckley]] at [[Arizona State University]] in June 1995, catalogs scientific literature on the federally endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) from the [[Colorado River]] system. The work spans 12 volumes containing references from Abbott (1861) through Wydoski et al. (1991), documenting over 130 years of research on this critically imperiled cyprinid. The bibliography was prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and Arizona State University's Hayden Library Special Collections, serving as a foundational resource for recovery efforts.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) is a large-bodied cyprinid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was first described by Baird and Girard in 1853. Historical records document its presence throughout the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and major tributaries including the [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], [[White River]], [[San Juan River]], and [[Gila River]]. The bibliography encompasses taxonomic descriptions, life history studies, population surveys, habitat assessments, and conservation efforts spanning multiple states: Arizona, Nevada, Utah, Colorado, New Mexico, and Wyoming.

Key taxonomic and systematic works include early descriptions by Cope (1871), Girard (1856, 1858, 1859), Jordan and Evermann (1896), and modern systematic studies by Holden and Stalnaker (1970), Smith et al. (1979), and Rosenfeld (1991) examining relationships within the genus *Gila*. The bibliography documents the species' decline from a common component of the Colorado River fish fauna to one of North America's most endangered fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bibliography documents dramatic habitat alterations in the [[Colorado River Basin]] that contributed to the bonytail's decline. Major factors include:

**Dam Construction and Flow Modification**: References document impacts from [[Glen Canyon Dam]], [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Hoover Dam]], and numerous other water development projects. Studies by Vanicek (1967), Holden and Stalnaker (1975), and Tyus and Karp (1989, 1991) examined streamflow needs and habitat use patterns. The Green River fish eradication project (1962-1963) using rotenone is extensively documented (Binns et al. 1963, Andriano 1963, Franklin 1963).

**Reservoir Ecology**: Multiple studies examined bonytail populations in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Mead]] (Jonez and Sumner 1954, Wagner 1955, Bozek et al. 1984, Paulson et al. 1981, Marsh and Minckley 1989, 1992). Bozek (1984) investigated reproductive success, finding factors limiting natural recruitment in reservoir environments.

**Nonnative Species**: The bibliography includes references to competition and predation from introduced fishes, including rainbow smelt introduction to [[Lake Powell]] (Gustaveson 1989) and general impacts of nonnative species on the Colorado River fish community.

**Water Quality**: Studies examined temperature tolerance (Marsh 1982, 1985), toxicity of pesticides (Beyers et al. 1994), contaminants (Hamilton and Buhl 1992), and total dissolved solids preferences (Bulkley et al. 1981, 1983).

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Distribution**: The bibliography documents the species' catastrophic decline. Kaeding et al. (1986) reported a recent capture representing the first bonytail in Colorado in 20 years. By the 1980s-1990s, wild populations were functionally extinct, with only occasional captures in [[Lake Mohave]] (Baucom 1991, 1993) and rare sightings elsewhere. Joseph (1978) mapped historical capture locations throughout the upper basin.

**Recovery Efforts**: Extensive documentation of recovery programs includes:
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] propagation efforts (Jensen 1980-1993, Hamman 1981, 1982, 1985, 1988)
- Recovery Implementation Program for the Upper Colorado River Basin (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1986, 1989, 1990)
- Revised Recovery Plan (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1990)
- Experimental stockings in refugia including [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]], [[Arizona State University Research Park]], and Palm Lake (Marsh 1988, Hendrickson 1988, 1990)
- A catastrophic fish loss at Dexter Hatchery in March 1992 killed numerous endangered bonytail (Jensen 1992, Frazier 1992, Self 1992, Hutcheson 1993)

**Life History and Reproduction**: Hamman (1982, 1985) successfully induced spawning of hatchery-reared bonytail. Marsh (1982, 1985) determined optimal incubation temperatures for embryo survival (18-24°C). Muth (1988, 1990) provided detailed descriptions for larval identification. Hybridization with other *Gila* species was documented in hatcheries (Hamman 1981) and wild populations.

**Genetics and Taxonomy**: Minckley et al. (1989) examined allozyme variation in hatchery broodstock, finding limited genetic diversity. DeMarais et al. (1992) demonstrated that *Gila seminuda* (Virgin River roundtail chub) originated through introgressive hybridization. Dowling and DeMarais (1993) discussed evolutionary significance of introgression in cyprinid fishes. Rosenfeld (1991) conducted systematic studies using protein electrophoresis and cytogenetics.

**Habitat Requirements**: Bulkley et al. (1981) determined tolerance and preference for habitat parameters. Valdez et al. (1987, 1990) developed habitat suitability index curves. Studies documented use of backwaters, eddies, and low-velocity habitats, particularly by juveniles.

**Legal and Management Framework**: The species was listed as endangered in 1980 (Federal Register 45:27710-27713). The bibliography includes numerous Section 7 consultations, biological opinions, critical habitat designations (Tyus 1993), and interagency coordination documents. The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (Public Law 93-205) provided the legal framework for recovery efforts.

**Historical and Archaeological Context**: Fish remains from archaeological sites (Miller 1955, Gehlbach and Miller 1961, Minckley and Alger 1968) documented prehistoric distribution and Native American utilization of bonytail and other Colorado River fishes.

**Monitoring and Survey Methods**: The bibliography documents development of sampling protocols, tagging methods (Valdez et al. 1981, Burdick and Hamman 1993), and annual monitoring programs in [[Lake Mohave]], the [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], and other locations throughout the 1970s-1990s.
