## Executive Summary
This paper describes **Hyalella texana**, a new species of freshwater amphipod (Crustacea: Amphipoda) discovered at Clear Creek Spring, Menard County, Texas, on the Edwards Plateau. It occurs sympatrically with the widespread **[[Hyalella azteca]]** but is morphologically, ecologically, and reproductively distinct, satisfying a biological species concept (no hybrids found despite co-occurrence and captive breeding). While the paper's core focus is invertebrate taxonomy and microhabitat ecology, it also touches on the endemic fish **[[Gambusia heterochir]]** (Clear Creek gambusia) and the widespread **[[Gambusia affinis]]** as predators of these amphipods, framing the system as a candidate for coevolutionary study of predator-prey morphology.

## Background & Description

### Hyalella texana (diagnostic features)
- **Coloration:** Uniform orange body with a distinctive darker orange middorsal stripe running from the first thoracic segment to the last abdominal tergite (absent in *H. azteca*).
- **Tergite spines:** Four large, posteriorly-projecting middorsal spines — one each on the last thoracic segment and abdominal segments 1–3 (vs. two short spines on abdominal segments 1–2 only in *H. azteca*).
- **Antennae:** First antenna less than half the length of the second; no accessory flagellum; second antenna exceeds half of body length; flagellar segment counts on the second antenna are roughly double those of similarly-sized *H. azteca*.
- **Mouthparts:** Enlarged; maxillipeds broad-based, overlapping anterior mouthparts, with three enlarged teeth on the inner plate and small apical spines on the inner anterior surface.
- **Gnathopods:** Sexually dimorphic; male second gnathopod propodus bears 4 posterior-border setae vs. 2 in females; second gnathopod much larger in males than the first.
- **Coxal plates:** Short and narrow; anterior plates do not overlap mouthparts; proportionally smaller fourth coxal plate than in *H. azteca* (diagnostic, sexually dimorphic trait).
- **Body size:** Frequently exceeds 8 mm total length; sex ratio skewed toward females in samples.
- **Ontogeny:** Juveniles <2 mm show only two tergite spines; a third spine (last thoracic segment) appears by 3 mm; the fourth spine (third abdominal segment) develops after 5 mm total length.

### Comparison to Hyalella azteca
- *H. azteca* is grayish, lacks a middorsal stripe, shows vertical orange banding on tergite sides and orange spotting at tergite–coxal/pleural junctions.
- *H. azteca* retains only two short tergite spines regardless of size increase beyond 2 mm.
- Body of *H. texana* is dorsally narrower and more laterally compressed anteriorly than *H. azteca*.
- Regression analyses (antennal segment counts, coxal plate dimensions vs. body length) confirm consistent allometric and diagnostic differences between the species (see Figs. 4–6 in source).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure (Clear Creek Spring system):**
- Spring-fed limestone stream with head springs, seeps, and three sequential man-made impoundments altering natural flow and vegetation.
- Upper impoundment: thermally stable (21 ± 1.5°C year-round), dominated by fine-leafed *Ceratophyllum* sp., with patches of *Ludwigia*, floating filamentous algae, and *Nuphar* (water lilies).
- Overflow/downstream zones: mixed *Myriophyllum* and *Ceratophyllum*, moss (*Fissidens*) mats, *Potamogeton*, and stiffer-fronded *Ceratophyllum demersum*; substrate transitions to muddy, sparsely vegetated shallows with strong seasonal/diurnal temperature fluctuation (7–30°C).

**Microhabitat partitioning between species:**
- **[[Hyalella texana]]**: Dominant and often the sole amphipod in the thermally stable upper impoundment; found in *Ceratophyllum* fronds, floating algal mats, rooted vegetation (*Nasturtium*, *Ludwigia*), and *Fissidens* moss mats below the dam. Restricted to within a few hundred meters of the spring sources.
- **[[Hyalella azteca]]**: Absent above the upper dam and from main springs/seeps; dominant in shallow, thermally fluctuating, sparsely vegetated detrital habitats (<0.5 m depth); ranges up to ~5 km downstream — a far broader spatial distribution than *H. texana*.
- **Zone of sympatry:** Both species co-occur near seep-influenced mixed vegetation below the upper dam, but no morphologically intermediate adults (e.g., three-spined individuals) were found, supporting reproductive/species distinctness.

**Population densities:**
- *H. texana*: ~1,300/m² in fine-leafed *Ceratophyllum*; ~9,555/m² in algal mats; up to **33,000/m²** in *Fissidens* mats below the dam.
- *H. azteca*: generally <1,900/m² where it occurred alone.

**Predator interactions:**
- **[[Gambusia heterochir]]** (endemic Clear Creek gambusia), restricted mainly to the area above the upper dam, feeds substantially on *H. texana*, aided by a comparatively powerful jaw, sturdier teeth, and blunter gill rakers — traits interpreted as adaptations for amphipod predation.
- **[[Gambusia affinis]]** (widespread mosquitofish) feeds only occasionally on *H. texana*, consistent with weaker dental/jaw specialization.
- The authors propose that *H. texana*'s enlarged tergite spines and possible predator-avoidance behavior may represent a coevolved anti-predator defense against *G. heterochir* predation pressure — a candidate system for coevolutionary study.

## Key Findings
- **New species description:** *Hyalella texana* n. sp. is formally distinguished from *H. azteca* by four large tergite spines, enlarged mouthparts, more numerous antennal segments, and a narrower, more laterally compressed body/appendage morphology.
- **Sympatric speciation without hybridization:** Despite co-occurring at Clear Creek Spring and being kept together in aquaria for months, no hybrid individuals were produced, and no three-spined intermediate adults were found in the wild zone of sympatry — supporting biological species status.
- **Habitat specialization drives coexistence:** The two species partition the spring system by thermal regime and vegetation type — *H. texana* restricted to stable-temperature, vegetated upper spring/impoundment habitats; *H. azteca* occupying fluctuating-temperature, detrital downstream habitats over a much larger geographic range.
- **Endemism and biogeography:** *H. texana* appears endemic to the Clear Creek Spring system on the Edwards Plateau; no other amphipod populations with four tergite spines were found elsewhere on the plateau, though some populations with three spines of uncertain taxonomic affinity exist.
- **Predator-prey coevolution hypothesis:** Differential feeding intensity between the endemic *[[Gambusia heterochir]]* and widespread *[[Gambusia affinis]]* on *H. texana*, correlated with jaw/dentition differences in the fish and defensive spine morphology in the amphipod, is proposed as evidence of localized coevolution within this isolated spring ecosystem.
- **Conservation/research significance:** Clear Creek Spring hosts multiple locally endemic taxa (*H. texana*, *G. heterochir*), making it a valuable natural laboratory for studying evolutionary and ecological processes in isolated freshwater spring systems.
