# Biological Opinion: Emergency Watershed Protection Project at TNC Residence on Aravaipa Creek

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) Biological Opinion (Consultation No. 2-21-93-F-166, dated October 20, 1993) evaluates an Emergency Watershed Protection (EWP) project undertaken by the Soil Conservation Service (SCS) at a [[The Nature Conservancy]] residence on [[Aravaipa Creek]], Graham County, Arizona. The project — streambank stabilization using rock rip-rap following severe erosion from winter 1992–1993 high flows — was assessed for effects on the federally threatened **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*) and **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*), both endemic to the Gila River basin and both potentially present in proposed critical habitat within the project area. The Service concluded the project **is not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of either species and **did not destroy or adversely modify** proposed critical habitat, although an unquantified number of individual fish may have been taken (killed/injured) during construction, which occurred prior to completion of formal consultation.

## Background & Description

- **Trigger:** High flows during winter 1992–1993 caused significant bank erosion at TNC property along Aravaipa Creek, prompting SCS to invoke the Emergency Watershed Protection program.
- **Consultation timeline:** SCS contacted the Service on January 24, 1993; emergency consultation requested February 26, 1993; formal consultation request received August 6, 1993. Because the action qualified as an emergency, construction proceeded under informal consultation before formal Section 7 review was completed.
- **Interim protective measures (specified March 10, 1993, tied to a Clean Water Act Section 404 permit):**
  1. Minimize machinery use in flowing or standing water.
  2. Minimize downstream sediment transport from the construction area.
  3. Minimize removal of fallen trees/downed vegetation from stream and riparian zone.
  4. Require an on-site biologist during all construction activities.
- **Project actions:** Removal of tree debris; re-grading of a sloping south bank using channel material; placement of approximately 525 linear feet of rock rip-rap on the new slope; a toe-of-slope trench dug and filled with rock; temporary diversion of creek flow to the north bank to allow dry-channel construction; use of small (4 ft × 10 ft) hand-built settling ponds to clarify pumped trench water before discharge back into the creek, reducing sediment loading. Native cottonwood and willow riparian plantings were planned for after November 15, 1993.

### Project Area Habitat Description
- Aravaipa Creek is a tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] in southeastern Arizona, draining portions of Graham and Pinal Counties.
- Land ownership in the basin: Bureau of Land Management, U.S. Forest Service, State of Arizona, and private entities (including TNC preserve lands).
- The lower creek near the San Pedro confluence is intermittent; the **project-area reach is perennial**.
- Habitat composition: dominated by **shallow runs** (sand, gravel, pebble substrates) with **riffles** (boulder, cobble, pebble substrates) comprising the remainder of available aquatic habitat (Arizona Game and Fish Department 1992).
- **Native fish species dominate the fish fauna** in this reach, making Aravaipa Creek a recognized native-fish stronghold — though non-native fish species have recently been detected in some areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Species Accounts

**[[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*)**
- Endemic to the Gila River basin; populations reduced to isolated remnants of former range in Arizona and New Mexico.
- Occupies small-to-moderate streams or tributary mouths of larger rivers.
- Adult habitat: flowing water usually <1 m deep over gravel/sand bars, downstream riffle edges, or shear zones.
- Juveniles: found near pool margins over fine-grained substrates.
- Spawning: shallow sand/gravel riffles; eggs likely adhere to substrate; primary spawning period **April–June**.
- Feeds in the water column (sight-feeder), making it vulnerable to suspended sediment affecting prey visibility.

**[[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*)**
- Endemic to the Gila River basin; similarly reduced to isolated populations in Arizona and New Mexico.
- Inhabits turbulent, rocky riffles in mainstem rivers and tributaries.
- Habitat features: shallow water, moderate-to-swift currents, gravel-to-cobble substrates.
- A benthic (bottom-dwelling) species utilizing interstitial spaces/shelter within substrate.
- Spawning period: **late March–early June**; adhesive eggs deposited in cavities under flattened rocks.
- Greater vulnerability to construction-related crushing/desiccation due to bottom-dwelling habit, though limited availability of preferred substrate in the project reach may have reduced overall risk.

### Environmental Baseline / Threats
- Both species listed as **threatened in 1986**; no substantial improvement in status documented since listing.
- Remaining populations are geographically **isolated**, precluding natural recolonization and increasing vulnerability to local habitat degradation.
- Basin-wide stressors: water diversions/return flows, livestock grazing, timber harvest, and altered flow regimes from dams.
- **Non-native fish species** presence compounds degraded-habitat effects; no pristine refugia remain, even in Aravaipa Creek.
- A separate, related consultation covered a similar EWP rip-rap bank-stabilization project at the "Jep White residence" downstream on Aravaipa Creek.
- Other bank disturbances from 1993 high flows occurred elsewhere in the drainage without SCS involvement or Section 404 permitting review.

## Key Findings

1. **Biological Opinion determination:** The project is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of spikedace or loach minnow, and proposed critical habitat was **not destroyed or adversely modified**.
2. **Direct effects (occurred prior to formal consultation, magnitude unquantified):**
   - Possible localized sediment increase from bank/channel disturbance, mitigated by flow diversion and settling ponds.
   - Suspended sediment could impair sight-feeding by spikedace (water-column feeder) and affect benthic invertebrate prey availability for loach minnow.
   - Possible mortality of individual fish from equipment crushing or desiccation after flow diversion; loach minnow considered more vulnerable due to bottom-dwelling habits, though limited preferred substrate in the immediate area may have reduced exposure.
   - Timing was favorable: construction completed **before the spring spawning season** for both species, and creek flows likely flushed excess sediment downstream and out of critical habitat.
3. **Habitat structure:** Placement of rip-rap on hard substrate (sand-to-cobble habitat) is not expected to degrade substrate long-term, but **armored/altered banks lack the natural microhabitat diversity** of unmodified streambanks and may alter natural channel sinuosity/dynamics over time.
4. **Incidental take statement:** An **unknown/unquantifiable number** of individual spikedace and loach minnow may have been taken via habitat disturbance (diversion, substrate shifting, sedimentation, rip-rap placement). The four March 10, 1993 minimization measures were implemented by SCS; no additional reasonable-and-prudent measures were deemed necessary.
5. **Cumulative effects:** Future non-federal bank-stabilization or flood-control activities on Aravaipa Creek would likely require Section 404 permitting (triggering federal nexus); no other private/state actions were foreseen at the time.
6. **Conservation recommendation:** Service recommended SCS evaluate the completed rip-rap project for potential habitat enhancement/restoration opportunities to offset impacts and benefit native fish habitat.
7. **Broader significance:** Aravaipa Creek is highlighted as a critical stronghold for **survival and recovery** of both threatened species; cumulative bank-armoring projects, even individually non-jeopardizing, risk eroding the creek's long-term value for these fish if unchecked.
