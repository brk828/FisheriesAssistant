# The Interdependence of Archeology and Ecology, with Examples from Middle America

#paleoecology #archaeology #palynology #limnology #ichthyology #marine-biology

> **Note on scope:** This source is fundamentally a paleoecological/archaeological paper centered on pollen (palynological) analysis of lake sediments to reconstruct past climates and human cultures in the [[Basin Of Mexico]]. It is **not** a fish-focused ichthyological study. The only direct link to fish science is a citation reference to the [[Boylston Street Fish-Weir]], an archaeological fish-trapping structure in Boston. Aquatic ecosystem content herein pertains to **lake sediment stratigraphy, lake-level fluctuations, and freshwater paleolimnology**, which are extracted below in place of fish-specific morphological/ecological data.

## Executive Summary

Paul B. Sears' paper argues for a mutually reinforcing relationship between [[Archeology]] and [[Paleoecology]], using pollen analysis (palynology) of lake and bog sediments to reconstruct vegetation, climate, and lake-level history in the [[Basin Of Mexico]] and correlate these with archaeological culture sequences. Central aquatic evidence comes from **lacustrine sediment cores** (peat, silt, clay) from ancient lake beds, which preserve stratified pollen records used to infer moisture regimes, lake level changes, and the timing of human settlement shifts (e.g., movement between lowland lake-margin sites and highland sites like [[Teotihuacan]]). A single explicit fish-related reference — the **Boylston Street Fish-weir** in Boston — is cited as a landmark collaborative archaeological-ecological study in North America, though no biological or morphological data on fish are provided in this text.

## Background & Description

No fish species, morphology, or anatomical data are described in this source. The paper's "specimens" of analytical interest are **pollen and spore fossils**, not fish anatomy:

- **Pollen/spore preservation**: Described as among the most resistant of fossils; wind-dispersed pollen is abundant and representative of dominant regional vegetation, while insect-dispersed pollen is rare and sporadic.
- **Indicator taxa** used to reconstruct paleoclimate/moisture gradients:
  - *Oak, alder* — indicate moist lowland/mid-elevation sites
  - *Fir* — indicates higher, moister elevations
  - *Pine* — indicates less humid conditions
  - *Cereal pollen (maize/Zea mays) and ruderals* (plantain, wormwood, amaranths, chenopods) — indicators of forest clearance and agriculture

No ichthyological morphological descriptions (fin counts, body form, scale counts, etc.) are present in the text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While not a fish-habitat study, the paper extensively treats **lacustrine ecosystem dynamics** relevant to aquatic paleoecology:

- **Lake sediment accumulation**: Occurs at a "remarkably uniform rate" except during volcanism, tectonic-driven erosion, human interference, or extreme aridity — a key control on the reliability of stratigraphic dating.
- **Lake-level fluctuation as climate proxy**: Lower lake levels correlate with dry (xerothermic) periods (e.g., ~500 B.C. desiccation event marked by a volcanic ash layer at the [[El Tepalcate]] site); higher lake levels correlate with humid intervals (e.g., resurgence near the 9th century A.D., coinciding with Nahua/Aztec lacustrine culture).
- **Human settlement and lacustrine resource use**: Two major "waves of lacustrine culture" occupied the lower Basin of Mexico — the Middle/Archaic culture (2000–500 B.C.) and the Nahua culture (800/900 A.D. through the Conquest) — separated by a prolonged dry interval during which cultural activity shifted to higher ground ([[Teotihuacan]]) reliant on groundwater rather than lake margins.
- **Analogous freshwater system**: The San Augustin Plains area of western New Mexico shows a parallel pattern — maize-growing cultures (Bat Cave) situated along a playa lake margin, with later cultures shifting to higher ground near groundwater sources following lake desiccation.
- **Fish-weir reference**: The [[Boylston Street Fish-Weir]] (Johnson, 1949) is cited as an example of intensive fish-trapping infrastructure examined jointly by archaeologists and ecologists, representing direct human exploitation of an aquatic/estuarine fish resource, though the current paper provides no further ecological detail on the fishery itself.

## Key Findings

- **Pollen stratigraphy method**: Establishing a regional pollen profile allows (1) stratigraphic placement of isolated archaeological finds, (2) reconstruction of vegetation/climate conditions tied to a culture's stratigraphic position, (3) cross-regional correlation of contemporaneous cultures, and (4) detection of the onset of agriculture via cereal and ruderal pollen.
- **Peat growth rate constant**: A conventional peat accumulation rate of ~300 years/foot was derived for the Erie Basin, later corroborated by annual layer compression rates and depth-dating of spruce needles — though the author cautions this constant must be applied carefully, especially where peat growth was interrupted.
- **Chronology alignment**: Sears' pollen-based dating of the xerothermic maximum (~3000 years ago) and the spruce-fir (Lake Mankato) period (~10,500 years ago) closely matches independent radiocarbon dates (~11,000 years).
- **Maize pollen discoveries**: Kathryn Clisby recovered abundant maize pollen at 9 meters depth (corresponding to early Archaic) under Mexico City, and additional maize-like pollen grains at 75 meters depth — far below any known level of human activity — raising unresolved questions about the deep-time origins of maize (later work by Wolfe & Barghoorn at Harvard supported a maize identification based on grain size metrics).
- **Deep sediment cores**: Two concordant deep cores beneath Mexico City, extending into the Pleistocene, corroborate a similarly deep core from northern New Mexico, with a further 645-foot core from western New Mexico planned for analysis — providing a long-term freshwater sedimentary record for cross-regional paleoclimate correlation.
- **Cultural-ecological interpretation**: The abandonment of Teotihuacan is attributed to deforestation-driven depletion of local groundwater/water supply (supporting and extending Vaillant's and Cook's erosion hypotheses), rather than solely to regional crop failure.
- **Broader implication**: Regional desiccation (e.g., ~500 B.C.) may have favored cultural expansion into humid tropical forest regions, while later humid reversals (post-9th century A.D.) likely had the opposite effect — though no direct pollen profiles from Maya lowland regions were yet available to confirm this.
