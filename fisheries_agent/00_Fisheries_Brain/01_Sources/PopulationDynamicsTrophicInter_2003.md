# Population Dynamics, Trophic Interactions, and Production of Northern Pike in a Shallow Bog Lake and Their Effects on Simulated Regulation Strategies

#ichthyology #Esocidae #northern-pike #bog-lake #population-dynamics #fisheries-management

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) in Camerton Lake (28.3 ha, maximum depth 3.0 m) formed a dense population with 59.0 fish/ha for age-2 and older fish. Population structure was dominated by young fish: most individuals were age-3 or younger, with less than 3% older than age-5. Maximum observed age was 7 years. Sex ratio was 50:50 overall, though males predominated in older age-groups. Total annual mortality was exceptionally high at A = 63% (instantaneous Z = 0.987 for ages 2–7), with males experiencing A = 56% (Z = 0.830) and females A = 67% (Z = 1.112). Recreational fishing mortality was minimal (F = 0.053, only 3.4% tag returns), indicating natural mortality was the dominant component of total mortality. Maturity information not reported. Fecundity not reported.

**Growth Parameters**: Growth was moderate and comparable to international standards through age-4. Mean backcalculated length at age-3 was 460 mm (SE = 3 mm) for females and 440 mm (SE = 3 mm) for males. Von Bertalanffy growth parameters were fitted separately by sex (specific parameter values not reported in text). Growth through age-4 matched the international growth standard (Casselman 1996), but male growth slowed after age-4 relative to the standard. Weisberg and Frie's linear growth model revealed negligible year-to-year (environmental) variation in growth rates; most variation in scale increments was attributable to age effects rather than year effects or year-class interactions. Scale increment growth was fastest through age-4, then slowed. The analysis included relatively cool summers (1992–1993), yet year effects remained unimportant.

**Habitat Associations**: Camerton Lake is a shallow bog lake with brown-stained water, low total alkalinity (3 mg CaCO₃/L), and pH 6.1. The lake is surrounded by bog and deciduous forest. Margins are covered with floating mats of sedges (*Carex* spp.), mosses (*Sphagnum* spp.), and ericaceous shrubs. The lake is shallow enough that wind mixes the entire water column, creating uniform temperatures with depth. The fish community includes [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), and minnows (Cyprinidae) in addition to northern pike. In Minnesota, 112 lakes share characteristics of shallow bog lakes according to Schupp's (1992) classification system.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Not reported. Fish were captured during spawning (ice-out, 6–15 April 1998) using trap nets with leads extending into shore, suggesting nearshore spawning habitat use typical of northern pike.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: [[Yellow Perch]] were the dominant prey item, sustaining much of the growth and secondary production of northern pike. Of 382 northern pike examined (mean length 441 mm, range 268–682 mm), 190 had empty stomachs. Among 192 fish with food, fish remains (especially yellow perch) were found in 148 stomachs. A total of 77 yellow perch were identified from 73 stomachs, with mean total length of consumed perch at 109 mm (SE = 7 mm)—substantially smaller than the 178 mm (SE = 2 mm) mean length of yellow perch caught in gill nets. Gill-net catch rates for yellow perch averaged 16 fish/net (SE = 2 fish/net). Other prey included 12 small minnows, 4 black bullheads, 4 northern pike (cannibalism), and 72 unidentified fish (likely many were yellow perch). [[Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*) were the second most important prey item, found in 19 stomachs (21 individuals total); crayfish were consumed during June–July when northern pike grow rapidly, suggesting significant contribution to annual production. Aquatic insects (Odonata, Trichoptera) and leeches (Hirudinea) were found in 18 stomachs. Other vertebrates (rodents, frogs, tadpoles) occurred in 11 stomachs.

**Environmental Drivers**: The shallow nature of Camerton Lake means air temperature influences water temperature more than in deeper lakes, yet the linear growth model showed very little year-to-year variation in growth rates despite including relatively cool summers (1992–1993). This suggests northern pike growth in this bog lake is relatively stable across environmental variation, possibly buffered by consistent prey availability (yellow perch).

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: The intensive removal experiment (nearly half of age-2 and older fish removed via gill netting, 15 June–30 July 1998) provided a snapshot of population structure. Petersen population estimates by age-group: age-2 = 626 fish (485–806, 95% CI), age-3 = 739 (621–887), age-4 = 192 (162–244), age-5 = 69 (56–109), age-6 = 42 (33–81), age-7 = 4 (3–11). Total population estimate for age-2 and older was 1,672 fish (59.0 fish/ha). Males: 814 fish (28.7 fish/ha); females: 814 fish (28.7 fish/ha). The high density (59 fish/ha) was among the highest reported in the literature (range 2.8–63.3 fish/ha from other studies). High natural mortality (M ≈ 0.85) prevented accumulation of older, larger fish. Long-term trajectory not reported; study was a single-year intensive removal.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Not reported. The study focused on population dynamics rather than conservation threats. Public access to recreational angling was not restricted, but fishing was somewhat limited by poor access road. Angling regulations followed statewide restrictions: three-fish bag limit, with only one fish allowed over 762 mm total length.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: High natural mortality (M = 0.85) critically limits the effectiveness of harvest regulations in restructuring the northern pike population. Simulation modeling demonstrated that minimum length limits produced the greatest improvements in sizes and ages of fish creeled, but at the expense of large reductions in yield. A 508-mm minimum length limit and 610-mm minimum length limit both increased mean harvest length (545 mm and 630 mm, respectively) and age (4.7 and 6.6 years) compared to no length regulation (473 mm, 3.2 years), but reduced yield from 2.1 kg/ha to 0.9 kg/ha and 0.1 kg/ha, respectively. Maximum length limits (508 mm and 610 mm) showed no benefits in standing stocks, catches, or yields compared to no length regulation. The 508–762-mm protected slot limit functioned identically to the 508-mm maximum length limit. Complete catch-and-release fishing increased standing stock to 31.9 kg/ha (versus 29.0 kg/ha with no regulation) but eliminated yield. High natural mortality prevented stockpiling of larger, older individuals under minimum length limit scenarios; comparatively few fish grew above minimum lengths and survived. Low exploitation of small fish, coupled with high natural mortality, constrain the ability to restructure northern pike populations in bog lakes through harvest regulation changes.

**Biological Reference Points**: Estimated density for age-2 and older fish: 59.0 fish/ha. Total annual mortality: A = 63% (Z = 0.987). Natural mortality: M ≈ 0.85 (critically high). Recreational fishing mortality: F = 0.053 (minimal). Exploitation rate at current effort (22 h/ha): u = 0.05. Even when fishing effort was tripled (66 h/ha) in simulations, exploitation remained u < 0.15. Secondary production: 8.3 kg/ha/year for all fish pooled (4.0 kg/ha for males, 4.8 kg/ha for females).
