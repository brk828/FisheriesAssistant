# Current and Predicted Future Status of the Spikedace and Loach Minnow in the Lower Gila River System of New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #ConservationPolicy

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of correspondence and an associated research report authored by James W. Hartshorne on behalf of the [[Gila Fish & Gun Club]], addressed to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]]. The material advocates against the designation of critical habitat for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) under the Endangered Species Act, in direct connection with efforts to revive the proposed **Conner (Hooker) Dam Project** on the Gila River in Grant County, New Mexico.

The central scientific argument advanced is that habitat loss from dam construction is *not* the primary driver of decline for these two native cyprinids. Instead, the report attributes their collapse to competitive displacement by the non-native [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), citing survey data from [[Minckley (1973)]] and [[Propst (1986)]] (NM Dept. of Game & Fish Endangered Species Report No. 15). The author concludes that the Spikedace and Loach Minnow are "destined for extinction regardless of whether or not the Conner Dam is constructed," and recommends that critical habitat designation be reconsidered or delayed, and that the dam construction prohibition be lifted.

## Background & Description

The source is policy/advocacy-focused and contains minimal direct morphological description. Species-specific identifying data extracted:

- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*): A small native cyprinid minnow endemic to the Gila River System; federally determined as a **threatened species** per 51 FR 23769 (July 1, 1986, Federal Register).
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Native cyprinid, co-occurring with Spikedace in the same habitat range; noted by the author to be "more prone to predation by the catfishes and possibly other species" compared to Spikedace, suggesting a bottom-dwelling or more sedentary ecomorphology consistent with the "loach" common name.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*): Non-native cyprinid introduced west of the Continental Divide in Arizona; described as a highly successful competitor/predator that "proliferates during periods of low water" due to restricted ranges of competing species (Minckley 1973).
- **[[Flathead Catfish]]**: Non-native predatory species noted as concurrently colonizing the Cliff-Gila Valley alongside Red Shiner by the 1983–84 survey period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native Range**: Spikedace and Loach Minnow are endemic to the **Gila River System** in Arizona and New Mexico, historically occupying waters up to roughly 5,000 feet elevation.
- **Critical Habitat Zones**:
  - **[[Cliff-Gila Valley]]**: Identified by USFWS biologist [[Gerald Burton]] as the "last stronghold" of the Spikedace in New Mexico.
  - **[[Redrock and Virden Valleys]]**: Site of a documented population collapse, used as a predictive case study for the Cliff-Gila Valley.
  - Reference populations in **[[Aravaipa Creek]]** and the **upper Verde River** (Arizona) are cited as locations where native species may be coexisting with or avoiding predation by Red Shiner — flagged as needing further study.
- **Invasive Species Dynamics**: The [[Red Shiner]], originally distributed only east of the Continental Divide in New Mexico ([[Koster 1957]]), spread west into Arizona and by 1970 had colonized most lower-elevation Gila Basin waters. By the late 1970s it had migrated into New Mexico's Redrock and Virden Valleys, and by 1983–84 into the Cliff-Gila Valley.
- **Competitive Exclusion Mechanism**: Minckley (1973) documented that "where the red shiner holds forth, the native minnows are essentially absent," implicating direct displacement/competition rather than habitat degradation as the principal mechanism of decline. Red Shiner is noted to proliferate under low-water conditions, while predation pressure on natives decreases at higher water levels due to greater species dispersal.

## Key Findings

1. **Primary Threat Reassessment**: Additional habitat loss (e.g., from dam construction) is argued **not** to be the major threat to Spikedace/Loach Minnow survival in the lower Gila River, NM — displacement by the invasive Red Shiner is presented as the dominant causal factor.

2. **Redrock-Virden Valley Collapse Timeline** (used as a predictive analog):
   - Pre-1970: Spikedace relative abundance = 13.6%
   - 1970–1979: Spikedace relative abundance = 24.8%
   - 1983–84 survey: Red Shiner relative abundance reached ~47%
   - Spring 1985 USFWS survey: **Zero** Spikedace or Loach Minnow found — full local extirpation in approximately 5 years or less.

3. **Cliff-Gila Valley Trend** (per David Propst survey data, 1961–1980 vs. 1983–84):
   - 1961–1980: No Red Shiner recorded in Cliff-Gila Valley waters.
   - 1983–84: Red Shiner present at ~2.2% relative abundance; Flathead Catfish also detected.
   - Spikedace relative abundance declined by 8.2% from the prior period.
   - Loach Minnow showed a slight 1.5% increase (attributed to possible statistical noise or differential predation susceptibility relative to Spikedace).
   - This shift occurred despite high spring runoff in 1983, undercutting arguments that high water/flooding alone protects native populations.

4. **Resilience of Red Shiner to Flooding**: Red Shiner populations were not suppressed by extreme flooding events of the late 1970s, and instead increased in relative abundance afterward — countering the hypothesis that natural hydrological disturbance would check the invasive species.

5. **Policy Recommendations Proposed by the Report**:
   - Declare the question of habitat loss in the lower Gila River Valley "moot."
   - Redefine or upgrade the basis for the "threatened" designation to reflect imminent displacement by Red Shiner, or alternatively designate both species as "endangered" for the same reason.
   - Eliminate prohibitions on dam construction (i.e., the proposed [[Conner Dam]]) in the lower Gila River System.
   - Direct more research resources toward understanding coexistence/predation-avoidance mechanisms observed in Aravaipa Creek and the upper Verde River.
   - Increase conservation effort in the "Forks" area of the upper Gila River System in New Mexico.
   - Extend the critical habitat designation deadline (originally set for June 1987) pending new survey data.

6. **Cited Foundational Sources**:

   - [[Minckley 1973]] – *Fishes of Arizona*, Arizona Game and Fish Department.
   - [[Propst, Bestgen & Painter 1986]] – *Distribution, Status, Biology, and Conservation of the Spikedace (Meda fulgida) in New Mexico*, Endangered Species Report No. 15, USFWS.

Final critical habitat rule was originally due no later than June 1987; this correspondence explicitly petitions for a delay to that rule pending new spring 1987 field survey data from David Propst.
