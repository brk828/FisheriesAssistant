# Values of Hunting and Fishing in Arizona, 1965

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #recreational-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This survey, prepared for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] by William C. Davis of the University of Arizona, estimates the economic and social value of recreational hunting and fishing in Arizona in 1965. It is the third in a series, following 1955 and 1960 studies, and was designed to be comparable with the national 1965 survey by the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. Based on a personal-interview sample of 1,000 licence holders drawn from 295,910 licences, the study estimates 276,500 participants who spent about $63.2 million, roughly 50 percent more than in 1960. Fishing accounted for 62.7 percent of spending. The report concludes that the psychological and social benefits of the sport exceed its monetary value, and it makes recommendations on license fees, communications, youth recruitment, and habitat improvement.

## Background & Description

**Objectives.** The survey aimed to (1) measure the direct economic contribution of expenditures, (2) identify motivational values that induce participation, and (3) record hunter and angler recommendations for improving State facilities and services.

**Scope.** Direct expenditures only were counted; secondary and tertiary income multiplier effects were excluded. The study followed the [[Stratified Random Sampling]] design of a two-stage cluster sample, with dealers as clusters. The stated accuracy was ±10 percent of the mean at the 95 percent confidence level.

**Categories studied.**
- *Fishing:* [[Cold Water Fishing]] (trout) and [[Warm Water Fishing]] (bass, catfish), plus [[Salt Water Fishing]] outside Arizona.
- *Game hunting:* [[Big Game Hunting]] (antelope, deer, bear, bighorn sheep, buffalo, elk, javelina, turkey), [[Small Game Hunting]] (squirrel, rabbit, quail, dove), and [[General Hunting]] (predators and varmints).
- *Waterfowl:* ducks and geese.

**Sample.** 1,000 completed interviews from 1,600 randomly drawn names; the sample was drawn from 41 dealers representing about 40 percent of 1965 sales.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes Arizona as a largely undisturbed landscape of mountains, deserts, ponderosa pine forests, and a limited number of lakes and streams. The author notes that Arizona has fewer lakes and streams than many states, which partly explains the lower fishing share relative to the national average (about 75 percent). Habitat concerns recur throughout: deer habitat was reported to be improving after reduced permits, and respondents urged more surface water storage, more lakes, and control of pollution and silting. Predator control by poisoning was criticised by hunters. Urban migration and posted private land were identified as factors limiting access and youth recruitment.

## Key Findings

**Participation**
- 276,500 sportsmen in 1965 (up about 50 percent from 183,000 in 1960); 244,300 (88%) were residents.
- Nonresidents rose from 15,000 to 32,200 (8% to 12% of participants).
- Roughly 2 in 3 participants began before age 20; about 15% started after age 30.
- Age 25 to 44 formed the most active group; young participants (12 to 19) declined as a share.

**Expenditures**
- Total spending of $63,227,900 (about 51% above the $41.75 million of 1960).
- [[Cold Water Fishing]]: $20,270,700 (32.1%); [[Warm Water Fishing]]: $19,361,700 (30.6%).
- Big game: $12,029,700 (19.0%); small game: $5,813,000 (9.2%); waterfowl: $822,700; general hunting: $735,500.
- Salt water fishing trips outside Arizona: $2,146,500 (3.4%), mostly to Mexico's Gulf of California (about 80% of that group).
- Licences, tags and stamps: $2,048,100 (3.2%).
- Transportation (mainly private cars) was the largest item at about 33%.
- Expenditure per man-day rose from $12.16 (1960) to $17.16 (1965).

**Harvest (1965)**
- Fish: 10,413,985 (cold water 4,449,275; warm water 5,964,710), up 42% from 7.3 million.
- Big game: 29,015 to 29,325 animals (deer down 50%, from 35,000 to 17,390; elk up 146%).
- Small game: roughly 3.6–3.7 million (mourning dove 1,194,000; quail 1,197,000; whitewing dove 668,955; cottontail 606,730).
- Waterfowl: about 29,890 (ducks 25,550; geese 4,340).
- Predators: 172,485.

**Motivations** (4,636 reasons from 1,000 respondents, about 4.6 per person)
- Recreation 37%; economic (game as food) 15%; associational 14%; aesthetic 11%; bodily health 10%; intellectual 10%; character 2%; religious and other 1%.
- Economic motives rose from 5% (1960) to 15%, though the author cautions the change partly reflects editorial interpretation.

**Influence on non-participants**
- About 1.5 million unlicensed people (mostly family) accompanied licence holders on trips.
- Fishing is more of a family activity; big game trips rarely include non-hunters.

**Public image of the Department**
- About 89% of respondents viewed the Department favourably; only 4.6% were displeased.
- Yet only two functions (enforcing game laws, about 65%; conserving wildlife, about 52%) were identified by more than half the sample; about 4% claimed no knowledge.
- Stocking was praised most (about 32% of positive replies); stricter law enforcement was the top complaint (about 26% of criticisms).

**Communications**
- Only 11.7% of licence holders received Department publications by mail; about 67% of the rest wanted to be added to the mailing list.
- 38% had seen hunting and fishing television programmes, which were judged poorly timed.

**Licence fees**
- A $1.00 increase would affect 4.7% of respondents; a $2.00 increase would affect 14.1%; a $1.00 cut would affect 0.7%.
- Sportsmen generally supported reasonable increases if the need was explained.

**Recommendations**
- More than 1,500 recommendations: convenience facilities (about 29%), regulation modification (24%), habitat improvement (22%), increased stocking (13%), access roads (7%), wildland preservation (3%).
- More than two out of three preferred more lakes to more stocking.

**Principal conclusions of the author**
1. An equitable increase in licence fees is warranted.
2. A more effective communications programme is needed.
3. Young Arizonans should be taught the values and techniques of the sport, given the apparent decline of younger participants.
4. Habitat should be improved, with more surface water storage.
