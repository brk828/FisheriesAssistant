# Chemical Composition of Insect Haemolymph in Relation to Phylogeny

#entomology #arthropod-physiology #haemolymph #insecta

## Executive Summary

This 1963 comparative biochemistry paper by D. W. Sutcliffe (Department of Zoology, University of Durham) tests whether the chemical composition of [[Insecta|insect]] haemolymph reflects evolutionary history. Using a compiled dataset from published analyses, the author expresses the osmotic contribution of one organic and six inorganic components as a percentage of total [[Osmotic Activity|osmolar concentration]]. The central claim is that a "basic" haemolymph type, dominated by sodium and chloride, is retained in [[Pterygote Insects]] and in related arthropods, and that this type was modified repeatedly during insect evolution. Chloride is proposed as the key diagnostic ion: high in [[Exopterygota]], low in [[Endopterygota]]. Lepidoptera and Hymenoptera show a more radical "specialized" type in which free amino acids dominate and inorganic ions are minor.

## Background & Description

The paper builds on earlier work on haemolymph ionic composition. [[Boné's Hypothesis]] (1944) proposed that the sodium–potassium ratio tracks diet, but Boné (1947), [[Tobias 1948]] and later reviews showed that ionic composition is a species-characteristic trait maintained by regulatory mechanisms. [[Duchateau Et Al 1953]] found that high sodium is associated with systematic group rather than diet, citing Odonata with about 85% of cations as sodium, and more than twenty lepidopteran species with sodium usually under 10%.

Insect haemolymph is rich in nitrogenous organic compounds, notably [[Free Amino Acids]], and in carbohydrates. Free amino acids alone accounted for 81% of freezing-point depression in the honey-bee larva *Apis mellifera* and 86% in the beetle larva *Popillia japonica* (Buck, 1953). Sutcliffe notes that the commonly cited chloride share of 10–15% needs revision.

Osmotic contributions were derived from concentrations in mM/l or mg ions/l, converted to percentages of total osmolarity. Osmolarity was calculated from freezing-point depression using 1 osmole/l = Δ 1.86°C. The method assumes no incomplete dissociation or ion binding, so each component's contribution is a maximum estimate. Free amino acid values were estimated by different techniques (Folin colorimetry, microbiological assay, micro-Kjeldahl nitrogen) and converted to molar concentrations using divisors of 120 or 14, which the author acknowledges probably overestimate the values.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The dataset spans aquatic and terrestrial species, and the paper treats both groups. The aquatic data come mainly from [[Aquatic Insects]] analysed in a companion study (Sutcliffe, 1962). Terrestrial data come largely from Duchateau et al. (1953) and other literature sources. Non-insect comparisons include a centipede (*Lithobius* sp.), a millipede (*Julus scandinavius*), a terrestrial isopod (*Oniscus asellus*), a bristletail (*Petrobius maritimus*), an arachnid (*Tegenaria atrica*) and several [[Crustacea]] that are marine, brackish or freshwater.

Some specialized haemolymph types were linked by Duchateau et al. to feeding on [[Angiosperms]], but Sutcliffe argues this explanation is not adequate. Some Scarabaeid beetles feeding on angiosperms show ordinary haemolymph, and locusts feed on plants without specialization. The author also notes the similarity between specialized haemolymph and the composition of certain tissue cells, such as the [[Carcinus Muscle Fibre]] of the shore crab *Carcinus maenas*, which may relate to the demands of rapidly developing pupal tissues.

## Key Findings

**Basic pterygote haemolymph (Type I)**
- In Type I, sodium and chloride account for major proportions of total osmolarity. Potassium, calcium, magnesium and inorganic phosphate are minor, and free amino acids are about 10%.
- Type I occurs in [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Odonata]], [[Plecoptera]], [[Dictyoptera]] and Hemiptera-Heteroptera. Example: *Aeschna grandis* larvae (sodium 36.7%, chloride 27.9%, free amino acids 9.8%, per Table 2).
- Sutcliffe proposes Type I as the ancestral haemolymph of pterygote insects. This is supported by its similarity to the apterygote bristletail *Petrobius maritimus*, the chilopod *Lithobius* and the diplopod *Julus scandinavius*.

**Type II**
- Type II resembles Type I but with lower sodium (about 24%) and chloride (about 22%). In *Locusta migratoria migratorioides*, around 30% of the total consists of unknown molecules.
- Type II occurs in some Orthoptera, one dermapteran (*Forficula auricularia*) and one isopteran (*Cryptotermes havilandi*). It is not characteristic of all orthopterans.

**Type III (endopterygote with low chloride)**
- Type III is characterized by very low chloride relative to sodium, with free amino acids up to 25% (in *Sialis lutaria* larvae).
- Sodium ranges from 21% (*Osmylus fulvicephalus*, adult) to 48% (*Tipula montium* larvae).
- It occurs in Trichoptera, Diptera, Megaloptera, Neuroptera and Mecoptera, and in most Coleoptera (where magnesium is about 10%).

**Type IV (specialized)**
- Type IV is characteristic of Lepidoptera and Hymenoptera. Sodium and chloride are each about 10% or less, and free amino acids are about 40% in several lepidopterans and in *Apis mellifera*.
- Lepidopteran larvae and pupae have very high free amino acid concentrations, estimated at 10–20 mg/ml (roughly 83–166 mM/l). In *Bombyx mori* larvae, free amino acids account for 0.16°C of a mean 0.60°C depression (26.7%, compared with 37.2% in Table 2).
- In *Telea polyphemus* pupae, free amino acids are only about 4% of osmolarity (Carrington & Tenney, 1959), so the high values are not universal.
- Four coleopterans show Type IV: *Timarcha tenebricosa* and *Agelastica alni* (Chrysomelidae), *Tenebrio molitor* (Tenebrionidae) and *Popillia japonica* (Scarabaeidae).

**Chloride as a diagnostic character**
- Chloride is a major fraction in Exopterygota and a minor one in Endopterygota (usually under 10%). The exception is *Aedes aegypti* larvae, with about 19%.
- Sutcliffe interprets the reduction in chloride as a single early event in endopterygote evolution, before the "panorpoid complex" diverged.

**Exceptions**
- *Anabrus simplex* (mormon cricket): sodium under 10%, with very high amino-N (187 mM/l, Pepper et al., 1941). Chloride was not measured.
- *Carausius morosus* (stick insect): sodium under 5%, but chloride at least 30%, so it is exopterygote-like in chloride. It has very high magnesium phosphate content.
- An unidentified jassid (plant bug): sodium 16% and chloride 11%, resembling Type IV.

**Other haemolymph components**
- Proteins and non-amino nitrogen compounds contribute very little to osmolarity.
- Trehalose contributes up to 8.5% in adult *Periplaneta americana*, 3.7% in *Bombyx mori* larvae and 2.3% in *Telea polyphemus* pupae.
- Citrate contributes 10–12% in *B. mori* larvae, 4–5% in *Prodenia eridania* larvae and 2–3% in *T. polyphemus* pupae.
- Unknown molecules make up 50–60% of total osmolarity in *T. polyphemus*, *P. eridania* and *Popillia japonica*.

**Phylogenetic interpretation**
- Ephemeroptera and Odonata form the Palaeoptera, which diverged early (Devonian or earlier). The Endopterygota are monophyletic.
- The "panorpoid" line includes Mecoptera, Trichoptera, Lepidoptera and Diptera. The other line includes Megaloptera, Neuroptera, Coleoptera and Hymenoptera.
- The specialized Type IV condition probably arose independently at least twice, because Lepidoptera are thought to derive from the panorpoid line and Coleoptera and Hymenoptera are not closely related.

**Comparison with other arthropods**
- In all Crustacea-Malacostraca, sodium chloride accounts for nearly all blood osmolarity, while potassium, calcium and magnesium are each about 1–3%.
- The blood of *Oniscus asellus* resembles that of the marine-littoral *Ligia oceanica* and the freshwater *Asellus aquaticus*. Similar blood also occurs in the arachnid *Tegenaria atrica*, the chilopod *Lithobius* and the bristletail *Petrobius maritimus*.
- In *Julus scandinavius* (diplopod), sodium is 29% and chloride 25% of blood osmolarity, which Sutcliffe describes as similar to some exopterygote insects.

**Conclusions and limitations**
- Sutcliffe states that the three haemolymph types are a convenient framework, not rigid classifications of species or orders. Several exceptions are known, and analyses from one species were sometimes used for another.
- The paper does not explain the mechanism behind the specializations. It calls for more information on ion uptake and excretion, citing Ramsay (1955, 1956).
