# Directions in Conservation Biology: Comments on Caughley

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This essay by [[Hedrick]], [[Lacy]], [[Allendorf]] and [[Soulé]] (*Conservation Biology* 10(5), October 1996) responds to [[Caughley]]'s (1994) review of conservation biology. Caughley described two approaches: the [[Small Population Paradigm]] and the [[Declining Population Paradigm]]. The authors argue that this division is a false dichotomy. Deterministic factors (habitat loss, overkill, introduced species) reduce populations, while stochastic factors (demographic, environmental and genetic) drive the final extinction of small populations. They propose an "inclusive" approach, termed [[Inclusive Population Viability Analysis]], that combines both views. The essay tests this argument against case studies of [[Cheetah]] genetics and [[Pacific Salmon]] management.

## Background & Description

- **Authors and context:** Written in response to Caughley's essay, which he wrote shortly before his death. The authors state that the paper grew out of a roundtable at the 1995 Society for Conservation Biology meeting in Fort Collins, Colorado.
- **Caughley's two paradigms:**
  - Small-population paradigm: effects of smallness on persistence, with extinction factors of environmental stochasticity and catastrophes, demographic stochasticity, and genetic deterioration.
  - Declining-population paradigm: causes of smallness and their cure, with extinction factors of overkill, habitat destruction and fragmentation, introduced species, and chains of extinction.
- **Authors' position:** The stochastic factors are the proximate causes of extinction. The deterministic factors are the ultimate causes that make populations vulnerable to random events. Without removing the deterministic drivers, a population will inevitably go extinct.
- **Conceptual tool:** [[Extinction Vortex]] (Gilpin and Soulé 1986), in which deterministic and stochastic forces interact and reinforce each other in small, fragmented, isolated populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Genetic factors.** Inbreeding caused losses of viability and fecundity in many captive stocks. [[Inbreeding Depression]] has also been linked to reduced fitness in wild populations of plants, topminnows (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), wolves (*Canis lupus*), African lions (*Panthera leo*) and [[Florida Panther]]s (*Felis concolor coryi*). The authors note that genetic data are useful for species identification, population structure and mating systems, not only for fitness.

**Demographic stochasticity.** Life-table analyses using long-term average vital rates systematically underestimate population decline when rates vary over time or space. [[Maximum Sustained Yield]] models that ignore extinction risk give lower mean yields and higher extinction risk than optimal harvest strategies. Stochastic events have caused:
- Collapse of many managed fisheries (Ludwig et al. 1993).
- Loss of the last wild population of [[Black-Footed Ferret]]s to disease.
- Extirpation of the last non-migratory population of [[Whooping Crane]]s after a hurricane.

**Pacific salmon and local populations.** Salmon homing produces networks of distinct local reproductive populations adapted to specific conditions. These populations may act as [[Metapopulation Dynamics]] over short time scales. Fishery regulation by stock, rather than by local population, has led to the disappearance of many local populations.

## Key Findings

1. **The paradigm split is artificial.** Stochastic and deterministic factors must be analysed together. Caughley's separation "may unprofitably polarize conservation biologists," particularly when political forces seek to discredit conservation.

2. **Inclusive PVA is the proposed goal.** The authors call for [[Population Viability Analysis]] that considers both anthropogenic ultimate causes and stochastic proximate causes. They argue that PVA is "an excellent tool for integrating deterministic and stochastic factors," and that it does not ignore habitat loss, over-harvest or exotic species.

3. **Genetics affects extinction in some circumstances.** Lethal alleles may be purged in small populations, but slightly deleterious variants may become fixed and lower viability. The authors cite evidence that extinction was caused by "genetic malfunctions" in [[Heath Hen]]s and a Swedish population of the [[Middle Spotted Woodpecker]]. They also cite Lande's (1995) estimate that the population size needed to maintain genetic variation is an order of magnitude higher than previously thought.

4. **Florida panther case.** PVA revealed the panther's vulnerability to inbreeding and to a lack of mates caused by locally variable breeder sex ratios. Conservation now includes genetic restoration and augmentation from the Texas population.

5. **Whooping crane case.** A tally of recruitment and deaths was kept every year since 1938. A PVA based on the 1938 status accurately projected population growth and fluctuations over the following 52 years. Inbreeding and demographic threats are declining, but local catastrophe remains the main risk. This risk is being addressed by a nonmigratory population established in Florida.

6. **Small populations and extinction.** The last five [[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]]s were all males, a stochastic event that effectively ended the taxon. The golden toad (*Bufo periglenes*) went extinct after an unusually dry year in a Costa Rican cloud forest.

7. **Deterministic impacts can be misjudged without PVA.** Common Ravens (*Corvus corax*) were long believed to be the main threat to young [[Desert Tortoise]]s. A PVA showed that mature females were by far the most sensitive stage, so reducing raven predation mistook a visible impact for a demographically significant one.

8. **Cheetah genetics are complex.** Allozyme variation is low, while mtDNA, minisatellites and microsatellites vary nearly as much as in other big cats. The authors argue that low genetic variation can reflect past bottlenecks, chronically low effective size, or metapopulation dynamics, and that it does not necessarily reduce fitness. Inbreeding depression has been observed in cheetahs. Main wild mortality comes from predation by lions, with habitat change also important. Southern African cheetahs have lower genetic variation than eastern African cheetahs but are denser and less endangered.

9. **Salmon need local-population management.** Declines in Columbia River salmon, largely from hydroelectric development, have been recognized for over 100 years. Caughley's recommendation to replace extirpated populations by [[Translocation]] ignores [[Local Adaptation]]. Transplants have generally failed in North America and Asia.

10. **Adams River case.** A logging dam built in 1908 blocked [[Sockeye Salmon]] access to the upper Adams River until 1921. The spawning area of 1.2 million m² could support about 6 million adult sockeye per year. Sixteen reintroduction attempts (1949–1975) failed, and only a few fish return today.

11. **Lower Fraser sockeye.** Reintroduction of lower-river stocks upriver has failed. These fish appear to lack the physiological capacity to store enough energy reserves for the long upriver migration.

12. **Supportive breeding can backfire.** Supportive breeding may increase population size but can reduce effective population size and genetic variation under some conditions (Ryman and Laikre 1991). Management based on demographic and genetic considerations can therefore conflict.

13. **Caughley's examples support integration.** The Lord Howe Woodhen case combined experiments on feral pig predation with a captive breeding program that maintained genetic variation. The revision of timber-harvest practices for the [[Northern Spotted Owl]] came from PVAs showing that habitat distribution across the landscape can cause extinction through local population processes.

**Conclusion:** The authors call for an [[Inclusive Population Viability Analysis]] framework that integrates small-population and declining-population theory. Its goal is a broader understanding of the factors that drive endangerment and extinction, which they see as shared ground for conservation biologists of all viewpoints.
