# Santa Clara Slough Surveys

## Executive Summary
This document is an internal Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) memorandum (September 8, 1992) outlining the Department's participation in the **Santa Clara Slough Project** (also known as **Cienega de Santa Clara**), a collaborative multi-agency effort involving AGFD, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), the Bureau of Reclamation (BR), the University of Arizona (UA), and Mexican institutions (Centro Ecologico de Sonora, Centro de Ecologia-UNAM). Rather than presenting original morphological or ecological data, the memo summarizes survey logistics and references an underlying research proposal by [[Edward P. Glenn]] et al. concerning the wetland's biological communities, including the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]. The wetland is threatened by planned operation of the BR's Yuma Desalting Plant, which is projected to reduce freshwater inflow by two-thirds and nearly triple water salinity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Location**: [[Santa Clara Slough]] (Cienega de Santa Clara), described as possibly the largest remaining marsh in the [[Colorado River Delta]], situated entirely within Mexico (Sonora) near the [[Upper Gulf of California]].
- **Hydrology**: The wetland's primary water source originates in the United States (agricultural drainage/return flows), despite the wetland itself lying entirely in Mexican territory — creating a transboundary conservation dependency.
- **Associated species**: 
  - [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) — federally endangered cyprinodontid fish.
  - [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*) — federally endangered marsh bird.
  - Diverse assemblages of neotropical migratory birds and wetland plant communities are also noted as present.
- **Threats**: Operation of the Bureau of Reclamation's **Yuma Desalting Plant** is expected to:
  - Reduce wetland water input by approximately **two-thirds**.
  - Increase water salinity by nearly **three-fold**.
  - These changes pose direct risk to the marsh ecosystem, its endemic/endangered fauna, and vegetation structure.
- **Baseline monitoring need**: Because the plant began partial operation (at one-third capacity) earlier than the pre-operation baseline data collection was completed, there is an urgent need to gather "before" ecological data to allow valid before/after comparison (Phase 1 of the project).

## Key Findings
- AGFD committed to surveying and providing technical assistance in four domains: (1) native fishes, (2) [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] populations, (3) other wildlife (shorebirds and neotropical migratory birds), and (4) water quality — none of which were adequately addressed in the original Glenn proposal, necessitating AGFD-developed survey protocols.
- A 6-person AGFD crew was slated for at least three 7-day field trips over 6 months (first trip tentatively September 28–October 4, 1992), coordinated with the [[Centro Ecologico de Sonora]] (CES).
- Funding: $4,000 secured from FWS for survey expenses; an additional $15,000 was pledged by FWS (Jack Woody, Albuquerque office) to fund Dr. Glenn's broader research proposal, though the Bureau of Reclamation had not committed funding as of the memo date despite a May 1991 proposal submission.
- The overarching Glenn/Felger/Burquez research proposal (UA Environmental Laboratory, Drylands Institute, and Centro de Ecologia-UNAM) aimed to: 
  1. Characterize wetland ecosystem structure before/after altered water flow,
  2. Develop a predictive simulation model for identifying biological stress points, and 
  3. Recommend mitigation alternatives to preserve wetland values.
- This project represents an early example of binational cooperative conservation planning for a shared, ecologically significant desert wetland threatened by upstream water management infrastructure — a case study relevant to [[Transboundary Water Management]] and [[Endangered Species Conservation]] in arid-region estuarine systems.
