# Feeds and Feeding of Warm-Water Fish in North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This review by E. W. Shell (Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station) summarizes North American research on the nutrition and feeding of warm-water fish, presented at the FAO World Symposium on Warm-Water Pond Fish Culture (Rome, 1966). The field is described as still in its infancy: the first controlled experiments date to the early 1950s, and most critical information was gathered after 1960. Nearly all work has focused on the [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), the only species considered to command a market price high enough to justify supplemental feeding. The review covers nutrient requirements (protein, carbohydrate, fat, vitamins, calories, minerals, bulk), practical diet formulation, feed processing, and feeding rates and schedules, with reference to other species where relevant.

## Background & Description

Warm-water fish nutrition research in the United States began differently from most countries. Instead of food-production pressure, the earliest experimentation came from [[Minnow Culture]] producers rearing [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) for sport fishermen. By 1946 southern goldfish producers used complex mixtures of animal and vegetable products, often as secret formulas. Their most significant contribution was demonstrating that production in static waters could be increased substantially by feeding.

Four active research stations were identified:

- [[Auburn University]] Agricultural Experiment Station, Auburn, Alabama
- [[South-Eastern Fish Cultural Laboratory]], U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Marion, Alabama
- [[Fish Farming Experimental Station]], U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Stuttgart, Arkansas
- [[Kansas State University]], Manhattan, Kansas

Two research approaches are used. The South-Eastern Fish Cultural Laboratory uses purified diets fed to fish in troughs without natural food, aiming to determine nutrient requirements. The Fish Farming Experimental Station tests diets built from ingredients common in animal feeds under actual pond conditions. The review notes that both approaches yield valuable information.

Other species mentioned include [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Speckled Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus nebulosus*), [[White Catfish]] (*Crotalurus catus*), and [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*, also *T. melanopleura* and *T. nilotica*). Shell notes that cold-water fishes evolved in environments never warmer than about 15–17 °C, so metabolic differences between the groups may affect nutrient needs, though comparative data are scarce.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several environmental factors are central to the review:

- **Natural food:** Ponds receiving supplemental feeding produced a much larger amount of natural food than fertilized-only ponds. Natural foods were judged adequate to supplement incomplete diets only until fish reached roughly 1,568 kg/ha (the text notes the limit may be somewhat higher). Natural foods supply less vitamin nutrition as stocking density rises.
- **Oxygen and density:** The limit to fish production imposed by vitamin availability was reported to lie somewhat below the limit set by oxygen supply. Apparent avitaminosis developed in crowded goldfish, without chemical or physical pond characteristics being implicated in the deaths.
- **Water quality:** Uneaten feed decays and pollutes the water, reducing feed efficiency. Excessive bread feeding of goldfish caused decay and death, identified as a limiting factor in static water production.
- **Temperature:** Water temperature strongly controls feed consumption and is discussed in detail under feeding rates.
- **Culture system:** Pond culture tolerates small errors in feeding rates because natural food compensates. Raceway culture, with little or no natural food, requires more precise feed amounts.

## Key Findings

### Nutrient requirements

- **Protein:** Growth in channel catfish was linear with dietary casein up to about 25–39 percent, with divergent estimates attributed to differing experimental procedures. The South-Eastern Fish Cultural Laboratory recommended 28 percent protein. Test diets at the Fish Farming Experimental Station contained at least 32 percent, and Auburn commercial and supplemental feed contained 46 percent.
- **Protein quality:** Using casein as the standard (100), fish meal scored 120, soybean meal 49, and feather meal 43. Casein outperformed purified soybean protein, with the gap widening at higher protein levels. A 1:1 casein–soybean combination matched casein at 5 percent protein and outperformed it at 30 percent.
- **Amino acids:** Channel catfish fed a purified diet with the amino acids required by [[Chinook Salmon]] failed to gain weight, and subsequent modifications did not rescue growth.
- **Carbohydrates:** Nail (1962) estimated that 1 g of carbohydrate spared 0.22 g of protein. Dupree found that starch and dextrin supported better growth than glucose or maltose. Dupree and Sneed recommended 20 percent carbohydrate, with acceptable growth from 10 to 20 percent.
- **Fats:** Best growth occurred with 7 percent corn oil; 15 percent reduced growth. Beef tallow growth declined as tallow levels rose.
- **Vitamins:** Channel catfish required vitamin D (Jewell et al., 1933). Dupree (1960) demonstrated requirements for pyridoxine, pantothenic acid, riboflavin, thiamine, folic acid, nicotinic acid, B-12, choline, vitamins A and K, and no requirement for biotin, inositol, ascorbic acid, or para-aminobenzoic acid. Vitamin premixes improved fingerling growth in practical diets.
- **Calories:** Hastings recommended total dietary calories above 2,964 per kg, with protein-derived calories above 1,334 per kg. Tiemeier et al. made no optimum recommendation.
- **Minerals:** Little research exists. Iron was reported (in personal correspondence only) to increase growth.
- **Bulk:** Best growth occurred at 10 percent alphacellulose bulk, with no difference between 0, 20, 40 and 50 percent. Fibre in test diets at the Fish Farming Experimental Station was kept below 20 percent.

### Additives

- Sulphamerazine (0.05 to 1.0 percent of feed) was used against bacterial gill disease in goldfish without mortality.
- Di-n-butyl tin oxide at about 250 mg/kg body weight controlled tapeworm and intestinal fluke in channel catfish (Allison, 1957).
- Fungal and bacterial amylases failed to improve growth or feed conversion.
- Several sex hormones tested on goldfish altered ovarian and testicular weight, without mortality.

### Diet formulation history

- Feeds were informal before the 1950s. Swingle fed bread to goldfish (1941) and reported 3.6 kg of bread per kg of bluegill gain (1943). A mixed feed of 35% cottonseed meal, 35% soybean meal, and 30% dry skim milk gave 3.4 kg feed per kg gain for bluegill (1947).
- Soybean cake alone was used as a standard feed by 1950, but in 1953–54 goldfish fed soybean cake in crowded ponds died in large numbers with tissue and cartilage erosion around the mouth and head. Switching to fish meal reduced mortality.
- Prather (1957) formulated **[[Auburn No.1 Feed]]** (35% soybean meal, 35% peanut meal, 15% fish meal, 15% distiller's dry solubles). **[[Auburn No.2 Feed]]** (1958) replaced the peanut meal with ground peanut cake. **Auburn No.3** replaced the peanut cake with cottonseed meal and gave essentially the same growth in tests.
- Hastings' formulation (1964), based on percentages of plant and animal product classes, contained 45% grain by-products, 10% fish meal, 12.5% animal protein concentrate, 22.5% plant protein concentrate, 4% dehydrated alfalfa, 5% distiller's solubles, and 1% mineralized salt. Its target nutrients were 32% protein, more than 4% fat, less than 20% fibre, more than 1% calcium and phosphorus. This class-based approach lets feed processors substitute ingredients as prices change.
- Tiemeier et al. (Kansas State) found no difference among diets containing 0.5 to 8.8% fish meal. Hartley's (1966) recommended Kansas formulation included 5% fish meal, 30% soybean meal, 20% meat scraps, and 18% wheat middlings.
- A trout-type dry meal diet used at an Arkansas hatchery (Crawford, 1957) and the commercial hog feeds (which likely harmed fish through antibiotic levels) were also examined.

### Feed processing

- Pelleting strongly improved efficiency. For carp, pelleted Auburn No.1 required 23 percent less feed per kg of fish than meal. Channel catfish showed a more dramatic response in a pond trial, with pellets producing far more fish than meal from the same feed.
- Fathead minnows gained most on 3/8-inch pellets (188 kg/ha), compared with crumbles (164) and meal (147), despite the pellet being too large for the fish.
- Grinding before pelleting, and adding organic flours (soy flour, dried wood pulp liquor, rice mill dust), improved pellet water stability. Gelatinized corn solids, guar meal and bentonite did not.
- Processing can destroy vitamins and enzymes without changing protein, fat or carbohydrate content.

### Feeding amount and rates

- Conversion efficiency plotted against feeding rate produced parabolic curves for channel catfish and Tilapia mossambica.
- Swingle (1967) gave daily feeding rates by length for channel and white catfish (above 16 °C): 5.0% of body weight at 2.4–12.0 cm, 4.0% at 12.0–15.6 cm, 3.0% at 15.6–24.0 cm, 2.5% at 24.0–28.5 cm, declining to about 1.5% for the largest fish.
- For tilapia (above 22 °C): 5% for 2.4–12.0 cm and 3% for 12.0–24.0 cm.
- Hartley's Kansas schedule (2,470 fingerlings/ha) peaked at 14.4 kg/ha/day in mid-August and fell to 6.3 kg/ha/day by early October.
- Prather's sport-fish schedule (7,410 fish/ha, Auburn No.1) rose from 3.64 kg/ha/day in March to 33.6 kg/ha/day in October.
- Tiemeier et al. recommended 4% of body weight daily, adjusted at two-week intervals.
- Swingle's conversion-based method estimates fish weight as feed fed divided by a conversion factor, and recalculates each one- or two-week period, with feeding at about 3% of body weight.
- Tilapia converted feed efficiently at 5% body weight per day only if fed in at least five equal portions.

### Feeding at low temperature

- Speckled bullheads did not use food below 16 °C.
- Channel catfish feeding is generally suspended below 15.6–18.3 °C. Swingle recommended feeding brood fish until water fell below 10 °C, and fingerlings at 1% of body weight until below 7 °C.
- Prather found fingerling catfish responded to winter feeding, but required about five times as much feed per unit of gain as in summer.
- Swingle and Shell found winter conversion very poor at 3–4% feeding rates (November–March), improved significantly at 1–2%.
- Hartley recommended reducing feed from mid-September to about half the summer maximum by mid-October.

### Research gaps

- Only channel catfish has been studied in depth; other species are poorly characterized.
- Amino acid, mineral and temperature-dependent feed requirement data are minimal.
- Feed rate schedules are largely based on field experience rather than quantitative size–requirement relationships.
