# Fisheries and riparian habitat improvement for the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Forest Service document, a fisheries and riparian management plan for the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] in east-central Arizona, sets out the legal basis, current condition, supply and value, and management practices for [[Riparian Habitat]] and [[Fish Habitat]]. It states that 72 percent of riparian forest is in unsatisfactory condition, that the Forests hold roughly 450 miles of fishable cold-water stream and about 2,000 acres of cold-water lakes, and that the fishery is worth $5,800,000 annually. The plan prioritizes habitat for threatened, endangered and sensitive fish, uses habitat-improvement structures and riparian rehabilitation, and commits the Forest Service and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] to a joint stream-survey and modeling program through the [[General Aquatic Wildlife System]].

## Background & Description

**Legal and policy basis.** The Forest Service's fishery responsibility traces to the Organic Administration Act of 1897, which emphasized water flow and timber. Later legislation, [[Forest Service Riparian Policy]] (FSM 2526.02), and the [[Multiple-Use Mandate]] refined this responsibility. Policy directs Forest Supervisors to inventory riparian areas, implement protection measures, and monitor their effectiveness.

**Riparian definition.** Following the Riparian Area Handbook (FSH 2509.23), riparian ecosystems are areas where free water is present within the rooting depth of native perennial plants at least seasonally (10 percent of the time or more). Riparian habitat need not depend on perennial flow.

**Riparian vegetation zones.**
- *High-elevation riparian scrub (above the Mogollon Rim, roughly above 7,000 ft):* shrub willows (Salix spp.), red-osier dogwood, shrubby cinquefoil and thinleaf alder, often within ponderosa pine and mixed conifer forest; subalpine wet meadows are associated.
- *Low-elevation riparian forest (below the Mogollon Rim, roughly below 6,500 ft):* mixed broadleaf communities (Arizona sycamore, velvet ash, Fremont cottonwood, Arizona alder, Arizona walnut) and cottonwood-willow communities.
- *Wetlands:* marshes dominated by cattail, bulrush and spikerush, largely in sinkhole-formed depressions.
- *Lakes and reservoirs:* most natural "lakes" on the Forest are actually shallow wetlands; most reservoirs have little or no riparian zone because of fluctuating water levels.

**Fish fauna.** About 30 fish species occur on the Forests. Sixteen are game fish in the family Salmonidae, including Rainbow, Brown, Apache, Cutthroat, Brook and Arctic Grayling trout. Two species are federally threatened ([[Apache Trout]] and [[Loach Minnow]]) and four are proposed or candidates for listing ([[Little Colorado Spinedace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Spikedace]], [[Gila Chub]]). Non-game and introduced species include minnows, suckers, catfish, sunfish and black bass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian condition.** A partial inventory found that livestock grazing has adversely affected riparian areas. Elk browsing has hindered willow re-establishment above the Rim. The Forest Plan describes riparian areas as generally in less than satisfactory condition, especially above the Rim.

**Riparian acreage.** The Draft Forest Plan identified 3,962 acres of open water and 2,222 acres of wetlands. After refinement to riparian-only acres, the best estimate is:

| Category | Acres | Percent |
|---|---|---|
| Open water | 3,962 | 0.2% |
| Wetlands | 2,222 | 0.1% |
| Riparian | 7,879 | 0.4% |
| Total | 14,063 | 0.7% of 2,003,552 Forest acres |

**Stream and lake resource.** The Forests have about 450 miles of fishable cold-water streams, the largest mileage of any National Forest in Arizona and second in the Southwest Region. This represents 18 percent of Regional stream miles and 44 percent of Arizona's. The Forests also provide 62 percent of cold-water lakes on National Forest lands in Arizona. Roughly 2,000 acres of lakes and reservoirs are present, mostly in timbered areas.

**Habitat needs.** Quality stream habitat requires stable, clean banks, suitable temperatures, clean spawning gravels, healthy streamside vegetation, and adequate instream cover (pools, riffles, undercut banks, logs, boulders).

**Fishing use and value.** The Forests recorded about 487,000 recreational visitor days (RVDs) in 1986 and contribute 71 percent of cold-water fishing on Arizona National Forests. The cold-water fishery is valued at $5,800,000 annually (1986). Wildlife and fish user days are estimated at 72 percent from fishing. Demand is projected to rise sharply as Arizona's population grows, and trout fishing demand in 1990 is expected to be about 45 percent above the level supported by current trout programs.

**Stocking.** The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] stocks about two million trout annually, mostly in lakes because natural reproduction there is limited. Catchable trout are stocked in several popular streams, but most streams hold wild, self-sustaining trout populations.

**Beaver management.** The document discusses [[Beaver]] (Castor canadensis) as a restoration tool in degraded riparian areas, while warning that "bad beaver" conditions (dam failure, bank cutting, downcutting, lost water tables) can damage fisheries and downstream users.

## Key Findings

**Condition and goals**
- 72 percent of riparian forest is in unsatisfactory condition (Forest estimate).
- The Forest Plan calls for complete riparian inventories by the end of the first decade (about 1994/1997 depending on the section) and satisfactory condition on all riparian areas by 2007 to 2030 (figures vary between sections of the document).
- Trout and native species targets: maintain or improve habitat capability for all stream reaches for Apache, rainbow, brown and brook trout, Loach Minnow and Little Colorado Spinedace to at least 60 percent of potential.

**Fish habitat management policy**
- Priority goes to threatened, endangered and sensitive species.
- Stocking of exotic fishes is permitted only after analysis.
- All waters, including intermittent and uninhabited ones, are considered to have fisheries value.
- Stream improvement structures will not be used to offset poor land management.
- Woody debris and revegetation are emphasized in structures.

**Habitat improvement techniques**
- Riparian rehabilitation uses willow stem cuttings and poles of Fremont cottonwood, with browse protection by fencing, wire cages and unpalatable species.
- Wetland improvement uses islands and potholes; optimum pothole ratio is one-third open water to two-thirds emergent marsh.
- In-stream structures (check dams, boulder placement, deflectors, cover structures, gravel traps, migration barriers) are rated by stream type in Tables 3 and 4. Ratings are guidelines only and do not replace site analysis.

**Grazing**
- Fencing is the preferred starting point where other methods cannot work, though it is costly (about $6,000 per stream mile, with $60–$200 annual maintenance per mile).
- Rest-rotation grazing has mixed results; Platts and Nelson (1985) found preselected use levels and time-controlled grazing more effective.
- Riparian pastures and corridor fencing are rated highest for fishery compatibility (Table E.1.2).

**Monitoring and surveys**
- Five-year stream survey plan covers about 1,158 man-days across priorities 1–4 (roughly 290 survey days for a four-person crew, requiring 5–10 years).
- Priority 1 includes threatened and endangered species streams (24 streams, 165 miles); Priority 2 includes 46 streams and 178 miles.
- Aquatic macroinvertebrate sampling uses a Surber sampler at 4 key streams (Little Colorado River, Black River, Blue River, Chevelon Creek), estimated at about $50 per sample.

**Inter-agency agreement (1987)**
- A cooperative Memorandum of Understanding between the Arizona Game and Fish Commission and the Forest Service establishes a core team from each agency to conduct stream surveys and develop a fish habitat relationship model.
- Shared products include a stream survey, a land-systems inventory, a trout biomass model, and a final report and map.

**Recommended actions**
- Establish a fisheries biologist position on the Forests for FY-1988.
- Develop 5-year action plans tied to stream priorities.
- Designate reference areas of undisturbed riparian habitat for comparison.
