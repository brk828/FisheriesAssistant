# Riparian Habitat Management and Cold-Water Fisheries on the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service document (apparently a Forest-level fisheries and riparian management plan, with supporting appendices) addresses the management of riparian habitats and aquatic resources on the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] in Arizona. It states that riparian areas support diverse aquatic and terrestrial species, that [[Cold-Water Fishery]] resources are valued at roughly $5.8 million to $10 million annually, and that the Forest is among the most important cold-water fisheries in Arizona. Current conditions are described as largely unsatisfactory: an estimated 72 percent of riparian forest is in unsatisfactory condition, and livestock grazing has significantly affected riparian areas. The document sets goals to improve riparian condition, inventory streams and lakes, protect native and sport fish, and provide high-quality angling. It supplies a menu of restoration techniques (willow and cottonwood plantings, structures, grazing strategies, beaver management) and recommends monitoring, interagency coordination, and a cooperative aquatic resource inventory with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

## Background & Description

**Riparian definition.** Riparian habitats are identified by vegetation requiring free or unbound water, or conditions that are moister than normal. The Forest Service [[Riparian Area Handbook]] (FSH 2609.23) defines riparian ecosystems by the presence of free water within the common rooting depth of native perennial plants, at least seasonally (often 10 percent of the time or more). Riparian areas are associated with streams, springs, seeps, ponds or lakes, and wetlands, and they are not dependent on perennial surface flow.

**Policy.** Forest Service riparian policy is set out in FSM 2526.02. Its objectives are to manage riparian areas to benefit riparian-dependent resources, recognize their unique values, and emphasize protection, management, and improvement during planning and implementation. Supporting direction (FSM 2526.04) calls for inventory, resource development to protect riparian areas, and monitoring of management effectiveness.

**Legal basis.** The Forest Service responsibility for fishery habitat derives from the Organic Administration Act of 1897 and the Multiple Use Sustained Yield Act, which require consideration of all resources in planning. The Endangered Species Act of 1973 and its amendments further require special protection for listed species.

**Fishery context.** Fish are described as riparian-dependent resources. Fishery habitats are maintained and enhanced to increase productivity for game and non-game species, and to help threatened and endangered species recover. Document-referenced estimates indicate that about 72 percent of wildlife and fish user days (IFUDs) on the Apache-Sitgreaves result from fishing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian vegetation types.** The document distinguishes:

- **High-elevation riparian scrub** (above about 7,000 ft), associated with ponderosa pine and mixed conifer forests, dominated by shrub willows and alders, with Rocky Mountain maple and other shrubs.
- **Low-elevation riparian forest** (below about 6,500 ft), including broadleaf and cottonwood-willow communities along perennial or intermittent streams. Species include Arizona sycamore, Fremont cottonwood, Arizona alder, and velvet ash.
- **Wetlands** (marshes and meadows) where saturation is the dominant factor, often zoned along moisture gradients, with sedges and rushes.
- **Lakes and reservoirs**, which are difficult to separate from wetlands; most are relatively shallow and support emergent vegetation.

**Stream and habitat features.** Good fish habitat requires stable stream banks, cover, suitable temperatures, gravel for spawning, and adequate pools. Fish habitat structures are used to supply these conditions.

**Environmental pressures.** Livestock grazing is identified as the main pressure, with trampling and overgrazing preventing establishment of riparian vegetation. Roads, off-road vehicle use, and beaver-related changes also affect habitats. Stream flow changes and sediment inputs are noted as factors reducing riparian quality.

**Species at risk.** Several native fishes are listed or candidates for listing, including [[Apache Trout]], [[Loach Minnow]], and [[Gila Chub]]. The [[Spikedace]] and other native cyprinids are also noted as threatened or endangered species of concern.

## Key Findings

**Riparian extent and condition**

- The Forest contains about 3,192 acres of open water, including 2,220 acres of wetlands (figures given in the draft plan; the source is partly garbled in OCR).
- Partial inventories show that livestock grazing has significantly affected riparian areas, and that about 72 percent of riparian forest is in unsatisfactory condition.
- The Forest Plan calls for improving unsatisfactory riparian conditions on 100 percent of acres by 2000 (the target year as stated).

**Fishing and recreation**

- The Forest has approximately 460 miles of fishable streams and about 9,000 acres of lakes and reservoirs, making it the largest provider of cold-water fishing in the Southwest Region.
- Fishing contributes about 71 percent of cold-water fishing and 49 percent of total fishing on Arizona National Forests (as stated).
- Recreational value is estimated at $5.8 million to $10 million annually for the Southwest Region's cold-water fishery.
- Roughly 1,000 to 2,000 miles of fish-bearing streams and more than 200 lakes are noted in the Forest, with about 2,000 acres of lakes and reservoirs.

**Fish species**

- About 30 species of fish occur on the Apache-Sitgreaves, including several [[Native Fish]] species and game species such as [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], and [[Apache Trout]] (the state fish).
- The Forest supports a hatchery-stocked cold-water fishery, with most game trout stocked, as natural reproduction is limited in many popular waters.

**Management goals and objectives**

- Protect and enhance riparian and aquatic habitats, restore threatened and endangered fish, and provide high-quality angling and recreation.
- Inventory riparian areas and classify them by type, with monitoring of effectiveness.
- Coordinate with the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Arizona Cold Water Fisheries Strategic Plan.

**Restoration techniques recommended**

- Planting cuttings of willow and cottonwood in stream margins, with specified cutting sizes, timing, and planting methods.
- Installing fish habitat structures (log check dams, boulder placements, deflectors, cover structures, bank cover, and channel constrictors), selected by stream type using the document's classification tables.
- Managing grazing through rest-rotation, deferred rotation, and riparian pasture separation, with controlled stocking and timing.
- Beaver reintroduction in degraded riparian systems, with careful site selection, and recognition that beaver can alter flow and sediment.
- Fencing corridors to exclude livestock, with a cost estimate of about $6,400 per stream mile (as stated) and maintenance costs of $1,800 to $1,900 per stream mile per year.

**Monitoring and research**

- Monitor riparian habitat condition using the riparian survey procedures in FSH 2509.23 and Riparian Analysis guidance, and conduct fish surveys with electrofishing, Surber samplers for aquatic invertebrates, and temperature monitoring.
- Implement a five-year stream survey plan, with a priority list of streams ranked by resource value.

**Cooperative agreement**

- A Memorandum of Understanding (approved September 1987) between the Arizona Game and Fish Commission and the Forest Service establishes a joint field survey to assess aquatic resources and riparian communities, to develop a fish habitat relationships model, and to standardize data. The Forest Service is responsible for a land systems inventory and a computer model for predicting trout biomass; the Commission for field survey teams and fish population estimates.

**Conclusion**

The document concludes that riparian management can benefit both fish and livestock, that grazing strategies must be tailored to site conditions, and that prioritized, coordinated restoration and monitoring are needed to meet fishery and riparian goals on the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests.
