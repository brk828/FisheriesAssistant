# Disappearance of Dead Pink Salmon Eggs and Larvae from Sashin Creek, Baranof Island, Alaska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #spawning-ecology #egg-survival

## Executive Summary

This study documents the persistence and decomposition of dead [[Pink Salmon]] (Oncorhynchus gorbuscha) eggs and larvae in [[Sashin Creek]], [[Baranof Island]], [[Alaska]] from October 1961 through March 1963. Researchers tracked the 1961 brood year to determine whether residual dead eggs remained long enough to compete with subsequent brood years for dissolved oxygen. Dead eggs disappeared slowly from spawning beds between spawning (October 1961) and fry emergence (March 1962), with significant egg fragments still present 18 months post-spawning (March 1963). Dead larvae decomposed more rapidly, disappearing by August 1962. Dissolved oxygen levels in intragravel water averaged near 50 percent saturation when decomposing eggs were present, suggesting that decomposing organic matter may impair water quality for more than one year beyond spawning.

## Background & Description

[[Sashin Creek]] has been a site for [[Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]] research on pink salmon production since 1934. The stream supports spawning escapements of nearly 50,000 female pink salmon with potential deposition of approximately 100 million eggs on 13,600 square meters of spawning ground. The 1961 brood year experienced heavy spawning (approximately 2,000 eggs per square meter potential deposition), followed by exceptionally light 1962 spawning (less than 50 eggs per square meter from pink, [[Chum Salmon]] (O. keta), and [[Coho Salmon]] (O. kisutch) combined). This provided an ideal natural experiment to observe decomposition of residual spawn with minimal disturbance from subsequent spawning activity.

The study area encompassed 4,067 square meters (30 percent of total spawning area) with average gradient of 0.3 percent. Stream bottom composition: 48 percent particles >25 mm, 44 percent between 1-25 mm, and 8 percent <1 mm. Sampling employed a hydraulic egg and larval sampler collecting 0.2 square meter circular areas at randomly selected points. Collection net mesh openings were 2 mm diameter, limiting recovery of smaller fragments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Pink Salmon]] in [[Sashin Creek]] spawn in late summer and early autumn as water temperatures decline from 13° to 10°C. Hatching occurs in autumn and early winter at temperatures near 1-2°C. Fry emerge in spring as temperatures increase from 2° to 8°C. The 1961 brood year eggs were subjected to temperatures as high as 16°C by August 1962.

Stream discharge ranged from 10 to 570 cubic feet per second (c.f.s.) during the study period, with 10 c.f.s. representing approximate minimum winter flow and 620 c.f.s. the recorded maximum. The 1961-62 period showed near-normal hydrological conditions: 47 days with discharge <20 c.f.s. (11-year average: 42 days) and only one occasion exceeding 500 c.f.s. (11-year average: 2.4 times).

Water quality measurements in late August 1962 revealed:
- **Dissolved oxygen**: 5.1 mg/L (approximately 50% saturation at 13°C)
- **Free carbon dioxide**: 5.0 mg/L
- **Ammoniacal nitrogen (as NH₃)**: 0.09 mg/L

These values indicated high biochemical oxygen demand (B.O.D.) being satisfied in spawning beds. Previous studies in three other Southeastern Alaska streams reported mean dissolved oxygen levels of 6-8 mg/L during August in 4 out of 5 years, with only August 1957 showing comparable low levels (3-4 mg/L) associated with high early egg mortality.

## Key Findings

**Population Dynamics of 1961 Brood Year:**

- Potential egg deposition: 8,533,000 eggs in study area (2,100 per square meter)
- Estimated actual deposition at spawning end: 1,100 eggs per square meter
- Egg loss during spawning: approximately 50%
- Preemergent fry population (March 1962): 6,300,000 live pink salmon (90% confidence limits ±1,090,000)
- Fry counted leaving stream (mid-June 1962): 5,940,000
- No evidence of high mortality during emergence and migration

**Dead Egg Persistence:**

- October 25, 1961: 156 dead eggs per square meter (14% of collected eggs), no fragments recorded
- March 1962: 420 dead eggs per square meter (all eggs dead after hatching terminated)
- July 1962: 159 dead eggs per square meter
- August 1962: 98 dead eggs per square meter
- October 1962: 153 dead eggs per square meter (apparent increase possibly due to early mortality of 1962 brood year [[Chum Salmon]] eggs or fragment counting bias)
- March 1963: 70 dead eggs per square meter (18 per square meter whole eggs thought to be 1962 brood year)

**Dead Larval Disappearance:**

- March 1962: 104 dead larvae per square meter
- July 1962: 31 dead larvae per square meter
- August 1962: No larval remains found
- Dead larvae disappeared within 2 months after fry emergence

**Compositional Changes:**

Dead eggs progressively fragmented over time. By August 1962, the population of dead 1961 brood year eggs comprised mostly fragments. Dead larvae disappeared more rapidly than eggs, with complete absence by first week of August 1962. Decomposition caused eggs to disintegrate within 12 months of death, though fragments persisted for 18 months post-spawning.

**Water Quality Implications:**

Dissolved oxygen levels at 50% saturation in August 1962 coincided with approximately 100 dead eggs per square meter. High free carbon dioxide levels can reduce blood oxygen affinity ([[Bohr Effect]]). Ammoniacal nitrogen in un-ionized form is highly toxic to fishes. The evidence did not contradict the hypothesis that decomposing eggs may impair spawning bed water quality for periods exceeding one year.

**Environmental Factors:**

Water temperatures in 1961-62 were generally above normal (autumn below average, winter-spring-summer above average), potentially favoring higher than average decay rates. Nearly normal hydrological conditions prevailed, suggesting that low dissolved oxygen was not primarily due to unusual streamflow patterns but potentially related to decomposing organic matter.

**Methodological Considerations:**

- Spawning density in 1961: 1.1 females per square meter
- Losses from redd superimposition likely high due to spawning density
- Fragment counting introduced potential upward bias in later population estimates
- Small mesh size (2 mm) limited collection of smaller fragments
- 1962 brood year egg mortality (particularly [[Chum Salmon]]) introduced error to October 1962 and March 1963 estimates

The study demonstrates that residual dead eggs can persist in spawning beds for extended periods, potentially affecting water quality for subsequent brood years, particularly when spawning densities in the following year are low and insufficient to cleanse the streambed through redd construction activities.
