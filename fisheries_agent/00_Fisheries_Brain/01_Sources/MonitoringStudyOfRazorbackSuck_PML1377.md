# Monitoring Study of Razorback Suckers (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Reintroduced into the Lower Colorado River: April Monthly Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game monthly report covers April monitoring of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae after their reintroduction into the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Monitoring combined [[Q-Beam Spotlight Survey]] counts of larvae with [[Trammel Net]] sampling for predators. Larvae were abundant on the first survey day but declined sharply within two weeks of stocking. The report attributes the decline to predation, dispersal out of the stocking sites, or the loss of larval phototaxis as the fish grew. Non-native fish predation is identified as a major threat to native Southwestern fishes. (The report's year is garbled in the OCR text and is not reliably recoverable.)

## Background & Description

The Razorback Sucker is a native Southwestern catostomid whose populations have declined. Its larvae are strongly [[Phototaxis|phototactic]] for roughly the first three weeks of life, which allows them to be attracted to a handheld 0.5-watt spotlight (the OCR text reads "Q-3bam"; the method is the Q-Beam spotlight). The method was used to document larval presence and dispersal within each stocking site.

The [[Larval Fish Sampling]] procedure, revised after a March 25 meeting, was as follows:

1. Begin spotlighting one hour after sunset.
2. Anchor the boat at a predetermined sampling station. Stations are mapped for each stocking site, and their number ranges from 8 to 10 depending on site size.
3. Direct light at a small area (about 2 square meters) beside the boat for 10 minutes.
4. Capture larvae with a fine-mesh aquarium net, placing an inverted dip net in shallow water so larvae can swim free.
5. Count at least 30 larvae in the 10-minute period. If numbers are extremely high, give the best ocular estimate.
6. Preserve larvae in 10% formalin solution.
7. Place preserved larvae in tagged vials, recording the date, time, and location of capture.
8. Record site data, including water temperature, cover and vegetation, water depth, larvae captured, and larvae observed.
9. After 10 minutes, move to the next station and repeat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Surveys began one day after stocking so that the reintroduced larvae could acclimate to their new environment. The [[Parker Division Backwaters]] were surveyed first. Larvae were too numerous to count at several stations, and these were estimated at more than 1,000 each.

Counts generally increased with distance from the stocking location, except at the backwater above Island Lake, where few larvae were captured at any one time and no clear pattern emerged. Larval counts had dropped dramatically two weeks after stocking. The report suggests three possible explanations:

- Predation
- Transport out of the stocking site
- Larvae outgrowing their phototactic behavior

Larval swimming ability had noticeably improved over time, so larvae were much harder to capture several weeks after stocking. When numbers were low, all larvae that were captured were preserved and returned to the laboratory for identification.

Other species encountered included [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), several [[Sunfish]] species (*Lepomis cyanellus*, *L. macrochirus*, *L. microlophus*), and unidentified [[Centrarchidae]] individuals. The report notes that predation by non-native fish has been implicated as a major cause of the decline of native Southwestern fishes. Predator sampling used trammel nets (45 m long) set along shoreline cover where larvae had been released, with two nets at each stocking site. Fish were removed approximately every 2-3 hours and preserved whole so that stomach contents were retained. Water bodies sampled included [[Parker Division Backwaters]] (CA), [[Oxbow Pond]], the [[Backwater-River Confluence]], and the backwater near [[Tepaz Lake]] (CA). Tepaz Lake was not netted because of an earlier [[Rotenone Treatment]].

## Key Findings

- **Larval abundance declined sharply** within two weeks of stocking. The report offers predation, dispersal, and loss of phototaxis as possible causes.
- **Initial abundance was high.** Several Parker Division stations held an estimated 1,000+ larvae each on the first survey day.
- **Larval swimming ability improved** over time, making later capture much more difficult.
- **Non-native predators** were present and are considered a major threat to native Southwestern fishes.
- **Planned activities for May** include:
  - Removing about 500 juvenile Razorback Suckers from the [[Blythe Golf Course Ponds]] and restocking them into the Parker Division backwaters.
  - Netting the backwater-river confluence to document movement out of or retention within the backwater.
  - Conducting [[Stomach Content Analysis]] of previously captured predators.
- **Staff effort:** Staff worked 40 hours each week during April, across the work weeks of April 1-3, 6-10, 13-17, 20-24, and 27-30.
