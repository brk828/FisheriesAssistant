# Long-Term Trends of Native and Non-Native Fish Faunas in the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This research article by [[J. D. Olden]] and [[N. LeRoy Poff]] (Animal Biodiversity and Conservation, 2005) quantifies more than 160 years of distributional change for the fish fauna of the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. Using the [[SONFISHES Database]], which holds tens of thousands of locality records, the authors assess the decline of 28 native species and the spread of 48 non-native species. Native fishes show widespread, significant range losses, with the most severe declines in endangered and threatened taxa. Non-native species are expanding quickly, with the most aggressive invaders being [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Western Mosquitofish]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. The study also finds that species introduced after 1950 spread more slowly than those introduced earlier, and that native species' declines correlate strongly with their [[Local Extirpation Risk]], supporting proactive conservation.

## Background & Description

The article opens with the history of the [[Colorado River]], which has been dammed and diverted into one of the most controlled rivers on Earth. Irrigation covers more than 3.7 million acres of farmland, and the river supplies water and power to about 30 million people in the United States and Mexico. Water development began in the early 1900s and had converted much of the mainstem into a system of dams and diversions by the 1960s.

Of the 31 native fish species in the Lower Colorado Basin, 25 are extinct, extirpated, listed under the [[U.S. Endangered Species Act]], or believed to have suffered significant declines. Deliberate introductions of non-indigenous fishes began in the late 1800s, and more than half of these introduced species are considered established. Prior assessments of replacement of native fauna by non-natives were largely anecdotal, and quantitative data existed only for a limited number of mainstem "big-river" species.

The study has three aims: (1) quantify long-term distribution trends for the entire present-day species pool; (2) test whether these trends can serve as a surrogate for local extirpation risk; and (3) test the biological basis of the Endangered Species Act by comparing trends with official status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and data.** The study covers the lower basin, roughly 26,000 km of streams and rivers from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to the Gulf of California. The dataset includes 28,755 locality records. Records and total river kilometres surveyed rose across periods: 1,463 native and 462 non-native records before 1960, rising to 5,389 native and 5,334 non-native records in 1990–1999.

**Drivers of change.** Environmental alterations, including flow, temperature and sediment regime changes from dams, have compromised native fish life-history adaptations. These changes also facilitated human-assisted invasions by predatory and competing non-natives.

**Comparative regions.** Great Plains streams in Wyoming, Oklahoma and Kansas share harsh, disturbed conditions with desert streams and have also received many non-native species. Comparisons showed similar extant distribution sizes for 15 shared species, although spread rates differed, likely due to differing timing and rates of human introductions.

**Data limitations.** Sampling intensity increased over time, so the native declines reported are likely minimum estimates, while non-native spread rates may be overestimated. Reach-scale analyses may overestimate declines compared with drainage-scale analyses. Presence data are less informative than abundance data, and local population fluctuations in variable desert streams may confound trends.

## Key Findings

**Native fish declines**
- Of the 28 native species analysed, the largest declines were in [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (100% decline, not captured since 1975 in the lower basin), [[Desert Pupfish]] (100%), [[Moapa Dace]] (100%), [[Bonytail]] (87.7%), [[Gila Trout]] (84.0%) and [[Woundfin]] (78.9%).
- [[Spikedace]] declined 45.9%, and [[Speckled Dace]] declined 16.5%, with present-day distributions highly fragmented.
- Species with apparent increases or stability included [[Humpback Chub]] (–6.1% decline, shown as an increase), [[Longfin Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Sonora Sucker]].
- Declines were strongly correlated with local extirpation probability (R² = 0.807, P < 0.001). Extant distribution size also showed a significant negative quadratic relationship with extirpation probability (R² = 0.571, P < 0.001). A threshold appears near 10% extant distribution, with species below it facing much higher risk.

**Endangered Species Act status**
- Endangered species declined more than threatened species (t = 2.93, P = 0.01) and unlisted species (t = 4.33, P < 0.001).
- Endangered species had smaller extant ranges than threatened (P < 0.001) and unlisted species (P < 0.001). Threatened species had marginally smaller ranges than unlisted species (P = 0.08).
- Unlisted species had roughly three times larger extant distributions than threatened species, which in turn had about two times larger distributions than endangered species.
- Candidates for listing were proposed: [[Headwater Chub]] (threatened, on distribution grounds), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Virgin River Spinedace]] (endangered). [[Apache Trout]] was proposed for upgrading from threatened to endangered status. The authors note that the 1975 downlisting of Apache trout to facilitate recreational angling illustrates how socioeconomic factors can outweigh biology in listing decisions.

**Non-native fish spread**
- Most non-native species expanded their distributions. [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Red Shiner]] spread at over 50 km per year since introduction.
- Spread rate correlated positively with extant distribution size (R² = 0.874, P < 0.001).
- Species introduced after 1950 had limited distributions (<10%), while earlier introductions (especially 1920–1950) show much greater spread. The five fastest-spreading non-natives were all introduced between 1920 and 1950.
- Recent fast spreaders such as [[Blue Tilapia]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] are exceptions, probably due to inadvertent aquarium-trade releases and unauthorized introductions.

**Conservation implications**
- Monitoring and management of declining species before they reach extreme rarity is recommended as a proactive strategy.
- Education efforts should target reducing human-mediated introductions, since these drive spread.
- The authors conclude that the Endangered Species Act's biological basis is largely supported, while identifying specific listing and delisting candidates.
