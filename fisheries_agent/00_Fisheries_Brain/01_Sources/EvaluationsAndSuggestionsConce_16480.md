# Evaluations and Suggestions Concerning Personnel, Facilities, and Programs at Dexter National Fish Hatchery

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #endangered-species #hatchery-management #genetic-conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1985 evaluation by W. L. Minckley provides comprehensive recommendations for [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), an advanced-concept conservation center dedicated to maintenance and recovery of endangered fish species. The document addresses three major categories: personnel organization, facility requirements, and program development. Key recommendations include restructuring staff to encourage long-term expertise retention, expanding laboratory and holding facilities, implementing genetic monitoring programs, establishing backup stocks in aquaria, and developing a Fisheries Assistance Program for field monitoring. The author emphasizes that DNFH holds irreplaceable genetic stocks of species critically endangered or extinct in nature, including [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Woundfin]], [[Big Bend Gambusia]], [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], and [[Desert Pupfish]]. The facility's success in production must be balanced with rigorous genetic management and security protocols to prevent contamination and loss of these invaluable broodstocks.

## Background & Description

[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] serves three primary functions: refuge (providing perpetuation of unique species in their original state of genetic fitness), production (generating fish for reintroduction), and research (conducting in-depth study toward species recovery in natural habitats). The facility maintains broodstocks of multiple endangered species, some representing larger gene pools than exist in nature. Several species are restricted to single localities in the wild and could be lost through catastrophe, making DNFH stocks irreplaceable.

The document identifies critical species categories requiring refuge status:
1. Species lacking reproduction and approaching extinction in nature ([[Bonytail]], potentially [[Razorback Sucker]])
2. Species restricted to single vulnerable localities ([[Woundfin]], [[Big Bend Gambusia]], [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]], potentially [[Pahranagat Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]])
3. Species extirpated in the United States and unavailable from Mexico ([[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], [[Desert Pupfish]])

The facility has demonstrated spectacular success in producing diverse fishes including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], most chubs of genus *Gila*, pupfishes, and most poeciliids. However, challenges remain with specialized forms like [[Woundfin]] and [[Yaqui Chub]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses multiple ecological and management contexts:

**Genetic Considerations**: Maintaining original genetic fitness in broodstocks is complex. Selection and bottlenecks must be avoided to preclude major changes in gene frequency. Original heterogeneity should be maintained. For [[Razorback Sucker]], 500-1,500 wild-caught fish should be maintained to ensure genetic integrity based on the Lake Mohave population. [[Bonytail]] stock consists of approximately 1,500 fish originating from only 12 wild-caught adults, necessitating genetic analysis and pedigree breeding to avoid genetic drift and inbreeding.

**Security Threats**: Multiple contamination incidents documented:
- [[Pecos Gambusia]] contaminated and replaced a [[Sonoran Topminnow]] broodstock
- Male [[Leon Springs Pupfish]] appeared in experimental [[Razorback Sucker]] pond (spring 1985)
- Terns killed while preying on hatchery fish had cyprinids other than those held on station in their crops
- Osprey fed on fishes and could move individuals pond to pond
- [[Largemouth Bass]] appeared in eastern sump from unknown sources

**Habitat Requirements**: Large species like [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] require large ponds and substantial expenditures. Smaller poeciliids and cyprinodonts face challenges from diminutive sizes (potential misidentification), mixing risks between congeneric stocks, and tropical origins making overwintering difficult in hatchery ponds.

**Alternative Refuge Strategies**: The author recommends utilizing U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Refuge lands for big-river fishes and contract maintenance of smaller species by universities, state agencies, or private organizations. Isolated waters could be created on refuge lands, stocked with Dexter progeny, and managed as refuges and holding areas. Cost estimates for university maintenance: initial $5,000 with annual costs of $2,800 for one species, $4,000 for two species.

## Key Findings

**Personnel Recommendations**:
- Implement in situ progression system (Assistant → Assistant Manager → Manager) to encourage long-term expertise retention
- Reorganize under single Hatchery Manager with two Assistant Managers (one for physical facilities, one for production/broodstock or refuge/research)
- Add Office Manager, technical assistant, clerk/typist, two non-technical personnel, and two trainees
- Trainees should have training in basic ecology, systematics, or related fields with emphasis on fishes
- All technical personnel should be exempt from maximum-time limitations and encouraged to publish research results

**Facility Requirements**:
- **Laboratory**: One or more large rooms (15' × 18' or larger) with variable-height work tables, double stainless steel sinks, exhaust hood, twice standard electrical outlets, ample freezer/refrigerator space
- **Library**: Sorely lacking; must be developed with budget for books, journal subscriptions (minimum: *Copeia*, *Southwestern Naturalist*, *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, *Progressive Fish-Culturist*, *Journal of North American Fisheries Management*, regional academy journals)
- **Holding Facility**: Strongly recommended aquarium facility to house 20+ species in tanks large enough for 30+ individuals of largest species. Individual in/outflow of well water, moderate climate control (15-18°C winter, <25°C summer), natural lighting, isolated from public access, top security
- **Public Display**: Needs improvement; streamline operations by providing living food tank in building, freeze large volumes of plankton from pond drainage
- **Housing**: On-site housing major drawing card for trainees, postdoctoral personnel, visiting scientists; recommend semipermanent prefabricated housing (trailers)

**Production Program**:
- Balance needed between demand for [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] for reintroduction and need for ponds to develop techniques for difficult species like [[Woundfin]]
- Recommend two planning sessions per year including hatchery personnel, Albuquerque representatives, and Recovery Team Leaders
- Use [[Razorback Sucker]] as example species for genetic monitoring: pedigree spawning, monitoring of heterozygosity, gene frequency in carefully planned long-term study
- For [[Bonytail]]: genetic analysis and pedigree breeding almost a necessity (stock of ~1,500 fish from 12 wild-caught adults)
- Smaller fishes: train personnel in species identification of all life-history stages; maintain permanent stocks in holding facility to preclude overwintering problems

**Genetic Management**:
- Recent assessment of [[Sonoran Topminnow]] in journal *Science* criticized reintroduction program; broodstocks identified as homozygous and of low fecundity (latter in error)
- Heterozygous stocks now transferred to Dexter; records of relative success will provide field tests of fitness
- Preservation of samples: substantial series of 50+ specimens secured at least twice yearly, retained at hatchery minimum five years, then deposited in creditable museum facility

**Security Measures**:
- Recommend barriers (framed netting) to deter bird activities for critically endangered species maintained with congeners
- Ongoing program of removal of predators/potential fish transporters should be intensified
- Recommend minimum two hours daily by trained observer visiting all ponds in use, recording observations in log, including weekends and holidays
- Careful examination and identification of individuals when transferring stocks pond to pond
- If contamination detected, replace stock from holding facility or nature

**Research Program**:
- Potentials limited only by imagination, facilities, and money
- Two major areas: 1) propagation, production, and rearing investigations; 2) field experimentation and natural history (growth, foods, feeding, reproduction, ecological interrelations)
- Recommend forming Research Advisory Group (maximum eight technical personnel from agency and academic communities) to identify priorities, select appointees, advise on funding
- Trainees should come highly recommended from active research-oriented educators with directed research as duty assignment
- Senior scientists screened on demonstrated intent to work on specific endangered species problems

**Fisheries Assistance Program**:
- Logical progression of Service commitment to endangered species
- Duties split three ways: 1) field monitoring/evaluation of reintroduced and natural populations; 2) on-site studies at DNFH; 3) liaison with state/academic personnel and meeting attendance
- Field studies mandatory for evaluation and fine-tuning of recovery efforts; data acquisition must not be superseded by administrative duties
