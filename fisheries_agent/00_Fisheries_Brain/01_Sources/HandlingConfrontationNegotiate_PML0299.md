# Handling Confrontation: Negotiated Adaptive Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conflict-resolution

## Executive Summary

This working paper, an edited talk by Curt Brown of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], argues that endangered species and water conflicts in the American West are usually managed "at the brink" of extinction, resource exhaustion, or full allocation, and that the prevailing [[Legal-Regulatory Model]] of conflict worsens these disputes. The author proposes a [[Negotiated Adaptive Management]] alternative that defines conflict as a divergence of interests rather than a legal violation, begins negotiation early, treats knowledge as incomplete, and builds monitoring and dispute-resolution procedures into the agreement. The paper also reviews barriers to this approach and offers practical recommendations for building trust among parties.

## Background & Description

The paper was published as Working Paper 93-13 (October 14, 1993) by the [[Conflict Resolution Consortium]] at the University of Colorado, Boulder, as part of the Intractable Conflict/Constructive Confrontation Project. The talk was given April 10, 1993. The author notes that the views are his own and do not represent the Consortium, the University, or the [[William and Flora Hewlett Foundation]], which funded the project.

The paper opens by noting that every major western river system (the Platte, Colorado, Sacramento, San Joaquin, Snake, and Columbia) is involved in endangered species issues, with major studies underway. The author describes the situation as polarized, politicized, and litigated, with attorneys engaged and injunctions filed. He points to the interest of [[Interior Secretary Bruce Babbitt]] in ecosystem-based management that protects potentially imperiled species at lower cost and conflict.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is not a biological study and reports no species counts, population data, or water-quality measurements. Its environmental content is limited to the following:

- **Endangered species issues** on major western rivers, with the central [[Platte River]] under [[Section 7 Consultation]] over potential impacts to [[Whooping Crane]] habitat.
- **Instream flows** on National Forest lands, which the [[Forest Service]] sought to protect for aesthetic, recreational, and biological purposes.
- **Riparian values** of Platte River water, which the author says are not recognized in law and therefore often go undiscussed.
- **Growth impacts** on [[Rocky Mountain National Park]] from surrounding communities, addressed through the [[Gateways Project]].

## Key Findings

**1. The legal model constrains conflict resolution.**
- Under the [[Legal-Regulatory Model]], a problem is defined as a legal violation (for example, of Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]), which narrows the issues.
- The Forest Service instream-flow case illustrates this: the agency had to argue geomorphology tied to stream cross-sections, although its real concerns were aesthetic, recreational, and biological.
- Parties in the Platte negotiations avoid raising riparian values because the law does not recognize them, leading to frustration when proposals miss unnamed concerns.

**2. Legal decisions are often made before knowledge is adequate.**
- Courts are asked to predict impacts over the next twenty years on species that are not fully understood, producing poor or inflexible decisions.
- The author does not reject litigation. He acknowledges its value in exposing environmental impacts and protecting less powerful groups.

**3. Power has become more balanced.**
- The author states that environmental groups and traditional water-development interests are now roughly in equilibrium.
- He cites a National Water Resources Association conference, where a developer questioned why the Bureau of Reclamation supported solutions favored by conservation groups. The author says this view is now common among developers.

**4. Negotiated adaptive management offers an alternative.**
- Conflict is defined as competing interests, so any interest of a party can be placed on the table.
- Negotiation should start early, before a lawsuit, including negotiation of the dispute-resolution process and the [[Monitoring Program]].
- Small initial actions are implemented, monitored, and adjusted. Parties also negotiate how new proposals will be formulated in response to changing conditions.
- The [[Platte River Management Study]] is cited as an example. Parties have met for several years, established monitoring, and are nearing implementation. The Gateways Project is cited as a second example.

**5. Barriers to adoption are financial, institutional, psychological, and political.**
- **Financial:** Funding is hard to secure for long-term negotiation with no immediate product and open-ended monitoring. Results do not fit congressional election cycles.
- **Professional:** Many agencies and interest groups are led by attorneys who default to court, especially after past litigation successes.
- **Institutional:** Jurisdiction is unclear before a conflict crystallizes, and responsibility is diffused across agencies.
- **Psychological:** Trust in out-of-court processes is hard to build. Managers are focused on crises and reluctant to redirect resources toward emerging conflicts.
- **Political:** Litigation is more visible and aids fundraising than quiet negotiation. [[The Nature Conservancy]] is cited as a notable exception to this pattern.

**6. Conditions are changing.**
- New tools for water management, transfer, and compensation of water-right holders are emerging.
- The author argues that the best alternative (BATNA) for key players is shifting from litigation toward long-term negotiation. He notes that environmental groups may "win the battle but lose the war" with Section 7 suits.

**7. Recommendations.**
- Avoid situations that force one-shot, final decisions, since these heighten conflict and polarize issues.
- Begin dialogue early and start with willing participants. The author reports that others often join to monitor the process, and that critical mass can form quickly.
- Build trust through data sharing, joint data collection, and joint pursuit of funding. Once funding is secured, parties have a stake in defending the activity against budget cuts.
- Collect baseline data, which the author calls essential for long-term management.
- Agencies should reward negotiated approaches. Small sums that are trivial for a public agency can enable interest groups to attend meetings or collect data, and so serve as a strong incentive to negotiate.
