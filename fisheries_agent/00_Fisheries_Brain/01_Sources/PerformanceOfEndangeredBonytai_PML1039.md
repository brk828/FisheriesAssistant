# Performance of Endangered Bonytails Fed Four Commercial Diets

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluated how four closed-formula commercial feeds affected growth, survival, feed utilization, and body composition of juvenile [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a federally listed endangered cyprinid of the [[Colorado River System]]. Juveniles were reared indoors in a [[Recirculating Aquaculture System]] for 120 days at the [[Mora Fish Technology Center]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, New Mexico) between February and June 2002. The feed containing 40% crude protein and 10% crude lipid marketed for shrimp (the high-protein shrimp diet) produced significantly greater growth and better feed conversion than the catfish, trout, and low-protein shrimp diets, without reducing survival. The authors suggest this feed could support bonytail husbandry and recovery stocking, while noting that its nutritional basis and palatability effects remain unresolved.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** The bonytail is a relatively large cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River system. Populations have collapsed across most of its historic range, and the species is federally listed as endangered (USDI 1980). Decline is attributed mainly to habitat modification and land-use change.
- **Current range:** Restricted to isolated locations in the southwestern United States.
- **Recovery approach:** Captive propagation and population augmentation, with growing interest in water-reuse systems driven by water conservation needs (USFWS 1990).
- **Prior culture work:** Published husbandry information is limited. [[Hamman]] (1982) described induced spawning, egg incubation, and fry husbandry; [[Sowka and Brunkow]] (1999) studied cage culture of juveniles; [[Tomasso et al.]] (2003) examined nitrite toxicity and its mitigation with chloride. Dietary guidelines for captive bonytails were scarce.
- **Natural diet:** In the wild, bonytails and related *Gila* species (humpback chub, roundtail chub) feed on aquatic invertebrates and terrestrial insects falling into the water. They shift from planktonic feeding to surface feeding as they mature. Bonytails are omnivorous, eating insects, plant matter, and small fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Experimental system:** Sixteen 432-L circular fiberglass tanks (four replicates per treatment, 25 fish per tank) on a recirculating reuse system exchanging roughly 25 water volumes per day. Filtration included a rotating drum microscreen, a bead filter for nitrification, and a heat exchanger.
- **Photoperiod:** Natural-style photoperiod lengthened from 10.5 h light/13.5 h dark (day 0) to 14.5 h light/9.5 h dark (day 120).
- **Water quality (means):** Temperature 21.6 °C (range 21.2–22.3 °C); total ammonia nitrogen 0.04 mg/L (range 0.00–0.11 mg/L); nitrite 0.002 mg/L (range 0.000–0.007 mg/L); pH 8.5 (range 7.8–9.0); dissolved oxygen 6.3 mg/L (range 6.1–6.5 mg/L), close to saturation.
- **Initial stock:** Juveniles of the same year-class from multiple paired matings, averaging 19.0 g wet weight (range 16.1–20.9 g) and 132.0 mm total length (range 127.3–136.0 mm), at an average water temperature of 27.6 °C at stocking.
- **Feeding regime:** Hand-fed to satiation four times daily. Feed-consumption thresholds were raised from 1.5% to 2.0% of body weight per day as fish grew.

## Key Findings

**Diets tested** (2.4-mm pellets; nominal labels reflect target species and protein:lipid ratio):
1. Catfish diet: 42% crude protein, 12% crude lipid
2. Trout diet: 42% crude protein, 15% crude lipid
3. High-protein shrimp diet (HP): 40% crude protein, 10% crude lipid
4. Low-protein shrimp diet (LP): 28% crude protein, 10% crude lipid

**Growth:**
- At day 120, mean weight on the HP diet was 36.3 g, significantly higher than catfish (26.6 g), trout (24.7 g), and LP (24.2 g) diets.
- Specific growth rate at day 120 was 0.51% for HP, versus 0.24% (catfish), 0.28% (trout), and 0.23% (LP).
- Total length at day 120 averaged 146.9 mm overall; HP fish were significantly longer (161.2 mm).
- Condition factor did not differ significantly among treatments at the start or end.
- The authors describe HP-fed fish as more than twice as heavy as fish on the other diets after 120 days.

**Feed utilization:**
- Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was lowest on HP (2.5). Trout (6.0) and LP (6.2) were highest and significantly different. Catfish (4.6) overlapped with the others. FCRs across treatments ranged from 2.5 to 6.2.
- Protein efficiency ratio (PER) was highest on the shrimp diets (HP 2.68, LP 2.60) versus trout (1.86) and catfish (1.58).
- Apparent net protein retention (ANPR) was highest on HP (21.0%), lower on trout (19.3%) and catfish (13.8%), and intermediate on LP (16.4%).

**Survival:**
- Overall survival averaged 93.5% (range 84–97%) with no significant differences among diets (P > 0.05).
- Survival at day 120: LP 84.0%, catfish 97.0%, HP 96.0%, trout 97.0%.
- No mortality occurred during the first 44 days.

**Body composition:**
- Pre-experiment fish: 15.4% crude protein, 16.4% crude lipid, 2.1% ash, 65.9% moisture (pooled sample).
- At day 120, LP-fed fish had the lowest crude protein (13.9%), significantly below catfish-fed fish (15.8%), and higher lipid (23.5%) than HP-fed fish (17.2%).
- Moisture was highest on HP (65.1%) and lowest on LP (60.2%).

**Interpretation and recommendations:**
- Authors attribute the HP advantage to a combination of higher protein quality and digestibility, a lower FCR, and a higher protein-to-energy ratio. They suggest that feed attractiveness and palatability may have contributed, since fish on the two shrimp diets were more active feeders and wasted little feed, though palatability was not formally quantified.
- The HP diet was tested for 120 days only. Its suitability for longer rearing periods was not tested.
- Proprietary formulations were not fully disclosed, so the exact ingredient composition could not be verified.
- Recommended next steps: improve bonytail husbandry, shorten rearing cycles, and better coordinate stocking schedules. Developing a species-specific bonytail diet was also proposed to reduce reliance on proprietary feeds, along with examining digestible protein and energy in the test diets.
