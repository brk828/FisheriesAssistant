# Post-Flood Algal Recovery in Brier Creek, Oklahoma

#phycology #freshwater-ecology #algae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how a June 1983 flood reorganized the attached algal community of [[Brier Creek]], a small clear-flowing stream in S-central Oklahoma. Sampling before and after the flood, and again during a recovery period, shows that the flood scoured most attached algae, reshuffled substrates, and shifted the dominance of [[Filamentous Green Algae]]. Persistence depended on the alga's method of attachment and on substrate stability, not on whether a site was a pool or a riffle. Recovery was driven partly by [[Spirogyra]] accumulating as floating mats, while [[Rhizoclonium]] was more resistant to scour. The authors frame floods as a recurring disturbance that structures stream primary production.

## Background & Description

Floods are a major disturbance in streams. Their effects depend on flood frequency and magnitude and on antecedent conditions, and they are a central factor in [[Disturbance Ecology]] (Grimm and Fisher; Minshall et al.). Attached algae are a primary producer and a food base for many stream organisms, including grazing fishes, so changes in algal communities can ripple through the food web.

**Study site.** Brier Creek is a clear-flowing stream with a sandstone bedrock bed in the study reach. Riffles and pools were both sampled. Riffle substrates were dominated by cobbles and boulders. Pools were often sand-covered, and their algal communities differed from those of riffles.

**Study design.** The authors sampled 37 cross-stream transects (170 sites in total) before the flood, relocating 34 of them afterward. Sites were scored for dominant substrate, algal type, height, and condition, with percent cover estimated visually. Flood height and velocity were measured with a staff gauge and cross-sectional calculations, with velocity taken as 0.8 of surface velocity. A simulated scour experiment tumbled algae-covered cobbles in a [[Cement Mixer Flood Simulator]] to test attachment resistance.

**Flood timeline.**
- Pre-flood census: 13 June 1983.
- Crest: the largest flood of the year, with stage rising rapidly on 13 June.
- Water level had dropped to 0.71 m above base flow by 10:30 on 14 June.
- Post-flood census: 18 June.
- Recovery census: 8–10 July 1983.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dominant algae before the flood.** Filamentous green algae covered most substrates. [[Spirogyra]] was common in pools, and [[Rhizoclonium]] dominated riffles. [[Chara zeylanica]] was the dominant alga at about 22% of sites. [[Diatoms]] and [[Bluegreen Algae]] grew mainly under and among the filamentous greens.

**Substrate and algal retention.** Only 38% of sampled sites kept the same dominant substrate after the flood, and substrate change was slightly higher in pools (44%). Substrate stability rose with particle size: bedrock slabs were not moved, while some boulders over 256 mm median diameter were. The proportion of sites retaining algae rose with particle size:

| Substrate (Table 5 values) | Proportion retaining algae | Sites (n) |
|---|---|---|
| Mud/sand | 0.00 | 10 |
| Pebbles | 0.17 | 48 |
| Cobbles | 0.32 | 28 |
| Boulder/bedrock | 0.60 | 43 |

The overall association was significant (chi-square = 25.28). Retention was not tied to substrate change itself: the proportion of changed and unchanged sites retaining algae did not differ (chi-square = 3.04, df = 1, not significant).

**Attachment and resistance.** In the tumbling experiment, [[Rhizoclonium]] cobbles retained on average 12.4 (±1.6 SE) of 30 scored points, whereas [[Spirogyra]] cobbles retained only 1.5 (±0.6 SE), a significant difference (P < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U test). Rhizoclonium has basal attachment cells that anchor filaments to stones. Spirogyra is largely unbranched and free-floating, attached only weakly by lateral or terminal outgrowths. Pools and riffles did not differ in scour vulnerability: 5% of pool sites and 8% of riffle sites retained their original flora (P > 0.20).

**Post-flood communities.** Bluegreens (mainly [[Oscillatoria]]) dominated 5 of 90 pool sites and 3 of 80 riffle sites. Diatoms dominated 4 of 90 pool sites and 11 of 80 riffle sites. Increases in both groups were significant. Oscillatoria formed turquoise, bubble-studded skins on boulders and cobbles, where its photosynthesis appeared intense after release from the filamentous overstory.

**Recovery (18 June to 8–10 July).** No floods occurred, although one minor rise of a few cm occurred. Substrates remained unchanged at 67% of pool sites, 45% of riffle sites, and 59% of sites reach-wide, higher than during the flood interval. Spirogyra increased to 39% of sampled sites, while Rhizoclonium increased mainly in riffles and decreased in pools. Spirogyra replaced Rhizoclonium on 39% of the pool sites where Rhizoclonium had dominated four days after the flood. Floating Spirogyra mats shaded attached algae and may have reduced their growth.

**Transport and rafting.** Coarse particles (3–12 cm) bearing algal tufts were redeposited in pools, with about 3–6 stones per m² in one pool. The authors suggest these "rafts" aided recolonization of pools.

**Grazing and epiphytes.** Epiphytic diatoms heavily overgrew Rhizoclonium but not Spirogyra. Preliminary work suggested epiphytes increase preference for Rhizoclonium by the dominant grazer, the minnow [[Campostoma anomalum]]. Floods that strip epiphytes and displace Spirogyra mats could therefore periodically favor Rhizoclonium.

## Key Findings

1. **Flood scour was extensive.** The June 1983 flood removed most filamentous green algae, leaving only thin fringes on cobbles, boulders, and bedrock edges. Only 2% of the sampled sites retained their original algal cover after the flood (for Spirogyra and Rhizoclonium, respectively, the scan is partly illegible).
2. **Occurrence fell unevenly.** Rhizoclonium occurrence declined fourfold across all 170 sites, while Spirogyra occurrence declined twentyfold.
3. **Attachment mode controls persistence.** Rhizoclonium resisted tumbling far better than Spirogyra (12.4 vs 1.5 retained points, P < 0.001).
4. **Substrate stability is key.** Algal retention increased from mud/sand (0.00) to boulder/bedrock (0.60), and bedrock was not moved by the flood.
5. **Habitat type did not change vulnerability.** Pools and riffles lost algae at similar rates (5% vs 8% retention).
6. **Community shifted toward diatoms and bluegreens.** Post-flood increases in bluegreens (Oscillatoria) and diatoms were significant, and Oscillatoria persisted on rocks after overstories were abraded.
7. **Recovery was rapid but patchy.** Within about three weeks, Spirogyra overtook Rhizoclonium in frequency, especially in pools, through floating-mat accumulation.
8. **Flood frequency matters.** The authors argue that frequent floods favor tightly attached, resistant forms and would prevent Chara from persisting in Brier Creek, while small floods can trigger filamentous blooms.
9. **Disturbance regime shapes coexistence.** Without floods, Spirogyra and Rhizoclonium likely compete for space and nutrients. Floods may maintain their coexistence by periodically removing the dominant competitor, consistent with the disturbance-coexistence ideas of Sousa (1984).
