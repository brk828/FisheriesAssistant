# Movements and Growth of Fishes in the Gila River Drainage, Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 study in *The Southwestern Naturalist* (vol. 32, no. 3, pp. 351–356) reports a [[Mark-Recapture]] program on native and introduced fishes of the [[Gila River]] drainage in New Mexico and Arizona, run from April 1983 through May 1985 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish. Of 1,754 tagged fishes, only 53 were recaptured, and just two of these had moved away from their release site. The authors conclude that the fishes were highly [[Sedentary Behavior]]. Growth was slow, especially in adult [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Mountain Sucker]]. The contrast with a more mobile population in [[Aravaipa Creek]] is attributed mainly to differences in stream size and thermal regime.

## Background & Description

Movement patterns and [[Home Range]] use of fishes in the lotic desert streams of the American Southwest were poorly understood before this work. Most earlier information was anecdotal, and the few quantitative studies came from the [[Colorado River]] system, where [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were shown to make large migrations. The authors combined two concurrent fishery inventories in the Gila and San Francisco rivers, and each included a tagging component designed to reveal movement and home range.

The study taxa were:

- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*), a native cyprinid
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*), a native catostomid and the most heavily tagged native species
- **[[Desert Mountain Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus clarki*), a native catostomid
- **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*), an introduced cyprinid
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*), **Yellow Bullhead** (*Ictalurus natalis*), and **[[Flathead Catfish]]** (*Pylodictis olivaris*), ictalurids
- **[[Smallmouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus dolomieu*), a centrarchid

Eight species were tagged in total.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Collections covered a large area, but most effort was concentrated in the Gila River from [[Mogollon Creek]] downstream to the Middle Box reach, including the [[Cliff-Gila Valley]]. Initial surveys sampled stream reaches about every 3 to 5 km to define distributional limits. Several sites were then chosen for regular sampling. Canyon reaches were sampled less intensively than valley reaches, so few recaptures came from canyon habitat.

**Sampling methods.** Study sites were 100 to 300 m long. Fish were collected with [[Electrofishing]] (pulsed DC gear), using one to three passes per site and sampling all available habitats within each site. Subadult and adult fishes were tagged with [[Floy Tag]]s, Carlin fingerling tags, or small mammal ear tags, then released within the sample site. Total length, weight, and tag number were recorded. Fishermen's reports of recaptures were also collected. Movement was defined as recapture outside the release site. Because of imprecise weight data, only total length was used to evaluate growth.

**Physical setting.** The Gila and San Francisco rivers are much larger and deeper than Aravaipa Creek, with greater discharge. The authors suggest that this difference reduced any need for seasonal movement into shaded canyon reaches to avoid intense solar radiation. The study also noted two major floods during the period, which did not appear to displace the fishes.

## Key Findings

**Recapture and movement**

- 1,754 fishes were tagged, and 53 (about 3%) were recaptured. Recaptures came from five species directly and three species more than once.
- Only two recaptured fishes, both *C. insignis*, were found away from their release site. One, released 13 December 1983, was recaptured 162 days later at a distance given as 5.3 km. The other, tagged 8 December 1983, reappeared 167 days later 4 km upstream.
- No movement was detected among the multiple recaptures (one channel catfish, three Sonora suckers, and one desert mountain sucker, each recaptured twice).
- Spawning did not appear to trigger movement. Ripe Sonora suckers tagged on 22 March 1984 were recaptured at the same location on 30 May 1984, a period that spanned the estimated spawning season.
- The authors conclude that the fishes were evidently sedentary and that the few vagile individuals were rare.

**Time between tagging and recapture**

- Mean interval was 128 ± 23 days (95% confidence limits).
- Maximum interval was 428 days.
- One fish was recaptured the same day as release during repeated collections.

**Growth**

- Growth data were available from 41 recaptures. The other 12 could not be determined accurately and were excluded.
- Of 33 recaptured Sonora suckers, 16 increased in total length, 4 were unchanged, and 13 showed apparent decreases. Increases averaged 19 mm (range 1 to 53 mm). Decreases were small and probably reflected fin erosion or measurement error.
- Of six recaptured desert mountain suckers, five increased by an average of 6 mm (range 1 to 12 mm). One showed an apparent decrease.
- The twice-recaptured channel catfish grew 28 mm during its first release period (76 days) but added only 3 mm over the next 259 days.
- Overall, growth was slow, and many individuals showed none.

**Interpretation**

- The slow growth is probably not an artifact of tagging. Fish appeared healthy and active on release, with no disorientation, excessive tissue damage, or blood loss. Recaptured fish showed only minor tag irritation.
- The authors attribute the slow growth mainly to low adult growth rates. This is consistent with other western cypriniforms, which grow quickly to adult size and then grow very little over long adult lives. The same pattern was reported for [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] in the White River, Colorado, and for Gila robusta in this study area (Bestgen 1985).
- Sedentary habits may be energetically conservative and increase familiarity with local habitat. Movement, by contrast, may raise vulnerability to predation.

**Comparison with other studies**

- Siebert (1980) documented seasonal movements of the same species in [[Aravaipa Creek]], moving into broad shallow reaches in summer and back to canyon reaches in winter. Siebert hypothesized that this avoided high temperatures and solar radiation.
- The authors suggest that Gila River fishes may move under different discharge or thermal regimes, and that the lack of movement here reflects habitat dimensions and physical conditions rather than a fixed trait.
- Radio-telemetry studies of Colorado squawfish and razorback suckers have found that some individuals are sedentary while others make large movements, often tied to sexual maturity.
