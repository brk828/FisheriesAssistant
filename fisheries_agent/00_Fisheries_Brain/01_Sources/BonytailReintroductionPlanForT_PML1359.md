# Bonytail Reintroduction Plan for the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources final report (March 1996, Publication No. 95-8) presents a plan to reintroduce hatchery-reared [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) into the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The authors state that wild bonytail captures are infrequent, that numbers are too low for natural recovery, and that reintroduction is the only realistic route to recovery; without it, the species should be considered extirpated from the UCRB. The plan is an adaptive, interactive framework built on a six-year schedule in which laboratory and field experiments inform stocking decisions in later years. It was prepared under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] and coordinated with the [[Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan]] and the [[Genetics Management Plan]].

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and identity.** Bonytail is an endemic cyprinid of the Colorado River system, closely related to [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]. Pre-1970 records are only tentatively attributable to *G. elegans* because of possible hybridization and identification difficulty. Its taxonomic integrity remains debated, and mtDNA is rare but present in the UCRB.

**Listing and decline.** Bonytail was listed as federally endangered in 1980 after near-disappearance. Declines are attributed to habitat loss from dams, interactions with nonnative species, active elimination of native fish in dam tailwaters, and water pollution from industry and railroads dating to the 1930s. The USFWS ranked its recovery priority as 5C (high priority) because of low numbers, undocumented wild recruitment, and conflict with economic development.

**Historical distribution.** Early accounts place bonytail throughout the Colorado system more than 100 years ago, but its historical range is considered unknown, partly because common-name use in early records confounds distribution data. Its concentration was reported in Split Mountain Canyon of the Green River through [[Dinosaur National Monument]].

**Current status.** Very few positively identified bonytail have been captured in the UCRB since 1980: Valdez (1985) reported 14 possible individuals (including juveniles) in Cataract Canyon; Kaeding et al. (1986) captured one in Black Rocks; Tyus et al. (1987) captured one in Gray Canyon; and a suspected adult was taken near the Green River confluence in 1993. Relict populations persist in [[Lake Mohave]] and Lake Havasu in the Lower Basin. Hatchery broodstock derives from Lake Mohave fish collected 1974–1989 and is held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico.

**Recent reintroduction.** A 1988 study (Bureau of Reclamation, USFWS, UDWR and Utah State University) surgically implanted 86 fish with radio transmitters and released them into the Green River in Dinosaur National Monument. Documented mortality was 74.5% (likely higher), with 67 deaths occurring while fish were held in tanks; mortality was attributed to domestication and stress.

**Life history.**
- *Habitat:* Historically in open mainstem reaches; adults use eddies and pools near swift currents over silt and boulder substrates, and mixing zones between eddies and runs. Juveniles prefer low-velocity, sheltered habitats with boulder cover.
- *Movement:* Chart and Cranney (1993) recorded diel movement peaking at crepuscular periods, greater winter activity in mornings, mostly downstream movement, and higher movement at high discharge.
- *Swimming:* Berry and Pimentel (1985) found bonytail better swimmers than [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], but comparable to many freshwater fishes.
- *Feeding:* Adults feed mainly on terrestrial insects, plant debris and algae, likely as surface feeders. Age-1 fish fed like trout near the substrate, suggesting an ontogenetic shift from benthic to more generalized or pelagic feeding.
- *Reproduction:* Spawning was observed in Lake Mohave (May 1954) as broadcast spawning over gravel, with females escorted by three to five males. Wild spawning has not been observed under riverine conditions. Based on available knowledge, spawning likely occurs in late spring on the descending limb of the hydrograph. Marsh (1985) reported highest larval survival at 15 and 20 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hybridization.** Hybridization with humpback and roundtail chub is a central reintroduction concern. Vanicek and Kramer (1969) found bonytail and roundtail chub in the same areas but never in the same gill net, suggesting spatial rather than temporal isolation. Hamman (1982) produced viable hybrid offspring artificially, but such crosses did not occur naturally in hatcheries. Hybridization may reflect a breakdown of natural isolation caused by human activity, or a natural hybrid swarm within the *Gila* complex. Flow regimes resembling natural spring peaks and late-summer lows may support spatial, temporal or ethological isolation.

**Nonnative fishes.** Nonnative fish compete with and prey on native fishes, and their impacts on bonytail have been only minimally assessed. Nonnative fish gradually dominated native fish during low-discharge periods, while native fish dominated during high flows. Preliminary work (Bissonette and Crowl, 1995) suggests feeding efficiency of age-1 bonytail declines in the presence of red shiners. Predation on humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker and other natives by nonnative fish is confirmed; predation on bonytail is undocumented but modeled.

**Predation vulnerability.** Alder and Crowl (1995) applied gape-limitation theory to Green River predators. The largest predators in the Jensen–Sand Wash section are northern pike (to 710 mm TL) and channel catfish (to 490 mm TL). Bonytail of 150 mm TL are beyond most existing predation risk, so this size was adopted as the target stocking length.

**Domestication.** Captive-reared fish may lose the ability to adapt to wild conditions. Strategies include shortened captivity (Nehring 1991), pre-release physical and behavioral conditioning, and acclimation to natural river water.

**Recovery relationship.** The [[Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan]] includes a goal to "reintroduce hatchery-reared bonytail chub into the wild" (Goal 1.3). The [[Genetics Management Plan]] (Wydoski 1995) recommends a bonytail restoration stocking plan, which this document fulfills.

## Key Findings

**Goal:** Establish four stable, self-sustaining bonytail populations in the UCRB through a stocking program.

**Preliminary objective:** Minimize handling, tagging and radio-implant stress, and determine protocols that do so.

**Objective 1 – Minimize hybridization risk.** Determine the extent of habitat segregation (Lab Years 1–3; Field Years 4–6) and spatial, temporal or ethological spawning segregation (Field Years 4–6). Evaluate hybridization impacts through lab crosses of hybrid versus pure offspring and field monitoring of genetic markers (allozymes, mtDNA) for baseline levels in Year 1 and changes in Years 3 and 5.

**Objective 2 – Minimize nonnative interactions.** Determine size-specific predation vulnerability at release and the impacts of competition with nonnative species on bonytail fitness.

**Objective 3 – Condition bonytail for post-stocking survival.** Determine effects of flow training on muscular fitness and feeding, and identify the most effective conditioning regime. Existing trials on 180 age-1 fish from the 1993 year class (raised at moderate flow 0.03–0.06 m/s) showed changes in mitochondrial volume and muscle fibre diameter and number. Data are still needed for ages 2–4.

**Objective 4 – Experimental stocking.** Determine optimal number, size, condition, timing and location of releases.

**Year 1 stocking plan:**
- *Source:* Lake Mohave broodstock at Dexter NFH; ten thousand larvae to UDWR ponds at Wahweap Hatchery for 5–6 months of growth and flow training; family lots of about 100 each.
- *Number:* At least 4,000 bonytail (400 per family lot) pit-tagged; 2,000 released at each of two sites.
- *Size:* Target 150 mm TL, released in late summer (September) after acclimation.
- *Condition:* Flow conditioning, live food supplementation, and two weeks of acclimation to river water in holding pens in slack water.
- *Timing:* Late-summer base flows, allowing six to eight months of acclimation before spring high flows.
- *Locations:* Two sites chosen to minimize hybridization: Green River valley below Desolation Canyon (RM 132–129) and Moab Canyon (RM 60–20), or Professor Valley (RM 90–80). Table 3 lists about ten candidate sites.
- *Monitoring:* Bimonthly sampling for the first 2–3 months, then monthly sampling in March and April of Year 2 for overwinter survival.

**Survival projections:** Using razorback sucker survival estimates as a surrogate, Table 2 projects 600 surviving bonytail in Year 1 (or 180 by Year 2, depending on the table reading), with the expectation that estimates will be revised with actual data.

**Years 2–6:** Stocking numbers and release sites should increase as lab results become available. Table 4 projects 2,000–5,000 fish per site for FY 1996–1998 at Green River and Moab Canyon sites. Chart and Cranney (1993) found surviving fish from the 1988–1989 stockings settled into localized areas, so additional sites will likely be needed.

**Power analysis:** A sample-size formula was specified for the field experiment using a power of 70%, based on Year 1 release–recapture data from three lots of 100, 125 and 150 mm TL fish, resampled every three weeks for three months.
