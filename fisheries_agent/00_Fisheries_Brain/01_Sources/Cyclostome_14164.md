# Cyclostome

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Petromyzonidae #Myxinidae #jawless-vertebrates #Agnatha

## Executive Summary

This encyclopedic entry provides a comprehensive overview of cyclostomes—the lampreys and hagfishes—which constitute the class [[Cyclostomata]]. These primitive, jawless, eel-shaped fishes represent approximately 50 species inhabiting temperate waters worldwide. The document covers their economic importance (primarily as parasites damaging commercial fisheries), distinctive anatomical features, complete life history, evolutionary relationships to fossil [[Ostracodermi]], and taxonomic classification into two major groups: [[Hyperoartii]] (lampreys) and [[Hyperotreti]] (hagfishes). Cyclostomes are scientifically significant as the most primitive living vertebrates, with the lamprey larva (ammocoete) considered the closest living facsimile of ancestral backboned animals.

## Background & Description

Cyclostomes are aquatic, jawless vertebrates characterized by round mouths, eel-shaped bodies, and smooth, scaleless, slimy skin. Maximum size ranges from less than six inches to approximately three feet. The approximately 50 known species inhabit temperate zone waters; most are marine, though some lampreys are confined to freshwater lakes and streams, and sea lampreys spawn in fresh waters.

**Distinctive Anatomical Features:**
- No jaws (gill arches not converted to jaw structures)
- Complete absence of paired fins (pectorals and pelvics)
- Cartilaginous skeleton without calcification
- Persistent notochord throughout life, nowhere constricted
- No true vertebrae (centra)
- Muscular, protrusible tongue bearing horny teeth (not dentine or enamel)
- Gills in spherical pouches connecting to pharynx and exterior through tubes
- Internal ear with only one or two semicircular canals (horizontal canal missing)
- Small, primitive brain; incomplete brain case (especially in hagfishes)
- Single median olfactory sac with external opening on midline (classified as [[Monorhini]])

**Lamprey-Specific Features:**
- Nasal opening on top of head
- Pituitary canal closed internally
- Well-developed eyes and pineal eyes
- Oral funnel (buccal disc) with teeth
- Seven gill openings close to head
- Conspicuous branchial basketwork skeleton
- One or two well-developed dorsal fins
- Compact kidneys
- Minute eggs without horny shell

**Hagfish-Specific Features:**
- Nasal opening at front of head
- Pituitary canal opening into pharynx
- Cartilaginous rings around pituitary canal
- Vestigial eyes; degenerate pineal eyes
- Six tentacles about mouth and nostril
- 5 to 14 gill openings remote from head
- Greatly reduced branchial skeleton
- No dorsal fin or only trace
- Very large eggs with horny shell

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Distribution:**
Cyclostomes occupy diverse aquatic habitats across temperate zones. Marine species include seagoing lampreys and all hagfishes. Freshwater species include lake and stream-dwelling lampreys. [[Petromyzon marinus]] (sea lamprey) demonstrates anadromous behavior, migrating from marine or large lake environments into streams for spawning.

**Feeding Ecology:**
Parasitic lampreys attach to fishes using their buccal disc and drain blood, causing significant mortality to host species. Larger lampreys in the [[Great Lakes]] feed extensively on [[Lake Trout]]. Hagfishes exhibit internal parasitism, eating through flesh of food fishes, causing greater damage than lampreys. Fish caught in nets or on set lines are particularly vulnerable. Nonparasitic brook lampreys do not feed as adults; their gut shrinks to a solid strand after metamorphosis.

**Economic Impact:**
Most cyclostomes are considered harmful because they destroy food and game fishes. The sea lamprey in the Great Lakes inflicts vast damage after entering via canal systems. Control efforts include weir placement in spawning streams to catch mature adults and chemical treatments to destroy larvae. Conversely, lampreys are esteemed as food in many regions, and larvae are extensively used as bait by freshwater anglers. Adult brook lampreys and larvae of all lampreys provide natural food for [[Trout]] and other game fishes.

**Geographic Distribution:**
- **Northern Hemisphere:** [[Petromyzon marinus]] (both sides of Atlantic), genera [[Eudontomyzon]], [[Entosphenus]], and [[Lampetra]] (Eurasia and North America), [[Ichthyomyzon]] (eastern North America only), [[Caspiomyzon]] (Caspian Sea basin)
- **Southern Hemisphere:** [[Geotria]] and [[Mordacia]] (Chile, New Zealand, Australia coasts)
- **Hagfishes:** North Atlantic, western North America, Japan, New Zealand, Chile, Patagonia, South Africa; some species in deep tropical waters

## Key Findings

**Life History of Lampreys:**
All lampreys spawn in spring on gravel beds in streams. Marine species migrate into streams to breed, ascending vertical walls by adhering with sucking mouth discs. Males arrive first and prepare nests by fashioning shallow depressions through removal of pebbles. Females maintain hold on stones while males clasp females and fertilize eggs during extrusion. Parents cover eggs with loosened pebbles, then become emaciated and die shortly after spawning completion.

Eggs hatch after approximately two weeks. Larval (ammocoete) stage lasts several years (at least three, often more). Wormlike, toothless, nearly blind larvae inhabit burrows in mixed sand and mud bottoms, emerging at night to feed on organic ooze strained through thick cirri in hoodlike mouth. Multiple size groups indicate successive year classes.

Rapid transformation (metamorphosis) occurs in late summer and fall. Simultaneous changes include: eye enlargement and development, hood replacement by disc and cirri by teeth, nostril opening migration from front to top of head, body becoming rounder in cross section with firmer flesh and brighter coloration. Lamprey shrinks considerably in length during metamorphosis.

Post-metamorphosis, sea lampreys become silvery and large-eyed, then promptly migrate to ocean. Some nonparasitic brook lampreys pass through macrophthalmia stage despite abandoning seaward journey. All parasitic lampreys feed and grow rapidly on fish blood diet. Freshwater species live thus for one year or more; larger size reached in sea suggests faster growth rather than longer lifespan. After sexual maturity, parasitic species spawn once and die. Nonparasitic species metamorphose at size comparable to parasitic relatives but develop poorly formed teeth and solid gut strand. Without feeding, they complete sex cell ripening begun before metamorphosis, overwinter, then spawn and die.

**Hagfish Life History:**
Hagfishes deposit large, yellowish, yolk-filled eggs enclosed in protective horny shells, attached to sea bottom by threadlike tendrils. Development is slower than lampreys; young are much larger when hatched and resemble adults, developing gradually without metamorphosis. Life history and structure differ markedly from lampreys.

**Evolutionary History and Fossil Relatives:**
Fossil record and comparative anatomy indicate [[Cyclostomata]] are cousins of earliest and lowest vertebrates. Oldest fossilized vertebrate remains from [[Silurian]] deposits (with traces in [[Ordovician]] rocks) show similarity to lampreys and hagfishes despite superficial differences. Extinct forms resembled cyclostomes in lacking jaws, lacking true paired fins (though some developed flipperlike flaps), gill structure, having two semicircular canals in internal ear, and possessing single median nostril.

Three fossil groups (orders) show relationship with lampreys and hagfishes, all Paleozoic, none persisting beyond [[Devonian]]:

1. **[[Anaspida]]** (Silurian and Devonian): Most lampreylike fossils, primitive due to small size (none exceeding ten inches), almost ordinary fish form, small scales overlaying muscle segments. Lateral eyes, single nostril in front of pineal eye, row of porelike gill openings on each side.

2. **[[Osteostraci]]** (Late Silurian and Early Devonian): Median superior nostril and row of porelike gill openings, but head enclosed in flat bony shield. Abundant in Late Silurian and Early Devonian, disappeared by end of Devonian.

3. **[[Heterostraci]]**: Armoured fishes closely resembling Osteostraci. Armour lacked bone cells characteristic of Osteostraci. Paired eyes far apart, no dorsal nostril. Olfactory organs close together but paired, supposedly opening into mouth cavity.

**[[Palaeospondylus]]** (Devonian group Cycliae or Palaeospondyloidea): One of most problematical fossil vertebrates. One- to two-inch fishlet remains abundant in one Scottish quarry. Some consider it ancient lamprey or lampreylike animal; if so, living lampreys are very degenerate, lacking vertebral centra well-developed in fossil. Others regard it as larval arthrodire. Opinions differ on presence of gills or paired fins; relationships remain uncertain.

Most early fishes heavily armoured with dermal bone (group name [[Ostracodermi]]). Logical assumption: both armoured and progressive types derived from unarmoured fishes with usual fish form. Heavily specialized mailed kinds probably evolved for protection from enemies like gigantic "water scorpions." Such types had greater fossilization and preservation chance than slender, soft-bodied, free-swimming ancestors. Highly evolved, heavily armoured types persisted throughout Late Silurian and all Devonian, alongside earlier jawed ([[Gnathostomata]]) fishes classified as [[Placodermi]]. No Ostracodermi known beyond end of Devonian, but some primitive [[Agnatha]] member must have persisted to give rise to living group. Following evolutionary rule, highly specialized group died out while generalized relative lived on.

**Taxonomic Classification:**
Cyclostomata divided into two subgroups with extensive, fundamental differences leading some authorities to question relationship, though remote common ancestor appears likely:

**Family Petromyzonidae** (chief lamprey family): All northern hemisphere species. [[Petromyzon marinus]] occurs both sides of Atlantic. Related genera [[Eudontomyzon]], [[Entosphenus]], and [[Lampetra]] occur in Eurasia and North America. Primitive genus [[Ichthyomyzon]] (most species) inhabits only fresh waters of eastern North America. [[Caspiomyzon]] confined to Caspian Sea basin. Approximately half of Petromyzonidae species are dwarfed, nonparasitic brook type with weak teeth. Nearly all arose through degeneration from different parasitic kinds, named as distinct species.

**Families Geotriidae and Mordaciidae**: Distinctive southern hemisphere lampreys, each with single genus ([[Geotria]] and [[Mordacia]]). Live along coasts of Chile, New Zealand, and Australia. Spawn in fresh water like all lampreys.

**Family Myxinidae** (hagfishes): All marine, fewer than 24 species recognized. Except few species in deep tropical waters, all inhabit temperate zones. Some authorities restrict Myxinidae to genera (principally [[Myxine]]) with single external gill opening each side, placing genera ([[Eptatretus]], etc.) with 6-14 openings in separate family Eptatretidae. Family separation generally abandoned due to similar basic structure of all hagfishes and closely approximated gill openings of genus [[Paramyxine]].

**Scientific Significance:**
Cyclostomes are of great scientific importance as most primitive living vertebrates. Lamprey larva (ammocoete) regarded as providing closest facsimile of ancestor of backboned animals. For this reason, ammocoete recommended as study type in comparative anatomy courses. Adult lampreys and hagfishes depart from ancestral form through specialization for parasitic existence.
