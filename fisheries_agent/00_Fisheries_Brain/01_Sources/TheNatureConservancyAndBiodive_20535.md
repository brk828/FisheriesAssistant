# The Nature Conservancy and Biodiversity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #biodiversity #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This January/February 1994 magazine column by [[John C. Sawhill]], President and Chief Executive Officer of [[The Nature Conservancy]], introduces a special issue on [[Biodiversity]]. Sawhill frames the term as a widely used but loosely defined buzzword and presents four expert essays on its definition, protection, management and relationship to people. The column centers on the Conservancy's expansion into [[Indonesia]], a [[Biodiversity Hotspot]] that the author describes as "the Amazon of Southeast Asia," and it reports the organization's 1993 accomplishments in the United States and abroad. Fish appear only incidentally, through a clownfish observation and a species survey in Palau. The document is a promotional and programmatic statement rather than a research study.

## Background & Description

The column opens with an anecdote from [[Lore Lindu National Park]] in Indonesia, where a guide's imitation of a bugle call was answered by a [[Spectral Tarsier]], a small nocturnal mammal that can jump about 6 feet. Sawhill uses the episode to show how much biodiversity remains hidden from people. He quotes [[E.O. Wilson]]: "The loss of species is the folly our descendants are least likely to forgive us."

The issue assembles four expert contributions:

- [[Peter Raven]] (Missouri Botanical Garden): defining biodiversity, assessing losses and explaining why it must be saved.
- [[Bruce Babbitt]] (Secretary of the Interior): the past, present and future of biodiversity protection.
- [[Joan Bird]] (writer and environmental consultant): managing biodiversity, arguing that species and ecosystems cannot simply be left alone.
- [[Thomas Lovejoy]] (Smithsonian Institution): the relationship between people and biodiversity.

Each essay is paired with a short Conservancy perspective piece.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sawhill presents Indonesia as a priority for global conservation. The figures he cites are:

- Indonesia has 210 endangered species, more than any other country, according to the [[International Union for the Conservation of Nature]].
- Its 17,000 islands form the world's largest archipelago and hold 10 percent of the world's rain forests.
- The country's tropical forests contain more than 10 percent of the world's plant species, 12 percent of its birds, 12 percent of its mammals and 16 percent of its reptiles and amphibians.
- Its rain forest and marine ecosystems are described as unparalleled in biological wealth.

The Conservancy's Indonesia work focuses on two protected areas on [[Sulawesi]]: Lore Lindu National Park and Morowali Nature Reserve. Together they cover 454,000 hectares (1.25 million acres) and support endangered species including the [[Babirusa]], [[Anoa]], [[Sulawesi Civet]], [[Red-knobbed Hornbill]] and [[Maleo]] fowl. The column also mentions a [[Clownfish]] living among the tentacles of a sea anemone as an example of the marine diversity of the region.

Sawhill describes three lessons carried over from the organization's 20 years of work in [[Latin America]]:

1. **National parks:** Parks often exist only on paper and lack adequate protection, but their protected status and their potential for cooperation with governments and nongovernmental organizations make them strong targets for involvement.
2. **Partnerships:** Building strong in-country partner organizations that can eventually work without outside help. In Indonesia this includes equipping park guards with backpacks, compasses and radios.
3. **Integrated development:** Economic development must be combined with environmental protection. Examples include a program to promote sustainably produced [[Rattan]], which is often taken by poachers and used for furniture, and soil and water conservation work with [[CARE International]] to counter unsustainable agriculture.

## Key Findings

**Indonesia partnership and policy**
- The Conservancy is providing technical support to the [[Indonesian Institute of Sciences]] to build a national biodiversity database covering the status, distribution and value of plant and animal species and ecosystems.
- The database will use the [[Biological and Conservation Data System]], software developed by the Conservancy and used in every US state and in 14 Latin American countries.
- Sawhill discussed the project with [[President Soeharto]], who pledged continued efforts to protect biodiversity. The two also agreed on "South-North-South" exchanges, in which Indonesian officials and nongovernmental leaders would visit Conservancy projects in the United States and Latin America. Soeharto, as chairman of the [[Non-Aligned Movement]], was described as having an important role as an environmental leader.

**1993 accomplishments**
- The Conservancy protected more than one million acres, its second-best year, and its membership reached an all-time high of 720,000.
- With the [[Walt Disney Company]] in Florida, it protected the 8,500-acre Walker Ranch, which Sawhill expects to become a model for mitigation agreements.
- In South Carolina it obtained the largest easement in the state's history, protecting a 29,000-acre watershed near Greenville.
- In California it reached an agreement with the [[Irvine Company]] to manage 17,000 acres of undeveloped land.
- At the [[Tallgrass Prairie Preserve]] in Oklahoma, crews began planned burns across 24,000 acres in March, and in October 300 bison were released.
- At the [[Gray Ranch]] in New Mexico, a partnership with the [[Animas Foundation]] was formed to protect 502 square miles.
- At the [[Virginia Coast Reserve]], the Conservancy and Old Dominion University announced the Virginia Coast Institute, a facility for applied research on sustainable economic development.
- The Conservancy passed along the largest single land donation in history, about 182,000 acres, to the government of Venezuela.
- It helped partners purchase critical areas in Paraguay, Belize and Panama, and played a role in creating Jamaica's first terrestrial national park.
- In Palau, a Rapid Ecological Assessment of the atoll's coral reef recorded 100 fish species previously unknown to Palau. This is the only fish-specific finding in the document.

**Program and methods**
- The national stewardship team applied fire ecology expertise in a dozen new areas, from the Florida Keys to Arizona and Peru.
- In California, a Conservancy-led effort turned winter rice fields into temporary wetlands for migratory waterfowl.
- Bioreserve planning produced projects such as medical clinics in remote parts of Brazil and incentives to stop landowners in Colorado from riprapping riverbanks.

**Conclusion**
Sawhill concludes that protecting biodiversity means working with government, business, farmers, ranchers and individuals to protect all living things. He presents the Conservancy's progress in Indonesia and elsewhere as evidence that the organization is moving in the right direction.
