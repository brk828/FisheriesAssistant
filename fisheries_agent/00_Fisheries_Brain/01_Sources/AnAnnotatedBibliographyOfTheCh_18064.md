# Annotated Bibliography of the Chain Pickerel, *Esox niger* (Esocidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This Royal Ontario Museum Life Sciences Miscellaneous Publication (1973), compiled by [[E. J. Crossman]] and [[G. E. Lewis]], is an annotated bibliography of the [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox niger*), a freshwater pike of eastern and southern North America. The document contains 630 references, of which 175 (27.8%) are [[Dingell-Johnson Project Reports]]. It is a source book rather than an evaluative review, and it states that it is not complete. The authors observe that much of the literature is semi-popular or repetitive, and that serious studies giving accurate biological data usable for management are few.

## Background & Description

The chain pickerel is the fourth largest of the five species in the holarctic, circumpolar family Esocidae. It is generally distributed in fresh water, and rarely brackish water, along the Atlantic seaboard from Nova Scotia (including parts of Quebec) to central Florida, westward along the Gulf coast to Texas, and north in the Mississippi valley to Missouri. It has also been introduced outside this range.

- **Angler record (Field and Stream):** 31 inches (787 mm) long, 14 inches (356 mm) girth, 9 lb 6 oz, taken at Homerville, Georgia, 17 February 1961.
- **Unverified report:** a 19 lb chain pickerel from Quebec, recorded by le Club de Pêche, Montréal.
- **Nomenclature:** the species has a confusing synonymy. The document lists about 10 scientific names, including *Esox reticulatus* LeSueur (1818), *Esox niger* LeSueur (1818), *Esox tredecim-lineatus* Mitchill, and *Esox affinis* Holbrook. About 30 common names are recorded, including "jack," "jackfish," "grass pickerel," and "federation pike."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes the species' role in several contexts:

- **Predator and sport fish:** the species is valued as a sport fish and as a potential predator for control of unwanted fishes. In the northern part of its range it is often regarded as a destructive predator of young Atlantic salmon and brook trout.
- **Eutrophication:** in parts of its range, eutrophication has made environments favourable to this warm-water species, which now forms an increasing share of the fauna.
- **Habitat and tolerance:** the literature reports preferences for shallow areas in spring and fall and deeper water in summer, clear and weedy waters, and tolerance of a range of conditions. One experiment suggests the species does better in clear than in muddy water. Reported high-pH and crayfish-predation mortality of an experimental population also appear in the literature.
- **Hybridization:** hybrids with the [[Northern Pike]], [[Muskellunge]], [[Redfin Pickerel]], and [[Grass Pickerel]] are described in several studies, and hybridization is common among esocids of similar size.

## Key Findings

**Structure and scope of the bibliography**
- 630 references in total, of which 175 (27.8%) are Dingell-Johnson Reports.
- Of the remaining 455 references, about 75% fall under three subject headings: [[Distribution and Range]] (39.4%), [[Taxonomy, Nomenclature, and Systematics]] (20.%), and [[Management]] (15.2%).
- About 36% of titles are cross-indexed in more than one category.
- Subject categories include Accounts by Political Areas, [[Age and Growth]], [[Anatomy]], [[Behaviour]], [[Culture and Spawning]], [[Food and Food Habits]], [[Hybrids]], [[Life History and Habitat]], [[Parasites, Pathology, and Diseases]], [[Popular Accounts]], and [[Predation]].
- The sources searched include Biological Abstracts, Zoological Record, Dissertation Abstracts, Sport Fishery Abstracts, and the Dean Bibliography.

**Gaps identified by the authors**
- The authors state that the general biology is known, but much of it is superficial.
- Very little is available on physiology, biochemistry, anatomy, behaviour, ecology, and the true role of the species as a predator.

**Selected quantitative findings from annotated studies**
- Louisiana (D'Arbonne Lake, 1968): 1.30 to 16.07 lb/acre of chain pickerel sampled by rotenone.
- Spawning in Pennsylvania: temperatures of 44 to 50 °F (6.7 to 10 °C) at the surface. Cannibalism was noted after fingerlings reached two inches.
- Florida (south central, Underhill-era study cited by Buntz, 1967): of 115 adults from 6 lakes, 38.3% contained food; 49.9% of recognizable prey were spiny-rayed fish.
- Virginia (Flemer, 1959): fish under 74 mm fed on insects 48% of the time and fish 30%; larger fish ate mainly fish (63%).
- Crossman (1960): of 32 chain pickerel, 47% had asymmetrical branchiostegal rays.
- Hybrid trial (Armbruster, 1966): a 14-inch female crossed with 18-inch northern pike males gave 65.5% hatching success; hybrid growth was intermediate.
- Rhode Island (Saila, 1956): a 12-inch (305 mm) minimum size limit was judged best for maximum yield.
- New Hampshire (Jerome, 1962): handling mortality was about 10% for normal processing, 5% for shocking and transport, and 8% for hooking.
- Dryden Lake, New York (Green, 1968): exploitation rates of .429 and .207 for pickerel; the high exploitation rate was a major factor in a pickerel decline (Green, 1970).
- Illinois (Buck and Thoits, 1967): pickerel fry and fingerlings were highly vulnerable to largemouth bass predation, and the pickerel was an inefficient predator of bluegill.

**Predation and diet**
- Crossman (1962c) reported that young bullheads were the most frequent prey in one study of *E. niger*, although other species were more abundant.
- Keith and Barkley (1971) found that 54.5% of pickerel 15 inches and over had consumed stocked trout in Lake Ouachita.
- Warner and colleagues (1968) found chain pickerel to be the most common predators of stocked landlocked salmon in Maine lakes.

**Recurring themes in the literature**
- Interspecific competition with largemouth bass, yellow perch, and trout.
- Harvest and minimum size limits.
- Pond reclamation and management.
- Hybridization with other esocids.
- Parasitology, with about 34 parasite species recorded for the species (Hoffman, 1967).

**Document type and purpose**
This is a source book, not an evaluation. The authors note that the annotations vary from simple content lists to full abstracts and that many references were not directly examined. Readers are asked to send missing references to the senior author for a possible revision.
