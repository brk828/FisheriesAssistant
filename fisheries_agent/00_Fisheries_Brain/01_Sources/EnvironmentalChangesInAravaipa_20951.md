# Environmental Change in Aravaipa, 1870–1970: An Ethnoecological Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management (BLM) Cultural Resource Series monograph (No. 7, 1991), prepared by [[Diana Hadley]], [[Peter Warshall]] and [[Don Bufkin]] of Hadley Associates, reconstructs a century of human-caused and natural environmental change in the [[Aravaipa Canyon]] area of southeastern Arizona. It uses an [[Ethnoecology]] approach that combines documentary research, field observation and oral history interviews with more than forty Aravaipa residents and former residents. The stated purpose is to give the Safford District's land managers "a greater time-depth" for present management decisions. The study area covers about 450 square miles, centred on the [[Aravaipa Wilderness Area]], the first wilderness area managed by the BLM.

The report traces Aravaipa's history from prehistoric Hohokam, Mogollon and Salado occupation, through Sobaipuri and Apache use, to Euroamerican settlement beginning in the late 1870s. It covers mining, cattle and goat ranching, farming, trapping and hunting, and the shifting land ethics of residents. The foreword notes the study was the first of its kind funded by the BLM.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Aravaipa Creek is one of the few remaining perennial streams in southern Arizona. It cuts directly through the Galiuro Mountains, a rare through-mountain channel in western geology. Its flow is an isolated perennial reach bounded by dry upper and lower segments. The canyon is narrow (sometimes 100–130 feet wide) and its walls reach about 700 feet. The upper valley is roughly twelve miles wide.

**Scope and method.** The report is organized by economic sector rather than by resource, with chapters on settlements, mining, cattle ranching, goat ranching, farming, land and water, wildlife, land ethics, and whether "the land can be healed." Oral history was cross-checked against documents and physical evidence. The authors acknowledge that memory is selective, that women were strongest on family and community life, and that men were better informed on mining and ranching.

**Historical framing.** The Apache band resident in the area called itself [[Tsé Jiné Clan]] (Dark Rocks People). Spanish records from [[Coronado Expedition]] onward describe the area, and the [[Camp Grant Massacre]] of 1871 is a central event. Relocation to [[San Carlos Reservation]] followed in 1873, and Euroamerican settlement began soon after.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** Rainfall is biseasonal, with winter frontal storms, summer monsoon and occasional tropical storms. Annual precipitation at Klondyke averaged 14.1 inches (1952–1977) and both ends averaged about 17.5 inches (1978–1989). Potential evapotranspiration ranges from 25–30 inches in the high Galiuros to 40–45 inches at the west end. One estimate holds that 94.3 percent of precipitation is evaporated or transpired.

**Hydrology.** Groundwater from the upper valley alluvial aquifer is forced to the surface at the canyon entrance. Streamflow at the west exit was estimated at 30.1 cubic feet per second (21,800 acre-feet per year) over a thirteen-year record. Roughly 33 percent of average rainfall leaves the watershed as runoff, and about 2.4 percent becomes groundwater. The alluvial aquifer supplies about 8,500 acre-feet per year to the canyon stream, and about 3,100 acre-feet per year were pumped in recent years. In 1989 the BLM acquired an instream water right for the canyon.

**Water quality.** Quality is high except during floods, when sediment and heavy metals from historic mining enter the flow. Mercury has at times exceeded aquatic standards, cadmium has approached sensitive levels, and Turkey Creek has manganese above drinking standards.

**Biodiversity.** The area supports about 600 plant species, of which 445 vascular species were collected by Warren and Anderson, and roughly 237 bird species. The report lists 49 mammal species and 68 amphibian and reptile species. Aquatic invertebrates include 56 aquatic insects among 109 insects recorded. Aravaipa is the westernmost limit of [[Woodhouse's Toad]] and the northernmost breeding area for coatis. Its vegetation spans Sonoran Desertscrub, Desert Grassland, Interior Chaparral, Madrean Evergreen Woodland and Deciduous Riparian Forest, with about 24 terrestrial vegetation associations.

**Fire and vegetation change.** Lightning fires occur from April through November, peaking before the July monsoon. The authors argue that fire declined sharply after the Apache were removed and cattle arrived, which may have slowed the return of semi-desert scrub to grassland.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna (Aravaipa Creek).** The creek supports twelve fish species in the canyon and tributaries: seven native and five introduced. Native species listed include [[Sonoran Sucker]], [[Desert Mountain-Sucker]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]] (osculus race), [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] (grahami race).
- The [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] are federally listed as threatened and endemic to the Gila River drainage. The loach minnow has a relatively sustainable population only in Aravaipa Creek.
- The roundtail chub grahami race reaches its southernmost U.S. locality in Aravaipa Creek.
- Dry channel reaches and catastrophic floods have protected natives from exotic invaders.
- Exotic fishes include catfish, largemouth bass, mosquitofish and green sunfish (temporary populations, destroyed by floods). The [[Red Shiner]] invaded the creek in 1990, a major threat to the loach minnow.

**Land change drivers.** The report identifies the following as the main human impacts:
- Cattle and goat grazing, followed by overgrazing and changes in vegetation.
- Mining, with tailings, sediment, and heavy metal leaching.
- Irrigation diversions and channel changes.
- Introduction of exotic plants (37 collected, perhaps 30–40 more) and non-native animals.
- Arroyo cutting and widened creekbeds.
- Loss of beaver, [[Phragmites]] marshes and cienegas, and the spread of alder.

**Historical timeline highlights.**
- Floods, drought and freezes are recorded across the 1800s and 1900s, including the worst drought of 1933–34.
- The Camp Grant Massacre (April 1871) killed more than 100 Apache, mostly women and children.
- Settlement peaked in the early twentieth century, with almost 1,000 people, two farm villages, five mining camps and many ranches.
- By the 1970s, mining towns were ghost towns, goat ranching had ended, and many ranches were absentee-owned.

**Population and settlement.** The east-end population was about 334 in 1910 (Klondyke precinct), and the Hispanic-to-Anglo ratio there was roughly 60:40. The 1880 census listed about sixty residents for the canyon (incomplete). Population declined from the 1950s after the Athletic Mine closed in 1957.

**Mining.** Five districts were active by the early 1890s. Principal mines include the Aravaipa townsite mines, the Grand Reef (zinc and lead, the most profitable in the area), Table Mountain, Stanley and Copper Creek. Mining proceeded in three boom phases (late 1880s–1910s, World War I to early Depression, and World War II to the 1950s).

**Land ethics.** Residents held conflicting values about edible food, trapping, predator control and stocking rates. The report treats ethnoecology as a way to reconcile these perspectives with environmental history.

**Conclusions and recommendations.** The authors conclude that human transformations of Aravaipa must be judged on whether they were desirable, and that future management "cannot be divorced from the cumulative impacts and decisions of the past." The foreword notes that management implications remained to be written to guide on-the-ground work. The report ends with suggestions for future studies to support restoration and rehabilitation of the canyon's ecology.
