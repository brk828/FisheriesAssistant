# Flathead Catfish Record — San Pedro River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #freshwater-fish #invasive-species #SanPedroRiver

## Executive Summary
This document is a brief internal **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS)** telephone conversation record, dated shortly after November 20, 1995, documenting the capture of a large **[[Flathead Catfish]]** (*Pylodictis olivaris*) from the **[[San Pedro River]]** in Arizona. The call was placed by Sally Stefferud (USFWS, Phoenix) to Mark Fredlake (BLM, Sierra Vista) to verify details following a report relayed through Rob Clarkson (Bureau of Reclamation). The specimen — measuring 30 lbs and 40 inches — was caught by BLM maintenance worker J.J. Swift while angling under the Charleston Bridge. This capture is notable as it represents a **new distributional record** for flathead catfish in this reach of the San Pedro River, a system of significant conservation concern due to its native fish fauna and status as a critical desert riparian corridor.

## Background & Description
Identification was made in the field by Mark Fredlake, an experienced angler familiar with catfish species, based on the following diagnostic features consistent with *Pylodictis olivaris*:

- **Size:** Approximately 30 pounds (~13.6 kg); 40 inches (~102 cm) total length — indicating a large, mature adult individual.
- **Caudal fin:** Rounded tail (distinguishing it from forked-tail ictalurids such as blue or channel catfish).
- **Pectoral fins:** Diagnostic characters noted in the field observation.
- **Jaw structure:** Protruding lower jaw (underbite), a classic diagnostic trait of the [[Flathead Catfish]] relative to other North American catfish species.
- **Condition:** The fish reportedly appeared to be in good physical condition at time of capture.

No physical specimen, tissue, or skeletal material was retained at the time of the call; only photographic documentation was taken by Fredlake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Captured in a lotic (flowing water) reach of the [[San Pedro River]] near an anthropogenic structure (Charleston Bridge), suggesting use of deeper pool/bridge-scour habitat typical of flathead catfish, which favor submerged cover, undercut banks, and slow-moving deep pools.
- **Native range status:** *Pylodictis olivaris* is **not native** to the Arizona/Southwestern river systems; its presence in the San Pedro River represents a non-native (introduced) occurrence with potential implications for the endemic and imperiled native fish assemblage of the region (e.g., [[Gila River basin fishes]], native suckers and cyprinids).
- **Trophic role:** Flathead catfish are apex/near-apex piscivorous predators in freshwater systems, capable of exerting strong top-down predation pressure on native fish populations, amphibians, and crayfish — a concern relevant to Endangered Species Act consultations conducted by the USFWS Arizona Ecological Services Office.
- **Significance of location:** The San Pedro River is a designated **Riparian National Conservation Area** and one of the last major undammed rivers in the Southwest, supporting several federally listed and sensitive native fish species; introduction of large predatory catfish poses a potential ecological threat.

## Key Findings
- **First documented capture** of a flathead catfish in this reach of the San Pedro River, representing a **new species record** for the area — Fredlake himself was unaware of this significance at the time of reporting.
- Specimen was a large adult (30 lbs / 40 in), suggesting the species may have been established in the system for some time prior to detection, rather than a very recent introduction.
- **No biological specimen was preserved** at the time; USFWS staff requested:
  1. Photographic prints be reproduced and deposited into the **Arizona State University (ASU) museum** collection.
  2. Follow-up with angler J.J. Swift to recover any retained fish parts (e.g., skeletal remains) for voucher deposition.
- This record underscores the importance of **incidental angler-reported catches** in tracking range expansions of non-native predatory fish in sensitive desert riverine ecosystems.
- Highlights inter-agency communication pathways (USFWS–BLM–Bureau of Reclamation) used to verify and document unusual or significant fish occurrence reports in the [[San Pedro River]] watershed.
