# Food of Young-of-the-Year Striped Bass in the Sacramento-San Joaquin River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game study (Heubach, Toth and McCready, 1963) describes the year-round diet of young-of-the-year [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*, cited in the source as *Roccus saxatilis*) in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] and adjacent waters. Stomach contents of approximately 2,500 fish collected between 1956 and 1961 were analysed. The authors found that the diet was dominated by the mysid shrimp [[Neomysis Mercedis]], calanoid and cyclopoid copepods, cladocerans, the amphipod [[Corophium Spinicorne]], and tendipedid (chironomid) larvae. Diet composition tracked salinity and water flow, which the authors identified as the main controls on prey distribution. The work was framed around the anticipated effects of the [[California Water Plan]] and the introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]].

## Background & Description

The study was motivated by three concerns:

- **Predicting environmental change.** The planned California Water Plan would substantially alter flow and salinity in the Delta, shifting the distribution and abundance of food organisms. Knowledge of the diet was needed to guide project design.
- **Threadfin shad introduction.** Threadfin shad were introduced to upstream reservoirs in 1959 and 1960 as forage and had begun migrating into the Delta. [[Kimsey]] (1958) suggested competition with small bass could be severe.
- **Habitat monitoring.** Many invertebrates in the study are among the first organisms affected by pollution, so diet data could serve as indicators of habitat quality.

Earlier food studies in California (e.g., Scofield, Shapovalov, Johnson and Calhoun) covered adults. Only three prior studies addressed young-of-the-year, and none described general population diet.

The study area includes roughly 1,000 miles of waterways. Young bass are concentrated in the Delta and San Pablo Bay. The system is estuarine: tidal flow reverses as far upstream as Courtland (Sacramento River) and Stockton (San Joaquin River). Salinity ranges from near-seawater in San Pablo Bay to fresh water in the upper Delta, and turbidity is high, with Secchi readings under 1 foot in summer. The Sacramento River supplies more than 65 percent of Delta flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Fish were collected from 1956 to 1961. Summer samples came mainly from tow-net spawning surveys, and fall, winter and spring samples from seining. Fish were preserved in 10 percent formalin. Bass were grouped into size classes (0.1–1.0 inch, 1.1–3.0 inch, 2.0–4.5 inch). Stations were grouped into 10 ecological areas (I–X), with [[Diet Analysis Methods]] based on frequency of occurrence and numerical abundance per stomach.

**Digestion bias.** A feeding experiment at 61 °F showed differing digestion rates among prey. Copepods broke down within hours, while [[Corophium Spinicorne]] remained identifiable for up to 8 hours, and some individuals were still moving after 1½ hours. The authors concluded that these differences inflated the apparent importance of Corophium and, to a lesser degree, Neomysis.

**Environmental controls.** Salinity largely determined copepod genus composition: brackish forms such as *Eurytemora affinis* and *Pseudodiaptomus euryhalinus* dominated high-salinity stations, while freshwater forms (*Diaptomus*, *Cyclops*) increased upstream. Water flow was also decisive. In Area VIII (tidal, fresh) plankton was the main food, whereas in Area X (lotic, one-way flow) plankton was presumed scarce and tendipedids dominated the diet.

**Invertebrate distribution.** Corophium was historically classed as brackish-water but was collected in fresh water up to Ryde and in the San Joaquin River up to Stockton. Neomysis was most abundant above San Pablo Bay, where salinity is low, and [[Tattersal]] (1932) identified salinity as the key limiting factor.

## Key Findings

**Summer diet (June–August)**
- Neomysis was the most important food for bass from Carquinez Strait to Rio Vista on the Sacramento River and for most of the population's area.
- Above the mouth of the Mokelumne River and in the sloughs south of the San Joaquin, copepods (chiefly *Diaptomus* and *Cyclops*) dominated.
- Neomysis and Corophium mattered more to bass over 1 inch long; copepods mattered more to the smallest fish. Cladoceran consumption was unrelated to size.
- Estimated population utilisation (frequency of occurrence across the population): Neomysis 59%, copepods 50%, cladocerans 23%, Corophium 12%. The authors noted these are likely overestimates for the total population, since tow-net surveys sampled only major areas.

**Fall diet (September–November)**
- Copepod consumption rose sharply in the San Joaquin River, while Neomysis declined in most areas.
- In the Sacramento River, Corophium was a key prey in some areas; in San Pablo Bay (42 stomachs), amphipods, Nereis and Neomysis were principal prey, and over one-third of the bass contained clam siphons, probably from *Macoma*.
- Tendipedid larvae and pupae became dominant in the upper, fresh rivers above Rio Vista (Sacramento) and above Mossdale (San Joaquin).

**Winter and spring**
- Sample sizes were small (e.g., 70 bass from the San Joaquin River, December 1956–April 1957). Neomysis (73%) and copepods (58%) dominated, with Corophium at 23%. No major seasonal shift could be confirmed.

**Short-term variation**
- Bass sampled at Antioch from 1956–1957 showed large sample-to-sample swings in Neomysis, copepods and Corophium, with no clear seasonal trend. Feeding intensity did not decline in cooler months.

**Other food and fish**
- Tule fragments (*Scirpus*) and plant debris appeared in many stomachs; whether this was deliberate was unknown.
- Fish were unimportant in the diet. Only two bass contained fish remains, and one bass contained an American shad (1 inch) at Freeport.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- Diet composition tracked plankton distribution, which was controlled mainly by salinity and water flow. The authors expected that altering these factors through the California Water Plan would change bass food supply.
- Copepods and cladocerans were critical for bass under 1 inch, and the authors suggested prey availability to newly hatched larvae may strongly influence survival and abundance.
- The study found no evidence of major invertebrate competition with threadfin shad, which was outside its scope, but it identified shared planktonic prey as the point where competition could arise.
