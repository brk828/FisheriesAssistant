# Niche Breadth (Food) of *Notropis lutrensis* as a Possible Explanation of Extensive Distribution

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #feeding-ecology #NicheBreadth

## Executive Summary
This 1979 student paper (Eric M. Surat, under Dr. Clark Hubbs) investigates whether the extremely wide distribution and abundance of the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) across Oklahoma and Kansas streams, rivers, and lakes is explained by a broad dietary [[Niche Breadth]] (generalist feeding strategy) rather than strict habitat specialization. Using Pianka's (1973) niche breadth equation and occurrence-based stomach content analysis (per Hynes 1960), the study compared *N. lutrensis* diets against the next most common sympatric species at nine Oklahoma collection stations. The central hypothesis — that *N. lutrensis* would exhibit a wider feeding niche than co-occurring species — was largely supported, particularly in small, dynamic, turbid, or intermittent-flow habitats, while the species appeared to specialize in larger, more stable water bodies.

## Background & Description
- Minnow (Cyprinidae) possessing **pharyngeal teeth** used to shred food items, which complicates quantitative gut-content analysis and necessitated use of the qualitative **occurrence method** (Hynes 1960) rather than volumetric/numeric diet quantification.
- Ten individuals of *N. lutrensis* and ten of the associated comparison species were sampled per station; specimens were sexed, measured, and assigned a relative index of stomach fullness.
- Preservation protocol: 10% formalin in the field, transferred to 70% isopropyl alcohol for storage.
- Invertebrate prey items in guts were identified to order (or finer taxonomic resolution where feasible), including: Ephemeroptera, Nematoda, Chironomid larvae, other Diptera larvae, Diptera pupae, Odonata, Coleoptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera, Annelida, and terrestrial invertebrates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- *Notropis lutrensis* is described as an extremely abundant, well-distributed [[Cyprinid]] occupying highly varied lotic and lentic habitats — creeks, rivers, and reservoirs — including systems characterized by **frequent high turbidity** ([[Cross 1967]]) and **intermittent flow** ([[Metcalf 1959]]).
- Documented as the dominant fish species in the Neosho and Marais des Cygnes Rivers (Kansas) following drought conditions ([[Deacon 1961]]), suggesting strong resilience to hydrological stress.
- Prior literature reflects highly variable dietary characterizations: predominantly algal (~80%) per [[Hubbs and Strawn 1956]], primarily insectivorous per [[Lewis 1959]], and *entirely* insectivorous per [[Paloumpis 1956]] — an apparent contradiction that this study interprets as evidence of broad **omnivory** and dietary plasticity rather than inconsistency.
- Habitat selection behavior in this species has been independently documented by [[Matthews and Hill 1979]], and environmental tolerance (to physico-chemical parameters) by [[Matthews and Hill 1977]].
- Nine sampling stations spanned a gradient from small, dynamic, turbid creeks (e.g., Rubottom Creek, Turkey Creek, Caddo Creek, unnamed Mannsville creek) to large, stable water bodies (South Canadian River, Washita River, Lake Texoma islands, Mayfield Flats).
- Comparison ("sympatric") species varied by station and included [[Notropis Girardi]], [[Fundulus Zebrinus]], [[Notropis Stramineus]], [[Ictalurus Punctatus]], [[Notropis Venustus]], and [[Gambusia Affinis]].

## Key Findings
- **Niche breadth (B)**, calculated via [[Pianka's Niche Breadth Equation]] (1973), was wider for *N. lutrensis* than for the comparison species at **6 of 9 stations**.
- At the four stations representing larger, more stable water bodies (South Canadian River, Washita River, Lake Texoma islands, Mayfield Flats), *N. lutrensis* showed **narrower** niche breadth (apparent dietary specialization) in 3 of 4 cases.
- In **all** smaller, more hydrologically dynamic streams sampled, *N. lutrensis* consistently exhibited a **wider** "B" range than co-occurring species, supporting the generalist-feeding hypothesis under environmentally stressful/variable conditions.
- In turbid conditions specifically, *N. lutrensis* diet shifted toward **terrestrial invertebrates**, consistent with a switch away from vision-dependent benthic foraging when benthic prey visibility/availability is reduced.
- The authors propose that dietary generalism functions as a **survival mechanism during environmental stress** (droughts, high turbidity, intermittent flow, temperature/pH extremes), allowing the species to persist in isolated pools or degraded habitat by switching among available food resources (including algae) when preferred prey are scarce.
- The study concludes that a **combination of physico-chemical tolerance** ([[Matthews and Hill 1977]]) **and dietary plasticity/wide niche breadth** together best explain the extensive distribution and high abundance of *Notropis lutrensis* across west-central U.S. drainages.
- Findings align with [[Starrett 1950]]'s proposition that non-specialized (generalist) feeders reduce interspecific food competition by exploiting abundant, variable food resources, thereby enhancing survivability across diverse habitats.

> **Note on methodology limitations:** Due to pharyngeal tooth mastication of prey, quantitative (volumetric/numeric) diet analysis was not feasible; results rely on presence/absence (occurrence) data per Hynes (1960), which may understate the relative dietary importance of heavily-digested or fragmented food items.
