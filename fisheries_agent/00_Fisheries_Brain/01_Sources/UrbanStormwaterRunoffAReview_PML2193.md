# Urban Stormwater Runoff: Literature Review for the Salt River Project

#aquatic-ecology #urban-hydrology #water-quality

## Executive Summary

This review, prepared by Edward D. Ricci of the [[Center For Environmental Studies]] at Arizona State University for the [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) Environmental Services Department (October 1984), synthesizes the literature on [[Urban Stormwater Runoff]] quantity and quality, the legal framework governing it, and prior work in Arizona. Its central concern is that stormwater can degrade the water SRP delivers and expose municipalities to mitigation obligations. The review finds that little stormwater work has been done in the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]], that no [[Nationwide Urban Runoff Program]] (NURP) sites are located in Arizona, and that [[Retention Basins]] are a logical first target for scientific study.

## Background & Description

The report has five chapters: a general review of contaminant origins and dynamics, a review of legal mandates, a review of Arizona studies, conclusions and recommendations, and references. SRP delivers water to agricultural, commercial, industrial, and residential users in the Phoenix valley. Stormwater enters the SRP canal system through municipal drains, outfalls, and [[Retention Basins]], and some of it reaches the [[Salt River]] bed directly.

Contaminants in urban runoff come from three sources: atmospheric washout and fallout, washoff of ground-surface pollutants, and miscellaneous waste disposal. Reported contaminant groups include:

- Dissolved and suspended solids
- [[Fecal Coliform Bacteria]] and other coliforms
- [[Heavy Metals]]
- Nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus)
- [[Hydrocarbons]]
- Organochlorines, phenols, and phthalates

Land use shapes the contaminant mix. Nutrients are highest in residential basins, from lawn fertilizer and pet waste. Commercial areas have the highest overall pollutant concentrations, with trace metals and hydrocarbons particularly high. Industrial runoff can carry high metal and toxic organic loads that reflect each enterprise's wastes. Dust loading in the Phoenix area has ranged from 5 to 17 tons per square mile per month (personal communication, Arizona Department of Health Services). Eight street sweepers collect about 9,600 tons of solids each year from Phoenix streets.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Particulates and availability.** Most contaminants in runoff are bound to solids. The clay and silt fraction (<62 µm) is the most geochemically active. Although fines are only about 6% of street dust solids, they carry about 25% of total oxygen demand, up to 50% of total phosphorus and nitrogen, and more than 50% of total trace metals (Bradford, 1977). Particulate-associated fractions reported by Forstner (1977) include lead 73–84%, chromium 70–76%, mercury 56–59%, copper 55–64%, zinc 37–45%, and cadmium 30–45%. Between 80% and 95% of hydrocarbons are particulate-bound. Only a small part of total pollutant load is likely available to affect receiving waters.

**Metal availability.** Findings conflict. Angino et al. (1972) found no lead in filtered samples. Wilber and Hunter (1979) found about 20% of lead and zinc in exchangeable form. Harrison et al. (1981) found only cadmium in a significant exchangeable fraction, with zinc and lead bound mainly to carbonates and iron-manganese oxides.

**Retention basin performance.** Basins act mainly as sedimentation basins. Their effectiveness depends on equalization and on settling velocities. Trap efficiency falls as storm frequency rises or duration increases. Ferrara and Witkowski (1983) found basins generally effective for solids, COD, and total phosphorus but ineffective for nitrogen. Whipple and Hunter (1981) reported a fecal coliform reduction of about one order of magnitude. Continuous pumping during floods can release settled, persistent pollutants such as metals and organochlorines back to the canal system.

**Pollutant loading and washoff.** Accumulation between storms is nonlinear and may be most pronounced in dry climates like Phoenix's. Loading depends more on the flushing effect of precipitation than on time since the last storm. Concentrations and loads are often highest at the start of a storm because of [[First Flush]] effects. Whipple (1977) found that high-frequency storms may generate the greatest loadings to a stream.

**Cross-site comparisons.** Suspended solids in combined-land-use runoff were 30 to 70 times higher than in secondary effluent. BOD and COD were roughly equal to secondary effluent, and nutrients were generally lower. Hydrocarbon concentrations in Philadelphia bridge runoff were 6.0–24.0 mg/L, compared with 0.2–7.5 mg/L in adjacent urban areas (Hunter et al., 1979).

## Key Findings

**Legal framework**
- [[Section 208]] of PL 92-500 requires areawide water quality planning. [[Maricopa Association Of Governments]] (MAG) is the designated 208 agency for Maricopa County.
- [[NPDES]] (Sections 122 and 123) regulates point sources. Storm sewers are treated as point sources, but Arizona's Water Pollution Control Law does not yet specifically regulate stormwater runoff.
- The [[Safe Drinking Water Act]] sets enforceable maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) for inorganics and some organics. Recommended levels cover taste, odor, and appearance. Arizona standards duplicate federal inorganic limits and add a total phenolics standard and a cyanide standard.
- SRP projected that urban water's share of its total demand would rise from about 26% to about 72% by 2000 (SRP, 1982), which raises the stakes for drinking-water-quality compliance.

**NURP (EPA, 1982, preliminary results)**
- NURP covers 28 cities, with no Arizona sites. The closest are in Fresno and Castro Valley, California.
- 62 of 127 priority pollutants (129 by then) were detected in urban runoff samples.
- Zinc was detected in 100% of samples, lead in 93%, copper in 91%, and arsenic in 58%.
- Total lead exceeded EPA drinking water standards in 63% of samples.
- Recoverable lead, copper, zinc, and chromium exceeded acute aquatic life criteria in over 37% of samples and chronic criteria in over 53%.
- The most threatening organics appear to be phenols, chlorinated organics, and phthalates.

**Arizona studies**
- **[[Salt And Verde Rivers]] (USGS):** Lower Salt and Verde waters are generally of high quality. Cadmium and lead were consistently below standards, though historical exceedances occurred. The mercury standard was violated most often.
- **[[Tucson Urban Watersheds]] (Resnick et al., 1983):** Three urban and one rural watershed were studied from 1968 to 1975. About 55–65% of annual runoff occurred in summer. Fecal coliform numbers were about two orders of magnitude above Arizona drinking water standards, which may signal a problem for Phoenix. Three days of retention improved turbidity and COD but gave little further gain. Alum treatment at 50–60 mg/L reduced bacteria by 90% and COD by 80% (Dharmadhikari, 1970).
- **[[Schmidt Drain Study]] (MAG):** Six storm events were sampled at the [[15th Avenue And 43rd Avenue Outfalls]] in December 1979 and January 1980. Average annual urban storm runoff for Phoenix was estimated at 12,000 acre-feet. Estimated annual runoff was about 300 acre-feet at 15th Avenue (industrial, commercial, and residential land use) and about 150 acre-feet at 43rd Avenue (residential and heavy industrial). Sodium, sulfate, chloride, and bicarbonate were highest in the early storms, and a flushing effect was noted. Only cadmium and lead exceeded Arizona standards. Grease and oil loads were higher in the industrial lower reach of the 43rd Avenue drain.
- **[[Street Contaminants And Loadings]] (Sartor and Boyd, 1972; Pitt and Amy, 1973):** Metal concentrations and loadings were two and three orders of magnitude higher than in sanitary sewage. Coliform bacteria were the only contaminant found at higher levels in sewage. Industrial and commercial areas had the highest metal loads. Highways produced the highest loadings per curb kilometer. Concentrations of all metals sampled exceeded Arizona drinking water standards: lead by about five orders of magnitude, chromium by four, copper and zinc by three, cadmium by two, and manganese by one.
- **[[Mesa Retention Basins]] (City of Mesa, 1984):** Three basins were sampled on December 28, 1983. Fecal coliform counts ranged from 1,020 to about 1,200 MPN/100 mL. Copper, lead, and mercury were below detection limits.

**Recommendations and conclusions**
- Start scientific study with [[Retention Basins]], since they are a major share of stormwater inflow to the canals and offer single, sampleable points.
- Monitor basins and receiving canal water before, during, and after storms, in both summer monsoon and winter cyclonic seasons. Grab samples will suffice, with continuous samplers as a refinement.
- Model basin function and pump activation against storm duration and frequency. Group basins by design, since volume and configuration control settling.
- Extend the SRP Water Quality Model (a QUAL II variant by Camp Dresser and McKee, 1983) to include metals and to handle infrequent, short basin discharges.
- Quantify inflows from miscellaneous drains and compare them with basin inflows. Determine the share of stormwater reaching the Salt River bed versus the canals.
- SRP should formalize its position on water quality standards, intended use, and responsibility for quality impacts. Municipalities bear ultimate responsibility for contaminant loading to the SRP supply.
- Relevant to the Western Canal alternatives to the Gila Drain: as usage shifts from agriculture to municipal and industrial supply, water quality demands will shift too, so SRP and municipalities should adopt a management strategy covering both quality and quantity.
