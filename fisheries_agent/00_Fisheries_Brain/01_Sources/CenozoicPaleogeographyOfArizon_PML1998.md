# Cenozoic Paleogeography of Arizona

#paleogeography #geology #cenozoic

## Executive Summary

This paper, a chapter from the *Cenozoic Paleogeography of West-Central United States* volume (Rocky Mountain Section SEPM), reconstructs the Cenozoic geography of Arizona from sedimentary and volcanic stratigraphy. It uses paleogeographic maps (Figs. 2–7) to show how mountains, plateaus, sedimentary basins, and drainage systems changed through successive tectonic episodes: the [[Laramide Orogeny]], the [[Mid-Tertiary Orogeny]], and the [[Basin and Range Disturbance]]. The authors are Nations, Wilt, and Hevly. The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic-species data. Its aquatic relevance is limited to lakes, playas, and river systems as geological features, plus a short section on paleobiogeography based on plant and fossil records.

## Background & Description

The paper is organized as a stratigraphic and tectonic synthesis rather than a field study. Its main points are:

- **Data basis:** Locations of Paleogene and Neogene basins and drainages are interpreted from scattered outcrops and well data. Chronology relies mainly on [[Isotopic Dating]] of intercalated volcanics, supplemented by paleontological correlation and magnetic-reversal data.
- **Regional setting:** Arizona is divided into the [[Colorado Plateau]] (north), the [[Basin and Range Province]] (south), and a narrow [[Transition Zone]] between them. The two regions responded differently to Laramide and Tertiary tectonism, which the authors attribute to differences in crustal thickness and composition.
- **Tectonic framework:** Three major events structure the record:
  - The Laramide orogeny, lasting from about 85 Ma (Late Cretaceous) to about 43 Ma (late middle Eocene), per Coney (1972).
  - The mid-Tertiary orogeny, from late Eocene to about 13 Ma.
  - The Basin and Range disturbance, from 13 Ma to the present.
- **Map sequence:** Seven paleogeographic intervals are mapped, not standard epochs: Paleocene–middle Eocene (Fig. 3), late Eocene–middle Oligocene (Fig. 4), late Oligocene–middle Miocene (Fig. 5), late Miocene–early Pliocene (Fig. 6), and late Pliocene–Recent (Fig. 7).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's environmental content concerns landscape, climate, and drainage rather than organisms:

- **Drainage history:** Pre-Laramide regional drainage flowed east from the [[Sevier Thrust Belt]] and north from the [[Mogollon Highlands]]. Laramide uplifts reversed or fragmented these systems. The [[Colorado Plateau]] was converted into a large internally drained basin during the Eocene. Later, the [[Colorado River]] developed as an integrated system.
- **Lacustrine and playa settings:** Many basins hold fine-grained lacustrine deposits. Oligocene limestones are described as "commonly fetid," indicating high organic carbon and deposition in lakes. Some Miocene basins contain thousands of meters of evaporites, which indicate closed basins with no through-flowing drainage.
- **Base-level change:** The opening of the [[Gulf of California]] after about 17 Ma lowered base level and drove headward erosion. This extended to the lower Colorado River and the Little Colorado River system.
- **Climate signals from plants:** The authors cite palynological and macrofossil evidence for shifting climate. Eocene pollen indicates warm temperate to subtropical environments. The Oligocene record shows a shift toward a more modern aspect, with reduced evergreen angiosperm diversity and increased savanna, woodland, and chaparral elements. Analyses of North American fossil floras indicate cooler, drier, and more variable climates after the Eocene.
- **Paleobiogeography:** Neogene plant and animal fossils are "relatively common" in Arizona. Fossil vertebrates mentioned include an oreodont from central Arizona and a rhinoceros from the Oligocene. The text also cites [[Bidahochi Formation]] and [[Muddy Creek Formation]] fossil assemblages and a Pleistocene extinction discussion. Taxonomic detail is not reproduced in the paper.

## Key Findings

**Stratigraphic and tectonic sequence**

1. **Laramide phase (Late Cretaceous–Eocene):** Compression produced north-trending uplifts and monoclines, the [[Sevier Foreland Basin]], and the [[Coconino Basin]] and [[Baca-Eager Basin]]. Paleocene sediments include coarse fanglomerates adjacent to uplifts and fine clastics and evaporites in closed basins. The [[Colorado Plateau]] was partly eroded to a beveled surface. The paper dates Paleocene strata at 56.4–57.8 Ma.
2. **Mid-Tertiary phase (late Eocene–middle Miocene):** Southern Arizona subsided and formed local basins. Early Oligocene strata are mostly fine-grained and lacustrine. Middle and late Oligocene strata are coarse-grained, reflecting the onset of the orogeny. The [[Ignimbrite Flareup]] of silicic volcanism began in the middle Oligocene. Lacustrine intervals alternate with thick volcanic packages, and the [[Mogollon Rim]] formed by scarp retreat, reaching up to 600 m of relief by the late Oligocene.
3. **Basin and Range phase (13 Ma–Recent):** Faulting created numerous grabens with internal drainage. Basin fills include evaporites and lacustrine sediments. The [[Bouse Formation]] records a marine/estuarine incursion from the proto-Gulf of California in the latest Miocene to early Pliocene.

**Drainage evolution**

4. Between about 6.7 Ma and the Pliocene, the [[Kaibab Uplift]] was breached by headward erosion of the lower Colorado River at its present confluence with the [[Little Colorado River]]. This drained the [[Bidahochi Formation]] basin. The [[Lower Colorado River]] segment cut through the 5.9 Ma Fortification Hill basalt and was integrated between 5.9 and 3.8 Ma.
5. The mouth of the Little Colorado River was lowered to about 900 m (2700 ft) above sea level, well below the 1970 m (5900 ft) lowest elevation of the base of the Bidahochi Formation. This base-level change removed much of the formation from the ancestral valley.
6. The authors interpret a reversal of the Colorado Plateau's regional drainage in the late Miocene, caused by southward tilting linked to Basin and Range subsidence. The paper notes that some interpretations remain contested, including the origin of the Muddy Creek Formation (marine/estuarine versus lacustrine) and the cause of ponding for the Bidahochi Formation. The authors state that the Bidahochi ponding question "has been discussed by several authors, but still has not been explained adequately."

**Dating and data limitations**

7. Dates for Paleogene and Neogene units rely mainly on isotopic dating of volcanics. The paper explicitly notes that the areal extent of Paleogene basins is "highly interpretive" because of later deformation and burial, and that source areas are poorly known.
8. Some proposed ages are contested, such as an Artillery Formation age revised by Otton (1982) based on a 43.3 Ma date.
