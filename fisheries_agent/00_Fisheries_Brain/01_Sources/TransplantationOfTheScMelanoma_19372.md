# Transplantation of the Sc Melanoma in Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 paper by [[Theodor Ricardo Marcus]] and [[Myron Gordon]], published in *Zoologica* (New York Zoological Society, Vol. 39, Part 3), reports the first successful transplantation and growth of a melanoma in fishes. Tumor fragments from [[Sc Melanoma]] in [[Xiphophorus]] hybrids were implanted into the integumentary tissue between the scales of recipient fish. Melanomas in fish had previously been transplanted successfully only in mammals and amphibians. Host genetic constitution proved decisive: autotransplants were most successful, related hosts less so, and genetically unrelated hosts gave no success. Using the transparency of the skin, the authors traced individual pigment cells over time and concluded that some [[Melanocyte]]s mature into [[Macromelanophore]]s, and that the implant eventually resembles a premelanomatous condition called melanosis.

## Background & Description

The study concerns swordtail fish of the genus [[Xiphophorus]], in which a dominant gene, Sc (spotted caudal), causes expression of large black pigment cells called macromelanophores on the caudal fin. Melanomas arose spontaneously in first-generation and backcross hybrids of [[Montezuma Swordtail]] (*X. montezumae*) and [[Common Swordtail]] (*X. helleri*) carrying the Sc gene. Melanoma tissue from these fish was used as donor material.

Donor fragments (0.2 to 0.8 mm) were placed under a raised scale using a blunt glass needle, after brief anaesthesia in MS-222 (Tricaine methanesulfonate, 1:3,000) with Merthiolate (1:10,000) added. The method was adapted from the transparent-window technique of [[Algire (1943)]] used in mouse melanomas. Histology used Bouin's fixative with Masson's trichrome or hematoxylin and eosin staining.

Cell nomenclature followed the Third Conference on the Biology of Normal and Atypical Pigment Cell Growth: melanoblast, melanocyte, macrophage (melanophage) and melanophore.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a laboratory experimental study on captive, genetically defined swordtail stocks; no field habitat, ocean basin or location data are reported. Work was supported by a National Cancer Institute grant and used facilities of the American Museum of Natural History.

Comparative context discussed by the authors includes:
- Mouse melanomas: the Harding-Passey melanoma transplants across many strains, whereas the Cloudman S-91 melanoma succeeds only in the DBA strain.
- Axolotl melanotic tumors (Sheremetieva-Brunst & Brunst), whose invading cells follow connective-tissue paths.
- Goldfish scale transplantation (Goodrich & Nichols, 1933), in which melanophores persisted and invaded unpigmented dermis.

## Key Findings

**Transplant outcomes (57 implants total, Table 2):**

| Category | Autotransplants | Homotransplants Series "A" | Homotransplants Series "B" | Heterotransplants | Totals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Implants | 9 | 9 | 21 | 18 | 57 |
| Successful takes | 6 | 3 | 0 | 0 | 9 |
| Non-persisting cell outgrowths | 3 | 6 | 8 | 1 | 18 |
| No activity | 0 | 0 | 13 | 17 | 30 |

- **Autotransplants:** 6 of 9 developed successfully. Some were fixed at 8 months, others at 24 months.
- **Homotransplants:** In Series "A" (donor and host both dominant for Sc), 3 of 9 persisted for 8 to 24 months. In Series "B" (recessive hosts), none succeeded. Several transplants in both series grew for three to six weeks and then regressed.
- **Heterotransplants:** 17 of 18 failed to grow. One grew for two weeks and disappeared. Hosts included an albino swordtail and hybrids of other species.

**Implant development in situ:**
- Within 5 to 36 hours, free melanin granules around the implant were engulfed by macrophages.
- By about one week, most melanophages had migrated to the scale edge and been cleared.
- From day 3 to 6, dendritic pigment-cell processes emerged from the implant margin.
- After day 6, two melanocyte types appeared: a small, dendritic type (similar to the "small melanoblast" of [[Grand & Cameron (1948)]]) and a large type with broad pseudopodia, which the authors interpret as a juvenile macromelanophore.

**Cell maturation and migration:**
- A large melanocyte was followed through transformation into a typical melanophore over about six days.
- A melanophore was also observed reverting toward a melanocyte form, showing the transition is not strictly one-way.
- Melanocytes were migratory (one moved 170 µm in 120 hours), whereas melanophores were not. Most melanocytes became sedentary after three to six days.
- Trypan blue dye tracking showed that dye-laden macrophages cleared out, while melanocytes and melanophores did not take up the dye.

**Histology:**
- Long-term implants (8 to 24 months) became densely pigmented melanosis, a premelanomatous stage, lacking the high vascularity and blood-filled lacunae of the original melanoma.
- Pigment cells invaded the ventral scale surfaces, fascia, and dermis, while subcutaneous muscle was rarely and only slightly involved.
- No obvious host vascular response was seen around the implant.

**Conclusions (as stated by the authors):**
1. Host genetics is critical: autotransplants succeeded most, homotransplants only in dominant-Sc hosts, and other transplants failed or lasted only briefly.
2. Skin transplantation allows continuous observation of individual cells.
3. Established implants show melanosis, produced by hypertrophic macromelanophore growth.
4. Two immature melanocyte types arise from the implant.
5. Some large melanocytes transform into macromelanophores, supporting the hypothesis that smaller cells are precursors of larger ones proposed by [[Reed & Gordon (1931)]].

The authors note that these ontogenetic observations were corroborated almost simultaneously by [[Regeneration of Melanomas in Fishes]] studies (Ermin & Gordon, 1952, 1954) on platyfish × swordtail dorsal fins. The evidence is described as still fragmentary.
