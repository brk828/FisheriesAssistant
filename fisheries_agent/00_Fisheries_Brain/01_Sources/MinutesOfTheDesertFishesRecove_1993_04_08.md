## Executive Summary

These minutes document a 1993 meeting of the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] Gila Topminnow Subgroup convened to revise the federal recovery plan for the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), a critically imperiled poeciliid endemic to the Gila River basin of Arizona and Sonora, Mexico. Discussion centered on unresolved legal questions regarding the species' listing status in Mexico versus the U.S., the need for critical habitat designation, a fundamental reframing of the recovery plan around "extinction prevention" rather than premature downlisting criteria, and a substantial review of allozyme and mitochondrial DNA data bearing on the genetic structure and management of remaining populations. The tone of the meeting reflects consensus among team members that the species is very near extinction in the United States and that recovery planning must prioritize the protection of remaining natural populations over continued reintroduction efforts.

## Background & Description

No external morphological descriptions are provided in this source; the meeting instead addressed the species' **genetic and physiological characteristics** as revealed through laboratory and molecular work:

- **Allozyme variation**: Data cover all Gila basin populations except the [[Santa Cruz River]] and Mexican rivers. The [[Sharp Spring]] population is the only U.S. population with detected heterozygosity at surveyed loci.
- **Fixed genetic differences**: Both [[Cienega Creek]] and [[Monkey Spring]] populations show fixed differences at two allozyme loci, consistent with long-term isolation.
- **Homozygosity and fitness**: The Monkey Spring stock is homozygous across a sample of 25 allozyme loci and exhibits **lower fitness** than the heterozygous Sharp Spring stock under laboratory conditions. Monkey Spring fish are reportedly difficult to maintain in captivity, with sex ratios skewing progressively toward males over time.
- **Mitochondrial DNA**: Only a **single haplotype** occurs across the entire Gila drainage, in contrast to 2–3 haplotypes per river basin documented in Mexican populations — indicating a strong south-to-north cline in genetic diversity across the range of *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*. A newer, more sensitive mtDNA technique was being tested at the time to detect finer-scale differentiation within Gila basin populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range**: Historically distributed across the [[Gila River]] basin, including the [[Santa Cruz River]] and [[San Pedro River]] systems, spanning the U.S.–Mexico border in Arizona and Sonora.
- **Habitat type**: Desert springs, cienegas (marshy wetlands), and small perennial streams — highly localized and vulnerable aquatic refugia within an arid landscape.
- **Documented sites**: Specific localities referenced include [[Redrock Canyon]] (Coronado National Forest watershed action plan), [[Cottonwood Spring]] (livestock exclusion fencing agreement), [[Bylas Springs]] (monitoring/survey site), [[Middle Springs]] (documented extirpation per Marsh and Minckley 1990), [[Sheehy Spring]] (extirpation status undetermined), Monkey Spring, Sharp Spring, and Cienega Creek.
- **Threats**: Habitat loss at potential recovery sites, livestock grazing impacts on spring/riparian habitat, competitive displacement or hybridization risk associated with **mosquitofish** (*Gambusia* spp.), and general habitat isolation limiting gene flow between remnant populations.
- **Interspecies management concern**: A proposal was raised to legally prohibit the use and sale of mosquitofish as part of extinction-prevention measures, reflecting concern over competitive/predatory interactions with topminnow in shared habitats.

## Key Findings

- **Listing status ambiguity**: A transcription error in 1967 combined domestic/foreign species lists, erroneously recording the Gila Topminnow as listed range-wide; internal FWS review concluded the original listing was U.S.-only, but no formal Federal Register correction was ever published. This unresolved legal ambiguity led the Phoenix FWS office to limit the revised recovery plan to U.S. populations only, over the objection of [[W.L. Minckley]] and others who argued this excludes ecologically critical Mexican reaches of the Santa Cruz and San Pedro Rivers.
- **Critical habitat**: The subgroup recommended that the revised plan explicitly call for designation of critical habitat, including currently unoccupied but recoverable sites, given the limitations of Section 7 consultation for protecting unoccupied habitat.
- **Shift in plan emphasis**: Consensus emerged that natural population protection should take funding and effort priority over reintroduction programs, reversing the historical emphasis of recovery efforts.
- **Delisting deemed unrealistic**: All attendees agreed that full delisting is not a realistic goal for this species given its status.
- **New "extinction prevention criteria" framework**: Because most participants felt uneasy with the plan's implicit assumption that downlisting was likely, a new section was proposed to define minimal actions necessary to avert extinction before addressing longer-term downlisting. Proposed criteria included:
  - Natural populations must be "secure" (per the definition used in the [[Desert Pupfish]] recovery plan).
  - At least one captive replicate of each natural population must be established.
  - Ongoing monitoring of natural populations (though some members questioned including this as a formal criterion).
- **Downlisting criteria framework**: A tiered system modeled on the desert pupfish plan was proposed for reintroduced populations — Tier 1 sites (high-quality habitat, strong protection) versus Tier 2 sites (lower-quality habitat, replaceable upon extirpation). No consensus was reached on whether tier assignment should occur at stocking or after post-stocking monitoring. Additional recommended downlisting criteria included designation of critical habitat, acquisition/easements on all natural population sites, captive and wild replication of all natural populations, and comprehensive population monitoring.
- **Genetic panmixia and management implications**: [[Dean Hendrickson]] and [[Joe Quattro]] concluded that Gila basin topminnow populations were likely originally panmictic (genetically continuous), suggesting reintroduction stocking should draw on mixed stocks from multiple extant populations rather than single-source lineages. However, the group determined that **field-based experimental stock mixing is not currently practical**; laboratory mixing experiments were considered a more feasible near-term approach.
- **Isolation and adaptive potential**: The long-term geographic isolation of Monkey Spring and part of Cienega Creek by topographic barriers may have produced a stock better pre-adapted to highly isolated reintroduction sites, despite its lower measured fitness and high homozygosity.
- **Data limitations**: [[W.L. Minckley]] questioned whether allozyme data alone are sufficient for genetic management decisions; [[Tom Dowling]] and Quattro agreed current data are inadequate, though Quattro doubted additional allozyme work would substantially change conclusions already drawn.
- **Aquarium trade as conservation tool**: [[Dean Hendrickson]] proposed incorporating regulated aquarium-trade participation (via contracts, inspections, and reporting) to maintain refugium stocks of Gila Topminnow, supporting captive replicate goals under the extinction-prevention criteria.
- **Process outcome**: Given the scope of changes agreed upon, the subgroup concluded that individual written review comments on the existing draft plan would serve little purpose, as the draft would be substantially rewritten following this meeting.
