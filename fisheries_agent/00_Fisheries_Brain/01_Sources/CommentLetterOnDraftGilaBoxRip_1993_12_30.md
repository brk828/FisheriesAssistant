## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment letter from American Rivers to the U.S. Bureau of Land Management (BLM), critiquing the Draft Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area (RNCA) Interdisciplinary Activity Plan/Environmental Assessment (IAP/EA). While primarily a policy critique, the letter contains substantive scientific commentary on native fish conservation within the [[Gila River]] and [[Bonita Creek]] systems. Key fish-related concerns include: inadequate fish management prescriptions in the plan's alternatives, insufficient monitoring frequency for fish population trends, the threat posed by non-native predatory catfish to native species (particularly the candidate-listed [[Gila Chub]]), non-self-sustaining [[Razorback Sucker]] populations, and the potential ecological benefits of beaver activity for native fish habitat. The letter frames fish conservation as central to the legislative mandate establishing the RNCA.

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory Context**: The Gila Box RNCA was established under the Arizona Wilderness Act of 1990 (16 U.S.C. 460ddd), modeled after the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]], with a Congressional mandate to "conserve, protect, and enhance" riparian and associated aquatic resources.
- **Habitat Significance**: The 26-mile reach of the [[Gila River]] within the RNCA is described in the IAP/EA as "one of the few remaining segments of low desert river left in a largely natural state," representing a rare and critical refuge for river-adapted native fish species in the Southwest.
- **Bonita Creek**: Recognized by the American Fisheries Society in 1988 for BLM's management, [[Bonita Creek]] reportedly supports the **highest density of fish biomass of any stream in the Southwest**, underscoring its exceptional conservation value.
- **Species of Concern**:
  - **[[Gila Chub]]** — a federal candidate species for listing under the Endangered Species Act, threatened by predatory non-native catfish in the Gila River and lower Bonita Creek.
  - **[[Razorback Sucker]]** — present in the system but with populations that are not self-sustaining; current monitoring plans focus only on adults, without addressing recruitment failure.
  - **Predatory Catfish** (non-native, species unspecified in text) — identified as a direct threat to native fish populations in the Gila River and lower Bonita Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat–Fish Linkage**: The letter emphasizes that riparian vegetation health is directly tied to fish habitat quality — restoring riparian vegetation abundance and diversity is expected to restore aquatic features necessary for supporting the full native fish life cycle.
- **Threats to Fish and Habitat**:
  - **Off-road vehicle (sandrail) use** in the streambed: the IAP/EA itself acknowledges that sandrail travel can injure or displace fish, crush eggs in spawning areas, and impair the productivity of shallow riffle habitats that supply food for fish. Adverse impacts are considered most severe during drought years when water quantity/quality reach subcritical survival thresholds.
  - **Livestock grazing** in riparian corridors, which degrades riparian vegetation and consequently aquatic habitat structure.
  - **Riparian roads**, which fragment and degrade streamside habitat.
  - **Non-native predatory fish** (catfish), directly preying on or competing with native species.
- **Beneficial Ecological Interactions**: The existing **beaver population** in Bonita Creek is highlighted as a potentially valuable ally in native fish conservation via:
  1. Predation on exotic fish species.
  2. General enhancement of riparian ecosystem health (e.g., pool creation, vegetation dynamics), indirectly benefiting native fish.
- **Monitoring Gaps**: 
  - Fishery trend monitoring is only planned **twice over the 15-year life of the plan**, deemed statistically insufficient for meaningful trend analysis.
  - Razorback sucker monitoring is limited to adult individuals, neglecting investigation into recruitment failure or causes of non-self-sustaining population dynamics.

## Key Findings

1. **Insufficient Fish Management Detail**: Despite BLM's demonstrated in-depth knowledge of fish ecology in the RNCA, the plan's alternatives lack specific, actionable fish management prescriptions. Proposed measures — a fish barrier at the mouth of Bonita Creek, predator fish control on the Gila River, and a low-flow evaluation on Bonita Creek — are mentioned in Alternative 1 but never explained or evaluated elsewhere in the document.

2. **Unanswered Management Questions**: The letter identifies critical unresolved questions necessary for sound fish management:
   - What type of fish barrier would be appropriate at Bonita Creek's mouth?
   - What would a "low flow evaluation" entail methodologically?
   - How effective would predator (catfish) control measures be for native fish recovery?
   - Could increased recreational sport fishing serve as an effective predator-control tool?

3. **Endangered Species Risk**: The presence of predatory catfish in the Gila River and lower Bonita Creek is directly linked to increased extinction risk for the [[Gila Chub]], a species already a federal listing candidate. The letter argues BLM has a proactive responsibility to manage habitat and predator pressure to avoid triggering formal ESA listing.

4. **Sandrail Vehicle Impact on Fish (Alternative 4)**: A proposal to permit sandrail vehicle use across 14 miles of the Gila River streambed is flagged as a serious oversight — despite the IAP/EA's own text acknowledging fish injury, egg crushing, and riffle habitat degradation from such use, no alternative (including Alternative 4 itself) includes management prescriptions to mitigate this impact.

5. **Grazing–Fish Habitat Connection**: Riparian livestock grazing is identified as a major limiting factor for fish habitat recovery; the letter recommends full exclusion of grazing from riparian corridors (allowing continued grazing only in upland areas) as the most effective habitat-improvement strategy for native fish.

6. **Monitoring Recommendations**:
   - Increase frequency of fishery trend monitoring beyond twice per 15-year plan cycle.
   - Expand razorback sucker monitoring to include juvenile/recruitment-stage individuals and investigate underlying causes of non-self-sustaining population status.
   - Institute formal monitoring protocols (purpose, method, frequency, and management response triggers) for all riparian-impacting uses, not just wildlife/fish.

7. **Beaver as a Conservation Tool**: Although the IAP/EA does not address beaver management, the letter recommends continued support for the existing beaver population in [[Bonita Creek]] as a low-cost, nature-based mechanism for both exotic species suppression and riparian habitat enhancement.

8. **Comparative Model — San Pedro RNCA**: The [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] is cited as the intended management blueprint, having prioritized riparian/aquatic system health as the primary management objective and sought an initial return to natural baseline conditions — a standard the Gila Box IAP/EA reportedly fails to meet, instead defaulting to a multiple-use management framework.
