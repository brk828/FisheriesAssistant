# Memorandum on Long-Term Monitoring Program for Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #riverine-systems #conservation-monitoring

## Executive Summary

This 1993 internal memorandum from Richard Valdez (BIO/WEST, Inc.) to Duncan T. Patten (Senior Scientist, Glen Canyon Environmental Studies) provides a critical peer review of a draft long-term monitoring plan for the Grand Canyon reach of the Colorado River, prepared in response to Glen Canyon Dam operations. Rather than presenting new empirical fish data, the document functions as a **programmatic and methodological critique**, arguing that the draft monitoring plan under-develops its core scientific sections (particularly for native and non-native fish) relative to administrative and background content. Valdez advocates restructuring the plan to center on attribute-specific sections (e.g., native fishes) developed by subject specialists — including BIO/WEST, Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), Arizona State University (ASU), and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) — under the guidance of [[W.L. Minckley]]. The memo's most scientifically substantive content addresses sampling design for [[Humpback Chub]] and other native [[Colorado River Fish Fauna|Colorado River fishes]], cohort-strength monitoring, statistical limitations of CPUE-based trend detection, and the ecological distinction between true backwaters and geomorphologically isolated pools.

## Background & Description

The memo does not provide direct morphological descriptions of individual species. However, it implicitly distinguishes fish life-history categories relevant to sampling design:

- **Long-lived species** (e.g., adult native and non-native large-bodied fishes such as [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]]) — historically proposed for 4-year sampling intervals.
- **Short-lived species and young-of-year (YOY)** (e.g., [[Speckled Dace]], small cyprinids) — proposed for annual sampling.
- Size-class distinction is highlighted regarding [[Humpback Chub]]: fish captured in the **mainstem** Colorado River tend to be the "largest fish," not necessarily representative of all fish moving between the mainstem and the **Little Colorado River (LCR)** — cautioning against conflating body size with resident status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Uses both the mainstem Colorado River and the Little Colorado River (LCR); movement patterns between these habitats are size- and age-structured. Relationship between dam-induced flow ramping and chub activity/movement is explicitly noted as **not yet established** (a proposed causal statement regarding ramping and turbidity-linked movement was flagged for deletion due to insufficient evidence).
- **Backwaters / Eddy Return Channels**: Serve as critical **nursery habitat** for young native fish (chub, suckers, dace). The memo stresses a geomorphological distinction: true "backwaters" (a fisheries-biologist term) are more precisely **eddy return channels**; when disconnected from the main channel they become **isolated pools**, which have **reduced nursery value** and may disproportionately favor non-native fish establishment over native species like humpback chub.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]]**: Require cohort-strength and recruitment-success monitoring analogous to humpback chub; inhabit similar backwater/shoreline nursery habitats.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Noted as **extremely rare** in Grand Canyon, making it impractical to target specifically within a monitoring program.
- **Non-native invasive risk**: Small cyprinids — [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Sand Shiner]] — pose an invasion threat and occupy the same backwater/shoreline niches as native juveniles, necessitating concurrent annual monitoring. Larger non-natives ([[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]]) require dual monitoring: YOY annually (via the native fish sampling regime) and adults every 3–4 years.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (above Lee's Ferry): Population structure (size, condition, age) is poorly captured by creel census and angling-guide survey data alone; these methods are considered useful only for catch information, not full population characterization.
- **Comparative case study — [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]**, Upper Colorado River Basin: Cited as a cautionary example where five years of structured YOY monitoring failed to detect statistically significant CPUE differences between years despite apparent 100%+ differences in mean catch, due to random/clumped distributions, high frequency of zero-catches (non-normality), sampler variability, and fluctuating environmental conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Cohort-strength monitoring is essential**: Native species (humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, speckled dace) require tracking through their first ~3 years of life (to age of recruitment), using seines, electrofishing, and small-mesh experimental gill nets targeted at small fish to minimize adult handling.
- **Annual non-native monitoring is critical**, independent of the long-lived/short-lived species dichotomy, due to invasion risk from small cyprinids and the need to track YOY of larger invasive species.
- **Sampling stratification by life-history duration (long- vs. short-lived) is scientifically inappropriate**; the memo argues monitoring frequency should instead be driven by explicit objectives: (1) assessing status/trends of native species, and (2) documenting invasion dynamics and relative densities of non-native species.
- **Statistical power limitations**: Real-world experience monitoring [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] YOY demonstrates that traditional CPUE trend analysis often lacks power to detect meaningful population changes, even under rigorous structured sampling — underscoring the value of complementary modeling approaches (including [[Population Viability Analysis]]) and professional interpretation of ambiguous monitoring data.
- **Geomorphological habitat classification matters ecologically**: Distinguishing "eddy return channels" (functional backwaters) from "isolated pools" (disconnected, degraded nursery habitat) has direct management implications, since isolated pools may favor exotic over native fish recruitment.
- **Creel/angler survey limitations**: Such data cannot substitute for direct population structure assessment (age, size, condition) of [[Rainbow Trout]] populations, though they retain value for catch/harvest metrics.
- **Programmatic recommendation**: Fish (and other attribute) monitoring sections should be authored by specialist teams (BIO/WEST, AGFD, ASU, USFWS) under senior scientific guidance (W.L. Minckley), each with clearly defined objectives, methods, statistical analysis plans, and integration strategies, ultimately unified in a "Program Integration" section.
