## Executive Summary
This 1987 letter from W. Hartshorne to Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) discusses the precarious conservation status of the [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], two native cyprinid fishes of the [[Gila River]] system. The correspondence addresses concerns over the invasive [[Red Shiner]] displacing native species in the Cliff-Gila Valley of New Mexico, and references David Propst's 1986 findings that upstream canyon reaches (Turkey Creek to the Forks Area) offer only limited refuge habitat due to flooding and predation by non-native [[Smallmouth Bass]]. The author concludes that habitat availability is no longer the primary limiting factor for these species' survival — biotic interactions (invasive species pressure) have superseded abiotic habitat constraints as the critical threat.

## Background & Description
The source text does not provide direct morphological descriptions of the [[Spikedace]] or [[Loach Minnow]]. However, contextual and inferred characteristics include:
- **Spikedace (Meda fulgida)**: A small-bodied native cyprinid adapted to warm, low-elevation riverine habitat within the Gila River drainage.
- **Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis)**: A small benthic cyprinid, co-occurring with spikedace in similar habitat niches, typically associated with swift-flowing riffle habitats.
- **Red Shiner (Cyprinella lutrensis)**: Non-native cyprinid competitor; per Minckley's 1973 published report, exhibits an upper elevational range limit near 5,000 feet, consistent with warm-water thermal tolerances.

*Note: This letter is a policy/status correspondence rather than a primary morphological study; detailed anatomical data would require reference to Minckley (1973) or Propst (1986) directly.*

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Elevational Range**: The warm-water reach suitable for [[Red Shiner]] invasion terminates near [[Mogollon Creek]], at approximately 5,000 feet elevation — considered the upper thermal/elevational limit for this invasive species per Minckley (1973).
- **Refuge Habitat**: The canyon reach between [[Turkey Creek]] (~4,800 ft) and the [[Forks Area]] of the Gila River (~6,000+ ft) represents a potential high-elevation refugium, though characterized as containing only "limited habitat" for native species.
- **Threats to Refugia**: 
  - Flooding disturbance regimes in canyon reaches.
  - Predation pressure from introduced [[Smallmouth Bass]] (reported by M. H. Salmon, Streams Commission).
  - As a result, native spikedace and loach minnow were reportedly **not found** in this canyon refuge reach.
- **Critical Population Node**: The [[Cliff-Gila Valley]] population of spikedace is identified as a linchpin population — its loss is projected to jeopardize the overall species' survival, per Propst's assessment.
- **Interspecific Competition**: The central ecological conflict is competitive/predatory displacement of native cyprinids by non-native [[Red Shiner]] (competition) and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (predation), rather than abiotic habitat degradation.

## Key Findings
- The [[Red Shiner]] invasion front is approaching the Cliff-Gila Valley but is elevationally constrained near Mogollon Creek (~5,000 ft), limiting further upstream expansion based on known thermal tolerance.
- Construction of a dam ("Conner Dam") in this stretch was assessed as unlikely to facilitate further upriver range expansion of red shiner, based on existing elevational limits.
- The canyon reach between Turkey Creek and the Forks Area, despite higher elevation, does **not** currently support native spikedace/loach minnow populations due to flood disturbance and smallmouth bass predation — undermining its potential as a refuge.
- The Forks Area and upper Arizona reaches of the Gila River may represent the **last viable survival habitat** for these two native species.
- Loss of the Cliff-Gila Valley spikedace population is predicted (per D. Propst) to critically endanger overall species survival, suggesting this population is not merely a local stronghold but a key demographic source.
- The author explicitly argues that **habitat quantity/quality is no longer the binding constraint** on native fish survival — the primary threat has shifted to biotic factors (invasive competitors and predators).
- Ongoing 1987 field sampling was anticipated to confirm whether population declines of spikedace and loach minnow were actively occurring in real time.
