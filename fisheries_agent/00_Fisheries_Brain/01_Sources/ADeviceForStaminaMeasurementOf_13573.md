# A Device for Stamina Measurement of Fingerling Salmonids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 research report from the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] describes a [[Stamina Tunnel]], a closed-circuit water tunnel built to measure the physical stamina of hatchery-reared salmonid fingerlings. The authors, based at the [[Salmon-Cultural Laboratory]] in [[Longview Washington]], argue that stamina is a useful proxy for a fingerling's ability to survive after release. The tunnel subjects groups of fish to controlled, stepped water velocities and records how quickly they leave. Two evaluation methods are presented: a [[Performance Index]] that describes the swimming ability of the middle 50 percent of a sample, and a [[Performance Rating]] based on the energy the fish expend. The authors report that the tunnel, tested over four years on thousands of fish, can detect differences in stamina caused by disease, diet, and rearing environment.

## Background & Description

The authors frame the problem as evaluating the quality of fish produced by [[Hatchery Propagation]]. They set four requirements for a useful device: it must test representative samples rapidly, reproduce a measured stress exactly, allow stress to be increased as fish improve, and be sensitive enough to detect differences created by artificial propagation.

Earlier swimming-test designs were reviewed and found wanting for large-scale hatchery work:

- **Annular "fish wheel" troughs** (e.g., Bainbridge and Brown, 1958; Paulik and DeLacy, 1957) test one fish at a time, may slip relative to the water, and produce laminar rather than turbulent flow.
- **Open straight troughs and flumes** (e.g., Reimers, 1956; Vincent, 1960; Kerr, 1958) show large velocity differences across the cross-section, so fish are not subjected to the same swimming demands.
- **Timed fright-exercise methods** (e.g., Miller et al., 1959; Hochachka, 1961) give nonuniform treatment.
- **Closed-circuit tunnels** (e.g., Mar, 1959; Katz et al., 1959; Davis et al., 1963) offer controlled velocity but test only a few fish at once.

The [[Stamina Tunnel]] was designed to combine controlled velocity with the capacity to test large samples.

**Apparatus.** Water is recirculated by a 1,500 gallon-per-minute [[Axial Flow Pump]] driven by a 5 hp, 1750 RPM motor, with a four-speed transmission and variable-speed pulley. The tunnel itself is a 12-inch outside-diameter [[Plexiglass]] tube, 6 feet long, fed through perforated baffles and a well-rounded bell mouth. Velocity is set by the differential in water level between head and tail boxes and by pump speed. An [[Electrical Field Fish Barrier]] at the outlet, with three aluminum bands and a variable transformer, holds fish in the tunnel until they tire. Water heating from the pump was computed at under 0.5°F per hour at 2 feet per second, and is offset by adding water at 53°F between tests.

**Procedure.** Fish are randomly distributed into groups of 100, acclimated to 53°F water for 16 to 20 hours without feeding, and then loaded through a removable chute. After orientation, velocity is raised in increments. Fish ranging from about 1 to 4.5 grams are normally tested in second gear (0.45 to 1.70 feet per second), and larger fingerlings in third gear (0.75 to 2.70 feet per second). The test uses two identical velocity plateaus for both gears (1.2 and 1.7 feet per second), plus two additional plateaus (2.2 and 2.7 feet per second) for third gear. Fish that leave the tunnel are counted at one-minute intervals, and the test ends when at least 75 percent have left. The tunnel is designed for fingerlings from 1 to 50 grams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report treats stamina as shaped by the fish's physical state and rearing conditions, and it identifies several controllable variables that must be standardized.

**Size.** Swimming ability rises with body size. In a season of biweekly tests on fall chinook fingerlings, 500 fish were tested at each interval, for a total of 9,000 fish used to build a [[Normal Performance Curve]]. Kerr (1958) and Bainbridge (1960) report similar size effects in other species.

**Temperature.** Temperature affects both physiology and physics. Brett et al. (1958) found that coho cruising speed rose with temperature up to the lethal limit, while sockeye cruising speed fell near lethal temperatures. Brett (1959) reported that sockeye speed increases until about 60°F, and that energy reserves are exhausted 1.5 to 2 times faster at 66°F than at 50°F. Stamina tests therefore use a constant 58°F as a common base. Within the tested range (47.5 to 60°F for summer chinook; 38 to 54.5°F for fall chinook), performance improved with warmer water. Water viscosity also rises as temperature falls, which increases resistance.

**Oxygen.** Reduced oxygen lowers maximum sustained swimming speed (Davis et al., 1963; Katz et al., 1959). Tests were run at 90 percent of oxygen saturation, with water replenished between tests to avoid oxygen deficiency.

**Sampling and feeding.** Random sampling is needed because biological variation among fish from the same diet or environment is considerable. Dip-net and seine samples introduce bias. Fish tested within a few hours of feeding perform poorly. Starvation tests found no loss of swimming ability for:

- fall chinook averaging 1 gram, until after 5 days without food
- fall chinook averaging about 3 grams, for at least 4 days
- summer chinook averaging about 15 grams, for at least 40 hours

**Retesting.** Immediate retesting of summer chinook reduced performance substantially. A 24-hour rest produced a significant drop below the initial level. For fall chinook, performance had returned to the previous level after 3 days of rest.

**Species and races.** Fall chinook generally outperformed spring and summer chinook of the same size, possibly reflecting an inherent difference between races (Collins, 1958). Steelhead fingerlings outperformed comparable fall chinook, and coho were slightly weaker than fall chinook. Paulik and DeLacy (1957) found steelhead could outperform coho at high velocities, and sockeye performance declined with distance from the ocean (Paulik and DeLacy, 1958). The authors interpret these as heritable species differences.

**Wild versus hatchery fish.** Wild fall chinook from an eddy area of the Cowlitz River gave much lower results than hatchery fish, though the authors judged these specimens probably atypical. Wild coho yearlings performed poorly owing to malnutrition, improving by 50 percent after 8 weeks of hatchery feeding and doubling after 6 weeks, according to the report.

## Key Findings

**Tunnel performance and design**

- The tunnel produces water velocities from 0.4 to 5.0 feet per second, adjustable in fine increments by pump speed.
- Four years of testing involved thousands of fish samples from different sources. The design needed no alterations.
- Velocity profiles at the outlet were uniform. The fish-holding section sits near the outlet, where the flow is most stable.

**Evaluation methods**

- **[[Performance Index]]**: the summed times when 25 percent and 75 percent of fish have left the tunnel. It describes the middle 50 percent of the sample and excludes the strongest and weakest fish. Differences of about 3 index points between fish of the same size were usually significant.
- **[[Performance Rating]]**: based on the work done against fluid drag, $D = C_D A \rho V^2$, expressed for fish as a function of length, velocity, time, and viscosity. It compensates for fish weight and water temperature. A constant of 174 places chinook fingerlings reared in raceways at a rating of 100. Ratings should not be applied to fish longer than 50 cm.
- The authors conclude that both methods are needed: the index for swimming ability, and the rating for comparison with expected performance at a given size.

**Quantitative examples from the derivation**

- At the same speed, water at 35°F gives 41 percent greater resistance than water at 82°F.
- A 20-centimeter fish has 2.83 times the drag of a 10-centimeter fish at the same speed. If its power scales with weight, its work is eight times greater.

**Factors detected by the tunnel**

- Gill infections (proliferation of the epithelium or fusion of lamellae) produce the largest reductions in performance.
- Fingerlings with hematocrit below 30 percent perform poorly.
- Excessively fat fish have lower stamina.
- Rearing ponds with adequate flow produce fish with superior stamina, while overcrowded ponds reduce it.

**Survival relevance**

- The authors believe tunnel stamina may predict survival potential at release, though experiments on adult survival were still in progress.
- Survival studies at [[Spring Creek Hatchery]] (Junge and Phinney, 1963) showed much higher survival for fingerling releases than for fry. At the [[Coleman Hatchery]], Cope and Slater (1957) found higher survival for larger fall-released chinook.

**Standardization recommendations**

- Test at a constant 58°F, using fish acclimated for 16 to 20 hours without food.
- Use groups of 100 fish, with 2 to 5 tests per population for statistical analysis.
- Establish a standard performance curve for each rearing season, because size strongly affects results.
