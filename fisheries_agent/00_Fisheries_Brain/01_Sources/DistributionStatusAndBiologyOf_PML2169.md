# Distribution, Status, and Biology of the Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Department of Game and Fish final report (Propst, Bestgen, and Painter, 1985; published 1986) documents the current range, status, microhabitat use, life history, and decline of the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a small cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila Basin]]. Sampling from December 1982 through September 1985 across the Gila-San Francisco warmwater reaches found the species confined to a few fragmented areas in New Mexico, with the largest and most viable population in the [[Cliff-Gila Valley]]. The species has been extirpated from the [[San Francisco River]] in New Mexico. The report attributes the decline to habitat modification from land and water use, and to predation and competition by non-native fishes, especially the [[Red Shiner]]. It closes with ten recommendations, including federal listing, critical habitat designation, and reintroduction planning.

## Background & Description

The spikedace belongs to the tribe Plagopterini, a group of minnows endemic to the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was first collected in 1851 by J.H. Clark on the San Pedro River in Arizona. In New Mexico it was first documented in 1872 by E.D. Cope. Historically it occurred in the upper Gila drainage of Arizona and New Mexico, and possibly in Sonora via the San Pedro River. The species prefers low-to-moderate gradient, intermediate-sized streams where water velocities are slow to moderate and the substrate is sand, gravel, and cobble. It forms aggregations and feeds on drifting macroinvertebrates. Its natural predators were the [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]].

The study area comprised the warmwater reaches of the Gila and San Francisco drainages in Catron, Grant, and Hidalgo counties. The Gila River is perennial and typically low in turbidity, while the San Francisco River flows through canyons for most of its New Mexico course and is often dry in late summer where irrigation diverts it.

The project was funded through the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish's federal aid projects, including Section 6 Endangered Species Act funds, and it had five objectives: survey distribution and abundance, collect life-history and ecological data, record associated species and habitat parameters, assess the feasibility of habitat control, and assess the feasibility of transplantation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat degradation.** The report identifies the following human-caused changes as the main drivers of decline:
- Irrigation diversion, which dried some reaches and reduced flows in others.
- Livestock grazing, which damaged riparian vegetation and banks and increased sedimentation.
- Overgrazing and watershed denudation, which caused erosion and arroyo cutting.
- Ground-water pumping, which lowered water tables and dewatered streams.
- Channelization, dams, and water-quality decline from mine tailings, industrial discharges, and municipal and agricultural effluent.

**Non-native fishes.** Non-native fishes established by the 1930s and later include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Western Mosquitofish]]. Predation by catfish and bass is considered the most apparent threat. Competition is considered more insidious. The report notes that impacts of non-natives and habitat loss are often inseparable.

**Floods and drought.** The October 1983 flood (peak of 2,376 m³/s near Gila and 356.8 m³/s near Redrock) caused heavy displacement. Non-native fishes generally increased in relative abundance after floods, and native fishes responded by moving to stream margins. Drought was not directly observed during the study. The authors expect that drought would cause high mortality and scattered populations.

**Red Shiner interaction.** Red shiners were first recorded in the Gila system of New Mexico in 1978 and became dominant in the Redrock and Virden valleys. Field observations showed red shiners disrupting feeding aggregations of juvenile suckers and spikedace. The authors could not yet separate displacement from occupancy of vacant niches.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- The species has been eliminated from the San Francisco River drainage in Arizona and New Mexico.
- In New Mexico it survives in the lower West and Middle forks, the [[Forks Area]] (East Fork headwaters), the Cliff-Gila Valley, and a very small population at the Conner site. Its survival in the upper East Fork and at the Middle Box mouth is "not assured."
- The Cliff-Gila Valley population is the largest in New Mexico, and possibly the largest surviving population in its range.
- Viable populations are not present in the Redrock and Virden valleys. Age-0 fish found there in 1983 probably came from displacement by high spring runoff.

**Microhabitat**
- Larvae are most common in water below 15.2 cm/s, shallower than 27.4 cm, over sand-dominated substrate.
- Juveniles use faster and deeper water over sand and gravel.
- Adults occur over a wide velocity range (0.0–79.2 cm/s), mostly in shallow water.
- Seasonal shifts were detected in the Forks Area (depth) and the Cliff-Gila Valley (velocity).

**Life history**
- Two age classes (I and II) made up the reproductive population. Age-I fish produced most of the eggs.
- Spawning occurred in shallow sand and gravel riffles, from early April to May in 1984. In 1983 it extended to early June, following high runoff.
- Fecundity estimates: Age-I females may produce up to about 200 ova and Age-II females up to about 300.
- Maximum longevity is about 24 months, but few survive beyond 13 months. Maximum size was about 58 mm SL.
- Food was mostly aquatic insects, principally mayflies (occurring in 71% of stomachs, N=54), followed by true flies and caddisflies.

**Community dynamics**
- In the Cliff-Gila Valley, spikedace became the most common species in 1961–1980 (29.3%) and remained among the dominant species in 1983–1984 (21.1%).
- In the Forks Area, the lower East Fork fish community was largely eliminated by 1983–1984. Only five samples yielded 178 specimens, 65% native.
- The Riverside, Cliff-Gila Valley, and West Fork sites maintained relatively stable native communities.

**Recommendations**
1. List the spikedace as a federally threatened species (U.S. Dept. Interior 1985).
2. Designate critical habitat in New Mexico's Cliff-Gila Valley and the lower West and Middle forks.
3. Maintain the Cliff-Gila Valley population as the species' stronghold.
4. Develop cooperative agreements with landowners and agencies.
5. Develop a recovery plan if listed as endangered under the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act.
6. Continue monitoring.
7. Continue ecological research, including interactions with introduced fishes.
8. Identify intensive management areas: Cliff-Gila Valley, East Fork, and Redrock Valley.
9. Re-establish the species in historic areas, with hatchery stock developed at the Dexter National Fish Hatchery.
10. Establish guidelines for instream modifications that could damage spikedace habitat.
