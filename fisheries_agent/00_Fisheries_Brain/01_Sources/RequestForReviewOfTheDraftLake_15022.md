# Lake Mohave Backwater-Rearing Facility Management/Research Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft plan, circulated by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Regional Environmental Officer to the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) in 1990, proposes on-site rearing of [[Razorback Sucker]] to a size (25-30 cm) at which they can resist predation by non-native fishes. The aim is to determine whether backwater production and grow-out can augment the aging adult population in [[Lake Mohave]] and is a fiscally responsible alternative to remote hatchery rearing. The plan sets out a multiphase research and management program, built around two isolated backwaters, [[Yuma Cove]] and [[Davis Cove]], with tagging by [[PIT Tagging]] and long-term monitoring. Comments were requested by October 15, 1990, for discussion at a NFWG meeting on November 19, 1990.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid endemic to the Colorado River system. It was once common from Wyoming to Mexico but now persists in remnant populations, including the Green River, upper Colorado River and Lake Mohave. The species was proposed for federal listing as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

Key background points:

- **Stocking history:** The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) operated a 10-year [[Memorandum of Understanding]] for reintroduction. About 9.5 million larvae and juveniles were released from 1980 to 1990, with little sign of reestablishment.
- **Predation:** Stocked suckers were rapidly preyed upon by catfish and other non-native species, according to [[Marsh and Brooks]] predation studies, which recommended stocking larger fish.
- **Lake Mohave population:** The reservoir holds the largest remaining population, estimated at 60,000 individuals, aged 25 to 45 years. Larvae are successfully spawned and hatched but disappear at 10-12 mm, and non-native predation is considered the main cause of recruitment failure.
- **Bonytail chub:** The plan notes the adult populations of both [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] in Lake Mohave are aging.

The 1984-1986 on-site trials at Yuma Cove, described as moderately successful, are summarized under Key Findings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave's reservoir elevations drive backwater conditions. Yuma Cove is partly blocked by a natural sand and gravel berm and is shallow and gravelly, with a minimum of about 5 feet of water until September or October, when depths fall to 2-4 feet. Davis Cove is larger and deeper, keeping a minimum depth of about 10 feet and roughly 1.5 surface-acres at minimum reservoir elevation. Yuma Cove could dry up.

Predatory non-native fishes are the central threat. The 1985 study recorded [[Green Sunfish]] predation on larvae, with stomach analyses confirming larval consumption. Storm-driven wave action washed out the berm in April 1985, allowing reinvasion.

Other context includes:

- Ongoing Grand Canyon native fish studies, which might support similar facilities in Grand Canyon or [[Lake Mead]].
- Enhancement of lower Colorado River backwaters by dredging and revegetation, with rearing facilities possibly built alongside.
- Other agencies that should be invited: Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Indian Affairs, California Department of Fish and Game, and private organizations.

## Key Findings

**1. Prior on-site trials showed reproduction and recruitment in predator-free habitat.**
- 1984-1986 trials at Yuma Cove followed piscicide removal of non-native fishes in autumn 1984 and stocking with adults obtained nearby.
- The first stocking spawned in March 1985; larvae reached over 16 mm before disappearing within a month of reinvasion. This was more than a third larger than maximum lake-larval sizes.
- The second stocking (January 1986) produced larvae of 16-22 mm by late March 1986, with growth comparable to hatchery conditions: 10 cm by June 1986 and 18.2-20.4 cm by December 1986.
- Breaching the berm in April 1987 allowed exchange with the lake. The backwater then held abundant exotic fishes but only one razorback sucker.
- Growth to 25-30 cm, which is largely immune to predation, is expected in 12 to 18 months.

**2. Phased program.**
- **Phase one:** A 5-year study with four annual stockings of about 10,000-50,000 subadult razorback suckers. Fewer than 10 percent are expected to reach adulthood.
- **Phase two:** Expansion to other basin areas, possibly including [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]] or [[Humpback Chub]], possibly concurrent with phase one.
- **Phase three:** A management agreement among agencies to maintain the adult population at 56,000-64,000 in Lake Mohave, formalized by an MOU.

**3. Two rearing approaches.**
- **Preferred:** Spawn only at Yuma Cove (January-April). In September, collect young fish, tag them and move them to Davis Cove and/or cage facilities. Yuma Cove receives spawners again in January. By October, young suckers should be about 15 cm.
- **Alternate:** Alternate coves in alternate years, which requires deepening Yuma Cove by 5-10 feet. Davis Cove has limited wave-washed gravel, so fry from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] may be needed.
- Cage or pen rearing at marinas (Katherine's Landing, Cottonwood Cove or Willow Beach) would provide security and a public education opportunity.

**4. Stocking and reproduction mechanics.**
- Target stock: 20 females and 60 males, measured, sexed, assessed and PIT-tagged.
- If adult reproduction is absent or poor by mid-April, stock 50,000 larvae from Dexter.
- Larval monitoring begins in early March, weekly. Up to 25 specimens per date are preserved for diet study. Juveniles are sampled monthly once no longer phototactic.
- Elevation triggers: below 635 feet, weekly backwater checks; below 633 feet, or if conditions deteriorate, salvage fish to the lake, Davis Cove or cages.
- Rearing runs 18-24 months, with an anticipated 2,000 or more young fish of suitable size annually.

**5. Collection methods (ranked by preference).**
- **A. Seining** (bag or purse seine, about 200 × 6 feet): effective, low stress, rapid and high recovery. A custom seine costs about $1,000-2,000 and lasts several years.
- **B. Trammel netting:** successful in Lake Mohave but slightly higher mortality than seining.
- **C. Fyke/hoop nets:** successful and less stressful but time-consuming.
- **D. Electrofishing:** effective for adults but possibly as lethal as other methods, and less effective deeper than 6 feet.
- **E. Combined methods** and **F. Berm breaching:** Breaching at 645 feet in June would require removing about 150 cubic yards at Yuma Cove to create a 2-foot passage, with a hoop net at the outlet. Fish would leave voluntarily.

**6. Tagging decision.**
- Tags used previously were Carlin (UNLV) and Floy (ASU), but these have poor retention and wound problems.
- The Dexter hatchery trial found PIT tags superior. PIT tags in dorsal musculature performed best in 120 mm razorback suckers, with 100 percent tag verification and 83 percent survival.
- Costs: PIT tags about $4-5 each, detectors $1,777 each, injectors about $30 each. Tagging 1,000 fish with three detectors costs about $10,500. Coded wire tags ($0.73 each) would cost about $16,500 with injectors and detectors.
- Four to six people could tag 1,000 fish in two days. A minimum of 1,000-2,000 fish per stocking is planned.

**7. Monitoring of recruitment.**
- Prior tagging from the March "razorback roundup" (1988-1990) tagged 2,152 suckers, with 116 "new" and 62 (2.8 percent) recaptures from previous years.
- Sampling will occur twice yearly, in November and March, using trammel nets, electrofishing and beach seining. Collectors scan for PIT tags and dart-tag untagged fish.
- Monitoring focuses on the release area and other known adult aggregations, since most recaptures occur near initial release.

**8. Schedule and responsibilities.**
- Key 1990 milestones: NEPA environmental assessment (July, NPS), first and second ichthyocide applications (September and October, AGFD; $500-1,000), Section 7 Biological Opinion (September, FWS), and berm modification (October, Reclamation; $10,000).
- Davis Cove modification is scheduled for July 1991 ($15,000). Tagging equipment costs $10,500-15,500 in 1991 and $5,000-10,000 for tags in 1992.
- Annual progress reports are due each October, with a final report by the end of 1995.
- Reporting is distributed among FWS/ASU (monitoring), AGFD (renovation), NPS (NEPA) and Reclamation (design, construction and consolidation).
- A public relations program includes brochures, signs at Lake Mohave, a display and a video, coordinated by Reclamation.

**9. Governance.**
- A MOU will provide the framework for planning and funding. All contributions (labor, equipment and funding) are voluntary.
- Long-term management of permanent facilities and temporary barriers will be set by agreement between the land-managing agency, FWS and cooperating agencies.
- The authors describe this rearing program as probably the "last hope" for these species in the lower basin short of major changes to sportfisheries or water operations.
