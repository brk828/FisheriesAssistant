# Lake Mohave Fisheries Management Plan (1988 Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This draft plan from the [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] sets fisheries management goals for [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the [[Colorado River]] formed by [[Davis Dam]] in 1952. The lake supports an endemic native fish population and a managed warm and cold water sport fishery. The plan states that endemic species receive priority in all management decisions, with the aim of maintaining viable populations of [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] in the reservoir. The cold water fishery depends on hatchery-reared [[Rainbow Trout]]. The warm water fishery depends mainly on [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]], and the plan documents a sharp decline in largemouth bass harvest. The plan also addresses the unwanted [[Striped Bass]] population and the abundant [[Common Carp]].

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The reservoir lies on the lower Colorado River in the northern Mojave Desert. Its entire area is within the [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]], administered by the [[National Park Service]]. The Nevada Department of Wildlife and the Arizona Game and Fish Department jointly manage the aquatic resources. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] regulates flows and water levels.

The first 20 miles are confined between the walls of Black Canyon. The remaining 47 miles have many small coves, with sand, gravel, or rock shorelines. Water levels are not allowed to fall below 630 ft elevation to protect navigation, and the normal operating range is roughly between 636 and 640 ft (the scan is partly illegible here).

**Fish community.** The reservoir has three large native species from the pre-impoundment river: [[Colorado Squawfish]], Bonytail Chub, and Razorback Sucker. The squawfish is considered extinct in the reservoir. A small remnant bonytail population persists, and the razorback sucker maintains a large population. Introduced species include warm water fish (largemouth bass, striped bass, channel catfish, [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]], common carp, and [[Threadfin Shad]]) and cold water trout. Most warm water species were established before Davis Dam was built. Threadfin shad were introduced as forage in 1954–55. Striped bass were never intentionally stocked in the lake and are believed to enter through [[Hoover Dam]] as eggs, larvae, or small fish.

**Protection status.** The state of Nevada classifies the razorback sucker as protected and the bonytail chub as endangered. The federal government classifies the razorback sucker as a candidate for listing and the bonytail chub as endangered. Possessing these fish is illegal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and temperature.** Water flows are regulated for flood control, hydroelectric power, irrigation, and treaty obligations with Mexico. Flows are generally low in winter, rise in spring, peak in late spring or summer, and decline through fall. Average monthly releases from Hoover Dam were about 5,000 cfs in January and 15,000 cfs in the late spring through August for 1976–1980. Releases in 1984–85 never fell below 20,000 cfs and peaked above 31,000 cfs. Water exchange slows as inflow rises. Average inflow of 29,028 cfs in 1984 gave an exchange rate of 33 days. The plan states that high flows flush nutrients through the system before they can be used, which likely reduces productivity.

Winter water temperatures range from 52 to 58 °F. Water released from the hypolimnion of Lake Mead stays cold for up to 20 miles downstream. Thermal stratification runs from May or June through October. Surface temperatures may reach 85 °F in midsummer. The [[Thermocline]] is deepest in the mid-Cottonwood Basin and reaches as deep as 30 feet in September. At the "point of underflow," cold discharge from Hoover Dam dives beneath warmer lake water.

**Water quality.** Water entering the lake is of good quality, with no chemical constituents considered harmful to aquatic life. Visibility usually exceeds 10 feet and often extends past 20 feet in winter. Reductions in visibility are normally caused by [[Phytoplankton]] blooms or localized flash flooding.

**Primary producers.** Kisor (1978) identified 84 algal species. Phytoplankton concentrations generally rise in spring and summer, with peaks between March and September. Dominant aquatic plants include the pondweed, water milfoil, and naiad families. Tamarisk and willows are the most important shoreline plants, and they provide cover and food for fish.

**Zooplankton.** The community includes 19 rotifer, 12 cladoceran, and 8 copepod species. Peak abundance generally occurs from late winter to early summer.

**Macroinvertebrates.** The upper, thermally uniform cold region is dominated by chironomid midges (order Diptera, family Chironomidae). Amphipods and two fingernail clam species are also present. Diversity is low because of constant cold temperatures and fluctuating flows and levels. Warmer sections of the reservoir support more diverse communities, including crayfish, snails, dragonflies, damselflies, beetles, water bugs, and leeches.

**Trophic relationships.** Rainbow trout eat zooplankton, macroinvertebrates, and threadfin shad. Striped bass eat threadfin shad, trout, large macroinvertebrates, and zooplankton. Largemouth bass eat other fish, including their own young. Bluegill and green sunfish eat macroinvertebrates, zooplankton, and fish eggs and young. Threadfin shad feed on plankton and provide most of the forage for piscivorous fish. Fish-eating birds, mainly cormorants, great blue herons, and western grebes, are abundant. Their effect on fisheries is unknown.

## Key Findings

### Cold Water Fishery
- Trout are stocked from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (500,000 to 800,000 trout annually) and the [[Lake Mead Fish Hatchery]] (up to 300,000 fish per year). Recent stocking has averaged about one million 8–10 inch fish per year.
- Trout harvest from 1970 to 1986 ranged from a low of 85,539 (1976) to a high of 515,000 (1978), averaging 313,683 fish.
- Trout angling pressure rose from 130,000 angler days (1975) to 271,000 (1979), then fell to 201,000 (1985). The 1976–1985 average was 204,000 angler days.
- Projected trout angler days are 285,683 by 1990, 300,866 by 1995, and 313,012 by 1999.
- Over 95% of [[Creel Census]] anglers at Willow Beach pursue trout. Roughly 60% of trout anglers concentrate on a small part of the lake, which the plan identifies as a risk of a put-and-take fishery.

### Warm Water Fishery
- Largemouth bass are the most sought-after warm water species. Channel catfish rank second. Black crappie, bluegill, green sunfish, and striped bass attract little angling.
- Largemouth bass harvest fell from about 130,000 fish in 1980 to under 10,000 in 1985 and 1986. The plan links this decline to a lack of forage for young-of-year bass switching from invertebrates to fish. Fishing pressure is also dropping, from 84,000 angler days (1976–1985 average) to 23,000 in 1985.
- Channel catfish harvest peaked at 30,000 in 1978, fell to 13,000 in 1986, and averaged 20,500 from the 1970s through 1986. Angling pressure peaked at 25,000 angler days in 1980, fell to 7,000 in 1985, and averaged 14,000.
- Black crappie harvest reached 17,000 in 1977 and 1978, with an average of about 5,000 fish. Striped bass harvest has never exceeded 1,000 fish.

### Management Problems and Strategies
1. **Largemouth bass recruitment.** Young bass may be failing to survive their first winter. The plan's strategies are stricter regulations (reduced bag limits, season restrictions, size limits, catch-and-release) and fertilization ([[Lake Fertilization]]) to boost forage. Tertiary treatment of [[Las Vegas Wash]] wastewater is a suspected cause of reduced nutrient input to Lake Mead, which may have reduced forage.
2. **Trout size.** Stocked trout grow to 16–20 inches and then disappear. Strategies include using long-lived, large-growing salmonid strains, developing a trout strain adapted to the lake, and increasing nutrient input.
3. **Angling concentration.** Pressure concentrates at Willow Beach, risking a put-and-take fishery there. Strategies include encouraging use of the rest of the reservoir and applying different regulations at Willow Beach, although enforcement could be difficult.
4. **Striped bass predation.** Striped bass could reproduce without limit and damage the stocked salmonid program. Strategies include suppression through harvest, life-history research, and adjusting stocking to reduce predation.
5. **Common carp.** Carp make up a large part of the lake biomass. Strategies include encouraging angling, allowing commercial harvest (which the plan says may not be profitable), and considering legislation to allow carp sales.

### Native Fish Objectives
- **Objective 1:** Maintain viable populations of razorback suckers and bonytail chubs in Lake Mohave by maintaining protected status and monitoring populations through routine sampling.
- **Objective 2:** Continue research on the life histories and population dynamics of these species so management procedures can be identified.
- Most native fish research has been conducted by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], Arizona State University, and the University of Nevada, Las Vegas. Nevada's role is primarily supportive.

### Management Alternatives and Preferred Options
- **Cold water fishery:** Four alternatives were evaluated. The [[Trophy Fishery Alternative]] requires size and bag limits, and the lake has potential for it. The [[Quality Fishery Alternative]] targets catch rates of 0.30–1.25 fish per hour and 2.0–3.5 per angler day. The [[General Fishery Alternative]] targets 0.25–0.75 fish per hour and 1.0–2.0 per day. The [[Put and Take Alternative]] would concentrate effort at Willow Beach. The **preferred alternative is quality**, with objectives of 0.25–0.75 trout per angler hour, 1.2–3.5 trout per angler day, an average length of at least 12 inches, and a return of at least 10% of stocked weight.
- **Largemouth bass:** Trophy, quality, and general alternatives were considered. The **preferred alternative is quality**, with targets of 1.0–2.0 bass per angler day and an average length of at least 13 inches. The trophy alternative defines a trophy fishery as an average catch of 15 inches or more.
- **Striped bass:** Three options were considered: elimination (judged to have very little potential), suppression through liberal bag limits and possible commercial harvest, and replacement of the stocked trout fishery with a self-reproducing striped bass population. The **preferred alternative is suppression**, with monitoring of trout returns and catch rates, population sampling, and investigation of striped bass reproduction.
- **Channel catfish and panfish:** The plan calls for monitoring through field surveys and creel census. Anglers will be encouraged to fish channel catfish as an alternative to trout and bass, and bluegill and green sunfish as an abundant alternative.
- **Black crappie:** The plan aims to raise the harvest to 5,000–30,000 fish per year through nutrient increase and introduction of brush habitat.

### Monitoring
The plan calls for continued field surveys and [[Creel Census]] to identify population trends and emerging problems in all fisheries, including threadfin shad, which is an important forage fish for both warm and cold water sport fisheries.
