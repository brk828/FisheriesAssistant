# Sebec Lake creel census

## Executive Summary
This 1954 report by Regional Fishery Biologist Kendall Warner (Dingell-Johnson Project F-8-R) documents a weekend/holiday creel census conducted in May 1954 at [[Sebec Lake]], Piscataquis County, Maine, in cooperation with the Sebec Lake Fish and Game Association. The study was prompted by concerns over declining average size and catch success of [[Landlocked Salmon]]. The census quantified fishing pressure, gear-specific catch success (particularly fly-fishing vs. bait/general-law methods at Early's Salmon Pool), and collected scale samples for age-and-growth analysis. Findings indicate that Sebec Lake salmon exhibit notably slow growth compared to state-wide averages, with fish not entering the legal fishery in significant numbers until their fifth summer of growth. The report also addresses competitive dynamics between salmon and abundant warmwater species ([[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[White Perch]]), and evaluates habitat quality of key spawning tributaries, [[Wilson Stream]] and [[Ship Pond Stream]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
[[Sebec Lake]] is a large (6,803-acre), deep (max. 155 ft) coldwater lake within the [[Penobscot River]] drainage (via the Sebec River and Piscataquis River), historically accessible to sea-run salmon prior to dam construction. The lake supports a mixed cold- and warm-water fish community:

- **Cold-water guild:** Landlocked Salmon, Lake Trout ([[Salvelinus namaycush]]), Eastern Brook Trout ([[Salvelinus fontinalis]])
- **Warm-water/other guild:** Smallmouth Bass, White Perch, Yellow Perch, Chain Pickerel, Smelt, Eel, White Sucker, Minnows, Cusk

Habitat structure: much of the shoreline is rocky/boulder-strewn with limited littoral zone; shallow coves in the southern basin support warmwater species and limited aquatic vegetation. Recent surveys (referenced) noted abundant cold, well-oxygenated water at depth through late summer, supporting the coldwater fishery.

**Spawning/nursery habitat:** Of ~23 tributary streams in the 400-square-mile drainage, only [[Wilson Stream]] and [[Ship Pond Stream]] provide suitable salmon spawning and nursery habitat. Wilson Stream (and its tributaries [[Davis Brook]] and [[Monson Stream]]) contains extensive, high-quality salmon spawning/nursery areas, though two sets of falls near its mouth present partial obstacles to spawning migration. Ship Pond Stream offers less extensive but suitable habitat, with passable — though still obstructive — falls at its mouth.

**Interspecific competition:** Smallmouth bass (introduced late 1800s) and white perch are described as the chief ecological competitors with salmon for food and space, complicating management due to divergent angler interest groups (summer bass fishery vs. spring salmon fishery).

## Key Findings
1. **Fishing pressure & catch composition:** Over 8 census days, 272 fishermen fished 1,056 line-hours, yielding 94 fish (90 salmon, 3 brook trout, 1 lake trout) — a catch rate of 0.091 fish/fisherman/hour, comparable to other Maine salmon waters (e.g., Cold Stream Pond: 0.10; Green Lake: 0.03; Rangeley Lake: 0.09).
2. **Gear-specific success at Early's Salmon Pool:** Fly-only anglers (27 fishermen, 106 fishing hours) caught 16 legal salmon (69.5% of the pool's sampled catch, catch rate 0.15/hr), far outperforming general-law gear users (81 fishermen, 205.5 hours, 7 salmon, 0.03/hr) and worm-only anglers (14 fishermen, 97 hours, 1 salmon, 0.01/hr).
3. **Slow growth rate:** Sebec Lake salmon grow approximately one year "behind" the Maine state-wide average length-at-age across all sampled age classes (III–VII), resulting in an atypically high proportion of older fish (age V+) in the catch, whereas state-wide fisheries are typically dominated by age III–IV fish.
4. **Delayed recruitment to fishery:** Due to slow growth, salmon do not reliably reach the 14-inch legal minimum until their fifth summer growing season.
5. **Lake trout status:** Rare in small sizes; large individuals caught are hypothesized to represent downstream migrants from lake trout waters via Wilson Stream rather than a self-sustaining Sebec Lake population.
6. **Management tension:** Existing recommendations (Maine Lake Survey, Cobbe et al. 1953) called for removing harvest restrictions on warmwater game fish, but practical reduction of bass/perch populations (via trapping, netting, or spawning-ground poisoning) was deemed infeasible due to resource constraints and the fact that these species are already well-established throughout the broader drainage north of the lake.
7. **Structural obstacles:** Falls at the mouths of both Wilson Stream and Ship Pond Stream partially impede salmon spawning migration, representing a candidate target for fish-passage modification.
