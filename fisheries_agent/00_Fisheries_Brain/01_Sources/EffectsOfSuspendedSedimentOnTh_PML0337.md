# Effects of Suspended Sediment on the Reproductive Success of the Tricolor Shiner, a Crevice-Spawning Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Burkhead and Jelks tests whether suspended sediment reduces reproduction in [[Tricolor Shiner]] (*Cyprinella trichroistia*), a crevice-spawning minnow endemic to the [[Coosa River]] system. Using replicated laboratory spawning tubs exposed to 0, 100, 300, and 600 mg/L of a weathered red clay, the authors found that higher sediment concentrations reduced the frequency of spawning, lowered the number of eggs laid when spawning occurred, and delayed spawning. Egg and larval mortality was negligible. Linking the results to 1963 daily sediment records from three [[Georgia]] rivers, the authors estimate that about 20% fewer eggs would have been laid during that spawning season than under control conditions. The paper argues that sublethal reproductive effects of excessive [[Sedimentation]] are a significant, under-recognized threat to imperiled southeastern fishes.

## Background & Description

The tricolor shiner is a small cyprinid that is widespread in the eastern [[Mobile River Basin]] above the [[Fall Line]]. It is closely related to the federally threatened [[Blue Shiner]] (*C. caerulea*) and shares habitat affinities with several endangered and threatened species. Although the tricolor shiner is considered less sensitive to disturbance than the blue shiner, its numbers and range have declined over recent decades, and it is declining in the [[Conasauga River]] system.

Tricolor shiners spawn in rock and log crevices through ritualized behavior typical of the genus [[Cyprinella]]. Females lay multiple clutches over a protracted season, and peak spawning is thought to occur in June. The genus was chosen for study because its members spawn in captivity, produce large numbers of eggs, occupy many biotopes, and vary in sensitivity to disturbance. Most earlier experimental work on suspended sediment has used game species such as salmonids and centrarchids. For native North American minnows, the authors note that the only relevant data before this study concerned [[Creek Chub]] persistence in coal-fine-polluted streams.

The southeastern United States is described as the richest temperate freshwater fish region in the world and a global freshwater hotspot. Between 1986 and 1998, the number of federally threatened and endangered fishes from the region rose from 16 to 38 species. The authors attribute this decline mainly to loss of lotic habitat linked to rapid regional growth, with excessive sedimentation, impoundments, channelization, urbanization, and pollution as the main drivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors review the known effects of excessive sedimentation on fishes. These include increased predation on eggs, greater adult vulnerability to predators, reduced reproductive success, physiological stress, gill damage, reduced feeding and prey detection, increased parasitism, and simplified community structure.

Sediment data for the study were drawn from three monitoring sites in the current and historic range of the species: the Conasauga River at Tilton, the Coosawattee River at Pine Chapel, and the Etowah River at Canton, all in Georgia. Only 1963 included daily sediment concentrations suitable for frequency analysis. During the April–August spawning season, 38% of values exceeded 100 mg/L and 2% exceeded 600 mg/L. The three treatment levels were chosen to represent field conditions and to be large enough to produce an effect.

The clay used was collected near Dalton, Georgia. Heavy metal concentrations were within normal geochemical background levels for north Georgia stream sediments. Pesticides were below quantification limits, although a toxaphene signal indicated past agricultural exposure. The authors argue that this soil is representative of material available for transport in agricultural landscapes of the region.

## Key Findings

**Methods and design**

- The experiment used a two-way ANOVA design with four treatments (0 [control], 100, 300, and 600 mg/L), three trials, and four replicates per treatment, giving n = 48 tubs.
- Each trial used 16 tubs of 33 L, with circulating current set to 15 cm/s, a constant photoperiod of 13 h light and 11 h dark, and mean water temperature of 23.8 °C.
- Spawning towers were built from stacked black tiles separated by washers to create crevices averaging 2.5 mm.
- Adult fish were seined in April–June 1997 from Rock Creek, a Conasauga River tributary in Murray County, Georgia. Each trial used 16 males and 32 females (one male and two females per tub).
- Sediment concentrations declined over trials because of settling. Turbidity was converted to sediment concentration with the regression sc = 0.80(t) + 22.7 (R² = 0.99, P < 0.0001), which was used to estimate the sediment level at spawning.
- Developmental stage was used to date spawns, assuming about 45 h to eye development and about 120 h to hatching.

**Spawning effort and output**

- Spawning effort, the fraction of tubs in which spawning occurred, decreased with sediment concentration (R² = 0.99, P < 0.003). It was 83% in the control, fell by 30% at 300 mg/L, and fell by 70% at 600 mg/L.
- Where spawning occurred, sediment significantly reduced the proportion of available eggs spawned (F = 9.92, df = 3, P = 0.009). Dunnett's one-tailed test found no significant difference between the control and 100 mg/L, but significant differences at 300 and 600 mg/L.
- Control tubs spawned more than twice as much as the 100 and 300 mg/L treatments and more than ten times as much as the 600 mg/L treatment.

**Timing of spawning**

- Higher sediment delayed spawning. Developmental-stage distributions differed significantly among treatments (P = 0.001).
- Control tubs had more larvae, 100 mg/L had more eyed eggs, and 300 and 600 mg/L had more clear eggs (P < 0.001).
- In the 600 mg/L treatment, spawning apparently occurred after about 95 h, when sediment had fallen to about 160 mg/L.

**Survival**

- Egg mortality was negligible: 7 of 3,197 eggs died (about 0.02%), all in the 100 mg/L treatments.
- Viable eggs were found embedded in fine sediment in the 300 and 600 mg/L treatments.

**Fish characteristics**

- Mean standard length did not differ significantly among treatments or trials.
- Post-experiment females carried a mean of 228.4 mature or ripe ovarian eggs (SD 80.2; range 20–467; females 46–54 mm SL).
- Mean egg diameter was 1.06 mm (range 0.9–1.3 mm; n = 45 eggs).
- Male size had no significant effect on eggs spawned (R² = 0.04, P = 0.15).
- Sex misidentification occurred in eight of 48 replicates, which left those tubs with one female.

**Field extrapolation**

- Weighting the laboratory reduction by the proportion of the 1963 spawning season above each treatment level, the authors estimate that about 20% fewer eggs would have been laid in these streams than under control conditions.
- The model assumes uniform spawning and no compensation by females. The authors consider it a rough approximation.

**Comparison with other studies**

- The authors applied their data to the nonsalmonid model of [[Newcombe and Jensen Meta-Analysis]], which predicted sublethal effects, including probable physiological stress and reduced feeding.
- Other studies cited showed egg hatching delays in white perch and striped bass at concentrations above 100 mg/L and high egg and larval mortality in rainbow trout and striped bass at concentrations from 21 to 485 mg/L, depending on exposure time.
- Reduced feeding has been reported in Atlantic herring at concentrations as low as 20 mg/L, and reduced prey location in bluegills at 15 mg/L.

**Conclusions**

- Suspended sediment reduces tricolor shiner reproduction through lower spawning frequency, lower egg output, and delayed spawning, with little direct mortality. The authors argue that sublethal behavioral effects can still severely affect population stability and longevity.
- The authors state that excessive sedimentation remains "an ignored and largely unappreciated threat" to aquatic ecosystems and call for strategic daily monitoring of suspended sediment at more sites.
- The method may be applied to other fish species and could support the development of water quality criteria for suspended sediment, although criteria development was not a goal of this study.
- Mitigating suspended sediment is described as an important conservation action for southern Appalachian fishes that spawn in benthic habitats.
