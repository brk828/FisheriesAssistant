# Biological and Chemical Study of Alamogordo Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #reservoir-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This job completion report, prepared for the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish under Federal Aid Project F-22-R-2 (Work Plan 4, Job B-1), is a **progress report** on a multi-season study of [[Alamogordo Reservoir]], one of New Mexico's largest and most fluctuating artificial impoundments. Data were gathered from April 1960 to April 1961 on hydrology, water chemistry, temperature, turbidity, bottom fauna, plankton, and fish populations. The authors note that results will be compared with later segments before any final management conclusions are drawn. The reservoir is irrigation-oriented, so recreation is secondary, and the water quality is judged adequate to support a fish population.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Gather historical, physical, chemical, biological and fishery data to support better management of the reservoir.
- **Dam:** The [[Alamogordo Dam]] is an earth and rock fill structure on the [[Pecos River]], about 21 miles above Fort Sumner. Built by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (construction began February 1936, completed December 1937, cost $2,088,488), with 16 feet added in 1956 for dam safety.
- **Dimensions:** Length 4,100 ft; height 150 ft; storage capacity 122,000 acre-feet; surface area at full pool 4,650 acres.
- **Funding and purpose:** The [[Carlsbad Irrigation District]] repays construction costs at $66,000 per year, with payments completed in 1986. Priorities are irrigation first, then flood control and river regulation.
- **Drainage:** The watershed of 4,385 square miles originates in the [[Sangre de Cristo Mountains]]. Most inflow arrives through the Pecos Arm.
- **Spillway and outlets:** Three 45 × 22.5 ft tainter gates control the concrete spillway (maximum discharge 44,800 cfs). Two 66-inch service pipes and an emergency earth spillway on the east side (activates at elevation 4,282.0 ft) complete the works.
- **Access and use:** Reached via U.S. Highway 84 and State Road 203. Facilities are limited; picnic and camping areas were built in the prior year, and boat docks are restricted to Alamo Boat Club members.
- **Regional history:** The area was once the site of the [[Bosque Redondo]] reservation, where Navajo and Apache people were held from 1862 until the fort was abandoned in 1866.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and weather (1959–1960, Alamogordo Lake Weather Station):**
- Annual precipitation: 12.76 in (1959), 22.45 in (1960).
- Average annual temperature: 58.7 °F (1959), 57.3 °F (1960).

**Geology and terrain:** The shoreline is underlain by [[Santa Rosa Sandstone]], about 350 ft thick. The bottom is sandstone and silt, largely covered by a slimy red silt. Vegetation includes saltcedar (*Tamarix* sp.), juniper, forbs, cactus, grasses and cottonwoods (*Populus* sp.) at the lake head. Cattle and sheep ranching surrounds the reservoir.

**Water level:** Storage fluctuated from 70,335 acre-feet (June 5, 1960) to 123,460 acre-feet (December 1960 and April 1961). Drawdown followed the irrigation season, and heavy July 1960 rains brought the lake near normal by August.

**Thermal structure:**
- Gauging station vertical temperatures ranged from 78.0 °F (July) to 39.0 °F (January).
- Boat dock vertical temperatures ranged from 77.8 °F (August) to 39.2 °F (January).
- The water column showed stratification in summer, with surface temperatures warmer than deep water.

**Clarity:** Secchi disk readings ranged from 48 to 234 inches. A Jackson candle turbidimeter gave less than 25 ppm.

**Water chemistry:**
- Dissolved oxygen was 8.9 ppm at the surface in May and fell to 0.5 ppm at 50–60 ft in August, indicating deep-water oxygen depletion.
- Free carbon dioxide was 0 ppm at most depths, except 3–7 ppm in deeper water in summer.
- pH ranged mainly from 7.3 to 8.5.
- Total hardness ranged from about 700 to 1,084 ppm, reflecting high mineral content.
- Specific conductance ranged from about 101 to 180 µmhos/cm.
- Detailed analysis by the New Mexico Department of Public Health (June 1959 and February 1961) found about 1,372–1,396 ppm total residue, 110 ppm total alkalinity, 280 ppm calcium, 800 ppm sulphates, and 66 ppm chlorides.

**Bottom fauna:** A total of 432 Ekman dredge samples were taken at 18 stations, covering 108 sq ft. Invertebrates were sparse, dominated by [[Tendipedidae]] (chironomid midges). The authors decided to discontinue this sampling because too few organisms were collected.

**Plankton:** Plankton hauls were sampled with a conventional net and quantified as cc per cubic meter of water.

**Aquatic vegetation:** Sparse, mainly pondweed (*Potamogeton* sp.) and filamentous green algae (Chlorophyceae).

## Key Findings

**Fish community (April–October 1960 gill netting, 18 stations, 2,538.50 net-hours):**
- Total catch: 1,905 fish weighing 902.50 lb.
- Six families and twelve species have been recorded to date.
- [[White Crappie]] was dominant at 61.78% by number and 15.79% by weight.
- [[Carp]] made up 13.86% by number and 36.35% by weight.
- [[River Carpsucker]] contributed 10.18% by number and 38.64% by weight.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] contributed 0.9% by number but 4.01% by weight, reaching up to 96 oz.
- Game fish were 75.62% by number and 24.76% by weight.
- The highest catch per netting hour was in May (1.15 fish).

**Seine hauls (August 23, 1960):** [[Threadfin Shad]] made up the largest number of fish collected. This species was introduced from Arizona on May 18, 1960, as a forage fish. Whether it can survive winter is uncertain given the low December 1960 and January 1961 temperatures.

**Condition factors:** The coefficient of condition was computed for all species. [[Bluegill]] and [[Green Sunfish]] had the highest values, around 76–85 and 68–80 respectively. [[Channel Catfish]] values were lower, around 22–37.

**Tagging program:** 947 fish were tagged with monel metal butt tags during the first segment. Two tagged fish had been reported by anglers by the report date, and none had been recaptured by netting, rotenone or seining.

**Disease and parasites:** Infections were incidental. Roundworms (Nematoda) were found on the intestines and stomach lining of largemouth bass and black bullhead.

**Food habits:** Stomach samples were taken from 31 fish. Contents included other fish (white crappie, sunfish, shiner, and largemouth bass fry), insects, chironomids, algae and plant matter.

**Conclusions and next steps:**
- Water quality is considered sufficient to maintain a fish population.
- Future work will compare results across segments, compile historical stocking records, and assess threadfin shad overwintering.
- Bottom fauna sampling will be discontinued due to low organism numbers.

**Related fisheries sources:** The methods drew on [[Lagler 1956]] (*Freshwater Fishery Biology*), [[Carlander 1953]] (coefficient of condition), and [[Pennak 1953]] (*Freshwater Invertebrates of the United States*).
