# The Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries #wildlife-management

## Executive Summary

This 1994 U.S. Department of the Interior (National Biological Survey) special publication by W. Reid Goforth documents the history, administration, personnel, and research contributions of the [[Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Units]] Program. The program originated in 1932-1935 with [[J. N. "Ding" Darling]], who funded the first unit at Iowa State College, and expanded into a national network of university-hosted units that combine federal scientists, state fish and wildlife agencies, universities, and the [[Wildlife Management Institute]]. The text covers the program's legal foundation ([[Cooperative Units Act of 1960]] and its 1978 amendment), organizational shifts within the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the budget crises of 1982-1984, and the research record across fisheries, wildlife, and aquatic ecology. The author also presents the program's administrative tensions and recommendations for improvement.

## Background & Description

The publication is a historical and administrative account rather than a primary research study. Its central figure is Darling, a political cartoonist and later Director of the Bureau of Biological Survey (appointed 10 March 1934), whose concern about declining wildlife and a shortage of trained managers motivated the first unit. The 1930 Report to the American Game Conference, chaired by [[Aldo Leopold]], had already highlighted the shortage of trained personnel and the need for research.

Key organizational milestones:
- **1935:** Nine wildlife units formed (Alabama, Connecticut, Iowa, Maine, Ohio, Oregon, Texas, Utah, Virginia), each funded jointly by the federal government, state agencies, and university host, with $3,000 from ammunition companies.
- **1960:** The Cooperative Units Act (P.L. 86-686) created a separate budget line item and added fisheries; the first 12 cooperative fishery units formed 1961-1964.
- **1973:** Wildlife and fishery units merged into a Division of Cooperative Research; fishery units were renamed "research units." Total: 45 units in 25 states.
- **1978:** The Fish and Wildlife Improvement Act amendment enabled direct federal research funding through Research Work Orders.
- **1982-1984:** The administration removed the program from its budget; Congress restored funding each year.
- **1993-1994:** The program moved into the newly formed National Biological Survey.

The text notes that units are not identical: "there is no typical unit—each is unique," shaped by their cooperating agreements and local traditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The publication frames the program within a broader history of American wildlife decline. Elk had been extirpated from the eastern woodlands, bison were gone from the Great Plains, passenger pigeons were virtually gone, turkeys had been extirpated from about 90% of their former range, and waterfowl populations were declining. Hunting license sales reached roughly 6 million in 1920, more than twice the number sold a decade earlier. Habitat pressure from population growth, overuse, and development was identified as the greatest threat to wildlife.

Units are described as field-oriented: their research generally required storage for boats, nets, large traps, and vehicles rather than extensive laboratories. The program's ecological reach extends from [[Arctic Grayling]] in Alaska to [[Hawaiian Stream Fishes]] and nearshore Pacific island fish communities.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and administration**
- Units are cooperative: federal scientists are paired with a host university, state fish and wildlife agency, and (in the early years) the Wildlife Management Institute. Combined fish-and-wildlife units have at least four signatories; fishery-only units have three.
- Each unit is governed by a [[Coordinating Committee]] that meets annually, approves a Statement of Direction, budget, and project starts, and generally operates by consensus.
- Unit leaders and assistant leaders hold appointments on university graduate faculties and may teach one graduate course per year. All unit personnel must hold a doctoral degree, though some early fishery staff did not.
- The [[Research Work Order]] (RWO) process allows federal agencies to fund research at units; it also introduced pressures on unit capacity and shifted some research toward Service priorities.

**Personnel and placement**
- As of June 1992, units reported 4,631 graduate students completed as advisees of unit biologists (most conservative count). First professional placements: 1,204 federal, 1,806 state fish and wildlife agency, 741 university, and 880 other or unknown. Goforth reports placement consistently above 95% in fishery, wildlife, and related professions.
- The author states that each dollar invested by a cooperator returns about a dollar's worth of services and products to all cooperators (a claim presented as evidence, not quantified in the text).

**Funding and salary concerns**
- A 1979 informal survey found new assistant unit leader (GS-11, step 1) salaries about 9% above beginning university assistant professor salaries. By 1987, they were 26% below. The federal scale for beginning scientists had regressed by 35% over that period.
- During the 1982-1984 budget crisis, roughly one-third of unit positions were vacant after three years. FY1985 funding was 35% below the level expected had the program remained in the president's budget, and units were 40% understaffed.
- As of FY1993, about 15% of agreed-upon positions remained vacant. Congressionally mandated new units were added without funding for some positions.

**Selected research highlights (Appendix K, summarized)**
- *Fisheries:* Overfishing of grayling in the Chena River, Alaska; recognition of the half-pounder steelhead's role in Klamath-Trinity populations; discovery that angler wading caused egg and pre-emergent fry mortality up to 96% under twice-daily wading; sediment-spawning survival models used by agencies and the U.S. Forest Service; rediscovery of stress physiology effects on salmonid immunity and development.
- *Wildlife:* Line-transect and probability sampling methods for pronghorn and mountain lion surveys; Canada goose marking studies in the Mississippi Flyway; black bear studies in Shenandoah National Park; wood duck nest-box management; sea otter–urchin–kelp trophic cascade research in Alaska.
- *Threatened and endangered species:* Research supporting recovery of the Ozark big-eared bat, interior least tern, Gulf Coast beach mice, and black-footed ferret; the leading role of unit research on the [[Northern Spotted Owl]] in Northwest forest management policy.
- *Methods:* The CAPTURE software for capture-recapture estimation and the TRANSECT software for distance sampling (used by biologists in 50 countries).

**Program improvement recommendations**
- Recognize units as a distinct program rather than a "13th research center," and give Coordinating Committees more latitude.
- Reduce duplicate federal and state inspection requirements.
- Hold regular meetings of unit personnel to address common problems.
- Maintain the cooperative character of units, which the author regards as central to their productivity.
- Clarify federal salary and grade-level policy for assistant leaders, which currently complicates recruitment.
