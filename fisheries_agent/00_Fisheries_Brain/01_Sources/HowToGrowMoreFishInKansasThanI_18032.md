# How to Grow More Fish in Kansas than in Minnesota

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #aquaculture #pond-management

## Executive Summary

This 1956 presentation by Frank B. Cross of the University of Kansas outlines fundamental principles for improving sport fishing in Kansas through better pond construction and management. Rather than focusing on stocking ratios or fishing regulations, the document emphasizes five basic infrastructure requirements that would increase fish production under any management program: adequate water supply, overflow control, livestock fencing, siltation control, and drainage facilities. The approach prioritizes pond fisheries because they offer the greatest potential for fish production per acre and provide the most opportunities for sportsmen to actively participate in conservation efforts.

## Background & Description

The document was prepared as notes for a talk to the Central Kansas Fish and Game Association in Salina on February 14, 1956. The author acknowledges that sport fishing is inherently unpredictable and that these improvements will not guarantee full stringers daily, but argues that vast room for improvement exists. The recommendations apply to any fish species, stocking ratio, or management program including fertilization.

The presentation emphasizes that farm ponds in Kansas have greater potential for fish production than streams and large natural or artificial lakes, making them the ideal focus for conservation efforts. The author notes that sportsmen must actively assist in these improvements for them to be accomplished.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Farm ponds in Kansas face multiple environmental challenges that limit fish production:

**Water Quality Issues**: Cattle wading in ponds muddy the water, which reduces fish-food production and makes it difficult for sight-feeding fish to find food. Muddy water also limits the number of fish that will discover anglers' bait. Heavy flocculent silt deposits reduce pond utility for all purposes.

**Oxygen Depletion**: Cattle standing in ponds during hot summer days cause fish kills by stirring bottom ooze, which releases decomposition gases that remove oxygen as they bubble to the surface, suffocating fish.

**Chemical Contamination**: [[DDT]] and other insecticides used for cattle insect control can kill all fish after cattle enter the pond, as these chemicals are toxic to fish.

**Population Imbalance**: Cattle "feathering" the shoreline to extreme shallowness and disturbing spawning areas in the shallows causes fish populations to become "out of balance."

**Uncontrolled Overflow**: Excess water flowing through ponds and over shallow surface spillways occurs multiple times during wet and normal years in almost all Kansas farm ponds. This causes stocked fish to escape and allows undesirable fish species to enter, largely negating fertilization and other management practices.

## Key Findings

### Five Basic Requirements for High Fish Production

1. **Adequate Water Supply**: Ponds must maintain water levels and be capable of refilling if they dry at critical times.

2. **Control of Overflow**: Uncontrolled overflow causes stocked fish to escape and allows undesirable species to enter. Methods for diverting excess runoff away from ponds are essential. The document provides three diagrammatic solutions:
   - Single pond with diversion terrace and control gate (Diagram 1)
   - Two-pond arrangement where upper pond catches runoff and releases controlled amounts to lower pond (Diagram 2)
   - System using existing large lake to supply water for intensive fish production in downstream ponds (Diagram 3)

3. **Fencing from Livestock**: Despite most farm ponds being built primarily for livestock water, fencing with piped water to tanks below the dam is recommended. This is the most important point from an overall conservation standpoint.

4. **Control of Siltation**: Heavy silt deposits reduce pond utility and fish production. This problem can be eliminated through overflow control and fencing.

5. **Facilities for Draining**: While recent developments in chemical control have reduced the necessity for periodic draining, it retains numerous advantages and should be included where ponds are not needed continuously for livestock water.

### Additional Benefits Beyond Fish Production

**Livestock Water Advantages**: Year-round open water below ice cover, clear rather than muddy water, cooler summer water from pond depths, possible increased milk production and weight gain from cattle preferring clear water, and increased pond lifespan due to reduced siltation.

**Wildlife Habitat**: Native birds and mammals use farm ponds, especially with suitable cover nearby. Fenced enclosures are ideal for wildlife food and cover plantings, with habitat improvement likely more successful and less costly than other farm areas.

**Aesthetic Value**: Properly improved ponds with thick grass cover to water's edge, shaded by trees and flowering shrubs, create attractive features rather than unattractive mudholes.

**Economic Returns**: One documented case showed a two-pond system in a deep gulley (previously wasteland) generating an average of $1,500 yearly income over five years from fishing leases at $5.00 per day, while also providing erosion control and livestock water.
