# Summer Mortality of Striped Bass in Reservoirs of the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #fisheries-management #limnology

## Executive Summary
This paper, by William J. Matthews, presents a survey-based investigation into summer die-offs of [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) across 80 reservoirs in the United States. The study found that 34% (27 reservoirs) reported some degree of summer mortality, predominantly affecting large adult fish during peak summer months (July–September). While reservoirs experiencing mortality tended to have significantly larger surface area, volume, and depth metrics compared to non-mortality reservoirs in univariate tests, a comprehensive multivariate analysis (Principal Components Analysis) using 20 limnological characteristics found **no discernible relationship** between specific reservoir types and the occurrence of mortality. The study concludes that summer mortality is a geographically widespread but highly variable and non-universal phenomenon, likely linked to the interacting stressors described in the companion paper by [[Coutant 1985]] (high temperature and low dissolved oxygen).

## Background & Description
While this paper is primarily an ecological/limnological survey rather than a strict morphological study, it provides critical data on the **size-selectivity** of the mortality event:
*   **Vulnerable Size Class:** Mortality overwhelmingly affected medium-sized to large adults.
    *   Fish > 9 kg: Reported in 3 reservoirs.
    *   Fish 7.3–9 kg: Reported in 11 reservoirs.
    *   Fish 5.0–7.2 kg: Reported in 16 reservoirs.
*   **Autopsy Findings:** Physical examination of specimens (collected in 13 of 27 reservoirs) revealed no single unifying pathology. Findings included:
    *   Suspected bacterial infection (1 case).
    *   "Evidence of suffocation" (consistent with hypoxia).
    *   Enlarged and darkened gall bladders, indicative of **starvation** or extended metabolic stress.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper frames the striped bass's summer mortality within the context of reservoir [[Thermal Stratification]] and habitat squeeze, aligning with the hypothesis proposed by [[Coutant 1985]].

*   **Habitat Stressors:** The study did not find a direct statistical link via multivariate PCA, but univariate ANOVA showed mortality reservoirs had significantly greater:
    *   Surface Area
    *   Volume
    *   Maximum Depth
    *   Outlet/Water Release Depth
*   **Geographic Distribution:** Mortality reports spanned the eastern (Ca-Mg chemical type, low elevation) and western (Na-K chemical type, high TDS, high elevation) reservoir classes identified in the PCA scatterplot (Figure 1), suggesting the trigger is not restricted to a specific regional water chemistry or reservoir morphometry type.
*   **Species Comparison:** Striped bass appear more susceptible to summer die-off than co-occurring species. Of the 27 reservoirs with striped bass mortality, only 10 reported mortality in other species (most commonly [[Walleye]], in 6 reservoirs).
*   **Trophic/Physiological Context:** The pattern of mortality (large adults, late summer, high-volume/deep reservoirs) supports the "squeeze" hypothesis where suitable cool, oxygenated water becomes unavailable, forcing bioenergetic stress that leads to death via suffocation or starvation.

## Key Findings
1.  **Prevalence:** 34% (27 of 80) of surveyed reservoirs reported summer striped bass mortality events.
2.  **Timing:** Mortality peaked in **July (20 reservoirs), August (18 reservoirs), and September (13 reservoirs)**.
3.  **Magnitude:** 6 reservoirs reported >500 dead fish in a single season; 8 additional reservoirs reported >100 deaths.
4.  **Statistical Significance (Univariate):** Reservoirs with mortality had significantly greater Surface Area (F=11.66, P<0.01), Volume (F=16.17, P<0.01), Maximum Depth (F=4.81, P<0.05), and Outlet Depth (F=7.29, P<0.01) than reservoirs without mortality (Table 2).
5.  **Statistical Insignificance (Multivariate):** Despite the univariate trends, PCA using all 20 limnological variables showed **no clustering** of mortality-reporting reservoirs; PC I and PC II explained 38.6% of total variance, but mortality occurred across all combinations of limnological conditions.
6.  **No Single Cause:** Autopsies did not reveal a consistent disease or pathogen, supporting the hypothesis of an environmental (abiotic) cause rather than a biological pathogen driving the die-offs.
7.  **Management Implication:** The author advocates for standardized record-keeping by state fisheries agencies to allow for more robust, prospective (rather than retrospective/anecdotal) analysis of the phenomenon in the future.
