# Timing of the Last Highstand of Lake Lahontan

#limnology #paleolimnology #lake-level-chronology

## Executive Summary

This paleolimnological rebuttal, published in the *Journal of Paleolimnology* (1991), argues that the last highstand of [[Lake Lahontan]] occurred between about 14,500 and 13,000 yr B.P. The author, L.V. Benson, disputes [[Bradbury et al. 1989]], who proposed that the last highstand fell between 30,000 and 25,000 yr B.P. and found no evidence of a roughly 14,000 yr B.P. lake in the [[Walker Lake]] subbasin. Benson reviews radiocarbon and [[Uranium-Series Dating]] ages from across the Lahontan basin, examines the limitations of carbonate and sediment records, and concludes that the Walker Lake sediment record is poorly suited to detecting a short highstand. He also finds little support for a Walker Lake highstand above the 1308 m spill point between 30,000 and 25,000 yr B.P.

## Background & Description

Lake Lahontan was a large pluvial lake system in the western Great Basin. Its subbasins include Smoke Creek/Black Rock Desert, Pyramid Lake, Winnemucca Dry Lake, Carson Desert, Walker Lake, Buena Vista, and Honey Lake, separated by primary sills such as the [[Adrian Valley Sill]] (1308 m, the highest sill in the system). The paper is a response to Bradbury et al. (1989), which Benson says is inconsistent with the full body of available chronological data.

The chronology rests on three groups of dated materials:

- **Carbonate materials** formed in the shallow benthos: [[Tufa]], oolites, gastropods, and *Chara*. These give minimum estimates of lake level.
- **Organic material** from deltaic sediments and [[Packrat Middens]], including *Juniperus*, *Neotoma* dung, and soil organic carbon.
- **[[Rock Varnish]]** on the highstand terrace.

Most ages come from [[Radiocarbon Dating]] (conventional method), supplemented by [[Uranium-Series Dating]] for materials beyond the radiocarbon range (>20,000 yr B.P.). Table 1 lists 59 radiocarbon ages: 16 organic samples deposited above lake level and 43 carbonate samples (34 from the western subbasins, 7 from Walker Lake, and 2 from Carson Desert). The organic samples cluster at about 11,200 to 12,300 yr B.P., and the carbonates between about 12,500 and 20,000 yr B.P.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is concerned mainly with paleoclimate and sedimentary processes, but several environmental constraints shape its interpretations.

**Carbonate reliability.** Tufa and gastropods can give misleading ages:
- Gastropods and tufas near spring discharge points can incorporate dead carbon, producing ages that are too old.
- Acidic rain (pH < 5.5) can dissolve and reprecipitate subaerial carbonate, adding modern carbon and producing ages that are too young.
- [[Monohydrocalcite]] and high-magnesium calcite are metastable and recrystallize to low-magnesium calcite, partially resetting radiocarbon ages.

Benson therefore screened samples with petrography and X-ray diffraction and acid-leached them before analysis.

**Walker Lake sediment conditions.** Between 26 and 16 m depth in core WLC84-4, [[Pore Fluid Chemistry]] shows dissolved solids of 128,000 to 136,000 mg/L, pH of 9.2 to 9.6, and dissolved silica of 400 to 500 mg/L. These alkaline, hypersaline conditions favor diatom dissolution and pollen destruction, so sediment from periods of very shallow or dry lakes may be poorly preserved.

**Sedimentation and erosion.** Sedimentation in WLC84-4 averages about 0.63 mm/yr. Exposed lake sediment deflates at a few millimeters per century, so wind erosion could have removed highstand deposits after the lake dropped.

## Key Findings

1. **Timing of the highstand.** Radiocarbon and uranium-series ages from multiple materials place the last highstand at roughly 14,500 to 13,000 yr B.P. Agreement between methods is cited as evidence of internal consistency. For example, sample PL 41 gives a uranium-series age of 14,000 ± 3,000 yr B.P., compared with a radiocarbon age of 13,430 ± 200 yr B.P.

2. **Independent support.** A 14,100 yr B.P. [[Ostracode]] age from a fresh, deep-water lake in the Carson Lake area of the Carson Desert supports a roughly 14,000 yr B.P. highstand (Thompson et al., 1990).

3. **Start and end of the highstand.**
   - The rise is constrained by a soil organic-carbon age of 15,660 ± 150 yr B.P. (sample PL 85-3s).
   - Packrat midden material indicates the highstand ended before 12,350 ± 180 yr B.P.
   - A minimum-limiting [[Rock Varnish]] age of 12,680 ± 105 yr B.P. indicates the fall began between 14,600 and 12,700 yr B.P.

4. **Duration is short.** Dense tufa of 10 mm thickness formed at elevations of 1308 to 1330 m. Tufa growth at about 0.05 mm/yr implies that the 10 mm coating could have formed in as little as about 200 yr. Benson therefore concludes that the highstand may have lasted only about 200 yr.

5. **Walker Lake sediment deposits would be thin.** At 0.5 to 1.0 mm/yr, a 200 yr highstand would deposit only about 0.1 to 0.2 m of sediment in the Walker Lake subbasin.

6. **Deflation likely erased the evidence.** Using a deflation rate of 2.0 to 3.0 mm/yr over about 9,000 yr of playa conditions (13,800 to 4,800 yr B.P.), 0.18 to 0.27 m of sediment could have been removed. Benson argues that all highstand evidence may have been lost from the Walker Lake subbasin before 4,800 yr B.P.

7. **Coring biases.** Core WLC84-4 had poor recovery in its upper 25 m: 32% no recovery, 61% disturbed, and 7% relatively undisturbed. Sediment squeezed in, stuck, flowed out of the barrel, or liquefied under overpressure. Sediment at 23 to 21 m, where a highstand signal would be expected, shows fluidization and dike-like injection, and some material may have fallen from up to 5 m above its original position.

8. **Sampling resolution.** Ostracode and pollen samples were taken at about 1 m intervals, and diatom smear slides were examined only every other slide. Benson argues this spacing could not reliably detect 0.1 to 0.2 m of highstand sediment.

9. **Rejection of a 30,000 to 25,000 yr B.P. Walker Lake highstand.**
   - Lao & Benson (1988) reported a lake joining the Pyramid Lake and Smoke Creek/Black Rock Desert subbasins at 29,000 ± 9,000 yr B.P. (minimum age 23,000 yr B.P.), with about three times the mean historical surface area.
   - A camel in the mud of Pyramid Lake (Dansie et al.) indicates the lake surface was below 1160 m at about 25,500 yr B.P.
   - Walker Lake may have reached its spill point (1308 m) intermittently, but there are no data showing it joined the Lahontan system and rose above 1308 m between 30,000 and 25,000 yr B.P.

10. **Diatom evidence is ambiguous.** The dominant diatoms in the 50 to 35 m interval (*S. excentricus* and *S. nevadensis*) are said by Bradbury et al. to indicate a saline lake. Benson argues that salinity can rise over time from river-borne solutes without a change in lake size. His calculation suggests dissolved solids could have increased by about 34,000 to 69,000 mg/L over 15,000 to 30,000 yr.

11. **Unreliable radiocarbon ages.** Bradbury et al. used total inorganic and total organic carbon ages. Benson prefers TOC ages, because monohydrocalcite recrystallization resets carbon. He notes that TOC ages above about 30,000 yr are questionable, since 2.3% modern carbon contamination can reset an infinitely old sample to 30,000 yr. Nine Walker Lake tufa ages of 36,990 ± 2,860 to 25,280 ± 750 yr B.P. were excluded, because uranium-series checks showed they were too young by about 19,000 yr. Lao & Benson (1988) concluded that tufa ¹⁴C ages above 20,000 yr B.P. should be considered unreliable unless corroborated.

12. **Overall conclusion.** Benson concludes that the Walker Lake record cannot confirm or refute a 14,000 yr B.P. highstand, and that little or no data support a Walker Lake highstand above 1308 m between 30,000 and 25,000 yr B.P.
