# Growth of Channel Catfish in Oklahoma Waters: 1954 Revision

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 report from the [[Oklahoma Fisheries Research Laboratory]] consolidates age and growth data for [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) across Oklahoma. It draws on 7,717 specimens from 107 bodies of water collected between 1946 and 1954. Growth is calculated from lengths at each annulus, not from mean lengths at capture, so the results offer a statewide standard for judging good and poor growth. The study reports average lengths and weights by age, a [[Length-Weight Relationship]], [[Annulus Formation]] timing, and growth differences tied to water size, [[Turbidity]], water age, and natural reproduction. It concludes with management recommendations centred on stocking, thinning of overpopulated lakes, and watershed treatment of turbid waters.

## Background & Description

The channel catfish is among the most valued sport fishes in Oklahoma. It is present in all large lakes and streams in the state and has been widely introduced into ponds and small lakes since artificial propagation began in 1925 at the Durant hatchery. Anglers use rod and reel, trot line, set line, limb line, and jug line, with baits ranging from shrimp to proprietary "stink bait."

The report supersedes an earlier average-growth study by Hall and Jenkins (1954), which was based on 4,054 individuals from 50 waters. That method averaged lengths of age-groups collected at different points in the growing season, which introduced error. The 1954 revision uses only calculated end-of-year lengths.

**Sampling scope**

- Total: 107 bodies of water and 7,717 specimens.
- Breakdown: 24 ponds (under 5 acres), 39 small lakes (5–110 acres), 15 large lakes (111–500 acres), 23 reservoirs (over 500 acres), and 6 streams.
- Data were collected from 1946 to 1954. Most were gathered after 1949 using rotenone, lake drainings, seining, gill netting, hoop netting, and angling. About 84 percent of collections were made from May to September.
- Ages were determined from annuli on cross-sections of pectoral or dorsal spines, following [[Sneed]] (1951) (see [[Spine Sampling]]).
- Sex data were incomplete, so a sex-related difference in growth could not be assessed.
- Early measurements were in standard length (mm). Conversion factors to total length were derived from 886 specimens (Appendix E).

Table 1 compares the sample with the statewide inventory. Sampled waters were 94,483 ponds (estimated), and the sample included [[Lake Texoma]] (93,080 acres) and [[Grand Lake]] (46,300 acres), among the largest reservoirs in the state.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Statewide growth by age (total length, inches)**

Average calculated lengths at the end of each year of life (ages 1–14) were:

4.0, 8.5, 11.9, 14.5, 16.1, 17.8, 19.9, 21.9, 23.9, 24.8, 25.4, 25.5, 25.8, 28.8.

Average weights for ages 1–14 were approximately 0.01, 0.15, 0.49, 0.95, 1.36, 1.92, 2.80, 3.88, 5.23, 5.93, 6.43, 6.52, 6.78, and 9.87 pounds.

Fish reached legal length (10 inches) mostly in their third summer, at two to three years old. Some reached it in their second summer, and others not until five to six years old.

**Age structure**

Fish older than 14 years were absent from the samples. Ninety-seven percent of fish were under eight years old, and few lived beyond seven. The oldest individuals came mainly from very slow-growing populations, as had been observed for [[White Crappie]].

**Comparison with other species and waters**

- [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[White Crappie]] were used as reference species. Channel catfish have the potential to exceed the maximum weight growth of largemouth bass from age four onward. The statewide average, however, grows much more slowly than that potential, which suggests substantial suppression under average conditions.
- The gap between the fastest and slowest channel catfish is wider than for either largemouth bass or white crappie. The authors interpret this as greater plasticity in growth under varying environmental conditions.
- The [[Upper Mississippi River]] (Appelget and Smith, 1951; 535 fish, 1945–1946) grew more slowly than Oklahoma fish through age six. The Mississippi population surpassed Oklahoma at ages 10–12. Oklahoma stream growth was slightly faster than the Mississippi through the first seven years.

**Environmental influences on growth**

- **Size of water body:** Growth was generally faster in small lakes. The slowest growth occurred in old reservoirs and large lakes, which were similar through age seven. The fastest growth was in new reservoirs under four years old. Stream growth closely matched the state average, within one inch in each year class. The authors judged size to be less important than age, turbidity, or reproduction.
- **Age of impoundment:** New waters (seven reservoirs) grew faster than old ones. In those seven reservoirs, lengths ran 0.26 to 4.6 inches above the state average and 1.0 to 6.4 inches above old reservoirs.
- **Turbidity:** Average lengths in 22 turbid lakes were 1.1 to 6.9 inches below those in 45 clear lakes. Turbidity suppressed growth within every size class. Spavinaw Lake (old reservoir class) and Mountain Lake (large lake class) were the only clear waters in their size groups that consistently beat the state average.
- **Reproduction:** Among 57 small lakes and ponds, 29 showed no reproduction and 28 showed reproduction. Waters without reproduction grew 1.1 to 4.3 inches faster over the first seven years. The authors ranked reproduction alongside age and turbidity as a major growth factor.

**Condition factor (C)**

The condition index C(T.L.) rose gradually with size. Oklahoma fish from 9.0 to 16.2 inches averaged 29 (range 25.8–32.5) across almost 3,000 fish. The literature value from [[Carlander]] (1950) was 27 (range 13–42, 20 fish). The authors judged the difference not significant.

**Timing of annulus formation**

Annuli formed over roughly seven weeks, from mid-April to early June. Most formation centred between the last week of April and the last week of May, similar to [[White Crappie]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Older fish began annulus formation later. Fish aged six to eight did not finish until the first week of June. A gap in one- and two-year-old samples left their timing uncertain, but the first two annuli appear to have formed before the third week of May.

## Key Findings

1. **Statewide growth is moderate.** Average total length reached 4.0 inches at age one and 28.8 inches at age 14. The fastest individual growth was much higher than the average, which the authors attributed to environmental conditions rather than genetics.

2. **Length-weight relationship.** The equation is log W = −5.3846 + 3.4069 log L, with W in pounds and L in total inches (4,617 specimens; fish under seven inches excluded). Weight rises faster than the cube of length. Key benchmarks:
   - Legal length (10 inches): about 0.3 pound.
   - 15 inches: 1 pound.
   - 2 pounds: slightly over 18 inches.
   - Largest specimen: 34.7 inches and 18.5 pounds.

3. **Environmental drivers, ranked.** The authors ranked factors affecting growth as follows:
   - Turbidity (clear waters grew faster).
   - Impoundment age (new waters grew faster).
   - Natural reproduction (non-reproducing waters grew faster).
   - Water-body size (least influential).

4. **Stunting in overpopulated waters.** Poor growth was concentrated in turbid lakes with high populations. The authors identified intraspecific competition as a more likely cause of stunting than competition from other species.

5. **Management recommendations:**
   - Ponds and small lakes often do not reproduce. Stock small numbers at intervals of four to five years rather than overstocking initially.
   - In large impoundments on main streams, natural river populations can supply sufficient catfish.
   - In waters isolated from streams, initial plantings must come from hatchery stock, with further stocking depending on spawning success.
   - Stocking is recommended in permanently turbid waters, since slow growth is offset by longer lifespan and fishing is less limited than for largemouth bass or white crappie. These waters tend to overpopulate quickly, however.
   - Overpopulated lakes need a reduction of 50 percent or more. Named lakes needing this include all old reservoirs except Spavinaw, plus Claremore, Clinton, Snyder, Ardmore Club, Cleveland, Cordell, Newkirk Club, State Penitentiary, Townsend, and Weatherford Club Lakes.
   - Techniques include complete removal of stunted populations (draining or [[Rotenone]]) followed by restocking; greater harvest of smaller fish of all species; removing channel catfish from the game-fish list in reservoirs; and removing competing rough fish commercially.
   - Watershed treatment to clear turbid lakes and reduce siltation is a prerequisite for other improvements.

6. **No protective regulation needed.** The authors concluded that channel catfish need no size or bag limits. The main problem is overcrowding and poor growth, not declining populations.

7. **Research gaps.** The authors noted no comparable channel catfish growth study existed in other states. Thinning operations begun in 1953 had not yet been evaluated. Sex-specific growth remained unknown.
