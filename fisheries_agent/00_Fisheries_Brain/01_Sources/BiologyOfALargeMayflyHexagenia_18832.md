# Biology of a Large Mayfly, Hexagenia bilineata, of the Upper Mississippi River

#entomology #ephemeroptera #ephemeridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the biology of [[Hexagenia Bilineata]] and its congener [[Hexagenia Limbata]], large mayflies whose mass emergences along the [[Upper Mississippi River]] create nuisances, navigation hazards and possible allergies in river cities. Fieldwork in 1957 and 1958 centered on [[Keokuk]], Iowa, with cooperation from towboat crews, lock personnel and bridge staff. The work covers the subimago, imago, nymph and emergence stages, the mating behavior of males and females, the trematode parasites of the insects, and possible control measures. The author concludes that broad chemical control is not feasible, that nymphs are an important fish food, and that lighting modification is the most promising way to reduce nuisance in towns.

## Background & Description

**Species and nuisance.** Only two Hexagenia species were collected in 1957 and 1958. [[Hexagenia Bilineata]] was by far the more abundant and is the main problem species. [[Hexagenia Limbata]] becomes increasingly common northward from Keokuk. Local names for the adults include Mormonflies, fishflies, willowbugs, 24-hour bugs and "those big, black bastards," the phrase used by towboat crews.

**Life stages.**
- **Subimago (dun):** The newly emerged winged stage, which is peculiar to Ephemeroptera. It is somber gray with setae on the cerci and cloudy wings. It rests on trees during the day, and about 25 percent of its body weight is lost, mainly as water, before it molts.
- **Imago:** The adult is more delicate, with transparent wings, larger eyes, longer legs and no cerci setae. It does not feed. Males are much smaller than females.
- **Nymph:** Burrowing and filter-feeding larva. Last-instar nymphs are identified by black wing pads and dark lateral cerci.

**Mating.** Males swarm in the lee of trees, buildings or bluffs, holding fixed positions about 1 foot apart. Unlike [[Hexagenia Limbata]], males of *H. bilineata* do not perform repeated upward flights. Experiments showed that males respond to fast, deliberate movement through the swarm rather than to female identity, attacking thrown insects and even a paper decoy.

**Reproduction.** Each female carries two egg packets. Four females carried 8,576, 8,936, 4,252 and 6,664 eggs. Eggs sink and adhere to the substrate. Incubation time depends on water temperature (per [[Hunt 1953]] for the congener: 11–14 days at 75–95°F, 18 days at 67–81°F, and 20–26 days at 62–73°F).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Nymphs occur only where currents are slow enough for silt to accumulate. They were found at depths of 1 to 25 feet, mostly in soft mud, and were most abundant in impounded areas. [[Keokuk]] is less troubled than [[Fort Madison]], which lies entirely along silted, impounded water.

**Food web role.** Hoopes (1959, cited) found that Hexagenia nymphs made up over 50 percent of the diet of channel catfish, freshwater drum, mooneyes, goldeyes and white bass, and over 40 percent of the diet of paddlefish and white crappies. Nymphs are also eaten by [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]], bluegill, black crappie and black bullhead. Fish also take many emerging subimagoes, so mortality is high at emergence.

**Transport.** Towboats carry large numbers of subimagoes. A tow of 15 barges covers about 2.5 acres. At 8–10 miles per hour, subimagoes may be carried more than 350 miles downstream or 250 miles upstream before they molt. Even the usual 8–18 hour pre-molt period allows dispersal of more than 100 miles.

**Nuisance effects.** Drifts of dead insects under street lights impede traffic and clog sewers. Reported dispersal of adults is limited, with few flying more than 3 miles from the emergence site. Allergies from mayflies have been reported by Figley (1929, 1940) and Parlato (1938).

## Key Findings

**1. Emergence timing and waves**
- Analysis of 150 reported emergences from 1958 showed waves about 6–11 days apart, with the maximum in mid-July.
- Monthly breakdown of the 150 reports: 2 in May, 36 in June, 90 in July, 17 in August and none in September.
- Over half of emergences were reported between 1 and 4 a.m.
- A mid-July 1958 wave covered a 440-mile stretch. Thirty observations were made uniformly across that stretch during a 3-night period, with no day-to-day southward lead.

**2. Single-year life cycle at Keokuk**
- Bottom sampling showed no nymphs of any size on Aug. 28, 1957, and none above the Keokuk dam on Aug. 20, 1958.
- About 30 mature nymphs per square foot were present on July 9, 1958, most emerging on the night of July 14.
- Small nymphs were abundant again by Dec. 6, 1958, and March 15, 1959. The authors concluded that *H. bilineata* completes a generation in 1 year.

**3. Environmental cues**
- No correlation was found between emergence and air temperature, water temperature, water level or moon phase.
- One of the heaviest 1958 emergences at Keokuk occurred from June 20–22 when the maximum air temperature was only 74°F.

**4. Nymph behavior and emergence**
- In situ, nymphs on a raised guard gate (about 3 inches of mud) reached 344 nymphs in 10.5 square feet, nearly all last-instar.
- Nymphs rise to the surface and molt within minutes. Those that remain at the surface too long usually die or fail to molt.
- Subimagoes that stepped from their exuviae into water failed in all 8 recorded instances. About 20 observed subimagoes expelled amber, water-soluble fluid before flight.

**5. Parasites**
- Large numbers of trematode metacercariae occurred in nymphs, subimagoes and imagoes, mostly in tracheal gills and abdominal musculature.
- Last-instar nymphs carried up to 185 metacercariae, small nymphs (13 mm) averaged 3, and adults carried fewer after losses during molting.
- Hall (West Virginia University) identified them as papillose allocreadiids. [[Megalogonia Ictaluri]] and [[Crepidostomum Cooperi]] were suggested as likely species, with fish as definitive hosts and sphaeriid clams as first intermediate hosts.
- Feeding experiments on day-old chicks, ringed turtle doves and goldfish produced no intestinal flukes. Effects on fish hosts are unknown.

**6. Control recommendations**
- Water-level regulation is not feasible because navigation requires an 8-foot channel. Mayflies are also important fish food, so eliminating them could create more problems than it solves.
- Chemical control with wettable powder or granular insecticides may work in shallows within 3 miles of a city, but it must be repeated yearly because of rapid recolonization. Such treatment should not be used without thorough study because of effects on other organisms and possible danger to people and animals.
- Killing adults has little value because they die within 24 hours of emergence. Insecticide fogs would not affect the total population.
- The most promising approach is lighting modification: replacing white lights with red or yellow, and shielding riverside lamps with yellow or red plastic, which may draw city-bound flies away from the river.
