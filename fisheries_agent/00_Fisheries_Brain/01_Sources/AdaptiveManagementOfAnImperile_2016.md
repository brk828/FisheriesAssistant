# Adaptive Management of an Imperiled Catostomid in Lake Mohave, Lower Colorado River, USA

#ichthyology #catostomidae #razorback_sucker #endangered_species #adaptive_management

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**  
[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a long-lived catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America. Historically distributed throughout the mainstem Colorado River and medium to large tributaries, the species has experienced severe range contraction and abundance declines over the last half century. In [[Lake Mohave]], the wild population was estimated at 60,000 individuals in the late 1980s, but these fish were 25–45 years old based on otolith examination, representing cohorts produced in the first few years after reservoir impoundment in 1951. No recruitment had been detected for 25 years prior to the late 1980s. Maximum length is thought to be near 1 meter, though few individuals longer than 700 mm are now encountered. The species was federally listed as endangered in 1991. The wild population in Lake Mohave is now functionally extinct, with fewer than 50 individuals remaining by the mid-2000s.

**Habitat Associations**  
Razorback sucker historically occupied mainstem river habitats and large tributaries. In Lake Mohave, a man-made reservoir impounded by Davis Dam in 1951, the species utilizes both the lacustrine basin area and the riverine section downstream of Hoover Dam. Spawning aggregations occur at multiple sites including Halfway Wash, Tequila Cove, Yuma Cove, and Owl Point in the basin, and upstream of Willow Beach in the riverine reach. Larvae are phototactic and easily captured at night using lights. Adults remain close to the substrate. The reservoir environment includes both calm lotic waters in the basin and swiftly flowing turbulent waters in the riverine section.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**  
PIT scanning data from 2011–2012 revealed partial demographic isolation between basin and riverine subpopulations. More than 80% of fish contacted in the riverine reach downstream of Hoover Dam had been released there, and more than 80% of fish contacted in the basin had been released in the basin. In year-to-year comparisons, fewer than 10% of fish contacted in one reach were contacted in the other reach the subsequent year. This finding contradicted the original assumption that razorback sucker moved freely throughout the reach between Davis and Hoover dams. Razorback sucker are observed spawning in both basin and riverine habitats, with the riverine subpopulation reproducing primarily upstream of Willow Beach. Adults aggregate at spawning sites during winter-spring months (January through April, peak in March).

**Trophic & Community Interactions**  
Complete removal of larval production from the entire reservoir through predation, mainly by introduced centrarchid species ([[Largemouth Bass]] *Micropterus salmoides*, [[Bluegill]] *Lepomis macrochirus*), was identified as the primary cause of recruitment failure. [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) also posed predatory threats. A critical shift occurred in 1983 when heavy winter rains led to opening of the Hoover Dam spillway, introducing large numbers of [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) into Lake Mohave. This obligate piscivore became predominant by 1990 and had immediate and severe impacts. Striped bass grew rapidly to sizes up to 1200 mm TL and more than 30 kg. One acoustic tag implanted in a 520-mm TL razorback sucker was recovered from the stomach of a 13-kg striped bass, demonstrating that no size is completely safe from predation. The trout fishery downstream of Hoover Dam, considered one of the best in the United States in the 1980s, collapsed by the mid-1990s as trout were rarely seen more than a few weeks after stocking. Resource limitation was investigated as an alternative mortality factor but could not be clearly identified; larval razorback sucker survived and grew inside protective enclosures placed in the reservoir.

**Environmental Drivers**  
Reservoir water level fluctuations posed challenges to early propagation efforts. In March 1993, unexpected water level lowering in a backwater exposed and killed most fertilized ova that had settled on the bottom. Habitat alteration through dam construction and water extraction, combined with introduction and establishment of more than four dozen nonnative fish species, are attributed as primary drivers of species decline basin-wide. The reservoir environment created by Davis Dam in 1951 initially supported successful cohorts but subsequently precluded natural recruitment for decades.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**  
The Lake Mohave razorback sucker population declined precipitously from an estimated 60,000 wild adults in the late 1980s to fewer than 50 individuals by the mid-2000s; the wild population is now functionally extinct. A repatriation program initiated in the 1990s has produced a repatriate population that plateaued at approximately 1,500 individuals from 1999 through 2004, despite stocking an average of more than 12,000 fish annually from 1997 to 2005. More than 200,000 fish have been repatriated to Lake Mohave since program inception, yet the repatriate population has remained at only a few thousand fish. By 2014, population estimates based on PIT scanning revealed two subpopulations of approximately 1,500 fish each (basin and river), for a total of ~3,000 individuals. The 2007 population estimate dropped to near 1,000 adult repatriates. Recent stocking of small fish (2012–2013: more than 22,000 razorback sucker released, only 41 over 450 mm TL) has resulted in near-complete failure to recruit to the adult population, with only 169 contacted more than 30 days after release. In contrast, 806 fish longer than 450 mm TL released during 2009–2011 resulted in 417 contacted by PIT scanners from 2011 through 2015.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**  
Primary threats include predation by nonnative fishes, particularly striped bass, and the inability to achieve natural recruitment in the reservoir environment. First-year survival of stocked fish was less than 2% overall, with survival strongly dependent on size at release. Acoustic telemetry studies confirmed that survival varied dramatically from year to year and that even large fish (500+ mm TL) remained vulnerable to predation. The low population size (few thousand individuals) raises concerns about maintaining genetic diversity long term. The complete failure of recently stocked small fish to recruit has erased gains in population size achieved from large fish releases in 2009–2011. Without continued repatriation efforts, the species would have disappeared from the reservoir. The current population is maintained only through annual stocking of more than 10,000 razorback sucker, representing an unsustainable management approach.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**  
Management must focus on releasing razorback sucker at or near 500 mm TL to maximize post-stocking survival. The size-survival relationship derived from mark-recapture analysis (Figure 4 in source) demonstrates that larger fish have substantially higher first-year survival, though considerable uncertainty exists due to low overall survival and recapture rates. Proportional representation of the riverine subpopulation in larval collections requires approximately half of the 20,000 larvae collected annually to be collected upstream of Willow Beach, rather than concentrating collections more than 10 km downstream. The program should prioritize creation of lakeside backwaters on Lake Mohave, which have proven most successful to date; the Yuma Cove backwater maintains a healthy population of several hundred razorback sucker, including self-recruited individuals. Experiments in three lakeside backwaters are assessing genetic contribution across generations. The overall conservation strategy should emphasize natural recruitment within protected off-channel habitats free of nonnative fishes, while maintaining a genetically diverse population of adults in the mainstem and reservoirs, with exchange of adults among populations to maintain genetic diversity. This approach would eliminate the need for hatcheries and massive stockings while permitting nearly natural selective pressures. Immediate needs include optimizing stocking regimes rather than attempting to understand the complete life cycle, especially when most research requires experiments with life stages absent from the natural system.

**Biological Reference Points**  
Target stocking size: 500 mm TL (originally 250 mm, increased incrementally to 300 mm, 350 mm, 380 mm, then 450+ mm based on survival data). First-year survival for fish released at 300 mm TL: near zero in recent cohorts. Survival of 806 fish released at 450+ mm TL (2009–2011): 417 contacted (52% detection rate) over subsequent 5 years. Population estimate methodology shifted from single census mark-recapture during March spawning aggregations to year-to-year estimates based on PIT scanning supplemented by netting data, with separate estimates for basin and river subpopulations. Annual recapture rates during March Roundup: less than 10%. PIT scanning contact rates: 477 razorback sucker in 2010, 670 in riverine section in 2011, 1,832 in riverine section in 2012, exceeding netting-based population estimates. Target larval collection: 20,000 annually, with approximately 10,000 from upstream of Willow Beach to represent riverine subpopulation. Off-channel habitat target under LCR MSCP: 360 surface acres of backwater.
