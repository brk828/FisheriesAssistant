# Brain Patterns in Fishes of the Genus Hybopsis in Relation to Feeding Habits and Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation (Oklahoma State University, 1966, by Billy Joe Davis) compares brain morphology, cutaneous and internal taste bud distribution, and habitat preferences across 21 nominal species of the North American cyprinid genus [[Hybopsis]]. Using 394 specimens, the study uses brain lobe measurements, serial histology and taste bud counts to infer feeding modes and habitat for poorly known species. The central conclusion is that brain lobe proportions track dominant sensory modality: turbid-water fishes are taste-feeders with enlarged [[Facial Lobe]]s, while clear-water fishes are sight-feeders with large [[Optic Lobe]]s. Intrageneric brain patterns are judged useful for ecology but not reliable as stand-alone taxonomic characters.

## Background & Description

The genus Hybopsis, as recognized by Bailey (1952), absorbed the former genera Couesius, Erimystax, Macrhybopsis, Nocomis, Oregonichthys, Platygobio and Yuriria. Members are small to moderately large minnows (about 50 mm to 300 mm standard length), usually slender and little compressed, with teeth in the range 4.4 to 2.4-4.2 and often poorly developed grinding surfaces. Barbels are typically present at the maxillary terminus (absent in some populations of [[Hybopsis amblops]] and [[Hybopsis hypsinota]]).

Methods included:
- Sixteen brain measurements plus four body measurements per specimen, recorded to 0.01 mm with a Whipple disc on a dissecting microscope.
- Serial histological sections of heads, with taste buds counted in five standardized regions (snout tip, anterior and posterior nasal rosette, posterior retina, and anterior cerebellum).
- Barbel histology at 10 microns, stained with Harris' hematoxylin and eosin.
- Statistical analysis on an IBM computer, with total brain length as the independent variable and significance at the 1% level.

Materials came from museum gifts and loans and personal field collections. Institutions named include Oklahoma State University, Kansas University, Tulane University, University of Texas and University of Richmond. Specimens were examined from 394 total, with [[Hybopsis Monacha]] unavailable and [[Hybopsis Cahni]] represented by a single specimen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The genus spans habitats from low-gradient, silty Great Plains streams to swift, clear, gravelly streams of northern and eastern North America. Several species illustrate the range:
- **Hybopsis aestivalis** (speckled chub) occurs in mainstream rivers of the Mississippi and Rio Grande systems, with six nominal subspecies. Adults in Oklahoma were taken mostly over fine to coarse gravel mixed with sand.
- **Hybopsis amblops** (bigeye chub) is intolerant of silt; numbers declined rapidly with increased turbidity in Ohio and Illinois streams.
- **Hybopsis gelida** (sturgeon chub) and **Hybopsis meeki** (sicklefin chub) inhabit strong currents of large, silty rivers.
- **Hybopsis gracilis** (flathead chub) is often the dominant minnow in highly turbid streams.
- **Hybopsis storeriana** (silver chub) prefers deep pools with sand or fine gravel, moving to riffles when siltation is heavy.
- **Hybopsis cahni** has a very restricted range in the Clinch and Powell rivers of Tennessee, where it was found in clear, swift water less than 18 inches deep.
- **Hybopsis crameri** (Oregon chub) is the only west coast representative, restricted to the Willamette and Umpqua rivers, Oregon.

Turbidity data cited include the Arkansas River (1961–62, 25 to 5000 Jackson units at Coolidge, Kansas), the Mississippi River at Cape Girardeau (120 to 1840, mean 363.9 Jackson units) and the Willamette River (3 to 26, mean 11.5 Jackson units). Human pressures noted include siltation, reservoir construction and detergent pollution.

## Key Findings

**Sensory adaptation and brain pattern**
- Fishes in turbid water have many cutaneous taste buds, often with abundant internal taste buds, reduced optic lobes, flattened tori and enlarged facial lobes and [[Valvula Cerebelli]].
- Clear-water fishes locate food mainly by vision. Most sight-feeders have few taste buds, but subgenus Hybopsis members are "mouth tasters" with abundant pharyngeal taste buds and enlarged [[Vagal Lobe]]s.
- Dorsal views of vagal lobes do not accurately reflect internal taste bud development.
- Variability in brain lobes is greatest in fishes inhabiting the most variable habitats (e.g., *H. aestivalis* versus the constant-habitat *H. harperi*).

**Taste bud patterns**
- Taste bud numbers increase with fish size, indicating that new buds are added during growth.
- Compound taste buds on the first pectoral interradial membrane are present in most species and in turbid-water forms such as *H. aestivalis*, *H. gelida*, *H. gracilis*, *H. meeki* and *H. storeriana*.
- Barbels in *H. aestivalis* and *H. gelida* are judged functionally useful, whereas barbels in *H. amblops* and *H. harperi* are of limited use.

**Functional groupings**
The authors divided the genus into three diffuse groups:
1. **Sight feeders**: *Nocomis*, *Erimystax*, *Oregonichthys*, *Couesius*, possibly *H. labrosa* and clear-water *Extrarius*.
2. **Skin tasters**: *Platygobio*, *Macrhybopsis* and silty-water *Extrarius*.
3. **Mouth tasters**: all subgenus *Hybopsis* except *H. labrosa*.

**Taxonomic and competitive conclusions**
- Intrageneric brain patterns are most useful for identifying dominant sensory modalities, not as species-level taxonomic characters, because ecological convergence and ontogenetic change confound them.
- Six sympatric *Hybopsis* species in a short stretch of the Powell River, Tennessee, appear to avoid direct competition through microhabitat and feeding differences.

**Threats and conservation**
- Siltation, reservoirs and detergents threaten taste-feeding species. Bardach, Fujiya and Holl (1965) found that 0.5 ppm alkyl benzene sulfonate destroyed 50 per cent of taste buds in *Ictalurus natalis* over about 24 days, and 10 ppm did so in one day. The author warns that these detergent effects could drastically reduce populations of taste-dependent turbid-water species such as *H. meeki* and *H. gelida*.
