# The Habits of the Black-Nosed Dace, Rhinichthys atronasus (Mitchill)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae #minnow #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive study by Jay R. Traver examines the life history, feeding habits, reproductive behavior, and age determination of the [[Black-nosed Dace]] (*Rhinichthys atronasus*) in [[Cascadilla Creek]], Ithaca, New York, from September 1918 to August 1919. The research corrects previous misconceptions about nest-building behavior and provides detailed quantitative data on diet composition across age classes. The study documents 248 specimens examined for food content, breeding observations during May-June 1919, and attempts at age determination through multiple methods. Key findings include ontogenetic dietary shifts, broadcast spawning without nest construction, and parasitism rates.

## Background & Description

### Historical Taxonomy
First described by Mitchill (1815) from the Walkill River, N.Y., as *Cyprinus atronasus*. Subsequently redescribed by Rafinesque (1817) as *Cyprinus vittatus*. The genus *Rhinichthys* was established by Agassiz (1850). The species exhibits "excessive variability, running into several varieties, the extremes of which seem like distinct species" (Jordan and Evermann, 1896).

### Morphological Characteristics
- **Size**: Maximum length 3-3.5 inches (76-89 mm)
- **Coloration**: Prominent dark band running entire body length from caudal fin base through eye to snout; brownish-olive back; silvery belly; parallel lighter line above dark band
- **Sexual dimorphism**: Breeding males display orange fins and orange/red lateral band in late spring and early summer
- **Scales**: Very small, giving "sleek, glossy appearance" (Edwards, 1858)
- **Mouth**: Subterminal position
- **Pharyngeal teeth**: First noted by DeKay (1842)

### Geographic Range
"New England to Minnesota, Northern Alabama, and Virginia" (Jordan and Evermann, 1896). Extended range: "From New Brunswick and rivers of northeastern Quebec through Hudson and Great Lakes to James and Roanoke, to Dakotas in northwest, and through Ohio basin to Iowa and northern Alabama" (Forbes and Richardson, 1908). Also found in western North Carolina (Jordan, 1883).

### Behavioral Characteristics
Described as "swift and active in all its movements" with ability to escape capture "by burrowing under stones... or else by leaping several inches from the water" (Abbott, 1874). "If frightened, the fish will dart with lightning rapidity under stones, roots, or into other hiding places" (Eggeling and Ehrenberg, 1908). Makes "most desirable aquarium pet" that becomes "extremely tame" (Edwards, 1858).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Associations
[[Cascadilla Creek]] study site characterized by:
- Rapid-flowing water over rocks and pebbles
- High oxygen content, low carbon dioxide throughout year (Clemens, 1917)
- Water temperature range: 0°C minimum to 28°C maximum
- Overhanging grassy banks and rock ledges providing shelter
- Flat stones in streambed serving as hiding places

### Fish Community Composition
**In Cascadilla Creek** (warm-water association):
- [[Horned Dace]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*)
- [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*) young
- [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus*)
- [[Johnny Darter]] (*Boleosoma nigrum*)

**In Dwyer's Pond** (deeper, quieter water—black-nosed dace absent):
- [[White Sucker]], [[Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*), [[Rock Bass]] (*Ambloplites rupestris*), [[Cayuga Minnow]] (*Notropis bifrenatus*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Pike]] (*Esox lucius*)

**Alternative association** (cold-water streams):
- [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Sculpin]]

### Invertebrate Fauna
**Mayflies**: *Chirotenetes*, *Leptophlebia*, *Baetis*, *Heptagenia*
**Stoneflies**: *Perla*, *Perlinella*, *Capnia*, *Acroneuria*, *Taeniopteryx*
**Caddisflies**: *Hydropsyche*, *Goera*
**Diptera**: *Tipula*, *Chironomus*
**Coleoptera**: *Haliplus*, *Psephenus*, *Hydrophilus*
**Hemiptera**: *Coriza*
**Algae and diatoms**: Rich growth on rocks seasonally

### Parasitism
- Internal nematode worms found in 26 of 248 fish examined (10.5%)
- Maximum 8 worms per fish (37 mm specimen, Nov. 9, 1918)
- External encysted parasites on body surface, sometimes numerous enough to give "freckled appearance"
- No apparent harm to host fish; parasitized individuals appeared "uniformly fat and healthy" (Breder and Crawford, 1922)
- Fowler (1908) noted fish "often greatly parasitized with tapeworms, abdomen then being greatly swollen"

## Key Findings

### Food and Feeding Habits

#### Methodology
- 248 specimens examined from 20 collection dates (September 1918-August 1919)
- Alimentary canal contents analyzed quantitatively
- Percentages estimated for each food item (entire contents = unity)
- Identification to genus or species when possible
- Young fish defined as ≤33 mm (autumn) or ≤42 mm (spring); older fish exceed these limits

#### Young Fish Diet (191 specimens, first year)
- **Chironomid larvae**: 42% (principal food item)
- **Mayfly nymphs**: 15%
- **Total insect food**: 67%
- **Nematode worms**: 2%
- **Crustacea**: 1%
- **Total animal material**: 70%
- **Plant materials**: 23% (diatoms 18%, filamentous algae minor)
- **Debris**: 7%
- **Empty fish**: 9 specimens

**Feeding zones**: Very young dace (July-August) feed largely near surface; older first-year fish feed from all zones but increasingly from bottom.

#### Older Fish Diet (57 specimens, second year and beyond)
- **Mayfly nymphs**: 21% (principal insect food)
- **Chironomid larvae**: 19%
- **Total insect food**: 47%
- **Nematode worms**: 5%
- **Crustacea**: <0.5%
- **Total animal material**: 52%
- **Plant materials**: 36% (diatoms 29%, filamentous algae 5%)
- **Debris**: 12%
- **Empty fish**: 2 specimens

**Feeding zones**: Primarily middle and bottom zones, occasionally surface.

#### Seasonal Patterns
- **Cold weather** (January): Young fish empty; older fish fed sparingly, mainly on aquatic insect larvae from bottom
- **Summer**: Greatest variety of foods corresponding to peak abundance of stream organisms
- **Crustaceans and rotifers**: Small percentage in first summer/autumn, negligible thereafter

#### Food Preferences
- **Preferred items**: [[Chironomid Larvae]] and [[Mayfly Nymphs]] strongly preferred over other aquatic insects
- **Avoided items**: [[Stoneflies]] and [[Caddisflies]] "almost as abundant in stream as mayflies and presumably as easy for fish to catch, formed but very small percentage of food"
- **Terrestrial insects**: Small amounts taken (Hymenoptera: Ichneumon, Cynipid; Hemiptera: Jassidae, Aphididae)

#### Comparison with Previous Studies
Results generally similar to Breder and Crawford (1922) study of 257 specimens from Oxon Run, D.C., though present study shows:
- More detailed identification of insect taxa
- Separate analysis of young vs. older fish
- Greater emphasis on diatoms as significant food source (not "purely accidental")
- Evidence for food selectivity rather than indiscriminate feeding

### Reproductive Biology

#### Breeding Season and Site Selection
- **Peak spawning**: First week of June in Cascadilla Creek
- **Optimal time**: 11 a.m. to 1 p.m.
- **Water temperature**: 72°F (May 31)
- **Habitat**: Shallow riffles and shoals; water 5 inches deep at observed spawning site
- **Migration**: Adults leave deeper stream sections to spawn in shallows

#### Breeding Population Characteristics
- **Female size** (9 specimens): Average 55.5 mm (range 52-62 mm), average weight 2.8 gm
- **Male size** (19 specimens): Average 54.8 mm (range 47-63 mm), average weight 2.7 gm
- **Sexual dimorphism**: Females slightly larger (0.7 mm, 0.1 gm average difference)
- **Age at maturity**: Majority do not breed until second year; smallest breeding male 44 mm (March 20)
- **Male coloration**: Brilliantly colored during breeding season

#### Spawning Behavior
**Courtship**: Male swims back and forth beneath or in front of female 6-7 times as preliminary.

**Spawning act**:
1. Male presses head and fore body against female's side
2. Throws tail over female's body
3. Sets body into rapid vibration
4. Splashes with tail on opposite side of female
5. Male vent positioned above and to side of female vent
6. Female remains passive, body without vibration
7. Duration: 1-2 seconds
8. Both settle to bottom side by side to rest, often female on her side
9. Water becomes slightly cloudy (milt visible)
10. Repeated after ~1 minute, same location or upstream

**Mating system**: 
- Multiple males pursue single female
- Males sometimes contest for access to female
- Two males may take turns spawning with one female
- No evidence of two males spawning simultaneously with one female (contrast with [[Red-bellied Dace]])

**Male territorial behavior**:
- Some males guard specific locations in riffles
- Chase off intruding males
- Guarded sites not associated with nest excavation
- Males may abandon territory to pursue passing female
- Two males without females sometimes "consort together in peculiar fashion": smaller male presses head against larger male's pectoral region, caudal fins approach and vibrate

**Spawning site characteristics**:
- Eggs scattered broadcast over gravel
- No nest construction observed
- No covering of eggs with pebbles
- No unusual mounds or depressions created
- No fish observed carrying pebbles
- Spawning location not carefully selected

#### Eggs and Development
- **Egg characteristics**: 0.8 mm diameter, light amber tint, nearly transparent
- **Fecundity**: 428-1,116 eggs per female (6 females examined), average 746 eggs (possibly low estimate)
- **Egg deposition**: Scattered among gravel, collected by stirring substrate and netting downstream
- **Hatching**: Few hours in laboratory; 4-5 hours maximum survival initially
- **Incubation**: 36 eggs/fry placed in cold running water hatchery survived to June 24 with yolk sacs still present

#### Fry and Early Development
- **Size at hatching**: 5+ mm
- **Yolk-sac stage**: Extended in cold hatchery water (still present June 24 from June 6 eggs)
- **First feeding**: Fingerlings collected July 7 had no yolk sacs and had been feeding
- **July 7 fingerlings**: Average 17.5 mm length, 0.089 gm weight
- **Habitat**: Mainly over shoals near banks, swimming in schools near surface
- **Coloration**: Already showing distinguishing black line around body

#### Correction of Literature
**Previous accounts disputed**: Gregg (1879) and Holder (1883) described elaborate nest-building with pebble-carrying and egg-covering behavior. Present study found:
- No nest excavation
- No pebble-carrying
- No covering of eggs
- Broadcast spawning over riffles

**Probable species confusion**: Nest-building descriptions likely refer to [[Horned Dace]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*) per Reighard (1910), or possibly [[Campostoma anomalum]] or [[Hypopsis kentuckiensis]].

### Growth and Age Determination

#### First-Year Growth
- **Hatching**: 5+ mm
- **July 7** (1 month): 17.5 mm, 0.089 gm average
- **End of first year**: Average 36 mm, 0.7 gm (maximum 41 mm, 1.0 gm for immature individuals)

#### Length-Weight Curves
**November 9 collection** (38 fish): Three apparent age groups:
1. First year: 21-33 mm, 0.15-0.5 gm
2. Second year: 37-44 mm, 0.7-1.45 gm
3. Older: 52-59 mm, 2.4-3.5 gm

**May 29-June 2 collection** (51 fish): Two groups:
1. Immature: 29-41 mm, 0.35-1.0 gm
2. Breeding: 47-63 mm, 1.4-4.7 gm (no clear subdivision)

**August 19 collection** (19 fish): Possible three groups:
1. First year: definite
2. Second year: 40-47 mm, 1.1-1.6 gm (uncertain)
3. Older: 52-54 mm, 2.7-2.9 gm (uncertain)

#### Maximum Size
No specimens from Cascadilla Creek exceeded 63 mm length.

#### Age Determination Methods
**Scales**: Methods employed failed to show growth ridges, annuli, or winter marks. Radii present in both anterior and posterior fields. Author suggests methods of Creaser (1926) might be more successful.

**Vertebrae and otoliths**: Possible to determine age to end of second year, but not beyond.

**Supratemporal bone**: Small flat circular bone anterior to pectoral girdle showed broad light zones alternating with narrow darker zones (no ridges). One bone from 63 mm fish showed 4 broad and 3 narrow bands, possibly indicating fourth year. Requires further study.

**Conclusion**: Reliable age determination achieved only for first-year and partially for second-year fish. Age groups beyond second year and maximum lifespan remain undetermined.

### Economic and Ecological Significance

#### As Prey
- Affords food for [[Trout]] to some extent (Kendall, 1913)
- One [[Brown Trout]] stomach contained young sucker and black-nosed dace (40% of food, Greeley, 1926)
- Occupies warmer stream portions in summer where trout absent

#### Food Competition
Greeley (1926) demonstrated black-nosed dace "compete with trout for food" through stomach content analysis.

#### Management Implications
- Breder and Crawford suggested introduction into trout streams lacking native forage
- Greeley (1926) noted complexity: dace both serve as trout food and compete with trout for food resources

#### As Bait
"Makes attractive bait for salmon and trout" (Kendall and Goldsborough, 1908).

### Methodological Contributions

This study represents one of the first detailed quantitative analyses of minnow feeding ecology with:
- Large sample size (248 specimens)
- Systematic monthly sampling
- Separation of age classes
- Detailed prey identification
- Volumetric percentage estimation
- Seasonal pattern analysis

The reproductive observations provide the first accurate account of black-nosed dace spawning behavior, correcting 40+ years of erroneous literature based on species misidentification.
