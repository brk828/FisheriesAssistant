# Animal Populations: Self-Regulation, Crowding and Feedback

#population-ecology #animal-populations #daphnia

## Executive Summary

This Voice of America [[Forum Lectures|Forum Lecture]] (Biology Series, No. 3) by [[Edward S. Deevey]], Professor of Biology at Yale University, argues that animal populations are self-regulatory systems. Populations keep near a statistical constancy because their members perceive crowding and respond to it, a property the lecture calls [[Homeostasis]]. The argument is developed through laboratory models of [[Paramecium]], [[Daphnia]], flour beetles, fruit flies and blowflies, then extended to birds and mammals, where behavioural responses, territoriality and social status complicate the picture. The lecture ends by noting that the simple [[Logistic Model]] is incomplete for any animal with age, size or sex classes, yet still captures the essential truth that populations limit their own growth.

## Background & Description

The lecture is one of the 20 titles in the "Forum" Biology Series, which was broadcast in English to cover mid-century American science. The speaker's profile lists Deevey as born in Albany, New York, in 1914; a Yale graduate who taught at the Rice Institute and was a research associate at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution before returning to Yale in 1946. He became Director of the Geochronometric Laboratory in 1951, served as a former Vice President of the Society of Limnology and Oceanography, held Guggenheim and Fulbright fellowships, and edited the journal *Ecology* for eight years. His fields were limnology, oceanography and ecology.

The lecture defines a population by its statistical properties: birth rates, death rates and age distribution. Deevey frames the subject as abstract and relies on models, graphs and equations, which he acknowledges can mislead. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Constancy and the Balance of Nature.** Deevey presents population stability as belonging to "bodies in interaction," part of what is called the Balance of Nature. His own garden examples show near-constant counts of juncos at a feeding station, and a Japanese beetle population in which each female of one year is represented by about one surviving female the next year. He states that the product of the probability of death and the probability of giving birth, summed over a generation, averages exactly one.

**Paramecium and the logistic model.** A single Paramecium in a drop of pond water grows more slowly than pure doubling. The population saturates at about ten individuals, the upper limit in the model of the Belgian mathematician Verhulst. Growth is depressed in proportion to the nearness of this limit. Deevey suggests the signal may be waste products such as carbon dioxide, or more generally crowding.

**Crowding responses across taxa.** Female fruit flies lay fewer eggs when crowded. In flour beetles, crowding causes cannibalism of eggs and pupae, and planarian flatworms shrink under crowding. These direct feedbacks stabilise populations.

**Daphnia oscillations.** In baby-food jars at Slobodkin's laboratory at the University of Michigan, a single parthenogenetic female water-flea produces populations that peak after about 25 days, then decline, rise and wobble for months toward a statistical constancy. Deevey attributes this to internal friction: separated life stages, mixed age classes, and delayed responses to food-based crowding signals. The result is a damped oscillation, which he describes as "hunting," as in engineering.

**Violent and artificial cycles.** Nicholson's blowfly populations in Australia oscillate regularly, but the rhythm is forced by keeping adults and larvae in separate cages. Predator-prey oscillations in the laboratory are similarly artificial. Field cycles, including the 3- to 4-year cycle of field mice and arctic foxes and the 10-year lynx cycle tied to snowshoe hares, are either less regular than claimed or are partly shaped by fur-trading records.

**Birds and mammals.** For the alpine swift in Switzerland, successful clutches average two and three-quarters eggs. Four-egg nests have a 2-in-5 chance that all nestlings die. European robins lay about a tenth of an egg more for each degree of latitude, while maintaining population numbers across about thirty-five degrees of latitude. Deevey suggests that fewer winter survivors where nights are longer may explain this. Territoriality is proposed as a way of keeping interactions manageable.

**Social status and stress.** Experiments on hens, mice and rodent crowding show status-related effects: hens change their peck-order rank in different flocks, and dominant male mice damage their own fertility. Crowded mother mice desert, eat or resorb their litters. Work by Thompson and by Keeley links crowding-induced maternal anxiety to effects on unborn mice. Deevey notes these effects were seen mainly in overcrowded cages and may be rare in wild populations.

## Key Findings

- **Populations are self-regulatory.** Stability arises because members perceive and respond to crowding, not only from external limits set by the environment.
- **Logistic model.** The Verhulst model describes growth slowing as numbers approach a limit (about ten Paramecia in a drop). It is a useful first approximation but fails for animals with age, size or sex classes, including Daphnia and insects with different larval and adult stages.
- **Feedback types vary.** Feedback ranges from waste buildup (suggested for Paramecium), to direct cannibalism (flour beetles), to planarian self-consumption, to behavioural and hormonal responses in vertebrates.
- **Daphnia show damped oscillations.** Internal friction from delayed, age-dependent responses produces a damped oscillation. This is described as the only unambiguous example Deevey knows.
- **Resilience has practical consequences.** Reducing a population can increase its growth rate, and moderate harvesting may increase sustainable yield. Pest control can backfire, as rat culls are followed by more rats, so targeted methods (e.g., mothballs against clothes moth larvae) can work better.
- **Clutch size and latitude.** Clutch size in the alpine swift reflects a trade-off in nest survival, and robin egg numbers increase with latitude, possibly because of winter mortality.
- **Vertebrate complexity.** Learning, territoriality and individual personality make vertebrate populations harder to model than invertebrate ones.
- **Limits of evidence.** Deevey stresses that many stability mechanisms need several years to observe in the wild, are hard to census without killing animals, and that some dramatic findings come from artificially crowded laboratory setups.
