# Lake Powell Forage Fish Introduction Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources assessment (March 1990) evaluates options for restoring the [[Lake Powell]] sport fishery. The reservoir's only pelagic forage fish, [[Threadfin Shad]], collapsed after predation by an over-abundant [[Striped Bass]] population. Stunted predators, falling angler effort and an estimated minimum loss of $6.5 million in 1988 expenditures prompted the proposal. The preferred alternative is the introduction of [[Rainbow Smelt]], a deep-water, cold-tolerant planktivore intended to buffer shad, feed walleye and adult striped bass, and diversify forage. Five alternatives were compared against five selection standards, several other options were dismissed, and the proposal was reviewed by interstate and federal bodies with mixed opinions.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and need.** The action aims to enhance forage for all established game fish by adding a deep, cold-water planktivore. Striped bass reproduction was not expected to succeed when the species was stocked in 1974, yet it became established and produced an unlimited population. Striped bass declined in size from a mean of 620 mm (1979–1984) to 348 mm in 1988 and 355 mm in 1989.

**Fishery history.**
- Stocking began in 1963 with [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and kokanee salmon. Trout and kokanee programs were discontinued.
- Threadfin shad were introduced in 1968–1969 to provide forage.
- [[Walleye]] were never stocked; the population originated from fish present at impoundment and peaked in the early 1980s.
- Angler catch rates fell sharply. Largemouth bass dropped from 0.22 fish/h (1979) to 0.04 (1988); black crappie from 0.16 to 0.00.
- Catfish (channel catfish and yellow bullhead) provide a seasonal fishery but do not reach large size.
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] were introduced from 1982 onward, with many satellite populations and natural reproduction documented.

**Physical setting.** The reservoir, impounded by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the Colorado River within the Glen Canyon National Recreation Area, is about 300 km long, covers 160,000 surface acres and has a mean depth of 50.9 m. Shoreline is roughly 2,930 km, mostly sandstone cliffs and talus slopes. Annual visitation exceeds 3 million.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and plankton.** Lake Powell is an oligotrophic, warm monomictic canyon reservoir. Stratification begins in April and often persists into December. Surface temperatures range from 6.7 to 29.4 °C, and Secchi depth is typically 3–9 m. Chlorophyll-a averaged about 1.5 µg/L (range 0.5–5.0 µg/L) in 1987–88, with phosphorus limiting. Zooplankton densities of 3–26 per liter were recorded in the upper 40 m in 1987–88, with about 70% in the upper 10 m. Plankton abundance has changed little since the 1960s and remains available to pelagic fish.

**Threadfin shad dynamics.** Shad were sampled by midwater trawl from 1977. Peaks occurred in 1978, 1981 and 1984, suggesting a roughly three-year cycle, but each successive peak was smaller. Shad were absent from pelagic trawls in 1987 (in most areas), 1988 and 1989. Shad occupy the upper 10 m and were not found below the thermocline. Reproduction continues in turbid backs of canyons, where refuge limits predation.

**Habitat changes.** Brushy cover decline drove much of the decline in [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]]. Annual water fluctuation removes soil and woody vegetation, reducing nursery and escape cover. Flood years (1983–1984) produced high survival of young bass.

**Threatened and endangered fishes.** Colorado River "big river" fishes ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and Colorado squawfish) do not complete their life cycle in the reservoir. Water temperature is the main factor limiting these riverine natives, and smelt would be thermally separated from larval native fishes.

**Environmental components not affected.** Floodplains and wetlands, water quality, terrestrial wildlife, farmland, cultural resources, agriculture and residential concerns are not affected by the evaluated alternatives. The introduction is described as having no effect on those components.

**Socioeconomics.** Anglers' visitation grew about 17% per year from 1966 to 1985. Fishing pressure fell from 128,186 boat-days (1985) to 98,148 (1988), a 23% decline. The estimated net loss in 1988 was 107,156 boat-days, equivalent to 278,606 angler-days, or at least $6.5 million at $23.19 per angler-day. Local businesses that support anglers (bait, tackle, restaurants, motels) report losses.

**Lake Texoma comparison.** [[Lake Texoma]] supports a diverse forage base (threadfin and gizzard shad, inland silversides, drum, blacktail shiner) and a healthy, naturally reproducing striped bass population, with 30–50% of striped bass caught in 1983–1986 exceeding 500 mm.

## Key Findings

**Alternatives evaluated**

1. **[[Rainbow Smelt]] introduction (proposed action).** Selected as the best alternative after considering endangered species. Expected to occupy cool hypolimnetic water (7–12 °C summer) and provide a schooling forage fish available year-round to walleye and striped bass.
2. **[[Pygmy Smelt]] introduction.** An obligate planktivore that does not hunt fish, but smaller size makes it vulnerable to the large predator base. Rated less favorable than rainbow smelt.
3. **Multi-species forage complex.** Would mimic Lake Texoma or Santee-Cooper. Rated as exceeding standards for fisheries objectives but as having serious downstream and competitive risks.
4. **Commercial fishery.** Opposed on policy grounds (the [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]] enabling legislation forbids commercial fishing) and for practical reasons (nets would impact non-target and endangered fish, and striped bass were in poor condition).
5. **Regulation changes.** Remove or increase bag limits and prohibit live release. Considered unlikely to control population size; a limit of 15–20 fish was judged the practical maximum.
6. **No action.** Predicted continued decline, walleye failing to recover, and increased risk of illicit bait-bucket introductions.

**Selection standards and matrix (Table 2).** Five standards were used: management objectives, threatened and endangered species protection, technical feasibility, monetary constraints, and socioeconomic considerations. Rainbow smelt rated "+" for all five standards; multi-species complex rated "++" for fisheries objectives and socioeconomics; no action rated "--" for fisheries objectives.

**Dismissed alternatives**
- Increasing reservoir productivity through fertilization (Lake Mead experience showed increased juvenile striped bass).
- Stocking additional threadfin shad (infeasible given required annual numbers).
- Sterile (triploid) striped bass (ineffective given high fecundity and lakewide spawning).
- A selective piscicide ("stripercide") (none developed; rotenone would kill all species).
- Limiting spawning (netting or explosives infeasible).
- Introducing a predator (no suitable pelagic predator identified).
- Protected shad nurseries (impractical given reservoir geometry).

**Candidate species screened.** Of the species considered in Table 3, most were rejected for not being schooling pelagic fish, having broad habitat requirements, lacking establishment expectations, using the littoral zone, or growing too large. Rainbow smelt was selected from this list.

**Rainbow smelt expectations for Lake Powell**
- Adult smelt would spawn in February–March on talus shorelines, with most reproduction in the reservoir rather than tributaries.
- Silt bars and rapids would limit upstream movement; downstream migration is expected.
- Smelt are expected to avoid temperatures above 15 °C and would be partitioned from warm-water larval centrarchids and shad.
- Smelt would establish populations in Lake Mead and Lake Mohave, but probably not in Lake Havasu.
- Predation risk to larval fish and walleye recruitment was noted as a concern.

**Threatened and endangered species assessment.** Smelt would have minimal interaction with [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Smelt avoid the warm temperatures these species require, and larval suckers are protected by gravel burial. Valdez (1990) concluded that smelt posed no threat to endangered fishes in Cataract Canyon and Lake Powell.

**Economic benefits.** The authors state that any alternative failing to restore the fishery results in minimum direct losses of $6.5 million per year. No economic analysis of a smelt introduction was conducted.

**Consultation record**
- 1983: [[Colorado River Fish and Wildlife Council]] Striped Bass Committee began a forage search.
- 1984: Rainbow smelt proposed by South Dakota as possible forage.
- 1987: Council adopted stricter guidelines for exotic species introductions.
- 1988: Utah's first proposal drew majority opposition from voting members; the Technical Committee also opposed it.
- 1989: Revised proposal received majority support from the Technical Committee; Council deferred action pending public environmental assessment review.
- September 1989: FWS Region 6 recommended more attention to alternatives.
- November 1989: FWS Region 2 and Glen Canyon National Recreation Area staff opposed any fish introduction into the Colorado River system.

**Management recommendations**
- Adopt rainbow smelt introduction as the preferred alternative.
- Reopen the dam sanctuary area during April–May to target spawning striped bass.
- Liberalize striped bass limits to 20 fish daily in 1990 (as noted in the document).
- Continue histological pathogen screening; use fertilized eggs if parasites of concern are later found.
