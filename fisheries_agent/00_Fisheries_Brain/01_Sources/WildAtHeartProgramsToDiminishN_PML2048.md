# Wild at Heart: Programs to Diminish Negative Ecological and Evolutionary Effects of Conservation Hatcheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Acipenseridae #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This perspective synthesizes three [[Conservation Hatchery]] programs for imperiled North American freshwater fishes: [[Lake Sturgeon]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]]. Traditional hatchery culture often reduces the ecological performance, [[Genetic Diversity]] and phenotypic diversity of hatchery-origin fish relative to wild fish. The authors argue that programs allowing fish to complete part of their life cycle in natural habitats (for example, [[Natural Spawning]] followed by collection of wild-fertilized eggs or larvae for hatchery rearing and repatriation) can avoid these costs. Collections across the full spawning period and across appropriate geographic scales help retain genetic variation for heritable life-history traits. The authors conclude that similar strategies could benefit other imperiled fishes and provide an incentive to protect native habitat so populations can eventually become self-sustaining.

## Background & Description

Freshwater ecosystems hold a disproportionately high share of global fish species and habitats, yet they face losses from dams and impoundments, water extraction, riparian transformation and non-native fishes (Sala et al., 2001; Millennium Ecosystem Assessment, 2005). Hatcheries are widely used in [[Fish Conservation]] and recovery, both to boost population persistence and to supply restocking after extirpation. Traditional practices, however, can alter evolutionary trajectories and reduce [[Genetic Diversity]]. The documented negative effects include [[Domestication Selection]] of traits such as spawning timing and behavior, which can make hatchery-origin fish less fit than wild counterparts.

The three focal species differ in life history but share threats common to freshwater fishes:

- **[[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*)**, family Acipenseridae: a long-lived, iteroparous, riverine-spawning fish of the Great Lakes region, now listed as a threatened species in Michigan and a species of concern in the US and Canada.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**, family Catostomidae: a large (over 750 mm total length), long-lived fish (over 44 years; McCarthy and Minckley, 1987) endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **[[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*)**, family Cyprinidae: a pelagophilic, semi-buoyant egg-producing minnow of the Middle Rio Grande, listed as endangered in 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Sturgeon.** The species needs clean rock substrate in high-gradient river reaches for spawning, and it is sensitive to poor water quality, river obstruction and altered flow from dams. Maturity takes roughly 10–25 years depending on sex and location. Females are highly fecund (about 11,000 eggs/kg of body weight), and sticky eggs of about 2.5 mm adhere to substrate after spawning. Egg and larval mortality is extremely high because of predation and arrested development. Dams, climate change and flow variability have produced "[[Ecological and Evolutionary Traps]]", in which cues such as rising water temperature and falling discharge no longer reliably predict good spawning conditions. Historical abundance has fallen sharply, and natural recruitment remains below the levels needed to sustain populations. Captive broodstocks are impractical for this species because of its large body size, cost and time constraints, so [[Streamside Hatchery]] facilities using natal-stream water were developed. Strong spatial genetic structure among remnant Great Lakes populations suggests that adults return to natal streams.

**Razorback Sucker.** The species was once abundant in large rivers and floodplains across the Colorado basin. Altered flow regimes and non-native predators, which feed on larvae, juveniles and adults, caused sharp declines. After reservoir construction, populations followed a declining trajectory to local extirpation. Recruitment dwindled as non-native fish expanded into reservoirs and preyed on larvae. Remnant populations persist in mainstem reservoirs (Lake Mead, Lake Mohave and Lake Powell) and in the Colorado, Green and Yampa rivers.

**Rio Grande Silvery Minnow.** Dam construction in the early 1900s, followed by levees, diversions and flood-control structures in the 1950s, reduced instream flow and peak runoff. The authors estimate a 95% loss of instream flow and peak runoff relative to unregulated conditions. The species has been extirpated from more than 90% of its former range and now occupies a 280-km reach of the Rio Grande. Extirpation from the Pecos River was linked to the introduced congener [[Plains Minnow]] (*Hybognathus placitus*). Spawning is triggered by spring snowmelt and storm-driven discharge. Drifting eggs and larvae are dispersed downstream, and intermittent flow can decimate adult populations.

## Key Findings

### Lake Sturgeon

- Captured larval drift in the stream varied from 437 to 16,417 individuals between 2001 and 2010 (Duong et al., 2013).
- Stream-collected larvae had significantly higher genetic diversity (heterozygosity and allelic diversity) and lower coancestry (relatedness) than offspring from direct gamete takes or from naturally produced eggs collected from stream substrate (Crossman et al., 2011a).
- Survival and growth of larvae reared in streamside facilities were significantly higher than those reared in traditional hatcheries (Crossman et al., 2011a, 2011b).
- Body size and growth traits have relatively high heritability (h² up to about 0.42), and large genotype-by-environment interactions suggest that maintaining high diversity among each year's offspring matters.
- The authors recommend collecting eggs or larvae across the entire spawning period and collecting from many adults, because few breeders may contribute to a single year class in a given year.

### Razorback Sucker

- Genetic diversity was maintained for more than 20 years in razorback sucker supplementation programs despite ongoing threats to wild populations (Figure S1B; supplementary data).
- The program uses wild-caught adults, spawning in the hatchery and then releasing progeny at sites that allow natural rearing, followed by monitoring of survival and [[Repatriation]] outcomes.

### Rio Grande Silvery Minnow

- The supportive breeding program began in 2003 with about 922,000 fertilized eggs from natural spawning in the Middle Rio Grande.
- Annual egg collections ranged from zero (2008–2009) to more than 825,000 (2006), depending on breeding-population size and spring flows.
- Stocking at a site is suspended only if fewer than half of sites are occupied or average reach CPUE falls below 1.0 fish/m², allowing curtailment in years of strong natural recruitment.
- Fish are reared in captivity for less than six months and contribute to the next spring's breeding population. Captive spawning uses random selection of equal numbers of males and females to reduce [[Domestication Selection]].
- Females produce roughly 2,000 eggs at age 1 and about 10,000 at age 4 in captivity.
- Fish densities fell by 99.6% during a drought period. Approximately 2.8 million fish have been released since program inception.
- Genetic monitoring since 1999 shows that a 20-year time series maintains diversity despite repeated near-elimination of the species. Natural-origin samples collected after supplementation began had greater microsatellite and mitochondrial diversity than those collected before, indicating that supplementation buffers against losses of diversity after bottlenecks.

### Synthesis and Recommendations

- Traditional captive breeding, in which progeny remain in hatcheries from egg to release, has well-documented negative effects on wild populations, but hatchery rearing is often necessary to prevent extinction.
- Programs that let individuals complete part of the life cycle in native habitat can reduce domestication selection and other negative ecological and evolutionary outcomes while retaining the benefits of hatcheries.
- Constraints on adoption include cost, logistics, knowledge gaps, sensitive habitats (for example, Pahranagat chub), species with coincident broadcast spawning, species whose eggs or larvae cannot be located (for example, [[Paddlefish]]), and situations where threats to adult survival cannot be mitigated (for example, bonytail chub).
- Implementation requires broader stakeholder engagement than traditional hatcheries and should be accompanied by protection of native habitat so that populations can eventually become self-sustaining.
