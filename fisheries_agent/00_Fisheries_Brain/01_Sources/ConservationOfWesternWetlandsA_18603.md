# Conservation of Western Wetlands and Their Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #wetland-conservation

## Executive Summary

This keynote paper by E. P. Pister and W. C. Unkel, of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], reviews the steep loss of western wetlands and their associated fish faunas since World War II. California has lost more than 90 percent of its historic wetlands, and the greatest losses of fishes have occurred in the desert Southwest. The authors argue that population growth, water demand, recreational pressure, and low precipitation keep these habitats under threat. They describe conservation successes, including the [[Devils Hole Decision]] of 1976, the protection of [[Fish Slough]] and Owens Valley, and the state's [[Natural Areas Program]], while stressing that continued vigilance and public support are required.

## Background & Description

The paper was delivered as a keynote address on March 24, 1986. Its body was written by Pister, and the section on California wetland habitat conservation was prepared by Unkel, Coordinator of the Lands and Natural Areas Program. The paper's introduction presents the overall relationship between western wetlands and their fishes, then closes with a philosophical note that allows for optimism through conservation success stories.

The [[Desert Fishes Council]], formed in 1970, was the first organized effort to protect desert fishes and their habitats. It began with the Death Valley hydrographic area and expanded to desert ecosystems across North America.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drivers of loss.** Three factors combine to imperil southwestern aquatic ecosystems: (1) large human populations, (2) intense recreational pressure on desert and mountain aquatic resources, and (3) very low precipitation. Water supply is already fully allocated in Arizona, as shown by a recent groundwater protection law.

**Habitat and taxa.** Williams et al. (1985) list 182 North American desert fishes as warranting concern, including 46 endangered, 46 vulnerable, 46 rare, 20 indeterminate, and 24 possibly extinct. Fifty-two of these species are undescribed. Meffe estimates that about one-third of desert fish taxa are restricted to springs and cienegas. Nearly all of the fishes discussed belong to three families: [[Cyprinidae]], [[Cyprinodontidae]], and [[Poeciliidae]]. Eight poeciliid taxa are now extinct.

**Regional losses.** California has lost 31 percent of its wetland habitats statewide, with a 36 percent loss in the Central Valley and more than 70 percent of its marshlands. Of 20 fishes native to the California desert, 7 are extinct, 6 are endangered, and only 7 are not considered endangered or threatened. Major causes include habitat disruption from 20 dams on the Colorado River and the introduction of more than 50 competing species. Dams and agricultural and urban development in the Central Valley, combined with introduced species, extirpated the Thicktail Chub and eliminated the Sacramento Perch from most of its range.

**Case examples.**
- Leon Creek Pupfish (*Cyprinodon bovinus*): now confined to Leon Creek and Diamond Y Spring near Fort Stockton, Texas, and threatened by oil refinery pollution.
- Gila Trout (*Salmo gilae*): exists only in tributaries of the Gila and Verde rivers, and is threatened by hybridization with rainbow trout.
- Sonoran Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): once among the most common fishes of the Colorado River basin, it was listed as endangered in 1975 and now occurs in only 13 locations, 3 of them springs or cienegas.
- Virgin River fauna: Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and Virgin River Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta seminuda*) are endangered.
- Ash Meadows, Nevada: a center of plant and animal endemism whose fauna was decimated by similar impacts.
- Pahrump Poolfish (*Empetrichthys latos*): in the early 1970s the entire surviving population was in a small horse trough at the Desert National Wildlife Range, and a replacement habitat was created after the Manse Spring type locality was destroyed.

## Key Findings

- **Historic wetland loss:** Western wetlands have disappeared rapidly since World War II, and California has lost more than 90 percent of its historic wetlands.
- **Desert fish status:** Of 182 North American desert fishes of concern, 46 are endangered, and 52 are undescribed.
- **Devils Hole Decision (1976):** A unanimous U.S. Supreme Court ruling protected Devils Hole, a disjunct portion of Death Valley National Monument, and its endemic Devils Hole Pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*). The decision set a precedent that helped the Endangered Species Act provide an ethical and legal basis for preservation.
- **Fish Slough:** Saved from development by a Congressional action passed a few hours before Congress adjourned in December; it is the only remaining area of Owens Valley with pre-European pristine characteristics.
- **Owens Pupfish (*Cyprinodon radiosus*):** Came within a few hours of extinction in August 1969 and is now in public ownership under five agencies.
- **Dexter National Fish Hatchery:** Runs a program to rear endangered fishes for reintroduction into rehabilitated habitats.
- **Captive-rearing limits:** No Devils Hole pupfish has been reared to adulthood in an aquarium, and fish moved to an artificial habitat changed in appearance and morphometry, which underscores the importance of maintaining the natural habitat.
- **Natural Areas Program:** Under the California Department of Fish and Game, it protects places containing rare species and natural communities, using the [[California Natural Diversity Data Base]] (nearly 16,000 locations of over 2,000 rare taxa) to set priorities. Fifty-one rare aquatic communities are recorded.
- **Protection tools:** Land acquisition, Ecological Reserves, Wildlife Management Areas, conservation easements, cooperative management agreements, and private landowner programs with The Nature Conservancy.
- **Interagency coordination:** The Interagency Natural Areas Coordinating Committee (INACC), modeled on a Tennessee group, pools administrative, political, and technical support among agencies. It was formed under a memorandum of understanding but was primarily inactive at the time of writing.
- **Conclusion:** Conservation depends on sustained public perception of wetland values. Following Aldo Leopold's land ethic, the authors call for a shift from conqueror to plain member and citizen of the land community.
