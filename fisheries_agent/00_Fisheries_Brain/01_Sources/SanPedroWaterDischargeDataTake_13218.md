# San Pedro River Streamflow Records (Gila River Basin, Arizona)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrology

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled series of U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) surface-water station records from the Gila River Basin in Arizona, Water Years 1904–1978 for the principal station. It covers the San Pedro River at [[Charleston Gaging Station]] (Station 09471000), the San Pedro River near [[Redington Gaging Station]] (09472000), the San Pedro River near [[Tombstone Gaging Station]] (09471550), the San Pedro River at [[Winkelman Gaging Station]] (09473500), the San Pedro River near Winkelman (09473400), the Gila River at Winkelman and at [[Kelvin Gaging Station]] (09474000), Huachuca Canyon near Fort Huachuca, Peck Canyon Tributary, and the St. David Ditch. The content is hydrological rather than ichthyological: it contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic-habitat biological data, only discharge, gage-height, drainage, and diversion information.

## Background & Description

The records describe the San Pedro River, a tributary of the Gila River whose drainage is shared between Arizona and northern Mexico. Each station entry follows a standard USGS format, giving:

- **Location:** coordinates, legal land description, and position relative to nearby towns, dams, and tributaries.
- **Drainage area:** for Charleston, 1,219 sq mi, of which 696 sq mi lies in Mexico.
- **Gage:** water-stage recorder with concrete control; datum of 3,954.01 ft above mean sea level (datum of 1929, supplementary adjustment of 1958) at Charleston.
- **Records available:** discharge measurements and gage heights from 1904 onward, with gaps (notably 1906–1910 and 1912 onward for some sites).
- **Remarks:** diversions mainly by groundwater pumping for irrigation (about 1,600 acres in early records, rising to 3,600 acres in 1966 and later reduced to 2,900 acres in 1974 and 3,200 acres in 1978) above Charleston, plus an unknown but estimated share in Mexico.

Record quality is rated "good" at Charleston and "fair" or "poor" at other sites, usually where gage-height data are fragmentary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The records bear on the aquatic environment only through flow regime:

- **Intermittency:** Flow at Redington and Tombstone commonly ceases for many days each summer; minimum daily flows of 0 cfs are recorded at several sites, while Charleston rarely drops below 1 cfs.
- **Flood extremes:** The 1926 flood (about 98,000 cfs at Charleston, Sept. 28, 1926; about 90,000 cfs at Redington) is the period-of-record maximum.
- **Anthropogenic influence:** Groundwater pumping for irrigation and municipal use, and regulation of the Gila River by San Carlos Reservoir (since November 1928), alter downstream flows.
- **Drought effects:** Diversions at Charleston and Redington were reduced by an unknown amount in 1956 because of prevailing drought.

No fish, macroinvertebrate, or habitat data are included, so this document cannot be used to assess aquatic species presence or condition.

## Key Findings

- **Charleston, long-term average:** Average discharge for 40 to 45 years of records ranged from about 65.5 to 68 cfs during the 1950s and 1960s. The record average for 1904–1979 was about 60 cfs, and the median of yearly mean discharges was about 50–56 cfs (about 36,000–40,000 acre-ft/yr).
- **Charleston, notable floods:** 5,730 cfs on July 2 (gage height 7.4 ft) in an early 1950s water year; 23,600 cfs on Aug. 15 in water year 1953–54 (per the record); 14,400 cfs on Aug. 6, 1955; 8,590 cfs in 1952; 13,100 cfs on July 20, 1974; and 23,700 cfs on Oct. 9, 1977, the highest in the later record.
- **Charleston, minimum flows:** Minimum flows of 0.4 to 1.5 cfs were recorded in many years, with the most recent minimum of 0.4 cfs on June 7, 1974 and the period-of-record minimum since 1928 at 0.5 cfs (July 6, 1966; July 11, 1971).
- **Redington:** Average discharge over 1943–1970 was about 35–57 cfs. The maximum known discharge was about 90,000 cfs on Sept. 28, 1926, and the largest recorded peak was 28,600 cfs on Aug. 2, 1951 (gage height 18.0 ft, from the record). Diversions above the station were about 4,000 acres, rising to 9,700 acres by 1966.
- **Tombstone:** The station recorded a period-of-record maximum of 18,500 cfs on July 20, 1974 (gage height 10.43 ft), with an average of about 40–41 cfs over the 1970s.
- **Winkelman (San Pedro):** Average discharge was about 36–49 cfs in the 1960s–70s, with a maximum of about 15,000 cfs on Dec. 20, 1967 and 16,800 cfs during the Dec. 22–23, 1965 flood at a site 3.5 miles upstream.
- **Gila River at Winkelman and Kelvin:** The Gila River at Winkelman averaged about 185 cfs (adjusted for San Carlos Reservoir storage) over 1941–1959. At Kelvin, average discharge was about 463–465 cfs over 54–59 years. The maximum since completion of Coolidge Dam was 42,800 cfs on Aug. 8, 1930, and the period-of-record maximum was about 132,000 cfs on Jan. 20, 1916.
- **Diversions:** Groundwater pumping for irrigation was roughly 1,600 acres (early records), 2,000 acres (1955–1960s), 3,600 acres (1966), 2,900 acres (1974), and 3,200 acres (1978) above Charleston.
- **Data-quality caveat:** Many monthly and peak values are marked as estimated, partial, or based on related stations; the document is itself partly OCR-degraded.
