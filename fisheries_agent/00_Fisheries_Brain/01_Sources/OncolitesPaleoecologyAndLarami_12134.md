# Oncolites, Paleoecology, and Laramide Tectonics, Central Utah

#paleontology #sedimentology #gastropoda #goniobasis-tenera

## Executive Summary

This 1968 paper by Malcolm P. Weiss examines [[Oncolites]] (algal-accreted limestone concretions) in the Late Cretaceous to Paleocene [[North Horn Formation]] and the Paleocene to Eocene [[Flagstaff Formation]] of central Utah. The oncolites are shown to be homologous with modern freshwater and marine examples and to indicate shallow, nearshore lacustrine conditions. Their distribution follows positive tectonic lineaments associated with the [[Laramide Orogeny]], which the author uses to reconstruct a six-stage history of [[Flagstaff Lake]]. The most distinctive finding is that many Flagstaff oncolites are cored by empty shells of the freshwater snail [[Goniobasis Tenera]], which the author interprets as autochthonous and rapidly encrusted by algal mats.

## Background & Description

The study addresses limestone oncolites in the mixed lacustrine and fluvial North Horn Formation and the lacustrine Flagstaff Formation, which are locally abundant enough to form "oncolite conglomerates." Earlier workers, including [[Bradley 1929]], [[Eardley 1932]], and [[Loughlin 1920]], had described these bodies as algal balls, nodules, or concretionary limestone, and had linked comparable algal forms in the [[Green River Formation]] to shallow, active water. Weiss notes that the Green River beds contain reefy masses of *Chlorellopsis coloniata*, which are not known from the North Horn or Flagstaff.

Most oncolites are dark-yellowish-brown limestone (10YR 4/2). Over two-thirds of the several hundred specimens examined are subellipsoidal, subspherical, or subconical. Pebble sizes predominate in both formations. The largest Flagstaff specimen measures 90 mm in diameter. North Horn cobbles are more numerous, and some compound masses reach 30–50 cm, with the largest near 150 cm across and 30 cm thick. The author regards these giants as aggregates of stromatolites rather than true oncolites.

Original algal tissue is not preserved. The author interprets faint calcite-filled tubules as casts of algal filaments, and suggests that leaching and dissolution removed the organic material.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Depositional setting.** Oncolites occur preferentially in shore and nearshore facies, interstratified with redbeds, conglomerates, and sandstones near the southern Wasatch Mountains. Land snails support this interpretation. The tiny land snail [[Albertanella Minuta]] occurs mostly in low, transgressive Flagstaff beds and in nearshore beds near Thistle and Salina. The author estimates oncolite-forming water was less than 4 m deep, with enough agitation to turn the algal-sedimentary bodies repeatedly.

**Matrix and productivity.** Oncolite abundance correlates with low acid-resistant residue in the host rock. Rocks with 10–50 volume percent oncolites have residues averaging 8 weight percent. Mudstones and sandstones with less than 10 percent algal laminite have residues from 4 to 60 weight percent (average 24 percent). The volume of algal material is treated as a rough measure of local productivity.

**Growth and seasonality.** The paired laminae are interpreted as annual varves, with a dark, fine-grained layer and a light, granular layer. Laminae range from 0.1 to 2 mm, giving varves of roughly 0.2 to nearly 4 mm. Comparison with [[Minckley 1962]] in a Kentucky stream suggests that growth was faster in spring and early summer. The author proposes spring turbidity followed by clarification as a plausible explanation for the light-dependent growth pattern.

**Snail-shell cores.** Of 136 collected specimens, 132 are oncolites, and 92 (70 percent) are grown on snail shells. Of these snail cores, 96 percent are *Goniobasis tenera*, a gill-breathing freshwater species. Original shell material persists in 63 percent of the shells. Intraclasts of mud and laminite fragments filled the shells soon after the snails decomposed.

## Key Findings

- **Shallow-water origin.** Geographic, stratigraphic, petrologic, and paleontologic evidence supports a shallow, nearshore lacustrine origin for the North Horn and Flagstaff oncolites. Allochthonous oncolites are distinguishable from autochthonous ones.
- **Tectonic control.** Oncolite beds cluster along positive Laramide lineaments, including the [[Sevier Orogenic Belt]], the [[Sanpete-Sevier Valley Anticline]], and the Redmond Hills. They therefore serve as indicators of nearshore facies even where other indicators are absent.
- **Six-stage lake history.** Weiss proposes the following sequence, summarized in a synoptic table:
  1. Uplift of lineaments formed the Pavant-Valley and eastern basins of the younger North Horn lakes (early Paleocene?).
  2. Relative stability allowed upper North Horn fluviatile sediments to spread (mid-Paleocene?).
  3. Renewed uplift of the Sanpete-Sevier and Redmond Hills axes formed lower Flagstaff (Phase 1) lake basins (later mid-Paleocene).
  4. Increasing elevation of the Sevier belt formed a higher western barrier and a larger lake (late Paleocene).
  5. Infilling and quiescence reduced relief and nearly obliterated the lake by mid-Eocene.
  6. Locally, Flagstaff Lake persisted and merged with [[Green River Lake]].
- **Snail demographics.** The shell-whorl distribution suggests that small, young snails are scarce in oncolites, and that most shells are late adult or old-age *Goniobasis tenera* with 7–10 whorls. The few individuals with 11–16 whorls are interpreted as unusually long-lived.
- **Damage and transport.** Unconformities and multiple varve sets are more common in oncolites without shells (32% with one set, 45% with two) than in shell-cored oncolites (82% with one set, 14% with two). The author interprets shell-cored oncolites as mostly autochthonous and unbroken, and other oncolites as mostly allochthonous.
- **Modern analogue.** [[Minckley 1962]] documented *Goniobasis* enclosed in algal biscuits in Doe Run, Kentucky. Minckley sectioned 25 biscuits and found *Goniobasis* in seven. Weiss notes that the Kentucky biscuits are more porous and lighter because they lack lithifying cement, and that the stream setting differs from the lake setting of the fossil beds.
- **Taxonomic caution.** Wells's (1942) specimens, earlier described as *Zonotrichites* sp., are reinterpreted as pseudo-algal structures from the Flagstaff. Weiss advises against applying botanical nomenclature without preserved tissue.
