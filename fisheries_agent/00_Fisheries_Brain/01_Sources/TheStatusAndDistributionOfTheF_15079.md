# Status and Distribution of the Freshwater Fishes of Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1993 review by [[Swift et al. 1993 Fish Survey|Swift, Haglund, Ruiz and Fisher]] documents the status and distribution of native, estuarine and introduced fishes across southern California's Owens, Mohave, Colorado and coastal drainages. The authors recognize 38 native freshwater taxa and 23 estuarine species, and record at least 100 introduced species, a total the paper describes as exceeding every other area of California. Most native freshwater taxa are extirpated, endangered or severely reduced, and the authors argue that immediate habitat protection and restoration are needed to prevent further loss.

## Background & Description

The region's fauna is small and highly endemic. The authors attribute this to high topographic relief, long geological isolation and inland aridity. The landscape is marked by a sharp contrast between steep, rocky, youthful mountain streams and flat deserts or coastal plains, with little foothill habitat in between. The coastal drainages have a Mediterranean climate, while the inland deserts have shifted from wetter, lake-dominated conditions to today's arid climate.

Methods drew on museum collections (including [[Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County]]), agency files from the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] and [[U.S. Forest Service]], and the [[California Fish Data Base]]. Taxonomy follows [[Moyle and Williams 1990]] and conservation status follows multiple state and federal reviews. Introductions are defined as any human-mediated movement of fish, regardless of origin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat zones.** The authors describe four physiographically defined freshwater fish communities: upland (high gradient, dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]]/[[Steelhead]] and [[Speckled Dace]]), lowland (low gradient, historically with [[Arroyo Chub]] and [[Threespine Stickleback]]), anadromous (migratory routes for [[Pacific Lamprey]] and steelhead), and coastal lagoon (estuarine and freshwater species such as [[Tidewater Goby]]). Foothill communities are largely absent.

**Historical change.** Late Miocene to Pliocene drainage patterns shifted as the [[Pacific Plate]] subducted under the [[North American Plate]], producing today's mountains. Human population growth, flood control, water diversion, groundwater drawdown and urbanization have converted coastal lowlands toward desert-like hydrology. Lagoons were replaced by smaller, tidally maintained estuaries. The [[Post-1986 Drought]] further reduced habitat.

**Threats.** Key pressures include predation and competition from introduced species (e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]]), hybridization with introduced congeners, warm sewage effluent, channel concrete-lining, and brown trout predation. The authors also note that the lower Colorado River and Salton Sea have experienced recurrent introductions and tilapia expansion, which contributed to the decline of [[Desert Pupfish]].

## Key Findings

**Native and estuarine diversity**
- 38 native freshwater taxa (species, subspecies and geographic groups) and 23 brackish or estuarine species.
- At least 100 introduced species, including established populations of many bait, game and aquarium fishes.
- Only one estuarine species, [[Tidewater Goby]] (*Eucyclogobius newberryi*), is formally threatened. It was petitioned for federal threatened status. About 28 localities remained in the study area, with only fourteen south of Point Conception.

**Status of key native taxa**
- [[Owens Tui Chub]], [[Mohave Tui Chub]], [[Owens Pupfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]] are state and/or federally endangered.
- [[Arroyo Chub]] is common at a few localities but scarce overall; reduced populations warrant monitoring.
- [[Santa Ana Sucker]] and [[Santa Ana Speckled Dace]] are rare, and the latter may be extirpated from some streams.
- [[Pacific Brook Lamprey]] (*Lampetra cf. pacifica*) was last recorded in the Los Angeles and Santa Ana drainages in 1955 and is considered extirpated. A single adult was observed in March 1991 near the Santa Ana River.
- Several taxa are extirpated in California, including [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Woundfin]], [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Drought and recovery**
- The 1986–present drought reduced habitat and concentrated fish in refugia. Heavy rains in 1991–1992 altered abundances in some systems.
- Tidewater goby was re-established at [[Malibu Lagoon]] after 52 adults from the Ventura River were released in June 1991. Successful establishment was indicated by fish seen in August 1992.

**Conservation recommendations**
- Priority 1: protect remaining coastal and interior habitats still holding native fauna.
- Priority 2: restore or rehabilitate degraded lowland areas that link headwaters to the coast, including removal of barriers and improved flows.
- The authors propose 100 Aquatic Diversity Management Areas (ADMAs) for the study area, emphasizing sites where native fishes persist or can recover.
- Native fishes require protection from predatory and competitive introductions. The authors also caution against non-native genetic additions, which are generally detrimental to native stocks.

**Other notes**
- Native southern California trout populations are not taxonomically distinctive, and the [[Introduced Fishes Of California]] list is extensive, with a total of at least 100 non-native species.
