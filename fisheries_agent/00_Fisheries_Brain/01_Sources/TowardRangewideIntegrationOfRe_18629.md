# Toward Range-Wide Integration of Recovery Programs for the Big, Native Fishes of the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This draft policy document examines recovery and regulatory compliance programs for four endangered native fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Written in January 1996, it analyzes how recovery efforts have become compartmentalized into separate programs for the Upper Basin, San Juan River, Grand Canyon, and Lower Basin, largely due to regional jurisdictions of federal agencies (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Regions 2 and 6, Bureau of Reclamation regions). The authors argue that this fragmentation undermines effective range-wide recovery planning and call for re-invigorated, consolidated recovery goal-setting that would guide customized subbasin implementation programs while maintaining consistency across the species' ranges.

## Background & Description

Historically, these four native "big river fish" were abundant throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. By 1996, all four were listed as endangered and dangerously close to extinction. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] has been extirpated from the Lower Basin except for experimental re-introductions, with natural populations persisting in the Upper Basin's Yampa and Green rivers. The [[Bonytail Chub]] is the rarest, with virtually no recruitment and very few wild captures in the preceding decade. The [[Humpback Chub]] may be most numerous in the Lower Basin near the confluence of the Colorado and Little Colorado rivers in Grand Canyon, with Upper Basin populations in Black Rocks/Westwater Canyon, Dinosaur National Monument, and Cataract Canyon. The [[Razorback Sucker]] population consists mostly of older adults concentrated in Lower Basin mainstem reservoirs (especially Lake Mohave) and the lower Yampa and Green rivers.

Two major factors contributed to decline: (1) dam construction fragmenting habitat, blocking migrations, altering flows and temperatures, and flattening the hydrograph; and (2) introduction and proliferation of non-native fishes that compete with and prey upon native species, particularly young fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document details the legal and ecological framework governing recovery efforts under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). Critical habitat was finally designated in March 1994 for all four species, covering 1,980 river and reservoir miles and associated 100-year floodplains. The designation identified three essential "constituent elements": (1) water in sufficient quantity, quality, and hydrologic regime; (2) physical habitat for spawning, nursery, feeding, and rearing including bottomlands, side channels, and backwaters; and (3) biological environment including food supply and interactions with non-native species.

However, the critical habitat designation remained general, not prescribing specific instream flows or precise locations of essential features. Recovery plans for the squawfish (revised 1991) and two chubs (revised 1990) call for protecting "naturally self-sustaining populations" but lack specific habitat prescriptions. The razorback recovery plan was still in technical draft as of January 1996.

The ecosystem has been fundamentally altered by the federal dam and reservoir system. Flow regulation has eliminated natural flood events and spring peaks to which native fishes adapted over millennia. Reservoir releases often feature unnaturally high late-summer flows, dampened spring peaks, and hourly fluctuations that disrupt riverine food webs. Lower water temperatures from reservoir releases limit recruitment in some areas.

## Key Findings

**Upper Basin Recovery Program (initiated 1988):**
- Covers Upper Colorado River Basin excluding San Juan, coordinating recovery for squawfish and two chubs while managing razorback to avoid listing
- Major elements include: instream flow protection through state water rights, reoperation of federal projects, acquisition of water rights, floodplain habitat restoration, fish refugia and population augmentation, and non-native fish management
- Colorado Water Conservation Board filed innovative "modifiable, converse" instream flow water rights in December 1995, protecting baseflows while allowing sandwiched water development
- Section 7 Agreement (1993) provides regulatory certainty: projects depleting <3,000 acre-feet/year generally receive no-jeopardy opinions if program is making sufficient progress
- FY 1996 budget approximately $11 million from federal appropriations, hydropower fund, state contributions, and depletion charges
- Organization includes Implementation, Management, and three technical committees seeking consensus

**San Juan Recovery Program (formalized 1993):**
- Emerged from 1992 biological opinion on Animas-La Plata project requiring Navajo Reservoir reoperation to mimic natural flows
- Memorandum of Agreement among states (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah), tribes, and federal agencies, though Navajo Nation declined participation
- Seven-year research period (1994-2000) with annual funding ~$420,000 from BOR and FWS, far short of projected $786,000/year research costs
- Section 7 consultations require "sufficient progress" but with less regulatory certainty than Upper Basin; 3,000 acre-feet threshold for minor depletions over research period
- Biological opinions can be reopened if flow shortage issues unresolved, tribal water rights asserted, or governmental parties withdraw

**Lower Basin Programs:**
- Management Prospectus (February 1995) emphasizes hatchery propagation and recruitment in controlled, predator-free habitats rather than flow restoration
- Focuses on Lake Mohave stocks of razorback and bonytail, using grow-out facilities to rear fish to ~300mm before release
- Differs fundamentally from Upper Basin approach: assumes long-term intervention necessary, does not prioritize river reoperation or natural flow protection
- Multi-species Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP) under development via August 1995 Memorandum of Agreement among three Lower Basin states
- HCP planning area: Glen Canyon Dam to Mexican border, mainstem and 100-year floodplain, excluding Gila River tributaries
- Three-year planning process with $1.5 million/year state funding; 50-year implementation estimated at $185 million
- Interim Conservation Measures and "mileposts" to demonstrate sufficient progress and provide regulatory compliance during plan development
- Conservation groups (American Rivers, Environmental Defense Fund, Defenders of Wildlife) criticized HCP as prioritizing water development over species recovery, lacking adequate public involvement

**Grand Canyon:**
- Humpback chub population at Colorado/Little Colorado confluence identified as one of five primary recovery areas
- ~$9 million spent on research under Grand Canyon Environmental Studies program
- October 1993 biological opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations called for adaptive management but experimental high flows cancelled in 1994-1995
- Controversy continues over flow regimes needed to rejuvenate backwaters and enhance native fish habitat

**Major Outstanding Issues Identified:**
1. Habitat prescriptions remain too general; need specific, defensible flow and habitat requirements across species ranges
2. Extent to which federal project operations should be permanently changed for listed fishes
3. Control of non-native fish stocking and management of existing non-native populations
4. Appropriate level and location of artificial propagation and population augmentation
5. Long-term funding mechanisms for all programs
6. Organizational inclusiveness and workability
7. Definition of "recovery" - can it mean different things in different basins? Can populations be independently recovered and delisted?
8. Integration of water quality issues (addressed in San Juan, not Upper Basin program)
9. Whether managed exclusion of predators constitutes adequate "habitat" for recovery
10. How Lower Basin HCP will mesh with Section 7 requirements and approved recovery plans

**Critical Conclusion:**
Range-wide recovery planning has been neglected in favor of compartmentalized implementation programs. Regions 2 and 6 of FWS are asserting divergent recovery definitions without reconciliation. Updated, consolidated range-wide recovery plans are urgently needed as foundation for coordinating customized subbasin programs. Current approach risks overlap, inefficiency, and counter-productive competition between programs. The 1994 critical habitat designation failed to provide workable prescriptions, obscuring what constitutes "adverse modification" and confusing rationale for restocking efforts. Without prescribed habitat that can be protected and restored, artificial population augmentation will not lead to self-sustaining populations but only genetic banks requiring perpetual intervention.
