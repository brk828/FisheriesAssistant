# Aquatic and Biological Factors in Natural Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #radioecology

## Executive Summary

This 1960 conference paper by [[Louis A. Krumholz]] (University of Louisville) reviews how aquatic organisms take up, concentrate, and retain radionuclides from natural waters. Radioactive input comes from natural sources, [[Radioactive Fallout]] from weapons tests, and [[Radioactive Waste Disposal]] from hospitals, laboratories, and atomic energy facilities. The paper argues that biological uptake is governed by many interacting factors rather than by any single property, and that the problem requires coordinated research across several disciplines. Most of the evidence is drawn from phytoplankton, algae, invertebrates, and freshwater and marine fishes, with the cited studies largely based on [[White Oak Lake]] and the [[Columbia River]].

## Background & Description

- **Sources of radioactivity:** (1) natural radioactive materials in the crust, a constant source; (2) fallout from weapons tests, variable with political conditions; and (3) waste from peaceful uses of nuclear energy, which the paper expects to grow. The Joint Committee on Atomic Energy (1959) acknowledged that dilution by the environment will always be needed for low-level wastes.
- **Isotope behavior:** Radioactive and stable isotopes of an element behave chemically the same, and organisms cannot distinguish them. Organisms selectively accumulate elements used in metabolism and eliminate the rest, so radioisotopes of "used" elements concentrate in their usual sites.
- **Half-lives:** Loss occurs only through [[Physical Half-Life]] decay or through [[Biological Half-Life]], the average time atoms remain in body tissues. Potassium turns over quickly, while calcium, locked into bone, may remain for years. Biological hazard therefore depends on both measures.
- **Water types:** Natural waters are classed as [[Lentic Waters]] (lakes, ponds) and [[Lotic Waters]] (rivers, streams). Organisms range from vascular plants, algae, and [[Phytoplankton]] to bacteria, invertebrates, and vertebrates.
- **Natural radionuclides:** [[Natural Uranium]] (uranium-238 makes up more than 99 per cent), plus smaller amounts of uranium-235, uranium-234, and thorium-232 with their daughters. Their very long half-lives make them an almost constant source.
- **Fission products:** About 15 biologically important nuclides, including [[Strontium-90]], [[Cesium-137]], [[Zirconium-95]]/[[Niobium-95]], and [[Ruthenium-106]]. Activity shifts over time: at three years about 99 per cent comes from five nuclides, and at thirty years two supply about 96 to 97 per cent (the OCR text reads "968"). Cesium-137 (half-life 26.6 years) cannot accumulate after a single release but builds toward equilibrium under constant input.
- **Artificial activation products:** Sodium-24, [[Phosphorus-32]], scandium-46, chromium-51, manganese-54, iron-55 and iron-59, cobalt-60, copper-64, zinc-65, and arsenic-76. Most have short half-lives, but iron-55 (2.9 years) and cobalt-60 (5.2 years) persist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Chemical variability:** Sea water composition varies by more than two orders of magnitude for some elements but is broadly uniform. Fresh water varies enormously in basin shape, watershed fertility, pH (about 2.2 to 10.5), and total dissolved solids (less than 5 to more than 1,000 ppm). The physical and chemical state of an element controls its availability to biota, and the same is assumed for fresh water.
- **Uptake pathways:** (1) ingestion with water or food; (2) absorption through body surfaces by [[Ion Exchange]]; (3) physical [[Adsorption]] to body surfaces. The first two are biological and enter metabolism. Adsorption depends strongly on surface-to-volume ratio, surface texture, and bacterial films. Foster and Davis (1955) linked high activity in Columbia River plankton and sponges to their large surface areas.
- **Element interactions:** Uptake of one radioelement may be changed by stable elements of similar chemistry (substitution), by inhibition, or by synergism. Goldberg et al. (1951) found [[Asterionella japonica]] phosphate content depended on environmental phosphate.
- **Food webs and trophic levels:** Energy flows from primary producers to primary consumers (herbivores) to secondary consumers (carnivores). Biomass and production form a pyramid, and [[Food Web]] structure varies with place, season, and time. Radionuclides are transferred, not removed: they become unavailable to other organisms while the host lives and return to the environment at death, possibly in altered form.
- **Monitoring:** Krumholz and colleagues call for continued monitoring of many environmental media, and for accurate records of the nuclide composition of waste to interpret its effects.

## Key Findings

**Uptake and concentration**

- Uptake is generally proportional to the environmental concentration of the element. [[Oysters]] took up cesium in proportion to stable cesium in sea water (Chipman et al., 1954). Added strontium increased strontium uptake by [[Goldfish]] (Prosser et al., 1945).
- [[Phosphorus-32]] was studied more than any other non-fission nuclide. Whittaker (1953) reported algae concentrated the tracer to about 300,000 times water levels, with maximum accumulation reached in 16 days. Davis et al. found [[Phytoplankton]] factors of 100,000 to 200,000 in the Columbia River. Krumholz (1951) reported [[Spirogyra]] at about 650,000 times water levels in White Oak Lake.
- Rapid [[Phosphorus-32]] uptake by phytoplankton occurred in the first hours, followed by a shift to accumulation on aquarium walls after about 15 hours.

**Mechanisms in algae**

- Williams and Swanson (1958) found that [[Chlorella pyrenoides]] took up cesium-137 in proportion to cell numbers and linearly up to a concentration threshold. Dead cells kept high concentration factors, suggesting structural adsorption sites.
- Rice (1956) found yttrium-90 adsorbed to the surface of [[Certeria]] (as printed), while strontium-90 entered the cell body. Rice and Willis (1959) found particulate cerium-144 in [[Nitzschia closterium]] associated with cells faster than ionic cerium, through both adsorption and uptake.

**Higher plants and animals**

- Krumholz (1954) found dead [[Cattails]] (Typha latifolia) held 5 to 9 times more radioactivity than live parts. Dead tissue selectively adsorbed ruthenium, zirconium, and niobium. About half of the strontium accumulated in life was lost after death.
- Boroughs et al. (1958) found much of the yttrium in an equilibrium mixture with [[Artemia salina]] adsorbed onto glassware.
- Animal plankton, aquatic insect larvae, [[Mussels]], other invertebrates, fishes, waterfowl, and mammals selectively concentrate radionuclides from their food. Uptake in cold-blooded animals fluctuates seasonally with temperature-driven metabolism.
- A female [[Muskrat]] in an area of high radiostrontium accumulated one microcurie of strontium-90 per gram of bone throughout her skeleton (Krumholz and Ruste, 1952).
- Carnivorous fishes take up radioisotopes from their prey at [[White Oak Lake]] and the [[Columbia River]].

**Governing factors**

The paper lists nine interacting factors controlling radionuclide concentration: (1) organism elemental composition and metabolic requirements; (2) physical and chemical availability; (3) abundance relative to stable isotopes or similar elements; (4) physical half-life; (5) biological half-life; (6) inhibiting or enhancing elements; (7) environment type (fresh, brackish, or marine) and functional niche; (8) season; and (9) species composition of the biota. The author concludes the problem is complex and needs close cooperation among several disciplines.
