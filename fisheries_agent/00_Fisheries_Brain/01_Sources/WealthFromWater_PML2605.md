# Wealth from Water: Colorado River Basin Development

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This chapter, "Wealth from Water," argues that the undeveloped land, water, and minerals of the [[Colorado River Basin]] can become national wealth once they are put to use. It makes the case for basin-wide development to meet population growth, expand food production, generate hydroelectric power, control floods and silt, supply municipal and industrial water, and create recreation and fishing opportunities. The document is a policy and economic justification rather than a fisheries study. Fish and angling appear only as one benefit among many. The authors conclude that benefits to the West and the Nation would exceed costs, though the total construction cost and benefit figures are partly illegible in the scanned text.

## Background & Description

- **Population pressure:** In the 169 years since the United States became a nation, population grew from about 3 million to 135 million. The text cites a growth rate of nearly one million people per year, and says that existing frontiers are now closed.
- **Resource argument:** Large areas of desert land, unused river water (described as millions of acre-feet annually flowing to the Pacific), and buried minerals such as copper and coal are called "potential wealth" that becomes real only when developed.
- **Legal mandate:** The [[Boulder Canyon Project Act]] of 1928 directed the Secretary of the Interior to investigate feasibility of irrigation, power, and other projects for a comprehensive scheme of control and use of the river and its tributaries. The chapter presents potential developments as a response to that directive.
- **Scope of development:** Final project selection awaits detailed surveys, allocation of water among the Colorado River Basin states, and state preferences among alternatives. Some urgently needed projects could proceed earlier.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water is described as the most important single resource, because no living thing (plant or animal) can exist without it. The basin is in the arid West, where water is scarce, and the document notes that irrigation is essential to making desert land productive.

Key environmental themes:

- **Flooding and silt:** The river is described as a "natural menace" in its uncontrolled state. Silt from the [[San Juan River]] and [[Little Colorado River]] is a major threat to reservoir capacity, and the authors warn that [[Lake Mead]] capacity will decline unless silt is controlled.
- **Watershed management:** The text argues that natural silting can be combated only by proper watershed management plus a sufficient number of dams and tributary reservoirs.
- **Fish habitat in reservoirs:** Reservoirs with capacity below outlet structures could provide permanent fish habitat. They could be operated to control turbidity at high flows and maintain stream water for fish during dry seasons.
- **Erosion on national cropland:** The Department of Agriculture estimated (in 1937) that 61 percent of domestic cropland, about 263 million acres, was either subject to continued erosion or too poor to return a satisfactory income.

## Key Findings

### Irrigation and Food Production

- In 1939 there were 530 million acres available to grow crops in the United States, about four acres per person. About 45 million acres formerly grown for horse and mule forage were then producing food for people.
- Agricultural imports in 1937 were equivalent to the normal production of a very large acreage (the scanned figure is partly illegible).
- The national dietary goal would require substantially more protective foods. The text cites a potential rise of 20 percent in milk, 16 percent in butter, and 55 percent in eggs, and a 100 percent increase in leafy and yellow vegetables. Meeting this could require millions of additional acres of cropland.
- Western states have about half the national average of farm land per person and rely partly on imports.
- Potential irrigation projects could bring water to more than one million acres of dry land and supplement inadequate supplies on additional acres. The text estimates that new and expanded farms could support about 1,000,000 returning servicemen.
- Projected gross crop returns from new irrigated land are estimated at about $65 million per year, though the scan gives conflicting figures for this value.

### Hydroelectric Power

- The Colorado River falls more than 10,000 feet on its way to the ocean, providing some of the best power sites in the world.
- A proposed "stairway of reservoirs" would extend upstream from [[Parker Dam]] through Arizona, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming, and Colorado, with branches on the San Juan, Green, and Yampa rivers, totaling a continuous length of about 1,600 miles (figure partly garbled in the scan).
- The power developments could generate 19 billion kilowatt-hours of electricity per year, in addition to [[Boulder Dam]] output. The text estimates annual value at power markets in the tens of millions of dollars, and gives a benefit rate of 4 mills per kilowatt-hour.
- Arizona and Utah together produced 70 percent of US copper, but most was shipped out of the basin for electrolytic refining, because the region lacked low-cost power.

### Industrial and Defense Effects

- Cheap power from Boulder Dam helped Los Angeles rank first in US aircraft and oil-well machinery manufacturing.
- The Colorado River could supply power for a proposed $2 billion industrial expansion, providing employment for 550,000 workers and supporting about 2 million more people.
- Wartime industry on the Pacific Coast was built on hydroelectric power. The population of Los Angeles and San Diego counties grew by 600,000 (18 percent) from 1940 to 1944.
- Unemployment-insured workers in California, Oregon, and Washington rose by about 65 percent between 1940 and 1943.

### Flood and Silt Control

- Boulder Dam gives full control of the river at Black Canyon, ending the largest destructive floods on the lower river. Lower-magnitude floods still occur downstream.
- Proposed sediment-control projects include the Bluff project (San Juan River), Coconino project (Little Colorado River), Alamo project (Williams River), and Sentinel project (lower Gila River). Other projects, including Bridge Canyon and Marble Canyon-Kanab Creek, would provide little storage capacity.
- The text concludes that flood and silt control is economically justified and necessary to protect the national stake in the basin.

### Municipal and Industrial Water

- Municipal water is given priority over other uses, and the text notes that most parts of the basin can supply municipal water at nominal cost.
- Projects are outlined to supply Grand Junction (Colorado), Tucson (Arizona), and San Diego (California), along with expanded municipal and industrial supplies.
- Future municipal needs are expected to be met through minor adaptations of the basin plan. Community growth is expected to occur largely on irrigated or irrigable land.

### Recreation and Fishing

- Lake Mead, formed by Boulder Dam, is described as the "Eden of all bass fishermen" and is noted for its scenic beauty.
- Improved roads, power plants, and tunnel portals would open scenic and fishing areas to travel.
- Cooperation is required from the National Park Service, Fish and Wildlife Service, Forest Service, and state and local agencies.

### Economic Feasibility and Costs

- Total basin-wide construction cost cannot be estimated until a final project selection is made. The text gives an approximate figure for potential projects, though the scanned value is garbled.
- Estimated annual operation and maintenance cost is roughly $2 million (scan partly illegible).
- Repayment terms: irrigation costs are repaid over 40 years without interest; power costs carry 5 percent interest; municipal water costs are repaid over up to 40 years at up to 3 percent; flood control costs are largely nonreimbursable and financed by the federal government.
- The text estimates that about $25 million of construction cost could reasonably be allocated to flood control.
- The authors state that benefits to the West and the Nation would exceed costs of constructing all projects, even though some public benefits (such as fishing and recreation) cannot be included in cost allocation.

### Conclusions

The chapter concludes that the Colorado River Basin's water, power, minerals, and land should be developed under a comprehensive basin-wide plan. It argues that the resulting economic benefits, including agricultural production, industrial growth, flood protection, municipal supply, and recreation, justify the investment. Exact figures for several cost and benefit items are illegible in the scan and should be checked against the original publication.
