# Annual Reintroduction and Monitoring Report for Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), Gila River Basin, Arizona, 1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Xyrauchen-texanus #razorback-sucker #endangered-species #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1985 report by James E. Brooks (AGFD) documents the ongoing reintroduction of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) into the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona, part of a program begun in 1981. Despite stocking nearly **9.5 million** razorback sucker since program inception (over **3 million** in 1985 alone), recaptures remained extremely scarce. The report identifies **predation by introduced ictalurid catfishes** (channel and flathead catfish) as the dominant factor limiting reintroduction success, with an estimated **558 razorback sucker fingerlings consumed per hectare per 24-hour period** in a Gila River study reach, translating to roughly **50,000 stocked fish lost over two days** across a 35-km reach downstream to Safford. The report recommends stocking larger fish (≥250 mm TL), exploring winter stocking to reduce predator activity, and considering predator removal in discrete river reaches to improve survival of reintroduced razorback sucker.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), family [[Catostomidae]], a large-bodied catostomid endemic to the Colorado River Basin, notable for its pronounced dorsal keel.
- **Program history**: Reintroduction efforts began in 1981 as described by Johnson (1985) and Minckley (1983), aiming to reestablish razorback sucker in the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] and their tributaries following historic decline.
- **Rearing sources**:
  - **Dexter National Fish Hatchery (DNFH)**: primary source of fry and fingerlings for Gila and Salt River stockings.
  - **Page Springs Hatchery (PSH)**: reared fingerlings (from Dexter-produced fry) for Verde River stockings.
- **Stocking composition (1985)**: Of ~3,026,687 fish stocked, **86.5% were fry** (~8 mm TL), **13.4% young-of-year** (40–180 mm TL), and **<0.1% were 1+ year-olds** (300–380 mm TL) (Table 2).
- **Monitoring methods**: Seines, hoop nets, gill nets, trammel nets, and electrofishing (backpack, raft, boat) were used across mainstem rivers, tributaries, and downstream reservoirs (San Carlos, Roosevelt, Bartlett).
- **Predation/dispersal study design**: Conducted near Clifton, AZ, on the Gila River using hoop nets placed at set distances (0.5 km upstream; 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.5 km downstream) from a stocking site of 25,875 razorback sucker (90 mm TL ± 22) stocked 13–15 September 1985. Catfish population estimates used Schumaker-Eschmeyer mark-recapture methods (per Gerking 1953, Ricker 1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Gila River**: Shallow, wide, shifting channel; warm in summer, highest discharge in winter. Tributaries Bonita and Eagle creeks are more heterogeneous, less disturbed, flowing through canyon/trough habitats.
- **Salt River**: Drains White Mountains; flows over Apache Falls (a natural fish movement barrier) through Salt River Canyon; characterized by long deep runs, gravel riffles, and boulder habitat. Tributaries (Coon, Cherry, Canyon, Carrizo, Cedar creeks) are perennial, narrow-canyon streams draining the Mogollon Rim.
- **Verde River**: Headwaters in Big Chino Valley; flows through Perkinsville canyon into Camp Verde alluvial valley, then a long low-gradient valley to Horseshoe Lake. Lower reaches of Oak, West Clear, and Wet Beaver creeks are heavily modified by diversions, gravel pits, and development.
- **Introduced predator community**: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) are well-established, non-native piscivores throughout the study area and represent the primary predation threat to stocked razorback sucker.
- **Reservoir fish communities** (San Carlos, Roosevelt, Bartlett — sampled March 1985): Dominated by non-native species — largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) and carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) most abundant; **Sonora sucker** (*Catostomus insignis*) was the only native species collected; no razorback sucker detected.
- **Native fish community trends**: Native fishes were more abundant and diverse in tributary streams than in mainstem rivers. In the Verde River, native fishes still comprised **~38%** of collections despite numerous introduced species present.
- **Habitat degradation**: Present habitat conditions are degraded relative to historic conditions (per Minckley 1973, Brown 1982, Hendrickson and Minckley 1984), but the report concludes this is **less significant than predation** in determining reintroduction outcomes.

## Key Findings

### Stocking Summary (1985)
- **>3 million** razorback sucker stocked at **15 localities** across the Gila River drainage (Table 1).
- **86.5%** of total stocked fish were released as fry in the **Salt River drainage** (Table 2).
- Verde River stockings were smaller in number (45,089 total) and used older fingerlings (75–125 mm TL) reared at Page Springs Hatchery.

### Recapture & Growth
- Prior to 1985, only **two recaptures** of stocked razorback sucker had ever been recorded (Table 3).
- In 1985, several recaptures occurred, including two large adults from the Gila and Verde rivers.
- Growth was documented: subfingerlings **more than doubled in size**; Bonita Creek fingerlings grew ~25% larger by recapture.
- **Backwater growth disparity (Bonita Creek, Table 4)**: Razorback sucker grew best (mean TL 116 ± 21 mm, weight 16.7 ± 11.1 g) in the **upper backwater**, where other catostomids (e.g., *Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*) were absent from seine collections, versus poorer growth in backwaters where competing catostomids were present.

### Distribution & Native Fish Community
- Razorback sucker were collected via seining in the **Gila River** and **Bonita, Carrizo, and Cedar creeks** (Table 6); Gila River fish were captured within days of stocking, while tributary fish were recovered 2–3 months post-stocking.
- No razorback sucker were detected in mainstem electrofishing/netting surveys outside of the dispersal/predation study (Table 5).
- Salt River could not be surveyed via electrofishing/netting in 1985 due to equipment failure.
- Native species (*Gila* spp., *Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*, *Ptychocheilus lucius*) were notably more prevalent in Eagle Creek and Verde River than in the Gila River mainstem, which was dominated (92.8%) by stocked razorback sucker at time of sampling (Table 5).

### Dispersal Study (Gila River, September 1985)
- Following stocking of 25,875 razorback sucker (mean 90 mm TL ± 22), **dispersal was predominantly downstream**.
- Only **one fish** was captured in the upstream hoop net (0.5 km).
- Catch was initially high in the 0.5 km downstream net but declined rapidly as fish dispersed further downstream.
- Movement **increased at night**, with the 1.5 km downstream net showing elevated nighttime catches.
- Hoop net catches declined over the following two days; **no razorback sucker were caught during daylight hours** by 15 September, when flooding terminated the study (Fig. 2).

### Predation Study (Key Quantitative Result)
- Razorback sucker occurred in **46% of channel catfish stomachs** and **79% of flathead catfish stomachs** (Table 8).
- Despite lower occurrence frequency, **channel catfish consumed razorback sucker at a higher per-capita rate** than flathead catfish (Table 9).
- Predator size was strongly correlated with prey size consumed (**r² = 0.76**):
  - Channel catfish (216 ± 69 mm SL) consumed smaller razorback sucker.
  - Flathead catfish (287 ± 108 mm SL) consumed larger razorback sucker.
- **Population estimates** (Table 9): 281 channel catfish/ha and 41 flathead catfish/ha (>150 mm SL and >100 mm SL respectively) in the study reach.
- **Combined potential predation**: **558 razorback sucker fingerlings consumed per hectare per 24-hour period**.
- **Extrapolated impact**: Estimated **~50,000 stocked razorback sucker fingerlings consumed within two days** across the ~35 km reach from the study site downstream to Safford.
- Catfish stomach contents also contained red shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*), aquatic insects (notably Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Megaloptera in channel catfish), algae, and plant material — indicating **omnivorous diet supplementation** alongside piscivory (Table 8).

### Management Recommendations
- **Increase stocking size**: Recommend stocking no smaller than **250 mm TL** (requiring an additional year of hatchery rearing) to reduce vulnerability to predation.
- **Predator control**: Consider removal/reduction of introduced catfish via piscicide or mechanical means in discrete reaches (1–2 km) or larger stretches (e.g., ~90 km of Salt River Canyon between Apache Falls and the Roosevelt Lake diversion).
- **Winter stocking trials**: Proposed for 1986 in the Gila River to test whether colder temperatures reduce predator activity and downstream dispersal.
- **Raceway conditioning experiments**: Proposed comparisons of pond-reared vs. raceway-conditioned razorback sucker, [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] at DNFH to evaluate effects of hatchery rearing environment on post-stocking survival and dispersal behavior.
- **Upstream stocking site selection**: Suggested to partially offset well-documented downstream dispersal tendencies.
