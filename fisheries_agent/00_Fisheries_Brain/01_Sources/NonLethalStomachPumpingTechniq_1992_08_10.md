# Non-Lethal Stomach Pumping Technique to Recover Gut Contents of Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta*)

## Executive Summary

This 1992 methods study, submitted to the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Aquatic Coordination Team (ACT), evaluated a **non-lethal stomach pumping technique** for recovering gut contents from [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) as a **surrogate species** for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Forty adult roundtail chub were captured by electrofishing from the Colorado River near the Colorado/Utah state line — 20 as a stomach-pumped test group and 20 as an unpumped control group. Key results:

- The stomach pump was **100% efficient** at evacuating gut contents (via the anal vent, not the mouth).
- **No damage** to the pharyngeal teeth ("pharyngeal mill") was detected in pumped fish relative to controls.
- Fish were **not anesthetized** during pumping, and **no short-term ill effects** were observed.
- Long-term effects were **not assessed** (fish were not held for observation).
- The authors concluded the technique is **safe and recommended** for use on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in Grand Canyon, given the close phylogenetic and morphological similarity (gut configuration, pharyngeal mill) between the two *Gila* species.

## Background & Description

### Study Rationale
BIO/WEST, Inc. was contracted to assess food resource availability and use by [[Humpback Chub]] in Grand Canyon in relation to [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, particularly regarding [[Cladophora glomerata]]-based food webs previously documented as important to rainbow trout (Leibfried 1988). Because humpback chub are endangered, a **non-lethal** method was required to evaluate diet without population-level removal. Since sacrificing endangered fish was not permissible, the study used **roundtail chub** as a morphologically and phylogenetically similar surrogate.

### Study Objectives
1. Evaluate the **efficiency** of stomach flushing (percent of gut content recovered).
2. Determine whether the stomach pump tube causes **damage to the pharyngeal mill/teeth**.

### Chronology
The project traces back to a December 1990 ACT proposal request, followed by a pilot study on 8 adult humpback chub and 10 rainbow trout near the Little Colorado River (Jan 1991). Subsequent attempts to identify an appropriate surrogate species (bonytail at Dexter NFH, Utah chub, Dolores River roundtail chub, Elkhead/Kenny reservoirs) were unsuccessful or discouraged by USFWS, until roundtail chub were finally captured from the Colorado River near the Colorado/Utah stateline in August 1992.

### Methods
- **Capture**: Electrofishing (16-ft flatbottom aluminum boat, Coffelt VVP-15, 2 spherical anodes, 2 cable cathodes; 120V, 8A, 30% pulse width, 60 pulses/sec) — a system consistent with standard endangered-fish sampling protocols in the upper basin.
- **Design**: Stomach pump based on Gengerke's (1973) modification of Seaburg's (1957) design — flexible Tygon tubing connected to a hand-held rubber bulb (gasoline pump-style), with interchangeable tube sizes.
- **Procedure**: Clear outlet tube inserted into buccal cavity; water pumped through inlet tube into stomach; gut contents flushed out through the **anal vent** (not the mouth) into a collecting jar.
- **Groups**: 20 test fish (pumped, then euthanized with MS-222 and dissected to assess pharyngeal condition and residual stomach content); 20 control fish (handled identically but not pumped).
- **Dissection**: Longitudinal cranial cut + transverse cut to expose neurocranial cavity; otoliths (sagittae) and scales extracted for aging; pharyngeal teeth examined and photographed.
- **Measurements**: Volume of flushed food items (graduated cylinder) vs. volume of residual unflushed gut content; efficiency = % of food recovered by volume.
- Fish also examined for electrofishing-related injury (frozen, later X-rayed and filleted) as part of a coordinated study with Reclamation and the CSU Larval Fish Laboratory.

### Pharyngeal Tooth Biology (Background)
- Pharyngeal teeth are one of three tooth types in [[Osteichthyes]] (jaw teeth, mouth/palatine teeth, pharyngeal teeth) and are the **only teeth** present in [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows) and [[Catostomidae]] (suckers).
- Located on the fifth ceratobranchial (gill arch), forming the "pharyngeal arch"/"pharyngeal bone."
- New World cyprinids possess 1–3 rows of pharyngeal teeth per arch; typical *Gila* formula is **2,5-4,2** (left secondary, left primary – right primary, right secondary), consistent with Miller (1946).
- Bilateral asymmetry (more teeth on the left arch) is common among North American cyprinids (Eastman and Underhill 1973).
- Teeth are **deciduous/polyphyodont**: enamel organs are endodermally derived (unusual among vertebrates), forming from anlagen in the pharyngeal epithelium; teeth ankylose to the arch and are later resorbed/replaced via osteoclastic activity — meaning damaged teeth would be naturally replaced.
- *G. robusta* and *G. cypha* pharyngeal teeth are described as thick-based, pointed, hooked, with a weak grinding surface (Miller 1946; Jordan and Everman 1896). Observed tooth sizes in this study ranged **~1–7 mm**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Colorado River mainstem, upstream of Westwater Canyon near the Utah/Colorado state line — a large-river, native fish habitat within the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Ecological relevance**: Food habits data for [[Humpback Chub]] are used in conjunction with drift and benthic food-availability sampling to assess impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] flow regulation on food resources in the Grand Canyon reach. Diet shifts tied to flow changes can help distinguish movement/behavioral responses driven by food availability versus habitat change.
- **Comparative feeding ecology**: Rainbow trout below Glen Canyon Dam derive nutrition from lipid-rich diatoms epiphytic on *Cladophora glomerata* (Leibfried 1988); a key open question was whether humpback chub exploit a similar food resource, given that *Cladophora* productivity is closely tied to dam-regulated stream flow.
- **Gut morphology driving methodology**: Unlike salmonids, centrarchids, and esocids (which have convoluted guts and a pyloric sphincter enabling backpressure and mouth-flushing), *Gila* spp. have a relatively straight, S-shaped gut and **lack a pyloric sphincter**, causing flushed material to exit via the **anal vent** rather than the mouth — a previously untested flushing pathway.
- **Conservation context**: The surrogate-species approach was necessitated by the endangered status of [[Humpback Chub]], requiring validation on a related, non-listed species ([[Roundtail Chub]]) before applying the technique to the target species under a USFWS Amended Collecting Permit (June 11, 1992).

## Key Findings

### Efficiency of Stomach Pumping
- Of 20 pumped chub: 8 yielded measurable food, 8 yielded only trace algae/debris, 4 yielded no food.
- Flushed material consisted mostly of **insects**, sometimes mixed with algae.
- Gut contents were expelled via the **anal vent** with relatively **low water pressure**, suggesting minimal physical stress to the intestine.
- **100% efficiency**: no measurable food remained in any dissected gut following flushing.
- No sedation (MS-222) was required for the pumping procedure itself; MS-222 was used only for post-study euthanasia. This eliminates the need for anesthesia when applying the method to endangered [[Humpback Chub]].

### Pharyngeal Tooth Condition
- **No broken pharyngeal teeth** observed in pumped fish.
- Loose teeth (interpreted as natural shedding/ankylosis process, not pump-induced damage) were found in **9/20 (45%) test fish** vs. **7/20 (35%) control fish** — a comparable rate between groups (Table 3: 16/40 total = 40% with loose teeth; 24/40 = 60% with all teeth intact).
- No physical marks on the pump tubing indicated contact with the hooked pharyngeal teeth.
- Because pharyngeal teeth are naturally deciduous and continuously replaced, even hypothetical minor damage would not represent a long-term welfare concern.

### Conclusions & Recommendations
- The stomach pump method (Seaburg 1957 design, modified by Gengerke et al. 1973) is **efficient, safe, and non-lethal** for *Gila robusta*, and by extension recommended for *Gila cypha*.
- This is the **first documented case** of stomach pump content being evacuated via the **anal vent** rather than the buccal cavity, linked to the straight gut and absent pyloric sphincter in *Gila* spp.
- BIO/WEST recommended resuming stomach pumping of humpback chub in Grand Canyon starting **September 1992**, per USFWS Amended Collecting Permit (June 11, 1992), targeting up to 20 adult humpback chub (>250 mm TL) in each of September and November 1992 (40 total for the year).
- Given favorable results on roundtail chub as small as 220 mm TL, the method was deemed suitable for humpback chub **>250 mm TL**.

### Literature Context
- Prior stomach-pumping validation exists for salmonids, centrarchids, ictalurids, percids, and esocids (Meehan and Miller 1978; Swenson and Smith 1973; Seaburg and Moyle 1964), generally showing no significant mortality (e.g., Gengerke et al. 1973: 864 stomachs pumped, no mortality).
- Prior to this study, **no data existed** on stomach-pump-induced mortality or pharyngeal damage specifically in [[Cyprinidae]].
