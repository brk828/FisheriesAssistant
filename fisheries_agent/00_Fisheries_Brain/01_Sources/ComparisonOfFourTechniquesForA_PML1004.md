# Comparison of Four Techniques for Aging Adult Colorado Pikeminnow (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ptychocheilus

## Executive Summary

This study compares four structures for estimating the age of the federally endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): scales, [[Vertebral Centra]], [[Whole Otoliths]], and [[Sectioned Otoliths]]. Ages were estimated from 48 fish captured in the [[Green River Basin]] of Colorado and Utah. Vertebrae gave the most precise age estimates, with the strongest relationship to [[Total Length]] (R² = 0.7361). Sectioned otoliths ranked second and agreed closely with vertebral ages. Scale ages underestimated vertebral ages, and whole-otolith ages were the most variable and least related to length. The authors recommend vertebrae as the preferred structure for this species, particularly where lethal sampling is limited or where historic skeletal remains are studied.

## Background & Description

Fish age is usually determined by counting growth rings (annuli) in hard body structures. [[Scale Aging]] has long been the standard because scales are easy to collect, need little preparation, and do not require sacrificing the fish. Scale ages are, however, affected by calcium reabsorption and regeneration, false annuli, and misreading. Otoliths and vertebrae are harder and more permanent structures, and their ring deposition is annual in many species. Spines, rays, opercles, and cleithra have also been used.

The Colorado pikeminnow is a federally listed endangered fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It historically reached up to 1.8 m total length (TL) and potentially 50 years of age. Earlier age estimates from scales were validated only to about age 10. Aging older fish was hampered by missing annuli in young fish, indistinct annuli in older fish, and a lack of validation for older individuals. A comparison with recaptured fish also suggested that scales are unreliable for fish older than age 10 ([[Osmundson et al. 1997]]).

The authors hypothesized that using other structures, or a combination of methods, would improve age estimates. The goal was a better way to determine the ages and growth rates of this endangered species, and possibly other long-lived fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Status:** The species is federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]], which controls possession of the fish or its parts. This limited the number of specimens available.
- **Distribution:** The species is endemic to the Colorado River basin. Study fish came from the Green River basin, and the wild fish used for validation came from the upper Colorado River basin, where scale ages had been validated. Reference data also came from the [[Yampa River]].
- **Growth:** Age is closely related to TL in this species. Growth rates for larger, older fish are slow (cited as 10.2 to 11.9 mm/year, from [[Tyus 1988]] and Osmundson et al. 1997).
- **Conservation relevance:** The authors argue that age and growth data could help evaluate how environmental conditions affect the fish across time and space. Differential growth among river locations, or in habitats that have changed over time, could guide conservation.

## Key Findings

### Methods and sample

- **Fish sampled:** Ages were estimated from 48 Colorado pikeminnow. Half were released after capture; the rest were fresh specimens killed by sampling or frozen specimens collected by others.
- **Total length:** Mean TL was 481 mm (SD = 98.5; range 275 to 745 mm).
- **Structures obtained:** Scales were taken from all 48 fish. Otoliths (lapillus and sagitta; n = 55) and vertebral centra (n = 24) were retrieved from fish that died during sampling.
- **Reading protocol:** Scale annuli were counted by at least two readers, and the modal age was used. Whole otoliths were read by three readers using [[McCarthy and Minckley 1987]] methods. Sectioned otoliths followed [[Schultz and Taylor 1982]]. Readers were blind to fish size.
- **Uncertain ages:** Where the last annulus could not be distinguished, age was recorded as ">x" and then as "x+1."
- **Validation:** Otoliths from 13 hatchery-reared fish at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] of known age (ages 0 to 5) were compared with whole-otolith ages.
- **Analysis:** Linear regression of age against TL was used to rank structures by precision (coefficient of determination, R²). The [[Sign Test]] compared average ages between structures.

### Precision ranking (R² with TL)

| Structure | R² | n | Mean age (years) | SD | Age range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vertebral centra | 0.7361 | 24 | 7.5 | 4.70 | 2 to 13 |
| Sectioned otoliths | 0.6102 | 21 | 6.95 | 2.82 | 4 to 14 |
| Scales | 0.5848 | 48 | 7.02 | 2.37 | 3 to 13 |
| Whole otoliths | 0.329 | 34 | 7.76 | 2.75 | 4 to 16 |

### Main results

- **Vertebrae were most precise.** Vertebral ages had the highest R² with TL.
- **Vertebrae and sectioned otoliths agreed closely.** The two structures were strongly correlated (R² = 0.861, n = 21). Neither produced consistently larger ages (Sign Test, P < 0.05).
- **Scales underestimated age.** Scale ages were lower than vertebral ages, though they were still reasonably correlated with TL.
- **Whole otoliths were least reliable.** Whole-otolith ages were the most variable and least related to TL. Imprecision occurred mainly in larger, slow-growing fish, where closely spaced annuli were hard to read. The authors attribute this to difficulty reading closely spaced annuli rather than to missing growth information.
- **Otolith methods differed.** Whole and sectioned otolith ages differed by as much as 4 years. Their correlation was weak (R² = 0.399, n = 10).
- **Early growth detectable.** The first 5 years of growth were detected in otoliths of known-age hatchery fish.

### Practical recommendations

- **Vertebrae preferred:** Vertebrae are relatively large, sturdy, and useful for historic skeletal remains and preserved specimens. They are less costly than otolith preparation, which is time-consuming and tends to restrict sample size and inflate sampling error ([[Worthington et al. 1995]]).
- **Limits of vertebrae:** Vertebrae are not as delicate as scales or otoliths, and extracting them from skeletons may cause loss of material.
- **Further work:** The authors called for larger sample sizes and for using age and growth data across river reaches and habitats that have changed over time.

### Conclusions

Vertebral centra offer the most precise age estimates for adult Colorado pikeminnow. Sectioned otoliths are a close second. Scales and whole otoliths are less reliable, and scale ages may underestimate age in older fish.
