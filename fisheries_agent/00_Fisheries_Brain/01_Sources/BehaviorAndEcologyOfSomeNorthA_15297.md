# Behavior and Ecology of Some North American Cyprinid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by Rudolph J. Miller, published in *The American Midland Naturalist* (Vol. 72, No. 2, pp. 313–357), describes the nest-building, reproductive, territorial and social behavior of several stream cyprinids during the spring breeding seasons of 1959–1961. The core focus is [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus*), [[Rosyface Shiner]] (*Notropis rubellus*) and their hybrid, which co-occur with a high incidence of hybrids at Catatonk Creek, New York. The work compares ten behavioral characteristics among the parent species and the hybrid, and evaluates the environmental and social mechanisms that maintain species isolation or permit hybridization. Over 400 hours of observation and about 2000 ft of 16 mm film underpin the descriptions.

## Background & Description

The study builds on earlier accounts by [[Raney]] (1940a, 1940b) and [[Pfeiffer]] (1955). It centers on a single population at Catatonk Creek, a permanent tributary of the Susquehanna River at Candor, New York, where the three shiner forms and several [[Pebble Nest Builders]] (*Hybopsis micropogon*, *Semotilus* spp., *Exoglossum maxillingua*) co-occur. Supplementary observations came from Cascadilla Creek (Ithaca) and Buttermilk Creek (Cayuga Lake drainage, isolated above impassable falls).

Methods included weekly to biweekly sampling of the Catatonk population across the year, daily reproductive observations between 10 May and 10 July, tape recording, cine-filming, still photography and time-motion film analysis. Marking experiments used pelvic fin clips and cadmium sulfide injection.

The species group comprises:

- **Pit diggers / non-nest builders:** [[Common Shiner]], [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum pullum*), [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*, the territorial pit-user).
- **True nest builders (pebble mound constructors):** [[River Chub]] (*Hybopsis micropogon*), [[Fallfish]] (*Semotilus corporalis*), [[Cutlips Minnow]] (*Exoglossum maxillingua*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and temperature.** The three streams flow through gently rolling farmland with partly wooded banks. Catatonk Creek has a gravel and rubble bottom; water temperatures ranged from about 75 °F in midsummer to 32 °F in January–February, occasionally reaching 80 °F. Rainstorms, especially after spring tilling, strongly affected temperature and clarity; ten-degree drops in early spring were common. Species spawned between 58 and 75 °F from mid-April to July.

**Seasonal movement.** Fish moved out of fast water as winter approached and congregated in deep pools and backwaters. Large shiners and chubs often overwintered in the rivers, particularly in severe winters. Marking experiments showed that fish marked in autumn 1959 (143 *N. cornutus* marked, 42 recaptured between 15 November 1959 and 21 April 1960) often remained in the same or nearby areas, including backwaters, despite spring floods.

**Winter capture frequency at Catatonk Creek (decreasing order):** *N. cornutus*, *S. atromaculatus*, *S. corporalis*, *C. anomalum*, hybrid, *E. maxillingua*, *H. micropogon*, *N. rubellus*.

**Spawning sequence.** Spring return began in late March or April. [[Stoneroller]] initiated nest building first (mid-April, 55–60 °F), followed by *S. atromaculatus*, then *N. cornutus* territorial aggregates and *S. corporalis* nests in May, and *H. micropogon* nests in late May–early June. *N. rubellus* appeared late May and spawned from early June.

**Substrate.** All species used gravel of about 5–60 mm for nests or spawning beds. Current speed was the likely cause of minor differences in nest-site selection.

## Key Findings

**Hybopsis micropogon (River Chub)**
- Nests were built in water 9–30 inches deep, almost exclusively in raceways with moderate current near banks.
- A survey of a 200-yard stretch yielded 21 new nests between 30 May and 19 June 1961, with 27 nests measured in the 1961 depth table.
- Nest diameter fell from an average of 27.2 inches (first 20 nests) to 19.2 inches (last 7 nests), while height stayed similar (mostly 3–7 inches). The author attributed the decline to smaller builders, nest abandonment or a scarcity of unspawned females.
- Chubs were wary, and resident males tolerated accessory males with little aggression.

**Nest associates and social attraction (Table III, 56 carefully observed nests, 1959–1961)**
- *Hybopsis* was present at 14 nests and was alone at 6.
- *N. cornutus* was dominant at 41 of 56 nests.
- Hybrids alone occurred at one nest; *N. rubellus* alone occurred at 4.
- Shiners returned to nests after disturbance even without a working chub, indicating that the nest itself was the main attractant.

**Campostoma anomalum pullum (Stoneroller)**
- Spawns at 58–75 °F, mid-April to early June. Dominant males moved constantly between pits.
- Males lost to larger chubs and shiners in almost all encounters; the stonerollers were displaced even from their own pits.

**Semotilus atromaculatus (Creek Chub)**
- Forms a stone ridge that is defended against other males and egg predators.
- Larger males displaced smaller males; creek chubs were 1–3 inches larger than the shiners they competed with and dominated most nest sites.
- Parallel swimming ("deferred combat") between roughly equal males was described in detail. Parallel swims in crowded situations were shorter and faster than those in isolated situations.
- Overall, the chub was more successful than the stoneroller but less successful than *N. cornutus*, relative to size.

**Exoglossum and Semotilus corporalis**
- Nests of *Exoglossum* were usually under 15 inches in diameter; *S. corporalis* nests were over 18 inches long, with pebbles up to 3+ inches.
- Only one or two *S. corporalis* nests were found per season; spawning could not be observed directly.

**Notropis cornutus (Common Shiner)**
- Spawning temperatures were 62–78 °F at Candor. Buttermilk Creek fish spawned at 58 °F, a week earlier than elsewhere, and were smaller and more hyperactive, suggesting a racially divergent population.
- Frontal attacks and parallel swims are the core agonistic behaviors. Size is the principal factor in territorial encounters.
- The tilt display by males is the key recognition signal for females. The clasp was described as present only in *N. cornutus* among the parents.
- A 1-day massed aggregate at Cascadilla Creek (24 May–13 June 1959, 18 days of activity) contained 40–100 males. Spawning peaked on 30 May at 74 °F.
- Dominant-male associations at Catatonk Creek: dominant males spawned more often than peripheral ones. In one 5-minute comparison (5 June 1960), the dominant male spawned 8 times and gave 36 tilt displays, while the peripheral male spawned 0 times and gave 4 tilts.
- Laboratory tests: removing food from conditioned fish induced territorial aggression without courtship or spawning, indicating partial independence of agonistic and reproductive systems.

**Notropis rubellus (Rosyface Shiner)**
- Spawned only at temperatures above 68–70 °F; in 1961 the temperature never exceeded 68 °F, so spawning probably did not occur in the study area that year.
- Spawning schools (10–20 adults) occurred over *Hybopsis* nests in moderate current and lacked fixed bottom territories or a clasp.
- Males engaged in close, continuous head and body contact during parallel swims.

**Hybrid (N. cornutus x N. rubellus)**
- Hybrids were captured with *N. cornutus* in 14 of 14 collections (8 Nov 1959–21 May 1960). They were almost never found without *N. cornutus*.
- Of ten behavioral characteristics compared, the hybrid resembled *N. cornutus* in six and *N. rubellus* in three (Table V).
- Hybrids showed no true territoriality, but they defended nest pits for short periods. The tilt display was seen once, and the clasp was not seen.

**Interspecific encounters and reproductive isolation**
- Physical environment: differing spawning temperatures (for example, *Campostoma* versus *N. rubellus*) and habitat specialization (for example, *Exoglossum* and fast-water nest builders) reinforce isolation.
- Social environment: female *N. cornutus* select mates through the tilt display, and males apparently respond to less specific stimuli.
- Hybridization mechanism: the author concluded that most hybrids arise accidentally. A dam created a dense concentration of pebble nests and spawning fish downstream. Eggs of *N. cornutus* remained fertilizable for at least two minutes after stripping (100% fertilization and hatching in 2 July 1960 trials). Parental species alternately spawning in the same anterior nest pit was judged the main cause.
- Experimental crosses (*N. cornutus* male x *S. atromaculatus* female, 1961): about half the embryos survived to maturity, although some were deformed.

**Relevance**
- The paper offers a detailed behavioral baseline for cyprinid reproduction and interspecific competition, and identifies [[Creek Chub]] as a promising model for quantitative field and laboratory behavioral analysis.
