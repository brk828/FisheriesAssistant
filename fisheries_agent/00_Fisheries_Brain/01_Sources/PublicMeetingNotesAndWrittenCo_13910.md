# Public Meeting Notes and Written Comments on Final Biological and Conference Opinion on Lower Colorado River Operations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #ConservationPolicy

## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary research paper but a compilation of **public meeting notes and stakeholder comments** responding to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's (FWS) Final Biological and Conference Opinion (BO) on Lower Colorado River (LCR) Operations and Maintenance, issued to the Bureau of Reclamation (BOR) on April 30, 1997. The BO addresses management of the LCR from Lake Mead to the Southerly International Boundary and includes a Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) and Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs) intended to avoid jeopardy to listed species, principally the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Chub]], as well as the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]. Public meetings were held in Las Vegas, Phoenix, and Yuma (June 16–18, 1997), followed by a written comment period closing July 29, 1997. The notes reveal significant scientific, procedural, and management uncertainty regarding native fish recovery, non-native species interactions, critical habitat designation, and the efficacy of proposed conservation actions.

## Background & Description

No direct morphological data (e.g., meristics, body measurements) are presented in this document, as it is a regulatory/administrative record rather than a taxonomic or anatomical study. However, the species under discussion belong to well-defined groups:
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — family Catostomidae, named for its distinctive dorsal keel.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) — family Cyprinidae, a large-river minnow historically abundant in the mainstem LCR.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) — family Cyprinidae, closely related to bonytail; hybridization concerns are noted between the two taxa in Lake Mead reintroduction efforts.

Comments raise concern over a proposed **bonytail reintroduction into Lake Mead**, citing risk of genetic introgression/hybridization with resident [[Humpback Chub]] populations — an important consideration for taxonomic integrity of reintroduced stocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Backwater habitats**: Identified in the BO as critical to endangered native fish; commenters questioned the strength of the evidence linking backwater habitat quality to main-channel population support, while others argued the report itself emphasized backwaters' importance to the main channel food web and nursery function.
- **Impoundments/offstream habitats**: Proposed as habitat interventions (RPA #3) for native fish recovery; public commenters supported the "offstream impoundment concept" as a management tool, though acreage-setting methodology was questioned.
- **Predation pressure**: Native fish declines were attributed partly to **non-native fish predation** on eggs and juveniles, and **non-native bird predation** on fish in shallow backwaters — raising concern that current RPAs inadequately address multi-trophic pressures.
- **Flow regime and habitat naturalness**: Concerns were raised that the LCR is no longer a "wild river," making restoration to historic natural flow/habitat conditions unrealistic; a documented decline in fish abundance from the 1930s to present was linked to reduced flows, suggesting flow augmentation as a potential (but logistically difficult) recovery lever.
- **Critical habitat questions**: Debate centered on designating critical habitat in reaches with little or no flow (e.g., lower Gila River) and on the Limitrophe Division of the river, raising questions about the ecological validity of critical habitat boundaries in intermittently dry or binational reaches.
- **Cross-species conflict**: Concerns about simultaneous management for the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (a riparian obligate) and native fish species highlighted competing habitat needs (e.g., cattail marsh creation timelines, nest-driven access restrictions) within the same riverine/riparian ecosystem.

## Key Findings

- The BO's RPA and RPM provisions apply to native fish recovery **without an underlying finalized FWS Recovery Plan** for razorback sucker, bonytail chub, and other LCR fishes at the time of the BO's issuance — a point of significant stakeholder concern regarding the scientific basis for prescribed actions.
- Stakeholders questioned the exclusion of the [[Humpback Chub]] from the BO's scope, suggesting its range may overlap the study area (Lake Mead), warranting reconsideration.
- The **Mohave Recovery Program** was cited as incorporated into the proposed action, though its actual conservation benefit to native fish populations was disputed by commenters.
- A recurring theme was the distinction between **maintaining existing fish populations vs. achieving natural recruitment** — commenters argued the RPA language (RPM 1) did not adequately allow for graduated management responses based on demonstrated impact.
- RPA Provision #10 (research on recruitment) drew criticism for potentially duplicating existing literature rather than funding new studies; commenters urged a literature review before committing further funds.
- Use of **surrogate species** to estimate "take" exceedance was challenged as non-representative of actual listed species responses, except in the specific context of fish entrainment at power plant intakes.
- Suggestions were made to use hatchery-reared **razorback sucker and bonytail fry** (e.g., from Mohave Generating Station-associated stock) for restocking efforts below Parker Dam, and even provocatively to use these federally listed species "as bait fish," reflecting skepticism among some stakeholders about the value of recovery efforts.
- Questions were raised about whether **razorback sucker and bonytail chub populations persist below Parker Dam**, indicating uncertainty in current distributional data supporting the BO's management actions.
- Concerns were raised regarding inconsistent reporting of **parasitism rates** across the BO document, indicating potential internal data-quality/consistency issues within the Biological Opinion itself.
- The overall discussion underscores tension between single-species (or dual-species: fish + flycatcher) regulatory compliance under the Endangered Species Act and the recognized complexity of managing a highly altered, multi-stakeholder river system (the [[Lower Colorado River]]) for ecological recovery.
