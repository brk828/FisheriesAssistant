# Bonytail (Gila elegans) in the Lower Colorado River Basin: Distribution and Management Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[C.O. Minckley]] and [[M.S. Thorson]] of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Arizona Fishery Resources Office summarizes roughly three decades of work on the [[Bonytail]], an endemic cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species has declined sharply since the 1930s. Wild individuals may persist only in [[Lake Mohave]]. Predation by introduced fishes, especially on small stocked fish, is considered the main cause of poor recruitment. Management has centered on [[Broodstock Development]], stocking of hatchery fish, [[Grow-Out Ponds]], and [[Native Fish Habitat]] development. Stocking of larger, [[PIT Tagging|PIT-tagged]] fish into [[Lake Havasu]] has produced recaptures, which the authors see as encouraging. A multi-agency [[Management Plan]] for [[Big-River Fishes]] has been completed.

## Background & Description

The bonytail is one of the [[Big-River Fishes]] of western North America, alongside the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. All four are endemic to the Colorado River drainage and are listed as endangered. Adults show typical big-river traits: embedded scales, a large body (up to about 60 cm and 1 kg), an elongate caudal peduncle, and an enlarged caudal fin. These traits suit swimming through turbulence and holding station in flowing water.

Taxonomy within the genus *Gila* has been confusing. Intermediate forms have been reported where bonytail, humpback chub, and roundtail chub are sympatric. Morphological and genetic analyses now indicate that these taxa remain distinct when sympatric, although past hybridization ([[Hybridization]] or introgression) has occurred.

Habitat use is poorly documented. Telemetry studies in Lake Mohave and the [[High Levee Pond]] found that adults shelter during the day and become active at night. Cover includes deep water and, at High Levee Pond, riprapped banks. Historical observations suggest adults occupied mid-channel areas and fed on drifting particles. The species was most abundant where the river left canyons and entered broad alluvial valleys with runs, pools, braided channels, backwaters, and riparian vegetation. These backwaters served as nursery areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Lower Colorado River basin runs from Lees Ferry to the Mexican border. Historically the river reached the Gulf of California, but today its water rarely if ever does. Major lower-basin tributaries include the Virgin, Paria, Little Colorado, Bill Williams, and Gila rivers.

Bonytail declined from the mid-1930s onward. The authors attribute the decline mainly to predation and competition from introduced fishes. Dams and their cold-water releases also harm native fish, but researchers broadly agree that viable populations of big-river fish would persist in the lower basin without introduced species. Early records include Lake Mead, where bonytail were collected and seen spawning, and Lake Mohave, where collection records dropped sharply in the 1950s. Lake Havasu shows a similar trend.

In the High Levee Pond native fish habitat, bonytail and razorback sucker persisted from 1993 to April 1994. Largemouth bass were then introduced and decimated the native fish populations. Bass are therefore a key threat to stocked fish in constructed habitats. See [[Largemouth Bass]].

## Key Findings

**Historical and current status**
- Bonytail were historically common in the mainstem and tributaries from Glen Canyon Dam to Mexico.
- Wild bonytail are "extremely rare." Lake Mohave is the only known site where wild fish may still occur.
- Anglers reported bonytail from Grand Canyon in the 1940s. Angler-taken fish were common below Davis Dam before 1950.

**Broodstock**
- The first broodstock was developed at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in 1980–1981 from a small number of wild Lake Mohave fish. The authors consider it genetically sound.
- Attempts to add wild fish in 2005 to boost genetic diversity were unsuccessful. The original broodstock is aging. A second broodstock is being developed at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center. It uses five family lots and four years of non-sibling matings, with founder contributions made equal.

**Stocking in Lake Mohave**
- Between 1981 and 2002, 180,129 bonytail were stocked.
- Of these, 155,005 (86%) were under 10 cm, stocked between 1981 and 1995.
- The remaining 25,124 (14%) averaged 25 cm and were stocked between 1996 and 2002.
- Stocking is ongoing. The stated goal is a population of 50,000 large individuals.

**Stocking in Lake Havasu**
- A second program began in 1993, with the goal of stocking 30,000 bonytail over 25 cm by 2003. The authors report this goal was achieved. (The abstract gives 1994 as the first stocking year; the body text gives 1993 as the program start.)
- Early attempts to grow fish in lake backwaters were logistically difficult, so hatchery-raised fish from Willow Beach and grow-out ponds near Parker, Arizona, were used instead.
- All fish stocked into Lake Havasu carry 125 kHz PIT tags.

**Recapture and monitoring**
- Lake Mohave: spring monitoring trips from 1980 to 2003 captured 113 bonytail. One coded-wire-tagged fish was recaptured. It had been stocked less than 2 months earlier. One fish tagged in a 1994 telemetry study was caught in 2003, but its origin (wild or hatchery) is unknown.
- Lake Havasu: monitoring began in 1996, and 19 bonytail have been captured. Ten came from the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]. Two were caught and released by anglers, and seven came from other survey work.
- Fish at large for a short time were within 9 km of their release sites. One fish at large for two years had moved about 56 km into the Colorado River.
- The authors note the small number of returns, particularly from Lake Mohave, is a concern. They also consider the recapture of stocked fish in Lake Havasu so soon after stocking to be encouraging.

**Grow-out ponds and native fish habitats**
- Grow-out ponds were intended to raise bonytail above 25–35 cm before release, and have produced several hundred fish. They were less successful than expected.
- Production of larger fish needs to increase through hatcheries and grow-out ponds. Isolated backwaters that mimic historical conditions are also needed so the species can persist outside hatcheries.

**Management plan**
- The 2004 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service [[Management Plan]] for Big-River Fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin supports recovery through grow-out ponds, native fish habitats, and augmentation with larger fish. It complements the 2002 Recovery Goals and the forthcoming multi-species conservation plan.

**Conclusion**
- The authors conclude that predation on small fish is the main reason for poor recruitment. Stocking larger fish appears to be a viable way to establish bonytail, though the number of returns so far is small.
