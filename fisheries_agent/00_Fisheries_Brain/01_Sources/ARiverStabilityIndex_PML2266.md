# River Stability Index: A Predictive Methodology for Channel Stability, Sediment Yield, and Fish Habitat

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #stream-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This undated research proposal by [[David L. Rosgen]] of Wildland Hydrology describes a predictive methodology, a "River Stability Index," for assessing and predicting channel stability in streams. The stated goals are to reduce non-point source pollution from accelerated sediment yield and to maintain or improve the physical and biological function of rivers. The approach combines physical channel data (morphology, hydraulics, sediment transport, watershed condition) with biological data (fish populations, habitat, food chain, water quality). It is organized into seven research phases, a hierarchical four-level river inventory, and a planned computer model and database for monitoring and validating restoration and mitigation measures. The document is a proposal and contains no measured results, sample sizes, or statistics.

## Background & Description

The proposal states ten research objectives, including:

1. Develop a procedure to predict channel stability.
2. Relate channel stability to sediment transport (suspended and bedload).
3. Develop dimensionless sediment rating curves that can be extrapolated to rivers of different sizes and geology.
4. Measure bedload and suspended sediment for stable stream types across a range of discharges, including bankfull.
5. Evaluate bank erosion as a contributor to channel instability and sediment source.
6. Build a computer model and database for applying the procedure.

**Core concept.** An index of stability would determine a channel's existing physical and biological condition, its sensitivity to change, and the consequences of that change. [[Channel Stability]] is defined (after Rosgen, 1996) as the ability of a stream, over time, to transport the sediment and streamflows produced by its watershed so that channel dimension, pattern, and profile are maintained without aggrading or degrading. Biological data are to be collected alongside physical data to identify a possible threshold or optimal range for natural stream systems. The author suggests this could be an alternative to a [[Total Maximum Daily Load]] (TMDL) or a flat-rate sediment standard.

**Stream classification.** Comparisons rely on [[Rosgen Stream Classification]] types (Rosgen, 1994 and 1996), with level I to level IV inventories and stream types labeled A through G.

**Hierarchical approach.** A broad-level evaluation covers large areas quickly. A detailed validation-level investigation (Level IV) measures physical and biological parameters expected to change, and is used to verify and improve the predictions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The proposal links channel instability to several ecological and environmental consequences:

- Streambank erosion and increased sediment loads.
- Loss of floodplain and wetland function due to lowering of the local base level (channel incision).
- Loss of aquatic and terrestrial habitat.
- Degraded water quality.

Sediment sources to be identified include mass wasting, surface erosion from roads, rill and gully erosion, and flow-related channel adjustments. Watershed factors include vegetation alteration (clear-cuts, partial cuts, basal area reduction), road density, and stream crossings (using the [[Road Impact Index]], Rosgen, 1993). Land-use and forest-harvest effects are assessed by comparing undeveloped and developed watershed hydrographs ([[Flow Departure Hydrograph]]s).

**Biological context.** The fisheries component includes:

- [[Habitat Limiting Factor Analysis]].
- Fish species composition, standing crop, population estimates, migration, and habitat preference (impacted versus non-impacted reaches).
- Natural reproduction and age-class distribution.
- Food chain elements, including phytoplankton and primary productivity.
- Macroinvertebrate diversity indices.
- Water quality: temperature, specific conductance, anions, and pH.
- Seasonal distribution of adult fish.

The proposal states the aim of linking biological condition to channel stability and of establishing thresholds based on natural variability. It does not name any specific fish species, and the text does not report biological measurements.

**Models.** Existing tools named for sediment and water-yield estimation are [[HYSED]] (Rosgen and Silvey, 1983) and [[WRENNS]] (USEPA, 1980). The proposal intends to integrate these into a cumulative-effects watershed model.

## Key Findings

The document presents a plan rather than results. Its main points are:

**Research phases**

- **Phase I (Design):** Select study reaches and watersheds across a wide range of stream types. Characterize morphology, sediment, and hydrology. Collect streamflow and suspended and bedload sediment data across flows and seasons. Measure channel morphology (stream type, slope, bed material size distribution, bar deposition, meanders, width/depth ratio, sinuosity).
- **Phase II (Physical and biological data at the reach scale):** Conduct Level II and III studies (riparian vegetation, channel stability ratings, streambank stability, depositional patterns). Calculate stream power, shear stress, energy slope, and other hydraulic characteristics. Estimate sediment transport, maximum particle sizes moved at bankfull, and streambank erosion rates (tons/year). Conduct habitat limiting factor and fisheries analyses.
- **Phase III (Watershed assessment):** Obtain hydro-geomorphic, soils, vegetation, road, and precipitation data. Calculate equivalent clear-cut condition (analogous to percent impervious area). Validate with streamflow records.
- **Phase IV (Validation):** Apply the Level IV inventory. Measure bank erosion with bank pins, install permanent cross-sections, and measure scour depth and entrainment. Compare biological responses in impacted and non-impacted reaches.
- **Phase V (Analysis):** Compare predictions with observed responses. Relate biological condition to channel stability. Set thresholds and acceptable ranges for natural variability in sediment concentration and transport.
- **Phase VI (Data base and model):** Develop a database and computer model to implement the procedure.
- **Phase VII (Testing):** Monitor to evaluate thresholds and test the procedure against data from other hydro-physiographic provinces. Test the effects of various Best Management Practices (BMPs) on channel stability and biological function.

**Methods and data**

- **Paired comparisons:** Streams with the same lithology and morphology but different watershed impacts are compared as "above vs. below" impacts or against an undisturbed control reach. Expected contrasts include changes in width/depth ratio, bank erosion rates, sediment supply, and biological populations.
- **Basic data list:** Watershed (drainage area, geology, vegetation, timber harvest, roads, stream order), climatologic (precipitation, intensity, duration, return period for runoff), hydrologic (flood frequency, hydraulic geometry, flow duration, annual hydrograph), hydraulic (energy slope, velocity, shear stress, stream power, roughness), sedimentological (suspended and bedload yield, rating curves, size distributions, entrainment, scour), and stream morphology (entrenchment ratio, width/depth ratio, sinuosity, particle size, slope).

**Predicted outputs and stated expectations**

- A quantifiable, measurable, and reproducible index of channel stability.
- Dimensionless sediment rating curves for each stream type and stability condition.
- Threshold ranges for allowable increases in sediment yield that stay within acceptable physical and biological limits.
- An alternative to TMDL or flat-rate sediment standards.
