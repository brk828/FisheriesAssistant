# Ecological Studies of Five Species of Southwestern Fishes

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #goodeidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This final progress report documents research on five native southwestern fish species conducted 1965-1967 under Sport Fishing Institute funding. The primary objective—delimiting present ranges of [[Lepidomeda vittata]], [[Meda fulgida]], [[Tiaroga cobitis]], [[Cyprinodon macularius]], and [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] in Arizona—was accomplished. Secondary objectives involving laboratory studies and facility development partially failed due to infrastructure challenges. All five species show dramatic range contractions from historical distributions, with introduced species identified as a major factor in native fish decline. [[Meda fulgida]] and [[Tiaroga cobitis]] are considered extremely endangered, restricted to three stream segments each. Laboratory maintenance succeeded for some species ([[Cyprinodon macularius]], [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]) but failed for others.

## Background & Description

The project focused on five native fish species of the [[Gila River Basin]] and surrounding waters in the southwestern United States. These species represent different families and ecological niches:

**[[Lepidomeda vittata]]** (Little Colorado Spinedace, [[Cyprinidae]]): Originally considered endangered but reclassified as "rare" requiring monitoring. Shows sensitivity to introduced fishes. Laboratory maintenance achieved but spawning unsuccessful. No reproductive behavior data obtained from field or laboratory observations.

**[[Meda fulgida]]** (Spikedace, [[Cyprinidae]]): Historically widespread in [[Gila River Basin]]. Now restricted to three stream segments: [[Aravaipa Creek]], mainstream [[Gila River]] near Gila and Cliff, New Mexico, and upper [[San Pedro River]] near Charleston, Arizona. Classified as extremely endangered. Successfully reproduced in artificial stream in 1965.

**[[Tiaroga cobitis]]** (Loach Minnow, [[Cyprinidae]]): Similar distribution pattern to [[Meda fulgida]]—three stream segments including [[Gila River]] in New Mexico, [[Aravaipa Creek]], and Whiteriver on [[White Mountain Apache Reservation]]. Habitat requirements similar to eastern darters. Laboratory maintenance largely unsuccessful.

**[[Cyprinodon macularius]]** (Desert Pupfish, [[Cyprinodontidae]]): Three well-marked forms in Arizona. One inhabits Quitobaquito Spring in [[Organ Pipe National Monument]], second at Monkey Spring near Patagonia, third apparently extinct (formerly widespread in lower [[Gila River Basin]], last taken 1955 in headwaters of [[San Pedro River]] in Sonora, Mexico). Widespread and abundant around [[Salton Sea]], California. Easily maintained in laboratory with successful reproduction.

**[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]** (Gila Topminnow, [[Goodeidae]]): Extinct throughout most native range, restricted to two springs in [[Santa Cruz County]], Arizona. Population at Safford, Arizona decimated between 1955 and 1967, apparently due to introduction of [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquitofish). Live-bearer, easily maintained in laboratory with established stocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The research documented severe range contractions across all five species, with introduced fishes identified as the primary threat. Major water development projects (impoundments) were scheduled for critical habitat areas including the [[Gila River]] and upper [[San Pedro River]], threatening remaining populations.

**Habitat Requirements**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] served as a critical study site supporting both [[Meda fulgida]] and [[Tiaroga cobitis]] in sufficient abundance for intensive life-history studies. Monthly and bi-monthly sampling initiated nine months prior to report submission. [[Tiaroga cobitis]] requires flowing current and specific temperature conditions similar to eastern darters.

**Introduced Species Impact**: [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquitofish) specifically implicated in decimation of [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] at Safford. General sensitivity to exotic fishes noted for [[Lepidomeda vittata]] and [[Meda fulgida]]. Introduced species identified as major factor in decline of indigenous southwestern fish forms.

**Conservation Status**: [[Meda fulgida]] and [[Tiaroga cobitis]] classified as extremely endangered due to: (1) restriction to flowing streams becoming increasingly reduced, and (2) sensitivity to introduced species. [[Lepidomeda vittata]] downgraded from "endangered" to "rare" but position considered precarious long-term due to relatively restricted range.

**Protected Populations**: Whiteriver locality on [[White Mountain Apache Reservation]] important for [[Tiaroga cobitis]] as area may be perpetuated as natural flowing stream. Cooperation obtained from landowner at Monkey Spring near Patagonia for maintaining [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] and [[Cyprinodon macularius]].

## Key Findings

**Range Delimitation Results**:
- [[Lepidomeda vittata]]: Range delimited, manuscript submitted to Southwestern Naturalist
- [[Meda fulgida]]: Restricted to three stream segments from formerly wide range in [[Gila River Basin]]
- [[Tiaroga cobitis]]: Restricted to three stream segments
- [[Cyprinodon macularius]]: Three forms—two extant in Arizona (Quitobaquito Spring, Monkey Spring), one apparently extinct (lower [[Gila River Basin]])
- [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]: Extinct throughout most native range, restricted to two springs in [[Santa Cruz County]]

**Life History Studies**: Intensive study of [[Meda fulgida]] and [[Tiaroga cobitis]] initiated in [[Aravaipa Creek]] approximately nine months before report. Monthly and bi-monthly samples collected from selected areas, continuing through breeding season. Specimens preserved for analysis of age and growth, seasonal food habits variation, breeding condition, fecundity, and diurnal activity cycles. General and reproductive behavior studied in field. Data on five other indigenous fishes of [[Aravaipa Creek]] also collected. [[Tiaroga cobitis]] coming into breeding condition at time of writing with plans to observe spawning in nature within weeks.

**Reproductive Biology**: Fecundity and reproductive aspects studied from preserved specimens for [[Tiaroga cobitis]]. No reproductive behavior data obtained for [[Lepidomeda vittata]] despite laboratory maintenance and field searches for spawning individuals. [[Meda fulgida]] successfully reproduced in artificial stream in 1965.

**Laboratory Maintenance Success**:
- Successful: [[Cyprinodon macularius]] (both Arizona stocks reproducing and maintaining populations), [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] (multiple stocks established, attempted introductions to springs north of Phoenix)
- Unsuccessful: [[Lepidomeda vittata]] (maintained but no spawning), [[Tiaroga cobitis]] (inadequate facilities for current and temperature requirements), [[Meda fulgida]] (maintained temporarily, reproduced 1965, facilities inadequate for continuation)

**Laboratory Facility Challenges**: Agreement with [[Phoenix Zoo]] to utilize old fish hatchery failed due to: (1) mosquito control treatments by four Public Health Agencies (city, county, state) causing spontaneous fish kills, (2) canal dry-up cutting water supply, (3) heavy public use and vandalism. Hatchery returned to Zoo, operations re-establishing on university campus under closer control. All materials purchased with Sport Fishing Institute funds transferred to university.

**Mosquito Control Application**: Projected work with [[Maricopa County Public Health Service]] using [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] for mosquito control not feasible. Initial stockings in golf course ponds destroyed by pesticide blow-over from local applications. Public Health Service could not justify continuing experimental program, exclusively using [[Gambusia affinis]] due to greater availability. Successful use would require large populations for immediate effective stocking.

**Conservation Recommendations**: [[Lepidomeda vittata]] should remain listed as "rare" form requiring monitoring despite no longer being considered "endangered." Prognosis for [[Meda fulgida]] in nature considered fairly poor due to major impoundments scheduled for both [[Gila River]] near Gila and Cliff, New Mexico, and upper [[San Pedro River]] near Charleston, Arizona as part of regional water development plan.
