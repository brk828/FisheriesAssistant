# Biological Opinion on Federal Aid Support for Rainbow Trout Stocking in Little Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #conservation-management #predator-prey-interactions

## Executive Summary

This Biological Opinion, issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on August 2, 1995 (revised November 20, 1995), evaluates the effects of Federal Aid's support to the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] for stocking catchable [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) into three reservoirs ([[Nelson Reservoir]], [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]], and [[Knoll Lake]]) within the [[Little Colorado River]] drainage. The consultation addresses potential impacts to the threatened [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*) and its designated critical habitat. The opinion concludes "no jeopardy" to the spinedace and "no adverse modification" of critical habitat, contingent upon implementation of specific conservation measures including coded wire tagging of all stocked trout, delayed stocking until after spring runoff, and comprehensive monitoring programs.

## Background & Description

### Species Description

The [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] is a small (100 mm) native cyprinid endemic to the [[Little Colorado River]] drainage in eastern Arizona. Described by E.D. Cope in 1874, the species exhibits olivaceous, bluish or lead grey coloration. The spinedace was listed as threatened with designated critical habitat on October 16, 1987. Critical habitat designation includes 44 stream miles: 18 miles of [[East Clear Creek]] above [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]], 13 miles below Blue Ridge in [[Coconino County]], 8 miles of [[Chevelon Creek]] in [[Navajo County]], and 5 miles of [[Nutrioso Creek]] in [[Apache County]].

Miller (1963) characterized the spinedace as "trout like" in behavior and habitat requirements, suggesting that prior to 1900 the species likely occupied habitats now dominated by nonnative salmonids. The species exhibits wide habitat tolerance, occurring in environments ranging from stagnant pools to permanent flowing water with substrates from fine sediments to bedrock. Water temperatures in occupied habitats range from 58 to 78 degrees Fahrenheit.

### Population Status

Five disjunct populations are known: [[Chevelon Creek]], [[Silver Creek]], [[Nutrioso Creek]], [[East Clear Creek]], and the [[Little Colorado River]] proper. Population estimates fluctuate drastically year to year. Extensive collections by Miller (1963) indicated the spinedace had been extirpated from much of its historic range between 1939 and 1960. Spinedace are currently considered rare in the [[East Clear Creek]] system and have not been collected from [[Silver Creek]] or its tributaries since the mid-1970s. The species demonstrates ability to persist through severe drought and winter temperatures, repopulating when conditions improve. Spinedace are late spring or early summer spawners.

### Project Description

The proposed action involves continuation of a modified sport fish stocking program into three locations in the [[Little Colorado River]] basin, funded in part by the Service's Sport Fish Restoration Program (Dingell-Johnson & Wallop-Breaux). The consultation covers a 5-year period from January 1, 1996 to December 31, 2000.

**Nelson Reservoir**: Created in 1892, this 60-acre reservoir on [[Nutrioso Creek]] in [[Apache County]] will be managed as a "put-and-take" trout fishery. Initial stocking rate: 20,000 catchable rainbow trout per year, providing approximately 20,000 angler days annually. Average depth 2.5 m (8.3 ft), maximum depth 7.5 m (24.6 ft). Critical habitat for spinedace is designated in [[Nutrioso Creek]] from Nelson Reservoir dam downstream to the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest boundary. Spinedace occur both upstream and downstream of the reservoir.

**Blue Ridge Reservoir**: Completed in 1964, this 70-acre reservoir on [[East Clear Creek]] in [[Coconino County]] has total capacity of 19,500 acre-feet, spilling at 15,000 acre-feet at elevation 6,720 feet. Initial stocking rate: 15,000 catchable rainbow trout per year, providing approximately 15,000 angler days. Critical habitat is designated immediately above and below the reservoir.

**Knoll Lake**: This 55-acre lake in upper [[Leonard Canyon]] on the [[Coconino National Forest]] in [[Coconino County]] will provide approximately 20,000 angler days. Initial stocking rate: 20,000 catchable rainbow trout per year. Spinedace occur upstream and downstream in [[Leonard Canyon]], consistently found in [[Dines Tank]] approximately 7 km downstream but not below the Tank. Critical habitat occurs approximately 15 miles downstream where [[Leonard Canyon]] meets [[East Clear Creek]].

### Management Provisions

All three stocking sites incorporate the following precautions:

1. Stock catchable rainbow trout only (8-10 inches)
2. All stocked fish marked with coded wire tags
3. Stocking delayed until flow over dam ceases following spring snowpack runoff
4. Stocking ceases if water quality deteriorates or no later than Labor Day
5. Comprehensive monitoring including:
   - Creel census: stratified random, 2 weekday and 2 weekend/holiday per month April-September
   - Population surveys upstream and downstream during low flow periods (May-June, September-October) to detect movement of tagged fish

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Degradation and Threats

The environmental baseline reveals extensive habitat alteration in the [[Little Colorado River]] watershed. Human impacts include increased sedimentation from logging and road construction, stream channel modification, dam construction, groundwater pumping, water diversion, and introduction of nonnative fishes. The use of piscicides as fish management tools has also impacted spinedace populations (Miller 1963; Minckley 1984).

### Nonnative Fish Community

Sampling by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] from May 1990 to November 1991 documented eight species of nonnative fishes in [[East Clear Creek]] drainage: [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), rainbow trout, [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Brown Trout]], [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus zebrinus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). Additional nonnatives found co-occurring with spinedace include [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), and [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).

Native fishes associated with spinedace include [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus [Pantosteus] discobolus*), [[Little Colorado Sucker]] (*Catostomus* species), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), and [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*).

### Predation Studies

Blinn and Runck (1990) demonstrated rainbow trout predation on spinedace through aquaria experiments. Four spinedace and one trout were placed in aquaria with and without vegetation or rock refuges. Wild trout obtained from [[Nutrioso Creek]] consumed spinedace in aquaria both with and without rock refuges. The largest spinedace consumed by a rainbow trout was 71 mm; the trout was 240 mm. Significantly, hatchery-reared trout obtained from [[Page Springs Hatchery]] did not consume spinedace.

Field experiments in [[Nutrioso Creek]] using 2 by 3 meter mesh enclosures confirmed laboratory findings. Although spinedace were lost from enclosures with and without trout (probably from snake and belostomatid insect predation), significantly more spinedace were lost from enclosures with rainbow trout (Blinn et al. 1993). Even with abundant macroinvertebrate communities present, trout fed on spinedace. Trout presence also modified spinedace behavior, pushing individuals into open water possibly making them more vulnerable to visual predators.

### Inverse Distribution Pattern

An inverse relationship between trout and spinedace abundance occurs throughout [[Nutrioso Creek]] (Blinn and Runck 1990; Blinn et al. 1993) and in the [[East Clear Creek]] drainage (Denova and Abarca 1992). The uppermost area of [[Nutrioso Creek]] contained healthy populations of trout, the area just downstream had few trout and spinedace, while areas farther downstream had only spinedace. This pattern is confirmed by consistent presence of spinedace in [[Dines Tank]] and absence of rainbow trout.

During 1991 and 1992, isolated pools below [[Nelson Reservoir]] were sampled and consistently found an inverse relationship between spinedace and rainbow trout numbers. In 1994, spinedace numbers increased in [[Nutrioso Creek]] from 1993 levels at Correjo Crossing and at the Rudd Creek/Nutrioso Crossing. Notably, trout were not stocked in [[Nelson Reservoir]] during 1994 when the increase in spinedace numbers was observed.

### Historical Stocking Practices

[[Nelson Reservoir]] was created in 1892 for irrigation; [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] first introduced trout in 1958. In previous years, rainbow, brown, brook (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), and cutthroat (*Oncorhynchus clarki*) fingerlings were stocked. The reservoir was managed as "Basic Yield" under AGFD's Coldwater Strategic Plan, meaning fingerlings or subcatchables were stocked and held over to grow to catchable size. In 1983, water rights were transferred from irrigation to recreation, eliminating irrigation drawdowns; the reservoir now remains at spillway elevation most of the year.

[[Blue Ridge Dam]] was completed in December 1964. The reservoir has been managed as a "put and take" trout fishery with catchable and fingerling rainbows stocked along with brown trout fingerlings. Gage data (1984-1994) show the spillway crest at 15,000 acre-feet is rarely exceeded in early May. However, in 1992, the reservoir was above 15,000 acre-feet throughout most of May into early June, and again in late August and early September.

[[Knoll Lake]] was previously stocked with catchable rainbow trout and fingerling brown trout. Annual water level measurements (1983-1993) suggest water levels may approach spill levels during the stocking season, with distances below spillway ranging from 1.5 to 13.9 feet.

## Key Findings

### Direct and Indirect Effects

The proposed project may result in rainbow trout entering spinedace habitat despite precautions. All three stocking sites have provisions that trout will not be stocked until after snowpack runoff to minimize downstream movement. However, unavoidable spills are possible, as demonstrated by [[Blue Ridge Dam]] in 1992. Strict adherence to the no-stocking-until-after-runoff requirement may delay stocking beyond Memorial Day weekend in some years or cancel the stocking season in others.

Information on flows immediately below [[Nelson Reservoir]] is limited, but Blinn and Runck (1993) report that Nelson spills each spring "possibly introducing rainbow trout into lower [[Nutrioso Creek]]." No similar precautions exist to limit upstream movement of trout. Currently no barriers exist to upstream movement from Nelson, Knoll, or Blue Ridge reservoirs. All three incoming drainages are generally dry during the majority of the stocking season in average precipitation years. During wet years or periods of high runoff, trout may move out of stocked areas into spinedace habitat. Similarly, spinedace may move into trout areas.

Stocked rainbow trout in the three impoundments are subject to heavy fishing pressure. Under the Intensive Use Management program, these stocking programs do not manage for trout that overwinter. AGFD coldwater strategic plans call for return to creel of at least 50 percent on put-and-take trout fisheries. Most put-and-take fish are caught by anglers, preyed upon by other predators, or succumb to other causes. The number of stocked fish expected to overwinter is considered insignificant. However, fish that do overwinter are harvested at much larger sizes and are prized by anglers. [[Nelson Reservoir]] once held the inland state record for rainbow trout (5.02 kg).

### Critical Habitat Assessment

Approximately 82 percent (36 of 44 miles) of designated critical habitat for spinedace is affected by this action:
- [[East Clear Creek]]: 13 miles above [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] from upper end to Potato Lake area
- [[East Clear Creek]]: 18 miles below Blue Ridge Dam to confluence with [[Leonard Canyon]]
- [[Nutrioso Creek]]: 5 miles below [[Nelson Reservoir]] dam to Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest boundary

When critical habitat was designated in 1987, these areas were reported as "presently support(ing) healthy self-perpetuating populations of the Little Colorado spinedace" (USFWS 1987). Since that time, habitat degradation, nonnative fishes, and water scarcity have resulted in low spinedace numbers in [[East Clear Creek]] and [[Leonard Canyon]], with consistent rainbow trout findings in [[Nutrioso Creek]].

The act of stocking predaceous fish above or below critical habitat contributes to disjunct distribution patterns and the species' retreat to suboptimal habitats. Stocking predaceous fish is contrary to the Service's Draft Recovery Plan objectives to "protect existing populations of spinedace" and "improve or restore habitat occupied by spinedace populations and the native species assemblage present" (USFWS 1994).

However, the Biological Opinion concludes that while the proposed action affects critical habitat, it does not appreciably diminish the value of constituent elements essential to spinedace conservation. The action is reversible and does not diminish or preclude the role of habitat for survival and recovery. Therefore, the adverse modification threshold is not exceeded.

### Cumulative Effects

Cumulative effects of rainbow trout in spinedace habitat may be significant. In addition to stocking programs established by AGFD and the Service's Fisheries Program, private water bodies are stocked in the area. Spinedace may be affected by accidental or intentional transfer of fishes. The opportunity for bait bucket transfers into spinedace habitat is readily available, as is dumping of bait fish or pet fish. The delay in the trout stocking program has frustrated fishing communities; some anglers have threatened to stock other fish in the absence of State or Federal programs.

### Incidental Take Statement

The purpose of stocking rainbow trout is to provide recreational fishing opportunity, not to prey on spinedace. However, given the described effects, the action will likely result in incidental take of an unknown number of spinedace. Approximately 36 of 44 miles of critical habitat are likely affected. Requiring coded wire tags for all stocked fish will assist in determining if rainbow found in spinedace habitat have been stocked or naturally produced in the [[Little Colorado River]] drainage.

The Service anticipates incidental take will be difficult to detect because: finding a dead spinedace that has been eaten is difficult; evaluating effects of rainbow trout that overwinter may be challenging; and use of coded wire tags for screening stocked trout has not been proven for this area.

**Level of take is considered exceeded if:**

1. Through surveys, rainbow trout are found in occupied or critical habitat with spinedace in their stomachs
2. Any of the three reservoirs spill during the stocking season for any reason (e.g., miscalculation of snowpack, unusually heavy rains)

**Revised criteria (November 1995 opinion):** Consultation will be reinitiated on the three reservoirs individually if more than 1 spinedace is taken from stomachs of tagged trout or collection of more than 10 tagged trout occurs during monitoring in spinedace habitat.

### Reasonable and Prudent Measures

1. Minimize opportunity for interaction between spinedace and stocked rainbow trout
2. Evaluate food habits of rainbow trout collected within occupied or designated critical habitat

**Terms and Conditions:**

1. Modify stocking schedule until water level is clearly below spillway level; this may cancel stocking programs during high runoff years or postpone first stocking later than Memorial Day weekend
2. During the 5-year stocking period, conduct analysis of stream hydrology in [[Little Colorado River]] basin to evaluate appropriate timing of rainbow trout stocking and probability of spills
3. Remove all trout from spinedace occupied or critical habitat during stream monitoring and collect stomachs for analysis
4. During the 5-year consultation period, ensure all fisheries projects in spinedace habitat are reviewed by Ecological Services

### Conservation Recommendations

1. Conduct comprehensive study on all fish stocking in the [[Little Colorado River]] watershed
2. Verify retention rate and persistence of coded wire tags in stocked populations
3. Submit list of actions considered necessary by AGFD to maintain or enhance recreational fishing opportunity while proceeding with spinedace recovery
4. Submit list of actions deemed necessary by AGFD to recover the spinedace
5. Evaluate potential of using native [[Apache Trout]] in lieu of rainbow trout in acceptable habitats in [[Little Colorado River]] watershed to provide recreational fishing opportunity

### Consultation History and Policy Issues

This represents the first section 7 consultation for the subject stocking sites, although support to Arizona's sport fish stocking program has occurred for many years. Informal consultation between Fisheries/Federal Aid and Ecological Services was initiated in 1992 on the statewide stocking program but was withdrawn for various reasons. A separate consultation was completed for the Fisheries [[Willow Beach Facility]] (USFWS 1994).

The original Biological Opinion was issued August 2, 1995, by the Acting State Supervisor but failed to conform to established Service Policy regarding opportunity for Intra-Service review and issuance by the Regional Director. A revised opinion was issued November 20, 1995, by the Regional Director to correct these deficiencies.
