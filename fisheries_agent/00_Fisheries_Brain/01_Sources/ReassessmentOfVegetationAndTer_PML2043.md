# Reassessment of Vegetation and Terrestrial Vertebrates Along the Rio Grande Between Fort Quitman and Presidio, Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-vertebrates

*Note on scope: the source is a terrestrial and riparian wildlife study. It contains no fish or aquatic-invertebrate data, apart from a planned aquatic survey that was postponed. The frontmatter species fields therefore list only the most prominent taxa mentioned, and the ichthyology tag is kept as the template requires, but the note itself covers birds, mammals, amphibians, reptiles and vegetation.*

## Executive Summary

This 1993 report, prepared by Robert D. Ohmart, Paul C. Marsh, Scott Gende and Anthony Zamora of the [[Center for Environmental Studies]] at Arizona State University for the U.S. Section of the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] (contract IBWC-91-29), reassesses riparian vegetation and terrestrial vertebrates along roughly 90 km (about 55 mi) of the [[Rio Grande]] between Fort Quitman and Presidio, Texas. It is a follow-up to the 1977–78 baseline study by [[Engel-Wilson and Ohmart]] (1978), and it evaluates the [[Biological Mitigation Program]] (BPP) established under the Final Environmental Impact Statement (FEIS).

Vegetation was restructured by [[Saltcedar Invasion]] and by channel clearing, and the authors report marked increases in bird and small-mammal densities in 1991–92. They link these increases mainly to a wetter summer and higher seed and insect production. Mitigation features, including five ponds, 40 wildlife cover huts and 60 snags, were variably functional, and the report calls for repairs, pond dredging and channel maintenance.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The reach forms part of the international boundary. The BPP was designed with an 8 m (25 ft) vegetation fringe and a 30 m (100 ft) cleared overbank.
- **Motivation:** The 1977–78 study recorded the riparian community before mitigation was complete. The current study was intended to compare before-and-after populations and to assess how well the mitigation worked.
- **Methods:** Five principal vegetation communities were censused, with foliage height diversity measured along 33 transects in four community types. Birds were counted by transect census, with white-winged dove nesting also studied. Small mammals were sampled with trap grids, larger mammals by sightings and tracks, and amphibians and reptiles with pitfall-type drift fences and traps. Fisheries and aquatic invertebrates were planned but postponed indefinitely.
- **Foliage structure types:** Six structural types (I–VI) were recognised, based on the proportion of foliage volume in the understory, midstory and overstory strata (after [[Anderson and Ohmart]] 1984).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation change:** Cottonwood-willow and screwbean mesquite stands were largely lost. Saltcedar (*Tamarix*) dominated the floodplain. Thorny shrub habitat was becoming increasingly rare. Heavy grazing reduced forage, and flooding and channel plugs favored saltcedar.
- **Climate drivers:** Summer 1991 rainfall was much higher than in 1977. The authors identify summer rain and mild winter temperatures as the main drivers of bird and rodent population increases, through food and cover production.
- **Water quality at ponds (July 1992):** [[Livingston's Pond]] pH 2.9, EC 2.640 ppt, DO 7.3 mg/L. [[Aguila's Pond]] pH 7.8, EC 4.380 ppt. [[Prieto's Pond]] pH 7.7, EC 1.360 ppt. The [[State of Texas Pond]] pH 7.4, EC 4.200 ppt. [[Cecil's Pond]] held no water.
- **Flooding:** Extended flooding (2–4 years in some reaches) caused siltation and gave carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) access to Livingston's Pond. Channel plugs also dammed flow and caused prolonged inundation, which killed trees and shrubs.

## Key Findings

**Birds**
- Avian richness, individual density and total density rose sharply between 1977–78 and 1991–92. Some species increased 9–10 fold, with sparrow densities up to 10 times higher.
- Granivores accounted for 93% of the increase. Summer precipitation was the most important climatic variable.
- Total density in the 1992 transect tables ranged from about 477 to 1,267 birds per 100 acres, depending on community and season.
- A total of 21 species had been counted at the time of the summary. Wintering and summering species were classified by relative abundance (abundant, common, fairly common, rare, absent).
- Heavily grazed transects held fewer bird species than lightly grazed ones. Of 44 census visits in 1992, 32 showed more species in lightly grazed areas.

**White-winged Doves**
- Nests per 0.4 ha fell from 28.4 (1977–78) to 4.3 (1991–92). Eggs per 0.4 ha fell from 1.9 to 1.7. Fledglings per 0.4 ha fell from 26.0 to 3.6. Percent of eggs hatched fell from 66.0% to 58.0%.
- The decline is attributed to loss of wheat-field habitat and tree and shrub expansion.

**Mammals**
- Small-mammal densities were higher in 1991–92 than in 1977–78, with the largest gains in saltcedar grids.
- Deer mice (*Peromyscus maniculatus*) and western harvest mice were the dominant species.
- Winter rodent densities in 1992 (Table 6) ranged from about 8 to 10 per 219 trap nights across habitats.
- Collared peccary and desert cottontail were the most frequently sighted large mammals. No gray wolf was observed.

**Amphibians and Reptiles**
- Amphibian species richness was similar between saltcedar and thorny shrub habitats (five species each). Great Plains narrow-mouthed toad (*Gastrophryne olivacea*) dominated saltcedar, and Texas toad (*Bufo speciosus*) dominated thorny shrub (66%).
- In reptiles, the study recorded roughly 30 species across trap and sighting data. Thorny shrub habitat supported higher density and diversity, and the western diamondback rattlesnake was among the most frequently sighted snakes.

**Endangered Species**
- No peregrine falcon (*Falco peregrinus*) and no gray wolf (*Canis lupus baileyi*) were observed.

**Mitigation Projects**
- Revegetation at Cecil's site (4.8 ha, planted 1985): 90–95% of quail bush drowned. Of 562 trees in the bermed area, 79% were alive in February 1987. All quail bush died.
- Wildlife cover huts: of 40 structures, 28 were in poor or inoperative condition. Wildlife tracks showed use by badger, bobcat, coyote, collared peccary and others (Table 12).
- Snags: 79.6% of 60 snags had cavities. Mean cavities per snag was 3.22 (SD 3.51). Most birds used snags as perches.
- Spoil mounds: honey mesquite and spiny shrubs were the dominant volunteers, and livestock limited recovery.
- Tree fringe survey: of 43.8 km (27.2 mi) in Area 1, about half was inaccessible and only 21% met the FEIS vegetation fringe and cleared overbank requirements. In Area 2, 45.3% of 119.6 km (74.3 mi) had a fringe of more than 7.6 m (25 ft).

**Recommendations**
- Repair or replace the wildlife cover huts, and remove the black plastic.
- Deepen the five ponds to remove silt, and fence them against livestock.
- Maintain the channel by using an amphibious dragline to open plugs quickly, and deepen the channel below the floodplain.
- Keep spoil mounds fenced and reseeded with honey mesquite.
- Continue monitoring the mitigation features and the riparian bird and mammal populations.
