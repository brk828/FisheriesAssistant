# The Muskellung in Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1948 report by John E. Williams, published through the Institute for Fisheries Research (Michigan Department of Conservation), synthesizes historical and contemporary reports on the distribution, abundance, growth, and capture methods of the [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy masquinongy*) in Michigan. Rather than presenting new specimen-based research, the author compiled angler reports, contest records, and museum/library archives to map musky distribution across the state, establish a length-weight relationship model, and estimate age-growth patterns. The [[Great Lakes]] variety dominates Michigan's population, with [[Lake St. Clair]] and the [[Detroit River]] representing by far the most productive fisheries. A notable secondary focus is on the [[Tiger Musky]] variant found in Lac Vieux Desert, believed to be of Wisconsin origin.

## Background & Description

- **Subspecies noted:** Great Lakes muskellunge (*Esox m. masquinongy*) predominant; a distinct "Wisconsin variety" or [[Tiger Musky]] hybrid form likely present in Lac Vieux Desert (Gogebic county).
- **Growth and size records:**
  - World record (1940–1947): 62.25 lb, caught by Percy Haver in Lake St. Clair (rod and reel).
  - Haver also caught specimens of 59, 58.25, and 56.25 lb from the same lake; held a single-day record of five muskies totaling 187 lb.
  - Largest musky on record in Michigan: a **110 lb, 88-inch (7 ft 4 in)** individual netted in Intermediate Lake, Antrim county, in 1919 — described as unusually long and "racy" (slender) for its weight, likely due to advanced age (est. 25–30 years).
  - Largest musky caught by a woman (per Detroit Free Press Fishing Contest records, 12-year span): 39 lb.
- **Length-weight relationship:** Based on 162 plotted specimens, an average length-weight curve was established. Fish plotting above the average line were interpreted as either naturally stout individuals, fish that had recently ingested large prey (e.g., a 5–10 lb walleye), or gravid females — large females may carry over 15 lb of eggs prior to spawning.
- **Age and growth pattern:**
  - Age 5: ~32 inches long, 7–8 lb.
  - Age 10: ~20–25 lb.
  - Age ~15: approaching 50 lb, roughly 1 lb per inch of length.
  - Growth accelerates markedly in "prime of life" then plateaus in old age, producing an S-shaped (sigmoid) length-weight growth curve — slow in youth, rapid in maturity, leveling in senescence.
- **Girth anomalies:** Some reported specimens show inconsistent girth-to-weight ratios (e.g., 41 in/37 lb with only 18 in girth vs. 43 in/17 lb with 20.5 in girth), suggesting probable recording or transcription errors in historical data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Preferred habitat:** Large, fairly deep lakes with abundant forage; also large connecting rivers and Great Lakes bays/embayments.
- **Geographic distribution:** Found in at least 17 Michigan counties, almost exclusively in counties bordering the [[Great Lakes]] (Barry county being the sole inland exception, home to [[Gun Lake]] and [[Thornapple Lake]]).
- **Primary population centers:**
  - [[Lake St. Clair]] and [[Detroit River]] — most abundant and largest specimens (98 and 26 reports respectively); example: 2,500 muskies speared through ice in winter 1937–38.
  - Secondary hotspots: [[Elk Lake]], [[Gun Lake]], [[Thornapple Lake]], [[Lac Vieux Desert]], [[Cheboygan River]], [[Manistee Lake]], [[Round Lake]], [[Brevort Lake]], [[Hamlin Lake]], [[Pentwater Lake]].
  - Offshore/bay habitats: [[Les Cheneaux Islands]] and [[Betsie Bay]].
- **Limiting factors on inland distribution:** Dams and waterfalls likely block upstream colonization of inland lakes; many inland waters may simply lack suitable habitat/prey base.
- **Trophic interactions:** Apex predatory behavior inferred from stomach contents (e.g., ingestion of large walleye up to 10 lb), consistent with the species' role as a top piscivore in Great Lakes and connected lake ecosystems.
- **Reproductive ecology:** Females carry substantial egg loads (>15 lb) prior to breeding season, indicating high fecundity investment relative to body mass.

## Key Findings

1. **Distribution concentrated near Great Lakes:** Muskellunge occurrence in Michigan is almost entirely restricted to counties bordering the Great Lakes, with [[Lake St. Clair]]/[[Detroit River]] as the dominant fishery by an order of magnitude over other locations.
2. **Capture method influences catch size:** Trolling produced roughly 5x more muskies than still-fishing, but still-fishing yielded comparably large average fish (~26.25 lb), matching large trolled plugs. Spinners/spoons yielded smaller average fish (8–22 lb).
3. **Lure effectiveness:** 3/0 spinners, No. 3 spoons, and black-and-white "dare-devil" spoons were noted as particularly effective, alongside large plugs like the pikie minnow.
4. **Length-weight modeling as a citizen-science tool:** The author's graphical length-weight comparison method was proposed as a practical tool for anglers to benchmark their catches against statewide averages.
5. **Age-growth trajectory:** Muskellunge exhibit relatively fast early growth (up to ~32 in by age 5) followed by an accelerating "prime of life" growth phase and eventual weight-growth plateau in old age (est. 25–30 years), exemplified by the 1919 Intermediate Lake giant (110 lb/88 in).
6. **Possible hybrid/subspecies presence:** [[Lac Vieux Desert]] is suspected to harbor the Wisconsin-origin [[Tiger Musky]] variety rather than the standard Great Lakes muskellunge found elsewhere in the state.
7. **Data quality caveats:** The author acknowledges potential misidentification with [[Northern Pike]] by enthusiastic anglers, and possible transcription errors in historical length/weight/girth records, urging caution in interpreting outlier data points.
8. **Citizen data collection appeal:** The report closes with a call for anglers to submit specimen data (length, weight, girth, capture method, date, location, and scale samples) to the Institute for Fisheries Research to support ongoing population studies.
