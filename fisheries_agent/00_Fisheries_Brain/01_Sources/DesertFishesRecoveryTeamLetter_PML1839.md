# Desert Fishes Recovery Team Letter on Genetic Integrity of Endangered Fish Hatchery Stocks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #cyprinidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This October 1985 letter from the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] (DFRT) leader to [[Michael Spear]], Regional Director of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in Albuquerque, addresses the maintenance of genetic integrity in hatchery stocks of endangered and threatened desert fishes. The letter summarizes prior genetic research, reports the Team's concerns and recommendations, and asks the Region to adopt specific safeguards before large-scale [[Reintroduction]] or stocking proceeds. Its central point is that small founder populations, unintentional and intentional selection in hatcheries, and possible [[Hybridization]] or [[Inbreeding]] could reduce the fitness of reintroduced populations. The Team nonetheless judges that hatchery propagation remains preferable to extinction for critically endangered species.

## Background & Description

The letter reviews the genetic literature on several desert fishes:

- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*): Turner (1983) studied genetics of wild refugia and hatchery stocks and reported unusually high genetic variability in many populations. Hatchery stock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) proved identical to wild-caught fish, and evidence of significant hybrid influence was not detected. Echelle et al. (1984) evaluated potential genetic changes under hatchery conditions in several other pupfishes at DNFH and found no significant change.
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*): The DNFH stock originated from wild adults captured over a 10-year period at [[Lake Mohave]].
- **[[Chihuahua Chub]]** (*Gila nigrescens*): The stock was founded from about 10 wild adults, a small number that the Team considered a serious genetic concern.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*): A stock was established from about 10 individuals collected from the White River system.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): Uijenhoek et al. (1985) criticized a stocking program because the DNFH stock showed lower heterozygosity than other available stocks. The DFRT notes that greater heterozygosity generally indicates higher fitness, and that homozygous populations may be less able to adapt when reintroduced.

The DFRT's 3–3 meeting (minutes referenced in the letter) discussed these issues and produced cautionary guidance relevant to current and projected programs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter stresses that small founder populations carry several linked risks:

- **[[Genetic Drift]]:** The original genetic composition of small hatchery stocks may be altered over generations.
- **[[Unintentional Selection]]:** Differential survival of individuals in hatchery culture, transport, or handling may favor genotypes that adjust to captivity, and those losses may be random or related to unknown factors.
- **[[Intentional Selection]]:** Staff may repeatedly select larger individuals for handling or artificial spawning, or reuse the same specimens year after year. The Team attributes this mainly to a lack of attention to detail rather than deliberate choice, and notes that the DNFH staff is aware of the problem and has been commendably responsive.
- **Volunteer production:** Volunteer production of bonytail and Chihuahua chub at DNFH ponds poses a further risk. A single pair can produce thousands of offspring, and reintroducing such highly homozygous progeny could alter the genetic composition of wild populations or contribute to species or population extinction.

For the bonytail, the Team considers the wild Lake Mohave population so small that reintroductions of volunteer-produced fish from DNFH can readily be justified. For the Chihuahua chub, the Team considers volunteer production inadvisable.

## Key Findings

1. **Genetic monitoring is needed.** The DFRT calls for careful maintenance of genetic integrity in hatchery stocks of endangered and threatened fishes, given the low founder numbers of several stocks.
2. **Hatchery stock quality varies.** Desert pupfish stock matches wild fish, but Gila topminnow stock has lower heterozygosity than alternative stocks, and small founder numbers raise concern for Chihuahua chub and roundtail chub.
3. **Small size is preferable to extinction.** The Team acknowledges that small captive stocks are less than ideal but argues that for critically endangered species, they are better than extinction.
4. **Selection must be controlled.** Intentional and unintentional selection in hatchery breeding should be avoided, and breeding adults should be drawn from pre-established, genetically representative lines.
5. **Volunteer production should be restricted.** Volunteer production of Chihuahua chub is judged inadvisable. Reintroduction of volunteer-produced bonytail is justified given the small wild population.
6. **Recommendations for the Region** (summarized from the letter's three numbered concerns and final suggestions):
   - Manual spawning should be carried out at least once before reintroduction of the Chihuahua chub.
   - More wild fish should be collected and added to the DNFH broodstock before large-scale reintroductions are considered.
   - Hatchery stocks should maintain genetic integrity through breeding protocols that prevent genetic drift and inbreeding.
   - Reintroduced populations should be designated as an *experimental nonessential* population under the Endangered Species Act, so that management flexibility is maintained.
   - A genetic management plan and pedigree-based breeding system should be implemented immediately.
   - An advisory group of genetic and hatchery specialists should be formed to assist the effort.
7. **Cooperation.** The DFRT offers to help form and expedite the advisory group and the proposed programs.

Signed by the DFRT Leader, with copies relevant to Regional genetics and hatchery management.
