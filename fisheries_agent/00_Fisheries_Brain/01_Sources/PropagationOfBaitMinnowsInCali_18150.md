# Propagation of Bait Minnows in California

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Cyprinidae #bait-fish #pond-management

## Executive Summary

This 1956 administrative report (revised 1960) by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] provides comprehensive guidance on commercial bait minnow culture in California. The document focuses on three introduced cyprinid species—[[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), and [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*)—selected to improve forage for game fish and supply the bait market. It covers pond design, water quality requirements, spawning protocols, feeding and fertilization regimes, disease prevention, harvest methods, and regulatory frameworks. The report emphasizes that these non-native minnows are suitable for California's warmwater fisheries and would not harm existing aquatic ecosystems.

## Background & Description

### Species Introductions and Rationale

California historically lacked suitable native bait fishes. Three cyprinid species were introduced to serve dual purposes: forage for warmwater game fish and commercial bait supply. All three are members of the family [[Cyprinidae]].

**[[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)**  
- Morphology: Heavy, blunt head; spine-like first dorsal ray; reaches ~3 inches  
- Sexual dimorphism: Males larger with tubercles on head during breeding  
- Spawning: Begins at 64°F (March–May), continues to August; adhesive eggs deposited on undersides of boards, rocks, stumps  
- Fecundity: 200–500 eggs per female; single female may spawn 12 times in 11 weeks (up to 4,000 eggs total)  
- Production: Up to 300 pounds per acre under ideal conditions  
- Brood stock: 1,000 adults per acre; 3:1 female-to-male ratio recommended  
- Life history: Short-lived; suitable for bait in second year; males guard eggs (incubation 5–6 days)

**[[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*)**  
- Morphology: Silver or golden coloration, small mouth, deep body; reaches 3 inches by end of first year, up to 8 inches maximum  
- Sexual dimorphism: Females larger than males  
- Spawning: March–July on plants, sticks, debris, or artificial spawning mats (upholstering fiber or Spanish moss on wire grids, 3 ft² × 4 inches deep, anchored 4 inches below surface)  
- Production: Up to 200,000 fish per acre documented  
- Brood stock: 500–1,000 fish per half-acre pond  
- Handling: More delicate than fathead; scales fall off easily; jumps frequently when seined; requires care during warm-weather transport

**[[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*)**  
- Morphology: Dark back, silvery-blue sides, red fins; reaches ~3 inches; males display orange and red coloration on head and body during breeding  
- Origin: Stream fish that adapts readily to pond culture  
- Spawning: Broadcast eggs over coarse sand or gravel in 3–5 feet depth; also utilizes sticks, debris, algae, and shallow sandy bottoms  
- Management: Similar to golden shiner

### Regulatory Framework

- **Domestic Fish Breeder's License** required for all pond operators  
- Additional regulations govern capture, possession, transportation, and sale of live freshwater bait fish  
- Reference materials: [[Inland Fisheries Informational Leaflets]] Numbers 7 and 8 (available from regional offices)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Water Quality Requirements

**Physical-Chemical Parameters:**  
- pH: Slightly alkaline preferred; caution advised for pH <7 or >8  
- Dissolved oxygen: Minimum 4 ppm; drops during high temperatures, crowding, fertilization, nighttime, cloudy days, and organic decay  
- Temperature: Summer surface temperatures should reach 70°F for successful spawning and growth  
- Alkalinity: Alkaline waters more productive than acidic waters  
- Contaminants: No tolerance for suspended silt, mud, or industrial pollution

**Water Sources:**  
- Spring water: Best source  
- Pumped well water: May lack oxygen, contain excess CO₂ and nitrogen  
- City mains: Expensive, often chlorinated  
- Irrigation district sources: May be suitable  
- Stream diversions: Problematic due to winter flooding, uncertain summer flows, and introduction of wild fish/parasites  
- Filtration: Sand and gravel filters recommended; only effective method to prevent fish/parasite movement between water sources

### Pond Design and Management

**Recommended Specifications:**  
- Size: Multiple small ponds (¼–⅓ acre) preferred over single large pond for easier management, higher productivity, species separation, and grading  
- Dimensions: Narrow, rectangular shape; 4–5 feet depth at outlet, sloping bottom  
- Banks: 3:1 slope to discourage emergent vegetation ([[Cattails]], rushes)  
- Infrastructure: Independent drainage and water supply for each pond; complete drainage capability; truck road access along all banks  
- Example configuration: 10 ponds (⅓ acre) + 5 ponds (¼ acre) + several smaller ponds (~1/10 acre)

**Fertilization and Feeding:**  
- Fertilization increases natural food (microscopic plants and animals)  
- Commercial fertilizer: 8-8-4 (N-P-K) at 100 lbs/acre; target visibility reduced to 12 inches  
- California-specific: 16-20-0 may be suitable (potash often naturally available in soil)  
- Caution: Over-fertilization causes heavy algal blooms; decay reduces oxygen, potentially killing fish  
- Supplemental feed: Prepared pellets or mixture of 1 lb soybean meal + 3 lbs wheat shorts; 5 lbs/acre/day  
- Winter feeding: 5–30 lbs/day/acre (November–May) brings small fish to market size by spring  
- Protein content: 25–35% for effective feeding  
- Winter stocking density: Up to 150,000 minnows per acre

### Aquatic Vegetation Control

**Problem Plants:**  
- Filamentous algae ("moss"): Clogs screens, uses oxygen during cloudy days/night  
- Planktonic algae: Single-celled; causes green water; decay uses oxygen  
- Emergent plants: [[Cattails]], bulrushes, willows interfere with access, reduce open water  
- Submerged rooted plants: Pondweed, coontail, parrot feather, plantain, smartweed obstruct seining, trap fish

**Control Methods:**  
- Copper sulfate: 2 lbs per million gallons for algae; extended treatment during hot weather to prevent acute decay  
- Cattails: Hand-cutting of spring growth; spray with 25% 2,4-D in oil or 2,4-D + 2,4-5-T combination  
- Submerged weeds: Sodium arsenite at 4 ppm (11 lbs arsenic trioxide per acre-foot); spring fertilization to create light-blocking algal blooms (12-inch visibility prevents submerged plant growth)  
- Mechanical: Cutting before harvest

## Key Findings

### Production and Harvest

**Harvest Methods:**  
- Seining: Requires 20-ft seine (¼-inch mesh, 4 ft deep), 40-ft seine (½-inch mesh, 4 ft deep), 100-ft seine (¼-inch mesh, 6 ft deep)  
- Grading: Grading boxes with spaced dowels separate market-size fish from small fish  
- Live cars: Hardware cloth boxes (2.5 × 2.5 × 4 ft) for temporary holding  
- Timing: Regular grading prevents crowding, maintains spawning and growth rates  
- Draining: Complete drainage required; avoid leaving pools

**Transportation and Handling:**  
- Temperature: Avoid >10°F rapid change (will kill/injure fish); temper water gradually  
- Salt addition: 2 oz NaCl per 10 gallons improves condition during transport  
- Aeration: Essential when crowding; rule of thumb: 1 gallon water per 1 pound fish with aeration  
- Timing: Schedule long trips at night, during cloudy/cool periods; use ice in hot weather  
- Pre-shipment: Discontinue feeding 1–2 days before transport  
- Equipment: Compressed air, motor-driven air pumps, recirculating pumps, or surface beaters (small tanks ≤75 gallons)

**Retail Holding:**  
- Tank temperature: Ideal 60°F; refrigeration or ground-insulated cement tanks recommended  
- Flow rate: 1 gallon/minute per 50 gallons tank volume in crowded conditions  
- Capacity: Inversely related to temperature; lower temperature = higher poundage capacity  
- Tank materials: Redwood good; steel poor (no insulation); avoid galvanized pipes/fittings and chlorinated water in closed systems

### Disease and Parasite Management

**Fungus Disease (*Saprolegnia* spp.):**  
- Cause: Mold-like organism; requires physical injury (handling, scale loss, mucus removal)  
- Symptoms: Gray or white tufted patches  
- Treatment: (1) 3% salt dip until distress; (2) 1:2,000 copper sulfate for 1 minute; (3) 1:10,000 malachite green (zinc-free) for 10 seconds  
- Prevention: Routine post-handling dip in copper sulfate, salt, or malachite green

**Columnaris Disease (*Flexibacter columnaris*):**  
- Cause: Bacterium; outbreaks follow handling, especially in warm weather  
- Symptoms: White/gray patches (not fuzzy) with reddish border; frayed, ragged fins; gill involvement; death within 2 days  
- Treatment: Same as fungus disease; sulfamerazine in feed (6 grams per 100 lbs fish daily until losses cease)  
- Prevention: 1:2,000 copper sulfate dip for 1 minute post-handling

**Black Spot (Black Grub):**  
- Cause: Larval flukes (digenetic trematodes); requires snails as intermediate host  
- Symptoms: Black cysts giving speckled appearance  
- Treatment: No effective treatment for infected fish  
- Prevention: Eliminate snails via pond drying or chlorine gas (10 ppm overnight, fish removed) or copper sulfate (20 ppm, fish removed)

**Anchor Worm (*Lernaea* spp.):**  
- Cause: Parasitic copepod; visible without magnification  
- Symptoms: Slender, straw-like organism extending from body; lesions and tumors at attachment point  
- Prevention: Purchase clean brood stock  
- Treatment: Chlorine (as for black spot) or benzene hexachloride (BHC) at 1:10,000,000 (1 ppm active ingredient); Lindane 99% BHC, Lexone 63% BHC

**Predators and Pests:**  
- Insects (diving beetles, backswimmers): Control with kerosene spray (5 gallons/acre surface) plus 1 gallon gasoline + 2 quarts lubricating oil; repeat in 2 weeks  
- Tadpoles: 1 lb Paris green + 7–8 lbs cottonseed meal in shallow water (will kill fish)  
- Crayfish: BHC spray at 1.5 ppm (4–5 lbs/acre-foot); 1 application/week for 3 weeks  
- Snakes, turtles, muskrats: Keep levees weed-free; shoot or trap as needed  
- Birds (heron, kingfisher): Rarely serious; Federal permit required for shooting  
- Undesirable fish (goldfish, carp, [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]]): Enter via unfiltered creek water or contaminated brood stock; predators consume minnows; control via quarantine, inspection, pond draining, or rotenone (5% at 3 lbs/acre-foot; wait weeks before restocking)

**Sterilization Protocols:**  
- Equipment: Dip nets, live cars, seines, grading equipment, tanks, boots in Clorox/Purex solution (1 quart per 50 gallons water) for 30 minutes; rinse and dry  
- Tanks: Fill with water + Clorox/Purex; flush well after treatment  
- Ponds: Sodium hypochlorite powder spread on drained bottom; refill to 10 ppm chlorine for 24+ hours  
- Monitoring: Hellige comparator or orthotolidine solution (deep yellow/brown indicates sufficient chlorine)  
- Fish toxicity: 0.5–2 ppm chlorine kills fish

### Market and Economic Considerations

**Primary Markets:**  
- [[Central Valley]] (California)  
- [[Colorado River]] region  
- Market selection determined by rearing station location

**Production Economics:**  
- [[Fathead Minnow]]: Up to 300 lbs/acre  
- [[Golden Shiner]]: Up to 200,000 fish/acre  
- Fertilization and feeding increase production but add costs  
- Multiple small ponds more productive than single large pond  
- Regular grading and harvest maintain optimal stocking density

### Water Equivalents and Calculations

**Key Conversions:**  
- 1 gallon = 8.33 lbs water  
- 1 cubic foot = 7.48 gallons = 62.4 lbs water  
- 1 acre-foot = 43,560 cubic feet = 325,850 gallons  
- 1 ppm = 8.33 lbs per million gallons water  

**Pond Volume Formula:**  
(Length in feet × Width in feet × Average depth in feet) ÷ 43,560 = acre-feet  
Acre-feet × 0.3 = million gallons  
Million gallons × 8.33 = pounds chemical for 1 ppm

**Treatment Calculations:**  
All treatments based on 75°F water temperature; adjust chemical amount or exposure time if temperature exceeds 75°F to avoid fish losses.
