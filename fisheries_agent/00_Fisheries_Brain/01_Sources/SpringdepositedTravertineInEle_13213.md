# Spring-Deposited Travertine in Eleven Western States

#hydrogeology #geochemistry #travertine #carbonate-deposits

## Executive Summary

This USGS Water-Resources Investigations Open-File Report (79-35, by J. H. Feth and Ivan Barnes, 1979) compiles and interprets occurrences of [[Spring-Deposited Travertine]] across eleven western states. The authors identified more than 300 deposits from the [[Front Range]] to the Pacific coast, with a few in the Appalachians and scattered examples in British Columbia and Alaska. The report summarizes the chemistry of travertine-depositing waters, discusses uncertainties in location and activity records, and synthesizes formation mechanisms. Its main conclusions are that deposition is controlled by CO₂ loss from supersaturated water rather than by evaporation or spring temperature, and that travertine occurrences are strongly associated with active faults and tectonically active belts.

## Background & Description

**Definition and scope.** The authors define travertine as a carbonate-mineral deposit dominated by calcium carbonate and formed through spring water. The term includes tufa, onyx marble, calcareous sinter, and calcareous spring deposits. Carbonate deposits in caves, shore-formed deposits, and bioherms are excluded, except where algae help precipitate calcium carbonate from spring water.

**Why it matters.** Extinct travertine terraces lie hundreds of meters above present spring activity. The authors argue they could reveal ancient hydrologic systems, downcutting rates, and tectonic uplift rates, a potential that remains largely unexploited. Examples include the Pinnacles at Pyramid Lake, Nevada, and more than 500 pinnacles at [[Searles Lake]], California, which range from 3 to 45 m high. Some spring orifices at these sites lie more than 30 m above present land surface, implying hydrostatic heads that have vanished.

**Age and dating.** Travertine of [[Eocene]] age has been reported, but most inferred ages are Pleistocene or younger, suggesting the deposits are geologically transient. Biologic material in travertine can supply carbon for [[Radiocarbon Dating]], but ¹⁴C ages are unreliable if carbonate has been redeposited from older limestone.

**Structural association.** Many deposits lie on or near faults, so travertine in otherwise featureless alluvial valleys may indicate a hidden fault. The authors consider the relation of travertine to geologic structure, geomorphology, and Pleistocene hydrology a neglected area of study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Deposits are sparse east of the Rocky Mountains but numerous from the Front Range to the Pacific. Clusters occur along the [[Wasatch Fault]] and related tectonic areas in Utah and southern Idaho, and near the fault zone at the eastern boundary of the [[Sierra Nevada]] in California. Travertine is scarce north of latitude 45°, though latitude is not considered a controlling factor. The authors judge the distribution to be largely real rather than an artifact of survey intensity, since statewide spring surveys covered seven of the eleven states, including Oregon and Washington.

**Rock associations.** Sedimentary terrain predominates, but travertine also occurs in volcanic and granitic terrain, with notable abundance in those settings. Figure-based frequency data show sedimentary (shale and sandstone) and limestone or marble settings as most common, followed by volcanic, granitic, metamorphic, and serpentine or ultramafic rocks.

**Temperature.** Actively depositing springs range from 5 °C to 96 °C, with the greatest concentration between 10 °C and 30 °C. The authors conclude that deposition does not depend on spring temperature.

**Water chemistry.** Chemical water types do not organize deposition: scattergrams "result in shotgun patterns," and dissolved solids span orders of magnitude. A table of 42 representative travertine-depositing waters with reasonably complete analyses reports concentration ranges, medians, and means for major cations and anions, including HCO₃⁻ + CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, and Cl⁻. The authors note that Ca and carbonate values are probably underestimated because CaCO₃ precipitates between the orifice and the lab.

**Representative sites.** Among the inventory's listed springs, flow rates and deposits include:
- Blue Spring, Arizona (Little Colorado River): reported "large flow" of more than 2,600 L/s, causing extensive channel deposits.
- Havasu Spring, Grand Canyon, Arizona: about 1,900 L/s, with travertine forming in Hermit Creek.
- Fossil Creek Springs, Arizona: about 1,180 L/s, with an inactive terrace roughly 1.5 km long.
- Montezuma Well, Arizona: discharge deposits nearly 90 kg of CaCO₃ per day.
- Big Horn Hot Springs, Wyoming: about 820 L/s, with terraces 12 m and up to 210 m above the river.
- Mammoth Hot Springs, Yellowstone National Park: a major travertine system cited as a comparison standard.
- Searles Lake, California: more than 500 Pleistocene pinnacles formed beneath lake water.

**Data reliability.** The authors flag three sources of uncertainty:
1. **Location:** Many locations were derived from older text descriptions and map transfers, so some are interpreted rather than exact.
2. **Activity:** Some reports do not state whether deposition is occurring, and springs change in both discharge and depositional capacity.
3. **Time:** Discharge and temperature values are "as of" the visit date, some dating to the late 19th century, and current conditions may differ.

## Key Findings

**1. Deposition is controlled by CO₂ loss, not evaporation.** Supersaturation can arise from CO₂ loss to air, CO₂ gain from air, mixing of incompatible waters, or CO₂ uptake by photosynthesis. Stable isotope evidence from [[Friedman (1970)]] showed evaporation was slight at a hot spring, with deposition driven by CO₂ loss to air. Fractionation of ¹³C between solution and travertine decreased from 4.8 ‰ at 75 °C to 0 ‰ at 20 °C.

**2. Mantle-derived CO₂ at Pescadero Creek.** At a spring issuing from Franciscan greywacke along the [[Sargent Fault]] in Santa Clara County, California, the water has a CO₂ partial pressure of 35.5 kPa, against about 0.03 kPa in air. Its δ¹³C of −7.29 ‰ is typical of mantle-derived CO₂. Magnesian calcite precipitates as CO₂ escapes. The high silica and magnesium concentrations suggest reaction of CO₂-rich metamorphic water with serpentinite at depth. The travertine (−0.06 ‰ δ¹³C) would falsely suggest a marine carbon source if considered alone.

**3. Radiocarbon ages can be spuriously young.** Because the dissolved carbon is out of isotopic equilibrium, ¹⁴CO₂ enters solution from the air. Apparent ages of 20,500 ± 450 years B.P. (SrCO₃) and 14,670 years B.P. (travertine) are reported in the Pescadero Creek table, illustrating how dating errors arise.

**4. Serpentinization waters.** Low-temperature serpentine formation yields waters with pH up to 12.0 and up to 53 mg/L Ca²⁺, with no detectable carbonate. These waters gain CO₂ from air and deposit aragonite, calcite, or both, with a kinetic ¹³C fractionation of about 10 ‰.

**5. Mixing of incompatible waters forms solid solutions.** Travertines from mixing Mg²⁺-HCO₃⁻-rich and Ca²⁺-rich waters contain calcite ranging from CaCO₃ to Ca₀.₅Mg₀.₅CO₃ and dolomite near Ca₀.₅Mg₀.₅CO₃. Up to 10% of Mg²⁺ may occur as the MgHCO₃⁺ ion pair, which is attracted to crystal surfaces and enables growth of Mg-rich carbonates in dilute, low-temperature water.

**6. Stream travertine is common.** Calcium carbonate leached from rocks by CO₂-rich groundwater reprecipitates when CO₂ degasses after entering a stream channel. A study of Birch Creek, Inyo County, California (Barnes, 1965), found CO₂ loss was almost entirely due to photosynthesis, with evaporation less than detectable. Deposits form rims on obstructions, creating a series of pools separated by travertine dams.

**7. Lithology sets the calcium supply.** CO₂-rich water in granitic terrain does not dissolve silicate calcium, so travertine in silicate rock implies disseminated or subsurface carbonate (marble, limestone, or other calcareous material) as the calcium source.

**8. Temperature is not a control.** Deposition does not depend on spring temperature, so evaporation is not an important cause.

**9. Spatial pattern.** More than 300 deposits are identified, clustered along major faults and seismic belts. The authors conclude the overall distribution is real.

**10. Research gaps.** The authors call for further study of travertine relative to geologic structure, geomorphology, relative ages, and Pleistocene hydrology, noting that paleohydrologic, uplift, and downcutting applications remain largely untapped.
