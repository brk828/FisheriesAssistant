# Protractile Apparatus of the Mouth in Catostomid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a classic functional-anatomy paper on the protrusible mouth of the [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), a family of freshwater bottom-feeding fishes. Using dissection, histology and electrical stimulation, the author concludes that mouth protrusion is produced by the same muscle movements that open the jaws in other teleosts, and that its extent depends on the anatomy of the [[Premaxillary]] spine, its ligaments, and the shape of the [[Mandible]]. The document also contains a second, truncated paper on cutaneous reddening and swelling around the genitalia of the rhesus monkey, which lies outside the aquatic scope of this note.

## Background & Description

Suckers are described as among the best-known freshwater fishes, identified by a [[Protractile Mouth]], fleshy sucker-like lips and toothless jaws. They are bottom feeders that eat vegetation, small [[Mollusca]], [[Entomostraca]], worms, aquatic insect larvae, eggs and mud, the last ingested for its organic matter. Feeding involves drawing the mouth downward and forward like a spout and taking food in by suction. The literature reviewed by the author contained no detail on this mechanism beyond what is known for fishes with protractile premaxillaries.

The study was undertaken at the suggestion of Prof. Raymond C. Osburn to identify the factors behind the characteristic protrusion. Six sucker species were dissected:

- [[Common Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*)
- [[Hogsucker]] (*Catostomus nigricans*)
- [[Common Redhorse]] (*Moxostoma aureolum*)
- White-nosed sucker (*Moxostoma anisurum*)
- Spotted sucker (*Minytrema melanops*)
- Chubsucker (*Erimyzon sucetta oblongus*)

For comparison, the skulls of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and the [[Stoneroller Minnow]] (*Campostoma anomalum*), which have sucking mouths but less modified ones, were also dissected, along with several other teleost families. Three methods were used: dissection, longitudinal and cross-sections of the lips for microscopy, and electrical stimulation with an induction coil.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is a laboratory anatomy study and contains no field data, population counts, water chemistry or habitat measurements. Its ecological relevance lies in the feeding biology it describes: the suction-feeding, bottom-dwelling habit of suckers is linked directly to the anatomy of the jaws and mouth. The author notes that differences in mouth shape, size and protrusion "largely depend upon differences of habit and food," citing [[Boulenger]] (1904).

## Key Findings

**Anatomy of the jaws**
- The premaxillary spine is attached to the preethmoid (rostral process) by an elastic ligament, rather than gliding in a groove on the ethmoid as in most teleosts. The spine is flexible, bending nearly vertical on retraction (ligament slack) and horizontal on protraction (ligament taut).
- The premaxillary is not attached to the palatines, which allows greater upper-jaw movement.
- The premaxillary connects below to the ventral tip of the [[Maxillary]] by a tendinous ligament. A cartilaginous rod links the maxillary's posterior condyle to the [[Vomer]], forming a movable joint.
- The mandible is peculiarly shaped: it runs forward and then turns nearly at right angles to the vertical at the angle of the mouth, giving a deep overhanging dentary. This permits more pronounced protrusion.
- The upper and lower jaws both attach to the [[Hyomandibular]] suspensorium, so the two move together.

**Muscles involved in protrusion and retraction**
- [[Geniohyoideus]]: paired muscle that depresses the mandible and opens the mouth. Its action is assisted by the [[Sternohyoideus]], the main retractor of the hyoid.
- [[Protractor Hyomandibularis]]: contraction thrusts the hyomandibular, and with it the upper and lower jaws, forward.
- [[Adductor Mandibulae]]: retracts the jaws and closes the mouth. In suckers it is divided into three fasciculi, which differ from the arrangement described in perch:
  - Adductor 1 retracts the upper jaw by pulling the maxillary posteriorly.
  - Adductor 2 closes the mouth.
  - Adductor 3 pulls the mouth angle posteriorly.
- Suction feeding relies on alternate enlargement and constriction of the oral cavity, using the protractor hyomandibularis, geniohyoideus, sternohyoideus and [[Levator Arcus Palatini]] for enlargement, and the adductor muscles and [[Adductor Hyomandibularis]] for constriction.

**Mechanism of protrusion**
- Depressing the mandible draws the premaxillary down and forward through tension on the ligament joining the jaws at the mouth angle.
- Three factors facilitate protrusion: the loose premaxillary-ethmoid ligament, the peculiar mandible, and the action of the protractor hyomandibularis.

**Experimental and histological results**
- Stimulating the sucker-like lips before and after severing the trigeminal nerve produced no protrusion, suggesting that the lips contain no muscle fibers that drive the movement.
- Stimulating the geniohyoideus and sternohyoideus thrust the premaxillary downward and forward. Stimulating the adductor mandibulae retracted the jaws.
- Lip sections stained with Delafield's hematoxylin with eosin counterstain and with Van Gieson's stain showed no muscle tissue.

**Conclusions and summary**
- The author concludes that protrusion uses the same movements that open the jaws in other fishes. The bones are anatomically identical but differ morphologically, and the muscles differ little except in attachment.
- The nine-point summary restates these results: the distinct premaxillary spine and ligament, the absence of a premaxillary-palatine link, the movable maxillary-vomer joint, the modified mandible, the modified adductor mandibulae, and the verification of muscle roles by stimulation.
- The bibliography lists about 15 references, mostly early 20th-century comparative anatomy works, including Owen (1866), Boulenger (1904), Jordan (1904) and Osburn (1901).
