# Distribution, Biology and Status of the Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta) in the Gila River Basin, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado State University master's thesis (Kevin R. Bestgen, Fall 1985) reports a two-year study (December 1982 to December 1984) of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*, treated as *G. r. grahami*) in the [[Gila River]] drainage of New Mexico. The study documented historic and present distribution and abundance, systematics, habitat preferences, food habits, reproduction, age and growth, and interactions with [[Introduced Species]].

The species has declined substantially. It is presumed [[Extirpation|extirpated]] from the [[San Francisco River]], and it has declined sharply in the Gila River below [[Mogollon Creek]]. The populations above Mogollon Creek appear stable. The main cause identified is predation by non-native piscivores, especially [[Smallmouth Bass]]. The thesis also argues that a proposed dam on the Gila River would worsen the decline.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Gila* is a genus of 15 recognized species (Robins et al. 1980). The roundtail chub complex has four subspecies within the Colorado River drainage. The New Mexico population is referred to as *G. r. grahami*, which is nearly endemic to the Gila River system. Its taxonomic status is still under study, and the morphometric and meristic data were "variable" with "no taxonomic conclusions" drawn.
- **Conservation status:** The species appears on rare fish lists for Arizona and New Mexico, but little was known about its New Mexico biology before this study.
- **Study design:** Fish were collected mainly by [[Electrofishing]], with seines, gillnets, dipnets and angling. Five geographic subgroups were examined. Permanent sites were sampled repeatedly. [[Turkey Creek]] was added as a permanent collection site on 5 May 1983 because it was the only known population that could withstand large removals.
- **Ageing:** [[Scale Ageing]] was the only calcified structure judged reliable. Back-calculated lengths used a correction factor, and the author noted doubts about the reliability of scale-based ages for old fish.
- **Historical context:** The region has a long human history, including Spanish exploration, mining and cattle ranching. Arroyo cutting beginning around 1890 lowered water tables, reduced streamflow and removed riparian vegetation. The author attributes the habitat loss mainly to overgrazing and considers it the ultimate cause of wide-scale habitat change.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Gila River rises on the Mogollon Plateau and flows about 177 km through New Mexico, draining roughly 8,296 km². Elevations range from 3,320 m to 1,158 m. Most headwater reaches are in the [[Gila National Forest]] and the Gila Wilderness. The San Francisco River drains about 4,500 km² of New Mexico.

**Hydrology.** Both rivers have highly variable, arid-land flow regimes. Flow in the Gila River near Virden averages 5.24 m³/s, with a peak of 1,660 m³/s (19 December 1978) and a record low of 0.028 m³/s. Irrigation diversions near Cliff and in the Redrock and Virden valleys have often dried the channel. Flow was perennial throughout the study period. Water temperatures range from near 0°C to 32°C, with diel fluctuations of up to 15°C.

**Turkey Creek.** This steep-walled canyon stream is a critical refuge. Perennial flow extends 19 km upstream of the confluence. Deep bedrock pools exceed 3 m in depth. A 3 m barrier falls isolates the upper population, and 74°C hot groundwater enters just below it, creating a cool-water barrier to downstream fish movement.

**Vegetation and climate.** Riparian vegetation is dominated by Fremont cottonwood, Arizona sycamore, velvet ash and mesquite. Precipitation ranges from about 22 cm to more than 75 cm per year, with more than half falling in July through September.

**Associated species.** Twenty-two fish species were captured. Native species include longfin dace, spikedace, [[Loach Minnow]], speckled dace, Sonora sucker and desert sucker. The [[Gila Trout]] was not collected but occurs in high tributaries. Introduced predators and competitors include several catfish, sunfish, bass and trout species (see Key Findings).

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Abundance
- The study conducted 347 collections at 185 sites and captured 69,340 fishes. Roundtail chubs were captured in 52 (15%) collections from 29 localities, totaling 857 individuals.
- Of the 857 chubs, 590 (69%) came from Turkey Creek (11 collections at 3 sites), 138 (16%) from the Middle Fork of the Gila River (3 localities), and 129 (15%) from the mainstream Gila and East and West Forks (31 collections at 23 localities).
- Chubs occupy almost exclusively mainstream and large tributary habitat with deep pools, plus Turkey Creek.
- **San Francisco River:** Not collected since 1948 (20 specimens near Frisco Hotsprings, 1 near Reserve). The species is presumed extirpated.
- **Above Mogollon Creek:** Occurrence was 71% (historic to 1960), 30% (1961–1982) and 54% in the present study (45% adjusted to remove bias from repeated Middle Fork sampling).
- **Below Mogollon Creek:** Occurrence fell from 59% (historic to 1960) to 18% (1961–1982) and 17% in the present study. Chubs averaged fewer than 3 individuals per productive sample. Historic collections yielded up to 71 fish (1949), and the largest recent collection was 17 fish.
- The Gila River population has been reduced to small fish. Only four chubs over 200 mm were found below Mogollon Creek. The East Fork population had only one chub under 175 mm, and historic collections showed a range down to 39 mm SL.
- The author estimates a decline in range of about 20% in the Gila River.

### Systematics and Morphology
- Morphometric and meristic traits were highly variable among five subgroups and within subspecies.
- Chubs from Hardin Cienega Creek, Arizona, keyed to *G. intermedia* on fin ray and scale counts and the HL/CPD ratio. Their taxonomic placement was considered uncertain.
- The Turkey Creek population trended toward a thicker body and lower meristic counts, possibly due to isolation above the barrier falls.

### Habitat Preferences
- Adult chubs (over 120 mm TL) occupied deep, slow pools near banks (mean depth 64 cm, mean velocity 15 cm/s, mean distance to bank 0.3 m), usually over sand or sand-gravel substrate.
- Small chubs (under 95 mm TL) used shallower, slower water near the bank (mean depth 32 cm, velocity 6 cm/s, distance to bank 0.5 m).
- After a major October 1983 flood, small chubs were found in shallow riffles away from the bank (mean distance 4 m, velocity 19 cm/s, gravel-cobble substrate), suggesting flexibility when optimal habitat is lost.
- Riparian tree loss, grazing and channelization reduce the deep pools that chubs require.

### Food Habits
- 199 stomachs were examined (50–344 mm TL). Chubs were primarily insectivorous, with algae, gastropods, crayfish and fish also eaten.
- Chubs in the Gila River had low stomach fullness (about 13% for fish under 100 mm).
- The gut length to standard length ratio (0.99:1.0, n = 56) suggested carnivory.
- Comparison with syntopic rainbow and cutthroat hybrid trout (n = 13) implied that chubs are more selective. Trout ate a wider range of terrestrial invertebrates, and no competition was evident because chubs were dense and healthy.

### Reproduction
- Gonads cycle annually, with spawning in late spring and early summer. Spawning timing varied by more than a month between 1983 and 1984.
- Temperature appears to be the most consistent spawning cue. Ripe fish were found at 22°C on 3 May 1983, and the author cites an 18°C threshold from earlier work.
- Turkey Creek fish matured at a younger age and smaller size than mainstream fish. Turkey Creek females (n = 10) averaged 3.2 years, 152 mm TL and 5,449 eggs. East Fork females averaged 5.2 years, 306 mm TL and 33,440 eggs.
- [[Fecundity]] ranged from 600 to 13,546 in Turkey Creek and from 24,812 to 45,125 in the East Fork. Fecundity is strongly related to total length.
- The author proposes that Turkey Creek's early maturation offsets high early mortality in a small habitat, while larger Gila River fish are favored by high fecundity.

### Age and Growth
- Of 464 Turkey Creek fish that could be aged, 17% were Age 0, 52% Age I, 19% Age II, 5% Age III, 5% Age IV and less than 1% Age V.
- Turkey Creek fish grew more slowly and reached a maximum age two years less than Gila River fish (Gila River fish reached VII+).
- Turkey Creek fish showed a pattern described as a reversed [[Lee's Phenomenon]]. Back-calculated lengths declined with age in some cohorts, and the cause remains unknown.
- The author interprets the life-history differences as consistent with the [[Habitat Size Hypothesis]] (G. R. Smith 1981).

### Interactions with Introduced Species
- Predatory and potentially competing introduced fishes include [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Red Shiner]].
- Smallmouth bass have reduced or eliminated chubs in at least two localities. In the East Fork, the chub population is now composed only of large adults, and native cyprinids once common there have disappeared.
- Below the "keyhole" barrier falls in Turkey Creek, at least one large smallmouth bass occurs in every pool, and bass are reproducing. The continued survival of the upper Turkey Creek population is considered tenuous.
- Flathead catfish were the largest predator recorded (665 mm, nearly 6 kg). Most adults had empty stomachs, suggesting nocturnal feeding or rapid digestion.
- Predation is considered the primary cause of historic chub reductions, but direct evidence is limited because predation is hard to document after a species has declined.

### Conservation Conclusions and Threats
- Only Turkey Creek and the Middle Fork support populations the author considers plentiful, and even these are tenuous.
- A proposed large dam on the Gila River near the Middle Box, upstream of Redrock, would inundate about 20% of the habitat occupied by roundtail chubs. It would also create a reservoir for predatory fishes with access to the river.
- The Gila River in New Mexico supports seven native species (eight with the Gila trout) and is one of the last free-flowing major river segments in the southwest.
- The author concludes that the species will persist if natural attributes are preserved, and that alteration of the river and its biota would lead to further decline and eventual loss.
