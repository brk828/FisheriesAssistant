# Age and Growth of Channel Catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) in the Salt River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the age and growth of introduced [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) in the upper [[Salt River]] of central Arizona, a remote, nearly natural reach above [[Roosevelt Lake]]. Based on 66 fish collected in June and July 1972 using gill nets, seines and other methods at thirteen stations, the authors found that the population grew faster than channel catfish in most compared rivers and reservoirs, and that age groups II and III dominated the sample. Growth was attributed to a long growing season and high total dissolved solids (about 1000 mg/L), with the authors noting that sampling bias may have affected the age structure.

## Background & Description

The channel catfish is widespread in Arizona and heavily sought by anglers, yet had received little scientific study in the state. It is not native to Arizona; interviews indicate it was present in the [[Colorado River]] by 1906 and was introduced into the Salt River above Roosevelt Lake in 1938. Most prior growth studies came from lakes, reservoirs and rivers in other regions, whose climatic and edaphic conditions differ strongly from the Southwest.

The Salt River originates at the confluence of the Black and White Rivers, flows about 160 km through Upper and Lower [[Sonoran Desert]] and drops from roughly 1450 m to 700 m at Roosevelt Lake. It is then impounded in four contiguous reservoirs and diverted into canals serving the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]]. The upper river is remote, with no dams, diversions or nearby population centers.

**Methods:** Catfish were captured with monofilament gill nets (bar mesh 13–39 mm) at thirteen stations, and seined at all stations, with habitat under 1.5 m deep seined in replicate. Standard length was recorded and the left pectoral spine removed; ages were determined by annular ring counts following [[Sneed Method]] spine-preparation techniques, with age-group assignment following [[Tesch Age Classification]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Channel catfish occurred at all sites except Station 4, a fast-water pool; individuals there occupied deep, slow water.
- **Water quality:** Total dissolved solids near 1000 mg/L, which the authors suggest raises productivity and growth ([[Ryder Productivity Method]]).
- **Climate and season:** A longer growing season in the Southwest is proposed as a driver of rapid growth.
- **Predators:** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was reported to prey on catfish but is extinct or nearly so in the Salt River, so it is unlikely to affect population structure.
- **Hydrology:** The two dominant year classes hatched after peak discharges in December of the preceding years ([[Rinne Hydrology Study]]).

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 66 channel catfish were collected; 52 (about 79%) belonged to age groups II and III.
- **Age structure:** Young-of-the-year and yearling fish were seldom taken, possibly because they occupy water too deep to seine yet are too small for gill nets, or because of weak year-class strength in 1972.
- **Growth pattern:** Fish grew most rapidly in their first year and at a decreasing rate thereafter, consistent with the general U.S. pattern.
- **Comparative growth:** Mean length at age I exceeded that of all compared rivers except the [[Potomac River]]; by age III, Arizona fish were larger than Potomac fish.
- **Versus Colorado River:** Salt River fish were 15 to 40% longer at the same age than fish in the Colorado River under similar climate (the percentage range is as given in the source text), suggesting factors besides climate matter.
- **Versus reservoirs:** Growth and year-class sizes in midwestern and southeastern reservoirs were generally lower, with exceptions at a newly constructed reservoir and [[Reelfoot Lake]], Tennessee, both attributed to high productivity or "new water" conditions.
- **Supplementary sample:** A later collection of 30 fish (29 analyzed) was dominated by age groups II and III (24 fish), with four in age group IV and one of indeterminable age measuring 600 mm SL; 25 were taken by hook and line and five in trammel nets.
- **Sampling bias:** Gill nets may favor larger fish, and pectoral spine snagging may favor age groups II and III, whose spines have sharper recurved teeth.
- **Large individuals:** One fish of about 500 mm was seen, and remains of a very large catfish (age group VII or VIII) were found, suggesting older age groups were scarce.
- **Management note:** The authors argue fishing pressure is unlikely to explain the dominance of age groups II and III, since anglers typically keep fish large enough to eat.
- **Summary:** The upper Salt River population showed larger-than-average length at age and high growth rates, with age groups II and III most abundant; low numbers of age groups 0 and I may reflect either depressed year classes or sampling limitations.
