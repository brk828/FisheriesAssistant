# Origin, Status, and Future Management of the Cottonball Marsh Pupfish (*Cyprinodon milleri*) in Death Valley, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game report (Inland Fisheries Endangered Species Program Special Publication 80-4, August 1980, by [[Louis A. Courtois]]) reviews the origin, taxonomy, habitat, and status of the [[Cottonball Marsh Pupfish]], *Cyprinodon milleri*. The species is the most recently described cyprinodont of the [[Death Valley System]] and occupies a single habitat, [[Cottonball Marsh]], within [[Death Valley National Monument]]. The author concludes that the species should remain on the State list as "Rare," that its existing range is fully protected, and that continued habitat protection and periodic monitoring are the main management needs. A joint management program between the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] and the [[National Park Service]] is proposed.

## Background & Description

**Origin.** The pupfish is believed to have differentiated from a generalized progenitor that inhabited [[Lake Manly]], a large Pleistocene lake covering most of Death Valley. Drying and tilting of the valley floor over roughly the past 2,000 years isolated populations, and extreme environmental conditions at Cottonball Marsh drove further divergence. The species was first noted in the early 1960s (Hunt et al. 1966) and formally described by [[La Bounty and Deacon]] (1972) after more than six years of study.

**Physical description.** Data are taken from the holotype (UMMZ 188995), with a standard length of 28.1 mm. Key counts include:
- Fin rays: dorsal 8, anal 9, pectoral 15, caudal 15, pelvic 5
- Scales: lateral series 29, around body 40, around caudal peduncle 20, predorsal 28
- Gill rakers 19; vertebrae 27; preorbital pores 2; preopercular pores 14

**Sexual dimorphism.** Males are larger, with deeper bodies and heads, more anterior dorsal and pelvic fins, and a larger anal fin. Males show a blackened dorsal fin and deep blue sides with turquoise breeding coloration. Females show 7–8 narrower, wedge-shaped vertical bars, interrupted black blotches on the dorsal fin, and a distinct ocellus near its base.

**Diagnostic traits.** The species differs from relatives by shorter pelvic fins with fewer rays (except *C. diabolis*, which lacks pelvics), lower ray counts in other fins, higher scale counts (most similar to *C. salinus*), a shorter and more slender caudal peduncle, and fewer or no preorbital pores.

**Taxonomy and nomenclature.** The species has been assigned under several names in the literature (e.g., *C. salinus* and *Cyprinodon* sp. in early surveys), reflecting the isolated populations' earlier assumption of a single species. Its closest relative, both geographically and taxonomically, is [[Cyprinodon salinus]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic history.** Lake Manly, roughly 160 km long and up to 185 m deep at its maximum, desiccated after the pluvial period (8,000–12,000 years ago). Increasing aridity 4,000–8,000 years ago left isolated spring-fed pools and desert streams. The killifish was the only endemic fish able to tolerate the resulting temperature, salinity, and food extremes. Today the remnant basins are Cottonball Basin, Middle Basin, and Badwater Basin.

**Habitat.** The marsh covers 260 ha in two surface-water areas (56.7 ha and 202.5 ha), lies 80 m below sea level, and is the largest marsh on the Death Valley floor. Water covers about 40% of the marsh in winter and 10% in summer. Pools are interconnected by channels about 1 m wide and 35–40 cm deep. Salinity ranges from 14 to 160 ‰ across the marsh. Salt-tolerant vegetation includes pickleweed (*Allenrolfea occidentalis*) and salt grass (*Distichlis stricta*), and aquatic emergents include *Ruppia maritima*.

**Water quality.** Groundwater supplies the marsh, probably via faults from [[Mesquite Flat]], at an estimated 44 L/s. Chemical comparisons (Table 3, Hunt et al. 1966) show similar major-ion composition at the two sites, with Marsh chloride at 6,290 mg/L versus 3,430 mg/L at Mesquite Flat and boron at 30 mg/L versus 8.0 mg/L. The pH is 7.0 at the Marsh and 7.6 at Mesquite Flat.

**Thermal regime.** Pool temperatures range from near freezing in winter to nearly 40°C in summer. Shallow pools may vary by up to 15°C daily, while channels vary 2–3°C. Fish retreat to cooler channel water (32°C). The species tolerates an upper maximum of 43°C.

**Algae and invertebrates.** [[Hunt and Durrell]] (1966) recorded blue-green algae in layers beneath gypsum lumps, with alkaline micro-zones and osmotic pressures up to 50 atmospheres. Associated fauna include a hydrobiid snail (*Tryonia* sp.), the amphipod *Hyalella azteca*, and an unidentified ostracod. Diet data are not published, though Moyle (1976) suggests the fish feed on these organisms and on algae.

**Threats.** Exotic species are the main concern. The mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) probably cannot establish because of the high salinity. More salt-tolerant introductions, such as tilapia and *Poecilia*, could still enter the system. Hybridization with related pupfish is a further risk to species purity.

## Key Findings

- *C. milleri* occupies its entire known range, the 260 ha Cottonball Marsh, making it the only location in the world where the species occurs.
- The species differentiated after isolation caused by valley tilting and uplift along the Kit Fox Hills Fault, a process that likely took only a few thousand years.
- Elevated salinity correlates with increased scale counts and decreased fin ray counts across Death Valley cyprinodonts (La Bounty and Deacon 1972, Table 2).
- Water flow from groundwater is the critical factor for habitat integrity. The author recommends quarterly monitoring with staff gauges, with the first ten years serving as a baseline.
- Visitor impact has been negligible. Access is limited to one administrative jeep trail, and NPS aerial and patrol surveillance continues.
- The species should remain on the State "Rare" list. Its population is small and vulnerable to habitat modification, though it is not currently threatened with extinction.
- The NPS proposed wilderness subzone designation; a recommendation has been submitted to Congress, but no action has been taken.
- **Recommended actions (NPS and CF&G):** install staff gauges (1980–81); consult with [[United States Geological Survey]] on monitoring and threats (1980–81); conduct annual habitat inspections; run electrophoretic comparisons of Death Valley cyprinodonts (1980–81); field studies of early life history and behavior (1981–82); and physiological ecology studies (1982–85).
- Research gaps include early life history, food preference, spawning and territorial behavior, and physiological ecology.
