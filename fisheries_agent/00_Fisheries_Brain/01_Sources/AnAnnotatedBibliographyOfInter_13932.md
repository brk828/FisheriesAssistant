# Annotated Bibliography of Interspecific Hybridization of Salmonidae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Circular (FIRI/C133, Rome, September 1973), compiled by James R. Dangel of the College of Fisheries, University of Washington, is an annotated bibliography of interspecific hybridization within the family [[Salmonidae]]. The author aims to include all known published and unpublished literature on salmonid hybrids. The [[Whitefishes]] and [[Graylings]] are treated as separate families and excluded. The compilation lists roughly 327 numbered entries, spanning 1854 to 1973, and covers trout, char, salmon and related hybrids in natural waters, hatcheries and experimental settings. Most entries are brief, and many are cited only second-hand through other authors.

## Background & Description

The bibliography is arranged alphabetically by first author, with each entry giving the reference, a numeric code and, where obtained, an annotation. Abstracts taken from original papers are quoted verbatim; other annotations were written by the compiler. Papers not obtained for review are listed without annotation unless cited elsewhere. The compiler requests additions and corrections for future revisions or addenda.

Hybrid groups covered include:

- **Char and trout hybrids:** [[Splake]] (Salvelinus fontinalis × S. namaycush), the most intensively studied salmonid hybrid, along with brook × brown "tiger trout", [[Sunbeam Trout]] (rainbow × brown backcross), and [[Cranbrook Trout]].
- **Pacific salmon hybrids:** crosses among chum, pink, sockeye, masu, chinook, coho and kisutch, widely studied in the USSR, Japan, Canada and the United States.
- **Atlantic salmon and sea trout hybrids:** especially work in Ireland, Sweden and Germany.
- **Cutthroat and rainbow trout introgression:** hybrid swarms following introductions in western North America, with threats to native populations such as the [[Gila Trout]].

Early entries (1854 to 1910s) are often drawn from hatchery reports and popular journals. Later work (1950s to 1970s) includes biochemical and cytological studies using [[Lactate Dehydrogenase]] isozymes, electrophoresis and chromosome analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The compilation documents hybridization in both natural and managed settings. Key ecological points include:

- Introductions of [[Rainbow Trout]] into waters where only [[Cutthroat Trout]] was native often produced extensive hybridization and, in some cases, elimination of pure native populations (Behnke, 1969; Hubbs, 1955).
- Natural hybrids between brown trout and brook trout have been recorded in Montana and elsewhere, and hybridization between salmon and trout in the Rhine was reported as early as the 19th century.
- Splake planted in lakes such as Lake Huron fed on smelt and alewives, grew faster than native and planted lake trout, and contributed to a commercial fishery within 2 years (Berst and Spangler, 1970).
- Hybrid vigour and growth advantages (e.g., chum × pink hybrids in Sakhalin hatcheries) were a major motivation for aquaculture programmes.
- Some hybrid lines showed differences in thermal preference and swimbladder gas retention, relevant to deep-water stocking to avoid [[Sea Lamprey]] predation.

## Key Findings

- **Scale:** The bibliography contains about 327 numbered entries from roughly 1854 to 1973, many in German, Russian, Japanese, Scandinavian and English sources.
- **Splake:** Multiple studies (e.g., Martin and Baldwin, 1960; Buss and Wright, 1958; Christie, 1960) show that splake is fertile and behaves as a true hybrid, with growth often exceeding either parent. Backcross progeny have also been reported as fertile.
- **Pacific salmon:** Foerster (1935) provided the first comprehensive study of crosses among all five North American *Oncorhynchus* species. Russian and Japanese workers (Smirnov, Terao, Shitov-era programmes, Oshima) reported hybrids with rapid growth, early maturity and good edible qualities, notably chum × pink and chum × sockeye crosses.
- **Reproductive barriers:** Hybrid fertility varies widely. Some reciprocal crosses (e.g., brown trout × brook trout) produce sterile or limited-fertility progeny, while others (e.g., rainbow × cutthroat) are fully viable. Wright and Buss (1958) discuss barriers to artificial hybridization.
- **Atlantic salmon:** Salmon female × trout male crosses succeed more readily than the reciprocal cross (Hofer, 1909; Scheuring, 1930; Svardson, 1945). Piggins (1965 to 1971) reported hybrid vigour and recaptures of hybrid sea trout-like migrants.
- **Biochemical and cytological work:** Goldberg and co-workers (1967, 1969) and Massaro and Markert (1968) used LDH isozymes to show that salmonids are tetraploid, likely from allotetraploidization of an ancestral hybrid. Chromosome studies (Rees, 1967; Kobayashi, 1963, 1964) confirmed normal fertilization and chromosome behaviour in several crosses.
- **Conservation concern:** Hybridization is repeatedly identified as a cause of decline in native cutthroat, Gila and interior trout populations (Behnke, 1969, 1970a; Mulch and Gamble, 1954; Miller, 1960).
- **Gaps and limitations:** Many entries are known only from secondary citations, and the compiler notes that numerous annotations are brief or unavailable. No new experimental data are presented; the work is a literature synthesis for future revision.
