# Zoogeography of Western North American Freshwater Fishes and Intracontinental Tectonism

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter (Minckley, Hendrickson and Bond, 1986) examines the distribution of freshwater fishes across western North America in relation to Cenozoic geology, paleohydrography, and paleoclimate. The authors argue that the region's fish distributions reflect a long history of plate-tectonic displacement, block faulting, volcanism, stream capture, and progressive aridification, rather than dispersal alone. Using vicariance biogeography and area cladograms, they conclude that much of the regional phylogenetic pattern may date to the Oligocene. The chapter also documents a fauna under severe threat of extinction, with many endemic taxa already lost or restricted to refugia.

## Background & Description

The study region runs from the Continental Divide west to the Pacific Ocean, bounded by the Columbia River system to the north and the Río Grande de Santiago basin of Mexico to the south (the faunal break recognized by Meek 1903, 1904 and R. R. Miller 1959a). Most of the area lies within the [[Basin and Range Province]], which is largely endorheic and bounded by [[Sierra Nevada]], [[Colorado Plateau]], and [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] highlands.

Key conceptual framework:
- **Dispersal** is defined narrowly as movement via direct surface hydrographic connections; [[Stream Capture]] is treated as a form of vicariance.
- The authors assume **allopatric speciation** predominated, applying the principle of minimum age of origin from fossil evidence.
- Western drainages are described as **insular** over long time spans, so fish persistence depends on continuous aquatic habitat.
- Taxonomy follows Robins et al. (1980), with noted exceptions (e.g., *Pantosteus* retained as a genus, *Gila intermedia* given specific status, and *Crenichthys* and *Empetrichthys* placed in Goodeidae).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic and tectonic setting.** The region comprises allochthonous terranes accreted to the North American craton from the Paleozoic to the Late Miocene. The Basin and Range formed from about 27 mya onward through extension, with the Colorado Plateau and Sierra Nevada acting as relatively stable blocks. The [[San Andreas Transform]] formed after roughly 29 mya when the East Pacific Rise collided with the continent, and it displaced microplates northwestward, including the [[Salinian Block]] and Peninsular Ranges of Baja California.

**Paleohydrography.** Cretaceous drainages flowed east and north. Laramide uplift produced many intermontane lakes in the Eocene. Major integrations occurred later: the upper and lower [[Colorado River]] integrated between about 10.6 and 3.3 mya, and the Colorado reached the Gulf of California in the Pliocene. The [[Columbia River Basalts]] (17-6 mya) repeatedly displaced drainages, and the [[Snake River Plain]] volcanic sequence reshaped the upper Snake system.

**Paleoclimate.** The region shifted from subtropical forests in the Paleocene to temperate and then arid conditions by the Pliocene. Pleistocene lakes such as [[Lake Lahontan]] and [[Lake Bonneville]] fluctuated with glacial-interglacial cycles, but the general trend was toward aridity, with modest precipitation increases during glaciation reducing evapotranspiration.

**Biogeography of fauna.** Sixty-nine families and about 334 species are native to nonmarine habitats. Nine families and 107 species are primary or secondary freshwater fishes. Of the other 50 families, 35 include marine dispersants that enter rivers. Table 15.2 lists about 180 marine-derived species recorded in continental waters from Oregon to mainland Mexico; the authors note that the mainland Sinaloa coast alone accounts for 126 of 178 listed species. Endemism is high, and many species have restricted distributions in single springs or lake basins.

**Regional subfaunas.** Major subfaunas include the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin System]] (largest fauna due to anadromous and marine-derived taxa), the [[Lahontan Basin]], the [[Bonneville Basin]], the [[Death Valley System]], the [[Colorado River Basin]], and the Mexican drainages (including the [[Yaqui River]], [[Río Fuerte]], and the [[Mesa del Norte]]).

## Key Findings

- **Fossil timeline.** Cretaceous western ichthyofaunas contained only archaic fishes. Teleost-dominated freshwater faunas first appear in the Late Paleocene (about 55 mya) and during the Eocene (53-37 mya). Modern genera and many species were present by the Miocene.
- **Extinction patterns.** Hiodontids, umbrids, esocids, ictalurids, and centrarchids were reduced to or near extinction across much of western North America by the Pliocene (5-1.8 mya).
- **Vicariance-driven pattern.** Area cladograms built from geologic history were compared with two species-level cladograms (*Catostomus*/*Pantosteus* and *Chasmistes*/*Deltistes*). The authors report "strong congruence" with area relationships, suggesting that regional phylogenetic patterns may date to Oligocene vicariance rather than Pleistocene dispersal.
- **Fish hook biogeographic pattern.** Fossil and living records link Snake River Plain fauna westward to the Sacramento and Klamath systems and southward along the east side of the Sierra Nevada to the Mohave Desert. The Snake-Sacramento track is considered older than the "fish hook" track.
- **Tectonic barriers.** Faunal breaks largely coincide with seismic boundaries separating aseismic subplates. Systems crossing seismic zones, such as the Columbia and Colorado, show composite faunas.
- **Relicts and spring fauna.** Spring-dependent taxa such as *Relictus solitarius*, *Moapa coriacea*, and *Empetrichthys* represent relict faunas. Goodeids and cyprinodontoids are considered older than Pliocene-Pleistocene events, possibly Late Cretaceous to Early Tertiary in origin, though this is debated.
- **Hybridization.** Hybrid swarms are common in western fishes (e.g., *Catostomus*, *Cyprinodon*, *Gila*), and the authors argue that repeated cycles of drought and reconnection have sorted these faunas over time.
- **Unisexual Poeciliopsis.** Natural all-female strains arising from interspecific hybridization occur in *Poeciliopsis*, with diploid and triploid forms reproducing by hybridogenesis and gynogenesis.
- **Conservation warning.** Extinction rates are rapidly increasing, and a substantial percentage of the western ichthyofauna is in imminent danger of disappearing. Notable losses include *Empetrichthys merriami* (recently extinct) and the *Cyprinodon diabolis* population at [[Devils Hole]], which the authors describe as extremely isolated.
- **Limitations.** The authors acknowledge that the area cladograms involve assumptions, including that vicariant events produced allopatry and that dispersal was unfailing within composite areas, and that the sample size is small, so no statistical tests of fit were applied.
