# Preliminary Report on Fish and Wildlife Resources in Relation to the Tuttle Creek Dam and Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catfish

## Executive Summary

This 1953 preliminary report by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Missouri River Basin Studies, Billings, Montana) assesses how the planned [[Tuttle Creek Dam]] and [[Tuttle Creek Reservoir]] on the [[Big Blue River]] would affect fish and wildlife. The reservoir is a flood-control project within the [[Missouri River Basin]]. The report concludes that the project will be detrimental to both fish and wildlife. It estimates an annual fishery loss of $9,600 and an annual wildlife loss of $12,100. Its main recommendations are a minimum release of 200 cubic feet per second below the dam, periodic inundation of about 1,000 acres in October through December for waterfowl, maintenance of access roads and bridges, and public access to federal lands.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The water development plan provides flood protection to the flood plains of the Big Blue, [[Kansas River]], and Missouri Rivers. The report is based on the [[Corps of Engineers]] Definite Project Report (January 1952) and supplemental data. It values fish and wildlife in monetary terms using long-term price projections. Intangible and aesthetic values are excluded. The area of influence includes 53,500 acres of land, 67.1 miles of the Big Blue River, and 95.7 miles of tributaries within the flood-control pool. The fishery of about 9.5 miles of river below the dam is also considered.

**Physical setting.** The Big Blue River rises in south-central Nebraska near Aurora and drains 9,600 square miles. It falls from about 1,650 feet to about 980 feet at its confluence with the Kansas River. Major tributaries entering the reservoir area are the [[Little Blue River]] (3,497 sq mi), [[Black Vermillion River]] (510 sq mi), and [[Fancy Creek]] (290 sq mi). Smaller streams include [[Mill Creek]]. The large tributaries are often turbid during floods, while smaller streams are usually clear. Channel substrates are sand, gravel, and limestone. The climate is humid, with rainfall of 23 to 32 inches per year. The growing season is about 171 days.

**Streamflow.** The U.S. Geological Survey has maintained records since April 1918 at Randolph, Kansas. Over 31 years the average daily discharge was 1,510 cubic feet per second. Recorded extremes were 98,000 and 31 cubic feet per second.

**Project engineering.** The dam is an earthfill structure about 7,500 feet long and 135 feet high. Its spillway has twenty 40-by-20-foot Tainter gates. Releases pass through two 20-foot tunnels with invert elevation 998 feet. Full-pool outlet capacity is 45,000 cubic feet per second, but releases are planned not to exceed 35,000. At full flood-control pool (elevation 1,136 feet), the reservoir holds 2,280,000 acre-feet over 53,500 acres.

**Human context.** The three counties had a 1950 population of 63,675. About 8,500 people live in the area to be flooded. [[Manhattan, Kansas]] (19,056) is the largest nearby city. Agriculture is the main income source, with corn, wheat, alfalfa, sorghum, and red clover as major crops.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishery without the project.** The Big Blue River supports a catfish fishery known across Kansas and neighboring states. Anglers travel up to 100 miles to fish it. [[Channel Catfish]] make up about 70 percent of the catch and [[Flathead Catfish]] about 5 percent. Carp, buffaloes, bullheads, and freshwater drum account for most of the rest. Pool areas, undercut banks, and wooded banks provide habitat and insect food. Springs keep flows dependable, and stream flow has seldom dropped below 100 cubic feet per second. Untreated domestic and industrial waste causes significant pollution below Marysville, Blue Rapids, and Frankfort. The [[Rocky Ford Dam]] already blocks upstream fish movement except during high water.

**Wildlife without the project.** Cover is mainly cropland (about 65 percent of the pool area), pasture (19 percent), and river channels (5 percent). Riparian strips of cottonwood, elm, ash, willow, and oak line the streams. Hunted species include [[Bobwhite Quail]], [[Eastern Cottontail]], [[White-Tailed Deer]] (small population), mourning doves, and fox squirrels. Fur animals include [[Muskrat]], [[Raccoon]], [[Mink]], and [[Beaver]]. Waterfowl use the river mainly during migration.

**Operation regime.** Water supply studies from 1895 to 1951 were used to project operation. Impoundment would have occurred about 10 percent of the time. No flood storage would have occurred in 9 of 57 years. The maximum pool range was 126 feet, and the average annual range was 43.8 feet. The pool would have exceeded elevation 1,050 feet in about 50 percent of years. Peak stages would occur May through July, and dry-bed conditions would occur November through January.

## Key Findings

**Fishery impacts**

- No reservoir fishery can develop, because the pool is drawn down or left at channel level every year.
- Recurrent inundation and siltation will destroy woody bank vegetation and channel pools below elevation 1,050 feet, eliminating undercut-bank catfish habitat.
- The dam will block upstream fish movement. Release velocities through the tubes are expected to exceed what channel and flathead catfish can handle.
- Annual fishery value falls as follows:

| Reach | Without Project | With Project | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big Blue River, reservoir area | $27,000 | $18,400 (text: $18,100) | $8,600 |
| Tributaries, reservoir area | $9,500 | $8,500 | $1,000 |
| Big Blue below dam | $8,300 | $8,300 | none |
| **Total** | **$44,800** | **$35,200** | **$9,600** |

The report gives $18,100 in the text and $18,400 in Table 2. The difference is not explained.

**Wildlife impacts**

- Upland game habitat in the lowest reaches will be permanently lost. Flooding during spring and early summer breeding and nesting seasons will disrupt reproduction. Loss of timber below the 1,090-foot contour will eliminate fox squirrels from much of the area.
- Woody vegetation below 1,050 feet will be eliminated.
- Annual wildlife values:

| Item | Without Project | With Project | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upland game | $21,600 | $14,200 | $7,400 |
| Fur animals | $14,200 | $9,500 | $4,700 |
| Waterfowl | $200 | $200 | none |
| Big game | not valued | not valued | — |
| **Total** | **$36,000** | **$23,900** | **$12,100** |

**Recreational access and public use**

- The area draws many anglers and hunters. A population of about 100,000 lives within 25 miles of the reservoir, and the need for hunting and fishing areas will grow.
- Loss of roads and bridges and removal of local residents will reduce use of the area.
- About 2,500 fisherman-days per year are spent on the reach below Rocky Ford Dam.

**Recommendations**

1. Maintain a minimum flow of 200 cubic feet per second below Tuttle Creek Dam during impoundment.
2. Inundate about 1,000 acres each year from October through December to provide waterfowl resting and feeding areas.
3. Maintain access roads and bridges within the reservoir area.
4. Open federally owned project land to free public recreation, except for areas reserved for safety, operation, or property protection.
5. Require federal land leases to guarantee public access for hunting, fishing, and other non-commercial recreation.

**Additional operational guidance**

- Release rates should change gradually where practicable. Sudden changes displace fish, injure them through turbulence, and discourage angling below the dam.
- A valuable tailwater fishery is expected to form below the dam because fish congregate at the release point.

**Conclusions**

- Construction and operation will cause an annual fishery loss of $9,600 and an annual wildlife loss of $12,100.
- If Recommendation 2 is carried out, a considerable waterfowl value would result.
- The Fish and Wildlife Service asks to be notified of any change in plans so that a revised report can be prepared.
- The report is based on the January 1952 Definite Project Report and was approved by the Acting Supervisor on September 4, 1953.
