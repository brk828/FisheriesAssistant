# Redlands Dam Fishway Feasibility Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (Walla Walla District) feasibility report, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 6) in December 1986, evaluates fish passage concepts at the existing [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on the [[Gunnison River]] near Grand Junction, Colorado. The goal is to let [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], both threatened with extinction in the Colorado River system, reestablish themselves in part of their historic range. Because exact biological criteria for the two species are lacking, the facility is treated as experimental and a prototype, and concepts were judged against best available criteria for these or similar species.

Nine preliminary alternates (A through I) were reviewed at a scoping meeting. Five final alternates (No. 1, No. 1A, No. 2, No. 3 and No. 4) were then studied in detail. The recommended option is **Alternate No. 1A**, a vertical slot/orifice [[Vertical Slot Fishway]] beside the existing sluice gates, with a total design and construction cost of $782,900. Design was scheduled to start in early 1987, with construction completed by April 1988.

## Background & Description

**Dam and water rights.** The [[Redlands Water and Power Company]] (RWPC) built the diversion dam in 1918 and has since modernized it. It is a concrete buttress dam 8.5 ft high with a 312-ft spillway, a 6-ft crest, and two 10-ft by 6-ft sluice gates. The primary water right of 750 cfs is diverted through four 14-ft headgates into the power canal for irrigation and hydroelectric power. The City of Grand Junction holds a secondary right of 120 cfs. The dam was designed to pass 30,000 cfs floods with about 1 ft of freeboard.

**Operations.** Diversion is year-round. The irrigation season runs about 6 months, from April 15 to October 15. The required upstream water depth is about 12 ft. During low flow, flashboards are placed on the spillway crest, starting at the left abutment and proceeding toward the right abutment. The power canal is dewatered for 1 week in the fall and 1 week in the spring for maintenance.

**Target species.**
- Colorado Squawfish: the largest cyprinid native to North America. Historical weights reached 80 lb, but recent large adults weigh 10 to 15 lb and are about 3 ft long. The species now occupies about 25 percent of its original range, roughly 800 river miles above Lake Powell, and is federally listed as endangered. Migrations of more than 200 miles have been documented, mostly near spawning.
- Razorback Sucker: the largest individuals reach 18 to 24 inches and about 5 lb. Only adults have been collected recently, with no evidence of natural recruitment, so the species may be close to extinction. It is state-listed as endangered in Colorado, protected in Utah, and a candidate for federal listing.

**Current barrier effects.** The dam is an effective barrier to upstream migration. Fish pass downstream over the dam, mainly at high flows, but adults moving downstream can enter the power canal and become disoriented there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Functional criteria.** Both species are only moderately strong swimmers and are bottom-oriented. Key design requirements include:
- Entrances placed in the main channel, with inverts flush with the river bottom.
- A ladder floor sloped from entrance to exit, with slot and orifice inverts flush with the floor.
- Orifices at least 12 in wide by 18 in high; slots at least 12 in wide, extending above high-flow water surface.
- Auxiliary attraction flow of three times the maximum ladder flow.
- Screens with 1.0- to 1.5-inch spacing to exclude adults from the power canal and intakes.
- Pools of about 8 ft by 10 ft by 3 ft, with head drops of 0.5 ft per pool, adjustable from 0.25 to 1.0 ft.

**Technical context.** Vertical slot fishways let fish choose their swimming depth, maintain constant flow patterns across operating depths, and tolerate wide headwater and tailwater ranges. Denil fishways were rejected for these species because fish must swim over baffles rather than along the floor. A [[Baffled Flume Fishway]] was retained for study but found to lack supporting evidence.

**Hydrology.** Appendix B presents simulated flows from the [[HYDROSS Model]] for 1952 to 1982. Flows are shaped by upstream regulation and by Aspinall Unit operations, which were not modeled in detail. The maximum simulated monthly value was 9,233.20 cfs (June 1957). Reasonable design flows were 300 cfs (August, low) and 6,000 cfs (May, high).

**Sedimentation and debris.** Structures in the river alter flow and can cause deposition upstream and downstream. Trash can damage fishway structures, so a [[Trash Shear Boom]] or louver system is needed.

**Expert input.** Interviews with fishway specialists (including a Fish and Wildlife Service biologist and state biologists) noted that overflow and weir/orifice ladders have poor success with suckers. Auxiliary attraction water and lower head drops per pool improve passage. Razorback suckers may not use a ladder in its first year and do not migrate every year. A fish trap at the upstream pool was recommended.

## Key Findings

**Alternates reviewed.**

| Alternate | Description | Total cost | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| No. 1 | Vertical slot ladder, west and east entrances, 24 pools | $839,200 | Retained; reconsidered if 1A fails |
| No. 1A | Vertical slot ladder, three entrances, fewer mechanical items | $782,900 | **Recommended** |
| No. 2 | Short experimental ladder (four pools) | $410,300 | Eliminated (+$120,000 if expanded in two seasons) |
| No. 3 | Right-bank vertical slot ladder with new sluice gate | $575,100 | Rejected (fish may not be attracted from main channel) |
| No. 4 | Collection channel to prefabricated baffled flume | $512,500 | Rejected (poor entrance location, no flexibility) |

**Why Alternate 1A was recommended.** Its entrances are in the main-channel location most likely to attract fish. It offers operational flexibility for attraction and passage. Its initial cost is lower than No. 1 because it needs fewer mechanical items. Its spillway capacity is reduced by 5,500 cfs, compared with 2,000 cfs for No. 1. If final design shows problems with that reduced capacity, No. 1 should be reconsidered.

**Standard operation (Alternate 1A).**
- Low tailwater of 4,563.0 ft (300 cfs, August) and an upstream level of 4,575.0 ft give a head change of about 12 ft.
- The ladder has 23 weirs, with a ladder flow of about 18 cfs at low flow.
- For flows above 6,000 cfs and below 8,000 cfs, the ladder is closed at the forebay entrance.
- Above 8,000 cfs (up to 24,500 cfs), the spillway carries most of the flow.

**Design life and maintenance.** The design life is 50 years, and the existing dam is 68 years old. The log trash boom (10-year life for timber, 25-year for cable) is the only component needing replacement. Its present-worth cost is $34,000 for 1A. The dam is dewatered for maintenance only briefly each year. FWS would operate the fishway, check the trap daily during migration, adjust weirs and gates, and remove debris.

**Cost basis.** Estimates include a 25 percent contingency and a 20 percent allowance for engineering, design and supervision. The Alternate 1A total job cost is $782,900. Estimate tables for Alternates 1, 1A, 2, 3 and 4 are summarized in the cost tables.

**Schedule for the recommended alternate.**
- Start design: 1 January 1987
- Plans and specifications complete (advertise): 1 June 1987
- Notice to proceed: 1 September 1987
- Construction complete: 1 April 1988

**Source discrepancies.** The Section I purpose statement and Section IV describe four final alternates. The Executive Summary and Section V describe five, because Alternate 1A was added at the 60-percent review. An early disclaimer also corrects a description of how flashboards are placed. Dam personnel raise and lower the flashboards manually, with a block-and-tackle system attached to a cable suspended over the crest.
