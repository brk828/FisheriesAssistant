# Morphological Distinction of Diploid and Triploid Grass Carp x Bighead Carp Hybrids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study tests whether external morphology can separate diploid from triploid [[Grass Carp]] x [[Bighead Carp]] hybrids. Such hybrids were proposed for [[Biological Control]] of aquatic weeds because triploids should be sterile. Diploids, however, may be fertile and are more often deformed. Ploidy is normally confirmed by [[Chromosome Analysis]], [[Erythrocyte Nuclear Volume]], [[Flow Cytometry]] or [[Protein Electrophoresis]], all of which are accurate but slow and often lethal. The authors used [[Discriminant Function Analysis]] on 26 morphological and meristic characters from 205 fish. About 97% of hybrids were classified correctly, but no single character reliably separated the two ploidy levels, and the authors conclude that morphology alone is not safe for release decisions.

## Background & Description

**Species and hybrid origin.** Grass carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) females were crossed with bighead carp (*Hypophthalmichthys nobilis*, also listed as *Aristichthys nobilis*) males. The first viable intergeneric hybrids were reported from Russia (Makeeva and Sukhanova 1966; Aliev 1967). Hungarian researchers (Marian and Krasznai 1978) later reported that these hybrids were triploid. In theory, the extra chromosome set makes triploids sterile through failed gonadal development or aneuploid gametes.

**Application interest.** In the United States, interest centers on the hybrid's potential for [[Aquatic Weed Control]]. A sterile exotic fish could be released into lakes and canals without risk of naturalization. Under U.S. production conditions, a single cohort may contain diploid, triploid and [[Gynogenesis|gynogenetic]] individuals. The reproductive status of diploids is unknown, and they are thought more likely to be deformed and stunted.

**Study material.** Fish came from the 1980 year class of an Arkansas commercial producer. All hybrids had previously been karyotyped as diploid or triploid (Beck and Biggers 1982). Measurements followed Trautman (1981).

**Sample sizes (from the Methods section):**

| Group | n |
|---|---|
| Grass carp | 25 |
| Bighead carp | 25 |
| Diploid hybrids | 101 |
| Triploid hybrids | 54 |

Twenty-six characters were recorded. These included body and fin dimensions, head and snout measures, caudal-peduncle measures, eye and pupil diameter, lateral-line scale counts, scale rows above and below the lateral line, and scale width. Weight was in grams and other measures in millimeters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses ecological risk more than habitat or behavior. A triploid hybrid released into a water body is expected to pose little reproductive risk. A diploid that escapes could breed, and its reproductive capacity is unknown. Specimens varied in size, so [[Analysis of Covariance]] was run with standard length as a covariate. Only predorsal length still varied with body size after this adjustment. The authors also note that morphology may reflect environmental conditions and genetic stock. Variation between year classes could therefore limit how far the results generalize.

## Key Findings

- **Correct classification.** Stepwise discriminant analysis of the 12 significant characters correctly classified 97% of hybrids as diploid or triploid. The canonical correlation was 0.892, and the final [[Wilks Lambda]] was 0.20387 (P < 0.01).
- **Reduced character sets.**
  - Subset I (caudal-peduncle length, head length, snout length, head width, dorsal-fin length, standard length) gave 90% correct classification, better for diploids than triploids.
  - Subset II (scale rows above and below the lateral line, caudal-peduncle length, head length, snout length, head width) gave 97%, equal to the full 12-character result.
- **Best discriminators.** Head length, head width, snout length, caudal-peduncle length and width of the scale above the lateral line had the highest discriminant coefficients.
- **Scale counts.** Diploids had 11-14 scale rows above the lateral line and triploids 10-11, with overlap at 11 rows. No single character gave consistent separation. Adding scale width to scale-row counts raised accuracy to 90%.
- **Canonical scores.** Centroids on the first canonical axis were -1.4 for diploids and 2.7 for triploids.
- **Morphological abnormalities.** Diploids showed more abnormalities than triploids, mainly vertebral-column and mandibular defects. Diploids were also generally smaller than triploids.
- **Residual error.** About 3% of hybrids were misclassified even with the best characters. The authors state that morphology alone could allow diploids to be released into the wild.
- **Reproductive uncertainty.** Triploids are expected to be sterile. Krasznai et al. (1982) reported abnormal gonadal development in 5-year-old triploids, and Beck and Biggers (unpublished) found poor gonadal development in 4-year-old U.S. triploids that still showed secondary sexual characters. Diploid fertility has not been assessed.
- **Conclusions.** Morphological methods are a useful screening tool but not a substitute for laboratory ploidy confirmation. Evaluating reproductive capacity in both diploids and triploids is essential before the hybrid is used in biological control programs.
- **Generalizability.** The 97% accuracy comes from one year class, so the discriminant rules need testing on other cohorts.
