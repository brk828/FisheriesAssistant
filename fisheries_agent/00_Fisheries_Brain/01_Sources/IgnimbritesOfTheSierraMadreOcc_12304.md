# Ignimbrites of the Sierra Madre Occidental and Their Relation to the Tectonic History of Western Mexico

#geology #volcanology #ignimbrites

> **Scope note:** This document is a geological and geochemical study of mid-Tertiary volcanic rocks. It contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton, water-chemistry, or habitat data. The sections below are filled from the geological content only.

## Executive Summary

McDowell and Clabaugh (Geological Society of America Special Paper 180, 1979) describe two large, largely coextensive calc-alkalic igneous sequences in the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] of western Mexico. The older sequence, a [[Lower Volcanic Complex]] of batholithic and volcanic rocks, spans roughly 45 to more than 100 m.y. and is mostly intermediate in composition. The younger [[Upper Volcanic Supergroup]] is dominated by rhyodacitic to rhyolitic [[Ignimbrite]]s erupted from [[Caldera]] complexes. Its volcanism was concentrated between 34 and 27 m.y. ago, with some activity continuing to 23 m.y. ago.

The authors link the igneous chronology to plate-tectonic events along the North American margin. They find that the timing of the 27 m.y. decline fits known spreading-centre reorientation and ridge-trench interaction. The 45–34 m.y. magmatic gap and the abrupt 34 m.y. onset do not match known sea-floor-spreading events. They also conclude that the brief, bimodal composition of the younger sequence is atypical for a continental-margin province.

## Background & Description

**Regional setting.** The Sierra Madre Occidental is a high plateau forming the western margin of the central Mexican upland. It is deeply dissected by west-flowing rivers that descend rapidly to sea level. The crest is capped by nearly flat-lying mid-Tertiary volcanic rocks, including spectacularly exposed rhyolitic ignimbrites. The authors describe it as probably the largest continuous ignimbrite expanse in the world, forming a south-east-trending belt about 250 km wide and 1,200 km long.

**Study traverses.** Two traverses were studied:
- Near latitude 24°N, between [[Mazatlan]] and [[Durango City]], where contiguous mapped areas cross the range.
- Near latitude 28°N, where separate areas west and north of [[Chihuahua City]] were mapped.

**Lower Volcanic Complex.** This older sequence includes batholithic and associated volcanic rocks, mainly intermediate in composition (dark andesitic rocks dominate the extrusive portion). The rocks are commonly altered and host much of Mexico's mineral deposits. Key localities include [[Tayoltita]] (over 2,000 m of volcanic rock intruded by a granodiorite batholith) and Topia (at least 1,400 m of tilted volcanic rock). Batholithic activity in southern Sinaloa ran from 102 to 45 m.y. ago.

**Upper Volcanic Supergroup.** The younger sequence consists mainly of moderately to densely welded rhyodacitic to rhyolitic ignimbrites, with subordinate mafic lavas near the top of sections. Nearly all volcanic rocks in each area were emplaced within a short interval, typically 3 to 5 m.y.

**Pre-Cretaceous rocks.** Scattered Permian, Triassic, and Jurassic volcanic occurrences are recorded, but the authors state that the pre-Cretaceous volcanic history of western Mexico is difficult to reconstruct from them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not address ecology, aquatic habitats, or biota. Its environmental context is geological and structural:

- **Faulting.** Normal faults are minor in the central Sierra. They are much larger on both flanks, where basin-and-range style fault-block mountains and sediment-filled grabens occur. Some displacements on the western side exceed 1 km.
- **Rift relationships.** The authors consider whether the eastern fault zone is a southward extension of the [[Rio Grande Rift]]. They note that [[Basin And Range Faulting]] and rifting of [[Baja California]] are also open questions.
- **Erosion and drainage.** Headward erosion by Pacific-draining rivers is excavating graben sediments, and the western slope has deep canyons.
- **Mineralization.** The older volcanic rocks host major ore deposits, and trace sulfides occur in nearly every large outcrop.

## Key Findings

1. **Two calc-alkalic sequences.** The Sierra contains two vast, largely coextensive calc-alkalic igneous sequences, both including ignimbrites. The older (45 to over 100 m.y.) is dominantly intermediate. The younger (34–27 m.y., with activity to 23 m.y.) is dominantly rhyodacitic to rhyolitic.

2. **Magmatic gap and abrupt onset.** Magmatism was interrupted from about 45 to 34 m.y. ago, followed by an abrupt onset of major ignimbrite volcanism. The authors state that neither event matches known sea-floor-spreading events.

3. **Brief, bimodal volcanism.** Much of the younger volume was emplaced in about 7 m.y. (34–27 m.y. ago). Its SiO₂ content is distinctly bimodal. Of 42 analyses from the main Sierra, none fall between 55% and 65% SiO₂.

4. **Chemical dataset.** Rocks from all stratigraphic sections were analyzed for 13 major-element oxides by wet chemical methods. The sample set comprises 42 analyses from the main Sierra (region I) and 23 from eastern Chihuahua (region II). The authors report 79 analyses in total, all subalkalic and calc-alkalic on an AFM diagram.

5. **Alkalinity gradient.** Three broad bands are distinguished by alkalinity ratio, from west to east: calc-alkalic Sierra Madre Occidental proper, intermediate eastern Chihuahua and adjacent Texas, and alkalic [[Trans-Pecos Texas]]. The most pronounced change may occur within Texas.

6. **Caldera abundance.** Mapping of about 2,000 km² near Durango City revealed one large caldera complex and evidence of two others. Mapping of about 1,500 km² in western Chihuahua outlined one large caldera and evidence of two more. The authors estimate 200 to 400 calderas for the 250,000 km² central belt, and they consider this realistic.

7. **Caldera distribution.** Calderas are less common along the margins, where they are obscured by faults and graben fill. Near El Sueco–Sierra Gallego, the authors found prominent rhyolitic flow domes aligned in a northwest trend, suggesting passive, linear vents rather than calderas.

8. **Tectonic correlation.** The older sequence is similar in age and composition to [[Cretaceous]] to early Tertiary batholithic terranes of the western United States. Both sequences are interpreted as emplaced during convergence along the western margin of the North American plate.

9. **Timing of decline.** Volcanism declined about 27 m.y. ago, close to the reorientation of an east Pacific spreading centre (about 26 m.y. ago, from magnetic anomalies) and early ridge-trench interaction at the continental margin.

10. **Possible triggers for the gap.** The authors discuss two alternatives for the magmatic gap: a pause or decline in plate convergence, or subduction of an active ridge. For the Sierra, they consider the gap certain. Their best estimate of its duration is 45 to 34 m.y. ago.

11. **Inland alkalic volcanism.** Alkalic rocks of Trans-Pecos Texas (45 to 20 m.y. ago) are attributed by [[Barker]] (1977) to mantle diapirism and crustal rifting. Whether inland volcanism reflects continental-margin tectonics is left unresolved.

12. **Unresolved questions.** The authors identify these open issues: the time gap across the 1,200 km length of the Sierra, consistent transverse chemical variation, relations to Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona volcanic provinces, and the link to rifting and basin-and-range faulting. Trace-element and isotopic studies are needed.
