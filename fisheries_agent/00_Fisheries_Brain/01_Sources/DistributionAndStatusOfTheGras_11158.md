# Distribution and Status of the Grass Carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) in Missouri Streams

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #invasive-species #Grass-Carp

## Executive Summary

This 1978 study by [[William L. Pflieger]] documents the invasion and early establishment of the [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), a large Asian [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]], in Missouri's major river systems following its 1963 importation into the United States for [[Biological Control of Aquatic Vegetation]]. First recorded in Missouri waters by 1971, Grass Carp were widely distributed by 1976 across the [[Missouri River]], [[Mississippi River]], and [[St. Francis River]]. The species originated from large-scale stocking efforts by the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission beginning in 1971, with fish appearing in the lower Mississippi River a full year before appearing in more northern Missouri streams — consistent with a southern (Arkansas) source population and northward dispersal. Commercial harvest data show a steady year-over-year increase in Grass Carp catch from 1971–1976, though the species remained a minor component (0.8%) of total commercial fish harvest by weight. Age analysis of 18 specimens indicated most fish present in the Missouri River in 1974 were of the 1971 year class, with growth histories suggesting a hatchery-pond origin for most individuals. Critically, **no evidence of natural reproduction** was found in Missouri waters during the study period, despite targeted seining efforts for young-of-year fish.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy**: Large Asian cyprinid; congeneric comparisons made against [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*).
- **Weight range (commercial catch)**: 0.45–10.35 kg, averaging 4.52 kg.
- **Regional size variation**: Specimens from the upper Mississippi River were notably smaller, averaging 2.07 kg, compared to fish from the Missouri River and lower Mississippi River.
- **Condition**: All examined specimens were in good physical condition with substantial visceral fat deposits; no parasites were noted.
- **Gonadal development**: Gonads in examined specimens were generally underdeveloped, precluding easy sex determination by superficial examination; however, fishermen reported females with well-developed ovaries, and a ripe male was captured in the St. Francis River (July 1974).
- **Age structure (18 aged specimens, Missouri River, 1974–1975)**:
  - 1 individual: 1970 year class (5.4 kg)
  - 14 individuals: 1971 year class (2.6–5.0 kg, avg. 3.5 kg)
  - 3 individuals: 1972 year class (6.4–7.6 kg, avg. 6.9 kg) — notably larger despite being younger, indicating more rapid first-year growth.
- **Scale/growth pattern (aging methodology)**:
  - Annuli identified via interrupted circuli and "cutting over."
  - 1971 year class showed a distinct mid-first-year growth check (reduced circuli spacing), interpreted as a hatchery-pond crowding effect followed by growth resumption after transfer/release.
  - 1972 year class showed uniformly wide circuli spacing in the first growth increment, suggesting faster, less-interrupted early growth — potentially indicative of a more favorable rearing environment or possible natural reproduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Large, lowland, lotic river systems — the [[Missouri River]], [[Mississippi River]] (both upper and lower reaches relative to the Missouri River confluence), and the [[St. Francis River]] bordering Arkansas.
- **Distribution pattern**: Widespread in commercially-fished Missouri rivers by 1976; largely absent or unconfirmed in smaller, non-commercially-fished streams except for a few records near the Missouri River (Gasconade River, Maries River) and one notable outlier in Shoal Creek (Grand River tributary, Caldwell County).
- **Diet**: Digestive tract analysis (n=17, 6 with contents) revealed filamentous algae as the dominant food item, with minor amounts of terrestrial vascular plant material; one specimen had ingested a plastic bread wrapper — an early example of anthropogenic debris ingestion.
- **Habitat barriers**: The upper Mississippi River, which is impounded by a series of navigation dams, showed markedly lower Grass Carp abundance than the free-flowing Missouri River and lower Mississippi River — suggesting dams may impede upstream movement and dispersal.
- **Reproductive status**: No young-of-year Grass Carp were found despite repeated summer seining (1974–1976) of the Missouri River near Columbia and its tributaries, indicating no confirmed [[Natural Reproduction]] in Missouri waters during the study period.
- **Trophic/ecosystem role**: Positioned as a potential [[Invasive Species]] with unclear net ecological impact — noted by the author as potentially the most significant addition to the Mississippi Valley fish fauna since the introduction of [[Common Carp]] roughly a century earlier.

## Key Findings

- Grass Carp were first documented in Missouri waters by 1971 and were widely established in the Missouri, Mississippi, and St. Francis rivers by 1976.
- Source population traced to Arkansas: Grass Carp became prevalent in the lower Mississippi River commercial catch in 1973, one year earlier than in more northern Missouri rivers, consistent with a southern origin from large-scale Arkansas Game and Fish Commission stocking (380,000 fish across >100 lakes by 1975).
- Commercial harvest questionnaire (April 1975): 18% of 396 responding Missouri commercial fishermen reported catching Grass Carp; highest reporting rate from the St. Francis River (33.3%) and Missouri River (22.1%).
- Total reported Grass Carp harvest rose from 3 fish (12 kg) in 1971 to 3,039 kg in 1976 alone (total across years: 254 fish / 4,988 kg documented, with 1975–76 numbers incomplete).
- Grass Carp constituted only 0.8% of total commercial fish harvest (308,200 kg) in the first half of 1976, with the highest relative contribution in the lower Mississippi River (1.61%) versus the Missouri River (0.35%) and upper Mississippi River (0.04%).
- Most 18 aged specimens belonged to the 1971 year class, and their growth histories (mid-year growth check) strongly suggest early rearing in crowded hatchery pond conditions prior to release or escape.
- No evidence of natural reproduction was detected in Missouri despite dedicated seining programs (1974–1976), though the author notes this does not rule out reproduction occurring closer to the original Arkansas release sites.
- The species was rarely found outside commercially-fished major rivers; a notable exception was Shoal Creek (Grand River tributary), a small, geographically isolated stream, raising questions about extent of secondary dispersal.
- Citing [[Stanley 1976]], the author suggests self-sustaining reproduction in the Mississippi Valley was plausible by 1978–1979, and that establishment of Grass Carp as a naturalized species may no longer have been preventable by the time of publication.
- Food habit analysis confirmed a primarily herbivorous diet (filamentous algae, vascular plant material), supporting the species' intended original purpose as a biological control agent for aquatic vegetation, while also revealing incidental ingestion of anthropogenic debris.
