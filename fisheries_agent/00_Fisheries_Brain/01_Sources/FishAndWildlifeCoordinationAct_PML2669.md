# Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act Report: Central Arizona Project Verde and East Verde River Water Diversions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act (FWCA) report, prepared by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Arizona Projects Office, assesses impacts to fish and wildlife resources from proposed [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water exchanges and diversions on the [[Verde River]] and [[East Verde River]], Arizona. Eight CAP allottees totaling 17,343 acre-feet per year propose to exchange allocations with water rights holders and withdraw water directly from the rivers. The report concludes that the diversions would significantly degrade habitat for native fishes, riparian vegetation and wildlife, and recommends against unrestricted withdrawals, along with minimum flows, diversion caps, long-term monitoring, and preparation of an Environmental Impact Statement. The report was prepared in cooperation with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], which concurred.

## Background & Description

The report covers the Verde and East Verde Rivers, which together support about 155 miles of high-quality aquatic and riparian habitat, including extensive Fremont cottonwood stands. The system holds a significant remnant native fish community. Three federally and state-listed species occur in the area: the [[Spikedace]] (threatened), the [[Bald Eagle]] and the [[Peregrine Falcon]] (both endangered). Nine other state-listed species are also present, and the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] have been reintroduced. A State Park ([[Dead Horse Ranch State Park]]), a National Monument and a [[Wild and Scenic River]] segment lie within the study area.

The report addresses only the effects of water removal on aquatic and riparian habitat. It does not evaluate water rights, pipelines or pumping plants. The analysis rests on an [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) study, supplemented by the [[Great Plains Method]], field experience and literature.

Project components:
- Total CAP allocation for the eight parties is 17,343 acre-feet per year, for agricultural, municipal and industrial uses.
- Three entities submitted detailed plans: the City of Prescott and Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community (combined system, French drain at Reach 1), and the Town of Payson (French drain, 1.7 cfs rising to 3.5 cfs after 2010, Reach 8).
- Conceptual plans were developed by Reclamation for the Camp Verde Indian Community (10 cfs pulses, 1,200 acre-feet/year), the Tonto Apache Indian Community, Cottonwood, Camp Verde and Pine/Strawberry.
- The 2010 Payson rate is equivalent to 23.9 cfs when constant over the project life.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and hydrology.** The Verde River is perennial from near Sullivan Lake to [[Horseshoe Dam]], about 125 miles of undammed flow. Flow varies strongly along its length. At the Paulden gage the 19-year mean daily discharge was 42.9 cfs (median 29 cfs). Below Tangle Creek the 37-year mean daily discharge was 546 cfs (median 384 cfs). The East Verde River has a perennial length of about 40 miles and receives intermittent trans-basin water from Blue Ridge Reservoir (about 30 cfs for roughly 50 percent of the year, per the report).

**Vegetation.** Riparian zones range from Arizona alder and ponderosa pine in the headwaters to cottonwood-willow and mesquite bosques downstream. Dead Horse Ranch State Park supports probably the largest contiguous stand of native cottonwood on the Verde.

**Fish community.** Native species in the headwaters include longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker, roundtail chub, speckled dace and spikedace. Non-native species include red shiner, channel and flathead catfish, carp, smallmouth bass and green sunfish. Native dominance declines below Perkinsville and non-natives dominate by Tapco. Spikedace are essentially absent below Sycamore Creek. The red shiner established in the upper Verde in the mid-1950s, yet spikedace persist there.

**Wildlife.** Four bald eagle nesting territories occur above Horseshoe Dam. The introduced Louisiana otter population extends from Perkinsville to Horseshoe Dam on the Verde and from SR 87 to the confluence on the East Verde. Riparian birds, including the yellow-billed cuckoo and southwestern willow flycatcher, and herpetofauna depend on the riparian corridor. The black hawk takes more than 78 percent of its prey from riparian habitats.

**Land use.** Cattle grazing, off-road vehicle use, residential and commercial growth, and sand and gravel operations affect the river. Prescott's population was 20,055 in 1980 and projected at 23,720 by 1988. Verde Valley population was projected to reach 98,620 by 2030. Payson's population was 7,745 in 1988, projected at 9,560 by 2000.

**Riparian resource status.** About 95 percent of Arizona's historic riparian habitat has been lost, leaving roughly 101,793 acres (0.15 percent of the state).

## Key Findings

**Flow impacts.** As originally proposed, diversions would reduce normal flow by about half in the upper Verde (Reaches 1 and 2) and by about two-thirds on the East Verde. The Verde Valley (Reaches 4 and 5) would see a median-flow reduction of about 26 percent, and up to about half under simultaneous maximum rates. Expected drop in water surface elevation in Reach 1 is 1.9 to 3.1 inches. Diversions of 42 to 53 percent of median monthly flow in the upper Verde would severely reduce habitat for native fishes. Roundtail chub habitat is essentially eliminated below 10 cfs.

**Fishery and habitat findings from IFIM.** Adult fish were identified as the limiting life stage. Weighted usable area for spikedace and suckers decreases with withdrawals, while red shiner habitat increases substantially. The report links this shift to concern over spikedace persistence. On the East Verde, trout habitat declines steadily with decreasing flow, and the put-and-take trout fishery would be significantly reduced or eliminated.

**Riparian impacts.** Brock (1987) found riparian vegetation in the Verde and East Verde is maintained largely by subsurface flow and bank storage. Reduced water tables could significantly reduce seedling survival in years 1–5 and lead to narrowing of the riparian zone and channel widening.

**Species of concern.** Reduction or loss of spikedace habitat could adversely affect its survival and recovery. The upper Verde headwaters are vital to recovery of the state-listed roundtail chub and razorback sucker, the endangered Colorado squawfish, and the potential recovery of the endangered Gila topminnow (listed here as "woundfin") and threatened loach minnow.

**Mitigation categories.** The Service and AGFD assigned Resource Category 1 (no loss of existing habitat value) to Reaches 1–5, 6, 7 and 8 and Category 2 to the remaining East Verde reaches (per Appendix D, AGFD classifications). Only Aravaipa Canyon was identified as holding more native fish species than the upper Verde. Six native fish species remain, representing 55 percent of the original Verde River native fish fauna.

**Service recommendations:**
1. Prepare an Environmental Impact Statement addressing Reclamation's selected alternative.
2. No water withdrawals from the Verde River (Reaches 1–7).
3. Maintain minimum flows listed in Table 9 in the East Verde River reaches. Natural flow should be provided when natural flow falls below the indicated amount during periods without trans-basin diversion.
4. Cap maximum diversion rates on the East Verde at 1 cfs for the Tonto Apache Indian Reservation and E&R Water Company, and 4 cfs for the City of Payson.
5. Conduct long-term monitoring of riparian and aquatic resources on the East Verde, beginning before any diversion or construction.
6. Re-analyze any changes to diversion plans or additional diversions or impoundments.

**Conclusions.** The Service does not believe the proposed Verde River diversions should proceed without adequate study of CAP diversion alternatives. Reclamation must complete additional data collection and analysis under the FWCA before authorizing diversions, and compliance with the [[Endangered Species Act]] and the [[Clean Water Act]] is required. The report states that conditions could be partly offset by watershed improvements, and that diversion amounts cannot be changed once granted as state water rights.
