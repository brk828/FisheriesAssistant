# Endangered Fishes of the United States: Legislation, Case Studies and Recovery Efforts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Endangered-Fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1977 *Frontiers* feature (Summer 1977 issue) reviews the decline of North American freshwater and estuarine fishes and the legal and scientific response to it. It traces US wildlife protection from early legislation around 1900 through the [[Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966]] to the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]], and then examines case studies in which courts, dams and recovery programs determined whether species survived. The article's central argument is that habitat alteration, pollution, nonnative species and overfishing threaten fish faunas, and that legal protection of [[Critical Habitat]] is essential to conservation.

## Background & Description

- **Historical diversity:** When European settlers arrived, more than 725 fish species lived in the springs, lakes and rivers of the continental United States.
- **Current status:** At the time of writing, 34 species and subspecies of native fishes were listed as endangered or threatened. The article notes that more than 90 additional fishes may need protection worldwide.
- **Legislative timeline:**
  - Around 1900, legislation to protect wildlife was initiated, but it took 66 years for legislation specifically protecting endangered species to be enacted.
  - In 1966, the Endangered Species Preservation Act authorized an official list of endangered species and allowed use of Land and Water Conservation Fund money to acquire habitat.
  - The 1973 Endangered Species Act directs federal agencies to conserve listed species and their ecosystems, covers all animals and plants, and prohibits the taking, trade or harmful acts affecting listed species.
- **Extinct and lost species:**
  - The [[Harelip Sucker]] (*Lagochila lacera*) was described in 1883 by [[David Starr Jordan]] and A. W. Brayton from Chickamauga Creek, Georgia, and was last seen in Ohio's Auglaize River in 1893. Its extinction was probably due to suffocation under silt and pollutants.
  - At least 11 other species and 4 subspecies are lost. The [[Ash Meadows Killifish]], Pahranagat spinedace, whitline topminnow and other fishes are cited as extinct examples.
- **Bison comparison:** [[American Bison]] numbered about 60 million in the 1840s, but by 1884 only a few hundred remained. Protection and parks brought them back, illustrating that recovery is possible when action is taken.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article identifies three categories of habitat alteration in aquatic ecosystems, where components are tightly linked so that an effect on one alters others:

1. **Physical changes:** water diversion, [[Dams]], stream channelization and dredging.
2. **Chemical alteration:** pollution that upsets acid-base balance, lowers dissolved oxygen, and adds metals, pesticides and industrial chemicals such as PCBs, which are described as subtle but ultimately lethal.
3. **Biological alteration:** introduction of nonnative or exotic species causing predation, competition or hybridization.

The article notes that [[Overfishing]] can decimate a species, but endangerment generally occurs only when overfishing is combined with habitat alteration.

**Stated value of conservation:** The article lists ecological (system stability and diversity), genetic, medical, economic, educational, aesthetic and ethical reasons. It cites [[Jean Mayer]], President of Tufts University, on the rights of species to survive, and quotes the 1976 presidential candidate [[Jimmy Carter]] on the importance of abundant and diverse fish and wildlife.

## Key Findings

### Case Study: Snail Darter and Tellico Dam
- The [[Snail Darter]] (*Percina tanasi*), a 3-inch perch-family fish found by David Etnier in 1973, occurs only on clean gravel shoals in a confined portion of the Little Tennessee River, Loudon County, Tennessee.
- It was listed as endangered in November 1975, and the lower 17 miles of the Little T were designated critical habitat in April 1976.
- The [[Tennessee Valley Authority]]'s [[Tellico Dam]] would eliminate the last free-flowing stretch of the lower Little Tennessee, burying the habitat under 50 feet of water.
- In May 1976 the District Court of Knoxville denied the Tennessee Endangered Species Committee's request for a permanent injunction. On appeal, the [[Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals]] ruled unanimously on January 31, 1977, to halt the project despite about $100 million already spent.
- Over 700 darters were transplanted to the Hiwassee River, and recent evidence suggests reproduction has occurred. The larvae swept downstream are trapped below the dam and subject to heavy predation, and stranded darters were released into the Little Tennessee after the appeal decision.

### Case Study: Colorado River Fishes
- The [[Colorado River]] system is described as having one of the most depauperate fish faunas in North America, with fewer than ten native species in the main channel before nonnative introductions. The river falls over two miles in its 1,700-mile course and ranges from 3,000 to 380,000 cubic feet per second.
- Four species are in trouble: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, endangered), [[Humpback Chub]] (endangered), bonytail chub and humpback sucker (under review).
- The Colorado squawfish is the largest minnow in North America, reaching 4 to 5 feet and 80 pounds before 1900, and was commercially fished until about 1910.
- Since [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1935), over 20 mainstream and tributary dams have altered flow, temperature and sediment regimes. Exotic fishes now predominate, and only one of the four big river species, the humpback chub, was taken in 285 miles between Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead.

### Case Study: Desert Pupfishes
- Of the 35 protected native fishes, 10 belong to the pupfish and mosquitofish families of the arid Southwest. Most are under 2 to 3 inches long.
- The [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) lives in a pool 10 by 50 feet, 60 feet below the desert floor in Death Valley National Monument, Nevada. It depends on a submerged 10 by 20 foot shelf for spawning and algal growth. Increased groundwater pumping in the late 1960s threatened the shelf. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled to protect the pupfish in 1976. After the water level was stabilized and the artificial shelf removed, the population grew to nearly half its original size.
- The [[Owens Pupfish]] was believed extinct in 1940 due to predation by introduced bass, but a relict population of under 200 was found in Fish Slough, California, in 1964.
- The [[Amistad Gambusia]] was discovered in 1968 in Goodenough Spring, Texas, and was lost when Amistad Reservoir submerged its habitat under about 90 feet of water. Forty individuals were collected before this, and the species is now extinct in the wild but propagates in captivity.

### Case Study: Pyramid Lake Fishes
- The [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a large sucker weighing up to 6 pounds, lives in [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada, and historically spawned in the Truckee River. Its population declined in the 1960s to the point that only the Paiute tribe could fish for it.
- The [[Newlands Reclamation Project]] (1906) blocked spawning grounds at Derby Dam and lowered the lake, leaving a silt-clogged delta.
- A Fish and Wildlife Service recovery program began in 1972. In 1976 the Hardscrabble Creek Hatchery produced 1.4 million eggs and 800,000 fry. A fish ladder was built over the delta in 1975, but its success is not yet known.
- The [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] shares these problems, and recovery and reintroduction efforts are also under way.

### Other Cases and Notes
- The [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser brevirostrum*) ranged from the St. Johns River in Florida to the St. John River in New Brunswick. It was commercially overfished through 1900, and its river-estuarine habitat is vulnerable to municipal and industrial pollution and dredging.
- The [[Razorback Sucker]] is protected under the law, and anglers who accidentally catch one are told to return it to the water and report its time, place and size.
- The article notes that the 1973 Act includes protection for some foreign fishes that are listed in international appendices, and that the U.S. and other countries implement the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES).

### Overall Conclusions
- Endangerment can now be recognized before most species vanish, so there is still time for action.
- Protection depends on legal authority, scientific monitoring and sustained public support, and the article argues that the 1973 Act is among the most important animal protection laws ever enacted.
