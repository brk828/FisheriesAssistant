# Lower Colorado River Carp Problem: Feasibility of Commercial Fishing and Chemical Control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

A 1956 study by fisheries biologist Wm. L. Haskell assessed common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) on the [[Lower Colorado River]] in Yuma County, Arizona, where carp were judged abundant enough to threaten the maintenance of a [[Game Fishery]]. The study examined three control approaches: [[Commercial Carp Fishing]], [[Selective Internal Poisoning]] and [[General Poisoning]], plus a general biological investigation. Commercial fishing was concluded to be economically unfeasible at that time, general poisoning was judged unsatisfactory, and selective internal poisoning was identified as the most promising option, pending discovery of a suitable lethal agent.

## Background & Description

The document is a two-part report (dated June 1956 and July 1956) presenting a trapping and poisoning study. The carp population of the lower river and its diversion network of canals and drainage ditches was described as prolific, dense and generally healthy, with very little evidence of disease. Carp were noted to be most numerous in backwaters, bays, lakes and sloughs beside the river, and only in small numbers in the main channel.

Study methods included:
- **Trapping:** simple cylindrical wire traps, six feet long and three feet in diameter, with variable funnel mouths. Seining was rejected because stumps, rocks and debris tore nets and made it prohibitively expensive.
- **Baiting:** commercial cattle feed pellets, cottonseed meal, surplus tortilla dough, and bakery bread dough, held in plastic screen or 1/4-inch mesh bags. An oval bag of about two quarts capacity was found most effective.
- **Poison trials:** insecticides, rodenticides and calcium carbide mixed into attractive baits, with oil of anise used to mask flavor.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal feeding:** Catches were consistent for just over a year, except for nearly three months (January to March) when catches fell to practically nil. Feeding largely stopped when bottom water temperatures dropped below about 56° F, and the lowest winter temperature recorded (1955-56) was 48° F, with no catch in any trap.
- **Spawning:** Spawning began with very few individuals in early April and continued through summer, with most fish spawning later in the season. Spawning was not concentrated in a short window, so little control could be applied by timing. Eggs were preferentially deposited on submerged aquatic vegetation, which becomes dense in late summer and hampers trapping and seining.
- **Game fish impacts:** General poisoning would also kill young game fish sheltering in weed beds.
- **Water chemistry:** Stomach and intestine of about 50 samples were only slightly acidic, with pH a little over 6.
- **Environmental safety:** Any poison must kill carp without harming livestock, humans or other aquatic life. Tested poisons were not expected to harm mammals except rats.
- **Climate:** High temperatures in the area made rapid handling and preservation of harvested fish essential.

## Key Findings

**1. Commercial fishing (Markets, Marketability, Prices, Costs)**
- Local markets in Yuma County and Imperial County were judged negligible. Los Angeles was the main viable market, with an established demand for carp. Phoenix and San Diego were considered minor markets.
- Demand was for fresh whole fish of 3 to 7 pounds. Of several thousand carp trapped and weighed, only 10% weighed 3 pounds or more.
- Prices to fishermen in Los Angeles ranged from about 3 to 10 cents per pound, averaging about 5 cents per pound. Price depended on fish size under contract.
- Transport distance to Los Angeles was roughly 300 miles from Yuma. Icing or holding costs would fall on the fisherman, and live boxes complicated handling.
- A dog and cat food manufacturer could absorb unmarketable fish, but would require at least two years of consistent catch records. Fertilizer use was considered but excluded.
- **Conclusion:** Commercial carp fishing on the lower Colorado was not economically feasible at that time, particularly for small operations without ample working capital.

**2. Selective Internal Poisoning**
- Calcium carbide failed because carp thoroughly mouth food, exposing carbide to water, which released acetylene and caused rejection. Carp also refused solid objects in the mouth. Encapsulated carbide in doughballs was quickly ejected even in the smallest capsules.
- Insecticides tested: Toxaphene, DDT, Malathion, Chlordane. Rodenticides tested: Pival, Red Squill.
- Some poisoned baits were readily consumed and dissections showed bait in intestines, yet no ill effects were observed. The poison appeared neutralized in the gut, or the quantity taken was insufficient.
- Increasing doses led carp to reject baits despite anise oil, indicating a fixed tolerance threshold.
- Results to date were negative. Work was to continue in a large pond at the Papago Hatchery.

**3. General Poisoning**
- Judged infeasible for routine use because carp are widespread and reproduce rapidly, and game fish would recover more slowly than carp.
- Localized treatment might help when large schools move into shoal water, typically in late spring and early fall, but timing is unpredictable and aerial observation would be expensive.

**4. Biological Investigation**
- Age determination from scale annuli proved unreliable due to indistinct rings, false checks and fusion. Winter cold rarely lasted long enough to form a clear annulus.
- Population appeared well fed, strong and disease-free.

**Overall Conclusions (July 1956)**
1. Commercial fishing is not practical for economic reasons.
2. General poisoning is unsatisfactory except in rare, unpredictable cases.
3. Selective internal poisoning is an excellent possibility if a suitable lethal agent is found; present baits are satisfactory as a delivery vehicle.
4. The biological findings are informative, but restraining carp remains unsolved.
