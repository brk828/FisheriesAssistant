# The Bullfrog (Rana catesbeiana) in the Lower Colorado River, Arizona-California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ranidae #herpetology #invasive-species #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1986 study by Clarkson and DeVos investigated the ecology of the introduced [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) along the [[Lower Colorado River]] between Laguna and Morelos Dams (the "Yuma Division"), Arizona-California. Conducted in summer 1981, the research quantified bullfrog density, habitat associations, population structure, and feeding habits in a **lotic (flowing water) riverine environment** — a departure from most prior bullfrog studies conducted in lentic (pond) systems. Key findings include an average density of 9.1 adult frogs/linear km, strong positive association with reed cover (*Phragmites australis*, *Arundo donax*), a highly generalist/opportunistic diet including novel prey records (muskrat, rattlesnake), and discussion of how unidirectional river current likely disrupts classic pond-based bullfrog breeding/territorial behaviors. The study was motivated by the need to predict ecological effects of future flow and hydrology changes on the river.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Rana catesbeiana* Shaw — one of the largest and most widespread frog species in North America.
- **Native range**: Permanent ponds and slow streams east of the Rocky Mountains, from Canada to northeastern Mexico.
- **Introduction history**: Introduced to California and the western U.S. in the early 1900s (Storer, 1922); rapidly established across a wide geographic range.
- **Ecological impact**: Implicated in the displacement or extirpation of native amphibian species (e.g., [[California Red-Legged Frog]], leopard frogs of the *Rana pipiens* complex) via predation and competition (Moyle, 1973; Jennings and Hayes, 1985).
- **Study rationale**: Baseline ecological data were needed to predict how changes in river discharge and hydrology might affect this semi-aquatic/terrestrial species, given its dependence on both aquatic and riparian habitats.
- **Study design**: 32 transects (0.4 km each) established along the river between 2.2 km below Laguna Dam and 1.3 km above Morelos Dam. Nighttime visual surveys via boat with 12V DC lamp; frogs collected by gig and dip net; snout-vent length (SVL) and weight recorded; stomach contents analyzed via a modified point-volume method (Hynes, 1950).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**River hydrology (1981 discharges):**
- Below Laguna Dam: 350–609 cfs
- Near River Mile (RM) 27.5 (below Yuma Main Canal wasteway): 590–883 cfs
- Above Morelos Dam (international boundary): 3400–3700 cfs
- Current velocities were generally <0.5 m/sec except near Laguna Dam and a stretch near RM 24.

**Bank vegetation classification (5 categories):**
1. Reeds (*Phragmites australis*, *Arundo donax*) — **49%** of bank cover (dominant), slightly more abundant on California side
2. Open bank — 36%
3. Salt cedar (*Tamarix* sp.) — 6%
4. Emergents (cattail *Typha domingensis*, bullrush *Scirpus californicus*) — 5%
5. Riprap — 4%

**Thermal ecology considerations:**
- [[Bullfrog]] body temperatures are typically maintained between 26–33°C, with a Critical Thermal Maximum (CTM) of 38.2°C (Lillywhite, 1970).
- Yuma-area nighttime air temperatures often approach CTM in summer, while river mainstream water temperatures remain <33°C.
- Extreme daytime heat (>40°C) and low humidity likely explain the complete absence of daytime bullfrog observations.
- Possible land-water "shuttling" thermoregulatory behavior (per Lillywhite, 1970) may bias nighttime visual density counts downward.

**Co-occurring species:**
- Two individual leopard frogs (*Rana pipiens* complex) were observed — notable because Vitt and Ohmart (1978) had previously considered this species extirpated from the lower Colorado River.
- Prey base included [[Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarki*), an invasive Asiatic clam (*Corbicula fluminea*), and multiple unidentified fish species, indicating the bullfrog's integration into a broader invasive-dominated aquatic food web.

**Human impact:**
- Legal bullfrog hunting season begins July 1 in both Arizona and California (bag limit: 12 frogs).
- Boat access/launch sites correlated spatially with lower bullfrog densities, suggesting harvest pressure and/or boat traffic disturbance as confounding factors in density patterns.

## Key Findings

### Distribution & Abundance
- Mean density: **9.1 adult bullfrogs per linear km** of river (both banks combined).
- Arizona-side bank: 4.7/km; California-side bank: 4.4/km.
- Highest densities occurred near River Mile 30 and adjacent to the dams.
- Three repeated visual counts (July 10–August 13, 1981) showed no significant difference (two-tailed t-test, α=0.05), indicating stable detectability over the survey period.
- No larval bullfrogs (tadpoles) were found despite extensive searches in mainstream and backwater habitats — a key unresolved finding suggesting either unsurveyed breeding/rearing habitat or extensive larval/adult dispersal (>10 km).

### Habitat Associations
- Transects with **≥50% reed cover** had significantly higher bullfrog density (5.9/km per bank) than those with <50% reed cover (4.6/km; P<0.05).
- Transects with **≥50% open bank** (including riprap) had significantly lower density (2.3/km) compared to 25–50% open bank (4.6/km) and 0–25% open bank (5.9/km).
- Frogs were typically found in short open-bank reaches *between* dense reed patches — suggesting optimal habitat requires a **mosaic of cover and open escape routes** to water, rather than either extreme alone.
- Frogs preferentially perched on bare mud-silt banks (within 1–2 m of shore) or flat-faced riprap boulders; rarely observed on floating reed mats in the mainstream.

### Population Structure
- Sex ratio (n=61 dissected): **1:1**, consistent with literature values (Schroeder and Baskett, 1968; Emlen, 1976; Durham and Bennett, 1963).
- All female specimens were spent or immature.
- SVL size-frequency histogram showed no discrete size classes — consistent with known overlap in body size/age classes in adult bullfrogs.
- Two specimens (SVL 54 mm and 47 mm) represented recently metamorphosed adults, within the literature range of transformation SVL (25–60 mm; Collins, 1979).

### Feeding Ecology
- 100 adult bullfrog stomachs examined (Aug 15–Sept 24, 1981); average fullness = 29%; 17% were nearly empty (≤5% full).
- Highly opportunistic/generalist diet: 32 total prey taxa identified; average of 1.9 taxa per stomach; each food category averaged only 2.4% relative volume and appeared in only 6.5% of stomachs (19 of 32 categories found in just two stomachs each).
- **Highest volume contributor**: [[Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarki*) — 19.2% mean relative volume, found in 24% of stomachs.
- **Most frequent prey**: Wolf spiders (Lycosidae) — 15.3% relative volume, present in 36% of stomachs.
- Other frequent items: long-horned earwigs (Labiduridae, 20%), sowbugs (Oniscidae, 25%), German cockroaches (14%), crickets (16%), and vascular plant material (36%).
- Inorganic material (sand/gravel) found in 12% of stomachs.
- **Novel/notable prey records**: young muskrat (*Ondatra zibethicus*), western diamondback rattlesnake (*Crotalus atrox*), kingsnake (*Lampropeltis getulus*), soft-shelled turtle (*Trionyx spiniferus*, carapace ~45mm), Asiatic clam (*Corbicula fluminea*), scorpion (*Hadrurus arizonensis*), several unidentified fish species, and numerous coleopteran insects.
- This study adds the **rattlesnake and muskrat as new prey records** for the species.

### Breeding Biology in Lotic vs. Lentic Systems
- Proposed life history timeline for the Lower Colorado River population (synthesizing Haskell 1956, Dowe 1979, and this study):
  - Breeding begins early April when daytime water temps exceed 20°C.
  - Peak breeding: late April–early May and late May–early June, with sporadic calling into July (ceasing by August).
  - Eggs hatch in 2–4 days; larvae remain near hatching site ~3 days before dispersing.
  - First-clutch larvae typically metamorphose in 3–6 months (Aug–Oct); second-clutch larvae may metamorphose in the same period or overwinter as tadpoles, transforming the following March–April.
- **Key ecological problem identified**: Unidirectional river current fundamentally challenges lentic-pond breeding models (territorial male oviposition-site defense, floating egg films, chorus structure as described by Emlen 1968/1976, Howard 1978a/b, Ryan 1980).
  - Eggs deposited as a surface monolayer (per Aronson, 1943) would not remain in a male's territory in flowing water unless anchored to structure.
  - Comparison to other lotic-breeding ranids (*Rana boylei*, *R. tarahumara*, *R. aurora aurora*, *R. a. draytonii*, *R. pretiosa*) shows these species universally attach eggs to submergent/emergent structures — suggesting Lower Colorado River bullfrogs may do the same, though this remains unconfirmed.
  - True multi-directional "chorus" behavior (per Howard's lek concept) may not be possible in a lotic system; territorial defense is hypothesized to occur primarily along a single bank dimension.

### Broader Significance
- Bullfrog densities in this river system were comparable to those reported by Treanor (1975) in California canals, indicating successful establishment in a highly modified, turbid, thermally extreme lotic system quite unlike the species' native lentic habitats.
- The findings underscore the bullfrog's ecological plasticity and its status as a well-established invasive component of the [[Lower Colorado River]] aquatic community, with direct implications for **native amphibian conservation** (e.g., leopard frogs) and for anticipating impacts of future river flow management changes.
