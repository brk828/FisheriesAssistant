# Inter-basin Water Transfer: Ecological Concerns

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #percidae #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Michael R. Meador]] of the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] examines the ecological consequences of [[Inter-basin Water Transfer]], the movement of water from one drainage basin to another. The paper briefly traces the history of such projects from ancient Mesopotamia to the [[California Water Project]], identifies four categories of ecological impact, and proposes research and planning guidelines. The author argues that these impacts are the most serious consequences of water transfer, yet they have received limited study. The paper calls for long-term, multidisciplinary baseline research incorporated into the planning phase of future projects.

## Background & Description

**Definition and scope.** The paper notes that municipal and domestic water needs have long taken priority over environmental, recreational, industrial, and hydropower uses. As human populations grow, demand often exceeds regional supply, so many water projects move water from areas of surplus to areas of deficit. [[Canada]] defines a water transfer by two criteria: (1) diverted flow that does not return to the stream of origin or parent stream within 20 km of the withdrawal point, and (2) mean annual flow transferred of at least 0.5 m³/s (Quinn 1981).

**Historical development.** Inter-basin transfer is very old. Archaeological evidence suggests a development project in Babylonian times connected the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. In the Western Hemisphere, ruins in Peru indicate a canal carried water from the Andes to the capital. American Indians in central Arizona built an extensive canal system in what is now the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]]. Spanish acequias in the southwestern United States are not classed as inter-basin transfers but may have influenced later large-scale projects.

**California.** [[California]] was the first U.S. state to develop an inter-basin transfer to meet regional demand, because most of its usable water originates in the north while most demand lies in the semiarid south. Proposals to carry water from the Sacramento Valley to the San Joaquin Valley date to 1873. The [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]] (1913) carried water from the Owens Valley on the eastern slope of the [[Sierra Nevada]]. A 389-km aqueduct, the [[Colorado River Aqueduct]] (1941), moved water to the Los Angeles area. The [[California Water Project]] (1972), one of the most complex and expensive transfers, moved water from the [[Feather River]] to southern California using 21 dams and reservoirs, 22 pumping plants, and an extensive network of canals, tunnels, and pipelines. The author notes that Apollo astronauts reportedly identified only two major structures from orbit: the [[Great Wall of China]] and the main aqueduct of the California Water Project.

**Other North American transfers.** Settlers in the late nineteenth century diverted water from the western slope of the [[Rocky Mountains]] through tunnels such as the Alva B. Adams, Hillcrest, Vasquez, Gumlick, and Roberts tunnels. The [[Big Thompson Project]] moved Colorado River water to the South Platte basin, and the [[Grand Ditch]] diverted Colorado River water into the Arkansas basin. Texas has considered transfers from the Mississippi River, and [[New York City]] draws water from the [[Delaware River]] basin. Transfers have also been proposed for Florida, Connecticut, and Virginia. The proposed [[Grand Canal]] (Great Lakes–James Bay scheme) and the [[North American Water and Power Alliance]] illustrate the scale and complexity of international proposals, which have not been built.

**Global context.** [[China]] and India operate inter-basin transfer programs. The United Kingdom has proposed a large transfer to meet growing demand. The author suggests that about 9% of the total mean annual runoff of the world may soon be diverted from one river basin to another (Petticrew and Davies 1985).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper argues that the most serious ecological impacts arise because water moves between drainage basins, which can bring biota, chemistry, and flow regimes into systems where they did not previously occur. Most transfers have been completed with limited consideration of ecological impacts, which were often treated as secondary concerns. Ecological impacts of transfers are poorly documented, and the author notes that few published accounts of their consequences existed at the time of writing.

### Four Categories of Impact

**1. Introduction of aquatic organisms.** Breaching basin boundaries allows introduction of parasites, pathogens, plants, invertebrates, and fish. Such introductions have been reported in the former Soviet Union, where diseases and non-native species followed inter-basin transfers. A non-native fish, the [[Zander]], was reported in the lower River Stour in England after the [[Great Ouse]] transfer. The author notes that whether a species is "native" or "exotic" often depends on how these terms are defined. In the [[Orange River]] transfer in South Africa, fish species once thought to be exotic were later considered native to both basins (Laurenson and Hocutt 1986; Laurenson et al. 1989).

**2. Changes in flow regime.** Flow changes are a major concern. In the Orange River to Great Fish River transfer, the upper Great Fish River received a large increase in flow. Water diverted from the Colorado River increased mean flow in the North Fork of the South Platte River. In South Carolina, the [[Santee River]] was diverted into the [[Cooper River]]. Hydropower production reduced flow in the Santee River, while the Cooper River received an increased flow (Meador et al. 1984). In the Great Fish River, O'Keefe and De Moore (1988) reported that shifts in the benthic invertebrate community were driven mainly by flow changes. Only 33% of invertebrate taxa were common to pre- and post-transfer surveys. Dominant chironomid, hydropsychid, and simuliid taxa changed, and a blood-feeding black fly that harms livestock replaced earlier dominant species.

**3. Alteration of habitat.** Changes in the volume and timing of discharge can seriously affect aquatic habitat. In many recipient basins, seasonally intermittent waters have become perennial. Before transfer, the Great Fish River consisted of isolated pools. It is now a permanently flowing water body. The effects of such habitat changes on the seasonal life cycles of stream fishes are not yet clear. Habitat alteration may favour introduced species that then eliminate native fauna through predation, competition, and higher reproductive rates (Mooney and Drake 1986; Moyle et al. 1986).

**4. Changes in water quality.** Moving water also moves physicochemical conditions. In arid lands, conductivity changes are a common problem. [[Lake Texoma]] is relatively saline (about 1300 µS/cm) because of the [[Red River]], and water pumped to the fresher [[Lake Lavon]] (where conductivity often exceeds 300 µS/cm) will alter its chemistry. Transfer from the Santee River to the Cooper River in South Carolina caused saltwater intrusion in the Santee River and increased silt in the Cooper River, requiring silt removal from Charleston Harbor. Transfers from the [[Snowy River]] in the [[Snowy Mountains]] of eastern Australia into the western [[Murray River]] and [[Murrumbidgee River]] lowered water temperature for considerable distances downstream, and also lowered turbidity, salinity, and pH. These changes appear to have affected spawning of native fishes in the Murray and Murrumbidgee rivers.

### Striped Bass in Lake Texoma

The transfer of water from Lake Texoma to Lake Lavon has raised concern for the [[Striped Bass]] fishery in Lake Texoma, which supports the largest landlocked population of striped bass in the United States. Coutant (1985) suggested that summer mortality of reservoir striped bass resulted from loss of suitable habitat. Mortality occurred when epilimnetic water temperatures exceeded 25°C and dissolved oxygen in the hypolimnion fell below 2 mg/L. The author concludes that the depth of water intake for transfer is critical to the physicochemical habitat of striped bass.

### Ecological Knowledge Gaps

The paper notes that ecological functioning in rivers is poorly understood. Impact assessment therefore needs broadly based research across hydrology, limnology, invertebrate ecology, botany, fisheries biology, and systems analysis. Table 1 of the paper suggests specific topics for each discipline: hydrologists should study water quantity and sedimentation, limnologists and chemists should study water quality, invertebrate ecologists should study temporal variability and drift, botanists should study riparian vegetation, fisheries biologists should study introductions and fish disease, and systems analysts should evaluate responses under varying discharge rates.

## Key Findings

- **Consequences are under-studied.** Ecological impacts of inter-basin transfers have received limited attention, and in most cases environmental considerations were secondary to engineering and economic ones.
- **Four impact categories** were identified: introduction of aquatic organisms, changes in flow regime, alteration of habitat, and changes in water quality.
- **Flow and invertebrates.** Flow changes altered benthic invertebrate communities in the Great Fish River, with only 33% of taxa shared before and after transfer.
- **Water quality shifts** include saltwater intrusion, increased silt, and altered temperature, turbidity, salinity, and pH, with effects on native fish spawning.
- **Reservoir fish habitat** is sensitive to intake depth. Striped bass summer mortality in Lake Texoma is linked to temperature and dissolved oxygen thresholds (25°C; 2 mg/L).
- **Impacts depend on definitions.** Whether species count as native or exotic depends on political or biological criteria.
- **Recommended research design.** The author proposes a minimum of 3 years of intensive preoperational baseline studies, followed by a multi-year operational period and multi-year postoperational studies. Research should use multidisciplinary, coordinated methods that account for long-term variability across basins.
- **Distinguish impacts.** Direct impacts of transfer should be separated into effects in the exporting basin, the receiving basin, and the conveyance mechanism. These should be distinguished from ecological changes caused by subsequent water use.
- **Policy conclusion.** Quoting Thomas and Box (1969), the paper calls for ecological studies to be built into the initial planning of large-scale water movement. Petticrew and Davies (1985) urged national ecological impact assessment and a workshop to develop methods.
- **Fisheries role.** Fisheries scientists should participate in identifying research needs to anticipate impacts (Kapuscinski and Halverson 1990), but the author stresses that the proposals are meant to stimulate discussion rather than serve as final guidelines.
