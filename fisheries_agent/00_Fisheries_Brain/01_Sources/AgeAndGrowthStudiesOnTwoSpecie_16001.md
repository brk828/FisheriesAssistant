# Age and Growth Studies on Two Species of Whitefishes from Point Barrow, Alaska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonine

## Executive Summary

This paper, published in the [[Stanford Ichthyological Bulletin]] (Vol. 4, No. 3, 1954) by [[Daniel M. Cohen]], describes the age and growth of two coregonine whitefishes near [[Point Barrow]], Alaska: [[Coregonus Nasus Kennicotti]] and [[Coregonus Sardinella]]. The author states these are the first age and growth studies of fresh-water fishes in the Alaskan Arctic. Fish were collected mainly in [[Ikroavik Lake]] and in a brackish inlet connected to the Arctic Ocean. The study found that kennicotti spawns early (July or earlier), that its eggs hatch faster than in related coregonines, and that the sardinella populations fall into three scale-defined groups with different growth rates. Lake fish grew faster than marine fish, the reverse of the pattern seen in many temperate anadromous salmonids. The author concludes that local fresh-water whitefish populations are small and slow-growing, making a commercial fishery improbable, though local Eskimo use could be sustained.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and context.** The study aimed to quantify age and growth to support understanding of fishery population dynamics. The author notes that, according to [[Norman J. Wilimovsky]], Eskimo use of Alaskan arctic fresh-water fishes has been inefficient and of secondary importance, and their potential is unexplored.

**Study species.**
- *Coregonus nasus kennicotti* Milner: according to [[V. C. Wynne-Edwards]] (1952), it occurs in the Mackenzie and Yukon rivers and has a Siberian distribution. No prior growth studies on this species were known.
- *Coregonus sardinella* (Valenciennes): a cisco or lake herring. Apart from one specimen note by Clemens, Boughton, and Rattenbury (1944), no other growth observations were known.
- The author notes that *Coregonus* replaced *Leucichthys* following [[V. Legendre]] (1954) and Wilimovsky (1954).

**Study area.**
- [[Ikroavik Lake]]: a shallow northern lake about six miles south of the [[Arctic Research Laboratory]]. Both species were collected here.
- First Inlet: a body of water about three miles southeast of the laboratory, roughly one mile long and at most 350 yards wide. A sand spit separates it from [[Elson Lagoon]] through a channel 18 to 20 feet wide. The deepest part is a five-foot channel scoured by tidal current. Most of the inlet is three feet deep or less, with a sticky gray silt bottom overlying permafrost. Water turbidity limited visibility to a few inches.

**Methods.**
- Capture: a 200-foot seine (15 ft deep at center, tapering to 6 ft; three-quarter inch mesh) and 100-by-six-foot gill nets (one inch mesh) at both sites. Two experimental 250-by-six-foot multi-mesh gill nets were used in Ikroavik Lake in early September.
- Measurements: standard, fork, and total lengths to the nearest millimeter.
- Ageing: scales were taken from the area between the lateral line and the anterior dorsal fin (chosen following [[Van Oosten]] 1928), mounted in glycerin and gelatin (after [[Karl F. Lagler]], 1952), and read on a projection microscope.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning and hatching.** Kennicotti in Ikroavik Lake spawned in July based on gonad examination, and newly hatched fry were captured in June 1951, suggesting even earlier spawning. Eggs hatched in 30 to 60 days, compared with 100 to 150 days in other coregonines. The development temperatures (printed as "6° and 1 C" in the scan, versus the usual 4 °C in southern deep-lake congeners) were attributed to the lake's peak temperatures during spawning and hatching. This parallels [[John W. Price]] (1940) on [[Lake Whitefish]] incubation, where warmer temperatures sped development.
- **Hereditary versus environmental timing.** Because sardinella in the same lake spawn in September, the author argues the early kennicotti spawning may be hereditary rather than environmental, pending studies of other kennicotti populations.
- **Sardinella life cycle.** Sardinella spawn in September, probably near the fall freeze-over, hatch under the ice, and form scales the following summer.
- **Temperature and growth.** Lake fish grew faster than marine fish. The author suggests cooler ocean temperatures during the growing season explain this. [[Van Oosten]] (1923), working with aquarium coregonines, found temperature to be the main factor controlling seasonal growth variation.
- **Migration.** Two adult, sexually mature [[Pacific Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus keta*) were captured in Ikroavik Lake, and the author discusses whether "X" type fish migrate in from elsewhere (see Key Findings).
- **Lake outlet and downstream habitat.** The outlet stream was judged unsuitable as permanent habitat. Downstream, another lake's fish status is unknown, and [[Iko Bay]] is a known fish habitat, with tidal estuary depths reported to reach ten feet. No fish were taken there during the study.
- **Population size.** Based on [[Donald E. Wohlschlag]] (1953), Ikroavik Lake fish populations are small, and the author cites this as a limit on potential harvest.

## Key Findings

**Sample and sex ratio (Ikroavik kennicotti)**
- 22 specimens were sexed: 15 males and seven females (the Summary states seven females). The author notes that in the Mackenzie River, up to 15 females per male occurred at spawning.

**Age determination**
- Age equals the number of annuli plus one, because the first annulus forms after the second winter. For example, fish with scales first laid down are about one year old, and fish with one distinct annulus are two years old.

**Length–scale relationships (least-squares and log regressions, Y = fork length in mm; X = 43 × scale radius in mm)**
- Kennicotti: Y = 57.2 + 1.92X (linear).
- Marine sardinella: Y = 52 + 1.68X.
- "X" type sardinella: Y = 78 + 1.44X.
- "F" type sardinella (n = 200): log Y = 1.077 + 0.636 log X.
- Conversions: SL = 0.924 FL; TL = 1.091 FL.

**Year-class abundance**
- Kennicotti year-classes were highly irregular, and no correlation with Point Barrow weather was found. The author attributes this to small sample size and chance.
- For "F" type sardinella, 54.3% were three-year-olds, 15.3% five-year-olds, and only 6.7% four-year-olds. Roughly half of the three-year-olds came from a single seine haul. The author suggests unknown age-specific survival effects, consistent with [[Wohlschlag]] (in press) on increasing age-specific mortality.

**Sardinella scale types**
- "F" type: wide, clear annular bands free of circuli, from Ikroavik Lake.
- "X" type: annuli marked by extreme crowding of circuli, from Ikroavik Lake. A regenerated scale comparison was included to rule out regeneration.
- "M" type: evenly spaced circuli, with annuli lacking clear bands, mostly from the brackish inlet.
- Of 93 small sardinella captured in July 1952 (42–93 mm), 32 had no scales, 32 had scales only on the caudal peduncle, and 29 were scaled in the sampling area.

**Growth patterns**
- Lee's Phenomenon (apparent decline in growth in older year-classes, attributed to higher mortality of faster-growing fish) was detected in kennicotti and in all three sardinella populations. The author notes that negligible fishing in Ikroavik Lake suggests differential natural mortality.
- Growth ranking: "F" type fastest, "M" type slowest. The "X" type curve intersects the other two and declines more sharply with age.
- Lake fish grow faster and reach larger size than marine or brackish fish, which is the reverse of many temperate anadromous salmonids.

**Origin of "X" type fish**
- Most "X" type scales show a marginal zone of "F" type growth, sometimes up to two annuli, suggesting these fish entered the lake from elsewhere. Three kennicotti also showed "X" type growth, suggesting scale pattern is environmental rather than hereditary.
- No "X" type fish were taken outside the lake, though collection outside it was limited. Thirty-three fish with definite "M" type scales were taken in the lake, so "X" type fish are concluded not to originate there but to migrate in.

**Air temperature correlation**
- "X" type and, to a lesser extent, "F" type fish show some correlation between annual growth increments and average May–September air temperatures. "M" type fish show none. The author notes ocean currents and drifting ice could weaken such links in marine waters.

**Fishery conclusions**
- Two conclusions: populations are small, and they grow slowly as a resource. A commercial export fishery is improbable, especially with only air transport available. Local populations could tolerate more short-term Eskimo harvest for local use. River whitefish populations need much more life-history work before being considered a food resource.

**Companion paper.** The same bulletin issue (pp. 189–209) contains [[Donald E. Wohlschlag]]'s paper on growth peculiarities of the cisco near Point Barrow, which is not summarized here.
