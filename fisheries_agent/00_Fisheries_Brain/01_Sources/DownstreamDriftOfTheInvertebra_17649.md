# Downstream Drift of Stream Insects in the Speed River, Ontario

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study by J. E. Bishop and H. B. N. Hynes, published in [[Archiv für Hydrobiologie]], quantifies the downstream drift of benthic invertebrates in the Speed River, Ontario, using monthly 24-hour net samples from July 1966 to September 1967. Drift was strongly structured by time of day, discharge and [[Moonlight Effects on Aquatic Insects]]. Only a very small fraction of standing stock (0.0002–0.004 %) entered the water column at any moment, indicating that drift was largely passive and not density dependent. The drifting biomass was judged sufficient to sustain the resident [[Fish Standing Stock]], which was estimated at 3.82 g/m², yet drift represented only about 15 % of annual benthic production.

## Background & Description

Drift is framed as a consequence of current forces acting on a fauna adapted to lotic conditions. Morphological adaptations such as dorso-ventral flattening, streamlining and attachment organs were reviewed, along with the boundary-layer concept of [[Boundary Layer Hydraulics in Streams]]. Earlier authors had proposed competing explanations, including the [[Colonization Cycle Hypothesis]] of Müller and the carrying-capacity model of Waters, which links drift to excess benthic production. The study tested whether drift is density dependent, whether it is light controlled, and how much drift feeds fish.

Study site: the Speed River (48° 48' 54" N, 80° 16' 24" W) was 5–10 m wide and 8–15 cm deep, with discharge of 0.1–20 m/s, a glacial gravel and sand substrate, and a watershed of mixed agriculture. Water was moderately hard (pH 7.5–8.6; 90–200 ppm CaCO3), with nutrients peaking in late summer.

Methods: drift was collected in tapering terylene nets (mesh 167 × 560 µm) emptied every three hours. Sampling was timed to begin at twilight. Volumes were recorded using nine-point velocity transects across a 5 m board. Fish were sampled by five successive electrofishing sweeps over a 98 m riffle on 16 June 1967, applying the [[Removal Sampling Method]] (Libosvarsky 1966).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal drivers:** Drift peaks followed life-cycle events and disturbance. Peaks were linked to the [[Allocapnia pygmaea]] and *Leptophlebia* activity in February, spring-thaw spates in April 1967, and a wet June 1967 spate. Maxima in Ephemeroptera followed Baetis hatching in September.
- **Lunar effects:** Full moon depressed the nocturnal drift peak on 30 November 1966, 30 December 1966 and 24 May 1967. Cloud-obscured full moons had no effect.
- **Terrestrial input:** Terrestrial organisms made up only about 3 % of the drift, which the authors attribute partly to heavy fish predation along the bank.
- **Recolonization:** Drift can repopulate denuded areas, but normal productivity may require one or more seasons. Intermittent-stream recovery depends on subsurface refugia and fly-in colonization.
- **Pollution:** The fate of drift entering polluted reaches was not resolved; the authors note that no normal drift taxa were found on substrates in heavily contaminated areas.

## Key Findings

1. **Taxonomic participation:** All benthic taxa except Pelecypoda (bivalves) occurred in the drift at some stage. Of 159,087 aquatic drift organisms sampled, Diptera contributed 51.64 % of the total drift, Ephemeroptera 11.87 %, Trichoptera 7.70 %, Plecoptera 3.28 % and "Others" (mainly Copepoda and Hydracarina) 21.66 %. Total drift amounted to 163,923 organisms, with terrestrials at 2.95 %.
2. **Low proportion in drift:** Only 0.0002–0.004 % of benthic standing stock was in the drift at any time. The authors concluded that drift was passive rather than triggered by a concerted release mechanism.
3. **Diel periodicity:** All Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera and some Trichoptera were night-active. [[Helicopsyche Borealis]] (early instars), Hydracarina and Ostracoda were day-active. Diptera showed no consistent preference. Copepoda were mostly night-active.
4. **Discharge and sampling:** Drift density and biomass varied with discharge and water volume but did not correlate with benthic density (no density-dependent drift).
5. **Fish standing stock:** On the 98 m riffle, five sweeps captured 295 fish with a total weight of 1763.3 g, yielding an estimated population of 305 and a biomass of 3.82 g/m². Dominant species included [[Catostomus commersoni]], [[Semotilus atromaculatus]], [[Notropis cornutus]] and [[Rhinichthys]] spp. The estimate is compared with 1.95 g/m² from warm-water streams in southern Ontario (Johnson 1965).
6. **Production budget:** Annual benthic production was about 620 g/m² (Coleman 1968). Annual drift loss was 17.3–17.5 kg per transect width (about 70 g/m²/yr from a hypothetical 50 m source reach). Fish consumption was estimated at 25–30 g/m²/yr. Drift accounted for roughly 15 % of production, and about 85 % remained unaccounted for, attributed to adult emergence losses, invertebrate predation, flood stranding and natural mortality.
7. **Conclusion:** Drift in the Speed River was sufficient to support the resident fish population, but its magnitude was controlled by light, flow and benthic life stage rather than by competition for space.
