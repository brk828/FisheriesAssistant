# Endangered Native Desert Fishes of the Southwest

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This popular article by David Henderson describes three native fishes of the desert Southwest, the [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Bonytail Chub]], and the [[Desert Pupfish]], which the author says are now, "for all practical intents and purposes," extinct in the wild. Their decline is attributed mainly to loss of water habitat from [[Irrigation And Water Diversion]], with [[Predation By Introduced Fishes]] described as a serious additional threat. The article draws on comments from [[Chuck Minckley]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and contrasts the low public profile of these fishes with that of charismatic endangered animals.

## Background & Description

- **Historical fish community:** About 200 years ago, the lower [[Colorado River]] held only seven fish species, all native, according to Minckley.
- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]:** Described as growing to six feet in length, although the article's name for it is "seemingly misnamed."
- **Razorback sucker:** A bottom-dwelling fish that can grow to three feet and weigh up to 18 pounds.
- **Bonytail chub:** Slightly smaller, reaching about two feet.
- **Desert pupfish:** The smallest of the three, averaging about 1.5 inches. It was once widespread along the lower Colorado River and the [[Gila River]].
- **Comparison with charismatic species:** The author uses pandas, gorillas, tigers, rhinoceroses, manatees, fur seals, Mexican wolves, and snow leopards to illustrate how endangered fish struggle for public support.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss.** The three species lived in river channels and water courses of the desert Southwest. Their decline is linked to the loss of water habitat through irrigation and water diversion. Only a handful of remote desert springs still hold the desert pupfish.

**Predation by introduced fishes.** Non-native species were a major cause of mortality. Minckley explains that the razorback sucker and bonytail cannot compete with introduced fishes. Although razorbacks produce hundreds of thousands of eggs, the spawn and fry are quickly eaten. Predators named in the article include:
- Carp, catfish, bluegill, bass, striper, and trout (fish)
- Crayfish, snails, and dragonfly nymphs (invertebrates)

Minckley's summary is that young razorbacks and bonytails are "everyone's free lunch."

## Key Findings

1. **Three native fishes are functionally extinct in the wild.** The razorback sucker, bonytail chub, and desert pupfish have declined sharply and are now largely confined to captivity or remote refugia.
2. **Habitat loss is a primary driver.** Irrigation and water diversion removed the flowing water these fishes depended on.
3. **Predation has been devastating for larval and juvenile fish.** Introduced fishes and invertebrates eat the fry of razorbacks and bonytails, even though adults reproduce successfully.
4. **The fish community has changed substantially.** The lower Colorado River historically had seven native species, and the introduction of non-native fishes has altered it.
5. **Public awareness is a challenge.** The author argues that these fishes lack the public appeal of charismatic endangered animals, yet conservation advocates still work to protect them.
