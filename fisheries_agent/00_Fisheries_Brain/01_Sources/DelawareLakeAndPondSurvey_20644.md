# Delaware Lake and Pond Survey: Fisheries Publication No. 3 (1954)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Delaware Board of Game and Fish Commissioners publication (Fisheries Publication No. 3, Federal Aid Project F-1-R-2, by fisheries biologist Jay L. Harmic) reports the second phase of a survey of the state's freshwater ponds, carried out in 1952 and 1953. It covers six impoundments in Kent County and New Castle County: [[Killen's Pond]], [[McCauley's Pond]], [[Coursey's Pond]] and its [[Tumblehole]], [[Voshell's Pond]], [[Wyoming Pond]], [[State Pond]] (western section), and [[Lake Como]]. For each, the survey reports physical characters, limnology, bottom fauna, stomach contents, fish species abundance, and creel census results, followed by management recommendations. The report argues that most Delaware ponds are overpopulated with undesirable fish (especially [[Carp]] and stunted pan fish), suffer from siltation, aquatic weeds and neglected water-control structures, and that [[Reclamation]] (draining, netting, rotenone treatment and restocking with bass and bluegill) is the principal management tool.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** To determine the biological, physical and chemical condition of the state's impoundments and to increase freshwater sport fishing, which the authors consider below its potential given Delaware's high fishing area relative to its population.
- **Program history:** A preliminary analysis of nine ponds was made in summer 1950; a full-time fisheries program under [[Federal Aid to Fish Restoration]] followed, with the second phase in summer 1952.
- **Methods:** Procedures followed the first unit survey. Data came from seining, trapping, creel census, secchi disc visibility, limnological sampling (air and water temperature, dissolved oxygen, CO2, pH, plankton) and bottom fauna sampling at several stations per pond.
- **Setting:** Most ponds are former millponds built for water power, with dikes often carrying roads, and many are drained by manually operated gates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Overpopulation and stunting:** At [[Killen's Pond]], every 20-foot seine haul along the shore yielded 50 to 100 common sunfish, all about four inches long. Carp were overpopulated, and carp and sunfish were seen in hundreds at [[Coursey's Pond]].
- **Siltation:** Killen's Pond had about two feet of silt across most of its bottom. The authors note that Delaware's topsoil erosion is low (about 0.2 of 1% per year), but large watersheds still deliver enough sediment to fill ponds, and about one pond per year was being lost.
- **Vegetation:** Dense blanket algae (probably *Lyngbya* sp.) and spatterdock dominated Killen's Pond; water willow prevailed at [[McCauley's Pond]]; sago pondweed, *Elodea* and coontail were abundant at [[Voshell's Pond]]; and blanket algae covered most of [[Wyoming Pond]]. The authors state that large vegetation is not needed for productivity and can impede forage fish capture by game fish.
- **Water quality:** Summer surface temperatures ranged from about 81 to 92 °F. Secchi visibility ranged from 22 inches (Coursey's Pond) to 44 inches (Lake Como). Dissolved oxygen fell to 0 ppm near the bottom at 4–6 feet in several ponds, indicating stratification and low deep-water oxygen. Water pH ranged from about 5.4 to 7.0.
- **Bottom fauna:** Total organisms per square meter were modest, for example 39 to 49 at Killen's Pond, 40 to 59 at McCauley's Pond and 35 to 73 across stations at other ponds.
- **Human pressures:** Garbage, sewage and rubbish dumping at Wyoming Pond was identified as a health hazard, and [[Snapping Turtle]] predation was suspected but unconfirmed.

## Key Findings

**Pond-by-pond results**

- **[[Killen's Pond]] (74 acres, Murderkill system):** Fishing was extremely light. Carp were overpopulated and common sunfish stunted. Creel census (July–August 1952) recorded 0.237 bass and 0.493 combined fish per hour. Management recommended full reclamation, including drawdown, seining, rotenone treatment and restocking.
- **[[McCauley's Pond]] (55 acres, reclaimed):** Reopened after reclamation and restocking with bass and bluegill in 1950. Bass catch per fisherman was the highest of any pond in the state, although most bass ran about 1 to 1.5 pounds. Creel census showed 0.308 bass per hour and 0.418 combined fish per hour. Stocking of any species except bass was recommended for discontinuation.
- **[[Coursey's Pond]] and Tumblehole (60 acres and one acre):** The pond was closed to fishing during the survey. The tumblehole creel census recorded 0.043 bass and 0.450 combined fish per hour across 91 hours. Recommendations were conditional: reclamation only once the pond opens to the public, after Killen's Pond is reclaimed, and with repair of the leaking spillway.
- **[[Voshell's Pond]] (31.5 acres):** Good bass and pickerel catches, with bass up to nine pounds. Golden shiners were the most effective bait. Water-level drops from leaks reduced productivity, and dense weeds made summer fishing difficult. Creel census gave 0.10 bass and 0.433 combined fish per hour.
- **[[Wyoming Pond]] (52 acres):** Highly productive, with high bass and pickerel populations and bass over five pounds. Abundant golden shiners and chub suckers kept gamefish well fed and hard to catch. Creel census gave 0.096 bass and 0.333 combined fish per hour. Weed control and an end to dumping were urged.
- **[[State Pond]] (western section, 23 acres, Kirkwood):** Easy shoreline fishing and few fishers, with an unbalanced population of stunted sunfish, white and yellow perch, shiners, bullheads and crappies. Bass catch was 0.035 per hour and combined fish 0.230 per hour. Full reclamation of both sections was recommended, including a channel through the separating marsh.
- **[[Lake Como]] (44 acres, Smyrna):** Reclaimed in March–April, with commercial netting removing 16,350 pounds of carp and about 300 pounds of live bass. Roughly 4,000 pounds of dead fish were removed after rotenone treatment, for a total of about 20,650 pounds, of which carp made up 80%. Restocking used 91 largemouth bass and 200 bluegills. Reproduction of both bass and bluegill was later confirmed, though crappies and white perch reappeared.

**General conclusions and policy**

- Many Delaware ponds are out of balance, with carp the most serious offender. The authors recommend eliminating carp and other undesirable species and recognize that reclamation gives only temporary benefit unless management continues.
- Water-control structures need regular maintenance. Neglected gates and dikes have led to lowered water levels, vegetation growth or washouts, and the report recommends watershed-wide acquisition and maintenance rather than replacement after loss.
- Weed control is needed but no universal method exists; mechanical removal was ineffective, and chemical control is risky because the margin between a plant-killing and fish-killing concentration is narrow.
- Facilities (boat liveries, parking, public access) and regulations should be improved. Regulations should be pond-specific, with pan fish harvest increased and carp and redfin pickerel treated as undesirable and unprotected.
- The next survey segment will cover Sussex County ponds.
