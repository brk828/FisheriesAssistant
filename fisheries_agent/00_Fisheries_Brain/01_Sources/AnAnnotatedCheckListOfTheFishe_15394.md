# Annotated Check List of the Fishes of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This is the first published checklist of the fish fauna of [[Arizona]], compiled by Robert Rush Miller (University of Michigan) and Charles H. Lowe (University of Arizona). It is based on fieldwork and a review of the literature, and it covers both native species and species introduced by agencies and individuals, mainly for sport fishing. The list contains 68 species: 27 native, 37 established exotics and 4 hypothetical aliens that may become permanent residents. Between 1850 and 1960 the state's aquatic habitats changed sharply. Reduced surface flows restricted native ranges, and stocking of exotic species replaced and reduced native fishes. Six native species were identified as nearing extinction in Arizona.

## Background & Description

Published records of Arizona fishes date back to 1848, but no statewide inventory existed before this work. Early collections came from the [[United States and Mexican Boundary Survey]] (1851–54), with later key contributions from [[Cope and Yarrow]] (1875), [[Evermann and Rutter]] (1895) and [[Gilbert and Scofield]] (1898). Snyder (1915) reported Mexican Boundary resurvey collections made by Dr. Edgar A. Mearns (1892–94). The authors state that the "lower Colorado River" refers to the section below [[Lake Mead]].

Common names follow the second edition (1960) of the American Fisheries Society's *A List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada*, with two exceptions (*Gila robusta* and *Fundulus zebrinus*) explained by Sigler and Miller (1963).

The number of species grew quickly. Before 1950 there were 25 native and 19 introduced species. By 1960 the estimated fauna was 61 species, including 33 alien forms. The present total of 68 species is expected to keep rising as more introductions establish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat change:** Contraction and elimination of surface flows, reservoir construction and stocking of exotics are the main drivers of decline. The authors describe this change as "continuing undiminished." No indigenous fish is yet known to have been eliminated from the state.
- **Threatened natives:** The species nearing extinction are the [[Gila Trout]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]].
- **Reservoirs and canals:** Reservoirs such as [[Roosevelt Lake]], [[San Carlos Lake]] and [[Lake Havasu]] now hold many introduced sport and forage species.
- **Biogeographic links:** Several native species, including [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Shiner]] and [[Sonora Topminnow]], are restricted to the [[Yaqui River Basin]] in southeastern Arizona, and their ranges extend into Sonora, Mexico. Others, such as [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], are endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]].
- **Marine-derived species:** The lower Colorado River holds a few species of Gulf of California origin, including [[Striped Mullet]] and [[Spotted Sleeper]].

## Key Findings

**Overall inventory**
- 68 species total: 27 native, 37 established exotics, 4 hypothetical aliens.
- The exotic count has grown rapidly. It rose from 19 introduced species before 1950 to 33 by 1960 and 37 in the present list.
- The authors recommend continued study of native fishes, which they describe as "yet inadequately studied."

**Species of conservation concern**
- **[[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*):** Now extinct over most of its range. Surviving populations lie above 10,000 feet in the Salt River drainage and in tributaries of Eagle Creek. The [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] is restoring it through artificial propagation.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*):** Described in 1946 from Grand Canyon. No further Arizona specimens had been captured at the time of the checklist.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):** Once reached nearly 100 pounds. Only a few adults have been caught recently, and two young were collected in Salt River in 1950.
- **[[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta elegans*):** Apparently extinct in the Gila drainage, with the last known capture in 1926. It is becoming scarce in the Colorado River.
- **[[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*):** Wiped out over most of its range. In August 1960 no specimens were found in the Little Colorado River or most tributaries. It persists only in Clear Creek.
- **[[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** Extinct in the lower Gila River (no capture since 1894). It survives in the Virgin River basin.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*):** Known in Arizona from only two localities: Monkey Spring near Patagonia and Quitovaquito in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):** Rapidly approaching extinction. It was completely replaced by [[Western Mosquitofish]] in Arivaca Creek between April 12, 1957 and March 30, 1959.

**Effects of introductions**
- The [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), introduced to California in 1922 and reaching Arizona soon after, "has driven its native ecological counterpart... almost out of existence."
- The [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) became established in the lower Colorado River in 1953 and is now widespread in warm-water habitats.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) were introduced to [[Lake Havasu]] in 1954–55, using broodstock from the Tennessee River, and are now abundant in the Colorado.
- The [[Yellow Bass]] (*Roccus mississippiensis*) was stocked in Roosevelt Lake between 1929 and 1932. It disappeared after a flood around 1934, which the fishery's owner attributed to heavy silting.

**Native-species and conservation notes**
- No native cutthroat trout existed in Arizona in historic times. Early "Colorado River cutthroat" records are misidentifications of *S. gilae*. Cutthroat trout stocked later were probably Yellowstone cutthroat.
- The [[Rainbow Trout]] hybridizes with both cutthroat and Gila trout, to their detriment.
- The [[Sand Shiner]] (*Notropis stramineus*) most likely arrived as an accidental contaminant of [[Largemouth Bass]] shipments stocked in 1935.
- [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*) is native to the lower Colorado River. Its establishment there may be linked to the decline of the Colorado squawfish and to the canal and drain system.
- [[Spotted Sleeper]] (*Eleotris picta*) was taken in 1952 near Winterhaven, California, the only record north of the Yaqui River.
- [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) hybridizes with *Catostomus insignis* and *C. latipinnis*. Once common in the Colorado basin, it persists in Salt River reservoirs and the lower Colorado.

**Sport fisheries**
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) is among the most widely stocked panfish. It is abundant in many reservoirs and often forms stunted populations in ranch ponds.
- [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) is the most commonly taken panfish in Arizona.
- The [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) was recorded in Arizona in 1950 in Bonita Creek. It was stocked in six areas of the Colorado River in 1962, and its arrival route is unknown.
- The [[Largemouth Bass]] is found in virtually all suitable warm-water lakes and reservoirs in the state.

**Addendum**
- [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Mollienesia latipinna*) is abundant in Salt River canals near Phoenix and in the river upstream to Roosevelt Dam.
