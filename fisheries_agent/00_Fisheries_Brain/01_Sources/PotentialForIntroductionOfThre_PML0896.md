# Potential Introduction of Three Nonnative Fishes into Central Arizona via the Central Arizona Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #Cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation literature review (March 1984, Denver) assesses the risk that three nonnative fishes, [[Striped Bass]], [[White Bass]] and [[Blue Tilapia]], could be transferred from the Colorado River into central Arizona receiving waters during operation of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The study covers life history, water quality tolerances, existing fisheries of [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Pleasant]], [[Alamo Lake]] and the Salt and Verde Rivers, pump passage effects, and potential impacts on the southern [[Bald Eagle]] population. No field sampling was conducted; findings rest on literature, agency records and personal communications. The accompanying correspondence (October 1983) includes critiques by reviewers, notably [[Jerry C. Marsh]], who challenged citation practices, the native status of local fish faunas, and the reliance on unverified tilapia records.

## Background & Description

The CAP was authorized in 1968 to deliver Colorado River water to Maricopa, Pinal and Pima counties. Water is pumped from Lake Havasu through the Havasu Pumping Plant and the Granite Reef Aqueduct, with in-line plants at Bouse Hills, Little Harquahala and Hassayampa, into Lake Pleasant and the Granite Reef Forebay. A Salt River Project (SRP) interconnection would allow transfer into the Salt and Verde Rivers.

The three species of concern:

- **Striped bass** (*Morone saxatilis*): native to Atlantic and Gulf coasts; introduced to the Pacific coast in the late 1800s and to landlocked reservoirs across the US. Seven or eight black lateral lines distinguish it from other percichthyids; 67 lateral line scales.
- **White bass** (*Morone chrysops*): native to the Mississippi basin and Great Lakes region; introduced into Arizona reservoirs, first in Lake Pleasant (1959 stocking of 160 fish, 254-305 mm).
- **Blue tilapia** (*Oreochromis aureus*, formerly *Tilapia aurea*): an African cichlid, widely introduced in the southwest; taxonomy and identification in Arizona are problematic, with hybridization with *T. nilotica* and *T. mossambica* noted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish fauna and introductions.** Reviewer Marsh noted that most fish in the Phoenix metropolitan area (Salt and Verde Rivers) are introduced; only about four native species occur (longfin dace, roundtail chub, desert and Sonora suckers), and the bonytail chub remains in upstream Gila basin tributaries. Lake Havasu holds three native species (bonytail, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker), but none had been taken there recently.

**Lake Havasu.** Warm-water sport fishery dominated by largemouth bass, black crappie and channel catfish. Surveys in 1974-1976 (Minckley) recorded 19 species. Striped bass recruitment is thought to originate near Bullhead City and Parker Dam. Tilapia were not confirmed from Havasu in the agency records reviewed.

**Lake Pleasant.** Largemouth bass, channel catfish, crappies, bluegill and white bass form the sport fishery, with threadfin shad as primary forage. Cover is limited (about 16 percent of underwater habitat suitable). Total dissolved solids were expected to rise with CAP water.

**Alamo Lake.** Stocked with largemouth bass, channel catfish and forage species; dominated by sunfish and bass. Tilapia were reported recently (1982 collection of 28 fish, 86-374 mm), likely *T. aurea*, possibly reaching Lake Havasu in high flows.

**Salt and Verde Rivers.** Water temperatures frequently exceed 20 °C in summer, and occasionally 26 °C. Fish are dominated by carp, desert and Sonora suckers, and channel and flathead catfish. Tilapia were recorded in the Salt River canals and Granite Reef area.

**Bald eagles.** Breeding pairs nest along the Verde River (Bartlett and Fort McDowell) and the Salt River (Blue Point/Stewart Mountain). Fish made up roughly 70-83 percent of prey remains; carp, channel catfish and suckers dominated. Striped bass, white bass or tilapia could alter this base if they displaced native prey.

## Key Findings

- **Pump passage:** Striped bass larger than about 2.5 cm may escape intake velocities, but smaller fish will be entrained. Pump passage mortality is uncertain; one literature-derived estimate of about 15 percent was applied to a hypothetical 100-fish sample, with survival of about 72 to 85 percent after successive plants. Reported survival at the A. D. Edmonston plant (California) favored small fish (mean length 4 cm).
- **Striped bass:** Likely to be lifted from Lake Havasu, but numbers entering cannot be predicted. Reproduction in Lake Pleasant and the Salt and Verde Rivers is expected to be limited; eggs would be flushed downstream before hatching in the rivers. Recruitment would mainly come from repeated introduction. Summer temperatures above 30 °C and low oxygen at depth create a "temperature-oxygen squeeze."
- **White bass:** Less vulnerable to entrainment because they concentrate along distant beaches and backwaters. Spawning in Lake Pleasant occurs in early spring. Competition with striped bass for shad is possible in Lake Pleasant.
- **Blue tilapia:** Hardy, tolerant of low oxygen, and likely to survive pump passage. Winter die-offs occur below about 10 °C, but the Salt and Verde Rivers may sustain populations. Largemouth bass predation can limit tilapia numbers, and tilapia may compete with sunfish for spawning sites.
- **Bald eagles:** The worst effect would be displacement of carp, catfish and suckers. Raptor biologists were divided on whether eagles could switch prey successfully.
- **Taxonomic and data limits:** Arizona tilapia identification is uncertain; voucher specimens are needed before any Lake Havasu record is accepted. The authors' predictions depend on incomplete data on fish abundance at the Havasu intake.
- **Reviewer critique:** Marsh criticized heavy reliance on personal communications, unpublished reports and secondary sources, and the characterization of introduced fauna as "native." He concluded that the document could not reliably guide decisions on introduction mechanisms.
- **Conclusion (authors' summary):** Establishment of the three species is considered possible, with the greatest uncertainty for striped bass and tilapia. A monitoring program in the Havasu intake area was recommended before final conclusions.
