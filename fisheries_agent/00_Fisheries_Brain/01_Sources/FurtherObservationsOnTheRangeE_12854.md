## Executive Summary

This study documents the continued range expansion of the invasive North American amphipod **[[Gammarus tigrinus]]** in the Netherlands during 1975–1976, following its probable introduction in 1964. Surveys focused on the boundaries of the species' 1973 distribution (established by Smit, 1974) and revealed substantial new range extensions in the northeastern (Groningen), central (IJssel deltaic area), and southwestern (Deltaic Region, Zuid-Holland) parts of the country. In most newly colonized habitats, *G. tigrinus* has displaced native gammarid species — **[[Gammarus pulex pulex]]**, **[[Gammarus duebeni duebeni]]**, and **[[Gammarus zaddachi]]** — through superior reproductive output in oligohaline conditions. Intensive sampling along the Dutch-German border found no evidence of secondary invasion from German populations (introduced into the Weser and Werra rivers). A pathogenic fungus, tentatively identified as *Saprolegnia* sp., was documented killing *G. tigrinus* in both field and laboratory settings, representing the first reported pathogen/parasite for this species in the Netherlands.

## Background & Description

The source text is ecological/biogeographic in nature and does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of *G. tigrinus* or the native congeners. Key distinguishing ecological/physiological traits noted include:

- **Reproductive biology**: *G. tigrinus* exhibits a markedly shorter egg-incubation period compared to *G. duebeni* and *G. pulex*, and reaches sexual maturity far more rapidly than all three indigenous species (Pinkster, 1975).
- **Reproductive period**: *G. tigrinus* has a more favorable (extended) reproductive period relative to *G. duebeni* and *G. zaddachi*, which normally restrict reproduction to summer months in higher salinities.
- **Pathology**: Field-collected dead specimens (notably August–September) were found covered with a fungal growth identified as *Saprolegnia* sp.; sexual reproductive fungal stages were absent, precluding full species-level identification. Notably, this fungus killed *G. tigrinus* in laboratory co-culture but did not affect co-housed *G. p. pulex*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preferences:**
- *G. tigrinus* predominantly occupies **oligohaline waters** (chlorinity ranging roughly 62–1,480 mg Cl/L per Table I data), including ditches, canals, polders, and brackish basins.
- No records exist of *G. tigrinus* in running freshwater or in its native tidal-belt habitat (per Bousfield, 1958), with the exception of one polluted salt-marsh locality in England (Holland, 1976).
- The species tends to avoid clean, oligohaline ditches where *G. p. pulex* remains dominant, despite chlorinity levels (130–400 mg/L) that would not otherwise be limiting — suggesting competitive or physiological exclusion mechanisms not yet fully understood.

**Salinity gradients among competing species (per Holland, 1976, corroborating Dutch findings):**

| Species | Chlorinity Range |
|---|---|
| [[Gammarus zaddachi]] | >2,000 mg/L |
| [[Gammarus duebeni duebeni]] | down to 500 mg/L |
| [[Gammarus tigrinus]] | ~100 to several thousand mg/L |
| [[Gammarus pulex pulex]] | rare in brine-influenced waters |

**Interspecific dynamics:**
- In areas of range overlap (e.g., Haringvliet, Waterland), *G. tigrinus* and *G. duebeni* show **microhabitat partitioning**: *G. duebeni* persists on exposed, stony substrates, while *G. tigrinus* occupies all other niches.
- **[[Interspecific competition]]** and aggression (Dennert, 1974) vary with temperature and salinity, contributing to displacement dynamics.
- Severe organic pollution (e.g., south of Groningen-Winschoten-Nieuweschans line) creates anoxic conditions in summer that eliminate gammarid fauna entirely — polluted rivers (Brabantian rivers) act as dispersal barriers rather than corridors, contrary to earlier predictions (Den Hartog, 1964).
- Native species decline in the eastern Netherlands is attributed more to progressive water pollution than to direct competitive exclusion by *G. tigrinus*.

## Key Findings

1. **Range extension 1975–1976**: Significant expansions documented in Groningen (nearly all inland waters north of the Groningen-Winschoten-Nieuweschans line), the Deltaic Region (islands of Voorne, Putten, Beijerland — previously near-devoid of gammarid fauna), and isolated colonization events (e.g., Kralingse Plassen population spreading to Bergsche Plassen and Schiebroeksche Polder).

2. **Native species displacement**: *G. tigrinus* has replaced indigenous gammarids across most newly invaded areas; *G. duebeni* and *G. zaddachi* now persist mainly in restricted refugia with higher chlorinity (3,000–10,000 mg/L), such as Goeree-Overflakkee.

3. **No cross-border invasion**: Despite deliberate German introductions into the Weser and Werra (and subsequent Schlei colonization; Bulnheim, 1976), intensive Dutch-German border sampling found zero evidence of German-origin *G. tigrinus* populations entering the Netherlands — supporting a single, independent Dutch introduction event (~1964).

4. **Reproductive advantage mechanism**: *G. tigrinus*'s competitive dominance in oligohaline waters stems from superior reproductive output (shorter incubation, faster maturation, extended reproductive season) relative to native congeners; this advantage diminishes at higher salinities when native species extend reproduction into summer.

5. **Novel pathogen discovery**: First documented case of a pathogen (*Saprolegnia* sp.) affecting *G. tigrinus* in the Netherlands, with species-specific lethality (no effect on co-occurring *G. p. pulex*), suggesting potential future biocontrol or population-limiting relevance.

6. **Persistent stronghold of native species**: A discrete area in central Zuid-Holland — characterized by unpolluted, floristically diverse oligohaline ditches — remains dominated by *[[Gammarus pulex pulex]]*, despite being surrounded by *G. tigrinus*-occupied waters, offering a natural experimental site for future invasion-front monitoring.
