# Pre-Impoundment Survey of Fishes of the Arkansas and Cimarron Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #pre-impoundment-survey

## Executive Summary

This 1961 report by Thomas L. Linton, issued by the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] (Report No. 81), documents a pre-impoundment survey of the reaches of the [[Arkansas River]] and [[Cimarron River]] that would be inundated by the proposed [[Keystone Reservoir]] in northeastern Oklahoma. The study covered age and growth of ten abundant sport and rough fishes, a standing-crop estimate in a 0.6-acre creek section, and a creel census in July to September 1960. River Carpsucker dominated the collections, while sport-fish catches were poor. The author used the results to predict how impoundment would affect the fish community, based on comparisons with [[Canton Reservoir]].

## Background & Description

The [[Keystone Dam]] was authorized by Congress under the [[Flood Control Act of 1950]]. It was to be built about one mile below the confluence of the Arkansas and Cimarron rivers near the community of Keystone, with multipurpose goals of flood control, navigation, and water supply. Power generation was not planned initially, though the dam was designed so it could be added later.

Key physical parameters reported:
- Conservation pool elevation: 714.5 ft above sea level
- Reservoir area: 20,500 acres, with about 260 miles of shoreline
- Upstream extent: 30 miles on the Arkansas River and 32 miles on the Cimarron River
- Dam: earth-fill embankment with concrete spillway, 4,570 ft long and 121 ft high
- Drainage basin: 74,350 square miles

The survey was carried out while the author was employed by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]], during June, July, and August 1960, with joint support from the Corps and the [[Oklahoma Wildlife Conservation Department]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reservoir lies on Late Pennsylvanian rocks, dominated by easily eroded shale with thinner sandstone and limestone beds. Heavy erosion makes both rivers silt-laden, especially at flood stage, and the deposits form a wide floodplain. The main channel has unstable sand bottoms that shift constantly, and no aquatic vegetation grows in the main course because of current speed and water-level fluctuation.

Water chemistry is strongly influenced by natural salt beds and gypsum deposits, as well as oil-field brine. Annual data from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] station on U.S. Highway 64, seven miles below the confluence near Sand Springs, reported:

| Parameter | Value |

| Calcium | 9,500 ppm (max) |
| Magnesium | 2,125 ppm (max) |
| Sodium | 827 ppm |

Sampling used seines, gill nets, hoop nets, electrofishing, and rotenone. Seines were used mainly in tributaries. Rotenone was applied nine times in tributaries but only once in the Cimarron because of the large quantity needed and the risk of killing fish downstream. Electrofishing was limited by steep banks and few access points.

## Key Findings

**Age and growth (Table 2 and Figures 2–9)**
- Ten species were studied and compared with [[Oklahoma State Average]] growth rates where available.
- [[Paddlefish]]: The Arkansas and Cimarron fish (19 aged) grew similarly to those in [[Fort Gibson Reservoir]] through the third year, then Fort Gibson fish grew much faster. The author suggested impoundment-driven habitat change as the cause.
- [[Gizzard Shad]]: Arkansas River growth compared favorably with the state average. The state average exceeded the river average by no more than 0.9 inches in any year. The author expected shad to be an abundant, possibly overabundant, forage source after impoundment.
- [[Smallmouth Buffalo]]: Verdigris River fish outgrew Arkansas River fish in all six years, with the gap widening each year (0.5 to 6.3 inches).
- [[River Carpsucker]]: Growth was poor. Statewide averages exceeded Arkansas River fish by 1.9 inches in year one and 6.9 inches by year seven. Cimarron fish were slower after year four.
- [[Carp]]: Cimarron River carp grew more slowly than Arkansas River carp in all eight years. Arkansas River carp exceeded Rock Creek fish by 2.1, 3.7, and 5.0 inches in years eight to ten.
- [[Channel Catfish]]: The Arkansas River sample (61 fish, 10 year classes) matched the state average in year one, but the state average was larger in years two to seven. Arkansas River fish were larger in years eight to ten.
- [[Flathead Catfish]]: Arkansas River fish (24 fish) exceeded state averages in the first four years. Cimarron fish (16 fish) grew more slowly, exceeding the state average only in years one and two.
- [[Black Crappie]]: Arkansas River tributary fish (29 fish) were smaller than state averages at every age. The gap was greatest in year six (about 4.1 inches), though that year had fewer than five fish.
- [[White Crappie]]: Cimarron tributary fish (12 fish) were smaller than state averages at every comparable age.
- [[Freshwater Drum]]: Cimarron fish (27 fish, maximum age six) grew more slowly than state averages, with the difference reaching 5.7 inches in year six. This was the only game or rough fish reported as growing below the state average.

**Population and standing crop (Mud Creek, 0.6 acre)**
- Mark-and-recapture used 45 River Carpsuckers and 2 Carp, of which 27 carpsuckers (65%) and both carp were recaptured.
- Rotenone killed 1,098 River Carpsuckers, 106 Longnose Gar, 24 Carp, and 22 Gizzard Shad, among others.
- River Carpsucker had the highest standing crop, about 750 lb/acre more than Longnose Gar.
- Channel Catfish was the top sport fish at 7.3 lb/acre, followed by White Crappie at 3.7 lb/acre.

**Gear selectivity (Tables 4–5)**
- Mean River Carpsucker lengths were: gill net 15.62 in, rotenone 12.76 in, shocker 11.67 in, hoop net 11.46 in.
- Five of six pairwise comparisons were significant (P < 0.005). Shocker and hoop net means did not differ (P < 0.80).
- The author attributed the larger gill-net mean to small fish passing through the mesh. Rotenone's larger sample was judged the most accurate estimate of the population's mean length.

**Creel census (Table 6)**
- Interviews covered about 75 miles, from Tulsa to Cleveland on the Arkansas and to Drumright on the Cimarron, over 24 census days (July 7, August 14, September 3).
- Catch per unit effort was very poor: one fish per 4.25 hours in July, 5.75 hours in August, and 13 hours in September.
- Only five species were caught: Channel Catfish (most frequent), Flathead Catfish, Carp, Freshwater Drum, and Longnose Gar.

**Predictions for Keystone Reservoir**
- Keystone should resemble [[Lake Texoma]] and [[Canton Reservoir]] in water conditions, and it should offer more game-fish habitat than the rivers.
- White Crappie should be important as a sport fish in the first few years, as at Canton. Black Crappie should be present but not important.
- Carp and River Carpsucker are expected to become abundant, and Gizzard Shad may reach overpopulation.
- Channel Catfish should become an important sport fish, supported by flooded croplands, as at Canton.
- Buffalo are expected to become very abundant.
- The author concluded that increased access, boat launches, and fishing areas would bring more boaters, water skiers, and anglers.
