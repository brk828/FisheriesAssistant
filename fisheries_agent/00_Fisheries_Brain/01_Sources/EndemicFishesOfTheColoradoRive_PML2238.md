# Endemic Fishes of the Colorado River System: A Status Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cypriniformes

## Executive Summary

This 1977 status report, published by the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] and prepared by its Endemic Species Committees, reviews the 34 endemic fish species of the Colorado River drainage and assesses each species' status in the seven member states. It is an educational booklet intended to show that state fish and game agencies can cooperate across boundaries. The report links the decline of many endemic species to dam construction, river channelization, and the introduction and dominance of exotic species. It also treats endemic fishes as environmental indicators: a population's health reflects the condition of its habitat.

## Background & Description

**Council origin and mandate.** An interstate committee was formed in 1956 to coordinate protection and management of the Colorado River's fish and wildlife resources. It was formalized as the Colorado River Wildlife Council on March 31, 1964. Its members are the fish and game agencies of Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming. Federal agencies such as the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Bureau of Land Management]] participate as nonvoting members. The Council's goals include promoting sound management, reviewing federal projects that affect the drainage, advising on interstate planning, and recommending laws and regulations.

**Committee structure.** A professional Technical Committee advises the Council. It is divided into Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming) and Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada) subcommittees. The Prohibited and Protected Fishes, Amphibians and Reptiles Committee oversees the Endemic Species Committees, which consolidate management programs and coordinate study groups and recovery teams.

**Scope of the report.** The report lists 34 endemic species in eight families:

- **Catostomidae** (6 species): desert, bluehead, Sonora, flannelmouth, mountain and humpback (razorback) suckers
- **Cyprinidae** (17 species): the largest group, including chubs, dace, spinedace, woundfin and Colorado squawfish
- **Salmonidae** (4 species): Arizona trout, Colorado cutthroat trout, Gila trout and mountain whitefish
- **Cyprinodontidae** (3 species): White River killifish, desert pupfish and Monkey Spring pupfish
- **Cottidae, Elopidae, Mugilidae and Poeciliidae** (1 species each): mottled sculpin, machete, striped mullet and Gila topminnow

The Council and the report's authors used the 1970 [[American Fisheries Society]] list of common and scientific names for taxonomic consistency. Several entries are footnoted as federally listed or proposed for listing, and the report notes that the Kendall Warm Springs dace's taxonomy is uncertain.

**Document note.** The source is a duplicated scan. It includes an errata sheet with corrections, such as the spelling of the genus *Ptychocheilus* and a revised California status for the desert pupfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report presents endemic fishes as indicators of environmental health. The status of a species can be correlated with the condition of its environment, so a depleted or endangered population signals a corresponding environmental problem. The stressors named are:

- **Dams:** [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] construction is cited as the reason [[Colorado Squawfish]] became extinct in Wyoming through loss of habitat.
- **Channelization:** the straightening of large river stretches.
- **Exotic species:** introduction and dominance of non-native fishes.
- **Isolation and restricted habitat:** several species survive in isolated springs or limited reaches, including the relict White River system.

Management responses include captive breeding, transplantation, reintroduction, and federal grant-in-aid funds under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. Monitoring is also under way. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service began a long-term turbidity, temperature and sediment monitoring program on the [[Yampa River]] near Maybell and at Echo Park on the [[Green River]]. The University of Nevada was studying population dispersion, community structure and phenology of fishes in the [[Virgin River]] system of Utah, Arizona and Nevada.

## Key Findings

**Status by species group**

- **Most endangered taxa:** [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Woundfin]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] are federally listed or under review. Several are rarely found across much of their former range.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** extinct in Wyoming because of habitat loss from Flaming Gorge Dam. Its population in Colorado declined sharply over the previous decade. It is endangered in California, Colorado and New Mexico, and declining in Utah.
- **Humpback Chub:** "Endangered" in Colorado, where occurrence is extremely unusual with very few documented records in the past decade. It is rarely found in Arizona and Utah.
- **Bonytail:** extinct in Wyoming and not found in California in recent years. It is rarely found in Colorado, Nevada and New Mexico.
- **Humpback Sucker:** extinct in Wyoming. It is rarely found in California and has very limited occurrence in Colorado. Its New Mexico status is unconfirmed, though it is assumed to occur in the San Juan River.
- **Woundfin:** endangered in Nevada, occasionally found in Arizona, and has limited distribution in Utah. The Woundfin Recovery Team is studying its status.
- **Moapa Dace:** endangered in Nevada. It is listed as protected.
- **Pahranagat Spinedace:** believed extinct.
- **Flannelmouth Sucker:** extinct in California.
- **Gila Topminnow:** locally common in Arizona, but its New Mexico drainage population is probably extirpated and may be reintroduced.
- **Desert Pupfish:** restricted distribution in Arizona and California. The errata sheet corrects the California entry to "declining, restricted distribution in Salton Sea area."
- **Kendall Warm Springs Dace:** restricted range and endangered in Wyoming. Only hook-and-line capture is legal, and permits are not issued for seines, traps or bait use.
- **Gila Trout:** found in seven streams in New Mexico that are closed to fishing. A Gila Trout Recovery Team has been established.
- **Arizona Trout:** locally common in Arizona and protected by game fish regulations. An official recovery team has been established.
- **Colorado Cutthroat Trout:** restricted range and threatened in Colorado, where it may be taken only by angling.
- **Common species:** Several species, including longnose dace, speckled dace, mottled sculpin and mountain whitefish, are common across much of their range.

**State authority and management**

Each state has different capacity for nongame and endangered species work:

- **Arizona:** two biologists assigned to nongame species, with emphasis on federally listed species.
- **California:** three full-time wildlife biologists and two full-time fishery biologists assigned to rare, endangered and nongame species.
- **Colorado:** a Nongame Staff of one supervisor, one secretary and five biologists. Its primary focus is recovery of listed species through captive breeding, transplantation and reintroduction. In 1977 the governor signed a law creating a state income tax check-off that lets taxpayers donate to the nongame budget.
- **Nevada:** two nongame biologists, with priority for birds and mammals.
- **New Mexico:** an Endangered Species Program established in 1974 with four biologists.
- **Utah:** three nongame biologists, with emphasis on nonaquatic species.
- **Wyoming:** no formal endangered species unit, but all management units share responsibility for nongame and threatened species.

**Research gaps**

The Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team was investigating the true status and life history of several species. The Woundfin Recovery Team was studying status and life history, and the report notes that the Kendall Warm Springs dace status study was underway.
