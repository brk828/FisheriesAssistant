## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled email thread (FWIM-L / AFS-L listserv, July 1999) summarizing responses from fisheries biologists, engineers, and agency staff to an inquiry from Luca Cargnelli (Vermont Fish and Wildlife) about constructing a barrier to prevent the invasive [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*) from spreading from Lake St. Catherine into Lake Champlain. Rather than primary morphological or ecological research, the thread functions as an expert-crowdsourced literature and technology review on **fish exclusion barriers** (mechanical screens, acoustic/electric deterrents, chemical renovation) and consistently concludes that **complete containment of alewife—particularly at egg and larval stages—is functionally unachievable** with current barrier technology. The consensus recommendation, where eradication is still feasible, is whole-lake chemical treatment (rotenone) rather than reliance on physical/behavioral barriers.

## Background & Description

Limited direct morphological data are given, as this is a management correspondence rather than a taxonomic study. Relevant size/life-stage notes include:

- **Larval/early juvenile alewife** are extremely small — cited as passing through even fine mesh screens; effective exclusion mesh (1/4 inch) only becomes functional once juveniles reach **20–22 mm** in length (per Terry Toner, Nova Scotia Power).
- **Egg and larval diameter** is smaller than 1.1 mm, allowing passage through fine stainless steel screens (1.1 mm mesh) designed for European carp exclusion in Tasmania (John Diggle); this implies alewife eggs/larvae are of comparable or smaller scale.
- Alewife are repeatedly described as **more sensitive** to physical stress than salmonids (citing Bouck & Smith 1979 on descaling mortality in coho salmon smolts as a comparative reference point).
- No detailed body plan, fin, or scale count data provided — the source is ecological/management-focused rather than descriptive morphology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Both anadromous (sea-run) and landlocked (lacustrine) populations exist; the discussion concerns a landlocked population established in [[Lake St. Catherine]] (Vermont) threatening to invade [[Lake Champlain]].
- **Invasion pathway**: Likely introduced via bait-bucket transfer (misguided angler releases), a pattern echoed in a parallel case at [[Conesus Lake]] (NY Finger Lakes), where an alewife population explosion co-occurred with **zebra mussel** introduction, drastically altering a warm-water fishery.
- **Reproductive strategy**: High fecundity and rapid larval dispersal are cited as the core management obstacle — "millions of egg and larval stage alewife" pass downstream annually during high-flow spring conditions (J. Downing).
- **Trophic impacts**: Alewife are known for **selective size feeding on zooplankton**, driving trophic cascades. A correspondent cites **Lake McConaughy, Nebraska**, where alewife-driven shifts toward smaller zooplankton size structure impaired fish recruitment — a classic [[Alewife-Zooplankton Trophic Cascade]] effect.
- **Zooplankton competition risk**: In Nova Scotia, managers avoided smaller-mesh screens (2 mm) despite better fish exclusion, because of concern over impacts to *Holopedium*-dominated zooplankton communities, a key food resource for other watershed species.
- **Comparable invasive dynamics**: The thread draws repeated analogies to **common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*)** containment efforts in Hamilton Harbour, Ontario (Cootes Paradise "Project Paradise"/Ecowise) and in Tasmania, Australia (Lake Crescent), where carp threaten native fish communities and non-native trout fisheries.
- **Vector for further spread**: Concern raised that anadromous **blueback herring** (*Alosa aestivalis*) could independently reach Lake Champlain via the Hudson River/NY Barge Canal, compounding the alewife problem (Brian Jessop).

## Key Findings

1. **No barrier technology reliably achieves 100% exclusion.** Multiple experts (NY DEC, Nebraska Game & Parks, FWS Southwest) report physical/electric/acoustic barriers topping out at **85–90% effectiveness at best**, insufficient given alewife reproductive capacity.
2. **Larval and egg-stage transport is the critical failure point.** Nearly every respondent (Jessop, Taggart, Diggle, Fischer, Downing, Gibson, Cichosz) independently identifies that adult-focused barriers fail because eggs and larvae passively drift downstream and evade standard mesh sizes.
3. **Mesh size trade-offs are severe**: Fine mesh (1/8 inch or smaller) needed to stop larvae clogs rapidly with debris and requires costly, frequent maintenance (e.g., daily high-pressure cleaning in the Tasmanian carp barrier system, 1000–4000 L/s discharge).
4. **Chemical eradication (rotenone) is repeatedly proposed as the only realistic solution** for isolated source lakes, though it requires whole-lake treatment and subsequent native species reintroduction (Bruce Schmidt, Don McAllister, Steve Fischer). Powdered rotenone is preferred over liquid formulations for environmental safety.
5. **Precedent case (Nebraska, unspecified 30,000-acre reservoir)**: A state wildlife agency chemically renovated a 600-acre reservoir after failing to contain alewife spread via a hydroplant outlet, but conceded permanent establishment in the larger source reservoir was unavoidable — illustrating the practical limits of containment once alewife are established in a large connected system.
6. **Acoustic deterrents show promise but require precise engineering**: High-frequency sound (~420+ kHz, sufficient source level) has repelled alewife in Great Lakes research (Gary Curtis, BioSonics Inc.), but effectiveness is highly dependent on transducer placement relative to flow channels (Dennis Dunning, NYPA).
7. **Analogous international case studies reinforce partial-success expectations**: The Tasmanian carp containment program (John Diggle/Nic Bax) achieved measurable population reduction (est. <2,000 carp remaining in Lake Crescent, ~10% of original) using combined screening, water-level management, and radiotagged "Judas fish" (called "Jonah Fish" in text) targeting — but did not achieve full eradication.
8. **Human-mediated transfer remains the dominant uncontrolled risk factor**, cited by nearly all respondents as more consequential than any engineered barrier — recommendations include public education campaigns (leveraging public library networks, per Frederick Stoss) and regulatory controls on live bait and recreational equipment transport.
9. **Key technical/expert resources identified**: Ned Taft (Alden Research Laboratory) on fish barriers/transport at power plant intakes; Dave Smith (Smith-Root) on electric barrier systems; BioSonics Inc. (Seattle) on acoustic deterrent equipment; historic power-plant entrainment literature from the 1970s (Boreman 1977; Edwards & Hutchinson 1980; Popper & Carlson 1998) remains foundational to barrier engineering discussions.
