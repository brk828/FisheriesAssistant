# River Conservation in Latin America and the Caribbean

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter reviews the status of river and stream systems across [[Latin America]] and the [[Caribbean]]. It argues that freshwater biodiversity in the region is more seriously threatened than terrestrial biodiversity, yet has received far less conservation attention. The main pressures are untreated sewage and industrial pollution, [[Deforestation]], agricultural agrochemicals and irrigation, mining, petroleum extraction, exotic species and, above all, [[Hydropower]] development. The chapter also reviews conservation responses, including transboundary parks, debt-for-nature swaps, community-based management and advocacy by non-governmental organizations (NGOs). It concludes that the region needs integrated catchment-scale planning, better monitoring and stronger enforcement of existing law.

## Background & Description

**Scope and rationale.** The authors frame Latin America as a high priority for riverine conservation because of its large aquatic resources, diverse fauna and flora, rapid population growth and environmental problems. Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru are among the 12 [[Megadiversity Countries]], which together hold 70% of world biodiversity (Mittermeier, 1989). The chapter deliberately excludes marine coastal and coral reef issues, although it notes that these are affected by river sediment, nutrient and pesticide loads.

**Biodiversity context.** The [[Amazon River]] is the largest tropical watershed in the world (5,711,000 km²). It holds well over 2,000 fish species, about 90% of them endemic, and a 1996 survey of deep-water Amazonian habitats revealed more than 240 new fish species. Olson et al. (1998) identified 117 freshwater ecoregions in the region. Central America's San Juan drainage has 138 fish species, while Puerto Rican streams support fewer than 10, typical of oceanic islands.

**Physical setting.** Mean annual rainfall is about 1,500 mm, 50% above the world average, and the region contributes almost one-third of global land drainage to the oceans. It also contains some of the world's most arid areas, such as the [[Atacama Desert]]. Central American rivers are mostly short, with headwaters in volcanic mountains. The Caribbean islands have mostly small catchments of under 250 km², and their perennial streams are largely confined to larger, mountainous islands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human pressures and socio-economic drivers.** The population was about 450 million, growing at 2.1% a year, and was projected to approach 800 million by 2020. External debt was US$387 billion in 1987. These factors limited governments' capacity to respond to environmental concerns. About 80% of tropical diseases are reportedly water-related.

**Deforestation.** Costa Rica's deforestation peaked at 40,470 ha/yr before falling to about 8,000 ha/yr. Forest burning in the Amazon rose 28% between 1996 and 1997. Brazil loses more rainforest than any other country, about 15,000 km² per year by satellite imagery, plus about 10,000 km² thinned by logging. Logging along rivers removes floodplain and riparian buffers, altering sediment loads, light and temperature regimes, and the supply of wood and other energy sources that sustain stream habitat.

**Agriculture and pesticides.** Fertilizer use rose 97% between 1973 and 1985. Brazil accounts for about half of Latin American pesticide use and ranks among the world's top five users. A survey of 12 river basins in Paraná, Brazil, found agrotoxic residues in 91% of samples. Pesticide poisonings were 1,800 per 600,000 people per year in Central America, compared with 1 per 600,000 in the United States. Irrigation is a major pressure, and salinization affects an estimated 34% of Peru's coastal area.

**Urbanization and industry.** Less than 2% of urban sewage receives treatment. Sewer systems reach only 41% of the urban population, and over 90% of collected wastewater is discharged untreated. The [[Tiete River]] in São Paulo carries an estimated 60% untreated residential waste in the dry season. Puerto Rico has 138 dump sites in 8,700 km² and six of every 10 hazardous sites overlie vulnerable limestone aquifers.

**Hydropower.** Dam building has been rapid since 1950, and reservoir storage rose more than 20-fold after 1945. Brazil has 79 dams built or planned in Amazonia. Dams fragment migratory corridors used by characins and catfish, and isolate [[Amazon River Dolphin]] populations. They also cause deoxygenation and hydrogen sulphide release, as seen at the [[Brokopondo Dam]] and the [[Balbina Dam]].

**Mining, petroleum and exotic species.** The Rimac River in Peru, which supplies 60% of Lima's population, is contaminated by arsenic, cyanide, lead, chromium and selenium. Up to 100 t of mercury may have entered the Rio Madeira between 1974 and 1985. In Puerto Rico, exotic species dominate the freshwater fish fauna. Aquaculture using African tilapia and Asian carp is common in the Amazon basin.

**Freshwater priorities.** Of the 117 ecoregions, 82% were classed as critically endangered, endangered or vulnerable. Ten were critically endangered and 44 endangered. A subset of 10 highest-priority and 36 high-priority ecoregions was identified, including the western Amazon arc, the Amazon main channel and the [[Pantanal]].

## Key Findings

- **Threat status:** Freshwater biodiversity is more seriously threatened than terrestrial biodiversity in Latin America and the Caribbean, in both geographic extent and severity.
- **Water quality:** Sewage treatment is minimal, so untreated waste drives both water pollution and water-related disease.
- **Hydropower risk:** Dams are considered potentially the most dangerous near-term threat to Amazonian fisheries. Many projects were approved without pre-impoundment ecological data.
- **Case example, Tucuruí Dam:** Gillnet comparisons showed a 49% reduction in fish diversity below the dam, a 50% reduction above, and a 55% reduction in the reservoir.
- **Chixoy and El Cajón dams:** Both suffered faster-than-predicted silt deposition. Chixoy cost US$1.2 billion, 521% above the 1974 forecast.
- **Regional patterns:** Coastal-plain rivers face the greatest direct human impact. Montane streams are damaged by intensive coffee farming. Dryland rivers are highly threatened by abstraction and pollution.
- **Knowledge gaps:** Taxonomic information is sparse, few pollution-effect data exist, and monitoring networks are inadequate. Caribbean freshwater inventories are needed.
- **Case Study 1, Caribbean National Forest, Puerto Rico:** More than 50% of river water draining the forest is abstracted for municipal supply, leaving streams dry below intakes. This is causing mortality of migratory shrimps and fish.
- **Case Study 2, San Juan River:** The river's 41,600 km² catchment is 70% in Nicaragua and 30% in Costa Rica. The SIAPAZ transboundary protected area totals 11,500 km².
- **Case Study 3, Puerto Viejo de Sarapiquí, Costa Rica:** The population rose from 3,000 to 30,000 in three years, driven by banana plantations. An outreach and volunteer stream-monitoring programme was launched.
- **Case Study 4, floodplain lake reserves:** Community-based lake reserves restricted gillnets and limited commercial fishing to six months of the year. They improved lake productivity but did not revitalize local economies alone.
- **Case Study 5, Hidrovia Paraguay–Paraná Waterway:** The project would convert 3,400 km of river into a shipping canal, requiring dredging at 93 sites and straightening meanders at seven points. It threatens the Pantanal, which hosts over 260 fish species. Brazil abandoned its plans in 1998.
- **Legislation:** Honduras has 37 water laws with 420 articles and no uniform criteria. Costa Rica set water discharge standards in 1995, while most Central American countries had none. Existing laws are rarely enforced.
- **Environmental sustainability of dams:** Vegetation clearance before flooding is legally required but often incomplete, for example less than one-fifth at Tucuruí and 2% at Balbina.
- **Conservation tools:** Transboundary protected areas, debt-for-nature swaps (Costa Rica converted US$80 million of debt), joint implementation (Rio Bravo, Belize) and locally managed reserves (Mamirauá) are examples of mechanisms in use.
- **Recommendations:** Integrated catchment and basin management, pre-project baseline studies, fish-passage design, peer-reviewed loan appraisal, and stronger monitoring and enforcement.
