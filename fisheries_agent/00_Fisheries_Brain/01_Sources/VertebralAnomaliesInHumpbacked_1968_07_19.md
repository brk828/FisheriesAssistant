# Vertebral Anomalies in Humpbacked Specimens of Menhaden, Brevoortia tyrannus

## Executive Summary
This short scientific note documents the first recorded instances of vertebral anomalies and "humpbacked" (kyphotic/lordotic) conditions in the Atlantic menhaden, [[Brevoortia tyrannus]]. Three anomalous specimens were identified among a sample of 338 menhaden collected via bag seine from the [[Slocum River Estuary]] in [[Buzzards Bay]], Massachusetts. Radiographic analysis revealed distinct patterns of vertebral fusion, kyphosis (spinal curvature), and lordosis, with vertebral counts in the most severely affected specimen falling below the previously reported species minimum. The causative mechanism—genetic versus environmental—could not be determined.

## Background & Description
- **Species:** [[Menhaden]] (*Brevoortia tyrannus*), family [[Clupeidae]].
- **Context:** Vertebral anomalies and humpbacked conditions have been documented in numerous fish species (Dawson 1964, 1966), but had not previously been recorded in menhaden.
- **Sampling event:** A single 36 ft bag seine haul collected 338 total menhaden — 335 phenotypically "normal" individuals and 3 humpbacked specimens (designated **A**, **B**, and **C**).
- **Collection conditions:**
  - Time: 1030 hr E.D.T.
  - Water temperature: 20°C
  - Water depth: 2 feet
- **Specimen sizes (standard length):**
  - Specimen A: 39 mm
  - Specimen B: 59 mm
  - Specimen C: 40 mm
- **Methodology:** Radiographs were produced following the technique of Bartlett and Haedrich (1966), conducted with assistance at the [[Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Menhaden are a schooling, estuarine-dependent [[Clupeidae|clupeid]] species widely distributed along the U.S. Atlantic coast, and juveniles commonly use estuaries such as Buzzards Bay as nursery habitat.
- The co-occurrence of only 3 anomalous individuals among 338 total specimens (~0.9% incidence) suggests such skeletal anomalies are rare but not undocumented within menhaden populations.
- The study authors note the inability to distinguish whether the observed anomalies arose from **genetic factors** or **environmental stressors** (e.g., temperature, pollutants, developmental disturbance) during larval or juvenile development — a common ambiguity in [[Fish Teratology|teratological]] studies of wild-caught specimens.
- Only specimens with obvious external humpback deformities were selected for radiography; the authors caution that additional individuals in the sample may have harbored undetected vertebral anomalies, potentially underestimating true incidence.

## Key Findings

### Specimen A (39 mm SL)
- Fusion of abdominal vertebrae 2 through 5.
- Spinal kyphosis at the site of fusion.
- Vertebral counts: 14 abdominal + 29 caudal (including penultimate vertebra) = **48 total vertebrae**.
- Appeared as healthy and robust as normal specimens of equivalent length.

### Specimen B (59 mm SL)
- Fusion of abdominal vertebrae 2 through 6 (similar to Specimen A).
- Followed by 8 additional abdominal vertebrae and 31 caudal vertebrae.
- Vertebral counts: total = **47 vertebrae**.
- Appeared as healthy as unaffected specimens.

### Specimen C (40 mm SL) — Most Anomalous
- Kyphosis at the 4th abdominal vertebra.
- Additional kyphosis at the 8th abdominal vertebra.
- Fusion of the subsequent 4–5 abdominal vertebrae, accompanied by strong **lordosis**.
- Followed by 1 abdominal vertebra and 28 caudal vertebrae.
- Vertebral counts: total = **41 vertebrae** — fewer than the minimum previously reported for *B. tyrannus* by Hildebrand (1963).
- Notably thinner in body condition compared to Specimens A and B and to normal individuals.

### General Observations
- The **penultimate vertebra** was consistently included in caudal and total vertebral counts across all specimens.
- Anomaly severity varied independently of body condition: Specimens A and B showed vertebral fusion/kyphosis without apparent effects on health or growth, while Specimen C's more complex anomaly (kyphosis + fusion + lordosis) correlated with poorer body condition (thinner body form).
- Etiology remains undetermined — the authors could not attribute the anomalies definitively to genetic or environmental causes, consistent with broader difficulties in interpreting wild-caught teratological specimens.
