# Native Arizona Fishes Part III: Chubs, With Notes on Stinging Insects and Agency Acronyms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Wildlife Views compilation contains three main parts. The principal item is the third installment of the "Native Arizona Fishes" series on chubs (genus *Gila*, family Cyprinidae) by John N. Rinne and W. L. Minckley of the Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, Tempe. It covers the six *Gila* species of Arizona, their distributions, declines, morphology, feeding, angling value, and hybridization concerns. The remaining material is a short "Wildlife Views" article on stinging insects of the state, including [[Velvet Ant]], [[Fire Ant]], [[Harvester Ant]], paper nest wasps, and [[Honeybee]]s, and a "Weary Willy's Alphabet Quiz" of outdoor-agency abbreviations. The quiz answers are referenced as being on page 27, but they are not included in this text.

## Background & Description

**Chubs (Genus *Gila*)**

- The name "chub" is generally applied in Arizona to the genus [[Gila]], which comprises almost half the native minnows of the state.
- Arizona has six *Gila* species: two mostly southern (Mexican) in distribution, three fairly wide-ranging, and one special form restricted to the swiftest parts of the mainstream [[Colorado River]] in Grand Canyon and above in Utah.

**Mexican species**

- [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*): enters the United States only in the extreme headwaters of the Rio Yaqui on the San Bernardino Ranch, east of Douglas, Cochise County. Rarely exceeds six or seven inches in Arizona. Deep-bodied, darkly pigmented, with rounded fins, relatively large scales, and a distinct caudal spot. Fig. 1 specimen was about four inches in total length.
- [[Sonora Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*): lives in Sycamore (Bear) Canyon, west of Nogales, Santa Cruz County. Not known to science until 1945. Fine-scaled, darkly olivaceous, with a whitish belly and two variably developed lateral bands (the name *di* = "two" and *taenia* = "bands"). Fig. 2 specimen was about five inches in total length.

**Gila Chub**

- [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*): restricted to the [[Gila River]] system of southern Arizona. Similar in appearance to the Yaqui chub. Fig. 3 specimen from O'Donnell Creek was about six inches in total length.

**Colorado River (Roundtail) Chub**

- [[Colorado River Chub]] (*Gila robusta*): the most widespread and abundant chub in Arizona streams. Three subspecies occur in Arizona: *G. r. robusta*, *G. r. grahami*, and *G. r. seminuda* (the last in the Virgin River basin in the northwest corner of the state; more silvery and slimmer).
- Often reach total lengths of perhaps 20 inches and weights of more than 1.5 pounds.
- Fine-scaled, with sickle-shaped fins and slender bodies. Coloration ranges from silvery-white to darkly blotched.
- Anglers have given it names such as "Verde trout," "River trout," and "Gila trout," and also "bonytail," a name the authors say should technically be reserved for *G. elegans*.

**Swift-water forms**

- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*): formerly lived throughout the larger rivers of the lower Colorado basin but is now restricted to the Colorado mainstream. Depressed skull grading gently to a smooth dorsal hump. Fig. 5 specimen from the Green River, Utah, was about 15 inches in total length.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): described as one of the most unique fishes in North America. Differs from the bonytail in having an abrupt dorsal hump, a shorter caudal peduncle, and an inferior, horizontal mouth. Often greatly reduced scalation.
- Both species have greatly streamlined bodies, slender caudal peduncles, small or absent scales, and often large fins.

**Stinging Insects (Wildlife Views Notes)**

- [[Velvet Ant]]: a brightly colored, hairy, wingless female wasp, not a true ant, also called cow ant, ground wasp, cow killer, or wolly ant. Black with red, yellow, orange, or chocolate-brown abdomens. The most common forms measure approximately ¼ to 1 inch (OCR reads "14 to 1 inch"). Parasites of bees and other wasps. Emits a faint squeaking sound when handled.
- [[Fire Ant]]: described as a wingless wasp with a very potent sting. Accompanying photographs show an attack on a thumb.
- [[Harvester Ant]]: red with a brown abdomen. Normally does not use its bite, but may inflict a painful sting that can spread through the body along the lymph channels.
- Paper nest wasps ([[Paper Nest Wasp]], *Polistes* spp., *Vespula* spp., and *Mischocyttarus* spp.) and the [[Tarantula Hawk]] (a spider wasp) are mentioned as common stinging wasps of Arizona.
- [[Honeybee]]s: well established in desert conditions but rarely encountered outside apiaries or wild bee trees. Stings are usually local, but allergic reactions, and deaths from many stings, have been recorded in people and domestic animals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat change and status of chubs**

- Changes in aquatic habitats in southern Arizona during the past century have adversely affected the Gila chub. It is now absent from many localities of former occurrence.
- The Yaqui chub is now restricted to a single spring, where it is abundant alongside Sonora topminnows (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*). A pre-1900 record from Morse Canyon (Wilcox Playa drainage) is unverified because the specimens were apparently lost.
- The humpback and bonytail are adapted to swift, silt-laden mainstream Colorado River waters. Reduced scalation in the humpback chub is presumed to reduce friction in these waters.

**Hybridization**

- Evidence of massive hybridization among *G. elegans*, *G. cypha*, and *G. robusta* has been found in the area of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] near Page, Arizona, during the past few years. Specimens from that area should be carefully preserved or frozen and sent to the university for analysis.

**Feeding and interactions**

- Adult chubs are principally carnivorous, feeding on aquatic invertebrates and sometimes other fishes. Smaller individuals often feed on aquatic plants, usually algae.
- They may compete with game species as adults, but this has not been specifically studied in Arizona.

**Reproduction**

- Gila chubs reproduce in the spring, when males often become reddened on their fin bases, jowls, and lower body.

**Human use**

- Larger chubs are taken by anglers. In the Phoenix canal system they provide sport for children.
- Chubs are considered too bony for modern palates, but their flesh is sweet and firm, and Indigenous people of Arizona ate them in the past.

**Scientific significance**

- The genus *Gila* is distributed throughout much of western North America and northern Mexico. Determining relationships among chubs and their probable ancient connecting water routes may clarify prehistoric drainages of the arid American Southwest.

## Key Findings

- Arizona has six *Gila* species: two Mexican (Yaqui and Sonora chubs), three widespread, and one restricted to swift Colorado River mainstream habitat.
- The Yaqui chub is restricted to a single spring, and the Gila chub has disappeared from many former localities due to habitat change in southern Arizona.
- The Colorado River (roundtail) chub is the most widespread and abundant Arizona chub, with three subspecies and adults up to about 20 inches.
- The bonytail (*Gila elegans*) and humpback chub (*Gila cypha*) are streamlined swift-water specialists now confined largely to the Colorado mainstream.
- Massive hybridization among *G. elegans*, *G. cypha*, and *G. robusta* has been documented near Glen Canyon Dam; authors request preserved or frozen specimens for analysis.
- Chubs are principally carnivorous as adults and herbivorous or algal feeders as juveniles.
- The authors emphasize scientific value, particularly for reconstructing prehistoric drainage connections in the Southwest.
- Stinging insect notes advise scraping rather than pulling out a honeybee sting, since squeezing the venom sac may inject more venom.
- A 30-item "Weary Willy's Alphabet Quiz" lists outdoor-agency abbreviations (e.g., [[Bureau of Land Management]] as BLM, [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] as FWS, [[National Park Service]] as NPS, USDA, and others). The answer key is not included in this text.
