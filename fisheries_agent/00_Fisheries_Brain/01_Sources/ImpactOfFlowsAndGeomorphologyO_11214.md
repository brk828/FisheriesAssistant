# Impact of Flows and Geomorphology on Food Availability for Colorado River Native Fish Community

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #river-ecology #geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This FY-1994 proposal from the Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin outlines a comprehensive, interdisciplinary ecosystem study to assess how flow alterations and geomorphic changes affect food availability for the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius). The study proposes a two-year field investigation examining relationships between flow regime, channel morphology, primary and secondary productivity, native forage fish abundance, and adult squawfish carrying capacity. The research team combines expertise in fish biology, fluvial geomorphology, and stream ecology to evaluate whether flow modifications have reduced system productivity and native fish populations, ultimately limiting squawfish carrying capacity in the upper Colorado River. The study area encompasses the mainstem Colorado River from the Green River confluence to Rifle, Colorado (240 river miles) and the lower Gunnison River (57 river miles).

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] was historically widespread throughout the Colorado River basin but has experienced substantial population and range reductions. By the early 1970s, the species became extinct in the entire lower basin from Glen Canyon to the Gulf of California and is now restricted to the upper basin. Within the upper basin, squawfish inhabit the Colorado River from Lake Powell to Palisade, Colorado, and the Green River from its confluence upstream to Echo Park, with additional populations in large tributaries including the [[White River]], [[Yampa River]], [[Gunnison River]], and [[San Juan River]]. The Green River currently supports the largest population with significant recent reproduction documented.

In the upper Colorado River, adult squawfish show concentrated distribution in the upper 60 miles of accessible range above Westwater Canyon, with few large individuals found downstream despite more optimal temperature regimes. Evidence suggests larvae drift downstream to lower gradient nursery habitats, remaining there as juveniles before migrating upstream as subadults, presumably seeking better adult habitat. Distribution data from trammel netting and electrofishing surveys (1992-1993) reveal longitudinal variation in native forage fish abundance, with higher concentrations of [[Bluehead Sucker]] (Catostomus discobolus), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Catostomus latipinnis), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (Gila robusta) in upstream reaches where adult squawfish concentrate.

The proposal hypothesizes that adult squawfish migration to upstream reaches responds to better food availability and that downstream reaches have low carrying capacities. Three factors are proposed to have reduced food availability: (1) altered flow regimes allowing sedimentation of coarse substrates, reducing primary (algal) and secondary (invertebrate) production; (2) reduced spring overbank flooding decreasing nutrient inputs; and (3) introduction of nonnative fishes competing with native forage species but unsuitable as squawfish prey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study applies the [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al. 1980), which proposes that river community structure and function respond directly to physical conditions changing from headwaters to mouth. The biological community responds predictably to physical habitat characteristics largely regulated by fluvial geomorphic processes. Flow regimes shape gross physical river structure including channel morphology and substrate distribution, which determine habitat quantity and quality for fish and influence water properties (velocity, depth, temperature, turbidity, chemistry). The biotic environment, including densities of food items, predators, and competitors, is also greatly affected by the flow-influenced physical environment.

Recent flow alterations in the Colorado River basin are hypothesized as major factors in native fish reductions, with four species now listed as endangered. While impoundments directly alter fish distribution and movements, effects from flow changes are less obvious. Studies in the [[White River]] ecosystem found that macroinvertebrate and native fish numbers generally declined downstream, corresponding to gradient-induced changes in substrate particle size and habitat type. Periphyton and macroinvertebrate biomass related to dominant substrate particle size, with invertebrate collectors of coarse particulate organic matter increasing in areas of high leaf litter input from cottonwood bottoms.

Research in the [[Green River]] below Flaming Gorge Dam documented simplifications of aquatic invertebrate taxa and functional groups and higher but less efficient primary producer communities attributed to flow alterations. Increased invertebrate diversity, degree of autotrophy, efficiency of primary producers, and concentrations of suspended and dissolved organic materials in the Green River below the Yampa confluence were attributed to inputs from the unregulated Yampa.

The study area is divided into eleven strata based on channel gradient, geology, and geomorphology, spanning from the Green River confluence (RM 0) to Rifle, Colorado (RM 240) on the mainstem Colorado River, plus the lower Gunnison River (RM 0-57). Habitat characteristics, substrate distribution, and average channel dimensions are expected to vary between strata in response to differences in geology, geomorphology, flow volumes, and position along the river continuum, affecting biological components. Turbidity, nutrient inputs, and organic inputs will also vary between strata, affecting productivity.

## Key Findings

**Six Primary Objectives:**

**Objective 1:** Determine if longitudinal variation in adult squawfish abundance associates with native prey densities. Tests Hypothesis H1: Longitudinal distribution of adult squawfish is related to densities of potential prey items. Will compare relative numbers of squawfish and native prey species using historical and new data, with prey species identified from stomach content analysis.

**Objective 2:** Determine if relative abundance of prey fishes suitable to adult squawfish relates to algal and invertebrate production. Tests Hypothesis H2: Longitudinal distribution of roundtail chub, bluehead sucker, and flannelmouth sucker is correlated with densities of their primary foodstuffs. Will compare native fish numbers to periphyton and macroinvertebrate biomass, using habitat distribution to assess productivity potential and stomach contents to identify important food resources.

**Objective 3:** Determine longitudinal patterns of riverine physical attributes (habitat distribution, substrate particle size, depth of interstitial voids, light transmission, sediment organic content) and productivity (periphyton biomass, macroinvertebrate density/biomass/diversity). Tests Hypothesis H3: Primary and secondary productivity varies longitudinally and correlates with sediment organic content, light transmission, gradient, substrate particle size, and/or depth of substrate interstitial voids. Will quantify distributions before runoff and in late summer, comparing physical and biological factors.

**Objective 4:** Characterize fluvial geomorphology of upper Colorado River at present and assess degree of change in channel morphology and bottomland flooding due to flow alterations. Tests Hypothesis H4: Geomorphically important habitats have changed significantly over time with trends of loss or simplification and sedimentation of coarse bed material. Will use aerial photographs to infer changes and develop mass balance of sediment movement through selected reaches.

**Objective 5:** Assess whether carrying capacity for adult Colorado squawfish has been affected by changes in channel geomorphology. Will synthesize results from Objectives 1-4, using relationships between productivity and physical factors to infer whether changes in channel morphology and sedimentation from flow alteration could have modified productivity and native prey fish availability.

**Objective 6:** Assess whether flows may be modified to cleanse within-channel substrates to enhance system productivity and increase energy input via organic debris entrainment. Will determine magnitude, duration, and frequency of spring high flows required to cleanse substrates and maintain productive conditions using hydraulic modeling and field measurements of sedimentation rates.

**Proposed Methods:**

- **Physical Parameters:** Water depth, velocity, gradient, substrate composition, embeddedness, interstitial sediment characterization, particulate organic matter, water quality (nutrients, turbidity), light extinction, habitat mapping from aerial photographs
- **Biological Parameters:** Periphyton biomass (chlorophyll a), benthic and drifting macroinvertebrate density and biomass, coarse particulate organic matter
- **Fisheries Sampling:** Electrofishing surveys for relative density and size structure, stomach content analysis using Seaburg samplers for chub, whole digestive tracts for suckers, and emetics for adult squawfish
- **Geomorphology:** Photogrammetric analysis of historical aerial photographs (1930s to present) using GIS overlays, analysis of USGS streamflow and sediment records, cross-section surveys and hydraulic modeling, sediment trap installation to measure fine sediment infiltration rates

**Sampling Design:** Two-year field effort with sampling twice annually (March pre-runoff, September post-summer storms) at three randomly selected reaches within each of eleven strata. Five sample locations across transects in riffles and runs, plus five locations parallel to shore at similar depth/velocity/substrate for longitudinal comparisons. Synoptic survey in 1994 for chlorophyll, turbidity, light transmission, and nutrients at numerous locations along entire study area.

**Expected Outcomes:** Flow recommendations to increase carrying capacity for adult squawfish in Colorado and Gunnison Rivers, important for Aspinall consultation process and refinement of 15-mile reach flow recommendations. Management improvements for native fish community will benefit the top predator, Colorado squawfish.
