# The Colorado River Basin: Instability and Basin Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #river-basin-management

## Executive Summary

This is a 1985 monograph by geographer and fluvial geomorphologist William L. Graf, published by the [[Association of American Geographers]] as part of its Resource Publications in Geography series. It argues that the Colorado River Basin is an unstable, arid-region human-environmental system, and that management strategies built on assumed averages fail when the environment is variable. The book examines hydrologic, riparian-ecological, channel, reservoir and chemical instability, then concludes with future trends, management perspectives and the role of geographers. The document is a scholarly book, not a fish-focused study; fish are mentioned only peripherally (e.g., [[Colorado Squawfish]] and trout fisheries below dams).

## Background & Description

- **Author and purpose:** William L. Graf, a field geomorphologist writing for university students, instructors, professional geographers and informed laypeople. Mathematical detail is deferred to cited references.
- **Editorial context:** Foreword by C. Gregory Knight (Pennsylvania State University), editor of the Resource Publications in Geography series; the AAG disclaims endorsement of the views expressed.
- **Scope:** The basin spans three geologic provinces: the [[Rocky Mountains]], the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the [[Basin and Range Province]]. Its vegetation follows elevation zones after Merriam (1890), from Lower Sonoran desert to Alpine.
- **Institutional setting:** Land is mostly federal (about 51.7% of the basin states' area, by the mean in Table 1). Water rights follow prior appropriation in most states, and the "Law of the River" governs allocation. Agencies include the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] and the [[Federal Emergency Management Agency]].
- **Structure:** Eight chapters covering the basin, hydrologic flooding, riparian vegetation, arroyo development, channel migration, high dams, chemical instability, and futures and conclusions, plus a bibliography of roughly 300 references.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and climate.** Water is delivered mainly by Pacific frontal and low-pressure systems in winter, when the polar jet stream steers storms into the basin. Three flood types are identified: snowmelt floods, long winter-precipitation floods, and short summer thunderstorm flash floods. The jet stream shifts southward roughly every other decade, and the book notes that the 1978–1983 period produced prolonged flooding.

**Riparian vegetation.** Natural riparian communities mix willow, cottonwood, mesquite and other species. Only about 15% of Arizona's natural riparian communities survived streamflow interruption for irrigation. Introduced [[Tamarisk]] (salt cedar) and [[Russian Olive]] have transformed riparian zones, spreading from about 4,000 ha in 1920 to 526,000 ha by 1970 in the western United States.

**Wildlife.** Riparian zones enhance bird diversity, provide habitat for [[White-Winged Dove]] and nesting sites for [[Bald Eagle]], and support fish and wildlife. Dams shifted fish communities: the [[Colorado Squawfish]] largely disappeared, while a heavily fished trout habitat developed below Glen Canyon Dam.

**Chemistry.** Salinity rose from about 250 ppm at Lee's Ferry under natural conditions to roughly 800 ppm at Imperial Dam by the 1970s. Mercury from weathered Colorado Plateau rocks accumulates in Lake Powell, where bass tissues reached over 700 ppb, above the EPA's 500 ppb recommended limit for human consumption.

## Key Findings

1. **Thesis of instability.** Management based on predictable averages is ineffective in an unstable environment. Planning must account for variability and spatial structure, and flood planning must be non-structural as well as structural.

2. **Allocation was based on an anomalously wet period.** Basin allocations exceeded the long-term average flow by at least 15%, so promises may exceed deliverable water. Litigation and economic or environmental crises may follow.

3. **Flooding.** In 1905 the Alamo Canal breach diverted the Colorado into the Salton Sea area. In the early 1980s drought was ended by floods causing about $200 million in damage. The 1983 "artificial" floods forced Parker Dam to release over 1,260 cms (45,000 cfs) for several weeks, after reservoirs lacked storage for unexpectedly rapid late-winter snowmelt.

4. **Flash floods.** A 1974 thunderstorm in Eldorado Canyon, Nevada, produced a peak of 2,100 cms (about 76,000 cfs) and killed 9 people.

5. **Riparian vegetation.** Tamarisk spread at about 20 km per year and outcompeted willow and cottonwood. Clearing efforts on the Gila River (USGS study, 414 two- to three-week measurement periods) produced an estimated water salvage of 45.7 cm per year, but the 95% confidence interval ran from −10.2 cm to +104.1 cm. Cleared areas were recolonized within 18–24 months, and replacement grasses were not successfully established. The authors conclude that the most reasonable course may be no action.

6. **Evapotranspiration.** Reported annual rates for tamarisk range from 34 cm to 280 cm (Table 2), making reliable water-savings estimates infeasible.

7. **Arroyo development.** Arroyos reach depths of 21 m and lengths of several km. Three hypotheses are considered (overgrazing, climatic change, internal adjustment), and the authors conclude that any or all may operate, a condition termed "equifinality." Sediment records show that many arroyos began filling after the early 1940s.

8. **Channel migration.** The Fremont River was converted from a 30 m channel to a 7 m deep, 400 m wide trench by 1910. Dams and floods caused the Gila River to widen to about 1.6 km in 1891. Rillito Creek damage in 1983 occurred largely outside mapped floodplains, and simulations suggest erosion losses may be five times inundation losses over 50 years.

9. **Floodplain mapping.** The HEC-2 method and the concept of floodplain were developed for humid-region streams and are poorly suited to arid, braided channels. The Agua Fria River case led to Arizona's longest civil court case.

10. **High dams.** Hoover Dam was closed in 1936 and Lake Mead lost about 5% of its capacity within 14 years. Upstream sedimentation affects channels and promotes phreatophyte invasion. Downstream, Glen Canyon Dam reduced mean sediment concentration from 1,500 ppm to 7 ppm and increased rapids and armouring.

11. **Recreation.** Whitewater recreation on the canyon rivers is regulated by quotas and licensing. The Grand Canyon river-running industry generates about $20 million annually, with about 15,000 river runners per year.

12. **Salinity and Mexico.** Under the 1944 treaty, Mexico was promised 1.5 million acre-feet. Its water averaged 1,500 ppm after 1961–1962, with peaks of 2,700 ppm. A desalinization plant was estimated at $333 million plus $12 million per year to operate, while retiring Wellton-Mohawk lands was estimated at about $550 million.

13. **Heavy metals.** Copper concentrations in Queen Creek sediments ranged from 15 to 4,400 ppm, against a drinking-water guideline of 1 ppm.

14. **Radioactivity.** The 1979 Church Rock tailings dam failure released almost 100 million gallons of liquid and 1,100 tons of sediment into the Puerco River. Radioactivity alternated along the river because contaminants moved in sediment pulses rather than dispersing smoothly, exceeding the 30 picocuries per gram safe limit at intervals.

15. **Future trends.** Population growth and urbanization are expected to continue, and declining groundwater will force a shift to surface water. Management is shifting from federal to state-sponsored projects and from structural to non-structural flood control. The authors argue that inter-basin transfers and cloud seeding face regional political barriers.

16. **Role of geographers.** Geographers serve in planning, resource, cartographic and environmental impact roles. A 1984 Corps of Engineers vacancy for a Phoenix-area "Geographer" (salary $25,300) is cited as an example.

17. **Conclusion.** Adaptation to the environment is preferable to attempts at complete control. The Kokopelle image represents this accommodation philosophy.
