# The Effects of Supplemental Feeding and Fall Drawdowns on Largemouth Bass and Bluegills at Ridge Lake, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Volume 31, Article 1, January 1973) by [[George W. Bennett]], [[H. Wickliffe Adkins]] and [[William F. Childers]] evaluates a combined management program at [[Ridge Lake]] in which fish were fed a commercial pelleted diet daily during summers and the lake was drawn down each early fall. The program ran from 1965 to 1969 and was compared with earlier periods of biennial draining and culling, fall drawdowns without feeding, and stable water levels. The program produced larger, fatter [[Bluegill]] and substantially higher bluegill angling yields, but it did not improve [[Largemouth Bass]] fishing, [[Warmouth]] or [[Channel Catfish]] yields.

## Background & Description

Studies of the Ridge Lake fish population had run for 21 years before 1963. These included annual controlled public fishing (June through August), draining censuses, and several manipulations: 10 years of biennial draining and culling, 5 years of fall drawdowns, 4 years of stable water levels, and 3 years of testing hybrid sunfishes for angling.

The fish community descended from stocked fish: 435 largemouth bass (1941), 129 bluegills (1944), 138 warmouths (1949), 558 [[Lake Chubsucker]] (1960), and several groups of 6–12 inch channel catfish (1951, 1952, 1957, 1969). Channel catfish generally could not reproduce successfully in the lake. The other species maintained populations through natural reproduction after their original stocking.

Drawdowns began in 1951 and lowered the lake 4.6 m (15 ft), reducing surface area from 6.9 to 2.0 ha. Bluegill losses were severe, so later drawdowns were limited to 3 m (10 ft). The authors' rationale for supplemental feeding came from laboratory work with [[Hybrid Sunfish]] showing that centrarchids quickly learn to eat trout pellets, and from 1953 and 1956 census observations of bluegills that were large but comparatively thin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Restocking (April 1963):** 9,244 fishes weighing 510.6 kg (1,125.5 lb) were returned, equal to 78.8 kg/ha (70.3 lb/acre). The pre-restocking standing stock was 287 kg/ha, nearly four times higher.
- **Pre-experiment yields (1963–1964):** 15.6 and 32.0 lb/acre, well below the 1941–1963 average.
- **Feeding regime:** A 32% protein, 4% fat commercial pellet (Splash Expanded Fish Food) was broadcast by hand twice daily (10:30 AM and 6:30 PM) at about 2 lb/acre/day, mainly over spawning beds and around the boat dock. Seasonal cost was $11.25–$12.19 per acre ($27.80–$30.12 per ha).
- **Drawdown schedule (1965–1969):** Lake levels were lowered each September, ranging from 3.0 to 4.6 m. Refilling occurred by April, and no winterkill was observed.
- **Bluegill condition cycle:** Bluegill condition is lowest in winter, peaks at the start of spawning in late May or early June, and typically declines through summer. No annual condition cycle has been reported for largemouth bass.
- **Predation and stranding:** The authors argue that reduced habitat forces small fish into open water (predation) and strands them in settling plant mats and shallow pockets. The relative importance of these two mechanisms remains uncertain.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and population dynamics**
- Largemouth bass catches were dominated by small fish: 2,984 bass under 254 mm, 962 at 254–305 mm, and only 91 over 305 mm over seven years.
- Bluegill numbers rose from 4,492 restocked fish (1963) to a 1970 census of about 9,500 fish over 75 mm. Under stable water, bluegill numbers reached 50,000–93,000. Fall drawdowns reduced populations to roughly 7,500–17,000.
- The 1970 census contained 2,420 bass, 9,546 bluegills, 556 warmouths, 1,477 channel catfish, 232 lake chubsuckers, and 3 fishes of other species (14,234 total, 1,440.0 kg).
- Fishermen caught 16,476 large bluegills (≥152 mm) and 7,575 small ones over 1965–1969. Desirable bluegills averaged 127 g.

**Angling yields**
- Average bluegill yield under the feeding-drawdown (f-d) program was 71.6 kg/ha (63.9 lb/acre), higher than any other period.
- Average bass yield was 18.0 kg/ha (16.1 lb/acre), lower than any comparison period and about half the 1948 bass yield (32.1 kg/ha).
- Warmouth and channel catfish yields stayed small, reflecting low numbers.

**Condition**
- Bass condition averaged slightly below normal (Index of Condition near 4.5 against a normal range of 4.6–5.5).
- Bluegills reached the fat category (C ≥ 8.1) in June of 1966, 1967 and 1968, with the feeding effect most evident in those years.
- Bluegills in 1954–1955 under drawdowns without feeding were fat only in June 1954.

**Growth**
- Bass growth was slower under the f-d program than under biennial culling. Bass reached 254 mm after two growing seasons under culling but needed three under the f-d program.
- Bluegills grew nearly as fast under drawdowns with feeding as under drawdowns alone, and both programs reached useful size early in the third summer. Under stable water, bluegills grew slowly and rarely exceeded about 165 mm.

**Economics and conclusions**
- Daily food cost was about 12 cents per acre, and fishermen valued larger, fatter bluegills and improved flavor.
- The authors conclude that the f-d program is effective for bluegills and probably catfish, but not for bass. They suggest that one severe drawdown every 2–3 years with annual feeding might better balance the fishery.
- They propose maintaining at least 100 channel catfish per hectare to add angler interest.
