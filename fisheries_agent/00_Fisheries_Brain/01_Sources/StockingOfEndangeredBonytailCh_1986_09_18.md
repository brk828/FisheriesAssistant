# Stocking of Endangered Bonytail Chub into Selected Ponds on the Buenos Aires NWR

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #freshwater-conservation

## Executive Summary
This 1986 internal memorandum from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service documents an emergency conservation action for the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), described as "perhaps the most endangered of all the fish which are listed." Approximately 10,000–12,000 juvenile chubs, produced via uncontrolled natural spawning of captive broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], required immediate relocation before pond draining. As an interim measure, the FWS proposed translocating these fish to six ponds at the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (Arizona) at a density of approximately 1,000 fish per surface acre. Three target ponds were fishless; the remaining three contained exotic fish species requiring eradication with the piscicide Fintrol (antimycin A) prior to stocking, to be conducted by Dr. W.L. Minckley and graduate students from [[Arizona State University]]. The action was framed as a stopgap measure pending longer-term refuge planning for endangered fish rearing and holding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native Habitat**: Historically inhabits large, turbid, fast-flowing rivers of the [[Colorado River Basin]]; considered one of the most imperiled fishes in this system.
- **Captive/Refuge Habitat**: The memo describes an atypical ecological context — captive propagation ponds at Dexter NFH, where uncontrolled natural spawning of broodfish occurred, producing a large cohort of offspring.
- **Recipient Habitat**: Six ponds at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] were selected as interim holding habitat, split between:
  - Fishless ponds (no competitive or predatory pressure).
  - Ponds containing exotic fish species, requiring active removal via the piscicide **Fintrol** (antimycin A) before bonytail introduction.
- **Biotic Interactions**: The presence of exotic fish species in some ponds is identified as a management concern, implying potential competition and/or predation risk to the endangered native species — a common driver of decline for [[Gila]] chubs and other Colorado River Basin cyprinids.
- **Stocking Density**: Proposed at ~1,000 fish/surface acre, indicating a high-density interim holding strategy rather than a naturalized reintroduction density.

## Key Findings
- **Population Source**: 10,000–12,000 bonytail chub juveniles were generated incidentally through uncontrolled natural spawning of broodstock held in hatchery ponds — an unplanned but valuable production event for this critically endangered species.
- **Conservation Urgency**: Scheduled draining of the Dexter NFH ponds created a time-sensitive crisis requiring rapid relocation to prevent mortality of a significant portion of the captive-bred population.
- **Interim Management Strategy**: Rather than long-term release, the action was explicitly framed as an "interim measure," with intent to revisit long-term rearing/holding infrastructure for endangered fish at Buenos Aires NWR during future master planning.
- **Exotic Species Removal Protocol**: Three of the six recipient ponds required chemical eradication of exotic fish using **Fintrol**, an FWS-approved piscicide, executed by external academic researchers ([[W.L. Minckley]] and ASU graduate students) rather than refuge staff — highlighting interagency/academic collaboration in endangered species recovery.
- **Administrative Urgency**: The memo explicitly requests expedited approval due to time constraints ("the fish will not wait"), illustrating the operational challenges of coordinating endangered species conservation across federal hatchery and refuge systems.
- **Cross-Reference**: This action is an early example of using [[National Wildlife Refuge]] pond systems as ex-situ conservation habitat for endangered freshwater fishes, a strategy later expanded for other Colorado River Basin natives such as the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
