# Effect of Tree Leaves on Water Quality in the Cacapon River, West Virginia

#limnology #water-quality #gyraulus

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Keith V. Slack]] (U.S. Geological Survey Professional Paper 475-D, Article 161, 1964) examines how accumulated tree leaves in low-flow pools of the [[Cacapon River]], [[Morgan County]], West Virginia, change stream water chemistry, dissolved oxygen, and temperature. Decomposition of leaf litter in isolated pools produced strong water color, very low [[Dissolved Oxygen]] concentrations, and a surface film interpreted as [[Ferric Hydroxide]]. Flushing by a rise in stream stage in November 1961 removed the color and restored water quality. The work is framed as part of a broader study of [[Biological Influences on Water Quality]].

## Background & Description

- **Phenomenon:** Autumnal accumulation of [[Leaf Litter]] in streams during the annual low-flow period, where decomposition greatly exceeds production of new organic matter. The document describes this as a short-lived but serious degradation of water quality that can kill aquatic organisms.
- **Timing:** Discoloring begins when leaves start to drop, usually in September. Leaves collect at the downstream ends of pools, sink into a blanket over the streambed, and can form waterlogged masses that inhibit flow over riffles.
- **Conditions favoring discolored water:** A pooled stream with little or no surface flow and deciduous trees near the channel. Frequent rains during peak leaf fall can flush the organic load downstream and dilute leached substances.
- **Named phenomenon:** In parts of the Midwest, extreme discoloration is called "[[Black Water]]," and early-stage pool water resembles [[Bog Water]].
- **Study reach:** About 14 stream miles above the mouth of the Cacapon River, shaded by a hardwood forest of maple, cottonwood, tulip tree, blue beech, sycamore, elm, dogwood, aspen, and oak. Channels separating elongate wooded islands from the right bank become dry or reduced to a few pools at low flow.
- **Sampling sites (Figure 161.1):**
  - **A:** Main-stream riffle, about 18 cm deep.
  - **B:** Slightly colored pool or backwater, connected to the river, sampled about 5 m from its junction.
  - **C:** Isolated, highly colored pool of about 6 × 3 m area at the base of a steep bank.
  - **D:** Moderately colored pool, sampled in a temperature study on October 30, 1961.
- **Sampling dates:** October 22, October 29, October 30, and November 19, 1961.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Literature on leaf-litter effects:**
  - [[Schneller (1955)]] and [[Slack (1955)]] reported that leaf decay in pools increased water color, produced free CO₂, and lowered dissolved oxygen. Both found alkalinity increased with color in Indiana samples.
  - [[Sylvester (1959)]] reported increased water color after replacing native coniferous forest with deciduous trees in the Wenatchee River basin, Washington.
  - [[Hynes (1960)]], describing [[Huet (1951)]], noted a substance toxic to fish derived from spruce and red cedar needles.
  - [[Chase and Ferullo (1958)]] found that after 386 days, maple leaves consumed oxygen equal to 75 percent of their initial dry weight, while oak leaves and pine needles exerted demands of about 50 percent of dry weight.
  - [[Myers (1961)]] suggested manganese in San Clemente Reservoir, California, was leached from oak trees.
  - [[Robinson and Others (1958)]] showed hickory leaves concentrate [[Rare-Earth Elements]] to a remarkable degree.
- **Mechanisms considered for mineral increases:** Leaching of soluble substances, evaporation, release of minerals from sediments by organic complexing and chemical reduction, and bacterial breakdown of leaf tissue. The document states the relative contribution of each is not known.
- **Redox and oxygen:** Decomposing leaves in waterlogged pools deplete dissolved oxygen, creating near-anaerobic conditions. The document links these conditions to higher iron, manganese, and free CO₂ in the most colored pool.
- **Biota:** Snails (*Gyraulus*) lived in the isolated, highly colored pool. They were present in large numbers on leaves at the surface along its margins, which the author attributes to their ability to breathe air at the surface. Similar groups were absent from the slightly colored pool, which stayed connected to oxygenated river water.
- **Broader drainage:** Leaf litter from other parts of the drainage area also contributes particulate and dissolved load. This effect is expected to be delayed until heavy rains transport material downstream to larger water bodies.

## Key Findings

### Chemical composition
- Concentrations of many substances increased with water color. Calcium, bicarbonate alkalinity, dissolved solids, and specific conductance were, however, higher in the slightly colored pool (B) than in the highly colored pool (C).
- Platinum-cobalt color, October 22: main stream 7, slightly colored pool 20, highly colored pool 120. On October 29: 4, 60, and 90. On November 19: 2, 3, and 3.
- Aluminum and nickel increased with water color. Iron and manganese were higher in colored waters than in the main stream.
- Sulfate was lower in both colored waters than in the river. The author suggests this may reflect reduction of sulfate to sulfide.
- Lead was lowest in the slightly colored pool (B).
- The author tentatively concludes that differences between river and colored waters result from leaching and decomposition of leaf litter in isolated environments. Differences between the two pools likely reflect differences in water source.
- Free CO₂ was higher in the highly colored pool, with values of 5.6 ppm in the October 22 sample and 5.5 ppm in the October 29 sample. These values are read from the garbled table and should be verified against the original.

### Calcium depletion in the highly colored pool
- Pool volume was roughly 2,000 liters, indicating a deficit of 18.5 mg of calcium per liter relative to the other waters, or a total loss of 37 g.
- If spread among an estimated 100 snails, each would need to take up 0.37 g calcium (0.92 g calcium carbonate), which the author considers excessive for this thin-shelled form.
- Possible explanations include dilute runoff or ground water, ion-exchange reactions with leaf material, and biological uptake for shell formation. Biological uptake cannot be excluded without better population data.

### Dissolved oxygen
- Highly colored pool (C), October 30: average of 0.09 ppm, about 0.8 percent of saturation (two samples, Alsterberg azide-modified Winkler method, which the author notes may read low because of interfering substances).
- Slightly colored pool (B), October 30: 1.9 ppm (18 percent of saturation).
- Main stream (A), October 30: 10.3 ppm (99 percent of saturation).
- Lowest value recorded: 0.06 ppm, near the upstream end of pool C, where decomposition seemed most advanced.
- Polarographic oxygen electrode readings, October 23: 2 percent of saturation at the bottom (24.5 cm) in pool C and a maximum of 6 percent elsewhere.
- The author concludes that water color correlated well with dissolved-oxygen content.

### Surface film
- A thin, inflexible film, believed to be ferric hydroxide formed by air oxidation of ferrous iron, was present in small patches on October 22 and covered the entire pool surface a week later with few tears.
- Films remained near the center of open water. Reducing conditions near the margins may have prevented oxidized film formation.
- The author notes the film may inhibit evaporation and reaeration at the air-water interface.

### Temperature
- On October 23, pool C rose from 6.6 °C at 2.5 cm depth to 10 °C at 11.5 cm. Below 11.5 cm the water was isothermal.
- On October 30, temperature decreased with depth in all colored pools. The steepness of the gradient varied directly with color intensity. The author attributes this to rapid absorption of solar radiation near the surface by colored water.
- No temperature differences were noted in the main stream at comparable depths.
- Bottom-water temperature in pool C was lowest on 4 of 5 days measured. Pool B's bottom temperature was always higher than pool C's, possibly from mixing with warmer river water or inflow of cool ground water through pool D.
- Average thermal gradients ranged from 0.00 to 0.32 °C per cm across the sampled pools (table values; column assignment not fully legible).

### Recovery and flushing
- Pool C lost its color by November 19. The author infers it was flooded and flushed by a rise in river stage on November 7–8.
- Discharge at a gaging station about 7 miles downstream was 59 cfs on October 29, rose to 240 cfs on November 8, and fell to 82 cfs on November 19.
- Flushing by a rise in stream stage reduces color and improves water quality.

### Overall conclusion
- The effect of leaves on stream-water quality and temperature is a localized interaction between marginal vegetation and water in low-flow, backwater, or flood-plain pools.
