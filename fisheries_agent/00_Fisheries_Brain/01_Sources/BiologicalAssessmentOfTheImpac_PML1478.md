# Biological Assessment of Impacts of the McAlister Intake Facility on Federally Listed Fishes in Lake Mohave, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This biological assessment, prepared by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) for Stovall Engineers, Inc. and the [[National Park Service]] (Lake Mead National Recreation Area), evaluates potential effects of constructing and operating the [[McAlister Intake Facility]] at [[Telephone Cove]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. The assessment addresses four federally listed endangered fishes: [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It was prepared to support National Park Service compliance with Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]].

The author concludes that the facility is unlikely to affect humpback chub, Colorado squawfish, or razorback sucker populations, and is not expected to affect proposed [[Critical Habitat]] for razorback sucker. Low intake velocities and the project's small withdrawal rate limit entrainment risk. The assessment recommends timing construction for late summer to early autumn, on-site monitoring, a protocol for handling any mortalities, and a summary report to agencies.

## Background & Description

**Lake Mohave.** The reservoir was formed by closure of [[Davis Dam]] on the Arizona-Nevada border. Construction began in 1942, stopped during wartime, resumed in 1946, and the reservoir reached the tailrace of [[Hoover Dam]] in May 1951. The original operating level of 196.0 m was raised in 1954 to 197.2 m. Key morphometry includes:

- Length of about 108 km between Davis and Hoover dams; maximum width 6.4 km
- Maximum depth 42 m; mean depth 18.5 m
- Surface area about 115 km²; volume about 2.3 km³
- Located about 120 km southeast of Las Vegas, Nevada

The lake is described as a "run of the river" reservoir. Its only continuous inflow is the [[Colorado River]] through Hoover Dam, and there are no major diversions. Its substrate is largely sedimentary clay, silt, and sand, plus alluvium from surrounding mountains.

**Native fish history.** Before the lakes formed, the mainstream Colorado River supported nine native species, including five cyprinids (bonytail, humpback chub, roundtail chub, speckled dace, and Colorado squawfish) and four catostomids (razorback, flannelmouth, desert, and bluehead suckers). Carp and channel catfish were stocked in the late 1800s. After Hoover Dam closed in 1935, many non-native fishes were introduced for sport fisheries, and native fauna declined severely. Today most native species are extirpated, rare, or of uncertain status. Bonytail and razorback sucker persist in the area.

**Species of concern.**

- **Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*): a moderate-sized cyprinid (to about 65 cm TL) endemic to the Colorado basin. It was once common in the reach now inundated by Lake Mohave. It was federally listed as endangered in 1980. Lake Mohave is the only locality where it can reliably be captured at certain times. Apart from one individual captured in the late 1980s, there is no recent evidence of successful reproduction in the lake.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): a moderate-sized cyprinid (to about 50 cm TL). Its largest remaining population is in Grand Canyon, the only lower-basin reproducing habitat. It was listed as endangered in 1967. Recent surveys in Lake Mead and Lake Mohave failed to detect it, and it is presumed extirpated from Lake Mohave.
- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): North America's largest minnow (to 1.8 m TL). It was listed as endangered in 1967. The last Lake Mead record was in 1962–1967. It is considered locally extirpated from Lake Mohave.
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a large catostomid (approaching 100 cm TL). Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining wild population, consisting exclusively of old adults with near-total recruitment failure since the early 1950s. The species was proposed as threatened in 1978, then re-proposed and listed as endangered in 1991.

**Intake design.** The McAlister facility will withdraw an estimated 44,000 gallons per day (0.0681 CFS). Design assumptions include an 8-inch inlet pipe, a 1.5 HP pump, a 6-inch outlet, and a static head of about 870 ft.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Lake Mohave is slightly alkaline (pH near 8). Total dissolved solids average about 700 mg/L, mainly sulfate, carbonate, and chloride with sodium, calcium, magnesium, and potassium. Phosphorus is extremely low and limiting (<0.015 mg/L), nitrogen is moderate (0.2–0.5 mg/L), and silica is abundant (7–8 mg/L).

**Fish community.** [[Table 1]] in the source lists native and non-native fishes with probable status codes (abundant, common, uncommon, rare, extirpated, or indeterminate). The table includes 8 native species and 22 non-native species. Non-native species include threadfin shad, five salmonids, carp, goldfish, golden shiner, fathead minnow, red shiner, Utah sucker, three ictalurids, mosquitofish, striped bass, and five centrarchids such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. Largemouth bass and threadfin shad are listed as abundant. Non-native fishes are cited as a factor in humpback chub decline through competition and predation.

**Razorback sucker habitat and behavior.** Adults congregate in autumn along upstream shorelines of [[Cottonwood Basin]] and in [[Arizona Bay]]. Spawning occurs from November to May over clean, wave-washed substrate, mainly along the Nevada shoreline of Cottonwood Basin and in Arizona Bay. Annual netting by Arizona State University and cooperators since 1974 has found few adults in lower Cottonwood Basin. Larval surveys have not found young razorback suckers in lower Lake Mohave.

**Telephone Cove conditions.** Electrofishing in Telephone Cove during 1988–1991 captured no razorback suckers. No larvae were found in winter-spring collections in 1993–1994. The substrate and bottom topography of the project area do not appear optimal for staging, spawning, feeding, or resting.

**Hydraulics.** The inlet pipe velocity of 0.287 FPS (6.3 cm/s) falls to 0.012 FPS (about 0.4 cm/s) within the first foot from the inlet. Beyond 3 feet (91 cm), velocity is virtually nil, at 0.002 FPS (0.06 cm/s) or less.

## Key Findings

1. **Humpback chub and Colorado squawfish:** Neither species is present in Lake Mohave in a viable population. The nearest populations are in Grand Canyon (humpback chub) and Lake Powell (Colorado squawfish), and neither would be affected. Critical habitat for both species excludes Lake Mohave, so no critical habitat is affected.
2. **Bonytail:** Wild and re-introduced fish may occur in Telephone Cove. Projected current velocities are too slow to entrain or harm these relatively large fish, based on [[Grabowski et al. 1984]] and references therein.
3. **Razorback sucker:** The project is unlikely to affect the Lake Mohave population, and it is not expected to affect proposed [[Critical Habitat]] for the species. Abundance is highest upstream of the project site. Disturbance to critical habitat is expected to be innocuous and temporary, limited to short-term disruption of bottom materials and turbidity in shallow water. No effect is expected on the constituent elements (water, physical habitat, biological environment) or on the additional selection criteria specified by [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1993a).
4. **Overall determination:** The McAlister Intake Facility is not expected to significantly affect any of the four target fishes.

**Recommendations:**

- Build the facility in late summer to early autumn, when adult bonytail and razorback suckers are dispersed in deeper offshore waters and larvae are absent, to reduce disturbance risk.
- Station a trained observer on site periodically to monitor biological effects during installation, using surface observation and other methods. Keep a photographic record of project activities.
- If any bonytail or razorback sucker mortality occurs, immediately freeze the specimen or remove and freeze tissues. Transfer them to a recipient designated by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, or deposit them with an institutional collection for permanent curation.
- Provide a summary report to state and federal resource management and regulatory agencies and to interested parties.

**Scope note:** The assessment covers only the four target fishes. It is not a comprehensive treatment of Lake Mohave's native fishes or of effects on other species. Several agency representatives were consulted during preparation, including staff from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the Nevada Department of Wildlife, and the National Park Service.
