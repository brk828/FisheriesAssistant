# Page Springsnail Population Study Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This 1999 proposal, submitted by Michael A. Martinez of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Phoenix, Arizona), requests funding for a population ecology study of the [[Page Springsnail]] (*Pyrgulopsis morrisoni*), a freshwater gastropod endemic to the Verde River drainage of central Arizona. The species is a candidate for listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and has suffered population declines and habitat modification. The study proposes quantitative grab sampling of at least two occupied spring sites on lands managed by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], with the aim of informing a site-specific management plan and a [[Candidate Conservation Agreement]] that may preclude listing.

## Background & Description

The proposal argues that invertebrates are central to ecosystem energy flow and nutrient cycling, yet are often overlooked by managers because they are neither exploitable nor aesthetically appealing. Springsnails are presented as sensitive [[Biological Indicators]] of spring health and aquifer integrity. Southwestern deserts host many endemic aquatic mollusks, largely relics of the Pleistocene, and these are declining as human water demand grows.

The Page Springsnail is a gastropod in the family Hydrobiidae. Its known populations occur in a complex of springs along Oak Creek. The document notes that *Pyrgulopsis* taxonomy has been reviewed by [[Hershler]] (1994, 1998) and that the species' ecology depends on [[Spring Ecosystems]] and [[Cienega Habitats]], which are isolated, mid-elevation, permanently saturated, spring-fed communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** A 1.5 km stretch on the west side of Oak Creek near Page Springs, at roughly 1070 m (3500 ft). The complex includes Fry, Lolomai, Bubbling, Turtle, Bass, and Page springs.
- **Priority sites:** Bubbling, Bass, and Page springs, plus possibly part of Turtle Springs, are on Department-managed land.
- **Habitat:** The most common habitat is a rheocrene (a spring emerging as a flowing stream). Substrate is typically firm and mixed with cobble, gravel, and woody debris. *Pyrgulopsis* snails are rarely found in soft sediment.
- **Vegetation:** Algae and macrophytes, notably watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*), where snails are commonly found.
- **Measured parameters:** Depth, temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, and conductivity.

## Key Findings

**Objectives**
1. Survey population status of the Page Springsnail within and among occupied spring sites.
2. Assess associations between habitat components, especially substrate size, and snail density.
3. Collect baseline water quality measurements for future trend analysis.
4. Provide recommendations for a site-specific management plan, a conservation strategy, and a Candidate Conservation Agreement.

**Methods**
- **Sampling design:** Systematic random sampling with parallel line transects and grab samples at repeated intervals; the first grab on each transect is random.
- **Grab sampling:** Substrate grabs provide quantitative abundance data. Substrate is classified on a modified Wentworth scale (Cummins 1962).
- **Abundance estimation:** Total population size is estimated following Seber (1982) and Cochran (1977) using a sampling-plot framework, with density (N/A) and variance calculated from plot counts.
- **Surface area:** Spring surface area is estimated using the line-intercept method (Lehman 1991).
- **Analysis:** Density is compared across substrate strata and sites using a two-factor analysis.

**Budget**
- Equipment list (about 35 items) from Forestry Suppliers, Inc. and Cabela's, including a dissolved oxygen meter, a multiparameter (pH, conductivity, temperature) meter, a Ponar grab sampler, sieves, microscopes, and field supplies.
- Total cost: **$14,732.70**, of which **$11,850.00** covers 15 biologist days (about $790/day, including overhead) and **$790.00** covers vehicle gas for 5 trips.

**Literature**
- The reference list (about 15 entries) includes [[Hershler]] (1994, 1998) on *Pyrgulopsis*, [[Williams et al. 1985]] on desert aquatic ecosystems, Seber (1982), Cochran (1977), Cummins (1962), and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1997) candidate notice.

**Plan and expected outcome**
The proposal's central premise is that empirical population and habitat data are a prerequisite for effective conservation planning. Protecting lower-trophic microfauna is presented as a practical route to conserving the diversity and productivity of southwestern water resources.
