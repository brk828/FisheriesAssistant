# Comparative Limnology of Differentially Regulated Sections of a Colorado Mountain River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This study by [[James V. Ward]] compares four sections of the [[South Platte River]] in the Colorado mountains, sampled over one year between 1972 and 1975, to assess how [[Deep Release Dams]] affect receiving streams. Sites ranged from highly regulated reaches below [[Cheesman Lake]] (Cheesman Canyon) to effectively unregulated conditions at the [[North Fork]] confluence. Macroinvertebrates showed high standing crops but low diversity below the dam, and low standing crops with high diversity at unregulated sites. The author attributes these differences to six mechanisms: chemical limiting factors, submerged angiosperm and epilithic algae distribution, organic matter input diversity, predation and competition, environmental stability, and thermal signals.

## Background & Description

The study extends an earlier year-round investigation (1972–78 record in the introduction, cited as Ward 1974) of the stream immediately below a hypolimnial release reservoir, which found large standing crops of macroinvertebrates and epilithic algae but very low diversity, attributed largely to the modified thermal regime. The present work was conducted in a canyon section about 82 km below the dam and at stations above the confluence with an unregulated tributary, to provide comparative data on the composition and diversity of macroinvertebrate communities.

The South Platte River rises on the East Slope of the [[Continental Divide]] and flows through [[Antero Reservoir]], [[Elevenmile Canyon Reservoir]] and Cheesman Lake. The [[North Fork]] originates on the slopes of Mount Evans and is unregulated, although occasionally augmented with West Slope water through a tunnel system (used on only two days during the study year).

Study sites (Figure 1):
- **Cheesman Canyon (Sites 1–3):** 0.25, 2.4 and 5 km below the dam; reached by foot trail. A fourth site at 8.5 km was excluded because of ecotonal properties.
- **Waterton Canyon (Sites 4–6):** 0.5, 4.4 and 6 km below the North Fork; accessed by private road.
- **Confluence sites (SP and NF):** immediately above the North Fork mouth. SP had sparse riparian cover and dense algal Aufwuchs; NF had dense Rocky Mountain alder cover and leaf litter as its main energy source.

All sites were rubble riffles in trout water of granitic foothills streams, at elevations from 2018 m (Site 1) to 1757 m (Site 6). Cheesman Lake is a 65 m deep storage reservoir releasing water from 46 and 60 m depths.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling and methods.** Sites 1–3 were sampled about every 28 days (June 1972 to May 1978). Sites 4–6 were sampled every three weeks in summer and monthly thereafter (June 1973 to May 1974; no January samples at Sites 5 and 6). SP and NF had physicochemical sampling four times and macroinvertebrate, temperature and pH sampling monthly (April 1974 to March 1975). Macroinvertebrates were collected with a [[Surber Sampler]]; epilithon by timed scraping. Diversity was calculated with the [[Shannon-Weaver Index]] (log base 2, bits per individual).

**Physicochemistry.** Free CO₂ was negative at nearly all sites and dates (the exception being 0.5 ppm at Site 4 in June), reflecting photosynthetic uptake by algae and macrophytes. Cheesman pH was high (8.2–8.4 medians), Waterton lower (7.6–7.8), and the Cheesman water was "hard" by Pennak's classification versus "medium" at Waterton. Bound CO₂ averaged 55.1 ppm in Cheesman, 36.0 ppm at SP and 15.0 ppm at NF. Dissolved oxygen was near saturation throughout. Total suspended matter was 2.2 mg/l at Site 1 (reservoir clarification) and increased downstream, while total dissolved matter was 357 mg/l at Site 1 versus 141 and 161 mg/l at Sites 4 and 6. At confluence sites, dissolved matter was 191.5 mg/l at SP versus 67.6 mg/l at NF.

**Flow.** The regulated flow averaged 206 m³/min at Site 1 against 739 m³/min at Site 4 (inflated by an exceptional spring runoff). The reservoir spreads flow evenly across the year and produces long periods of constant discharge (e.g. late March to early May). Mean flows were 308 m³/min at NF and 279 m³/min at SP (April 1974 to March 1975).

**Temperature.** Annual mean temperatures were similar in the two canyons, but the thermal regime differed: Cheesman had low summer temperatures, ice-free high winter temperatures, diurnal and seasonal constancy, and a delayed seasonal maximum (28 October, at reservoir overturn). Waterton ranged from near 0 °C to 15 °C with a mid-August maximum and a rapid spring rise. Diurnal fluctuations of up to 6 °C occurred 8.5 km below the dam.

**Epilithon.** Standing crops were much greater below the dam (for example, 9.73 g dry weight per 5-minute sample at Site 1 versus 0.64 g at Site 4). Chlorophyceae dominated in Cheesman, and Myxophyceae comprised 1% or less of the epilithon in both canyons. Submerged angiosperms were abundant in Cheesman and absent from all lower sites.

## Key Findings

**Macroinvertebrate standing crop and diversity**
- Cheesman Canyon averaged 2784 organisms/m² and 29.5 g wet weight/m²; Waterton averaged 716 organisms/m² and 8.2 g/m².
- SP had 4786 organisms/m² and 80.9 g/m²; NF had 406 organisms/m² and 16 g/m².
- Species totals were 82 (Cheesman), 70 (Waterton), 64 (SP) and 49 (NF).
- The NF community had the highest Shannon-Weaver index because of more even distribution among species. The Cheesman index was 2.6, similar to values in organic pollution recovery zones.

**Composition**
- Of 82 taxa from the canyon sites, only 20 were shared; 62 occurred in only one canyon. Waterton contributed 50 of the single-canyon taxa and Cheesman 12.
- Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera, Diptera and Trichoptera made up 97.1% (Cheesman) and 95.4% (Waterton) of numbers.
- Plecopterans were under 1% of the Cheesman biomass but 34.1% of Waterton biomass. Only one stonefly, *Isoperla patricia*, occurred in Cheesman versus 18 species in Waterton.
- Only Ephemeroptera showed similar percentage composition in both canyons.
- Of 41 taxa shared between SP and NF, 31 were limited to one of the two sites, despite their proximity.

**Taxa restricted by regulation**
- Below the dam only: [[Gammarus Lacustris]], lymnaeid and physid gastropods, and *Eiseniella tetraedra* (earthworm; truly aquatic populations appear restricted to the reach between Cheesman Dam and the North Fork confluence).
- Above the dam only (downstream canyon): filipalpian stoneflies such as *Isogenus expansus*, heptageniid mayflies (e.g. [[Rhithrogena Doddsi]], the most abundant mayfly in NF), blepharocerids, and *Brachycentrus*.

**Mechanisms proposed by the author**
- **Limiting factors:** gastropods are restricted by calcium, pH and bound CO₂ (>15 ppm); *G. lacustris* requires cool summer water; the epilithic algal cover may exclude suction- or friction-pad forms.
- **Trophic relationships:** reduced chemical and particle diversity of organic inputs; plankton is not a reliable food source below the dam, and clarification may suppress filter-feeders.
- **Stability and competition:** constant conditions intensify competitive exclusion; predators are scarce below the dam, so a few species dominate.
- **Thermal effects:** the thermal pattern may lack the cues needed for life-cycle completion. The author refers to the Cairns (1974) view that diversity should not be disrupted until its link to ecosystem stability is clarified.

**Conclusions**
- Deep release dams increase standing crop but reduce macroinvertebrate diversity, with effects still evident 32 km downstream.
- Whether dam effects are beneficial depends on reservoir trophic state, retention time, drawdown, reservoir temperature profile and maintenance of a natural seasonal flow pattern. The author states that much more data are needed.
