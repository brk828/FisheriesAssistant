# Juvenile Razorback Sucker in a Managed Green River Wetland, with Notes on Toad Specimens and Pacific Yew Cosexuality

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from *Great Basin Naturalist* (vol. 56, no. 4, 1996, pp. 374–378) contains three unrelated items. The main fish paper, by Timothy Modde, reports juvenile and adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from [[Old Charley Wash]], a managed 60-ha wetland beside the [[Green River]] in Utah. The 28 juveniles collected during draining suggest that floodplain wetlands may serve as nursery habitat for this federally endangered species. The other two items are a fragment of an appendix on [[Bufo Americanus]] and [[Bufo Woodhousii]] specimen localities and parasite accession numbers, and a short note by Hogg, Mitchell and Clayton confirming [[Cosexuality]] in [[Pacific Yew]]. The toad paper's main text is absent, so only the appendix data are summarized.

## Background & Description

**Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** is a large, endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was once widespread and abundant, but its numbers and range declined after mainstem dams were built and many nonnative fishes were introduced. It was federally listed as endangered in 1991 (USFWS 1991). Little was known about juvenile habitat needs. Earlier records included 8 juveniles (90–115 mm TL) from Colorado River backwaters (Taba et al. 1965), and a small number of juveniles collected from Green River backwaters in 1991 and 1993 (Gutermuth et al. 1994; R. Muth, personal communication).

**American toad (*Bufo americanus*) and Woodhouse's toad (*Bufo woodhousii*)** are the taxa in the appendix fragment. The appendix lists museum (ASU) numbers and localities for specimens from Arizona. It also records accession numbers for helminths in the [[U.S. National Parasite Collection]]. The main study, which concerns mating calls, body size, temperature and hybridization, is not included in this excerpt.

**Pacific yew (*Taxus brevifolia*)** is normally dioecious, with male and female reproductive structures on separate trees. The authors describe a case of cosexuality, meaning male and female structures on one tree.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Old Charley Wash** lies on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] at river kilometer 402 on the Green River. It is a historical type IV wetland (Cowardin et al. 1979). Its main aquatic plants are smartweed (*Polygonum* sp.) and sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*). Dikes retain water through summer and fall, and inflow and outflow are controlled. Water enters the inlet at river flows of about 240 m³/s. Typical management is to fill the wash in spring and maintain water through summer and autumn.

**Hydrology in 1995:** Spring flow of the Green River peaked at about 595 m³/s. The wash was dry before inundation and was flooded between 23 May and 1 July at river flows above 481 m³/s. Maximum depth exceeded 2 m. After runoff receded, no water was added, so the wetland was isolated from the river. It was drained from 25 September to 12 October 1995.

**Spawning timing:** [[Tyus and Karp 1990]] reported that razorback suckers spawn on the ascending limb of the hydrograph. Drifting larvae then disperse during peak runoff and can reach wetland habitats.

**Fish assemblage:** Fifteen species were recorded in the wetland. The most important by weight were common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*), green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*), channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and black bullhead (*Ameiurus melas*). Other species included [[Razorback Sucker]], northern pike (*Esox lucius*), bonytail (*Gila elegans*-group, listed as *Gila atraria*), flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*).

**Helminth parasites:** Accession records list *Distoichometra bufonis*, *Glypthelminthes quieta*, *Aplectana incerta*, *Aplectana itzocanensis*, Physalopteridae, *Physocephalus* sp. and *Rhabdias americanus* across the toad taxa and their hybrids.

## Key Findings

### Razorback sucker in Old Charley Wash
- **28 juvenile razorback suckers** were collected when the wash was drained in fall 1995. Mean TL was 94 mm (range 74–195 mm), and mean weight was 9.5 g (range 3–18 g). Voucher specimens are catalogued as LFL 24874 at Colorado State University.
- **8 adult razorback suckers** (461–525 mm TL; 1034–1650 g) were also captured. Six were taken before the wetland was drained, and two during draining.
- Juveniles were collected from a 12-m concrete-lined outlet channel, built in April 1995 to concentrate fish for seining.
- The origin of the juveniles is unknown. They may have drifted from riverine spawning sites or been produced in the wetland itself.
- The author suggests that floodplains may be important razorback sucker nursery areas. Supporting evidence comes from Tyus and Karp (1990) and Modde et al. (1996).
- Recovery support came from the Recovery Implementation Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin.

### Toad appendix (*Bufo* specimens)
- ***B. americanus* (*microscaphus*)** specimens came from Maricopa County (N = 6), Yavapai County (N = 61) and Coconino County (N = 10).
- ***B. woodhousii* specimens** came from Maricopa County (N = 53), with additional localities in the same county.
- **Hybrids** were recorded in Yavapai County (N = 8), with 7 from one locality and 1 from another.
- Helminth accession numbers fall in the USNPC series 85910–85924.

### Pacific yew cosexuality
- Pacific yew is normally dioecious. The authors observed cosexuality on a single yew tree in a stand of coastal Douglas-fir on southern Vancouver Island (48°26'N, 123°28'W), near Victoria, British Columbia. Sampling took place in July 1993.
- Female and male structures occurred within a few millimetres of each other on one branch of an otherwise male tree.
- DiFazio (1995) found cosexuality in **17 of 58 male trees (29.3%)**.
- It is not known whether these female buds on male trees produce viable seed.
- Male buds are small (2–3 mm), round, green, and in clusters. Female buds are erect, oval (2–3 mm), green, and usually solitary. Arils become visible from late July or early August.
