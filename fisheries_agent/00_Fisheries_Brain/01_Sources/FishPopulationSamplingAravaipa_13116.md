# Fish Population Sampling: Aravaipa Creek, Arizona, 1991–1992

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for [[The Nature Conservancy]] under contract AZFO-053091-1 and submitted by [[W. L. Minckley]] with lead author Anthony L. Velasco, describes monthly fish monitoring in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a perennial tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] in southeastern Arizona. The creek supports the most diverse native fish assemblage persisting in a single stream in the desert Southwest. The study was designed to track the expected establishment of [[Red Shiner]], a non-native species linked to declines of federally threatened natives. When red shiners failed to establish after February 1991, the study was reframed as a baseline dataset on population trends, native–non-native interactions, and responses to discharge. The authors conclude that non-native fish invasion remains a major management concern.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek flows from the eastern valley (~1050 m elevation) through a steep 18-km [[Aravaipa Canyon]] with vertical walls exceeding 200 m, then across a western floodplain to its confluence with the San Pedro River at 650 m. Riparian vegetation includes cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*), sycamore (*Platanus wrightii*) and willow (*Salix* spp.) in the eastern and canyon reaches, while the western reach is dominated by mesquite (*Prosopis velutina*). Precipitation is bimodal, with a winter wet season, summer monsoons, and flash floods. A major spate of >85 m³/s occurred in March 1991.

Seven native fishes and eight non-native fishes have been recorded. Native species are listed in Table 1 and non-native species in Table 2, with habitat preferences summarized in Table 3. Juvenile versus adult classification followed estimated total lengths at sexual maturity (Table 4), for example 45 mm for longfin dace and 150 mm for roundtail chub.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical controls.** Fish habitat is governed mainly by sediment input and transport, which depend on precipitation and runoff. Canyon reaches, with high gradient, have coarse, bedrock-dominated substrates. Eastern and western reaches have wider floodplains and more fine sediment. Winter floods scour and reset channels, while long periods of normal flow lead to armoring, sand burial and pool filling.

**Sampling design.** Three reaches were each assigned two 200-m sites (the canyon reach had additional quarterly sites), sampled with 1.83 × 1.22 m (0.32 cm mesh) and 4.57 × 1.22 m (0.64 cm mesh) seines and backpack electrofishers. Long-term seining data have been maintained by [[Minckley]] since 1965 (see [[Long-Term Monitoring Dataset]]). Electrofisher "seconds" did not consistently reflect real time and no cross-calibration was done, so CPUE is only roughly quantitative.

**Flood effects.** Native fishes were scarcely affected by floods, while non-native fishes were removed by high flows, particularly in constrained reaches. Non-natives were concentrated in the western and canyon reaches.

## Key Findings

**Study context and results**
- A single red shiner was collected on 29 September 1990. In October 1990, 27 adults were caught, prompting monitoring for expected establishment.
- Fewer than 10 further shiners were caught, and none after February 1991. Only three red shiners and one fathead minnow were captured in 1991–92 seining.
- Monthly 1991–92 numbers and relative abundances were "unremarkable" compared with annual data from 1965–90, aside from spikedace.

**Native fish dynamics**
- Short-lived species (spikedace, loach minnow, speckled dace, longfin dace) showed strong juvenile pulses and annual recruitment. Spikedace adults rose sharply from late summer–autumn 1991.
- Spikedace were absent from the western reach in 14 of 16 months in 1991–92, but absent in only 4 of 25 years between 1965 and 1990.
- Long-lived species (roundtail chub, desert sucker, Sonora sucker) were dominated by adults. Juvenile desert suckers peaked in spring–early summer in the canyon and western reaches.
- Relative abundance (seining, eastern reach): longfin dace ~35%, western reach ~80–85%; spikedace in the eastern and canyon reaches ranged from ~25% to ~75%.
- Electrofishing over-represented larger species such as desert and Sonora suckers.

**Spawning and recruitment**
- Suckers were thought to spawn after peak winter floods. Short-lived species spawn later, responding to summer–autumn spates. Longfin dace spawn whenever loose sands become available.
- Successful recruitment is most strongly correlated with winter flooding or monsoon spates that redistribute sediment. The triggering cues remain unknown.

**Non-native species**
- Direct predation was considered possible. Yellow bullhead, green sunfish and largemouth bass consumed native fishes roughly in proportion to their abundance (stomach analyses, Appendix III, limited sample sizes).
- Non-natives made up a small, variable share of the seine catch in 1991–92 and were lower than in the preceding years of the long-term record.
- Three distinct invasion pathways were identified: (1) movement from the San Pedro River during high-discharge connection; (2) green sunfish colonizing from livestock tanks after a 1983 flood, subsequently removed by a >85 m³/s flood in 1991; (3) a single-cohort largemouth bass invasion in 1991 via a riparian pond connected through a diversion, displaced by spates in 1992.
- Modal discharge rose over the past 10–15 years, plausibly increasing connectivity and invasion, including the red shiner.

**Management conclusions**
- The study yielded mainly life-history information. It is intended as a baseline for detecting future community change.
- Invasion by non-native fishes "remains a major management concern." Recommendations include eradication of non-natives upstream, prevention of entry from downstream, enforcement against importation, and public education.
