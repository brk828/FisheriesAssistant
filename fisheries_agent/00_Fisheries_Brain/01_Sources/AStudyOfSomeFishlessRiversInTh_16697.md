# A Study of Some Fishless Rivers in the Industrial Midlands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 paper, reprinted from the Journal of the [[Institute of Sewage Purification]], investigates why several rivers in the [[Industrial Midlands]] of England are free of fish. The authors, D. W. M. Herbert, D. H. M. Jordan and R. Lloyd of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] at Stevenage, tested whether the toxicity of complex river water could be predicted from the measured concentrations of its individual poisons. Caged or aquarium-held [[Rainbow Trout]] were used as test animals in field experiments on the [[River Cole]], [[River Tame]] and [[River Stour]] in 1963 and 1964. The central finding is that the toxicity of mixtures can be estimated by summing each poison's fraction of its threshold concentration, and that predictions matched observed trout mortality reasonably well. The paper also suggests that fish can persist where toxicity stays below about 0.2 of the expected threshold, provided dissolved oxygen is adequate.

## Background & Description

The paper begins from the observation that many fishless UK rivers drain densely populated industrial areas. They carry potentially toxic industrial wastes and organic loads from industrial and domestic effluents, which depress [[Dissolved Oxygen]]. Low oxygen can be the sole cause of fish absence, or it can act jointly with poisons, since low oxygen increases toxicity. Metallurgical industries were expected to add [[Zinc]], [[Copper]] and [[Cyanide]] hazards, with [[Phenols]] and [[Ammonia]] also important.

Laboratory work had shown that the toxicity of pairs of poisons (ammonia, zinc, copper and phenols) could be predicted from single-poison toxicities. The same rule appeared to work for several sewage effluents. The 1963 study extended this to rivers.

**Study sites**
- **West Midlands area:** Birmingham, Wolverhampton, Stourbridge and Halesowen, population 2,359,000, straddling the Trent and Severn watershed.
- **[[River Cole]]:** studied in greatest detail. Its load came mainly from [[Yardley Sewage Works]]. The river was fishless below the works, although clean tributaries held roach and sticklebacks.
- **[[River Tame]]:** heavily polluted along its length. Experiments were below [[Minworth Works]].
- **[[River Stour]]:** a single experiment at a site near Stourport, a Severn tributary.

**Methods**
- Rainbow trout (usually 5 to 10 one-year-old fish) were held in stainless-steel cages in the river, or in 35-litre aquaria on the bank when oxygen fell below about 35 per cent of air saturation.
- Times of death were recorded and water samples taken every 2 h.
- Analyses followed Ministry of Housing and Local Government methods and standard procedures, including the Ammonia, Epstein-modified cyanide and 4-amino-antipyrine phenol methods.

**Table 1 (River Cole, 3 to 6 September 1963)** gave mean values: total hardness 159 p.p.m. CaCO3 (range 146 to 171), alkalinity 144 p.p.m. CaCO3, zinc 0.85 p.p.m. (0.61 to 1.18), copper 0.34 p.p.m. (0.23 to 0.51), and chromium 0.19 p.p.m. (below 0.005 to 0.50). Nickel and cyanide were also measured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threshold concentrations used in prediction**
- **Ammonia:** threshold calculated from temperature, oxygen, free CO2, pH and alkalinity, using [[Lloyd Graphs]].
- **Phenols:** a gas-liquor mixture gave 4.4 p.p.m. as C6H5OH, taken as the threshold.
- **Zinc:** 3.9 p.p.m. Zn, a 2-day TLm slightly above the true threshold. Trout acclimatized to hard water were used as the reference.
- **Copper:** laboratory thresholds in hard water ranged from 0.27 to 1.1 p.p.m., with a mean of 0.8 p.p.m. A value of 0.8 p.p.m. was used. Organo-copper complexes in effluent may reduce toxicity.
- **Nickel:** about 25 p.p.m., so not very toxic.
- **Chromium:** considered unlikely to contribute to short-term toxicity.
- **Cyanide:** free HCN could not be reliably estimated in metal-cyanide complexes, so its contribution was excluded.

**Corrections.** Low oxygen raises the toxicity of phenols and metals, and correction factors were applied from [[Lloyd Graphs]]. Temperature between 10 and 20°C speeds death but does not change the threshold.

**Dilution water.** Threshold values depend on water hardness, pH and oxygen, so these were measured at every sampling.

## Key Findings

1. **Prediction rule.** Toxicity is judged by the sum of threshold fractions, Ps/Pe + Qs/Qe + Rs/Re ... = 1. Fish are expected to die when the sum exceeds 1. Mixtures of pairs of poisons (zinc-copper, ammonia-phenol, ammonia-zinc, zinc-phenol) followed this rule in controlled tests.

2. **Effluent validation.** Of 24 sewage effluents examined, 6 were correctly predicted non-toxic. The predicted toxicities of 13 of the remaining 18 were within ±30 per cent of observed values.

3. **River Cole.** Dissolved oxygen was a major limitation. Copper and zinc entered at a fairly steady rate, while phenols occurred sporadically at high concentration. Trout acclimatized to the softer Cole water would be more sensitive, increasing the importance of heavy metals.

4. **River Tame.** Zinc, phenols and ammonia were the most important poisons, with copper contributing comparatively little. Undiluted river water killed half the trout in just over 3 h. Diluted with an equal volume of clean water, median survival rose to 35 h (Experiment 6).

5. **River Stour.** Zinc appeared to be the only important lethal agent (Experiment 8, 20 per cent mortality, with the sum of fractions usually 0.5 to 1.0).

6. **Experimental agreement.**
   - Experiment 2: median survival 42 h, average sum of fractions 0.88 (close to the expected 1.0).
   - Experiment 3: median survival 17 h, average sum 0.87 (shorter than expected).
   - Experiments 1, 2 and 4 gave undiluted median survivals of 35, 42 and 44 h.
   - Experiments 5 and 7 produced no deaths, consistent with a sum below 0.7. In Experiment 7 the predicted toxicity never exceeded 0.55.

7. **Safety thresholds.** In laboratory tests, no more than 5 per cent of trout died within 2 days when the sum of fractions stayed at or below about 0.7. Field evidence from Luxembourg (phenols at about 0.22), Canada (salmon migrating through 0.35 to 0.43 of threshold) and a coarse-fish watercourse (about 0.3 to 0.4) suggests fisheries can persist below about **0.2** of the expected threshold, if oxygen is also adequate.

8. **Time to effect.** Ammonium chloride at twice its threshold killed 50 per cent of trout in about 50 min at 17.5°C. Zinc sulphate at twice its threshold took 8 to 9 h.

9. **Management implications.** Restoring fisheries on the Cole and Tame would require preventing low-oxygen periods and reducing copper, zinc, phenols and ammonia. The method is considered a tool for planning controls, not yet a complete predictor.

**Discussion highlights (1965 meeting)**
- Mr. Spencer questioned whether dilution or aeration of the Tame was practicable. Herbert judged dilution impracticable and suggested reducing discharges instead.
- Dr. Coackley raised the use of invertebrates such as [[Daphnia]] and recreational value of fisheries. Herbert favoured fish as standard test organisms and proposed a "trout standard" for waters with migratory salmonids.
- Mr. Edwards reported that copper 48-h TLm values in hard water were 0.7 to 0.8 p.p.m., and that about half the toxicity in the River Don was due to free cyanide. Rudd threshold for zinc was about 20 p.p.m., roughly five times that for trout. Fluctuating concentrations were tentatively found to act like continuous exposure at equivalent threshold levels.
