## Executive Summary

This document is a 1997 letter from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Arizona Ecological Services Field Office to the Arizona Game and Fish Commission, addressing proposed guidelines for the reintroduction of two endangered native fish species: the [[Gila Topminnow]] and the [[Desert Pupfish]]. While not a primary field-data research paper, the letter contains substantive ecological and regulatory information relevant to the recovery biology of both species, including critical constraints on reintroduction site selection (namely, the intolerance of both species to non-native fish presence), legal mechanisms under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) for managing reintroduction sites on public and private lands, and formal recovery-plan criteria defining a "secure population." The USFWS expresses strong support for the Department's proposed reintroduction guidelines as a mechanism to advance recovery and eventual downlisting of both taxa.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphological data (e.g., meristics, coloration, size) for either species. However, taxonomic identity is confirmed:
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) — a small live-bearing fish native to the Gila River drainage, family Poeciliidae.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*) — a small killifish adapted to extreme desert aquatic conditions, family Cyprinodontidae.

Both are characterized in the text only functionally, as small-bodied native fish highly vulnerable to competition and predation from introduced sport fish species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat requirements**: Both species occupy isolated desert aquatic habitats (springs, small streams, and refugia) within the Sonoran Desert region, historically part of the [[Gila River]] and associated desert drainage systems.
- **Non-native fish exclusion**: A central ecological constraint noted is that reintroduction sites "should not contain any non-native fish." The Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan explicitly states that "the mere presence of non-native fish is a threat to the viability of a pupfish population."
- **Interspecific competition/predation**: The letter states that "Gila topminnow and desert pupfish do not survive in the presence of most non-native sport fish," indicating extreme sensitivity to introduced predatory/competitive fish species (e.g., sport fish such as bass or sunfish).
- **Spatial risk management**: If reintroduction occurs upstream of an existing sport fish population, incidental "take" of the listed species can be managed through ESA Section 7 or Section 10 mechanisms — implying that downstream drift or connectivity poses an ongoing ecological risk even in "protected" reintroduction sites.
- **Land use compatibility**: The species' habitats often overlap with grazing allotments and livestock watering sources, requiring habitat maintenance to balance conservation with human land use (e.g., livestock watering, water source maintenance).

## Key Findings

- Both the **[[Gila Topminnow]]** and **[[Desert Pupfish]]** are identified as "eminent candidates for downlisting" under the ESA, described as biologically recoverable "with minimal impacts to or conflicts with other uses."
- The **Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan** and the (implied) **Gila Topminnow Recovery Plan** define specific criteria for a "secure population" — and critically, the USFWS asserts that **experimental nonessential populations** (designated under ESA Section 10(j)) **do not meet these criteria** and therefore **would not count toward recovery or downlisting goals**.
- Establishing a Section 10(j) experimental nonessential population rule would take the Service "one to two years, and potentially longer" — creating a strong incentive to use alternative regulatory tools.
- **Section 7 consultations** conducted prior to reintroduction can proactively address foreseeable land-use conflicts (livestock watering, water source maintenance, habitat maintenance) on both public and private lands, avoiding the need for lengthy 10(j) rulemaking.
- **Section 10 Habitat Conservation Plans (HCPs)** and **Safe Harbor Agreements** are identified as viable tools for reintroductions on private land, with Safe Harbor Agreements allowing landowners to remove a reintroduced population under specified conditions — reducing regulatory burden as an incentive for landowner participation.
- Multiple additional agencies and private landowners (beyond three cited in the original Department memorandum) have expressed interest in hosting reintroduced, fully-protected populations of topminnow or pupfish.
- The USFWS recommends revising proposed guideline Item 1-b: rather than requiring the *concurrence* of a grazing permittee/lessee for reintroduction, only their *input* should be required, preserving landowner/land-manager discretion over multi-use management while still incorporating stakeholder perspectives.
- Overall, the letter reflects a collaborative regulatory framework wherein the [[Endangered Species Act]] Sections 7 and 10 are positioned as more practical near-term tools than Section 10(j) experimental population designations for advancing the recovery of these two endangered desert fish species.
