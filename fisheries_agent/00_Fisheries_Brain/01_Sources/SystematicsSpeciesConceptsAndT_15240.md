# Systematics, Species Concepts, and the Evolutionarily Significant Unit in Biodiversity and Conservation Biology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #systematics #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[Richard L. Mayden]] and [[Robert M. Wood]] in the American Fisheries Society Symposium 17 (1995), argues that accurate recognition of biodiversity depends on [[Phylogenetic Systematics]] and an appropriate [[Species Concept]]. The authors contend that most species concepts in use (the [[Biological Species Concept]], [[Phylogenetic Species Concept]], and [[Recognition Species Concept]]) are inconsistent with evolutionary theory and exclude natural diversity, and that only the [[Evolutionary Species Concept]] is fully consistent with it. They also critique the [[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]] (ESU) as currently formulated under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Illustrated with North American freshwater fishes, particularly darters and studfishes, the paper concludes that conservation decisions built on non-phylogenetic or inappropriate species concepts risk losing evolutionary legacy.

## Background & Description

The paper defines biodiversity as "the collection of unique biological lineages historically derived through descent," building on the [[Office of Technology Assessment]] (1987) definition emphasising variety and variability among living organisms. Phylogenetic systematics, following [[Willi Hennig]] (1966), is presented as the only demonstrably accurate method for recovering descent. The authors explicitly reject phenetics, numerical taxonomy and evolutionary systematics as classification approaches based on overall similarity.

Key conceptual tools discussed include:

- **Four types of similarity**: convergence (type 1), shared retained primitive traits (type 2), shared derived traits from an immediate common ancestor (type 3, the only informative type for grouping), and gene exchange (type 4).
- **Seven principles of phylogenetic systematics** (phylogenetic, relationship, auxiliary, grouping, character placement, inclusion-exclusion, and classification principles), with [[Outgroup Comparison]] as the most reliable method for polarising characters.
- **Dimensionality**: diversity is revealed by more attributes (morphology, color, genetics, ecology, behavior), each evolving at unpredictable rates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors argue that history matters as much as current environmental conditions in conservation. Attributes inherited from ancestors may be inflexible under rapid environmental change, so predicting the biology of endangered taxa from close relatives is more reliable than assuming traits are adaptively optimised. Phylogenetic information also underpins [[Historical Biogeography]], which the authors contrast with ecological biogeography that cannot address the origins of species associations.

Allopatric speciation is described as the dominant mode, representing roughly 70 to 93% of speciation events in studies cited (Lynch 1989; Grady and LeGrande 1992; Chesser and Zink 1994). This undermines criteria based on sympatric reproductive isolation.

Two worked examples illustrate conservation stakes:

- **Nothonotus darters** (subgenus of *Etheostoma*, Percidae): 19 species are recognised by morphology; genetic fixed alleles recognise 5 taxa, fixed and unique alleles recognise 10, and reproductive behaviour recognises 2 (substrate spawners versus egg clumpers). Habitat alone recognises one. The authors argue no single dimension captures the full diversity.
- **Hypothetical Pacific Northwest salmonid communities**: seven aquatic communities in parallel rivers were assessed with three focal species, one anadromous species with three run times. Phylogenetic analysis falsified an initial hypothesis of independent evolution of run times, and grouped communities into biologically equivalent units (a: A–C; b: D–E; c: F–G).

## Key Findings

- **Biodiversity is defined historically**: lineages derived through descent are the fundamental units; systematics is therefore the appropriate discipline for their recognition.
- **Only the evolutionary species concept accepts all known types of species**: the BSC, PSC and RSC each exclude some diversity (sibling/cryptic species, asexual species, hybrid-origin species, ancestral species or retained primitive recognition systems).
- **Biological Species Concept (BSC)**: "limited usage" recommended. Criticisms include lack of a lineage perspective, nondimensionality, operational inconsistency, exclusion of asexual organisms, reliance on group selection, relational nature, teleology, and typological tendencies.
- **Phylogenetic Species Concepts (PSC)**: "cautious use" recommended. They are operational and lineage-based, but can exclude ancestral species, over- or under-estimate diversity, and rely on relational comparisons.
- **Recognition Species Concept (RSC)**: "cautious reliance" recommended. It is non-relational and operational via the specific mate-recognition system, but may underestimate diversity where recognition systems are retained and excludes asexual species.
- **Evolutionary Species Concept (ESC)**: recommended as the conceptual basis for units of biodiversity. Defined as "an entity composed of organisms which maintains its identity from other such entities through time and over space, and which has its own independent evolutionary fate and historical tendencies." Its only perceived drawback is limited operationality.
- **ESU critique**: the ESU (Waples 1991) is defined as a population that is "substantially reproductively isolated" and "an important component in the evolutionary legacy of the species." The authors argue it is confusable with species definitions, relies heavily on genetic data, excludes asexual and hybrid species, and should be grounded in the ESC if it is to protect evolutionary units reliably.
- **Taxonomic examples of misclassification**: the spotted darter (*E. maculatum*) was reclassified after [[Etnier and Williams]] (1989) described the boulder darter, and [[Wood]] (1993) using electrophoresis; the southern studfish (*Fundulus stellifer*) split into the stippled studfish *F. bifax* (Cashner et al. 1988); and golden trout (*Oncorhynchus aguabonita*) is most closely related to the redband trout rather than rainbow trout.
- **Cryptic diversity is widespread**: 15 of the 50 North American freshwater fish families contain cryptic species with minimal morphological divergence; these families account for 871 of 1,061 known species (82% of the fauna) (Burr and Mayden 1992).
- **Conservation recommendation**: integrate phylogenetic systematics, the ESC and historical biogeography into biodiversity inventories, prioritisation, and recovery planning; use closest relatives' life histories to predict needs of imperilled taxa.
