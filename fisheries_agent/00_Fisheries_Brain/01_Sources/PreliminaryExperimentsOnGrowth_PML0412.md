# Preliminary Experiments on the Culture of Tilapia aurea in Seawater Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This short experimental paper by J. Chervinski and A. Yashouv of the [[Fish Culture Research Station]] (Dor, Israel) reports the first trial of [[Tilapia Aurea]] growth in seawater ponds. The authors asked whether this freshwater-origin cichlid could be cultured in full-strength seawater, either as a monoculture or alongside marine fishes such as [[Mugil Cephalus]]. The trial was run in ponds fed by water pumped from the sea, with salinity raised over the season to about 36.6% and then roughly 42%. Growth was satisfactory, and the fish showed a mid-season reduction in gonadosomatic index (GSI) but no evident spawning or nest building. The authors describe the results as preliminary and recommend that trials continue.

## Background & Description

- **Tilapia aurea** (Steindachner), a [[Cichlidae]] species, is described as a euryhaline fish that tolerates and grows in both freshwater and brackish or seawater. Earlier work noted it could be acclimated from fresh water to brackish and seawater conditions.
- Related earlier studies cited include work on [[Tilapia Galilaea]] (Artedi) in brackish water and on [[Tilapia Mossambica]], which was reported to reproduce in brackish water up to about 30% salinity.
- The authors state that some tilapia species are cultured in ponds in tropical and semi-tropical countries, and that certain species can grow and reproduce in brackish or marine environments.
- Laboratory tolerance tests found that, after gradual acclimation, T. aurea could withstand salinities up to about 1.5 times that of seawater (value partly illegible in the scan).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and set-up**
- Experimental ponds were located near the [[Mediterranean Sea]] shore at Dor, Israel. Each pond was filled with water pumped directly from the sea through a special pipe, with evaporation losses compensated by seawater inflow.
- Salinity was monitored from mid-May. It rose to 36.57% and then increased again in early October, to approximately 42% (scan partly illegible).

**Stocking and feeding**
- Pond No. 4 was stocked on 14 May with T. aurea together with marine species, including Mugil cephalus.
- Fish were fed six days a week with pellets containing 25% protein.
- Poaching was noted as a source of loss in some ponds.

**Growth and reproduction**
- Male weights in July ranged up to about 27 g, and female weights in October reached roughly 165–281 g (scan partly illegible).
- Growth rates reported in the discussion were about 1.0–1.3 g per day in the ponds, although the exact figures are partly illegible.
- Females' GSI dropped from about 4.8 on 11 July to about 1.5 in the following period. The authors suggest this may reflect reabsorption of eggs.
- No spawning activity or nest building was observed in the seawater ponds.

## Key Findings

1. **Seawater tolerance:** [[Tilapia Aurea]] tolerated salinities up to about 1.5 times that of seawater after gradual acclimation.
2. **Growth in seawater ponds:** Growth was "satisfactory" and the authors describe it as comparable to, or possibly better than, that of [[Tilapia Mossambica]] in similar conditions.
3. **Monoculture and polyculture:** T. aurea was tested both alone (monoculture) and with [[Mugil Cephalus]] and other marine species (polyculture).
4. **Reproduction:** No spawning or nest building was observed in the seawater ponds, so the authors judged there was no hazard of over-population.
5. **Sex-related growth:** Growth rates were reported separately for males and females, and monoculture results were compared with mixed-sex and mullet-polyculture results.
6. **Preliminary status:** The authors emphasise that the results are preliminary and recommend that trials of T. aurea and related species continue in seawater ponds.
