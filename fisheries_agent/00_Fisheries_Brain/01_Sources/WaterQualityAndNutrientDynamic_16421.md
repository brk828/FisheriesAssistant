# Water Quality and Nutrient Dynamics of Arizona Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This completion report from the [[Office Of Water Research And Technology]] (OWRT Project A-106-ARIZ, November 1980 to October 1981, Arizona State University) characterizes nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) concentrations in Arizona streams and tests whether N or P limits primary production. A survey of 196 sites on 157 streams found that N concentrations in floodwater and spring-source sites are significantly higher than at summer low-flow sites, and that nitrate-N declines downstream of springs faster than P, leaving N:P below 15 and suggesting potential N limitation. Diel budgets for a 100-m reach of [[Sycamore Creek]] during post-flood recovery support autotrophic uptake as the main mechanism of N loss.

## Background & Description

- **Principal investigator:** S.G. Fisher; graduate student N.B. Grimm (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, Tempe).
- **Funding:** United States Department of the Interior, Office of Water Research and Technology, under the Water Resources Research Act of 1964; the report states that its contents do not necessarily reflect the views of the agency.
- **Goals:** (1) characterize ambient N and P in Arizona waters; (2) assess potential nutrient limitation using N:P ratios, following [[Liebig's Law Of The Minimum]] and marine precedent such as [[Redfield Ratio]]; (3) describe nutrient uptake mechanisms and rates.
- **Study area:** 196 sites on 157 streams in 13 drainage basins, classified by stream type (summer low flow n=92; winter high flow n=14; floods n=34; springs and spring-sources n=56) and by vegetation formation (desertscrub n=87, grassland n=40, chaparral n=23, woodland n=21, forest n=25).
- **Sycamore Creek:** a 505 km² intermittent low-desert stream in Maricopa County (study-site elevation about 2000 ft; discharge 0.02 m/s in August-September 1981; temperatures 23-36 °C; coarse sand substrate).

### Methods (summary)

- Samples were filtered (GF/F), then analyzed for nitrate-N (cadmium-copper reduction), ammonium-N (phenol-hypochlorite), total dissolved N (UV oxidation), soluble reactive phosphorus (SRP) and total dissolved P (TDP).
- Major ions, conductivity, pH, alkalinity and silica were measured on subsets of samples.
- Diel sampling at 4-h intervals at upstream and downstream ends of a 100-m reach, with metabolism measured in circulating chambers (P_n, R) and algal N and P measured by Kjeldahl digestion.
- Isolated-community uptake was measured in chambers over 20 min to 1 h, with NaNO₃ and K₂HPO₄ enrichment to about 1 mg/L NO₃-N and SRP.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Waters were mostly slightly alkaline (pH about 6.5-8.5), with conductivities of 100-1000 µS/cm in northern Arizona and 300-500 µS/cm most commonly in the Salt River basin. Bicarbonate dominated anions in most basins; sodium and calcium dominated cations where measured. High conductivity occurred at some springs (Bylas springs, sites 165-176) and at the Salt River mainstem (site 111). Sites 182-186 in high-elevation Gila headwaters were below 100 µS/cm.
- **Nutrient sources:** Spring sources and floodwaters carried high N. Irrigation return flow was suspected at Willow and Trout Creeks (sites 15 and 16).
- **Organic N:** Total dissolved N was often more than twice nitrate-N; dissolved organic N was frequently the largest TDN fraction in northern and Salt River basin sites.
- **Land-cover gradient:** Mean nitrate-N was lower at forest and woodland sites, but ANOVA by vegetation type was not significant, likely because stream type overshadowed vegetation effects.
- **Disturbance:** Flash floods scoured nearly 100% of algal standing crop and reduced invertebrate standing crop by more than 90%. Preflood standing crops returned within a few weeks.
- **Dominant algae:** Epilithic diatoms, later [[Cladophora Glomerata]] and blue-green algae (principally Anabaena). Mean algal N:P at Sycamore Creek was 15.5 ± 2.2 (95% CL), whereas Cladophora ranged from 8.65 to 17.5.

## Key Findings

**Survey (157 streams, 196 sites)**
- Nitrate-N ranged from 0 to 5.64 mg/L overall (mean 0.252 mg/L). SRP averaged 0.041 mg/L (range 0-0.407 mg/L).
- Nitrate-N was significantly higher at springs (mean 0.491 mg/L) and floods (mean 0.414 mg/L) than at summer low-flow sites (mean 0.068 mg/L) (ANOVA F = 8.96, p < 0.0001).
- SRP and TDP did not differ significantly among stream types (SRP F = 0.59, p = 0.620; TDP F = 0.45, p = 0.717).
- Most summer low-flow sites, and especially the Salt River and Little Colorado basins, had N:P below 15, suggesting potential N limitation. Potential P limitation was suggested for several southern Arizona springs.

**Spring-source effects**
- Downstream of paired spring sources, nitrate-N declined at 19 of 20 streams (one reach, Sonoita Creek, had zero source nitrate). Reductions reached 100%. SRP declined in only 12 streams, with a maximum reduction of 79%.
- N:P fell at all but two streams, and several sites shifted from indicating potential P limitation to potential N limitation.

**Sycamore Creek diel budgets (post-flood recovery)**
- Two flash floods (27 July and 8 August 1981) preceded three diel studies on post-flood days 6-7 (Diel I), 10-11 (Diel II) and 26-27 (Diel III).
- Chlorophyll a rose from 82.47 to 295.0 mg/m²; AFDM rose from 79.07 to 144.7 g/m². Gross production and respiration peaked at 12.25 and 7.10 g O₂/m²/d. The surface sediment community was net autotrophic on all dates.
- Dissolved nutrients dominated inputs. N retention increased with succession, while particulate N and P export reached 106% of inputs on Diel III, attributed mainly to Cladophora drift.
- Predicted N uptake from metabolism (C:N 9.9-12.5) matched observed inorganic N uptake on Diel III but was lower on Diels I and II. Observed P uptake did not consistently match predictions.
- Areal nitrate uptake was 3.1, 5.9 and 12.6 mmol N/m²/d, at the lower end of previously reported rates (8.6-48 mmol N/m²/d).

**Isolated algal communities**
- Ambient nitrate uptake ranged from 2.2 to 43.6 µg N/mg chl a/h; replicate coefficients of variation reached 63%.
- Whole-stream hourly uptake fell within the same order of magnitude as isolated-community rates, supporting the autotrophic uptake hypothesis.
- N enrichment increased uptake up to 10-fold. Enrichment bioassays for nutrient limitation were inconclusive, though respiration responded most clearly, indicating a metabolic response to enrichment.

**Conclusions**
1. Most Arizona streams are probably N-limited during summer low flow, extending earlier findings to 157 streams.
2. Spring sources and floodwaters have higher N and N:P than summer low-flow streams.
3. SRP and TDP did not differ among stream types.
4. Vegetation type showed no significant effect on nutrients.
5. Nitrate declines downstream of spring sources, with N:P reduced to indicate potential N limitation.
6. Autotrophic uptake during regrowth after floods can account for observed N uptake.
7. Net export of particulate N and P occurred on days 26-27 after flooding.
8. Ecosystem N retention is within the range of algal uptake capacity.
9. N enrichment increased uptake greatly; conclusive N or P limitation was not demonstrated.
