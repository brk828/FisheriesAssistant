#Copepoda #Calanoida #Cyclopoida #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1953 survey by Gerald A. Cole documents the calanoid and cyclopoid copepod fauna of the Louisville, Kentucky region, based on examination of more than 100 samples collected from fall 1949 to fall 1952 across 61 stations (56 in Jefferson County, plus sites in Oldham, Daviess, and Bullitt Counties). The study identifies four calanoid species (genera [[Diaptomus]] and [[Osphranticum]]) and ten cyclopoid species (genera including [[Cyclops]], [[Eucyclops]], [[Macrocyclops]], [[Mesocyclops]], [[Microcyclops]], [[Orthocyclops]], and [[Paracyclops]]). The paper is a faunistic/taxonomic contribution addressing a regional gap in North American copepod records, and provides notes on habitat preference, seasonality, and relative abundance for each species. Sampling employed dip nets in shallow waters and plankton tow-nets in deeper waters, both constructed of No. 20 silk bolting cloth.

## Background & Description

The paper does not provide detailed morphological descriptions (it references established taxonomic authorities for identification — e.g., Herrick, Forbes, Fischer, Jurine, Koch, Lilljeborg), but several taxonomically diagnostic notes are given:

- **[[Cyclops Bicuspidatus Thomasi]]**: Subspecies-level identification within *Cyclops bicuspidatus*, notable for an encystment life-history strategy in mud during late spring.
- **[[Eucyclops Prasinus]]**: Described as a "small species," distinguishing it from congeners in mixed plankton samples.
- Ovigerous (egg-bearing) females were specifically noted for **[[Orthocyclops Modestus]]** and **[[Paracyclops Fimbriatus]]**, both represented by single specimens.
- Verification of **[[Diaptomus Stagnalis]]** identification was performed by Gabriel Comita (University of Washington), indicating morphological identification challenges within the genus [[Diaptomus]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types sampled:** flooded fields, wheel ruts, drainage ditches, streams, farm ponds, water-filled limestone quarries, artificial lakes, and vernal (temporary) pools. Jefferson County was noted as largely devoid of natural permanent waters outside of creeks and rivers.

**Species-specific niche notes:**
- **[[Diaptomus Pallidus]]**: The most common calanoid in the region, found at 21 stations; typical of quarries, permanent ponds, and lakes. Also the dominant *Diaptomus* species in the Cincinnati area (cf. [[Turner 1892]], [[Ward 1940]]).
- **[[Diaptomus Sanguineus]]**: Found at 5 stations in spring, in temporary pools and small permanent ponds.
- **[[Diaptomus Stagnalis]]**: A vernal (spring-only) species, collected in a single April sample from a pond in Oldham County; co-occurred with *D. sanguineus*.
- **[[Osphranticum Labronectum]]**: Rare, found at 3 stations (winter/spring) — two temporary pools, one permanent lake; contrasts with Reelfoot Lake records ([[Hoff 1944]]) showing summer persistence.
- **[[Cyclops Bicuspidatus Thomasi]]**: A winter/early-spring dominant at 7 stations, with populations crashing by late spring, likely due to mud encystment of new generations (a phenomenon first described by [[Birge And Juday 1908]]). Found mainly in small ponds and one deep limestone quarry.
- **[[Cyclops Vernalis]]** and **[[Eucyclops Agilis]]**: The two most abundant and ecologically generalist copepods in the region — found in 32 and 31 stations respectively (11 shared), across all habitat types and seasons. Their short life cycles ([[Ewers 1930]]) favor colonization of ephemeral habitats (wheel ruts, ditches).
- **[[Eucyclops Agilis]]**: Despite broad distribution, shows strong littoral preference in larger water bodies (e.g., restricted to shallow margins in Tom Wallace Lake, while absent from open-water plankton tows).
- **[[Eucyclops Prasinus]]**: A planktonic member found at 6 stations year-round, with fluctuating abundance; co-occurs with *D. pallidus* in open lake plankton.
- **[[Macrocyclops Albidus]]**: Third most common cyclopoid, found in 14 ponds/lakes across all seasons; occasional planktonic occurrence in Tom Wallace Lake.
- **[[Macrocyclops Fuscus]]**: Rare — a single individual from a shallow Louisville pond in April.
- **[[Mesocyclops Edax]]**: Found in 3 ponds, 1 quarry hole, and 1 stream.
- **[[Microcyclops Varicans Rubellus]]**: Found in 6 small ponds across winter, spring, and summer.
- **[[Orthocyclops Modestus]]**: Single record (ovigerous female) from a weedy Louisville pond in January.
- **[[Paracyclops Fimbriatus]]**: Single record (ovigerous female) from a shallow drainage ditch in January.

**Comparative regional fauna:** The author flags three species collected in the nearby Cincinnati region by [[Ward 1940]] — *Ectocyclops phaleratus*, *Cyclops ater*, and *Diaptomus siciloides* — as likely present but undetected in the Louisville area, warranting future targeted collecting.

## Key Findings

- A total of **4 calanoid** and **10 cyclopoid** copepod species/subspecies were identified from **61 Kentucky stations**, primarily in Jefferson County.
- **[[Diaptomus Pallidus]]**, **[[Cyclops Vernalis]]**, **[[Eucyclops Agilis]]**, and **[[Macrocyclops Albidus]]** were identified as the most common and widespread representatives of the two orders in the region.
- **[[Cyclops Vernalis]]** and **[[Eucyclops Agilis]]** dominate ephemeral/temporary habitats due to short life cycles, while other species are more restricted to permanent water bodies.
- Seasonal encystment behavior in **[[Cyclops Bicuspidatus Thomasi]]** parallels earlier findings by [[Birge And Juday 1908]], supporting a general model of resting-stage survival strategies in temperate cyclopoid populations.
- The genus **[[Diaptomus]]** in the Ohio Valley is dominated locally by *D. pallidus* rather than *D. siciloides*, contradicting the earlier assertion by [[Marsh 1918]] that *D. siciloides* was the Ohio Valley's most likely *Diaptomus* species — a conclusion corroborated by both this study and prior work by [[Turner 1892]] and [[Ward 1940]].
- Harpacticoid copepods (notably genus *Canthocamptus*) were commonly observed in winter/spring samples but were excluded from species-level identification in this study, representing a gap for future taxonomic work.
- The study underscores the paucity of prior copepod records from Kentucky and calls for continued regional sampling, especially during under-sampled midsummer periods.
