#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary
This note summarizes findings from Modde (1996), documenting the occurrence of juvenile and adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in Old Charley Wash, a 60-ha managed floodplain wetland on the Ouray National Wildlife Refuge, adjacent to the Green River in Uintah County, Utah. The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large, endangered, endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Drainage]], listed as federally endangered in 1991. While adult populations spawn successfully in the middle Green River, recruitment has historically been poor, and little was known about juvenile habitat requirements prior to this study. The capture of 28 juveniles during a controlled wetland drainage event in fall 1995 provides direct evidence supporting the hypothesis that seasonally inundated floodplain wetlands serve as important nursery habitat for this species.

## Background & Description
- **Juvenile Razorback Sucker** (n=28, collected fall 1995):
  - Mean total length (TL): 94 mm (range 74–125 mm)
  - Mean weight: 9.5 g (range 3–18 g)
  - Voucher specimens deposited: catalog number LFL 24874, Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University
- **Adult Razorback Sucker** (n=8):
  - Total length range: 461–525 mm
  - Weight range: 1034–1650 g
  - 6 captured prior to draining, 2 captured during draining
- Historical comparative juvenile size records:
  - Taba et al. (1965): 8 juveniles, 90–115 mm TL, Colorado River backwater (Moab to Dead Horse Point, Utah, 1962–1964)
  - Gutermuth et al. (1994): 2 juveniles (37 mm, 39 mm) from lower Green River backwater (1991); 2 additional juveniles (59 mm, 29 mm) from Ouray NWR backwater (1993)
- Other co-occurring species captured during draining, in descending order of contribution by weight: *Cyprinus carpio*, *Pimephales promelas*, *Lepomis cyanellus*, *Ictalurus punctatus*, *Ameiurus melas*, *Cyprinella lutrensis*, *Pomoxis nigromaculatus*, *Xyrauchen texanus*, *Esox lucius*, *Gila atraria*, *Catostomus latipinnis*, *Catostomus commersoni*, *[[Ptychocheilus lucius]]* (7 individuals, 175–207 mm TL, 33–62 g), *Gila robusta*, *Culaea inconstans*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type**: [[Old Charley Wash]], a historical Type IV wetland (per Cowardin et al. 1979 classification), dominated by smartweed (*Polygonum* sp.) and sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*).
- **Hydrology**: Natural levees reinforced with dikes to retain water through summer/fall. Inlet flow control at river discharge <481 m³/s; inundation occurs at flows >481 m³/s. In 1995, the Green River peaked at ~595 m³/s, inundating the wash from 23 May to 1 July. Maximum wetland depth exceeded 2 m. Following peak runoff, the wetland became hydrologically isolated from the river (no additional water added).
- **Sampling methodology**: Fyke and trammel nets, minnow and light traps, and seines used weekly (23 May–1 July) then biweekly (2 July–31 August). Wetland drained 25 September–12 October, with daily/every-other-day sampling at the outlet structure.
- **Total biomass removed during draining**: 10.1 metric tons of fish across all species.
- **Spawning ecology context**: [[Razorback Sucker]] spawn on the ascending limb of the Green River hydrograph (Tyus and Karp 1990), which facilitates larval drift and dispersal into floodplain/wetland habitats during peak runoff — a strategy thought to maximize access to nursery habitat.
- **Recruitment context**: Despite successful spawning in the middle Green River, Lanigan and Tyus (1989) reported little to no recruitment in the riverine population, underscoring the potential importance of off-channel wetland habitats like Old Charley Wash for juvenile survival.

## Key Findings
- This study represents one of the largest documented captures of juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (28 individuals) from a floodplain wetland, substantially expanding known juvenile occurrence records compared to prior studies (Taba et al. 1965; Gutermuth et al. 1994).
- Co-occurrence of both juvenile and adult razorback suckers in the same managed wetland suggests the habitat may support multiple life stages.
- It remains unknown whether the juveniles collected originated from riverine spawning sites (via larval drift) or were spawned directly within Old Charley Wash itself.
- Findings support the hypothesis (Tyus and Karp 1990; Modde et al. 1996) that seasonally connected floodplain wetlands function as important nursery areas for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]], with implications for [[Colorado River Basin]] endangered fish recovery programs.
- Study conducted under the Recovery Implementation Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper [[Colorado River Basin]].
