# Further Observations on the Feeding Habits of the Montana Grayling and the Bluegill in Ford Lake, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1939–1940 paper by Justin W. Leonard, of the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]], follows up an earlier study of [[Montana Grayling]] introduced into Ford Lake, a small landlocked lake in northern Michigan. Stomach analyses of five collections (April, May, July and October 1938, and March 1939) describe the grayling's diet, which was dominated by predaceous aquatic insects, notably Odonata and Coleoptera. An unauthorized stocking of [[Bluegill]] after a [[Rotenone Treatment]] had cleared the lake changed the study. Once grayling reached about 246.5 mm standard length, they began eating young bluegills. The author concludes that bluegills are direct food competitors of the grayling, that the grayling population is already declining, and that the non-reproducing grayling may be unable to sustain itself.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Montana grayling (*Thymallus montanus*), 5,000 of which were introduced as 4-inch fingerlings. The fish were hatched on June 24, 1936, and were in their third year of life in 1939.
- **Comparison species:** Bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*), a prolific [[Centrarchidae]] member whose stocking was unplanned ("owing to a misunderstanding," summer 1937).
- **Study site:** Ford Lake, a small landlocked lake in northern Michigan. The lake was treated with rotenone before the grayling were added so that no competing or predatory fish would be present.
- **Methods:** Grayling were collected on artificial flies, by graded gill net, and by angling through the ice with burrowing mayfly nymphs as bait. Stomach contents were expressed as percentages of total food volume, with finely comminuted debris excluded from calculations. Life stages were recorded as L (larva), P (pupa), N (nymph) and A (adult). A bluegill collection of over 100 fish was also made on May 24, 1939, using artificial flies.
- **Context:** The paper is a contribution from the [[Michigan Institute for Fisheries Research]] and was published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 69, 1939).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Grayling diet:** Predaceous aquatic insects, especially Odonata (dragonfly and damselfly nymphs) and dytiscid and gyrinid Coleoptera (diving and whirligig beetles), formed the main food groups. Diptera (midges, dance flies) were the largest group in some collections.
- **Seasonal and environmental shifts:** Diptera peaked during the chironomid emergence in April (30.6% by volume). Empidids dominated the May diet. Plankton (mainly *Bosmina*) reached 4.3% in July. Terrestrial insects rose in October as surface waters cooled, which suggests grayling fed at the surface when temperatures allowed. In July, surface water warmed to 76° F, which may have kept grayling out of the upper layer.
- **Bluegill diet by size:** Three size groups were recognized, probably representing only two ages. The smallest fish ate mainly plankton, small mayfly nymphs and chironomids. Middle-sized fish ate plankton, chironomids and aphids, with many Odonata. The largest fish ate mostly anisopteran nymphs.
- **Overlap:** Grayling and bluegills in Ford Lake fed largely on the same invertebrate groups.
- **Comparative literature:** Grayling are not known to feed on fish in the Michigan population. Records of fish consumption exist for the European grayling (minnows, fry, *Cottus poecilopus*), the Baikal white grayling (fish and amphipods), and the Montana grayling (small trout fry near a hatchery outlet, described as unnatural).
- **Competition from introduced species:** Some workers consider introduced trout (brown, rainbow and eastern brook) to be the main cause of the Michigan grayling extinction and of the recent decline of Montana grayling in some native waters.

## Key Findings

- **Shift to fish predation:** Grayling began to prey on bluegill fingerlings at about 246.5 mm standard length (about 16 months old). Fish made up 3.5% of the grayling diet in October 1938 and 34.0% in March 1939.
- **Competition:** All bluegill size classes except fingerlings are direct food competitors of the grayling, since the grayling and bluegills share the same invertebrate prey.
- **Population decline:** Observations point to a marked drop in the grayling population, which the author attributes to bluegill competition. Final figures await removal of the population.
- **Likely outcome:** The non-reproducing grayling population is expected to struggle to maintain growth and viability against the prolific bluegill. The author states that the completion of the experiment should show whether a non-reproducing grayling population can survive alongside such a competitive species.
- **Methodological note:** The bluegill stocking was unplanned, which complicated the experiment, but the author treated the resulting observations as informative.
