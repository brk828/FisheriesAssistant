# Habitat Use and Streamflow Needs of Rare and Endangered Fishes in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #flow-ecology #dam-impacts

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive report evaluates habitat use and streamflow requirements for four federally listed or proposed endangered fishes in the [[Green River]] basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The study synthesizes data from multiple investigations conducted under the [[Flaming Gorge Studies Program]] (1986-1991) to inform [[Bureau of Reclamation]] dam operations and provide a biological opinion under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Operations of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] since 1962 have drastically altered the natural flow regime—eliminating spring peaks, increasing winter baseflows, and causing extreme daily fluctuations—resulting in habitat degradation and extirpation of native fishes upstream of [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The [[Yampa River]], the last major unregulated tributary, maintains critical spring runoff patterns that support downstream endangered fish populations. The report documents life history requirements across all seasons and life stages, demonstrating that natural flow variability (high spring flows followed by gradual summer decline to low autumn-winter baseflows) is essential for migration, spawning, larval drift, nursery habitat formation, and overwinter survival. Proliferation of introduced predatory and competitor fishes (channel catfish, northern pike, red shiner, green sunfish) under regulated conditions poses additional threats. Management recommendations include restoring spring peak flows (>367.9 m³/s at Ouray), providing warmer water releases, gradually declining summer flows to ~51 m³/s, stable low autumn-winter flows, bottomland restoration, and control of non-native species.

## Background & Description

The [[Green River]] is the largest sub-basin of the upper [[Colorado River]] system, draining approximately 116,000 km² and flowing ~800 km from the [[Wind River Range]] in Wyoming to its confluence with the Colorado River in [[Canyonlands National Park]]. The study area extends 552 km from the Colorado River confluence (km 0) to the [[Yampa River]] confluence in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (DNM). Historically, the Green River exhibited a natural hydrograph with spring peak flows averaging 401.86 m³/s (April-July, 1895-1962 at Green River, Utah) driven by snowmelt runoff, followed by low autumn-winter baseflows (~64.81 m³/s September-February). Construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962 and [[Fontenelle Dam]] fundamentally altered this regime: spring peaks were eliminated or greatly reduced, winter baseflows increased substantially (from ~79 to 107 m³/s), and extreme daily fluctuations (peaking power operations) became routine. The [[Yampa River]], with average annual discharge of ~1.5 million acre-feet, remains unregulated and provides the only significant spring runoff signal to the downstream Green River system.

The native fish fauna includes 14 indigenous species: cyprinids (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub, roundtail chub, speckled dace), catostomids (razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, mountain sucker), salmonids (Colorado River cutthroat trout, Rocky Mountain whitefish), and sculpins. Four species are federally endangered or proposed: Colorado squawfish (endangered 1967), humpback chub (endangered 1967), bonytail chub (endangered 1980), and razorback sucker (proposed endangered, listed 1991). These fishes have been extirpated from the Green River between Flaming Gorge Dam and the Yampa confluence. Approximately 10 non-native species are now common, including [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).

The report consolidates findings from six major study initiatives: (1) summer-autumn requirements of age-0 Colorado squawfish; (2) winter habitat and flows for adults and young; (3) spring-early summer flow requirements for spawning and migration; (4) fish community interactions with introduced species; (5) trophic dynamics in backwater habitats; and (6) remote sensing mapping of backwater availability. Methods included radiotelemetry (tracking 153 Colorado squawfish, 17 razorback suckers, 17 adults for winter studies), mark-recapture (922 young Colorado squawfish fin-clipped, 230 recaptured; 410 razorback suckers captured, 68 recaptured), seine and electrofishing surveys (11,379 age-0 Colorado squawfish collected 1979-1988), drift net sampling (601 larvae), diet analysis (1,024 Colorado squawfish stomachs), behavioral experiments, aerial photography/videography of backwater habitats, and age determination via otoliths and vertebral centra.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The endangered Colorado River fishes evolved under a regime of extreme seasonal and annual flow variability characteristic of snowmelt-dominated arid-land rivers. This "natural flow regime" featured predictable high spring flows (March-July) that scoured substrates, inundated floodplains, mobilized nutrients and sediments, and created ephemeral shoreline habitats, followed by low stable autumn-winter flows. Native fishes exhibit life history adaptations to this variability: potamodromous spawning migrations timed to flow cues, broadcast spawning on cobble substrates during declining flows, passive larval drift to downstream nursery areas, use of warm backwater nurseries by young, and cold tolerance during low-flow winters.

[[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations have fundamentally disrupted this regime. The dam blocks 128 km of former habitat, traps sediments (altering downstream channel morphology), releases cold hypolimnetic water (reducing temperatures 5-10°C), eliminates or reduces spring peaks (preventing floodplain inundation), increases winter flows (flooding preferred low-velocity habitats), and causes extreme daily fluctuations (0.5-2 m stage changes from peaking power). These changes have cascading ecological effects: (1) loss of spring flooding reduces formation of productive backwater nurseries and prevents access to bottomland spawning/rearing areas; (2) cold water temperatures delay spawning, reduce egg hatching success, and may limit food production; (3) altered sediment regime allows vegetation encroachment (especially [[Saltcedar]] *Tamarix pentandra*) into the flood zone, stabilizing banks and reducing habitat diversity; (4) stable, moderated flows favor establishment of introduced mesic-adapted fishes over native species adapted to flow extremes; (5) daily fluctuations strand fish, reduce backwater productivity, and increase stress-induced movements.

The [[Yampa River]] provides critical mitigation by maintaining a spring hydrograph peak that propagates downstream in the Green River. At the Yampa-Green confluence (Echo Park), the combined spring peak can reach 254.7 m³/s (Yampa contribution), partially restoring natural flow patterns for ~400 km downstream. This Yampa influence is considered essential for maintaining extant populations of all four endangered species below DNM. The Green River basin now supports the largest known riverine populations of Colorado squawfish (estimated several hundred adults) and razorback sucker (948 fish in upper Green River, 95% CI: 758-1,138), though razorback sucker recruitment has been absent since the 1960s (all captured fish are old, 20-40+ years based on otolith aging).

Introduced species pose severe threats through predation, competition, and hybridization. Channel catfish are abundant (14% of electrofishing catch, 70% of angling catch in canyon reaches), widely distributed, and consume native fishes when large (>420 mm TL). Northern pike, illegally stocked in the Yampa River in 1977, rapidly invaded the Green River (recaptures showed 78-110 km downstream movement in one year) and prey on small fishes. Laboratory and field studies document predation by channel catfish, green sunfish, and black bullhead on young Colorado squawfish. Behavioral experiments show that green sunfish, red shiner, and fathead minnow are more aggressive than young Colorado squawfish in shared backwater habitats, potentially affecting growth and survival through interference competition. Razorback sucker hybridizes with native flannelmouth and bluehead suckers on spawning grounds, and introduced common carp and catfish are implicated in predation on razorback sucker larvae and juveniles.

## Key Findings

**Colorado Squawfish Life History and Habitat Requirements:**

- **Adult Distribution and Movements:** Adults widely distributed in Yampa and Green rivers, most prevalent in low-gradient reaches near Ouray and Labyrinth Canyon. Winter habitat use includes slow runs (highest use), slackwaters, eddies, and backwaters; fish remain in selected reaches and are active all winter. Radiotracking of 153 fish (1980-1988) revealed 63% are highly mobile: 41% migrated to known spawning sites, 11% to suspected sites, 11% to other locations; 18% non-migratory (linked to non-annual spawning or immaturity). Spring-summer adults use seasonally inundated shorelines, backwaters, and flooded bottomlands (e.g., Old Charley Wash) for feeding and physiological conditioning. Mid-late summer adults occupy eddies, pools, runs, and shoreline backwaters over sand-silt substrates, often using cover (boulders, vegetation).

- **Migration:** Potamodromous spawning migrations average 140.7 km (range 32-372.8 km) in both upstream (23.8%) and downstream (73%) directions. Migration initiation occurs ~28 days after peak spring flow (range 2-50 days, N=7 years, 29 fish in Green River; range 2-42 days, N=4 years, 24 fish in Yampa River), associated with water temperatures ≥9°C (average 14°C). Earlier peak flows correlate with longer intervals before migration (r=-1.0, P<0.01 Yampa; r=-0.8, P<0.03 Green). Migration timing: May 23 to July 22 (average June 21 in Green River, June 15 in Yampa River). Homing behavior documented: fish return to same spawning sites in multiple years, suggesting stock differentiation and possible olfactory orientation to chemical cues (tributary inputs, seeps, reproductive by-products).

- **Spawning:** Two spawning areas identified: [[Yampa Canyon]] (lower Yampa River) and [[Gray Canyon]] (Green River). Spawning occurs during declining flows following spring peak at mean water temperatures of 23°C (range 22-25°C, N=13 ripe females). Spawning period lasts ~26 days (range 17-33 days), occurring earlier in low-water years (1981, 1987, 1988) and later in high-flow years (1983, 1984). Spawning begins ~26 days after migration initiation. Peak discharge preceding spawn and minimum temperatures during spawn highly correlated with spawning period (r=0.84, r=0.88, P<0.05). Ripe fish (N=194 males, mean TL 555 mm; N=14 females, mean TL 654 mm) concentrated in reaches with deep pools, eddies, and submerged cobble-boulder bars. Radiotracked fish moved from pools/eddies to bars (presumed spawning) then returned. Eggs require clean cobble surfaces for adhesion; spring scouring and declining flows prevent siltation. Not all adults spawn annually; male and female maturation may require different environmental stimuli.

- **Larvae and Drift:** Larvae emerge as sac-fry from cobble bars and drift downstream with declining flows. Drift captured in nets (N=601 larvae) for 2-6 weeks in June-July. Transport time from Yampa spawning grounds (km 26.4-29.1) to Yampa mouth: ~6 days. Laboratory experiments show drift rate inversely related to fish size; larval fish exhibit diel pattern of downstream movement; relative swimming ability correlates with body length. Drift is passive dispersal mechanism to downstream nursery habitats ~150 km from spawning areas. Mortality factors include cold water (<15°C adversely affects larvae acclimated to 22°C), predation by non-native fishes, and stranding in isolated pools during abrupt flow fluctuations.

- **Age-0 Nursery Habitat:** Small postlarvae captured by seining shorelines (N=3,079) in July-August; by September-October concentrated in low-gradient Green River reaches (N=6,459 captures). Young fish (N=1,240) captured in spring indicate successful overwintering in autumn-occupied areas. Nursery habitats are shallow shoreline embayments (backwaters) that are warm (mean 17.0°C), deep (mean 38 cm), large (mean 826 m²), and turbid. Age-0 fish most abundant in backwaters in spring (April, 68% of captures), showing diel movement between backwaters and main channel associated with temperature (backwaters warmer than river). Mark-recapture of 922 fin-clipped fish (230 recaptured, 25%) documented use of multiple habitats and movement between backwaters, shoreline eddies, and main-channel runs. Abundance and size inversely correlated with high summer-autumn flows (r values not specified but relationships significant). Catch and growth highest in 1979-1980 (low-flow years), lowest in 1983-1984 (high-flow years when dam releases inundated nurseries). Aerial photography/videography showed maximum backwater availability at flows of 28.3-50.94 m³/s at Jensen gauge; flows >50.94 m³/s reduced backwater numbers. Gradually decreasing summer hydrograph required to maximize backwater formation and quality.

- **Overwinter Survival:** Overwinter mortality not detected as significant in winters 1985-86, 1987-88, 1988-89. High spring catches (6.5-28.9 fish/100 m² seined) indicate high survival in some years. Laboratory studies: all age-0 fish survived simulated winter (210 days at 3-4°C) when fed; only smaller individuals with low lipid content died when starved. Fish actively foraged in winter field conditions. Low stable winter flows reduce ice scouring and provide suitable shoreline habitats. Five young Colorado squawfish collected in one backwater December 1987; few fish captured in baited minnow traps. Fluctuating flows in 1987-88 caused shoreline scouring by moving ice blocks and alternate flooding-dewatering at Ouray due to ice jams. Lower, more stable flows in 1988-89 resulted in shallower backwaters but less scouring.

- **Juveniles:** Little known; captured in various shoreline habitats including backwaters and flooded bottomlands. Most abundant in lower Green River; long-distance upstream movement by late juveniles/early adults needed to repopulate upstream areas. Predation by introduced fishes documented: yellow bullhead and largemouth bass predation on stocked fish in Verde River, Arizona; largemouth bass, green sunfish, black crappie, and black bullhead predation in Colorado River gravel pits; channel catfish predation in Dolores River.

**Humpback Chub:**

- **Distribution and Status:** Occur in canyon-bound sections: lower Little Snake River, Yampa Canyon, Whirlpool Canyon, Desolation-Gray canyons. Only 133 juvenile and adult fish captured in DNM during spring sampling 1986-1989 (<1% of all fish captured), indicating rarity. Confusion with specific identification of some individuals (intermediate forms with roundtail chub) complicates status assessment. Morphological variation in areas of sympatry may be induced by recent habitat change; intermediate forms common in altered systems (Green River, Colorado River), rare in unaltered rivers (Yampa River, Little Colorado River).

- **Habitat Use:** Most prevalent in high-gradient whitewater reaches dominated by rocky runs, riffles, rapids. Adults most often collected in seasonally flooded shoreline eddies downstream of large boulders and upstream of rapids. Juveniles more common in smaller eddies in rocky shoreline runs. Stomachs contained hymenopterans, plant debris, Mormon crickets. Fall-winter observations suggest fish remain in pools and eddies of impounded water and rapids in low-flow conditions. Paucity in upper Green River canyons may be linked to reduction in invertebrate taxa following dam closure.

- **Spawning:** Spawn in spring-early summer following highest spring flows at river temperatures ~20°C. Timing: May-June in low/average-flow years (1987, 1988, 1989), extending into July in high-flow year (1986). Ripe fish (N=39 humpback chub, N=242 roundtail chub) predominantly captured in shoreline eddy habitats in upper 48 km of Yampa Canyon. Some indication fish remain in or near specific eddies for extended periods and return to same eddy in different years. Spawning habitat: shoreline eddies associated with boulder-sand substrates. Sympatric with roundtail chub during 5-6 week period following highest spring runoff. Availability of shoreline eddy habitat greatest with spring flooding, decreases with declining summer flows.
