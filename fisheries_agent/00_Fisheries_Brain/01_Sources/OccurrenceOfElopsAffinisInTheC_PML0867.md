# Occurrence of Elops affinis in the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #galeidae #elopidae

## Executive Summary

This short California Department of Fish and Game note contains two items. The first is a commentary on the [[Tiger Shark]] (*Galeocerdo arcticus*), a shark recorded in California only from a pair of jaws near San Diego. The second reports the first known occurrence of [[Elops Affinis]] (a [[Tarpon]] relative) in the [[Colorado River]], with two specimens taken below [[Laguna Dam]] in August 1941. Both items are informal, observational accounts without sample statistics.

## Background & Description

**Tiger shark.** The species belongs to the family [[Galeidae]], which also includes the great blue, leopard, soupfin and smooth-hounds. It is cosmopolitan in warm or temperate oceans. E. C. Starks (1917) noted that it was reported by fishermen off southern California but known to zoologists from only a single set of jaws captured near San Diego. W. L. Scofield (1941) noted that the species has a reputation for voracity, with stomach contents including nearly everything except human flesh, even land mammals such as sheep and cattle. Its diet frequently included other sharks.

**Elops affinis.** Pacific coast specimens of Elops, formerly treated as the Atlantic *Elops saurus*, are now recognised as the distinct *E. affinis*. The fish attains a length of about three feet and is closely related to the tarpon. Two specimens measured 11 and 14 inches. They were caught by angling on shrimp bait and chicken entrails, and they fought vigorously with many leaps. Identification was confirmed by P. S. Barnhart of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Marine range:** Elops abounds in the [[Gulf of California]] but is not well known along the Pacific coast of Mexico.
- **Freshwater entry:** Because the tarpon ascends fresh water, the authors considered its presence in the Colorado River plausible. The river has been heavily collected and angled, so a resident population would probably have been reported earlier.
- **Management hope:** The authors hoped the fish would establish itself in the river and move into the [[Salton Sea]], where it could become a valued game fish.
- **Naming problem:** The species has many common names, most unsatisfactory, including ten-pounder, big-eyed herring, big-sized herring, largemouth herring, John Mariggle, bonyfish, bonefish, matajuelo real, lisa francesca and awa.
- **Shark rarity:** Interest in sharks rose during the period when shark liver oils were valued for vitamins. Scofield suggested that the tiger shark's scarcity in California waters was fortunate.

## Key Findings

1. The [[Tiger Shark]] had been documented in California only from a single set of jaws near San Diego (Starks, 1917). No later local records were known as of 1941.
2. The tiger shark is considered a voracious predator, with stomach records showing a diet that includes other sharks and land mammals.
3. Two *Elops affinis* specimens (11 and 14 inches) were collected from the Colorado River below Laguna Dam on August 6, 1941, which was the first recorded occurrence of the species in that river.
4. The identification of the Colorado River fish as *Elops affinis* was made by P. S. Barnhart, Scripps Institution of Oceanography.
5. The species is a sport fish that takes spoon lures and cut bait and fights strongly, so the authors hoped it would establish a population in the Colorado River and the Salton Sea.
6. The authors called for a standard common name, since the existing names for the species are numerous and inconsistent.
