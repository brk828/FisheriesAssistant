# Genetic Differences among Rainbow Trout Spawned on Different Days within a Single Season

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*, formerly *Salmo gairdneri*) fish that spawn at different times in a single season differ genetically. Using [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] at protein loci, the authors examined progeny from two hatchery strains: the [[Eagle Lake Strain]] (1983 and 1985 seasons) and the [[Arlee Strain]] (1984 season). Allele frequencies differed significantly among spawning dates in all three data sets. Progeny from mid-season spawning were generally more heterozygous than early or late progeny. The authors conclude that spawning time is probably controlled by many genes and recommend that hatchery [[Brood Stock Management]] retain progeny from the central two-thirds of the spawning season to conserve [[Genetic Variation]].

## Background & Description

The authors frame [[Genetic Variation Loss]] as a serious problem for hatchery brood stocks, especially those used to found or supplement natural populations or to preserve wild genetic diversity. Reduced variation can impair survival, growth, feed conversion, body shape, and normal development, and can lower adaptability and increase susceptibility to epizootics. Variation can be lost through founder effects, [[Inbreeding]], and [[Genetic Drift]] when few parents are used.

Brood stocks of salmonids are often spawned over 1 to 3 months. The authors hypothesize that spawning time has an additive genetic component. Because heterozygous individuals are expected to have intermediate phenotypes, individuals spawning in mid-season should be more heterozygous than early or late spawners. Selecting for early or late spawning would therefore reduce variation at genes that influence spawning time.

The Eagle Lake strain is maintained by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service at the [[Creston National Fish Hatchery]], Montana. It was acquired from the California Department of Fish and Game and has been held there since 1980. The Arlee strain is maintained by the Montana Department of Fish, Wildlife, and Parks at the [[Jocko River State Trout Hatchery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses a hatchery management problem rather than wild ecology. Eagle Lake fish begin spawning earlier at Creston (January) than in their native California (March), a difference the authors did not anticipate. Because of this, the 1983 first spawning date included eggs from females that had been ripe for more than a week, and the earliest maturing females were probably not spawned that year.

Progeny were reared in a common partitioned trough to reduce the chance that environmental differences would produce apparent genetic differences. Mortality was low, so surviving progeny were considered representative of their parents' spawning dates. Samples were collected at about 75 mm total length.

The authors also note that the genetic signal of spawning time may be influenced by linkage to genes on the same chromosome, so observed associations do not necessarily indicate direct control.

## Key Findings

**Sampling design**
- Eagle Lake fish were spawned weekly in 1983 and 1985. Eggs from 25 females were pooled and fertilized with sperm from about 25 randomly chosen males.
- Arlee fish were spawned every two weeks in 1984. Males were used only once. In 1985, some Eagle Lake males may have been reused, which the authors suggest would obscure rather than create differences.
- Enzyme assays used [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] with standard buffers and stains. Roughly 20 variable loci were scored across the strains (for example, [[SOD-1 Locus]], [[IDH-2 Locus]], [[MDH-3,4 Locus]], [[G3P-1 Locus]], [[GL-1 Locus]]).

**Allele-frequency differences**
- Significant differences occurred at 4 loci in 1983 Eagle Lake progeny, 7 loci in 1985 Eagle Lake progeny, and 4 loci in Arlee progeny.
- Summed [[Chi-Square Analysis]] across all loci was highly significant (P < 0.001) for each data set. The 1983 Eagle Lake summed chi-square was 152.24 (df = 96).
- Nei's partitioning showed that within-sample heterozygosity accounted for 98.4% (1983 Eagle Lake), 98.6% (1985 Eagle Lake), and 97.7% (Arlee) of total variation. Spawning-time differences therefore accounted for about 2%.

**Correlations with spawning date**
- Significant correlations between allele frequency and spawning date occurred at 5 loci (1983 Eagle Lake), 4 loci (1985 Eagle Lake), and 3 loci (Arlee).
- [[IDH-2 Locus]] (100 allele) was positively correlated with spawning date in all three data sets (r = 0.889** in 1983, 0.391 in 1985, 0.895* in Arlee). Its significance was confined to the 1983 and Arlee sets.
- [[SOD-1 Locus]] (100 allele) was positively associated with spawning date in both Eagle Lake years (r = 0.831** in 1983, 0.640* in 1985) but negatively with Arlee spawning date (r = -0.807*). The authors interpret this as evidence of linkage to nearby loci rather than direct control.

**Heterozygosity and spawning date**
- The number of heterozygous loci per fish varied significantly among spawning dates in all three data sets (P < 0.001 for 1983 Eagle Lake; P < 0.01 for 1985 Eagle Lake and Arlee).
- In Arlee fish, the earliest and latest spawners had lower heterozygosity than mid-season spawners. Late-spawning Eagle Lake fish also had reduced heterozygosity, but early-spawning Eagle Lake fish did not, possibly because early fish were absent in 1983.

**Biological implications**
- Genetic differences linked to spawning time amount to about 20% of the differentiation observed among populations. Allendorf and Phelps (1981) reported that allele-frequency differences accounted for 8% of total variation among samples from 37 northwestern USA rainbow trout populations.
- The authors suggest that hatchery strains have a background level of inbreeding of about 1 to 2%. This is viewed as largely inherent to the species' biology and difficult to eliminate. Managers should avoid increasing it by selecting for spawning time or other traits.
- Reduced hatching success has been observed among eggs from early and late-spawning Arlee fish (personal communication cited in the paper).

**Recommendations for brood-stock management**
- Retain progeny from at least the central two-thirds of the spawning season to minimize selection for spawning time.
- Retain a number of fish from each spawning date proportional to the number of females spawned that day, since female spawning time is thought to be most closely tied to genetic variation.
- To limit genetic drift, at least 100 pairs of individuals should contribute to the future brood stock, a value the authors attribute to Shaklee (1983) and Allendorf and Ryman (1987).
- Example: for the 1984 Arlee season, brood stock should come from the 2nd to 5th spawning dates. Females were very unevenly distributed (about 40% from the second period, 25% each from the third and fourth, and 10% from the fifth). A more even distribution could be achieved by reducing the second-period share to about 25%. Eagle Lake females were more evenly spread, with each day representing 6 to 16% of the total.
- Monitoring with protein electrophoresis should sample the pooled brood stock, not a single spawning date. Single-date sampling can falsely suggest allele-frequency changes between year classes.

**Conclusions**
- Spawning time is probably influenced by many genes distributed across the genome, which is why mid-season progeny retain more variation.
- Maintaining the genetic variation of a hatchery brood stock requires using progeny from throughout most of the spawning season.
