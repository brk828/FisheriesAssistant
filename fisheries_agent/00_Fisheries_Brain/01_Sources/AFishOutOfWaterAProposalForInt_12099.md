# A Fish Out of Water: Instream Flow Rights for the Lower Colorado River and the Totoaba

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This legal-policy comment argues that the [[Totoaba]] (*Cynoscion macdonaldi*), an endangered sciaenid of the upper [[Gulf of California]], is declining because of overfishing and loss of its estuarine spawning habitat in the [[Colorado River Delta]]. Diversions by upstream dams have removed nearly all freshwater flow to the delta, making the estuary hypersaline and degrading the nursery and spawning grounds. The author proposes two actions: (1) a bilateral United States–Mexico agreement recognizing [[Instream Flow Rights]] for the lower Colorado River, and (2) stronger use of existing law, namely the [[Endangered Species Act]], the [[National Environmental Policy Act]], and the [[Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species]] (CITES). The document is a legal proposal rather than a field study, so its ecological data are drawn from older fisheries and survey literature cited within it.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The totoaba grows to about 2 m and up to 135 kg. It was historically important to both sport and commercial fisheries.
- **Life history:** Adults live in the deep, cool upper Gulf of California and migrate seasonally to the formerly brackish Colorado River estuary to spawn. The historical spawning season ran from late February or early March to early June; it has contracted to a March or April event. Spawning fish congregate in large groups, and the authors cite a "critical mass" requirement for effective spawning.
- **Juveniles:** Young live in shallow, muddy estuarine waters for about two years before migrating south. Recruitment into the spawning population takes six to seven years, at roughly 23 kg. Otolith studies show that fish caught in the fishery are often twenty years or older.
- **Protection status:** Mexico closed the fishery on August 2, 1975. The species was listed in CITES Appendix I in 1977 and on the US Endangered Species list in 1979.
- **Companion species:** The [[Vaquita]] (*Phocoena sinus*), the world's smallest porpoise and most endangered marine mammal (population of several hundred), drowns in totoaba gillnets, which links its conservation to the totoaba's.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishing pressure.** Illegal fishing continued after the ban, with an estimated total harvest of 40 to 70 tons per year, sold locally and exported to the US as unidentifiable fillets. A shark gillnet fishery using 10-inch mesh probably also takes young adult totoaba. Shrimp trawling in the estuary took large numbers of small juveniles. Shrimp production fell from 6,400 tons to 1,300 tons between the 1980 and 1990 seasons, and the fishery has since largely collapsed. The Mexican government responded by declaring the northern gulf and estuary a [[Biosphere Reserve]] on June 10, 1993, covering approximately 3,600 square miles.

**Habitat degradation.** Flows into the gulf were about 13 million acre-feet in 1942, but main-stem dams have reduced them to negligible amounts. Flow measurement at El Mérfimo ended in 1968 because there was not enough water to measure. Evaporation and hypersaline agricultural runoff have raised salinity; the upper gulf reaches up to 40 parts per thousand (ppt), compared with about 35 ppt in normal marine water. Agricultural runoff also adds unknown pesticide levels. Historically, a distinct freshwater–saline line acted as a [[Salinity Barrier]] that excluded predators from the estuary. Without it, juvenile totoaba are exposed.

**Ecosystem productivity.** The river was the main nutrient source for a gulf that the text describes as one of the most productive and diverse seas, with an estimated 10,000 invertebrate species. Reduced nutrients and higher salinity are said to affect organisms from primary producers up to top predators, although no study has directly assessed the totoaba's food supply.

**Data gaps.** The author states that there is little systematic research on the totoaba's habitat needs, that most gulf research is underfunded Mexican work, and that there is no adequate pre-dam baseline.

## Key Findings

1. **Multiple, compounding threats.** Overfishing (including illegal harvest and bycatch), shrimp trawling, and habitat loss from reduced flow and higher salinity have together driven the decline. The author judges that extinction within a decade is likely without protection.
2. **Positive feedback in the fishery.** Because the fish aggregate for spawning, fishing effort can concentrate on fewer fish, which lowers spawning success and further reduces the population.
3. **Enforcement gap in CITES.** In 1992, US imports included over 70,000 wildlife shipments worth more than $700 million. Sixty-five Fish and Wildlife personnel monitored less than 20 percent of them. Most totoaba trade crosses by car, not through the nine designated wildlife ports, and fillets are hard to distinguish from white sea bass. CITES also cannot address habitat or in-country enforcement.
4. **Legal leverage under the ESA.** Section 7 requires US agencies to ensure that their actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species. The author argues it should extend to actions abroad, citing lower-court rulings and the 1978 regulations, and notes that it may apply to dam licensing, the [[Yuma Desalinization Plant]] operation, and interstate water allocation. Because no NEPA or ESA review has considered the totoaba, the author argues these laws remain unused.
5. **Proposed water solution.** The author proposes amending the [[Treaty of 1944]] or drafting a new agreement so that surplus US water reaches the delta. The treaty currently gives fishing and hunting the lowest priority, so the amendment would need to recognize brackish-ecosystem preservation as a beneficial use.
6. **Water source identified.** The [[Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation District]] in Arizona is described as an inefficient, subsidized project. Buying out its irrigators could free over 370,000 acre-feet, and the $32 million annual desalination operating cost could be avoided. That water could flow to the delta.
7. **Projected benefits.** The author expects the United States to gain international standing, endangered-species and aesthetic value, and renewed access to totoaba. Mexico would gain eco-tourism and fishery revenue. Restoring the estuary would also protect shrimp, crab, and other estuarine fish.
8. **Recommendations for further work.** The author calls for more research on the totoaba's reproductive biology, the economics of northern gulf fishing villages, Colorado River water rights, and ecosystem-wide effects, along with a broad dialogue among water users, agencies, and scientists in both countries.
