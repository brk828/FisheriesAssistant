# Introduction of Rainbow Smelt into Lake Powell - Colorado River Wildlife Council Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae #fisheries-management #species-introduction

## Executive Summary

This 1987 proposal to the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] recommends introducing [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]] to address forage deficiencies for game fish populations. The reservoir's sole pelagic forage species, [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), has been depleted by predation, resulting in stunted [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) populations with few individuals exceeding 20 inches. The proposal argues that smelt would occupy cold hypolimnetic waters (7-12°C) below the thermocline, thermally partitioned from shad and most game fish, thereby increasing forage diversity and channeling primary productivity more efficiently to piscivorous predators. Expected benefits include improved growth rates for striped bass and [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), reduced competition among forage species, and potential development of a recreational smelt fishery. The document extensively addresses potential impacts on three endangered [[Colorado River]] native fishes: [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*).

## Background & Description

Rainbow smelt are slender, silver fish (178-203 mm average length, maximum 356 mm) native to the Atlantic coast from Labrador to New Jersey, with indigenous landlocked populations in New England and eastern Canada. They possess an adipose fin, deeply forked caudal fin, incomplete lateral line, and iridescent reflections on sides. Smelt were introduced to [[Lake Michigan]] in 1906-1912 and subsequently colonized the entire [[Great Lakes]] complex within 25 years. Western introductions include [[Lake Sakakawea]] and [[Lake Oahe]] in the Missouri River system (established 1971), and multiple Colorado reservoirs during the 1970s-1980s (Clear Creek, Twin Lakes, Turquoise, Pueblo, Quincy, Rampart, and Horsetooth Reservoirs) as forage for trout and other predators.

Smelt are schooling, pelagic fishes sensitive to light and temperature. They inhabit midwaters of lakes, concentrating within or immediately below the thermocline at temperatures of 5-16°C, with 12.8°C as thermal preference. They avoid temperatures above 15°C except for brief surface excursions and cannot survive temperatures exceeding 21-26°C. In [[Lake Oahe]], smelt occupied 6-14°C waters, suspending near bottom during day and moving upward to thermocline at night. This thermal partitioning effectively separates smelt from warmwater species and reduces competition and predation with young-of-year game fish.

Spawning occurs in spring (March-May) shortly after ice-out at temperatures of 2-18.3°C, typically peaking at 6.1°C. Smelt are anadromous but rarely ascend streams more than a quarter mile and are stopped by falls exceeding one foot vertical drop. They spawn nocturnally on gravel substrate in stream mouths or on lake shoals. Females produce 10,000-43,000 eggs that adhere to substrate via short pedicels. Incubation requires 8-60 days depending on temperature (8-29 days at 4.4-21°C). Egg survival to prolarvae is 1-2%. Smelt mature during their second or third year; in [[Lake Superior]] 41% of two-year-old males and 18% of females were mature, with shortest mature individuals at 127 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Smelt are opportunistic planktivores feeding primarily on copepods, cladocerans (*Daphnia*, *Leptodora kindtii*), amphipods (*Mysis*, *Pontoporeia*, *Gammarus*), and aquatic insects. Larger individuals (>143-180 mm) occasionally consume small fish including alewives and their own young, but extensive literature review indicates smelt are not significant piscivores. Van Oosten (1953) concluded "nowhere have investigators found [game and commercial fishes] present in the stomachs of smelt in any significant quantities." MacCrimmon and Pugsley (1979) found "no evidence from any study that any segment of the smelt population becomes totally piscivorous" or that smelt predation suppresses sympatric populations. Smelt may restructure plankton communities by eliminating larger zooplankton species as "selective planktivores."

Smelt have proven valuable as forage for [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), other salmonids, [[Walleye]], [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), and both [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Introductions have resulted in increased growth and abundance of game species in multiple systems. In the Missouri River reservoirs, smelt presence benefited walleye, pickerel, and pike populations. Striped bass and smelt coevolved in the North Atlantic and stripers utilize smelt in New England coastal waters. Alewives, occupying nearly identical niches, rapidly became dominant food for striped bass after introduction to [[Smith Mountain Lake]], Virginia.

[[Lake Powell]] exhibits oligotrophic conditions with chlorophyll-a near 2 μg/L but plankton densities of 3,000-26,000/m³ in upper 40 m. April plankton abundance (17,600/m³) compares favorably to [[Lake Mead]] (23,000), [[Bull Shoals]] (24,600), and Great Lakes average (12,400). The reservoir stratifies during summer with hypolimnetic temperatures of 7-12°C, well within smelt's preferred range. Current pelagic plankton is utilized primarily by juvenile striped bass; smelt introduction would create more efficient nutrient cycling.

## Key Findings

**Rationale for Introduction**: [[Threadfin Shad]] populations in Lake Powell have been reduced by predation to levels inadequate for supporting pelagic and deepwater predators. Striped bass populations are stunted with few fish exceeding 20 inches. Both walleye and striped bass suffer recurring declines in physical condition attributable to insufficient shad forage. Juvenile striped bass maintain body condition by feeding directly on large zooplankton, indicating a missing trophic link. Primary productivity is under-utilized by the predator-impacted shad population.

**Expected Benefits**: Smelt would increase forage diversity and abundance, occupying cold hypolimnetic waters thermally separated from epilimnetic shad during stratification. This would more effectively channel primary productivity to all piscivorous predators. Smelt grow larger than threadfin shad (up to 356 mm vs. smaller shad), allowing striped bass to attain larger sizes. Providing hypolimnetic forage could shift striped bass biomass from epilimnion to depths, with healthy adult populations helping control juvenile recruitment. Larger striped bass would increase angler interest and harvest, providing population control of this long-lived predator. A spring smelt spawning run could develop into a dipnet and hook-and-line fishery similar to New England and Missouri River systems, benefiting local economy during slow periods.

**Impacts on Endangered Species - Humpback Chub**: Most humpback chub are confined to the [[Little Colorado River]] and backwaters 60+ miles below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in waters warmer (15.2°C) than main channel (10-11°C). Spawning occurs March-June at 16-20°C when main channel temperatures (~11°C) may be too cold to initiate spawning. The Little Colorado River, typically 9°C warmer than main channel, is the most important tributary for humpback chub survival. Smelt avoid temperatures exceeding 15°C and die at 21°C, thus would not be found near spawning adult or larval humpback chub. Young-of-year chubs spend first summer/fall in warm tributaries, growing to ~70 mm before migrating to main channel in October when smelt predation risk is minimal due to size and seasonal feeding patterns. Habitat overlap at tributary confluences would be minimal; smelt preferentially eat amphipods (*Gammarus*) while humpback chub prefer chironomids, simulids and fish. Smelt would be thermally separated from native fish larvae and not pose significant competition threat to warmwater species.

**Impacts on Endangered Species - Bonytail Chub**: Bonytail chub, the rarest Colorado River native fish nearest extinction, is a riverine species precluded from using Colorado River habitat by cold water releases from reservoirs. Individual captures result in transfer to secure refugia; restoration depends on stocking. As a sedentary insectivore with 20°C preferred temperature, bonytail would be least likely native species to compete with or face predation from cold-water, planktivorous rainbow smelt.

**Impacts on Endangered Species - Razorback Sucker**: Razorback sucker populations have declined but a remnant population exists in [[Lake Mohave]]. Spawning occurs November-May at 10-15°C over shallow gravel flats (<1 m depth). Violent spawning deposits eggs deeply into gravel, protecting most from predators. Adult smelt cease feeding during spawning; razorback eggs and larvae in gravel would be unavailable to smelt predation. Smelt eggs adhering to gravel would be available to [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Carp]], potentially buffering razorback eggs from predation. After spawning, adult smelt would retreat to depths as temperatures increase; juvenile suckers would remain hidden in macrophyte beds (*Myriophyllum*, *Potamogeton*) at 5-10 m depth. Razorback suckers prefer 23°C, above smelt's lethal temperature range, ensuring thermal separation in Lake Mohave. Adequate productivity and living space exists for both species.

**Migration Concerns**: Smelt upstream migration is stopped by barriers or falls with one-foot vertical drop. The turbulent rapids and falls of [[Cataract Canyon]] would confine smelt to Lake Powell headwaters, as would rapids in the [[San Juan River]]. Smelt avoid turbid waters and should avoid inflow areas. Downstream migration is expected; smelt would pass through tailwaters but not establish reproducing populations in riverine sections. They would not establish in warm, non-stratifying waters of lower Colorado River below [[Lake Havasu]]. Experience from Missouri and Mississippi River systems shows smelt pass through river systems annually but do not establish populations, instead providing seasonal forage for trophy trout in tailwaters without detrimental effects on native or exotic warmwater fish.

**Predation and Competition**: Some habitat overlap between adult smelt and walleye fry is expected during spawning, potentially limiting walleye recruitment. However, smelt spawning lasts ~3 weeks with most activity in one week; larvae hatch in ~10 days at 12°C and are light-sensitive, forcing them deep during day. Adult smelt would be forced from shallows by warming temperatures in early April before centrarchid spawning begins (minimum 15°C required). Shad spawn at 20.5°C in mid-May and remain in upper 10 m during summer, separated from adult smelt by thermocline. Young-of-year smelt may interact with shad at thermocline, but shad currently use warm shallow canyon waters with no open-water population. Smelt would initially utilize pelagic plankton currently used only by juvenile striped bass. If smelt buffer predation on shad as expected, shad populations would gradually increase and reoccupy open water, sharing plankton resources at thermocline. Fall turnover (November) allows interaction of all species, but by then striped bass predation will have reduced annual smelt crop; most young-of-year game fish will be too large for smelt consumption.

**Disease and Hybridization**: Smelt host various parasites, particularly *Glugea hertwigi* (white cysts in body cavity). Examinations of spawning smelt from Lake Oahe by Utah and South Dakota found fish free of prohibited parasites; histological testing detected no prohibited pathogens. If parasites of concern are found, fertilized egg transfers would avoid parasite transmission. No potential exists for hybridization of smelt with any Colorado River system species.

**Alternative Species**: [[Pygmy Smelt]] (*Osmerus spectrum*) is a valid lacustrine sibling species distinguishable from rainbow smelt. Pygmy smelt spawn later without temporal overlap, do not engage in long-distance spawning movements, remain planktivorous throughout life with no piscivorous tendencies, grow more slowly (125 mm TL), have lower longevity, and spawn younger. Introduction of pygmy smelt might reduce predation on larval fishes and downstream migration. However, their smaller size renders them more vulnerable to predation, reducing likelihood of coping with Lake Powell's large predator populations.

**Post-Introduction Monitoring**: The smelt introduction would be constantly monitored and reported annually under Job I (Forage Condition Study) of Dingell-Johnson Project F-46-R, currently administered through [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]].

**Conclusion**: Rainbow smelt meet many criteria for desirable forage fish: prolific, trophically efficient, vulnerable to predation, and apparently harmless west of Mississippi. Lake Powell's absence of deepwater forage fish and hypolimnetic temperatures of 7-12°C make it excellent for smelt introduction. Smelt would provide schooling forage occupying same strata as walleye and striped bass year-round, thermally partitioned from shad and young-of-year game fish in stratified reservoir, offering little threat of direct competition. Rare and endangered endemic fishes would have minimal interaction with smelt and would not face significant competition for food and space. Smelt have proven beneficial in Missouri River system without significant negative impacts; North Dakota fisheries personnel state "The rainbow smelt is the salvation of the Missouri River system reservoirs."
