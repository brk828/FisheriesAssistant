# The Endangered Species Act and Southwest Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 review by [[James E. Johnson]] and [[John N. Rinne]], published in the American Fisheries Society bulletin *Fisheries* (Vol. 7, No. 3), argues that the native fishes of the American Southwest are in severe, continuing decline due to habitat loss. It reviews the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 and its three main components (listing, protection, and recovery), describes recovery actions for five focal species, and proposes future directions for conservation. The authors conclude that recovery depends on protecting aquatic habitat, reintroducing extirpated species, and expanding interagency cooperation, and they suggest amending the Act to ease reintroductions.

## Background & Description

The Southwest's native fish fauna is characterized by high endemism and adaptations to harsh aquatic environments. The region's fishes range from the large [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] to the tiny [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]] and the colorful [[Arizona Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]].

Key figures on the scale of the problem:

- **Listed species:** 25 of the 1973 Act's US endangered fish species are endemic to the Southwest.
- **Candidate taxa:** [[Deacon]] et al. (1979) listed 251 fish taxa in North America as likely candidates for federal protection, of which 94 (47%) occur in the Southwest.
- **Arizona:** 22 of 27 (81%) native species are listed by state or federal agencies as nearing extinction; 20% of native fishes have been extirpated.
- **New Mexico:** 31 of 73 (42%) native species are listed or designated.
- **Regional losses:** Seven Southwestern fishes are extinct and six others are endangered in California alone.

The authors describe the Southwestern fauna as a whole as an endangered faunal group. The decline is attributed mainly to the loss of aquatic habitat through dams, diversion, pumping and pollution, which has caused range reductions and local extirpations.

The paper's study area includes Arizona, New Mexico, west Texas, parts of California, Nevada, Utah, and Colorado.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado River.** Dams have transformed the river. [[Boulder Dam]] (completed 1932) began control of the [[Colorado River]], which is described as perhaps the most regulated river in the world. The natural flood-drought cycle is largely lost. Cold tailwaters and upstream lacustrine environments favor the more than 100 introduced fish species over the 30 native species (Minckley 1973). Constricted canyon walls prevent solar warming between dams. Remaining semipristine reaches in Desolation, Grand, and Westwater canyons still hold a few native species, but they are threatened by cold water, non-native fishes, proposed dams, and dewatering.

**Rio Grande.** Similar impacts have occurred on the [[Rio Grande]]. Typical Southwestern desert streams are now dammed, diverted, pumped, and polluted, and often run dry or carry only irrigation return water and sewage effluent.

**Desert springs.** Springs range from seeps to flows of hundreds of cubic meters per minute. Their isolation and stable conditions have produced endemic fish and invertebrate species. Dewatering of these "aquatic islands" through groundwater pumping could cause the greatest faunal loss in North America within 20 years. Texas illustrates the problem: of 281 major historic springs, only four were very large (over 100 cubic feet per second), and only two, Comal and San Marcos, remain in that class. Sixty-three springs have completely failed.

**Causes of decline.** Non-native salmonids, especially [[Rainbow Trout]], cause genetic swamping, competition, and hybridization. Habitat modification is also a major factor. For [[Gila Topminnow]], non-native [[Mosquitofish]] and habitat loss were key threats.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Endangered Species Act and its implementation

- **Purpose:** The Act conserves endangered and threatened species and the ecosystems they depend on. Federal agencies must conserve listed species, and private citizens may not take them without a permit. Citizens may also file civil suits to enforce the Act.
- **Administration:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] implements the Act within US coastal boundaries, focusing on listing, protection, and recovery.
- **National status (USDI 1982):** 148 vertebrate species are listed as endangered and 33 as threatened. Of these, 32 endangered and 12 threatened species are fishes.
- **Amendments:** The Act was amended in 1978 and 1979. Reauthorization was due in 1982.

### 2. Listing and protection

- **Listing** requires surveys, economic and environmental assessments, public meetings, and two *Federal Register* publications. [[Critical Habitat]] designation protects habitats needed for survival.
- **[[Section 7 Consultation]]** reviews federal actions that could affect listed species. In the decade since the Act began, 1,632 consultations were reviewed in Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, and Oklahoma. Only 13 found that a federal project would jeopardize a listed species, and alternatives were suggested in each case.
- **Enforcement** focuses on deterrence, since losing a listed species is harder to reverse than prosecuting violators.

### 3. Recovery actions

- **Scope:** Recovery goes beyond listing and protection to actively reverse declines through recovery teams, recovery plans, reintroduction, and habitat acquisition.
- **Plans:** About 15 approved [[Recovery Plans]] operate for Southwestern fishes. Early plans included the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]] (1975), the Unarmored Threespine Stickleback (1977), and the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Cui-ui]] (1978).

### 4. Focal species case studies

- **[[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*, endangered):** Fewer than 10,000 individuals persist in eight headwater streams in New Mexico and Arizona. Hatchery rearing began as early as 1923. The first reintroduced population was established in McKnight Creek, New Mexico, in 1970. Recovery requires replicating the five natural populations before downlisting to threatened status.
- **[[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*, threatened):** The species now occupies about 5% of its former range. The White Mountain Apache Tribe closed angling in streams with the species by 1955. Arizona Game and Fish began rearing it in 1962 and started reintroductions in 1965. The recovery goal is 30 self-sustaining populations before delisting. Both trout recovery plans were being implemented, and the authors expected both goals to be reached within the decade.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Along with the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]], this species has declined sharply over 50 years. It is the only one of the four able to persist in reservoirs, but only as adults, and reservoir populations cannot replenish themselves. Most reservoir populations disappeared within several decades of dam closure. The species left Arizona's riverine habitats in the 1950s. It was proposed as threatened in 1978, but the proposal was withdrawn in 1980 because of listing procedure changes. A 1981 memorandum of understanding between the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Fish and Wildlife Service addressed reintroduction into the Virgin River, and the Service began experimenting with hatchery production at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].
- **[[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):** Endemic to the Gila River system, the topminnow was once abundant in nearly every cienega, spring, and tributary. Dams, overutilization, and non-native mosquitofish drove it toward extinction. Recovery began at Dexter, New Mexico, where fish were produced in 1981, and by the late 1970s only a few wild populations remained. A reintroduction program is the first in the species' history.
- **[[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** Once widespread in the lower Colorado and Virgin rivers, the species was first listed as endangered in 1967. Its range has been reduced to the Virgin River, where a proposed water-power project threatened flows. Recovery required consultation with the Fish and Wildlife Service under the Act and monitoring to document survival and reproduction.

### 5. Recovery strategy and future directions

The authors predict that Southwestern fish recovery will proceed slowly over the next decade, with emphasis on bird and mammal species in some programs. They expect recovery to depend on public support for protecting aquatic habitats. They recommend three priorities: (1) developing specific goals for habitat protection, (2) reintroducing extirpated species into remnant habitats, and (3) increasing agency cooperation.

**Habitat protection** methods include:

- Increased federal management of aquatic habitats under federal control.
- Acquisition of key habitats through purchase or easements.
- Prioritization of protected habitats by focusing on single springs or core populations, even when marginal habitats are involved.

The authors also favor an **ecosystem-based approach**. Recovery teams for the Colorado River fishes, the Gila River fishes, and the Eastern Mohave Desert combined multiple species into single plans.

### 6. Policy recommendations

- **Experimental classification:** The authors support a new "experimental" listing category, endorsed by the Fish and Wildlife Service's Endangered Species Committee, for reintroduced populations. Under this category, reintroduced populations would not trigger critical habitat or Section 7 consultation requirements.
- **Elimination of the critical habitat requirement:** The 1978 amendment requiring critical habitat designation at proposal has caused legal conflicts and public opposition. The San Marcos River example shows a lawsuit filed in 1980 against critical habitat designation. The authors argue that species can be protected without it.

### 7. Cooperation, public awareness, and recent acquisitions

- **Cooperation:** Researchers, managers, and politicians are increasingly sharing knowledge about non-game fishes. The Colorado Squawfish Recovery Team (1975–76) brought together biologists who had once tried to eradicate the species.
- **Bonytail stocking:** On November 9, 1981, over 42,000 [[Bonytail Chub]] fingerlings were stocked into [[Lake Mohave]], beginning the first recovery phase for the species.
- **Acquisition:** In April 1982, the Fish and Wildlife Service bought the San Bernardino Ranch in southeastern Arizona to protect the only remaining Rio Yaqui aquatic habitat in the United States. Six fish species are expected to return, three of them extirpated from the country.
- **Extinction:** The [[Tecopa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis calidae*) was declared extinct during this period.

The authors conclude that habitat protection, supported by public awareness and education, is the long-term answer. Recovery efforts will sometimes fail, but they hope future generations will see native fishes in historic habitats rather than only in collections or articles like this one.
