# Food Habits of California Striped Bass

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #diet-study #estuarine-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study analyzed 387 [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*) stomachs collected by anglers over one year (1947-1948) in [[San Francisco Bay]] and the [[San Joaquin River]] Delta. The sample was divided into summer/fall (229 stomachs) and winter/spring (158 stomachs) collections. [[Crago]] shrimp and [[Northern Anchovy]] (*Engraulis mordax*) dominated summer diets, while small fish and mysid shrimp ([[Neomysis]]) were more important in winter. The study revealed seasonal dietary shifts and highlighted the importance of shrimp as a major food source, comprising 53% of identifiable material by volume in summer samples.

## Background & Description

The [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*) is an introduced species in California waters that supports an important recreational fishery. All examined fish exceeded the minimum legal length of 12 inches. Stomachs were collected on successive Saturdays throughout the year by two anglers (Leon and Emil Adams), following principal bass migration patterns. Collection localities included the region between San Rafael and Martinez (summer/fall) and the delta portion of the San Joaquin River between Antioch and the mouth of Middle River (winter/spring).

No significant differences in food types were found between large (esophagus diameter >0.55 inches) and small (esophagus diameter <0.55 inches) fish in this sample. Stomachs were preserved in formalin, and contents were sorted, identified, and measured for both number and volume of organisms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study period (1947-1948) coincided with a decline in [[Striped Bass]] abundance since approximately 1944, as indicated by catch records. This decline potentially correlates with a progressive decrease in [[Crago]] shrimp populations from approximately 2,500,000 pounds during the 1930s to 500,000-1,000,000 pounds at the time of study.

Summer represents the principal feeding and growing season for [[Striped Bass]], while winter feeding and growth are at a minimum. The [[San Francisco Bay]] and Delta system supports abundant populations of mysid shrimp ([[Neomysis]]) throughout the year, which are particularly important for young-of-year bass.

[[Striped Bass]] are known to be voracious and relatively undiscriminating feeders, consuming available fishes and invertebrates of appropriate size. Schools feed intermittently, which affects stomach content analysis from angler-caught specimens.

## Key Findings

**Summer/Fall Sample (229 stomachs):**
- 72% contained food of some sort
- 28% were empty
- [[Crago]] shrimp present in 35% of stomachs, representing 53% of identifiable material by volume
- [[Northern Anchovy]] (*Engraulis mordax*) found in 11% of stomachs, comprising 39% of total volume
- Other prey included isopods (6% occurrence), mysid shrimp ([[Neomysis]], 2%), [[Staghorn Sculpin]] (*Leptocottus armatus*, 1%), flatfish (0.5%), and smelt (0.5%)
- 23% contained sardine bait
- 30% contained unidentifiable digested material

**Winter/Spring Sample (158 stomachs):**
- 42% were empty stomachs
- [[Neomysis]] most frequently occurring food (20% of stomachs), but only 20% of identifiable volume
- [[Crago]] shrimp in 8% of stomachs, 13% of volume
- Remains of small fish found in 9% of stomachs but comprised 64% of total identifiable volume
- Other items: isopods (3%), smelt (2%), [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*, 1%), tule fragments (*Scirpus*, 1%)
- 28% contained sardine bait
- 32% contained unidentifiable digested material

**Methodological Limitations:**
The authors acknowledged significant sampling biases: (1) stomachs obtained only from principal fishing grounds, missing widely foraging fish; (2) intermittent feeding behavior of bass schools means many angler-caught fish had recently begun feeding on bait after digesting natural meals; (3) high percentages of empty stomachs (28-42%) and sardine bait (23-28%) confirm this bias. Previous studies found similar patterns: 31 of 48 angling-caught bass had empty stomachs (Shapovalov, 1936), and 57% of 224 gill-net caught bass had empty stomachs (Hatton, 1940).

The authors concluded that angler-collected stomachs cannot provide an adequate picture of adult population food sources regardless of sample size, and discontinued this line of work.
