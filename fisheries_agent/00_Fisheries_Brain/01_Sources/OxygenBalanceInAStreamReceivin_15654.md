# Oxygen Balance in a Stream Receiving Domestic and Oil Refinery Effluents

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology #community-metabolism #phytoplankton

*Note: This is a doctoral dissertation on the ecology of a polluted prairie stream. Its primary subjects are water chemistry, algae, and whole-community oxygen metabolism. Fish and macroinvertebrates appear only as secondary indicators of recovery, so no single fish species is the focus.*

## Executive Summary

This 1966 Oklahoma State University dissertation by [[Ray K. Baumgardner]] examines the [[Oxygen Balance]] of [[Skeleton Creek]], a small prairie stream in north-central Oklahoma receiving sewage from several municipal and institutional sources and effluent from an [[Oil Refinery]]. Monthly physico-chemical and biological sampling ran from March 1963 to July 1964 at eight stations spanning 97 km. Twenty-three upstream-downstream diurnal dissolved oxygen analyses were used to estimate [[Community Metabolism]] through gross photosynthesis and community respiration. The study concludes that photosynthesis is a substantial oxygen source in this system, supplying roughly 40% of community oxygen demand in the [[Oxygen Sag Zone]], and that the stream recovers toward net autotrophy downstream.

## Background & Description

The dissertation begins from the premise that many stream oxygen budgets ignore photosynthesis as unimportant, although it may be significant and must be accounted for in [[Oxygen Balance]] studies. Earlier work relied on [[Light and Dark Bottle Method]]s, which capture only phytoplankton, whereas the [[Upstream-Downstream Diurnal Method]] (after [[Odum]], 1956) measures whole-community metabolism, including benthic processes.

**Study area:** Skeleton Creek originates in Garfield County, about 13 km northwest of Enid, and flows about 121 km southeast to the Cimarron River in Logan County. Elevation falls from 387 m to 277 m, with an average gradient of 1.1 m/km. The channel is a shallow prairie stream, 6–9 m wide (about 15 m near the mouth), with riffles of 15–30 cm and pools exceeding 1.5 m. The watershed covers approximately 161,840 ha, about 80% of it cultivated or open pasture. The basin sits on Permian sandstone, clays and shales ("Red Beds"), and winter wheat is the main crop. Mean annual rainfall is 76 cm, with wide variation.

**Waste sources:** Inputs included about 340,650 liters/day of domestic sewage from two lagoons, effluent from Northeastern State Hospital, Air Force Base sewage on Boggy Creek, and Enid municipal sewage. The [[Oil Refinery]] discharge of about 2.7 million liters/day passed through a separator, a holding lake, settling pits, CO₂ pH adjustment and five oxidation ponds. Oil field brines from older wells (between km 10 and 71) also reached the creek.

**Sampling stations:** Eight stations were numbered by distance (km) below the Boggy Creek confluence: 6, 18, 24, 31, 40, 50, 71 and 97.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physico-chemical conditions:**
- Seasons were defined as cool (December–February) and warm (March–November).
- Mean warm-season water temperature was 22.4 °C and cool-season 3.3 °C. Solar radiation averaged 461 cal/cm² day (warm) and 265 cal/cm² day (cool).
- Mean monthly flow at km 76 peaked at 1081.6 m³/min in July, with a maximum of 10,302 m³/min on 12 July after a 16.5 cm storm. The stream overflowed its banks at least six times.
- Turbidity rose from 13 ppm (Station 6) to 175 ppm (Station 97) in the warm season.
- Alkalinity was mainly bicarbonate, ranging from about 181 to 445 ppm. pH ranged from 7.1 to 8.6.
- Conductivity and chloride were elevated by [[Oil Field Brine]] inputs, with chlorides from 132 to 349 ppm.
- BOD fell 56% over the 91 km stretch in January (21.0 to 11.76 ppm). COD fell about 30% in both seasons.
- Dissolved oxygen (DO) showed a classic [[Oxygen Sag Zone]] below outfalls. Cool-season DO ranged from 3.9 to 21.0 ppm, and warm-season DO from 0.2 to 16.6 ppm.

**Biological conditions:**
- Twenty-seven algal genera were identified (55 species or groups across five phyla). The dominant genera were [[Euglena]], [[Navicula]] and [[Chlamydomonas]], with [[Algal Bloom]]s of Euglena and Chlamydomonas observed.
- Phytoplankton species richness stayed roughly constant (about 15 to 18) along the stream, with a peak of 22 at Station 71. Fish species rose from 1 at Station 6 to 17 at Station 97, and macroinvertebrates from 6 to 24, per [[Phillips]] (1965) and [[Wilhm]] (1965).
- Benthic chlorophyll a was dominant in the upper reaches, reaching 0.318 g/m² at Station 6, while planktonic chlorophyll was higher downstream, reaching 0.240 g/m² at Station 40.

## Key Findings

1. **Seasonal water quality:** Flow and turbidity were lower in the cool season. Alkalinity, pH, conductivity, BOD, COD, total residue and DO were higher in the cool season.
2. **Oxygen sag:** DO variation was large in both space and time. The sag minimum shifted, occurring at Station 26 in June, Station 39 in May, and below Station 39 in February. At Station 26, DO reached 0.5–1.5 ppm (6–9% saturation).
3. **Community metabolism in the sag zone:** Five stretches were analyzed over one year. Highest respiration occurred at Stretch 26.6–28.2 and lowest at Stretches 30.2–31.5 and 48.3–49.4. Stretch 36.3–38.7 had the highest productivity, ranging from 13.5 g O₂/m² day (January) to 29.4 g O₂/m² day (July). It was the only sag-zone stretch where photosynthesis supplied more oxygen than atmospheric diffusion.
4. **Photosynthetic share:** Photosynthesis supplied about 40% of total oxygen demand in the sag zone.
5. **Upper-reach productivity:** The highest gross production measured was 60.4 g O₂/m² day in the shallow upper region (Stretch 9.3–11.2, June 1963), with respiration of 81.8 g O₂/m² day.
6. **P/R ratios:** Productivity exceeded respiration only in the lower reaches. The P/R ratio rose from 0.25 in the sag zone to 3.6 in the lower reaches, indicating recovery. The lower reaches' P/R ratio (3.6) was higher than that reported for the White River recovery zone (3.2).
7. **Photosynthetic efficiency:** Efficiency ranged from 0.48% to 6.4% (mean 2.1%). The highest value occurred in June at Stretch 9.3–11.2, and the lowest in the sag zone at Stretch 30.2–31.5 after flooding.
8. **Recovery pattern:** Skeleton Creek resembled the sewage-polluted White River, Indiana. Water quality improved substantially after about 60 km of flow, and macroinvertebrate and fish populations rose markedly in the productive sag-zone stretch.
9. **Methodological limits:** Diffusion coefficients measured with the plastic dome and the Scholander device were 1.02–4.06 g O₂/m² hr in summer. Night-time calculations gave unrealistic values, so estimated exchange coefficients (1.3–3.7 g O₂/m² hr) were used instead.
10. **Management implication:** The author notes that many states require DO not to fall below 5 ppm for extended periods. Photosynthetic oxygen production should be included in such assessments.
