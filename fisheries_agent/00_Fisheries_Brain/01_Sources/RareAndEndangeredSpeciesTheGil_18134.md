# Rare and endangered Species: the Gila and Apache trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #conservation-biology #[[Salmonidae]]

## Executive Summary

This report by Robert Behnke synthesizes early taxonomic and ecological data on two rare native trout of the American Southwest: the [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*), described by R.R. Miller (1950) from Diamond Creek, Sierra County, New Mexico, and the closely related but morphologically distinct [[Apache Trout]] (tentatively *Salmo gilae apache* or *Salmo apache*) native to headwater tributaries of the White River (Salt River drainage) in the White Mountains of Arizona. Both taxa, once widespread throughout the [[Gila River Basin]], were driven to the brink of extinction following European settlement, primarily through habitat degradation and introgressive hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]]. The paper argues that the Gila and Apache trouts share more morphological affinity with each other — and with the golden trout lineage (*Salmo aguabonita*, *Salmo chrysogaster*) — than either does with true rainbow or cutthroat species, suggesting a third, intermediate evolutionary lineage within the genus *Salmo*. The document also catalogs pure population localities, proposes conservation and brood-stock strategies, and lays groundwork for a comprehensive 1967 systematic survey.

## Background & Description

### Distinguishing the Gila/Apache Group from Rainbow and Cutthroat Trouts
- **Body form:** Chunky, deep-bodied trout with long heads and jaws, long fins; dorsal fin origin slightly posterior to the midpoint of standard length.
- **Pyloric caeca counts:** Significantly lower (range 22–42, mean ~28.1–34.9) than rainbow trout (50–60) or cutthroat trout (35–50). Only the [[Colorado Greenback Cutthroat]] (*Salmo clarkii stomias*) approaches this low caeca count.
- **Vertebrae:** Fewer vertebrae than rainbow or cutthroat trout (precise counts pending x-ray analysis at the time of writing).
- **Gill rakers:** Not diagnostic — overlaps with both rainbow and cutthroat ranges, though Diamond Creek Gila trout show a slightly lower mean than Apache trout samples.
- **Fin rays and branchiostegal rays:** Not useful for differentiating any of the four groups.
- **Basibranchial (hyoid) teeth:** Absent in Gila and Apache trout, a rainbow-like trait, despite general resemblance to cutthroat trout in most other characters.
- **Dorsal and adipose fins:** Longer in both Gila and Apache trout compared to rainbow or cutthroat trout.

### Differentiating Gila Trout from Apache Trout

| Character | Gila Trout (Diamond Creek) | Apache Trout |
|---|---|---|
| Spotting | Smaller, more profuse spots, especially on dorsal/caudal fins | Sparser spotting pattern, resembling interior cutthroats |
| Scale counts | Fewer scales, esp. above lateral line | More scales, esp. above lateral line |
| Dorsal fin length | Shorter | Slightly longer |
| Adipose fin length | Longer | Shorter |
| Parr marks | Slightly more pronounced | Present, well-defined only in specimens <6 in. |
| Dorsal fin position | More anterior (per Regan 1964a on Ord Creek specimens) | More posterior relative to Gila trout |

### Coloration
- Both taxa exhibit a pale yellow cutthroat mark and grayish-white or yellow tips on dorsal, anal, and pelvic fins.
- Apache trout: dull, brassy, yellow-olive base coloration with purple and pink tints visible in certain light; lacks the bright red, yellow, or orange shades typical of true cutthroats.
- Black pigmentation on the iris creates a "mask-like" effect, which fades upon preservation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** Historically widespread across the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico. Gila trout confirmed in Diamond Creek, Eagle Creek, Spruce Creek, and McKenna Creek. Apache trout confirmed as pure populations in [[Ord Creek]], [[Firebox Creek]], [[Deep Creek]], the East Fork of the White River, and Paddy Creek (Little Colorado basin).
- **Habitat:** Small, brushy headwater rivulets in the White Mountains of Arizona — the "hub" from which the San Francisco, Salt, and Little Colorado River drainages radiate.
- **Species interactions:** In [[Ord Creek]], Apache trout coexist with introduced [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] in lower reaches, while pure Apache trout dominate headwaters. 1966 sampling suggested Apache trout were "holding its own" in interspecific competition.
- **Threats:** Primary conservation concern is introgressive hybridization with introduced rainbow and cutthroat trout, which erodes reproductive isolation and taxonomic purity. Neither Gila nor Apache trout maintain full reproductive isolation from these introduced species when sympatric.
- **Historical range questions:** Trout once inhabited the [[Verde River]] system (Salt River division) but their identity relative to Apache trout is unresolved. Debate exists (Behnke vs. Miller) over whether Apache trout are native or introduced to the Little Colorado River basin; Behnke argues for natural headwater transfer based on drainage topography and historical (1872 Wheeler Survey, Cope & Yarrow 1875) specimen records.

## Key Findings

1. **Taxonomic status:** Behnke proposes subspecies-level recognition — *Salmo gilae gilae* (Gila trout) and *Salmo gilae apache* (Apache trout) — rather than full species status, given their close mutual affinity relative to rainbow and cutthroat trout.
2. **Phylogenetic placement:** Gila and Apache trout show closer affinity to the [[California Golden Trout]] (*Salmo aguabonita*) and [[Mexican Golden Trout]] (*Salmo chrysogaster*, Needham & Gard 1964) than to rainbow or cutthroat lineages, supporting a proposed third "golden trout" evolutionary line within *Salmo*.
3. **Historical misidentification corrected:** Specimens from the Little Colorado River previously identified by Jordan and Evermann (1896) as Colorado River cutthroat (*Salmo clarkii pleuriticus*) were re-examined by Miller (1961) and confirmed to actually be Apache trout; true Colorado River cutthroat did not naturally occur in Arizona.
4. **Pure population sites identified:** Contrary to the prevailing view that only Ord Creek held pure Apache trout, Behnke's examinations confirmed additional pure populations in Firebox Creek, Deep Creek, the headwaters of the East Fork White River, and Paddy Creek.
5. **Conservation recommendations:**
   - Rigorous protection of all native trout streams (and tributary lakes) from further introductions.
   - Stocking of fishless, isolated lakes with Apache or Gila trout where spawning habitat is suitable.
   - Limited sport fishing to build public appreciation without threatening population viability.
   - An "inter-racial hybridization" experiment combining brood stock from multiple pure Apache populations (Ord Creek, E. Fork White River, Deep Creek, Firebox Creek) to increase heterozygosity for future introductions — explicitly not to be released into existing native streams.
6. **Cytogenetic context:** Simon (1964) found diploid chromosome numbers of 58 in California golden trout, 60 in rainbow trout, 70 in coastal cutthroat, and 64 in interior cutthroat — suggesting chromosome counts on Gila/Apache trout could offer supplementary (though not definitive) evolutionary insight.
7. **Future work planned:** A comprehensive 1967 collecting expedition was proposed to resolve outstanding systematic questions across the Gila River basin, particularly in the White Mountains "hub" region, with cooperation from Arizona and New Mexico Fish and Game Departments.
