# Biological Recovery Goals and Criteria for the Four Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service-linked report proposes quantified, biological recovery goals and criteria for four federally listed Upper Colorado River fishes: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. Because no existing document provided downlisting and delisting criteria, the authors developed population-based benchmarks (number of populations, distribution, abundance, abundance trend, natural recruitment, and size structure) that can be estimated annually from standardized monitoring data held in the [[Recovery Program]] centralized database.

The overarching principle is that populations must be maintained through natural recruitment in the wild. The goals describe viable, self-sustaining populations and are explicitly limited to biological criteria; habitat protection and institutional commitments are noted as necessary but are not quantified here. Colorado Pikeminnow populations appear stable or increasing, Humpback Chub populations have persisted at all known sites, and Razorback Sucker and Bonytail are considered clearly endangered, with data judged inadequate to set contextual baselines for the latter two.

## Background & Description

The four species are large-bodied, long-lived native fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Colorado Pikeminnow is a late-maturing, long-lived predator; Humpback Chub is a canyon-dwelling cyprinid; Razorback Sucker and Bonytail are native catostomid and cyprinid species with severely reduced wild populations. Recovery planning had been fragmented: the 1992 Colorado Interim Recovery Objectives, the Colorado and Utah stocking plans, and separate population viability analyses addressed parts of recovery but gave no integrated downlisting or delisting criteria.

The Endangered Species Act (ESA) defines "conservation" as the use of all methods necessary to bring a species to the point where protections are no longer needed. The report interprets recovery as restoring demographic status to a condition where the species is no longer at risk of extinction, not restoration to full historic range.

Methods relied on [[Pit Tag]] (Passive Integrated Transponder) mark-recapture data, which replaced externally attached tags and reduced tag loss. Population estimates used the Chapman modification of the Petersen method, with confidence intervals from Poisson tables (Ricker 1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Population-level criteria are linked to ecological processes. Recruitment into the adult population reflects successful reproduction, nursery habitat use, and dispersal, particularly for Colorado Pikeminnow, whose Green River nursery habitats support tributary adult populations in the Yampa and White rivers. Connectivity among population segments, through passage structures such as the Redlands passage on the Gunnison River, supports gene flow and recolonization.

Principal threats cited include altered flow regimes from dams ([[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]), loss of flooded bottomland and backwater nursery habitat, barriers to instream passage, and predation and competition by nonnative fishes. The report notes that the contribution of these threats to ongoing declines has not been documented for most populations.

Genetic viability is addressed through effective population size (Ne). The authors apply Ne values of 500 to 1,000 and a Ne/N ratio of 0.5, while acknowledging that Ne/N estimates for these species are uncertain and that alternative values (for example, 0.1 in Frankham 1995) would greatly increase the required adult population.

## Key Findings

**Overall framework**
- Recovery criteria are population parameters: number of populations, distribution, abundance, abundance trend, natural recruitment, and size structure.
- Abundance and trend criteria use a generation-time basis: 12 years for Colorado Pikeminnow and Razorback Sucker delisting, 8 years for Humpback Chub and Bonytail.
- Recruitment and size-structure goals use rolling averages, with five-year averages for Colorado Pikeminnow and three-year averages for Humpback Chub.

**Colorado Pikeminnow**
- Population estimates were developed for Green, Colorado, White, Yampa, and San Juan populations, mostly 1991–1997 data; the Colorado River estimates (900–1,200 approx.) and Green River estimates (approx. 2,000–7,400) were the largest.
- Abundance and fish/mile appear stable (Colorado, Yampa) or increasing (White, Green), though yearly changes are unlikely to be statistically significant given overlapping 95% confidence intervals.
- Recruitment on the Colorado River declined from over 30% (1991–1992) to 4% (1995–1997), which the authors do not consider an immediate concern given the abundance trend.
- Downlisting requires three required populations (Green, Colorado, San Juan), with only two needing to meet criteria; delisting adds stricter recruitment (15% versus 10%) and a 12-year trend requirement.
- The authors propose a minimum adult target of about 1,600 fish for the Green River, 1,600 for the Colorado River, and 1,200 for the San Juan River, with an augmented mortality buffer of 14%.

**Humpback Chub**
- Seven population segments are known, aggregated into five proposed populations or complexes: Black Rocks-Westwater Canyon, Little Colorado-Grand Canyon, Yampa-Whirlpool-Split Mountain, Cataract Canyon, and Desolation-Gray Canyons.
- Black Rocks population estimates were about 455 fish in 1997 at 59% survival; Westwater Canyon estimates declined steadily from 1993 to 1997 under all three survival scenarios, though the authors note this may partly reflect improving estimate accuracy.
- Grand Canyon population estimates include 5,000 to 9,000-range values reported elsewhere (e.g., 53,707 fish in the Colorado River in Grand Canyon, Valdez and Ryel 1997), while Little Colorado River estimates were 4,508 fish (Douglas and Marsh 1996).
- Downlisting requires two of five populations to meet an abundance goal; delisting requires three.
- The proposed abundance target is 2,000 adult fish (over 175 mm), with a buffer for 24% mortality giving 2,500 fish.
- Proposed generation time is about eight years, and the trend criterion requires at least five estimates over that period.

**Razorback Sucker**
- Only one wild population remains in the middle Green River, with recruitment inadequate to offset a declining abundance trend.
- Lake Mojave is maintained by augmentation stocking and is considered functionally extinct in the wild for the species' broader range.
- Five prospective recovery populations were proposed (Lake Mojave, middle Green, lower Green, Colorado-Gunnison, San Juan), with a 5,400-fish downlisting abundance requirement for the chosen populations.
- The authors state that abundance requirements are arbitrary at present because historic data are inadequate.

**Bonytail**
- Functionally extinct in the wild; the species is represented almost entirely by a captive stock from Dexter National Fish Hatchery, derived from a restricted genetic base (wild adults captured from Lake Mojave in 1980s).
- Downlisting requires two populations and delisting three, with abundance goals identical for both.
- Recommended potential reaches include the Yampa-middle Green, lower Green, upper Colorado (Debeque Canyon, Grand Valley), and Moab Canyon-Professor Valley reaches.
- Stocking plans recommend restoration stocking to develop populations, and quantitative downlisting and delisting criteria are deferred until more data are available.

**Caveats**
- Estimates are sensitive to assumed survival rates (for example, 0.70 vs. 0.86 for Colorado Pikeminnow), and the report acknowledges closure and equal-sampling assumptions are simplifications.
- Goals are described as provisional and subject to revision as new PIT-tag and monitoring data accumulate.
