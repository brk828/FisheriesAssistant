# Gonopodial Function and Copulatory Behavior in Gambusia (Poeciliidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 study in the *Canadian Journal of Zoology* describes how male [[Gambusia]] use the [[Gonopodium]] (the modified anal fin used to transfer sperm) and the [[Pectoral Fin]] during [[Copulation]]. Using super 8-mm film and hollowed-out [[Artificial Female Models]] that recorded gonopodial imprints, the author proposes that the gonopodium is folded and twisted beneath the pectoral fin before thrust, and that males often rotate their bodies head-to-tail with the female during thrust. The study covers 14 Gambusia species and shows that this copulatory pattern differs from that described for related genera such as [[Xiphophorus]] and [[Poecilia]].

## Background & Description

The genus *Gambusia* contains more than 30 known species in Central America, the West Indies, and the southeastern United States. The gonopodium is the main character used for its taxonomy and classification, but earlier work had described little about how its parts function during copulation (Rosen and Gordon 1953; Rosen and Tucker 1961).

Males used in the study were small, about 20–30 mm total length, and moved too quickly for direct observation. The author therefore relied on indirect methods:

- **Courtship trials:** Male and conspecific female pairs were held in aquaria (61 × 21 × 16 cm), separated by a partition for at least 24 h, and courtship was recorded for the first 10–15 min of each observation period.
- **Artificial models:** Plaster of Paris female models with a hollowed anal region filled with shortening recorded gonopodial imprints. A painted black anal spot motivated courtship.
- **Film:** Super 8-mm cameras at 24 frames per second recorded courtships.

Gonopodial terminology follows Rosen and Bailey (1963). The paper focuses on *Gambusia heterochir*, which has more specialized fin and genital structures, but it also reports on other species, with *Xenodexia* and *Xiphophorus helleri* used for comparison.

**Courtship displays.** Two displays were identified. The [[Frontal Display]] is conspicuous: the male quivers, swims in an arc to approach from behind, and thrusts below the female's anal area. The [[Lateral Display]] is slow and inconspicuous, with the male drifting alongside the female and then swimming backward to thrust upward. Most often, however, males did not display and simply approached from behind to thrust.

**Key definitions:**
- **[[Thrust Behavior]]:** any upward propulsion of the gonopodium toward a female or model after it has been swung into the precopulatory position.
- **[[Copulation]]:** any insertion of the gonopodium into the female's [[Urogenital Sinus]], inferred to allow insemination. Sperm confirmation was considered hazardous because females store sperm across several ovulatory cycles.
- **[[Nibbling Behavior]]:** contact of the mouth or snout with the female's abdomen, distinct from a vigorous nip.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study is laboratory-based and addresses behavior and morphology rather than field ecology. Several points relate to the species' reproductive biology:

- **Female receptivity.** Virgin females were often frightened by males and were not receptive, while females reared with conspecific males copulated. In related work, receptivity in [[Poecilia]] was linked to a female response to male courtship (Liley 1966).
- **Female genital anatomy.** In species whose female urogenital sinus opens posteriorly ([[Gambusia Marshi]] and [[Gambusia Regani]]), a posteriorly directed thrust may be more effective for insemination.
- **Sperm storage.** Females store sperm from earlier courtships, so copulation can be observed even when sperm is not confirmed.
- **Species differences.** Differences in gonopodial morphology were expected to produce behavioral differences that could act as partial isolating mechanisms, which the author reserved for a separate paper.

## Key Findings

**1. Position of the gonopodium before thrust.** Before thrust and copulation, the gonopodium is folded beneath the pectoral fin, with gonopodial ray 3 twisted under ray 5. This orients the spines of ray 3 laterally and points the elbow of ray 4a upward, where it may contact or lock with the pectoral fin. The author states this differs from the arrangement described for *Xiphophorus*, where the groove faces upward.

**2. Pectoral fin support.** Thickened outer halves of pectoral rays 2 or 3 through 5 face downward and support the gonopodium. Scuff marks posterior to gonopodial imprints on models supported the conclusion that the gonopodium lies beneath the fin. Pelvic fins also appear to support the gonopodium, though their precise position was not determined. [[Xenodexia]] males likewise place the gonopodium below the right pectoral fin.

**3. Body rotation during thrust.** Males of all 14 studied species were observed on occasion to rotate their bodies until they faced head-to-tail with females and models. Ryder's (1885) nineteenth-century observation of this behavior, which had been questioned by later authors, is judged likely to have been correct. The rotation is in a direction that helps maintain the twist and folding of the gonopodium, and the author notes it may be unique to Gambusia among poeciliids.

Observed frequencies of full body rotation during thrust (Table 2):

| Species | Thrusts on models with full rotation | Thrusts on models without rotation | Thrusts on film with rotation | Thrusts on film without rotation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *G. affinis* | 8 | 15 | 3 | 2 |
| *G. marshi* | 26 | 4 | — | — |
| *G. regani* | 20 | 0 | 7 | 5 |

Rotation was recorded in other species too (e.g., *G. gaigei*, *G. heterochir*, *G. atrora*, *G. vittata*), but these rows are summarized here. For *G. marshi* and *G. regani*, nearly all thrusts that left imprints involved rotation. Of their thrusts, 19 of *G. regani* and 23 of *G. marshi* displaced shortening entirely posteriorly.

**4. Gonopodial and pectoral adaptations to rotation.** Body rotation probably places stress on the twisted gonopodium, and pectoral support helps withstand it. The upward bend of the distal pectoral rays keeps the fin tip away from the female as the male rotates underneath her. The author concludes that the direction of body rotation and gonopodial twisting probably aids efficient sperm transfer.

**5. Function of gonopodial parts.** The author proposes the following roles:
- **[[Gonopodial Spines]] (ray 3):** Terminal, thin spines are the leading structures entering the urogenital sinus and act as a thin blade that the rest of the gonopodium follows. This differs from earlier interpretations of tactile function.
- **[[Gonopodial Hooks]] (rays 4p and 5):** Hooks thread the gonopodium in the direction of body rotation and prevent unwinding after entry. Species with small hooks on these rays have female urogenital sinuses that lead posteriorly.
- **[[Gonopodial Elbow]] (ray 4a):** The elbow hooks onto the modified pectoral rays. This is supported by an experiment on *G. heterochir*: in nine males with the gonopodial tip amputated distal to the elbow (controls), 15 of 19 thrusts left deep holes, whereas in nine males with the elbow amputated, all 25 thrusts left only shallow grooves (chi-square p < .05 for control versus experimental imprint distribution, reported as p < .001 for the experimental group in Table 3). The author notes the pooled data were modest in size, with three or four males contributing thrusts to each group.
- **Gonopodial serrae (ray 4p):** The function was not clearly determined.

**6. Species variation.** *G. vittata* lacks a gonopodial elbow and gonopodial notch. Its males nibble the female's belly for many seconds before each thrust, and nibbling is the dominant pre-copulatory activity, suggesting sensory cues are transferred during nibbling. Across species, nibbling was infrequent in most, and nibbling did not precede most recorded thrusts and copulations.

**7. Courtship and copulation frequencies (Table 1).** Male courtship toward live females over 10–15 min periods included, for example:
- *G. affinis*: 9 males, 90 approaches, 28 thrusts, 1 copulation.
- *G. heterochir*: 9 males, 86 observation periods, 1357 approaches, 242 thrusts, 2 copulations.
- *G. geiseri*: 2 males, 314 approaches, 157 thrusts, no copulations.
- *G. nobilis*: 3 males, 397 approaches, 62 thrusts, no copulations.

Copulation was rare in all species. No copulation followed courtship display in this study, so the author concludes that courtship displays are not required for copulation in Gambusia and that Liley's distinction between thrust and copulation attempt should not be applied to Gambusia.

**8. Sperm in models.** Large clusters of spermatophores were found in holes made by *G. heterochir* gonopodia in shortening on at least three occasions. Because shortening is not equivalent to live female tissue, copulation was counted only with live females.

**9. Broader significance.** The author argues that the gonopodium's role in classification makes its function important for [[Gambusia Systematics]]. Variation in gonopodial characters could be modified through character displacement in sympatric populations. The study's methods, especially artificial models, could be applied to other poeciliid genera in future work.
