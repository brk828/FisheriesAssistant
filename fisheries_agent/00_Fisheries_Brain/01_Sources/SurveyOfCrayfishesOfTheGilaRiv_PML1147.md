# Survey of Crayfishes of the Gila River Basin, Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 survey, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Phoenix Area Office) by Arizona State University researchers, is the first systematic inventory of crayfishes in the [[Gila River Basin]] and, more broadly, in Arizona. No crayfish species are native to the [[Colorado River Basin]], including the Gila drainage, yet the animals have been widely introduced and are naturalized in the wild.

Field collections were made at 94 locales in Arizona and New Mexico, and retail aquarium and pet stores were visited a dozen times in the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]]. Two non-native species were captured in the wild: [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*) and [[Northern Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*). Both were widespread, common to locally abundant, and occupied both lotic and lentic habitats. The authors judged that very large populations are likely detrimental to native fauna, and that the release of pet-trade crayfish should be discouraged.

## Background & Description

Crayfish belong to the decapod order Decapoda, in the infraorder Astacidea alongside marine lobsters. Two superfamilies are recognized: Astacoidea (families Astacidae and [[Cambaridae]]), restricted to the Northern Hemisphere, and Parastacoidea (family [[Parastacidae]]), restricted to the Southern Hemisphere. North America north of Mexico holds 338 recognized taxa (308 species and 30 subspecies). Arizona is the only continental US state with no native crayfish species.

Crayfish reached Arizona about 30 years before the survey, stocked by the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service for aquatic weed control and as forage for sport fish. Other introduction routes include discard by aquarium owners and escape or release from [[Bait Bucket Release]] events. The authors note that AZGFD records list introductions at only three stream and lake sites (1971 and 1991), so the documented record is likely incomplete.

Species were identified mainly from Form I adult males, using pleopod morphology, following Hobbs (1972). Appendix A provides a dichotomous key. Because molting can change body size, life stage should not be judged by size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species accounts**

- ***Procambarus clarkii* ([[Red Swamp Crayfish]])**: Native to northeastern Mexico and the south-central US. It is highly adaptable, has been introduced to 14 states and at least 17 countries, and is a nuisance in places where it damages rice, taro, netted fish, dikes, and water control structures through burrowing. It is a [[Tertiary Burrower]] that digs holes 1 to 2 m deep. It is an opportunistic detritivore and can tolerate brackish water up to 10 ppt. Eggs number 50 to 600 per clutch, and the species has a lifespan of about one year to 18 months in the wild.
- ***Orconectes virilis* ([[Northern Crayfish]])**: Historically distributed across northern North America, and now the most widely introduced crayfish in North America, with range extended to 16 states. It tolerates water temperatures of 1 to 32 °C and was found in Arizona from about 300 m at Tempe to more than 2,500 m in the White Mountains. Its burrows are generally under 1 m long and pose little threat to structures. Females reach maturity at about 2 years, and the maximum reported natural lifespan is 3 years.
- ***Procambarus alleni* ([[Procambarus Alleni]])**: Native to central and southern Florida. No wild introductions outside its native range were recorded, and the survey found no specimens above or below [[Lake Pleasant]], where it was sold as bait. Trade names include "white prince" and the misleading "Australian blue lobster," which is neither Australian nor a lobster.
- ***Procambarus paeninsulanus* ([[Procambarus Paeninsulanus]])**: Native to the Choctawhatchee River Basin in southern Georgia, Alabama, and Florida, with a conservation status considered stable. Advertised in a Tempe store, but the specimens were later identified as *P. clarkii*, and the authors could not confirm that it was sold.
- ***Orconectes causeyi* ([[Orconectes Causeyi]])**: Its taxonomic status is disputed. The authors judged the specimens they examined morphologically indistinguishable from *O. virilis* and treated *O. causeyi* as a synonym.
- ***Cherax* (possibly *C. quadricarinatus*, [[Red Claw Crayfish]])**: An unidentified Australian parastacid, 18 cm long, found only as a display specimen. Cherax is the most widespread and numerous Australian genus (30 species) and is valued in aquaculture.

**Habitat associations**

Both introduced species are generalists. Samples included about three times as many lotic as lentic sites, so the data are biased toward streams. [[Lotic Habitat]] use was more common for *P. clarkii*, while *O. virilis* was more often found in [[Lentic Habitat]]. Crayfish were generally absent from dense plant beds. Abundance was higher in moderately turbid water, and finer substrates were preferred. The authors stress that sample sizes were small and the data were not statistically analyzed.

**Distribution in neighboring regions**

No bordering state has native crayfish in the Colorado drainage. Native species in neighboring states (California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, and Utah) and 46 named Mexican taxa (all native) have potential for interbasin transfer into Arizona.

**Impacts on native fauna**

The authors cite several documented effects:
- *O. virilis* preys on eggs of [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] and may indirectly alter habitat, which is a concern for this imperiled species.
- *O. virilis* is linked to declines of [[Lake Trout]] in the Great Lakes and to the decimation of a trout fishery in Newcastle Reservoir, Utah.
- Crayfish threaten the [[Spikedace]], which is endemic to the Gila basin and survives only in a few remnant populations.

## Key Findings

- **No native crayfish** occur in Arizona or the Colorado River basin.
- **Two non-native species** were found in the wild: *P. clarkii* and *O. virilis*. Both were widespread and common to locally abundant. The two were allopatric except at one Phoenix-area canal.
- ***O. virilis* was collected across all major Arizona drainages**, while *P. clarkii* was found at fewer sites and was mostly restricted to the Colorado River mainstem, along with the Salt River and associated canals and a Hassayampa River tributary in the Gila basin.
- **Pet-store survey**: three taxa were encountered, but only two were accurately catalogued. [[Procambarus Alleni]] was almost always available at several retail outlets, while *P. paeninsulanus* could not be confirmed.
- **Sampling scope**: 94 field localities and 12 retail store visits. Appendix E lists habitat and elevation for each site; sites ranged from about 46 m (Colorado River, Yuma) to above 2,700 m (White Mountains).
- **Management recommendations**: future introductions are likely and should be discouraged. Research on ecological effects of *P. clarkii* and *O. virilis* is needed, especially where endangered native fishes occur.
- **Regulatory note**: the only crayfish family prohibited in Arizona is Parastacidae.
