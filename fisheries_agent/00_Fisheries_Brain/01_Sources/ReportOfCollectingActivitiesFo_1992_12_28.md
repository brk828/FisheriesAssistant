# Report of Collecting Activities for 1992 in Virgin River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This document is a 1992 annual **Report of Activities** submitted by Ross Timmons (Arizona State University, Dept. of Zoology) to the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (DWR), fulfilling the reporting requirement for a scientific collecting certificate. The collections were conducted in the **[[Virgin River]]**, Washington County, Utah, in the reach extending downstream from the **Washington Fields Diversion**. The source material is a heavily degraded OCR scan of a handwritten DWR "Report of Activities" form (form LO-26A), documenting fish species collected across multiple dates and specific Township/Range/Section locations in 1992. The cover letter also requests renewal of collecting permits for 1993, indicating this was part of an ongoing, likely multi-year monitoring effort of the Virgin River fish fauna, a system of high conservation concern due to its endemic and federally listed species.

## Background & Description
Due to the fragmentary and degraded nature of the OCR'd data table, detailed morphological descriptions are not present in the source text. The report is a regulatory/administrative log rather than a morphological study. Species identifiable from the abundance/collection table include:

- **[[Pantosteus clarki]]** (Desert Sucker) — a benthic catostomid characterized by a subterminal, cartilage-edged mouth adapted for scraping algae/periphyton from cobble substrates.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — a large-bodied catostomid with a distinctively fleshy, papillose lower lip ("flannel mouth") used for benthic foraging in sand/gravel runs.

No quantitative morphometric data (length, weight, meristic counts) survived the OCR extraction; the table columns for "Age/Sex" and "Number Collected" contain values that could not be reliably parsed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: The Virgin River in this reach is a warm-water, turbid, sand-and-cobble-bedded desert river typical of the lower Colorado River Basin tributaries. The collection sites span multiple Township/Range/Section designations (e.g., T42S R15W, T42S R15W S28-33) below the **[[Washington Fields Diversion]]**, indicating sampling across a longitudinal gradient of altered flow (a diversion structure) and downstream riverine habitat.
- **Habitat Index / Abundance Index**: The DWR form includes standardized "Habitat Index" and "Abundance Index" fields (coded per instructions on the back of the original form), suggesting semi-quantitative habitat association and relative abundance scoring was intended for each species/site combination, though the specific codes are not recoverable from this OCR.
- **Community context**: Both **[[Desert Sucker]]** and **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** are native catostomids that co-occur in the Virgin River alongside other endemic cyprinids such as the **[[Virgin River Chub]]** (*Gila seminuda*), **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), **[[Virgin Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*), and **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*) — though only the sucker taxa are legibly confirmed in this particular OCR extraction. The Virgin River is recognized as a biogeographically distinct and imperiled fish assemblage within the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Diversion impacts**: The specific framing of the study area as beginning at "Washington Fields diversion downstream" implies an interest in documenting fish distribution and abundance in relation to irrigation/diversion infrastructure, a well-documented stressor on native Virgin River fishes (dewatering, fragmentation, non-native fish introduction via canals).

## Key Findings
- The report documents field collections of at least two native catostomid species — **[[Desert Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus clarki*) and **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — from the Virgin River below the Washington Fields Diversion during 1992.

- The submission is explicitly tied to a state regulatory mechanism (Utah DWR Scientific Collecting Certificate), underscoring that this work fed into official state records on current distribution and status of Utah's wildlife species.
