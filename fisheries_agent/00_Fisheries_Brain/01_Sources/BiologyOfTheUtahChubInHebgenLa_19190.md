# Biology of the Utah Chub in Hebgen Lake, Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by Richard J. Graham (Oklahoma State University) documents the biology of the [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*) in [[Hebgen Lake]], an artificial impoundment on the [[Madison River]] in Montana. The species was probably introduced by live-bait fishermen around 1935 and became the most abundant fish in the reservoir, reaching peak abundance about 1948. The work covered abundance and seasonal distribution from gill-net surveys (1948, 1953-54), food habits from stomach analyses, spawning timing and egg production, fry distribution, and age and growth from scales. The study was undertaken to support a fisheries management program for the lake, in which the chub is regarded as a poor food fish and a nuisance to anglers because it readily takes trout baits.

## Background & Description

- **Native range:** Bonneville Basin of Utah and the upper Snake River drainage of Wyoming and Idaho (Simon, 1946).
- **Introduction:** Presumed accidental introduction into Hebgen Lake by [[Live-Bait Fishing]] anglers. Schools of minnows were first seen in 1935-37.
- **Spread:** Chubs have been collected in tributaries several miles above the lake and are known below the lake throughout the [[Madison River]].
- **Reservoir characteristics:** Created in 1914 on the [[Madison River]] in Gallatin County, about 1 mile west of [[Yellowstone National Park]]. Spillway elevation 6,544 ft; capacity 344,730 acre-feet; surface area 13,700 acres; maximum depth 61.5 ft. Maximum surface temperature 76° F. Ice forms late October to early November and clears late April to early May. Methyl orange alkalinity 77 p.p.m.; pH 7.2 (November 11, 1954).
- **Water management:** Since 1953 water has been released rapidly in fall, so the minimum level is usually reached in November. In a normal year the lake drops about 17 feet, reducing volume to 160,000 acre-feet.
- **Fish community:** Utah chub is the most abundant fish. [[Brown Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] rank next. [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Arctic Grayling]], longnose sucker, longnose dace and [[Mottled Sculpin]] occur in limited numbers.
- **Study areas:** Most work was in the shallow [[Grayling Arm]] (mostly under 15 ft, muck bottom, extensive vegetation in 1948 that declined by 1953) and the [[Madison Arm]] (about 5 miles long, 1 mile wide, sand and gravel bottom, pondweeds most abundant).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal distribution.** Gill nets indicated chubs concentrate in shallow water in spring, spread widely through summer and fall, and move to deeper water in winter. In 1954 a shallow set on May 5 under ice caught only trout and whitefish; after ice-out (May 23) nets took many chubs. A November 11, 1954 net in the shallows of Grayling Arm caught whitefish but no chubs. The author compares this with the [[Tui Chub]] in [[Eagle Lake]], California (Kimsey, 1954).

**Diet shift.** Diet changed between study periods as aquatic vegetation declined. The author links the change to the vegetation loss, which occurred in the same areas.

**Spawning environment.** Eggs were broadcast over the bottom; no nest construction or egg adhesion to plants, snags or artificial substrates was found. Fry were concentrated in protected shallow shorelines, with the heaviest concentrations in the Madison and Grayling arms. Exposed northern shores, buffeted by prevailing southwesterly winds, had few fry. In Grayling Arm, fry in 1948 occurred in silt and muck areas with abundant vegetation, but were scarce in 1953-54 when vegetation was nearly absent.

**Interaction with trout.** Graham notes that [[Jordan and Evermann]] (1896) described the chub as "extremely destructive of other fishes, especially to young trout," but no fishes were found in the stomachs of the 374 chubs examined. Limited data from Hebgen Lake did not show the trout decline reported at [[Fish Lake]], Utah (Davis, 1940; Sigler, 1935). Rainbow trout hatchery traps on tributaries took very few chubs, and no chub movement into tributaries was observed.

## Key Findings

**Food habits**
- 1948 (165 stomachs, 5.3–14.5 in): algae 48.2%, higher aquatic plants 21.1%, microcrustaceans (Cladocera) 24.3%, insects 0.7%, unidentified material and debris 3.6% (volume).
- 1953-54 (209 stomachs): insects 36%, microcrustaceans 36%, higher aquatic plants 19%, debris 9%.
- Algae occurred in 31% of stomachs in 1948 but only 7% in 1953-54. Daphnia was the most important crustacean. Diptera larvae and pupae made up about 75% of insect volume.

**Spawning**
- Peak spawning occurred in late June to early July in both 1953 and 1954.
- Most spawning occurred at water temperatures above 54° F. Average temperature during the 1953 peak (June 17–July 9) was 60.2° F (range 54–68° F).
- Spent females rose from about 33% (June 15–28) to about 76% (June 29–July 12) in 1953. In 1954, spent females were about 1% (May 23–30), 13.5% (May 31–June 20), about 63% (June 21–July 11), and over 95% from July 12 to August 6.
- Eggs were recovered from 25 of 47 bottom samples (June 25–July 9), mostly from sand and gravel, at depths of 1 to 4 ft and 2 to 150 ft from shore.
- Average fecundity was 40,750 eggs per female (range 15,900 for an 8.8-inch fish to 62,400 for a 13.8-inch fish; seven females).

**Early life history**
- First fry: August 3, 1953 (average 0.8 in) and July 7, 1954 (average 0.3 in).
- Fingerlings averaged 1.3 in on October 31, 1953. Scales with circuli appeared on 1.0-inch fish, and some fry may not form scales in their first season.

**Age and growth**
- Males mature about a year earlier than females. No mature males under 3 years or mature females under 4 years were found.
- Scale analysis of 475 chubs (ages I–VIII) gave calculated mean total lengths at each annulus: I 1.6 in, II 3.5, III 6.1, IV 8.2, V 9.7, VI 10.9, VII 12.4, VIII 13.6.
- Females grew larger and lived longer; 27 females and 7 males were age VII, and 4 females (no males) were age VIII.
- Growth was faster than at Fish Lake, Utah (Hazzard, 1935) after the second annulus. Growth at [[Panguitch Lake]] and [[Navajo Lake]], Utah (Neuhold, 1955) was also compared.

**Management implications**
- The chub is a poor food fish and a competitor for angler interest, and it readily takes trout bait. The study was intended to inform a fisheries management program for Hebgen Lake. The document does not state a specific control measure.
