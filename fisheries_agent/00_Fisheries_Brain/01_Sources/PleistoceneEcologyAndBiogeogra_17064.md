# Pleistocene Ecology and Biogeography: Vegetation, Climate, and Late Pleistocene Extinction

#paleoecology #mammalogy #palynology

## Executive Summary

This scanned journal article, attributed to [[Paul S. Martin]] and titled in its running heads "Pleistocene Ecology and Biogeography," reviews palynological, fossil, and radiocarbon evidence for late Pleistocene vegetation and fauna in North America, Mexico, Central and South America, and the West Indies. The author argues that full-glacial vegetation included a narrow tundra zone and boreal forest in the south, not a glacial "azonal" mosaic, and that the Late Pleistocene extinction of large mammals was abrupt, selective, and not explained by climatic change alone. Martin proposes a descriptive [[Pleistocene Extinction Model]] based on body size, habitat, and geographic range, and suggests that prehistoric human predation is the only clearly identified agent. Much of the scanned text, especially the first pages and some reference lists, is badly degraded by OCR and is only partly legible.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses three linked questions:

- **Vegetation during glaciation:** Whether [[Full-Glacial Tundra]] and boreal forest were present south of the ice sheet, and whether they can be reconciled with pollen and macrofossil records.
- **Post-glacial climate and biota:** The [[Hypsithermal]] (Thermal Maximum), which the author describes as extending from about 9,500 to 2,000 years ago and as the most important climatic event of the period.
- **Late Pleistocene extinction:** The loss of large mammals, the role of climate versus humans, and the chronology of extinction from radiocarbon dates.

Key concepts and sites discussed include [[Two Creeks Forest Bed]], the [[Valders Readvance]], the [[Prairie Peninsula]], the [[Seminole Field Fauna]] of Florida, and the [[West Indies]] insular fauna. Dating relies on [[Radiocarbon Dating]] and [[Pollen Analysis]]. The author defines tundra to include Arctic vegetation, treeless alpine zones, and Pleistocene pollen zones with high herb pollen and a small amount of spruce.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation and climate**

- Full-glacial spruce occurred in Louisiana and east Texas, but the author considers this a possible mixture of temperate and boreal elements rather than proof of continuous boreal forest.
- Fossil wood in glacial drift is not taken as proof of forest destruction by ice. The author suggests it may reflect a taiga-type woodland at the ice margin.
- In the Late-glacial, Valders-age pollen indicates boreal forest in southern New England and mixed hardwoods and conifers in the southern Appalachians. Mixed deciduous forest replaced pine-spruce forest on the Carolina Coastal Plain.
- Spruce in the Southwest, such as the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] outposts at about 8,500 feet, required a temperature depression that the author considers too large to be consistent with simple vertical displacement. The author uses an average lapse rate of 0.6°C per 100 m, giving a total drop of 7.2°C for the displacement from 8,500 to 4,500 feet.
- Snowline and treeline depression in New Mexico were on the order of 4,000 to 4,800 feet, according to [[Antevs]] (1954).
- Pleistocene spruce is reported in the Valley of Mexico, but spruce disappeared from areas south of Chihuahua in post-glacial time. Subalpine conifers at 10,000 to 12,000 feet in the transverse volcanic belt are considered superficially suitable for spruce.

**Late Pleistocene fauna**

- Extinction was not uniform. Cetaceans and pinnipeds were largely unaffected. Large herbivores in grasslands and deserts were lost, while moose, elk, white-tailed deer, and probably bison survived in temperate forests.
- Surviving continental refugia for large mammals included eastern temperate forests and tropical rainforests, along with the Floridian savanna, which the author describes as serving as a refuge for plains herbivores.
- Small-mammal extinction was largely confined to islands, including the Greater Antilles, where [[Simpson]] (1956) listed 22 extinct genera of terrestrial mammals against four surviving genera.
- The [[Plana Keys]] hutia (*Capromys ingrahami*) and [[Galápagos Tortoise]] populations survived on islands that were apparently not permanently inhabited in prehistoric times. The author uses these cases to argue that the model applies only to regions permanently occupied by prehistoric humans.

## Key Findings

- **Glacial vegetation:** The author accepts a narrow Full-glacial tundra zone but rejects an "azonal" model with tundra at the ice margin and maple or oak elsewhere. Fossil wood alone is not proof of forest. Boreal and temperate elements in the south are interpreted as marginal populations or mixed floras.
- **Late-glacial pollen:** Valders-age pollen diagrams indicate boreal forest in Connecticut and tundra in northern New England. Radiocarbon dating of pollen zone A-4 in Connecticut indicates boreal forest rather than taiga or tundra.
- **Hypsithermal effects:** Spruce and fir appear to have moved upslope by more than 1,000 feet in the Smoky Mountains during the Thermal Maximum. Changes in plant formations were otherwise judged small enough not to warrant separate mapping.
- **Biogeographic relicts:** [[Smith]] (1957) interpreted vertebrate splits in species such as the copperhead and chorus frogs as post-Xerothermic isolation of prairie-associated populations.
- **Extinction rates:** Extinction rates in genera per million years were 25 in the Miocene, 40 in the Pliocene, and 203 in the Pleistocene. Rates rose sharply for several mammalian orders, including artiodactyls, proboscideans, marsupials, edentates, rodents, perissodactyls, fissipeds, and primates.
- **Timing:** Using arbitrary durations for glacial and interglacial periods, the author estimates Wisconsin extinction at 31.0 genera per 100,000 years, against 6.0 for the Aftonian and 5.0 for the Kansan. He concludes that extinction was largely a Wisconsin phenomenon and not closely tied to glaciation.
- **Extinction without replacement:** Roughly three-quarters of the mammalian herbivore fauna disappeared, including elephants, mastodons, camels, horses, and the shrub-oxen, along with pronghorns and giant beavers.
- **Radiocarbon chronology:** Mexican and Alaskan large mammals appear to have disappeared first, in the Late-glacial. Plains megafauna disappeared early in the post-glacial. Florida and South American extinction postdated the Hypsithermal. A radiocarbon date from the Seminole Field deposits (L-2N) gave about 2,040 years, which the author considers anomalously young.
- **Mastodon and horse:** Mastodons persisted in eastern North America until at least 6,000 years ago and were associated with archaic artifacts. The author notes a gap of roughly 3,000 to 6,000 years when horses were absent from the Americas, before post-Columbian reintroduction.
- **Extinction model:** The author proposes that extinction probability increased with large body size, low intrinsic rate of increase, long generation time, open habitat (savanna, grassland, tundra, desert), and limited range. Survival was enhanced by small size, forest habitat, and wide range. Fig. 6 presents this model as a generalized diagram.
- **Human role:** The author considers the multiple-cause hypothesis inadequate for the observed patterns (differential loss of large animals, no major climatic change during the extinction interval, a narrow chronological window, and removal without replacement). He concludes that "Man is the only one clearly identified" as the agent.
- **Caveat:** The author acknowledges that the extinction pattern has exceptions, including survival of South American camelids and Galápagos tortoises, and the unexplained late dates for Florida fauna.
