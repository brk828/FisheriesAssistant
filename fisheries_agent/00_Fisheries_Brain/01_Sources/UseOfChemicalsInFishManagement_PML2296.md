# Use of Chemicals in Fish Management and Fish Culture: Past and Future

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzontidae #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This chapter by Rosalie A. Schnick (from the edited volume *Xenobiotics in Fish*) reviews the history, regulation and future prospects of chemicals used in fish management (fish toxicants and aquatic herbicides) and fish culture (drugs, anesthetics and disinfectants). It traces how registration under [[Federal Insecticide, Fungicide and Rodenticide Act]] (FIFRA) and [[Food and Drug Administration]] drug approval procedures have made it costly and slow to bring products to market, and how public-private partnerships, notably the [[IAFWA Project]], have since increased drug approvals. The author anticipates that approximately 20 drugs will have gained approvals for aquaculture use by 2002, and that few new fish-control pesticides or aquatic herbicides will be developed.

## Background & Description

Chemicals have been used in fish management in the United States since the 1930s and in fish culture since the late 1800s. Fish toxicants serve to eradicate undesirable fish, restore threatened species, eradicate disease vectors, treat drainages before stocking, aid population assessment and eliminate competing species in hatcheries. Early screening programs at the [[Hammond Bay Biological Station]] and the [[Fish Control Laboratory]] (FCL, La Crosse, Wisconsin) sought selective toxicants against the [[Sea Lamprey]] and general toxicants against [[Common Carp]].

Regulation has changed substantially over the last 35 years. Drugs are approved through the [[Center for Veterinary Medicine]] (CVM) of the FDA, and pesticides through the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA). Key legislation includes the 1972 and 1988 amendments to FIFRA, which required reregistration and reclassification of pesticides, leading to withdrawal of several aquatic pesticides.

Fish culture chemicals are used to control disease, aid spawning, manipulate sex, control vegetation and algae, and disinfect water. Sodium chloride was the main drug until 1909, when formalin was found useful against fish disease.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document emphasises that aquatic pesticide and drug use is shaped by environmental and social pressures: pollution effects on fish populations, recreational demand, introduction of exotic species, commercial fisheries, and intensification of aquaculture. Chemical use is also constrained by environmental safety data. For example, [[Rotenone]] was reregistered after data indicated it is safe for the environment and humans, although a 1976 EPA challenge over tumorigenicity was later proven false (1981). [[TFM]] reregistration was delayed by EPA concerns over toxicology data, and [[Antimycin]] was proposed for reclassification as a restricted-use pesticide because of its use to remove fish competing with endangered species such as Gila trout and Apache trout.

Sea lamprey invasion of the upper Great Lakes destroyed the lake trout fishery in the 1940s and 1950s, providing the main impetus for lampricide development. Controls must also protect non-target organisms, such as [[Northern Squawfish]] (a selective-toxicant target in the 1950s and 1960s) and the many species affected by malachite green concerns.

## Key Findings

**Historical toxicant registrations**
- Selective toxicants registered: [[TFM]] (3-trifluoromethyl-4-nitrophenol), first registered by FWS in 1964; [[Bayluscide]] (niclosamide), registered in 1968.
- General toxicants registered: [[Rotenone]] (1947, S. D. Penick & Company) and [[Antimycin]] (three formulations from 1966).
- Screening of over 6,000 chemicals began in 1951 at Hammond Bay; Sawyer investigated 129 compounds, six of which were selectively toxic to sea lamprey.
- Cost of registering [[Squoxin]] was estimated at $436,600 to $2,076,000 (1984 dollars), so no organization funded it.

**Current status (1998)**
- Only two selective toxicants (TFM and Bayluscide) and two general toxicants (rotenone and antimycin) were registered or being reregistered.
- Aquatic herbicides and algicides registered 1950s–1970s include copper chelates, copper sulfate, dichlobenil, diquat dibromide, endothall, fluridone, glyphosate and 2,4-D.
- The 1988 FIFRA amendment led to withdrawal of several aquatic pesticides, including dichlone, potassium permanganate, simazine, trichlorfon and xylene.

**Rotenone**
- Used since the 1930s; by 1949, 34 states used it routinely.
- The [[American Fisheries Society]] recognized in 1993 the need for stewardship guidelines; the "Rotenone Stewardship Program" was funded by FWS in December 1997.

**TFM and Bayluscide**
- TFM reregistration expected by 2001; Bayluscide reregistration expected by 2000–2001.

**Aquaculture drug approvals**
- From 1970 to 1986, oxytetracycline, nifurpirinol, sulfamerazine, MS-222, Romet-30 and formalin gained limited approvals.
- Formalin's data requirements were accepted by CVM in 1983; the first New Animal Drug Application (NADA) was approved in 1986, a 13-year interim period.
- The Joint Subcommittee on Aquaculture (JSA) identified 24 urgently needed drugs; priority drugs included oxolinic acid, oxytetracycline, Romet-30, flumequine, formalin, malachite green replacement, trichlorfon, praziquantel, diflubenzuron and benzocaine.
- The 1995 JSA priority list included amoxicillin, benzocaine, chloramine-T, copper sulfate, erythromycin, formalin, hydrogen peroxide, oxytetracycline, potassium permanganate, praziquantel and several sea-lice agents, among others.

**Malachite green replacement**
- Malachite green, used against fungal infections, was considered potentially hazardous to humans and caused developmental anomalies in some species.
- Hydrogen peroxide was identified as a likely replacement; [[Pycezer]] (pyceze, a UK-identified fungicide) was also pursued.

**Federal-State Partnership**
- The [[IAFWA Project]] (Federal-State Aquaculture Drug Approval Partnership) began in July 1994 with 39 states pledging funds; 37 are active participants. It was originally planned to run until June 1999 and was extended to 2002.
- Objectives included extending formalin approval, expanding oxytetracycline, gaining approvals for benzocaine, chloramine-T, copper sulfate, hydrogen peroxide, potassium permanganate and sarafloxacin, and developing a crop-grouping concept for surrogate species.

**Other programs**
- The [[National INAD Office]] in Bozeman, Montana, coordinates FWS INAD activity involving 12 individual INAD exemptions, 90 facilities and about 300 INAD units.
- Twenty states held 61 individual INAD exemptions for eight therapeutants, one anesthetic and three spawning aids.
- Erythromycin feed additive approval (funded by Bonneville Power Administration, 1989) was extended across the Pacific Northwest in 1992.

**International harmonization**
- EPA and Canada's Pest Management Regulatory Agency met in 1996 on sea lice bath treatments, which Canada regulates as pesticides and the US as drugs.
- Two International Harmonization Workshops were held in 1997 and 1998.

**Conclusions**
- Partnerships are expected to continue as the main route to drug approval; approximately 20 drugs are expected to be approved for aquaculture by 2002.
- Few new fish-control pesticides or aquatic herbicides are expected.
