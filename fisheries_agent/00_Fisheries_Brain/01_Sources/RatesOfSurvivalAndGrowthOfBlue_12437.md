# Rates of Survival and Growth of Bluegill Yolk Fry Stocked at Different Intensities in Hatchery Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #freshwater-fisheries #aquaculture #[[Bluegill]]

## Executive Summary

This study, conducted over two summers (1941–1942) at the Wolf Lake Hatchery in Michigan, examined how stocking intensity affects survival and growth of [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) yolk fry in five hatchery ponds. Fry were stocked at rates ranging from ~2,500 to ~323,000 per acre. Contrary to expectations, there was **little correlation between stocking intensity and survival rate**. No pond retained more than 40% of stocked fry after the first summer, and by the end of the second summer (17 months), only 1–20% of the original stock remained. Growth, however, was clearly density-dependent: fish stocked at lower intensities grew substantially faster. Under the most favorable conditions (low density, abundant food), bluegills grew fast enough to **spawn at 12 months of age** and reach an average of 6 inches total length by 17 months. Interspecific competition — from [[Iowa Darter]] in one pond and [[Green Sunfish]] in another — significantly altered survival and community composition, with green sunfish eventually numerically and biomass-dominating one pond by the study's end.

## Background & Description

- **Species of primary interest:** [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), family Centrarchidae (sunfishes).
- **Historical context:** Since Lydell (1921), Michigan hatcheries have used a method of collecting bluegill yolk fry from natural lakes (rather than holding adult "breeder" stock) and transferring them to hatchery ponds for rearing to fingerling size. This method reduced the need to overwinter or capture adult breeders.
- **Knowledge gap:** Despite widespread use of this rearing method, little was known about survival rates of bluegill from the yolk-fry stage onward. Hazzard (1945) had shown extremely low survival to adulthood under natural lake conditions (e.g., only 3 of 100,000 bluegill fry survived 4 years in one wild population study, compared to higher survival for [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]], [[Rock Bass]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]).
- **Study rationale:** Fish hatcheries exist in part to reduce this "tremendous natural waste" and improve survival relative to wild spawning, where nest-building sunfishes are believed to have better egg/fry survival than broadcast spawners like [[Yellow Perch]] or [[Common Carp]].

### Study Design
- **Location:** Five ponds (0.86–1.60 acres, 7–8 ft max depth) at Wolf Lake Hatchery, Michigan Department of Conservation, near Kalamazoo.
- **Pond substrates:** Ponds 1 & 2 — soft mud with sand shores; Pond 3 — sand/mud mix; Ponds 4 & 5 — sand.
- **Vegetation:** Cattails along Ponds 1, 2, 3; *[[Chara]]* (algae) heavy in Ponds 2, 3, 5.
- **Fry source:** Collected from Lime Lake, Van Buren County, Michigan.
- **Stocking method:** Fry placed on cheesecloth trays ~18 inches below surface, covered with coarse screens (per Lydell 1921 protocol), released once free-swimming.
- **Fry quantification method:** Fry drawn into a 0.5-inch diameter glass tube; column height measured to nearest 1/8 inch; converted to fry counts using an estimated fry-per-inch figure (1,800/inch for Ponds 3–5, 1,500/inch for Ponds 1–2).
- **No supplemental feeding** was provided at any point in the experiment.
- **Fertilization treatment:** Pond 1 only — treated monthly (June–Sept, both summers) with 100 lbs 10-6-4 commercial fertilizer + 50 lbs crushed limestone.
- **Sampling/draining schedule:**
  1. October 1941 (end of first summer)
  2. April 1942 (end of first winter)
  3. October 1942 (end of second summer; experiment concluded, fish redistributed)
- **Population estimation:** Via subsampling (e.g., ten 1,000-fish samples weighed to get average weight/1000, then total weight ÷ average = population estimate). In Pond 3, ratios of bluegill:green sunfish established from samples were extrapolated to the full population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stocking accuracy issues:** Actual fry counts deviated substantially from intended targets (up to 61.7% error), attributable to fry fragility, developmental stage-dependent packing density in sampling tubes, and yolk-sac-driven activity differences. Younger, more yolk-laden fry (golden colored, less active) packed more densely and were undercounted at the assumed rate.
- **Interspecific competition:**
  - **[[Iowa Darter]]** invaded Pond 4 and appeared to "take over" the pond, severely suppressing bluegill survival during the first summer (only 1.8% survival) — though darters were removed by October 1941.
  - **[[Green Sunfish]]** entered Pond 3 as young-of-year through the water supply during the first summer. By the second summer, green sunfish outcompeted bluegills, growing from 11.4% to 52.2% of total pond biomass between April and October 1942, while their numbers surged from 409 to an estimated 48,500 individuals (bluegills meanwhile declined from 22,024 to 4,594).
- **Fertilization effects:** Fertilizer in Pond 1 boosted total food supply and first-summer survival (39.3% vs. 18.0% in unfertilized, equally-stocked Pond 2), but the resulting higher fish density suppressed per-capita growth — Pond 1 fish grew much more slowly than Pond 2 fish despite better survival.
- **Density-dependent growth:** Across all ponds, lower stocking density and lower population density (whether from planned stocking or attrition) consistently produced faster individual growth, illustrating a classic density-dependent growth response in [[Centrarchidae]].
- **Cannibalism:** Adult bluegills in Pond 5 exhibited notable cannibalism on their own young-of-year — 45% of sampled adult stomachs contained small bluegills, contributing to a strong reduction in juvenile numbers relative to Pond 4 (5,125 vs. 22,321 young recovered), despite similar spawning bed counts.
- **Sexual size dimorphism:** Males grew to larger average sizes than females by the second year in both spawning ponds (Pond 4 and Pond 5), consistent with prior findings for bluegill, green sunfish, and [[Longear Sunfish]] (Hubbs & Hubbs 1933; Hubbs & Cooper 1935; Bailey & Lagler 1938) — sexes grow similarly in year one, males outpace females from year two onward.

### Survival Summary
- **First summer (May–Oct 1941):** Highest survival in Pond 1 (39.3%, fertilized), lowest in Pond 4 (1.8%, Iowa darter competition). Pond 2 (unfertilized, same stocking rate as Pond 1): 18.0%. Ponds 3 and 5 survival ~32–36%.
- **First winter (Oct 1941–Apr 1942):** Generally lower mortality than summer; ≥55% survival typical. Exception: Pond 4 had the highest overwinter mortality (44.8%) despite darter removal — cause unclear. Pond 5 had the lowest overwinter loss (20.2%).
- **Second summer (Apr–Oct 1942):** "Normal" mortality ~30–40% in Ponds 1, 2, 5. Pond 4 showed high survival (90.5%) attributed to small population size. Pond 3 survival dropped to 20.9%, linked to green sunfish takeover.
- **Cumulative survival at 17 months (of original May 1941 stocking):**
  - Pond 1: 18.7%
  - Pond 2: 7.8%
  - Pond 3: not directly stated as cumulative (affected by green sunfish dynamics)
  - Pond 4: low (compounded by first-summer bottleneck)
  - Pond 5: 19.9% (highest overall cumulative survival)
- **No consistent relationship** was found between stocking intensity and overall survival rate — factors like food supply, space, and interspecific competition were more influential than initial density alone.

### Growth Summary (Table 4)
- **End of first summer (Oct 1941), average total length:**
  - Pond 1: 1.0 in (0.6 lb/1000)
  - Pond 5: 3.9 in (38.7 lb/1000) — fastest growth, lowest stocking density
- **Growth generally inversely related to population density**, not simply initial stocking rate.
- **Winter growth (Oct 1941–Apr 1942):** Length increases ranged from 2.6% (Pond 5) to 20.0% (Pond 1); weight increases ranged from 1.8% (Pond 5) to 50.0% (Pond 1). Authors note apparent growth may partly reflect selective mortality of smaller individuals rather than pure somatic growth.
- **Second summer growth (Apr–Oct 1942):** All ponds showed ≥50% increase in average length and >250% increase in average weight.
  - Pond 4: grew from 3.4 in / 24.1 lb per 1000 to 5.7 in / 134.9 lb per 1000 — attributed to very low population density (202 fish/acre in April 1942).
  - Pond 5: grew from ~3.9 in to 5.9 in (+2.0 in) and from 39.4 to 145.2 lb/1000.
  - Pond 1: 1.2 → 2.3 in; 0.9 → 6.6 lb/1000 (slow growth despite fertilization, due to high density).
  - Pond 2: 2.0 → 3.2 in; 4.7 → 17.1 lb/1000.
  - Pond 3: 2.1 → 3.9 in; 5.8 → 32.1 lb/1000 (amid green sunfish proliferation).

### Reproduction & Sexual Dimorphism (Ponds 4 & 5, second summer)
- Bluegills in the least dense ponds (4 and 5) **spawned at only 12 months of age** — an unusually rapid maturation attributed to superior growth conditions (low competition, ample food).
- Pond 5: 331 bluegill nests counted (June 29, 1942); Pond 4: 29 nests.
- Young recovered at final draining (Oct 1942): Pond 5 = 5,125; Pond 4 = 22,321 (difference linked to cannibalism in Pond 5).
- **Cannibalism:** 45% of 60 sampled adult bluegill stomachs from Pond 5 contained small bluegills; none found in 30 Pond 4 adult stomachs.
- **Sex ratios and sizes at 17 months:**
  - Pond 5: 618 adults recovered — 59% male (365), 41% female (253); ratio 144:100 (M:F). Male average length 155.7 mm (6.13 in); female average 146.5 mm (5.77 in). Difference: 9.2 mm (0.36 in).
  - Pond 4: 181 adults recovered — 38% male (78), 62% female (103); ratio 76:100 (M:F). Male average length 152.5 mm (6.00 in); female average 138.8 mm (5.46 in). Difference: 13.7 mm (0.54 in).
- Findings align with prior literature (Hubbs & Hubbs 1933; Hubbs & Cooper 1935; Bailey & Lagler 1938): sexes grow similarly in year 1, with males outgrowing females from the second year onward in [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Longear Sunfish]].

### Broader Implications
- Survival of bluegill yolk fry to fingerling stage is governed by a complex interplay of **food supply, space/density, and interspecific competition**, rather than stocking intensity alone.
- The greatest bottleneck to survival occurs during the **first summer of life**, though the precise timing (e.g., pre- vs. post-emergence from cheesecloth trays) remains undetermined.
- Practical hatchery implication: lower stocking densities favor faster individual growth and can accelerate time-to-maturity, but interspecific invaders (via water supply) can drastically restructure pond fish communities within a single season.
