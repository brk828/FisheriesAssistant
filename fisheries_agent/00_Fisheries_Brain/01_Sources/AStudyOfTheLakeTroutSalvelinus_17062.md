# Lake Trout Depth Distribution, Feeding, and Growth in Two Algonquin Park Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1947 field study examined the [[Depth Distribution]], [[Feeding Ecology]], and [[Growth Rate]] of [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) in two [[Algonquin Park]] lakes, [[Lake Louisa]] and [[Redrock Lake]], Ontario. The two populations differ sharply in diet. Redrock trout fed mainly on [[Yellow Perch]] in summer, while Louisa trout, which lacked forage fish, relied largely on plankton. The diet difference is linked to slower growth in Louisa and to a smaller size at maturity. The authors argue that these differences make a single size limit impractical across Algonquin Park lakes.

## Background & Description

The lake trout was described as one of the least known commercial and game fishes. Knowledge gaps arose from its deep-water habitat, the difficulty of capturing young fish, the challenge of studying reproduction, uncertain age determination, and variability among water bodies. The study aimed to explain differences in size composition, feeding habits, and size at maturity observed in angler catches between the two lakes.

**Study setting:**
- Both lakes lie within [[Algonquin Park]] on the Canadian Shield and are headwaters of the Madawaska (Louisa) and Petawawa (Redrock) river systems, which drain to the Ottawa River.
- **Lake Louisa:** about 1,500 acres (2.3 sq mi), maximum length 4.3 miles, maximum depth 220 ft, mean depth 65 ft. Basin is steep-sided, with a shallow area only at the eastern end.
- **Redrock Lake:** 746 acres (about 1.1 sq mi), maximum length 2.3 miles, maximum depth 70 ft, mean depth 29 ft. Gradual slope and extensive littoral zone.
- Fieldwork: Louisa from July 7 to August 24 and October 10 to November 4, 1947; Redrock from May 10 to July 7 with periodic trips until September 11, 1947. Creel census and earlier stomach and scale collections were also used.

**Fish communities:**
- Lake Louisa: 11 species, including [[Eastern Brook Trout]], [[White Sucker]], and [[Pumpkinseed]]. Yellow perch was absent, though it is common in regional lakes.
- Redrock Lake: 12 species, including [[Eastern Brook Trout]], [[Round Whitefish]], and [[Yellow Perch]]. The lake is noted for a very large perch population.
- [[Burbot]] was absent from both lakes, although it is a potential competitor elsewhere in the park.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and oxygen:** Louisa developed a well-established thermocline that weakened into mid-October, and the lake stayed well oxygenated throughout summer. Redrock developed a sharply defined thermocline in late June, with somewhat lower oxygen concentrations than Louisa. Bottom temperatures in Louisa fell to 3.9 °C, and bottom oxygen ranged from 4 to 5 ppm.

**Benthos:** Louisa's bottom fauna was relatively sparse. [[Chaoborus]] sp., [[Hexagenia]] sp., and [[Chironomidae]] dominated, with Chaoborus and Hexagenia most abundant in the first 40 ft and chironomids predominant in deep water. Miller (1937) found Redrock to be the most productive of five Algonquin Park lakes studied, and Wood's 1947 survey supported a richer bottom fauna there. The authors note Redrock's richer benthos as an important factor in growth.

**Basin structure and food availability:** Thermal conditions separated Louisa forage fish from the trout. Most forage species were confined to shallow water above or in the upper thermocline (for example, redbelly dace and common shiner were not found below 15 ft). Redrock trout and perch overlapped in the 12 to 18 °C zone.

## Key Findings

**Depth distribution**
- In spring, Redrock trout were widely dispersed, with about as many fish below the 30-ft contour as above it.
- As surface water reached 14 to 15 °C in late June, the population moved downward, with trout ranging from 15 to 55 ft. The upper limit of distribution corresponded roughly to the 13 to 15 °C isotherms.
- Summer concentrations in Redrock were in the lower thermocline and upper hypolimnion, overlapping with perch.
- Trout in both lakes frequently entered the 15- to 20-ft zone in midsummer. The upper limit of distribution was associated with the 18 °C isotherm. Overnight sets captured most fish, and daytime sets were largely unproductive, suggesting nocturnal, feeding-related movement into shallow water.
- In October, 56 percent of an overnight catch in Louisa Basin E (depths 4 to 70 ft) was taken in the 4- to 12-ft zone.
- Smaller trout generally occupied deeper water than larger trout. In Louisa, older fish concentrated above the 50-ft contour in summer. Small trout were often grouped, and the authors suggest that yearling and two-year-old trout use boulder or rocky habitat to avoid older fish, as observed in [[Great Bear Lake]] and [[Keuka Lake]].
- Pelagic netting was ineffective: four lake trout were captured in 108 hours of suspended sets in Louisa, and the authors concluded that trout were largely confined to a zone just above the bottom.

**Feeding**
- Analysis of 276 stomachs from Redrock and 449 from Louisa showed that diet varied strongly by season and lake.
- **Spring:** a varied diet, with insects prominent. Surface feeding was on insects only. A March fly (*Bibio* sp.) emergence in June 1947 was heavily consumed in Redrock. Bottom feeding was dominated by mayfly nymphs (*Hexagenia* sp.).
- **Summer, Louisa:** plankton-dominated. Plankton occurred in 62 percent of all stomachs and 73 percent of those containing food. Crustacea, especially Cladocera, were predominant. Stomachs usually contained a single prey group, suggesting selection rather than random straining.
- **Summer, Redrock:** piscivorous. Fish made up 85 percent of total stomach weight, almost exclusively yellow perch. Perch of the year first appeared in late July and were taken in large numbers; up to 80 percent of trout feeding on perch in late July and early August had eaten perch hatched that spring.
- **Fall:** Redrock stomachs were almost 80 percent empty in the first half of September, and those with food contained fish only. Feeding increased in late September and October. Louisa data suggested a gradual increase in empty stomachs through summer, peaking in early fall.
- Fish other than perch in Redrock diets were eaten mainly by the largest trout, mostly white sucker.
- Small trout in Louisa ate more plankton and fewer fish than large trout, except in spring and fall, when fish were more important for small fish. Large trout consumed more bottom and surface items.

**Food competition and cannibalism**
- Competition was judged of little consequence. In Redrock, perch are abundant enough to support both trout and brook trout.
- Cannibalism was rare: one instance in 725 stomachs examined (two 4-inch trout in a 13-inch Louisa trout).

**Growth**
- Growth was similar in both lakes through age group V, then diverged. Between age groups VI and VII, Louisa increased by 0.9 inches and Redrock by 2.0 inches.
- Redrock growth resembled that of piscivorous populations (for example, [[Proulx Lake]] and [[Opeongo Lake]]), whereas Louisa grouped with slow-growing plankton feeders ([[Brewer Lake]] and [[Chicadee Lake]]).
- Within Louisa, females grew faster than males through age group VI; no sex difference was detected in Redrock.
- Based on the compilation of published curves, growth declined with latitude, from northern populations (for example, [[Great Bear Lake]]) to southern ones (for example, [[Seneca Lake]]). Fish in their fifth year ranged from 7 inches in Great Bear Lake to 21 inches in Seneca Lake.

**Age, maturity, and year-class strength**
- Scale samples comprised 347 fish from Louisa and 195 from Redrock.
- Maturity occurred at age V in both lakes, with no fish maturing before age VI. In Louisa most fish matured in their sixth year, while in Redrock only a few males and no females matured then.
- Louisa trout matured in large numbers at about 12 inches; most Redrock trout matured at about 16 inches.
- Year-class strength was highly variable. In Louisa, the 1941 year class was strongest, comprising over 40 percent of angler-catchable fish, and the 1944 year class was also strong (about one fish in five). The 1943 year class was weak.
- In Redrock, the 1943 and 1942 year classes together made up about half the catch. The authors caution that the 114-inch smallest mesh may have underrepresented the 1944 and 1945 year classes in Redrock and the 1945 and 1946 year classes in Louisa.

**Angler catches and management implications**
- Louisa angler catches averaged 14 to 15 inches over 12 years, with few fish over 16 inches. Redrock catches averaged 17 to 19 inches, with fish up to 25 to 30 inches.
- Size at maturity ranged from 10 to 19 inches across Algonquin Park lakes. The authors concluded that this diversity makes uniform legislation to protect immature fish impractical, even in a small area.
- In Louisa, many mature fish are untouched by current angling, so spawning escapement is high. In Redrock, substantial angling pressure falls on trout before their first spawning.

**Overall conclusions**
- Depth distribution, diet, and growth are closely interrelated. The slower growth of Louisa trout is attributed mainly to their separation from forage fish by thermal stratification, which forces a plankton diet. Redrock's perch availability and richer benthos support faster growth.
- Temperature is considered a secondary factor, creating a thermal barrier rather than directly limiting growth.
- Food studies conducted at a single time of year are unlikely to represent the species' overall diet, given strong seasonal shifts.
