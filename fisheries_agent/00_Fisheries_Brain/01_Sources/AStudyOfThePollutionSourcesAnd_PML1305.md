# Final Report: Pollution Sources and Their Effect on the Aquatic Habitat of Eagle Creek Watershed, Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 final report, submitted by [[Boyd E. Kynard]] (Assistant Professor of Fisheries Science, School of Renewable Natural Resources, University of Arizona) under Cooperative Agreement No. 1d-51l-CA, assesses non-point and point pollution sources and their effects on the aquatic habitat of [[Eagle Creek]] and [[Chitty Creek]] in the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest. Fieldwork ran from April 1975 to February 1976. The study measured physico-chemical habitat conditions, benthic macroinvertebrates, native fish condition and abundance, and the taxonomic status of a Chitty Creek trout population. The authors concluded that water chemistry was generally acceptable, that fine sediment and summer stream temperatures were the main habitat concerns, and that the Chitty Creek trout is a distinct, geographically isolated population of [[Arizona Trout]] warranting subspecies-level recognition.

## Background & Description

The Eagle Creek watershed lies on the western edge of Greenlee County, draining south from the [[Mogollon Rim]] toward the Gila River about 15 miles west of Morenci. Chitty Creek, a small, steep canyon stream with a roughly 10 m waterfall, joins Eagle Creek about 7 miles from its eastern origin. Eagle Creek is intermittent above Dry Prong Creek and perennial from Dry Prong downstream. Dry Prong Creek is a dry wash with permanent water only in its lower 100 m.

The riparian zone is a pine-forest transition life zone with cottonwoods and sycamores along Eagle Creek and ponderosa pine on the slopes. Chitty Creek is almost entirely shaded. The watershed is used for cattle grazing, camping at [[Honeymoon Campground]], hiking, fishing and hunting. The study area ranged from about 7,600 ft (upper Chitty Creek) to about 5,500 ft (Honeymoon Campground).

Earlier work was limited. Prior taxonomic studies (Miller 1972; Mulch and Gamble 1954; Miller and Lowe 1964) and Minckley (1973) had described native trout and fishes, but the aquatic habitat had received little study. Minckley (1973) considered the Eagle Creek "pure strain" trout extinct or indistinguishable from introduced rainbow trout.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Ortho-phosphate and nitrate-nitrogen were the main nutrient indicators. Nitrate was generally below 0.05 mg/L. Ortho-phosphate was high in upper Chitty Creek and upper Eagle Creek and was largely taken up by aquatic plants in lower reaches. Total dissolved solids were low for southern Arizona streams, but Dry Prong Creek contributed a sharp rise in TDS to lower Eagle Creek. pH stayed within acceptable limits (roughly 7.0 to 8.5).

**Temperature.** Chitty Creek was the coolest site, with summer daily minima of 10°C to 14°C and maxima of 17°C to 19°C. Eagle Creek below Dry Prong was warmer and exceeded recommended summer trout maxima. Mean daily highs at Four Drag Ranch from 26 July to 24 August were 23.1°C, which the report describes as 3.1°C above the federal summer standard for resident trout waters. Dry Prong water was sometimes up to 10°C warmer than Eagle Creek.

**Sediment.** Fine sediment (under 0.85 mm) was high. Chitty Creek had about 15 to 25% fines, while Eagle Creek sites had about 35 to 42%. Dry Prong Creek was the main source of fine sediment and turbidity.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Daytime levels stayed above 5 to 6 mg/L. Night-time levels were not measured.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Chitty Creek had the lowest abundance and biomass but the highest diversity (H' = 2.1 to 3.4 range reported in the table, with Chitty Creek H' = 3.451 and E = 0.785). Eagle Creek at Dry Prong had the highest standing crop by total biomass but the lowest diversity (H' = 1.505 at Honeymoon and lower at Dry Prong, E = 0.432 to 0.601 across sites). The Tendipedidae made up over 58% of invertebrates at Dry Prong.

**Fishes.** Native species were all present in substantial numbers. Dry Prong and its vegetated pools were an important rearing habitat. Condition factors for native species fell within the range reported elsewhere in the United States.

## Key Findings

- **Chemistry is generally acceptable.** pH, dissolved oxygen, nitrate and TDS posed no serious problem for aquatic life, though ortho-phosphate in Chitty Creek reached levels linked to excessive plant growth in summer.
- **Temperature is the main limit for trout.** Lower Eagle Creek exceeded recommended trout temperatures from late June to September, so the author judged it marginal even for adult rainbow trout.
- **Sediment is a key degradation source.** Dry Prong Creek delivers large fine sediment loads. Fine particles under 4 mm made up about 50% of the bottom, which is comparable to Big Bonito Creek, a Salmo apache stream.
- **Invertebrate indices suggest degradation downstream.** Chitty Creek was the least degraded community. Dry Prong showed the lowest diversity and evenness, and Honeymoon was intermediate.
- **Native fish are healthy.** Five native species (Agosia chrysogaster, Rhinichthys osculus, Gila robusta, Pantosteus clarkii, Catostomus insignis) were present in substantial numbers, with normal length distributions and condition factors. No stunting was evident.
- **Chitty Creek trout are a distinct subspecies.** Multivariate discriminant analysis (100% correct reclassification) and t-tests showed Chitty Creek trout differed from Gila trout, hatchery rainbow trout and Big Bonito Creek Arizona trout. The population most resembled Arizona trout (Salmo apache), and the report calls it the first reported subspecies of Arizona trout. Hybridization with rainbow trout was ruled out.
- **Recommendations.** The author advised against establishing [[Smallmouth Bass]] or [[Brown Trout]] in Eagle Creek, because they could displace native fish. He recommended continuing the spring stocking of catchable rainbow trout and maintaining native fish populations. He also recommended riparian planting to shade Dry Prong and lower Eagle Creek, and seasonal studies of invertebrates.
- **Data limits.** Only one year of monthly sampling was possible, the Ryan thermograph failed, and night-time oxygen was not measured. The report notes that management decisions should be revisited once seasonal data are available.
