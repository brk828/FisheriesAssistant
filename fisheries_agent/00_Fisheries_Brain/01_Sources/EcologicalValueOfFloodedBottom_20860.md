## Executive Summary

This report (Wydoski 1994, Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program) synthesizes literature on the ecological role of **flooded bottomland habitats** in supporting endangered fish populations in the Upper Colorado River Basin. The central thesis is that high turbidity and sediment-bound phosphorus in the main river channels severely limit [[Phytoplankton]] and [[Zooplankton]] production, starving the food web needed by larval endangered fishes. Off-channel, low-velocity habitats — particularly flooded bottomlands — provide the nutrient availability, sunlight penetration, and warmer temperatures required for productive zooplankton communities. The paper argues, via the **Match-Mismatch Principle**, that the loss of these bottomlands (due to river channelization and levee development) is a key driver of recruitment failure in the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Humpback Chub]].

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily ecological rather than morphological, it provides one key larval life-history metric:

- **Razorback Sucker larvae**: Approximately **10 mm total length** at the onset of experimental starvation trials, representing the critical post-yolk-sac swim-up stage when exogenous feeding becomes obligatory.
- No detailed morphological descriptions of adult endangered fishes are provided in this source; the focus is on larval feeding ecology and habitat-linked survivorship.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Types & Productivity Gradient
| Habitat Type | Turbidity | Water Velocity | Temperature | Productivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main river channel | High | High | Cool | Very low (phytoplankton/zooplankton limited) |
| Backwaters/embayments | Moderate | Low | Warmer | Intermediate |
| Flooded bottomlands / off-channel wetlands | Low (clearer) | Very low | Warmest | High |

### Nutrient Dynamics
- Phosphorus is the primary limiting nutrient for [[Phytoplankton]] production in north temperate freshwaters (Schindler 1978).
- In the Colorado River near Moab, UT, only **21–49%** of total phosphorus was bioavailable (mostly as calcium-bound phosphorus); primary production was found to be **inversely related to turbidity**, not nutrient-limited (Watts and Lamarra 1983).
- High sediment loads bind phosphorus, making it largely unavailable to algae in the main channel (Ellis and Stanford 1988).

### Trophic Ecology of Larvae
- Larvae of all endangered Colorado River fishes are obligate **zooplanktivores** early in life.
- First foods of larval [[Razorback Sucker]] in ponds: **diatoms, detritus, algae, and rotifers**, followed by selection for larger cladocerans as zooplankton grazers.
- The critical zooplankton density threshold for larval survival (**20 organisms/liter**) was regularly met in flooded bottomlands, rarely in backwaters, and never in the main river channel.

### Nonnative Species Interaction
- Nonnative fishes comprise **76.4%** of the present fish community in the upper basin (Tyus et al. 1982), contributing to predation and competitive pressure on native larvae.
- Ironically, the same off-channel warmwater habitats that benefit endangered larvae are also ideal for nonnative fish, creating a management tension between habitat enhancement and nonnative fish proliferation.

## Key Findings

1. **Turbidity, not nutrients, limits primary production** in the main channel of the Upper Colorado River Basin — phytoplankton and zooplankton production are consistently very low in riverine habitats compared to off-channel habitats.
2. **Flooded bottomlands provide optimal conditions** (nutrient deposition, sunlight penetration, warmer temperatures) for phytoplankton and zooplankton production, exceeding both backwaters and the main channel.
3. **Match-or-Mismatch Principle** (Hjort 1914, 1926) applies directly to razorback sucker larvae: food organisms of the correct size and sufficient density (≥20 brine shrimp nauplii/L) must be available within **8–19 days** post-yolk-sac absorption, with a **"point of no return"** for starvation occurring between **19–23 days** at 18°C (Papoulias and Minckley 1990).
4. **Recruitment failure**, not adult mortality, is identified as the major limiting factor for endangered Colorado River fish populations (USFWS 1987, 1990a, 1990b, 1991).
5. Loss of flooded bottomland habitat via **channelization and levee development** near population centers and agricultural land is hypothesized to be a primary driver of the observed decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]] and near-extirpation of the [[Bonytail]] in the upper basin.
6. Zooplankton generated in productive off-channel habitats likely **subsidizes main-channel habitats** (e.g., backwaters used by [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]]), extending the ecological value of bottomlands beyond their immediate footprint.
7. Habitat enhancement of bottomlands is proposed as a recovery strategy, but must be balanced against the risk of enhancing nonnative fish habitat simultaneously.
