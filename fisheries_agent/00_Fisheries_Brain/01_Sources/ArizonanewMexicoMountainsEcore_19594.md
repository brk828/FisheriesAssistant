# Arizona / New Mexico Mountains Ecoregion Experts Conservation Workshop

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1997 workshop packet from [[The Nature Conservancy]] (TNC) for a two-day Experts Conservation Workshop, held April 8–9, 1997, in Albuquerque, New Mexico. Its goal is to produce a list of mapped conservation sites in the [[Arizona-New Mexico Mountains Ecoregion]] that, if managed for conservation, would protect the ecoregion's biodiversity. The packet contains the agenda, logistics, panel rosters, conservation target lists, site nomination criteria, and a draft [[Coarse Filter]] methodology. Fishes and aquatic habitats are one of seven expert panels. The fish target list is among the most imperiled in the ecoregion.

## Background & Description

**Ecoregion setting.** The packet draws on the USDA Forest Service description (Bailey 1995, Province M313). The province covers about 50,000 mi² (130,000 km²) of Arizona and New Mexico. Elevations range from 4,500 to 10,000 ft, with some peaks reaching 12,600 ft. Average annual precipitation is 10–35 in, most of it falling as snow in the mountains. Vegetation zones grade from [[Ponderosa Pine Forest]] and mixed grassland in the foothills to Douglas-fir, Engelmann spruce, and alpine belts at the highest elevations. The ecoregion contains some of the oldest mountains in the Southwest, with Precambrian igneous rocks about 1.5 billion years old. The [[Mogollon Rim]] runs more than 300 miles and forms the southern edge of much of the ecoregion. Much of the land is federally owned, mainly by the U.S. Forest Service, and includes parts of several tribal nations (White Mountain Apache, Mescalero Apache, Navajo, and Zuni).

**Workshop structure.** The workshop ran on Tuesday, April 8 and Wednesday, April 9, 1997. Day 1 included seven concurrent expert panels:
1. Communities, Landscapes & Natural Processes
2. Birds
3. Mammals
4. Herptiles
5. Fish, Aquatic Invertebrates & Aquatic Habitats
6. Invertebrates
7. Rare Plants

Each panel had a facilitator and a recorder. Day 2 used three geographic breakout groups: the Mogollon and Central Arizona highlands; the Chuska Mountains and west-central New Mexico; and the Sacramento and Guadalupe Mountains. The fish, aquatic invertebrate, and aquatic habitat panel was facilitated by Jeff Baumgartner. Its roster included six Arizona experts (including Paul Marsh, Wendell Minckley, John Rinne, Terry Myers, Kirk Young, and Sally Stefferud) and one New Mexico expert (Steve Platania). Invitee confirmation was current as of March 21, 1997, and not all invitees had confirmed.

**Conservation framework.** TNC's goal is "the long-term survival of all viable native species and natural community types." Because data are incomplete, the workshop relied on a portfolio of sites. It focused on vulnerable species (Heritage global ranks G1–G3 or T1–T3) and viable native communities, using a coarse filter as a surrogate for what is unknown. "Vulnerable" is defined by evidence of decline, threat of future decline, and rarity, with greater emphasis on decline and threat than in past TNC practice.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The packet notes that the ecoregion has more bird and mammal species than any other place in the Southwest. It also holds more than 100 rare plants and animals, over 25 of which are federally or state listed as endangered or threatened. Of special concern are critically imperiled aquatic species, including:
- [[Gila Chub]]
- [[Chihuahua Chub]]
- [[Gila Trout]]
- [[Apache Trout]]
- [[Roundtail Chub]]
- [[Loach Minnow]]
- [[Spikedace]]
- [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]]

The ecoregion's headwaters feed the Little Colorado, Gila, Mimbres, and Verde rivers. Winter snows and summer monsoon rains drive the river systems. The packet states that the processes sustaining these systems (fire, flooding, and natural hydrology) are themselves imperiled. Causes include long-term fire suppression, poorly managed livestock grazing, and stream channelization.

**Target lists.** The draft target lists (March 12 and 21, 1997) were compiled by the New Mexico Natural Heritage Program, the Arizona Data Management Systems, and the New Mexico Field Office of TNC. They include:
- **Fish:** about 14 entries, including several suckers (*Catostomus* spp.), chubs, spinedace, dace, spikedace and trout. Entries are coded for endemism, decline, global rarity, peripheral status, and representativeness.
- **Amphibians and reptiles:** about two dozen species, including Arizona toad, Chiricahua leopard frog, Sonoran mud turtle, and several whiptails and lizards.
- **Birds:** a long list of Partners in Flight priority species, including Mexican spotted owl, Gila woodpecker, and American peregrine falcon, with Heritage ranks.
- **Mammals:** about 25 to 30 entries, including Apache-region bats, chipmunks, the Mexican vole, and the New Mexican jumping mouse.
- **Invertebrates:** about 15 entries, mostly aquatic snails (*Pyrgulopsis*), the Socorro isopod, and the Page Spring micro-caddisfly.
- **Plants:** well over 100 vascular plant species, across many global and state rank categories.

**Natural communities.** A draft list of plant associations, arranged by the NatureServe-style hierarchy (class, formation, alliance), covers forest, woodland, riparian and wetland, shrubland, and grassland types. Declining communities for the coarse filter include alpine tundra, old-growth ponderosa pine, bristlecone pine, montane scrub, Madrean evergreen woodland, montane and floodplain riparian forest, arroyo riparian scrub, lacustrine and palustrine wetlands, seeps, hot and cold springs, and montane grasslands.

## Key Findings

- **Purpose and output:** The primary product is a list of mapped conservation sites, with boundaries and Site Nomination Forms, plus a list of critical data gaps. Each panel also produces its own site map. Sites were to be amalgamated into larger composite sites, ideally several thousand acres, based on watersheds, mesas, ownership, or managed natural areas.
- **Complement to GAP:** The [[Ecoregional Analysis]] combines three data layers: [[Gap Analysis Program]] remote-sensing vegetation products, [[Heritage Data]] element occurrence records, and expert knowledge. GAP identifies gaps in species protection. The ecoregional analysis identifies the sites needed to close them.
- **Coarse filter scoring:** Natural community attributes score 0.5 for secondary presence, 1.0 for a primary feature, and 2.0 for an outstanding example. The landscape score is the sum of four attributes, each scored 0–2:
  - *Size:* under 10,000 acres scores 0; 10,000–35,000 scores 0.5; 35,000–100,000 scores 1.0; over 100,000 scores 2.0.
  - *Integrity:* the percentage of undisturbed 20-acre mapping units, scored from 2.0 (80–100%) down to 0 (below 40%).
  - *Context:* the ratio of surrounding natural land to site area, scored from 2.0 (over 10×) down to 0 (under 2×).
  - *Natural processes:* scored from 2.0 (intact) down to 0 (severely impaired).
- **Priority thresholds:** *Priority landscapes* have a function score of 7 or more and a landscape score of 2.5 or more. *Priority local sites* have a function score of 7 or more with a landscape score below 2.5, or a function score above 5 combined with a best known example of a target community. All other sites are non-priority.
- **Protection Index:** Communities represented in fewer than five protected landscapes receive an index of 2.0. Those in five to ten receive 1.5, and those in more than ten receive 1.0. This rewards representation of poorly protected communities.
- **Site ranking example:** A hypothetical matrix ranks seven sites (Silver Slough, Black Mesa, Mimbres Peak, Gila Bench, Ruidoso Ranch, Guadalupe Pass, and others). Gila Bench has the highest natural community score (11). The packet notes that Black Mesa and Mimbres Peak have similar scores and suites of communities, so only one may be needed. The packet also cautions that site rankings do not by themselves determine a final portfolio.
- **Efficient portfolio design:** Sites should add the greatest new conservation value to the existing portfolio. For example, a site that protects a rare species and a viable community contributes more than a site with only the species. Sites should also retain as much ecosystem integrity as possible.
- **Partnership and socio-economic stance:** The portfolio will include sites where TNC is neither lead nor player, and it must respect local communities and tribal traditions. Strategies will emphasize collaboration, partnership, and stewardship recognition more than land acquisition.
- **Logistics and participant requirements:** Participants were asked to bring site lists, completed Site Nomination Forms, maps (preferably USGS 7.5′ quads at 1:24,000), and references. The workshop was held at a Howard Johnson's hotel in Albuquerque, with airline and hotel information provided.
