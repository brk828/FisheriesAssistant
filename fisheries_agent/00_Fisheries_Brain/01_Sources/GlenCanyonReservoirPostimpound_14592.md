# Lake Powell Post-Impoundment Investigation (1966), Progress Report No. 4

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Salmonidae #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This progress report from the cooperative Utah Division of Fish and Game and Arizona Game and Fish Department programme documents the fourth year of post-impoundment study of [[Lake Powell]], a reservoir formed behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]] (impoundment began January 1963). Utah was responsible for physical-chemical limnology and fishery development; Arizona for creel census, harvest and plankton. The study period ran from February 1, 1966 to January 31, 1967 and was financed partly under Section 8 of Public Law 485 (the [[Colorado River Storage Act]]).

Key results include an extreme drawdown after a poor 1966 runoff (about 6.7 million acre-feet versus a normal ~11 million), a [[Thermocline]] that deepened to over 100 feet, a [[Density Current]] that reached the reservoir late in the year, and fish populations dominated by introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], with [[Carp]] the most numerous non-game species.

## Background & Description

The reservoir was studied through a fixed network of 17 chemical and physical stations, plus three temperature-only stations added in June 1966. Fish were collected by horizontal and vertical gill nets, a barge electroshocker, a 100-foot beach seine and creel interviews. Age was determined from scales read on a microprojector, using the Lee/oak-tag method and a body-scale intercept assumed to be 15 mm for centrarchids and 20 mm for trout.

Stocking in 1966 consisted only of about 2.1 million two- to three-inch [[Rainbow Trout]] fingerlings, spread by aircraft from May 6 to 11 across eight areas (for example 500,000 each at Bullfrog Bay and Cape Good Hope). Cumulatively, the reservoir had received about 13.2 million rainbow or kamloops trout, 2.9 million largemouth bass, 635,000 [[Kokanee Salmon]] (1963-1964) and 44,050 [[Black Crappie]] (1964-1965).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water management.** Storage fell from 8,775,000 acre-feet at the start of 1966 to about 850,000 acre-feet less by year end; the reservoir dropped 18 feet to elevation 3517. The authors judged this sudden loss harmful to early life stages, most probably to [[Centrarchidae]] that spawned late and lost littoral refuge.

**Aquatic plants.** Rooted aquatic vegetation was sparse. Non-aquatic species, including [[Russian Thistle]] (*Salsola kali*), [[Tamarix]] and saltbush (*Atriplex garrettii*), established between high and low water levels and provided cover for young fish during rising water.

**Stratification and thermal regime.** Lake Powell was classified as monomictic. The [[Colorado River]] entered as a cold density current in winter (about 34 °F in January-February) and as a warm, turbid, organic-rich inflow in summer. Epilimnion temperatures reached 80-83 °F in July, and the thermocline ran from about 30 to 100 feet, more than 70 feet thick. Average hypolimnion temperatures in 1966 were 5-10 °F colder than in 1965. The authors linked the deepened metalimnion to the river's entry depth and to penstock withdrawal levels.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Summer thermocline oxygen fell to 2.6-3.2 ppm, while winter river inflow restored deep-water oxygen (up to 11.2 ppm near the upper bottom in December). Water quality in the thermocline degraded from its formation until about December.

**Water chemistry.** Bicarbonate alkalinity averaged 144 ppm at peak readings (December surface); carbonate remained trace-level. Sulfate in March averaged 247 ppm at the surface, Colorado River inflow contributed 580 ppm. Silica ranged 13-17 ppm, iron 0.01-0.1 ppm and conductivity 660-900 mmhos/cm. Total phosphate fell from 0.70 ppm (1964) to 0.60 (1965) and 0.40 ppm (1966). Secchi transparency averaged 12 feet in March and 27 feet in April; the Colorado River was consistently under one foot.

**Turbidity.** Colorado and San Juan river sediment dominated runoff turbidity from about April to July or August, affecting more than two-thirds of the reservoir and likely reducing fish habitat.

## Key Findings

- **Growth gradient.** [[Largemouth Bass]] grew fastest in the upper reservoir (Rincon to Tapestry Wall), with the 1963 year class reaching 275 mm at age I there versus 210 mm in the Dam-Gunsight section. Growth differences between areas diminished with each year class; the 1965 class showed only one significant regional difference.
- **Declining upper-reservoir growth.** Age I bass length dropped from 265 mm (1963 class) to 234 mm (1965 class) in the upper reservoir, while the lower reservoir rose from 209 to 226 mm. The authors concluded that factors favouring fast upper-lake growth had largely disappeared.
- **Age-growth and condition.** Scales from 584 bass, 361 rainbow trout and 28 black crappie were read. Mean bass total length was 227 mm at age I, 348 mm at age II and 410 mm at age III. Bass condition factors (K) averaged 2.36 in 1966 against 3.55 for 1964 fish of equal length.
- **Bass reproduction.** Spawning occurred across the reservoir, mostly early May to late June in the lower reservoir and to late July above Aztec. Nests in shallow water were abandoned by mid-May and contributed little. Young-of-year catch was 16.2 per 1,000 sq ft in the lower reservoir and 12.6 in the upper reservoir, above 1965 values of 10.1.
- **Bass harvest.** An estimated 57,363 bass were harvested February-October 1966 (plus about 4,407 November-January, unsampled). The 1965 year class supplied 53% of the catch, the 1964 class 38% and the 1963 class 9%.
- **Bass diet.** Fish dominated the diet (99.2% by volume in the lower reservoir). Green sunfish were the main prey, with [[Crayfish]] important only in the upper reservoir. No bluegill were found in bass stomachs.
- **Rainbow trout survival.** Trout concentrated near the dam and in Wahweap Bay. Catch per net-hour fell about 80% between the dam and Antelope Creek, and the lower-reservoir population declined about 75% from late 1965 to April 1966 and to roughly 5% of original numbers by the end of summer 1966. Natural reproduction was "largely unsuccessful."
- **Trout growth and harvest.** Mean trout lengths were 164, 285 and 350 mm at ages I, II and III. Of about 39,733 trout harvested, 94% were 1964 year-class fish. Returns from planted fish were 0.3% (1963), 1.4% (1964) and 0.07% (1965).
- **Trout depth.** Trout concentrated in the thermocline between 60 and 80 feet in September and near penstock depth, despite low dissolved oxygen (3.2-3.6 ppm).
- **Carp.** Carp were the most abundant non-game species, forming large schools in the upper arms. Lengths ranged 150-430 mm, and the authors judged Lake Powell "less than favorable carp habitat."
- **Green sunfish and bluegill.** Green sunfish declined in large sizes after 1965 but were still abundant as fry. Bluegill, first recorded in 1965, were rapidly increasing in the upper reservoir (57 collected in 1966; median 220 mm).
- **Channel catfish.** Three year classes were present (1963-1965). The 1965 class was the first clearly successful one since impoundment. A nodule-forming internal parasite was observed in larger fish.
- **Black crappie.** Twenty-one fish were collected. Mean first- and second-year growth was 108 and 103 mm. The first evidence of natural reproduction was young-of-year fish taken in March 1967.
- **Walleye.** Four specimens (379-560 mm) were netted and aged IV, IV, III and VI, so the species was not established as a fishery.
- **Non-game species.** Other taxa recorded included [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila robusta elegans*, the only chub form found), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (440-620 mm), black bullhead and a second record of yellow bullhead. The red shiner was found only once and the fathead minnow and mosquitofish were absent after 1964.
- **Management implications.** The authors recommended marking future trout plants by reservoir area to test stocking success, and noted that 1963-1965 trout plantings had produced low returns.
