# Selectivity and Efficiency of Commercial Fishing Devices in Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 study, published in the *Journal of the Arizona Academy of Science*, evaluates the selectivity and efficiency of several commercial fishing devices in two central Arizona reservoirs, [[Apache Lake]] and [[Roosevelt Lake]], on the [[Salt River]]. Fieldwork ran from 1966 to 1968 and was funded by a Grant-in-Aid from the [[Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]] (under P.L. 88-309) to the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. The authors compared [[Gill Nets]], [[Trammel Nets]] and [[Electrofishing]] across more than 500 net nights. The central finding is that the 51 mm inner-wall [[Trammel Nets]] captured the most commercially valuable fish, mainly [[Buffalofishes]], while electrofishing was largely ineffective for them. The study frames commercial harvest as a multiple-use question that often conflicts with [[Recreational Fisheries]].

## Background & Description

The authors note that commercial fishing in United States reservoirs is often opposed, and that it is increasingly proposed as a "multiple use" of impoundments. Conflicts with recreational anglers commonly arise from a lack of information on fish sizes, qualities, and abundance, and from a lack of knowledge about suitable harvest techniques.

Commercially important species in the study lakes were [[Buffalofishes]] (*Ictiobus* spp.) and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*). The dominant sport fishes were [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) and various sunfishes (*Lepomis* spp.). Before this study, the region had only a limited commercial fishery that could not meet demand in the Phoenix metropolitan area, so buffalofishes were imported from adjacent states.

**Methods.** The authors evaluated:
- **Seines**, **experimental gill nets** (float- and lead-line), and **fyke nets**, which were used but not specifically evaluated.
- **Experimental nylon gill nets** (flag type), with mesh sizes from 13 to 127 mm (bar measure), set 1 to 2 m below the surface.
- **Nylon trammel nets**, with outer walls of 102 to 127 mm and inner walls of 51 or 64 mm, set with corklines or leadlines.
- **Electrofishing** from a boat with a 230-volt, 2,650-watt A.C. generator and two 75-watt floodlights for night illumination.

All nets were set overnight. Effort was standardized as a "net night," defined as 30.4 m (100 ft) of net fished for a single night of about 11 hours. Mesh sizes are reported in millimeters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Buffalofish populations.** The buffalofish population in Roosevelt Lake is a single-species form of *Ictiobus cyprinellus*, described as aberrant and distinct from the multi-species population in Apache Lake (*I. cyprinellus*, *I. niger* and *I. bubalus*).

**Seasonal movement.** Buffalofish catches varied with general population movements. In Roosevelt Lake, catches were highest in May and lowest in September–October. The authors attribute the spring peak to a general migration of fish into shallow water, with males moving first. Males were up to 90% of the early-May catch, and females appeared later in May. The largest catches of the year, up to 150 fish per net night, were taken in warmer periods, and catches declined on cooler nights. After spring, fish moved to deeper water, where they appeared to wander randomly. The autumn decline is attributed to a general lack of movement or to retirement to deep areas.

**Habitat constraints.** Seines and fish traps were ineffective in these lakes, largely because buffalofishes avoid shallow waters.

**Lake drainage.** Carp weighing more than 10 kg were common in Canyon and Saguaro lakes before those reservoirs were drained in the late 1960s.

**Fouling.** Algae and other materials accumulating on nets reduced efficiency, likely by making nets more visible to fish. Accumulation could occur within two or three days, and commercial fishermen were said to routinely clean nets during summer.

## Key Findings

**1. Buffalofishes were most susceptible to the 51 mm inner-wall trammel net.** More than 41% of buffalofishes taken from Roosevelt and Apache lakes were caught by this unit. The 64 mm inner-wall trammel net caught fewer, and the 38 mm and 32 mm gill nets caught about equal numbers of buffalofishes, but less than half as many as the small-mesh trammel net.

**2. Efficiency of the main devices.** The most efficient netting for commercial species was the 51 mm trammel net, which averaged 1.8 fish per net night, with 0.7 sport fishes per net night. The 64 mm trammel net took less than half as many commercial fish as the 51 mm trammel net. A 38 mm gill net took only 0.9 commercial fishes per net night and 0.3 sport fishes.

**3. Electrofishing was selective but inefficient for commercial species.** Electrofishing was relatively ineffective for buffalofishes, which escaped laterally or passed through the electrical field before being immobilized. Fish were stunned only when trapped in narrow, shallow bays. Electrofishing was effective for [[Largemouth Bass]], especially in spring. Per man-hour per usable fish, it yielded much lower efficiency than entanglement devices.

**4. Common carp were taken mainly in smaller meshes.** Carp were most frequently caught in 38 mm and 32 mm gill nets. Their serrated dorsal and anal spines make them highly susceptible to entanglement. Carp were taken in all mesh sizes except the 64 mm trammel net. Their absence from larger-mesh nets was attributed to the lack of large fish in Apache and Roosevelt lakes, for reasons unknown. Carp were moderately susceptible to electrofishing at all times of the year, especially in spring and early summer.

**5. Largemouth bass were the key sport species.** Largemouth bass were caught in the same gill-net mesh sizes as carp, at about the same efficiency. They formed a higher proportion of autumn–winter gill-net catches than carp, a trend reversed in spring–summer. Other game species, including [[Black Crappie]] and [[Channel Catfish]], made up a small proportion of the total catch in every device. Channel catfish have serrated spines, but their habits meant they were not caught in large numbers.

**6. Species and size absences shape catch composition.** Several species were absent from the catch, and small buffalofishes are generally absent from these lakes. Differences in body shape, spines, movement and population size structure all influence capture. The authors conclude that each net and mesh size samples a different portion of the lake's fish community.

**7. Data-quality caveat.** The 51 mm and 64 mm inner-wall trammel nets differed in efficiency, but the authors consider it unlikely that the difference resulted from inner-wall mesh size alone (51 mm vs. 64 mm). They suggest that net age or colour (one net was darker and stained brownish, the other whitish) may have contributed, along with other factors not visible to the eye.

**8. Summary of conclusions.** The authors conclude that the 51 mm inner-wall trammel net is the most effective device for harvesting commercial species in these reservoirs. Gill nets are useful but less efficient, and electrofishing is not a practical commercial method. Harvest efficiency depends strongly on seasonal fish movement, net condition, and the size and species composition of the population being exploited.
