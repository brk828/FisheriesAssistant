# *Branchinecta gigas*: A Non-Filter-Feeding Raptorial Anostracan

#anostraca #branchiopoda #aquatic-ecology #feeding-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by Geoffrey Fryer, published in the *Proceedings of the Linnean Society of London*, shows that [[Branchinecta Gigas]], the largest known extant anostracan (fairy shrimp), is a carnivore that captures and eats large prey, mainly other anostracans. Gut analyses and morphology show that the species cannot filter-feed. Its trunk limbs form a prey-retaining cage, and its mandibles carry a posterior tooth that allows transverse biting. Fryer also reports that several other *Branchinecta* species feed by [[Scraping Behaviour]] on bottom material, which challenges the common view that anostracans are microphagous filter-feeders.

## Background & Description

Anostracans are generally considered filter-feeders, and detailed studies by [[Harold Cannon]] on [[Chirocephalus Diaphanus]] described the mechanism in depth. Cannon suggested that observations on *Chirocephalus* could be applied to all anostracans. Fryer notes, however, that the order shows considerable diversity of feeding habits, and that *C. diaphanus* can also collect large particles.

*Branchinecta gigas* was first described by [[Lynch]] (1937), who noted that some guts were red from ingested [[Diaptomid Copepods]]. The largest specimen Fryer examined was a female almost exactly 10 cm long. Its trunk limbs lack filter plates, and its gnathobases carry widely separated spines. Fryer's study was based on specimens supplied by Lynch and on dried mud, from which hatching failed, so the living feeding mechanism was not observed directly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species occurs in alkaline ponds and lakes in the Pacific Northwest of the USA. Its gut contents reflect the local prey community:

- **Eastern Washington sample:** five specimens, all feeding apparently exclusively on other anostracans.
- **Buckaroo Lake, Oregon, and Upper Grand Coulée, Washington:** eight specimens in total, with anostracans predominating at each locality, though other material was also taken.
- Prey were ingested whole. Prey belonged to a different species, mainly [[Branchinecta Mackini]], which Lynch described as a constant associate of *B. gigas*, so cannibalism was not evident.

Other items included [[Calanoid Copepods]], the species' own eggs, and a beetle larva. Two small specimens (4 and 5 cm) that had been scraping the bottom contained only detritus, and the presence of detritus indicates that scraping is sometimes employed.

Fryer also reviews related species in the same genus:

- [[Branchinecta Ferox]] (up to 7 cm, probably the second-largest extant anostracan) is also incapable of filtration, but has an intermediate limb structure.
- [[Branchinecta Packardi]] and [[Branchinecta Mackini]] scrape bottom material persistently.
- [[Branchinecta Paludosa]] is a filter-feeder whose gut contents nevertheless included bottom-derived material.

## Key Findings

**Diet and feeding behaviour**
- *B. gigas* feeds largely on large food masses and is a carnivore, which had not previously been reported for Anostraca.
- Gut contents included at least five anostracans in one individual and four in another. Some prey were at least 1 cm long in life.
- One preserved specimen was captured in the act of passing a *B. mackini* between its mandibles. A second, collected alongside a 5 cm individual, had at least five other anostracans in its gut.
- Regurgitation during fixation was common, as shown by the usually empty anterior gut.

**Structural adaptations**
- No filter is present on any trunk limb. The endites and endopodites carry stout, widely separated spines, which form a cage closed by the endopodites, endites and trunk.
- Endites 4 to 6 carry up to ten anterior spines each. Other *Branchinecta* species typically have two in females, and the number is greater in males.
- Anterior spines of one limb interdigitate with posterior spines of the limb in front. Food is moved forward by promotion and remotion, where promotion pushes material and remotion scoops it onto the next limb.
- The posterior gnathobasic spines point inward and seal the cage in the midline.
- Mandibles carry a large sharply pointed posterior tooth that allows a measure of transverse biting without changing the axis of mandibular movement. The mandibles act as crushing rollers.
- Maxillules carry a stout ventral tooth suited to gripping and pushing large masses.
- A series of minute denticles on the inner surface of the labrum handles large food masses.

**Comparison with other Branchiopoda and Malacostraca**
- Fryer argues that the posterior mandibular tooth is a branchiopod specialisation of independent origin, and that it is comparable to the incisor process of lower Malacostraca, which evolved in association with raptorial habits. The tooth does not appear homologous to the incisor process.
- A similar convergent cusp appears in the cladoceran *Eurycercus lamellatus* and in the insects *Petrobius* and *Tomocerus*.

**Scraping and bottom feeding (other species)**
- *B. packardi* scraped for 12 to 35 per cent of the time (average just over 22 per cent) in eight 10-minute observation periods involving four adult females, and scraping continued even at early post-naupliar stages.
- *B. mackini* was observed scraping bottom mud and carrying large loads of substratum. Its gut contents contained large quantities of scraped inorganic particles.
- Scraping spines show a coarse-scraper and fine-brush arrangement. Their form differs between the sexes, and in *B. mackini* the female's scrapers appear most developed.

**Evolutionary interpretation**
- Fryer interprets the non-filtratory feeding of *B. gigas* as habit reversal, following the exploitation of large size, rather than a primitive condition.
- He suggests that the earliest anostracans may have been incapable of filter-feeding, and that the mid-Cambrian [[Opabinia]] was possibly such a species, following Hutchinson's interpretation.

**Limitations**
- Feeding was not observed in living *B. gigas*, because hatching from dried mud failed.
- Feeding habits of large *B. ferox* could not be established, because insufficient material was available. Its gut from one small individual contained detritus, arthropod spines and filamentous algae (*Zygnema* or *Spirogyra*), which did not permit firm conclusions.
