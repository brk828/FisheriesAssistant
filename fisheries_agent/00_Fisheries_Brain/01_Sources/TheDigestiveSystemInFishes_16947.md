# The Digestive System in Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review, attributed to William G. Gelling, describes the anatomy and physiology of the [[Digestive System]] in fishes across major taxa. It follows the four-part framework of Bertin (1958): the [[Buccal-Pharyngeal Cavity]], the [[Anterior Intestine]] (esophagus and [[Stomach]]), the [[Middle Intestine]] (the true intestine), and the [[Posterior Intestine]] (rectum and cloaca). The paper compares structures across cyclostomes, sharks, rays, chimaeras, sturgeons, bony fishes and lungfishes, with particular emphasis on the freshwater cyprinids. It concludes that digestive form is closely tied to feeding habit, and that rates of digestion and absorption depend on temperature and food quantity.

## Background & Description

The author adopts Bertin's functional outline because it gives immediate insight into how the system works while staying simple enough to be practical for classification. The alternative "Kopfdarm" and "Kumpfdarm" division of Banki (1936) is set aside in its favour. Figure 1 illustrates digestive tracts of representative fishes, including a lamprey, a chimaera, and a perch.

**Buccal-pharyngeal cavity.** This region includes the jaws, tongue, [[Palatine Organ]], [[Buccal Glands]], [[Buccal Denticles]], [[Pharyngeal Teeth]], [[Pharyngeal Pockets]] and [[Gill Rakers]].

- **Jaws:** Jaws are generally poorly developed compared with higher vertebrates. Cyclostomes lack jaws entirely. In sharks the cartilaginous jaws derive from the modified first branchial arch. Romer (1955) describes three jaw suspension types: [[Hyostylic Jaw Suspension]], [[Amphistylic Jaw Suspension]] and [[Autostylic Jaw Suspension]]. Most modern fishes are hyostylic.
- **Tongue:** A thickening of the buccal floor over the hyoid arch, lined with stratified epithelium rich in mucous cells, and sometimes toothed. It is not a true tongue.
- **Palatine organ:** A fleshy pad rich in mucous cells. When expanded it presses against the branchial basket and aids water filtering, digestion and taste.
- **Buccal denticles:** Present across the whole cavity in primitive sharks, restricted or lost in more evolved groups, and generally absent in teleosts (with some present on the gill arches of *Esox*). They help seize and hold prey.

**Pharyngeal structures.** Some fish have pharyngeal brushes on the superior pharyngeal bone that filter sand and mud from inhaled water. Pharyngeal teeth sit on the inferior pharyngeal bone. Cypriniformes show the most extensive development. Catostomids generally have a single row of unspecialized teeth, whereas cyprinids have double or triple rows. Cyprinid pharyngeal teeth are an important taxonomic character, each species being assigned a tooth formula. For example, *Notropis volucellus* has two single rows of four teeth (formula 4-4), and *N. atherinoides* has 2,4-4,2. Moore (1957) uses these counts in his key to *Notropis*.

**Anterior intestine.** The esophagus is a short, thick-walled tube with mucosa and muscularis layers, plus a thin mesenteric layer where it enters the body cavity. Its muscle shifts from striated anteriorly to smooth posteriorly. The [[Stomach]] is defined functionally as the region that secretes pepsin, since many groups lack a morphologically distinct stomach. Bertin (1958) lists three stomach types: rectilinear, siphonal and coecal (see Fig. 2).

**Middle intestine.** The boundary between stomach and intestine is hard to fix. It is marked by the [[Pyloric Valve]] or [[Pyloric Sphincter]], or by the entry of the bile duct, although the bile duct position can vary. The intestinal epithelium is simple columnar, with [[Goblet Cells]] for secretion and absorptive columnar cells. Intestinal length varies widely: it is short and uncurved in chondrichthyans, chondrosteans, holosteans and dipnoans, and often many times body length in teleosts.

**Spiral valve.** This structure enlarges the absorptive surface in chondrichthyans, chondrosteans, holosteans and dipnoans, and survives as vestigial remnants in some teleosts. Parker (1885) recognized four types, ranging from a simple attached valve to a many-layered tube. Spiral turns range from 4 to 50 in selachians, 5 to 10 in dipnoans, 5 to 8 in chondrosteans, and 2 to 5 in holocephalans and holosteans.

**[[Pyloric Caeca]].** These blind outgrowths of the anterior intestine vary from one to over a thousand (Suyehiro 1941; Rahimullah 1945). They usually stay separate but can fuse into a gland-like organ, and they increase the absorptive surface.

**Posterior intestine.** The rectum is distinguished by its narrower diameter and circular musculature. Selachians have a caecal gland that secretes mucus. A true [[Cloaca]] occurs in selachians and dipnoans, but is only embryonic in higher fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding habit and gut form.** Imhof (1936) found that herbivores generally have more buccal glands than carnivores. Many authors link intestinal length to diet: herbivores are proposed to have long intestines, carnivores short ones, and omnivores intermediate. The author cautions that exceptions are numerous. Fish lacking well-developed stomachs, including minnows (Cyprinidae), loaches (Cobitidae), killifishes (Cyprinodontidae) and clingfishes (Gobiesocidae), usually have a strong masticating apparatus and eat prey that needs little preparation before chemical digestion (Bertin 1958).

**Gill rakers and feeding.** Gill rakers form a branchial filter that protects delicate gill filaments and strains food. In the pike, which feeds mainly on other fish, rakers are reduced and allow larger food particles through. In clupeids feeding on micro-crustaceans, rakers form long, closely set bristles (Norman 1949).

**Temperature and food quantity.** Maltzan (1935) fed coloured food to carp and found gut transit took 18 hours at 10°C and 4.5 hours at 26°C. Moore (1941) found that *Perca* and *Pomotis* fed two or three times daily assimilated no more food than those fed once daily, implying less complete absorption when food is abundant. Temperature and food amount are therefore key controls on absorption rate.

**Chemical environment of digestion.** Hydrochloric acid, produced in the cardiac stomach mucosa, lowers the pH of the food mass so that [[Pepsin]] can break down proteins. Pepsin acts only at low pH, with an optimum near 2.0. In *Esox*, surface pH of the food mass ranged from 2.4 to 3.6, but pH below the surface was often near 5.0 (Vonk 1929). This slows digestion of large prey, which can take several days (the source's exact range is garbled in the scan). The intestine then becomes neutral to slightly alkaline, probably mainly through intestinal secretions, which supports the action of [[Digestive Enzymes]] (proteases, carbohydrases and lipases) from the liver, pancreas and intestinal wall.

## Key Findings

- The digestive system is organized into four functional regions (Bertin 1958), providing a practical framework for comparing fishes.
- Jaw suspension is hyostylic in most modern fishes, and jaws are poorly developed relative to higher vertebrates; cyclostomes have none.
- Pharyngeal tooth formulae are a key taxonomic character in cyprinids, for example 4-4 in *Notropis volucellus* and 2,4-4,2 in *N. atherinoides*.
- Stomach presence is not universal; many cyprinids, loaches, killifishes and clingfishes lack a well-developed stomach.
- Spiral valves and pyloric caeca both increase absorptive surface. Pyloric caeca number from one to over a thousand.
- Intestinal length tracks diet broadly (herbivores long, carnivores short), though the author stresses numerous exceptions.
- Gut transit time in carp was 18 hours at 10°C and 4.5 hours at 26°C (Maltzan 1935), showing strong temperature dependence.
- Feeding more often did not increase assimilation in *Perca* and *Pomotis* (Moore 1941), suggesting absorption is less complete when food is plentiful.
- Digestion depends on HCl lowering pH for pepsin, with optimum pepsin activity near pH 2.0, and surface and interior food pH can differ markedly.
