# Responses of Fish to a Strobe Light/Air-Bubble Barrier

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-behavior #clupeidae #osmeridae #moronidae #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tested whether [[Strobe Light]] combined with an [[Air-Bubble Barrier]] can exclude fish at power-plant intakes. Freshwater species ([[Alewife]], [[Rainbow Smelt]], [[Gizzard Shad]]) and estuarine species ([[White Perch]], [[Spot]], [[Atlantic Menhaden]]) were tested under varied bubble spacing, illumination, [[Current Velocity]], [[Turbidity]], [[Flash Rate]] and [[Acclimation]] conditions. All species avoided strobe light, and most avoided bubbles. Adding strobe light to the bubble barrier generally increased avoidance, though not for white perch. The authors conclude the combined system is promising for fish management but has not been tested in the field.

## Background & Description

Behavioral deterrents are being developed to manage fish movement at [[Power Plant Intakes]]. Light has been used to exclude fish, guide them to bypass systems, and increase commercial catches. Bubble screens have a mixed record: earlier reports described them as ineffective at night or in turbid water. The authors attributed some earlier failures to weak experimental design. They hypothesized that illuminating a bubble screen would improve it, and that intermittent [[Strobe Light]] (intense, short-duration flashes) would repel fish more effectively than continuous light.

The study drew on research from [[Ontario Hydro]] and the [[University of Maryland]] Horn Point Environmental Laboratories, conducted independently and combined for publication. Freshwater species were chosen because they make up most impingement at Ontario Hydro's Great Lakes stations. Estuarine species were the main impinged species at [[Potomac Electric Power Company]] facilities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Freshwater species.** Adult gizzard shad (about 25–40 cm) were collected by electrofishing in [[Lake Ontario]] near the [[Pickering Nuclear Generating Station]], about 70 km northeast of Toronto. Alewife (11–16 cm total length) and smelt (7–10 cm) were beach-seined at night. Fish acclimated to a 14 h light : 10 h dark photoperiod.

**Estuarine species.** White perch, spot and menhaden were collected by seine, trawl and trap net from the [[Choptank River]] on the eastern shore of [[Chesapeake Bay]], Maryland. Standard lengths were 8–22 cm (white perch), 8–16 cm (spot) and 13–20 cm (menhaden). Fish were held at least 3 days on a 12:12 h cycle.

**Key environmental variables tested:**
- Current velocity: 0.11–0.32 m/s in freshwater tests; 0.2 and 0.5 m/s in estuarine tests.
- Turbidity: clear, 1.0 NTU and 3.0 NTU, created with fine sediment (<2 mm) from the Pickering discharge.
- Illumination: simulated day, night, and red light (630 nm peak) for video recording, which the authors judged to have little or no effect on fish.
- Flash rate: at least 300 flashes/min in freshwater tests; 300 and 600 flashes/min in estuarine tests.

**Test facilities.** Freshwater tests used a 5.5 m circular pool with a 3.6 × 1.5 m runway, and a 9.1 × 4.6 m oval pool with a 5.0 × 1.5 m zone. Estuarine tests used a 7.9 × 2.4 m rectangular tank with a 1.8 × 1.2 m test area. Fish were tracked by [[Closed-Circuit Television]] and visual observation.

**Equipment.** The freshwater bubble wand was porous ABS plastic (1.0 cm I.D., 3.0 cm O.D.) with bubbles under 1 mm, spaced 0 (continuous), 5, 10 or 20 cm apart. Estuarine bubbles came from compressed air through aqua-mist bars. Strobes were xenon units with about 1 W flash power and about 80 µs flash duration, with a fairly uniform spectrum over 400–700 nm.

## Key Findings

**Strobe light alone**
- **Gizzard shad:** Passages fell about 50% relative to controls (P<0.01). Encounters 0.6 m and 1.2 m from the light fell about 64% and 54%. Passages were highest at 0.32 m/s, suggesting avoidance weakens at higher current.
- **Estuarine species:** All three species avoided strobe light.
  - Spot: 9–100% effectiveness. The highest (100%) occurred in dark-acclimated fish at 600 flashes/min. Only one condition (0.2 m/s, 300 flashes/min, light-acclimated) was not significant.
  - Menhaden: 9–37% effectiveness, significant under all conditions.
  - White perch: 12–36% effectiveness at 0.2 m/s. At 0.5 m/s with 600 flashes/min, the fish showed slight attraction rather than avoidance.
- Flash rates of 300 and 600 flashes/min produced similar effectiveness. Avoidance was greater in dark-acclimated fish.

**Air bubbles alone**
- Under low-level light, effectiveness averaged about 98% for shad, 70% for alewife and 92% for smelt.
- Under simulated night, effectiveness fell to about 80% for shad and 51% for alewife.
- Bubble spacing significantly affected shad and alewife (P<0.01). Shad performed best at 5 and 20 cm spacing; smelt performed best at 0 (continuous) and 5 cm.
- At night, the 20 cm barrier excluded alewife better than 5 cm (P<0.05).
- Illumination raised effectiveness by about 18% (shad) and 19% (alewife) relative to darkness.

**Combined strobe light and air bubbles (freshwater)**
- For alewife, bubbles alone were 38–73% effective across turbidity and current conditions. High turbidity lowered this to 59% (0.15 m/s) and 38% (0.32 m/s).
- With strobe light added, effectiveness rose to about 90–98% (P<0.01). The source gives the baseline range as "38 to 73%"; the Interpretive Analysis gives "88 to 73" and "98–98%," which conflicts with these figures.
- Current and turbidity significantly affected passages (P<0.01). Increases in either generally increased passages.

**Combined strobe light and air bubbles (estuarine)**
- Spot: effectiveness rose from 9% to 63% in light-acclimated fish and from 79% to 85% in dark-acclimated fish.
- Menhaden: 67% (light-acclimated) and 81% (dark-acclimated). The authors state this was at least 50% higher than strobe alone.
- White perch: 36% (light-acclimated) and 58% (dark-acclimated). Fish readily penetrated the barrier, so it may be ineffective for this species.

**Sample sizes and statistics**
- Freshwater bubble tests: 5 replicates per spacing, 20 or more fish per replicate, and 10 controls.
- Freshwater strobe tests: 5 replicates of 30 min each, 25 fish per replicate, and 5 controls per current speed.
- Combined freshwater tests: 5 replicates per condition, 25 fish per test, 30-min duration.
- Estuarine tests: 4 replicates per condition, 5 fish per test.
- Analysis: ANOVA with Student–Newman–Keuls comparisons (freshwater); chi-square with the pre-strobe distribution as the expected value (estuarine).

**Interpretation**
- Pelagic species (alewife, smelt, shad) were repelled by bubble curtains. Demersal species (white sucker, spot, white perch) have been reported to be attracted to them, so response may depend on habitat.
- Bubble screens worked better in low light than in darkness, which suggests they act as a visual cue rather than a tactile one. Sound or pressure effects were not excluded.
- Avoidance appears linked to flash rate and flash duration rather than spectral composition, though the mechanism is only partly understood.
- Strobe light alone was less effective than the combined scheme for reducing passage.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Alewife, gizzard shad and smelt avoided the air-bubble barrier. Illumination and spacing significantly affected performance.
- All species avoided strobe light, with responses varying by current, flash rate and acclimation.
- Avoidance was generally enhanced when bubbles were combined with strobe light. The authors judge the combined system promising for power-plant diversion and possibly fish harvesting, but it needs rigorous field testing.
