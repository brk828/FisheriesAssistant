# Geographic Distribution of the Striped Mullet (*Mugil cephalus*) in the Atlantic and Eastern Pacific Oceans

## Executive Summary
This 1993 paper by Carter R. Gilbert re-evaluates the commonly cited geographic range of the [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*), one of only ~14 inshore marine fish species with a circumtropical/worldwide distribution (per Briggs 1960). Through literature review and museum specimen surveys, Gilbert demonstrates that the species' true Atlantic distribution is far more restricted than previously reported in standard references. Contrary to widespread claims of continuous distribution around the entire Atlantic (including the West Indies, Central America, and South America), voucher-specimen evidence supports a much narrower range: in the western Atlantic, populations are essentially confined to continental waters from New England to the Gulf of Campeche, Mexico, with no confirmed occurrences on any West Indian island. In the eastern Atlantic, the range extends south only to Ghana (~5°N), not to the tip of Africa as some sources claim. In contrast, the eastern Pacific population maintains the previously documented wide range from central California to northern Chile, including offshore islands — a notable ecological contrast to the "continental species" pattern seen in the Atlantic.

## Background & Description
- No comprehensive modern morphological analysis of global *M. cephalus* populations exists apart from [[Ebeling 1957, 1961]] studies.
- Ebeling compared eastern Pacific, Hawaiian, Floridian, Italian (Mediterranean), and Romanian (Black Sea) populations using standard morphological characters and **found no discernible differences** among these geographically disparate populations.
- Greatest morphological variation detected was in **dentition details**, and this variation was found *within* eastern Pacific populations rather than between ocean basins.
- **Diagnostic distinction from sympatric congener [[Mugil Liza]]** (western Atlantic "liza"/"lebrancho"), per Rivas (1980):
  - *M. liza* reaches greater maximum length (commonly >2 feet / ~61 cm)
  - *M. liza*: 31–36 lateral line scales vs. *M. cephalus*: 38–42 lateral line scales
  - *M. liza* has a less evenly concave caudal fin
  - Additional differences in several mensural (measurement-based) characters
- In the eastern Pacific, no congeneric species of *Mugil* creates confusion with *M. cephalus* identification (unlike the Atlantic situation with *M. liza*).
- A voucher specimen from Ghana (USNM 305487) measured ~140 mm standard length (SL).
- A voucher specimen from El Salvador (USNM 87279) measured 320 mm SL.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Catadromous life history**: spawning occurs in salt water, but many adults spend the majority of their lives in fresh water, sometimes migrating hundreds of miles upstream ([[Burgess 1980]]; [[Robison and Buchanan 1988]]).
- Classified as an important **commercial fish species** across many regions, including Florida ([[Rivas 1980]]; [[Collins 1985]]).
- Exhibits **"continental species" ecology** in the western Atlantic — a concept defined by Robins (1971) and Gilbert (1972), whereby such species are excluded from insular (island) habitats not due to physical dispersal barriers, but due to differing ecological conditions:
  - Continental waters: variable turbidity, salinity, and temperature
  - Insular waters: comparatively stable environmental conditions
- Notably **absent from all West Indian islands** despite presence of congeneric *Mugil* species there (confirmed via absence from FLMNH, University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, National Museum of Natural History, and University of Puerto Rico collections).
- **Contrasting habitat plasticity in southeastern Pacific and eastern Atlantic**: unlike its strict continental affinity in the western Atlantic, *M. cephalus* is known to inhabit offshore islands along the coasts of [[Peru]] and [[Chile]], and is reportedly present around [[Madeira]], the [[Azores]], and [[Hawaii]]. No explanation is currently available for this apparent habitat-preference discontinuity between ocean basins.
- General accepted latitudinal range (per Collins 1985): coastal regions worldwide between 42°N and 42°S.

## Key Findings
1. **Western Atlantic range is much more restricted than literature indicates.** Confirmed distribution extends from New England (occasional strays to Maine/Nova Scotia) south to the **Gulf of Campeche, Mexico**, with the southernmost confirmed record at **Tampico, Mexico** ([[Jordan and Dickerson 1908]]).
2. **No confirmed West Indian records.** Extensive review of literature (Cuba, Puerto Rico, Haiti, Jamaica, Bahamas, Hispaniola, Bermuda) and museum collections found **zero voucher specimens** of *M. cephalus* from any West Indian island, despite the presence of other *Mugil* species there. Prior claims of West Indies occurrence are considered erroneous, likely confusions with *[[Mugil Liza]]*.
3. **Absent from Caribbean coasts of Central/South America.** No records from Panama (Caribbean side), Costa Rica (Tortuguero), Belize, or Venezuela despite substantial survey efforts.
4. **Eastern Atlantic range does not reach southern Africa.** The species does *not* range down to the Cape of Good Hope as some literature suggests. Confirmed southern limit is **Ghana** (~5°N), with a possible historical record from **Togo**, based on a newly rediscovered 1962 voucher specimen (USNM 305487) that corroborates an earlier suspect report by Cadenat (1951).
5. **Rare occurrence in Senegal** (~15°N) based on limited specimens (Cadenat 1951; Massuti 1967).
6. **Reappears in southwestern Indian Ocean** (South Africa, east of Cape of Good Hope), per Smith (1935), suggesting a distributional gap along the rest of the west African coast.
7. **Eastern Pacific range is well-documented and broad**: from central California (stray records to San Francisco Bay; Reilly & Sakanari 1982) south through the Gulf of California (common; Thomson et al. 1979) to northern Chile (~20°S; Ebeling 1957, 1961).
8. **Apparent gap in Central America (El Salvador to Ecuador) likely a collecting artifact.** Despite lack of museum vouchers, credible eyewitness market surveys (Lavenberg 1993; Bussing 1993) confirm presence in Pacific fish markets of Nicaragua, Panama, Colombia, and Costa Rica, though the species is not common throughout this stretch.
9. **No cryptic species split supported by genetics — yet.** Although Briggs (1960) predicted that modern techniques would reveal *M. cephalus* to be a cryptic species complex, and preliminary mitochondrial DNA ([[Crosetti et al. 1993]]) and allozyme ([[Rossi et al. unpubl.]]) studies show **considerable genetic variation among populations**, no study to date has found differentiation sufficient to justify recognizing separate species.
10. **Recommendation for future records**: any reported range extensions should be verified with voucher specimens, given the demonstrated pattern of erroneous literature-based range claims — particularly given potential confusion with *[[Mugil Liza]]* in the western Atlantic.
