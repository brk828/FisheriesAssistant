# A Salt Spring in Borrowdale

#limnology #hydrogeology #salt-spring #freshwater-biology #geochemistry

> **Note on scope:** This source is a historical-geological investigation into the origin, chemistry, and human use of an inland salt spring at Manesty, Borrowdale, in the English Lake District. It does **not** contain fish, invertebrate, or fisheries data. In keeping with the instruction to synthesize faithfully to actual source content, this note documents the site's hydrochemistry, historical use, and its stated relevance to aquatic biological research, rather than forcing a fish-centric framework.

## Executive Summary

Charlotte Kipling, writing for the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], investigates the long-documented "salt spring" or "salt well" at Manesty in [[Borrowdale]], Cumberland — a saline/brackish inland spring long rumored to have been exploited for salt production by the monks of [[Furness Abbey]] (owners of Borrowdale ~1210–1537). Through examination of monastic charters, Dissolution-era inventories, Duchy of Lancaster records, and 18th–19th century medicinal water treatises, the paper traces the site's history from unsubstantiated monastic salt-making claims through its documented use from 1555 onward as a mineral/medicinal spa, to its eventual abandonment and its modern (20th century) significance as a site of interest for **biological research** rather than commercial or medicinal use. The paper closes by noting that the salt well and an adjacent salt-water-bearing mine shaft remain extant, with renewed scientific interest in their potential value for biological study — implicitly as an unusual inland saline habitat.

## Background & Description

Not applicable — no biological organisms (fish or otherwise) are morphologically described in this source. The "morphological" data available instead concerns the **physical and chemical characteristics of the spring water itself**, relevant background for any aquatic life inhabiting the site:

- **Water character**: Described repeatedly as a "meer Brine of inland salt," "strong brine," clear but rising with "a kind of moss or scum" (Hutchinson).
- **Salinity**: Per Thomas Short (1740), roughly 1/16 of the water's volume is pure salt (compared with ~1/22 for seawater) — i.e., the spring is *more concentrated in salt than the open ocean*.
- **Mineral composition** (per Rutty, 1757 and Monro, 1770): Contains marine salt (sodium chloride) combined with a "calcareous Glauber salt" (magnesium/calcium sulfate-type mineral) and a light calcareous earth (~6 drachms per gallon of the 8 oz total solid residue). Water curdled milk, suggesting acid/mineral salts; reacted variably with tincturing reagents (oil of tartar, sugar of lead, silver solution, syrup of violets, logwood, sumach, galls) in period chemical tests.
- **Taste/physical properties**: Brackish, bitterish, warm on the tongue; described as sulphureous in later (19th century) sources; associated with an absence of grass growth in the immediate vicinity ("no grass grows as far as this water reaches").
- **Geological setting**: Springs from a "hard blewstone" (a Lapis Scissilis / slate-like rock) adjoining a peat moss; situated in a boggy, level area near Grange, ~3 miles from Keswick.
- Modern (unpublished) chemical analysis (Mackereth, cited in text) confirms the water would yield salt suitable for domestic use if processed traditionally, though bitterer and not identical to sea salt.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While the paper is historical rather than ecological in focus, it establishes the site as a distinctive **inland saline microhabitat**:

- The spring occupies a **freshwater/saline interface** unusual for an inland, high-rainfall upland region (Borrowdale is noted for exceptionally high rainfall, which the author notes would make solar evaporation of brine "an extremely doubtful proposition").
- Site sits within a peat moss/bog margin, creating a localized halophytic-hostile zone (vegetation notably absent near the outflow).
- A second, chemically similar spring exists nearby at "Manesty Nook," and an old mine shaft in an adjacent field also contains salt water — suggesting a broader zone of saline groundwater intrusion or mineral vein exposure (associated historically with copper and lead [graphite/black lead] mining veins in the area).
- The author explicitly flags the site's **modern significance for biological research** due to its unusual saline chemistry within an otherwise freshwater upland ecosystem — implying interest in it as a natural experimental site for studying organisms tolerant of atypical salinity, though no specific biological survey data is presented in this paper.
- Historically, the spring was associated with mineral veins (copper, and the highly valuable graphite/black lead deposits), indicating a hydrogeologically active, mineral-rich substrate underlying the site.

## Key Findings

- **No direct evidence** exists in Furness Abbey's own records (charters, the Coucher Book, 1537 Dissolution inventories under Abbot Roger and the Royal Commissioners) that the monks manufactured salt at Borrowdale; charters mentioning "Salthous in Com. Cumbriae" more plausibly refer to salt-works near [[Millom]], not Manesty.
- Traditional medieval salt-making methodology (solar evaporation in a marsh reservoir + wood-fired boiling) was chemically feasible with this water but **practically unlikely** at Borrowdale given unsuitable terrain (no reservoir/embankment evidence) and the region's high rainfall.
- The **earliest firm documentary reference** to the salt well is from 1555 — a Crown commission inquiring into minerals (including "anie well of watar salt or brackishe") in the Manor of Borrowdale, followed by a December 1555 mineral-rights lease to Ambrose Dormer explicitly including "the Foss of Salt water."
- The salt well was consistently valued far below other local mineral resources: rent of **2 shillings** for the salt well vs. **13s 4d** for the graphite deposits in the 1555 lease — indicating low commercial value.
- In the 1615 **Great Deed of Borrowdale**, the salt well was sold together with the land to local tenants (unlike the valuable graphite holes, which the sellers retained) — reflecting its negligible economic importance by that date.
- Through the **17th–19th centuries**, the spring gained a secondary life as a **medicinal spa** ("Borrowdale Well"), described in period medical treatises (Short 1740, Rutty 1757, Monro 1770) as a strong purgative brine, used for treating dropsy, cachexy, jaundice, and cutaneous eruptions. A stone bathhouse was built (~1812, by Sir John Woodford) but fell into disuse due to lack of public interest.
- By 1871 (Macpherson) the spring's medicinal reputation had faded entirely ("It is now never heard of").
- By the time of writing (1961), the site was neglected and boggy, with the original bath wall remains still visible, and a secondary salt-water-bearing mine shaft identified in an adjacent field.
- The author's closing emphasis — that modern interest in the well stems from **its potential value for biological research**, not medicine or commerce — signals its intended relevance to freshwater/aquatic biologists (consistent with publication auspices, the [[Freshwater Biological Association]]) as an anomalous saline site within a freshwater catchment, warranting further limnological or biological investigation.
