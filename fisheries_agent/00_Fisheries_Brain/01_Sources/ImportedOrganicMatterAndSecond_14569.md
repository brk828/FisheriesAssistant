# Imported Organic Matter and Secondary Productivity in Streams

## Executive Summary
This seminal 1963 paper by H.B.N. Hynes challenges the assumption that in-stream primary production (algae, macrophytes, phytoplankton) is the principal energy base for lotic (running water) ecosystems. Hynes argues that most streams and rivers, due to their inherent physical instability (scour, turbidity, shading by riparian vegetation, wash-out during spates), cannot sustain substantial autochthonous (in-stream) primary production. Instead, he demonstrates — using invertebrate biomass data from a Welsh trout stream and comparative studies — that **allochthonous organic matter** (leaf litter, terrestrial detritus, dissolved organic matter from the surrounding landscape) forms the dominant energy base supporting secondary (invertebrate) and tertiary (fish) production in both **rhithron** (upland, stony, cold, turbulent) and **potamon** (lowland, sand/mud, warmer, laminar-flow) stream reaches. This work is foundational to the later development of the [[River Continuum Concept]] and to understanding [[Allochthonous Organic Matter|allochthony]] in freshwater food webs.

## Background & Description
While this is primarily a trophic-ecology paper rather than a morphological study, it references specific functional feeding morphologies and taxa:

- **Detritivorous/herbivorous grazer fishes**: *Campostoma anomalum* (central stoneroller) and *Hypentelium nigricans* (northern hog sucker) — both possess specialized mouthparts/gut adaptations enabling direct detritus feeding in rhithron habitats.
- **Detritivorous potamon fishes**: *Dorosoma cepedianum* (gizzard shad), *Rutilus rutilus* (roach), *Chondrostoma nasus* (nase), and an *Ictalurus* species — these possess gut and feeding morphologies suited to processing dead vegetable/organic matter directly.
- **Invertebrate functional feeding groups** referenced (relevant to fish diet composition):
  - Net-spinning caddisflies (**Hydropsychidae**) — filter-feeding structures capable of producing up to 2,620 sq.cm. of filtering net per hectare of streambed (Miller, 1956).
  - Black fly larvae (**Simuliidae**) — indiscriminate filter feeders.
  - Wood-eating stoneflies (e.g., *Pteronarcys*) and Trichoptera with mandibles/guts adapted to xylophagy (wood consumption).
  - Algal-scraping specialists: *Brachyptera* (Plecoptera) and Glossosomatinae (Trichoptera) with scraper-type mouthparts — a minority functional group compared to detritivores.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### Stream Classification (Illies, 1961)
Hynes adopts a bipartite classification of running-water habitats, foundational to stream typology:

| Feature | **Rhithron** | **Potamon** |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | Rock/stone/gravel | Sand/mud |
| Oxygen | Consistently high | May be low at times |
| Flow | Turbulent | Laminar |
| Summer temp | Relatively low | Relatively high |
| Dominant fauna | Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Diptera, Isopoda, Amphipoda, Decapoda | Chironominae, Tubificidae, Sphaeriidae, Unionidae, Dreissenia, Prosobranchia, burrowing Ephemeroptera |
| Seasonality | Strong winter/summer growth cycle | Largely smoothed out by continuous downstream detritus input |

This classification links directly to [[Rhithron]] and [[Potamon]] as core concepts in [[Stream Ecology]].

### Trophic Base
- **Primary production limitations**: Most streams support minimal standing algal/macrophyte biomass due to:
  - Riparian shading (pre-human landscapes universally forested/vegetated along watercourses).
  - Substrate instability (scour and spate-driven wash-out of periphyton, per Butcher 1933, 1946).
  - Turbidity in lowland (potamon) rivers limiting light penetration.
  - Exceptions: stable mossy rapids (low productivity due to slow moss growth), impounded reaches with planktonic blooms (e.g., Thames, Ohio, Nile), and rare stable shallow chalk streams supporting macrophytes (Edwards & Owens 1960: up to 520 g/sq.m dry wt/year).
- **Allochthonous input as energy base**: Leaf litter (especially from nitrogen-fixing riparian alders, *Alnus*), fallen elm leaves (7.5% air-dry wt. extractable protein per Boulter analysis), wood, and dissolved organic matter constitute the dominant food source for invertebrate detritivores, which in turn support fish production.
- **Microbial role**: Ivlev (1945) and Fredeen (1960) demonstrated that chironomid larvae and *Simulium* can be reared on bacteria alone, suggesting microbial conditioning of detritus is critical to nutritional availability — an early articulation of the [[Microbial Loop]] concept in streams.
- **Fish-invertebrate trophic linkage**: Allen's (1951) New Zealand trout stream study (reinterpreted by Hynes 1961 and Gerking 1962) found fish consumption of invertebrates at even conservative estimates to be ~6-7× the standing stock — implying massive turnover of allochthonous-derived secondary production up the food chain to support [[Salmonidae|trout]] fisheries yielding tens of kg/ha.

## Key Findings
1. **Allochthonous dominance**: In a Welsh trout stream (Hynes 1961), invertebrate "index units" (a biomass-weighted abundance metric) showed winter-active ("herbivore"/detritivore) taxa (487.5 units) outweighing summer-active taxa (188.5 units) by a ratio of **2.59:1**, and even after reallocating ambiguous non-seasonal taxa, winter dominance persisted (ratio >1). Carnivore biomass followed the same pattern (ratio 66:15 = 4.4, or 66:56 = 1.18 conservatively).
2. This demonstrates that stream secondary production in winter — when algal photosynthesis is negligible — is **at least as great, and probably twice as great**, as summer production, directly implicating terrestrial leaf-fall (autumn/winter allochthonous input) rather than contemporaneous algal photosynthesis as the energy driver.
3. **Fish as direct detritivores**: Several fish species (*Campostoma anomalum*, *Hypentelium nigricans* in rhithron; *Dorosoma cepedianum*, *Rutilus rutilus*, *Chondrostoma nasus*, *Ictalurus* sp. in potamon) feed directly on dead vegetable matter, bypassing the invertebrate intermediary entirely and directly linking fish production to terrestrial carbon inputs.
4. **Landscape–fishery correlation**: Badcock (1953) found heavily wooded Swedish streams supported more invertebrates than comparable open streams in Wales; Jewell (1927) found Kansas prairie streams (low terrestrial organic input) were comparatively unproductive — both supporting the allochthony hypothesis at the landscape scale, a precursor to riparian-buffer management concepts (see [[Riparian Zone Management]]).
5. **Filter-feeder amplification**: Hydropsychid caddisflies and simuliids proliferate dramatically in response to increased organic matter/particulate loading in water (e.g., below organic pollution sources or lake outflows), functioning as a critical trophic conduit converting fine particulate/dissolved organic matter and bacteria into biomass available to predatory fish.
6. **Potamon productivity paradox**: Despite minimal in-situ primary production (heavy scour, turbidity, unsuitable substrate for attached algae), potamon (lowland, turbid) rivers like the Missouri "teem with fishes," reinforcing that fish productivity in these systems is decoupled from local photosynthesis and instead tied to continuous downstream allochthonous detrital transport, smoothing out the sharp seasonal cycle seen in rhithron reaches.
7. **Conceptual implication**: Without terrestrial organic matter subsidy, running waters would be "almost a desert habitat" — establishing allochthonous input as a near-obligate energy source for lotic secondary and fish production, a concept later formalized in the [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al. 1980).
