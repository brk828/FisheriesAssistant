# Status of the Native Arizona Ichthyofauna, 1970

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 annotated checklist by [[W. L. Minckley]] of Arizona State University, Tempe, summarizes the current status of the native fishes of [[Arizona]]. Roughly 35 native kinds, including subspecies and suspected distinctive forms, are recognized. Although Arizona is largely desert, its aquatic habitats support more than 100 fish species, about 75 per cent of which have been introduced. Most present-day aquatic habitat is artificial, created through hydroelectric, irrigation and livestock-watering developments.

The native fauna has been greatly reduced. Desiccation of the lower [[Gila River]] and of lower segments of the [[San Pedro River]] and [[Little Colorado River]] shrank the ranges of aquatic organisms, and none of the native species appears to do well in artificial lakes, while few persist in canals. Of 79 recorded occurrences of native fishes in river basins or segments, 22 (almost 28 per cent) are extinct. Of 23 populations sampled well enough to estimate abundance trends, 20 show declines.

## Background & Description

The checklist reconstructs the original Arizona fish fauna using the extensive collections and field notes of [[R. R. Miller]] and [[Carl L. Hubbs]]. It draws on earlier reviews of habitat and fish change by Hastings (1959), Miller (1961), Hastings and Turner (1965), Lowe (1967), Miller and Lowe (1967), and Minckley and Deacon (1968). Details already published in those reviews are not repeated; new field data and taxonomic revisions are deferred to a forthcoming comprehensive publication on the State's entire fish fauna.

Interpretation of range statements is explicit: a phrase such as "range similar to that occupied in the past" means that the fish occupies waters persisting within its native range at a density similar to that recorded earlier, after accounting for massive losses in the quantity and quality of surface water.

Relative status is coded in Table 1 as follows:
- **A (abundant):** more than 50 per cent of estimated individuals in a river system
- **C (common):** an estimated 10 to 49 per cent
- **S (scarce):** 1 to 9 per cent
- **R (rare):** less than 1 per cent
- **E (extinct):** not taken for a period of years despite intensive collecting
- **+ / -:** estimated increase or decrease over the past 100 years
- **?:** insufficient information

Two-symbol entries (for example "R-A" for [[Gila Trout]]) give overall status first, then status within the preferred stream type or elevation range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss and water development.** Pristine Arizona habitats, including large lowland rivers, tree-lined creeks, ciénegas and oxbow lakes, supported a small but spectacularly endemic fish fauna. Surface-water losses reduced native ranges substantially, and the effects extended to terrestrial animals and plants dependent on water or riparian habitat. Changes noted include desiccation of most desert watercourses and modification of many intermediate-elevation streams. Upstream impoundments such as [[Glen Canyon Dam]], which was operational at the time, were expected to alter the [[Colorado River]] fauna rapidly.

**Habitat specialization.** Several species are tied to specific habitats that are now scarce. [[Loach Minnow]] requires shallow, swift, gravelly riffles, an environment becoming rare in Arizona. [[Longfin Dace]] favors warm, shallow, sandy-bottomed desert creeks. [[Gila Chub]] and others use slow-water habitats such as cienegas.

**Introduced species and biological interactions.** Introduced cyprinids have infested or displaced native minnows, and [[Speckled Dace]] stocks appear to be replaced by [[Longfin Dace]] in some intermediate-elevation creeks. Introduced [[Largemouth Bass]] caused the collapse of a population of [[Desert Pupfish]] at [[Monkey Spring]]. Hybridization between [[Humpback Chub]] and two other [[Gila Chub]] relatives has been detected below Glen Canyon Dam.

**Direct human impacts.** Cattle trampling reduced the last natural population of [[Yaqui Chub]] at Astin Spring to a quagmire in 1969. Highway construction endangers [[Woundfin]]. Changes in water use and rebuilding of ponds on the San Bernardino Ranch nearly extirpated [[Beautiful Shiner]] in its Yaqui form.

**Climate and salinity.** Increased precipitation and runoff in 1964 to 1968 allowed [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] to rebound after drastic reductions in the 1950s and early 1960s. A possible increase in [[Pacific Tenpounder]] abundance may relate to increased salinity in the lower Colorado River, and tiny young [[Striped Mullet]] suggest local reproduction near Yuma, possibly in response to local salinities.

**Basin patterns.** Extinction or decline rates are highest in the southern basins, ranging from about 55 per cent in the lower Colorado River to 33 to about 38 per cent in the [[Yaqui River]] and [[Gila River]]. The [[Little Colorado River]] has lost about 38 per cent of its 8 known species, reflecting decline of the Colorado River mainstream fauna. The [[Bill Williams River]] and [[Virgin River]] have lost none of their original fauna according to available records. The upper Colorado River, as defined here, also retains all known original fauna.

## Key Findings

### Overall status
- About 35 kinds of native fishes, including subspecies and suspected distinctive forms, occur in Arizona.
- Of 79 recorded occurrences of native fishes in river basins or segments, 22 (almost 28 per cent) represent extinct populations.
- Of 23 populations well enough sampled to estimate intraspecific change, 20 are on the decline side.
- Thirteen additional recordings are inconclusive due to lack of basic data; the remainder are extinct or occupy habitat too altered for conclusions.
- No native species was reported to thrive in artificial lakes, and few persist in canals.

### Species accounts (selected)
- **[[Pacific Tenpounder]] (Elops affinis):** Predominantly marine; taken in the lower Colorado River in the 1940s and sporadically since. No definite change in status, except a possible increase linked to salinity.
- **[[Gila Trout]] (Salmo gilae):** Extinct except for a few remnants in the upper Gila basin, New Mexico.
- **[[Apache Trout]] (Salmo sp.):** Widespread in the Salt River basin in the late 1800s and early 1900s; rare in the 1950s; now essentially domesticated and stocked. Natural remnant populations persist only on the White Mountain slopes.
- **[[Gila Chub]] (Gila intermedia):** Spotty distribution; locally abundant populations persist, but range is drastically reduced.
- **[[Colorado Chub]] (Gila robusta):** Abundant in remaining flowing waters of the upper Salt River; greatly reduced in the Verde, upper Gila and Little Colorado rivers; local populations only in the Colorado mainstream.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]] (Gila elegans):** Extinct in the Gila and Little Colorado basins; small numbers in the Colorado mainstream and its larger impoundments.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha):** Rare and probably declining, restricted to swift, inaccessible reaches of the Colorado River; hybridization below Glen Canyon Dam may contribute to its decline.
- **[[Sonora Chub]] (Gila ditaenia):** Occurs in the United States only at Sycamore Canyon, west of Nogales. Status in Arizona unchanged, but modifications of the Rio Magdalena in Sonora may have extirpated it locally.
- **[[Yaqui Chub]] (Gila purpurea):** The last natural Arizona (and U.S.) population was apparently eliminated in 1969. A 1969 introduction into Leslie Creek, Swisshelm Mountains, has undetermined results.
- **[[Colorado River Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius):** Known only from the Colorado mainstream upstream from Lake Mead, in small numbers.
- **[[Longfin Dace]] (Agosia chrysogaster):** Abundant in remnant waters of the Gila and Bill Williams rivers; introduced into the Virgin River mainstream; may have extended range to higher elevations.
- **[[Speckled Dace]] (Rhinichthys osculus):** Widespread and abundant at intermediate and high elevations; southern populations greatly reduced, northern populations flourishing.
- **[[Loach Minnow]] (Tiaroga cobitis):** Formerly throughout the Gila basin upstream from Phoenix; now rare, persisting in Aravaipa Creek and some upper Salt basin streams.
- **[[Spikedace]] (Meda fulgida):** Recently rediscovered in the upper Verde River system, fairly common in the Salt mainstream, and abundant in Aravaipa Creek. Experimentally stocked with loach minnow in Cave Creek, Maricopa County.
- **[[Woundfin]] (Plagopterus argentissimus):** Restricted to the lower Virgin River mainstream; endangered by highway construction; efforts underway to establish it in the Paria River and possibly the upper Verde River.
- **[[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (Lepidomeda vittata):** Abundant early in the 1900s, drastically reduced in the 1950s and early 1960s, rebounded in 1964 to 1968; common in unmodified streams of the Little Colorado basin.
- **[[Middle Colorado Spinedace]] (Lepidomeda mollispinis):** Abundant in Virgin River tributaries, but possibly affected by the same modifications affecting Woundfin.
- **[[Beautiful Shiner]] (Notropis formosus):** The Yaqui form (N. f. mearnsi) was almost extirpated from San Bernardino Ranch ponds; only two juveniles were taken in the 1970 attempt to sample.
- **[[Mexican Stoneroller]] (Campostoma ornatum):** Restricted to a few U.S. locations, including Rucker Canyon, Chiricahua Mountains; abundant in its restricted range.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus):** Extinct in the Gila system, rare below Lake Havasu, and persisting, perhaps without successful reproduction, in upstream Colorado River lakes.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]] (Catostomus insignis):** Depleted in the southern Gila basin but persists elsewhere in great abundance.
- **[[Yaqui Sucker]] (Catostomus bernardini):** Extinct in Arizona and the U.S., disappearing from San Bernardino Ranch waters between 1967 and 1968.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Catostomus latipinnis):** Two distinctive forms: a slim, sandy-colored, large-finned form in the Colorado, Virgin and Salt basins (very rare in the Salt); and a thick-bodied, sharply bicolored, small-finned form in the Little Colorado. Both are locally abundant, but downstream habitat drying has reduced ranges.
- **[[Bluehead Mountain-Sucker]] (Pantosteus discobolus):** Relatively common in the Little Colorado and Colorado mainstream, its native range in the State.
- **[[Gila Mountain-Sucker]] (Pantosteus clarki):** Abundant in the northern Gila basin; rare in the south and in some Bill Williams streams. Taxonomic status of mountain-suckers in the Bill Williams and Virgin systems remains uncertain.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon macularius):** The most widespread form (Gila and lower Colorado basins) is extinct. Two remnants persist in isolated spring-fed habitats: at [[Quitobaquito Spring]], [[Organ Pipe National Monument]] (habitat rehabilitated and native form restocked after pressure from an introduced cyprinid), and at Monkey Spring, Santa Cruz County (essentially extirpated by introduced largemouth bass between 1968 and 1969; maintained as brood stock by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and Arizona State University).
- **[[Gila Topminnow]] (Poeciliopsis occidentalis):** The Gila form (P. o. occidentalis) is restricted to two spring-fed areas and has been introduced with varying success elsewhere; the southern form (P. o. sonoriensis) remains abundant locally in the upper Yaqui system, Cochise County.
- **[[Striped Mullet]] (Mugil cephalus):** Occurs only in the lowermost Colorado River below Imperial Dam; recent collection of tiny young suggests possible reproduction near Yuma.
- **[[Spotted Sleeper]] (Eleotris picta):** One specimen taken near Yuma on the California side of the Colorado River, presumably an accidental stray from the Gulf of California.

### Conservation and recommendations
- Native fishes persist only in remnant natural and semi-natural streams; none appears to adapt to the artificial habitats that now predominate.
- Remaining populations of endangered species are being maintained through brood stocks, reintroductions and experimental stocking, including attempts with Woundfin in the Paria River and Spikedace and Loach Minnow in Cave Creek.
- Some extinction verdicts are acknowledged as provisional, as species may be rediscovered after intensive collecting.
