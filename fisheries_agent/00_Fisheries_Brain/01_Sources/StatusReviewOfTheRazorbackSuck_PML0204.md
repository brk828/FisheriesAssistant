# Razorback Sucker Status Review and Recovery Road Map Draft

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 draft status review, prepared by Kevin R. Bestgen of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Upper Colorado Regional Office, compiles historic and present information on the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) across its range in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The report documents sharp declines, summarizes life history, identifies likely causes of decline, reviews current research and management, and offers research and management recommendations for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]. The central conclusion is that non-native predation on early life stages is the most important cause of recruitment failure, and that simply stocking hatchery fish will not recover the species without controlled experimentation on limiting factors.

The cover memorandum (August 1990, from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Ecological Services office) and a reviewer-comment letter (July 1990) accompany the report, along with a mailing list of roughly 30 agency and university recipients.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an endemic member of the [[Colorado River Basin]] native fish assemblage and one of three monotypic genera in the family Catostomidae. It was originally described in 1860 from a mounted specimen collected in Arizona. Historically it was widespread and common in warmwater reaches from Wyoming to Mexico and was used extensively as food by Aboriginal cultures; marketable quantities were still taken in Arizona as late as 1949.

Diagnostic features include an abruptly rising bony dorsal keel immediately posterior to the occiput (the origin of the common names "humpback" or "razorback" sucker), an elongate, laterally compressed body, a short deep caudal peduncle, and a ventral mouth with a cleft lower lip. Adults commonly measure 400–700 mm total length and weigh under 3 kg, though they can reach about 1 m and 5–6 kg. Breeding males show bright yellow and orange coloration and pronounced tuberculation; females are generally longer and heavier. Low, broad predorsal keels occur in some females and may lead to misidentification as hybrids.

The report's stated objectives were to document historic and present status, summarize life history, assemble information on causes of decline, outline current research and management, and recommend future actions. Its scope was deliberately limited to technical review, with broader policy and listing questions treated as beyond scope.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and movement.** Historically, the species used mainstream rivers and associated low-velocity habitats such as backwaters, sloughs, and oxbow lakes. Adults overwinter in deep, slow water with silt and sand substrate and move to shallower, faster riffles and gravel areas to spawn. Larvae concentrate in warm shoreline margins, and in reservoirs they occupy nearshore zones near spawning areas. Spawning is linked to off-channel habitats and tributaries, which were lost as dams and channelization reduced seasonal flooding.

**Temperature.** Optimal summer temperatures were estimated at 22–25 °C, with avoidance at 27.4–31.6 °C depending on acclimation.

**Reproduction.** In Lake Mohave, spawning ran from late November to May, peaking January–March at 10–12 °C. In the Upper Basin, ripe fish were collected from mid-April to mid-June at 12–16 °C. Egg hatching time and success depended strongly on incubation temperature; fertilization at temperatures well above incubation temperatures was associated with poorer hatching.

**Diet.** Larvae feed on small zooplankton in lentic habitats and on chironomid larvae in streams. Adults are omnivores, planktivorous in reservoirs and benthic in rivers.

**Hybridization.** Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] are widespread in the Colorado River Basin and have been recorded in Lake Mohave, Grand Canyon, and the Upper Basin; hybrid incidence appeared to be increasing in the Upper Basin, although some apparent hybrids may be misidentified low-keeled razorback suckers.

**Disease.** Blindness in one or both eyes was common in Lake Mohave fish, and *Lernaea cyprinacea* infestations and vertebral deformities were reported.

**Non-native competitors and predators.** More than 50 non-native fish species have been introduced into the basin. Channel catfish, [[Common Carp]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] are among the predators documented on eggs, larvae, or stocked juveniles. Avian predators and cormorants also caused significant losses in hatcheries.

## Key Findings

- **Historic and current distribution.** The species was once widespread but is now restricted. The largest remaining population is in [[Lake Mohave]], estimated at about 60,000 fish (range 15,000 to 120,000) by a multiple-census approach. In the Upper Basin, the largest concentration is in the upper Green River, where Lanigan and Tyus (1989) estimated 948 fish (95% CI 758–1,138) from 1980 to 1988 capture-recapture data. Only 13 fish were captured in the lower Green River in the same period.
- **Upper Basin status.** Populations are dominated by large, presumably old adults, and recruitment to adulthood appears to be absent. In the Colorado River near Grand Junction, only seven adults were collected from 1985 to 1988, and the species was less common there than [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The San Juan River arm of [[Lake Powell]] held a small, reproducing-looking population, with 12 fish captured in 1987 and 14 more in April 1990.
- **Lower Basin status.** Lake Mohave juveniles have been extremely rare since the early 1950s. Four small juveniles were caught in 1987. Larvae are abundant, and predation is considered the factor most likely to limit recruitment.
- **Stocking outcome.** Of more than 12 million stocked razorback suckers of all sizes, only 118 had been recovered through 1988, and most of those were recovered soon after release.
- **Causes of decline.** Major factors include mainstream dam construction, flow alteration, loss of off-channel and seasonally flooded habitat, channelization, water-quality degradation, and non-native predators. The authors conclude that non-native predation on early life stages is the main recruitment limitation, while habitat alteration and cold-water releases likely contribute.
- **Age and growth.** Otolith analysis from Lake Mohave indicated fish up to 44 years old. Wild adults in the Upper Basin grow very slowly (about 2–3 mm/year), while hatchery fish reared in predator-free ponds grew rapidly, reaching about 422 mm TL after 2.6 years (survival 99.3%).
- **Genetics.** Studies by [[Buth et al.]] found genetic variation in Dexter hatchery stocks comparable to the Lake Mohave parental population, with low introgression of flannelmouth sucker genes. Electrophoretic and mitochondrial DNA studies were underway.
- **Research recommendations.** The report recommends resolving genetic diversity questions, identifying causes of recruitment failure through controlled experiments, defining movements and habitat needs of all life stages, and investigating basic life history of riverine populations.
- **Management recommendations.** These include developing broodstock and refugia populations from all known areas of occurrence (brood collection began in spring 1990), refining reintroduction strategies to improve survival, identifying predator-free reintroduction sites with monitoring, and continuing population monitoring.
- **Reviewer feedback.** Reviewers' comments were largely editorial. The Denver team recommended limiting the report to a technical review, and the reviser noted that the recommendations were the most controversial part of the first draft and that the final review deadline was 30 September 1990.
