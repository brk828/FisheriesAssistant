# Virgin River Management Plan (Draft Concept, March 1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft concept, prepared by the [[Washington County Water Conservancy District]] (WCWCD) in March 1994 for discussion only, proposes a cooperative river management framework for the Virgin River in Washington County, Utah. Its goals are to acquire and set aside riparian, wetland and riverine habitat within the 100-year floodplain, secure water for the county's growth, provide for incidental take of endangered aquatic species during diversion operations, and maintain instream flows for the native fish community. The central mechanism is the removal of [[La Verkin Springs]] from the river, with its water piped below the [[Washington Fields Diversion]], cooled and returned as instream flow, freeing 10–12 cfs for habitat restoration.

## Background & Description

The Virgin River floodplain supports diverse wildlife, including bald and golden eagles, peregrine falcons, beaver, bighorn sheep and reptiles. The document lists six native fish: the [[Woundfin]] (endangered), [[Virgin River Chub]] (threatened), [[Virgin Spinedace]] (proposed threatened), [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]].

Washington County lies largely in the Virgin River Basin, with elevations from about 2,500 ft near St. George to 10,000 ft above Zion National Park. About 86% of the county is state or federal land. The county is the fastest-growing in Utah, averaging over 6% annually since 1970, growing from 29,900 (1980) to 47,825 (1990), with a projection of 268,000 by 2040. The document gives the basin's average long-term flow as 155,000 acre-feet and total depletions from culinary and irrigation use as 75,050 acre-feet.

The document traces water use from [[Ancestral Puebloan Agriculture]] (Anasazi, abandoned about 800 years ago) through [[Mormon Settlement of the Virgin River]] beginning in 1854 under Jacob Hamblin. Settlements such as Santa Clara, Washington, Grafton, Virgin, Rockville and Springdale were established between 1856 and 1861. By 1902, there were at least 15 diversions and 41 ditches irrigating 6,548 acres. The river had been intermittently dry since settlement began.

Water rights evolved from informal church-mediated division to county water commissioners (1901) and later irrigation companies. Table 3 of the source lists dozens of surface water rights held by towns, irrigation companies, the WCWCD and private owners, with priority dates from 1857 to 1989. The most important is the [[Washington Fields Diversion]], which holds a water right in excess of 86 cfs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality:** Water above La Verkin Springs is generally good and could be treated for culinary use. Below the springs and at the Washington Fields Diversion, quality is described as "extremely poor," primarily due to mineral contamination from La Verkin Springs. Attachment 3 contains monitoring tables (1993–1994) for sites including the Virgin Bridge, Pah Tempe Hot Springs, La Verkin Bridge and the Diversion Dam. Those tables record field and laboratory parameters such as pH, conductivity and dissolved solids, but the OCR is too degraded to reproduce values reliably.

**Hydrology and dewatering:** The river has been dewatered in sections below Bloomington during dry months, and reaches below the Washington Fields dam are frequently dry. The [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] (completed 1985, holding 40,325 acre-feet) is an off-stream municipal reservoir fed by a pipeline from a diversion above Hurricane.

**Floodplain biodiversity:** Attachment 1 lists about 150 vertebrate species that may use the floodplain, including amphibians (bullfrog, Arizona toad, spadefoot), over 100 bird species (raptors, herons, waterfowl, flycatchers, owls, sandpipers), mammals (beaver, bobcat, desert bighorn, mule deer, muskrat, bats) and roughly 60 reptile species (lizards, snakes, the desert tortoise). The figure for the bird list is approximate; the full list is summarized here, not reproduced.

**Non-native fish:** A fish barrier built in the 1980s near the entrance to the Virgin River gorge aims to stop Red Shiner dispersal upstream. The plan calls for additional barriers at Ft. Pierce, the Seegmiller drain and below the existing barrier.

## Key Findings

- **Habitat protection:** Acquire private floodplain land from La Verkin Springs to the Washington Fields Diversion for a managed natural parkway with trails, maintained by the District.
- **Water conservation:** Removing La Verkin Springs and improving irrigation practices would make 10–12 cfs available for instream flow. Winter irrigation to flush salts would no longer be needed.
- **Proposed winter flows:** At least 50 cfs measured at the Washington Fields Diversion from November through April, without augmentation from Quail Lake, to be finalized with state and federal agencies.
- **Spring runoff and post-runoff flows:** 86 cfs, or natural flow.
- **Wetlands:** Identify and acquire wetlands adjacent to the river, enhance them where possible, and bank them as mitigation credits for future District projects.
- **Non-native fish:** Remove non-native fish from the river from the Johnson Diversion through the Virgin River Gorge, and build fish barriers.
- **Monitoring:** Use an independent contractor, accepted by UDWR and FWS, to monitor native fish populations from the Ash Creek confluence to the Arizona barrier, reporting on trends, recruitment and threats.
- **Research:** Fund biological studies on native Virgin River fish needs, shared between UDWR and FWS. The document cites seven years of prior biological studies.
- **Agency roles:** UDWR and FWS provide manpower, oversight, permits and technical review. The US Army Corps of Engineers assists with parkway review, wetland banking and floodplain identification. Local governments zone and enforce floodplain protection.
- **Stated benefits:** The plan aims to protect the 100-year floodplain, align with the [[Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan]], and provide certainty for water development to meet the needs of a growing county.
- **Status:** This is a draft for discussion. Several figures, including the flow values and the agency responsibilities, are proposals still under negotiation rather than agreed measures.
