# The Display of the Siamese Fighting Fish, *Betta splendens* (Simpson, 1968)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anabantidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by M. J. A. Simpson, published as Volume 1, Part 1 of [[Animal Behaviour Monographs]] (1968), describes the threat display of [[Siamese Fighting Fish]] (*Betta splendens*) and asks how the display of two like-sexed fish relates to the outcome of their encounter, in which one fish ceases to display and retreats. Because both participants perform the same stereotyped moves, differences between winners and losers must lie in the frequencies, sequences and timing of those moves. The work combines laboratory encounters between paired fish, analysis of facing-broadside cycles, and tests with a mechanically rotated [[Stationary Puppet]]. The author states that the monograph raises questions and proposes methods but does not provide a definitive description of the display.

## Background & Description

The monograph was written as a substantial descriptive and experimental study of behaviour, of the type the journal was founded to encourage (see [[Editorial Policy]]). The threat display was chosen because it is easily elicited in domesticated males, but these displays are rarely allowed to run to completion because of damage, so the link between display and outcome had seldom been tested. Earlier work by [[Braddock and Braddock (1955)]] on female pairs had reported that the winner-to-be raised its gill covers and bit twice as often as its partner in fights lasting over an hour.

The display is built from a small set of units:

- **Turning to face** and **turning broadside**, which alternate, typically six to twelve times per minute.
- **Gill cover erection** (usually coinciding with turning to face) and lowering.
- **[[Pelvic Fin Flickering]]**, performed while broadside, with the offside fin moved through an arc of about 20° to 100°.
- **[[Tail Beating]]** and **[[Tail Flashing]]**, both performed almost exclusively from the broadside position.
- **Biting**, nipping and occasional mouth locking.

Courtship shares all of these moves, so the author argues that neither display can be fully understood without studying both. Methods are described for keeping fish in tanks of Cambridge tapwater at 26.7 ± 1 °C (80 ± 2 °F), feeding on [[Tubifex]] and other live foods, and recording with a ten-pen time-event recorder.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish were maintained in 24 × 12 × 8 in. tanks, with males usually isolated and females kept in groups of four to five. Tank fauna included *Planorbis corneus* and *Physa* snails. Fish were kept under fluorescent lighting with a 12 hr night. The author notes that temperature changes as small as 2 °C affect nest building, air gulping and display readiness. Air gulping occurs roughly every minute at 27 °C in still water, and fish can obtain their whole oxygen supply this way in deoxygenated water.

Wild-type males hold territories and patrol their boundaries, whereas domesticated Bettas do not. Colour matters: domesticated males lacking the wild-type features (white pelvic-fin tips, blue-green dorsal and caudal streaks, bright iris patches) often do not darken or display as intensely. Albino fish display little, and blue fish were more ready to display to a stationary puppet than crimson-maroon ones.

## Key Findings

- **Predicting outcomes:** The monograph's criterion for a good description of the threat display is that it should predict encounter outcomes from differences between the participants' displays.
- **Facing and broadside cycles:** Facing fish turn broadside more often when the partner is facing than when it is broadside. A fish is also more likely to turn broadside shortly after its partner turns to face than shortly after its partner turns broadside.
- **Taking turns:** Paired males separated by a [[Perspex Screen]] turned to face at similar rates over 5-min periods (Table I). Within-pair similarity exceeded between-pair similarity (rS = +0.84).
- **Temporal patterning:** Pelvic fin flickering is most frequent early in a broadside bout, tail beating in the middle, and tail flashing is never most frequent in the first interval and often occurs last. A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test supported non-random tail beating across 1.5 sec intervals after gill cover lowering (N = 81 tail beats, deviation significant at P < 0.05).
- **Gill covers and tail beating:** After a partner raises its gill covers, most fish flicker pelvic fins first, and tail beating later increases while the partner's gill covers stay erect. Gill cover lowering by the partner is most likely after a burst of tail beats, suggesting the partner lowers its gill covers in response to tail beating.
- **Encounters between females:** In eight pairs of previously isolated females (24 or 48 hr isolation, versus at least 7 days in [[Braddock and Braddock (1955)]]), encounters lasted from under 4 min to about 14 min. The median duration of the eight analysed encounters was between 6 min 38 sec and 8 min. The 40-min encounter was excluded as a draw. Winners spent a median of 33.7% of the whole watch with gill covers erect, against 15.8% for losers. The difference was not significant by the Mann-Whitney U test. Winners and losers differed significantly only in the last 2-min period (U = 3, P = 0.002).
- **Winner-loser asymmetry:** Winners maintained or increased tail beating as partners' gill raising bouts lengthened, while losers' tail beating fell (rS = −0.88, P = 0.01 to 0.001 for losers). Seven of eight losers beat their tails at higher rates than their partners over the whole encounter.
- **Responsiveness after defeat:** Losers stopped responding to mirror images immediately after the encounter (thirteen pairs tested), while winners displayed vigorously. The author suggests this change may be specific to the mirror rather than due to fatigue.
- **Puppet experiments:** Eighteen adult male fish were tested for 40 min against a stationary puppet that turned facing or broadside every 2 min. Fifteen of eighteen displayed; three did not. Time spent with gill covers erect correlated with time spent near the puppet (rS = +0.89, P < 0.001). Tail flashing per broadside bout was an inverse measure of approach readiness (rS = −0.74, P < 0.001).
- **Approachers and avoiders:** Fish that spent more time near the puppet than expected by chance ("approachers," six fish) tended to increase their facing and gill raising when the puppet faced. "Avoiders" (ten fish) did not.
- **Reinforcement:** Opportunity to display acts as a reinforcer for domesticated males (Thompson, 1963). The author proposes that gill cover raising, tail beating and facing by a partner may each reinforce different parts of the display, though this is untested.
- **Conflict interpretation:** The author argues that the display probably varies along one dimension, approach readiness, rather than reflecting separate attack and flight tendencies. A conflict model is described as useful but not necessary.
- **Limitations and future work:** Inconsistencies remain between puppet and live-fish results, and the author calls for round-robin stranger encounters, developmental studies, and analyses of visiting and leaving behaviour. Recording two fish simultaneously was difficult and possibly biased, though the author reports that observer handedness was balanced.
