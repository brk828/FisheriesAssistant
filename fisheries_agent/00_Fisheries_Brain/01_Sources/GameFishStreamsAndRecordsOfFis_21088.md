# Game Fish Streams and Records of Fishes from the Shenandoah-Potomac River System of Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Virginia Commission of Game and Inland Fisheries technical bulletin (April 1959), written by Robert D. Ross, reports a summer 1956 survey of game fish streams in the [[Shenandoah River]] and direct tributaries of the [[Potomac River]] above Goose Creek, Loudoun County. The survey combined physical, chemical and hydrographic observations with fish collections made by [[Electroseine Sampling]] at about 62 stations. It documents habitat quality for [[Smallmouth Bass]], trout and other game species and lists the fish taxa recorded in each drainage, with a species count of about 48 by this note's tally of the checklists.

## Background & Description

The survey ran from June 15 to September 15, 1956, as Federal Aid Project No. F-8-R-3, a cooperative effort between the [[Virginia Commission of Game and Inland Fisheries]] and the Virginia Cooperative Wildlife Research Unit. Game wardens nominated trout and bass streams, and the field crew worked mainly in those waters. Collections used a 2500-watt generator and an alternating-polarity electroseine in three lengths (16, 30 and 100 feet). Game fish were returned alive, while non-game species were preserved in formalin and alcohol for the Virginia Polytechnic Institute collection. Scale samples of game fish were retained by the Division of Fisheries.

The report covers these drainages:

- **[[North Fork Shenandoah River]]:** basin of 1036 square miles and about 100 river miles. Six stations on the main stem, plus [[Linville Creek]] and [[Smith Creek]]. Tributaries include [[Big Stony Creek]], [[Cedar Creek]] and [[Passage Creek]].
- **[[South Fork Shenandoah River]]:** basin of 1638 square miles and 96 river miles between Port Republic and Front Royal. Drains [[North River]], [[Middle River]] and [[South River]].
- **Potomac direct tributaries:** [[South Fork Potomac River]] with [[Strait Creek]] and [[Laurel Fork]], [[Back Creek]], [[Opequon Creek]], [[Goose Creek]] and [[Catoctin River]].

Flow data come from [[Holmes Flow Records]] (1957) and from field estimates. Table 1 covers North Fork gauges, Table 2 covers South Fork gauges and Table 3 covers Opequon and Goose Creeks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and flow.** Flow varied widely. The North Fork's average discharge at Strasburg was given for 1925–1955, with extreme maxima during October 1942 floods. The Front Royal gauge on the South Fork averaged 1,668 cubic feet per second (cfs), with a maximum of 130,000 cfs on October 16, 1942. Many small streams were very low. North River headwaters fell to about 3 cfs on July 17, 1956, and Little and Upper Hawksbill Creeks held about 1 cfs in summer. Dry River was described as dry except during heavy rain and spring runoff.

**Geology and springs.** Most of the North Fork basin east of Big North Mountain sits on porous limestone. The report links this to low summer stages, which it treats as normal under present conditions. Springs are scarce, and none in the North Fork area is thermal. North River sinks into a cobble-filled basin west of Mt. Solon, a feature the report ties to [[Karst Hydrology]]. Reservoirs and low dams along the North Fork produced plankton blooms and green water in July and August 1956.

**Water quality and temperature.** Streams varied from cool, shaded trout water (Passage Creek upper reaches, Beaver Creek, Laurel Fork) to warm, turbid, [[Eutrophication]]-prone waters (Linville Creek, Smith Creek, Muddy Creek). Waynesboro pollution colored the South River black. Agricultural clearing warmed Passage Creek and raised silt loads in Cub Run.

**Biota and habitat use.** The report notes that bass and sunfish dominate warm, well-shaded pools and that yellowbelly sunfish build dense nesting colonies on gravel in Lower Middle River. Plant beds in Lower Middle River shelter young fish. [[Water Snake]] populations at Damtown Dam were abundant, and the shelter of rocks and abundant prey supported them.

## Key Findings

- **Best bass stream:** the North Fork below Bergton was judged the best [[Smallmouth Bass]] stream observed on the survey, with pools and a well-developed flood plain.
- **Lower North River:** described as an excellent bass stream with a rich, varied fish fauna, in contrast to its nearly dry headwaters, where few fish were found.
- **Trout waters:** Laurel Fork may be one of the best trout streams in the Potomac drainage in Virginia, with trout "everywhere" and little human disturbance. Upper Strait Creek and Beaver Creek were judged suitable for trout, and Pitt Spring and Cub Run were described as potential trout streams limited by flow.
- **Species richness:** the checklists record about 48 taxa across the families Centrarchidae, Salmonidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Anguillidae, Ictaluridae, Percidae, Cottidae, Esocidae and Cyprinodontidae, with Cyprinidae (about 20 taxa) the most diverse group.
- **Game fish distribution:** [[Northern Smallmouth Bass]] is the most widespread game species, recorded at most stations. [[Northern Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Rockbass]] and [[Yellowbelly Sunfish]] are also common. [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] were recorded at a limited set of sites, and the report notes that stocked trout in Back Creek did not appear to survive the summer.
- **Common non-game fish:** [[Common White Sucker]], [[Hog Sucker]], [[American Eel]], [[Northern Muddler]] and [[Fantail Darter]] were recorded across many drainages.
- **Management recommendations:** the North Fork's shaded pools and flood plain were identified as offering development potential for sport recreation. Opequon Creek and Goose Creek were managed for bass and sunfish, and Goose Creek was called an excellent bass-sunfish stream. Passage Creek's upper reaches were managed as trout water.
- **Flow thresholds:** the report notes that small tributaries can drop to about 1 cfs in summer, which sustains pools but not continuous flow, a constraint on trout survival.
