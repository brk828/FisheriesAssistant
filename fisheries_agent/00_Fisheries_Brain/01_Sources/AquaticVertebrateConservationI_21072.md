# Aquatic Vertebrate Conservation in Pima County: Concepts and Planning Development

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This June 2000 planning document, prepared for the [[Sonoran Desert Conservation Plan]] by Philip C. Rosen of the University of Arizona, argues that the native aquatic vertebrates of [[Pima County]] have been largely displaced by introduced species. Non-native fishes, [[Bullfrog]]s, and [[Crayfish]] dominate remaining perennial waters, and habitat modification (ponds and lakes) has amplified their advantage. Recovery is presented as possible through a combination of removing exotics (chiefly by drying and toxicants) and re-creating natural flow regimes. The document proposes three habitat types for the [[Tucson Basin]] floor, gives an annotated list of mountain canyons and valley sites, and outlines priorities, public-private cooperation strategies, and an education campaign.

## Background & Description

The author frames the problem around the fact that native fauna evolved in flowing, highly variable water subject to flood scour and seasonal drying. Introduced species are mainly pond and lake taxa. The focal native taxa are:

- **Fishes:** [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]] (Gila River basin form), [[Longfin Dace]], [[Gila Chub]], native suckers, and the large [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), which is proposed as a possible predator but is not known to eat bullfrogs.
- **Amphibians:** [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] and [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]].
- **Reptiles:** [[Mexican Garter Snake]] and [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]].

The problem exotics are [[Bullfrog]]s, catfish, [[Green Sunfish]], bass, [[Mosquitofish]], and other fishes (carp, cichlids). The author considers fish the easiest to control, since they can be drying- or poison-eliminated and cannot disperse overland. Crayfish are harder to eradicate, and bullfrogs are the most difficult to control because of their overland dispersal. The Tucson Basin's native fauna was documented by verified museum records, and only the Sonoran Mud Turtle survives there today.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat modification drives exotic success.** Ponds and lakes favor bullfrogs and non-native fishes, while streams and springs favor leopard frogs. Floods favor native fishes, especially longfin dace, over introduced ones.

**Ecological release.** Removing exotic fish from pond-type habitats is expected to cause bullfrog populations to explode, which would preclude native frogs and snakes. Fish management and amphibian/reptile management therefore have to be planned together.

**Mosquito control.** Native topminnows and pupfish are described as effective larval predators. Leopard frog habitat without fish is expected to create mosquito problems.

**Vegetation and springs.** Springs in the Tucson Basin tend to form broad wet meadows that cattails (*Typha*) and tules (*Scirpus*) choke with speed. Concrete channels, periodic clearing, or drying cycles are needed to keep open water.

**Disease.** Chytridiomycosis (chytrid fungus) was first documented in North America at [[Cienega Creek]] (Pantano) in 1995, and is implicated in amphibian declines. Leopard frogs have also been losing populations in the mountains around Tucson.

**Metapopulation dynamics.** Connected sites, arranged as stepping stones, allow dispersal and recolonization, which the author regards as central to persistence. Mountain canyons and valley-floor sites are seen as parts of the same system.

## Key Findings

**Historical and current status**
- The lower [[San Pedro River]] originally supported at least 13 native fish species; the Santa Cruz Basin at least 8. The present fish fauna is described as "little more than a shadow" of this diversity.
- Six or more major isolated lowland leopard frog populations in the Tucson-area mountains disappeared in the last three decades: three from introduced species and two or three from short-term drying.
- Lowland leopard frogs remain abundant in the perennial lower San Pedro and lower Cienega Creek, and in good numbers at about 7 isolated canyons in the Rincon, Santa Catalina, and Whetstone Mountains.
- Chiricahua leopard frogs occur in Pima County only at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (two populations), [[Empire-Cienega Ranch]] (one tiny population), and possibly two in the northern Santa Rita Mountains.
- Mexican garter snakes persist in Pima County in Cienega Creek; the author notes the Cienega Creek population "may be the best one left in the United States."
- Lowland leopard frogs were detected in Sabino Canyon in early June 2000, after an absence of 1.5 to 2 decades, following green sunfish removal in 1999.

**Habitat type recommendations (Tucson Basin floor)**
1. **In-channel, high-flood systems:** runs and riffles seeded with longfin dace and lowland leopard frogs, sited as stepping stones about 1 km or more in length. The lower San Pedro (Cascabel to Dudleyville) is the exemplar.
2. **Low-flood systems:** managed spring runs or reclaimed-water streams in small arroyos (e.g., Agua Caliente Park, Arroyo Chico, Alamo Wash, West Branch of the Santa Cruz River) for topminnows, pupfish, and chubs. Agua Caliente spring-run extensions of about 1/4 to 1/2 mile are proposed.
3. **Pond/lake systems:** used only where required, with provisions for prompt drying. Examples include the Roger Road Wastewater Treatment Facility ponds (native fish for mosquito control) and Reid Park golf course ponds (currently without bullfrogs).

**Immediate priorities**
- Protect the Empire-Cienega Ranch area from invasive exotics, especially fishes, by removing exotics from the surrounding basin.
- Remove green sunfish from Romero, Bear (including Rose Canyon Lake), Agua Caliente, Tanque Verde, and Paige Canyons.
- Seek a long-term solution at Arivaca Cienega and Arivaca Lake, where non-native species have overwhelmed Chiricahua leopard frogs, Mexican garter snakes, and Gila topminnows.
- Support cooperation among Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and ranchers for leopard frog restoration in ponds and springs.

**Specific sites**
- **Rincon Mountains (Chiminea Canyon):** described as an outstanding, highly isolated, and essentially undisturbed leopard frog site, and the only one where disease symptoms have not appeared.
- **Saguaro National Monument (Rincon):** about 4 leopard frog sites, the only Tucson-region sites where chytrid has not been confirmed; proposed as control sites.
- **Cienega Creek Natural Preserve and Empire-Cienega Ranch (BLM):** the "wetland gem" of the County, with Sonoran mud turtles and longfin dace (both abundant) and Gila chubs and topminnows (both superabundant). The author estimates the fish population at about 1–2 orders of magnitude above all other Gila topminnow individuals. Keeping exotic fish out is called the County's highest wetland conservation priority.
- **Arivaca Cienega:** bullfrogs are extremely abundant, and leopard frogs and garter snakes are extinct there. Removal of bullfrogs is considered intractable at present.
- **Palo Alto Ranch (Altar Valley):** recommended for high acquisition priority to support lowland leopard frog restoration next to BANWR.

**Public-private cooperation and education**
- Six categories of cooperation are identified: land acquisition, public water for wetland fauna, voluntary avoidance of exotic habitats by developers, backyard ponds for natives, supervision of aquarium and pet suppliers, and ranch-based conservation.
- Ranchers are described as important, since stock tanks have often preserved leopard frogs. The author urges compensation and paid roles for ranchers rather than grazing-lease removal.
- The author recommends a sustained education campaign covering non-native species, habitat modification, emerging disease, groundwater, and aesthetics. Signage should discourage releasing pet crayfish and fish.
- State legislation is recommended to fully prohibit bullfrog possession and introduction, which the author says is currently legal to buy out of state and release on private land.
