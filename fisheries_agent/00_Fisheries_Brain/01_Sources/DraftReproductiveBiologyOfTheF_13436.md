# Reproductive Biology of the Flathead Catfish, Pylodictus olivaris, in a Turbid Oklahoma Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #freshwater-ecology #reproductive-biology

## Executive Summary

This 16-month study examined the reproductive biology of [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) in [[Lake Carl Blackwell]], a 1,486-hectare turbid reservoir in north-central Oklahoma. The research documented sex ratios, age at sexual maturity, spawning sites and season, gonadal development cycles, nutritive relationships during spawning, and fecundity-body size relationships. Key findings include spawning at water temperatures of 24-29°C in late July through August, sexual maturity at age IV-V for males (406-421 mm) and age V-VII for females (458-573 mm), and fecundity ranging from 4,076 to 31,579 oocytes for fish weighing 1.04-11.66 kg. Notably, approximately 50% of sexually mature females did not spawn and reabsorbed their eggs. Ovary weights reached up to 16% of body weight in ripe females, representing 17.9-57.4% of net annual production depending on age class. The study provides critical baseline data for fisheries management and ecosystem modeling in turbid reservoir systems.

## Background & Description

[[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) is a large, highly piscivorous ictalurid that thrives in turbid impoundments throughout its native range, particularly in the Kansas, Arkansas, and Red River basins of Oklahoma and Kansas. The species attains large sizes (>20 kg documented) and feeds on forage fishes too large for most other predators. In [[Lake Carl Blackwell]], the standing crop approached 5 pounds per acre (approximately 5.6 kg/ha), representing a considerable biomass channeling forage fish production through the reservoir ecosystem.

The study site, [[Lake Carl Blackwell]], is located 11 km west of Stillwater, Payne County, Oklahoma (36.08°N, 97.11°W). During the study period (1967-1968), average water level was reduced to 284 m MSL, creating a surface area of approximately 1,040 hectares (70% of spillway capacity), volume of 33.9 million cubic meters, and average depth of 4.0 m. The reservoir is characterized by high turbidity (20-180 JTU), low transparency (Secchi disc typically <50 cm, maximum 120 cm), and total dissolved solids of 337 ppm. Seven consecutive years of declining water levels had exposed extensive mud and sand flats and left much of the rocky shoreline above water by 1968.

Sexual dimorphism in [[Flathead Catfish]] is evident in the genital region. Males possess a distinct, posteriorly-directed genital papilla with a small round urogenital opening at its tip. Females have a more recessed, less distinct papilla with a larger longitudinal slit-like urogenital opening. The genital papilla of adult females becomes noticeably inflamed during late spring and summer. This field sexing method approached 99% accuracy for fish >1 kg when verified by dissection. The smallest positively sexed male and female were 208 mm and 217 mm, respectively.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Flathead Catfish]] occupy an important ecological niche in reservoir ecosystems as apex piscivores. The species is of considerable commercial importance in several states, though catch statistics are typically pooled with other ictalurids. In Oklahoma reservoirs (July 1967-June 1968), flathead catfish composed 0.4% of the estimated 1,126,537 pounds total commercial harvest from four reservoirs but ranked second in total value and first in price per pound.

Spawning sites in [[Lake Carl Blackwell]] were inferred to be in secluded locations at 2-5 m depths, characterized by either natural rock outcroppings (near the south end of the dam and parts of the southeast arm) or dense stands of submerged timber in open water areas. These sites provided essential cover for nest preparation and guarding behavior. The movement of males to spawning sites was evidenced by dramatic shifts in sex ratios captured in gill nets: from 1:1 in April-May to 1.7:1.0 (female:male) in June, 4.6:1.0 in early July, and 9.3:1.0 in late July, returning to 1:1 by mid-August.

Water temperature appears to be the primary environmental cue for spawning. In 1967, spawning occurred August 15-30 at bottom temperatures of 24-27°C (75-80°F). In 1968, spawning occurred 3-4 weeks earlier (late July to early August) at temperatures of 26-29°C (79-84°F). Both years required a lengthy period (3-6 weeks) of water temperatures >25°C before spawning commenced. The earlier spawning in 1968 corresponded with earlier attainment of threshold temperatures, with July 1968 averaging approximately 2°C warmer than July 1967.

Nutritive relationships during reproduction showed pronounced seasonal patterns. Liver-somatic index (LSI) values for mature females averaged 1.7% of body weight in late May when gonadal development accelerated, declining to only 0.7% when females were ripe in July. This inverse relationship between LSI and gonadal-somatic index (GSI) suggests nutrient mobilization from liver reserves to support ovarian development. Feeding activity declined dramatically during the spawning period: 44.4% and 38.1% of stomachs were empty in April and May, respectively, increasing to 84.6% in June and 94.1% in July. Males showed similar LSI declines, from 1.4% in early May to 0.7% during June-July.

The prolonged drought and declining water levels (1961-1968) may have influenced spawning success through increased competition for limited spawning sites and food resources. The exposure of previously submerged rocky areas and reduction of surface area to 70% of spillway capacity likely reduced available spawning habitat, potentially contributing to the high proportion (approximately 45%) of mature females that failed to spawn in 1968.

## Key Findings

**Sex Ratios and Sexual Maturity:**
Of 404 fish with definitively determined sex, 61% were females and 39% males, not significantly different from a 1:1 ratio (P>0.05) except during June-July 1968 when males moved to spawning sites. Females reached sexual maturity at ages V-VII (458-573 mm total length), with 10% of age V females still immature. Males matured earlier, with all age V males and 25% of age IV males sexually mature. The smallest mature males were 406-464 mm (age IV-V), while the smallest mature females were 458-479 mm (age V). Thirty percent of females in the 500-600 mm size class remained immature.

**Gonadal Development and Spawning Season:**
Gonadal-somatic index (GSI) values for mature females ranged from 0.2-16.4% of body weight, with most ripe females exhibiting GSI values of 8.0-12.0%. A few females <3 kg reached values as high as 16.4%. Immature females typically had GSI <0.5%, while spent or undeveloped adults ranged from 0.5-1.0%. Male GSI values ranged from 0.05-0.35% for mature individuals, with testes of ripe males comprising 0.15-0.35% of body weight—far less than the massive ovarian investment of females.

In 1967, spawning occurred August 15-30 at water temperatures of 24-27°C. The first spent female was collected August 30. In 1968, spawning began approximately 3-4 weeks earlier (late July to early August) at temperatures of 26-29°C, with the first spent female collected July 30. GSI values showed distinct seasonal cycles: low values (0.6-1.0%) from October through April, sharp increases beginning in early May (2.4%) when water temperatures reached 16-17°C, and continued increases through June-July to peak ripe condition (8.7-11.6%).

**Oocyte Development and Atresia:**
Oocyte measurements revealed two distinct modes: a "maturing" mode of opaque, cream to yellow-orange oocytes destined for spawning, and a "non-maturing" mode of opaque, white to grayish immature oocytes. Average diameter of ripe ova was 3.7 mm (range 3.33-4.50 mm for most ripe fish). The maturing mode increased from an average of 2.29 mm in early May to 3.74 mm at spawning in late July/early August. Maximum oocyte diameter was reached by mid-June, with little subsequent growth.

The non-maturing mode (0.50-1.50 mm diameter) showed rapid proliferation in mid-July, with the ratio of maturing to non-maturing oocytes changing from 1.00:0.19 in early May to 1.00:1.00 by late July. This recruitment of oocytes into the non-maturing mode likely represents the stock for the following year's spawning.

Approximately 45-50% of sexually mature females collected in August 1968 had not spawned, as evidenced by degenerating mature ovaries. These unspawned females showed: (1) decreased GSI values; (2) abnormally low numbers of mature oocytes (40% below expected based on body weight); (3) relatively large numbers of non-maturing oocytes; (4) reduced average diameter of mature oocytes (3.29 mm vs. 3.69 mm in ripe females); and (5) elevated LSI values (1.1% vs. 0.7% in spent females). Degenerative changes were observed within two weeks after the assumed start of spawning, suggesting a narrow window of physiological readiness. Unspawned females in August had LSI values significantly higher (P<0.01) than spent females, indicating nutrient transfer from reabsorbing ovaries back to the liver.

**Fecundity Relationships:**
Fecundity, measured as the number of oocytes in the maturing mode, was highly correlated with body weight (r=0.87, F=174.85, P<0.005). The linear regression Y = 3,872.9 + 2.54X (where Y = number of maturing oocytes and X = body weight in grams) provided the best fit for 57 fish. Individual estimates ranged from 4,076 oocytes for a 1.04 kg fish to 31,579 oocytes for an 11.66 kg fish. The average for the sample was 14,898 oocytes at 4.34 kg body weight.

Fecundity per kilogram of body weight decreased with increasing size: 4,476 oocytes/kg for 2 kg fish, 3,315 oocytes/kg for 5 kg fish, and 2,927 oocytes/kg for 10 kg fish. [[Flathead Catfish]] are not highly fecund relative to their size and the large biomass of ovary tissue. The relationship between oocyte number and total length was also significant, with both linear (Y = -22,680.3 + 56.53X, r=0.86) and curvilinear (Y = 9,786.8 - 40.27X + 0.0708X²) regressions providing good fits.

Analysis of covariance revealed no significant differences in fecundity relationships among fish grouped by oocyte maturity stage (average diameters 1.79-2.55 mm, 2.56-3.33 mm, and >3.33 mm), indicating relatively little recruitment or reabsorption within the maturing mode from May until spawning. No significant relationship was found between body weight and mature oocyte diameter (F=0.13-0.16, P>0.05), suggesting that heavier fish do not produce larger eggs.

**Reproductive Investment and Production:**
Ovary weights represented an enormous reproductive investment, reaching up to 16% of body weight in ripe females. The estimated wet weight of spawned ova comprised 17.9% of net annual production for age V females, increasing progressively to 21.4% (age VI), 26.1% (age VII), 33.0% (age VIII), 46.0% (age IX), and 57.4% (age X), before declining to 46.7% for age XI. This increasing proportion resulted from relatively constant ova biomass combined with decreasing annual weight increments in older fish. For age groups VII-X, annual weight increments of males exceeded those of females by 58-323 g, with males 670 g heavier at age XI, suggesting that the massive reproductive investment in females constrains somatic growth.

Male reproductive investment was far less significant, with ripe testes comprising only 0.15-0.35% of body weight, representing <3% of annual weight increment unless the increment was <500 g. The fraction of population-level net annual production lost to spawning would be somewhat less than individual female losses because approximately half the population is male and up to 50% of mature females may not spawn in a given year. Nevertheless, gamete loss represents a substantial component of annual production that must be accurately accounted for in population models.

**Spawning Behavior (from literature):**
Although not directly observed in this study, previous aquarium observations documented courtship behavior beginning up to two weeks before spawning, characterized by repeated contact between male and female and a stereotyped action where the male flexed its caudal peduncle, partially encircling the female's head with its tail while quivering strongly. Both sexes prepared the nest depression. During spawning, male and female lay side-by-side with bellies together when expelling gametes, with spawning lasting approximately 4 hours. The male guards the nest and newly hatched fry, hovering over the egg mass and agitating eggs with fins. Males remain highly aggressive while guarding, with eggs hatching in 6-8 days and males continuing to guard fry for seven days post-hatching. Females are typically chased away from nests after spawning.

**Population and Management Implications:**
The estimated standing crop of [[Flathead Catfish]] in the 3-9 kg range was approximately 3.4 kg/ha in 1968. The high proportion of non-spawning females and evidence of egg reabsorption suggest possible density-dependent effects or habitat limitation. The addition of artificial spawning structures (e.g., 55-gallon drums) might increase spawning success, though current low reservoir levels may not support sufficient forage fish populations to sustain increased flathead catfish abundance without reducing growth rates. The species' role as an apex piscivore channeling substantial forage fish biomass makes it ecologically important for reservoir ecosystem function and commercially valuable despite comprising a small fraction of total harvest.
