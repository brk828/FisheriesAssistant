# Lake Mohave Fish Sampling Survey (14–17 May 1991)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #[[Bonytail]] #[[Razorback Sucker]]

## Executive Summary

Arizona State University personnel conducted a 4-day fish sampling survey of [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona–Nevada border) from 14–17 May 1991, focused on recovering adult [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], two imperiled endemic [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes. Trammel nets deployed in [[Cottonwood Cove]] captured 5 bonytail and 15 adult razorback suckers, alongside substantial bycatch dominated by non-native [[Carp]] (95.1% of total catch). A parallel effort in the [[Arizona Bay]] (Yuma Cove) backwater, targeting young razorback suckers previously observed as larvae (Feb–Apr), failed to capture any fish, likely due to ontogenetic habitat shift and loss of phototactic behavior in juveniles.

## Background & Description

**Bonytail (*Gila elegans*)** — n = 5 captured:
- Two fish measured and PIT tagged: 37.5 cm TL / 420 g and 42.0 cm TL / 580 g
- Both tagged individuals were ripe, tuberculate males, classified as "young" despite lacking fin-clip marks
- Two additional fish estimated at 45 and 47 cm TL (transported live to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]])
- One mortality: 44.0 cm TL, 860 g, tuberculate male (retained for ageing and genetic analysis)
- All captured in shallow water (1–3 m) over cobble substrate at night

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** — n = 15 adults (excluding one recapture):
- Males (n = 8): mean 37.1 ± 2.1 cm TL, range 52.5–62.5 cm
- Females (n = 7): mean 63.9 ± 2.4 cm TL, range 61.5–68.5 cm
- One female (63.0 cm) was a documented recapture — originally PIT tagged and released in the same area on 18 March 1991, confirming short-term residency/site fidelity

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Cottonwood Cove]]**: Shallow (1–5 m) nearshore habitat over cobble substrate; used by adult bonytail and razorback sucker, particularly during nocturnal periods. Overwhelmingly dominated numerically by invasive **[[Carp]]**, indicating heavy competitive/ecological pressure on native catostomid and cyprinid species.
- **[[Arizona Bay]] (Yuma Cove) backwater**: Nursery habitat previously documented (Feb–Apr 1991) to support larval razorback sucker recruitment via phototactic behavior detectable by spotlighting. By May, presumed growth of larvae to "a few cm" size correlated with:
  - Loss of phototactic response (reducing spotlight detectability)
  - Shift to deeper, central backwater habitat not accessible to small seine gear
- Adult razorback suckers were observed resting near bottom in shallow water (0.5–1 m) within the backwater, but no other fish species (native or introduced) were detected during this sampling event — suggesting the backwater may function as a relatively isolated, low-diversity native fish refuge at this life stage.
- Non-native game/sport fish (striped bass, largemouth bass, channel catfish, rainbow trout) co-occur with native species in Cottonwood Cove, representing potential predation and competition pressures on native fish recruitment, particularly for [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] juveniles.

## Key Findings

1. **Bonytail recovery**: Only 5 adult bonytail were captured over 3 nights of trammel netting — reflecting the species' critically low abundance in Lake Mohave, consistent with its status as one of the rarest native fish in the Colorado River system.
2. **Sex-linked reproductive condition**: Captured bonytail were reproductively active (ripe, tuberculate) males, indicating sampling coincided with spawning season (mid-May).
3. **Razorback sucker recapture confirms residency**: A female tagged and released in March 1991 was recaptured in the same location in May 1991, supporting evidence of localized habitat use/site fidelity in adult razorback sucker populations at Cottonwood Cove.
4. **Sexual size dimorphism**: Female razorback suckers were notably larger (61.5–68.5 cm TL) than males (52.5–62.5 cm TL), consistent with known dimorphism patterns in the species.
5. **Larval recruitment habitat shift**: Failure to detect any fish in the Arizona Bay backwater in May — despite confirmed larval presence earlier in the season — implies a critical ontogenetic shift in habitat use and behavior (loss of phototaxis, movement to deeper water) that complicates monitoring of young razorback sucker survival and growth.
6. **Non-native dominance**: Carp constituted 95.1% of total trammel net catch by number, underscoring the scale of non-native fish biomass relative to native target species in Lake Mohave.
7. **Management implication**: The report explicitly calls for improved sampling methodologies (entanglement netting, deep-water trapping, SCUBA survey) to better assess survival and growth of young razorback suckers in backwater nursery habitats — highlighting a recognized methodological gap in monitoring recruitment of this endangered species.
