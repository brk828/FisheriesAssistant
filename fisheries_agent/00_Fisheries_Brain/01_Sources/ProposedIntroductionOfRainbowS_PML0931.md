# Proposed Introduction of Rainbow Smelt into Lake Powell: Agency Correspondence and Impact Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1990–1991 set of agency correspondence and an impact-assessment summary from the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] concerning a proposal to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) as a forage fish into [[Lake Powell]]. The proposal responded to a forage shortage that had followed unprecedented [[Striped Bass]] reproduction and stunted the reservoir's sport fishery. Comments were received from three federal agencies, six state agencies, and sixteen independent reviewers; opposition was strong, and the Division concluded that the risk to endemic fishes was low and that the introduction was justified. The Colorado River Fish and Wildlife Council had not yet approved the action, and the Division was studying its options at the time of the final letter.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Striped bass in Lake Powell, introduced in 1974, reproduced naturally from 1979. The population exceeded the forage base, leading to poor condition, a 1987 die-off of adults, and a decline in mean length of harvested fish from 520 mm (1979–1984) to 348 mm (1988).
- **Forage base:** [[Threadfin Shad]] was the only pelagic forage fish. Shad populations cycled on a roughly three-year pattern, and each peak since 1978 was smaller. No peak occurred in 1987, and shad were absent from pelagic trawl samples in 1988 and 1989.
- **Proposed species:** Rainbow smelt was selected in 1987 after a list of candidate species was screened. A formal proposal was submitted in 1988. A majority of the Technical Committee recommended introduction; the Council deferred action pending an impact assessment.
- **Rainbow smelt biology (as cited):** A deep-water pelagic planktivore with a pelagic larva, demersal juvenile and hypolimnetic adult stage. Evans and Loftus (1987) describe it as typical of deep, cold, transparent, low-productivity lakes.
- **Alternatives evaluated:** [[Pygmy Smelt]] introduction, a [[Multi-Species Forage Complex]], commercial fishery, regulation changes, and no action. Dismissed options included additional threadfin shad stocking, stripercides, sterile striped bass, limiting spawning, a predator for striped bass, and protected shad nurseries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Powell physical setting:** Impounded by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the Colorado River in 1963. The reservoir has an average depth of 50.9 m and a maximum capacity of 9.12 × 10⁸ m³ (Johnson and Merritt 1979 as cited). It is an oligotrophic, warm monomictic canyon reservoir with surface temperatures of 6.7–29.4 °C and Secchi readings up to 13 m. Shoreline (2,930 km) is mostly sandstone cliffs and talus. Annual visitation exceeds 3 million.

**Predator–prey dynamics:** Adult striped bass move into the cool hypolimnion, where they cannot effectively compete with juveniles that feed on shad in warm, turbid shallows. Juvenile striped bass can filter-feed on zooplankton; adults cannot. Smelt would occupy the cold, deep zone, the hypolimnion, which is largely void of fish.

**Largemouth bass and black crappie:** Declines are linked to loss of brushy cover under annual reservoir fluctuation. Smallmouth bass were introduced in 1982 and established satellite populations.

**Walleye and catfish:** Walleye boomed in the late 1970s, peaked in 1981 and depend on threadfin shad. Channel catfish and yellow bullhead support a seasonal fishery but do not reach large size.

**Native fishes:** [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and flannelmouth sucker are endemic "big river" fishes. Adults are rare in the reservoir, and the lake does not provide spawning or young-rearing habitat for them.

**Downstream and other reservoirs:** The introduction raised concerns about [[Lee's Ferry]] tailwater trout, [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. Smelt have been established in other reservoirs, including Lake Texoma and Lake Huron-linked systems, with mixed outcomes.

## Key Findings

- **Public comment (March 1990 draft):** Three federal agencies, six state agencies and sixteen independent reviewers commented. Supporting comments came from Governor Norman Bangerter, the Utah Wildlife Board, Nevada and Wyoming wildlife agencies and C. C. Coutant. Opposing comments came from the Arizona Game and Fish Department, California Fish and Game, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the National Park Service (Grand Canyon), the Bureau of Reclamation (Salt Lake City), the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists, and several professors. Neutral comments came from J. J. Ney.
- **Issue 2 (predation and overlap):** Evans and Loftus reported little evidence that smelt reduced zooplankton, and noted that smelt were usually found in deep, cold lakes. Striped bass were identified as the only fish species likely to be affected.
- **Issue 3 (illegal transfer):** The Division acknowledged the risk of illegal stocking. It proposed an education campaign and noted that the transfer of other forage species had already occurred in many western waters without known smelt transfers.
- **Issue 7 (Threadfin Shad):** Supplemental stocking of shad was dismissed because it would only add to brood stock and would not solve predation on young shad. Shad were also identified as having had moderate levels of selenium in fish tissue.
- **Issue 11–12 (endemic fishes):** The Division concluded that smelt would not impact native fishes in reaches where conditions are suitable for native survival. Native fishes "have evolved in an extremely turbulent, turbid, tepid riverine environment" that is alien to smelt.
- **Issue 16 (establishment):** The Division conceded a chance smelt would not establish but considered success likely due to cold-water behavior and feeding habits.
- **Selection standards:** Rainbow smelt was chosen on five criteria: reproductive potential, population stability, trophic efficiency, vulnerability to predation and innocuousness (Ney 1981).
- **Pygmy smelt:** Identified as a valid lacustrine sibling species with later spawning, slower growth (125 mm TL) and non-migratory behavior, but smaller and more vulnerable to predation.
- **Stocking history:** Lake Powell received stockings of largemouth bass, rainbow trout, kokanee, black crappie, threadfin shad (1969) and striped bass (1974–1978), and smallmouth bass (1982–1988), with extensive records in Table 1.
- **Council and public process:** The Colorado River Fish and Wildlife Council instituted stricter guidelines for exotic introductions in 1981. The Utah proposal (1988) was opposed by a majority of voting members.
- **Conclusion (Division position):** Introduction of the species "will in no way jeopardize the endemic fishes" and "more than justifies the modest cost," per the Division. The Division stated it was "studying our options" in the final letter of the correspondence (1991).
