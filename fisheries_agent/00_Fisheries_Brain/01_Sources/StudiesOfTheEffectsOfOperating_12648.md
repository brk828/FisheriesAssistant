# Studies of the Effects of Operating the Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant on Twin Lakes, Colorado: 1979 Report of Findings

#ichthyology #limnology #salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #pumped-storage #baseline-study

## Executive Summary

This report documents the 1979 pre-operational ecological baseline for [[Twin Lakes]], Colorado, prior to the 1981 commissioning of the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]]. The study encompasses comprehensive physical, chemical, and biological limnology of a two-lake mountain system at 2802 m elevation. [[Upper Twin Lake]] (263.4 ha, 28 m max depth) and [[Lower Twin Lake]] (736.5 ha, 27 m max depth) were monitored monthly for temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, nutrients, primary productivity, chlorophyll a, phytoplankton, zooplankton, and benthic macroinvertebrates. The lakes are oligotrophic, calcium-bicarbonate systems with extremely soft water (TDS 20-200 mg/L). The 1979 data reflect recovery from both a 1975 winterkill event (caused by trace metal toxicity during severe winter stratification) and drought conditions in 1976-77. Above-normal runoff in 1978-79 increased nutrient inputs and biological productivity throughout the food web. The fishery is dominated by introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), and the mysid shrimp [[Mysis relicta]]. This baseline will enable quantification of powerplant operational impacts during planned post-1981 monitoring.

## Background & Description

[[Twin Lakes]] is located on [[Lake Creek]] at the eastern front of the [[Sawatch Range]] in the upper [[Arkansas River Valley]] of central Colorado. The lakes originated from morainal damming and are the largest natural mountain lakes in Colorado. [[Lower Twin Lake]] is the state's largest natural mountain lake. The system has been significantly modified: outlet control works were installed, the inter-lake channel was dredged, and non-native species were introduced, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], and [[Mysis relicta]] (introduced 1958 from Clearwater Lake, Minnesota).

The lakes are oligotrophic with calcium-bicarbonate/calcium-sulfate chemistry. Water is extremely soft with TDS ranging 20-200 mg/L (average ~120 mg/L world rivers). Calcium dominates cations (64-73 percent), while bicarbonate (50-68 percent) and sulfate (27-46 percent) dominate anions. Sulfate composition is notably higher than the global river average (16.1 percent). Total phosphorus averaged 1.8 µg/L (ultra-oligotrophic range), nitrate averaged 108 µg/L in inflow and 46 µg/L in lakes, and total Kjeldahl nitrogen averaged 106 µg/L in both lakes.

The study began in 1971 to document pre-operational conditions. Previous severe impacts include a 1974-75 winterkill in [[Upper Twin Lake]] when 158 days of ice cover with heavy snowpack caused bottom anoxia, releasing toxic concentrations of copper (0.09 mg/L), zinc (0.07 mg/L), manganese (2.5 mg/L), and iron (1.7 mg/L) from sediments, nearly eliminating the benthic community. Recovery was slow, compounded by 1976-77 drought conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lakes exhibit strong seasonal stratification patterns. Ice-on typically occurs late November to early December, with ice-off in late April to mid-May. Ice cover duration in 1978-79 was 163 days. Winter stratification leads to hypolimnetic oxygen depletion and pH decline to acidic levels (minimum 6.4). Spring runoff recharges oxygen and drives circulation. Summer stratification develops by mid-August with thermocline at 6-12 m depth. Fall turnover occurs by mid-October.

The 1978-79 winter was severe but not as extreme as 1974-75. By April 4, 1979, [[Upper Twin Lake]] showed warning signs: minimum dissolved oxygen saturation of 14 percent, redox potential of 261 mV, and elevated metals (manganese 2.60 mg/L, iron 2.08 mg/L, copper 0.02 mg/L, zinc 0.013 mg/L). However, sudden warming in late April increased [[Lake Creek]] inflow from 0.28 to 0.57 m³/s, rapidly recharging the lake and averting a repeat winterkill. Metals reoxidized and precipitated before reaching toxic concentrations.

Runoff patterns strongly influence lake ecology. Water year 1979 runoff was 119 percent of the 31-year mean, following 124 percent in 1978, contrasting sharply with the 1977 drought (47 percent of mean). Peak runoff in June 1979 reached 68 m³/s, flushing [[Upper Twin Lake]] completely every 7 days. This high turbidity (light extinction coefficient maximum 1.48 m⁻¹) suppressed primary productivity in the upper lake until late July.

The phytoplankton community is dominated by the diatoms [[Asterionella]] sp. and [[Synedra]] sp. (winter-spring, primarily in lower lake) and the chrysophyte [[Dinobryon]] sp. (summer-fall, both lakes). In 1979, the green alga [[Dictyosphaerium]] sp. comprised 20 percent of lower lake flora in June-July. Average phytoplankton density was 4800 cells/L in lower lake versus 1000 cells/L in upper lake. The lower lake consistently showed 3-5 times greater biological productivity.

Zooplankton composition has changed dramatically since Juday's 1902-03 surveys, when cladocerans ([[Daphnia]] spp.) comprised 20-45 percent of fauna. In 1974-79, cladocerans never exceeded 1 percent. The fauna is now dominated by rotifers ([[Kellicotia]] sp., [[Keratella]] sp., [[Polyarthra]] sp.) and copepods ([[Diaptomus shoshone]], [[Cyclops longispinius]]). Average 1979 zooplankton density was 47/L in lower lake versus 13/L in upper lake. The introduced [[Mysis relicta]] is abundant but nocturnal, not captured in daytime samples. The shift from cladoceran to rotifer dominance likely relates to [[Mysis relicta]] introduction and/or other anthropogenic changes (fish introductions, physical modifications, hydrological alterations).

Benthic communities in 1979 showed [[Lower Twin Lake]] with twice the total biomass of [[Upper Twin Lake]]. Chironomids averaged 2023/m² (1.96 g dry wt/m²) in lower lake versus 682/m² (0.78 g/m²) in upper lake. Oligochaetes showed opposite pattern: 1132/m² (0.37 g/m²) in upper lake versus 524/m² (0.21 g/m²) in lower lake. Fingernail clams occurred consistently in lower lake (132/m²) but only once in upper lake. [[Mysis relicta]] appeared in benthic samples July-November in lower lake (up to 172/m²), corresponding with fall population peaks.

## Key Findings

**Physical-Chemical Parameters (1979):**
- Surface temperatures ranged 1.0-16.4°C; bottom temperatures 2.2-8.6°C
- Dissolved oxygen: surface 7.2-11.2 mg/L; bottom 1.3-9.9 mg/L
- pH: surface 7.1-8.6; bottom 6.3-7.7
- Conductivity: 42-122 µS/cm
- Light extinction coefficients: 0.27-1.48 m⁻¹
- TDS decreased 80 percent during peak runoff (June-July) but annual yield remained high

**Primary Productivity:**
- [[Lower Twin Lake]] consistently more productive than [[Upper Twin Lake]]
- Maximum rate in lower lake: 21,696 µgC/(m²·h) on May 19, 1979 (highest ever recorded at Twin Lakes)
- Minimum rate in upper lake: 99 µgC/(m²·h) on June 19, 1979 (near-zero due to flushing and turbidity)
- Lower lake showed bimodal pattern with peaks after spring and fall turnover
- Upper lake productivity suppressed January-June by trace metals, flushing, and turbidity; recovered July-November
- Rates fall within oligotrophic range (4100-25,000 µgC/(m²·h)) reported by Lieth and Whittaker (1975)

**Chlorophyll a:**
- Average concentrations: 3.84 mg/m³ in lower lake (low mesotrophic), 1.49 mg/m³ in upper lake (oligotrophic)
- Maximum concentration: 51 mg/m³ at 9 m thermocline in lower lake, August 2
- Seasonal pattern: low during turnover periods, maximum in August, concentrated at thermocline during stratification

**Biological Recovery:**
- 1979 data indicate culmination of recovery from 1975 winterkill and 1976-77 drought
- Zooplankton abundance in August 1979 far exceeded any previous year (1974-78)
- Benthic populations recovered in upper lake, though clam populations may still be depressed
- Increased nutrient inputs from above-normal runoff (1978-79) drove higher productivity throughout food web

**Lake Currents (Colorado Cooperative Fishery Research Unit studies):**
- Maximum surface currents: 14 cm/s (upper lake), 13.7 cm/s (lower lake)
- Maximum bottom currents: 9.0 cm/s (upper lake), 15.2 cm/s (lower lake, near inflow)
- Upper lake surface currents strongly wind-dependent, moving nearly straight downwind
- Lower lake currents more variable due to complex shoreline and sheltering by [[Mackinaw Point]] and raised land
- Strong bottom currents (7-11 cm/s) documented at known [[Lake Trout]] spawning areas (Rocky Point, Hartman Point, southeast shore of upper lake), likely maintaining clean substrate
- No lake trout eggs found in water <1 m depth; shallow spawning (<2 m) unlikely

**Critical Winterkill Risk Factors:**
- Complete, continuous snow cover maintained through April creates high winterkill risk
- 1978-79 winter approached 1974-75 severity but was averted by late-March warming
- Barcia and Mathias (1979) model predicts [[Upper Twin Lake]] would suffer anaerobiosis under extended April snow cover
- Weather in final month of ice cover is critical to winter survival

**Trophic Classification:**
- By TDS (20-200 mg/L): oligo-mesotrophic
- By total phosphorus (1.8 µg/L): ultra-oligotrophic
- By total Kjeldahl nitrogen (106 µg/L): oligotrophic
- By chlorophyll a: lower lake low mesotrophic (3.84 mg/m³), upper lake oligotrophic (1.49 mg/m³)
- Overall: oligotrophic system with lower lake showing mesotrophic tendencies

**Allochthonous Control:**
- Lake ecology strongly controlled by external (allochthonous) nutrient and trace element inputs via [[Lake Creek]]
- TDS and nutrient concentrations in lakes closely track inflow concentrations
- Flow characteristics have significant chemical and biological impact
- This finding is critical for predicting effects of powerplant operation and interbasin water transfers

**Post-Construction Predictions:**
- Increased fetch from enlarged [[Lower Twin Lake]] may increase current velocities, potentially affecting [[Mysis relicta]] (sensitive to turbulence), bottom sediments, and larval fish distribution
- Powerplant tailrace currents will add new dimension, continuing through winter, potentially affecting ice safety, under-ice turbidity, and energy budget
- Daily water level fluctuations (1-1.5 m) during pumping-generating cycle will expose shallow substrates; however, [[Lake Trout]] spawning appears confined to >1 m depth (likely >2 m considering ice effects)
- Field studies planned to continue through 1984 for minimum 3-year post-operational comparison
