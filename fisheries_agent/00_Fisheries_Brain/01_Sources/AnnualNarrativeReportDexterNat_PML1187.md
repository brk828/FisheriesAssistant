# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Annual Narrative Report, Calendar Year 1986

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual narrative report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in the [[Pecos River Valley]] of southeastern New Mexico (submitted by Hatchery Manager Buddy Lee Jensen, January 20, 1987) documents the 1986 season of a facility devoted to rearing, spawning and distributing imperiled fishes of the southwestern United States. Most of the output went to recovery and augmentation stocking of federally listed species, chiefly [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Woundfin]]. The year was poor for several species. Colorado squawfish spawning and egg quality declined sharply, and the squawfish pond return was 18.1%. Woundfin overwinter survival remained a persistent problem, and a fish kill destroyed most of a future razorback brood lot. Razorback sucker work, by contrast, was described as successful.

## Background & Description

The hatchery was established in 1932 and has operated under its present mission. Its current culture program consists entirely of rearing, spawning, and distributing native fishes of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. In 1986 the hatchery distributed fish across portions of the Gila, Salt, Verde and Colorado river systems and their tributaries in central and southern Arizona, western Arizona, eastern California, and various sites in New Mexico and Texas.

Staffing in 1986 comprised eight paid employees, including the Hatchery Manager, a Fishery Biologist, an Assistant Manager, a Fishery Biologist, a Management Biologist, two Maintenance Workers, and an Office Assistant. Donald Eales joined the staff on November 10, 1986.

The hatchery held 14 species in 1986 (summarized in a federal status table). The species are listed as Endangered, Threatened, or Candidate for future listing under the federal list dated December 16, 1985. Key species included:

- [[Razorback Sucker]] (Endangered)
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Endangered)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (Endangered)
- [[Woundfin]] (Endangered)
- [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Yaqui Chub]] (both transferred off-station during the year)
- [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]] and [[Chihuahua Chub]]
- [[Beautiful Shiner]]
- [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Big Bend Gambusia]]

The hatchery also maintained [[Largemouth Bass]] and goldfish, the latter used as forage for squawfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Razorback sucker larval predation.** An experiment examined whether predation by [[Green Sunfish]] was limiting recruitment in [[Lake Mohave]] ponds. Larval survival was highest in ponds free of sunfish and lowest where sunfish density was greatest, so survival appeared directly proportional to predator density. Other monitored variables showed no significant differences among ponds except pond size. Survival exceeding 100% in some ponds was attributed to enumeration problems.

**Starvation physiology.** Research by Diana Papoulias (Arizona State University) conducted at the hatchery found that the point of irreversible larval starvation was between 17 and 21 days after hatching at 18°C.

**Predation and cannibalism in raceways.** Colorado squawfish held in raceways and not feeding were subject to cannibalism. Mortality was attributed in part to predation while fish were bunched together and off feed, and it became significant when fish had been placed on prepared diets for two to ten days. Reared squawfish also did not readily accept feed once moved from ponds to raceways or other holding tanks.

**Disease and parasites.** [[Asian Tapeworm]] tested negative in beautiful shiners and in all lots of Colorado squawfish, and both squawfish brood lots were negative. The woundfin lot was lost to a disease that was controlled with [[Malachite Green]], under an exemption request. [[Ichthyophonus]] is suspected in Leon Springs pupfish but had not manifested in the population.

**Water and pond management.** Production ponds were fertilized and filled with well water. Zooplankton blooms collapsed before fry could be stocked because the spawning season was extended, and no additional ponds were available to ferti-lize for later spawns.

## Key Findings

**Razorback sucker (Endangered): a successful year**
- 92 four- and five-year-old female razorback suckers were spawned using [[Gonadotropin]] to induce ovulation, yielding an average of about 82,500 eggs per female.
- Razorback fry were stocked at multiple sites, including Dexter production ponds, Page Springs Hatchery (Arizona), Imperial Valley Hatchery (California), Lake Havasu NWR, and the Colorado River Indian Reservation.
- A total of 1,707 pounds of razorback suckers were distributed. A conversion factor of 1.42 was used for count estimates.
- 397 two-, four-, and five-year-old brood fish were maintained.
- A fish kill destroyed 83% of a future brood lot (about 491 of 591 fish). The 100 survivors were combined with the current brood stock, and additional Lake Mohave fry were to be collected in 1987.
- Sixteen razorbacks stocked in 1984 or 1985 were recaptured in 1986: two from Eagle Creek and eleven from Bonita Creek.

**Colorado squawfish (Endangered): a poor year**
- Only 14 of 105 injected 12-year-old females (11.9%) spawned, and many did not spawn out fully.
- Average egg take was 56,441 per female, down 32% from 82,778 in 1985.
- Egg viability averaged 43.2%, down 11.5 points from 54.7% in 1985.
- Swim-up fry were 49.6% of expected, down 6.9 points from 56.5%, and fry appeared weaker than in previous years.
- Five ponds were stocked with 479,313 fry. Only 86,956 fingerlings (18.1%) were harvested. One pond returned 75%, while two returned less than 1% each.
- Fingerlings were restored in rearing ponds, producing 70,700 for an 81.3% return.
- Fingerlings were stocked in Arizona: 35,692 in the Verde River, 17,650 in Cherry Creek, and 5,793 in Upper Canyon Creek. A total of 59,395 survived to stocking.
- The first recovery in the Salt River was a six-inch individual taken during annual monitoring.

**Bonytail chub (Endangered)**
- No bonytails were spawned from captive brood stock, but fish spawned naturally in rearing ponds.
- 9,478 three-inch fingerlings were harvested and distributed to [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]], with others going to research ponds.
- The hatchery maintained 500 adult brood stock and 1,372 excess adults, averaging about 14 inches and one-half pound at five years old.

**Woundfin (Endangered): chronic overwinter losses**
- The hatchery entered winter with 379 fish and recovered 120 in spring, a 31.5% survival rate.
- Overwinter survival has never exceeded 30–40% despite several methods. Older fish, which may be naturally aging, are suspected to account for many losses.
- 500 woundfin were collected from the Virgin River in February. Chub disease required treatment with malachite green.
- An experimental spawning pond held at ambient temperatures without flowing water produced 463 fry, and 293 young fish were removed for rearing.

**Other species**
- Pahranagat roundtail chub survival was 30.4% in 1985 (14 of 46) and 49% in 1986 (24 of 49).
- Chihuahua chub: 352 adults maintained.
- Beautiful shiner: over 2,000 overwintered.
- Leon Springs pupfish: over 2,000 maintained. Desert pupfish: over 6,000, both doing well.
- Gila topminnow and Big Bend gambusia both produced large populations. The topminnow stock was found to contain significant numbers of mosquitofish, and 553 hand-picked topminnows were restocked into a new pond. Thirty fish went to Rutgers University and 35 to Penn State.
- Largemouth bass: 643 were delivered to the Papago Indian Reservation.

**Visitors, cooperation and outreach**
- About 2,500 people visited the hatchery, plus official visits from regional and field station staff and university researchers.
- Staff collaborated with Arizona Game and Fish, New Mexico Game and Fish, Mescalero NFH, Bitter Lake NWR, Imperial Valley, and the Bureau of Reclamation.
- Staff served on the [[Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team]] (bluntnose shiner plan) and as consultant to the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] (Yaqui fishes plan).
- Presentations were given at the American Fisheries Society (Logan, Utah) and the Desert Fishes Council (St. George, Utah). A paper on induced spawning of hatchery-reared Colorado squawfish was published in the *Progressive Fish-Culturist*, volume 48.

**Facilities and budget**
- Cyclical maintenance items included a new distribution truck and fiberglass tank (about $29,000), pump and well maintenance ($2,600), pond maintenance ($14,400), equipment maintenance ($10,000), and landscaping (about $1,000). Some figures are partly garbled in the scanned copy.
- Pond 8A was deepened and leaks were sealed. A Gallup FAO property inventory was reconciled.
