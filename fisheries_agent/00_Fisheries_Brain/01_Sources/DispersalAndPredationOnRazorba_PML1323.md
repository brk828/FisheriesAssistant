# Dispersal and Predation on Razorback Sucker Reintroduced to the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tracks the fate of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae and juveniles stocked into backwaters of the [[Lower Colorado River]] in 1987, and estimates how much predation by non-native fishes reduced survival. Stocked larvae dispersed from release points within about two weeks, and juveniles moved toward the main channel and were no longer captured after about one week. [[Bluegill]] were the main consumers of larvae, and [[Largemouth Bass]] accounted for all recorded predation on juveniles. The author concludes that the reintroduction is unlikely to succeed with present methods, and that fish should be grown to larger sizes before release.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin of western North America. Historical records describe it as abundant and widely distributed in larger streams from Wyoming into Sonora, Mexico. It remained common in the lower basin until the early 1950s, after which numbers declined dramatically. A survey of the lowermost 274 river km found none, despite concentrated effort on potential spawning grounds.

In 1996, the State of California and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] agreed on a 10-year reintroduction program. Fish were to be produced at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) and stocked into suitable habitats, while the [[Niland Native Fish Ponds]] (Imperial Wildlife Area) would refine methods for rearing larvae to larger stocking sizes.

In 1985, approximately 1.4 million larval razorback suckers (10–18 mm total length) were stocked at six lower Colorado River locations from Lake Havasu downstream to Imperial Dam. Monitoring on five occasions failed to recapture any reintroduced fish, which prompted an intensive study beginning in 1987. Its objectives were to (i) survey stocking sites and surrounding waters to determine movement, dispersal, and habitat use, and (ii) estimate predation impacts by analyzing stomach contents of potential predators.

**Study area:** The study covered the reach forming the Arizona–California border, from Headgate Rock Dam to Imperial Dam. It lies in the [[Sonoran Desert]] scrub zone, with broad floodplain valleys bordered by rugged mountains and soils of sand and silt. The river and its surroundings have been heavily modified by dams, reservoir formation, water diversion, channel dredging, and bank stabilization. Riparian vegetation is dominated by non-native [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*), with only scattered remnant native cottonwood and willow. Aquatic vertebrates are dominated by established non-native fishes, and only the native [[Speckled Dace]]-group fish, the striped mullet (*Mugil cephalus*), remains abundant in the lowermost reaches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods overview**

- **Stocking:** About 900,000 one-week-old larvae (mean 9.3 mm total length) were stocked on 3 March 1987 into two small ponds (<1 ha) adjacent to, but isolated from, the river, and into five backwaters confluent with the main channel. Backwaters were selected because they are warmer and more productive, provide shoreline cover for escape from predators, and are easier to monitor than the main channel. Selected backwaters ranged from 0.1 to 1.5 ha, were 0.5 to 2.5 m deep, and had sand and silt substrates with common giant bulrush.
- **Juvenile rearing:** Fish produced at Dexter in 1985 and 1987 were reared at the Niland ponds and the Blythe Municipal Golf Course, then stocked at ages up to 1-plus years into other backwaters.
- **Larval sampling:** Larvae are phototactic, so [[Spotlight Surveys]] with a 400,000-candlepower beam were run at least one hour after sunset at up to ten fixed stations per stocking area. At least 30 larvae were captured by dip net and preserved for analysis.
- **Predator sampling:** [[Trammel Netting]] (40 × 2 m; 13 mm inner and 155 mm outer mesh) was set near shoreline cover. Fish were checked every 2–3 hours and fixed in 30% formalin to halt digestion. Stomach contents were examined under a dissecting microscope. Larvae were identified by myomere counts, melanophore pattern, and snout-to-anus length.
- **Juvenile sampling:** Trammel nets and [[Electrofishing]] (Coffelt RF-10 pulsed DC unit, 2–10 amps) were used. Electrofishing at Goose Flats was conducted one day, one week, and two weeks after stocking, and shorelines deeper than 0.75 m were sampled.

**Larval dispersal:** Larvae stayed near release points for several days, decreased in number within one week, and nearly disappeared by the end of two weeks at Parker Backwater (AZ). Larvae caught 8–10 days after release averaged 11.2 mm (N = 171, all sites combined). Those collected two weeks after stocking (N = 44) averaged 14 mm, a 50% increase from the stocking size. The largest specimen was 12.9 mm, taken 12 days after release. Earlier work at [[Lake Mohave]] showed that wild larvae shift to deeper, benthic habitats as they grow. Daily water-level fluctuations of 1–2 m may also have pushed larvae from shoreline cover into open water and eventually into the main channel.

**Juvenile dispersal:** At Parker Backwater (AZ), 500 one-year-old juveniles (mean 155 mm) were stocked on 7 May 1987. Only one fish was captured 2 hours after release and two after 6 hours, but 15 were taken at 0400 hours, which suggests nocturnal movement toward the main channel. Fish were mostly collected near shore in about 1 m of water, which suggests they followed the shoreline rather than dispersing into open water. A similar pattern occurred at Parker Backwater (CA) with 390 juveniles (mean 165 mm) stocked on 17 June 1987, where 3.5–3.8% of stocked fish were captured on each date. At [[Goose Flats Backwater]], 4,015 juveniles (mean 125 mm) were stocked on 17 November 1987. One day after release, 128 were captured, 57% downstream and 43% upstream of the release site, with 93% within 0.8 km. Only 13 were captured one week later, and none two weeks after stocking. The authors attribute this loss to movement into the main channel, predation, and survivors hiding in dense reed beds. Daily flushing of the backwater for irrigation and power generation may have helped drive emigration.

## Key Findings

**Predation on larvae (stomach analysis)**

- Stomachs from eight potential predator species were examined (N = 428). Larval razorback suckers were found in only 10 fish, from three species.
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*): 8 of 153 (5.3%) contained larvae, averaging 4.9 larvae per fish (range 1–19). Bluegill were the primary consumers of larvae.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*): 1 of 177 (0.6%) contained larvae, with four larvae in a 177 mm fish.
- [[Warmouth]] (*Lepomis gulosus*): 1 of 27 (3.4%) contained larvae, with two larvae in a 171 mm fish.
- The author notes that these figures probably underestimate consumption. Larvae in [[Green Sunfish]] stomachs became unidentifiable within four hours, and threadfin shad grind food in a gizzard-like foregut. Predator collections were also short and limited to small areas.

**Predation on juveniles**

- Among five potential predators at Parker Backwater (AZ), razorbacks were found only in [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Two bass (340 mm and 515 mm) contained razorbacks averaging 153 mm, and a 357 mm bass had eaten a single 109 mm fish stocked on 19 August 1987. None were found in 15 predators collected after the 4 June stocking.
- At Parker Backwater (CA), one 263 mm largemouth bass had eaten a single small razorback from 31 predators examined. The reported length is unclear in the source.
- At Goose Flats, 1-plus-year-old fish (191–198 mm) stocked in September were not found in 45 predators examined immediately after stocking. Smaller fish (127 mm) stocked in November were found in 26 predators examined, with consumed fish averaging 109 mm and predators (all largemouth bass) averaging 353 mm.
- Predation was higher on smaller (<130 mm) than larger (>190 mm) juveniles. This parallels earlier findings by [[Marsh and Brooks]] that smaller razorbacks were eaten in greater numbers.
- In a separate study by Marsh and Brooks, [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) consumed substantial numbers of razorbacks. Neither species appeared to prey on fish at Goose Flats, but few catfish were collected there. The authors suggest that shoreline-dwelling bass were more vulnerable to electrofishing than benthic catfish, so catfish predation may be underestimated.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Results of post-stocking monitoring "paint a bleak picture" for successful reestablishment with present methods, a conclusion also supported by other studies.
- Survival and growth have been strong for razorbacks stocked into protected, isolated habitats such as artificial waters at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] and the Blythe Municipal Golf Course. Fish reared in these facilities reached over 400 mm.
- Fish of this size appear largely resistant to depletion by non-native predators when stocked into open waters.
- Current California rearing capacity is limited to several 0.1–0.2 ha ponds at the Niland ponds. Crowding forced frequent removal of fish and led to stunting. Juveniles were then released from June (mean 53 mm) through November (mean 199 mm), which falls within the size range of fish eaten by resident predators.
- Recommended actions include growing fewer fish to larger sizes, securing additional grow-out areas (especially naturally isolated or semi-isolated backwaters on National Wildlife Refuges), and using innovative food-habit methods such as [[Biochemical Gut Content Analysis]] (ELISPOT immunoassay) to estimate predation.
- Razorbacks are long-lived (at least 43 years), so establishing large fish through reintroduction could provide decades to investigate why wild populations fail to recruit.
- Predation by non-native fishes "undoubtedly plays a significant, although yet unquantified role" in the failure of both wild and reintroduced populations. Future studies should focus on food habits and more intensive post-stocking sampling, and reintroduction strategies should emphasize growing fish to larger sizes before release.
