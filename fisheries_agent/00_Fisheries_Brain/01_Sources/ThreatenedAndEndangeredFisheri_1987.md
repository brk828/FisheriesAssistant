## Executive Summary

This 1987 management plan outlines a cooperative strategy between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Dexter National Fish Hatchery, [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]]), and the Arizona Game and Fish Department to use refuge stock-watering ponds as **growout and refugium habitat** for five federally threatened or endangered fish species native to the Colorado River Basin. The refuge had no historic native fish populations prior to the construction of ~40 stock ponds, most of which are fishless, access-controlled, and hold water year-round — conditions considered ideal for rearing endangered fish that cannot be adequately grown at the space-limited [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. A 1986 trial stocking demonstrated growth rates comparable to hatchery conditions during the standard April–October growing season. The plan establishes protocols for surveying, stocking, monitoring, harvesting, and interagency coordination, restricted to ponds within the north-flowing **Altar Wash drainage**.

## Background & Description

The source document is administrative/programmatic rather than descriptive, and does not provide detailed morphological data. Life-stage information relevant to stocking is limited to:

- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Stocked as fry (150,000–500 per site) and as adults (~500 per site), indicating dual life-stage rearing strategy (growout of fry to adults, and direct adult refugium stocking).
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Stocked exclusively as fry (25,000–300,000 per site), consistent with a growout objective (rear to larger size before eventual river release).
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (now known as [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]): Designated for growout use, though no specific 1987 stocking numbers are listed in the surviving schedule.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]**: Stocked in small, varied numbers (1,000–2,500) as mixed life stages, consistent with refugium (conservation banking) rather than growout use.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]**: Designated for refugium use only; no numeric stocking data provided in this schedule.

Growth performance note: fish stocked in refuge ponds in autumn 1986 achieved growth rates "equal to" those obtained at Dexter NFH during its normal growing season, suggesting refuge pond conditions (temperature, productivity) are comparable to or better than hatchery raceways for these species during equivalent periods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Refuge Pond Habitat**: Approximately 40 artificial water bodies on Buenos Aires NWR, originally constructed for livestock watering, several of which are perennial. These ponds are largely devoid of resident fish, offering a "blank slate" habitat free of competition or predation from established fish communities.
- **Watershed Constraint**: Only ponds within the **Altar Wash drainage** (a north-flowing tributary system) are approved for use, likely to prevent unintended dispersal of stocked fish into other drainages or mixing with the natural aquatic community.
- **Exotic Species Interaction**: The initial survey protocol explicitly screens for exotic fish species in candidate ponds, with removal planned prior to stocking — recognizing exotic species as a competitive/predatory threat to native T&E fish.
- **Growout vs. Refugium Function**:
  - *Growout ponds* ([[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and part of [[Bonytail Chub]] program): Fish are reared for ~2 years, then harvested and released into "selected rivers" — functioning as a stepping-stone between hatchery propagation and wild reintroduction.
  - *Refugium ponds* ([[Desert Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and part of [[Bonytail Chub]] program): Function as semi-permanent conservation populations/genetic reserves rather than production-for-release sites.
- **Source Population**: All stocked fish originate from **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]**, New Mexico, the Service's primary Colorado River native fish propagation facility, whose limited pond space and shortened growing season constrained production — the core rationale for the refuge partnership.

## Key Findings

- **Trial Success (1986)**: A pilot stocking in autumn 1986 confirmed that refuge ponds could match Dexter NFH growth performance, validating the refuge as a viable growout site.
- **Two-Year Growout Cycle**: Growout species are held for approximately two years before harvest via seining, transport by hatchery truck, and release into rivers — establishing a defined production timeline.
- **Concurrent Monitoring**: First-year population monitoring is timed to coincide with the harvest of two-year-old fish, streamlining field logistics (single site visit serves dual purposes).
- **Species-Specific Stocking Scale (1987 Schedule)**:
  - Bonytail chub: up to 150,000 fry per site across 6 sites (Road Camp Tank, Big Bertha Tank, Kings Tank, Hito Tank, State Tank, Aquahito Tank), plus adult stocking at two sites.
  - Razorback sucker: up to 300,000 fry at Mormon Lake, with smaller allotments (25,000–50,000) across 6 additional tanks.
  - Desert pupfish: small-scale stocking (1,000–2,500 individuals) at 4 sites, contingent in some cases on successful pond renovation (exotic species removal).
- **Governance Structure**: Named coordination responsibilities assigned to Jerry Burton (Endangered Species), James Brooks ([[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]), Wayne Shiflett ([[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]]), and Jennifer Fowler-Propst (Wildlife Resources), with annual February coordination meetings and annual reporting to Regional Office, refuge, hatchery, and state agency.
- **Funding Model**: Refuge funds are explicitly excluded from habitat development/maintenance costs; funding is provided by the Endangered Species program, with Fishery Resources conducting field operations contingent on fund availability — highlighting a cross-program funding dependency risk.
- **Formal Review Cycle**: The plan mandates a 3-year operational review by Refuges, Fishery Resources, and Endangered Species programs, reflecting an adaptive management approach.
- **Institutional Precedent**: This plan represents an early (1987) example of using a National Wildlife Refuge's incidental water infrastructure (stock ponds) as a conservation tool for endangered fish recovery, a model later expanded in Southwestern U.S. native fish conservation programs.
