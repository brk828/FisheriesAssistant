# Synoptic Surveys of Lakes Rotorua and Rotoiti

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #zooplankton #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1969 limnological paper by G. R. Fish (Fisheries Research Division, Marine Department, Rotorua) and M. A. Chapman (Zoology Department, University of Auckland) reports short synoptic surveys of [[Lake Rotorua]] and [[Lake Rotoiti]], New Zealand, carried out before a planned three-year monitoring programme. The surveys tested whether water quality differs horizontally, not just vertically, across two lakes of different character. Lake Rotorua showed only small horizontal differences, with higher [[Dissolved Inorganic Phosphate]] in the south (near urban effluent) and phytoplankton concentration in the north. Lake Rotoiti was thermally stratified, showed no important horizontal differences along its main axis, and had a distinct, unstratified western basin. A zooplankton survey at three Rotoiti stations showed that vertical [[Diel Vertical Migration]] combined with horizontal water movement produced a complex distribution.

## Background & Description

The authors frame lake monitoring as a response to human-caused degradation of fresh waters. Lake waters are mainly wind-circulated, so many surveys have sampled a single central station. Earlier work by Verduin (1951) and Tonolli (1955) showed that horizontal differences become more important as lake area increases, which motivated the sampling design.

Lake Rotorua is nearly circular, lies in a large [[Rotorua Caldera]], and has a smooth shoreline. Its 44.5 m hole near the southern shore may be a recent hydrothermal explosion crater, which gives it a volume development below 0.3. Lake Rotoiti is narrow and serpentine. It has been extended by headward erosion toward its single outlet, the [[Kaituna River]]. Its shoreline is much more irregular and it has cliffed, steep margins except in the shallow western basin. Its catchment is about one-sixth that of Lake Rotorua. Its main inflow is the [[Ohau Channel]] from Lake Rotorua, which enters the western basin.

**Methods:** Stations were positioned by bearings. Water was collected with a Ruttner sampler and analysed for dissolved oxygen, alkalinity and dissolved inorganic phosphate using [[Mackereth Water Analysis Methods]]. Phytoplankton was fixed in iodine solution and counted by the [[Utermöhl Sedimentation Method]] on an inverted microscope. Zooplankton was sampled with a Nansen closing net (10 in. diameter, 300 µm mesh). The mesh under-retained small plankters, but results were considered comparable between stations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Rotorua** receives the effluent of Rotorua city and most local thermal springs at its southern end. Most tributaries and cold springs enter along the northern and western shores. The only surface outlet is the Ohau Channel in the north-east. Seepage through the pumice bed may also be a substantial inflow and outflow. Diurnal changes in temperature, alkalinity and dissolved oxygen were attributed to photosynthesis by [[Aquatic Macrophytes]], mainly *Lagarosiphon major* and *Elodea canadensis*, in littoral zones and by large phytoplankton populations. The lake is described as very productive, with a long history of weed and algal bloom problems.

**Lake Rotoiti** was thermally stratified in March 1967. Its western basin, only about 10 m deep, received warmer water from Lake Rotorua. The bottom waters were de-oxygenated.

## Key Findings

**Lake Rotorua (39 stations, 8–9 February 1967):**
- Surface water was most often 21.0 °C and bottom water 18.8–19.0 °C. No sharp thermal discontinuity appeared in any vertical series. Inflows at 12–14 °C had no measurable effect on lake temperature.
- Dissolved oxygen was slightly higher and alkalinity slightly lower at the surface at most stations. Vertical phosphate differences were rare.
- The dominant plankton were the diatoms [[Melosira Granulata]] and [[Asterionella Formosa]]. Melosira was more abundant in bottom samples and Asterionella in surface samples. [[Staurastrum]] was sparse.
- Between northern and southern areas (Table 3), there was no significant difference in surface or bottom dissolved oxygen, or in Asterionella or Staurastrum counts.
- Inorganic phosphate was significantly higher in the south: surface means 0.0160 (north, n = 15) vs 0.0304 ppm (south, n = 24), t = 2.82, P = 0.007; bottom means 0.0207 vs 0.0321 ppm, t = 2.24, P = 0.031.
- Melosira in bottom waters was greater in the north, with odds of about 10:1 (not significant, P = 0.12 in Table 3). Phosphate in the south had odds of more than 20:1.

**Lake Rotoiti (12 stations, 9–10 March 1967):**
- Surface water in the main body was about 20.5 °C. Thermocline depth and temperature profiles varied among deep stations (Fig. 2). The authors interpret the water strata as oscillating in a unimodal seiche pattern, with vigorous mixing within but not between strata.
- Surface phosphate was slightly higher than in Lake Rotorua, and much higher below the thermocline. This suggests lower phytoplankton biomass in Rotoiti, particularly in the hypolimnion.
- Asterionella counts in the western basin were almost as high as in Lake Rotorua, probably carried in by the inflow. Heavier Melosira was not. A *Peridinium* species occurred in large numbers in the western basin only.
- The western basin differs from the rest of Rotoiti and from Lake Rotorua in water quality and phytoplankton. The authors treat it as a separate biological entity.

**Zooplankton (three axial stations S1 west, S2 middle, S3 east, 19 March 1967):**
- Total catch was much greater at S1 than at S2 or S3. S1 also had large populations of the alga *Volvox* and the rotifer *Asplanchna brightwelli* (tentative identification).
- The dominant zooplankter, [[Calamoecia Lucasi]], was concentrated in the 20–30 m zone, within the thermocline, at all stations.
- [[Bosmina Meridionalis]] was the only species with a zone of maximum abundance above 20 m, and only at S2.
- [[Ceriodaphnia Dubia]] occurred in small numbers, with differences between stations too minor to be significant.
- [[Macrocyclops Albidus]] juveniles were found throughout the water column at S1 and S3. At S2, adults and breeding females dominated below 30 m, which the authors considered unusual for a species generally regarded as benthic and littoral.
- Calamoecia life stages showed a clear depth split. Young copepodites (stages I–III) were mostly in the upper 20 m, while stages IV–V and most adults were at 20–30 m. At S1 and S2, many adults also occurred deeper. Example counts at S1 (0–20 / 20–30 / 30–47 m): males 465 / 5,366 / 1,656; females 693 / 3,612 / 5,244.
- All zooplankton, except Bosmina, peaked within the thermocline. Numbers fell rapidly below it as dissolved oxygen declined severely. The authors could not separate the effects of oxygen and light.

**Discussion and recommendations:**
- Lake Rotorua's large surface area and shallow mean depth mean wind usually mixes it fully, so little stratification was expected and largely confirmed. Nearly all plant nutrients come from the catchment rather than bottom deposits (Lund 1964).
- Greatest phytoplankton production was in the north-eastern quadrant. The authors discuss whether prevailing southerly winds, which would push algae north, were offset by currents toward the north-eastern outlet. Most permanent macrophyte beds are in the western half, along the cold-spring and river inflow shore.
- Zooplankton distribution along the Rotoiti axis reflects both vertical migration and horizontal wind-driven transport. The authors suggest the populations are potentially homogeneous but periodically redistributed.
- For the planned long-term programme, the authors recommend separate sampling of the south, north-west and north-east regions of Lake Rotorua. For Lake Rotoiti, they recommend at least three axial stations to track [[Seiche]] movements, plus separate sampling of the western basin.
