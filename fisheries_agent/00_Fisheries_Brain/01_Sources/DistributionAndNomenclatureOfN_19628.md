# Distribution and Nomenclature of North American Kokanee (*Oncorhynchus nerka*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 note by Joseph S. Nelson (University of British Columbia), published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, assembles locality records for indigenous North American [[Kokanee]] populations and reviews the conflicting nomenclature applied to them. The distribution pattern supports [[Ricker Hypothesis]] (1940) that most kokanee populations were independently derived from [[Sockeye Salmon]]. The author concludes that both forms should be designated *Oncorhynchus nerka*.

## Background & Description

Kokanee are freshwater populations of *Oncorhynchus nerka* (Walbaum), native to lakes of Pacific drainages in North America and northeastern Asia. In North America they occur naturally in Alaska, Yukon Territory, British Columbia, Washington, Idaho and Oregon, where their range overlaps that of anadromous sockeye.

The note draws on locality records from historical authors (e.g., [[Suckley]] 1861, Dawson 1879, Bendire 1878, Bean 1891, Evermann 1896, Chamberlain 1907, Dymond 1936), museum records (Nelson 1968) and government agency data. Locality maps (Figs. 1 and 2) distinguish allopatric kokanee, kokanee sympatric with sockeye, and sockeye-only waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Southern range:** Kokanee occur in most lakes where sockeye are present. Owikeno, Cultus, Chilko, Taseko and Harrison lakes (B.C.) may not hold self-sustaining kokanee.
- **Alaska exception:** In Bristol Bay, Cook Inlet, Prince William Sound and Kodiak Island drainages, where sockeye are numerous, kokanee are rare or absent. Kokanee are indigenous to Big Kitoi Lake (58°11′N, 152°21′W) and Skilak Lake (60°20′N, 150°30′W), Alaska.
- **Extirpation of sockeye by landscape change:** Falls formed through landform change and coastal uplift likely blocked sockeye from lakes now holding kokanee. Sockeye have been barred from the Upper Columbia (Okanagan and Arrow lakes, Snake River) within the last 80 years.
- **Natural blockages 150–300 years ago:** Sockeye were blocked from Kathleen Lake (Yukon) by Lowell Glacier on the [[Alsek River]], and from Sand and Lava lakes (Nass River system, B.C.) by a lava flow.
- **River piracy:** Natural diversion of tributaries may explain some kokanee (e.g., Arctic Lake, Peace River drainage; southeastern B.C. lakes), but coastal islands and mainland areas where piracy is unlikely also hold kokanee.
- **Distribution anomaly:** Kokanee distribution is unlike that of any other freshwater fish lacking a diadromous form. Large sockeye attempt to surmount impassable falls below several kokanee lakes, though the author considers it improbable that such individuals founded most populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Origin:** Little evidence supports a general origin of kokanee from other kokanee populations. Kokanee are indigenous only where sockeye probably once ran, supporting Ricker's (1940) hypothesis of multiple independent derivations from sockeye.
2. **Life-history flexibility:** Offspring of kokanee and sockeye may, contrary to their usual life history, become anadromous or remain freshwater-resident, respectively (Fraser 1918; Ward 1929, 1932; Foerster 1947; Rounsefell 1958; Ricker 1959).
3. **Nomenclature history:** The name has been applied variously as *Salmo kennerlyi* (Suckley 1861), *Oncorhynchus kennerlyi* (Jordan 1878), and *Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi* (Bean 1891). Evermann (1896) found no consistent structural differences and treated both forms as *O. nerka*. Subsequent authors used all three names, and Asian workers divided kokanee variously into species, subspecies, or a single binomial.
4. **Taxonomic recommendation:** Although the two forms are sympatric and each can persist in allopatry, the probable phenotypic basis of partial isolating mechanisms, interconvertibility of life histories, and multiple origins from sockeye make it advisable to designate both as *Oncorhynchus nerka*, avoiding two vernacular names for one taxon.
