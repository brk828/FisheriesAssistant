# Selected Aspects of the Natural History of the Desert Sucker (Catostomus clarkii)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #catostomidae #native-fishes #conservation

## Executive Summary

This master's thesis by Craig Stephen Ivanyi (University of Arizona, 1989) examines selected life history aspects of the [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarkii*, also referred to as *Pantosteus clarkii*) in four southeastern Arizona streams. The study focused primarily on [[Sonoita Creek]], with supplementary data from [[Bass Canyon Creek]], [[Babocomari River]], and [[O'Donnell Creek]]—all tributaries of the [[San Pedro River]] or [[Santa Cruz River]] systems. Field work spanned April 1987 through October 1988, investigating habitat use, reproduction, age class structure, and diet. The research was motivated by concerns over habitat degradation and water depletion threatening Arizona's native ichthyofauna, with 25 of 35 native species listed as threatened and 15 federally listed as threatened or endangered. The [[Desert Sucker]] is a highly specialized herbivore adapted to the [[Colorado River Drainage]] below the Grand Canyon and the [[Gila River System]].

## Background & Description

The [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarkii*) is a native catostomid found in the [[Colorado River]] drainage below the Grand Canyon, the [[Gila River]] system of Arizona and New Mexico, and the Virgin and White Rivers of Utah and Nevada. Adults attain standard lengths up to 325 mm. Diagnostic features include a subterminal mouth with large lips, small papillae on the lower lip and oral face of the upper lip, and a cartilaginous sheath on the lower jaw used for scraping. Darkening of the peritoneum is considered an adaptation associated with herbivory, making this species a highly specialized product of *Pantosteus* evolution.

Previous research on the species has been limited. Studies documented parasites in [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Mpoame 1982; Wier et al. 1983), tolerance to low oxygen (Lowe et al. 1967), movements in Arizona and New Mexico streams (Siebert 1980; Meffe 1987; Bestgen et al. 1987), and distribution (multiple authors). Fisher et al. (1981) found the sucker to be herbivorous, feeding heavily on epipelic diatoms in [[Sycamore Creek]], with crepuscular feeding peaks noted by Williams and Williams (1982).

The study streams were small first or second order systems with typical riparian vegetation (cottonwoods, willows, ashes). [[Sonoita Creek]] originates in the [[Santa Rita Mountains]] and flows southwest to the [[Santa Cruz River]] via Patagonia Lake reservoir. The perennial portion begins at Patagonia (elevation 1350 m) with groundwater and treated municipal wastewater providing mean discharge of 7300 cfs (1965-67). The study site on Circle Z Ranch extended over 1.1 km, characterized by long shallow riffles and numerous pools with fallen tree limbs, twigs, and leaves providing overhead cover.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's native ichthyofauna faces severe threats from human water demands, exotic species introductions, and habitat manipulation. Impoundments, diversions, and groundwater pumping have mediated or accelerated threats to native fishes. Habitat degradation in southwestern U.S. deserts has reduced endemic fish populations, resulting in extinctions and endangerment. Many smaller rivers and creeks are now dry except during high precipitation or snowmelt periods. Lush cienegas where fish were abundant have been replaced by dry, shifting washes with little or no fish habitat.

The U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (1975) reported that human water demands do and will continue to exceed current supplies, with demands increasing as Arizona's population grows. This water depletion puts additional pressure on remaining native fishes. Depleting available habitat before specific biological requirements are known increases the chance of irreparable damage to native fish populations.

Water quality in upper [[Sonoita Creek]] was acceptable for [[Desert Sucker]]s throughout the study period. Water temperature ranged from 9°C (December 14, 1987) to 32°C (July 15, 1988). Dissolved oxygen was at or near saturation and pH ranged from 6.5 to 8.6.

## Key Findings

**Diet and Feeding Behavior**: Stomach analysis of 30 fish (10 each from winter, spring, and summer) showed adults fed primarily on unidentifiable plant matter (presumably algae), with significant numbers of diatoms present (10-20%). Young-of-year stomachs contained approximately 70% diatoms and unidentifiable plant matter. Suckers were observed feeding in groups off rocks and gravel, scraping attached algae and associated organisms. In [[Bass Canyon Creek]], feeding occurred primarily on larger rocks and boulders. Only one of the dissected suckers had an empty stomach, indicating consistent feeding activity.

**Length-Weight Relationships**: A total of 93 suckers were collected and preserved. On July 15, 1988, 61 fish were captured, measured, and released, ranging from 35 mm to 150 mm total length and 1 g to 37 g body weight. Most fish measured 95-125 mm TL (10-22.5 g body weight).

**Age Class Structure**: Aging by scale analysis was not possible as annuli were not evident on scales examined from 15 preserved specimens. Desert suckers in southern Arizona do not experience extreme temperature differences between winter and summer, resulting in even growth without clear annuli formation. Length frequency analysis revealed only two distinct size groups: young-of-year (31-70 mm) and larger individuals (81-150 mm), with the latter likely containing multiple age classes.

**Reproduction and Fecundity**: In 1988, spawning commenced in May following a four-month period of enlarged gonads (late January through April). Males exhibited prominent caudal and anal fin tubercles and a horizontally striped color pattern from February through mid-April 1988. Male gonadal volume averaged 0.33 ml (range 0.1-1.0 ml) during the pre-spawning period, dropping to 0.036 ml (range 0.0-0.08 ml) in late April. Female gonadal volume averaged 1.28 ml (range 0.1-4.2 ml) during February-April, dropping to 0.096 ml (range 0.04-0.12 ml) in May. Gravid females carried an average of 1,140 eggs (range 450-2,772), measuring 1-2 mm in diameter. Fry were first collected in June 1988. No color changes or secondary sexual characteristics were observed in females. The specific spawning location, substrate type, and time of day were not determined as spawning was never directly observed.

**Habitat Use**: Almost all suckers (97.3%) were located and captured in pools, rarely in riffles (2.7%), despite pools representing only 3% of the study area. Of total captures, 68% occurred in debris pools (with overhanging limbs, twigs, leaves), 13.5% in undercut banks, 11% in backwaters (mostly fry), and 4.5% in open deeper pools. Pools occupied by suckers varied considerably: 1.2-9.5 m length, 0.3-2.7 m width, 7.6-99.1 cm depth. Pool substrates ranged from nearly all sand to 30-40% cobble mixed with sand and gravel. Adult-occupied pools lacked silt or mud substrates, while fry-used pools contained these fine sediments. Adult suckers preferred pools with approximately 80% visual cover (overhead obstruction) and high water flow. Fry were almost always found in backwaters or small puddles (5-10 cm diameter) adjacent to main riffles, possibly due to inability to maintain position in strong currents. No seasonal change in adult habitat use was apparent.

**Population Status**: Sampling efforts in [[O'Donnell Creek]], [[Babocomari River]], and [[Santa Cruz River]] produced few or no fish, suggesting very low population numbers or unsuitable habitat. [[O'Donnell Creek]] had few pools with overhead cover and little suitable habitat. The [[Babocomari River]] yielded only introduced species ([[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]]) with no suckers captured, suggesting predation pressure and unsuitable habitat (extremely slow flow, silt/mud substrate). The [[Santa Cruz River]] lacked good pool habitat and may have only ephemeral surface water. Both [[Bass Canyon Creek]] and upper [[Sonoita Creek]] contained numerous suckers and considerable suitable habitat, representing viable populations. However, neither stream has direct or continuous flow to downstream rivers, potentially isolating breeding groups and eliminating genetic exchange opportunities.

**Conservation Implications**: Habitat availability is paramount for the species' future survival. Man-made changes (damming, impoundments) alter stream characteristics rapidly, changing flow rates, causing fine particulate settling, and altering temperature regimes. These rapid changes preclude adaptation. Impoundments on [[Babocomari River]] and [[Sonoita Creek]] may have eliminated suitable habitat below dams. Protective measures may be required for long-term preservation of this species and its habitat. The study emphasizes the need to preserve and enhance existing habitat to ensure future survival of [[Desert Sucker]]s, particularly maintaining pools with overhead cover and adequate water flow.
