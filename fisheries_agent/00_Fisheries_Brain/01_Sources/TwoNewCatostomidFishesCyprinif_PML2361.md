# Two New Catostomid Fishes from the Northern Sierra Madre Occidental

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Siebert and Minckley (1986, *American Museum Novitates* no. 2849) describe two new species of *Catostomus* (sensu stricto) from northwestern Mexico: [[Catostomus Leopoldi]] (Bavispe sucker) and [[Catostomus Cahita]] (Cahita sucker). Both have unusually low [[Pharyngeal Teeth]] counts for the genus, which the authors interpret as a possible sign of close relationship between the two taxa. They are hypothesized to be relicts or derivatives of an old regional freshwater fauna that extends from central Mexico to the northwestern United States. The paper also records a probable natural hybrid between one of the new species and [[Pantosteus Plebeius]].

## Background & Description

Mexican catostomids were poorly known before this work. Six species were previously recognized from the region, and the two new taxa had been noted in the northern [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] since 1959 but had not been collected in adequate numbers or condition for description. Specimens came from ichthyological surveys of the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] (Hendrickson et al., 1981) and are deposited at AMNH, ASU, and UMMZ. Collections were made under Mexican permit No. 3618.

Morphological measurements follow Hubbs and Lagler (1970), with added measures of fin insertion widths and lip dimensions. Osteological comparisons used [[Weberian Apparatus]] and jaw structures, following Eastman (1977) for pharyngeal terminology.

**Catostomus leopoldi** (Bavispe sucker)
- Holotype: UMMZ 213391, a gravid female, 151.0 mm SL, from Rio Gavilán (locally Arroyo Moctezuma), Chihuahua, collected June 21, 1978, at 2180 m.
- Lateral line scales 78 to 88; 9 or 10 dorsal fin rays.
- Lips large and elliptical, with the lower lip completely cleft. Five or more rows of papillae cross the midline of the upper lip.
- Pharyngeal teeth 34 to 39 per arch (mean 36.3, SD 2.5, n = 3).
- Males show pronounced tubercles and have more expansive dorsal, pelvic, and anal fins.

**Catostomus cahita** (Cahita sucker)
- Holotype: UMMZ 213393, a female, 148.1 mm SL, from Arroyo Ahumado, a tributary of Rio Tomóchic, Chihuahua, collected June 16, 1978, at about 2000 m.
- Lateral line scales 78 to 92; 9 or 10 dorsal fin rays (rarely 11).
- Lips small; the lower lobes are cleft but closely appressed at the midline.
- Pharyngeal teeth 29 to 32 per arch in the osteological sample (mean 30.3, SD 1.5, n = 3); the original diagnosis gives a range of more than 29 but fewer than 35.
- Lip length and width are 27% and 36% of head length, respectively.

Paratype series are small but come from widely separated localities. For *C. leopoldi*, UMMZ 213392 includes 12 females and 8 males (80.0–137.5 mm SL). For *C. cahita*, UMMZ 213394 includes 4 females and 5 males (101.1–140.5 mm SL). The authors found no significant differences in scale or fin-ray counts among localities for *C. leopoldi*. For *C. cahita*, Rio Yaqui material had consistently higher counts than the two Rio Mayo populations, and the authors suggest further study may show these represent distinct populations.

**Etymology:** *leopoldi* honors Aldo Leopold, whose "Song of the Gavilán" (1940) was based partly on experiences along the type stream. The name *cahita* refers to the Cahitan subfamily of Uto-Aztec languages spoken by the region's indigenous peoples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat of *C. leopoldi***
- Restricted to headwater streams of the Rio de Bavispe, the major northeastern tributary of the Rio Yaqui.
- Habitats range from pools in high-gradient streams in [[Madrean Montane Forest]] to lower-gradient creeks in Montane Meadow Grassland, mostly above 2000 m.
- Streams are about 1 to 3 m wide. Pools where the sucker concentrated reached 2 m deep, though most were under 1 m.
- Bottoms were stony, with cobble and gravel on riffles and fine gravel, sand, silt, and organic material in pools. Fish were associated with boulders or undercut banks over clean gravel or sand.

**Habitat of *C. cahita***
- Inhabits streams of variable size and configuration, most abundant in small streams at high and intermediate elevations.
- Ranges from Madrean Montane Forest to lower [[Sinaloan Thornscrub]] (Brown, 1982).
- Prefers pools and avoids turbulence, occasionally taken from deeper runs.

**Associated fauna (Hendrickson et al., 1981; Table 4)**
- *C. leopoldi* is syntopic with [[Catostomus Bernardini]] and *Pantosteus plebeius* in parts of its range. It occurred with *C. bernardini* in Rio Negro and was the only catostomid in Arroyo de la Norteña and San Antonio.
- *C. cahita* is broadly syntopic with *C. bernardini*, especially in larger systems.
- Native cyprinids co-occurred with both species. Yaqui trout (*Salmo* sp.) was present at two localities. Introduced rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) and bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*) each occurred as a few individuals at separate sites with *C. leopoldi*.
- *C. cahita* shares high-elevation streams with [[Yaqui Trout]] and lower streams with an undescribed pupfish (*Cyprinodon* sp.) in the Rio Papigochic.

**Distribution**
- *C. leopoldi* is confined to the Rio de Bavispe headwaters.
- *C. cahita* is widely distributed in the southern and southeastern parts of the Rio Yaqui, mainly the Rio Papigochic drainage, and in the upper Rio Mayo basin.

**Biogeographic context**
- The [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] was built by volcanism between 30 and 22 million years ago (mya) in the north and 10 to 8 mya in the south. Surrounding [[Basin And Range Topography]] developed between 27 and 7 mya, so montane habitats have been available to fishes for a long time.
- Minckley et al. (1986) documented a southwestern North American ichthyofauna continuous from Mexico into California. They proposed that northward transport on displaced coastal terranes, dispersal across basin-and-range areas, or both could explain extensive linear distributions of ancestral catostomids. This pattern would have been set by the early or middle Miocene, and tectonic displacement along the [[San Andreas Transform]] is consistent with the timing.
- The authors note morphological resemblances to *C. fumeiventris* (Owens Valley) and the *C. microps* group of northern California and Nevada.
- Similar disjunct patterns appear in *Gila* subgenus *Tampichthys* and in cyprinodontoids such as goodeids and pupfishes.

## Key Findings

1. **Two new species are described.** *Catostomus leopoldi* and *C. cahita* are distinguished from each other and from other congeners by lip size, mouth orientation, pharyngeal tooth counts, and osteological features.
2. **Low pharyngeal tooth counts.** Both species have far fewer teeth per arch than most *Catostomus* (more than 45 in most other species). The authors suggest this may indicate close relationship between the two taxa and possibly with *Pantosteus*, which also has fewer than 40 teeth.
3. **Lip and mouth differences.** *C. leopoldi* has large lips and a slightly oblique mouth. *C. cahita* has small lips and a horizontal mouth. These differences reliably separate the two species.
4. **Osteological differences.** The mandible's ascending process rises more abruptly in *C. cahita*, and its maxilla is more robust. The tripus is subtriangular in *C. leopoldi* and inflated in *C. cahita*.
5. **Natural hybridization.** A skeletonized specimen from Arroyo Moctezuma (AMNH 56171SD) is likely a hybrid of *C. leopoldi* and [[Pantosteus Plebeius]], with an intermediate frontoparietal fontanelle. The authors note that this adds to evidence for hybridization in the genus *Pantosteus*.
6. **Restricted versus widespread range.** *C. leopoldi* is confined to headwater streams of the Rio de Bavispe. *C. cahita* is widely distributed across the southern and southeastern Rio Yaqui and the upper Rio Mayo.
7. **Possible relict status.** The authors propose that both species, along with likely *C. wigginsi* of the Rio Sonora, are relicts or derivatives of an old regional fauna. Their distributions are linked to Miocene tectonic history of the region.
8. **Limitations.** The authors acknowledge small sample sizes for tooth counts (n = 3 per species) and that subadult status could affect counts, although they found no evidence that tooth number changes after sexual maturity. Further study of Rio Yaqui and Rio Mayo *C. cahita* populations is recommended.
