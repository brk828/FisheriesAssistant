# Conservation Genetics of Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary

This paper, a review by [[Gary K. Meffe]] and [[Robert C. Vrijenhoek]] published in *Conservation Biology* (vol. 2, no. 2, June 1988), argues that the genetic structure of North American desert fishes must be considered in conservation and management. The authors propose two zoogeographic models of population structure, the [[Death Valley Model]] of isolated populations and the [[Stream Hierarchy Model]] of connected river systems, and use them to guide management of genetic diversity, habitat restoration and restocking. Using the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] as a case study, they recommend specific actions and close with a set of open research priorities.

## Background & Description

Fishes of the Sonoran, Mohave, Great Basin and Chihuahuan deserts are declining at an alarming rate; at least 20 taxa were reported lost in the preceding decades. The authors note that desert aquatic systems are geographically isolated, often small, and have produced high levels of endemism and divergence, which makes these fishes especially vulnerable to genetic consequences of habitat fragmentation, impoundment, groundwater pumping and introduced species.

The paper frames the genetic issues around:
- **Effective population size (Ne)** and **migration (gene flow, Nm)**, which together determine whether colonies diverge or remain similar.
- **Genetic diversity partitioning** into diversity within colonies (Hc) and differences among colonies (Dst) following [[Nei Diversity Partitioning]].
- Three open questions: relationships between heterozygosity and fitness, the value of mixing stocks, and the relative roles of genetic adaptation versus developmental plasticity in phenotypic differentiation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Death Valley Model.** Isolated spring-heads and small streams in intermontane basins were formed when pluvial lakes receded. Gene flow is currently zero, so divergence is accelerated by small colony size and local selection. Turner's electrophoretic study of pupfish found little variation within populations and considerable divergence among them. Conservation in this model focuses on maintaining large, isolated populations, as illustrated by the near-extinction of the [[Owens Pupfish]] in 1969 (about 800 fish were rescued and held in captivity) and by the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], whose habitat was protected by a Supreme Court decision.

**Salton Sea variant.** In *Cyprinodon macularius* around the Salton Sea, about 70% of genetic variance lay within lakeshore colonies and about 30% among colonies. The authors attribute this departure from the model to repeated drying and reflooding of the lake basin, which allowed reconnection. The isolated Quitobaquito population was recently described as the subspecies *C. m. eremus*.

**Stream Hierarchy Model.** Species in dendritic river systems show hierarchical gene flow that depends on connectivity and climate (wet years connect habitats, dry years isolate them). This model is typified by species in the Gila River drainage. Habitat alteration disrupts it by either introducing gene flow between isolated populations or fragmenting connected ones.

**Sonoran Topminnow.** Once abundant in the Gila River basin, the species declined from 15 natural habitats in the United States in 1983 to 12 natural localities in Arizona, and was listed as federally endangered in 1967. Decline is attributed to habitat destruction and replacement by introduced [[Gambusia affinis]]; the fish remains abundant in Mexico.

## Key Findings

- **Genetic partitioning in pupfish (Turner, 1983):** most variation was among, not within, remnant populations; most of 30 loci examined were homozygous within populations.
- **Echelle et al. (1987), Chihuahuan pupfish:** *C. pecosensis* had 7.7% of total genetic diversity among populations along a 200 km stretch of the Pecos River; differences among local colonies accounted for 10.8% in *C. elegans*, 1.4% in *C. bovinus* and 19.0% in *C. tularosa*.
- **Effective migration rate:** for *C. tularosa*, Nm was approximately 1, roughly one migrant per generation. Estimates for *C. pecosensis* and *C. elegans* were three and two individuals per generation.
- **Rule of thumb:** an exchange of one individual per generation among populations within a river system would maintain most allelic diversity without swamping local adaptation (after Allendorf 1983).
- **Sonoran topminnow genetic structure (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985):** 21 populations (5 Arizona, 15 Mexico, plus one reference) grouped into three genetic units by Distance-Wagner clustering. Partitioning gave 52.8% of diversity between groups, 21.3% within colonies, 25.5% among colonies within rivers, and 0.4% among rivers within subdivisions.
- **Hatchery stock recommendation:** the initial stock from Monkey Spring (no detectable allelic diversity across 25 loci examined, low fecundity) was to be replaced with fish from Sharp Spring (H = 3.7%, higher fecundity and growth). This was followed, and by 1986 Sharp Spring fish supplied all introductions within the Group I region. At least 100 field sites had been stocked through 1985.
- **Management recommendations:** manage each genetic group separately; avoid mixing fish from different groups or species (several *Cyprinodon* species can hybridize); restore gene flow where natural isolation has been disrupted; and separate true genetic differentiation from plastic environmental responses through critical experiments.
- **Research priorities:** test heterozygosity–fitness relationships; test controlled outcrossing (e.g., Cienega Creek × Monkey Spring crosses) for hybrid vigor; and determine the roles of genetic adaptation and developmental plasticity in phenotypic differentiation.
- **Guiding conclusion:** desert fishes are cost-effective model organisms for conservation genetics because they are easily manipulated and maintained in hatcheries or aquaria. The authors suggest the Sonoran topminnow as a focal species for recovery, with the broader goal of restoring the species to something like its historical condition.
