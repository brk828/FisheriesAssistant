# Mammals and Birds of the Lower Colorado Valley

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mammalogy #ornithology

*Note: this is a historical systematic and faunal account (Grinnell, 1914), not an ichthyological study. Fish are mentioned only incidentally. Tags are adjusted to the actual taxa covered.*

## Executive Summary

This account, based on a three-month expedition (February 14 to May 15, 1910) along the Colorado River from [[Needles, California]] to [[Yuma, Arizona]], documents the mammals and birds of the lower river valley. The collectors obtained 1,374 mammals, 448 reptiles and 22 sets of birds' eggs and nests, with a bird collection supporting the systematic accounts. Grinnell argues that the region lies within a single life-zone (Lower Sonoran), a single faunal area ([[Colorado Desert]]), but contains many distinct [[Associational Areas]], and that the river acts as a barrier to certain desert rodents while serving as a corridor for riparian species. The study introduces a three-part framework of zonal, faunal and associational control on distribution, and proposes that barriers drive speciation.

## Background & Description

The paper was published by the [[University of California Museum of Vertebrate Zoology]] in *University of California Publications in Zoology* (vol. 12, no. 4, pp. 51–294, 1914) by [[Joseph Grinnell]]. The party consisted of Grinnell, Frank Stephens, Joseph Dixon and Joseph Jones, working from boats with trap lines and shotgun collecting. The author states the work was intended to establish the systematic status of the mammals and birds before generalizing about geographic distribution, and that reptiles and amphibians were not yet worked up.

The lower river runs about 150 miles in a straight line but roughly 285 miles by river between Needles (elevation 460 ft) and Yuma (125 ft), an average fall of only a little over one foot per mile. The river carries very heavy sediment (0.1 to 0.2 percent by weight), flows from about 4,000 to 100,000 cubic feet per second, and has a high-water period roughly May 15 to July 1. Its channel alternates between narrow rock-walled gorges (such as the "box cañon" at The Needles) and broad, meandering floodplains (the "Great Valley of the Colorado") that shift continually.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Zonal, faunal and associational framework.** Grinnell defines three orders of distributional control: *zones* (controlled mainly by temperature), *faunas* (controlled by atmospheric humidity) and *associations* (controlled by local physical and biotic conditions such as soil and food supply). The whole region is placed in the Lower Sonoran zone and the Colorado Desert fauna, but has many associations.

**Riparian associations** identified include:
- Willow-cottonwood (the most important biotically; the river's main riparian forest)
- Tule (palustrine, small and scattered)
- Arrowweed (dense stands of tall stems)
- Quail-brush (dense thorny cover)
- Mesquite (the most important for animal life, with [[Mistletoe]] berries as food)
- Saltbush (on the second bottom, a terrace above flood level)

**Desert associations** include:
- Creosote (the most widespread desert shrub, with the least bird life)
- Sandy creosote, rocky creosote
- Catclaw / wash association (the most birdlife among desert types; ironwood, palo verde)
- Saguaro association (an incursion from Arizona into California)
- Encelia (rocky hill) association

**Laguna Dam** (completed 1909) raised water levels, silted the valley for about ten miles upstream, and drowned cottonwoods and mesquite; below the dam the channel deepened. This illustrates how human works reshape associations.

**Aquatic fauna** was described as very poor: the fish fauna is sparse in both species and individuals, with [[Colorado River Fishes]] such as the Colorado salmon (*Ptychocheilus*, a large minnow) and carp and catfish most common in backwater sloughs. Herons depended heavily on fish stranded in drying overflow ponds. Mammal and bird diversity was therefore limited by the poor aquatic food base.

## Key Findings

- **Expedition effort:** 1,374 mammals, 448 reptiles, and 22 sets of birds' eggs/nests collected, with the bird and mammal accounts forming the core.
- **Barrier effect:** Only desert rodents with restricted associations were stopped at the river. The Colorado River acts as an absolute barrier to [[Ammospermophilus harrisi]] and [[Ammospermophilus leucurus]], which occupy opposite banks within about 850 feet of each other, and to [[Perognathus]] species, while [[Beaver]], bats, and many birds cross freely.
- **Speciation hypothesis:** The river is a long-standing isolator; twelve riparian species and subspecies are peculiar to the Colorado fauna. Grinnell concludes that barriers are a prime factor in speciation.
- **Riparian birds of the Colorado:** Several species (e.g., Yellow-billed Cuckoo group, [[Vermilion Flycatcher]], [[Lucy's Warbler]], [[Abert's Towhee]], [[Gila Woodpecker]]) are documented with nesting data and distribution notes.
- **Gilded flicker variation:** Some Colorado Valley [[Gilded Flicker]] specimens show red-shafted coloration, which Grinnell interprets as a possible sporadic or incipient trait rather than hybridization.
- **Checklists:** The bird checklist totals 150 species (numbered entries), and the mammal checklist totals 53 species/subspecies, most of which are summarized in the associational lists.
- **Hunting/trapping pressures:** Beaver and deer were depleted by trapping and settlement; bighorn sheep persisted in rugged hills; mesquite was heavily cut for fuel.
- **Methodological note:** Grinnell emphasizes that associational analysis is needed because many species occupy restricted microhabitats, and that trap samples must be interpreted with caution, since nocturnal mammals roam beyond their home ranges.
