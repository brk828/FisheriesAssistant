## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993–1994 administrative record from the Bureau of Reclamation's Lower Colorado Regional Office concerning the **Native Fish Work Group (NFWG)**, a multi-agency body formed in 1990 to conserve the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) population of **[[Lake Mohave]]**. The core scientific content is not morphological but demographic and conservation-management data: the natural adult population of razorback suckers in Lake Mohave was in severe, accelerating decline (60,000 in 1988 → 47,000 in 1991 → 25,000 in 1993), motivating an intensive larval-collection, backwater-rearing, and stocking program. The record includes a detailed 1993 rearing summary showing 1,385 larval/juvenile razorback suckers processed through four ephemeral backwater nursery sites, with releases to both Lake Mohave proper and a managed rearing site called **[[Davis Cove]]**.

## Background & Description

The source contains no direct morphometric or anatomical description of *Xyrauchen texanus*. Indirect size data are provided via growth performance in rearing habitats:

- Juvenile razorback suckers reared in **Nevada-Larvae (NL) Backwater** starting from larvae in August reached **>200 mm** total length by the time of recapture in Davis Cove approximately 3+ months later, indicating rapid early growth rates achievable in productive ephemeral backwater habitats.
- Fish are tracked across life stages referenced in the agenda: larvae (wild-collected), juveniles (backwater-reared), and adults (wild spawning population, subject to sonic tagging and "Roundup" recapture efforts).
- No scale counts, fin ray formulas, coloration, or diagnostic taxonomic characters are given; this document is programmatic/management-focused rather than descriptive.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: [[Lake Mohave]], a mainstem Colorado River reservoir spanning the Arizona–Nevada border, supplemented by **ephemeral backwater habitats** that form and dry seasonally along the lake perimeter.
- **Nursery habitat strategy**: Backwaters cut off from the main lake (e.g., **Yuma Cove**, **Willow Cove**, **Arizona Juvenile Backwater**, **Nevada-Larvae Backwater**) serve as protected, food-rich larval/juvenile rearing environments, shielded from predation and turbulence found in the open lake. These are explicitly ephemeral — they dry up annually — yet are valued for their productivity during the period they hold water.
- **Spawning ecology**: Adult razorback suckers spawn in the lake, with a springtime "**Razorback Roundup**" used to capture spawning adults and confirm recruitment via recapture of marked/known year-class fish (e.g., 1992 year-class fish recaptured in March 1993).
- **Threats/limiting factors**: Implied competition/predation pressure from non-native species (motivating the Davis Cove barrier net/land barrier project to exclude non-native fish), habitat loss from lake-level fluctuation affecting backwater berm integrity, and the fundamental problem of insufficient natural recruitment of wild larvae to adulthood — hence the population "crash" trajectory.
- **Associated management habitats**: Additional razorback populations and habitats referenced include **[[Lake Mead]]**, **[[Lake Havasu]]**, **[[Floyd Lamb Park]]**, and the **Boulder City Golf Course Ponds**, indicating a broader metapopulation management context across the Lower Colorado River system.
- **Ex-situ/refuge component**: **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]** and **Willow Beach** are used as holding facilities for backup larvae/broodstock, representing a genetic reserve/insurance population strategy.

## Key Findings

- **Population decline trajectory**: Adult razorback sucker population in Lake Mohave declined from an estimated **60,000 (1988)** → **47,000 (1991)** → **25,000 (1993)**, a decline described as tracking toward a projected population "crash" by the turn of the century — the original impetus for forming the NFWG in 1990.
- **Evidence of program efficacy**: Four fish from the 1992 hatchery/rearing year-class were recaptured during the March 1993 Razorback Roundup in association with wild spawning adults, taken as preliminary (if "guarded") evidence that stocked fish are surviving to reproductive maturity and that rearing/release techniques are viable.
- **1993 rearing output**: A total of **1,385 juvenile razorback suckers** were processed across four ephemeral backwater sites (Yuma Cove, Willow Cove, Arizona Juvenile, Nevada-Larvae) between August 18 and September 23, 1993:
  - **487 fish released directly to Lake Mohave**
  - **833 fish released to Davis Cove**
  - **65 mortalities** (some sacrificed for genetic studies at Arizona State University, ASU)
- Over 500 of these reared juveniles were released to the open lake during the broader 1993 season, per the narrative summary.
- **Habitat expansion**: Three ephemeral backwaters were successfully used in 1993, and an aerial (helicopter) survey identified roughly ten additional water-filled, lake-disconnected areas; **four new backwater rearing areas were slated to go into production in 1994**, contingent on capturing sufficient wild-spawned larvae to stock them.
- **Genetic conservation component**: A companion **mtDNA diversity study** is referenced, informing the program's explicit goal of preserving genetic diversity while replacing the declining wild Lake Mohave population.
- **Infrastructure/management actions in progress at [[Davis Cove]]**: installation and monitoring of a barrier net, a permanent land barrier (pending NEPA/ESA/Section 404 permitting and engineering design), scheduled non-native fish harvest (spring–summer electroshocking/netting), and fall fish salvage prior to piscicide (poisoning) treatment — indicating Davis Cove was being developed as a predator-free, non-native-fish-free rearing refuge.
- **Monitoring framework**: Planned adult monitoring via **sonic tagging/tracking**, and backwater monitoring encompassing fish, aquatic weeds, plankton, and water chemistry — reflecting an ecosystem-level (not just single-species) approach to backwater nursery management.
- **Multi-site coordination**: The 1994 agenda explicitly extends monitoring/management efforts beyond Lake Mohave to Lake Mead, Lake Havasu, and localized populations (Floyd Lamb Park, Boulder City Golf Course Ponds), suggesting a regional metapopulation strategy for *Xyrauchen texanus* recovery under the [[Endangered Species Act]] framework (implied by ESA compliance references).
