# Variable Fish Communities and the Index of Biotic Integrity in a Western Great Plains River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study modified the [[Index of Biotic Integrity]] (IBI) for the plains portion of the [[Arkansas River]] basin in the Southwestern Tablelands ecoregion of southeastern Colorado and applied it to 12 sites on the [[Purgatoire River]] adjacent to the [[Pinon Canyon Maneuvers Site]], where [[U.S. Army]] mechanized infantry training began in July 1985. Only nine of the original IBI metrics were suitable because the regional fish fauna is depauperate and dominated by tolerant, trophic-generalist species. IBI scores rose at most sites between fall 1983 (pre-training) and fall 1987 (two years after training began). The increase was driven mainly by a 50% decline in adult [[Red Shiner]] abundance, not by any measurable environmental degradation. The authors conclude that natural variation in Great Plains fish communities is poorly understood, so the IBI cannot yet be reliably applied in such systems without substantial further study.

## Background & Description

- **Index of Biotic Integrity (IBI):** An ecologically based index (Karr 1981) originally developed for mesic midwestern U.S. streams with rich fish faunas. Its 12 original metrics fall into three categories: species richness and composition, trophic composition, and fish abundance and condition. Sites score 12 to 60 and are assigned to one of six integrity classes.
- **Study area:** The [[Pinon Canyon Maneuvers Site]] covers 988 km² near Trinidad, Las Animas County, Colorado. Climate is arid (mean precipitation 30 cm/year). The Purgatoire River is a seventh-order tributary of the Arkansas River, flowing through a 60–150 m deep canyon. Flow is perennial but punctuated by unpredictable flash floods from May to October. Habitat consists of long, deep pools over bedrock and boulders, separated by short cobble riffles, with very little large woody debris.
- **Army training:** Mechanized infantry training (M-60 tanks, M-113 armored personnel carriers) has occurred on the uplands since July 1985, concentrated in the [[Taylor Arroyo]] and [[Lockwood Canyon]] watersheds. Training did not occur in canyons or riparian zones.
- **Regional fauna:** The lower Arkansas basin in Colorado supports 26 lotic species that reproduce in streams, 24 native (one considered extirpated) and two introduced (common carp and western mosquitofish). Cyprinids make up 50% of the fauna (13 species). Only two species were classed as intolerant: southern redbelly dace and Arkansas darter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Nine sites were immediately downstream of tributary confluences and three were midway between them. All 12 sites were sampled in August–September 1983 (pre-training) and September–October 1987. Three sites were resampled in 1988 and 1989. Gear included 3–10 m seines, a gill net, and, in later years, kick-seining of riffles. Trap nets and minnow traps were excluded from IBI calculations. Only fish 30 mm TL and larger were used in IBI scores, because the 1983 and later seines retained different size classes.

**Environmental stability.** Physical habitat, temperature, and water chemistry showed no obvious change between 1983 and 1987–1989. Maximum water temperatures stayed below tolerance limits of representative Great Plains fishes. Of 36 chemical constituents monitored by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], only four heavy metals (copper, lead, manganese, zinc) exceeded tolerance levels, and these showed no trend among years. Suspended sediment was high (median 116 and 212 mg/L at two gages), limiting primary production.

**Flow.** The three years before September 1983 had more and higher floods (148–1,201 m³/s) than the four years before September 1987 (76–131 m³/s). Flood timing also did not explain the decline. The authors conclude that recruitment differences, which cannot be predicted from maximum discharge alone, are the more likely cause.

**Biological indicators of integrity.** No fish species were added or lost. Megalopteran larvae of *Corydalus cognatus*, an obligate riffle dweller requiring unsilted habitat, remained abundant. Large, long-lived fishes persisted in pools and runs.

**Climate and flow regime context.** Flow regulation from a reservoir built at [[Trinidad Reservoir]] in 1975 and upstream irrigation diversions had relatively little effect on base flow.

## Key Findings

1. **IBI increased after training began.** The IBI rose at most sites: 10 of 12 sites scored higher in fall 1987 than fall 1983. Mean score was 44 (range 39–52) in 1987 versus 37 (range 27–45) in 1983. Six sites rose across at least two class boundaries; three rose one class boundary; the Taylor site declined one class boundary.

2. **Metric reduction was necessary.** Only nine metrics (six from the original IBI, three from [[Schrader 1989]]) were suitable. Because the maximum possible total was 45 rather than 60, scores were multiplied by 1.33. Scores from 1983, when disease was not recorded, were multiplied by 1.5 after using eight metrics.

3. **Red shiner decline drove the change.** Red shiners fell 50% (from 1,031 to 516 individuals) at the nine sites with 1983 data. They were the most abundant species at eight of nine sites in 1983 but at only two sites in 1987. Tolerant species exceeded 50% of individuals at 10 of 12 sites in 1983 but at only one site in 1987. Increases in three metrics (tolerant species, omnivores, invertivores/piscivores) accounted for 70 points, or 80% of the total IBI increase.

4. **Sampling variation was unlikely to explain the change.** Riffle seining was added in 1987–1989, but red shiners were the third most abundant riffle species, so they should have been captured had they been present. Large 1983 numbers were unlikely to be missed.

5. **Natural variation is the likely cause.** Many other species did not decline (5 of 10 increased, 2 stayed the same, 3 declined by only 13, 8, and 2 individuals). Red shiner recruitment likely varies with flow and temperature in complex ways, so the authors suggest natural variation is more plausible than training effects.

6. **Long-lived fishes persisted.** Large individuals of [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[White Sucker]], and [[Green Sunfish]] were captured in both periods. The largest channel catfish were 400–450 mm TL, estimated at age 5 or older.

7. **Several metrics had limited utility.** The number of centrarchid species and the proportion of introduced species were poorly suited: green sunfish occurred at 3 of 12 sites in 1983 and 7 of 12 in 1987–1989, and introduced species were rare (under 10% of 137 lotic sites in the lower basin). Habitat-guild metrics also failed because most species are habitat generalists.

8. **Disease incidence was higher than expected.** Disease incidence was 2% or less in only 1 of 18 samples in 1987–1989, 2–5% in 15 of 18, and above 5% in 2 of 18. Average incidence was 3% (SE 0.13%), possibly higher than midwestern background levels.

9. **Perturbation responses may be reversed.** The authors argue that moderate sediment addition or flow fluctuations may cause little change, while flow-stabilizing or clarity-increasing activities, such as a dam, could cause profound change through invasion by introduced species.

10. **Broader conclusion.** Understanding of natural variation, structure, and function of Great Plains fish communities must increase substantially before fully appropriate biotic integrity measures can be defined. Alternative approaches, such as metrics based on benthic macroinvertebrates, may be needed in similarly depauperate arid systems.
