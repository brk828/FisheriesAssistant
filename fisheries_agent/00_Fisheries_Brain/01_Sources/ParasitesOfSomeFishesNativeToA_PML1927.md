# Parasites of Some Fishes Native to Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #parasitology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 parasitological survey, by [[Mpoame]] and [[Rinne]] and published in *The Southwestern Naturalist* (vol. 28, no. 4), documents the parasite fauna of 12 native fish species from Arizona and New Mexico. The authors recorded 22 parasitic species representing 18 genera, drawn from Platyhelminthes, Acanthocephala, Nematoda, Myxozoa and Protozoa. Most species occurred at few localities, and infection levels were generally low. The study aimed to characterize the parasite fauna, analyze host-parasite relationships (host specificity, endemicity, pathogenicity), and assess the role of exotic fishes as parasite sources for native hosts. Several recorded forms may represent undescribed species.

## Background & Description

Parasite reports for Arizona fishes were described as infrequent before this work, with earlier studies covering Catostomidae of the Gila River system, the Salt River basin and other drainages. The authors considered a parasite survey necessary to document the native fauna and to understand whether parasites posed a special pathogenic threat to species of conservation concern.

The 12 host species included cyprinids (chubs, dace, minnows), catostomids (suckers), a poeciliid ([[Gila Topminnow]]) and two salmonids: [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) and [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*). Several hosts were federally listed or rare, including [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** Fish were collected from 12 localities plus museum material from the Santa Cruz drainage (ASU 8605). Key sites included:

- [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham County), the most intensively sampled locality, with fish collected from February 1980 to July 1981.
- [[Oak Creek]] (Yavapai County, at Page Springs), and [[Sycamore Creek]] (Yavapai County).
- [[Verde River]] (Perkinsville, Yavapai County).
- [[Salt River]] (Salt River Canyon, Gila County).
- [[Hurricane Creek]] (Fort Apache Indian Reservation).
- [[White River]] (East Fork, Fort Apache Indian Reservation).
- [[Mojave Lake]] (Mojave County) and Willow Beach, U.S. National Fish Hatchery (Mojave County).
- [[McKnight Creek]] (Gila National Forest, Grant County, New Mexico).

**Sampling.** Fish were collected by seining (seines about 5 m long, 6-mm mesh), electrofishing, and gill nets used only in some waters. Specimens were iced in the field, and organs were examined for parasites after freezing or fixation in 10% formalin or alcohol-formalin-acetic acid (AFA). Helminths were stained (iodine, Harris's hematoxylin) and mounted for identification.

**Host-parasite and environmental factors.** The authors noted that the poor helminth fauna of *Xyrauchen texanus* likely reflected its herbivorous/planktivorous diet, while the richer intestinal helminth fauna of *Gila robusta* and *Catostomus insignis* may be linked to their diet of intermediate hosts. Infection levels for several parasites were not related to host sex, although a significant positive correlation between fish size and infection level was found for some hosts, consistent with larger fish having longer exposure to intermediate hosts. Sampling sites in Oak Creek and the Salt River contained about nine introduced fish species, and water temperatures in some reaches were unusually high in June 1981, which the authors linked to parasite distribution.

## Key Findings

**Parasite fauna overview**

- 22 parasitic species were identified across 18 genera.
- Groups included Platyhelminthes (digeneans, cestodes, metacercariae and plerocercoids), Acanthocephala (*Neoechinorhynchus* sp.), Nematoda (including *Rhabdochona decaturensis*, *Dacnitoides* sp.), Myxozoa (*Myxobolus* spp.) and Protozoa.
- Notable taxa included [[Ornithodiplostomum ptychocheilus]] (metacercariae in body cavity and mesenteries of several hosts), [[Ligula intestinalis]] (plerocercoids in body cavity, including *Gila robusta* and *Tiaroga cobitis*), *Clinostomum* sp. (larvae in skin, musculature, gills and mouth cavity of several hosts), and *Rhabdochona decaturensis* (intestinal in multiple species).
- Several parasites may represent undescribed species, including *Tetraonchus* sp., *Proteocephalus* sp., *Neoechinorhynchus* sp., *Dacnitoides* sp., and an ophiotaeniid cestode.

**Host specificity and distribution**

- Only 5 of the 22 parasitic species were found at a single locality. Examples include *Ornithodiplostomum ptychocheilus* in Aravaipa Creek and Verde River, *Isoglaridanis bulbocinus* in Mojave Lake, *Dacnitoides* sp. in Mojave Lake, and *Ligula intestinalis* in Hurricane Creek.
- High host specificity was associated with high endemicity; some species with restricted distribution (e.g., *Ophiotaenia* sp., *Proteocephalus* sp.) appear limited to a few Arizona hosts.
- Some apparent host specificity may reflect limited sampling, particularly for rare hosts.

**Pathogenicity and conservation**

- Infection levels for most parasites were low. There was no indication that high infection levels threatened the survival of native fish populations in the study.
- Nematodes, cestodes and myxosporidians were considered capable of affecting host growth and organ integrity, but the authors found no evidence that parasites were responsible for population declines or extinction threats.
- A copepod, *Lernaea cyprinacea*, which is normally associated with hatcheries, was recorded in some wild localities, possibly following high water temperatures. Its occurrence was considered an introduced-fish-related concern.
- Parasites from native fish hosts were considered potentially endangered alongside their hosts, given the restricted ranges of some parasite species.

**Exotic fishes**

- Introduced fishes in Oak Creek and the Salt River did not show a distinctive parasite pattern in native hosts (*Gila robusta*, *Pantosteus clarki*), possibly due to small sample sizes.
- The authors recommended further study of exotic-host transmission, as introduced fishes may act as reservoirs for parasites affecting natives.

**Conclusions**

- The Arizona native fish parasite fauna is diverse but dominated by rare or locality-specific species.
- Parasites were not identified as a primary threat to native fishes, although pathogenicity could increase under altered water temperatures or with increased introductions of exotic fishes.
- The study provides baseline data for monitoring parasite fauna of threatened native species.
