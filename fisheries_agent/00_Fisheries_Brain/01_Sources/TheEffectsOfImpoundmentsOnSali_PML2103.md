# The Effects of Impoundments on Salinity in the Colorado River

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #water-chemistry #salinity #reservoirs

## Executive Summary

This symposium preprint by Marsh and Paulson (University of Nevada, Las Vegas Limnological Research Center, 1981) examines how the large impoundments [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]] alter the total dissolved solids (TDS) and major ion chemistry of the [[Colorado River]]. Using [[U.S. Geological Survey]] flow and water-quality records for 1970–79 and earlier periods, the authors show that the reservoirs substantially change ion concentrations, so that simple flow-versus-TDS models no longer predict river salinity. Their central conclusions are that gypsum dissolution is the main salt source, calcite precipitation is the main salt sink, and that selective control of specific ions is likely more cost-effective than indiscriminate control of TDS point sources. The document does not address fish or other aquatic fauna.

## Background & Description

Salinity in western rivers is one of the most serious water-quality problems in the United States. On the Colorado River, TDS has risen from an estimated pristine level of about 180 mg/L (value as read from the scan) to present-day levels of about 825 mg/L at [[Imperial Dam]]. Causes include flow depletion from decreased runoff, increased evaporation and diversions, and high salt loading from natural and human-made sources.

Projected development could deplete flows by an additional 2 million acre-feet (2.5 × 10⁹ m³/yr). Salinity models predict this would raise TDS at Imperial Dam to 1150 mg/L, with major economic consequences for municipal and agricultural users. Salinity control programs were therefore implemented to hold TDS at or below 1972 levels.

Historical records, however, show that TDS has not risen as fast as models predicted. Concentrations in [[Grand Canyon]] and below [[Hoover Dam]] changed little after monitoring began, and TDS in the Lower Colorado River Basin has declined since 1972. The authors suggest this may be transient (changes in flow patterns, salt routing, or inundation of saline sources after the 1950s impoundments) or may reflect more permanent changes in reservoir chemistry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Impoundment chemistry.** Reservoirs are generally assumed to increase salinity because evaporation concentrates salts. Evaporation from Lake Mead reached as high as 900,000 acre-feet/yr in early impoundment, and Lake Powell's evaporation is estimated at about 500,000 acre-feet/yr. Evaporation raised TDS below Hoover Dam slightly and by about 16 mg/L below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The authors conclude that evaporation is small compared with salt dissolution inside the reservoirs.

**Regional geology.** Calcium, sulfate and carbonate historically made up 60–70% of Colorado River TDS. Sulfate is largely derived from dissolution of [[Gypsum]] deposits in the Muddy Creek formations of the Las Vegas Wash and Virgin Basin. Before inundation, the Bureau of Reclamation estimated only 22 acres (4.9 × 10⁴ m²) of exposed salt outcrops in the Lake Mead floor, with dissolution expected to slow as outcrops dissolved or silted over.

**Biological and chemical processes.** Calcite precipitation offsets part of the salt gain. Calcite precipitation is linked to phytoplankton productivity: productivity declines in Lake Mead after Lake Powell was formed reduced calcite removal. Sulfate-reducing bacteria, present in Lake Mead sediments, may also lower sulfate in the outflow. [[Polyphenols]] from forested upper-basin catchments inhibit calcite precipitation, especially in the upper third of Lake Powell during spring overflow.

**Tributary inputs.** The [[Little Colorado River]] enters about 25 miles above the Grand Canyon gage. Its base flow of 223 cfs (6.3 m³/s) from [[Blue Springs]] contributes about 550,000 tons of salt per year, mainly as sodium and chloride.

## Key Findings

**1. Flow–TDS relationships broke down after impoundment.**
- Regression analyses of TDS against flow were significant for pre-impoundment periods, but showed no meaningful relationship at Lees Ferry and Grand Canyon after Lake Powell formed, and below Hoover Dam during the post-Lake Mead periods.
- The authors conclude that simple inverse flow–TDS models, on which the 1150 mg/L projection depends, are no longer valid.

**2. Ion budgets show large, ion-specific changes.**

| Reservoir / period | Sulfate change | Carbonate change | TDS change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lake Mead, 1951–60 | +65 mg/L (over 50 mg/L above expected) | −29 mg/L below expected | +42 mg/L |
| Lake Mead, 1970–79 | +26 mg/L above expected | −9 mg/L below expected | +57 mg/L |
| Lake Powell, 1970–79 | +24 mg/L (about 10 mg/L above expected) | −14 mg/L | +34 mg/L |

- Lake Powell's carbonate loss offset nearly half its sulfate gain.
- Magnesium and potassium showed little change in most periods.

**3. Gypsum dissolution is the dominant salt source; calcite precipitation is the main sink.**
- Gypsum dissolution rates in Lake Mead declined from 125 mg/L (1935–48) to 75 mg/L (1951–60) and to about 37 mg/L (1970–79).
- Halite dissolution declined from 19 mg/L (1935–48) to 3 mg/L (1951–60). It could not be estimated for 1970–79.
- Calcite precipitation in Lake Mead fell from 48 mg/L (1951–60) to 15 mg/L (1970–79). Lake Powell precipitated about 23 mg/L during 1970–79.
- Combined, the two reservoirs remove about 39 mg/L of calcium carbonate per year, which the authors describe as highly significant for salinity control.
- The combined system now adds about 53 mg/L of calcium sulfate to the river, far less than Lake Mead alone contributed in the 1930s–50s.

**4. Lake productivity drives calcite removal.**
- Reservoir-wide productivity in Lake Mead fell by 78% after Lake Powell was formed. Calcite precipitation dropped from about 397 × 10³ t/yr to roughly 180 × 10³ t/yr (value garbled in scan).
- Reduced phosphorus loading from upstream reservoirs was linked to lower upper-basin productivity.
- Polyphenol inhibition appears to limit calcite precipitation in both reservoirs, so the authors argue that calcite removal would be higher without it.

**5. Evaporation is a minor factor.** Lake Mead's net water losses were higher in 1970–79 than in 1951–60, which the authors attribute to bank storage after water-level rises. Cold-water releases from Glen Canyon Dam have reduced evaporation and are estimated to lower average TDS by about 9 mg/L.

**6. Sodium and chloride budget anomalies reflect sampling error, not retention.** Sodium and chloride in Lake Mead were about 5 mg/L lower than expected in 1970–79. The authors attribute this to sampling bias in Grand Canyon after Glen Canyon Dam regulation: samples collected during high flows dilute these ions, which leads to underestimated loading. Daily flows vary from about 2,000 cfs to over 20,000 cfs. The authors recommend composite rather than grab sampling below point-source tributaries, including the Green River below the Price River.

**7. Accuracy caveats.**
- Salt storage in the reservoirs was excluded from the calculations because data were scarce. The authors acknowledge this may introduce errors but argue that it cannot explain the observed carbonate and sulfate differences, since estimates cover about three reservoir flushings.
- Sediment diagenesis, such as sulfate reduction, probably affects the calcium–sulfate stoichiometry, but its net effect is unknown.

**8. Management implications.**
- Congress authorized four salinity control projects under Title II of the [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Act]] (1974): Grand Valley and Paradox Valley (Colorado), Las Vegas Wash (Nevada), and Crystal Geyser (Utah). Crystal Geyser has since been dropped. Together, the projects would reduce TDS at Imperial Dam by 65 mg/L; if all planned projects were built, the reduction would be about 130 mg/L.
- Sulfate makes up nearly half of TDS but has little effect on irrigation (for example, gypsum is applied to soils) or municipal use. Hardness from calcium and carbonate, however, causes economic damage. Kleinman and Brown estimated $240,500 in damages to users in the Lower Colorado River (value as read from scan), likely an underestimate.
- Sodium and chloride pose the greater agricultural risk. Sodium adsorption ratios at Parker and Imperial Dams approach levels that could cause problems (unpublished data).
- The authors recommend controlling specific ions (sodium and chloride) and enhancing natural processes such as calcite precipitation, rather than controlling TDS at point sources. They argue this is a more cost-effective strategy.
