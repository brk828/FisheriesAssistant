# Biological Opinion: Cottonwood Spring Cooperative Agreement and Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This intra-Service biological opinion from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (April 15, 1993) evaluates a habitat-restoration project at [[Cottonwood Spring]], Santa Cruz County, Arizona. The project would exclude cattle from a [[Cienega]] and adjacent [[Mesquite Bosque]] along [[Sonoita Creek]], add two livestock drinkers, and install a solar-powered pump with a water filter and buried pipeline. The Service concluded the action is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of the [[Gila Topminnow]], a species listed as endangered in 1967. It anticipates overall habitat improvement, plus limited incidental take that is quantified only as mortality thresholds, because population counts are not obtainable.

## Background & Description

**Species.** The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is a small livebearing fish, 25 to 50 mm long, of the family [[Poeciliidae]]. The memorandum also refers to it as the Sonoran topminnow in the cited literature. It occurs in the Gila, Sonora, and de la Concepción River drainages of Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico. It was once among the most common fishes of the [[Gila River]] system. It was listed as endangered on March 11, 1967, and no critical habitat has been designated.

**Threats.** Losses are attributed to water diversion, stream downcutting, backwater draining, vegetation clearing, channelization, impoundment, and predation or competition from the non-native [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). These pressures have extirpated the species from most of its range. Only 9 naturally occurring U.S. populations remain, plus about 20 reintroduced populations.

**Cottonwood Spring population.** Cottonwood Spring holds one of the nine natural populations and is one of only three natural sites still free of mosquitofish. The [[Longfin Dace]] is the only other fish present. The authors consider this population very important to the species' long-term survival.

**Project setting.** Cottonwood Spring is a perennial spring draining into Sonoita Creek, an intermittent stream about eight miles north of Patagonia. The studied channel section is 1/2 mile long, with an active channel about 150 feet wide. The spring-fed reach passes through a cienega, with vegetation including cottonwood, Goodding willow, velvet mesquite, and [[Huachuca Water Umbel]] (a Federal candidate, Category 1). The 20-acre site is part of a private ranch owned by Davis Merwin and is used for grazing, with cattle present roughly November to April and for about six weeks in the fall.

**Agreement.** On July 1, 1992, the landowner and [[The Nature Conservancy]] signed a 20-year Partners for Wildlife Cooperative Agreement with the Service. Its aim is to protect and restore cottonwood/willow and cienega wetland habitat for the Gila topminnow, the Huachuca water umbel, the [[Huachuca Watersnail]] (Category 1 candidate), and neotropical migratory birds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Proposed works.** The project includes:
- A fence excluding cattle from the cienega and mesquite bosque, with the cienega crossing fence removed, plus repairs to existing fencing using barbless top and bottom wires per [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] specifications.
- Two livestock drinkers in the upland, one north and one south.
- A solar pump and solar cells on the slope above the spring, a water filter at the spring, and two pipelines from the pump.
- A 1.25-inch galvanized steel pipeline buried at least 12 inches deep through the cienega, a depth stated as twice the maximum depth of the deepest low-flow channel. Trenching uses a hand-held trencher, a 4 to 6 inch trench, and spoil stockpiled away from the channel. Work occurs in late spring after winter runoff and before summer monsoons.

**Flow.** The average spring discharge is 75 gallons per minute (gpm), and seasonal variation is unknown. The system is designed to divert a maximum of 6 gpm, with float valves so only enough water fills the drinkers. Diversion runs for six weeks in fall (north drinker) and November to April (south drinker). Drinkers are drained when not in use to prevent mosquito breeding. The spring's diverted maximum is under 10 percent of average flow, and peak cattle-season use is estimated at no more than 1,500 gallons per day. The Service judged this drop insignificant.

**Cumulative effects.** Future Federal actions are handled under Section 7 and are not treated as cumulative. Non-Federal monitoring of the topminnow by the Arizona Game and Fish Department, funded under Section 6, is treated as cumulative. The Service stresses that uncoordinated monitoring could harass the species and damage habitat.

**Legal framework.** Section 9 of the Endangered Species Act prohibits "taking," which includes significant habitat modification that impairs breeding, feeding, or sheltering. Incidental take is exempt only under the incidental take statement and its conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Opinion:** Not likely to jeopardize the Gila topminnow.
- **Expected benefit:** Excluding cattle should improve riparian and cienega vegetation and habitat, benefiting the population in the long term.
- **Incidental take sources:** Fish pulled into the pipeline when water is diverted, fish trapped in drinkers that run dry, and fish killed during trenching.
- **Take not quantifiable:** Population estimates are unreliable because of sampling difficulty and rapid population change in a short-lived, high-fecundity species.
- **Take thresholds (exceeded if any occurs):**
  1. More than 50 dead juvenile or adult fish during fence removal, trenching, or pump installation.
  2. More than 50 dead fish per drinker per year after construction.
  3. More than 50 dead fish during maintenance other than annual drinker draining.
- **Required actions if exceeded:** Reinitiate consultation immediately, stop operations if the additional take would be irreversible, and explain the causes.
- **Reasonable and prudent measures:** Minimize direct mortality and habitat degradation, keep complete records, and monitor the fish and habitat.
- **Key terms:**
  - Screens at pipeline mouths and a microfilter and wire screen over the filter.
  - Hand-digging the trench, backfilling, resodding, and trenching only after the cienega dries.
  - Annual report reviewed and filed.
  - Monitoring of the exclosure's effects on riparian and aquatic communities and channel morphology, with long-term photo points, cross-channel transects, and yearly fish sampling.
  - Visual monitoring of take by a qualified biologist during construction, of fish in drinkers during annual draw-down, and of emergency repairs.
  - Dead specimens sent to the Arizona State University Vertebrate Museum fish collection.
- **Conservation recommendation:** Survey the fenceline semi-annually for integrity, and notify the Service when conservation recommendations are implemented.
- **Reinitiation triggers (50 CFR 402.16):** Take limit exceeded, new information on unconsidered effects, subsequent modification of the action, or new listing of affected species.
