## Executive Summary
This 1974 note documents the first record of the non-native **tiger barb (Barbus tetrazona)** in the Owens Valley, California, specifically in the outflow stream of Warm Springs Sanctuary, Inyo County. Two mature, breeding-condition specimens were collected on July 6, 1973, just below the Sanctuary, which harbors one of only two known populations of the endangered **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (Cyprinodon radiosus). While no tiger barbs were found *within* the Sanctuary itself, the species' proximity and the site's suitable thermal conditions raised concern about potential establishment and negative impacts on the native pupfish population, echoing prior invasions by **[[Mosquitofish]]** (Gambusia affinis) in the same habitat.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: Barbus tetrazona (Tiger Barb), family [[Cyprinidae]]
- **Maximum size**: ~7 cm (per Frank 1969)
- **Collected specimens**: Two individuals, both 87 mm standard length (SL)
  - One male, one female
  - Both sexually mature with well-developed gonads, in **breeding condition**
- **Native distribution**: Sumatra, Borneo, and possibly Thailand (Sterba 1966)
- **Behavioral note**: Often aggressive; typically kept segregated from other species in aquaria

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Collection site**: Small stream flowing from **[[Warm Springs Sanctuary]]**, Owens Valley, Inyo County, California
- **Water temperature**: Sanctuary and outflow remain near 30°C for most of the year
- **Thermal spawning requirement**: Tiger barbs typically spawn at 24–28°C (Frank 1969) — the Sanctuary's warm water is described as "nearly ideal" for spawning
- **Co-occurring species**:
  - **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (Cyprinodon radiosus) — rare native species, first introduced to the Sanctuary in 1970 (Miller and Pister 1971)
  - **[[Mosquitofish]]** (Gambusia affinis) — an exotic species that had already colonized the Sanctuary since pupfish introduction
- **Physical barrier**: An effective barrier exists to prevent upstream fish movement into the Sanctuary, currently containing the tiger barb population downstream
- **Broader context**: Part of a documented pattern of exotic tropical fish introductions into isolated warm desert springs of the American Southwest, often linked to aquarium trade and brood pond practices (Minckley 1973)

## Key Findings
- **First documented occurrence** of Barbus tetrazona in the Owens Valley, California — an introduced, non-native population
- Specimens were **reproductively mature and breeding**, indicating the species may already be **established** in the outflow stream below the Sanctuary
- No tiger barbs were observed **inside** the Sanctuary at time of collection, but risk of upstream colonization exists absent barrier failure
- Potential threat to **[[Cyprinodon radiosus]]**, one of only two extant populations of this rare, threatened pupfish species (cf. [[Threatened Freshwater Fishes Of The United States]], Miller 1972)
- Reinforces broader concern regarding **exotic species introductions** into isolated spring ecosystems of the Southwest, historically associated with severe disruption to native fish faunas (Hubbs and Deacon 1964; Deacon et al. 1964)
- Authors explicitly note such introductions are **discouraged and illegal in most states**
- Recommends continued monitoring and barrier maintenance to protect the **[[Owens Pupfish]]** population from potential competitive or predatory effects of Barbus tetrazona
