# Artificial Propagation of Loach Minnow, *Rhinichthys cobitis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service station report, by [[Robert E. David]] and [[Lawrence J. Wirtanen]], describes the development of an artificial propagation protocol for the [[Loach Minnow]], a threatened stream fish listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Wild fish were collected in June 1996 from the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] and held at the [[Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]]. The study covered holding, feeding, spawning, egg incubation, larval rearing and juvenile growth. Of 179 larvae hatched, 164 sub-adults were produced (92% survival from sack fry). The authors present a 12-point set of recommendations, while noting that a reliable treatment for fungal infection of eggs was not found.

## Background & Description

The Loach Minnow is a small, elongate, slightly dorsoventrally flattened cyprinid that seldom exceeds 60 mm total length. It is mottled greenish-brown dorsally and laterally, with off-white ventral coloration. Two diffuse white spots at the base of the caudal fin distinguish it from similar daces. Breeding males show intense red on the lips, ventral surfaces and paired-fin bases; nuptial females show yellow to yellow-orange tones.

Historically the species was locally abundant in the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico and in the San Pedro River Basin of Arizona and Sonora, Mexico. It is now restricted to scattered tributary populations in Arizona and New Mexico. The species is listed as threatened by New Mexico and as a species of special concern by Arizona. The [[Recovery Plan]] (Marsh, 1991) identified captive holding and propagation as contingency measures, including determining suitable wild stocks, developing facilities and evaluating propagation techniques.

The project was an intra-agency agreement between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], operating under an Endangered Species Permit and written permission from the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]]. Host-population data were proprietary to the Tribe, so the report covers post-collection work only.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats in the wild:** Survival depends on natural stream flows, stable riffle substrate, adequate macrophyte cover, and freedom from non-native competitors and predators (Propst et al., 1991). The current population occupies about 14 miles of stream on the reservation.
- **Triggering event:** Low runoff, hazardous fire conditions and heavy fuel loading in the watershed in 1996 led to a decision to remove a small percentage of fish to conserve genetic resources.
- **Diet in the wild:** Adults feed mainly on chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs, about 1–5 mm in size (Propst et al., 1988).
- **Parasites and disease:** The fish proved highly susceptible to [[Ichthyophthirius]] ("Ich") in captivity, and a parasitic complex (Trichodina, Ichthyobodo, Chilodonella) was also diagnosed. Egg fungus was caused by [[Saprolegnia]].
- **Source water:** Hatchery production water came from the North Fork of the White River, which carried native and introduced fish and the parasite present in the wild population.

## Key Findings

### Collection and Holding

- **Collection:** On 06/11/96, 64 fish were seined from two isolated sites in one stream: 20 from Site A (50 m reach) and 44 from Site B (25 m reach). Collections were kept separate because of possible genetic differentiation. No mortality occurred during collection or transport.
- **Hatchery holding:** Fish were held in aluminum troughs on production water. Parasitic disease caused 27 deaths, all in Site B. Formalin treatment (167 mg/l for one hour, repeated) gave no noticeable reduction in mortality.
- **Recirculating system:** On 08/05/96, 37 survivors (17 from Site B, 20 from Site A) were moved to six 75 L aquaria on a closed recirculation system with a 16 µm mechanical filter, UV sterilizer, sump and biological media. Two further Site B deaths occurred after transfer. By 08/16/96, no epithelial cysts were visible and no further parasite mortality occurred.
- **Parasite control:** Gradual cooling (18.7°C to 13.3°C) was used to lengthen the parasite life cycle. The authors judged the UV unit (rated above 45,000 µW·s/cm²) and fine mechanical filtration effective against free-swimming infective stages.

### Feeding

- Brine shrimp nauplii did not elicit feeding in adults, although they are eaten by juvenile salmonids.
- Frozen commercial diets were accepted: adult brine shrimp, mosquito larvae, bloodworms and krill. Preference order was bloodworms, mosquito larvae, brine shrimp, then krill.
- Ration was about 0.40–0.60 g/fish/day at 15°C, with less below and more above that temperature.
- Dry diets were poorly accepted. Dry-diet training was not pursued because frozen diets were simpler to use.

### Spawning

- Aquaria were chilled from 15°C (October 1996) to 7.2°C by mid-November, held through winter under natural light, then warmed to 10–12.2°C in spring.
- The first spawn occurred on 04/27/97 at 12.2°C. Spawning activity included mock spawning and intensified male coloration. The last spawn was on 06/19/97 at 17.2°C.
- Two males with one female, and two females with one male, both produced fertile egg masses. Community aquaria (6–9 mixed-sex fish) also produced spawns, but losses to cannibalism or male damage were noted there.
- Inverted, broken clay potsherds served as shelter and spawning substrate. Eggs were strongly adhesive and could not be separated from gravel without damage.

### Egg Incubation and Hatching

- Eggs were 1.3–1.8 mm in diameter. Incubation was carried out in an upwelling hatching system with stainless steel sieves.
- Mean days to hatch for 174 eggs: 16.3 at 13.9°C, 16.5 at 14.4°C, 16.0 at 15.0°C, 14.2 at 15.6°C and 12.1 at 17.2°C.
- Sack fry measured 6.5–7.6 mm. Yolk sacks were approximately 2.0–2.5 mm in diameter at hatch.
- Fungus was the main obstacle. Infertile or damaged eggs seeded infection. Malachite green at 8.3 mg/l for one hour was the most effective treatment, but it induced premature hatching late in incubation. Formalin and malachite green at standard salmonid levels were unsuccessful.

### Fry Development and Rearing

- Fry showed pigmentation at about day 7 post-hatch, a functional mouth by about day 10, and a fully absorbed yolk sack by day 18 at 13.8°C.
- Sack fry became capable of feeding at 9–18 days post-hatch, depending on temperature (17.2°C to 13.8°C).
- Initial diet was the microalga [[Nannochloropsis Oculata]] (4–6 µm) and the rotifer [[Brachionus Calyciflorus]] (120–500 µm). Algae formed mats on nursery-tank floors that gave fry cover and attracted rotifers.
- Newly hatched [[Brine Shrimp]] (Artemia nauplii) were added once fry were older. Fry at 9–12 days post-hatch readily ate them, indicating earlier mouth and esophagus development than expected.
- Fry in the warmer tanks (15.5–17.2°C) grew faster. One group reached 17.6 mm at 50 days post-hatch.

### Production Results

- **Larvae hatched:** 179 from viable eggs.
- **Sub-adults produced:** 164, from Site B broodstock (119) and Site A broodstock (45).
- **Survival:** 92% from sack fry to sub-adult fingerlings.
- **Mean length:** 25 mm at about 75 days post-hatch for G-1 fish (the first grower group).

### Data Notes

- The Results section gives the collection date as 06/11/98, while the Methods and other sections give 06/11/96. The 1996 date is used here because the study timeline supports it.
- Some dates in the text are internally inconsistent (for example, the aquarium cooling dates), and some fry-development temperatures are approximate.

### Recommendations

1. **Disease control:** Hold wild fish in systems with mechanical filtration (particles above 20 µm) and UV sterilization rated at no less than 45,000 µW·s/cm².
2. **Infrastructure:** Provide reliable temperature control, aeration, waste removal and backup power.
3. **Lighting:** Use natural light or a broad-spectrum light system that reproduces natural cycles.
4. **Feeding:** Use frozen diets. Consider training to dry diets, which may require withholding natural foods and short periods of starvation.
5. **Spawning cues:** Chill for 3–4 months at about 7.2°C, then warm to spawn (first spawn near 14°C).
6. **Spawning substrate:** Use inverted clay potsherds fitted with a plastic loop for removal and inspection.
7. **Pairings:** Use one male with one female, or two males with one female, in separate tanks rather than community aquaria.
8. **Incubation:** Use screened-bottom incubators with upwelling flow. Longer incubation at cooler temperatures is preferred.
9. **Fungal control:** Enzyme removal of the gelatinous egg coating and prophylactic malachite green or formalin should be investigated. Malachite green is potentially carcinogenic and must be handled with care.
10. **First feeding:** Begin feeding before the yolk sack is fully absorbed, ideally when it is about 90% reduced.
11. **Larval diet:** Feed algae and rotifers for 10–15 days, then brine shrimp nauplii, with rotifers and algae until about 30 days post-hatch. Later, add frozen diets.
12. **Husbandry:** Frequent siphon cleaning was essential to water quality and fish health.

### Limitations

The authors state that the species may not be a strong candidate for intensive fish culture techniques, but they note that simpler methods may exist. Steve Vives (Georgia Southern University) reported spawning at 23°C on a 16/8 light cycle with power-head flow only. Many behavioral observations and culture details were excluded from the report.
