# Fertilization of Natural Lakes in Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Robert C. Ball (Department of Zoology, [[Michigan State College]]) tested whether inorganic fertilizer could increase fish production in two low-productivity, state-owned lakes in northern Michigan. A 27.5-acre warm-water lake ([[North Twin Lake]], Cheboygan County) and a 4.3-acre [[Trout Lake]] (Otsego County) were fertilized, while nearby lakes served as unfertilized controls. Fertilization produced a plankton-algae bloom and heavy filamentous algae growth in the warm-water lake, and a strongly altered, more eutrophic profile in the trout lake. Both fertilized lakes suffered an almost complete [[Winterkill]] in the winter of 1947–48, while the controls did not. The author concludes that fertilization of lakes at Michigan's latitude should be confined to experimental work until its effects and duration are better understood.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** Organic and inorganic fertilizers were already used in [[Fish Pond Management]] for farm ponds. The study asked whether extending this to natural waters would increase fish growth and whether the cost in time and materials could be justified. Michigan has approximately 11,000 lakes (Brown, 1943), so the author judged that widespread fertilization of warm-water lakes was not justified under existing conditions. The Director of the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]], Dr. A. S. Hazzard, considered small, low-productivity trout waters the most likely candidates, since trout lakes carry greater value to anglers.

**Study lakes.**
- *Warm-water lakes (Cheboygan County):* North Twin Lake (27.5 acres, maximum depth 15 ft, no inlet or outlet, pulpy peat bottom, non-stratifying) and South Twin Lake (54 acres, maximum depth only 4 ft). North Twin supported bluegill, pumpkinseed, black bullhead, yellow perch, smallmouth bass, and bluegill-pumpkinseed hybrids; its perch showed stunting and the fishery had a poor reputation. South Twin had largemouth bass, northern pike, and bluegill, with fish not stunted and fishing rated "fair."
- *Trout lakes (Otsego County):* A pair of small solution lakes in the [[Pigeon River Forest]], with similar size, shape, and chemistry. The fertilized lake (South Twin, 4.3 acres, maximum depth 39 ft) and its control (North Twin) both had firm sand slopes, sparse [[Potamogeton]] beds, and no surface drainage. Fish included many small yellow perch and minnows, plus remnant rainbow and brown trout. Both lakes were stocked each fall with [[Brook Trout]] fingerlings. Trout fishing had declined because of competition and predation by perch.

**Fertilizer.** A high-nitrogen, high-phosphorus commercial fertilizer (10-6-4) was applied at roughly three-week intervals: from a moving boat on the warm-water lake and from shore on the trout lake. The warm-water lake received 2,800 lb per application, and the trout lake 400 lb per application at 2 p.p.m. per week in 1946. In 1947 the trout-lake rate was raised to 3 p.p.m. per week, which increased particulate organic matter but produced no bloom.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Warm-water lake (North Twin).** Oxygen was present from top to bottom throughout the open-water season, and the water was moderately hard (50 p.p.m.). The lake responded quickly. A heavy [[Plankton Algae Bloom]] formed in the first summer, with a Secchi-disk reading of 18 to 36 inches. Sparse higher aquatic plants (pondweeds and [[White Water Lily]]) were smothered by algae, browned, and died. The pulpy-peat bottom appeared to liquefy during the first summer, probably from active decomposition stimulated by added nitrogen and phosphorus. In the second summer, plankton was scarce until midsummer, when dense mats of [[Filamentous Algae]] covered the shallows. After dying, the mats drifted for about 10 days, creating an unsightly and malodorous condition, after which the lake turned cloudy with plankton for the rest of the season.

**Trout lakes.** Before fertilization (June 1946), the two lakes were similar. Total alkalinity was 48 p.p.m. in the south lake and 42 p.p.m. in the north lake. In late October 1946, alkalinity in the fertilized lake had risen to 58 p.p.m., while the control stood at 41 p.p.m. August 1947, identified as the most critical summer period for Michigan trout lakes, showed clear divergence:
- The thermocline in the fertilized lake lay at about 8 to 20 ft, compared with 18 to 30 ft in the control.
- Oxygen depletion near the lower thermocline restricted the fertilized lake's fish to the stratum above 20 ft, whereas the control supported fish to 30 ft. The trout-habitat strata were of nearly equal thickness and differed mainly in depth.
- The upper 15 ft of the fertilized lake was cooler than the control, which the author attributed to light absorption by plankton. Trout were observed rising in the control's upper 20 ft, where temperatures exceeded 68 °F, suggesting that warm layers were not a complete barrier.
- Secchi-disk readings were 8.2 ft in the fertilized lake and 22.1 ft in the control. The fertilized lake never fell below 8 ft over both summers, whereas the control was visible at 20 ft or more.
- Logs, stones, and higher vegetation were coated with filamentous algae, which limited plant growth and caused plant death. Dislodged mats floated to the surface late in summer.

**Winter conditions and [[Winterkill]].** In January 1947, no severe oxygen depletion appeared in either warm-water lake. The author attributed the higher oxygen in the fertilized lake to wind removing snow, since even a few inches of snow can stop photosynthesis under ice (Greenbank, 1945). No trout-lake check was possible in 1947 because of deep snow.

By late February 1948, the period considered most critical, conditions had deteriorated sharply:
- *North Twin (warm-water, fertilized):* Oxygen was 0.7 p.p.m. at 2 ft and 0.2 p.p.m. at 10 ft. Carbon dioxide reached 40 p.p.m. at 10 ft. Decay odor was strong, dead [[Phantom Midge]] larvae (*Chaoborus*) floated into ice holes, and hydrogen sulfide was present at depth. Bottom dredging found many dead invertebrates, while large *Chironomus plumosus* larvae were alive but inactive. Few perch survived, and most fish died.
- *South Twin (warm-water, fertilized):* Oxygen fell to 0.3 p.p.m. at several locations, but carbon dioxide was low (8 p.p.m.) and no hydrogen sulfide was detected. The author noted that such low oxygen would usually be lethal, citing Cooper and Washburn (1949) on fish tolerance.
- *South Twin (trout, fertilized):* Oxygen did not exceed 0.6 p.p.m. at any depth and was below 0.2 p.p.m. at 3 ft. Carbon dioxide was 18 p.p.m. at 3 ft. Dead Chaoborus again appeared, and many dead fish were found at ice breakup in April 1948.
- *North Twin (trout, control):* Conditions were satisfactory, with ample oxygen to 21 ft or more and low carbon dioxide.

No winterkill had occurred in the warm-water lakes for at least 10 years before the study, based on fish ages and sportsmen's reports.

**Growth and reproduction.** The winterkill ended the study before growth effects could be fully evaluated. Scale analysis nonetheless indicated that fish growth had increased considerably. Filamentous algae covered the formerly suitable spawning shoal of North Twin during the 1947 centrarchid season, apparently interfering with nest building and causing abandonment of many nests. Seining late in the season yielded few young-of-the-year fish, suggesting possible adverse effects on reproduction.

**Comparison.** A New Brunswick trout lake fertilized by Smith (1948) showed no ill effects in the first winter, and its trout stomach contents increased markedly, which was attributed to more bottom invertebrates. Ball (1949) previously showed that fertilization increased invertebrate fish food in small Michigan ponds.

## Key Findings

- **Warm-water lake response:** Fertilization caused a plankton-algae bloom in the first summer and dense filamentous algae in the second. No bloom reached the concentration recommended for southern ponds (Swingle and Smith, 1942), and blooms always waned before the three-week fertilizer interval ended.
- **Trout lake response:** The trout lake responded far less. No bloom developed at any time, but the lake's profile shifted toward eutrophy: higher alkalinity (48 to 58 p.p.m.), a raised thermocline, reduced oxygen in the hypolimnion, and Secchi readings never below 8 ft (versus 22.1 ft in the control in August 1947).
- **Winterkill:** An almost complete winterkill occurred in both fertilized lakes in 1948. Both control lakes survived. The author concludes that fertilization "seems certain" to have caused the kill.
- **Oxygen thresholds:** Oxygen fell below 1 p.p.m. at all depths in the fertilized lakes, reaching 0.2 to 0.7 p.p.m. in the warm-water lake, while the control trout lake remained well oxygenated.
- **Fish effects:** Growth rates appeared to increase, but spawning was impaired by algae on the shoal, and few young-of-the-year fish were found.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Fertilization of lakes at Michigan's latitude "should be confined to experimental work" until the duration of changes is known.
  - Fertilizer amounts sufficient to raise productivity without winter oxygen depletion cannot yet be predicted.
  - The nuisance of filamentous algae (to anglers, swimmers, boaters, and shoreline owners) must be weighed.
  - Indiscriminate lake fertilization is not advised. Hasler (1947) cited European lakes where urban drainage replaced trout and coregonids with coarse fish.
- **Caveat:** The changes in the trout lake "may be only transitory," so long-term evidence is needed.
