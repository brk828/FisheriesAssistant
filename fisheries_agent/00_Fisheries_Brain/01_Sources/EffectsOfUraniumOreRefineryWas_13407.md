# Effects of Uranium Ore Refinery Wastes on Receiving Waters

#aquatic-ecology #radioecology #freshwater-biota

## Executive Summary

This 1958 study by the [[Public Health Service]] (Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center and Occupational Health Field Station) examines how liquid radioactive wastes from western [[Uranium Refining]] mills affect nearby streams. The main concern is [[Radium]], the most hazardous radionuclide in the process wastes. Surveys in 1950, 1955 and 1956 found elevated radioactivity in mill effluents, river water, [[River Mud]] and [[Stream Biota]] below several mills. The strongest evidence of accumulation came from the [[Mill D]] and [[Mill E]] streams. The authors judged that no domestic water supply was clearly unsafe, but they called for caution and control of waste disposal because of the very long half-lives involved.

## Background & Description

The uranium ore refining industry of the western United States is an important peacetime source of liquid radioactive waste. Mills separate and concentrate uranium from local ores. Recovery methods include acid and carbonate leaching, [[Ion Exchange]], and [[Solvent Extraction]]. Early mills were vanadium mills whose processes were adapted to recover uranium as a by-product.

- **Capacity:** 14 operating American mills, with capacities of 300 to 3,300 tons of ore per day. Total capacity is about 10,000 tons per day. Eight new mills under construction or planned would roughly double it.
- **Ore grade:** Average U₃O₈ content of known ores is about 0.28 per cent.
- **Location:** Most of the industry lies in the [[Colorado River Basin]], in the [[Four Corners Region]] where the Utah, Colorado, Arizona and New Mexico boundaries meet. Other mills operate near Spokane (Washington), Salt Lake City (Utah), Edgemont (South Dakota), northeast Wyoming and Grants (New Mexico).

### The Uranium-Radium Decay Series

[[Uranium-238]] is the parent of the [[Uranium-Radium Decay Series]], the main series of concern for waste disposal. Naturally occurring uranium is 99.28 per cent U-238 and 0.71 per cent U-235. Key facts:

- U-238 has a half-life of 4.5 billion years.
- Radium is the sixth member of the chain and has a half-life of 1,620 years. It is a bone-seeking alpha emitter.
- [[Radon]] is the seventh member, a gas that escapes from mines, ore handling, processing and waste piles.
- The final product, lead, is nonradioactive.

Only uranium is recovered. All other series members remain in the process wastes. Radium is no longer economically valuable enough to recover.

### Waste Estimates and Standards

- Based on 10,000 tons of ore per day and 0.28 per cent U₃O₈, about 2,500 g of radium are discarded each year. The total US supply of purified radium for medical and industrial uses is about 1,000 g.
- Planned expansion would raise waste radium to roughly 5,000 g per year.
- The maximum permissible body burden for radium is 0.1 µg. The maximum permissible concentration in domestic water is 40 pCi/L for soluble radium, and [[Handbook 52]] advises a safety factor of 10.
- The [[Atomic Energy Commission]] allows licensees an effluent concentration of no more than 4 pCi/L of radium plus 50 per cent of daughter equilibrium amounts, with exceptions possible.

Most radium in mill wastes is insoluble and bound to sands and spent slimes. Solubility depends on chemical form. For example, [[Radium Sulfate]] is much less soluble than radium chloride. At one refinery, about 1 per cent of radium in the wastes was estimated to be soluble.

Wastes are generally discharged to [[Tailings Pond]]s (lagoons) on stream banks, where liquid volume drops by seepage and evaporation. Direct discharges and seepage often reach the stream. At one mill, no tailings pond was in use and all wastes entered the stream directly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study tracked radioactivity through three phases of the stream environment: water (dissolved and suspended), river mud (silts), and biota. Radioactive material can alternate among these phases before radioactive decay removes it. Radium is partly taken up by [[Stream Biota]] and transferred to sediments when organisms die. Suspended material, including most of the radium discharge, settles into river silts. Flood flows can scour and redistribute these deposits. Radium may also leach from silts at low flows, though the rate was not known.

Conditions below one mill were biologically poor, but not because of radioactivity. Heavy discharges of fine suspended solids blanketed the stream bed with sand and silt. Flow conditions also mattered. During the 1956 survey, flow in the river below Mill D was at its lowest level on record. A major [[Flash Flood]] had occurred about a month earlier and had flushed the stream.

Downstream uses included irrigation of croplands, stock and game watering, and recreation. A small community below Mill D used the river as a water supply.

## Key Findings

### 1950 Previous Analyses
- Soluble radium in river water below mills (October 1950) was 16.9 to 45 pCi/L, compared with 0.13 to 0.36 pCi/L above the mills. Mill E's below-mill value of 34 pCi/L was an estimate from other data.

### 1955 Reconnaissance Survey
- Eight of nine operating mills were surveyed. Fish were not sampled. Samples were grab samples of water, mud and biota (algae and bottom insects such as caddis fly, stone fly and mayfly larvae).
- **Background:** Soluble radium averaged 0.3 pCi/L (range 0.2 to 0.4). Background river mud averaged about 18 pCi/g gross alpha and 48 pCi/g gross beta.
- **Effluents:** Soluble radium in single grab samples ranged from 4.5 to 920 pCi/L. Soluble vanadium ranged from 1.4 to 62 ppm, except at Mill G, which had 355 ppm. Uranium downstream of the mills was not appreciable.
- **Mud:** Some samples below mills showed gross alpha activity at least 100 times background.
- **Biota:** Radium in algae and insects below mills was about 100 times the value at clean sites. Radioactivity uptake depended on the element, its chemical form, organism needs and environmental factors.
- **Conclusion:** Several streams had excessive dissolved radioactivity, as did several effluents. Further investigation was judged desirable.

### 1956 Intensive Survey (Mills D and E)
- A 5-day survey was conducted at each refinery. Composite water samples were collected at key stations, and daily grab samples at others. Mud and biota were sampled once during mid-survey. Stream flows came from the US Geological Survey.
- **River D:** About 15 miles were sampled at four stations, one above and three below the mill. Average flow was 4.8 cfs.
- **River E:** A 40-mile reach was sampled at seven locations. Average flow was 14.5 cfs. The tailings pond was not in use at Mill E during the survey.
- **Dissolved radium added:** About 0.3 mg per day at Mill D and about 2.6 mg per day at Mill E.
- **Tap water:** Three consecutive-day samples from a community a few miles below Station 4 averaged 6.1 pCi/L soluble radium. This was about 20 times natural stream radium and below the 40 pCi/L MPC, but above the 4.0 pCi/L value suggested in Handbook 52.
- **Mud:** Background mud averaged about 15 pCi/g gross alpha and 35 pCi/g gross beta. Radium was about 1.0 pCi/g at background sites. Below the mills, mud radium was 1,000 to 2,000 times background. Radium made up about 21 per cent of gross alpha activity in River D and about 27 per cent in River E. Mud at River D Station 2 was 9,900 pCi/g gross alpha. Mud at River E Station 6 was 1,950 pCi/g gross alpha.
- **Algae:** At Station 2 below Mill D, radium was about 1,500 times the Station 1 value. Station 3 was about 100 times.
- **Insects:** Insects at Stations 3 and 4 contained about 100 times the radium of Station 1 insects.
- **Fish:** Juvenile suckers and dace were sampled. Fish gross alpha was slightly higher below Mill D, and radium was clearly higher. Bone from Station 3 had about 10 times the radium of Station 1. No detectable increase in beta activity was found. Fish were absent from the Mill E reach. A rotenone collection at Station 8, about 10 miles below Mill E, produced no fish. The apparent high radioactivity in one sucker sample at Station 11 was partly attributed to fine silt in the gut contents.
- **Downstream extent:** Increases in gross radioactivity and radium in stream biota were found as far as 38 miles below Mill E, at Station 11.
- **Deposition:** Estimated radium deposition on the river bed was about 10 mg per day between Stations 2 and 3 below Mill D. Below Mill E, about 170 mg per day was deposited between Stations 6 and 7 and about 30 mg per day below Station 7. At least 1.0 g of radium was in the mud within the first mile below Mill D.

### Public Health Conclusions
- No domestic water supply was clearly unsafe under current internal-exposure standards. The single tested supply was only slightly above the suggested general-population value and well below the occupational limit.
- Other exposure sources must be considered. These include other water uses, occupational exposure of miners and millers, radon from ore and tailings piles, and weapons-testing debris.
- Undissolved mill waste may accumulate in filter units of domestic water treatment plants and affect the radium content of finished water. The data were sparse on this point.
- The ultimate fate of radium is uncertain. The amount leaching from tailings or river muds is unknown.

### Recommendations and Future Research
- The authors recommended caution and control of waste disposal. They quoted the Handbook 52 philosophy that every effort be made to keep radioisotope concentrations in air, water and the body to a minimum, with no contamination if reasonably achievable.
- Open research questions include leachability of radium from river silts and mill tailings, the effect of mill processes on radium solubility, the extent of radium uptake by stream biota, and whether adsorption or true absorption is involved. The authors also planned studies on feasible waste treatment and containment methods.
