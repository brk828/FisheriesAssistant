# Juvenile Fish Ecology in Imperial Reservoir, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1971 Master of Science thesis from the University of Arizona, in Fishery Biology, examines juvenile game fish in [[Imperial Reservoir]] on the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Sampling from September 1969 to October 1970 at five small backwater "lakes" used [[Rotenone Sampling]] to capture fish aged up to 100 mm. The study focuses mainly on juvenile [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill Sunfish]], because of heavier sport fishing pressure. It describes habitat preferences, spawning times, diet, length-weight relationships, and parasites, and concludes that the reservoir's spawning and rearing conditions support a healthy, non-stunted fishery that would be threatened by planned dredging and channelization.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** The reservoir was tentatively scheduled for dredging and channelization, which could alter fish spawning and rearing habitat. Juvenile fish data for the reservoir were scarce.
- **Fishing pressure:** The adjacent [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] recorded 90,000 visitor-days of fishing in 1969 and expected 150,000 in the near future. The sport fishery was rated excellent, with a catch per unit effort of 0.54 fish per man-hour in 1969.
- **Objectives:** (1) estimate species composition of juvenile game fish; (2) document distribution, habitat preference, and spawning; (3) determine food preference; (4) estimate growth rates and the factors influencing them.
- **Study area:** The reservoir lies on the Colorado River (completed 1938, a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] facility) and covers 2,955 hectares (7,300 acres). Siltation has reduced its original storage capacity of 95,000 acre-feet and converted parts into marsh. Five small lakes on the Arizona side were studied, ranging from 0.91 to 3.32 hectares. Most have a maximum depth of about 3 m and an average depth under 1 m. Lakes are eutrophic, with sapropel bottoms over 0.6 m deep and submerged trees in many. Lakes are holomictic, mainly from wind action.
- **Climate:** Annual precipitation is 9.45 cm (3.72 in). Mean maximum air temperature is 46.1–48.9°C (115–120°F). Annual evaporation is 195 cm (76.89 in).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation:**
- [[Typha Latifolia]] (cattail) is the dominant emergent plant, forming dense stands up to 4.56 m (15 ft) tall along shorelines.
- [[Najas Marina]] (spiny naiad) is widespread. It becomes dense in spring and summer, regresses to deeper water in winter, and can impair navigation.
- Blue-green algal mats cover submerged vegetation and bottoms for most of the year.

**Habitat use:** Juveniles preferred shallow areas with mud, gravel, or rock bottoms. The Najas zone and the Najas–Typha combination (a 0.3–2.5 m strip of open water, usually under 0.6 m deep) held most juveniles. Submerged mesquite branches sheltered schools of bass.

**Water chemistry:** Water temperature ranged from a maximum of 32.2°C (90°F) in August to a minimum of 7.8°C (46°F) in January. pH stabilized near 8.0 for most of the year.

**Sampling methods that failed:** Electrofishing, gill nets, a miniature trapnet, and minnow traps were ineffective for juveniles. Rotenone (Pro-Noxfish, 2.5% emulsion, applied at 1–2 ppm) was used instead. Potassium permanganate neutralized the rotenone after each collection.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and sample size**
- Sampling ran from September 3, 1969 to October 31, 1970, with 101 samples collected at triweekly intervals and biweekly intervals during spring spawning.
- A total of 1,124 fish were collected. Lakes 1 and 5 were the most productive, partly because they were sampled most often.
- Stomachs from 401 bass and bluegill were analyzed.

**Spawning**
- [[Largemouth Bass]] spawned from March to May. Young bass (10 mm) were first collected on March 21, 1970, at water temperatures of 18.4°C (65°F) in Lakes 1, 2, 4, and 5, and 17.2°C in Lake 3.
- [[Bluegill Sunfish]] spawned from May to August. Young bluegill (19 mm) were first captured on June 2, 1970, at 26.2°C (80°F).
- Bass nests were 2.5–3.75 cm... more precisely 9 to 15 inches (23–38 cm) in diameter, in shallow water under 0.6 m deep with firm bottoms, mostly near shore away from vegetation.
- Staggered spawning let young-of-the-year bass feed on bluegill fry in the fall.

**Diet**
- Bass and bluegill mainly ate zooplankton ([[Daphnia]] and calanoid copepods).
- Bass favored shrimp (*Palaemonetes paludosus*) in the fall and zooplankton in winter, spring, and summer.
- Bluegill preferred chironomid (midge) larvae in the fall and zooplankton in other seasons.
- Warmouth ate chironomid larvae in fall, spring, and summer, and zooplankton in winter.
- Green sunfish ate mainly zooplankton in fall, and ostracods in summer.
- Odonate naiads were eaten by many species but never dominated in any season.
- Bass began eating fish at about 65 mm in length. The smallest bass recorded with a fish in its stomach was 65 mm, and the prey was a 22 mm bluegill.

**Growth and length-weight relationships**
- Bass: log a = −4.7449, n = 2.9065.
- Bluegill: log a = −4.7415, n = 2.9661.
- Both suggest approximately isometric growth. Bass n was slightly below comparison values (3.03–3.19 in Florida ponds), so growth was rated "good" rather than excellent.
- No stunting was observed.

**Parasites**
- Found: a trematode (*Posthodiplostomum minimum* metacercariae, the "Neascus" larvae) and nematodes *Contracaecum brachyurum* and *C. spiculigerum*.
- The trematode was the most prevalent in all fish except green sunfish, where *C. spiculigerum* dominated.
- Parasitized fish showed no difference in condition factor from unparasitized fish.

**Growth drivers**
- Ample food and a growing season of 11 months, with four months at optimal growth temperatures, support fast growth.
- The die-off of Najas in fall and winter releases forage fish, as previously reported by [[Bennett]] (1962).

**Conclusions and management implications**
1. Shallow, mud-bottomed areas with Najas and Najas–Typha cover are critical juvenile habitat.
2. Spawning periods are separated (bass March–May, bluegill May–August), which limits bluegill overpopulation and stunting.
3. The sport fishery (0.54 fish per man-hour) would be adversely affected if any of these ecological features were removed by dredging.
