# Terrestrial and Aquatic Vertebrates of a Moist, Relict Area in Central Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontiformes #herpetology #zoogeography

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by [[Gerald G. Raun]] (University of Texas) surveys the amphibians, reptiles, fish and mammals (birds excluded) of the moist marshes near Ottine in the San Marcos River Valley, Gonzales County, Texas. Fieldwork ran from August 1957 to June 1958. The author recognizes four ecological communities, documents their vertebrate faunas and zoogeographic affinities, and identifies disjunct populations that suggest the marshes are relicts of a wetter, Austroriparian-influenced past.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The western third of Gonzales County consists of low, rolling hills underlain by Eocene Claiborne group sands (Carrizo and Reklaw members). The San Marcos River has incised a deep valley containing Ottine and [[Palmetto State Park]], about five miles SSE of Luling.
- **Hydrology:** The river can rise as much as 35 feet overnight. Floods recede almost as quickly and keep the floodplain largely free of humus and fallen logs.
- **Prior knowledge:** The unusual flora had been noted since the early 19th century, but the fauna had not been studied before this survey.
- **Acknowledgments:** Work was supervised by Dr. W. Frank Blair, with identification help from B. C. Tharp, Clark Hubbs and F. R. Cagle, and permission from the [[Texas State Parks Board]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Four communities**, ordered from driest to wettest:

1. **Post oak-blackjack upland:** Deep, well-drained sands on the valley margins, dominated by *Quercus stellata*. This community supports 53 to 54 terrestrial vertebrate species and no fish, because its only permanent water is a hatchery.
2. **Wooded floodplain:** Heavy alluvial clays with seeps and springs. It holds the park lake, an old oxbow, and spring-fed pools. Dwarf palmetto (*Sabal glabra*) dominates the understory.
3. **Peat bog:** Two bogs, Rutledge Swamp and Soefje Bog, with [[Sphagnum]] at its southwesternmost known U.S. locality.
4. **Spartina-Phragmites:** About ten acres of flat, seasonally standing water, with only eight vertebrate species recorded.

**Zoogeographic pattern:** Across the 88 total species, 40 are eastern, 18 widespread, 16 western, 7 southern, 5 Texan and 2 exotic. The eastern affinity strengthens as communities get wetter. Eastern species made up 49% of the post oak-blackjack fauna, 64% of the wooded floodplain fauna and 47% of the peat bog fauna by one reading of Table 2. The paper explicitly notes that the upland community is still predominantly eastern, which is surprising given its location near the [[Balconian Biotic Province]] and [[Tamaulipan Biotic Province]].

## Key Findings

- **Fish:** Eleven species were collected from the park lake. The spring pools held only the sailfin molly (*Mollienesia latipinna*) and the common mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*). Those two species were also the only fish in the peat bog. Two fish species are exotic.
- **Amphibians and reptiles:** The cricket frog (*Acris crepitans*) and Gulf Coast toad (*Bufo valliceps*) were the most abundant amphibians. Water snakes (*Natrix*) and cottonmouths were the most abundant snakes. The wooded floodplain is noted for its turtle diversity.
- **Mammals:** The wood mouse (*Peromyscus leucopus*) was the most abundant mammal in the uplands. The swamp rabbit (*Sylvilagus aquaticus*) was the most commonly recorded mammal in the peat bog, where droppings covered nearly every log. In 110 trap nights at Rutledge Swamp, only two mice were taken.
- **Disjunct populations:** At least three vertebrates are disjunct at Ottine:
  - The common water snake (*Natrix sipedon*) is abundant in all three main communities. Large aggregations occurred at the hatchery and spring pools, including a breeding congregation on April 27, 1958, where 15 small specimens were collected in half an hour.
  - The narrow-mouthed toad (*Gastrophryne carolinensis*) marks the western limit of its range, 50 to 75 miles from the nearest eastern population. It was last collected at Soefje Bog in 1950 and was not found in this study. The author links its apparent loss to a seven-year drought before 1957.
  - The false map turtle (*Graptemys pseudogeographica*) was previously unknown west of the Colorado River drainage. The park lake was its only site, and the hatchery owner was trying to eliminate turtles from his ponds.
  - A fourth species, the flying squirrel (*Glaucomys volans*), is reported by local residents but is of questionable occurrence.
- **Possible additional disjuncts:** The canebrake rattlesnake (*Crotalus horridus*) and grey squirrel (*Sciurus carolinensis*) may also be disjunct.
- **Species at range limits:** Twenty species reach or approach their range limits near Gonzales County, 13 of them eastern and six western, plus one southern species.
- **Relict hypothesis:** The author draws on Bogush (1928), who proposed the marshes are relicts of a wetter period linked to East Texas river bottoms about 100 miles away. Pollen analysis by Graham (1958) dates the bottom of Soefje Bog to about 8000 years, with no significant floristic change during that time. The author proposes that an Austroriparian biota once occupied the Texan Biotic Province, retreated eastward as the climate dried in the Pleistocene, and left relict populations behind. Carrizo sands likely helped sustain this forest through the drying.
- **Recent range shifts:** Southern species such as *Dasypus novemcinctus*, *Didelphis marsupialis*, *Sigmodon hispidus* and *Baiomys taylori* have expanded their ranges northward and eastward in historic times.
- **Conservation notes:** Agriculture has reduced the floodplain forest, which is now mostly within park boundaries. The Rutledge Swamp and Soefje Bog peat bogs are now only two isolated sites.
