# Response to Petition to Designate Critical Habitat for the Gila Trout

## Executive Summary
This document is a formal 1994 response letter from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) to the Greater Gila Biodiversity Project, addressing a petition to designate critical habitat for the [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*, formerly *Salmo gilae*) under the Endangered Species Act. The USFWS ultimately **denied** the petition, concluding that critical habitat designation would provide no net benefit to the species. The Service argued that the primary threat to Gila Trout survival is **hybridization and competitive replacement by non-native trout species** (particularly [[Rainbow Trout]]), not livestock grazing as the petitioners contended. The letter provides an extensive point-by-point rebuttal covering fire ecology, grazing impacts, population dynamics, genetics, taxonomy, and stream-specific renovation/recovery status.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomic Note:** The American Fisheries Society's *Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada* (Robins et al. 1991) does not formally recognize the trinomial subspecies designation for Gila Trout, creating some taxonomic ambiguity referenced by the Service.
- **Historic Range:** Presumed historic range includes the Gila River drainage in New Mexico and Arizona, with disputed/uncertain historic occurrence in the **Verde** and **Agua Fria** drainages of Arizona due to a scarcity of confirmed specimen records.
- **Possible Cryptic Diversity:** The Service raises the possibility that trout historically occupying the Verde/Agua Fria drainages may represent an undescribed, distinct subspecies rather than true Gila Trout as currently recognized.
- **Governing Recovery Document:** The second revision of the **Gila Trout Recovery Plan** (USFWS 1993), developed with the Gila Trout/Chihuahua Chub Recovery Team, serves as the primary management framework, covering population monitoring, habitat evaluation/enhancement, regulation of human activities, and investigation of grazing impacts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Primary Threat — Hybridization/Replacement:** Citing [[Behnke 1992]], the dominant historical driver of decline in western native trout (including Gila Trout) is stocking of non-native/exotic trout leading to hybridization and competitive/predatory replacement of native populations — not livestock grazing. A cited example: non-native [[Brook Trout]] virtually replaced a native [[Cutthroat Trout]] population within 5 years in Black Hollow Creek, Colorado.
- **Grazing Impacts:** While excessive/mismanaged livestock grazing is acknowledged to degrade riparian and stream habitat, only **South Diamond, McKnight, and Little creeks** were identified as directly affected by grazing (USFWS 1993) — contradicting petitioners' broader claims.
- **Historic Grazing Trends:** Livestock numbers on National Forest lands have substantially declined from historic peaks (e.g., Diamond Bar Allotment: 2,255 head of cattle in 1927 vs. 677 head presently), countering the petition's claim of increasing grazing intensity.
- **Fire Ecology:** Roughly 80 years of unnatural fire suppression by the Forest Service has led to unnatural fuel buildup, contributing to catastrophic events like the **1989 Divide Fire**, which caused complete extirpation of the Main Diamond Creek Gila Trout population. Forest Service policy has since shifted toward allowing natural fires to burn under monitoring and implementing prescribed burns.
- **Beaver Population Dynamics:** Historic "elimination" of beaver in the early 1800s was likely only a temporary reduction; populations rebounded by the 1850s. Beaver activity (damming, cottonwood/willow browsing) can raise stream temperatures, trap sediment (suffocating eggs/fry), and inundate riffle habitat important for aquatic invertebrate production (a food source comprising approximately 80% of trout diet, per USFWS 1993).
- **Riparian Vegetation Function:** Intact riparian vegetation dissipates flood energy, moderates high flows, and root systems provide fish shelter during high-velocity events; canopy density also regulates solar gain necessary for primary production (per [[Turner 1989]] findings at Main Diamond Creek).
- **Reproduction/Spawning:** Spawning occurs within a approximately 2-week window between late March and early June, varying annually with spring runoff volume/timing. **Temperature** is the primary environmental spawning cue, with day length as a possible secondary cue.
- **Habitat Preference:** Gila Trout concentrate in pools (natural and artificial) for cover; this behavior does not necessarily indicate pools were historically more prevalent.
- **Species Movement:** Gila Trout are largely sedentary except when displaced by flooding, implying natural recolonization of vacated habitat is slow in human timescales.

## Key Findings

### Population Biology & Monitoring
- Species abundance naturally fluctuates cyclically due to natural events and traits inherent to the species; distinguishing natural cycle variation from human-caused decline is a central management challenge.
- Single-point population estimates are unreliable indicators of long-term status; estimates from low-flow years may read "unnaturally low," while high-flow year estimates may read "unnaturally high."
- Example of population volatility: Sheep Corral Canyon Gila Trout were nearly absent in late 1970s surveys but were found abundant with strong recruitment by 1991.
- Main Diamond Creek's pre-fire population was artificially inflated by Depression-era artificial stream structures that increased carrying capacity beyond natural levels.
- **Security metric:** The Service considers a distributed population (e.g., 5,000 fish across 10 streams) more secure than a single large population (e.g., 20,000 fish in one stream) due to reduced catastrophic risk.
- The Service employs a composite assessment approach: abundance, range, size-structure, recruitment, relative condition (length-to-weight ratio), and environmental context — tracked regularly per stream.
- 1993 Recovery Team sampling in **Iron, McKnight, Sacaton, and South Diamond creeks** found considerably more Gila Trout than petitioners' population estimates suggested, though sampling intensity is intentionally kept low to minimize adverse effects on the species.

### Genetics & Hybridization
- Small, isolated populations carry greater extinction risk than larger contiguous populations; genetic diversity preservation is deemed critical to long-term recovery (citing [[Behnke 1992]], pp. 227–234).
- The Service treats **McKenna Creek** and **Little Creek** populations as protected Gila Trout populations despite taxonomic uncertainty, due to potential unique conserved alleles.
- Hybrid populations can theoretically revert toward original native genotype over time if non-native stocking was limited (few individuals, few stocking events); however, repeated/multiple non-native stockings tend to permanently mask or eliminate unique native genetic material.
- No surviving pure **Cutthroat Trout** populations are known in the Gila drainage; only rainbow-hybridized populations persist. **Brown Trout** also occur in several streams and are identified as an additional problematic non-native (previously underemphasized by petitioners).

### Stream-Specific Renovation & Recovery Status (as of 1994)
- **Main Diamond Creek:** Extirpated by 1989 Divide Fire; tentatively scheduled to receive Gila Trout reintroduction in autumn 1994.
- **McKnight Creek:** Not impacted by Divide Fire; population was severely reduced by 1989 winter flooding but has since recovered to near carrying capacity per recent surveys.
- **Mogollon Creek** (above barrier falls): Tentatively scheduled for renovation in summer 1994.
- **West Fork Gila River** (above White Creek confluence, including tributaries): Thoroughly inventoried June 6–9, 1994.
- **Black Canyon drainage:** No known Gila Trout or identifiable hybrids present.
- **Negrito Creek:** Not being considered for renovation due to presence of several native (non-trout) fish species.
- **Dry Creek** (below barrier falls): Under consideration for restoration; a "boulder field" area presents significant logistical challenges to complete non-native salmonid removal.
- **Spider Creek and Little Dry Creek:** Both fishless; tentatively scheduled to receive Gila Trout in 1995.
- **Rain Creek and West Fork Mogollon Creek:** Planned for inventory in 1995.
- **South Fork Whitewater Creek** and other streams: Considered marginal habitat.
- No verified data (via morphometrics, electrophoresis, or mitochondrial DNA) substantiate "Gila-type trout" presence in most disputed streams beyond McKenna and Little creeks.

### Policy & Regulatory Context
- The Service determined that **critical habitat designation was not necessary** for recovery, since most current/future Gila Trout streams lie on Forest Service-administered public lands already subject to Section 7(a)(2) consultation requirements under the Endangered Species Act.
- The primary limiting factor in recovery is described as **insufficient funding** for implementing the Recovery Plan, not absence of critical habitat designation.
- An intra-Service Section 7 consultation with the USFWS Division of Federal Aid resulted in a commitment from the **New Mexico Department of Game and Fish** to cease stocking non-native trout in Gila River drainage waters where they could reach Gila Trout habitat.
- Wilderness designation provides partial habitat protection (prohibiting roads, mining, timber harvest) but does not prohibit pre-existing livestock grazing.
- Recommended reference literature: [[Behnke 1992]]; Meehan (1991) *Influences of Forest and Rangeland Management on Salmonid Fishes and Their Habitats* (American Fisheries Society Special Publication 19); Susan L. Flader's *Thinking Like a Mountain*.

### Corrections to Petition Claims
- No Gila Trout were lost during transport to Mescalero National Fish Hatchery (contradicting petition's claim of approximately 300 deaths).
- Eagle Creek is tributary to the **Gila River**, not the San Francisco River.
- Citation correction: **Smith and Stearley (1989)**, published in *Fisheries (Bethesda)* 14(1):5 — not "Trans. Amer. Fish. Soc. 122:1–33."
- [[Miller 1961]] based lower-elevation Gila Trout occurrence records on interviews with "old timers," not formal historical records.
- Citations to Levins (1970) and Lande (1988) referenced in the petition were not found in its literature cited section.
