# The Breeding Habits of the Silvery Minnow (*Hybognathus regius*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1939 study by Edward C. Raney, based on observations at the south end of [[Cayuga Lake]] near Ithaca, New York, documents the breeding biology of the [[Silvery Minnow]] ([[Hybognathus Regius]]), a [[Cyprinidae]] species. The author reports that the species is among the earliest northeastern cyprinids to spawn, migrating in large numbers into shallow, vegetated coves in spring. Spawning occurs in the daytime on the bottom of these coves, where non-adhesive eggs are scattered over decaying plant material. The paper also describes sexual dimorphism, fecundity, egg and larval development, early growth, and the species' value as a bait and forage fish.

## Background & Description

**Prior literature.** The author notes that little had been recorded on the species' breeding habits. Earlier accounts gave conflicting or general information:
- Bean (1903) reported spring spawning.
- Wright and Allen (1913) gave April 25 to June as the breeding period, with quiet water and riffles as the breeding place.
- Adams and Hankinson (1928) inferred spring spawning from schools of young about one inch long found in July in [[Oneida Lake]].

**Economic value.** The species is an important spring bait minnow for [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) and [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox niger*) in the Finger Lakes Region. The author notes it may be easily raised in small ponds, and results from the [[Cornell Experimental Hatchery]] were to be published separately.

**Sexual dimorphism.** During spawning, males are light yellow along the sides, with a silvery belly and faint yellow fins, and are much darker than females when viewed from above. Local bait fishermen considered the sexes to be separate species. Breeding males bear small light-colored tubercles on the head, chin, cheeks, opercles, scale edges and fins, most developed on the pectoral and ventral fins. Ripe females have only a few minute tubercles. Females are generally larger and have a swollen abdomen during spawning.

**Size records.** Reported maxima vary widely:
- Largest male examined: 83 mm standard length (SL).
- Largest female examined: 100 mm SL.
- Fowler (1909) stated 9 inches; Adams and Hankinson (1928) took "none more than four inches" at Oneida Lake; Smith (1907) gave 6–7 inches maximum.
- Largest of several thousand New York specimens: 120 mm (4.8 in) total length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spring migration.** Large numbers move into the coves off lower [[Fall Creek]] in mid-March, peaking in the last week of April. A few individuals move in during October and November, but no large fall schools were seen. Males arrive earlier and leave later than females. The species remained the most abundant bait-sized minnow in May.

**Habitat.** Spawning areas are quiet coves with decaying leaves, branches and grass on the bottom. Later in the season these coves become choked with vegetation, including water clover (*Marsilia quadrifolia*), pondweeds (*Potamogeton*) and spatterdock (*Nymphozanthus advena*). Shoreline [[Reed Canary Grass]] (*Phalaris arundinacea*) provides shelter when fish are frightened. Spawning took place in water up to about one foot deep near the grass, while the central, deeper waters (1 to 2.5 feet) held many females.

**Associated species.** The author lists common associates in the quiet coves, including [[Common Carp]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Common Bullhead]], [[Tadpole Madtom]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and several other cyprinids and centrarchids. The juveniles may serve as forage for juvenile Pickerel and Pike, which spawn several weeks before.

**Temperature.** During observations, air temperature ranged from 20°C to 28°C and water from 13°C to 20.5°C. A sudden cold snap dropped water temperature and halted spawning on April 30, 1938.

**Feeding.** Food consists mainly of microscopic material in the bottom ooze, especially diatoms and other algae. The species has very long coiled intestines suited to digesting this plant matter. Females' guts seldom contained food.

## Key Findings

**Migration and abundance**
- On April 24, 1938, commercial bait fishermen captured approximately 4,000 adults in a single 50-foot bag seine haul through a cove 75 feet wide and 200 feet long. Repeated hauls gave slightly smaller catches.
- Of 319 unselected adults taken over breeding areas during late April and May 1938, 61 were females and 258 were males.
- A Lake Ontario collection of 1 September 1931 contained 36 males and 52 females, suggesting that male predominance is seasonal.
- By June 1 only males remained in the spawning cove. After mid-June adults were rarely taken and had apparently moved into the lake.
- The author considers the collection of unspawned fish in such numbers over several years to be poor conservation practice.

**Spawning behavior**
- Spawning was observed April 26–28, 1938, and was probably continuing into mid-May.
- Spawning occurs only during daylight, from about 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., peaking between noon and 2 p.m. Fish rest quietly on the bottom at night.
- A female approaching spawning is followed by 1 to 10 males. The first two males swim under her on opposite sides, and the female and males vibrate rapidly for a second or two to release eggs.
- Only the two males alongside the female appear to be effective. Spawning sometimes occurred in small ponds without vegetation. No territory is held and no fighting was observed.
- Males often spawned again soon after with a different female. The number of eggs per spawning is probably small, and the interval between spawnings was not determined.
- Non-adhesive eggs are laid on the bottom ooze, sometimes partly buried under a few millimeters of mud. The breeding vibration makes the water temporarily turbid.

**Eggs, incubation and fecundity**
- Freshly laid eggs are slightly milky and about 1 mm in diameter.
- Eggs hatched in six to seven days at water temperatures of 13.3°C to 20.5°C.
- Egg counts: about 6,600 developed eggs in a 90 mm SL female; about 3,000 in females of 68–70 mm SL; about 2,000 in a 60 mm SL female.
- Eggs were matured in late fall; a large female taken in the Hudson River in late November had well-developed eggs.

**Larval and juvenile development**
- Newly hatched larvae averaged 5.5 mm SL (6 mm total length), recovered on May 2.
- Larvae first stay near the bottom and rise to the surface as the yolk sac is absorbed. About two weeks after hatching they form small schools near shore among emergent vegetation.
- Juvenile growth: about 20 mm SL on July 1; 30 mm SL by July 15; 40 mm SL by August 5 (near 2 inches total length).
- Some females mature and spawn at age one, at 50–55 mm SL. Males probably mature at age two.

**Unconfirmed observation**
- Males were seen several times apparently eating their own eggs, but stomach analysis did not confirm this.
