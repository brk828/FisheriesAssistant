# IBP Monthly Review: South American Productivity Programmes and North-East British Coastal Pollution Study (1967)

#marine-biology #fisheries-science #ecosystem-productivity

## Executive Summary

This October 1967 issue of the [[International Biological Programme]] (IBP) monthly review is a programme newsletter, not a primary research paper. It covers two items. The first is a report by Special Correspondent Angela Croome on the growth of IBP national committees in South America, the need for taxonomic work before productivity studies, the [[Humboldt Current]] fishery and [[Peruvian Guano Industry]] decline, and high-altitude adaptation research. The second is a note on a three-year [[Pollution Study]] of the north-east British coast under the Productivity of Marine Communities (PM) section. The issue also lists the IBP's section convenors and central office details.

## Background & Description

The IBP is organised into thematic sections, each with a convenor:

- **Productivity of Terrestrial Communities (PT)**: Prof. F. Bourlière, Paris
- **Production Processes (PP)**: Academician I. Málek, Prague
- **Conservation of Terrestrial Communities (CT)**: E. M. Nicholson, The Nature Conservancy, London
- **Productivity of Freshwater Communities (PF)**: Prof. A. Hasler, University of Wisconsin
- **Productivity of Marine Communities (PM)**: Dr B. H. Ketchum, Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution
- **Human Adaptability (HA)**: Prof. J. S. Weiner, Royal Anthropological Institute, London
- **Use and Management of Biological Resources (UM)**: Dr G. K. Davis, University of Florida

The President is Prof. J. G. Baer (Switzerland) and the Scientific Director is Dr E. B. Worthington. The central office is in London.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Humboldt Current upwelling (Peru and Chile).** The current flows from the southwest and is deflected by the South American coast, mainly off Peru. This produces strong upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich bottom water. The result is the largest fishery by tonnage in the world, at roughly eight million tonnes per year. Most of the catch is processed into fish meal rather than eaten directly, and about 20 per cent is wasted. The fishery was largely developed within the previous 15 years.

**Seabird and guano system.** The coastal guano industry, which had long supported the Peruvian coastal economy, is being displaced by the fishery. Estimates given in the review:

- Roughly 2.5 million tonnes of fish per year are converted into guano by seabirds (the text gives "2-5 million tonnes," which is reproduced as printed).
- Past guano yields are thought to have been supported by about 16 million birds.
- On the basis of current guano production, the bird population is estimated to have fallen to about five million.

The review frames the central IBP question as identifying the point at which exploitation gives maximum return without irreversibly damaging the resource.

**Andean high-altitude systems.** The review notes the human and animal populations of the Andes, including the [[Llama]], as subjects for adaptation research. South America has fewer large grazing animals than Southern Africa; the cattle and sheep on its extensive grasslands are introduced species.

**Inshore pollution (north-east Britain).** The review describes inshore pollution studies as a leading growth area for fisheries-relevant research, because pollution affects the populations most important to fisheries. The north-east coast is identified as severely polluted.

## Key Findings

**1. IBP expansion in South America.** In May 1967 Brazil was the only South American country with an IBP national committee. By the time of the report, Argentina, Chile, Uruguay and Venezuela had formed committees, and Colombia and Peru were preparing to do so. Political difficulties, rather than a lack of biological interest, had slowed progress. Latin American scientists see IBP as a means of improving regional communication and collaboration. Dr Worthington made these observations during meetings in July 1967 in the main Latin American centres.

**2. Systematics as a prerequisite.** A recurring theme was that taxonomic identification must precede productivity measurement. The review also notes local resentment over northern-hemisphere scientists who have collected extensively in the region and taken their specimens and knowledge home.

**3. Agricultural genetic resources.** South America is the origin of many crops, including potatoes, maize, cassava, okra and several cotton species. Plant breeders still draw on these gene pools, and the review says Latin American countries have much to contribute to the UM Plant Gene Pool project.

**4. Peruvian fishery sustainability.** The Humboldt fishery's scale and its effect on guano birds frame the IBP's ecological question about sustainable yield. Marine scientists in Peru, Chile and elsewhere are already carrying out population studies and want to extend them through collaboration.

**5. British north-east coast pollution project.** A three-year project under the PM section will study pollution "in depth" from Spurn Head to the Scottish border. Key details:

- Eleven research groups from five northern universities are involved.
- The Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Fisheries' Burnham-on-Crouch Shellfish Laboratory is co-operating.
- The project is co-ordinated by Professor R. B. Clark, head of the University of Newcastle's Dove Marine Laboratory.
- The budget is £75,000.
- The scheme has two parts: field studies of the sea and shore, and laboratory chemical investigations.
