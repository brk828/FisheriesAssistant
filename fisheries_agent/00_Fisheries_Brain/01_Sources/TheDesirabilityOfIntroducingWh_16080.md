# The Desirability of Introducing White Bass, Roccus chrysops, Into California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #serranidae #fisheries-management #species-introduction

## Executive Summary

This 1964 administrative report evaluates the suitability of introducing [[White Bass]] (*Roccus chrysops*) into California reservoirs to create a pelagic game fishery utilizing abundant forage fish populations, particularly [[Threadfin Shad]]. The assessment, based on literature review, correspondence, and field visits to key white bass waters, concludes that white bass would likely thrive in larger California reservoirs with threadfin shad populations, potentially increasing game fish yields substantially. The primary uncertainty concerns availability of suitable spawning substrate. The report also addresses potential impacts on [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] fisheries, concluding that while white bass would likely establish populations there, they would probably not significantly harm [[Striped Bass]] or [[King Salmon]] populations. The study was conducted under Dingell-Johnson Project California F-18-R, "Experimental Management of Warmwater Reservoirs."

## Background & Description

[[White Bass]] belong to the family [[Serranidae]], closely related to [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*), [[Yellow Bass]] (*Roccus mississippiensis*), [[White Perch]] (*Roccus americanus*), and two European species. They are distinguished from striped bass primarily by their deeper bodies (body depth more than one-third body length versus less than one-third in striped bass), arched backs, and differences in tongue dentition (single patch versus two parallel patches). Most white bass have 13 or 14 soft rays in the second dorsal fin, compared to 11 or 12 in striped bass. All serranids in this group display horizontal black stripes along their sides with dark gray or olive backs grading to white bellies.

White bass occur naturally in the [[Mississippi River]] drainage from Minnesota and Wisconsin to the Gulf of Mexico, throughout the [[Great Lakes]] (except Lake Superior), and in Gulf coastal rivers from Mississippi into northern Mexico. They have been successfully introduced into several western states (New Mexico, Utah, Colorado, Nevada) and southeastern states (Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia). The species is primarily a lake and reservoir fish, though populations exist in larger rivers. They have developed particularly important fisheries in large southern reservoirs.

White bass are relatively short-lived, with few individuals exceeding four years in the southern part of their range, though northern populations commonly include 7- and 8-year-old fish. Maximum weight rarely exceeds four pounds. No cases of stunting have been documented, distinguishing them from many other warmwater species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Requirements:** White bass thrive in large reservoirs (minimum successful populations in Oklahoma occurred in 330-400 acre impoundments) with abundant pelagic forage fish. They tolerate wide-ranging limnological conditions including varying depths (20-400 feet), alkalinities (30-110 ppm), turbidities, and thermal stratification patterns. They survived in [[Great Salt Plains Reservoir]], Oklahoma, until chloride concentrations reached 6,000 ppm. The species is primarily pelagic, rarely captured in cove sampling but commonly taken in open water and along beaches, particularly at night.

**Spawning Biology:** Spawning occurs in running water or shoal areas at temperatures of 58-75°F, typically April through June depending on latitude. The spawning period at any locality lasts 5-10 days. Males mature younger and smaller than females and arrive at spawning grounds first. Eggs are demersal and adhesive, requiring firm substrate (gravel, rocks, vegetation, logs, or riprap). Females produce 241,000-933,000 eggs (average 565,000). Eggs hatch in 46 hours at 60°F. White bass are gregarious spawners with multiple males surrounding each female. Spawning migrations can extend up to 150 miles upstream in some systems.

**Food Habits:** Young white bass feed primarily on crustaceans (particularly Daphnia) and insects (mayflies, chironomids). Adults consume predominantly fish (60-90% of diet volume), with [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Yellow Perch]], bluegill, and black crappie being primary prey species. The presence of abundant forage fish, particularly shad species, is essential for successful white bass populations. No cannibalism has been documented.

**Growth and Mortality:** Growth rates vary geographically. In Oklahoma, fish average 7.5 inches and 0.19 pounds at age 1, 12.2 inches and 0.84 pounds at age 2, and 14.4 inches and 1.39 pounds at age 3. Northern populations grow more slowly but live longer. Reservoir fish grow faster than river fish during their first year. Only 6% of Oklahoma samples exceeded 3 years old.

**Population Dynamics:** Northern populations exhibit extreme fluctuations, sometimes dominated by single year classes for extended periods. Southern populations are more stable but still show definite fluctuations. In Oklahoma, statewide average angler harvest is estimated at 10 pounds per acre per year, with measured harvests ranging from 0.3 to 22.3 pounds per acre. White bass often become dominant game fish within 2-3 years of introduction into suitable waters.

## Key Findings

**Suitability for California Reservoirs:**

1. California reservoirs larger than 500 acres with [[Threadfin Shad]] populations should satisfy space and food requirements for white bass establishment.

2. The primary limiting factor is likely spawning habitat availability. Most California reservoirs lack ideal spawning tributaries due to small size, seasonal drying, or cold temperatures from snowmelt. Major Sierra tributaries may not reach suitable spawning temperatures (60°F) until late May or June, later than typical spawning periods at comparable latitudes.

3. Success would likely depend on spawning in reservoirs themselves or minor tributaries. Potential spawning substrates include rock outcroppings, riprapped dams, terrestrial vegetation in drawdown zones, and small tributaries with suitable temperatures.

4. If established, white bass would almost certainly increase game fish yields in larger reservoirs, potentially providing harvests of 10+ pounds per acre annually.

5. Competition with existing game fish would likely be minimal and possibly beneficial (e.g., predation on stunted bluegill populations). White bass would utilize currently underexploited pelagic forage fish resources.

**Potential Delta Impacts:**

1. White bass would probably establish populations in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], competing with striped bass and king salmon to some degree.

2. The Delta is likely better striped bass than white bass habitat, so striped bass should remain dominant. Factors supporting this conclusion include: ideal striped bass habitat conditions, limited white bass spawning substrate (soft silt), white bass rarity as dominant river species, and their limited populations in Gulf Coast estuaries.

3. White bass predation on downstream migrant king salmon would likely be insignificant because: (a) white bass populations would probably be small, (b) any increase would be partially offset by decreased striped bass abundance, (c) existing striped bass predation shows positive correlation with salmon abundance (r=0.52, significant at 95% level for 1939-1961), indicating predation is not a dominant mortality factor, and (d) density-dependent compensation in salmon mortality would buffer impacts.

4. A management challenge would arise from the similarity between white bass and striped bass, making separate regulations difficult to enforce. Geographic limitation of regulations could address this issue.

**Fishery Characteristics:**

1. White bass provide extended fishing seasons in southern reservoirs (March through October or longer), not just brief seasonal opportunities.

2. They are easily caught by novice anglers and families, often in large numbers (200+ per day not uncommon in Oklahoma), using minnows or various artificial lures.

3. They rank among the most important sport fish in Oklahoma, rivaling white crappie, and have become dominant game fish in numerous reservoirs within 2-3 years of introduction.

4. Introduction into [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] resulted in white bass comprising 2.7-9.9% of angler catch within 11 years, though striped bass remained 10 times more abundant.

**Recommendation:** The report concludes that white bass are suitable for introduction into larger California reservoirs with threadfin shad populations, with spawning habitat availability being the primary uncertainty. Benefits would likely outweigh risks, though careful site selection would be important. Potential Delta impacts, while requiring consideration, would probably not significantly harm existing striped bass or king salmon fisheries.
