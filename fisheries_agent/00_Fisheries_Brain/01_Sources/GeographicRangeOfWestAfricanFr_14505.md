# Geographic Range of West African Freshwater Fishes: Role of Biological Characteristics and Stochastic Processes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Bernard Hugueny]] (1990, *Acta Ecologica* 11(3): 351–375) examines why freshwater fishes of the [[Nilo-Sudanian Region]] differ in geographic range size. Range size is defined as the number of rivers in which a species occurs, using presence/absence data from 20 river catchments and 363 species. The author tests a stochastic [[Null Model]] assuming equal extinction and immigration probabilities for all species, then uses multiple regression to identify biological traits correlated with range size. The main conclusion is that immigration-related factors, particularly dispersal capacity linked to body size, explain range size far better than extinction-related factors.

## Background & Description

- **Study question:** Are range sizes explained by chance alone, or by intrinsic biological traits and interspecific interactions?
- **Study region:** The Nilo-Sudanian region (per [[Durand and Lévêque]], 1980) includes the Senegal, Gambia, Niger, Chad, Nile, Volta and Omo systems, plus Ivorian rivers such as the [[Bandama River]] and [[Sassandra River]]. It is one of three main centres of endemism in west and central Africa.
- **Data sources:** Presence/absence data from Lévêque et al. (in preparation) and [[Teugels]] et al. (1988). The Mé and Tabou catchments were excluded because of underestimated richness.
- **Range size index:** Number of rivers occupied, chosen because presence/absence data do not allow calculation of range area.
- **Traits examined:** Body length (maximum standard length, cm), trophic level (four categories), accessory respiratory device, and primary/secondary status (sea-water tolerance). Peripheral (marine-origin) species were excluded.
- **Methods:** [[Multiple Regression Analysis]], [[Monte Carlo Simulation]] (after Connor and Simberloff, 1979, with 10,000 permutations for the Niger and sample size 100), and a [[Markov Chain Model]] for the stochastic model.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Rivers are treated as [[Biogeographic Islands]], separated by dry land or marine barriers that are difficult for strictly freshwater organisms to cross.
- Species richness of rivers is positively related to catchment area and discharge (Hugueny, 1989a), so richness differences reflect habitat characteristics rather than chance.
- Dispersal between catchments requires crossing watersheds on land, surviving sea water, or contact through lagoons, flood plains or stream capture.
- The [[Pleistocene Climate Fluctuations]] (alternating wet and dry periods; last dry period about 12,000 years ago) probably altered connections between catchments and may mask relationships between traits and range size.
- The author notes that the [[Equilibrium Theory of Island Biogeography]] predicts extinction risk rises as island area falls, which conflicts with the model's assumption of equal extinction probability across rivers.

## Key Findings

**Range size distribution**
- Range size frequencies follow a negative power function (r = 0.98, 15 d.f.), resembling "hollow curves" reported for other taxa.
- Many species occur in few rivers; 134 species occur in only one river, while a small number occur in 16 or more (Table I).
- The stochastic model reproduces a power-like shape only for small average range sizes. At the observed average of 4.3, its distribution is symmetrical and unimodal and its variance is smaller than observed, so the equal-ability hypothesis is rejected.

**Biological correlates of range size**
- Log body length correlates significantly and positively with log range size (the only significant partial coefficient in model 1). Large fishes tend to occupy more rivers, which the author interprets as larger species having greater dispersal ability.
- Trophic category contributes significantly in model 2 (F(288,3) = 2.92, p < 5%). Size-for-size, micro-predators (invertebrate feeders) have the widest ranges, followed by top predators, omnivores and herbivore-detritivores.
- Possession of an accessory respiratory device does not significantly affect range size once body size is accounted for.
- Primary versus secondary (sea-tolerant) status does not significantly affect range size once body size is accounted for.

**Species richness and average range size**
- Average range size of resident species falls as river species richness rises. Richness across rivers ranged from 18 (Dodo) to 425 (Chad) species in Table VI, with the Niger holding 212 species and average range size of 5.85.
- Monte Carlo simulations with fixed richness and range sizes produce a similar exponential relationship (r = 0.99 for simulated values). The observed and simulated curves are nearly superimposed, so the pattern reflects a statistical property of the presence/absence matrix rather than selective extinction.

**Conclusions**
- Effects of extinction-related factors appear negligible relative to immigration-related factors in explaining range sizes.
- The author proposes that dispersal ability, positively related to body size, explains the size–range relationship. Extinction-risk explanations (as proposed by [[Brown]], 1981, for mammals) are not supported for these fishes.
- Hollow-curve distributions may partly reflect the general rule that small species are more numerous than large ones ([[Van Valen]], 1972), since body length distribution resembles the range size distribution.
- Historical factors (Pleistocene climate change, taxon cycles) could not be assessed quantitatively due to insufficient phylogenetic and palaeontological data.
