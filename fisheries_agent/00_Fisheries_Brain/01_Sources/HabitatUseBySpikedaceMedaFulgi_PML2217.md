# Habitat Use by Spikedace, Meda fulgida, in Southwestern Streams with Reference to Probable Habitat Competition by Red Shiner, Notropis lutrensis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 study by [[Joseph N. Rinne]] (USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, Tempe, Arizona), published in *The Southwestern Naturalist* 36(1):7–13, quantifies the physical habitat used by [[Spikedace]] ([[Meda Fulgida]]), a threatened cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. Habitat was measured in several desert streams and compared with the habitat of the introduced [[Red Shiner]] ([[Notropis Lutrensis]]). Spikedace used habitat in proportion to its availability, and schools of more than 10 fish occupied deeper, slower water. The author concludes that habitat partitioning may occur where the two cyprinids co-occur, but that behavioral and controlled experiments are needed to confirm actual competition.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and range:** *Meda fulgida* is a monotypic genus endemic to the Gila River basin in Arizona and New Mexico. Its range has been markedly reduced, although the species can be spatially and temporally abundant where it persists.
- **Size:** A small cyprinid, under 100 mm total length.
- **Conservation status:** Listed as threatened by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission (1982) and by the U.S. Department of the Interior (1986). New Mexico lists it as endangered.
- **Prior knowledge:** Earlier work was largely qualitative and focused on distribution and taxonomy. Life-history information came mainly from [[Aravaipa Creek]] in southeastern Arizona. Earlier descriptions gave depths of "0.6 to 1.3 m" in "swift deep pools" and "deeper parts of long pools," with adults also noted on shallow, algae-dominated riffles.
- **Red shiner:** A non-native cyprinid introduced into Arizona in 1953. It has been suspected as a competitor because the two species were often collected together in the same seine haul or electrofishing pass in the upper [[Verde River]] and, to a lesser extent, in upper Gila basin streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study streams**

- **[[Aravaipa Creek]]:** Low-gradient, small (3–5 m wide), often braided, and hydrologically stable. Substrate was primarily gravel-pebble (3–64 mm). Modal discharge ranged between 0.5 and 1.0 m³/s, with peak flows in winter, although late-summer storms could produce sudden, violent floods.
- **[[Upper Gila River]] streams:** Lower Gila River at Cliff, and the Middle and West forks of the Gila River. These were higher-gradient (2–5%), wider (5–30 m), and located at higher elevation (>1,200 m). Discharges were highly variable, though modal flows were greater than in Aravaipa Creek.
- **[[Verde River]]:** Sampled in April 1985, December 1988 (data from these two dates were not significantly different and were combined), and January 1990.

**Methods (summary)**

- Spikedace were immobilized by [[Electrofishing]] and captured with a 1-m block net placed 1–2 m downstream.
- Physical habitat was measured along transects using a habitat sampler: depth (modified meter rule), velocity (5 cm above the substrate), and substrate (visually classified into seven classes, including sand-gravel and gravel-pebble intermediates).
- Aravaipa Creek was sampled seven times from 1982 to 1985 across winter, spring, and summer. The upper Gila basin was sampled once, in June 1983, at six sites; spikedace were taken at three.
- Statistical tests included unpaired t-tests, Chi-square analyses for substrate, [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA) with Bartlett's test for variance homogeneity, and Tukey's tests for pairwise differences.
- Because the same method was used in other studies of native fishes in Aravaipa Creek and the upper Gila basin, [[Habitat Availability]] could be estimated for those streams. Verde River data came only from locations where the two species were captured, so availability could not be estimated there.

**Habitat associations**

- Shear zones, eddies, and areas along canyon walls or downstream of large boulders created reduced, eddying current and pool formation. Spikedace were frequently associated with these structures.
- Preferred substrate was most often gravel-pebble with lesser sand.
- The Verde River reach containing spikedace began at the head of a higher-gradient riffle, and eddying currents and shear zones were common in that 30-m reach.

## Key Findings

**1. Habitat use and availability (upper Gila and Aravaipa)**

- In Aravaipa Creek, spikedace occupied significantly deeper and slower water over smaller substrate than was generally available.
- At Cliff and the West and Middle forks of the Gila River, depth and velocity at occupied sites did not differ significantly from unoccupied sites (P > 0.05).
- Substrate under occupied water was significantly smaller than that available in the Middle Fork (P < 0.001) and West Fork (P = 0.03), and smaller than that used in the lower Gila River.
- Depth variances were homogeneous. ANOVA found no significant difference in depth among streams where spikedace were present (P = 0.60).
- Velocities occupied by spikedace did differ among streams (P = 0.004). Tukey's test grouped the Middle and West forks (mean 14.2 cm/s) apart from the lower Gila and Aravaipa Creek (mean 30.1 cm/s). The Verde River mean was intermediate (21.0 cm/s).
- Available velocities varied significantly among upper Gila streams (P = 0.01) but not in the habitats occupied by spikedace (P = 0.20).
- Overall physical habitat availability did not differ significantly among the five streams, or between the streams and the habitat occupied by spikedace.

**2. Schooling and relative abundance**

- Using relative numbers of fish per transect, spikedace preferred deeper, slower water in Aravaipa Creek.
- Schools of more than 10 fish (n = 70 transects) occupied a mean depth of about 27 cm and velocities below 25 cm/s. Transects with a single fish averaged 15.7 cm deep and 36.6 cm/s.
- Transects with 12 or more fish had a mean velocity of 22.3 cm/s, and those with 10 or more averaged 25.7 cm/s.
- The same pattern did not appear in the upper Gila streams or the Verde River.

**3. Life-stage differences (Aravaipa Creek)**

- Mean depth increased from larval (14.4 cm) to juvenile (17.1 cm) to adult (about 20 cm), but the difference was not significant (P = 0.13).
- Larval and juvenile spikedace tended to occupy slower water (26.6 and 26.9 cm/s) than adults (35 cm/s). This difference was also not significant (P = 0.10).
- Substrate did not differ significantly among life stages.

**4. Verde River co-occurrence**

- Of 34 transects sampled in one Verde River reach in 1989, 24 contained only red shiners, 9 contained only spikedace, and 1 contained both species.
- In January 1990, spikedace were not captured at the 24 sites that had held only red shiners in 1989. They were captured in contiguous downstream reaches with depths and velocities similar to 1989 values.
- Red shiners occupied water depths similar to spikedace but significantly slower velocities.
- All 24 red-shiner-only sites had an organic-silt "false substrate" created by macrophytes (e.g., *Chara*, *Potamogeton*) trapping fine silt and sand. Those same riffles had been occupied by spikedace in the previous year, before the false substrate developed.
- All spikedace sites in 1990 had open, variable mineral substrate (gravel to cobble).

**5. Comparison with literature**

- Mean depths measured here (about 20–30 cm) were far shallower than the 60–720 cm figures reported by [[Miller and Hubbs]] (1960), but matched the 20–30 cm range reported by R. M. Anderson and D. L. Propst et al.
- Propst et al. reported adult spikedace in the upper Gila at a mean depth of 19.3 cm and velocity of 49.1 cm/s, which was higher than the present findings, possibly due to a different velocity measurement point (0.6 depth vs. 5 cm off bottom).
- Anderson reported spikedace in moderate to swift riffles less than 30 cm deep over gravel-rubble (15–120 mm).
- Larval spikedace in the upper Gila occupied shallower, slower water than juveniles or adults.

**6. Red shiner habitat and possible competition**

- Red shiners consistently occupy water deeper than 20 cm and were most abundant in slow (0–7 cm/s) backwaters and nonflowing pools over mixed substrate with little sand.
- The species is a generalist that tolerates turbid water, avoids clear water, and has a high critical thermal maximum.
- Matthews (1985), studying about 100 streams, found red shiners abundant in both "deep, swift" and "shallow, slow" water.
- Matthews found red shiners more abundant where native fish diversity was low. The low faunal diversity of western streams may give the red shiner a competitive advantage over native fishes.
- Tolerance of turbidity, warm water, and low dissolved oxygen may give red shiners an advantage over spikedace when western land use (timber harvesting, mining, agriculture) degrades habitat. Conversely, the clear, shallow character of many western streams may favor spikedace.

**7. Conclusions and recommendations**

- Spikedace habitat use in these streams confirmed earlier qualitative descriptions. Spikedace occupy deeper, slower, gravel-pebble habitats, especially in shear zones and eddies, and schools favor deeper, slower water.
- Comparison of habitat suggests overlap may occur, but preliminary Verde River data indicate different patterns of velocity and substrate use.
- The author recommends further habitat analyses in other streams where the species co-occur, plus controlled experiments on behavioral interactions among life stages, to define actual [[Competition]] between the two species.
- Both abiotic and biotic factors must be evaluated before conclusions on dispersion and competition can be drawn. The author cites [[Meffe]] (1984), who found abiotic disturbance key to the coexistence of the native [[Gila Topminnow]] and the introduced [[Mosquitofish]] in the Southwest.
