## Executive Summary
This memorandum documents the capture, treatment, and death of a single adult female [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) recovered from [[Lake Mohave]] near Katherine's Landing in April 1988. The fish was collected by Arizona Game and Fish personnel and transferred to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] for rehabilitation due to its poor physical condition, including apparent loss of caudal (tail) function. Despite chemical treatments for presumptive parasitic or fungal infection, the fish died after one week in captivity. A gonadal sample (ovary) was extracted post-mortem and shipped to Dr. W. L. Minckley at Arizona State University, indicating the specimen's value for ongoing reproductive/genetic research on this critically imperiled [[Colorado River Basin]] endemic.

## Background & Description
- **Total Length:** 43.5 cm
- **Weight:** 740.0 g
- **Sex:** Female (confirmed via ovary extraction)
- **Condition at capture:** Poor; notable loss of caudal fin/tail function
- **Locomotor impairment:** Fish was unable to use its tail for swimming; propulsion was generated primarily via anterior body movements — suggestive of caudal fin damage, disease, or severe muscular degeneration
- **Behavioral/postural abnormality:** Fish exhibited persistent inverted positioning in tank, indicating severe loss of buoyancy control or neurological/physical distress

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The [[Bonytail Chub]] is a large-river [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] historically native to the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and its major tributaries, adapted to swift, turbid, warmwater conditions. By the late 20th century, wild populations had collapsed dramatically due to damming, flow regulation, habitat alteration, and introduction of non-native predatory and competitive fishes. [[Lake Mohave]], an impounded reservoir on the lower Colorado River, represents one of the last refugial habitats supporting remnant wild-origin bonytail chub populations, though the reservoir environment differs substantially from the species' ancestral riverine niche. The species' presence in this artificial lentic system underscores its precarious conservation status and reliance on managed refugia and hatchery intervention (e.g., [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]) for survival.

## Key Findings
- **Rarity of specimen:** The capture of a wild bonytail chub in Lake Mohave in 1988 represents a significant conservation event, reflecting the species' extreme scarcity even at that time.
- **Clinical treatment protocol:** Sequential treatments included:
  - 1:40,000 Formalin bath (04/22/88) — standard for external parasites/fungal infection
  - 10 ppm Furacin (nifurpirinol) in Tank 2-A (04/25/88) — antibacterial/antifungal treatment
- **Outcome:** Despite intervention, the fish died on 04/28/88, one week after capture, likely due to severe pre-existing physical trauma or disease compromising caudal function and equilibrium.
- **Research value preserved:** Post-mortem, one ovary was excised and express-shipped to Dr. W. L. Minckley (a leading authority on southwestern U.S. desert fishes), indicating intent to salvage reproductive/genetic data despite the animal's death — critical given the species' endangered status.
- **Institutional collaboration:** The case illustrates coordinated multi-agency conservation efforts involving [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and academic researchers, typical of recovery programs for endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes such as the bonytail chub, [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
