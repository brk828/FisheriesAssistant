# Coded-Wire Tag Loss Study: Tag Retention and Natural Adipose Fin Loss in Pacific Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Washington Department of Fisheries technical report (Technical Report No. 65, August 1981, by Lee Blankenship) examines how often [[Coded-Wire Tag]]s (CWTs) are shed by juvenile [[Coho Salmon]] and [[Chinook Salmon]]. The study tested eight groups of roughly 10,000 fish at [[Minter Creek Hatchery]] and compared [[Standard Length Coded-Wire Tag]]s with the newer [[Half Length Coded-Wire Tag]]. Tag loss ranged from 1.13% to 5.33%, and most loss occurred within the first 2 to 4 weeks. The report also documents naturally missing [[Adipose Fin]]s in hatchery and wild salmon, which can be mistaken for tag loss. The author recommends adjusting release figures for tag loss, using estimates taken four weeks after tagging, and using standard-length tags for fish larger than 216/lb.

## Background & Description

The Washington Department of Fisheries (WDF) reports the estimated catch of its CWT-marked fish each year, using data from tag recoveries. Earlier reports handled tag loss inconsistently. The 1974 report used a 15% tag loss figure, based on adult hatchery returns, to adjust release numbers. Other reports adjusted based on tag checks made before release, though these checks were often missing or done too soon after tagging.

The CWT is implanted in the snout of the fish, and the adipose fin is clipped to mark it. Tag loss is the shedding of the implanted tag. The study had four goals:

- Measure tag loss rates.
- Identify factors affecting tag loss.
- Determine how long tag loss continues.
- Assess how much naturally occurring adipose fin marks could affect tag counts.

The study also tested the effectiveness of the half length tag, developed by [[Northwest Marine Technology]].

**Methods**

- **Tag loss experiment:** Eight lots of about 10,000 fish were tagged at Minter Creek Hatchery. Four lots were coho and four were chinook, ranging from 60/lb to 523/lb. Some lots used standard length tags and others used half length tags. The coho lots were checked 3 to 4 times in the first week, weekly for three weeks, then every other week until release. Chinook were checked more often.
- **Check procedure:** About 3,000 fish were crowded and randomly dipped into a holding pen. Sub-lots of about 50 fish were anesthetized in [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222). Fish without tags were identified, and each "no tag" was passed through a horseshoe magnet three times to confirm that the tag was absent.
- **Crew variables:** The half length tag at 523/lb was applied by experienced WDF staff. The other lots were tagged by a "typical" crew hired locally. For the chinook at 278/lb and 396/lb, the crew spent its first day tagging non-study fish and then alternated lot order on later days.
- **Natural adipose fin survey:** Tagging crews recorded missing adipose fins on juvenile coho from the 1977, 1978, and 1979 broods. Adult coho from four Puget Sound and Hood Canal hatcheries, plus [[Simpson Hatchery]] on the Washington Coast, were sampled in 1979. Snouts from fin-marked adults were X-rayed to confirm the absence of a CWT.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hatchery conditions appear to influence natural fin loss. The author suggests that fins are bitten off during feeding, possibly by other fish, and that crowding may increase the loss. Missing fins were often partial, and some had fresh wounds. Losses in hatchery juveniles were considered recent and ongoing, which suggests the observed rates were minimum values.

Wild and stream-reared coho showed much lower natural fin loss, around 0.05% to 0.06%. The author notes that other salmon species are known to show natural adipose marks as well, typically at lower rates than coho. This is likely because they are usually reared for shorter periods and at lower densities.

Fish size and tagging quality also shape tag retention. The author reports that tagging crews tend to have more tag loss on their first day, probably due to unfamiliarity with the equipment and adjustments to headmolds and tagging hardware. Tag loss is expected to increase as fish get smaller, but the author argues that experienced crews may still achieve successful tagging at sizes of 750/lb or smaller.

## Key Findings

**Tag loss results**

- The eight study groups were observed for 121 to 293 days, with an average observation period of 215 days.
- Tag loss ranged from 1.13% to 5.33%. Group values were:
  - Coho 523/lb, half length tag: 5.33%
  - Coho 210/lb, standard tag: 1.45%
  - Coho 110/lb, standard tag: 5.13%
  - Coho 60/lb, standard tag: 1.62%
  - Chinook 216/lb, standard tag: 1.96% (mean from day 29 onward)
  - Chinook 216/lb, half tag: 1.48% (mean from day 29 onward)
  - Chinook 278/lb, half tag: 1.13%
  - Chinook 396/lb, half tag: 1.94% (text value; [[Table 1]] lists 1.84%)
- Tag loss rose sharply for 2 to 4 weeks after tagging, then leveled off.
- [[Chi-Square Trend Test]]s found no significant increasing trend in total loss after day 29 for any group. Half length tag groups showed no significant trend after day 17.
- The chi-square test found no significant difference at the 5% level between half and standard tags on chinook at 216/lb (χ² = 2.22).
- A difference significant at the 5% level, but not the 1% level, was found between chinook at 278/lb and 396/lb (χ² = 5.03).

**Naturally missing adipose fins**

- Hatchery juvenile coho: an average natural adipose loss of about 0.0457% across the 1977, 1978, and 1979 broods. The 1977 brood totaled 1,348,000 fish sampled, and the 1978 brood totaled 1,584,000 fish sampled. Several hatcheries showed much higher local rates; for example, one hatchery's 1978 sample had 0.14%.
- Wild juvenile coho (1978 brood): an average adipose loss of 0.06%. The marks appeared old rather than fresh.
- Adult hatchery coho (1976 brood): an overall natural adipose rate of about 0.95% as printed in the text. Simpson Hatchery had a known problem with natural marks and was 2.12% by itself. Without Simpson, the rate was 0.52%.
- Native adult coho at Sunset Falls on the [[Skykomish River]], 1978: 0.04% (8 of 20,388).
- Fall chinook fingerlings (1977 to 1979 broods): 0.03% natural adipose marks.
- Puget Sound chum fishery (1980): 0.12% natural adipose marks (35 of 29,503).

**Recommendations and conclusions**

- The half length tag is a viable option for small salmon. Previous practice limited CWTs to fish at 250/lb (1.82 g) or larger. This study found low tag loss at 523/lb (0.87 g per fish).
- Tagging at 523/lb with the half length tag required twice as many tagging sessions as tagging at 225/lb.
- For fish larger than 216/lb, the author recommends standard length tags because the number of available half length codes is limited, and no significant difference was found between the two tags at that size.
- Tagging crew quality appears to matter more than fish size for fish larger than 500/lb.
- Tag loss should be adjusted by reducing release figures to the number of retained tags. Estimates taken four weeks after tagging provide a reasonable measure of final loss for practical purposes.
- Natural adipose marks must be separated from actual tag loss. Without that adjustment, an approximately 20% overestimate of a group's contribution could occur.
- The author acknowledges that this study was based on a small number of groups and that further work could refine these findings.
