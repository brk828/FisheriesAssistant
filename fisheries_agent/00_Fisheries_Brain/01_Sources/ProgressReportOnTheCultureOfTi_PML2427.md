# Progress Report on the Culture of Tilapia Hybrids in California (1963–1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tilapia

## Executive Summary

This progress report from the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (Inland Fisheries Administrative Report No. 66-9, May 1966) describes a study begun in December 1963 on the culture of a [[Tilapia Hybrid]] produced by crossing a male [[Zanzibar Tilapia]] with a female [[Javanese Tilapia]]. The study aimed to determine hatchery methods, growth, fecundity, temperature tolerance, optimal stocking size, catchability by anglers, and control of insects and aquatic plants. The authors report successful techniques for culturing all-male hybrids, growth of 1.0 to 2.4 inches per month, and a projected output of about 2,000 hybrids per breeding unit per year. Temperature is identified as the main limiting factor. The hybrids showed no apparent effect on mosquitoes but may help control aquatic plants through nest-building activity.

## Background & Description

The study was designed to answer several questions about the Zanzibar × Javanese hybrid:

- Production of all-male hybrids
- Culture and rearing requirements
- Growth rates and fecundity
- Temperature tolerances
- Optimal stocking size
- Catchability by anglers
- Control of insects and aquatic plants

The two parent forms were distinguished by [[Morphological Characteristics]] of the dorsal and anal fins, gill rakers, teeth, lateral line scales, and coloration. The author noted that the visual differences were not always consistent, so [[Sex Determination]] and identification relied on several features used together. The report also noted that the Javanese and Zanzibar forms differed in pectoral fin ray counts, although the scale of these differences was not fully documented.

The source notes that the hybrid had been reported in other studies, including work by [[McConnell]] in Arizona, which supplied the original test fish. Field experiences from other regions are summarized briefly, including reports of tilapia in Southern California and in Asia and Africa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Facilities and water quality.** Work was based at the [[Chino Fisheries Base]], where a storage room was converted into a heated tilapia laboratory. Holding tanks were made from modified wooden hatchery troughs, glass aquaria, sheet-steel tanks, and polypropylene tanks borrowed from the [[University of California, Riverside]]. Dissolved oxygen in the tanks ranged from about 6 to 8 ppm, with the lowest readings in tanks holding herring. Activated charcoal filters maintained clarity and purity. Artificial cover of black polyethylene strips was used, and it dispersed into a natural-looking vegetative cover.

**Temperature.** The authors conclude that temperature is the main limit on growth. Hybrids tolerated brief exposures to temperatures as low as about 40°F but generally could not survive prolonged cold. Growth slowed sharply in water near or below 60°F. Hybrids held in a pond for 59 days at an average of 76.5°F averaged 2.4 inches per month, while fish held 83 days at an average of about 71°F grew only 1.0 inch per month.

**Predation and food web.** Hybrids in the [[Santee Pond]] fed on dragonfly and damselfly larvae, water boatmen, amphipods, and other insects. Fish kills in August and September 1965 coincided with a heavy bloom of the blue-green alga [[Microcystis]]. Crayfish populations were studied at the [[University of California, Riverside]] ponds.

## Key Findings

**Hatchery and breeding**

- All-male hybrids were produced using a technique developed by the authors. A breeding unit of five females per male was considered suitable, with an estimated production of about 2,000 hybrid fry per unit per year.
- Fry were produced in a hatchery trough with floating cover. Females were checked daily for eggs, and eggs were removed to a separate tank to prevent parental predation.
- Average production of 203 fry per Javanese female was recorded in the laboratory work, from seven individually recorded females (fry counts ranged up to 319 per female in the summer data).
- Initial hybrid fry were observed on 13 March 1963 (year reported as written in the source), and [[Zanzibar Tilapia]] females were found to be sensitive to handling.

**Growth and feeding**

- Hybrids grew 1.0 to 2.4 inches per month, depending on temperature and feed.
- Fish stocked at 2 to 3 inches reached 5 inches or longer by late summer and fall, providing catchable fish.
- A standard dry trout meal was fed at about 3 to 5 percent of body weight per day, with occasional supplements of frozen brine shrimp and [[Daphnia]].
- A maximum hybrid size of 8.7 inches was obtained in the best ponds. The reported incubation period was 7 to 10 days.

**Pond stocking and recovery**

- Recovery of stocked fish from Chino Fisheries Base ponds ranged from 19 to 83 percent. The highest recoveries (83 and about 79 percent) came from ponds stocked with large fry. Intermediate recoveries were about 51 to 59 percent. The lowest (19 percent) occurred where fry were taken directly from the female's mouth and released into the pond.
- The authors recommend holding young hybrids to at least the size of free-swimming fry before stocking.

**Temperature tolerance and mortality**

- Upper lethal temperature was roughly 100°F, with a figure near 102°F also reported; scanned text is partly illegible at this point.
- Mortality was caused mainly by injury from handling and predation, and by [[Fungal Infection]] following exposure to low water temperatures. Overall mortality was low.

**Catchability and angler acceptance**

- No formal catchability tests were conducted. In one session in September, three hybrids (8.1, 8.0, and 7.9 inches) were caught on worms within a few minutes in the Chino pond at 71°F. Artificial flies did not produce strikes.
- Angler acceptance had not yet been determined.

**Pest control**

- Hybrids at the [[University of California, Riverside]] test ponds (14 fish per pond) did not appreciably reduce the crayfish population in an identified test. Hybrids were not considered to control mosquitoes, but they may help control aquatic plants through nest-building.

**Conclusions and future work**

- The authors conclude that the hybrid is a viable sport and forage fish in warm waters, although its cold intolerance limits its use in northern or cooler regions.
- Future studies planned include selective breeding for temperature tolerance, production of 10,000 to 20,000 fingerlings for introduction into a Southern California reservoir, and further evaluation of effects on [[California Native Fish]].
