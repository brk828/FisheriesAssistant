# Occurrence of the Lamprey *Ichthyomyzon greeleyi* Hubbs and Trautman in Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary
This 1955 paper by Clay and Lachner documents the first record of the non-parasitic lamprey **[[Ichthyomyzon greeleyi]]** (Allegheny Brook Lamprey) in Kentucky, based on four specimens collected in 1952 from **Russel Creek**, a tributary of the **[[Green River]]** in Adair County. This finding extends the species' previously known range—confined to the upper Ohio River system of western Pennsylvania and eastern Ohio—by approximately 400 miles southwest into the lower Ohio River system. The discovery is significant because the four non-parasitic *Ichthyomyzon* species have distributions that roughly surround Kentucky without overlapping, and this record helps bridge that geographic and taxonomic gap, informing questions of [[Allopatric Speciation|allopatry]] and potential intergradation among related species.

## Background & Description
Diagnostic comparison of the Kentucky specimens (n=4) against the four recognized non-parasitic *Ichthyomyzon* species was based on the **transverse lingual lamina**, myomere counts, oral cusp counts, and fin morphology.

### Key Diagnostic Differentiators
- **Vs. *[[Ichthyomyzon hubbsi]]***: Kentucky specimens possess a corneous layer and denticulations on the lingual lamina, plus a higher anterior dorsal fin (hubbsi has a lower fin relative to eye-nostril distance).
- **Vs. *[[Ichthyomyzon gagei]]***: Distinguished by cornified, denticulate, and more bilobed lingual lamina.
- **Vs. *[[Ichthyomyzon fossor]]***: Lingual lamina less strongly bilobed, more cornified/denticulate; higher trunk myomere count; three (vs. two) supraoral cusps; presence of 7–8 bicuspid circumorals (vs. complete absence in fossor)—considered the **most significant** distinguishing character.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
*Ichthyomyzon greeleyi* is a **non-parasitic** ("brook") lamprey, one of four such species in the genus, contrasting with parasitic congeners. Habitat consists of freshwater creek/stream systems within larger river drainages.

### Biogeography
- **[[Ichthyomyzon greeleyi]]** (Allegheny Brook Lamprey): Previously known only from the upper Ohio River system (western Pennsylvania, eastern Ohio); this record extends its range ~400 miles to the Green River drainage, Kentucky (lower Ohio River system).
- **[[Ichthyomyzon fossor]]** (Northern Brook Lamprey): Great Lakes drainage (New York to Ontario/Michigan) and Mississippi River drainage (Wisconsin, northern Indiana).
- **[[Ichthyomyzon gagei]]** (Southern Brook Lamprey): Western Florida and Georgia through Alabama and Louisiana to eastern Texas and eastern Oklahoma.
- **[[Ichthyomyzon hubbsi]]** (Tennessee Brook Lamprey): Upper Tennessee River system of North Carolina and Georgia.

Collectively, the pre-existing known ranges of these four species formed a distribution pattern that surrounded, but excluded, Kentucky — making this record notable for filling a distributional gap and raising questions about whether species ranges are truly discontinuous/[[Allopatry|allopatric]] or subject to intergradation at contact zones.

## Key Findings
- **First state record**: This is the first documented occurrence of any non-parasitic *Ichthyomyzon* species in Kentucky.
- **Range extension**: Extends the known distribution of *I. greeleyi* by ~400 miles southwest from the upper Ohio River system to the Green River drainage.
- **Taxonomic confirmation**: Identification was confirmed by Dr. Milton B. Trautman, co-describer of the species.
- **Diagnostic value**: The presence of 7–8 bicuspid circumoral teeth in the Kentucky specimens is highlighted as the most taxonomically significant character separating them from *I. fossor*.
- **Biogeographic implication**: The find supports the hypothesis that Kentucky lies at a potential contact/intergradation zone for multiple *Ichthyomyzon* species distributions.
- **Research context**: Study conducted under Federal Aid Project F-7-R (Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Department of the Interior) in cooperation with the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources.
