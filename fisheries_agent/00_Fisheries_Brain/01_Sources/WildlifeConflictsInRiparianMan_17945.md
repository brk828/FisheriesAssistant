# Water: What Is It? Stream Energy, Riparian Vegetation and Grazing in Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1977 symposium paper (Tucson, Arizona, July 9, 1977) by a U.S. Forest Service fisheries and non-game biologist argues that stream fisheries in the Southwest depend on energy and nutrients from riparian vegetation and the surrounding watershed. Uncontrolled livestock grazing removes riparian trees and shrubs, converts streamside vegetation types, widens and shallows channels, and reduces fish abundance and weight. The author proposes fencing cattle out of riparian strips so streams can progress through recovery stages. He also calls for stream classification by energy source, energy-budget studies, and research on riparian plant species that balance fish, wildlife, esthetic and water-yield needs. The paper is a synthesis of observations and cited literature, not a new controlled study.

## Background & Description
- Water in the riparian zone is never pure H2O. It carries dissolved salts (nutrients), dissolved organic matter, and fine and coarse particulate organic matter. It supports vertebrate and invertebrate life (fish, plankton, bacteria) and, through riparian vegetation, terrestrial wildlife. It is also an energy source, forming meandering channels and transporting particles.
- Riparian ecosystem interactions have received very little attention in Arizona and New Mexico.
- Southwestern streams span a wide gradient:
  - Headwaters up to 11,000 feet on Mt. Baldy (Apache National Forest), comparable to northern U.S. or Canadian streams.
  - Warm, algae-rich mid-elevation reaches.
  - Low-elevation desert streams, unique in the United States.
- Example of the fish diversity: Arctic grayling in a lake above the Mogollon, and Channel Catfish in the Verde River only about 50 miles away.
- Region 3 of the Forest Service (Arizona and New Mexico) has about 4000 fish habitat improvement structures in National Forest streams, built to create pools in long flat, shallow reaches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Energy dependence gradient:** High-elevation streams likely depend most on outside (terrestrial) energy. Mid-elevation streams can capture more energy in-stream via algae, diatoms and rooted vegetation. Low desert streams may fix substantial energy in the water but also receive large plant detritus inputs during storm flows.
- **Grazing-driven type conversion:** After 50 to 100+ years of grazing, old riparian trees die and seedlings are eaten. At high elevation, alders and willows are lost, leaving grasses. At mid elevation, sycamore and cottonwood are replaced by bermuda grass, desert willow, seep willow and, at some elevations, tamarisk.
- **Morphology:** Loss of riparian trees leaves streams less confined, with a more constant sediment load from unvegetated banks. Overgrazed watersheds produce higher peak storm flows, keeping streams in a young, raw condition.
- **Rio de las Vacas** ([[Santa Fe National Forest]], Cuba District, 7000 to 9000 ft): only a few remnants of willow and narrowleaf cottonwood remain. Only about 50 narrowleaf cottonwoods persist in eight miles of stream. Remaining riparian trees are [[Narrowleaf Cottonwood]], [[Arizona Alder]] and several willow species. Water temperatures reached 70°F in June 1977 in the fenced reach.
- **Water yield:** Watershed manipulation (chaparral, timber) to increase water makes the riparian zone, a water "pipeline," more important to manage. Little integrated work has addressed which riparian species mix best serves fish, wildlife, esthetics and water production.
- **Species at risk:** The author links the fate of the bald eagle and the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] to properly functioning riparian ecosystems.

## Key Findings
**Grazing and fish (cited evidence)**
- Rock Creek Floodplain Investigation (Marcuson 1970): 63 lb/acre of [[Brown Trout]] in a heavily grazed area versus 213 lb/acre in an ungrazed area.
- Bob Phillips (USFS, personal communication): 31 [[Steelhead]] in a 100-foot heavily grazed section versus 75 in a nearby lightly grazed section.
- Ensign (1957): terrestrial insects were 4% of annual brown trout food in grazed Mt. Vernon Creek, Wisconsin, versus 15% in ungrazed Black Earth Creek.

**Energy sources (cited evidence)**
- Fisher (1970): 99% of the annual energy budget of Bear Brook comes from the surrounding forested watershed or upstream.
- Berner (1951): 54% of organic matter ingested by fish in the Missouri River is of terrestrial origin.
- Likens and Bormann (1974): nutrient linkages between streams and watersheds; wise watershed management is key to wise aquatic management.

**Recovery succession (White and Brynildson 1967, Wisconsin trout streams)**
- Stage A (heavy grazing): banks eroding, shifting silts, maximum depth only 9 inches (22 cm), no shelter or spawning for trout.
- After 2 to 4 years of protection: watercress and turf constrict the channel, maximum depth about 20 inches (50 cm), shelter and spawning gravel available.
- About 3rd to 5th year: depth about 2 feet (60 cm), stream exposed width only about 4 feet, good shelter, food and spawning gravels.
- Few years later: channel narrowed 40% to 50% since stage A. Management goal: maintain stages D-E-F.
- 10 to 20 years: alders dominate; shade shuts out the turf. Many years later: mature forest, banks eroded, channel spread, depth under 1 foot, conditions almost as poor as stage A.
- Recovery would likely be faster at low elevations with long growing seasons and slower at 8500 ft on the Rio de las Vacas.

**Management proposals**
- Fencing cattle out of the narrow riparian zone is the alternative to habitat structures and their maintenance. Fencing alone starts recovery, but seedling plantings are needed on many streams. A one-mile fenced reach of the Rio de las Vacas is being documented with stream profiles and photos.
- Develop a stream classification system using energy sources as a criterion, and study energy budgets on typical cold, intermediate and warm water reaches.
- Research whether some species (e.g., Arizona walnut) transpire less water and provide better food for aquatic insects.

**Conclusions stated by the author**
1. Fisheries are often energy dependent on riparian vegetation and the watershed.
2. Uncontrolled grazing causes type conversions and prevents streams from stabilizing.
3. Riparian vegetation controls sediment, stabilizes streams, and narrows and deepens channels, benefiting fish.
4. Research is needed on the interactive and intrinsic value of riparian plant species.
5. Fishery, wildlife, esthetics, and water quality and quantity depend on these interactions; free-choice, uncontrolled grazing is incompatible with them.

The paper also quotes J. Stokley Ligon (1927) on the damage overgrazing has done to New Mexico's cold-water fishing, and the value of "little stream jungles." A literature cited list of about 35 references is included.
