# Terminology Associated with Introduced Organisms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #introduced-fishes

## Executive Summary

This terminology paper, by [[Paul L. Shafland]] and [[William M. Lewis]], was adopted by the [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS) [[Exotic Fish Section]] at its annual business meeting on 18 August 1983 in Milwaukee, Wisconsin. It responds to a recognized lack of consensus on the terms used for fish moved by humans. The authors review how AFS terminology has changed, propose a standardized set of terms, and recommend guidelines for coining new terms. The recommended scheme keeps the political (national-boundary) basis of the terms "exotic" and "transplanted," adds descriptive status categories for introduced populations, and calls for new, non-political terms to be developed separately.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** The paper was prepared after the AFS [[Exotic Fish Symposium]] (1981, Albuquerque, New Mexico). Participants agreed that terms for introduced fishes needed clarification.
- **Core problem:** Terms have "evolved variable meanings and applications based on individual interpretations and philosophies." The result is terminology that is "somewhat confusing, imprecise, and inconsistently applied."
- **Stated purposes:** (1) review the history of introduced-fish terminology used by AFS, (2) propose standardized terms for the AFS Exotic Fish Section, and (3) suggest guidelines for developing additional terminology.
- **Authors' expertise:** Shafland directs the Non-Native Fish Research Project at the Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission and studies temperature tolerances, life histories, and interactions of established exotic fishes with natives in Florida. Lewis was formerly Director of the Fisheries Research Laboratory at Southern Illinois University.
- **Scope:** The document is a terminology and policy paper. It contains no field data, sample sizes, or species-level measurements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical definitions**

- The first formal AFS attempt to categorize introduced fish was made at the 1969 [[Invitational Conference on Exotic Fishes and Related Problems]], sponsored by the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists. Following Webster's dictionary, conferees defined an exotic as a species "introduced from a foreign country." They defined transplanted species as those moved "between watersheds within the country of origin."
- The 1973 AFS "Position on Introductions of Exotic Aquatic Species" defined exotics as "species not native to an ecosystem." The authors note that "ecosystem" is vague, since ecosystems range from oceans to temporary ponds. This definition gained little acceptance, and the national-boundary concept generally prevailed.

**The fish nationality concept**

- Its advantage is simplicity: an organism is exotic if it has crossed a national boundary.
- Its disadvantage is that it classifies biological and economic outcomes solely on political criteria. Political boundaries are man-made and temporary, so one introduction may be a transplant one day and an exotic the next.

**Permanence**

- The authors argue that permanence determines the long-term effects of introductions and shapes monitoring, research, and management priorities.
- Neither reproduction nor elapsed time is an adequate test, because some species keep unstable populations for many years before disappearing.

**Problems with political categories**

- "Exotic" could cover moves between similar communities across a national border as well as moves between continents.
- "Transplanted" could cover moves between adjacent watersheds as well as moves across barriers such as oceans or major mountain ranges. Examples given are moves from Florida to Hawaii or Alaska.
- The authors suggest [[Community Similarity]] between native and introduced ranges as one useful non-political criterion. Species moved into communities distinctly different from their native ones would be distinguished from those moved between similar communities.

## Key Findings

**Recommended standardized terms (AFS Exotic Fish Section)**

| Term | Definition (paraphrased from the text) |
|---|---|
| [[Introduced Organism]] | A plant or animal moved from one place to another by man. Covers an individual, group, or population occurring in a locale due to human action. This is the most general term. |
| [[Exotic Fish]] | An organism introduced from a foreign country, meaning its entire native range lies outside the country where it is found. |
| [[Transplanted Fish]] | An organism moved outside its native range but within a country where it naturally occurs, meaning its native range includes part of the country where it is found. |

**Descriptive status terms**

- **[[Established Species]]:** an introduced organism with a permanent population, unlikely to be eliminated by man or natural causes.
- **[[Possibly Established Species]]:** an introduced organism without permanent-population status, but reproducing where elimination by man would be impractical.
- **[[Naturalized Species]]:** a confined, reproducing population of an introduced organism that can be eliminated by standard methods.
- **[[Reported Species]]:** an introduced organism collected without evidence of reproduction.

**Key conclusions and recommendations**

1. The national-boundary definitions of "exotic" and "transplanted" should be kept as the only acceptable definitions for now. Changing them would require concurrent changes to regulations, and the authors judge the case for change not yet strong enough.
2. Only stable or expanding populations unlikely to be eliminated should be called "established." Those with reproducing but potentially non-enduring populations are "possibly established."
3. New categories should be introduced with original terms or phrases, not by redefining existing ones. Redefining existing terms is discouraged.
4. New terminology should undergo extensive peer review and usage before being added to the standard.
5. Confusion can be reduced by using single, definitive interpretations. The recommended terms follow the historical and most commonly used definitions.

**Noted limitation**

Only "introduced," "exotic," and "transplanted" are formally recognized as subcategories. The authors have not coined terms for other introduction types, such as individuals moved within a species' native range during supplemental stocking.
