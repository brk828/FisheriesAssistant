# People and Biodiversity: Sustainable Development and The Nature Conservancy

#conservation-biology #biodiversity #sustainable-development

## Executive Summary

This 1994 *Nature Conservancy* magazine feature by [[Thomas E. Lovejoy]] argues that biological diversity underpins human health, agriculture, pollution control and basic science, yet human activity is driving extinction at rates roughly a thousand times the normal background rate. Lovejoy presents [[Sustainable Development]] as the only workable path between environmental destruction and a people-free planet. A closing letter from Greg Watson and a 1993 financial report describe [[The Nature Conservancy]]'s landscape-level projects and its finances.

## Background & Description

The article opens with the origin of the [[Polymerase Chain Reaction]]. Heat needed to separate DNA strands destroyed the enzymes required for replication until [[Thermus Aquaticus]] yielded a heat-resistant enzyme. The bacterium was found in a hot spring in [[Yellowstone National Park]], a park not established for biodiversity conservation. Lovejoy notes that rapid diagnosis of throat infections such as strep is now possible within hours, with economic value estimated at tens of billions of dollars annually.

Other examples cited:
- **[[Taxol]]**, a breast cancer drug isolated from the bark of the [[Pacific Yew]], once considered a trash tree in Northwest logging.
- Somewhere between one-half and three-quarters of all pharmaceuticals have a biological, primarily plant, origin.
- **[[African Clawed Frog]]** skins contain a new class of infection-resisting compounds.
- Wild crop relatives supply disease and pest resistance, a principle used in [[Integrated Pest Management]].
- **[[Bioremediation]]** uses microorganisms to clean oil spills and break down aromatic hydrocarbons. A U.S. Geological Survey bacterium breaks down CFC molecules, but only in polluted sediments.
- Historical discoveries include cowpox and milkmaids (vaccination) and a laboratory mold contamination (antibiotics).

The article also reviews the history of human-driven extinction. Prehistoric people exterminated dozens of species on Caribbean, Pacific and Madagascan islands. The best-known case is the [[Moa]] of New Zealand, about a dozen flightless species that disappeared after Polynesian arrival around 1000 A.D. Debate continues over whether humans or climate caused the late Pleistocene extinction of North American megafauna about 10,000 years ago.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key figures on current pressures:
- **Human population:** 5.5 billion people, growing by roughly 100 million annually.
- **Tropical forest loss:** More than half of tropical forests are gone. The most recent estimated loss rate is 15.4 million hectares per year. See [[Tropical Deforestation]].
- **Amazon population:** About 2 million people in 1965, rising to 17 million today. Lovejoy argues [[Amazon Basin]] deforestation will not be solved until poverty is addressed.
- **Extinction rate:** Estimated at a thousand times the normal rate.

**Freshwater status (United States):**
- 34% of 852 freshwater fish species are rare or extinct.
- 65% of crayfish species are rare or extinct.
- 73% of the 300 freshwater mussel species are rare or extinct.
- A 1982 rivers inventory found less than 2% of 5.2 million kilometers of streams in the 48 states were in high-quality condition. See [[Freshwater Biodiversity]].
- Hawaii ranks second only to California in number of endangered species.

Lovejoy identifies poverty, population growth and social and economic inequity, both between and within nations, as root causes. He cites former Brazilian president Collor de Mello at the [[Earth Summit]]: "You can't have an environmentally healthy planet in a world that is socially unjust."

Sustainable development examples:
- **[[Extractive Reserve]]** systems in the Amazon, where rubber, nuts and fruit are harvested sustainably.
- **Eucalyptus plantations** set within natural forest matrices by Brazilian paper pulp firms in the Amazon and Atlantic forest.
- A salt industry at **[[Scammon's Lagoon]]**, Baja California, coexisting with breeding [[Gray Whale]]s.
- Business-led efforts such as the Business Council for Sustainable Development and "Project 88", and architect William McDonough's "waste equals food" principle.

## Key Findings

**Lovejoy's main arguments**
- Biodiversity provides medicines, crop resilience, pollution remediation and foundational scientific discoveries.
- Human-driven extinction is accelerating, and the scale of modern impact far exceeds prehistoric effects.
- Sustainable development must balance human welfare with biodiversity protection. Isolated projects will not achieve it globally without major changes in energy and resource use.
- Changing public attitudes toward biodiversity is a central challenge, and the intrinsic fascination of living things can help.

**Watson's letter on Conservancy strategy**
- The [[Last Great Places]] initiative now treats humans as integral to ecosystems rather than externalities.
- Sustainable development is defined as integrating compatible human activities into biodiversity strategies.
- Projects include:
  - **[[Big Darby Creek]]**, Ohio: reducing soil erosion and chemical runoff with local farmers to protect aquatic biodiversity.
  - **[[Cache River]]**, Illinois: a bioreserve plan using ecological modeling to support tourism and outdoor recreation.
  - **[[Virginia Coast Reserve]]**: the flagship effort, including the [[Northampton Economic Forum]], formed in 1991 with the local NAACP chapter and Citizens for a Better Eastern Shore, and a planned Virginia Eastern Shore Sustainable Development Corporation.

**1993 financial report**
- Revenue reached a record high in fiscal year 1993 despite a difficult economic climate.
- Nearly 85% of revenue was devoted to program activities.
- Total assets were $900 million, largely trust funds and about 1.3 million acres of land dedicated to management. These assets cannot fund new activities, which rely on future member donations.
- Sources of funds: individuals 73.0% (private donations).
- Uses of funds: value of land sold and donated to government 34.5%; conservation programs 30.5%; net preserve additions 15.5%; endowment and land preservation fund additions 4.5%; fundraising 5.5%; membership services 3.5%. The general and administrative share is not legible in the scan.
- A five-year trend chart plots acres saved against membership growth from 1989 to 1993, but its values are not legible.
