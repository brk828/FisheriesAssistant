# Creek Chub Minnow Propagation

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Cyprinidae #bait-fish #hatchery-management

## Executive Summary

This report documents a two-year experimental project (1941-1942) on the propagation of [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus atromaculatus*) in hatchery ponds at State Fish Farm No. 1, [[St. Marys]], Ohio. Conducted by the Ohio Division of Conservation and Natural Resources, the study aimed to develop a simplified method for bait dealers to raise creek chubs in ponds, as previous attempts had only succeeded using labor-intensive stripping and battery hatching methods. The project demonstrated successful natural spawning and rearing in specially constructed ponds with artificial stream features, producing 134,772 fish per acre in 1941 and 63,910 per acre in 1942, with all fish reaching saleable bait size within one season.

## Background & Description

The [[Creek Chub]] is a popular bait minnow for bass fishing, and increasing demand led to interest in pond propagation methods. Previous successful propagation required stripping males and females, hatching eggs in batteries, and rearing fry in ponds—a complex process unsuitable for most bait dealers. The Ohio Division of Conservation had already helped dealers propagate [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Hyborhynchus notatus*) and [[Blackhead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), with one propagator selling 98,000 minnows one year after receiving breeders from the state.

The experimental ponds were constructed from old barrow pits along the east bank of [[Lake St. Marys]]. Ponds measured approximately 143 feet long by 13 feet wide, with depths ranging from 1 foot at the shallow end to 3 feet at the deeper end. A critical feature was a miniature stream built on the shallow end: 27 feet long, 27 inches wide, varying 2-10 inches deep, with heavily graveled banks and bottom to simulate natural spawning habitat. Water was supplied by [[Lake St. Marys]] through a gravity pipe system.

Female creek chubs 4.5 inches in length contained 4,193-4,671 ripe eggs plus 106-183 undeveloped eggs. Spawning occurred at approximately 60°F water temperature. First fry appeared 14-15 days after initial nesting activity was observed, compared to a 5-day incubation period for stripped eggs at room temperature reported by Markus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Early-stage fry (first two weeks) remained in very shallow water along shores with no vegetation or cover. At approximately 1 inch length, juveniles moved upstream against current and congregated in screens separating ponds, with up to 2,000 one-inch fry found in a single screen. In late summer with increased water temperatures, chubs moved to deeper pond areas. By October when water cleared, they were visible as large schools in deeper water.

Food studies revealed creek chubs began feeding at 7/16 inch length on [[Cladocerans]] and [[Copepods]] in equal numbers. [[Entomostraca]] remained important throughout summer for all sizes. Adult insects (primarily *Corixa*) became more abundant in larger fish. Fish 2.5 inches in length fed on smaller creek chubs. Various sizes had digestive tracts filled with *Spirogyra*. Forbes found filamentous algae common in digestive tracts along with entomostraca, insects, minnows, and crayfish.

Disease known as "tail-rot" killed large numbers of fingerlings during August 1941, with 25-30 fish often seen floating daily.

## Key Findings

**1941 Results:**
- Initial stock: 25 males and 25 females in one pond; 25 males and 29 females in another (total 104 breeders)
- Production: 11,231 creek chubs raised on 1/12 acre = 134,772 per acre
- Feed: 542 pounds mix (50% commercial meat scrap, 50% middlings) + 410 pounds ground carp = 952 pounds total
- Total fish weight: 137 pounds
- Feed conversion: approximately 7 pounds feed per 1 pound fish
- Feed cost: $38.76 (271 lbs meat scraps @ 3.7¢/lb, 271 lbs middlings @ 2.2¢/lb, 410 lbs carp @ 8¢/lb)
- Market value: $4,305.18 per acre (50¢/dozen for 3/4 of crop, 15¢/dozen for remainder)
- Size variation: up to 3.5 inches difference between largest and smallest of 228 measured fish
- Best growth: first few weeks and August (after changing to ground carp)

**1942 Results:**
- Stock: 20 males and 20 females per pond in three ponds (1/7 acre total)
- Production: 9,130 creek chubs = 63,910 per acre
- Feed: 776 pounds mix only (388 lbs meat scrap @ 4.1¢/lb, 388 lbs middlings @ 2.4¢/lb)
- Feed cost: $25.22
- Market value: $1,730.95 per acre (50¢/dozen for 3/4 of crop, 15¢/dozen for remainder)
- Size distribution: 43.2% under 2 inches, 36.1% between 3-4 inches, 18.0% between 4-5 inches, 2.7% over 5 inches
- Average size: 3 inches (0.75 inch shorter than 1941 crop)
- Reduced production attributed to: water system difficulties causing rapid fluctuations during spawning; most breeders less than one year old; lack of ground carp in diet

**Comparative Advantages:**
- 100% of creek chubs reached saleable bait size in first year, compared to only 20% of [[Bluntnose Minnow]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] crops at State Fish Farm
- Fewer creek chubs produced per acre than bluntnose/fatheads, but higher percentage of saleable fish eliminates expense of holding over and feeding small minnows through following summer
- Simplified natural spawning method proven successful, eliminating need for stripping and battery hatching

**Critical Success Factors:**
- Graveled artificial stream feature for spawning habitat
- Water temperature approximately 60°F for spawning
- Feeding regime of meat scrap/middlings mix plus ground carp
- Ground carp supplementation appeared to enhance growth rates
- Breeders should be at least one year old for optimal spawning success
