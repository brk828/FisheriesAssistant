## Executive Summary
This 1992-era press release commemorates the 20th anniversary of the establishment of a translocated population of the [[Pahrump Killifish]] at Shoshone Ponds, Nevada, marking two decades since its 1972 introduction as a conservation measure. The [[Pahrump Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys latos*) is a small (1–3 in), narrowly endemic desert fish first described in 1948 from three isolated springs in Pahrump Valley, Nye County, Nevada. Habitat loss from groundwater pumping and introduction of exotic species drove the species to the brink of extinction, prompting its inclusion on the first U.S. federal endangered species list in 1967. The document details a multi-decade interagency conservation effort involving the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], the [[Bureau of Land Management]], and academic researchers, culminating in the establishment of three geographically separate refuge populations. The release also situates the killifish within a broader regional context of endemic Great Basin desert fishes facing similar threats, including the [[White River Spinedace]], [[Railroad Valley Springfish]], [[Hiko White River Springfish]], and [[Big Springs Spinedace]].

## Background & Description
- **Size:** Adult body length ranges from **1 to 3 inches (2.5–7.6 cm)**, characteristic of a small-bodied killifish.
- **Taxonomic status:** Described as a distinct species in 1948, distinguishing it from other regional *Empetrichthys* populations.
- No further morphological/meristic detail (fin counts, coloration, sexual dimorphism) is provided in the source text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Endemic to isolated **warm-water desert spring and artesian pool systems** in the Great Basin region of Nevada — a highly restricted and fragile habitat type shared by several other endemic desert fishes.
- **Native range:** Originally restricted to **three springs in Pahrump Valley**, Nye County, Nevada (~50 miles west of Las Vegas), with the last native population persisting at **Manse Ranch Spring**.
- **Thermal/water regime dependency:** Populations rely on constant-temperature spring discharge and artesian flow; habitat is highly sensitive to **groundwater withdrawal/pumping**, which caused two of the three original springs to dry up prior to 1967.
- **Threats:**
  - Groundwater depletion from regional pumping.
  - Introduction of **exotic species** (e.g., goldfish) causing competitive/predatory pressure.
  - Physical alteration of native spring habitat.
- **Translocation-based conservation strategy:** Because of habitat vulnerability, managers established multiple **geographically separated refuge populations** to buffer against localized extinction events (a classic metapopulation conservation approach for spring-endemic fishes):
  - **Corn Creek Spring** (Desert National Wildlife Range, ~25 mi NW of Las Vegas) — established early 1970s from Manse Ranch Spring stock.
  - **Shoshone Ponds** (Spring Valley, ~40 mi SE of Ely, Nevada) — established via 66 individuals transplanted in 1972 and 1975 from Manse Ranch and Corn Creek Springs.
  - **Spring Mountain State Park** (~20 mi west of Las Vegas) — third population established in 1983.
- **Co-occurring endemic desert fishes** cited as facing analogous conservation challenges in east-central Nevada:
  - [[White River Spinedace]] — listed endangered September 1985; critical habitat at Preston Big Spring, Lund Town Spring, and Flag Springs (White River Valley).
  - [[Railroad Valley Springfish]] — listed threatened April 1986; critical habitat at Big and Little Warm Springs and springs near Lockes Ranch, Railroad Valley.
  - [[Hiko White River Springfish]] — listed endangered September 1985; critical habitat at Hiko Springs near Hiko.
  - [[Big Springs Spinedace]] — listed threatened March 1985; critical habitat at Condor Canyon near Panaca.

## Key Findings
- The **Pahrump Killifish** was among the original 13 species placed on the **first federal endangered species list in 1967**, following the drying of two of its three native springs due to groundwater pumping.
- A **habitat management plan** for a 1,240-acre area at Shoshone Ponds was developed in 1971 by BLM, NDOW, and Dr. James Deacon (University of Nevada–Las Vegas), leading to construction of a new well and three artificial ponds to serve as **alternate/refuge habitat**.
- Translocation of **66 individuals** in 1972 and 1975 established the Shoshone Ponds population, which grew to an estimated **800 fish by 1977** and **2,500 fish by 1979** — demonstrating successful population establishment in artificial refuge habitat.
- The **"Pahrump Killifish Recovery Plan"** was completed in 1980 by an interagency scientific team convened by the FWS, outlining specific recovery actions to reduce extinction risk.
- A third population was established at **Spring Mountain State Park in 1983**, further distributing extinction risk across multiple sites.
- As of the writing, NDOW was collaborating with a **parasitologist from Brigham Young University** to assess genetic/parasitic differentiation among the three extant populations (Shoshone Ponds, Corn Creek, Spring Mountain State Park) to evaluate the feasibility of **inter-population mixing** to maintain genetic viability — an early application of genetic management principles in endangered fish conservation.
- All species discussed are protected under the **Endangered Species Act of 1973**, which prohibits federal actions that jeopardize the species or its critical habitat, and prohibits direct killing of listed individuals.
- The broader pattern illustrated is that **isolated desert spring endemics** across the Great Basin (killifish, spinedaces, springfishes) share a common vulnerability profile: extremely restricted ranges, dependence on stable spring discharge, and high sensitivity to groundwater extraction and non-native species introduction, necessitating active, ongoing translocation-based management for persistence.
