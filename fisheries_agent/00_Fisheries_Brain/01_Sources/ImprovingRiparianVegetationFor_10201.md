## Executive Summary
On January 21, 1988, a 15-year federal-state agreement was signed to address the recovery of three endangered fish species native to the Upper [[Colorado River]] Basin: the **Colorado squawfish**, **bonytail chub**, and **humpback chub**. The agreement was signed by Interior Secretary Don Hodel, governors of three western states (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), and the Western Area Power Association. The primary driver for this intervention is **habitat damage caused by historical water development** (damming, diversion, and flow alteration) along the Colorado River system.

## Background & Description
*Note: The source text does not provide specific morphological measurements (length, weight, fin ray counts) for these species. Based on the scientific consensus for these specific taxa (external context added for completeness):*
- **Colorado squawfish (Ptychocheilus lucius):** The largest minnow native to North America; historically capable of reaching lengths exceeding 1.8m (though rare by 1988).
- **Bonytail chub (Gila elegans):** Characterized by a thin caudal peduncle and pronounced nuchal hump.
- **Humpback chub (Gila cypha):** Distinguished by a pronounced dorsal hump behind the head, an adaptation theorized to aid in navigating fast, turbulent currents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Primary Habitat:** Mainstem rivers of the Upper Colorado River Basin, specifically the **Green River**, **Yampa River**, and **White River**.
- **Required Conditions:** These species rely on specific instream flow regimes for spawning and rearing.
  - **Spawning Grounds:** Requires specific substrate and flow conditions to reproduce successfully.
  - **Backwaters:** Juvenile life stages depend on slow-moving backwater habitats for rearing, which are often eliminated by dam-regulated flows.
- **Threats:** The [[Ecological Niche|niche]] of these fish has been severely disrupted by "early water development," implying dams and diversions have altered the natural hydrograph (flow timing/volume) necessary for their life cycle.
- **Interacting Species:** This aquatic conservation effort parallels terrestrial riparian recovery efforts noted elsewhere in the newsletter, such as the recovery of **[[Bald Eagle|bald eagles]]** and cottonwood/willow galleries in the [[Tonto National Forest]], suggesting a broader regional trend of river ecosystem rehabilitation in the 1980s.

## Key Findings
1. **Legal Status:** All three species (Colorado squawfish, bonytail chub, humpback chub) were formally recognized as needing "sustained help" via a federal-state compact in 1988.
2. **Funding Mechanics:** 
   - Estimated **annual operating costs**: $2.3 million.
   - Additional **one-time funding requested** from Congress: $15 million.
   - Specific allocation for **water rights acquisition**: $10 million (earmarked to buy water rights from private citizens to maintain instream flow).
3. **Inter-agency Collaboration:** The recovery plan represents a joint effort between the **Bureau of Reclamation**, **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS)**, the **Western Area Power Administration** (Dept. of Energy), and the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming.
4. **Management Strategy:** The plan focuses on three specific engineering/hydrological interventions:
   - Increasing instream flow.
   - Creating spawning grounds.
   - Improving backwater habitats.
5. **Root Cause:** The recovery plan explicitly aims to "reverse habitat damage from early water development of the Colorado River," linking the endangerment status directly to 20th-century dam construction and water allocation policies.
