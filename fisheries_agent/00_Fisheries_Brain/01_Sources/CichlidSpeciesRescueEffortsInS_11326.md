# Cichlid Species Rescue Efforts in Southern Lake Victoria

## Executive Summary

This 1996 report by Ole Seehausen (Institute of Evolutionary and Ecological Sciences, Leiden University, in collaboration with the Tanzania Fisheries Research Institute, TAFIRI) documents an emergency captive-propagation "rescue" effort targeting critically endangered haplochromine cichlids and tilapiines endemic to southern [[Lake Victoria]]. Funded by a Kal Kan pet food grant through the [[Lake Victoria Cichlid Species Survival Plan]] (LVSSP, American Zoo and Aquarium Association), the project collected wild founder stock of three high-priority species — **H. (Lipochromis) cf. melanopterus**, **H. (Harpagochromis) serranus-like**, and **Oreochromis variabilis** — from remnant populations on isolated rocky islands (Makobe Island, Zue Island) in the Speke and Mwanza Gulfs. A related insectivorous species, **H. (Paralabidochromis) chilotes**, was also collected opportunistically. The effort succeeded in establishing breeding aquarium populations of two species (H. cf. melanopterus and H. chilotes), a small non-breeding group of H. serranus-like, but failed to secure founder stock of O. variabilis due to taxonomic identification difficulties among juvenile Oreochromis.

## Background & Description

The cichlid species flock of [[Lake Victoria]] represents one of the most dramatic vertebrate extinction events on record. More than 150 endemic haplochromine species have not been observed since the mid-1980s, primarily attributed to the population explosion of the introduced predatory [[Nile Perch]] (*Lates niloticus*) in the early 1980s (Witte et al. 1992), compounded by other slower-acting ecological factors (eutrophication, habitat degradation, hybridization).

Species profiled in this report:

- **H. (Lipochromis) cf. melanopterus** — A large paedophage (egg/larvae predator) of the *H. (L.) obesus* group. Of at least 24 known paedophage species in the lake, this was the only one still regularly observed in the study area, surviving at very low density at two rocky islands.
- **H. (Harpagochromis) serranus-like** — A large piscivore morphologically resembling *H. (Harpagochromis) serranus*, taxonomically unconfirmed at time of writing. Piscivores were considered the trophic group most devastated by Nile perch; of >100 known piscivore species, only two survived at densities allowing directed rescue, including a related orange morph, **H. "Orange rock hunter"** (resembling *H. howesi*), found only at a single islet.
- **Oreochromis variabilis** — One of only two endemic tilapiine species in Lake Victoria. Historically distributed along rocky shores; unseen since the mid-1980s until Seehausen rediscovered a remnant population at an island in Speke Gulf in 1993.
- **H. (Paralabidochromis) chilotes** — An insectivore with strongly lobed lips, ecologically and morphologically convergent with *Lobochilotes labiatus* (Lake Tanganyika) and lip-lobed haplochromines of Lake Malawi. Formerly widespread, now restricted to remnant island populations.

A broader candidate list (Table 1) of additional priority species for future rescue spans multiple trophic guilds: phytoplanktivores, detritivores, zooplanktivores, molluscivores, insectivores, piscivores/crab-eaters, paedophages, and tilapiines — several represented by species with evocative field names (e.g., "Red tridens," "Dusky wine red fins," "Straight cinctus," "Vertical blotch," "Kribensis").

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Location:** Southern Lake Victoria — Speke Gulf, Mwanza Gulf, and adjacent western shoreline areas, surveyed intensively since 1991 by [[HEST]] (Haplochromis Ecology Survey Team) / TAFIRI researchers.
- **Primary threat driver:** Introduction and population boom of [[Nile Perch]] (*Lates niloticus*) in the early 1980s, which decimated multiple trophic guilds of haplochromine cichlids, especially piscivores and paedophages.
- **Secondary threats:** Additional slower-acting ecological factors under separate investigation (Seehausen et al., submitted to *Conservation Biology*), likely including eutrophication-driven turbidity affecting mate recognition and habitat quality, and competition/hybridization with introduced tilapiines (*O. niloticus*, *O. leucostictus*).
- **Habitat specificity:** Remnant populations of endangered species were confined to isolated rocky islets (e.g., Makobe Island, Zue Island) — refugia less accessible to Nile perch or with rocky structure offering protection.
- **Community composition:** Juvenile Oreochromis populations in shallow rocky littoral zones were mixed assemblages of native *O. variabilis* and introduced *O. niloticus* and *O. leucostictus*, complicating field identification of endangered stock.
- **Environmental hazards during fieldwork:** Turbidity from storms, invasive water hyacinth mats damaging nets, and crocodile encounters directly impeded collecting efforts, illustrating logistical challenges of in-situ conservation work in the lake.

## Key Findings

- **H. (Lipochromis) cf. melanopterus** collections (Makobe Island, Feb & April 1995): Total of ~13 individuals captured across two trips; several lost to a research-station electricity failure (aerator/pump failure) and transport mortality. Seven individuals ultimately established a breeding population in Hannover, producing three fry broods (35, ~20, ~30 fry) from two females within months, with a third female gravid at time of writing.
- **Oreochromis variabilis**: Only one confirmed subadult (~20cm SL, with characteristic forehead bump) was found among hundreds of sorted juvenile Oreochromis; a large adult male (with genital tassels) was photographed and released as too large for shipment. Founder stock collection for this species **failed** — ambiguous subadults shipped to Leiden mostly resolved as *O. leucostictus* or probable *O. niloticus*/hybrid, with only one individual showing developing diagnostic traits.
- **H. (Harpagochromis) serranus-like** (Zue Island): Population density observed to decline sharply between 1993 and 1995. Only 5 females obtained on first visit (3 lost to power failure, 1 lost in transit). Second shipment attempts yielded very low catch rates (single fish per multi-hour session) due to low density, turbidity, and difficulty of gillnetting slender-bodied fish without gill damage/suffocation. Final surviving founder group: 4 individuals (2 females, 1 male reported, discrepancy noted in text) established in a large 2.5m×1m×0.6m community tank at Leiden.
- **H. (Paralabidochromis) chilotes**: Ten individuals collected at Zue Island with **zero mortality** during collection or transport — the most successful collection of the effort. Established at Leiden with four breeding females producing ~130 juveniles across four nests, suggesting strong captive breeding viability.
- **Collection methodology**: Underwater removal of fish from monofilament gill nets by divers immediately upon entanglement proved highly effective (near-zero loss rate) for robust-bodied species; hook-and-line was preferred for delicate/slender species but yielded lower catch rates.
- **Transport logistics**: Fish held in 60-liter plastic drums with hourly water exchange during boat transport (up to 4+ hours) to the TAFIRI research station; onward air shipment to Amsterdam/Leiden required ~32 hours, with mortality occurring at both the pre-shipment holding stage (equipment failure) and during transit.
- **Conservation recommendation**: The author advocates broadening the [[LVSSP]] captive program to include additional trophic guilds (e.g., sublittoral phytoplanktivores, detritivores) still occasionally found in the wild, while acknowledging some guilds (prawn eaters, parasite eaters, the scale-eater) may already be functionally extinct and unrecoverable.
- **Growth/husbandry note**: All three target species attain large adult sizes (piscivores exceeding 20cm total length), necessitating large tank infrastructure (e.g., 150×100×70cm breeding tanks) for sustained captive maintenance.
