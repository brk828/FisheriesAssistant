# Diagnoses and Key to the Juveniles of Tilapia Species in Lake Victoria

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 taxonomic paper in the *East African Agricultural and Forestry Journal* addresses the difficulty of identifying young [[Tilapia]] (Cichlidae) in the [[Lake Victoria]] basin. Recent introductions of tilapia species, largely linked to [[Fish Farming]] and fish ponds, had made juvenile identification harder because the young of several species look superficially alike and occur together in similar habitats. The authors provide diagnoses for eight species and a [[Juvenile Identification Key]] based on pigmentation, fin-ray counts and gill-raker and scale counts. Of the eight species, only two, [[Tilapia Esculenta]] and [[Tilapia Variabilis]], occur naturally in the lake.

## Background & Description

The paper responds to the rapid spread of introduced tilapia species into the Lake Victoria basin. Earlier identification work by [[Lowe]] (1955, 1956, 1957) and [[Greenwood]] (1958) dealt with adults, and the authors note that juveniles had been neglected. The material was drawn from fish farms, notably [[Kajansi Fish Farm]], and from collections dating back to 1953. The authors state that the identification features were checked for practicality at the [[Fisheries Research Laboratory Jinja]].

Juveniles of the genus are hard to separate because they share a similar shape and general colour. Key features are:

- **[[Tilapia Mark]]:** a dark spot or bar on the posterior dorsal edge of the operculum or on the soft dorsal fin. Its size, shape and persistence vary among species and fade with age.
- **Flank bars:** their number, width and persistence (for example, 5–8 broad bars in [[Tilapia Mossambica]] versus 8–10 bars in [[Tilapia Esculenta]]).
- **Fin pattern:** spotting on the dorsal, caudal and anal fins.
- **Meristic counts:** [[Gill Rakers]], lateral-line scales, caudal peduncle scales, and [[Dorsal Fin Formula]] and [[Anal Fin Formula]] counts.

The authors note that the Tilapia mark is often the most reliable feature in small fish, but that it may be hard to apply in fish under about 2 cm, and that it can be lost or obscured in some species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper gives habitat notes for each species:

- **[[Tilapia Nigra]]:** not recorded from the lake at the time, but likely to enter it accidentally from the many fish ponds of the basin. It is endemic to east-flowing rivers and is widely farmed.
- **[[Tilapia Nilotica]]:** usually found on "intermediate" muddy bottoms in shallow, partly exposed areas. It was previously absent from Lakes Victoria and Kyoga because [[Owen Falls]] acted as a natural barrier to its upstream movement from the Nile. The authors note that hybrids with *T. zillii* have been found in northern waters.
- **[[Tilapia Esculenta]]:** occurs with *T. zillii* and *T. variabilis* on beaches, and in *T. esculenta*'s case also in rivers. It was regarded, together with *T. variabilis*, as endemic to Lake Victoria.
- **[[Tilapia Zillii]]:** associated with sandy or rocky shallows. The authors consider it possible that it occurs with *T. nilotica* in the basin.
- **[[Tilapia Melanopleura]]:** very similar in appearance to *T. zillii*. Known from the southern part of Africa, and a possible introduction.
- **[[Tilapia Leucosticta]]:** apparently confined to lagoon areas behind fringing swamps, especially in the north, and to de-oxygenated zones. Originally endemic to [[Lake Albert]], [[Lake Edward]] and Lake George. Individuals have also been found on lake beaches.
- **[[Tilapia Mossambica]]:** native to the waters of southern Africa and widely stocked in basin ponds. The authors expect it to reach the lake at some point, since fish may enter from ponds during high rainfall.
- **[[Tilapia Variabilis]]:** often found over hard bottoms such as shelving rock or sand beaches. It has also been recorded in rivers and extends to the intermediate beach zone. Its "mantled" variety is orange and black as adults.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** eight species are dealt with (*T. nigra*, *T. nilotica*, *T. zillii*, *T. melanopleura*, *T. leucosticta*, *T. mossambica*, *T. esculenta*, *T. variabilis*). Five are certainly present in Lake Victoria, and three more may be introduced at any time.
- **Native status:** only *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis* occur naturally in the lake. They were the Tilapia endemic to Lake Victoria.
- **Identification method:** the paper proposes a dichotomous key for juveniles, mainly based on fin-ray counts, gill-raker counts and the Tilapia mark, with bar patterns used as supporting features.
- **Gill rakers (examples):** *T. nilotica* 22–28; *T. esculenta* 18–20; *T. variabilis* 19–21; *T. mossambica* 14–19; *T. leucosticta* roughly 18/19–24 (OCR-ambiguous).
- **Dorsal fin rays (examples):** *T. nilotica* XV–XVIII spines and 11–15 soft rays; *T. esculenta* XVII–XVIII and 10–11; *T. leucosticta* XV–XVIII and 10–13; *T. mossambica* XV–XVIII and 10–11.
- **Anal fin (examples):** *T. nilotica* III spines and 8–11 soft rays; *T. esculenta* III spines and 10–12; *T. mossambica* III spines and 9–12.
- **Hybridization:** *T. nilotica* and *T. zillii* hybrids have been reported in northern waters, with intermediate characters. The authors warn that more fish with intermediate characters may appear.
