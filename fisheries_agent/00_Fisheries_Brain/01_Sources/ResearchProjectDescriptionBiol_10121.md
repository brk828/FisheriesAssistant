# Biological Invasions and Water Extraction: Threats to Desert Spring Food Webs at Cuatro Ciénegas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This unfunded research proposal, drafted in 1998, examines how exotic species and water extraction threaten the endemic fish and mollusc communities of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in the Chihuahuan Desert of northern Mexico. The project combines historical reconstruction of [[Museum Collections]], current field surveys, [[Stable Isotope Analysis]], in situ food web experiments and [[Adaptive Management]] simulation modeling. Its three guiding questions concern changes in native distributions over the past 30 years, the mechanisms by which invaders and water diversion act, and which management options are most effective. The work is a three-year study led by Dean Hendrickson with collaborators from The Nature Conservancy and Profauna. The document also includes a draft support-letter correspondence that frames the collaboration and matching-fund arrangements.

## Background & Description

Desert springs are ranked as high-priority conservation systems because they are rich in fish and mollusc species, show high endemism, and are vulnerable to human disturbance. The proposal argues that isolation has made endemic species especially vulnerable to invaders, since they lack natural refuges and dispersal routes.

Cuatro Ciénegas is a high-priority conservation ecoregion, declared a protected area in 1994. The basin covers under 1,500 km² and contains more than 200 pools that differ in water quality, size and food web composition. The site-based conservation workshop held in spring 1998 identified water extraction and invading species as the two primary threats.

The food web centres on [[Cichlasoma minckleyi]], a trophic polymorph with detritivore (papilliform), molluscivore and piscivore morphs. Its high abundance and broad diet may make it the majority of biomass at several trophic levels in undisturbed pools. Other native taxa include [[Dionda episcopa]], [[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]], hydrobiid snails and aquatic insects. [[Largemouth Bass]] (Micropterus salmoides) occurs in larger pools. Six non-native aquatic species are documented in the basin. The study focuses on two: [[Melanoides tuberculata]] (an African snail introduced via the aquarium trade) and [[Hemichromis Sp.]] (a jewel cichlid).

Water has been diverted for agriculture to neighbouring areas up to 100 km east, through canals that alter flow, water level and invasion pathways.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrologic change:** Canals connecting pools have altered flow rates and water levels. Many pools have declined in level as extraction has increased, with visible evidence of previously higher levels.
- **Predicted mechanisms:** The authors hypothesise that extraction shrinks habitat, increases temperature and salinity, and pushes species beyond their tolerance ranges (Hypothesis 3). Modelling is planned over 10 to 50 year scales.
- **Invasion pathways:** Canals also facilitate exotic dispersal into neighbouring pools.
- **Food web role:** Herbivorous fish and hydrobiid snails graze epiphytic algae and mats. Aquatic insects occur mainly in lower-salinity pools. Piscivory by the piscivore morph and largemouth bass is restricted to larger pools.
- **Regional context:** Profauna (a Mexican NGO), SEMARNAP and TNC are compiling canal history, aerial photographs of the past 40 years and water-rights records.

## Key Findings

**Research questions and hypotheses**
1. Have native fish and mollusc distributions changed over 30 years, and are changes linked to exotics and hydrologic alteration? (Hypothesis 1)
2. Do *M. tuberculata* compete with native hydrobiid snails and resist predation, reducing food for the molluscivore morph (Hypothesis 2A)? Does *Hemichromis* sp. compete with the papilliform morph, shifting the polymorphism toward molluscivores (Hypothesis 2B)?
3. Does water extraction alter food webs by shrinking habitat and increasing temperature and salinity (Hypothesis 3)?

**Key observations cited as motivation**
- *Hemichromis* sp. was first observed in Laguna Churince in 1995 and was abundant by 1997.
- The papilliform morph of *C. minckleyi* in Laguna Churince declined from about 75% of the population before the introduction to about 10% in 1998. Circumstantial evidence suggests competition, but this is not experimentally confirmed.
- Historic records include more than 1,000 catalogued lots, scattered across eight institutions in non-uniform formats. Almost no records exist after the mid-1980s.

**Methods and tasks**
- **Task 1:** Consolidate historic collections into a standardised, geo-referenced relational database and GIS, published online.
- **Task 2:** A low-resolution survey at 50 sites, with water chemistry measured in the field and in the laboratory. A high-resolution survey at 10 sites, using mark-recapture, transects and visual census.
- **Task 3:** Stable isotope analysis (δ¹³C, δ¹⁵N, δ³⁴S) of historic and modern specimens. The authors predict enriched δ¹⁵N in the molluscivore morph and a shift in energy source in invaded pools.
- **Tasks 4 and 5:** Enclosure experiments on *M. tuberculata* predation, growth and competition, and on *Hemichromis* sp. effects on *C. minckleyi* morphs. Experiments run for about 4 to 8 weeks.
- **Task 6:** Simple simulation models built with reserve staff and TNC. Three modelling workshops are planned, using Stella.

**Management options considered**
- *Hydrologic:* do nothing; promote agricultural best practices; buy or trade water rights; restore flows by erecting dams in canals.
- *Invasive species:* do nothing; eradicate or reduce *Hemichromis* sp.; eradicate or reduce *M. tuberculata*. Feasibility and cost are rated qualitatively in Tables 3 and 4.

**Collaboration and significance**
- The authors state this is possibly the first use of historical reconstruction, isotope studies and field experiments together in conservation science, and an early example of ecosystem-based adaptive management.
- The team includes Dean Hendrickson (University of Texas curator of ichthyology), a researcher from Northern Arizona University, and a TNC Northeastern Mexico programme lead.

**Correspondence (draft support letter)**
- Hendrickson reported having sufficient matching funds without requiring matches from the co-investigator, and asked for a letter committing to consultation and georeferencing review of historic collections.
- The letter requests access to specimens and tissue samples for isotope analysis from the ASU collection, and to Profauna canal-history data.
