# Bullfrog Tadpole and Red Swamp Crayfish Predation on Early Life Stages of Endangered Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Introduced [[American Bullfrog]] tadpoles and [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] are widespread in the western United States. Their effects on native amphibians and crustaceans are well documented, but their influence on native fishes was largely unknown. Laboratory predator-prey trials showed that both species consumed eggs and larvae of the federally endangered [[Razorback Sucker]]. Field video monitoring at [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] found high densities of both predators among spawning fish. The authors conclude that these nontraditional predators may threaten recruitment of the razorback sucker and other imperiled native fishes, and they recommend closer scrutiny of predator-control programs.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) once occurred throughout the Colorado River basin. Its populations have declined, and its range and abundance have contracted. It was federally listed as endangered in 1991. Natural recruitment is intermittent. Nonnative fishes are rare at Cibola High Levee Pond, so they probably do not explain the lack of young fish alone. Earlier work attributed periodic recruitment failure to predation by nonnative fishes, but the authors suggest other predators may be responsible.

Bullfrog tadpoles (*Rana catesbeiana*) and red swamp crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*) are introduced taxa with broad distributions. Both are also abundant in culturing facilities. Known impacts include predation on native anurans and salamanders and on recreational and native fish eggs. Evidence that they consume live fish larvae has been limited.

The study site, [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] (CibolaHLP), is a human-made off-channel pond on the low [[Lower Colorado River]] that supports razorback sucker and [[Bonytail]] spawning. Razorback sucker eggs and larvae were supplied by [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] for the trials.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predator-prey context**
- Razorback sucker recruitment has been linked to removal of large nonnative predators. Removing these predators can allow nontraditional predators, such as predaceous insects, crustaceans, and amphibians, to increase in abundance.
- Previous work showed that razorback sucker larvae are highly susceptible to odonate predators.
- Tadpole predation has been documented on African catfish (*Heterobranchus longifilis*) larvae, which were most vulnerable before fin development (<6 d).
- Crayfish have been shown to eat young [[Lake Trout]], juvenile [[Gila Chub]], suckers, and speckled dace in laboratory settings.

**Site conditions**
- Cibola HLP holds large numbers of bullfrog tadpoles and crayfish.
- Nonnative fishes were rare, making up less than 0.7% of 3,760 fish sampled (Mueller et al., 2005).

## Key Findings

### Laboratory predation trials

Tests were run in aerated aquaria with separation screens, and no cover or substrate was provided so that prey could be counted accurately. Each tank held 20 razorback sucker eggs or larvae and either 4 tadpoles, 2 crayfish, or no predators (control). Exposure lasted 24 h.

| Predator | Prey | Consumption rate (individuals/day) |
|---|---|---|
| Bullfrog tadpole | Eggs | 2.2 |
| Bullfrog tadpole | Larvae | 1.4 |
| Red swamp crayfish | Eggs | 6.0 |
| Red swamp crayfish | Larvae | 3.5 |

- Tadpoles consumed an average of 2.2 eggs/d across tests, and 1.4 larvae/d (n = 7 tests).
- Crayfish consumed an average of 6.0 eggs/d and 3.5 larvae/d (n = 12 tests for larvae).
- Only 3 of 200 control fish died across the control tests (n = 10 tests), so control mortality was low.
- Larger fry (>14 mm) were used for some crayfish trials because of larval shortages.

### Preliminary test

Before the structured trials, a preliminary test was run at the site. One hundred eggs were placed in each of four tanks, and 25 bullfrog tadpoles were added to three of them. After 72 h, the eggs were absent from every tank containing tadpoles but present in the control tank.

### Underwater video monitoring

- Twelve 2-h daylight video sessions were analyzed, recorded between 18 February and 17 April 2003.
- Four areas were filmed: a razorback sucker spawning area, a bonytail spawning area, and two randomly selected areas not used by spawners.
- Bullfrog tadpole densities averaged 2.1 tadpoles/m² (range 0.9 to 3.7 tadpoles/m²) across 286 frames, and densities increased over the study.
- Adult crayfish densities averaged 0.1 crayfish/m² (reported range 0.0 to 11.1 crayfish/m²).
- Estimated population sizes at the pond were more than 48,000 tadpoles and more than 2,000 crayfish.
- Both predators were commonly seen feeding among spawning fish.

### Conclusions and management implications

- Both species consumed razorback sucker eggs and larvae under laboratory conditions, and both were abundant among spawners at Cibola HLP.
- Predation by these taxa may explain some of the intermittent recruitment of razorback sucker at Cibola HLP, though this was not directly tested.
- Predator-control programs should consider the effects of removing large nonnative fishes, which may cause [[Introduced Species]] such as bullfrogs and crayfish to increase.
- The authors call for closer evaluation of these interactions because they may threaten the early life stages of native fishes, not only razorback sucker.
