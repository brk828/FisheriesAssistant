# Locomotor and Feeding Behavior of Postlarval Clupeoidea

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeoidea

## Executive Summary

This 1943 paper by [[C. M. Breder Jr.]] and [[Louis A. Krumholz]] describes the locomotion, equilibrium and feeding behavior of laboratory-reared postlarvae of [[Anchoa Mitchilli]] and [[Harengula Pensacolae]], collected near [[Palmetto Key]], Florida, during work on the life history of the [[Tarpon]]. The postlarvae are in unstable equilibrium, stay afloat through alternating sinking and swimming, and capture plankton by stalking and striking. The authors argue that these traits disappear at adulthood and that they do not clearly indicate phylogenetic relationships within the Isospondyli.

## Background & Description

The study was carried out at the laboratory of the [[New York Aquarium]] on Palmetto Key, Florida. The authors were working on tarpon life history and needed to handle all other isospondylous fishes in the area, so they gave particular attention to their larvae. Postlarvae of two species were kept alive in laboratory aquaria: *Anchoa mitchilli*, which the authors refer to *A. m. diaphana* following [[Hildebrand 1943]], and *Harengula pensacolae*, which [[Storey 1938]] assigned as the correct name for the local form. The observations were made in the summers of 1940 to 1942. The paper is organized into locomotor behavior, feeding behavior, a discussion of phylogeny, and a summary.

The postlarvae were very fragile. Even gentle handling triggered the disturbances described below, so fin-clipping and similar direct methods were not practical. A few individuals did survive [[Tow-Net Sampling]] in large catches and could then be established in aquaria.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish inhabit shallow coastal waters. Schools of postlarval anchovies often settled quietly near pilings on wide shallow flats, while in the open sea they moved about more actively. Daytime tow-net samples contained far more of these fish near the bottom than at the surface. Only on dark nights could they be taken in any quantity at the surface. The authors suggest this reflects a negative phototaxis, with daylight holding the fish down. A bright light above an aquarium likewise forced them to a lower level.

Food was planktonic crustaceans, of many kinds, that were abundant in the local waters. Postlarvae were kept alive mainly by replenishing sea water in standing aquaria. A cross-species point appears in the paper: [[Mugil Cephalus]], a young striped mullet, briefly joined the herring school but did not show the sinking-swimming pattern at that size, and its attempts to school were eventually abandoned. The school broke up at darkness, which the authors take as evidence that schooling in these fishes is primarily visual.

## Key Findings

**1. Unstable equilibrium and buoyancy**
- Postlarval *A. mitchilli* are slightly heavier than sea water. Their [[Swim Bladder]] sits below the [[Center of Gravity]], producing an unstable equilibrium.
- When swimming stopped, active, apparently normal postlarvae sank, and on sickening or slight shock they turned over and sank ventral side up.
- Adults are closer to neutral buoyancy but still slightly heavier than water. They show a small corrective swimming effort and irregular pectoral fin movements. Sick adults also sink back-down before death.

**2. Anti-roll mechanism**
- The [[Pectoral Fins]] correct rolling. In a roll to one side, the fin on that side beats down and the opposite fin beats up.
- The fins then fold against the body and thrust out again to a position that can check a roll in either direction.
- The authors describe these movements as very slight and rapid, and say they are exaggerated in the diagrams.

**3. Sinking-swimming locomotion**
- Non-feeding postlarval *A. mitchilli* hold the body inclined upward and forward at about 30° to the horizontal.
- They sink for about one-half their length, then swim forward and regain depth. Each cycle carries them forward about 1.5 body lengths.
- The pattern produces forward translation interrupted by sinking, most visible in broad, quietly milling schools.
- The same sinking-swimming pattern persisted in adult [[Harengula Pensacolae]]. In a photograph of a school of about 30 mm standard length, five side-on fish showed angles of 22°, 88.5°, 32.5°, 32.5° and 40°, reported as a mean of 32.1°, close to the 30° estimate for *Anchoa*.
- *Note for review:* the five listed angles average about 43.1°, not 32.1°. The reported mean may contain a transcription error in the source.

**4. Disturbance by food and other factors**
- Ingested food, digestion and voided excrement shift the balance between the centers of gravity and buoyancy fore and aft, so equilibrium is constantly corrected.
- A copepod attached to one specimen's dorsum was fully compensated for, and that fish could not be told apart from its companions by behavior.

**5. Tarpon respiratory behavior**
- In [[Tarpon]] (*Tarpon atlanticus*) aquarium specimens of about 6 to 100 cm, respiratory gulps alter buoyancy. Fish become heavier as the time to rise for air approaches, then rush to the surface and gulp.
- After air ingestion they are lighter than water and often need to release small bubbles through the gill clefts before regaining balance.
- The authors suggest that the increase in specific gravity afterwards reflects progressive loss of buoyancy through branchial elimination of CO₂.
- They refer readers to [[Shlaifer and Breder 1940]] and [[Breder 1942a]] for details.

**6. Feeding strike**
- Postlarvae of about 15 to 20 mm stalk planktonic prey, orient to "point" it, adopt an S-shaped stance and then straighten explosively to ingest the prey.
- The strike is followed by a long sweeping curve that ends in normal stiff-bodied swimming.
- Approaches were always made from at least slightly below.
- *Harengula* postlarvae strike similarly, though with less pronounced flexure because of their shorter, deeper bodies. The behavior is abandoned at about the size shown in Plate I, when the body becomes stiffer and thicker.
- Prey never avoided the fish. The authors call the elaborate stalking unnecessary for such slow organisms.
- The behavior was also successful on newly hatched [[Artemia]], which the fish never encounter in the wild. The authors conclude that the behavior is not an adaptation to any single prey type.

**7. Phylogenetic interpretation**
- Following [[Gregory 1933]], the authors note that leptocephalus-like postlarvae are shared by many Isospondyli and [[Apodes]], which suggests a common origin.
- Eel-like striking in the [[Moray Eel]] and sea snakes is similar, but the authors argue the postlarvae are too short-bodied for eel-like locomotion to be of special benefit.
- Instability of equilibrium is widespread among Isospondyli and is probably ancient, so it is of limited value for phylogeny.
- [[Hubbs 1941]] reports that unrelated fishes have independently evolved similarly sub-leptocephaloid postlarvae. The authors call for comparative studies of feeding and stability in Isospondyli, Apodes and other groups before physical necessity can be separated from heredity.

**8. Developmental trajectory**
- Both the locomotor pattern and the feeding strike disappear as the fish mature. The authors summarize this as: "These features largely but not completely disappear as the adult form is reached," and the feeding feature "disappears as the adult form is reached."
- Adult postlarvae that are "bored" by the strike instead take prey in the usual fish manner, though they sometimes revert to postlarval patterns.

**9. Summary of conclusions**
- The postlarvae are in unstable equilibrium, and the paired pectoral fins chiefly maintain it.
- The instability makes locomotion an alternating series of sinking and swimming periods.
- The feeding pattern is unnecessary for capturing such inert plankton, and the authors suggest it may be linked to the instability, but the mechanism remains unclear.
- The behavior of young Isospondyli is currently too generalized to help resolve relationships.
