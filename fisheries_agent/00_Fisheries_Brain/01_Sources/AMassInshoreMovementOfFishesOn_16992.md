# A Mass Inshore Movement of Fishes on the Florida Coast

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ophichthidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Carter R. Gilbert, documents a mass shoreward movement of demersal fishes on the Atlantic coast of Florida. The event occurred on the afternoon of 17 September 1962 along the beach south of [[Marineland Florida]], about 50 miles south of Jacksonville. It was the first reported [[Jubilee Phenomenon]] from the US Atlantic coast. The author compares it with the better-studied [[Mobile Bay Jubilee]] in Alabama, which Loesch (1960) explained through low-[[Dissolved Oxygen]] water pushed inshore by wind and tide. The Marineland event differed from the Mobile Bay pattern in timing, tide stage, and setting, so its cause remained unresolved. The paper also catalogs resident and non-resident fish species found at the site and reports a first United States record of the deep-water eel [[Ophichthus Melanoporus]].

## Background & Description

Sporadic mass shoreward movements of marine animals are known from several regions. Periodic fish kills have been recorded at Walvis Bay, Southwest Africa, and Concepción Bay, Chile, under particular meteorological and oceanographic conditions. In US waters, the best-documented case is Mobile Bay, Alabama. There, the author reports 35 occurrences from 1946 to 1956, ranging from none in 1954 to ten in 1950. Locally these events are called "Jubilees," and residents gather the stranded animals.

The author also notes large aggregations of freshwater fishes, with no apparent spawning or physical barrier, such as one near Gainesville, Florida, examined in March 1963. Chemical analysis of that water showed nothing abnormal.

The focal event at [[Marineland Beach]] was seen at about 3:00 PM on 17 September 1962. The beach is composed of quartz sand typical of the upper east Florida coast and contains [[Coquina Rocks]] at or near the waterline. Many fishes, mainly eels, were stranded but many remained alive in their burrows. Mortality appeared to result only from prolonged air exposure, since fishes still in the water seemed unaffected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mobile Bay mechanism (Loesch, 1960).** Jubilees there occur only in summer, usually before sunrise, under a gentle easterly wind, and on a rising tide. A change in wind or tide stops them. A deep, saline, oxygen-poor pocket underlies fresh water from the [[Alabama River]]. Warm temperatures and decaying organic debris deplete oxygen and add carbon dioxide. Wind pushes surface water offshore, and the deeper water moves shoreward on the flood tide. Demersal animals crowd toward the shore as this layer approaches.

**Marineland contrasts.** The author lists seven differences from Mobile Bay:
- The event occurred in mid-afternoon, not before sunrise.
- The tide was falling (high tide about 10:30 AM, low about 5:00 PM by the approximate tidal charts), not rising.
- The area is not partially enclosed by land.
- The jubilee was also noted about 4 miles north, just above [[Matanzas Inlet]].
- No unusual invertebrate concentration was seen, apart from pteropods.
- No major freshwater stream enters the ocean there, so no large salt–fresh water meeting occurs.
- No large organic debris load was believed present.

Wind direction was not recorded, but the day was very calm, and any wind was presumably from the east.

**Pteropods.** Pelagic [[Pteropod Mollusks]] (Opisthobranchia) occurred in great numbers at Marineland and as far north as the St. Johns River. The author judged the pteropods and fishes probably coincidental, given their differing ecology.

**Weather and other hypotheses.** Local observers reported an earlier jubilee in late summer 1956 after several weeks of hot, calm weather, and similar conditions preceded the September 1962 event. Frederick H. Berry proposed deep subsurface waves (175–200 fathoms) over the northeastern Florida slope. Paul Struhsaker, who studied those waves, doubted a link. His analysis of [[Bottom Temperature]] data from the research vessel Theodore N. Gill found no late-summer condition that could explain the jubilee.

**Species assemblage.** The approximately 28 species named in the paper fall into three ecological groups:
1. **Resident open-bottom fishes** (5 species), such as *Trachinotus carolinus*, *Trachinotus falcatus*, *Menticirrhus littoralis*, *Umbrina coroides* and *Eucinostomus argenteus*.
2. **Resident [[Rocky Shore Fishes]]** (10 species), mostly young individuals, including *Centropristis striatus*, *Rypticus maculatus*, *Anisotremus surinamensis*, *Scorpaena plumieri*, *Abudefduf* spp., *Labrisomus nuchipinnis*, *Hypleurochilus geminatus*, *Hypsoblennius hentz* and *Gobiesox strumosus*.
3. **Probable non-resident species** (11 species), thought to have moved in from deeper water. Most are burrowers from three families: [[Bothidae]], [[Ophidiidae]] and [[Ophichthidae]]. All but one are often found in shallow water.

The author notes that the deep-burrowing snake eels are poorly sampled by trawls and that [[Rotenone Sampling]] has recovered many species from very shallow water, suggesting their true abundance is underestimated.

## Key Findings

- **Frequency of Mobile Bay jubilees:** 35 recorded from 1946 to 1956, with the annual count ranging from 0 (1954) to 10 (1950).
- **First Atlantic jubilee record:** The Marineland event of 17 September 1962 appeared to be the first reported from the US Atlantic coast, to the author's knowledge.
- **Mechanism at Mobile Bay:** Low-oxygen, high-CO₂ saline water pushed inshore by east wind and a rising tide, as described by Loesch (1960).
- **Marineland conditions:** The event occurred under calm, hot weather that followed several weeks of similar conditions, and the tide was falling. The author concluded that the cause remains undetermined.
- **Working hypothesis:** Fishes (and possibly pteropods) were driven shoreward by an oxygen-poor, CO₂-rich water mass of unknown origin. The absence of unusual bottom-invertebrate concentrations was described as puzzling.
- **Mortality:** Stranded fishes that stayed in burrows survived, and mortality was attributed to air exposure.
- **Non-resident species:** Eleven species, mostly burrowers from Bothidae, Ophidiidae and Ophichthidae, probably moved in from deeper water.
- **First US record of *Ophichthus melanoporus*:** The single specimen, collected by John Taylor on the beach just north of Matanzas Inlet, was previously known only from five specimens taken at 250 fathoms off Andros Island, Bahamas. The author suggests it was either a stray or that the species has a wider distribution than known.
- **Additional records:** *Rypticus maculatus* was previously reported from 15–50 fathoms, but the author found it common within a few feet of shore at Anna Maria Island (15 specimens, July 1963). *Bascanichthys scuticaris* was common at Cedar Key, Florida, in oyster beds.
- **Negative rotenone results:** Later collections at the jubilee site found no flatfishes, cusk-eels or snake eels, though the author notes this does not prove they were absent at the time of the event.
- **Remaining question:** The cause of the Marineland jubilee is not yet explained, and more data are needed on the meteorological and oceanographic conditions involved.
