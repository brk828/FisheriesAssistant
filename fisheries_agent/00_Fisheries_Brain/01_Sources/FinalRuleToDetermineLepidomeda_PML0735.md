# Little Colorado Spinedace Listed as Threatened with Critical Habitat (Federal Register, 1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Final Rule from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Federal Register, Vol. 52, No. 179, September 18, 1987) determines the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), a small cyprinid endemic to east-central Arizona, to be a threatened species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. The rule designates critical habitat on three stream systems, proposes a special rule allowing certain takes under Arizona collecting permits, and sets an effective date of October 16, 1987. Historic declines stem mainly from impoundment, water withdrawal, channelization, grazing, and other land uses, compounded by [[Exotic Fish Species]] predation and competition and the use of [[Piscicides]] (ichthyotoxicants).

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and type locality:** First collected west of the 100th meridian by the [[U.S. Topographical and Geographical Survey]] (Wheeler 1889) and described by E.D. Cope in 1874. The type locality, the "Chiquito Colorado," was later defined as the Little Colorado River between the mouth of the Zuni River and Sierra Blanca (White Mountain).
- **Family and size:** Member of the family Cyprinidae; generally less than 10 cm (4 inches) in total length.
- **Range:** Endemic to the upper Little Colorado River and its north-flowing permanent tributaries on the Mogollon Rim and northern slopes of the White Mountains. The historic range has been significantly reduced over the past 50 years.
- **Population dynamics:** Populations fluctuate dramatically with drought and rainfall cycles. Human water demand has intensified these lows and reduced highs. Populations have fallen to extremely low levels several times within the past 25 years, making range delineation difficult.
- **Habitat preference:** Inhabits very small to moderate-sized streams, typically pools with flowing water over fine gravel and silt-mud substrates. During drought it persists in springs and intermittent streambed pools; during flooding it disperses throughout the stream. Spawning occurs primarily in early summer and continues at reduced levels into early fall.
- **Regulatory history:** Listed as a Category 1 candidate in the Service's December 30, 1982 review. A petition from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] was received April 12, 1983, and a 90-day finding was published June 14, 1983. A "warranted" finding was published July 19, 1984, and a proposed rule on May 22, 1985. Arizona lists the species as Group 3 (threatened) under its wildlife rules.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Known populations (surveyed locations):**

1. **East Clear Creek and tributaries (Coconino County):** About 35 stream miles from the Clear Creek confluence to headwaters near Potato Lake, within the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] and [[Coconino National Forest]]. Leonard Canyon at Dines Tank is the only tributary currently known to harbor the species.
2. **Chevelon Creek (Navajo County):** About 8 miles from the Little Colorado River confluence near Winslow to Bell Cow Canyon, mostly private land, including the [[Chevelon Creek Wildlife Area]].
3. **Silver Creek (Navajo County):** About 20 stream miles, mostly on private land.
4. **Little Colorado River (Apache County):** Sporadic occurrence across about 10 miles from St. Johns upstream to Greer.
5. **Nutrioso Creek (Apache County):** About 12 stream miles, of which about 5 miles lie within the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest.

**Threat factors (Section 4(a)(1) analysis):**

- **Habitat destruction and modification:** Roughly 150 impoundments exist in the basin, ranging from small stock tanks to reservoirs up to 1,400 surface acres. Reservoirs are uninhabitable for the spinedace, inundate habitat, and cause downstream dewatering. Riparian destruction, mining, timber harvest, road construction, and livestock grazing add to the impacts. The Little Colorado River now flows perennially in only the uppermost 10 to 15 percent of its length, down from extensive historic perennial flow.
- **Proposed projects:** [[Wilkin's Dam]] (a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] project at the Clear and East Clear Creek confluence, inundating about 8 miles of stream) is currently inactive. The Arizona Game and Fish Department has identified nine potential reservoir sites within the species' range.
- **Predation and competition:** Exotic predators, including black bullhead, channel catfish, yellow bullhead, green sunfish, largemouth bass, and brown trout, are a significant threat. Golden shiner, red shiner (used as bait), and fathead minnow are additional competitive threats. The species is generally rare or absent where exotic fishes other than trout are present.
- **Piscicides:** Rotenone and toxaphene treatments were applied to much of the drainage. The Little Colorado River was treated for about 10 miles in 1981, and Chevelon Creek was treated twice in 1965 and again later. These treatments likely reduced both population size and range.
- **Regulatory inadequacy:** Group 3 status regulates take through collecting permits or licensed angling but provides no habitat protection and no management plan.

**Critical habitat designated:**

- East Clear Creek: about 18 miles from Leonard Canyon to Blue Ridge Reservoir dam, plus about 13 miles from Blue Ridge Reservoir to Potato Lake.
- Chevelon Creek: about 8 miles from the Little Colorado River confluence to Bell Cow Canyon.
- Nutrioso Creek: about 5 miles from the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest boundary to Nelson Reservoir dam.

Constituent elements include clean, permanent flowing water, pools, and a fine gravel or silt-mud substrate.

## Key Findings

- **Listing decision:** The species is listed as threatened rather than endangered. Its range is severely reduced, but it remains extant in several locations and threats are largely localized.
- **Primary threats:** Habitat alteration (impoundment, dewatering, grazing, and water development) is the principal threat. Exotic predators and competitors are a major secondary threat, and piscicide use has significantly reduced populations.
- **Critical habitat scope:** Designation covers only occupied, healthy stream segments (about 44 miles total, per the rule's Section 4 discussion). The Service declined to designate entire watersheds, judging that the best available data did not support their criticality, though buffer zones may be reconsidered later.
- **Section 7 consultation:** Federal actions that may affect the species or its critical habitat require formal consultation. Existing operations are not necessarily halted, though they would be subject to Section 7(a)(2) if Federal involvement continues.
- **Special rule (Section 4(d)):** Takes are permitted for educational, scientific, propagation, zoological, and other conservation purposes, provided a valid Arizona collecting permit is held. Piscicide use is effectively precluded unless part of an approved conservation plan. Incidental take by licensed anglers is not a violation if the fish is immediately returned.
- **Economic and environmental review:** The economic analysis found Federal costs minimal and no significant impacts on individuals, state or local governments, or the regional economy. An Environmental Assessment was not required under the Service's 1983 NEPA notice, and an EIS was judged impractical and unnecessary.
- **Opposition:** Phelps Dodge Corporation opposed the listing, citing water supply to its Morenci operations and future development limits. The Service responded that critical habitat does not prevent all development but prohibits Federal actions likely to destroy or adversely modify it. The U.S. Forest Service and Arizona Department of Transportation supported listing but opposed critical habitat. The Nature Conservancy supported both, recommending full watershed designation, which the Service declined.
- **Public input:** Fifteen comment letters were received; no public hearing was requested or held.
