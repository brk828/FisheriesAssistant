# River Resource Monitoring Project: Grand Canyon 1977 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 annual report, prepared by the [[Museum of Northern Arizona]] under a contract with the [[National Park Service]], documents the first year of the River Resource Monitoring Project in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The project tracked human impacts on beaches and riparian vegetation along the [[Colorado River]], inventoried riparian fauna and flora, and monitored the native [[Gila Genus]]. Its most significant fish result is that the only Gila taken was the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), 39 individuals in total, with several year classes present. A companion study found that human food litter raised densities of two beach invertebrates, the [[Red Harvester Ant]] and the [[Common Ant Lion]], by roughly an order of magnitude at campsites.

## Background & Description

The project was run in partial fulfillment of a 1977 General Work Plan and Schedule and the proposal under Project No. CX821070012. Its six objectives were:

1. Measure the rate and magnitude of environmental degradation in the riparian zone, with reference to recreation.
2. Identify which environmental elements best correlate with that degradation.
3. Develop simple tests that non-technical personnel can use for monitoring.
4. Predict vegetation trends in the riparian zone, assuming periodic releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]].
5. Inventory plants and animals of the riparian zone, with attention to legally endangered and threatened species.
6. Monitor population trends of the genus Gila and predict effects of time, dam construction and water releases on it.

Effort in 1977 included four river expeditions of 14 to 18 days (March, July, November and December) and a four-day land-based endangered species survey of the [[Little Colorado River]] in September. Field work totaled about 700 person-days, and preliminary data analysis about 250 person-days. The November–December expedition was funded by the Bureau of Reclamation, but its fish and terrestrial vertebrate data were included.

Early in 1977 the team found that several planned methods were oversampled, technically inadequate or logistically impossible. The Principal Investigators, [[Steven W. Carothers]] and [[Robert Dolan]], agreed changes with the National Park Service COAR, Dr. R. Roy Johnson. Key changes included:

- **Beach debris sampling:** Sand and charcoal samples per beach were doubled from 10 to 20, and the charcoal count area was enlarged from 1/4 m² to 1 m². The September sampling was dropped to offset the added effort, and the July sampling was slated for elimination in future surveys.
- **Vegetation transects:** Ground-truth transects on five intensively sampled beaches were postponed to March 1978 because low-altitude aerial photography was needed first.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regulation and vegetation.** Since Glen Canyon Dam was completed, flow has been largely controlled and the historic spring floods that scoured the lower riparian zone have stopped. Scoured zones are becoming permanently established, and zones once maintained by the high-water line are no longer recharged. A new artificial riparian zone has formed along the controlled shoreline.

**Beach types.** Beaches were classed by their source of sediment. Side-canyon floodplain beaches (for example Saddle Canyon and National Canyon) receive material from flash floods. Other beaches, such as Deer Creek, are built by the river or wind and are less stable. Eddies and slow backwaters, as at Saddle Canyon and Nankoweap, favor silt and nutrient deposition and vegetation.

**Four vegetation zones.** The zones are:

- Salt cedar–willow, dominated by *Tamarix chinensis* and *Salix* sp., found along the river edge on all five beaches.
- Sandy beach, with grasses (*Sporobolus*, *Stipa*) and shrubs (*Pluchea*).
- Rocky beach, with grasses, shrubs and forbs, including *Bromus rubens* and *Salsola kali*.
- Mesquite–catclaw, dominated by *Prosopis juliflora* and *Acacia greggii*, with understory mats of red brome and cheatgrass (*Bromus tectorum*) that stabilize sand. See [[Mesquite-Catclaw Zone]].

**Human food subsidy.** Littered food is the dominant energy input to the beach food chain at campsites. Harvester ants scavenge dropped food, and the energy then moves up to ant lions.

## Key Findings

### Human debris on beaches (Objectives 1–3)

Only preliminary interpretation is possible, since statistical tests require at least four sampling periods.

| Measure | March 1977 | July 1977 |
|---|---|---|
| Charcoal >1 cm, grand mean per m² | 10.3 | 7.9 |
| Charcoal <1 cm, grand mean per sand sample | 12.1 | 9.9 |
| Human debris, grand mean particles/m² | 0.88 | 0.79 |
| Mean charcoal particle size (<1 cm) | 0.12 mm | 0.08 mm |

- Trampling depth averaged about 6.5 cm and did not change between March and July.
- Mean sand grain size was about 0.25 mm and did not change.
- The apparent March-to-July decline in debris and charcoal may reflect natural purging, since river use was low from September 1976 to June 1977. There are not yet enough samples to confirm this.

### Harvester ants and ant lions (Objectives 1–3)

- Population densities in impacted camp habitats were roughly an order of magnitude greater than in low-impact areas, for both the herbivorous Red Harvester Ant (*Pogonomyrmex californicus*) and the carnivorous Common Ant Lion (*Myrmeleon* sp.). The density values in the source table are partly garbled by OCR.
- Impact extended to sleeping areas, where snacks carried from kitchens raised ant lion densities above those in unused areas.
- Ant lions rarely caught large harvester ants and relied on smaller insects, so the two species occupy different food chains.
- Harvester ant hill counts closely predicted colony density. A curvilinear model was judged more realistic than a linear one, because colonies cannot pack indefinitely. Yearly hill counts could therefore serve as inexpensive trend monitoring.
- Campsite ants were markedly less aggressive than those in non-camp areas. They collected liquid food and showed a division of labor for large food items. They are inactive at night, and caution around hills (within 50 cm) makes them a manageable nuisance.

### Inventory (Objective 5)

Bird, mammal, reptile and amphibian observations were recorded on each trip, and many plant specimens were collected. Neither set had been reduced to tables or identified at the time of the report.

### Humpback chub monitoring (Objective 6)

Monitoring used gill nets and seines, with 13 localities sampled in March, 11 in July and 27 in November. The humpback chub was the only Gila taken.

- **Catch by trip:** March 1 (230 mm total length), July 25 (88–423 mm), September 3 (278–295 mm), and November 10 (61–340 mm). Total: 39 chubs, 61–423 mm.
- **Locations:** The main concentration was at the mouth of the Little Colorado River. The September specimens came from 12.8 km upstream in the Little Colorado, the first records from that reach. November records added new Colorado River localities at river mile 27 (three chubs, 237–340 mm) and river mile 70 (one chub, 223 mm). No chubs were taken from other tributaries.
- **Associated species:** Flannelmouth sucker, bluehead mountain sucker, channel catfish and speckled dace.
- **Age structure:** Using length-based criteria, young-of-the-year made up 2.6% of the sample, and juveniles and adults were about equal (48.7%). By age class, 2.6% were class 0, 33.3% class I, 15.3% class II, 10.2% class III, and 35.8% older. Gill nets favor large fish, which likely explains the low count of young. January 1978 collections produced more young, suggesting better reproduction than 1977 indicated.
- **Reproduction:** No daily reproductive activity was observed, but the July catch may have included a spawning aggregation, and one gravid female was taken. The March chub showed reproductive coloration, suggesting spawning may run from March into July–August.
- **Life history:** Related Gila species live five to eight years, so *Gila cypha* may live as long or longer. Food habits, movement and general ecology remain largely unknown.

### Overall conclusions

- Human use measurably alters beach food chains and debris levels, though the 1977 data are too few for firm trend conclusions.
- Vegetation zones are shifting because flow regulation has removed periodic scouring.
- The humpback chub population in the Grand Canyon includes several year classes and appears to reproduce, but its status needs more extensive study.
