# Differentiation in *Coregonus zenithicus* in Lake Superior

## Executive Summary
This 1980 study by Todd and Smith re-examines taxonomic confusion surrounding [[Coregonus Zenithicus]] (shortjaw cisco) and two previously described but questionable taxa, [[Coregonus Nigripinnis Cyanopterus]] and [[Coregonus Reighardi Dymondi]], in Lake Superior and Lake Nipigon. Using multivariate morphometrics (principal-component analysis and mean character difference/"city-block" distance coefficients) on 302 specimens from historical (1917–1923, W. Koelz) and modern (1973–1974) collections, the authors demonstrate that morphological differences long used to separate these forms are attributable to **allometric growth** (age/size effects) and **environmentally induced developmental plasticity**, rather than genetic species-level divergence. The paper further shows that geographically distinct populations of *C. zenithicus* differ from each other by roughly the same magnitude as fall- vs. spring-spawning populations differ from each other — implying that reproductive (spawning-time) isolation and geographic isolation produce comparable levels of phenotypic differentiation, and that spawning-time divergence alone is insufficient grounds for species-level taxonomic separation in [[Coregoninae]]. The authors formally synonymize *C. n. cyanopterus* and *C. r. dymondi* under *C. zenithicus*, with important implications for the conservation management of this now vulnerable/endangered Great Lakes cisco.

## Background & Description
- **Diagnostic features of *C. zenithicus*** (relative to other Lake Superior ciscoes): longer snout, shorter fins, fewer and shorter gillrakers, lower jaw included within upper jaw, premaxillaries more vertically oriented.
- **29 morphometric/meristic characters measured**, including: standard length, gillraker number, lateral line scales, head length, occiput length, dorsal/anal base lengths, dorsal–adipose and adipose–caudal distances, body depth/width, snout–dorsal and snout–anal distances, eye diameter, maxillary length, snout length, mandible length, adipose height, gillraker length, paired-fin measurements, and fin ray counts.
- **Comparative gillraker counts** (Table 1, data from Koelz 1929):

| Taxon | Mean Gillraker Number | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| *C. n. nigripinnis* (L. Michigan/Huron) | 47 | 1.9 |
| *C. n. cyanopterus* (L. Superior) | 40.4 | 0.7 |
| *C. zenithicus* (L. Superior) | 40.2 | 0.9 |
| *C. r. dymondi* (L. Superior) | 36.8 | 0.9 |

- Gillraker number difference between *C. r. dymondi* and *C. zenithicus* (mean = 3.4) was comparable to differences observed between wild-caught parents and laboratory-reared coregonine progeny (Todd et al. 1981), supporting an environmental (developmental plasticity) rather than genetic origin.
- **Size vs. age**: *C. n. cyanopterus* specimens were consistently older, not a different species — their larger size matched expectations for old *C. zenithicus* individuals (allometric growth artifact).
- Principal Component I in PCA plots reflected size/age differences rather than true taxonomic separation between *C. zenithicus* and *C. n. cyanopterus*.
- PCA of *C. r. dymondi* vs. *C. zenithicus* (Thunder Bay) showed overlapping, parallel clusters resembling patterns between wild parents and lab-reared offspring — again suggesting conspecificity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Deepwater cisco occupying nearly all lake regions below the thermocline in Lake Superior, Lake Nipigon, and (for related *C. reighardi reighardi*) Lake Michigan and Lake Ontario.
- **Spawning ecology**: Two temporally distinct spawning populations documented in *C. zenithicus*:
  - **Fall/late-autumn spawners** (November–December) — historically best known, associated with *C. r. dymondi* phenotype in northern bays.
  - **Spring spawners** (May–June) — increasingly documented over the last 25 years (as of publication), most abundant near the Keweenaw Peninsula, but also recorded at the Apostle Islands (Wisconsin) and Grand Marais (Michigan).
- **Geographic structuring**: Bays (e.g., Thunder Bay, Coppermine Point) appear particularly effective at isolating distinctive local stocks, consistent with patterns in [[Coregonus Clupeaformis]] (lake whitefish).
- **Biogeography of synonymized taxa**: *C. r. dymondi* populations (=*C. zenithicus*) occur almost entirely in northern bays of Lake Superior and Lake Nipigon.
- Tagging studies (Smith and Van Oosten 1940) indicate coregonine movement generally does not exceed ~80 km from tagging location, while some *C. clupeaformis* populations show overlapping distributions approaching 160 km (Imhof 1977; Leary 1979) — suggesting variable population/stock structuring across the genus.
- Comparison to European congener [[Coregonus Albula]]: multiple populations spawn at different times of year without necessarily representing distinct species, analogous to findings for *C. zenithicus*.

## Key Findings
1. **Taxonomic synonymy established**: *Coregonus nigripinnis cyanopterus* and *Coregonus reighardi dymondi* are junior synonyms of *Coregonus zenithicus* (which has taxonomic priority, Jordan and Evermann 1909); differences are due to allometric growth (age-related size) and environmentally induced developmental plasticity, not genetic speciation.
2. **Spawning-time differentiation ≈ geographic differentiation**: Morphological distance between spring- and fall-spawning *C. zenithicus* populations is statistically indistinguishable from distances between geographically separate populations (Table 2), indicating spawning time alone should not be used to define separate species.
3. **Distance coefficient analysis (Table 3)**: *C. zenithicus* and *C. r. dymondi* from Lake Superior and Lake Nipigon show consistently low mean character differences (0.95–1.1), while comparisons with *C. r. reighardi* (Lake Michigan) show significantly greater distances (1.3–1.7), suggesting *C. r. reighardi* may warrant continued species-level recognition while *C. r. dymondi* does not.
4. **Species concept applied**: The authors adopt a pragmatic, phenetic approach to coregonine taxonomy given near-total interfertility among Great Lakes *Coregonus* species demonstrated in prior hybridization studies (Garside and Christie 1962), arguing species status should require morphological distinctiveness attributable to genetic (not purely environmental) causes.
5. **Conservation/management implications**: *C. zenithicus* is recognized as containing morphologically distinct, geographically localized stocks (e.g., Coppermine Point) that warrant management as discrete local populations rather than a single widespread stock — echoing historical over-exploitation failures such as the commercial extirpation of [[Coregonus Artedii]] (lake herring) in Lake Erie in the 1920s and the collapse of the Lake Huron chub fishery in the 1960s.
6. Recommends management strategies incorporating both depth stratification and fishing district boundaries to protect distinct local coregonine stocks pending further genetic/distributional research.
