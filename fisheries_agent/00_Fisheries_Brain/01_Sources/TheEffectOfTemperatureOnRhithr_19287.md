# The Effect of Temperature on Rhithrogena semicolorata (Ephemeroptera)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ephemeroptera

*Note: the source concerns a mayfly (order Ephemeroptera), not a fish. The tags above follow the required template; the taxon is aquatic insect, so [[Mayfly Life Cycle]] and [[Stream Temperature]] are the most relevant cross-links.*

## Executive Summary

T. T. Macan (1960, *Internationale Revue der gesamten Hydrobiologie* 45(2)) examines how temperature relates to the life cycle of the mayfly [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]] in a small stony stream. Using field catches, emergence-trap records and continuously logged temperatures, the study finds that growth continues through winter with little apparent temperature influence, that emergence timing is more regular than temperature alone would predict, and that emergence ends when mean weekly air temperature reaches about 16 °C for two or more weeks. The paper frames these results as a preliminary approach that would need experimental work to resolve.

## Background & Description

- **Life cycle:** One generation per year. Adults emerge in early summer. Hatching is deduced to run from about August to about January, based on very small nymphs, with some evidence of downstream migration before emergence (Macan 1957).
- **Study site:** A small stony stream with continuous temperature recording (Macan 1958).
- **Aim:** To correlate events in the life cycle with the temperature cycle as a first step toward experimental work.
- **Sampling constraints:** Growth measured from successive size-class catches is confounded by unhatched eggs entering later catches, nymphs that have finished growing, and upstream immigration. The author therefore used a percentage size-class histogram comparison, a rough by-eye method with no formal statistical testing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Growth and temperature:** Winter growth appeared roughly constant. The author reports that Harker's (1952) statement that about 6 °C is required for active growth did not hold for this stream. Nymphs in the Molle stream (Ines 1952) did not grow from early November to late February.
- **Emergence and temperature:** Emergence is governed partly by temperature but is "too regular" to depend on it alone. Peaks were in May in warm years (1952, 1953), in June in a cold year (1955), and a second peak in July in cool summers (1954, 1956). Temperature is considered one factor within a complex.
- **Lethal threshold:** Mature nymphs appear to be killed at an average temperature of about 16 °C. Average water temperature lags air temperature, so brief warm spells do not reach the water. Pleskot (1953) suggests reduced oxygen at high temperature may be more important than temperature itself. Ide (1935) reports that nymphs hatching too early, or emerging too late, are killed by high temperature.
- **Range implications:** The eggs' heat tolerance may limit southward range. A species whose emergence is inflexibly tied to non-thermal cues could be restricted by lethal summer temperatures before emergence is triggered.

## Key Findings

1. **One generation per year:** Adults begin emerging in May and eggs hatch in August (Summary, point 1).
2. **Steady winter growth:** Growth proceeds steadily through winter and appears only slightly influenced by temperature. A trend toward increased growth at the end of winter is possible but masked by nymphs that have stopped growing.
3. **Emergence timing:** The emergence peak is later and less distinct in colder years, yet it occurs annually closer to the same date than temperature alone would predict.
4. **End of emergence:** Emergence ends when average air temperature reaches about 16 °C and stays at or above that level for two weeks or more. In years without this, a few adults still appear as late as the end of August (1954 emergence continued until late August with temperature never reaching 16 °C).
5. **Unresolved questions:** The egg temperature tolerance, whether hatching is controlled by external or intrinsic factors, the emergence stimulus, and the exact lethal threshold remain undetermined. The author states the hatching time was not established precisely enough to justify a claim.
6. **Life-history implication:** A species with emergence insensitive to temperature would show a less variable life history. Two generations would require warm winters and cool summers, and eggs more tolerant of heat than nymphs.

**Reference citations used:** Harker (1952), Ide (1935), Ines (1952), Macan (1957, 1958), Pleskot (1951, 1953).
