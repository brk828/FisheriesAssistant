# Toxicity of Mixed Poisons to Rainbow Trout: Ammonium Chloride and Zinc Sulphate

#ichthyology #ecotoxicology #rainbow-trout

## Executive Summary

This 1964 laboratory study by D. W. M. Herbert and D. S. Shurben, from the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] at Stevenage, tested an empirical rule for the acute toxicity of mixed poisons in [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*). The rule states that a two-poison mixture reaches its threshold of acute toxicity when (Ps/Pt) + (Qs/Qt) = 1, where S is the solution concentration and T is the threshold concentration of each poison tested alone. The authors used [[Ammonium Chloride]] and [[Zinc Sulphate]] as the test pair because polluted river waters often contain several poisons.

Results were closest to the predicted value of unity in a hard, well-oxygenated water (within 4%). The fit was weaker in soft water (within 26%) and in hard water with reduced dissolved oxygen (within 17%). In every case the threshold rule described the data better than [[Independent Joint Action]] models. The authors concluded that the method is useful for estimating the acute toxicity of river waters containing these two poisons, although its validity is not yet firmly established.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** Hatchery-reared rainbow trout were used. The experiments used fingerlings, with 20 fish per concentration in the main hard-water test and 10 per concentration in the soft-water test. Fish were acclimatized for equal periods to each dilution water before exposure.

**Threshold concept.** Log time/log concentration curves for many fish poisons show a threshold below which a small concentration reduction greatly extends survival time. The threshold rule was previously supported for zinc with copper sulphate and for ammonium chloride with [[Monohydric Phenols]] (Lloyd, 1961; Herbert, 1962). The present study extended the test to ammonium chloride and zinc sulphate.

**Test procedure.** Individual poisons were first tested in serial dilutions for 2 days, and the concentration killing 50% of fish (48 hr TLm) was estimated by [[Probit Analysis]]. Mixtures were then prepared in chosen ratios. The authors noted that this non-random sequence carried some risk of bias from changes in fish susceptibility, which they judged acceptable.

**Mixture ratios.** Two mixtures were used in the main experiment, with concentration ratios of 2:1 and 1:2 for ammonium chloride to zinc sulphate, relative to their individual 50% kill thresholds.

**Analytical framework.** Median survival times were fitted to log-normal distributions. Survival times for required mortality levels were read from probit/log-time lines. The authors compared their results with two alternative prediction approaches: the [[Bliss Model]] of similar joint action, and the Plackett and Hewlett equations for independent joint action.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry drives toxicity.** The study showed that water chemistry strongly affects toxicity:

- **Hardness and alkalinity.** The hard dilution water had 320 ppm hardness as CaCO₃ and 240 ppm alkalinity. The soft water had 44 ppm hardness and 41.5 ppm alkalinity. Ammonium salts become less toxic as pH falls, and zinc and many heavy metals become more toxic in soft water.
- **[[Dissolved Oxygen]].** Hard water was tested at air saturation and at 47% of air saturation.
- **pH and temperature.** Hard-water tests were held at pH 7–8 and about 17.5 °C. Soft-water tests averaged 17.7 °C with pH controlled to 6.9.

**Relevance to river pollution.** The authors argued that a method predicting combined toxicity from single-poison data would help manage polluted river waters that often contain more than one poison. They also noted that the 50% kill level is too high for protecting fish populations in rivers. Predictions at 25% and 10% mortality were therefore also examined.

**Zinc speciation.** Calculations based on published stability constants showed that less than 1% of zinc and ammonia would combine as zinc-ammonium complexes at the threshold mixture concentrations. The two poisons should therefore act in their original forms until they reach fish tissues.

## Key Findings

### 1. Hard water, air-saturated dissolved oxygen (main experiment)

- **Individual threshold (48 hr TLm):**
  - Ammonium chloride: 24.6 ppm N (95% fiducial limits 23.5–25.6).
  - Zinc sulphate: 3.86 ppm Zn (95% fiducial limits 3.65–4.09).
- **Mixtures (2:1 and 1:2 ratios):** the sum (Ps/Pt + Qs/Qt) at 48 hr TLm was **1.04** and **1.03**, with 95% fiducial limits of 0.95–1.12 in both cases. This was within 4% of the predicted value of unity.
- **Lower mortality levels:** At 25% mortality the sum was 0.98. At 10% mortality it was 0.91 and 0.96 for the 2:1 and 1:2 mixtures. The rule therefore appeared to hold for more sensitive fish as well as median-susceptibility fish.
- **Survival in super-threshold concentrations:** The rule failed at higher concentrations, where median survival times were short. Neither of the two alternative prediction methods gave good fits across both mixtures. The authors concluded that the threshold rule applies only at threshold concentrations.

### 2. Soft water (44 ppm hardness, 20 fish per test replaced by 10 per concentration)

- **Individual thresholds:**
  - Ammonium chloride: 193.0 ppm N (95% fiducial limits 167.9–207.5). This is nearly eight times the hard-water value.
  - Zinc sulphate: 0.91 ppm Zn (95% fiducial limits 0.81–1.3). This is about one quarter of the equitoxic concentration in hard water.
- **1:1 mixture:** the best estimate of the sum was **1.26** (95% fiducial limits 1.01–2.24). The authors described this test as insensitive because of the wide interval, although the lower limit only just reached unity.

### 3. Hard water with reduced dissolved oxygen (47% air saturation)

- **Individual thresholds:**
  - Ammonium chloride: 30.1 ppm N (95% fiducial limits 28.6–31.6).
  - Zinc sulphate: 2.40 ppm Zn (95% fiducial limits 2.20–2.61).
- **1:1 mixture:** the sum was **1.17** (95% fiducial limits 1.09–1.26). The authors noted that a value of 1.1 falls within the 95% fiducial ranges for all four ammonia–zinc mixtures tested.

### 4. Mechanism of toxic action

- Gills from 28 fish killed in the mixtures, along with fish that survived, were examined histologically. In every case gill structure was normal.
- Earlier work by Lloyd (1962) had shown that zinc-killed trout suffer disruption and sloughing of the gill respiratory epithelium. This was absent here.
- The authors concluded that the sublethal effects of each poison sum within the individual fish to cause death.

### 5. Comparison with independent joint action models

- The toxicity of the mixtures was clearly not an example of similar joint action as defined by [[Bliss Model]] (1939).
- [[Independent Joint Action]] models (Plackett and Hewlett, 1948) were tested under complete positive, no, and complete negative correlation in susceptibility. In all four mixtures that killed 50% of fish, the expected mortality was distinctly lower than the mortality observed.
- The threshold rule described the data better than all these models.

### 6. Conclusions and limitations

- The authors judged the rule useful for estimating the acute toxicity of river waters containing ammonia and zinc, but they noted that its validity is not yet firmly established.
- The method is valid only at threshold concentrations. It does not predict survival times at higher concentrations.
- Limitations included non-random test order, small sample sizes (10–20 fish per concentration), and wide fiducial intervals in the soft-water and reduced-oxygen tests.
