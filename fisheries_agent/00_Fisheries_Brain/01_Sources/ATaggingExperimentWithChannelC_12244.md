# Tagging Experiment with Channel Catfish in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report by George W. McCammon (California Department of Fish and Game, Inland Fisheries Branch) describes an exploratory tagging study of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) in the lower [[Colorado River]] between [[Parker Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]], begun in 1953. The fishery was judged vitally important to southern California anglers, yet almost no data existed to manage it. The study aimed to estimate annual harvest rates and movements, and to refine trapping and tagging techniques. A total of 264 fish were tagged with two tag types and 53 tags (20.0 percent) were returned by anglers in the first year. The author concluded that the fishery was not overexploited, while noting that larger sample sizes are needed before a valid harvest estimate or migration pattern can be established.

## Background & Description

The lower Colorado River catfish fishery was considered critical to California anglers, particularly in heavily populated southern California, which has relatively few fishing waters. The fishery was estimated to have produced 750,000 catfish for California sport fishermen in 1953, about 10 percent of the state's total catfish catch and about 60 percent of the southern California catch that year.

The channel catfish is the most abundant catfish in the river. [[Dill 1944]] considered it the most important game fish by both numbers and pounds. [[Yellow Bullhead]] and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus* spp.) are also reported from the river, but the author considered their role in the fishery insignificant. Apart from Dill's general data, little information existed to support effective management.

The study area was the river section between Parker and Imperial dams, about 148 miles long. [[Largemouth Bass]] are abundant there, but the fishery is dominated by channel catfish. Two irrigation structures, [[Headgate Rock Dam]] (about 15 miles below Parker Dam) and the [[Palo Verde Weir]] (about 12 miles above Blythe), are described as apparent barriers to upstream catfish migration.

The study was performed under the Dingell-Johnson Project California F-2-R, "A Study of the Catfish Fishery of California," funded by Federal Aid to Fish Restoration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water temperature and catch.** Netting was unproductive in winter. In December 1953, water temperatures were around 52°F, and the author attributed the poor catch to cold water, consistent with Dill's observation that netting was least productive in early cold-water months. In April and May 1954, water temperatures ranged from 70 to 75°F.

**Habitat and net placement.** Catch success depended strongly on [[Net Location]]. The most productive set was just downstream of a sandbar formed on the inside of a meander bend, in relatively deep, quiet water. Nets placed below two additional sandbars also helped. The author concluded that net location was one of the most important factors in catfish trapping success on the Colorado River.

**Barrier effects.** The Palo Verde Weir appears to be an efficient barrier to upstream movement, and fish were observed gathering at its base, drawn by fast current and deep water.

**Non-target species.** Baiting nets with cottonseed meal pellets did not increase the catfish catch but significantly increased the catch of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*).

**Related species.** The [[White Catfish]] (*Ictalurus catus*) was the subject of parallel tagging work in the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, where [[Fyke Net]] trapping was also highly efficient.

## Key Findings

### Study Design and Trapping

- **December 1953 phase:** 44 fish were tagged at Locations A (1), B (25) and C (18). Netting totaled 170 net-days and yielded only 44 channel catfish over 7.0 inches, all taken in cold water.
- **April-May 1954 phase:** 220 fish were tagged at Location C, bringing the total to 264. The initial 72 net-days yielded 69 taggable fish. After nets were moved to productive sites, 27 net-days produced 151 taggable catfish. Nets were checked every 12 hours in this period, versus every 48 hours earlier.
- Fish under 7.0 inches were not tagged, partly to reduce tag harm and partly because they were too small to be caught on heavy angling gear.
- Of 160 sexed fish, 91 were female and 69 male. The author doubted this ratio represents the whole population.

### Tag Types

- **[[Disk-Dangler Tag]]:** a variation of the Atkins tag, made of cellulose nitrate disk and tantalum wire, with 164 fish tagged. Previous holding tests showed retention for more than one year.
- **[[Hydrostatic Tag]]:** a water-tight transparent plastic tube, 14 inches long, with a stainless steel bridle and a return-instruction message. Its specific gravity is close to water, and its message aimed to produce near-complete reporting. 100 fish received this tag. Earlier trials elsewhere (Collyer 1954; Kimsey 1956; unpublished Pelgen data) reported shedding and fouling. In this study, four recaptured hydrostatic-tagged fish had one tag shed and three with badly twisted bridles.
- The author estimated 15 to 20 percent shedding of hydrostatic tags within one year with steel bridles.

### Tag Returns

- Anglers reported 53 of 264 tags (20.0 percent) in the first year.
- First phase (Dec 1953): 8 of 44 tags returned (18.2 percent).
- Second phase (Apr-May 1954): 45 of 220 returned (20.4 percent).
- Hydrostatic: 21 of 100 returned (21.0 percent). Disk-dangler: 32 of 164 returned (19.5 percent).
- The difference between tag types was not statistically significant. A deviation as large or larger would occur by chance in 75 percent of similar experiments.
- The author estimated [[Angler Recapture]] reporting at about 100 percent for hydrostatic tags and about 85 percent for disk-dangler tags. This offset the shedding loss of hydrostatic tags.
- Reporting was encouraged by posters, a commendation card, and a prize drawing sponsored by the Foothill Sportsmen's Club of Oakland, which awarded an annual $100 prize plus fishing equipment.

### Size and Sex

- Tagged fish ranged from 7.0 to 27.0 inches fork length, with a mean of 13.4 inches.
- Returned fish averaged 14.3 inches, ranging from 7.6 to 23.0 inches. The difference was not significant at the 10 percent level, so return probability appeared independent of size.
- Of 37 tags recovered from sexed fish, 22 came from females and 15 from males. Expected returns were 21 and 16. The difference was not significant, suggesting no difference in harvestability between sexes, though the sample was small.
- Angler-reported lengths were often inaccurate, with apparent shrinkage in some reports, so growth was not analysed.

### Movement

- Recapture data allowed accurate movement tracking for 50 fish: 8 tagged at Locations B and C in December 1953, and 42 tagged at Location C in April-May 1954.
- Fish tagged in 1953 showed practically no movement. Fish tagged in 1954 moved considerable distances.
- There was a distinct tendency for downstream movement from the Palo Verde Weir, especially after the first three months. The author suggested this could be part of a seasonal pattern (upstream in spring and early summer, downstream in fall and winter), but more returns are needed.
- Greatest downstream movement: from the Palo Verde Weir area to Ferguson Lake, 76 miles in 180 days.
- Fastest movement: 46 miles downstream in 45 days.
- No fish tagged at Location C were recovered above the Palo Verde Weir, which the author considered strong evidence of an efficient barrier.

### Harvest and Conclusions

- The author treated the harvest estimate as tentative and of doubtful value for regulation. A 20 percent first-year return "indicates that the present rate of harvest is not excessive."
- The author concluded that a much larger number of fish, tagged across more river sections, is needed for a valid [[Harvest Rate]] estimate and for migration conclusions.
- Recommendations: improve hydrostatic tag attachment to anchor it more securely, and maximise reporting of disk-dangler recoveries.
