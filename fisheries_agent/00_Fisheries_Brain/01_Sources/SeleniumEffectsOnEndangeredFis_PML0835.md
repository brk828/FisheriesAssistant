# Selenium Effects on Endangered Fish in the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter reviews how [[Selenium]] contamination, largely from irrigation drainwater, may be contributing to the decline of the endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It combines historical accounts, [[National Water Quality Assessment Program]] (NWQP) monitoring data, floodplain restoration screening results and laboratory and field toxicity studies. The author concludes that waterborne selenium alone was not hazardous in tests, but that selenium-containing mixtures of inorganic contaminants and elevated tissue residues in wild fish are harmful to larval and adult [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Selenium should therefore be treated as an important factor in recovery, not as a minor issue.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin, isolated for a long time, historically supported a distinctive [[Ichthyofauna]] with several endemic species. Four fishes are now federally listed:

- [[Humpback Chub]] and Colorado squawfish, listed in 1967 under the Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966 and relisted in 1973.
- [[Bonytail]], listed in 1980.
- Razorback sucker, listed in 1991.

[[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and other native fishes are now species of concern. The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for the Endangered Fish Species of the Upper Colorado River Basin, begun in 1987, is a 15-year effort to re-establish self-sustaining populations while allowing water development. Its effort has focused mainly on bonytail and razorback sucker, because humpback chub and Colorado squawfish populations appeared stable.

Reported population estimates are:

- Colorado squawfish: 600 to 650 individuals in the upper Colorado River.
- Razorback sucker: 300 to 600 individuals in the Green River (Modde et al., 1996), after an earlier, higher estimate by Lanigan and Tyus (1989).
- Razorback sucker: only 10 found in the upper Colorado River between 1989 and 1996.

Historical accounts describe these fishes as abundant. Anglers in the 1920s to 1950s reported easy catches, and the fish were used for food. Their decline is attributed to dams, irrigation and dewatering, habitat loss, altered flow, temperature change, competition and predation by nonnative species, parasitism and changes in food base. Contaminant effects were considered in 1976 but were not seriously examined in the recovery program.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainwater and historical selenium.** Irrigation drainage ditches carried water to the Green River and other rivers. Razorback sucker stage in backwaters before spawning runs, and these backwaters can have high selenium concentrations. Two Green River staging sites, Ashley Creek and the Stewart Lake outfall, are close together and both have elevated selenium in water and food organisms. Reported selenium in water was about 2.5 to 22 µg/L in Ashley Creek and up to about 10 µg/L at the Stewart Lake outflow in 1986 to 1987 (Stephens et al.). Selenium was also elevated in backwaters on the Colorado River mainstem, such as the gravel pit at Walter Walker Wildlife Area (WWSWA) near [[Grand Junction, Colorado]].

**Tissue residues in fish across the basin.** Selenium has been measured in several fish species:

- Razorback sucker egg: 4.9 µg/g (1988, near Jensen, Utah); 28 µg/g (1991, Razorback Bar).
- Colorado squawfish (1982, Ashley Creek): muscle 2.7, liver 9.2, gonad 6.5 µg/g.
- Humpback chub (1986, Green River): muscle 4.3, liver 6, gonad 7 µg/g.
- Lake Powell fishes: 2.5 to 14 µg/g.
- Rainbow trout (Glen Canyon Dam to Lee's Ferry): 4.1 to 18 µg/g whole body and 7.8 to 73 µg/g in eggs.

**Lower Colorado River.** A 1986 to 1987 NWQP study of 11 locations between [[Davis Dam]] and the US-Mexico border found elevated selenium in water, sediment and biota. Radtke et al. (1988) found higher selenium in backwaters and oxbow lakes receiving Colorado River water than in irrigation return canals. They concluded the contamination originated upstream, not from local agriculture. Other studies have reported similar findings in [[Asiatic Clam]] and fish.

## Key Findings

**1. Floodplain restoration sites were chosen with little selenium data.** An early evaluation by Cooper and Severn (1994) based site suitability mainly on hydrology and food abundance. Restoration of Sheppard Bottoms at [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] was recommended using only one water selenium value, even though three NWQP reports showed elevated selenium there.

**2. Screening has now been completed at 30 sites.** Screening began in late 1994 and covered six sites on the Colorado River, three on the Gunnison River and 21 on the Green River. Ten sites were approved for restoration, 10 were placed on temporary hold pending selenium research, and 10 will not be restored. Seven of the rejected sites were dropped for elevated selenium in water or biota, and three for other inorganic constituents.

**3. Selenium alone was not hazardous, but mixtures were.** Toxicity tests from 1981 onward, conducted in water that simulated irrigation drainage, found that waterborne selenium itself was not a hazard to the fish. Six mixtures containing San Juan River selenium and two containing Green River (Ashley Creek and Stewart Lake outfall) selenium showed high hazard, with a biological-effects-to-environmental-concentration ratio reported as 100 (the symbol is partly illegible in the scan).

**4. Larval and juvenile fish were harmed at environmental concentrations.** In long-term tests with larval [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]], a mixture simulating Ashley Creek and the Green River caused adverse effects at less than 10 times environmental concentrations. Key effects included:

- Razorback sucker larvae: reduced survival after 40 days at 16X; reduced growth after 30 days at 8X and after 60 days at 4X; reduced critical swimming speed after 60 days at 8X.
- Bonytail: reduced survival after 20 days at 16X; reduced growth after 60 days at 8X; reduced critical swimming speed after 60 days at 16X.

**5. Food-chain transfer reduced larval survival.** Larval razorback suckers fed zooplankton from six Ouray NWR sites had 0 to 20% survival in four studies started at 5, 10, 24 and 28 days old. Selenium at 2.3 µg/g or greater in food organisms, combined with other inorganics, reduced survival. Both the Ashley Creek and Ouray studies show adverse effects at current upper basin concentrations.

**6. Adult reproduction was affected.** A nine-month study with adult razorback suckers near Grand Junction used a reference site, Adobe Creek and North Pond:

| Site | Muscle plug (µg/g) | Egg (µg/g) |
|---|---|---|
| Reference | 4.5 (unchanged) | 6.5 |
| Adobe Creek | 3.9 to 12 | 44 |
| North Pond | 4.1 to 17 | 38 |

Larval survival in the 30-day grow-out was low at all three sites. A heavy anchor worm infestation in the reference fish may have compromised their health during oogenesis, and brood stock held at the reference site had high larval survival. The study was being repeated, with results expected in 1998.

**7. Wild endangered fish are accumulating harmful selenium levels.** Muscle plugs from eight adult razorback suckers at Ashley Creek, one at Stewart Lake outfall and three at Razorback Bar exceeded 11.5 µg/g. Eggs from three of these fish contained 3.7 to 10.6 µg/g, with the highest muscle plug at 32 µg/g. Colorado squawfish at WWSWA averaged 16 µg/g in muscle plugs (maximum 31 µg/g), while fish about 7 km upstream or downstream averaged less than 5 µg/g.

**8. Conclusion.** The decline of the endangered fishes is linked mainly to physical and biotic changes, but the chemical effects of selenium appear to be an important additional factor. Selenium research has now been added to the recovery programs for the upper Colorado and San Juan rivers. Reducing selenium effects may be essential to recovering endangered fish in the Colorado River basin.
