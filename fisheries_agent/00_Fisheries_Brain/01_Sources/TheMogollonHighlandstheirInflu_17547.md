# Mogollon Highlands: Mesozoic and Cenozoic Erosion and Sedimentation

#earth-science #sedimentology #fluvial-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 paper by M. E. Cooley and B. S. Davidson of the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] reconstructs the history of the [[Mogollon Highlands]], a persistent upland in central and southern Arizona and adjoining New Mexico. The authors argue that the highlands were a major control on erosion and sedimentation from the Mesozoic through the Cenozoic. They trace how the highland shed sediments into the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the [[Basin and Range Province]], and how its outline shifted with regional uplift, volcanism and faulting. The paper is a regional stratigraphic and geomorphic synthesis. It contains no biological, fish or aquatic-ecosystem data.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The paper is a synthesis of the regional geology of the Mogollon Highlands, prepared in cooperation with the Arizona State Land Department and the Navajo Tribe.
- **Name and usage:** The term "Mogollon Highlands" was first used by Harshbarger, Repenning and Irwin (1957) for a highland that supplied Jurassic sediments to the Colorado Plateau. Smith (1951) had earlier proposed "Navajo Highlands" for the same source. Cooley and Davidson keep the Mogollon name for a positive area in central and southern Arizona and nearby regions that fed the Colorado Plateau and Basin and Range provinces from the Mesozoic onward.
- **Framing structures:** Eardley (1951) described broad geanticlines that controlled deposition in the region. These were the [[Manhattan Geanticline]] (Triassic), the [[Cordilleran Geanticline]] (Jurassic and Cretaceous) and the [[Occidental Geanticline]] (Jurassic and Cretaceous). The Mogollon Highlands lay between the [[Cordilleran Geosyncline]] to the west, the [[Mexican Geosyncline]] to the southeast and the [[Rocky Mountain Geosyncline]] to the northeast.
- **Paleogeographic context:** Earlier maps by Chamberlin and Salisbury (1906), Schuchert and Dunbar (1941), Ver Wiebe (1932), McKee (1951) and Eardley (1951) provided the regional framework the authors refine.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses physical landscape evolution rather than ecology, but its drainage history gives the environmental setting:

- **Drainage evolution:** Drainages flowed north and northeast toward the Colorado Plateau during much of the Mesozoic. In the Jurassic, streams drained toward the Rocky Mountain Geosyncline, with those in the far south flowing into the Mexican Geosyncline. Later, the Colorado River system and the Gila and Salt rivers were established, and headward erosion diverted southward-flowing drainages.
- **Depositional environments:** The sediments record fluvial, lacustrine, eolian, nearshore marine and volcanic settings. These include a large Late Jurassic floodplain, eolian sand sheets (such as the [[Navajo Sandstone]]), and brackish-water marine deposits along the lower Colorado River.
- **Tectonic and volcanic controls:** Regional upwarping, normal block faulting, and widespread Tertiary volcanism created barriers that blocked drainages and produced tuffaceous sediments and basaltic barriers.
- **Late-stage landscape:** By the end of the Pliocene, the Mogollon Rim had reached its present form. Terraces formed in the Quaternary as the major valleys deepened.

## Key Findings

### Mesozoic history

- **Permian to Triassic:** Late Paleozoic upwarping ended Permian marine deposition over most of Arizona and western New Mexico. The highlands and the [[Uncompahgre Highlands]] in Colorado were separated by upwarp. The unconformity between Triassic and Permian rocks reflects erosion that ran from Late Permian into Middle Triassic time.
- **Moenkopi Formation:** The Early and Middle(?) Triassic [[Moenkopi Formation]] is siltstone to fine sandstone. Its basal conglomerates contain quartz, quartzite, jasper, chert and limestone pebbles, which the authors argue could only have come from the highlands. This indicates that the highlands were relatively low and structurally stable.
- **Chinle Formation:** The highlands supplied much of the sediment of the [[Chinle Formation]]. Conglomerates in the Shinarump, Mesa Redondo and Petrified Forest members indicate local relief. Valleys in the southern Colorado Plateau were incised as much as 200 feet deep and 10 miles wide before the Shinarump Member was deposited. The [[Sonsela Sandstone]] marks the last major Triassic uplift.
- **Early volcanism:** Bentonite and ash beds appear through the Chinle Formation, and rhyolitic to andesitic pebbles occur only in the Mogollon slope and Zuni Mountains region. One possible source lies about 200 miles south in the Dragoon quadrangle.
- **Drainage reversal:** At the close of the Triassic, north- and northwest-trending drainages were diverted west and southwest. The Wingate Sandstone, Moenave and Kayenta formations were mostly derived from the [[Uncompahgre Highlands]], with some southeastern input.
- **Jurassic:** The Cordilleran Geanticline was completed from Canada to Mexico, and the Mogollon Highlands formed a crescent-shaped arch separating the Rocky Mountain and Mexican geosynclines. Most of Arizona and New Mexico tilted north. Late Jurassic rejuvenation produced the [[Morrison Formation]]. Its Salt Wash Member came from [[Mohavia Highlands]], where Kaibab Limestone chert was the dominant exposed rock. Its Recapture and Westwater Canyon members came from the Mogollon Highlands.

### Cretaceous history

- **Early Cretaceous:** Rocks of the Mexican Geosyncline exceed 20,000 feet in thickness in the Douglas-Lordsburg area and thin sharply along the southern front of the highlands. North of the highlands the [[Burro Canyon Formation]] is fluvial and lacustrine.
- **Tilting and planation:** Uplift tilted the Mogollon Highlands as a block to the northeast. Widespread planation preceded deposition of the [[Dakota Sandstone]].
- **Transgression:** Upper Cretaceous seas covered much of the continent and linked the Rocky Mountain and Mexican geosynclines. Intertonguing of the [[Mesaverde Group]], [[Mancos Shale]] and Lewis Shale records repeated transgressions and regressions.
- **Late Cretaceous withdrawal:** Regional upwarping completed the withdrawal of the seas by the end of the Cretaceous.

### Cenozoic history

- **Early Tertiary:** Sediments of the San Juan and Uinta basins were partly derived from the Mogollon and Mohavia highlands. The Baca Formation (probably Eocene) and the nonvolcanic sediments near Springerville contain Mogollon-derived clasts. The Silver Bell and Claflin Ranch formations, locally more than 5,000 feet thick, contain arkosic sediments and andesite flows.
- **Mid-Tertiary volcanism:** The [[Datil Formation]] of west-central New Mexico and east-central Arizona is more than 3,000 feet thick and formed a drainage barrier east of the Pinaleno Mountains. The [[Helmet Fanglomerate]] is correlated with the Pantano Formation and is affected by thrusting, tilting and normal faulting.
- **Valencia surface:** The initial stage of the [[Colorado River System]] is recorded by remnants of the Valencia surface at altitudes above 7,500 feet. Stripping during its formation removed as much as 2,000 feet of Tertiary and Mesozoic rock. The surface predates the cutting of the Mogollon Rim, Grand Canyon, Verde Valley, Tonto Basin and Salt River Canyon.
- **Bidahochi Formation:** Late Miocene to Pliocene volcanism at the Hopi Buttes created a dam on the Little Colorado River. The [[Bidahochi Formation]] was deposited upstream and was divided into lower lacustrine and fluvial, middle volcanic, and upper fluvial members. Entrenchment averaged about 1,200 feet below the Valencia surface.
- **Deformed gravel:** Coarse, tilted and deformed gravel (the [[Deformed Gravel]]) along the southern flanks of the highlands is equivalent in part to the Gila Conglomerate and Mimbres Conglomerate. It marks a major stage of Cenozoic deposition.
- **Block faulting:** Late Tertiary normal faulting produced displacements of more than 1,000 feet. Faulting was largely complete by the Pleistocene and formed the basin-and-range block structures.
- **Quaternary:** Up to seven terraces formed along the Colorado River, mapped over about 35,000 square miles. Terrace deposits can reach 200 feet thick. The [[Basin Fill]] in basin valleys is more than 1,500 feet thick and is locally derived, with some lacustrine clay and limestone. Downcutting near Holbrook reached about 800 feet below the base of the Bidahochi Formation.

### Methodological notes

- The authors note a general scarcity of datable fossils in Cenozoic deposits. They therefore rely on lithology, deformation style, volcanic associations and physiographic correlation to date and correlate units.
- Cobble and pebble composition (for example, the greenish chlorite-epidote andesite of the Silver Bell Formation) serves as a provenance and age marker.

### Unresolved questions

- The downstream course of the ancestral Colorado River is described as "an open question." Possibilities include a southward course near its present path, a westward course, or impoundment northeast of the highlands.
