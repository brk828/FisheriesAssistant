# Desert Fishes Symposium Proceedings: Dominican Pupfishes and Colorado River Fish Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *Proceedings of the Desert Fishes Council*, Volume XXX (1998 Symposium, published 15 November 1999), contains three short items: an introductory summary on desert fishes of the Dominican Republic, and two papers on fishes of the [[Colorado River]] in Arizona. The Dominican summary describes at least three pupfish species in hypersaline lakes and their freshwater-spring associates. The first Colorado River paper argues that non-native fishes dominate the regulated lower river and that native fish management must be segregated from non-native management. The second paper reports abundance, distribution and movement data for [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The excerpt is a partial proceedings page, so methods and full datasets are only partly available.

## Background & Description

The proceedings focus on fishes adapted to arid and semi-arid environments, a theme shared by the three contributions.

**Dominican Republic desert fishes.** The summary notes that hypersaline lakes host at least three [[Pupfish]] species of the genus [[Cyprinodon]]:
- *Cyprinodon bondi* (co-occurring with the American crocodile, [[American Crocodile]], *Crocodylus acutus*, in an isolated population)
- *Cyprinodon nicholsi*, found in two lakes in the southwestern part of the country
- *Cyprinodon higuey*, found in two lakes in the southeastern sector

Freshwater springs linked to these lakes contain several species of [[Limia]] and [[Gambusia]] (livebearers, family [[Poeciliidae]]), along with an endemic cichlid, [[Cichlasoma haitiensis]]. The fourth Cyprinodon name in the scan is partly illegible and is not reported here.

**Colorado River fish community.** The river is physically altered and hydrologically regulated. Dams have created impoundments and thermally depressed tailwaters that differ strongly from the pre-dam stream. The mainstem below dams is dominated by introduced species, while native fishes persist mainly as a few imperiled populations in isolated reaches.

**Flannelmouth Sucker study.** The species, *Catostomus latipinnis* (family [[Catostomidae]]), is a native sucker. The study area was the 26-km tailwater of the Colorado River immediately below Glen Canyon Dam, near Lee's Ferry, Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hypersaline and spring systems (Dominican Republic):** Lakes with high salinity support pupfish, while nearby freshwater springs support livebearers and an endemic cichlid. The coexistence of pupfish with the American crocodile is noted, showing that this is a shared habitat for a reptile and fishes.
- **Regulated river (Colorado River):** Dams alter temperature, flow and habitat structure. The authors argue that these new habitats should, in principle, support self-sustaining native communities, but evidence from case studies shows they do not.
- **Biological interactions:** The non-native fishes have feeding, behavioural and reproductive traits that let them displace, replace or exclude natives regardless of physical habitat quality. This makes biological interactions, not habitat alone, central to native fish decline.
- **Tailwater conditions:** Releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] create cold, stable tailwaters, which strongly influence which species can establish and persist.

## Key Findings

1. **Dominican desert fishes:** At least three *Cyprinodon* pupfish species occupy hypersaline lakes in the Dominican Republic: *C. bondi*, *C. nicholsi* and *C. higuey*. Freshwater springs associated with these lakes support *Limia* and *Gambusia* species and the endemic *Cichlasoma haitiensis*.
2. **Shared habitat with crocodiles:** *Cyprinodon bondi* coexists with an isolated population of the American crocodile.
3. **Non-native dominance on the Colorado River:** An introduced fish assemblage overwhelmingly dominates the lower river, reducing native fishes to a few imperiled species in isolated reaches.
4. **Mechanisms of displacement:** Non-native fishes displace, replace or exclude natives through feeding, behavioural and reproductive attributes, largely independent of physical habitat features.
5. **Management recommendation:** The authors recommend segregated management of native and non-native fishes as the only effective means to maintain self-perpetuating native populations.
6. **Flannelmouth Sucker below Glen Canyon Dam:** During the study period (dated as 1992–1994 in the scan), researchers captured 212 flannelmouth suckers by electrofishing in the 26-km tailwater and recaptured 21 fish that had been tagged previously by other workers. The excerpt does not report the abundance estimates, movement distances or distribution results derived from these data.
