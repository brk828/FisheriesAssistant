# A Study to Evaluate Several Tagging and Marking Systems for Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, and Bonytail

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #tagging-methodology #hatchery-management #upper-colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This study evaluated five tagging and marking systems for three endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). Conducted from October 1989 to March 1991 at three U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service facilities, the research compared passive integrated transponder (PIT) tags (two anatomical placements), visible implant (V.I.) tags, Carlin-dangler tags, and acrylic dye marks. Performance was assessed through tag retention, survival, growth, tissue compatibility, and readability over 511 days. PIT tags implanted in the body cavity demonstrated superior performance across all species and size classes, with verification rates exceeding 92% and no adverse effects on survival or growth. The study established minimum tagging sizes and recommended PIT tagging as the primary identification method for both wild and hatchery endangered fish populations in recovery programs.

## Background & Description

The [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] contains four federally endangered fish species requiring individual identification for effective management, research, and recovery operations. Previous tagging methods, particularly Carlin-dangler tags used extensively from 1979-1986, proved problematic due to poor retention, open wounds, and secondary infections. The Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species required improved identification systems for broodstock management, experimental stocking, monitoring, and genetic pedigree tracking.

The study design at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] included 60 fish per experimental group for each species, with equal distribution between an outdoor earthen pond (0.04 hectare) and outdoor cement raceway (14 m × 1.8 m × 0.6 m). All experimental fish received species-specific secondary marks (complete fin removal) to validate tag/mark loss. Test subjects included juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] (mean fork length 72 mm, range 55-88 mm), [[Razorback Sucker]] (mean FL 110 mm, range 84-217 mm), and [[Bonytail]] (mean FL 76 mm, range 62-116 mm). Additionally, 164 adult Colorado squawfish and 526 razorback sucker broodstock received body cavity PIT tags for long-term performance monitoring.

Companion studies at [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] evaluated 75 juvenile Colorado squawfish (mean FL 125 mm, range 110-150 mm) over 387 days. At [[Ouray Endangered Fish Hatchery]], age-0 razorback sucker (mean FL 92 mm, range 75-100 mm) were monitored for 265 days. PIT tags were 400 kHz glass-encapsulated units (11 mm × 2.1 mm diameter, <0.05 g weight) implanted using modified syringes with 12-gauge hypodermic needles, with immediate verification using portable interrogators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study revealed significant environmental effects on fish performance independent of tagging treatment. Survival was markedly higher in raceway environments compared to ponds across all species: Colorado squawfish survival was 8.7 times greater in raceways, bonytail 2.7 times greater, and razorback sucker 1.5 times greater. Greatest mortality for Colorado squawfish and bonytail in ponds occurred between October and May (days 0-195), while raceway mortality peaked between October and March (days 343-511).

Conversely, growth rates were substantially faster in pond environments despite lower survival. This pattern held consistently across all three species and all experimental groups, suggesting that natural food production in ponds (supplemented with Silvercup Salmon diet at 3% body weight daily) provided superior nutrition compared to raceway conditions. Water temperature remained constant at 17.8°C from ground water sources at Dexter NFHTC. At Hotchkiss NFH, constant 15°C temperatures curtailed growth of warmwater Colorado squawfish, demonstrating the importance of thermal regime for these species.

The only parasite encountered was *Ichthyophthirius multifillis* in raceway fishes, controlled with six treatments of formalin (25 mg/L) and malachite green (0.1 mg/L). Pond fish were not treated for disease or parasites during the 511-day study period. Ponds were covered October through April with polypropylene netting (2.54 cm mesh) to control avian predation.

## Key Findings

**PIT Tag Performance - Body Cavity Implantation:**
- Tag verification exceeded 97% for Colorado squawfish (mean FL 72 mm) throughout 511 days in both pond and raceway environments
- Razorback sucker body cavity tags showed 95-100% verification over 511 days
- Bonytail body cavity tags demonstrated 100% verification for all surviving fish at each observation period
- Adult broodstock: 100% tag verification for 148 Colorado squawfish after 24 months; 100% for 245 razorback sucker after 18 months
- At Hotchkiss NFH: 100% verification for 33 Colorado squawfish (mean FL 125 mm) at day 387
- At Ouray EFH: 100% verification for 39 razorback sucker (mean FL 92 mm) over 265 days
- Survival of body cavity PIT-tagged fish was not significantly different (P > 0.05) from control groups for all three species
- Only one tag failure *in situ* observed during entire study

**PIT Tag Performance - Dorsal Musculature Implantation:**
- Colorado squawfish: Poor performance with only 62% tag retention at day 511 in raceways; erratic swimming behavior immediately post-tagging suggested neurological damage
- Bonytail: Tag verification only 33-38% at day 195; survival significantly lower (P < 0.05) than controls in three of six instances
- Razorback sucker: 100% tag verification throughout 511 days, likely due to larger initial size (mean FL 110 mm)
- Wound healing slower than body cavity placement; 89-96% healing at day 195 for bonytail and razorback sucker

**Minimum Size Determinations:**
- Colorado squawfish and bonytail as small as 72-76 mm FL successfully PIT tagged in body cavity
- Razorback sucker as small as 75 mm FL successfully tagged
- Colorado squawfish and bonytail at 72-76 mm too small for safe dorsal musculature implantation (33% mortality for bonytail, 67% tag expulsion within 14 days)
- During initial 14-day period post-tagging: one squawfish died and 20 (33%) lost dorsal tags; 15 (25%) bonytail died and 40 (67%) expelled dorsal tags

**Tissue Compatibility and Wound Healing:**
- Body cavity wounds completely healed by day 195 for all three species (95-100% healing)
- PIT tags found embedded in posterior pyloric caeca or adjacent adipose tissue with no migration, swelling, redness, or edema
- Dorsal musculature tags remained within 5 mm of insertion point
- Carlin-dangler tag wounds healed slower: only 59% at day 195, 81-82% by day 511; some fish exhibited persistent red/pink coloration

**Alternative Tagging Systems:**
- **V.I. Tags:** Extremely poor retention for all species due to insufficient subdermal tissue thickness in fish <150 mm; verification 0-56% at day 195, declining to 0-44% by day 511
- **Carlin-Dangler Tags:** Razorback sucker showed 67-73% retention in ponds but only 0-4% in raceways by day 511; bonytail retention 0% at day 511
- **Acrylic Dye:** Poor mark recognition; red dye detected on only 4-24% of fish at day 195, 0% by day 343; external application method inadequate

**Secondary Marks (Fin Removal):**
- Provided adequate short-term (7-month) identification but unsatisfactory long-term
- By day 343, mean 23% of fish regenerated fins, making experimental group distinction difficult
- No adverse effects on growth or survival detected in separate fin-clip evaluation (n=10 per group, 100% survival at day 195)
- Fins removed with conventional scissors; regeneration particularly rapid in young, fast-growing fish

**Growth Patterns:**
- No significant differences (P > 0.05) in growth between experimental and control groups for any species
- Growth determined by environment, not tagging treatment
- Pond fish grew substantially faster than raceway fish across all species and treatments
- At Hotchkiss NFH, constant 15°C water temperatures curtailed growth of warmwater Colorado squawfish

**Cost Considerations:**
- PIT tags: $3.75-$5.00 per tag depending on order quantity
- Field scanner/memory detector unit: approximately $1,200 (model HS5105-L18K)
- V.I. tags: $0.25-$0.70 per tag (significantly less expensive but poor performance on small fish)
- PIT tags potentially recoverable from dead fish for reuse in hatchery settings

**Statistical Findings:**
- Overall survival percentages at day 511 varied significantly by environment and species
- Tag verification for body cavity PIT tags significantly greater (P < 0.05) than dorsal musculature placement for Colorado squawfish (X²=11.07, X²₀.₀₅, df=1, 5.02)
- Body cavity PIT tags significantly superior (P < 0.05) to V.I. tags for raceway squawfish (X²=43.00, X²₀.₀₅, df=1, 5.02)

**Recommendations Implemented:**
- February 1991: USFWS conducted hands-on PIT-tagging workshop with written protocols
- Demonstrated proper procedures for implanting PIT tags in endangered fish 175-250 mm and >250 mm
- PIT tagging approved for Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program field operations
- Carlin-dangler tag use discontinued for wild endangered fish; restricted use only for approved research projects with limited budgets
