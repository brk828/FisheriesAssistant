# Preliminary Study on the Effects of Organic Pollution of Banners Corner Cave, Virginia

#speleology #freshwater-ecology #asellidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary study by J. R. Holsinger (University of Kentucky) examines how sewage seepage affects the aquatic cave fauna of [[Banners Corner Cave]], Russell County, Virginia. Four seep-fed pools between the entrance and the siphon were monitored over roughly 28 months (November 1961 to February 1964). Three pools were visibly contaminated with [[Septic Tank Effluent]] from houses and a school built on the hill above the cave. All four pools supported large populations of the [[Troglobite]] isopod [[Asellus Recurvatus]] and the cave planarian [[Phagocata Subterranea]], which the author describes as unusually large for hypogean populations. The study proposes that sewage-derived organic matter is the basic trophic input of the polluted pools, feeding saprophytic bacteria, microfauna and much of the macrofauna. It is a descriptive, data-limited paper, and the author repeatedly states that few definitive trophic conclusions can be drawn.

## Background & Description

**Site and discovery.** The cave, also known as Rock House Cave and Big Spring Cave, was explored on 23 November 1961 by the author and a small party. It is excavated in the Maryville limestone (Cambrian), with development mainly joint-controlled under phreatic conditions. Strata strike N 70° E and dip southwest at about 23°. More than 300 m of passage were known at the time. A surface stream sinks about 70 m in front of the entrance and reappears only at the siphon at the end of the main passage.

**Pools.** Four seep-fed pools lie between the entrance and the siphon:
- **Pool 1:** about 1 × 2 m, with a mud bottom at one end and rock at the other, fed by a ceiling-crevice seep.
- **Pool 2:** about 7 m long, tapering from 1 m to 2.5 cm wide, up to 15 cm deep, fed by a spill-over from Pool 4a and a separate film source.
- **Pool 3:** a shallow clay-floored basin with no visible sewage, partly filled with vegetable debris, fed by a slow wall seep.
- **Pool 4:** divided by rimstone dams into **4b** (about 1 × 3 m, relatively unpolluted, fed by a wall seep) and **4a** (about 125 cm diameter, fed by a polluted seep, about 7.5 cm deep).

**Sources of contamination.** Two school buildings (1949) and nine houses (1950 or shortly after) were built on the hill above the cave. Each house has a septic tank, and the school uses two more. At least three, possibly four, tanks sit where overflow effluent can drain along bedding planes to the cave passage intersection, an estimated 13 to 16 m vertically below. Overflow material is thought to make up most of the sewage seeping into the pools. Sediments from Pools 2 and 4a were about 75% inorganic silt and clay and 25% organic detritus.

**Methods.** Six visits were made. The first five were observational. The sixth, in February 1964, included water chemistry, population counts and an initial microbiological study. Dissolved oxygen was measured by the Alsterberg (azide) modification of the [[Winkler Method]]. Chloride was measured by silver nitrate titration, alkalinity by methyl orange, and pH with a meter. Isopod density was calculated as individuals per square metre of pool area. Coliforms were tested with the Standard Presumptive Test (APHA, 1960). A comparison pool in [[Chadwell's Cave]] was sampled on 1 March 1964.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fauna.** [[Asellus Recurvatus]] was the most abundant animal in the cave over the study period and occurred in all four pools. [[Phagocata Subterranea]] was also abundant, and only in Pool 4a did it outnumber the isopod. The earthworm *Eiseniella tetraedra* was seen on each visit but was restricted to water films associated with Pool 4. Dense colonies of [[Tubifex Tubifex]] appeared in Pools 1 and 4a in April 1963 and were very numerous in Pools 2 and 4a in February 1964. Spring salamanders ([[Gyrinophilus Porphyriticus]]) were seen in Pool 2 (four or five in November 1961, three adults including one gravid female in April 1962, one in August 1962) and not afterwards.

**Microfauna and bacteria.** The sample from Pools 2 and 4a (4 litres, 29 February 1964) contained [[Paramecium Micromultinucleatum]] and *Spirostomum ambiguum* (?) as very abundant forms, plus an unidentified ploimid rotifer. Other taxa, including gastrotrichs, nematodes, oligochaetes and water mites, were scarce. The author notes that "shrimp pools" in [[Mammoth Cave]] held far fewer microorganisms. Coliforms were confirmed in Pools 2, 4a and 4b. Pools 2 and 4a showed abundant broth growth within 24 hours, while Pool 4b showed only moderate growth.

## Key Findings

1. **Isopod densities.** In February 1964, Pool 2 held about 35.0 individuals/m², Pool 4a about 25.0/m² and Pool 4b about 60.9/m². Pool 3 rose from a few animals in April 1963 to more than 30 in total by February 1964. [[Chadwell's Cave]], with much less organic input, had only 6.0/m². Isopod numbers fell in Pools 2 and 4a between June 1963 and February 1964, but the author could not quantify this decline because counts began only in February 1964.

2. **Flatworm dynamics.** Planarian numbers fluctuated more than isopod numbers. Pool 1 held an estimated 25 to 50 worms in April 1962 and only two in June 1963. Pool 2 had none in June 1963 but a count roughly equal to its isopods in February 1964. Pool 3's planarians fell as its isopods rose. The author notes that planarians burrow into mud, so accurate counts are often impossible.

3. **Habitat partitioning.** Isopods clustered in shallow, rocky-substrate areas of Pools 2 and 4a and in the silty centre of Pool 4b, while planarians concentrated in deeper central water in Pools 1 and 4a and along shallow edges in Pool 2. The author suggests low oxygen may limit isopod distribution, but the data are insufficient to set tolerance limits.

4. **Oxygen and chloride.** Pool 2 had the lowest dissolved oxygen (2.8 mg/l) and the highest chloride (14.0 mg/l), read as combined evidence of sewage and high BOD. Pool 4b's values were comparable to the unpolluted comparison cave.

5. **Trophic hypothesis.** Sewage-derived organic matter is proposed as the basic food input of the contaminated pools, feeding bacteria, protozoans, rotifers and gastrotrichs, and indirectly the macrofauna. The author argues that cave planarians probably feed mainly on fecal and organic detritus rather than on isopods, citing that the two largest North American cave planarian populations (Banners Corner and Bat Cave, Oklahoma) were in pools with large fecal deposits.

6. **Salamander predation.** *Gyrinophilus porphyriticus* was observed feeding on planarians and could be a predator on both planarians and [[Asellus Recurvatus]], though this was not confirmed in nature.

7. **Study limitations.** The author states that the trophic relationships between the larger aquatic organisms "have not been worked out," that the 28-month record is sparse, that laboratory planarian-feeding data are scarce, and that a *Phagocata* identification needed confirmation (only one sexually mature worm was found in the material examined by L. H. Hyman).

8. **Research value.** The author concludes that the site offers a rare chance to study both sewage effects on freshwater habitats and the little-known trophic dynamics of subterranean aquatic environments.

**Source note:** The English summary gives the study period as "November, 1964, to February, 1964," which conflicts with the body text (November 1961 to February 1964). The note follows the body text.
