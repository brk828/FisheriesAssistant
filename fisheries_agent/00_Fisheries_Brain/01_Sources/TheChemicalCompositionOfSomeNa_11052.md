## Executive Summary

This paper by Eville Gorham (1957) is **not a fish or ichthyological study**; rather, it is a foundational limnological survey of the chemical composition of natural waters (lochs, lochans, springs, and bogs) in the Cairn Gorm–Strath Spey district of the Scottish Highlands. The study analyzes 24 water bodies for pH, major cations (Na, K, Ca, Mg), major anions (HCO₃, Cl, SO₄, NO₃, PO₄), and silica (SiO₂), interpreting ionic variation in terms of geology (granite vs. Moine metamorphic schist), altitude, topography, evaporation, vegetation cover, and atmospheric deposition. While no fish or aquatic fauna are discussed, the data provide important **environmental/habitat context** relevant to understanding water quality and potential fish habitat suitability in oligotrophic Highland freshwater systems.

## Background & Description

- **Study area**: Cairn Gorm plateau (granite, ~1100–1200 m elevation) intruded into the metamorphic Moine Series (schists/gneiss), overlain in the Spey valley by glacial drift (sand, gravel, clay).
- **Lake origins**: Many lochs are moraine-dammed; [[Loch Morlich]] is a kettle-hole lake formed by melting of a buried ice block.
- **Climate**: ~75 cm annual rainfall; mean annual temperature ~7.5°C (summer average ~13°C); upland snow can persist into summer, with snow patches recorded even in the unusually warm July 1955 sampling period.
- **Vegetation zonation**: Open pine forest and heath dominate below ~450–600 m; montane heath/moorland communities extend to ~750 m, thinning above that.
- **Soils**: Strongly acid and base-deficient upland soils (pH 3.3–4.5) and even more acidic lowland mor humus layers (pH 2.9–3.3), attributed to sulfuric acid from oxidation of organic sulfur compounds. A representative lowland podzol profile near Loch Morlich showed classic podzolization with an iron hardpan at ~33 cm depth.
- **Lake typology**: Waters were classified as **drainage lakes** (inflow + outflow streams) versus **seepage lakes** (no significant surface inflow/outflow), plus three springs and several nearly desiccated peaty pools.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although this paper contains no direct fish data, its findings characterize the chemical environment that any aquatic fauna (including fish, if present) in this region would experience:

- **Habitat types surveyed**: [[Loch Einich]], [[Loch Morlich]], [[Loch an Eilein]], An Lochan Uaine, Dubh a'Chadha, and numerous small unnamed lochans/pools in corries such as Coire an Lochain and Coire an t-Sneachda, plus three cold springs (4–7°C).
- **Thermal regime**: Surface water temperatures in July 1955 ranged 20–25°C in most lochans, but snow-fed lochans remained much colder (as low as 10–15°C at inflow/surface, 12°C at depth) — indicating strong microhabitat thermal heterogeneity potentially relevant to cold-water-adapted species.
- **Water chemistry gradient**: Strong altitude-driven gradient in ionic concentration — granite-based upland waters are extremely dilute (total salts as low as ~0.02 meq/L in snowmelt) and acidic to circumneutral, while lowland Moine-derived waters are more mineral-rich (up to 1.52 meq/L in a lowland spring) with higher pH (up to 8.8 in an artificial lake choked with Nitella).
- **Acidity**: pH ranged from 3.9 (raised-bog pool) to 8.8 (post-aeration alkaline artificial lake); large lochs maintained a narrower, more circumneutral range (pH 6.4–7.5), which would be more hospitable to typical freshwater fish fauna than the strongly acidic peaty pools.
- **Nutrient scarcity**: Nitrate-N was consistently below 0.1 ppm and phosphate was undetectable (<0.001 ppm) in nearly all samples — indicating strongly oligotrophic conditions, which constrains primary and secondary productivity and thus potential fish carrying capacity.
- **Silica**: Ranged from 0.8 ppm (snowmelt) to 27 ppm (lowland spring), reflecting weathering intensity.
- **Anthropogenic influence**: The author notes probable historical influence of deforestation, grazing, and atmospheric pollution on water chemistry, relevant to long-term habitat change.

## Key Findings

- **Cation–anion balance**: Generally well balanced except in three nearly dry, brown peaty pools where cation excess suggests unmeasured organic acid anions.
- **Sea-spray influence**: Sodium and magnesium correlate strongly with chloride, indicating marine aerosol deposition as a major source; sodium ratios exceed seawater proportions due to additional weathering release, while magnesium/chloride ratios closely match seawater in dilute granite waters.
- **Altitude–evaporation effect**: Chloride concentration rises sharply with decreasing altitude (from ~2 ppm at 900 m to 9–10 ppm at 200–300 m), attributed primarily to evaporative concentration; smaller lakes show up to 40% higher chloride than larger lakes at comparable altitude due to greater surface-area-to-volume ratios.
- **Calcium/bicarbonate weathering signal**: Granite-derived waters are calcium-poor (0.2–1.3 ppm Ca), while Moine-derived waters reach up to 13.3 ppm Ca, reflecting more rapid weathering of mica-schists and occasional calcium-rich intrusive rocks (epidiorites, hornblende schists). Calcium and bicarbonate co-vary in near-equivalent proportions, pointing to rock/soil weathering as their common source.
- **Acid-sulfate dynamics**: The three most acidic samples show strong H⁺–SO₄ correlation, implicating oxidation of organic sulfur to sulfuric acid in peaty substrates as the acidifying mechanism.
- **Sulfate/chloride decoupling with vegetation and tree-line**: SO₄/Cl ratios are higher in upland granite lakes (0.6–1.5) than lowland Moine lakes (0.3–0.7), hypothesized to result from vegetation "capturing" larger sea-salt (chloride-bearing) particles below the tree line, while gaseous sulfate is less affected by this capture mechanism. Rocky, sparsely vegetated catchments show elevated SO₄/Cl ratios versus well-vegetated ones.
- **Potassium sourcing**: K/Cl ratios exceed seawater proportions, implicating weathering and blown dust as sources rather than marine spray; the highest K concentrations occurred in a nearly desiccated pool with exposed, decaying organic mud.
- **Atmospheric precipitation as primary ion source**: Comparison with English Lake District rainwater chemistry (Gorham 1955) shows strong similarity to granite-lake chemistry, reinforcing that rain is the dominant ion source, modified by soil percolation primarily through addition of calcium bicarbonate.
