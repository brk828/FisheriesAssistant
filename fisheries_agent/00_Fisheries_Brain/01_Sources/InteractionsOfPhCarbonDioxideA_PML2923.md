# Interactions of pH, Carbon Dioxide, Alkalinity and Hardness in Fish Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture-water-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This Southern Regional Aquaculture Center publication (SRAC No. 464, December 1992), by [[William A. Wurts]] and [[Robert M. Durborow]], explains how [[PH]], [[Carbon Dioxide]], [[Total Alkalinity]] and [[Water Hardness]] interact in warmwater fish ponds. These variables are interrelated and control pond productivity, fish stress and health, oxygen availability, and the toxicity of [[Ammonia]] and metals. The authors present practical management guidance: target ranges for each parameter, a method for estimating CO₂ from pH, temperature and alkalinity, and treatment options. They conclude that a working understanding of this water chemistry is essential for effective and profitable pond management.

## Background & Description

The publication is a extension-style technical guide aimed at pond managers rather than a research study. It reports no original sample data. Its figures are reference values drawn from the literature, mainly [[Boyd 1979]] and [[Tucker 1984]].

Water quality in ponds is not constant. [[Carbon Dioxide]] and pH fluctuate or cycle daily, while alkalinity and hardness are relatively stable and change over weeks to months. Their stability depends on the pH and mineral content of the watershed and bottom soils.

The document is organized into these topics:
- **pH and carbon dioxide:** daily cycles driven by [[Photosynthesis]] and [[Respiration]].
- **Alkalinity:** the buffering capacity of pond water, derived from limestone interactions.
- **Alkalinity, pH and photosynthesis:** how dense [[Phytoplankton]] blooms drive pH upward.
- **Hardness:** the role of divalent ions, especially calcium, in fish physiology.
- **Ammonia and metal toxicity:** how pH, alkalinity and hardness change the toxicity of these substances.

Two tables and two figures support the text: a 24-hour table of relative changes in dissolved oxygen, CO₂ and pH; a table of CO₂ factors by pH and temperature; and a figure of the daily pH cycle in high- and low-alkalinity water. The table and figure data are not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**pH and the fish's internal environment.** The recommended pond pH range is 6.5 to 9.0, and a desirable range is 7.0 to 8.0, close to the average fish blood pH of 7.4. Blood contacts the water across a thin barrier at the [[Gills]] and skin. Fish may become stressed or die if pH drops below 5 (for example, from acidic runoff) or rises above 10 (for example, from low alkalinity combined with intense algal photosynthesis).

**Daily cycle.** After sunset, dissolved oxygen falls as photosynthesis stops and all organisms respire. In heavily stocked ponds, CO₂ from respiration reacts with water to form carbonic acid and lowers pH. Dissolved oxygen, CO₂ and pH therefore move in opposite directions over the day.

**Source of alkalinity and hardness.** Rainwater is naturally acidic because it absorbs atmospheric CO₂. As ground- and rainwater percolate through [[Limestone]] (calcitic or dolomitic), CO₂-driven acidity dissolves the rock into calcium and magnesium bicarbonates. The result is water with higher alkalinity, pH and hardness. Groundwater typically has high CO₂ and low pH and oxygen.

**Buffering.** Bicarbonates and carbonates neutralize acid. In water with moderate to high alkalinity, pH stays near neutral to slightly basic (7.0 to 8.3) and changes little. Low-alkalinity water has little buffer, so pH can swing dangerously low at night and high during the day.

**Sodium-bicarbonate water.** Where [[Sodium Bicarbonate]] supplies alkalinity, a pond can have high alkalinity with low hardness. Such water is more prone to pH spikes during algal blooms.

## Key Findings

### 1. Carbon dioxide and oxygen stress
- CO₂ rarely causes direct toxicity to fish. Its main effect is to lower pH and blood pH at the gills, reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
- Fish may suffocate at a given dissolved oxygen level (example: 2 mg/L) when CO₂ is high, and appear unaffected when CO₂ is low.
- [[Channel Catfish]] tolerate 20 to 30 mg/L CO₂ if accumulation is slow and dissolved oxygen stays above 5 mg/L.
- In a reservoir or natural pond, CO₂ rarely exceeds 5 to 10 mg/L.
- High CO₂ almost always accompanies low dissolved oxygen. Aeration helps by improving CO₂ diffusion to the atmosphere.
- Chronic high CO₂ can be treated with [[Hydrated Lime]]. About 1 mg/L of hydrated lime removes about 1 mg/L of CO₂. Do not use it in low-alkalinity water, where pH can reach lethal levels, or where ammonia is high, because high pH increases ammonia toxicity.

### 2. Alkalinity thresholds
- A total alkalinity of at least 20 mg/L is necessary for good pond productivity.
- The desirable range for fish culture is 75 to 200 mg/L CaCO₃.
- Alkalinities of 20 mg/L or more trap CO₂ and increase its availability for photosynthesis. Ponds below 20 mg/L rarely support good phytoplankton blooms.

### 3. Estimating CO₂ from pH, temperature and alkalinity
- The method multiplies a factor from [[Table 2]] (chosen by pH and water temperature) by measured total alkalinity (mg/L as CaCO₃).
- Worked example from the text: pH 7.2, 25 °C (77 °F) and alkalinity 103 mg/L give a factor of 0.124, so CO₂ ≈ 0.124 × 103 ≈ 12.8 mg/L.
- The pH reading must be taken within 30 minutes of sampling to limit error. Because several error sources exist, a direct chemical CO₂ test is preferred.
- CO₂ is negligible above pH 8.4.

### 4. Photosynthesis and pH swings
- Phytoplankton use CO₂ and can convert bicarbonate to CO₂ and carbonate. Removing carbonic acid and releasing carbonate both raise pH.
- Dense algal blooms can push pH above 9 in two situations:
  - Low-alkalinity water (20 to 50 mg/L).
  - Moderate to high bicarbonate alkalinity (75 to 200 mg/L) with hardness below 25 mg/L.
- If calcium, magnesium and photosynthetically produced carbonate are present at pH above 8.3, limestone can precipitate.

### 5. Hardness and calcium requirements
- Hardness is the concentration of divalent ions, mainly calcium and magnesium, reported as mg/L CaCO₃.
- Hardness and alkalinity are often confused because both are reported as mg/L CaCO₃. They match only where limestone is the source of both. Sodium bicarbonate can give high alkalinity with low hardness.
- Calcium maintains body salts (sodium and potassium) in fish and helps prevent their loss to water. Low-calcium water causes fish to leak these salts, which costs energy to replace.
- Recommended free calcium is 25 to 100 mg/L (63 to 250 mg/L as CaCO₃ hardness).
- For [[Striped Bass]], [[Red Drum]] and [[Freshwater Crawfish]], free calcium of 40 to 100 mg/L (100 to 250 mg/L CaCO₃) is desirable. A value of 100 mg/L calcium matches fish blood.
- Channel catfish tolerate low calcium if feed supplies minimum mineral calcium but may grow slowly. [[Rainbow Trout]] tolerate free calcium as low as 70 mg/L if pH is above 6.5.
- Conversion: divide CaCO₃ hardness by 2.5 to get free calcium. Divide by about 4.1 to get free magnesium. The example gives 100 mg/L CaCO₃ as 40 mg/L calcium and 24 mg/L magnesium. (The magnesium divisor is garbled in the scan; the 24 mg/L result is stated in the text.)
- Hardness does not always mean high calcium, but in the southern United States, high hardness is usually a reasonable indicator of high calcium because of common limestone geology.
- Agricultural limestone raises calcium and alkalinity in acidic waters but does not dissolve at pH 8.3 or higher. [[Gypsum]] or food-grade calcium chloride can add calcium to soft, alkaline water, though cost may be prohibitive for large volumes.

### 6. Ammonia and metal toxicity
- [[Un-ionized Ammonia]] (NH₃), the more toxic form, increases in basic water. Ammonium (NH₄⁺), the less toxic form, dominates in acidic water. Because alkalinity raises pH, ammonia toxicity is greater in high-alkalinity water. Hardness does not typically affect ammonia toxicity.
- [[Copper]] and [[Zinc]] become more soluble and toxic in acidic water. High alkalinity forms less toxic or insoluble compounds.
- High calcium and magnesium hardness block copper and zinc at their toxic sites of action. These metals are therefore most toxic in soft, acidic, low-alkalinity water.

### 7. Management targets
The authors state the ideal aquaculture pond conditions as:
- pH 6.5 to 9.0
- Total alkalinity 75 to 200 mg/L CaCO₃, and not less than 20 mg/L
- Calcium hardness 100 to 250 mg/L CaCO₃

The authors conclude that water quality is water chemistry, and that managers need a working grasp of carbonate-bicarbonate buffering to manage ponds effectively.
