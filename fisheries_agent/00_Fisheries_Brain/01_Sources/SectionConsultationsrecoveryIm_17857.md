# Section 7 Consultations and the Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1989 legal and policy package from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Denver). It consists of a Regional Director's request for a legal opinion, the Regional Solicitor's memorandum (dated May 12, 1989), and two attachments. The subject is how the Service should handle [[Section 7 Consultation]]s under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for new water depletion projects affecting three [[Colorado River Endangered Fishes]]: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]].

The Service concluded there is "significant uncertainty" that instream flow protection under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] will be achieved in time. It therefore proposed that large projects also set aside water, a [[Conservation Measure]] backup, as an "insurance policy." The Solicitor found this position legally sufficient under the ESA and the [[Administrative Procedure Act]].

## Background & Description

The three species have declined because of habitat loss and other factors. Their decline is the basis for the Service's jeopardy determinations. The memo states that the Service has issued over one hundred [[Biological Opinion]]s on water projects since 1977. The Regional Director's memo gives 1978 as the start date, a discrepancy in the source.

The Recovery Program was developed over roughly four years by the Service, Wyoming, Utah, Colorado, water development interests and environmental groups. It was implemented in January 1988. Its purpose was partly to provide a process for handling [[Depletion Impacts]] in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] under Section 7.

Key terms in the source:

- **Large depletion project:** any project depleting more than 3,000 [[Acre-Foot]] per year. Smaller depletions can be treated as large case by case, depending on location, timing and impact on the fishes.
- **Reasonable and prudent alternative (RPA):** an action that can be implemented within agency authority, is economically and technologically feasible, and would avoid jeopardy.
- **Conservation measure:** an RPA the applicant agrees to before the final biological opinion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Service's uncertainty rests on the fishes' habitat needs and on the state of water law:

- **Flow needs are poorly known.** Data on the species are scarce, and large, turbid, inaccessible rivers make study difficult. Flow needs have been defined only for some reaches and some months, partly by professional judgment that water users have not readily accepted.
- **Depletion effects.** Under the Recovery Program, depletion impacts include flow reductions and corresponding changes in temperature, salinity and turbidity.
- **State law limits.** The [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] is the only entity that can hold an instream flow right under Colorado law. Wyoming instream flows cannot be protected within 1 mile of the state border, which may block delivery of water to occupied habitat in Colorado and Utah. Federal enforcement of a state instream right is limited if the state does not defend it.
- **Priority reaches.** The 15-mile reach of the Colorado River above the Gunnison confluence is a high-priority area. A filing there is expected to be opposed, and a state Supreme Court decision could take 6 years.

## Key Findings

**Legal conclusions (Regional Solicitor, May 12, 1989)**

- The Service's determination of significant uncertainty, based on nine enumerated factors, has an adequate record and is not a clear error of judgment under the APA (citing [[Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe]]).
- Under the ESA, the project, not the species, must bear the risk of an unworkable alternative. The Service must give the species the benefit of the doubt and exercise "institutional caution," drawing on [[TVA v. Hill]] (437 U.S. 153, 1978).
- In [[Sierra Club v. Marsh]] (816 F.2d 1376, 9th Cir. 1987), the Corps of Engineers' 188-acre wetland mitigation lapsed when land acquisition failed. The court enjoined the project. The memo uses this case to warn that backup alternatives must not lapse.
- The proposed additional measure is "legally sufficient" and "fully defensible in a court of law."

**Recovery Program mechanics**

- Project proponents make a one-time contribution of $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, adjusted annually for inflation.
- Instream flow protection under state law is the second offset element.
- The Service decides whether progress is sufficient 30 days before the last federal permit for a project.
- The Recovery Program does not serve as the RPA for existing [[Bureau of Reclamation]] dams or for projects in certain important reaches (section 4.1.5).

**Cost and water-rights findings (Regional Director memo)**

- Water rights are bought only from willing sellers. Key rights include the Juniper and Cross Mountain rights on the [[Yampa River]].
- Value of Juniper-Cross Mountain rights was estimated at $0 to $100 million.
- The cheapest Colorado River water, from [[Ruedi Reservoir]], is expected to sell for $50–60/af/year. Buying 50,000 af for two months would cost $2.5 million annually, which would exhaust the $10 million acquisition fund in 5 years.
- Agricultural rights sell for $200–$1,000/af of transferable consumptive use.
- The 15-mile reach flow determination for July–September took nearly 3 years.

**Progress reported (Attachment 2, April 1989)**

- Cooperative agreement signed. Congress appropriated $1M to acquire water rights.
- A $2.3M FY-89 work plan funds 39 high-priority projects.
- Ruedi Reservoir delivery agreement is near completion. A Colorado Water Conservation Board filing for the 15-mile reach is in progress.
- Work on the [[Green River]] basin includes Juniper-Cross Mountain negotiations, Little Snake River rights, a Flaming Gorge reoperation consultation, and the Yampa River Phase I biological need report (near completion).

**Outcome**

- No instream flows for the fishes had yet been legally protected under the program.
- Two large-depletion biological opinions were held in abeyance pending the legal opinion. A response was requested by May 10, 1989.
- The Service's proposed approach: require project proponents to withhold part of each project's annual yield for instream flows until there is reasonable assurance the Recovery Program will protect adequate flows.
