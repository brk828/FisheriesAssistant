# Ecology of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker and the Role of Off-Channel Habitats in Their Recovery

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary:** Bonytail (*Gila elegans*) and Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) are two of four endangered mainstem fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]], endemic to the basin and part of the Big River Fish Community. Bonytail are medium-sized (most often 30–40 cm; largest documented from CHLP a 52-cm female; maximum weight 1–1.5 kg). Razorback Sucker is the largest Colorado River sucker, reported approaching a meter and 5–6 kg, with a bony dorsal keel that develops with growth. Bonytail mature in their first or second year; females at 10 cm were found expressing eggs, and one report gives up to 20% of body weight in eggs. Razorback females mature after their third or fourth year and produce 75,000–200,000 eggs annually; males can be sexually active in their first year. Razorback longevity is reported at 24–44 years in Lake Mohave, with >40, possibly 50 years suggested; bonytail otolith ages were 7–13 years, with longevity estimated at 30+ years. Razorback growth is rapid in year one (0.48–0.77 mm/day over the first 160 days in one study) and falls sharply after about 45 cm. Bonytail growth at CHLP was about 3.5 mm/month for fish under 200 mm.

**Habitat Associations:** Historically both species occupied mainstem habitats and floodplain wetlands from the [[Colorado River Delta]] to headwater tributaries. Bonytail were never captured in swift current and were found in slack water near eddy lines; they show strong fidelity to dark cover such as riprap cavities, root wads, and drift piles. Razorback Sucker use large, deep main-channel pools and warmer off-channel backwaters, and spawn on clean gravel and cobble across standing and flowing water (0.25–25 m depth). Both species tolerated extreme conditions: Yuma flows ranged from 1.4 to 11,200 m³/s, and water temperatures from 0 to >35 °C. Early juveniles of both species are strongly tied to shallow, vegetated, or dark cover in backwaters and beaver dens.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration:** Little evidence shows bonytail migrate significant distances to spawn; daily movements of up to 10 km/day were recorded between canyon habitats and suspected spawning areas in [[Lake Mohave]], and displaced fish traveled 14 km/day to return home. Razorback Sucker moved 30–60 km one-way in rivers and 40–50 km in reservoirs during spawning migrations; the maximum observed was 17 km/day. Repatriated fish migrate roughly 40–50 km upstream from [[Lake Havasu]] to spawn near Needles, California. In winter, razorback remained within a 1.6–4.8 km reach.

**Trophic & Community Interactions:** Bonytail are omnivorous. In 72 CHLP stomachs, 7% of dawn-collected fish had empty stomachs versus 33% at dusk, suggesting nocturnal feeding. Larger fish (>40 cm) took more invertebrates and fish, and bonytail cannibalized recently spawned eggs. Razorback larvae feed on zooplankton; in one study, 30–75% of larvae had empty stomachs. Adults feed primarily on zooplankton (cladocerans in 100% of 24 Lake Mohave stomachs), and at CHLP form schools of more than 100 fish that herd zooplankton. Predation is the central interaction: larval suckers are eaten by odonate nymphs, common carp, red shiner, green sunfish, bullheads, and bullfrog tadpoles. Razorback adults raid bonytail spawning beds for eggs. Osprey and kingfishers prey on both species, and crayfish attack captured fish.

**Environmental Drivers:** Spawning timing tracks water temperature: bonytail spawned in early March–April at CHLP (18–20 °C), in May at Lake Mohave, and into July upstream; razorback spawning peaks at 14–16 °C, with optimal embryo survival near 20 °C and successful incubation possible at 10 °C. Spring runoff delays spawning in upstream reaches. Razorback spawning runs from late December to about June. Hot summer water (>34 °C) and low oxygen (<1 mg/L) cause adults to become inactive near the bottom, and capture stress from netting is a known driver of mortality.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory:** Wild bonytail are believed extirpated from the Upper Basin and nearly so in the Lower Basin; the last confirmed Upper Basin capture was in 1985 and the last Lower Basin wild capture in the mid-1990s. Wild razorback numbers are estimated at about 500 in Lake Mohave, 300 in Lake Mead, under 100 in the Green River, and under 1,000 in the mainstem overall. Propagation was described as the only action preventing extirpation. Dexter NFHTC alone produced more than 2.1 million bonytail and 15 million razorback over 25 years. Stocking in [[Lake Mohave]] (1992–2003, roughly 84,000 fish) produced an estimated 1,500 young adults, with survival closely tied to size. At CHLP in 2002, mark-recapture estimates were 5,979 bonytail (10–20 cm), 1,593 bonytail (>20 cm), and 1,111 razorback (>20 cm), with the community at 3,776 fish/ha and 552 kg/ha.

**Threat & Risk Assessment:** Nonnative predators are identified as the primary cause of recruitment failure; predator removal programs have been ineffective and costly, with targeted species rapidly recolonizing. At CHLP, largemouth bass spawned in 2004, and bass comprised 47% of fish caught by 2005. Handling is an additional risk: each handling event reduced bonytail growth by 9.5% and weight by 24.8% (Paukert and others, 2005); delayed mortality reached 77% after tissue sampling in one study; and 75% mortality occurred in a telemetry study. Electrofishing can cause spinal injury and hemorrhaging. Parasites include Lernaea (20+ per fish in some samples) and Myxobolus, which caused blindness in nearly 12% of Lake Mohave razorback. Habitat loss is also central: an estimated 1.9 million acres of delta wetland were drained, and Laguna Dam (1909) cut off upstream return.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations:** The report recommends a management plan for CHLP, which currently has no specific plan beyond the 1993 goal of raising fish for stocking. Options include: extending autumn electrofishing to two weeks with intensive netting for salvage; chemically renovating the pond at low water; restocking with 5,000 15-cm bonytail and 2,500 30-cm razorback in early spring; setting harvest criteria when populations exceed 5,000 bonytail (>15 cm) or 500 large razorback (>50 cm); designating CHLP as a research station; and adding riprap or brush piles to reduce avian predation, with avian spikes or sign relocation. For new sanctuaries, the report proposes three sizes: medium (2–5 ha) ponds for healthy populations, small (<1 ha) ponds for controlled predator-prey experiments, and large ponds to test techniques. Key design features include isolation from nonnative fishes, gravity drainage, maximum depth of 4 m, size under 2.5 ha, dissolved oxygen above 3 mg/L, spawning terraces at 0.5–2 m, and dark overhead cover.

**Biological Reference Points:** Stocking survival rises sharply with size: survival is closely correlated with stocking size and nearly doubles for razorback stocked above 35 cm; survival >30 cm is noted as much higher. For a population of about 1,000 mature razorback at CHLP, average recruitment of 50 individuals per year would sustain it if average age is 20 years; if average age were 10 years, 100 young adults per year would be needed. Bonytail at CHLP showed the sex ratio shifting from 1:1 at 350 mm to all females above 500 mm. Recruitment-related size thresholds include bonytail over 15 cm and razorback over 50 cm as harvest triggers.
