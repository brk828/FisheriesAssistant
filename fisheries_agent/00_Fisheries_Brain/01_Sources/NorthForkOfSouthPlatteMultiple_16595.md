# North Fork of South Platte Multiple Purpose Project 1947-1948

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fisheries-management #trout-stocking

## Executive Summary

This two-year fisheries management study (1947-1948) on the [[North Fork of South Platte River]] in [[Colorado]] evaluated the effectiveness of stocking legal-size [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] (referred to as "lochs") in a heavily fished mountain stream. Conducted by the [[Colorado Game and Fish Department]] under Ray H. Hess, the project employed extensive tagging (metal jaw bands), creel census, and electric shocking surveys across 14 miles (1947) and 5.2 miles (1948) of stream. The study addressed 16 specific management questions regarding stocking intensity, timing, species selection, fish movement, angler return rates, and closure policies. Key findings demonstrated that legal-size trout stocking is justified in high-pressure waters, with rainbows providing 16% better creel returns than brook trout (62% vs. 52.4% first-season recovery in 1948). Migration was extensive, with significant losses to inaccessible areas. The research provided quantitative data to optimize stocking programs for equitable angler distribution and maximum return on hatchery investment.

## Background & Description

The [[North Fork of South Platte River]] originates on the south slope of [[Mount Evans]] and flows into the [[South Platte River]] at the village of South Platte, approximately 50 road miles southwest of [[Denver]]. The study section was selected as representative of average [[Colorado]] trout streams, featuring normal pool frequency and grade, typical fish food production, mixed flow regimes, and discharge ranging from 50-200 cubic feet per second during the fishing season. Elevation ranged from 6,100 feet at the lower confluence to 6,700 feet at the upper study boundary near [[Pine, Colorado]].

The 1947 study encompassed 14 miles of stream (10 miles open to public fishing), while 1948 focused on a 5.2-mile section entirely open to the public. The stream was divided into tenth-mile increments marked with numbered white stakes for precise location recording. Both [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*, referred to as "lochs" or [[Loch Leven Trout]]) were stocked at legal size (averaging 8.5 inches).

Research methods included:
- Pre-season and post-season electric shocking inventories
- Metal jaw-band tagging of all stocked and resident legal-size trout
- Continuous creel census with 1-8 field staff members (1947) or check stations at section boundaries (1948)
- Fin-clipping of first 1948 plant to assess tag retention (under 1% tag loss detected)
- Detailed logging of species, size, date, and precise location for all tagged fish

Stocking intensity varied systematically in 1948: 500 fish/mile (May), 1,000/mile (June), 2,000/mile (July), and 4,000/mile (August), with 500 tagged fish per species per plant. The 1947 program maintained uniform intensity (~500/mile) but varied closure periods: 6 weeks (April), 1 week (June), and none (August).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study stream represented typical [[Colorado]] mountain trout habitat with mixed pool-riffle morphology and moderate gradient. Natural fish food production was described as normal for the region. Stream flow during the bulk of the fishing season ranged from 50-200 second feet, providing adequate habitat for both species.

**Resident Fish Populations**: Pre-season inventories documented existing populations of both rainbow and brook trout. In the 1948 study area, 50 resident rainbows and 281 resident brook trout were tagged during November 1947 census operations. Resident trout averaged 7.5 inches in length.

**Natural Reproduction**: Considerable natural reproduction was observed in the study section, though the report notes insufficient knowledge of carrying capacity, survival rates, and growth to determine adequacy of natural recruitment relative to angling pressure.

**Fishing Pressure**: The study documented extreme fishing intensity, particularly in 1948. Recorded fishing pressure was 2,308 fisherman-hours per stream mile (782 fisherman-days) in 1947, increasing dramatically to 7,175 fisherman-hours per stream mile (1,895 fisherman-days) in 1948. This intensity was attributed to publicity and stocking programs, representing conditions near population centers.

**Angler Harvest of Resident Trout**: Depletion of resident populations was substantial. In 1948, 44% of tagged resident rainbows (22 of 50) and 25% of tagged resident brook trout (69 of 281) were recovered in creels. The 1947 season showed 32% recovery for resident rainbows and 19% for resident brook trout. Resident trout contributed approximately 96 legal-size fish per surface acre per season, totaling about 16 pounds, yielding only 0.52-0.61 fish per angler-hour without supplemental stocking—far below the 1.0 target for "good fishing."

**Species Interactions**: Brook trout comprised 66% of resident trout in creels versus 34% rainbows, suggesting competitive advantage for brook trout in this system. However, the report cautions this does not prove brook trout would provide superior fishing as the sole resident species.

## Key Findings

**1. Justification for Legal-Size Stocking**

Creel checks in 1948 documented recovery of 62% of stocked rainbows and 52.4% of stocked brook trout during the season of planting. These represent minimum figures, as additional fish were caught outside the study area, remained in the stream for future seasons, or were taken by campers. The data strongly justify legal-size stocking in heavily fished waters where natural reproduction and resident populations cannot sustain adequate catch rates.

**2. Species Performance: Rainbow Superiority**

[[Rainbow Trout]] provided 16% greater creel returns than [[Brook Trout]] across both study years under identical stocking conditions. This substantial margin should guide species selection for put-and-take programs in similar waters. Rainbows also exhibited more rapid average growth (1.17 inches over one year versus 0.80 inches for brook trout in tagged resident fish).

**3. Seasonal Timing Effects**

Returns varied significantly by planting season:

*1947 Returns (including second-season recoveries):*
- April: Rainbows 42.0%, Brook trout 43.0%
- June: Rainbows 40.0%, Brook trout 17.8%
- August: Rainbows 52.2%, Brook trout 39.2%

*1948 Returns (first season only):*
- May: Rainbows 71.8%, Brook trout 65.3%
- June: Rainbows 62.4%, Brook trout 50.3%
- July: Rainbows 49.8%, Brook trout 32.6%
- August: Rainbows 72.6%, Brook trout 58.4%

Early season (April-May) and late season (August) plants consistently produced highest returns for both species. Mid-summer plants, particularly June-July, showed reduced returns, especially for brook trout.

**4. Stocking Intensity and Distribution Equity**

Stocking intensity (500 to 4,000 fish/mile) did not materially affect the percentage of planted fish ultimately caught. However, heavier plants concentrated catches among early anglers, reducing equitable distribution. Catch per angler-hour declined rapidly with cumulative fishing pressure:

*August Plant (4,000/mile) - Catch per Angler-Hour by Week:*
- Week 1: 1.44
- Week 2: 0.43
- Week 3: 0.22
- Week 4: 0.15

The first 500 fishermen averaged 1.96 fish/hour, while fishermen 4,000-4,500 averaged only 0.12 fish/hour. Light, frequent plants across multiple suitable waters optimize equitable distribution among licensed anglers and more efficiently utilize natural food resources.

**5. Duration of Fishery Contribution**

Stocked legal-size trout contributed materially to catches for an average of 5.5 weeks (rainbows 4.5 weeks, brook trout 6.5 weeks). Fishing quality declined substantially after 4 weeks. Cumulative removal patterns showed:

*Typical Removal Pattern (1948 May Plant):*
- Week 1: 18% (both species)
- Week 2: 36% rainbows, 63% brook trout
- Week 4: 65% rainbows, 76% brook trout
- Week 6: 79% rainbows, 83% brook trout

Heavier stocking intensity attracted more anglers, actually reducing the time period of contribution rather than extending it.

**6. Migration Patterns and Losses**

Fish movement was extensive and varied by species and season:

*Overall Migration (3,529 tagged fish recovered):*
- 26% of rainbows and 21% of brook trout caught within 0.1 mile of stocking location
- 34% of rainbows moved >0.1 mile upstream; 59% of brook trout moved upstream
- 39% of rainbows moved >0.1 mile downstream; 20% of brook trout moved downstream
- Average upstream movement: 0.66 miles (rainbows), 0.90 miles (brook trout)
- Average downstream movement: 0.49 miles (rainbows), 0.64 miles (brook trout)

*Seasonal Migration Patterns:*

Rainbows showed greater early-season movement with downstream bias:
- May: 63% downstream, 20% upstream
- August: 35% downstream, 34% upstream

Brook trout showed opposite pattern with increasing upstream movement:
- May: 27% downstream, 47% upstream
- August: 15% downstream, 72% upstream

*Management Implications for Inaccessible Waters:*

Predicted losses from stream sections bounded by posted/inaccessible areas:
- 0.5-mile open section: 55% rainbow loss, 46% brook trout loss (May plants)
- 1.0-mile section: 37% rainbow loss, 31% brook trout loss
- 2.0-mile section: 20% rainbow loss, 16% brook trout loss

**Specific Recommendations:**
- No plants closer than 0.5 mile to inaccessible areas
- Spring rainbow plants: minimum 1 mile above inaccessible areas
- Late summer/fall brook trout plants: minimum 1 mile below inaccessible areas
- Open sections <1.5 miles should not be stocked

Maximum documented individual movements: rainbow upstream 7.2 miles, rainbow downstream 6.9 miles, brook trout upstream 10.1 miles, brook trout downstream 6.7 miles.

**7. Statewide Stocking Requirements**

Extrapolating 1948 study returns to 1947 statewide data (12,732,319 fisherman-hours, 11,324,140 trout caught, 0.89 fish/hour):

To achieve 1.0 fish/angler-hour statewide would require:
- Using rainbows: 3,797,000 fish (682,000 pounds) - 135% increase over 1947 program
- Using brook trout: 4,257,000 fish (770,000 pounds) - 165% increase

The 1947 program stocked 1,617,000 legal-size trout (270,000 pounds). An additional 2,180,000 rainbows or 2,670,000 brook trout would have been needed to reach the 1.0 target.

**8. Closure Policies**

Post-stocking closures (6 weeks, 1 week, or none in 1947) did not prevent high initial harvest rates. The longest closure (6 weeks, April plant) actually preceded the highest first-week removal (71% of rainbows). Closures increased fishing pressure through necessary publicity and required substantial expenditures for posting, advertising, and law enforcement. The practice is not recommended.

**9. Angler Behavior and Fishing Pressure**

Fishing intensity proved highly responsive to stocking intensity and perceived fishing quality rather than fixed to "favorite" waters. Cumulative fishermen contacted per stream mile over 4 weeks:

*1948 Progressive Intensity Plants:*
- 1st plant (500/mile): 299 fishermen/mile
- 2nd plant (1,000/mile): 323 fishermen/mile
- 3rd plant (2,000/mile): 418 fishermen/mile
- 4th plant (4,000/mile): 690 fishermen/mile

This responsiveness allows management to influence fishing distribution through strategic stocking based on water suitability, extent, and regional fishing population rather than attempting to match pre-existing pressure on specific waters.

**10. Resident Trout Contribution and Limitations**

Resident trout alone cannot sustain quality fishing in heavily pressured waters. The study section yielded 96 legal-size resident trout per surface acre per season (approximately 16 pounds), supporting only 96 fisherman-days at 1.0 fish/hour. Actual pressure was 782 fisherman-days (1947) and 1,895 fisherman-days (1948). Supplemental stocking of legal-size trout is essential for streams near population centers.

**11. Unaccounted Fish**

Approximately 30% of stocked fish were unaccounted for in both species (not in creels, not in post-season census, not documented as migrants within study area). With few dead fish reported, these likely represent additional migration beyond study boundaries, though the final disposition remains uncertain.

**12. Tag Retention and Census Reliability**

Fin-clipping verification of the first 1948 plant revealed tag loss under 1%, validating the metal jaw-band methodology. Some resident fish remained in the same pool for over one year, documented through repeated census operations.
