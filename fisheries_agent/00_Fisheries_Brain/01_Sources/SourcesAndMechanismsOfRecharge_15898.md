# Sources and Mechanisms of Recharge for Ground Water in the West-Central Amargosa Desert, Nevada: A Geochemical Interpretation

#hydrogeology #groundwater-geochemistry #recharge #isotope-hydrology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Professional Paper (712-F), prepared with the [[U.S. Department of Energy]], uses geochemical, isotopic and hydraulic data to identify where ground water in the west-central [[Amargosa Desert]] came from and how it was recharged. The author concludes that most recharge was by overland flow of snowmelt in or near present-day stream channels, not by subsurface flow from highland recharge areas to the north. Carbon-14 ages range from about 7,000 to 20,000 yr B.P., and the absence of older water is attributed either to unfavorable climate or to flow that carried earlier recharge out of the study area. The work supports evaluation of radionuclide migration paths from the [[Nevada Test Site]].

The document is a hydrogeochemical research report. It does not address fish, invertebrates, plankton or other aquatic organisms, so the biological fields are marked N/A.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** Ground-water chemistry can define flow systems when combined with hydraulic data, but earlier attempts had limited success because the dissolution and precipitation mechanisms were poorly understood and samples were often not representative. The paper applies "chemically dynamic" concepts, rather than [[Thermodynamic Equilibrium]] models, to trace water sources and pathways. The study area is one possible migration path for radioactive wastes from weapons testing and stored wastes on the Nevada Test Site.

**Study area.** The surface-drainage area of the Amargosa Desert is about 6,700 km². Elevations range from about 670 to 2,100 m above mean sea level, with a few peaks near 2,400 m. Mean annual precipitation is 50 to 380 mm, and mean annual temperature is 6 to 16°C. Vegetation depends on slope, aspect and precipitation: steep south- and west-facing slopes are nearly bare, desert scrub covers gentle alluvial fans, and [[Juniper and Pinyon Woodland]] dominates the higher elevations.

**Geology.** Bedrock is mainly Tertiary rhyolitic volcanic ash falls and flows, plus Cambrian to Devonian carbonate, quartzite and argillite. Valley fill is derived from these rocks. [[Playa Deposits]] of salt, sand, silt and clay make up more than half of the valley fill in the mapped southeastern quadrant. Late Pleistocene erosion dissected these deposits to depths of at least 100 feet and was followed by gravel deposition from northern volcanic washes.

**Hydrology.** Water-level data from 1962 and 1974 showed changes of probably no more than 0.3 m across most of the area. A [[Confined Carbonate Aquifer]] controls the potentiometric surface east of the [[Gravity Fault]], while west of the fault, the valley-fill aquifer is unconfined. Hydraulic gradients are steeper where valley fill is finer, and gentler where sand and gravel dominate. Pumping in T. 16 S., R. 48 E. (1971–1974) is a plausible cause of very slight gradients there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is concerned with subsurface water chemistry and recharge, not with aquatic biota. No fish, invertebrate, plankton or aquatic-habitat surveys are reported, so no ichthyological or biological data are presented.

The environmental context that is described concerns:

- **Climate history.** The late Wisconsin was cooler and wetter, with greater winter precipitation. Its snowmelt is linked to recharge. Earlier climate is inferred from vegetation and packrat-midden studies.
- **Vegetation and CO₂.** Soil-zone CO₂ from root respiration affects the carbon isotope signal of recharging water. Dense highland vegetation produces more negative δ¹³C values.
- **Caliche.** Carbonate cementation of alluvium (caliche) is present in the region but is judged a minor control on ground-water chemistry.
- **Salinity sources.** Playa deposits and evaporation of non-recharge flood events add conservative salts (Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻) to ground water.

## Key Findings

**1. Recharge mechanism.** Ground water in the valley fill was recharged mainly by overland flow of snowmelt in or near present-day stream channels. The paper rejects a dominant contribution from subsurface flow out of northern highland recharge areas. Recharge depends on channel geometry and vertical permeability, and numerous small floods do not result in recharge.

**2. Water-quality evidence.** Na⁺ in most tuffaceous valley-fill water is inconsistent with derivation from northern bedrock aquifers. The author expects highland-recharged water to have Na⁺ greater than 70% of the three major cations. Ground water in the tuffaceous valley fill is chemically similar to shallow, perched water from the Nevada Test Site, which the author treats as an early stage of ground-water evolution. Water from the Nevada Test Site includes [[Montmorillonite]] and [[Clinoptilolite]] precipitation products.

**3. Carbon-14 ages.** Unadjusted ages from tuff-dominated water range from about 7,000 to 20,000 yr B.P. The youngest ages lie in or near present-day drainageways, which are interpreted as paleodrainages. Tuff-derived ground water has a median δ¹³C of −7.3‰ (mean −8.0‰), consistent with non-equilibrium recharge from runoff rather than equilibrium exchange.

**4. No pre-17,000 yr B.P. water.** No ground water older than about 17,000 yr B.P. was found. Two explanations are considered:
- *Flow-path removal:* at estimated velocities of 7 m/a (if recharged 17,000 yr B.P.) and 4 m/a (if recharged 27,000 yr B.P.), older water could have moved beyond the sampled region. Under the [[Spaulding Paleoclimate Model]], precipitation before about 20,000 yr B.P. was insufficient for snowmelt recharge, so velocities would be slower.
- *Climate:* the author favors the view that earlier snowfall was too small for significant recharge. The late Wisconsin then had precipitation about 40% above present, with about 70% more winter precipitation.

**5. Well depth.** The deepest wells do not yield older water. Wells farther from surface drainage yield older water, and the position of a well relative to drainage controls age more than depth. Wells J-12 and J-13 are a notable pair: J-13 is 710 m deeper and about 800 years older, but its additional depth adds little capacity, so the age difference is of borderline significance at the 95% confidence level.

**6. Upward leakage east of the Gravity Fault.** Water in valley fill near well 27 (16S/50E-7) is a mixture of local recharge and upward leakage from the [[Lower Carbonate Aquifer]]. Evidence includes:
- Carbonate-aquifer water temperatures of 27–31°C vs. about 21°C in distant valley-fill wells.
- A hydraulic-potential difference of about 6 m at site 27, compared with about 50 m in the township to the south.
- A 14C activity of 90% modern at well 16S/51E-23, indicating present-day recharge.
- Stable-isotope values (δD −105, δ¹⁸O −13.8 in valley fill vs. δD −103, δ¹⁸O −13.6 in carbonate) that differ by about analytical error.

**7. Ash Tree Spring anomaly.** Ash Tree Spring (site 20) has a ¹⁴C age of 15,900 yr B.P., and its water level is about 21 m higher than nearby wells. Its chemistry resembles tuff-derived water, but its isotope and hydraulic data do not. Two hypotheses are offered: (a) relict recharge from a higher land surface, with about 1.1 × 10⁹ m³ of dewatered aquifer; and (b) a separate sandstone-claystone conduit from the Resting Spring Range.

**8. Mixing and conservativeness.** Mixing calculations between the center of the tuffaceous trough (wells 9, 14, 15) and the carbonate/valley-fill end member (wells 27, 28, 30, 31) show good agreement between the eight-ion average and Cl⁻ alone for mixing percentages below 50%. Above 50%, a bias suggests precipitation or ion exchange, but the bias is too small to identify the precipitate.

**9. Concentration by evaporation.** Chloride and sulfate in precipitation are concentrated by factors of about 25 in tuffaceous valley fill, more than 200 along the upstream Amargosa River, and about 100 along the downstream reach. The paper concludes that more recharge-producing floods occur in Fortymile Canyon, which has a ~700 km² drainage, than in the Amargosa River, which has a ~2,600 km² drainage.

**10. Oasis Valley.** Oasis Valley water is chemically diverse. Some sites resemble the upstream Amargosa River valley fill, so underflow from Oasis Valley is a possibility. Because downstream chemistry can be explained only by surface-runoff recharge, the author considers surface runoff dominant along the entire Amargosa River reach.

**11. Carbon-isotope limits.** Carbonate dilution is judged small for tuff-derived water, so unadjusted ages are taken as true ages. Water from carbonate detritus can be up to about 5,600 years younger than its unadjusted age. Caliche is a minor factor because Ca²⁺ and HCO₃⁻ are not positively correlated, and median total inorganic carbon is about 2.6 mmol/L in tuff-derived water and 5.1 mmol/L in carbonate water.

**12. Conclusions.** Major recharge occurred at the end of the Pleistocene and in the early Holocene. The absence of water older than about 17,000 yr B.P. implies that no recharge occurred before then, and ground-water velocities are slower than 7 m/a and probably slower than 4 m/a. The author notes that slower, less permeable flow paths may still contain older water, but this cannot be verified until those paths are sampled separately.
