# Streamflow Requirements of Woundfin in the Virgin River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how spring streamflow affects reproduction and recruitment of the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*[[Plagopterus Argentissimus]]*, Cyprinidae) in the [[Virgin River]], a tributary of the [[Colorado River]]. Woundfin populations were sampled in spring, summer and autumn from 1977 to 1982. Poor recruitment coincided with low spring flows, and the adult population was curtailed after the year of lowest streamflow on record. Recruitment was most successful when mean spring flow fell between roughly 200 and 800 cfs. Irrigation diversions reduce flows in the lower river and were associated with poorer recruitment, and the authors conclude that diversions have a significant adverse effect on the species.

## Background & Description

The Virgin River is a small tributary of the Colorado River, which was impounded by [[Hoover Dam]] to form [[Lake Mead]] before 1941. The river harbors six native fishes: woundfin, [[Virgin Roundtail Chub]], Virgin spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*), speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and desert sucker (*Catostomus clarkii*). Woundfin and Virgin roundtail chub are listed as endangered. Thirteen introduced species have also been reported from the river.

Woundfin is a minnow about 90 mm in size. Cross (1978a) reported that adults occur mainly in runs over sandy bottoms. Greger and Deacon (1982) reported that woundfin select rocks 5–10 cm in diameter in currents for spawning at about 25 °C. Young reach 20–40 mm total length 30–60 days after hatching, and a total length of about 60 mm is reached in about five months at typical summer temperatures. Deacon (1979) had earlier linked mean spring flows below about 100 cfs with poor reproductive success. This paper examines that relationship in more detail.

The study area runs from [[La Verkin Spring]] to about 10 km below Riverside, Nevada. Several diversions alter flow along this reach:

- [[Hurricane Diversion]]: removes up to 50 cfs, varying with irrigation demand, plus a 35 cfs hydroelectric withdrawal that returns about 6 km downstream.
- [[Washington Fields Diversion]]: removes up to 90 cfs.
- [[Mesquite Diversion]]: withdrawal of about 60 cfs.

Five primary sampling stations were set up: two in canyon reaches near the [[Hurricane Gage]] and [[Littlefield Gage]], and one each below the Hurricane, Washington and Mesquite diversions. Data from other stations supplemented these.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Streamflow variability is a dominant feature of the Virgin River. It prevents the development of a macrophyte community, produces an unstable substrate and keeps turbidity high. The authors argue that this variability keeps habitats uniform and limits the fauna to species able to tolerate such conditions. Cross (1975) suggested that reduced flow variability could increase habitat diversity and fish species diversity. The present analysis indicates that flow variability influences recruitment and survival of woundfin and probably other native species.

The river has distinct reaches. Above the Washington Fields Diversion, the channel is deeply incised and narrow, with a steep gradient (5 m/km) and rock and gravel riffles. Below Washington Fields, the channel widens and sandy runs dominate. During low summer flows the upper end of the [[Virgin River Narrows]] is dry, and permanent flow resumes where subsurface flow surfaces near the lower end. Flows are almost always lower in the disturbed segments below diversions. Return flows, spring discharge and tributary inflows partly offset these losses. Flash floods are not uncommon. Early spring high-flow events coincide with an adult-dominated population.

Non-native and introduced fishes are encountered more often below diversions. A large permanent population of the [[Red Shiner]] has established below the Mesquite Diversion.

## Key Findings

**Sampling methods**
- Fish were collected by repetitive seining with 1.5 m × 1.8 m × 6 mm mesh nylon seines. Sampling areas were typically about 10 m long by 3 m wide and were seined until fewer than 10% of fish from the most successful haul remained.
- Woundfin were measured (total length), and the first approximately 100 captured at each area were measured. Sampled fish were returned alive to the stream.

**Flow and population patterns**
- In general, flows rise considerably in February or March, decline in June to a relatively consistent base flow of 60–120 cfs, remain low through October and increase slightly through January.
- Winter flows in 1977 were exceptionally low. Winter flows in 1981 were also considerably lower than in other study years, and 1982 flows were similar to 1981.
- Mean fall (September–November) woundfin size was related to mean spring (April–June) flow. Smaller mean size indicates successful recruitment, and larger size indicates poor recruitment.
- The smallest mean sizes occurred in 1978 and at selected locations in 1979 and 1980.

**Flash floods**
- A flash flood on 10 September 1980 raised the mean daily discharge at the Hurricane Gage sharply, with a peak discharge of 10,910 cfs. At Littlefield, mean daily discharge rose from 935 cfs on 9 September.
- A second event in August 1981 raised Littlefield mean daily discharge from 91 cfs on 10 August to a much higher level. It was less pronounced at the Hurricane Gage.
- These events may have affected results, especially the lower river in 1981 and the Hurricane reach in 1980. The authors note that late-summer timing probably limited their effect, since most young would have been large enough to survive floods. A decline in woundfin abundance between August and October 1980 may partly reflect the flood.

**Juvenile recruitment (Figures 6–7)**
- In undisturbed reaches, flows as low as 64 cfs were associated with excellent juvenile survival. Of 11 collections made at flows below 130 cfs, two had good juvenile populations. Of eight collections at flows of 290–820 cfs, seven had good populations and one had a moderate population.
- Flows above 1000 cfs were usually associated with poor juvenile populations.
- Below diversions, good juvenile recruitment occurred at flows as low as 64 cfs. Of 13 collections at flows below 129 cfs, four had good juvenile populations. Of eight collections at flows of 290–820 cfs, six had good juvenile populations.

**Thresholds for recruitment**
- Mean spring flow must exceed about 183–237 cfs (Littlefield and Hurricane gages, respectively) for a reasonable expectation of recruitment of a new year class below diversions.
- Flows above 400 cfs were usually associated with good recruitment in disturbed reaches.
- In undisturbed reaches, mean spring flow of 183–237 cfs was associated with moderate recruitment.
- Flows above 800 cfs were frequently associated with moderate recruitment. Flows of 400–800 cfs were associated with the best recruitment recorded in the five study years.
- Poorest recruitment occurred in 1977, when mean spring flows were below 100 cfs.
- Recruitment in 1981 was as poor in disturbed reaches as in 1977, indicating those flows were incompatible with good reproduction there. Undisturbed reaches in 1981 showed only moderate recruitment, suggesting such flows are near the minimum compatible with moderate recruitment.

**Conclusions**
- Spawning, fry development and recruitment are most successful when mean monthly streamflow in the Virgin River falls between about 200 and 800 cfs.
- The earlier estimate of spring flows below about 100 cfs as poor for reproduction is consistent with these data.
- [[Irrigation Diversion]]s have a significant adverse effect on woundfin recruitment, probably by reducing streamflow during spawning, fry development and recruitment.
- All river reaches could support good reproduction and survival, but disturbed areas require higher flows at the Hurricane and Littlefield gages to do so.
