# Instream Flows to Assist the Recovery of Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Report (No. 24, July 1994), authored by [[Jack A. Stanford]] of the Flathead Lake Biological Station, University of Montana, is an independent scientific review of the instream flow science used to justify flow recommendations for four [[Endangered Fishes]] of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The review was commissioned by the Instream Flow Subcommittee of the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]. It evaluates the ecological literature, the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM), and the flow recommendations for the Green, Yampa and 15-Mile Reach segments. The report concludes that the flow regimes should reestablish seasonality, that several critical uncertainties remain, and that management should follow an ecosystem approach with peer review and adaptive management.

## Background & Description

The four endemic big-river fishes are [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). All are protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Historically they were abundant in the potamon reaches of the basin, circa 1870s to 1950s. Current status is:

- Bonytail chub and razorback sucker are virtually extirpated from the Upper Colorado River Basin.
- Reproducing humpback chub populations are known only in five canyon segments: Black Rocks and Westwater canyons (Colorado), Gray and Desolation canyons (Green), and Yampa Canyon (Yampa).
- Squawfish remain comparatively abundant but are restricted by dams and diversions.
- Razorback sucker in the Green-Yampa system probably has not exceeded about 1,000 fish in the last two decades, and very little adult recruitment has occurred since the 1960s.

Declines are attributed mainly to habitat loss from reservoirs, reduced and regulated flows, predation by introduced species, and selenium from irrigation return flows. The [[Recovery Program]] emphasizes reregulation of flows and securing water rights, which is contentious because of [[Colorado River Compact]] entitlements.

The review used a [[Ecosystem Approach]] covering the entire Upper Colorado River Basin. The author states that his review was limited to documents and discussions with researchers, and that many key observations are published in peer-reviewed literature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime and habitat.** Regulation has truncated peak flows and raised and fluctuated baseflows, reducing seasonality. Low-velocity habitats such as backwaters, eddies, flooded bars and wetlands are essential for rearing, feeding and resting. Naturally functioning backwaters are warmer than the channel and support zooplankton, macrophyte-associated invertebrates and detritus-based food webs. High flows are occasionally needed to build habitat, but at a cost to reproduction.

**Channel geomorphology.** Squawfish spawn on clean cobble bars formed and cleaned by high flows. Low-velocity habitats are created by complex hydrologic processes linking sediment supply, channel morphology, bedrock and the magnitude and duration of peak flows. Regulation has reduced sediment transport, encouraged riparian encroachment into channels, and disconnected floodplain wetlands through revetments.

**Food webs.** Food webs are considered productive in the potamon zone, but the rhithron-potamon transition and downstream extension of coldwater trout fisheries alter the community. Hydropower-driven baseflow fluctuation can strand insects and fish and reduce backwater productivity.

**Water temperature.** Razorback sucker prefer 22-25 °C in shuttle box experiments, and optimal reproduction is cited at 20-22 °C. Tailwater releases from reservoirs such as Flaming Gorge and Glen Canyon produce cold water that alters downstream growth conditions.

**Nonnative species.** Predation and competition by introduced fishes are documented pressures, particularly on young squawfish and razorback sucker larvae. Information on nonnative ecology is limited.

## Key Findings

**Scope and methods.** The review synthesized the ecological and flow-methodology literature, flow recommendations from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and expert input from a panel including [[Edmund D. Andrews]], [[William J. Matthews]] and [[James V. Ward]]. Written drafts were circulated to the Instream Flow Subcommittee.

**Critique of methodology.**
- Morhardt (1986) reviewed 72 studies; only one (Binns and Eiserman 1979) produced a statistically valid general flow-productivity relationship.
- The IFIM was used on the big-river reaches where it is inappropriate. It maximized weighted usable area at very low flows that were exceeded most of the time in the historical record, which the author calls nonsense. The model cannot handle zero-flow habitats and underestimates low-velocity habitat.
- The IFIM is a physical habitat simulator, not a biomass predictor, and its suitability criteria and hydraulic predictors introduce bias.
- Recommendations for the Green River relied on professional judgment rather than the IFIM. The author argues that the Colorado River recommendations were logically sound, while the Green River peak-to-baseflow ratio of 12 (versus about 40 under predam flows) may do little ecological good.

**Key uncertainties identified.**
1. Whether flow seasonality and its correlates (temperature, physical habitat) are the actual limiting factors.
2. Whether water volume in the Colorado and Green rivers is sufficient for required flows, given compact entitlements and existing water rights.
3. The relationship between channel and floodplain morphology and flow area.
4. Whether food webs can reestablish in low-velocity habitats under hydropower operations.
5. Whether humpback chub can expand range given the downstream extension of coldwater tailwater environments.
6. Whether nonnative fish interactions will limit recovery regardless of flow provisions.

**Recommendations.**
- Implement interim flows that reestablish seasonality, with spring peaks approaching pre-regulation amplitude and frequency, and summer and winter baseflows with daily fluctuations limited to near pre-regulation conditions.
- Provide common understanding of water availability, including how interim flows relate to precipitation and legal flow in each subbasin.
- Improve the standardized monitoring program by adding a community ecology perspective, including data on all fishes and zoobenthos, quality control checks and annual reporting.
- Diversify the research program with process-oriented studies of geomorphology, food webs and floodplain wetlands, selected reaches in alluvial segments, and experimental manipulation of backwaters.
- Establish a peer review panel of three to six independent experts to evaluate annual research proposals.
- Implement adaptive management so that managers can switch to an alternative flow regime if monitoring shows failure.

**Overall conclusion.** The author is satisfied that existing ecological knowledge justifies endorsement of interim flows, while noting that the entitlement-based constraints may prevent the high-amplitude peak-to-baseflow regimes the fishes need. Success or failure will be judged by long-term population trends of the endangered fishes. The report also includes a proposed backwater field experiment and a list of cited literature.
