# Utilization of Plankton by Juvenile Gizzard Shad in a Shallow Prairie Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Joseph H. Kutkuhn]] (Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit, Iowa State College) examines the plankton diet of juvenile [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) in [[North Twin Lake]], Iowa, during irruptions in 1954 and 1955. It pairs analysis of fish foreguts with concurrent water sampling to test whether shad feed in proportion to plankton abundance, and whether heavy shad feeding could suppress nuisance algae or compete with game and panfish. Phytoplankton formed the bulk of young-of-the-year and yearling diets by volume, and shad ingested very small plankters. The author concludes that algal suppression and competition are plausible but unproven, and that shad may also buffer predation on sport fish.

## Background & Description

Gizzard shad had become widely regarded as a nuisance fish, yet little factual information existed on how shad populations affect coexisting game and panfish. The North Twin Lake irruptions prompted an effort to identify where competition between shad and desirable species might occur. The study was funded by the Iowa State Conservation Commission and Iowa State College, with cooperation from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

The paper focuses on young-of-the-year and yearling shad, which were judged to be the most abundant and therefore most influential age groups. Earlier work on plankton utilization by fish included [[Pacific Sardine]] (Lewis 1929; California Cooperative Sardine Research Program 1952), young [[Yellow Perch]] (Smyly 1952), and young [[Atlantic Shad]] (Maxfield 1953).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study lake.** North Twin Lake is highly productive and supports at least 22 fish species. Blue-green algae blooms, dominated by [[Microcystis Aeruginosa]], were a recurring nuisance; costly chemical control before 1954 gave only temporary relief. Blooms were minor in 1954 and absent in 1955, with no algae control attempted that year.

**Feeding niches.** Gizzard shad rely largely on phytoplankton and certain zooplankton. [[Yellow Bass]] and sport or panfish are insectivorous early in life and piscivorous as adults. Before these niches diverge, however, newly hatched shad and young sport fish consume the same plankton crustaceans, which creates potential for competition during spring and early summer.

**Sampling design.** Fish were seined in the littoral zone with a 30-foot seine. Collections began every four hours over 24-hour runs, roughly every two weeks from June to October 1955. Shad for gut analysis were preserved in formalin; game and panfish were refrigerated at 33–35°F and examined within 24 hours. Water samples (2 liters, taken 40–50 feet offshore) were concentrated by continuous-flow centrifugation and counted using Sedgwick-Rafter cells, with volumes derived from a [[Calculated Volume Method]].

**Analytical methods.** For shad, only the foregut (pharyngeal pockets, esophagus, gizzard) was examined, with duodenum substituted when foreguts were empty. Pharyngeal pocket contents were included despite debate over their digestive versus respiratory role (see [[Pharyngeal Pockets]]). Game and panfish guts were analyzed by [[Frequency of Occurrence Method]] because volume estimates were not feasible in fresh specimens.

## Key Findings

**Sample and diversity**
- Of approximately 358 phytoplankton and zooplankton species and varieties in North Twin Lake, 260 (73%) were found in shad digestive tracts.
- Analysis covered 57 shad (30 young-of-the-year, 27 yearlings) plus three two-year-olds sampled in late June. Ages were verified by scale reading.
- Young-of-the-year (24–82 mm) and yearlings (53–115 mm) differed in diet, so the author analyzed size groups separately.

**Diet composition (Table 1, percent of total volume)**
- Phytoplankton: 72.6% (age 0), 81.7% (yearling), 13.3% (two-year-olds).
- Cyanophyta were the largest volume contributor in yearlings (41.7%). Chlorophyta (green algae, mainly Chlorococcales) dominated qualitatively.
- Zooplankton averaged about one-quarter of volume in the young shad diet, and crustaceans were the chief component. Zooplankton made up 86.7% of volume in the two-year-olds, which were sampled during a period of high zooplankton abundance.
- Amorphous organic material was present in nearly all guts and may be incidental rather than food.

**Taxon-level food-supply relationships**
- Rotifers and crustaceans were about equally abundant in the plankton and in shad guts.
- Regression on 12 taxon comparisons (n = 8 age 0; n = 11 yearlings) showed significant correlation between environmental and gut abundance in 5 cases: Chlorophyta, Chrysophyta, Rotatoria, and Crustacea in young shad, and Rotatoria in yearlings.
- Green algae were ingested in proportion to abundance by young shad (r = 0.83) but not by yearlings, which showed irregular selectivity.
- Crustaceans (r = 0.76) and rotifers (r = 0.87) tracked abundance in young shad. Young shad took crustaceans far more than their environmental share would predict.
- Departure from one-to-one proportionality occurred in 4 of 12 sets. Cyanophyta, Chlorophyta, and Protozoa were under-utilized by yearlings.

**Selectivity at species level**
- Blue-green algae were not eaten in proportion to abundance. The most abundant plankter, [[Aphanocapsa Delicatissima]], formed only small portions of gut contents, while the scarcer [[Microcystis Aeruginosa]] and [[Anabaena]] were selected.
- Five yearling shad collected July 9 had mostly *Anabaena circinalis* in their foreguts, though this alga was scarce in the water samples from that area.
- Green algae (e.g., *Golenkinia radiata* on October 21) were occasionally highly selected.
- The rotifer *Anuraeopsis fissa* was often abundant in the plankton but rare in shad guts.
- Daphnids (*Daphnia longispina*, *D. pulex*) were primary foods for small shad and important for yearlings. Other crustacean foods included *Bosmina longirostris*, *Moina micrura*, and *Cyclops bicuspidatus*.

**Particle size**
- Smallest shad examined were 24 mm total length. These fish ate the largest zooplankton, such as *Daphnia longispina* and *Asplanchna priodonta*.
- Commonly ingested forms were as small as about 20 microns. Minute colonial or spined algae (e.g., *Golenkinia*, *Tetraedron caudatum* var. *longispinum*) may be taken by adhesion.
- Gill raker spacing in yearlings was as fine as 14 microns.

**Digestion and algal resistance**
- Rotifers were fully digested, with only empty loricae found. Diatoms were mostly digested, though some frustules were intact.
- Microcystis and Anabaena often appeared undisturbed. Bosmina, *Phacus longicauda*, and *Euglena oxyuris* were sometimes found intact in the intestine.
- Previous studies reported that blue-green and certain green algae resist fish and shellfish digestion (Velasquez 1939; Fish 1951; Flint 1956).

**Algae suppression hypothesis**
- Blooms were heavy in 1951–1952 and absent in 1955. The author suggests the decline may reflect heavy shad feeding, but states that this cannot be proven without data on algal production and consumption rates.
- Algal consumption must be weighed against resistance to digestion. The author notes a possible role for extracellular algal products that bind copper and reduce its toxicity (Fogg and Westlake 1955).

**Shad and sport fish interactions**
- In 1954 and 1955, young-of-the-year shad outnumbered young-of-the-year yellow bass by more than 10:1 in 1954 and about 3:2 in 1955.
- Shad made up about 90% of forage-fish volume eaten by predators in 1954, versus about 8% for young yellow bass.
- Yellow bass survival was comparatively poor in 1954 and 1955, when shad were abundant, though the strong 1953 year class had survived when shad were scarce. Causation is not established.
- Young shad appear to be a buffer species: predators may switch to shad when they are abundant and keep using them when availability falls. This may offset early competitive losses.

**Conclusions**
- Competition between shad and desirable species is plausible during early life, but the author states it cannot be demonstrated with current methods.
- Shad may suppress nuisance algae and buffer predation on game fish, but both effects remain circumstantial.
- The author concludes that adverse competitive effects during early development may be balanced by predator buffering and by the forage shad supply to game and panfish at later stages.
