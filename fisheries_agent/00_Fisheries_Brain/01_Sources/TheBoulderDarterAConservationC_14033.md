# The Boulder Darter: A Conservation Challenge

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Percidae #darters #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

The [[Boulder Darter]] (*Etheostoma wapiti*) is a small endangered member of the perch family (Percidae) listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1988. This bottom-dwelling darter survives in only approximately 63 miles (101 kilometers) of the lower [[Elk River]] system in southern Tennessee and northern Alabama, distributed discontinuously at six main channel sites and three tributary sites. The species has been extirpated from [[Shoal Creek]], Alabama. Laboratory observations in an artificial stream revealed critical reproductive requirements including highly specific nest cavity characteristics and constant water flow across eggs. Multiple anthropogenic stressors—impoundments ([[Tims Ford Reservoir]], [[Wheeler Reservoir]], [[Wilson Reservoir]]), thermal alteration, fluctuating water levels from power generation, pollution, and extensive siltation—have fragmented the population into small, isolated subpopulations. Without intervention, extinction is predicted within 10-20 years.

## Background & Description

The boulder darter is a small-sized member of the [[Percidae]] family. Darters comprise a diverse group of approximately 140 species of bottom-dwelling fishes in the United States that typically flourish in clean, flowing creeks and rivers. The boulder darter belongs to a reproductive guild known as "egg clusterers" because eggs are laid in clusters in spaces beneath rocks, with males remaining at nest sites to aggressively guard eggs against intruders.

The species' common name reflects its habitat preference: it lives among boulders in specific configurations. The fish is not found among boulders anywhere in the river bed; location and arrangement are critical. Required habitat features include: boulders in water 2-4 feet (0.6-1.2 meters) deep, moderate flow velocity (not swift riffles/rapids, not slow pools), and specific substrate arrangements. At two of six main channel sites, boulders are actually parts from old collapsed structures (a stone bridge and spillway dam).

The species shares attributes with the [[Maryland Darter]] (*Etheostoma sellare*), now believed extinct by some experts, suggesting a similar trajectory without intervention.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Historical Distribution and Decline

Historically, the boulder darter occurred in the [[Elk River]] system and [[Shoal Creek]] (a [[Tennessee River]] tributary in northern Alabama). The Shoal Creek population has been extirpated. Other populations probably existed in the Tennessee River but were extirpated by impoundments before detection.

### Anthropogenic Impacts

Multiple human-caused factors contributed to decline:

**Elk River impacts:**
- Impoundment of upper river by [[Tims Ford Reservoir]]
- Thermal alteration of tailwaters below [[Tims Ford Dam]] from warm to cold water
- Impoundment of lower Elk River by [[Wheeler Reservoir]]
- Fluctuating water levels (2-3 feet changes) from power generation at Tims Ford Reservoir
- Industrial, municipal, and agricultural pollution
- Extensive siltation from soil erosion

**Shoal Creek impacts:**
- Impoundment of lower creek by [[Wilson Reservoir]]
- Siltation from agricultural erosion
- Pollution from upstream municipalities in Alabama

### Current Habitat Conditions

Most of the Elk River between impounded reaches consists of long, heavily silted pools with little or no boulder substrate. The relatively few riffles and runs are predominantly floored with gravel and rubble substrates, not the boulder substrate required by the species. This rarity of preferred habitat, combined with severe and chronic degradation, makes the boulder darter's survival remarkable.

### Population Fragmentation Effects

The population has been reduced to small, isolated subpopulations—an alarmingly familiar pattern in southeastern rare fishes (some relatively common only 20-30 years ago). When a species becomes highly fragmented, it loses ability to respond to extremes of nature (extended severe weather, disease, dramatic food source fluctuations). Loss of biological adaptability arises directly from loss of genetic diversity, a consequence of losing population segments.

## Key Findings

### Laboratory Reproductive Biology Study

Ten darters were captured and transported to Gainesville, Florida, for study in an artificial stream (4 by 8 foot/1.2 by 2.4 m plexiglass aquarium with electric trolling motor generating current). The artificial stream mimicked important habitat aspects: flow, temperature, photoperiod, and substrate composition.

**Spawning observations (May-June 1991):**
- First observations of reproduction for this endangered species
- Spawning occurred in same habitat where fish normally live: boulders in flowing water with velocity approximately 1-2 feet (0.3-0.6 m) per second

### Specific Nest Cavity Requirements

Male boulder darters exhibit highly selective nest site preferences with five specific attributes:

1. **Boulder configuration:** Space must be between two boulders (not between boulder and gravel or boulder and rubble pieces), although boulder-bedrock spaces might be acceptable
2. **Wedge-shaped configuration:** Two boulders touching at relatively narrow angle, creating space into which female wedges eggs
3. **Current flow:** Current must flow across the site
4. **Horizontal orientation:** Cavity must be roughly horizontal (no vertical or nearly vertical spaces selected)
5. **Proper positioning:** Boulders must occur in correct depth and current ranges AND in certain configuration relative to current and to each other

### Critical Role of Water Flow

When one trolling motor failed during a weekend, all eggs in that nest died. Current is obviously important for:
- Oxygenating eggs
- Possibly keeping egg surface swept free of particulate debris and silt

This observation suggests persistent siltation of the Elk River must be very limiting to survival of egg and possibly larval life stages. Silt smothers fish eggs, and bottom-spawning species like the boulder darter are most vulnerable.

### Impact of Fluctuating Water Levels

Water level fluctuations from power generation (2-3 feet depth changes) significantly alter specific habitat features (depth and water velocity) of nest cavities males selected for spawning. Possible impacts include:

- Some nests may be stranded out of water when levels drop during decreased power generation
- If males spawn during low water, egg survival may be affected during high water levels
- Fluctuating water levels may significantly reduce reproductive success

### Conservation Prognosis

**Extinction timeline:** Without intervention, extinction may follow in the next 10-20 years.

**Proposed conservation measures:**
- State and Federal agencies working together to address ecosystem degradation
- Potential creation of new habitat
- Artificial propagation may play important role in recovery

**Certainty:** If nothing is done, the boulder darter will likely follow the [[Maryland Darter]] to extinction.
