# Impact of Selenium and Other Trace Elements on the Endangered Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Hamilton et al. 2002]] examined whether selenium and other trace elements in irrigation-influenced floodplain habitats near Grand Junction, Colorado, affect the reproduction and early life stages of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Four-year-old adults were held for roughly 286 days at three sites: a reference site at [[Horsethief Canyon State Wildlife Area]], [[Adobe Creek]], and [[North Pond]] within the [[Walter Walker State Wildlife Area]]. Adults were then induced to spawn, and eggs were tested for viability, hatch, survival and deformities.

Selenium accumulated in adult muscle and eggs at Adobe Creek and North Pond, reaching concentrations associated with reproductive failure. Egg viability was essentially zero for exposed young adults, which the authors attributed largely to a temperature shock during transfer rather than to selenium alone. Deformities were elevated in larvae from exposed and brood-stock adults. The authors conclude that selenium contamination of the Colorado River basin may be retarding recovery of the razorback sucker.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It is federally listed as endangered, and its decline has been linked to habitat alteration by main-stem dams and non-native species. Selenium contamination from irrigation drainage in the upper and lower basins was hypothesized to contribute to this decline ([[Hamilton 1999]]).

The Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program restores floodplain habitats to provide nursery areas for larvae and adults. The present study was a partial life-cycle exposure of adults stocked in flooded bottomland ponds, with the objective of determining whether selenium and other trace elements affected survival, growth, reproduction and offspring quality.

Fish were stocked on July 11, 1996 and August 27, 1996. Adults were from hatchery brood stock and from a previous study at the [[Wahweap Fish Hatchery]]. Exposed adults were held at Adobe Creek and North Pond, while Horsethief fish served as the reference group. Adults at Adobe Creek and North Pond were not fed artificial food and foraged naturally.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** North Pond had the highest conductivity (1240–4630 µmhos/cm), hardness (352–1460 mg/L CaCO₃) and salinity (1.0–5.0 g/L). Horsethief and Adobe Creek were closer to Colorado River values. Mean water selenium was 2.2 µg/L at Horsethief, 2.6 µg/L at Adobe Creek and 7.8 µg/L at North Pond, with a peak of 17.1 µg/L at North Pond on April 15, 1997. Groundwater from a nearby cobble aquifer contributed selenium at North Pond (82–152 µg/L at WW4a).

**Sediment and biota.** Sediment selenium was highest at North Pond. Zooplankton selenium ranged from 4.4–5.6 µg/g at the Horsethief east wetland, 15.6–20.0 µg/g at Adobe Creek and 31.9–48.1 µg/g at North Pond. Chironomid selenium was 1.4–2.0 times higher than zooplankton at the same station and time. Selenium in chironomids correlated with zooplankton (r = 0.94).

**Trophic pathway.** The authors concluded that adult selenium residues came mainly from food organisms, especially benthic invertebrates and detritus, and secondarily from water and sediment.

## Key Findings

- **Selenium accumulation in adults:** Muscle-plug selenium rose about 73% at Adobe Creek (9.6 to 16.6 µg/g by day 286) and about 80% at North Pond (12.2 to 25.5 µg/g). Fish had not reached equilibrium after 305 days.
- **Depuration:** After 86 days of depuration, fish lost 14% of muscle selenium at Adobe Creek and 21% at North Pond. The authors estimated a half-life greater than 100 days.
- **Growth:** Fish at Horsethief grew 4.7% in length and gained 26.8% in weight over the 286-day exposure. North Pond fish gained 25.6% in weight. Growth differences among sites were small.
- **Egg selenium:** Mean egg selenium was 40.1 µg/g at Adobe Creek and 54.7 µg/g at North Pond, compared with 6.0 µg/g at Horsethief and 6.9 µg/g for brood stock. Egg selenium correlated with muscle-plug selenium (r = 0.88, n = 20).
- **Egg viability:** Viability was essentially zero for exposed young adults. The authors attributed this to temperature shock (about 12°C at Horsethief versus 24°C at the holding facility), not to selenium alone.
- **Hatch and survival:** No significant differences in hatch or survival among egg sources. Hatch and survival were not significantly correlated with egg or muscle selenium. Egg diameter correlated with both survival (r = 0.49) and hatch (r = 0.64).
- **Deformities:** Larvae from Adobe Creek, North Pond and brood-stock adults had more deformities than typical background rates of 1–3%. Larvae from Horsethief showed the fewest. Cardiac and spinal deformities predominated, and some larvae showed microencephaly.
- **Comparison with thresholds:** Egg concentrations at Adobe Creek and North Pond exceeded the 10 µg/g reproductive-failure threshold (Lemly 1996) and Lemly's high-hazard level (>20 µg/g). Muscle concentrations exceeded the proposed 8 µg/g guideline. Wild Green River adults showed similar or higher muscle selenium.
- **Conclusion:** Selenium accumulated substantially in eggs and muscle at Adobe Creek and North Pond. Selenium contamination of the Colorado River basin may be retarding recovery of the razorback sucker.
