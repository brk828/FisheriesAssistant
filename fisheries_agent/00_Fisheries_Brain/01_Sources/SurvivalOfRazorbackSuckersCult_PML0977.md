# Razorback Sucker Pond Culture and Atlantic Salmon Alevin Feeding Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document compiles two hatchery-focused studies. The first, a 1985 pond culture trial at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], measured survival of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from swim-up fry through fingerlings in earthen ponds. Survival was high at every stage, and the authors attribute recent gains to improved fertilization and stocking practices. The second study, a laboratory experiment on [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) alevins, tested whether prey color and background color affect feeding efficiency. Both studies address the same practical goal: improving early-life survival of cultured fish.

## Background & Description

**Razorback sucker.** This is one of the larger [[Catostomidae]] in western North America, reported to reach total lengths near 1.3 ft (about 0.4 m) and weights of 11–13 lb. It is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], and the species has declined substantially over the past century. [[Endangered Species Listing]] status was assigned by the Fish and Wildlife Service in several states, including Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming, but the species was not federally listed at the time of the report. A propagation program began in 1977 at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Arizona, and moved to Dexter National Fish Hatchery in 1981. Reproductive biology and early life history had been studied, but little information existed on survival under culture conditions from fry to fingerling size.

**Atlantic salmon.** The second study uses alevins (8 weeks posthatch, 25 ± 2 mm total length) from the North American Salmon Research Center and the Florenceville Federal Fish Hatchery. Atlantic salmon are visual planktivores, so prey detection depends on contrast between prey and background. The study addresses high early mortality in hatchery-reared salmon, which has been linked to inadequate feeding responses at the onset of exogenous feeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Razorback sucker pond environment.** Ponds were 0.34–1.08 acres in area with mean depths of 3–4 ft. Water temperatures during culture ranged from 47 to 71°F for fry, 61 to 75°F for subfingerlings, and 67 to 76°F for fingerlings. Ponds were fertilized before and during rearing with organic and inorganic materials, including [[Alfalfa Pellets]] and [[Superphosphate]]. Fry were supplementally fed a commercial trout starter diet, which supplemented natural plankton in the ponds.

**Atlantic salmon laboratory environment.** Alevins were held in nine 77 × 32 × 37 cm holding tanks supplied with filtered running water from a nearby lake. Water was maintained at approximately 12°C under natural lighting. Fish were fed live [[Zooplankton]] at roughly 200 prey per liter, and feeding was stopped 18 hours before experiments. Background color was controlled by surrounding aquaria with red, blue, or aqua-green cloth, which also blocked outside movement that could startle the fish.

## Key Findings

### Razorback Sucker Culture (1985, Dexter National Fish Hatchery)

**Stocking and pond management**

- **Swim-up fry:** 276,200 fry were stocked in February and March 1985 into four earthen ponds (0.34–1.08 acres) at 101,000–109,000 fry per acre. Initial density ranged from 21,000 to 94,000 fry per pound.
- **Subfingerlings:** About 150,000 young subfingerlings were stocked into three ponds (0.86–0.98 acres) at 51,000–58,000 per acre. Initial size ranged from 724 to 549 per pound.
- **Fingerlings:** About 52,900 fingerlings were stocked into two ponds (0.98 acres) at 20,000–27,000 per acre, at 158 per pound.
- Ponds received alfalfa pellets (300 lb/acre) and superphosphate (50 lb/acre) before stocking. Afterward, fertilization schedules varied: two ponds received five weekly alfalfa applications (100 lb/acre), one pond received three weekly applications, and another received superphosphate (25 lb/acre).
- No significant handling mortality was observed after transfer.

**Survival and growth**

| Stage | Rearing period | Water temperature | Survival | Food conversion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Swim-up fry | 69–78 days | 47–71°F | 87.8–98.6% | Not calculated (supplemental feed) |
| Subfingerlings | 51–70 days | 61–75°F | 92.7–95.8% | 1.34–2.67 (highest in single-treatment pond) |
| Fingerlings | 50–55 days | 67–76°F | 96.2% (total) | 2.13 and 2.64 |

- The pond that received a single alfalfa treatment before stocking had the highest subfingerling survival (95.8%) but also the highest food conversion ratio (2.67) of the three ponds.
- Fingerling food conversion ratios were 2.13 and 2.64 across the two ponds.
- Average survival across all stages during 1985 was reported as 91.8%.

**Historical trends**

Survival from fry to fingerling improved after the program moved to Dexter in 1981. Reported values for 1981–1984 include 10.8%, 35.7%, and 23.6%; one value in the scan is illegible. The authors credit improved fertilization rates and stocking densities for better survival.

**Conservation implications**

The authors argue that hatchery propagation can help restore the species by supplying fish for reintroduction to former habitats and for supplementing native populations. Study of reproduction, early development, and habitat requirements may also improve the outcome of recovery efforts.

### Atlantic Salmon Alevin Feeding Study

- **Hypothesis:** Prey color and background color affect the feeding of Atlantic salmon alevins.
- **Approach:** Alevins were presented with different prey colors against red, blue, or aqua-green backgrounds to measure feeding responses. Feeding was withheld for 18 hours before trials.
- **Rationale:** Earlier work showed that salmonid alevins are visual predators whose feeding success depends on contrast. Improving feeding efficiency at first feeding could reduce the high early mortality common in hatchery-reared salmon.
- **Reported result (abstract):** Combinations of background and prey color affected feeding preference. Choosing optimal color combinations may increase feeding efficiency, growth rate, and survival of cultured Atlantic salmon.
- **Note:** The excerpt does not include the quantitative results, sample sizes per treatment, or statistical tests.

### Overall Significance

Both studies focus on reducing early-life losses in hatchery culture. The razorback sucker work shows that pond management strongly influences survival, while the salmon study suggests that simple sensory manipulations, such as prey and background color, may improve first-feeding success.
