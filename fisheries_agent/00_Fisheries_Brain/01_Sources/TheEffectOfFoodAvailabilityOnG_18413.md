# Effect of Food Availability on Growth and Mortality of Larval Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by Diana Papoulias of [[Arizona State University]] for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Office of Endangered Species, describes three laboratory experiments testing whether food availability controls mortality and growth in larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*). The work was motivated by the apparent failure of natural razorback populations to recruit to adulthood in [[Lake Mohave]]. Larvae starved from hatching died mainly between 20 and 30 days post-hatch. Larvae that began feeding after about 17 days showed high mortality, which the author interprets as the end of a window for recovery. The author concludes that food availability may explain the absence of larval razorback sucker longer than 10.6 mm in Lake Mohave.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The razorback sucker is a native fish of the Colorado River system. Lake Mohave larvae were collected at a mean total length (TL) of 10.6 mm, and their stomachs were mostly empty.

**Problem.** Starvation during the switch from endogenous yolk reserves to exogenous feeding is proposed as a major source of early mortality and reduced recruitment. Larvae must capture food of suitable size, quality and quantity during this transition. Earlier surveys in Lake Mohave found low zooplankton density at hatching time and no detected recruitment to adult populations despite extensive sampling ([[Minckley]], 1983; Minckley and Marsh, unpublished data).

**Experimental setup.**
- Indoor experiments at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico.
- One-gallon (3.9 L) glass jars arranged in raceways, held at a constant 18°C in a water bath, with continuous flow of about 100 mL/min.
- Wild Lake Mohave adults were induced with human chorionic gonadotropin on 11 February 1985. Fish were stripped and swim-up occurred on 26 February. Larvae of 10 mm TL were stocked at 10 per jar.
- Food was [[Artemia Nauplii]] (brine shrimp) fed three times a day at 8-hour intervals.
- Dead larvae were recovered, preserved and measured. Unrecovered fish were counted as mortalities.
- Data were compared using the [[Mann-Whitney U Test]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Critical period.** The [[Critical Period]] is the window between [[Yolk Sac Absorption]] and the point of [[Irreversible Starvation]], during which larvae must find adequate food or die (after [[Hjort]], 1926). Yolk sac absorption in razorback sucker occurs at about 7 days post-hatch (Minckley and Gustafson, 1982). In these experiments yolk was present at day 5, and at day 8 about two-thirds of larvae were feeding.

**Temperature.** Temperature affects incubation time, hatch size, yolk utilization and yolk depletion (Laurence, 1973; Houde, 1974). Lake Mohave is colder (11–15°C) during incubation and hatching than the 18°C used in the experiments. Marsh (1985) reported that razorback sucker hatched almost twice as slowly at 15°C as at 20°C, with no significant difference in total length at swim-up. Temperature effects on the critical period were not tested.

**Lake Mohave conditions.**
- Of larvae with food, about 70% had fewer than 10 food items (cladocerans, copepods or rotifers), with an average of 4 items.
- 67% of larval stomachs were empty (Langhorst and Marsh, 1986).
- Zooplankton density in the first four months of the year was below 5 organisms/L (Paulson, personal communication).
- Langhorst and Marsh (1986) reported zooplankton prey at an average of 1.5/L during the hatching period.
- Lake larvae appear to be transitioning from yolk to exogenous food, placing them within the critical period.

**Starvation physiology.** Fish use food energy directly for growth (Blaxter, 1969). Rapid growth reduces predation risk but increases dependence on reliable food because energy reserves are low (O'Connell and Raymond, 1970; Taylor and Freeburg, 1984). Starved fish can resorb tissue and lose length (Ivlev, 1961).

## Key Findings

**Experiment 1: Total starvation (unfed controls, six jars, 10 larvae each, 50 days)**
- 75% of larvae were dead by day 26 post-hatch.
- All larvae were dead by day 50.
- Most deaths occurred between days 20 and 30.
- Dead fish averaged 9.6 ± 0.8 mm TL.

**Experiment 2: Time to irreversible starvation (food started at 4-day intervals, 300 nauplii/L, 3 replicates)**

| Day food started | Mortality (%, mean ± SD) | Mean TL of survivors (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0 | 23.2 ± 2.3 |
| 9 | 10.0 ± 10 | 22.4 ± 2.1 |
| 13 | 10.0 ± 17.3 | 21.7 ± 1.9 |
| 17 | 26.7 ± 23.1 | 19.7 ± 2.0 |
| 21 | 56.7 ± 28.9 | 18.8 ± 2.9 |
| 25 | 86.7 ± 11.5 | 17.3 ± 1.2 |
| 29 | 93.3 ± 5.8 | 15.8 ± 0.9 |

- No mortality occurred when feeding began on day 5. Differences among days 5, 9, 13 and 17 were not significant (0.1 > p > 0.05).
- Larvae unfed for 25 and 29 days had the highest mortality (86.7% and 93.3%). Most of these deaths occurred between days 20 and 30.
- Dead larvae averaged 9.3 ± 0.7 mm TL.
- Survivors were largest (23.2 mm) when fed from day 5 and smallest (15.8 mm) when fed from day 29.
- The author places the point of irreversible starvation between 17 and 21 days post-hatch, after which larvae were too weakened to feed.

**Experiment 3: Effect of ration size (5 to 1,000 nauplii/L, 3 replicates each)**

| Nauplii/L | Mortality (%) | Mean TL of survivors (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 86.7 ± 5.8 | 14.9 ± 0.9 |
| 10 | 73.3 ± 5.8 | 13.6 ± 2.1 |
| 50 | 18.3 ± 11.5 | 16.1 ± 1.8 |
| 100 | 20 ± 18 | 19.4 ± 1.5 |
| 500 | 6.7 ± 5.8 | 25.0 ± 2.9 |
| 1,000 | 10 ± 18 | 23.7 ± 3.4 |

- Mortality was highest at 5 and 10 nauplii/L. It was below 20% at 50, 100, 500 and 1,000 nauplii/L, with no significant differences among these treatments.
- Mortalities leveled off when survivors had 10–20 nauplii per fish.
- Most deaths occurred between days 20 and 30, at a mean TL of 10.1 ± 1.2 mm.
- Survivors at 500 and 1,000 nauplii/L were larger than at lower ration levels.

**Main conclusions**
- Larvae can persist without food for about 10 days beyond yolk sac absorption at 18°C, with less than 30% mortality. Mortality rose sharply after that.
- The threshold prey density was 50 nauplii/L. Densities of 10 to 50 nauplii/L produced less than 50% mortality.
- A minimum of 10–20 nauplii per fish appears necessary for survival during the critical period.
- At 5 organisms/L, the author observed about 90% mortality, which is similar to the lake's zooplankton densities.
- The author concludes that food availability may explain the absence of larval razorback sucker larger than 10.6 mm in Lake Mohave. Temperature, prey patchiness and yolk efficiency at ambient lake temperatures were not fully assessed.
