# Rediscovery of Colorado Squawfish in Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This short research note reports the first recent capture of a [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in Wyoming. An adult was taken on 20 August 1990 in the [[Little Snake River]] near Baggs, Carbon County, after the species had been listed as extinct in the state since the 1960s. The authors use the record to argue that the Little Snake River may still support large-river fishes and should be considered for habitat improvement and as a potential recovery site for federally listed endangered fishes.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Basin]] holds 36 native fish taxa, 23 of them endemic. Four species of concern are noted: Colorado Squawfish, [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). All are federally listed or proposed for listing as endangered. Each was once widely distributed in large rivers, but their ranges and numbers declined sharply after human development began scarcely a century ago. Wyoming was excluded from the present ranges of these species in recent lists of imperiled fishes.

The captured specimen measured 56.5 cm standard length (SL) and 68.5 cm total length (TL). It appeared to be female, with undeveloped gonads, and it was measured, photographed, and released unharmed. It bore no tags, marks, or external parasites. Because Colorado Squawfish mature at about 50 cm TL and historically grew to 1.8 m, the fish was judged an adult.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Capture site (Little Snake River, Baggs):**
- The fish was taken in an experimental [[Gill Netting]] set about 5 m from shore, in a slow, turbid pool with a cobble and mud substrate. Current was visually imperceptible.
- Rooted aquatic vegetation was absent, but shoreline cover came from undercut banks, exposed roots, human refuse, and rip-rap of 0.25–1.0 m boulders.
- River flow near Baggs ranges from 0 to 283 ft³/sec, with an annual mean of 128 (USGS, 1985). The authors estimated about 0.75 m³/sec at an upstream riffle.

**Associated fauna:** Thirteen other fishes were captured at the Baggs site. Native species included [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*), plus a putative flannelmouth × bluehead hybrid. Nonnative species included [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), white sucker (*Catostomus commersonii*), and channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*). [[Seining]] in adjacent riffles and backwaters added [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), redside shiner (*Richardsonius balteatus*), and [[Mottled Sculpin]] (*Cottus bairdi*).

**Colder-water assemblage (Savery, 11 km east of Baggs):** This site had mountain whitefish (*Prosopium williamsoni*), trout (*Oncorhynchus* sp.), carp, speckled dace, redside shiner, creek chub (*Semotilus atromaculatus*), fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*), white sucker, and mottled sculpin. Speckled dace was the only native species shared with the Baggs sample.

**Historical context:** Colorado Squawfish once occurred in the [[Green River]] of Wyoming, from Green River city to the Utah state line. It was eliminated from that reach by chemical treatment during the closure of the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962. [[Baxter and Simon]] (1970) concluded that all three large-river species were extirpated in Wyoming, and the 1977 Colorado River Wildlife Council report listed them as extinct in the state.

**Connectivity:** Reproducing Colorado Squawfish occur in the [[Yampa River]], Colorado, of which the Little Snake is a tributary. No impassable structures were found in the mainstem Yampa or Little Snake. Anecdotal records of young fish exist from the Little Snake in Colorado, and [[Radio Telemetry]] located two tagged fish there in 1988. One was an 82.4 cm TL adult, caught and released about 10 km upstream from the Yampa. The Baggs site lies about 125 air km (perhaps 250 river km, given the river's meanders) from the Yampa, a distance Colorado Squawfish often travel in the Green River system. It is therefore uncertain whether the Baggs fish was a permanent or temporary resident.

## Key Findings

- **First recent Wyoming record:** A 68.5 cm TL adult Colorado Squawfish was captured in the Little Snake River on 20 August 1990, overturning the assumption that the species was extinct in the state.
- **Habitat is still suitable:** Suitable habitat for adult big-river fishes remains in the Little Snake River of Wyoming.
- **Other native species persist:** Roundtail chub, flannelmouth sucker, and bluehead sucker were also collected, alongside nonnative species.
- **Humpback chub recently found:** Humpback chub were recently discovered in the lower Little Snake River in Colorado (Wick et al., in press). Razorback sucker and possibly bonytail occur in the Yampa River and may extend upstream.
- **Status of "extinct" fishes unresolved:** Rigorous, long-term sampling of the Little Snake could lead to a reassessment of other species listed as extinct in Wyoming.
- **Management recommendation:** The Little Snake River may be amenable to habitat improvement, such as augmenting depleted flows, and should be considered among potential recovery sites for endangered fishes.
- **Permanence of the record uncertain:** Whether the Baggs fish was a permanent or temporary resident of the Little Snake remains unknown.
