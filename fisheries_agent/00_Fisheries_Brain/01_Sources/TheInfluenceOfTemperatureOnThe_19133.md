# Influence of Temperature on the Survival of Several Fish Species in Low Dissolved Oxygen

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

Downing and Merkens (1957) measured how long eight freshwater fish species survived under low dissolved oxygen (DO) at several water temperatures. Tests ran from under a day to one week in constantly renewed water, using native British species plus two introduced species, [[Mirror Carp]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Survival fell as oxygen tension fell, and warming reduced tolerance in most species. [[Tench]] were the most tolerant species and trout the most susceptible overall. The authors argue that brief oxygen shortages may be less damaging to fisheries than often assumed.

## Background & Description

Polluting effluents such as domestic sewage are often non-toxic to fish, yet bacterial oxidation of their organic load can lower river dissolved oxygen enough to asphyxiate fish. Earlier studies used sealed respirometers, constant-pressure chambers, or flowing-water systems. Most prior data came from short tests on species not found in the British Isles. This study aimed to supply longer-term data (up to one week) in a constantly renewed environment for native and introduced species.

Species tested:
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*)
- [[Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*)
- [[Roach]] (*Rutilus rutilus*)
- [[Mirror Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*, domestic form)
- [[Tench]] (*Tinca tinca*)
- [[Dace]] (*Leuciscus leuciscus*)
- [[Chub]] (*Squalius cephalus*)
- [[Bleak]] (*Alburnus alburnus*)

Methods:
- Six 40 L Perspex aquaria supplied with unchlorinated borehole water; DO and temperature controlled by an apparatus described by Merkens (1957).
- Test temperatures: 10, 16 and 20 °C. Total oxygen range: 0.8 to 450 % air saturation.
- Fish were acclimatized at least 5 days, fed during acclimatization but not during tests, and sorted at random into batches of nine or ten.
- Death was defined as loss of equilibrium with no respiratory movement for 15 seconds.
- Tests ran over 18 months as fish became available. Chub, dace, bleak and tench could not be tested at all temperatures.
- Mean fish lengths ranged from 7.5 ± 0.6 cm (tench) to 13.0 ± 1.1 cm (chub).
- Dissolved oxygen was measured by [[Winkler Method]]; pH was not controlled (8.1 to 8.5); free CO2 was below 4 p.p.m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study links an industrial and sewage problem (oxygen depletion from bacterial breakdown of effluent) to fish physiology. Oxygen solubility values were derived from [[Oxygen Solubility In Water]] data (Truesdale, Downing & Lowden, 1955). Temperature matters because warmer water both lowers oxygen solubility and raises fish metabolic demand. The authors cite earlier work showing that rising temperature shortens survival in lethal oxygen deficiency ([[Moore 1942]]; Graham, 1949). Results also draw on [[Shepard 1955]], who tested speckled trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) for up to 34 days.

## Key Findings

- **Oxygen dependence:** Survival time fell with declining oxygen tension at every temperature tested.
- **Temperature effect:** Warming from 10 to 20 °C reduced oxygen tolerance in all species except rainbow trout. For trout, the difference between 16 and 20 °C was not significant at the 5 % level, but the drop from 10 to 16 °C was considerable.
- **Tolerance ranking:** Tench were the most resistant. Trout were generally the most susceptible.
- **Mirror carp:** At 10 °C carp resembled roach. At 16 °C they survived about a day where roach, perch and dace survived only hours. At 20 °C they needed higher oxygen than trout to survive a week, yet tolerated lower tensions than trout over shorter periods.
- **Rapid lethal tests:** Median survival in rapidly lethal oxygen levels declined significantly with temperature in the coarse fish. Trout at the two highest temperatures did not differ significantly from each other, but both were significantly shorter-lived than at the lowest temperature.
- **Examples of tolerance:** At 10 °C, rainbow trout survived about 34 hours at 15.3 % air saturation. Tench survived one week at 3.1 % air saturation.
- **Replication check:** Twelve 1952 tests with fingerling rainbow trout at 16 °C (pH 7.8 to 8.8) gave no significant difference from the main experiment.
- **Management implication:** Oxygen levels tolerated for short periods are well below values usually considered necessary for fisheries. The authors suggest that temporary oxygen shortages may be less destructive to fisheries than previously assumed, though fish cannot survive long periods under such conditions.
