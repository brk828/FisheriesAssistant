# Preliminary Investigation of Induced Spawning of Native Southwestern Minnows: Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #rhinichthys-osculus

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[U.S. Bureau Of Reclamation]] under [[Federal Grant 1425-97-FG-00011]], describes a pilot study by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] on captive propagation of [[Speckled Dace]] (*[[Rhinichthys Osculus]]*). The goal was to gain experience with small crevice-spawning minnows and to test whether hatchery production of native Southwestern minnows is feasible for future recovery efforts. Wild dace were held at [[Bubbling Ponds Laboratory]] and manipulated through water temperature, photoperiod, water velocity, and [[Carp Pituitary Hormone]] injections. Dace spawned naturally in the laboratory and in raceways, and manual stripping with [[Dry Fertilization]] produced larvae that were reared to juveniles. Manual stripping was the most efficient way to obtain large numbers of gametes. Natural spawning was less productive and suffered from predation and fungal losses.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Speckled dace, a small crevice-spawning minnow of the Western United States. Prior captive work on this species was limited to [[Kaya 1991]], which described spawning of fish from the upper Green River drainage in Wyoming.
- **Rationale:** Spawning techniques differ among species because of differences in reproductive behavior, seasonal maturity, egg type, and egg-laying behavior. Captive propagation may be the only survival option for some nongame fishes (e.g., [[Fountain Darter]] in [[Brandt Et Al 1993]]).
- **Hormone use:** Hormone injections induce spawning in several minnow species but require skill and can be expensive. The authors considered them especially useful for fractional spawners, where they can improve the efficiency of stripping and fertilization.
- **Conservation context:** The work was intended to support future captive breeding of threatened and endangered minnows, including [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). The authors describe such techniques as an essential step toward recovery.
- **Study source:** Wild dace (n = 240) were collected from Rudd Creek within the Arizona Game and Fish Department's wildlife refuge near Springerville on May 13, 1997.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Holding facilities and conditions**
- Two indoor aquaria (570 L and 455 L) with undergravel filtration, held at 20–23 °C, supplied by an 18.5 °C artesian spring.
- An outdoor flow-through tank (1880 L) from January 1998, exposed to natural photoperiod and cooler water.
- Two 37 L experimental aquaria, used for temperature, hormone, and photoperiod trials (10, 15, 20, 21, 23, and 25 °C).
- Two linear raceways (0.5 × 5 m) with gravel substrate and flowing water (18.5 °C) for velocity trials.
- Spawning substrate: broken clay pot fragments and gravel.

**Photoperiod**
- Indoor tanks were set to approximate a June photoperiod (sunrise 05:15, sunset 19:30, 14.25 h daylight), controlled by an automatic timer.

**Health**
- Fish were given prophylactic [[Formalin Treatment]] (150 ppm, three treatments two days apart, one hour each). No disease was detected and no mortality resulted from the treatments.
- Fungal infection killed eggs in several trials, and the authors suggest it is a major source of loss in hatching jars.

**Behavioral and seasonal responses**
- Fish held at 10 and 15 °C were very inactive and spent most of their time hiding under clay pot fragments.
- Fish held at 10 °C showed no gamete production, which suggests that reproductive development was halted at a constant 10 °C.

## Key Findings

**1. No spawning in the first year; environmental manipulation was required**
- No breeding was observed between May and December 1997, and no gametes could be expressed from adults.

**2. Laboratory dace matured earlier than outdoor dace**
- Females in the laboratory (June photoperiod, 25 °C) expressed eggs on March 5, more than one month before outdoor females.
- Laboratory males expressed sperm nearly two months earlier than outdoor males.
- Outdoor females did not show visible secondary sexual characters until the end of March, and outdoor males first showed darker fins in late February. The first ripe outdoor male was detected on April 29.

**3. Hormone injection and dry stripping**
- Fish injected with carp pituitary hormone (100 mg/kg) on April 7 all expressed gametes the next morning.
- Eggs from 25 °C fish were stripped onto nylon mesh and fertilized, but they were highly adhesive and few separated into the hatching jar. None hatched, although a few eyed eggs were seen.
- Eggs from 20 °C fish were stripped onto a glass plate, fertilized, gently stirred with a syringe plunger for about one minute, and washed into an incubation aquarium. This method was more successful.

**4. Hatching and rearing**
- Eggs from the 20 °C tank hatched by April 14. By April 19 all surviving larvae (about 200) had completed swim-up.
- Feeding began April 20 with Biokyowa B-250 fish diet and Artemia brine shrimp. Developing dace were switched to a fish flake diet at the end of May.
- No mortality of swim-up fry, larvae, or juveniles was recorded. A length-at-age table was provided for lab-raised fish, but several values are illegible in the scan.

**5. Natural spawning in the laboratory**
- Natural spawning was first recorded on April 29 in tanks at 20 °C and 21 °C, where eggs were deposited under clay pot fragments.
- On May 6, five swim-up larvae were found in the 25 °C tank, from a fractional spawn the previous week.

**6. Experimental results**

| Experiment | Treatment | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Moved from 15 °C to 23 °C (May 15) | Spawned May 16–17; 15 °C control did not spawn; treatment eggs lost to fungus |
| 2 | Carp pituitary hormone at 23 °C (May 15) | Bright orange spawning colors, but no natural spawning in treatment or control |
| 3 | Raceway with 1–5 cm/s flow vs. near-zero flow (May 15) | Spawned May 16–17 in flowing raceway (eggs under clay pot, estimated 3 cm/s); none in control; first swim-up larvae May 30 (about 20) |
| 4 | Natural vs. extended (+2 h) photoperiod at 23 °C (July 19) | Both tanks spawned July 20; natural photoperiod deposited more eggs (July 23) |

**7. Methods comparison**
- Manual stripping with dry fertilization produced the most viable gametes from this fractional spawner.
- Natural spawning occurred under many conditions, but eggs were fewer and were lost to predation by adult dace.
- The authors suggest that a more complex environment with adequate spawning substrate would reduce predation and could raise natural egg production, avoiding the cost of hormones when strict parentage control is not needed.

**8. Recommendations and conclusions**
- Treatment effects will likely differ across minnow species, and techniques must be developed for each species through trial and error.
- Egg adhesiveness is a key obstacle. The authors recommend testing [[Bentonite]] during egg hardening to reduce adhesion, which they expect would improve hatching success.
- The study provides a foundation for future reproductive work on threatened and endangered southwestern minnows, including [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] work by [[Platania 1995]] and [[Platania And Altenbach 1996]].
