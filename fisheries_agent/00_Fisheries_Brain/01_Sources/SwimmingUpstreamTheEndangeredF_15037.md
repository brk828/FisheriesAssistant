# Swimming Upstream: The Endangered Fish of the Colorado River

#ichthyology #conservation #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This public education brochure describes four endangered native fish species of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). These species evolved in isolation over thousands of years to survive the river's harsh, fluctuating environment but have not adapted well to 20th-century alterations. The document serves as an angler identification guide and outlines proper catch-and-release procedures to prevent accidental harm to these federally and state-protected species.

## Background & Description

**[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): The rarest of the endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes, close to extinction in the wild. Maximum length approximately 18 inches. Features large fins and streamlined body that tapers to pencil-like thinness in front of the tail. Appears morphologically intermediate between [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]] in adaptations to torrential flows. Historically found throughout the river system in main channels and lower reaches of larger tributaries. No reproducing populations currently known; only a few individuals reported in the last 20 years. Listed as "endangered" under federal law and in Colorado, "protected" in Utah.

**[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): Not known to science until 1946. Usually 12-16 inches long. Distinctive features include prominent, smoothly rounded hump behind head; small eyes; long snout overhanging lower jaw. These features believed to aid swimming through turbulent canyon waters. Favors deepwater canyon areas. Largest known populations exist in [[Little Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]] and at [[Black Rocks]] and [[Westwater Canyons]] on upper mainstem Colorado. Also found in smaller numbers in [[Yampa River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] on Utah's [[Green River]], and [[Cataract Canyon]] on the Colorado River. Listed as "endangered" federally and in Colorado, "protected" in Utah.

**[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Top predator of the Colorado River for millions of years. Olive-green and gold back with silvery belly. Historically reached weights of 50-80 pounds, lengths of 6 feet, and possibly ages of 70+ years. Early settlers called it "white salmon" or "Colorado salmon" due to large size and long spawning runs. Historical menu from 1889 Christmas feast at [[Lee's Ferry]] lists "Colorado River Salmon." Once abundant enough for commercial harvest throughout river system. Wild populations now limited to [[Upper Colorado Basin]], especially [[Green River]], [[White River]], and [[Yampa River]]. Distinguishable from [[Roundtail Chub]] by eye position: squawfish snout is longer with thick folded lips extending to rear of eye, while roundtail's mouth extends only to front of eye. Listed as "endangered" under federal Endangered Species Act and in Colorado, "protected" in Utah.

**[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): One of largest suckers in North America, may weigh up to 12 pounds. Distinguished by abrupt, bony, keel-edged hump rising on back just behind head. Largest population lives in [[Lake Mojave]], but appears to consist entirely of fish 30-40 years old; though they spawn, young do not survive to adulthood. In unimpounded waters, limited to Upper Basin rivers, especially [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], and mainstem Colorado. Largest population estimated at approximately 1,000 adults near Jensen, Utah. No documented proof of successfully reproducing fish in Upper Basin. Listed as "protected" in Utah and "endangered" in Colorado; proposed for federal protection as "endangered species."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] system is one of the world's most colorful river systems, dropping more than two miles in elevation over a 1,700-mile journey from headwaters in Wyoming and Colorado to the [[Gulf of California]]. Before major dams, the river delivered more than 100,000 acre-feet of sediment annually to the Gulf. Historic flows at Yuma, Arizona ranged from a few thousand to almost 400,000 cubic feet per second, creating harsh and fluctuating conditions.

This vast period of isolation by high mountains produced native fish species found nowhere else in the world. The harsh environment required tough and adaptable creatures to survive the wide seasonal and annual flow fluctuations.

Reasons for species decline include alteration and reduction of habitat and competition from non-native fish species. The challenge is to balance development of the Upper Basin (above [[Lee's Ferry]], Arizona) with needs of native species.

## Key Findings

**Conservation Status and Legal Protection:**
- All four species are protected under federal and/or state endangered species laws
- Fines up to $20,000 and jail sentences possible for willfully destroying these fish
- Recovery efforts underway throughout Colorado system, with special focus on Upper Basin

**Proper Catch-and-Release Protocol for Anglers:**
1. If possible, leave fish in water while removing hook
2. Remove hook gently; avoid squeezing fish or inserting fingers in gills
3. If hook deeply embedded, cut line instead of pulling out hook
4. Release fish in quiet water only after equilibrium restored
5. If necessary, gently hold fish facing upstream and move slowly back and forth in water

**Tag Reporting:**
Tagged fish encounters should be reported to Colorado Division of Wildlife (NW Region, 711 Independent Ave., Grand Junction, CO 81501, 303/248-7175) or Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (Non-game Section, 1596 W. North Temple, Salt Lake City, UT 84116, 801/533-9333) with tag number, color, time and place of encounter.

**Recovery Implementation Program:**
Represents milestone cooperation among federal and state agencies in Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, plus water development and environmental interests. Program includes coordinated efforts to improve habitat, provide critical streamflows, reduce conflicts with non-native species, and stock endangered fish to augment wild populations. Goal is to balance protection and recovery with continued water development in Upper Basin.
