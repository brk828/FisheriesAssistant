# Federal Hatchery Use for Threatened and Endangered Aquatic Species: Issues and Alternatives

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-conservation

## Executive Summary

This four-page fax from the [[Intermountain Research Station]] (USDA Forest Service, Boise, Idaho) transmits a draft issues-and-alternatives analysis on using federal [[Hatchery Management|hatcheries]] to prevent extinction of threatened and endangered aquatic species. The analysis was prepared to meet a request from a colleague named Fred and was to be incorporated into copies for a meeting. The authors surveyed resource specialists nationwide. They report widespread support for federal hatcheries but identify eight issues and several alternatives for each. The document also argues that captive propagation is costly, risky, and a weak substitute for [[Habitat Protection]], and it concludes that agencies should prioritize managing natural systems.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) are responsible for doing everything reasonable to halt the extinction of the nation's flora and fauna. Hatcheries and other protective facilities are among the available tools.
- **Limitations of tools:** The authors state that hatchery use is "not inexpensive nor risk free."
- **Method:** Resource specialists from around the nation were consulted about their concerns regarding hatchery use for species rescue.
- **Governing framework:** The document refers to the [[Endangered Species Act]] and to the development of [[Ecosystem Management]] strategies.
- **Transmittal details:** The cover sheet lists the sender as the Intermountain Research Station, with a note that the author would "incorporate your changes" before making copies for the meeting. The header date is partly illegible; the printed year appears to be 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not name any particular species, watershed, or site. Its ecological content is general:

- **Captive propagation as a stopgap:** Hatcheries are described as a rescue tool for imperiled aquatic organisms. The authors warn that propagation can create "an illusion of recovery progress," which may encourage further development and habitat loss.
- **Genetic divergence:** Longer periods in culture are said to make a species "more likely to diverge from its founder stock," which is a concern for [[Genetic Management]] of hatchery populations.
- **Escapement and endemics:** Hatchery fish that escape into surrounding waters could threaten endemic organisms. The authors recommend stocking only where it does not jeopardize endemic species.
- **Disease:** Hatchery operations can transmit parasites and disease to wild populations (see [[Fish Disease Transmission]]).
- **Sport fish stocking:** Hatchery production of sport fishes is described as politically charged, which may make changes to stocking practices difficult to pursue.

## Key Findings

The document identifies eight primary issues. For each, it offers alternatives, summarized below. Alternatives are framed as options for discussion rather than final decisions.

**Issue 1: Number and location of facilities**
- **A:** Reclaim existing hatchery space and facilities as needed to comply with the law.
- **B:** Contract with private, state, or university aquaculturists to maintain each affected species until its return to natural habitat is reasonable.
- **C:** Develop support and funding for new regional aquaculture centers dedicated to threatened and endangered aquatic species.
- **D:** Some combination of A, B, and C.

**Issue 2: Information and technology needs**
- **A:** Maintain and develop technology centers to the extent required by law.
- **B:** Contract with universities and private or state [[Research and Development Laboratories]] as needs arise.
- **C:** Some combination of A and B.

**Issue 3: Status of species before propagation**
- Propagation decisions could be tied to a probability of extinction within a set time frame. The example given is no artificial propagation unless there is a 60% probability of extinction within 3 years, with alternatives such as 90% or 40% thresholds.

**Issue 4: Maximum time under propagation**
- Limits could be set by generation time. If a species cannot be restored to a self-sustaining wild population within 2–3 generations, it would be designated extinct for conservation purposes, with its genetic material preserved in a zoo population. Longer limits are possible, but the authors note that longer culture increases divergence from founder stock.

**Issue 5: Genetic management guidance**
- Implement strict guidelines for [[Broodstock Management]] of threatened and endangered species.

**Issue 6: Escapement into surrounding habitats**
- Develop and manage facilities from which fish cannot escape.
- Do not permit stocking that would jeopardize any endemic species.

**Issue 7: Political sensitivity of sport fish hatcheries**
- The authors warn that captive propagation and traditional hatcheries are often the first solution considered by politicians and administrators for complex, long-standing fisheries and habitat problems.
- Propagation may become institutionalized and expensive, drawing funding away from other recovery elements, particularly [[Habitat Protection]].
- Recommendation: stop any stocking that increases the probability of extinction for endemic organisms.

**Issue 8: Transmitted parasites and disease**
- The recommendation is the same as for Issue 7: stop any stocking that increases the probability of extinction for endemic organisms. The text does not add distinct disease-management measures.

**Summary conclusions**
- The number of species needing protection is growing quickly, so a reactive approach is "unreasonable."
- Artificial propagation may conflict with the intent of the Endangered Species Act and with ecosystem management, but hatcheries will likely continue to be used for imperiled species.
- Costs of propagation and of developing culture methods are rising and are unlikely to gain public support.
- The authors conclude that the most viable path is aggressive management of natural systems to protect all components. This would reduce the number of species that require extreme measures such as artificial environments.
