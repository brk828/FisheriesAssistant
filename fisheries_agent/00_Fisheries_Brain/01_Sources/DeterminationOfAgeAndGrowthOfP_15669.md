# Determination of Age and Growth of Populations of the White Sucker (*Catostomus commersoni*) Exhibiting a Wide Range in Size at Maturity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1973 study by Richard J. Beamish, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, evaluates methods for ageing [[White Sucker]] populations from Ontario lakes. It concludes that [[Scale Annuli]] should not be used to age mature fish, because scale-based ages become unreliable after [[Sexual Maturity]] (about age 4). Sections of [[Pectoral Fin Rays]] are recommended as the more accurate method for most lake populations, provided the position of the first annulus is correctly identified. The paper also questions whether a size-based subspecies, the dwarf white sucker (*C. c. utawana*), is valid, given the wide variation in size at maturity across the range.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*), a North American freshwater catostomid.
- **Prior work:** An earlier study (Beamish and Harvey 1969) showed that fin-ray annuli form annually and that scale ages could underestimate the true age of older fish by up to 5 years. Other authors (Dence 1948; Ovchynnyk 1965; Geen et al. 1966; Coble 1967) also doubted scale-based ageing.
- **Implication:** Earlier growth curves based on scale ages (Stewart 1926; Spoor 1938; Raney and Webster 1942, among others) were considered unreliable.
- **Subspecies question:** Dence (1948) recognised a slow-growing form as *Catostomus commersoni utawana* (the dwarf sucker). Beamish argues that size differences alone do not justify this designation.

### Materials and Methods

- Fish were sampled extensively from [[George Lake]] and [[Lumsden Lake]], with less detailed sampling at seven other Ontario lakes (Table 1 lists eight sites with coordinates, including [[Lake Simcoe]], [[South Bay]], [[South Pine Lake]] and [[Bridgland Lake]]).
- Most fish were taken with gillnets of 19–115 mm stretched mesh; small-mesh trapnets were used for smaller individuals.
- For each fish, sex, maturity and fork length were recorded, along with a scale sample and at least the first three pectoral fin rays.
- Fin sections (about 0.5 mm thick) were cut with a jeweller's saw after epoxy embedding, mounted in Permount and examined by compound microscope. Annuli appeared as optically less dense zones.
- Scales were read with a scale projector using Spoor's (1938) criteria: crowding and crossing-over of circuli in the posterior quadrant.
- In George Lake, 800 white suckers were tagged with numbered vinyl tubing (White and Beamish 1972). Laboratory-reared fish (held at 16 ± 2 °C, 13 h photoperiod, ad libitum liver diet) were compared with wild fish to test annulus formation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Growth variation:** George Lake suckers were fast-growing, approaching the largest sizes recorded in the literature. Lumsden Lake suckers were slow-growing and resembled the dwarf form.
- **Oxygen and acidity:** South Pine Lake is shallow and suffers severe winter oxygen depletion, which may explain the absence of older fish there. A weight decline in George Lake in 1972 may reflect increasing acidity (Beamish and Harvey 1972).
- **Lamprey predation:** Coble (1967) attributed the decline of larger fish in South Bay to [[Sea Lamprey]] predation.
- **Lake–stream structure:** In Lake Simcoe, a slower-growing population spawns in the Pefferlaw River. At Wadsworth Lake some suckers stay in the lake after spawning runs into the outlet creek. At Boulter Lake, fish move between the main lake and Little Boulter Lake.
- **Method limits:** Neither fin sections nor scales were reliable for suckers in small streams and rivers, which form many clear zones.

## Key Findings

1. **Scales are unreliable for mature fish.** Scale-based ageing should be avoided after maturity because crowding and cutting-over of circuli disappear in older fish, which leads to underestimates of age and growth.
2. **Fin rays are more accurate.** Pectoral fin-ray sections give reliable ages for most lake populations once the first annulus is identified. Fins and scales should be sampled together, as scales are useful for immature fish.
3. **False annulus.** In fast-growing fish, a false annulus forms about 6–8 months after hatching, and the first true annulus is the second clear mark. Earlier published ages were overestimated by 1 year for this reason.
4. **Annulus timing.** The true first fin-ray annulus appears by late May to early June, while the scale annulus forms in late June to early July after 20–40 circuli.
5. **Annual formation confirmed.** Of 41 tagged fish of known age released in 1969, 3 were recovered in May 1972. All three showed the 1970 and 1971 annuli, and the 1972 annulus was visible on two.
6. **Age range.** George Lake suckers reached up to 17 years, with little or no growth in later years. Most matured at age 3+ and first spawned at age 4. Lumsden Lake fish matured at age 2+ and first spawned at age 3, with none older than age 7.
7. **Physiological cause suggested.** Laboratory fish showed a false annulus and a synchronous growth decrease at 8 months, independent of temperature, photoperiod and food. Beamish suggests an inherent physiological change may be responsible.
8. **Size at maturity varies widely.** Ontario populations matured at ages 3–5 and showed wide differences in maximum size and growth patterns. Because of this variation, the validity of the *utawana* subspecies designation was called into question, and a further study was in progress.
