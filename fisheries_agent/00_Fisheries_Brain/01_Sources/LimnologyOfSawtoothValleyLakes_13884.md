# Limnology of Sawtooth Valley Lakes and Growth Potential of Juvenile Snake River Sockeye Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This limnological study, published in [[Northwest Science]] (1995), assesses whether five high-elevation lakes in the [[Sawtooth Valley]] of Idaho can support rearing of juvenile [[Snake River Sockeye Salmon]], an population listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1991. Sampling ran from May to October 1992 and covered temperature, oxygen, light, chlorophyll, phytoplankton and zooplankton. All five lakes were judged suitable for rearing, with no oxygen or temperature limitation for sockeye. The lakes differed substantially in zooplankton composition and biomass, which produced several-fold differences in predicted growth. All five lakes are oligotrophic. The authors recommend that [[Whole-Lake Fertilization]] and the impact of [[Piscivory]] be considered before recovery plans are finalized.

## Background & Description

**Species status.** [[Snake River Sockeye Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) were listed as endangered in 1991. Historically, returning adults likely exceeded 10,000 fish and numbers above 4,000 were still reported in the 1950s. Returns then declined sharply: only four adults came back in 1991 and one in 1992. The authors attribute the decline to dam passage problems on the Columbia, Snake and Salmon river systems, plus commercial and recreational fishing pressure.

**Life history.** Juveniles spend 1-2 years in the rearing lakes before smolting and migrating about 1400 km to the Pacific Ocean. Adults return after 2-3 years at sea to spawn in their natal watersheds. Progeny of the four 1991 adults and out-migrant smolts formed a captive [[Brood-Stock Program]] for the unit.

**Study lakes.** Morphometry of the five [[Sawtooth Valley Lakes]] is summarized below (data from Table 1):

| Lake | Area (km²) | Volume (×10⁶ m³) | Max depth (m) | Mean depth (m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Redfish Lake]] | 6.15 | 269.9 | 91 | 44 |
| [[Alturas Lake]] | 3.38 | 108.2 | 53 | 32 |
| [[Pettit Lake]] | 1.62 | 45.0 | 52 | 28 |
| [[Yellow Belly Lake]] | 0.73 | 10.3 | 26 | 14 |
| [[Stanley Lake]] | 0.81 | 10.4 | 26 | 13 |

Redfish Lake is the largest by volume, area and depth. Yellow Belly and Stanley each hold about 4% of Redfish's volume.

**Methods (summary).** Bathymetry was charted with an echo-sounder linked to differentially corrected GPS (accuracy about ±2 m). Temperature and oxygen were profiled with a YSI meter and checked by the [[Winkler Method]]. Light extinction was measured with a Li-Cor photometer, and transparency with a 20-cm Secchi disk. Chlorophyll a was analyzed fluorometrically, and phytoplankton was counted and biovolume-estimated. Zooplankton came from vertical tows (80 µm mesh), and diel vertical migration was tested with day/night depth-stratified tows in Redfish and Stanley Lakes in August 1992.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sockeye requirements.** Sockeye are cold-water, visually feeding zooplanktivores vulnerable to piscivorous fish. The authors cite preferred temperatures below 15°C and oxygen above 5 mg/L as tolerance guidelines.

**Thermal and oxygen conditions.** Surface temperatures peaked at 18°C only briefly and stayed mostly between 12-16°C. Hypolimnetic temperatures were about 4°C. The [[Thermocline]] formed in mid-May, at 8-18 m in the deeper lakes and 8-15 m in the shallower ones. Oxygen fell to below 3 mg/L at depth in all lakes except Redfish. Pettit, Stanley and Yellow Belly kept bottom water below 2 mg/L for prolonged periods. A [[Metalimnetic Oxygen Maximum]] of about 10 mg/L developed in four lakes, lasting from 12 days (Pettit) to 2 months (Alturas).

**Light and transparency.** The nominal photic zone (1% light level) was about 1.5-2 times the Secchi depth. Secchi transparencies ranged from 5-18 m, so the lakes are very clear. Light was suitable for visual feeding to near-bottom depths in Pettit, Stanley and Yellow Belly, and through 70-90% of the water column in Redfish and Alturas.

**Nutrients and productivity.** Mean epilimnetic total phosphorus was 6-11 µg/L and total nitrogen 66-102 µg/L. TN:TP ratios ranged from 6:1 to 11.5:1. Dissolved SRP was undetectable to 2.3 µg/L and nitrate 4.0-9.4 µg/L. Year-to-year and seasonal variation was large, but did not change the relative productivity rankings.

**Phytoplankton.** Mean epilimnetic chlorophyll a ranged from 0.44 to 0.90 mg/m³. Between 72 and 91% of chlorophyll was in size classes grazable by [[Crustacean Zooplankton]] (0.45-30 µm). Diatoms, [[Dinobryon]] and small chlorophytes dominated. A [[Deep Chlorophyll Maximum]] of up to 2.27 mg/m³ at 40 m was found in Redfish Lake, located near the 1% light level and 5-10 m below the metalimnetic oxygen maximum.

**Zooplankton.** Identified taxa included four cladocerans ([[Daphnia]] rosea, [[Bosmina]] longirostris, [[Holopedium]] gibberum, Polyphemus pediculus), the calanoid copepod [[Epischura]] nevadensis, and several cyclopoid copepods. Redfish and Alturas had the lowest epilimnetic crustacean biomass, with totals below 25 µg/L in mid-summer. Pettit, Stanley and Yellow Belly had higher cladoceran densities. Stanley showed a Holopedium-to-Daphnia succession, and Yellow Belly was dominated by Daphnia year-round.

**Diel migration.** In Redfish Lake, Bosmina moved from a mean depth of 46 m by day to 15 m at night. Holopedium moved from 10 m to 7 m. In Stanley Lake, Daphnia shifted from 11.8 m by day to 7.9 m at night. These movements matter because juvenile sockeye may exploit the same vertical patterns.

**Predators and competitors.** Yellow Belly holds only cutthroat trout. Pettit has brook trout, bull trout, stocked rainbow trout and redside shiners. Stanley has several trout species, redside shiners and resident [[Kokanee Salmon]]. Redfish and Alturas have large kokanee populations that may depress large zooplankton. Stanley and Yellow Belly appear to have more piscivores than Redfish and Alturas.

## Key Findings

1. **Suitability:** All five lakes can support juvenile sockeye rearing. Redfish retains nearly its whole water column within tolerance limits throughout the season. In the other lakes the bottom 5-15 m lacks adequate oxygen for permanent occupation.
2. **Oxygen and temperature** were not limiting in any lake for much of the water column. The ample suitable volume suggests sockeye could behaviorally select depths for growth and survival.
3. **Light and predation risk:** The high-light environment favors visual feeding, but offers little daytime refuge from piscivores. Following [[Levy 1990]]'s hypothesis, juveniles may be particularly exposed.
4. **Zooplankton differences** among lakes produced the largest contrasts in predicted growth. Yellow Belly, Stanley and Pettit had higher zooplankton biomass than Redfish and Alturas.
5. **Growth-potential rankings** varied by model (Table 2, predicted fish length in mm, or smolts per hectare for the euphotic zone model):

| Model | Redfish | Alturas | Pettit | Stanley | Yellow Belly |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorophyll (mm TL) | 99 | 100 | 98 | 108 | 99 |
| Secchi (mm TL) | 65 | 67 | 43 | 134 | 79 |
| Euphotic zone (smolts/ha) | 791 | 547 | 780 | 425 | 386 |
| Zooplankton/bioenergetics (mm TL) | 63 | 50 | 105 | 105 | 112 |
| Observed trawl catch (mm TL) | 65 | 55 | NA | 95 | NA |

6. **Model performance:** The [[Secchi Depth]] and zooplankton-bioenergetics models best matched observed fish sizes. The authors judged the zooplankton-foraging/[[Bioenergetics Model]] most suitable for this system. The euphotic-zone model was considered poorly suited, since it was designed for stained or glacial lakes where light limits phytoplankton.
7. **Productivity:** All five lakes are oligotrophic and relatively unproductive. Deep chlorophyll layers may mean primary production is underestimated from epilimnetic data alone.
8. **Management implications:** Stanley and Pettit are attractive for reintroduction because of planned modifications to low-water dams and possible fertilization. Redfish is the only lake with a current sockeye population, and Alturas had recent returns. Kokanee competition and predator-driven mortality may trade off against growth and must be assessed before recovery plans are finalized.
9. **Recommended actions:** Whole-lake fertilization, similar to approaches in Alaskan and British Columbian sockeye lakes ([[Stockner 1981]]), may enhance growth and survival. Further piscivore assessment, modeling and a field test of the bioenergetics model are needed.
