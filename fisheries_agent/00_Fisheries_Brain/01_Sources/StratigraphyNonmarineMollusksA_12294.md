# Quaternary Deposits and Non-Marine Mollusks of the Kotzebue Sound Area, Western Alaska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mollusca

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by [[David S. McCulloch]], [[Dwight W. Taylor]], and [[Meyer Rubin]] establishes a stratigraphic framework for Pleistocene and Recent sediments exposed in sea bluffs along the shores of [[Kotzebue Sound]], northwestern Alaska. Fossil non-marine mollusks from these deposits are linked to dated stratigraphic units, the first such assignment for Pleistocene and Recent faunas from Alaska or Arctic North America. The sequence records marine transgressions of Yarmouth and Sangamon age, glaciations of Illinoian and Wisconsin age, and two warm periods (Sangamon, and late Wisconsin to early Recent). Non-marine molluscan faunas from late Illinoian to Recent beds show little significant change.

The paper is not a fish study. It concerns freshwater and land molluscs and marine invertebrate faunas. It mentions fishes only as hosts for the larvae of freshwater mussels.

## Background & Description

Fossil freshwater and land molluscs have been collected in Alaska for more than 50 years, but before this work only one fossil assemblage had been assigned to a specific Pleistocene or post-Pleistocene interval. The authors' collections came from sea bluffs tens of miles long, where stratigraphic relationships are visible at many locations. Twenty-five mollusk collections containing nineteen species were made in summer 1961 by D. M. Hopkins, R. J. Janda, and McCulloch. Taylor identified the mollusks, and Rubin performed the carbon-14 analyses.

The paper's taxonomic notes cover uncertain identifications. [[Lymnaea]] specimens cited as *L. cf. L. turricula* belong to the *L. palustris* group, which may contain three species that cannot be separated by shell alone. [[Lymnaea randolphi]] is one of the few endemic freshwater molluscs of Alaska. *Gyraulus parvus* may be identical to Eurasian *G. laevis*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stratigraphic units (oldest to youngest):**
- **Yarmouth marine sediments:** at least 300 feet of olive-gray clay, silt, and fine sand. More than sixty marine mollusk species were identified by F. S. MacNeil. The beds were deformed by Illinoian glaciers into push moraines.
- **Illinoian drift:** till and outwash from piedmont glaciers fed by the Selawik, Kobuk, and Noatak valleys. Moraines rise up to 150 feet.
- **Late Illinoian deposits:** a loess blanket formed under permafrost, containing thaw-lake sediments and ice-wedge casts. A Kobuk River flood plain deposit contains wood dated older than 42,000 years.
- **Sangamon deposits:** a weathering profile, buried forest, ice-wedge casts, and marine beach deposits. Sea level was about 35–40 feet above present. The climate was milder than today.
- **Wisconsin loess and thaw-lake deposits:** up to about 30 feet of micaceous silt with two generations of ice wedges.
- **Late Wisconsin to early Recent warm period:** mollusk-rich ice-wedge casts, thaw-lake deposits, and [[Beaver Pond]] sediments with beaver dams and willow, birch, cottonwood, and spruce logs. Beaver lived beyond its present range.
- **Later Recent sediments:** water-laid silt with peat, cut by active ice wedges and thaw lakes.

**Radiometric dates (Table 2):** Th-U ages on Yarmouth marine shells were 170,000 ± 17,000 and 175,000 ± 16,000 years. Radiocarbon ages included 11,340 ± 400 to 7,270 ± 350 years for the warm period and 34,000 ± 2,000 years for Wisconsin loess. Several samples gave minimum ages of >38,000 or >42,000 years. The authors remain skeptical of the Th-U dates until the technique is better developed.

**Biogeography:** The genus *Helisoma* has a Pleistocene representative in the Kolyma River Basin of northeastern Siberia. The authors suggest that comparing Alaskan and Siberian faunas could clarify the history of the [[Bering Strait]] region. Freshwater mussels (Margaritiferidae and Unionidae) and their fish hosts were identified as a future research subject.

**Species distribution:**
- [[Anodonta beringiana]] is the largest Alaskan freshwater mollusk, reaching 13 cm. It occurs in lakes and large rivers. It is known in North America only from Alaska, and in Asia from Kamchatka, Sakhalin, and the Primorski Krai. Its sporadic distribution is attributed mainly to geologic history.
- [[Pisidium idahoense]] reaches about 1 cm. It is unknown in Asia and ranges south to California and the Great Lakes. In the conterminous US it is relictual, mostly in large lakes or cool streams. In Alaska it is found more often in rivers.

## Key Findings

- The sequence records marine transgressions (Yarmouth, Sangamon), glaciations (Illinoian, Wisconsin), and two warm periods, and it provides the first stratigraphic framework for Pleistocene and Recent non-marine faunas from Alaska and Arctic North America.
- Yarmouth age is assigned on stratigraphic grounds. The Th-U dates of 170,000 ± 17,000 and 175,000 ± 16,000 years are noted but treated with caution.
- The Sangamon warm period is supported by a weathering profile, forest advance into tundra, and thawing permafrost. Sea level was about 35–40 feet higher than today.
- Non-marine mollusk faunas show little significant change from late Illinoian to Recent times.
- Taxonomic uncertainty remains high in *Lymnaea*, *Gyraulus*, *Helisoma*, and *Succinea*. Recent Alaskan shells, and fossils even more so, may not be identifiable to species.
- The authors call for integrated study of freshwater mussels and their fish hosts to clarify land-sea relationships and drainage history along the North Pacific margin.
