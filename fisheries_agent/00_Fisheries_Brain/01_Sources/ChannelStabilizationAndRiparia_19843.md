# Channel Stabilization and Riparian Restoration Plan for the Lower Truckee River, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 final report by [[Jones & Stokes Associates]] for the [[Pyramid Lake Fisheries Restoration Project]], cosponsored by the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] Region 9, evaluates the decline of the riparian corridor along the lower 23 miles of the [[Truckee River]] between Wadsworth and [[Pyramid Lake]], and proposes a restoration plan. The stated primary objective is to stabilize river banks and reestablish bank vegetation, restoring forest canopy and streamside armor to define the channel and improve habitat for the federally endangered [[Cui-Ui]] and the threatened [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]].

The river's problems trace largely to the drop of Pyramid Lake by over 80 feet after diversions for the [[Newlands Project]] began in 1905. This base-level lowering triggered incision, bank erosion, braiding, and loss of mature cottonwood forest. The plan combines a four-site biotechnical pilot project (wood fence deflectors, low check dams, and planted cottonwood and willow cuttings) with riparian zone management (timed pulse flows, grazing exclusion, five-year beaver control, and weed removal).

## Background & Description

The lower river has been reshaped by human activity. [[Derby Dam]] diverts Truckee water into the Carson Basin, and [[Channelization]] by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers since 1963 converted a narrow, deep, meandering channel into a wide, shallow, straighter one. [[Marble Bluff Dam]] and the Pyramid Lake Fishway, completed in 1976, were built to help fish bypass the exposed delta.

Two federally protected fish are central to the report:

- **Cui-ui (*Chasmistes cujus*)**: a lake-dwelling sucker that spawns in the lower river from April to May. Females mature at 6–12 years and can live up to 45 years. The 1969 year class was the dominant population component, and the 1980–1987 spawning runs averaged 12,470 fish (range 5,000–36,300).
- **Lahontan cutthroat trout (*Oncorhynchus clarkii henshawi*)**: extirpated from Pyramid Lake around 1944. Hatchery plants now maintain the fishery, and river habitat is judged inadequate for reproduction because spring water temperatures often exceed 68°F.

The river is divided into reaches, including the Delta, Marble Bluff, Nixon Bridge, Siphon/Upper Nixon, Numana Dam, Dead Ox, S-Bar-S, and Fellnagle/Wadsworth reaches. Land is mostly Paiute Reservation land managed by the [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geomorphology.** The river is generally straighter, wider, and less confined than before 1900. Sinuosity, once as high as 2.0, is now typically 1.0–1.2. Between 1976 and 1987, about 62–64 percent of the river (14 of 22 river miles from Wadsworth to Marble Bluff Dam) changed alignment through channel migration.

**Riparian vegetation.** The study estimates that 108 acres of mature cottonwood forest disappeared between 1976 and 1987, with about 1,950 mature trees lost. Cottonwood regeneration sites totaled 15,700 linear feet in 1987, and regeneration has not kept pace with losses. The forest is described as an aged relict stand with little recruitment since about 1906.

**Water temperature and shade.** Loss of canopy and channel widening raise water temperatures. Maximum daily temperatures on the Truckee River at Clark (upstream of Derby Dam) reached 75–79°F in some summers from 1979 to 1989. Cui-ui egg survival is most sensitive to temperature, with a threshold of 63°F, and the report estimates that 1,000 cfs in May is needed to hold temperatures below that level.

**Exotic and competing species.** [[Tamarix]] (salt cedar) and [[Peppergrass]] (*Lepidium draba*) compete with cottonwood seedlings. Tamarix is reported to use about three times more water than willow. The [[Asian Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*) was abundant in riffles in October 1989 and may contribute to substrate armoring.

**Herbivory.** [[Beaver]] girdling of mature trees and cattle browsing of seedlings are major causes of mortality. Six of 20 survey stations showed active beaver damage.

**Other wildlife.** The report notes that riparian-dependent birds and mammals, such as the long-eared owl, Swainson's hawk, and red fox, will decline without restoration. No comprehensive wildlife inventory has been completed.

## Key Findings

**Factors limiting cui-ui abundance (Table 2):**
- Reduced inflow and lowered lake elevation most strongly reduced run size.
- Minimum attraction flow was estimated at 60 thousand acre-feet (TAF) when the delta is passable, and 176 TAF when only fishway passage is possible.
- Minimum May–June inflow of 1,000 cfs is needed for spawning; flows above 2,500 cfs may inhibit upstream movement.
- Lake elevations below 3,784 feet appear to prevent all spawning migrations.
- Spawning habitat is maximized at roughly 750–1,250 cfs.

**Pilot project (Chapters 5–6):**
- Four pilot sites were selected: RM 0.3–0.4 above Marble Bluff Dam (a known spawning site), RM 5.2 near the Siphon, RM 7.0 below Numana Dam, and RM 12.8–13.2 near the fish hatchery.
- Planned elements total about 14,800 linear feet of revegetation, 5,330 linear feet of fence deflectors, and 1,530 linear feet of gravel check dams.
- Estimated materials cost is about $11,000 for deflectors and $3,600 for check dams, with labor estimated at $43,000 and $6,500.

**Riparian management recommendations:**
- Pulsed flows timed to the cottonwood seed-release period.
- Grazing exclusion for 3–5 years from the 23-mile riparian zone, with full protection for at least 3 years in the Marble Bluff–Numana reach.
- A five-year beaver control program, with pelt payments raised from $10 to $50.
- Removal of tamarix and peppergrass at selected sites.

**Predicted outcomes:**
- Pilot sites are expected to become densely vegetated within 5 years and to reach nearly complete canopy closure along the shoreline after 10 years.
- Within 10 years, erosion and braiding should decline and cui-ui spawning above Marble Bluff Dam should increase.

**Conclusion:** The authors recommend a staged, decade-long recovery program. Structural measures are intended as temporary boosts (5–10 year lifespan) to natural processes, not permanent interventions.
