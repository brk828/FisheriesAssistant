# Field Methodologies of the Fish and Wildlife Service's Colorado River Fisheries Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This methods paper, written by Harold Tyus and Richard Valdez of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Colorado River Fisheries Project]], describes how the project studies two endangered cyprinids, the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Humpback Chub]], in the mainstem of the Upper Colorado and Green Rivers. The project was established in April 1979 under an agreement between the Fish and Wildlife Service and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. The approach combines a broad, statistically based survey of the whole river system with intensive studies of the habitat and life-history needs of the two target species. The paper explains the research design, sampling schedule, gear, physical and chemical measurements, and data management.

## Background & Description

**Project origins.** Other co-operators joined the project later, providing funding and field support. The team's stated aim is to explain methods in terms of research needs, not only to list what was done.

**Study system.** The authors describe rivers as unusual ecosystems because of strong currents, long-distance water transport, an extensive water/land interface, and extreme flow fluctuations. These conditions make reliable research difficult. The study area covers about 570 miles of the [[Colorado River]] and [[Green River]], including the lower reaches of the [[Gunnison River]].

**Two-tier approach.**
- *Broad surveys* assess fish populations and associated habitats across the entire system.
- *Intensive studies* examine spawning requirements, food habits, movement, interspecific relationships, and other life-history traits of the target species.

**Target species.** The document focuses on:
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), which is the most vulnerable to electroshocking among the fish collected.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The OCR is partly garbled, but this is the species implied by the text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling framework.**
- The river was divided into 17 strata, based on Table 1. There are six Green River strata, ten Colorado River strata, and one Gunnison River stratum.
- Each stratum is a homogeneous section 30 to 70 miles long. The sections range from high-gradient canyons to low-gradient, winding reaches and transition zones.
- Strata were defined using geophysical factors (channel configuration, substrate, gradient, and depth) and biological factors (productive backwaters, sterile sand runs, swift canyons, and point sources of allochthonous material).
- Strata were sampled in a [[Systematic Sampling With Random Start]] design. Sampling began at a randomly chosen stratum, moved downstream to the last stratum, and then returned to the uppermost stratum to complete the series. This reduced travel time compared with a fully random design.
- Within each stratum, sample stations were chosen randomly by river mile using a table of random digits.

**Habitat classification.** Eight major habitat types were recognized (Table 2 and Table 3):
- [[Main Channel]]
- [[Backwater Habitat]]
- [[Side Channel]]
- [[Eddy]]
- [[Pool]]
- [[Riffle]]
- [[Run]]
- [[Shoreline]]

Each habitat type was sampled separately within each 0.5-mile intensive reach, which allowed the team to estimate habitat preferences and the associated depths, velocities, and substrates.

**Substrate classes.** Substrate was categorized as follows:
- Silt: fine, gritty material
- Sand: less than 3 mm
- Gravel: 3 mm to 76 mm
- Cobble: 76 mm to 305 mm
- Boulder: 305 mm and larger
- Bedrock: rock outcropping

**Water quality.** Dissolved oxygen, turbidity, air and water temperature, conductivity, and pH were recorded at each intensive sample site. Temperature, pH, and DO were measured between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. to standardize the results. See [[Water Quality Monitoring]].

**Seasonal and daily scheduling.** Sampling was timed around the [[Spring Runoff]] and was carried out before, during, and after runoff. Each year included a period of inactivity when the river was frozen or weather was too severe for safe work. Each day was split into a daylight sampling period, a darkness period, and a midday period reserved for maintenance and travel. Daytime work included netting, trapping, electrofishing, and physical and chemical measurements. Nighttime work involved tending nets and traps and some seining.

## Key Findings

**Sampling design and effort**
- Each sampling station included two 0.5-mile intensive reaches. These were separated by a 4.5-mile reach that was surveyed by electrofishing.
- Each intensive reach was sampled for 24 hours.
- The team aimed to sample every habitat present in each intensive reach with every effective gear type.

**Field gear**
- **[[Electrofishing]]:** A rotating-field DC unit powered by a 4.0 kW generator was the main tool. It worked best at 200 to 250 volts and 8 to 12 amps. Conductivity fluctuated widely, so the equipment and electrodes needed continual adjustment. A 1.8 kW raft unit was less effective. Electrofishing was especially useful for [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- **[[Trammel Net]]:** Trammel nets (150 ft long, 6 ft deep) were set in slow-flowing pools and eddies. They caught squawfish, suckers, catfish, and carp, but they also collected a lot of debris. They were checked every two to three hours because fish often died in them after a while.
- **Gill nets:** These were used only occasionally. The authors dispute the view that gill nets are more lethal than trammel nets and believe they are less destructive to rare fishes.
- **[[Funnel Trap]]:** Wire mesh funnel traps (about 1.5 ft in diameter and 2.5 ft long) were very effective for catfish and [[Gila Species]] when set among large boulders or debris. Fish held in these traps for long periods could be abraded.
- **[[Seine]]:** The most-used gear was seines, mainly 15 × 4 ft nets with 1/8 in mesh and 30 × 6 ft nets with 1/4 in mesh. Seines were hung with the bottom line weighted and were effective in shallow areas. Effort was measured as seine length multiplied by the distance pulled.

**Fish handling and tagging**
- Fish over 100 mm were weighed and measured individually. Smaller fish were counted and measured in groups.
- Rare fish were handled with great care. Stomachs were removed from rare fish that died, and the carcasses were frozen for later study.
- Rare fish over 175 mm total length were tagged with a [[Carlin Dangler Tag]] attached below the dorsal fin.
- Field identification of the *Gila* group was difficult. Dorsal and anal fin characteristics were used in the past season, and the team planned to adopt the [[Gila Species Identification]] method of Smith, Miller, and Sable (1979) for future work.

**Physical and chemical measurements**
- Sample sites were located on river maps and aerial photographs. Sketches showed the arrangement of habitat types in each intensive reach.
- Cross-sectional depth profiles were recorded with an electronic depth recorder at each end of the 0.5-mile section and at its midpoint.
- Velocity was measured at 0.6 of the depth from the surface (Bovee and Milhous 1978). A [[Marsh-McBirney Velocity Meter]] (Model 201) cut the time needed for each measurement by about 70 to 80 percent compared with older methods.
- Conductivity and total dissolved solids were measured with a Yellow Springs Model 33 meter. Turbidity was reported in FTU, which the authors treat as equivalent to Jackson Turbidity Units.
- Fish weights were measured with Chatillon scales.

**Data management**
- Data were first recorded on field forms (Appendix A-1 through A-3). The forms were later revised to suit computer processing.
- Data were stored and analyzed in the [[MANAGE Database]], a program developed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Western Energy and Land Use Team at Fort Collins, Colorado.
- Planned analyses include comparing fish abundance and population parameters across strata. Multivariate comparisons will relate fish abundance to physical habitat characteristics, using the principle of habitat selectivity developed by the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] group (Bovee and Cochnauer 1977).
- A parallel hydraulic habitat survey was also planned. Its results would support the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] for assessing the impacts of water development projects.

**Planned special investigations**
- **[[Young-Of-The-Year Squawfish]] surveys:** A 1979 survey mapped young-of-the-year squawfish distribution and recruitment. Limited work was done on how these fish use backwaters. Food habits of young squawfish and other species were scheduled for the next year, along with more work on backwater use and the hydraulic factors involved.
- **[[Radio Telemetry]]:** A radio-tagging program was planned to track squawfish spawning behavior, define spawning areas, and identify the characteristics of spawning habitat.
- **[[Disease Surveys]]:** A disease assessment for both native and introduced species was planned for 1980. Field crews would make cursory checks, while specialists would sample all major components of both populations three times a year for major pathogens and parasites.
- **Humpback chub:** Young-of-the-year and movement studies were planned with the Colorado Division of Wildlife.
