# Physical Habitat Evaluation of Small Stream Fishes: Point vs. Transect, Observation vs. Capture Methodologies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares how [[Habitat Evaluation]] methods for small stream fishes perform in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, an upper [[Sonoran Desert]] stream. It tests two questions: whether physical habitat estimates from a point method match those from a [[Transect Method]], and whether the way fish are located ([[Underwater Observation]] or capture by [[Electrofishing]]) changes the results. Physical habitat variables were water velocity, water depth and [[Substrate Composition]]. Results were similar for depth and substrate, but velocity showed poorer agreement and greater variation. The author concludes that the choice of fish-location technique may introduce more measurement error than the choice of habitat evaluation technique. The author favors capture by electrofishing combined with the transect method, though the data do not show one method to be unequivocally superior.

## Background & Description

Habitat evaluation methods for fishes are numerous and are usually developed at local or regional scales. Philosophy, economics, agency needs and objectives shape which method is chosen. The author argues that any method must be cost-effective, efficient and reliable, and that reliability often suffers because of the first two factors. Ideally, methods should be consistent across regions so that results do not conflict and leave land managers unable to implement plans. The paper asks whether differing results reflect technique rather than real habitat differences.

Small southwestern fishes are mostly diminutive and occupy very specific microhabitats, so precise quantification of microhabitat use is important. A **microhabitat** is treated as the point where a fish is observed or captured. Three common approaches are:

- **Reach approach:** describes habitat over a stream reach of about 100 m. It gives general requirements but is often too imprecise for management.
- **Point approach:** records conditions at the single point where a fish is seen or captured.
- **Transect approach:** samples a small microhabitat area of about 1 to 2 m² where fish are captured, using data from multiple points along a meter-stick transect. It sits between the point and reach approaches.

Because fish move, both direct observation and capture could produce erroneous definitions of habitat use.

The study addresses two questions: (1) Do physical habitat estimates from point and transect methods differ? (2) Does the method used to locate fish (direct observation or electrofishing) affect results?

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site:** Aravaipa Creek, Arizona, an upper Sonoran Desert stream at about 300 m elevation with a flow of 0.60 m³/s.

**Species sampled:** Of seven native fishes in the creek, data were obtained for five:

- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*)
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarkii*)
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)

Data were separated into allopatric (one species per reach) and sympatric (multiple species in the same reach) groups. Sympatric data are from the same reach.

**Sampling equipment:** Fish were located by electrofishing with a backpack DC shocker, or by underwater observation. Physical habitat was measured with a 132-cm-long, 22-cm-wide habitat sampler divided into 5-cm sections. A meter rule and a current meter measured depth and velocity. Substrate was visually classified into size classes: sand (<2 mm), gravel, pebble, cobble (65 to 256 mm) and boulder (>256 mm).

**Velocity variation among sets:** Of the seven sets of microhabitat data, three were in higher-velocity areas (mean 58 to 84 cm/s) and one was in a lower-velocity area (mean 33 to 38 cm/s).

## Key Findings

### 1. Time and data density

- A full meter transect (21 velocity, substrate and depth points) took a mean of 3.8 minutes across 27 transects (range given as 2.1 to 5.2 minutes). Most of this time (50 to 75%) was spent measuring velocity.
- Single-point measurements of all three factors averaged less than 50 seconds.
- The transect approach required more time but yielded up to 20 times as many data points.

### 2. Intrasample variation

- Seven independent sets of three parallel transects were compared within single microhabitats.
- Depth and velocity variation differed significantly between transects in only one set (F = 4.14 and 5.74, DF = 60).
- Substrate variation differed significantly in only five data sets (14%). All of these were in the shallower, slower-water sample areas: three in cobble, one in pebble and one in gravel.

### 3. Sample size requirements

- Sample size (n) for ±10% precision of the mean was calculated from the variance.
- For 24 samples from April 1984, required depth sample sizes ranged from 2 to 49 (mean 13.77). Velocity sample sizes ranged from 3 to 70 (mean 26.6).
- Ten (42%) of the 24 velocity sample sizes exceeded 21. Only 4 (15%) of the 24 depth transects had inadequate sample sizes.

### 4. Transect versus point (August 1983 and related data)

- **Substrate:** Of 53 paired primary-substrate measurements, 29 (55%) agreed. Another 20 (38%) were partially in agreement, with the primary type of one method matching the secondary type of the other. The remaining 4 (8%) disagreed completely.
- **Depth and velocity:** At fish-capture points, mean values were not significantly different from transect means for either parameter (t = 1.05, DF = 52). Mean point velocity was 46.1 cm/s versus 42.8 cm/s for transects, and point depth was 171.9 cm versus 169.7 cm.
- Point estimates were reported as more subjective than transect estimates, and the transect method produced data on substrate percentages and an index of substrate types.

### 5. Observation versus electrofishing

- In the first comparison (Table 1, data set A), observation transects were located about 100 m upstream of the electrofished transects.
- Electrofishing-based estimates of velocity and depth were greater than observation-based estimates, and substrate index values were smaller. The exception was depth for the desert sucker, which did not differ significantly.
- In data set B, five electrofished transects within the observation reach agreed more closely with observation estimates. Even so, all velocity estimates and three of five depth estimates were significantly different. Substrate did not differ significantly.
- Comparisons in larger, more uniform riffles in spring and summer 1984 showed significant differences in 7 of 9 comparisons.
- Fish observed in narrow margins had lower velocity and less depth, and these areas were more commonly occupied. Electrofishing appeared to capture fish in faster, deeper central water, possibly because the fish were moved during capture.

### 6. Variability and reliability of single measurements

- Velocity recordings from the direct-reading current meter were very sensitive to turbulence and fluctuated widely.
- Based on the sample size data, the author suggests about 27 velocity points per microhabitat are needed for ±10% confidence.
- A single depth measurement may appear adequate in comparisons of point and transect means. However, about 14 depth points are needed for ±10% precision, so a single measurement is unlikely to be reliable.
- A single velocity measurement is unlikely to reliably estimate preferred velocity. Multiple points are needed for statistical comparison.

### 7. Conclusions and recommendations

- The data do not unequivocally show that one location method is superior. The author prefers capture by electrofishing combined with the transect approach, based on biological and statistical reliability.
- Electrofishing has several advantages:
  - It can capture more than one species and more individuals per species than observation.
  - It yields size, sex and sometimes species information that observation cannot reliably provide.
  - It works in turbid water where observation is impossible or unreliable.
  - It is especially suitable when schooling species are present.
- Limitations include the arbitrary selection of a single observed point when fish populations are dispersed and the fact that fish may be moved by capture.
- Choices among methods will also depend on personal preference, manpower and funding.
