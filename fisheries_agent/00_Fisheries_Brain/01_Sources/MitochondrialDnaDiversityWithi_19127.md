# Mitochondrial DNA Diversity Within and Among Populations of Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #conservation-genetics #population-structure

## Executive Summary

This study examined genetic variation within and among populations of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) using mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) restriction endonuclease analysis. Researchers analyzed 143 specimens from five locations across the [[Colorado River Basin]] using eight restriction enzymes, identifying 38 distinct haplotypes. Within-population genetic diversity varied clinally, declining progressively from downstream to upstream locations. Most genetic variation occurred within rather than between populations, and phylogenetic analyses revealed no geographic structure, indicating samples represent remnants of a single historically interconnected population. The findings support conservation strategies treating the species as a single breeding unit rather than managing isolated stocks separately.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America, historically occupying large rivers and associated floodplain habitats throughout the drainage, including the vast [[Gila River Basin]]. The species is characterized by large body size (>500 mm standard length), longevity (>50 years), and apparent preference for wide, slower-flowing, non-canyon reaches. Human-induced impacts including flow modification, dam construction, and introduction of exotic species have severely reduced the species' range and population sizes, leading to its listing as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1991.

The largest remaining populations occur in the upper [[Green River Basin]] (Colorado-Utah, estimated at fewer than 1,000 adults) and [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada, fewer than 25,000 individuals). Natural populations elsewhere, including throughout the [[Gila River Basin]], are either extirpated or too small for reliable population estimates. Most mainstem reservoirs on the Colorado River were constructed between 1935 and 1962, fragmenting formerly continuous habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River Basin]] underwent remarkable ecological changes throughout the Tertiary period, with tectonism causing dramatic shifts in drainage patterns and dynamic climatic conditions alternating between hot, dry periods and cool, wet pluvial periods. These conditions created episodic isolation of aquatic biota, with xeric conditions fragmenting habitats and wet periods promoting drainage interconnections and potential fish dispersal.

Paleoclimatic evidence indicates the last full glacial period peaked 18,000 years ago and ended approximately 10,000 years ago, with warm-season air temperatures on the [[Colorado Plateau]] averaging approximately 6.3°C cooler than present. These depressed thermal conditions would have excluded self-sustaining populations of [[Razorback Sucker]] from much of the upper mainstem Colorado and Green rivers, as the species requires water temperatures near or above 20°C for optimal spawning, hatching, and growth. Lower-altitude, lower-latitude regions likely served as thermal refugia during glacial periods.

Contemporary threats include cold hypolimnetic water discharge from reservoirs, which prevents reproduction and persistence in affected stream reaches. Temperature limitations likely constrain reproduction even under natural conditions in upstream portions of the range, suggesting upper-basin populations represent peripheral rather than central populations.

## Key Findings

**Genetic Diversity Patterns:**
- Analysis of 143 specimens with eight restriction endonucleases produced 182 fragments and 38 composite haplotypes
- [[Lake Mohave]] exhibited the highest haplotype diversity (h = 0.98) and number of haplotypes per individual (n_h = 0.67), among the highest values recorded for any organism examined to date
- Genetic diversity declined progressively upstream: [[Lake Mead]] (h = 0.82), [[Lake Powell]] (h = 0.81), [[Green River]]-[[Yampa River]] (h = 0.73), and upper [[Colorado River]] (h = 0.00)
- The upper Colorado River sample showed extremely low diversity, with all 27 individuals sharing a single haplotype (ABADA'AAA), the most common haplotype in [[Lake Mohave]]

**Population Structure:**
- Chi-square tests indicated statistically significant differences in haplotype frequencies among samples (χ² = 239.5 for 4+6 bp enzymes; χ² = 104.7 for 6 bp enzymes)
- However, only approximately 23% of mtDNA variation was distributed among populations (θ = 0.23), with most variation occurring within populations
- When the upper Colorado River sample was excluded, only ~11% of variation was distributed among populations
- Jackknife analysis across samples failed to detect significant heterogeneity, with mean θ values not differing significantly from zero

**Phylogeographic Analysis:**
- Average sequence divergence among all individuals was 0.00447, with only ~22% attributable to divergence among populations (0.00097)
- Maximum divergence between haplotypes was only 0.7%
- Phylogenetic analysis generated over 1,000 most-parsimonious trees but revealed no phylogeographic structure
- UPGMA clustering showed no geographic partitioning of haplotypes
- Individuals from all locations either shared or carried haplotypes most similar to those in [[Lake Mohave]]

**Hybridization:**
- Only 3 of 148 fish morphologically identified as [[Razorback Sucker]] from major populations possessed mtDNA introgressed from [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- Hybridization was more common in the [[Little Colorado River]], where 5 of 12 individuals exhibited flannelmouth sucker mtDNA
- All 12 Little Colorado River specimens showed intermediate morphology (color pattern and predorsal keel)

**Temporal Considerations:**
- Most sampled razorback suckers were old (>40 years in [[Lake Mohave]]), representing offspring from the population occupying the entire basin 40-80 years ago, before major human water resource development
- Younger fish (<15 years) from the [[Little Colorado River]] and ponds near Grand Junction reflect more recent, post-development habitat conditions

**Evolutionary Interpretation:**
- The clinal reduction in genetic diversity upstream is most consistent with either: (1) serial bottlenecks during post-glacial recolonization from a southern refugium, or (2) equilibrium conditions with population sizes declining upstream due to increasingly suboptimal thermal conditions
- The recolonization hypothesis is supported by progressive decline in diversity, increasing frequency of common [[Lake Mohave]] haplotypes upstream, and current clinal pattern of population abundance
- Historical gene exchange among distant regions was sufficient to prevent population divergence despite potential for isolation in non-canyon reaches

**Conservation Implications:**
- Absence of local genetic differentiation and evidence of historical interconnection support treating [[Razorback Sucker]] as a single breeding unit throughout its range
- Managing the species as discrete stocks would be problematic given insufficient numbers of brood fish in most populations
- Conservation strategies should focus on preserving genetic variation in a manner comparable to the natural state rather than maintaining isolated populations
- Concerns about loss of local adaptations may be unfounded, as moderate gene flow can maintain local adaptation even when populations are genetically connected
