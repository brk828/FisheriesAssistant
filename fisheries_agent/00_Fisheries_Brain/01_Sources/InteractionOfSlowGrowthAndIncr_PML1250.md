# Interaction of Slow Growth and Increased Early-Life Mortality: A Hypothesis on the Decline of Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper proposes that the interaction of slow growth and increased early-life mortality is an important cause of the decline of the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species, the principal native piscivore of the basin, was once widespread in large rivers and major tributaries but now occurs naturally only in upstream regions of its historic range and is threatened with extinction. The authors link a growth-rate versus temperature relation to temperature regimes in historic and present habitats, find a strong positive relation between temperature suitability and first-year growth, and use simulations to show that increased early-life mortality has a disproportionately large effect on slow-growing populations. The authors recommend water management to enhance growth temperatures and control of important introduced fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a cyprinid piscivore endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Historic range:** Large rivers and major tributaries across the basin, documented by early surveys (Jordan 1891; Evermann & Rutter 1895; Jordan & Evermann 1896; Gilbert & Scofield 1898).
- **Current status:** Restricted to upstream regions (Green, Yampa, White, upper Colorado rivers); threatened with extinction.
- **Comparative context:** Piscivores such as [[Northern Pike]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] show rapid early-life growth, which reduces the period when fish are vulnerable as prey and widens prey choice. Colorado squawfish lacks this rapid growth in the upper basin.
- **Known causes of decline (general):** Water-resource development, introduced non-native fishes, and poor land-use practices (Miller 1961; Minckley & Deacon 1968; Minckley 1973).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature and growth:** Growth depends on the interaction of water temperature and food availability. [[Black and Bulkley Growth Study]] (1985a) found growth was optimal at 25 °C and was 18%, 54% and 51% of optimum at 15, 20 and 30 °C. Growth appears to cease below about 13 °C.
- **Habitat use:** Adults and juveniles are most often captured in river backwaters. Backwaters show larger diel temperature variation than the main channel, and fish may move between habitats to reach near-optimal temperatures.
- **Spawning cue:** Spawning begins when water temperatures reach about 20–22 °C, normally in late July to August in the upper basin, and eggs hatch about 4–5 days later.
- **Temperature regimes:**
  - Upper-basin growing season: fewer than 6 months (above 13 °C).
  - Historic middle and lower basin: 7 to 9 months.
  - Relative suitability indices: Green River 3.2, upper Colorado 2.1, middle Colorado 3.9, lower Colorado 4.9.
- **Dam effects:** Cold hypolimnetic releases from dams reduce embryo survival (Marsh 1985), but analyses of USGS records showed dam operation did not reduce temperatures in most upper-basin reaches still inhabited by the species.
- **Introduced species:** Predators and competitors including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] have colonized occupied reaches. Green sunfish can greatly suppress native cyprinid populations (Lemly 1985).

## Key Findings

1. **Slow upper-basin growth is linked to temperature.** A strong, positive relation (p < 0.01, r = 0.95; r² = 0.91) exists between the mean total length of age-0 Colorado squawfish captured in fall from the Green and upper Colorado rivers and the temperature suitability index for the year of capture.
2. **Pond growth shows the species' potential.** In a pond study (Osmundson 1987), 5–6 month-old fish stocked at 58–75 mm grew to an average of 226 mm within one year (largest 304 mm), attributed to near-optimal summer temperatures and abundant food.
3. **Historic temperature regimes were more suitable.** Suitability of the Green River regime was 3.2 and of the upper Colorado 2.1, versus 3.9 (middle) and 4.9 (lower) in historic habitats. Correcting the Green River data for a worst-case 1 °C daytime bias would raise its index to 2.7, about 29% above the upper Colorado value.
4. **Climate correction was not possible.** Historic lower-river data averaged 0.8 °C cooler than the long-term Yuma mean, while historic middle-river data averaged 0.9 °C warmer than the Flagstaff mean. Adjustment would raise the lower-river averages and lower the others, but could not be applied.
5. **Simulations show growth amplifies mortality effects.** Fast-growing populations (maturity at 3 years) produced many more mature females than slow-growing ones (maturity at 6 years) at every early-life mortality rate tested (80–99%). The gap widened as mortality rose.
6. **Population capacity is low under high mortality.** At 99% early-life mortality, the slow-growing stock's net reproductive rate (Ro) was about 1.1 with a finite rate of increase (λ) of about 1.12, while the fast-growing stock's Ro was about 6.5 with λ of about 1.84. The present upper-basin population has an Ro of no more than 1, so it is worse than even the simulated slow-growing stock.
7. **Small age-0 size may limit recruitment.** Overwinter survival depends on fish size, and in [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] first-year growth affects adult year-class strength. The more frequent occurrence of large age-0 fish in the Green River may partly explain its relatively large adult population.
8. **Historic growth was likely higher.** The authors argue that slow growth is both historic and the result of suboptimal temperatures, and that growth in former downstream areas was probably rapid. Behnke & Benson (1983) hypothesized that replacement of large native prey by small introduced species caused the decline; the authors consider this less plausible given the abundance of prey sizes in upper-basin rivers.
9. **Management recommendations:**
   - Enhance growing-season temperatures through dam and reservoir operation, with caution because undesirable species may also benefit.
   - Avoid water-development programs that reduce available temperatures.
   - Investigate the relation between age-0 size and overwinter survival.
   - Prevent further introductions and identify non-native fishes that most harm the species, since eliminating established introduced fishes is impracticable in a river system this large.
10. **Limited compensation.** Compensatory responses (faster growth, earlier maturation) are expected when food competition limits a population. The authors argue that growth in upper-basin Colorado squawfish is limited by temperature availability, which is independent of population density, so such compensation would not operate.
