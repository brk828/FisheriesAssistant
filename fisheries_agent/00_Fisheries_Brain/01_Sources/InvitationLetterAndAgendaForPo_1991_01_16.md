# Population Viability Analysis Workshop on Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #population-viability-analysis

## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary research paper but a **workshop invitation, agenda, and draft project plan** from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) convened to determine whether **Population Viability Analysis (PVA)** and **Risk Analysis** could be used to refine recovery goals for four endangered/rare fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (referred to as "Colorado squawfish"), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The workshop, held February 6–7, 1991, in Lakewood, Colorado, brought together fisheries biologists, population geneticists, and quantitative ecologists to assess data availability (life tables, survival rates, fecundity, sex ratios, longevity, age at maturity, habitat requirements) and to outline a process for applying PVA and metapopulation theory to freshwater conservation planning. The document also includes an extensive theoretical background section explaining demographic, environmental, catastrophic, and genetic uncertainty as applied to extinction risk modeling.

## Background & Description

### Species Under Review
- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** ("Colorado squawfish", *Ptychocheilus lucius*): Recovery Plan (draft, under review) calls for self-sustaining populations in the Green River Basin (Green River from Colorado River confluence to Echo Park, lower 220 km of the [[Yampa River]], lower 240 km of the [[White River]]), the Colorado River from Palisade, CO to [[Lake Powell]], and the [[San Juan River]] from Lake Powell upstream to river kilometer 305.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): Recovery goal specifies **five viable, self-sustaining wild populations** plus **two refugia populations**. Candidate wild population locations: Little Colorado and Colorado rivers in [[Grand Canyon]], Black Rocks/Westwater Canyon (Colorado River), Yampa and Green rivers in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Gray and Desolation Canyons (Green River), and Cataract Canyon (Colorado River).
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): Recovery objective simply stated as "**prevent extinction**" — reflecting its critically depleted status at the time.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Listed as a "proposed species" at time of writing; no formal recovery goals yet established — this project aimed to develop them.

### Regulatory & Institutional Context
The workshop was part of the **Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Implementation Program**, involving the FWS Region 6 (Endangered Species and Environmental Contaminants Division), Colorado River Fishery Program biologists, Colorado Division of Wildlife, National Park Service (Dinosaur National Monument), and academic PVA specialists (University of Montana, University of Nevada, Duke University, Arizona State University, Colorado State University, Illinois Natural History Survey).

### Purpose of Workshop
1. Determine whether PVA can refine recovery goals for the four species.
2. Determine whether Risk Analysis can evaluate recovery activities and habitat threats (e.g., dams, diversions, land use, exotic species control).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Threats & Systematic Pressures
The draft plan identifies major **systematic pressures** driving extinction risk in Colorado River Basin fishes:
- Flow regulation (dams, diversions)
- Water quality degradation
- Habitat alteration and fragmentation
- Introduction of nonnative/exotic species

### Stochastic Perturbation Categories
PVA theory as applied to these species considers four types of stochastic uncertainty:
1. **Demographic stochasticity** — variance in survival, fecundity, reproduction, sex ratio; primarily affects small populations (10s–100s of individuals).
2. **Environmental stochasticity** — variation in flow, water quality, food supply, biotic interactions; persistence time increases *linearly* (not geometrically) with population size, implying no truly "safe" minimum population size.
3. **Catastrophic stochasticity** — floods, droughts; persistence time increases with the *natural log* of population size, implying eventual inevitability of extinction risk regardless of size.
4. **Genetic stochasticity** — inbreeding depression, genetic drift, founder effects; critical for maintaining evolutionary adaptive potential.

### Metapopulation Theory Applied to River Fishes
The plan proposes that Colorado River Basin fishes may be best conceptualized as a **[[Metapopulation]]** — a population of geographically isolated subpopulations connected by gene flow/recolonization corridors (e.g., river reaches, canyons). Key considerations:
- A metapopulation *may* have higher persistence probability and greater genetic variance than a single large population of equal total size (N=10,000 example given), **if** subpopulations occupy uncorrelated environments (i.e., a single catastrophe does not affect all subpopulations).
- However, subpopulations that are individually too small risk **inbreeding depression** and **genetic drift**, which could reduce fitness across the entire metapopulation.
- Local extinction of any single subpopulation reduces overall genetic variability of the metapopulation.

### Habitat/Patch Size Considerations
Extinction probability is inversely related to patch (habitat) area/carrying capacity. The plan calls for determining **Minimum Area Requirements (MAR)** — the habitat quantity and quality needed to support viable populations of each species, tying habitat conservation directly to demographic viability targets.

## Key Findings

### Data Needs Identified for PVA
Species experts were asked to inventory existing data availability on:
- Life table data
- Survival rates (with variance, by life stage)
- Fecundity and reproduction rates
- Sex ratio
- Longevity
- Age at sexual maturity
- Habitat quality/quantity requirements
- Density-dependent effects on survival/reproduction
- Inter-subpopulation migration/communication rates

### Core PVA Objectives for the Workshop
1. Develop models estimating probability of persistence / time to extinction for:
   - Single vs. multiple populations
   - Various population sizes
   - Various spatial distributions/locations
   - Various habitat area requirements
2. Determine population sampling strategies compatible with PVA model parameters.
3. Use PVA models to evaluate trade-offs among:
   - Habitat management/development options
   - Population augmentation (stocking) scenarios
   - Exotic species control programs

### Quantitative Framework Cited (Goodman 1987)
A modified birth-death process model (Goodman 1987) was proposed for estimating **expected time to extinction** as a function of:
- **r** = mean per capita population growth rate
- **Nm** = maximum population size (carrying capacity)
- **V** = variance in per capita growth rate

Where data are unavailable, **surrogate parameters** were proposed:
- Optimal net reproductive rate (rm), a function of body mass, as proxy for r.
- Carrying capacity estimates (also a function of body mass) as proxy for Nm.

### Genetic Viability Metrics Discussed
- Rate of loss of genetic variance: Vt = V(0) × (1 − 1/2Ne)^t, where Ne = effective population size.
- A **1% decrease in genetic variance** is associated with a **1–2% decrease in fitness** (survival, fecundity).
- Standard wildlife management threshold: **<1% inbreeding per generation** is considered acceptable to avoid serious fitness problems.
- Genetic interchange (even ~1 migrant per generation) between subpopulations can counteract drift and inbreeding depression, effectively rendering a subdivided population "panmictic" if migration is sustained each generation.

### Comparator Species with Prior PVA Applications
The draft plan cites precedent PVA applications on: Puerto Rican parrot, Florida panther, grizzly bear, Furbish's lousewort, Bay checkerspot butterfly, Northern spotted owl, Puerto Rican plain pigeon, Puerto Rican crested toad, red wolf, California condor, black-footed ferret, and Concho water snake — plus a **habitat-parameter-driven modified PVA** previously used on **Gila topminnow** and **pupfish** species, offering the most directly analogous freshwater precedent for the Colorado River fishes.

### Data Requirement Recommendations from PVA Experts
- **Mark Shaffer**: annual survival estimates (with variance) by life stage, reproduction rates, density-related effects on survival/reproduction, and inter-subpopulation migration data.
- **Dan Goodman**: population estimates, year-to-year variability in survival/reproduction/growth rates; suggests habitat extent/availability/future projections as viable surrogate parameters when demographic data are lacking.
- General expert consensus: data from **similar species in similar habitats** may serve as surrogates; professional judgment is acceptable for filling residual data gaps.

### Process & Milestones
A **Milestone Chart** (Nov 1990–Sept 1991) outlines the plan's institutional review pathway: recovery goal plan presented to Management Committee (11/26/90) → distributed for comment → PVA Workshop (Feb 1991) → results distributed to Biology/Management Committees, Recovery Team, Region 2, and lower basin states → final determination (by 9/91) of whether existing data suffice for PVA-based recovery goals or whether additional study plans are required.

### Key Terminology (Glossary Highlights)
- **[[Minimum Viable Population]]**: smallest isolated population with 99% probability of persisting 1,000 years despite demographic, environmental, genetic stochasticity and catastrophes (Shaffer 1981).
- **Effective Population (Ne)**: idealized population with random mating, equal sex ratio, no variance in offspring number, non-overlapping generations — used to model genetic drift rates.
- **Founder Effect**: larger numbers of unrelated founding individuals reduce required effective population size for maintaining genetic diversity.
