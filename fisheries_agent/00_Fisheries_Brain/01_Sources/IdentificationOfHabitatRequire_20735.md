# Habitat Requirements and Limiting Factors for Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chubs: Work Plan No. 2, Job No. 1 (1 July 1982 to 30 June 1983)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This progress report from the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], with cooperating [[Colorado State University]] researchers, covers the second year of a project to identify the physical and biotic factors that limit the distribution and reproduction of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] in Colorado. The study drew on 694 seine and ichthyoplankton (drift-net) collections across the [[Colorado River]] and [[Yampa River]] study areas. Sixteen young-of-year (YOY) Colorado squawfish were taken in the Colorado study area and 20 in the Yampa study area. Estimated spawning windows were 10 July to 15 August (Colorado) and 6 to 28 July (Yampa). From 1979 to 1982, spawning, with one exception, closely followed decreasing flows and water temperatures reaching about 22 °C. No razorback suckers were found. The 1983 plan adds intensive drift sampling in the Yampa River and a hatchery-based larval series of [[Roundtail Chub]].

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*; spelled "Ptychochielus" in the source) and the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) are federally and state-listed endangered fishes whose numbers and ranges have declined. Since 1977, the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] has sampled selected reaches of the Colorado, Gunnison, White, and Yampa rivers. Work was done cooperatively with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Colorado River Fisheries Project). Nongame Research (Fort Collins) focused on reproductive success, spawning timing, and habitat selection by early life stages.

The project had seven segment objectives:

1. Measure habitat parameters that limit squawfish and humpback chub distribution and abundance.
2. Identify macro- and microhabitat features (flow, temperature, substrate, depth) associated with YOY presence or absence.
3. Determine spawning timing and correlate it with adult data from other agencies.
4. Use a computer-compatible physical stratification system (FWS MANAGE) reflecting geomorphological, hydrological, and ecological variables.
5. Sample the lower Yampa River (Cross Mountain Canyon to the Green River confluence in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]) and the Colorado River (Loma to the state line).
6. Develop methods to tell apart larval and juvenile [[Humpback Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and hybrids.
7. Compare field-collected chub larvae and juveniles with known-age pure-stock and hybrid crosses from Willow Beach and Dexter National Fish Hatcheries.

The work is also meant to support future habitat enhancement, reintroduction, and mitigation for upper Colorado River water development projects. Part of the humpback chub work will go into a doctoral dissertation (R. T. Muth, 1984-85).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** The Colorado River site was the [[Black Rocks]] area of Ruby Canyon (river km 219.8 to 218.2) and the stratum H and K reaches from Loma to the state line. The Yampa River site covered strata 1 to 5, from Cross Mountain Canyon through Lily Park to the lower Dinosaur National Monument reach.

**Sampling methods.**
- *Seines:* used for YOY and juvenile capture. Most seine specimens came from low-velocity habitats.
- *Drift nets:* 0.5-m diameter conical plankton nets (560 µm mesh, 4 m long, mouth-to-mesh ratio 11:1), set near shore. Drift nets were used in 1982 to sample swift, deep water that seines miss, to measure ichthyoplankton abundance, and to test drift as a dispersal route. Sampling was at dawn, dusk, and midnight in the Colorado study area (30 min to 1 h per set) and was roughly balanced between night and day in the Yampa study area (30 min to 7 h). Flow was measured with a Marsh-McBirney flowmeter or pygmy flowmeter.
- *Preservation and analysis:* specimens were fixed in 10% formalin, transferred to 3% buffered formalin, identified to the lowest feasible taxon, and measured to 0.1 mm total length. Because YOY and juvenile chubs could not yet be reliably separated, all chubs were recorded as *Gila* spp. Drift rates were expressed per 1,000 m³. A nonparametric permutation test (Mielke and Iyer 1982) compared sampling times on the Colorado data only.

**Spawning-related conditions.** Most fishes in these reaches spawn from early June to mid-August. Earlier work showed larval drift density was higher along shorelines than mid-channel, and the drift design reflects this. Spawning dates matched decreasing flows and rising water temperatures. Growth rate is temperature-dependent ([[De Vlaming]] 1972), so late-August water temperatures likely slowed growth and pushed late spawning estimates forward.

## Key Findings

**1. Sampling effort and data status (1982)**
- A total of 694 collections: 384 in the Colorado River (5 March to 27 August 1982) and 310 in the Yampa River (18 April to 23 October 1982).
- 46 Yampa samples from 8 to 11 June were lost in a rafting accident.
- The MANAGE database holds records from 17 March 1981 onward (Table 2). Results will be checked and separated by study area before final analysis.

**2. Young-of-year Colorado squawfish**
- Colorado River: 16 YOY collected. Two were drift-netted on 27 July at Black Rocks; 14 were seined on 23 August at eight sites between km 216.1 and 244.4. All seine specimens came from low-velocity habitats (backwaters, embayments, a concavity).
- Yampa River: 20 YOY collected between 7 and 25 August at 10 sites between km 0.5 and 19.6 (stratum 1), mostly in backwaters and pools.
- Age at capture was 5 to 15 days post-spawning for drift specimens (27 July) and 9 to 45 days for seined specimens (Colorado).
- Estimated spawning: Colorado 13 to 23 July (drift) and 10 July to 15 August (seine); Yampa 6 to 28 July 1982.

**3. Multi-year YOY trends (1979 to 1982)**
- Colorado River: 102 YOY collected (89 in Stratum H, 13 in Stratum K).
- Yampa River: 89 YOY collected during 1980 to 1982, all in the lowermost 30 km of Dinosaur National Monument (Stratum 1). Stratum 3 was sampled in 1979.
- Estimated spawning ranged from 18 June 1981 to 26 August 1980 (Colorado) and from 16 June 1980 to 3 August 1981 (Yampa). In 1981 the Yampa estimate of 25 June agreed with radio-marked adults observed ripe over spawning gravels by [[Tyus et al.]] (26 June to 10 July, km 19.6 to 0.2).

**4. Temperature and flow as spawning cues**
- Except for 1979, spawning matched the earliest date that water temperature reached or exceeded 22 °C (Table 5).
- In 1979 the Colorado River may have spawned as early as 29 June at about 18 °C. This suggests either that the temperature gauge did not represent the spawning site or that some fish spawned at lower temperatures.
- A clear link between decreasing flow and spawning timing exists, but a quantified relationship between flow level, flow duration, and spawning success remains unclear. Analysis awaits 1983 data.

**5. Drift (ichthyoplankton) collections, 1982**
- 258 specimens were taken in 61 drift samples (48 Colorado, 13 Yampa), mostly larvae. Juvenile and adult forms made up under 3%.
- These represented 12 species in 4 families. Six species were native, making up 77% of the total.
- Colorado River: 118 specimens, 10 species, 4 families. The dominant natives were [[Bluehead Sucker]] (29%), [[Speckled Dace]] (27%), and unidentified *Gila* spp. (25%). [[Channel Catfish]] were the most abundant non-native (6%).
- Yampa River: 140 specimens, 7 species, 3 families. *Gila* spp. made up 53% of the catch; channel catfish made up 34%.
- Two larval Colorado squawfish were drift-caught on 27 July at Black Rocks. This was the first drift-net capture of larval squawfish in the upper basin, though larvae had earlier been taken with fine-mesh seines and dipnets.

**6. Diel drift patterns**
- Colorado River, all taxa combined: dawn:dusk:midnight density ratio of about 3:1:1 (P = 0.1476).
- *Gila* spp.: strongly higher at dawn, about 11:1:1 (P = 0.0064).
- [[Speckled Dace]]: about 4:1:2 (P = 0.1774), not significant.
- [[Bluehead Sucker]]: no difference among sampling times (P = 0.8562).
- Yampa River data were too limited for the same test.

**7. Razorback sucker:** No YOY or juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] were identified in either study area in 1982.

**8. 1983 plan**
- Intensive drift sampling in the Yampa River, with stations near Harding Hole (lower stratum 2) and at Boxelder Campground (stratum 1). Sampling will run up to 2 days per site with 1 to 2 hour samples at dawn, noon, dusk, and midnight.
- Regular YOY sampling continues in both study areas. Table 9 lists the full schedule, from May through November for the Colorado River and May through September for the Yampa River.
- A known-age larval series of [[Roundtail Chub]] will be made from upper Yampa or White River brood stock. Adults will be collected in mid-June, injected on site with acetone-dried carp pituitary extract, and their eggs stripped, fertilized, and water-hardened. Eggs and larvae will be reared at Colorado State University.
- Habitat association analyses for species other than squawfish and the morphomeristic analysis of *Gila* spp. are not finished and will be reported in the final report and dissertation.
