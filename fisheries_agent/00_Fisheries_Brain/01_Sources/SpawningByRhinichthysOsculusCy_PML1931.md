# Spawning by Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*) in the San Francisco River, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a cover letter and a short natural-history note on spawning by [[Speckled Dace]] (*[[Rhinichthys Osculus]]*, Cyprinidae) in the [[San Francisco River]], New Mexico. The letter, from a regional director of the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] to an associate editor of [[Southwestern Naturalist]], forwards the final draft of a note by [[Gordon A Mueller]]. The note reports mass spawning of several hundred dace on June 2-3, 1981, on a fresh, disturbed streambed created by construction of a farm diversion dam. The author proposes that physical disturbance (scour, turbidity, flow variation) is a major cue for reproduction in this species, and suggests that such cues could be used to stimulate spawning during drought or below regulated reservoirs.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The speckled dace is described as the most widely distributed and ubiquitous fish in the western United States, citing [[Moyle]] (1976). Despite this, little was known about its reproductive behavior or the environmental cues that trigger spawning.
- **Reporting context:** The letter is a transmittal for a note submitted for publication. It is not a research report in itself, and it contains no standalone statistical analysis.
- **Author affiliation:** Gordon A. Mueller, Environmental Sciences Branch, Bureau of Reclamation, Boulder City, Nevada.
- **Study site:** The San Francisco River flows south for 208 km from the San Francisco Mountains (Arizona-New Mexico) and enters the [[Gila River]] near Clifton, Arizona. Its discharge is bimodal: a brief, high spring snowmelt runoff, a long drought, and a midsummer period of rain-driven spates. Spring 1981 runoff was unusually low, following a long period of below-normal discharge (U.S. Geological Survey, *Water Resources Data for Arizona*, 1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning site**
- The site was a 1 m² section of shallow stream, located downstream of a farmer's newly constructed earthen diversion dam and canal, 4.8 km upstream from Reserve, Catron County, New Mexico.
- Construction scoured a 15 m reach of the 5 m wide channel.
- Substrate ranged from 1.0 to 5.5 cm in diameter, with most greater than 2.5 cm.
- Spawning activity and ova were observed to depths of 5 cm. No larvae or fry were found.
- Observations were made with binoculars from 10-12 m between 1000 and 1500 hours MST, followed by close examination of the site. Photographs are held by the Bureau of Reclamation, Boulder City, Nevada.

**Reach-level pattern**
- Dace were found throughout the river system, but reproductive behavior occurred only in the disturbed reach immediately below the diversion dam.
- Other reaches of the streambed were overgrown with diatoms, filamentous algae, and macrophytes.
- The author surveyed similar substrates, including deeper pools, riffles, and adjacent shorelines, and found no eggs. No additional scoured sections or spawning sites were found in the surveyed area.

**Comparison with earlier studies**
- [[John]] (*Copeia*, 1963) described spawning in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]], Arizona, with redd preparation by males and territorial behavior that gave way to group spawning. Eggs were never visible on the upper surfaces of stones in that study.
- [[Hinckley]] (1973) and John (1963) reported "west depressions" (redds). None were present at the San Francisco River site, and no purposeful substrate excavation was seen.
- Matthews et al. (*Copeia*, 1982) argued that some reported behavioral differences in the *R. atratulus* complex may have only local bases.

## Key Findings

1. **Mass spawning under disturbed conditions.** Several hundred speckled dace spawned on June 2-3, 1981, in a scoured, shallow, partly exposed area, with more than one active spawning "knot" at a given time. Several spawning clusters of more than 25 dace each occupied a 1 m² area.
2. **Physical disturbance as a cue.** Construction of the temporary diversion dam likely provided flooding-type cues (turbidity, scour, flow variation). The author proposes that these triggered local spawning.
3. **Spawning behavior.** Dace thrashed in mass on partly exposed rubble, and many spawners were partly or totally out of the water. Presumed females rested in deeper water nearby. Eggs (ca. 1 mm diameter) were scattered over the area of greatest activity. Most adhered to concealed surfaces within interstices.
4. **Spawning evidence.** Decomposing dace on shore suggested spawning had been under way for a few days. Ova were also found along banks 25 cm from the water.
5. **Behavioral deviations.** No territorial behavior or individual pairs were observed. Dace returned to the spawning area in groups of 7 to 20 individuals within 30 minutes after disturbance. Males moved along the shore and were joined by females to resume spawning.
6. **Limited spatial scope.** Spawning was restricted to the scoured area (1 m² spawning area within a 15 m scoured reach). The author examined the surrounding river system and found no other scoured sections with spawning.
7. **Hydrologic suppression.** Low precipitation and the resulting lack of natural scour, turbidity, and discharge variation are believed to have suppressed reproduction, consistent with John's observation that spawning is scattered and weak after a dry winter.
8. **Multiple spawning seasons.** Some populations spawn twice a year: after spring snowmelt runoff and after summer rain floods. In years of unusually low spring runoff, spawning effort is scattered and weak.
9. **Related species.** Spawning in response to flooding has been proposed for [[Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus phebeius*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), and speckled dace (Koster, 1963; Kepner, unpublished M.S. thesis, ASU, 1982). Other *Rhinichthys* taxa (*R. atratulus* and the *R. cataractae* complex) share broadly similar spawning on clean, shallow substrate, though behavior varies by taxon and season.
10. **Management implication.** The author proposes that flooding cues may be important for other native southwestern fishes. Mimicking these cues (mechanical scour, dam releases) could help stimulate spawning during prolonged drought or below regulated reservoirs.
11. **Limitations.** Most earlier reports come from a single season, so their conclusions apply only to those environmental conditions. The present study covers a single disturbance event, and its generality remains untested.
