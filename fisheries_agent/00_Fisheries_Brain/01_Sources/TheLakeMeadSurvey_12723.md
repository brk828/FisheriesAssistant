# The 1963-64 Lake Mead Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reservoir-sedimentation

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-OCE-70-21, August 1970, by J. M. Lara and J. I. Sanders) documents the 1963-64 resurvey of [[Lake Mead]], the reservoir formed by [[Hoover Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]]. The study updated reservoir area and capacity at the time [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963, and combined a geodetic (releveling) survey, a hydrographic survey, sediment sampling and laboratory testing, and a quantitative sedimentation analysis. The report finds that sediment accumulation since 1948-49 reduced the reservoir volume below elevation 1229 feet by 1,292,000 acre-feet, and that subsidence or rebound of the basin had an insignificant effect on capacity.

The document contains no fish, species, or biological survey data. Its aquatic content concerns reservoir hydrology, bathymetry, and sediment physics; it is therefore not a fish ecology study.

## Background & Description

- **Reservoir:** Lake Mead was formed by Hoover Dam, completed in 1935, in Black Canyon, about 30 miles east of Las Vegas. Original capacity was 32,471,000 acre-feet at elevation 1229 feet.
- **Motivation:** The 1948-49 survey, the first comprehensive resurvey, was prompted by the need to know capacity loss from sediment. The 1963-64 survey was designed to update capacity at the closure of Glen Canyon Dam, about 370 miles upstream.
- **Agencies:** A June 1963 cooperative agreement between the Departments of the Interior and Commerce assigned the hydrographic work to the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey. Bureau of Reclamation offices in Denver and Boulder City, Nevada prepared the analyses.
- **Geology:** The reservoir lies in the [[Basin and Range Province]] at the edge of the [[Colorado Plateau]], bounded by the [[Grand Wash Fault]]. The fault is traced for more than 110 miles, with the west side believed to have dropped about 16,000 feet relative to the east.
- **Survey extent:** First-order releveling covered about 340 miles of level lines. Field work ran from July 9, 1963 to October 14, 1964.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sediment regime:** The Colorado River near Grand Canyon, about 270 miles upstream, carried a measured 3,025 million tons of suspended sediment from 1935 to 1963 (excluding November 1942 through September 1943), an average of 108 million tons per year. Glen Canyon Dam closure in March 1963 shifted much sediment deposition to [[Lake Powell]].
- **Sediment yield:** The report judges that a reliable sediment yield rate cannot be determined with practical accuracy. Using an assumed 10 percent unmeasured load, a 118.8 million ton annual load converts to about 90,900 acre-feet per year, compared with 91,450 acre-feet per year from the survey.
- **Trap efficiency:** Estimated at 98 percent from the Brune curve at a capacity-inflow ratio of 2.56, and judged effectively 100 percent for practical purposes.
- **Subsidence and rebound:** Subsidence averaged 118 mm at Hoover Dam over 1935-63. Maximum subsidence exceeded 700 mm in [[Las Vegas Valley]], attributed to groundwater removal. Rebound since 1949 varied from 0 to 40 mm in Boulder and Virgin Basins.
- **Hydrographic conditions:** Low lake levels in 1963 prevented surface watercraft from reaching the Pierce Ferry delta, requiring an amphibian to transport men and equipment into Lower Granite Gorge.

## Key Findings

**Capacity and sedimentation**
- Sediment volume accumulated since 1948-49 totals 1,292,000 acre-feet at elevation 1229 feet, an average rate of 80,750 acre-feet per year.
- Total capacity loss since 1935 is 2,716,000 acre-feet, an annual rate of 91,450 acre-feet.
- About 47 percent (1.24 million acre-feet) of total sediment since 1935 lies in the upper Colorado River reach (Grand Bay, Pierce Basin, Lower Granite Gorge). Overton Arm holds about 4 percent (120,000 acre-feet).
- Lower Granite Gorge and parts of Boulder Basin showed a capacity gain since 1948-49 as profiles dipped, attributed to lake-level lowering, increased hydraulic gradient, and consolidation.
- Estimated representative sediment texture: 60 percent clay, 28 percent silt, 12 percent sand.
- Representative dry unit weight: about 60 lb/ft³, supported by a separate estimate of 47 lb/ft³ initial weight and a 30-year consolidation estimate of 60 lb/ft³.

**Geodetic findings**
- Releveling covered 340 miles, with an average adjustment of 0.35 mm/km and a maximum of 0.85 mm/km on a line of appreciable size.
- Maximum allowable misclosure was 3.0 mm√K, where K is run length in kilometers.
- Changes in capacity from subsidence or rebound were judged insignificant. The 200 mm maximum divergence (0.66 foot) lies within the survey's accuracy limits.

**Sediment sampling and testing**
- Sampling took place at 17 reservoir locations, with 215 laboratory samples collected in 1963 and 1964.
- Drive sampling at drill hole DH-1 in Pierce Basin reached 207 feet, about 35 feet short of the 1935 river bed. Of 46 open-drive attempts, 43 succeeded, with 93 percent average recovery.
- Piston-core sampling collected 19 cores from 16 locations, with 90 percent average recovery, producing 121 laboratory samples.
- Gamma probing at 15 sites reached an average depth of 21.7 feet, with a maximum of 40.5 feet at the upstream mouth of Boulder Canyon.
- Particle-size results from hydrometer and pipet methods were compatible, so either could be used.
- Mineralogy was dominated by a mixed illite-montmorillonite clay, with kaolinite a minor constituent and no gypsum observed.

**Conclusions**
- The 1963-64 survey provided updated area and capacity tables (Tables 3-5 and 3-6) for reservoir operations and planning, described as the largest reservoir on the Lower Colorado River System.
- Reservoir capacity at elevation 1229 feet fell to 29,755,000 acre-feet by October 1964, a storage-to-inflow ratio of 2.56.
