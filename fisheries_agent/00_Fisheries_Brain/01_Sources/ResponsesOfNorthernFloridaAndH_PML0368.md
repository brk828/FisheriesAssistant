# Responses of Northern, Florida, and Hybrid Largemouth Bass to Low Temperature and Low Dissolved Oxygen

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study compares two subspecies of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), the northern (*M. s. salmoides*) and Florida (*M. s. floridanus*) forms, together with their reciprocal F1 hybrids. Subspecies identity was verified by [[Protein Electrophoresis]]. The fish were tested for survival and feeding under low temperature and for physiological tolerance of low dissolved oxygen (DO) in [[Sealed-Jar Hypoxia Bioassay]] trials. The work was carried out at the San Marcos National Fish Hatchery and the Aquatic Station of Southwest Texas State University, with authors affiliated with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The authors argue that strain differences matter for hatchery production and stocking success, and that hybridization may explain inconsistent results in earlier studies.

## Background & Description

Largemouth bass are cultured in hatcheries for stocking and aquaculture. Both subspecies are stocked into temperate and subtropical waters that can differ strongly from hatchery conditions. Their historical ranges are implied by their common names, but an intergrade zone north and east of the Gulf of Mexico has been blurred by stocking. Many transplants succeeded, while others failed, possibly because of differing subspecies biology. Earlier attempts to separate the subspecies by survival or growth gave variable results. The authors suggest this may reflect inadequate verification of subspecies identity, since the two forms are morphologically similar but distinguishable by protein electrophoresis.

The study was framed around stress tolerance, since adaptability measured by stress responses affects survival after stocking. The authors measured:
- Survival and feeding behavior at low temperature.
- Residual DO at death, time to death, and average metabolic rate in sealed-jar tests.
- Effects of [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222), salts, ammonia, and temperature on hypoxia response.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental setting.** Fish were reared in open ponds, then held in 740-L tanks at 22 °C, pH 7.3, DO 5–6 mg/L, and hardness of 300 mg/L as CaCO3. Food was withheld for 24 h before experiments.

**Thermal challenge.** Simulated sudden weather changes or conditions in [[Power-Plant Impoundments]] were tested. Temperature was lowered about 1 °C/day for 20 d to 1 °C, then raised to 21 °C within 90 min. A second regime held fish at 2.0 ± 0.4 °C (mean ± SE) for 5 d to simulate severe winter conditions.

**Hypoxia tests.** Individual age-0 fish were placed in sealed jars (510 or 960 mL) starting near 100% saturation (8.1–9.4 mg/L). Death was defined as cessation of opercular movement and convulsive seizure.

**Water chemistry.** Ammonia was measured by direct nesslerization, DO and temperature by meters, and hardness by titration. Test additives were:
- Salts: NaCl (6,090 mg/L), KCl (250 mg/L), KH2PO4 (163 mg/L), and MgSO4 (118 mg/L).
- MS-222 at 25 mg/L.
- Un-ionized ammonia at 0.70 mg/L, near the 24-h median lethal concentration for largemouth bass.

## Key Findings

**Low-temperature survival**
- Fish cooled from 21 °C to 1 °C, held briefly, and rewarmed all survived and fed normally within 24 h.
- During the 5-d hold at 2.0 ± 0.4 °C, mortality was 48% for Florida (F × F) fish and 45% for the Florida-female hybrid (F × N). Northern (N × N) and northern-female hybrid (N × F) fish had zero mortality.
- Feeding slowed as temperatures fell to 12 and 6 °C, and no fish fed below 6 °C.

**Hypoxia response (residual DO at death)**
- At 22 °C acclimation, residual DO was lower for N × N and F × F fish than for hybrids, and lower for northern than Florida fish.
- At 14 °C acclimation, residual DO was similar across all strains.
- Adding salts, MS-222, or ammonia increased residual DO for all strains. Among ammonia-treated fish, residual DO at death was higher for F × F than N × N fish.

**Average metabolic rate**
- Metabolic rates were lower at 14 °C than at 22 °C, and were lowered by salts and MS-222.
- Ammonia had no detectable effect on metabolic rate, suggesting its toxicity is not directly metabolic.
- N × N fish had lower average metabolic rates than other strains, untreated or ammonia-treated.

**Oxygen uptake dynamics**
- In the first 30 min of sealed-jar tests (n = 3 per strain), F × F fish consumed more oxygen than N × N fish. Uptake curves suggest an initial agitated phase followed by fatigue and reduced activity as DO declined.
- Afterwards, oxygen consumption was similar between the two groups.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Strains differ in temperature and hypoxia responses, so managers should consider strain when choosing fish for stocking.
- Hybridization could explain inconsistent earlier results.
- Low temperature, salts, and anesthetics reduce metabolic rate in all strains, which may aid handling and transport.
- Sealed-jar hypoxia bioassays are a practical way to assess toxicant effects on fish, consistent with earlier work on [[Rainbow Trout]] exposed to pulp-mill effluent.
