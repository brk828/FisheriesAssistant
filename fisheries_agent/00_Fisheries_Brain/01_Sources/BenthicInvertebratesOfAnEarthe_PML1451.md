# Benthic Invertebrates of an Earthen Canal in the Phoenix Metropolitan Area, Arizona

#macroinvertebrates #freshwater-ecology #oligochaeta

## Executive Summary

This study describes the benthic macroinvertebrate community of a 100-m reach of [[Lateral 9.5 Canal]], an earthen irrigation canal in [[Mesa Arizona]] within the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]]. Sampling ran from November 1981 through November 1982. The community was dominated by non-insects, chiefly [[Oligochaeta]] and the introduced [[Corbicula Fluminea]] (Asiatic clam). Insects made up only about 4.5% of the fauna. The invertebrate community was similar in composition to that of the larger [[Coachella Canal]] in southeastern California, but both canal faunas are far less rich than natural low-elevation Arizona streams. The author suggests the invertebrates may serve as forage for canal fishes.

## Background & Description

Artificial waterways now make up a major share of lotic habitat in American arid lands. Southwestern canals support taxonomically and numerically depauperate invertebrate communities, yet they can hold substantial fish populations of potential importance to people. This paper is part of a continuing program evaluating the aquatic potential of canal habitats in the Southwest.

The study canal is primarily a conveyance for irrigation and floodwater return flows. It is partially dewatered each autumn as part of a system-wide maintenance program. Physical characteristics reported:

- **Cross-section:** trapezoidal, side slopes 1.5:1
- **Mean water width:** 7.7 m
- **Mid-channel depth:** reported as approximately 0.9 m (OCR-degraded)
- **Mean velocity:** 0.07 m/s
- **Substrate:** predominantly silt, with minor sand and rare fine gravel
- **Water temperature (midday):** 12.5 °C (January) to 30.5 °C (September)
- **Algae:** abundant in autumn and winter, including [[Cladophora Glomerata]], *Chara*, *Oedogonium*, *Oscillatoria*, and diatoms
- **Macrophytes:** *Myriophyllum spicatum*, *Potamogeton pectinatus*, and *Zannichellia palustris*, present but rare
- **Riparian vegetation:** bermuda grass (*Cynodon dactylon*)

**Sampling methods.** The canal was sampled 11 times between November 1981 and November 1982. On each date, five replicate samples were taken with a 232 cm² [[Ekman Dredge]]. Samples were sieved through 500 µm mesh, preserved in 10% formalin, and sorted, identified, and enumerated in the laboratory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community composition.** The fauna of the study reach contained the taxa listed in the paper's Table 1 (the OCR text is partly illegible; roughly 13 named taxa are identifiable). These include mayflies, damselflies and dragonflies, a riffle beetle, a caddisfly family, midges, oligochaetes, planorbid snails, the Asiatic clam, leeches, and a crayfish. Non-insects made up 94.5% of individuals.

**Comparison with other systems.** The Coachella Canal has fewer taxa than natural streams. Natural Arizona streams at similar elevation support more than 100 taxa, dominated by aquatic insects such as mayflies and Chironomidae. Sonoran Desert spring habitats, though also considered depauperate, may contain two to three times as many species as earthen canals. The author attributes low richness in canals mainly to structural habitat homogeneity, which offers less diversity than even natural springs.

**Disturbance.** Two management-related perturbations may limit diversity: annual dewatering and the intentional or inadvertent application of herbicides. The Coachella Canal, which has similar fauna, is not subject to these impacts. Neither canal shows evidence of substrate scour and invertebrate loss during high flows, unlike natural desert streams subject to flash floods (see [[Fisher Et Al 1982]]).

**Fish associations.** The canal supported substantial populations of introduced [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish). Other small canals in the Phoenix area held only this species and [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*). Large main-channel canal reaches support 16 to 22 fish species. The author argues food availability does not explain this difference. Likelier causes include habitat homogeneity, structural barriers to fish access in small canals, lack of refugia from herbicides, and poor water quality.

**Recreational and management implications.** Aquatic life is relatively abundant despite management activities intended to eliminate biological systems. As natural surface waters in the region decline and demand for nearby recreation grows, canal waters are expected to face recreational pressure. The author calls for studies of water quality, community structure, and distribution before urban canals are managed for recreation. Canals are considered good study systems because the sampling constraints of habitat diversity are largely absent.

## Key Findings

- **Total density:** mean monthly standing stock was 14,802 organisms/m², with monthly means ranging from 2,670 to 36,486/m².
- **Dominant taxa:**
  - Oligochaeta (*Aeolosoma* sp. and *Chaetogaster* sp.): 10,922/m² (73.8%)
  - *Corbicula fluminea*: 2,777/m² (18.8%)
  - Planorbidae: 288/m² (1.9%)
  - Chironomidae (*Cricotopus* sp. and *Chironomus* sp.): 665/m² (4.5%)
  - *Callibaetis* sp. (mayfly): 83/m² (0.6%)
- **Minor taxa:** Hirudinea (6/m²), *Procambarus clarkii* (2/m², <0.1%), and several odonates, a riffle beetle, and a caddisfly family, each under 1%.
- **Seasonal recruitment:**
  - Oligochaeta peaked in February and July, when mean individual size was small, indicating recruitment.
  - *Corbicula fluminea* was densest in February, with no secondary peak.
  - Chironomidae and *Callibaetis* sp. both peaked in November, driven by small individuals and suggesting a synchronized hatching period.
  - Larger individuals of major taxa were generally present only at times of peak abundance.
- **Faunal similarity:** species composition and relative abundances were generally similar to the Coachella Canal, though the Phoenix canal had fewer taxa.
- **Richness contrast:** canal faunas are dramatically less diverse than natural Arizona streams, which can exceed 100 taxa.
- **Fish:** the canal's fish community was limited to mosquitofish and red shiner, well below the 16 to 22 species typical of large canals.
- **Conclusion:** the aquatic community is relatively abundant despite management disturbance. Canals may become important recreational resources, and water quality and community studies are needed before they are managed as such.
