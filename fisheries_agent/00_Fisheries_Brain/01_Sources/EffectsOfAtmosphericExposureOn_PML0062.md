# Effects of Atmospheric Exposure on Epilithon Chlorophyll a, Biomass and Productivity in a Tailwater River

#phycology #freshwater-ecology #cladophora

## Executive Summary

This experimental study (Angradi and Kubly, *Regulated Rivers: Research & Management*, 1993) tests how stranding by air exposure affects the epilithon (periphyton on cobbles) of the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], Arizona. The epilithon is dominated by the filamentous green alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]]. Across six experiments run in 1991 and 1992, the authors found that chlorophyll a is far more sensitive to exposure than biomass, that gross primary productivity (GPP) falls with exposure, and that recovery after reinundation is slow. They conclude that the epilithon has low resistance and low resilience, and that losing it could affect higher trophic levels in the Glen Canyon food web.

## Background & Description

Most published work on periphyton disturbance concerns floods in unregulated streams. In regulated rivers, however, flow reductions can strand and desiccate periphyton. Periphyton is generally poorly adapted to stranding caused by large, unnatural stage fluctuations, even though flow regulation can enhance growth through altered physical and chemical conditions.

The study site is the epilithon of the Glen Canyon tailwater, which is dominated by [[Cladophora Glomerata]] and can reach high biomass. Its importance to trophic levels in Glen and [[Grand Canyon]] has been recognized, but the effects of dam operations on its ecological role were poorly understood at the time.

Methods were standardized across experiments:

- **Sampling:** Cobbles were removed from the river. A randomly located 4.15 cm² circle was scraped on each cobble. Authors note this probably slightly overestimates biomass per m² of littoral bottom.
- **Chlorophyll a:** Experiments I–III (1991) used ground samples extracted in 90% acetone. Experiments IV–V (1992) used unground samples boiled in methanol, which extracts more pigment, so absolute values were not compared across methods.
- **Biomass:** Ash-free dry mass (AFDM) was determined by loss on ignition (550 °C, two hours).
- **Light:** Photosynthetically available radiation (PAR, 400–700 nm) was logged with [[Li-Cor]] quantum sensors. Cumulative PAR (mol m⁻²) served as an index of solar exposure.
- **Statistics:** Data were log-transformed to correct heteroscedasticity. One-way ANOVA with Tukey comparisons was used, and analysis of covariance (with PAR as covariate) for productivity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study zones.** Two littoral zones were compared: the permanently inundated channel and the zone of daily water-level fluctuation. Cladophora colonization in the fluctuating zone was very slow and was probably reset by occasional exposure.

**Exposure regime.** Exposure in this tailwater is mostly nocturnal under normal operations. The authors note that Cladophora appears to have a competitive advantage under daily night-time exposure, but it is not resistant to prolonged daytime exposure, especially in summer.

**Habitat role.** In Glen Canyon, Cladophora provides habitat for trout and macroinvertebrates. [[Gammarus Lacustris]] was about eight times more abundant in the permanently inundated zone, where Cladophora biomass was high, than in the fluctuating zone where it was largely absent. [[Rainbow Trout]] is the most abundant fish species in Glen Canyon. Stable isotope data indicated that consumed carbon for the amphipod at 14 Mile Bar came from diatoms rather than from Oscillatoria, terrestrial detritus, or seston exported from [[Lake Powell]].

**Habitat scale.** Cobble bars make up only about 16% of total littoral habitat in Glen Canyon. The rest is largely sandy substrate with low chlorophyll a (about 10–20 mg m⁻²), so cobble-bar epilithon is disproportionately important.

**Flow thresholds.** Reach-wide littoral GPP was modelled as roughly linear with minimum flow. Loss of reach-wide GPP would rise sharply at flows below about 140 m³ s⁻¹ (the study's values include 566 m³ s⁻¹ as a reference level).

**Competing taxa.** Early colonizers and stress-tolerant filamentous taxa such as Oscillatoria, and the chlorophyte [[Ulothrix Tenuissima]], are more tolerant of exposure. [[Ulothrix Tenuissima]] rapidly colonized sandstone tiles in the sluiceway but is not abundant in Glen Canyon. Filamentous macroalgae other than Cladophora are rare in the tailwater.

## Key Findings

**Sensitivity of chlorophyll versus biomass**
- Chlorophyll a was much more sensitive to exposure than AFDM. Bleached filaments kept most of their biomass and fragmented incrementally after reinundation.
- Exposure caused a dramatic chlorophyll loss, but gross physical structure appeared largely intact.
- Significant chlorophyll reductions occurred for daytime exposures as short as six hours.
- Chlorophyll remaining after one day of exposure did not differ significantly from remaining after two days.

**Dose-response relationships**
- Inverse relationships between time exposed or cumulative PAR (400–700 nm) and percentage of initial chlorophyll a remaining were strong (r² > 0.73).
- In the short term, the authors propose a continuous inverse relationship. Models were fitted to percentage chlorophyll remaining, with the one-week model r² = 0.75 (exact coefficients are given in the source figure caption).
- Chlorophyll sensitivity appeared to shift with exposure duration. Eight-hour exposure produced greater loss than shorter exposures, while high irradiance destroyed pigment even in filaments with overlying protection.

**Zone differences**
- Epilithon from the permanently inundated channel had up to about 10 times more chlorophyll a than epilithon from the fluctuating zone.
- Chlorophyll loss in the fluctuating zone was less pronounced in some tests, possibly because that epilithon was already adapted to periodic exposure.

**Reinundation and recovery**
- In the sluiceway experiment (experiment II), chlorophyll a in exposed epilithon began to increase after 24 hours of reinundation.
- Biomass loss was significant only after two weeks. Epilithon exposed for 24 hours had 40% less biomass than unexposed cobbles.
- In situ work at [[14 Mile Bar]] (experiment III) confirmed the sluiceway findings. Exposure effects on chlorophyll were significant at all sample dates (after one day, one week and two weeks).
- Experiment III reported no difference between 24-hour and 48-hour exposures on any date.
- Colonization of sandstone tiles was rapid. Chlorophyll a accrued at about 50 mg m⁻² day⁻¹ and biomass at about 8 g AFDM m⁻² day⁻¹ between days 5 and 15.
- Field recolonization of introduced substrates at 14 Mile Bar would take four to six months to reach natural chlorophyll levels in the permanently inundated zone, and longer in the fluctuating zone.

**Productivity**
- Exposure reduced GPP in both zones, but did not greatly alter the assimilation ratio (GPP per unit chlorophyll a), which appeared to be a roughly constant epilithon property.
- Unexposed cobbles from the permanently inundated zone had significantly higher GPP than exposed cobbles (F₁,₁₀ = 65.8, p < 0.001).
- PAR explained much more variation in GPP and AR for unexposed cobbles (34–82%) than for exposed cobbles (0.09–0.19%), probably because exposed epilithon saturated at lower light.
- A simple model combining cumulative PAR and initial chlorophyll a predicted GPP in reasonable agreement with published studies.

**Management implications**
- Epilithon has low resistance to exposure and low resilience under hydropower operations, so recovery is protracted.
- Disruption of Cladophora, a key structural habitat for invertebrates, could cascade to higher trophic levels.
- The authors call for better understanding of secondary production on cobble bars versus other habitats, and of trophic linkages in the Glen Canyon ecosystem, before the full ecological impact can be evaluated.
