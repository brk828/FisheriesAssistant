# Golden Rivers and Treasure Valleys: Wealth from Wasted Waters

#water-resources #regional-development #historical-proposal #irrigation-projects

## Executive Summary

This 1935 advance print document by George Hebard Maxwell presents an ambitious proposal for water conservation and land reclamation in Arizona and California through comprehensive development of the [[Colorado River]]. The author advocates for the "Arizona Ten Projects"—ten regional development zones each centered on local water sources—to be supplemented by a massive water diversion system from the Colorado River via the Glen Canyon Dam and associated canal infrastructure. Maxwell frames this as both an economic development opportunity and a matter of national security, arguing against allowing Colorado River water to flow into Mexico. The document interweaves personal narrative (the story of "The Argonaut," Maxwell's father, a California Gold Rush pioneer) with technical proposals for dams, canals, forest plantations, and rural settlement systems. The proposal emphasizes non-competitive agriculture, reforestation for rainfall enhancement, and the creation of small family farms through what Maxwell calls the "Rural Settlements System."

## Background & Description

The document opens with an introductory summary listing ten Arizona projects: [[Tombstone]], [[San Simon]], [[Solomonville]], [[Springerville]], [[Holbrook]], [[Ash Fork]], [[Wickenburg]], [[Salome]], [[Gila Bend]], and [[Tucson]]. Each project is described as having its own "Golden River" (local water source) and "Treasure Valley" (irrigable land). Maxwell proposes that these projects would be anchored by a massive water conservation system centered on Glen Canyon Dam—described as "TEN TIMES BIGGER THAN THE ROOSEVELT DAM"—with tunnels and canals to transport Colorado River water throughout Arizona.

The technical proposal includes:
- Glen Canyon Dam for storage and power generation (334,000 horsepower)
- Bridge Canyon Dam for diversion (1,000,000 horsepower)
- Arizona-California Highline Canal running from Bridge Canyon southward
- Development of 15,280,000 acres in Arizona and 2,000,000 acres in Southern California
- Planting of 10,000,000 acres of "rain-making forests"
- 5,280,000 acres for intensive "garden-farms" in 5-10 acre parcels

Maxwell advocates for the [[Rural Settlements System]], modeled after Mormon colonies and California's "Littlelands Movement," featuring small family farms with intensive diversified cultivation. He proposes coordination between the [[U.S. Soil Conservation Service]], [[Forest Service]], and [[Civilian Conservation Corps]] to train workers as "forest-farmers and Homecrofters."

The document extensively discusses the [[Gold Bluff Mine]] discovery by Maxwell's father during the California Gold Rush, using this narrative to establish credibility and draw parallels between gold mining and water development as sources of wealth creation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Maxwell's proposal reflects 1930s-era thinking about environmental manipulation and resource development, with several notable ecological claims:

**Rainfall Enhancement**: The author repeatedly asserts that planting 10,000,000 acres of forest in Arizona would increase regional rainfall, stating that "rain clouds from those new forests would in time precipitate in the midwest region where drouth has taken such a terrible toll." This represents early 20th-century beliefs about forest-rainfall relationships that have since been substantially revised by modern climatology.

**Watershed Development**: The proposal identifies major Arizona watersheds for comprehensive development: [[Little Colorado River]], [[Bill Williams River]], [[Hassayampa River]], [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[Gila River]], [[Santa Cruz River]], and numerous washes. Maxwell advocates for the "Newlands-Olmsted plan" emphasizing:
- Conservation of natural rainfall
- Flood water capture
- Underground water utilization
- Supplementation with Colorado River water

**Reflow and Return Seepage**: The document emphasizes capturing "reflow from return seepage" where irrigation water percolates through soil and returns to surface waters. Maxwell claims this would "more than double" water availability in Arizona.

**Erosion Control**: The text describes severe erosion in valleys like [[San Simon Valley]], where "wheel tracks have become barrancas, and barrancas have widened into huge washes, and wide plains are honeycombed with deep, cavernous cuts." Maxwell advocates for the [[U.S. Soil Conservation Service]] work to address these issues.

**Climate and Elevation**: The San Simon Valley is described as having "the best climate of any section of Arizona" due to elevation tempering heat. Specific elevations are provided: Ash Fork (5,143 feet), Prescott (5,367 feet), Flagstaff (6,896 feet), Wickenburg (2,116 feet).

**Non-Competitive Crops**: Maxwell emphasizes "non-competitive" agriculture, particularly tree crops (dates, olives, figs, avocados, nuts) to avoid market competition, citing Prof. J. Russell Smith's "Tree Crops, a Permanent Agriculture."

## Key Findings

**Water Development Proposals**:
- Glen Canyon Dam: 334,000 horsepower capacity, serving as primary storage and power source
- Bridge Canyon Dam: 1,000,000 horsepower capacity for diversion
- Total Colorado River hydroelectric potential in Arizona: over 5,000,000 horsepower
- Arizona-California Highline Canal: gravity-fed system from Bridge Canyon to distribution points
- Proposed pump lift of 200-300 feet from Boulder Dam as temporary measure
- Total irrigable area: 17,280,000 acres (15,280,000 in Arizona, 2,000,000 in California)

**Land Use Allocation**:
- 5,280,000 acres for intensive 5-10 acre "garden-farms" in Rural Settlements
- 10,000,000 acres for rain-making forest plantations
- Each of the ten Arizona projects allocated 500,000 acres

**Specific Project Details**:
- **Tombstone Project**: Water from [[San Pedro River]] and subterranean sources beneath Tombstone, replenished from [[Dragoon Mountains]] watershed
- **San Simon Project**: Extends from Cienega south to Rodeo, New Mexico; includes [[Whitlock Valley]]
- **Solomonville Project**: Covers Upper [[Gila Valley]] with existing maps and reports by Engineer Harry F. Olmsted
- **Wickenburg Project**: Elevation 2,116 feet, described as "most favorably situated" for immediate development
- **Tucson Project**: 500,000 acre quota for "Homecroft Forest Plantations, date gardens, olive orchards"

**Mining and Mineral Resources**:
- Region north of Gila River between river and Santa Fe Railroad described as "most highly mineralized undeveloped mining region in the United States"
- Tombstone mines could be reopened below 500-foot level with pumping powered by Glen Canyon electricity
- Historic [[Gold Bluff Mine]] discovery described in detail as yielding gold from prehistoric river deposits

**Economic and Social Framework**:
- "Forest Farm Estate": 20-acre forest tract plus 5-acre garden farm
- "Homecroft Orchard Estate": 10-acre tract with 9 acres orchard, 1 acre for family food
- Training program through Civilian Conservation Corps to create 500,000 trained "forest-farmers" (50,000 per project)
- Emphasis on "Individual Independent Homes on the Land" for economic security

**Political and Strategic Arguments**:
- Maxwell argues against allowing Colorado River water to flow to Mexico, claiming it would create an "Asiatic Wedge" and potential military threat
- Proposes treaty with Mexico (modeled on 1853 Gadsden Purchase) to acquire all rights to Colorado River water
- Warns of "Asiatic-American war" if development proceeds in Mexico
- Frames water development as "measures of military security" and national defense

**Historical Context**:
- Detailed narrative of "The Argonaut" (Maxwell's father) and 1849 California Gold Rush
- Nine-month voyage from New Orleans to San Francisco via Cape Horn
- Gold Bluff Mine discovery following Indian attack and robbery
- Comparison of gold mining era to proposed water development era

**Implementation Timeline**:
- San Simon, Tombstone, and Tucson projects identified for immediate development "within less than one year"
- Charleston reservoir dam and San Simon Valley storage dam recommended for immediate construction
- Overall project framed as urgent due to competing Mexican development claims

The document represents an ambitious, comprehensive vision for transforming the arid Southwest through massive water engineering, reforestation, and agricultural settlement, reflecting both the technological optimism and the geopolitical anxieties of the 1930s era.
