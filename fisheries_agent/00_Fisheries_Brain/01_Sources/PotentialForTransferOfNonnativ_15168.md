# Potential for Transfer of Non-Native Fish in Central Arizona Project Canal Waters to the Gila River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae #cichlidae #invasive-species #conservation

## Executive Summary

This report assesses the potential for non-native fish species transported in [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal waters to enter the [[Gila River]] system and impact threatened or endangered native fish species, particularly [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) in tributaries such as [[Aravaipa Creek]]. Five species are evaluated: [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), [[White Bass]] (*M. chrysops*), [[Blue Tilapia]] (*Tilapia aurea*), triploid [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), and [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*). The 335-mile CAP Aqueduct delivers Colorado River water from [[Lake Havasu]] through [[Lake Pleasant]] to agricultural and municipal users, with distribution canals serving the [[Gila River Indian Community]] (GRIC) and [[San Carlos Irrigation and Drainage District]] (SCIDD) in close proximity to the Gila River above [[Ashurst-Hayden Dam]].

## Background & Description

The CAP Aqueduct system begins at Lake Havasu on the [[Colorado River]], lifts water to the Hayden-Rhodes Aqueduct, and carries it to Lake Pleasant on the [[Agua Fria River]]. The system continues south, passing under the [[Salt River]] and joining the Salt-Gila Aqueduct, which transports water beneath the Gila River bed. Distribution canals deliver CAP water to GRIC, SCIDD, and other agricultural users. The close proximity of these canal systems to the Gila River raises concerns about non-native fish introduction to waters containing threatened and endangered species.

**Species Characteristics:**

- **Striped Bass**: Currently occur throughout CAP Aqueduct; adults avoid waters >25-26°C; shift to fish prey at ~170-200 mm TL; require large volumes of swift water for successful spawning (minimum 52 km stream length with velocity >30.5 cm/s); self-sustaining populations require reservoirs >1,200 ha.

- **White Bass**: Present in [[Lake Pleasant]]; tolerate temperatures to 31°C (adults), 35°C (young); opportunistic feeders consuming shad, invertebrates, and zooplankton; spawn in lotic areas with adhesive eggs; require large reservoirs for self-sustaining populations in Southwest.

- **Blue Tilapia**: Collected from Lake Pleasant (1988) and [[Saguaro Lake]]; cold-sensitive with lower lethal temperature 6-10°C; omnivorous opportunists consuming algae, plankton, invertebrates, detritus; capable of reproducing in canals with quiet water areas.

- **Triploid Grass Carp**: Stocked in selected CAP reaches for aquatic weed control; tolerate 0 to >32°C; consume body weight in vegetation daily; bar screens (2-inch spacing) prevent movement from canals; functionally sterile but develop gonadal tissue.

- **Rainbow Smelt**: Proposed for [[Lake Powell]]; pelagic, cool-water species preferring 6-16°C; avoid water >15°C, lethal above 23-26°C; feed on deepwater invertebrates and zooplankton; rarely ascend streams except during spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Current Distribution**: Non-native species already established in central Arizona waters include [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Mosquitofish]], and others. Striped bass documented in CAP Aqueduct with 98% found in Reach 1 (first 27.4 km below Buckskin Mountain tunnel). White bass confined to Lake Pleasant. Blue tilapia present in Lake Pleasant, Salt River system, and historically in [[Picacho Reservoir]].

**Potential Escape Routes**:
1. Water released from Lake Pleasant to Agua Fria River (rare; ~25-50 year flood events)
2. Water released from CAP/SRP canals to Salt River below [[Granite Reef Dam]]
3. Water released from GRIC and SCIDD canals to Gila River below Ashurst-Hayden Dam (most probable route)
4. Human transport (common, difficult to quantify)

**Environmental Constraints**: The Gila River between [[Coolidge Dam]] and Ashurst-Hayden Dam experiences irregular flows, high seasonal temperatures, long periods of continuous high flow during irrigation season, and high suspended sediment levels. These conditions limit primary and secondary production. Water temperatures at USGS station at Kelvin (19 miles above dam) seldom exceed 25°C. The [[San Pedro River]] has relatively small, irregular flows with temperatures sometimes exceeding 31°C and high sediment loads.

## Key Findings

**Striped Bass**: Survival and growth possible in CAP Aqueduct and canals, especially for young fish (tolerance to 35°C). Reproduction in Gila River extremely unlikely due to: irregular flows causing egg/larva settlement or sweep-out (average travel time Coolidge Dam to Ashurst-Hayden Dam: 36-48 hours); high seasonal temperatures; lack of large reservoir for forage base development. Some individuals may survive in Gila River above Ashurst-Hayden Dam with cool hypolimnetic releases from San Carlos Lake, but numbers would remain low due to limited recruitment and poor habitat. Movement into San Pedro River and Aravaipa Creek unlikely; striped bass do not typically occupy waters as small and irregular as these systems. Field data show no striped bass in [[Bill Williams River]] between Lake Havasu and [[Alamo Lake]], or in [[San Juan River]] flowing into Lake Powell despite repeated surveys.

**White Bass**: Will be entrained from Lake Pleasant once connected to CAP. More tolerant than striped bass to high temperatures (adults to 31°C) and low oxygen. Vertical distribution in Lake Pleasant may limit entrainment; thermocline forms at ~8 m depth in May with virtually no oxygen below during stratification (May-October). Water withdrawn from below thermocline unlikely to carry many fish during stratification. Reproduction in Gila River unlikely; all reproducing populations in Southwest associated with moderate to large reservoirs with inflowing streams. Adhesive eggs provide advantage over striped bass, but lack of large reservoir precludes self-sustaining population. Some individuals may survive in Gila River with broader tolerances and flexible diet providing advantage over striped bass. Movement into San Pedro River and tributaries unlikely based on field evidence; white bass do not move into [[Agua Fria River]] above Lake Pleasant or [[Black River]], New Mexico.

**Blue Tilapia**: Most likely species to establish self-sustaining populations. Broad environmental tolerances and flexible diet ensure survival in CAP. Sensitive to cold; mortality begins below 10°C, lower lethal limit 6-10°C. Will survive and proliferate in Picacho Reservoir. Reproduction possible in canals with quiet water areas (primarily forebays of pumping plants). Will suffer mortality during cold periods and annual canal dewatering (late October to early December) but persist in perennial water sites. Likely to reproduce and proliferate in Gila River despite winter temperatures at Kelvin falling below 10°C in most years; temperature tolerable most of year, probably exceeds 22°C long enough for successful reproduction. High flows and benthic scour during irrigation season will limit numbers. Occurrence in Aravaipa Creek possible; winter temperatures can fall below 5°C but not sustained long enough for complete mortality. Summer temperatures tolerable, might permit reproduction. Short flood flow periods have more impact reducing non-native than native fish. Unlikely to be abundant in Aravaipa Creek but occasional occurrence likely once present in Gila and San Pedro Rivers.

**Triploid Grass Carp**: Bar screens prevent transport throughout canal system; released only by failure or overflow during extraordinary storm events. Direct transport from CAP Aqueduct to Gila River highly unlikely. If introduced to Gila River above Ashurst-Hayden Dam, water quality within tolerances and some foods available. Only large fish present; rarity of release results in few fish. Large species normally inhabiting large, low-gradient rivers; not expected to readily enter shallow, low-volume streams like San Pedro River or tributaries. Size and swimming ability might allow ascent during storm flows, but poor water quality would discourage movements. Residency and movements in San Pedro River and tributaries very unlikely event. Triploid fish develop gonadal tissue and may produce gametes, but laboratory work indicates few fertile eggs produced and embryos inviable; considered "functionally sterile" especially under field conditions.

**Rainbow Smelt**: If introduced to Lake Powell, will be carried to downstream reservoirs and may reach Lake Havasu and CAP Aqueduct. Most sensitive to warm temperatures of all species studied; avoid water >15°C, suffer mortality above 23°C. Water in CAP Aqueduct, lateral canals, sumps, Picacho Reservoir, Gila River and tributaries exceeds 23°C many days annually. Restricts survival to cool-weather periods; generally precludes survival, growth to maturity, and reproduction. Lack of large reservoir with cool, oxygenated hypolimnion and abundant plankton community works against survival and reproduction. Small size and low numbers likely present, limited survival period reduces potential for significant threat to aquatic species including fishes in Aravaipa Creek.

**Transfer Probability Assessment**: Most probable sites for fish transfer are GRIC and SCIDD canal areas near Ashurst-Hayden Dam. Regular release of water to Gila River bed at Olberg trash racks (Pima Lateral Canal under Gila River, ~30 miles below dam; sluicing occurs regularly, sometimes twice weekly) ensures some fish released regularly. Irregular spillage from two SCIDD wasteways within 6 miles of dam and canal failures near Gila River increase chances. At least one storm event in most years offers access to Gila River above dam. USGS data indicate average ~18,000 acre-feet water sluiced or spilled at Ashurst-Hayden Dam annually, providing opportunities for fish to travel to dam base. Ashurst-Hayden Dam not effective barrier to upstream fish movement. Release from Lake Pleasant or SRP canals far less likely due to rarity of releases (~25-year flood events) and great distances fish must travel.

**Human Transport**: Unauthorized transport and release by private citizens common phenomenon. Examples documented include: white bass introductions to [[Pecos River]] drainage and [[Lake Farmington]] in [[San Juan River]] drainage, New Mexico; [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]] in CAP Aqueduct (most likely by human transport); adult [[Largemouth Bass]] in CAP section only weeks after filling; 27-lb striped bass in urban lake near Mesa, Arizona. "Trespass angling" documented in CAP Aqueduct. Fishing permitted in irrigation sumps on GRIC lands. Fish carried in CAP water may be at least as likely to reach Gila River above Ashurst-Hayden Dam by human transport as by spillage release.

**Impacts on Native Fish**: Striped bass and white bass unlikely to establish self-sustaining populations; low numbers and rare introductions limit potential impact. Tilapia most likely to establish populations but unlikely to achieve high abundance in Gila and San Pedro Rivers due to cold temperatures and high velocities; impacts through behavioral interactions (harassment, spawning area occupation) associated with high densities unlikely. Grass carp highly unlikely to reach or reside in small streams; single fish presence would be exceptional event. Rainbow smelt could persist only few months; low numbers and brief survival make them less threat than non-native fish already present.

**Impacts on Bald Eagle Forage**: Small nesting population of [[Bald Eagles]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) occurs in Arizona with active nest downstream from Coolidge Dam. [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], Sonora and Desert Suckers most common fish prey. Striped bass and white bass introduction unlikely to result in significant changes; neither likely to sustain large numbers. Some individuals will prey on fish used by eagles but will be exposed to conditions causing death/morbidity and vulnerability to eagle predation. Tilapia likely to reproduce but highly vulnerable to predation in shallows and when cold temperatures reduce activity; not known to exclude carp or catfish. Grass carp very low introduction probability and low numbers preclude significant impact. Rainbow smelt unsuitable year-round environment precludes significant impact.

**Recommendations**:
1. China Wash fish barrier (Florence-Casa Grande Canal) hinders non-native fish introduction; reduces frequency of introduction; should be inspected and tested periodically to ensure effectiveness.
2. Electric fish barrier in Pima Lateral Canal serves no significant function now that CAP water delivered to Florence-Casa Grande Canal.
3. Regular monitoring for presence and abundance of species of concern in Gila and San Pedro Rivers should be conducted to verify or modify predictions.
4. Public education program on dangers and illegality of fish transport may reduce human transport incidence but unlikely to be completely effective.
5. Structural changes to Ashurst-Hayden Dam, cessation of water release to Gila River drainage below dam, or cessation of CAP water delivery to users near Gila River drainage would block fish passage but bear significant costs probably not commensurate with probable harm levels. Implementation would not preclude introduction by other means, especially human transport.

**Expert Review**: Dr. W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University) concurred with conclusions but noted report largely academic exercise since most species too large to inhabit relatively tiny waters of concern. Emphasized real threat of interconnected waterways is transport of smaller non-native fishes ([[Lepomis]] spp., [[Red Shiner]] *Cyprinella lutrensis*, catfishes, poeciliids) and general homogenation of fauna. Suggested reviewing overall potential problem of transport of organisms and propagules in general (plants, invertebrates) between Mohave and Sonoran deserts by CAP development; long-term problems may be plants and invertebrates rather than fishes.
