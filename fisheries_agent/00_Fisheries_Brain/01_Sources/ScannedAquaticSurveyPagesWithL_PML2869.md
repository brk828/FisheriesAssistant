# Scanned Aquatic Survey Pages with Largely Illegible OCR

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #brachionichthyidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This source contains two separate fish studies. The first is a short [[Threatened Fishes of the World]] profile of the [[Spotted Handfish]] (*Brachionichthys hirsutus*), a small, critically endangered benthic species endemic to south-eastern [[Tasmania]]. Its population has declined substantially over the past decade, and the cause is not yet known. The second is a field study of spawning ecology in the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) in two small tributaries of the lower [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The study documents spawning behavior, site selection, timing, and fish size, and it raises concern that spawning in the [[Paria River]] contributes little to recruitment.

## Background & Description

**Spotted handfish** (Lacépède, 1804; Brachionichthyidae)
- Small handfish, maximum size 120 mm.
- Body covered with small spinules; dermal tentacles usually absent.
- Body white or cream, with numerous small, closely set orange, brown, or blackish spots or, less commonly, stripes.
- Modified pectoral and pelvic fins are used to rest on and crawl slowly over the bottom.
- The modified first dorsal fin spine (illicium) probes egg masses and is sometimes extended to rest on the substrate.
- Diet includes small crustaceans and polychaete worms.
- Endemic to south-eastern Tasmania, in the lower [[Derwent River]] estuary, [[Frederick Henry Bay]], the [[D'Entrecasteaux Channel]], and the northern parts of [[Storm Bay]].
- Historically regarded as the most common of the shore handfishes (Last et al. 1983).

**Flannelmouth sucker** (Catostomidae)
- Native to the Colorado River drainage of the western United States, which has undergone a serious decline.
- Few published data existed on this species in Grand Canyon before this study.
- The study aimed to provide baseline data for monitoring after any change in dam management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Handfish habitat and reproduction**
- Occurs in coarse-to-fine sand at depths of 5–40 m, most commonly 5–10 m.
- Often found in shallow, shell-filled depressions or near low-relief rocks.
- Spawning occurs in September–October. Egg masses of 80–250 eggs are attached to vertical structures, including [[Ascidians]] (stalked), sponges, or seagrasses.
- Eggs are large (about 3–4 mm diameter), each housed in a flask-shaped capsule, and are linked in a single mass by fine tubules of unknown function.
- The female guards and tends the egg mass.
- There is no pelagic larval stage. Eggs hatch after 7–8 weeks as fully formed juveniles (6–7 mm SL).

**Sucker river environments**
- The [[Glen Canyon Dam]], completed in 1963, sits about 25 km upstream of Grand Canyon. It stabilized the seasonal temperature, discharge, and sediment load that characterized the unregulated river.
- At least a dozen non-native fishes have been introduced, altering the native fish community.
- Four of the original eight native species appear to have been extirpated from Grand Canyon, including [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
- The [[Paria River]] drains about 3650 km² of the Escalante Mountains and Paria Plateau in southern Utah and flows about 88 km before entering the Colorado River 25 km below Glen Canyon Dam. [[Speckled Dace]] is its only perennial resident fish.
- [[Bright Angel Creek]] drains 262 km² on the North Rim of Grand Canyon and enters the Colorado River 165 km below Glen Canyon Dam. Low summer temperatures (< 23 °C) support resident introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]].
- The Paria River has broader daily temperature swings, higher maxima, and a high incidence of summer flash floods, with summer temperatures reaching 34 °C. These conditions probably make it an unlikely rearing area.

## Key Findings

### Spotted Handfish

- **Conservation status:** Critically endangered on the 1996 IUCN Red List and endangered under Schedule 1 of the Australian Commonwealth Endangered Species Act.
- **Decline:** Abundance has declined substantially over the past ten years. The cause is undetermined.
- **Suspected threats:**
  - Predation by the recently introduced northern Pacific sea star [[Asterias Amurensis]], which may directly eat egg masses or indirectly reduce ascidians used as spawning substrate.
  - Siltation of the estuary, which reduces preferred habitat.
  - Heavy metal contamination of sediments.
  - Urban effluent.
- **Legal protection:** Listed under Tasmanian State Fisheries legislation in 1994 and under Australian Federal legislation in 1996.
- **Survey:** A survey of the previously documented range confirmed endangered status (Barrett et al. 1996).
- **Research and recommendations:**
  - Current work documents reproductive styles, habitat requirements, and captive husbandry techniques (Bruce et al. 1997).
  - Recovery is limited by the lack of basic biological and ecological data.
  - Priorities include identifying threatening processes, managing the Derwent River system, developing husbandry methods, and establishing a captive breeding program in case the wild population continues to decline.
- **Taxonomic remark:** Brachionichthyidae is one of the few marine fish families endemic to Australia. Five of its eight recognized species have among the most restricted ranges of the region's 4000+ marine fishes, which makes them potentially vulnerable.

### Flannelmouth Sucker (Grand Canyon)

**Spawning behavior**
- Spawning was observed on 20 March 1992 and 28 March–10 April 1993 in the Paria River, and 16–19 March 1993 in Bright Angel Creek. Detailed behavioral observations were made in Bright Angel Creek on 18 March 1993.
- Mating was promiscuous. Females often mated with two or more males and sometimes changed partners between events.
- In Bright Angel Creek, 34 spawning acts were observed, and eggs were deposited at 12 sites (mean 0.51 m² each; total 6.16 m²). One site sustained 60% of the observed spawning events.
- Fifty-nine percent of observed mating acts involved two males and one female.
- Mating lasted 2–8 seconds.
- Sex ratio of spawning fish was 2.3 : 1 (males : females) in Bright Angel Creek and 2:1 in the Paria River pooled catches. Nearly equal ratios were seen in seine catches from spawning areas.
- Flannelmouth suckers do not create nest depressions. Eggs settle into interstitial spaces in loose substrate.
- Eggs were 3.5 ± 0.11 mm (n = 50) in the Paria River and 3.3 ± 0.28 mm (n = 25) in Bright Angel Creek.
- Brown trout fed on recently deposited eggs. One captured trout contained more than 50 eggs.

**Habitat selection**
- Paria River spawning occurred at depths of 5–25 cm, mean column velocities of 0.16–1.0 m/s, and substrates of 2–97 mm.
- Bright Angel Creek spawning occurred at depths of 19–41 cm, velocities of 0.23–0.89 m/s, and substrates of 6–289 mm. Water temperatures were 13–15 °C.
- Both streams used substrate in the 16–32 mm category out of proportion to its availability.
- Shallow water was selected in both streams, but depth alone probably does not control spawning.

**Timing**
- Adult males entered the Paria River after a flash flood on 13 February 1993 at 2–8 °C. Females were first caught on 1 March.
- Spawning in the Paria River began on 28 March 1993 at 9 °C. Catch rates peaked on 8–10 April at 8–20 °C.
- A high-volume flash flood on 31 March 1992 was followed by the absence of adults upstream for the rest of that year.
- In Bright Angel Creek, spawning was interrupted on 19 March 1993 by early snowmelt discharge.
- Spawning was relatively synchronous across tributaries with different temperature and flow regimes, suggesting that temperature and discharge are not the only cues.

**Distribution and catch**
- Paria River spawning occurred at RK 2.8, 4.0, 6.0, and 9.2–9.8 km. Fish ascended at least 9.8 km.
- Bright Angel Creek spawning did not extend beyond RK 1.2, although no barrier existed below RK 9.5.
- Catches in the Paria River were patchy. Even when CPUE reached 30 fish per 100 m², 74% of 54 seine efforts caught 0 or 1 fish.

**Size structure**
- Spawning fish upstream in the Paria River measured 385–580 mm (476 ± 37.5 mm, n = 275). These were smaller than fish caught at the river mouth (502 ± 41.1 mm, n = 785).
- Females were significantly larger than males in the Paria River (511 ± 34.8 mm, n = 95 vs. 460 ± 24.4 mm, n = 172).
- Fish spawning upstream in 1993 averaged about 50 mm longer than those in 1981 (426 ± 33.4 mm, n = 286), suggesting an aging stock with low recent spawning success.
- The 21 largest fish (580–620 mm) were caught at the confluence and may include flannelmouth × razorback sucker hybrids.

**Recruitment concerns**
- Larval catches were very low. About 8 larvae were caught in 1992 and none in 1993 in the lowermost 10 km of the Paria River.
- Only two large juveniles were recorded in the Colorado River within 44 km of the Paria mouth from 1981–1995.
- The authors conclude that spawning in the Paria River may contribute little to the survival of this endemic species in Grand Canyon.
- Bright Angel Creek supports a relatively low and limited number of spawners, and baseline data are lacking to assess trends.

**Management implications**
- Spawning migrations into these tributaries appear to persist despite major changes to the Colorado River.
- The authors propose that the abundance, size structure, and spawning success of these populations could serve as sensitive, cost-effective indicators of the success of altered dam management.
