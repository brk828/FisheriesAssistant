## Executive Summary

This study presents the first documented analysis of F1 hybrids between the threatened, native [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) and the widely introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) in Arizona's White Mountains. Two cooperative hybridization experiments (1981, 1983) between the U.S. Forest Service and Arizona Game and Fish Department produced only four viable F1 hybrids total (two per experiment) from artificial fertilization efforts, reflecting extremely low survivorship (0.6% to fingerling stage) and high incidence of congenital lordosis (30%) in non-surviving fry. Contrary to the classical assumption that F1 hybrids display intermediate phenotypes between parental taxa (per [[Hubbs 1955]]), morphometric and meristic analysis revealed that **true intermediacy was rare** — occurring in only ~7% of morphometric characters and 17–50% of meristic characters. These findings challenge the common practice of using intermediate phenotype as diagnostic evidence of introgressive hybridization in wild trout populations, with significant implications for the conservation management of the Apache trout and other western native salmonids.

## Background & Description

**Broodstock & Crosses:**
- Female *S. apache* parents: two 3-year-old females from Soldier Creek brood stock (1975 origin), reared at Sterling Springs Hatchery.
- Male *S. gairdneri* parents: two 15-month-old males of the Plymouth Rock, Massachusetts hatchery strain.
- Fertilization via dry spawn technique ([[Leitritz and Lewis 1980]]; [[Piper et al. 1982]]).

**Reproductive Success (Experiment I, 1981):**
- 300 fertilized eggs → 6 hatched (2%) → 3 survived to swim-up (1%) → 2 survived to fingerling/sacrifice stage (0.6%).
- 30% of non-surviving fry exhibited **lordosis** (ventral curvature of the vertebral column), suggesting possible postzygotic developmental incompatibility.

**Morphometrics (29 characters measured, per [[Hubbs and Lagler 1970]] methodology, following [[Miller 1972]] diagnostic characters for *S. apache*):**

*Experiment I:*
- 18/29 (62%) characters varied <10 per mille points between hybrids and rainbow parents; 23/29 (79%) hybrid characters and 25/27 (93%) rainbow characters varied ≤20 per mille points.
- Apache trout (n=20, Soldier Creek stock) showed markedly wider variation than either hybrids or rainbow parents.
- Range-based comparison: hybrids trended toward Apache trout in 11/29 (38%) characters; intermediate in only 1 (hand/head width); no clear trend in 15/29 (52%).
- Of the 15 non-trending characters: 3 exceeded both parental ranges (head length, eye diameter, pectoral fin length); 3 were less than both parental ranges (snout length, dorsal-to-anal origin distance, middle caudal ray length).
- Using **means** (Table 2): only 1/29 characters (dorsal fin origin to pectoral fin origin) was truly intermediate; remaining trends split evenly or showed no clear parental affinity.

*Experiment II (1983):*
- 18/29 (62%) characters varied <20 per mille points; 9/29 (31%) varied <10 points.
- Based on means: hybrids nearer Apache trout in 1 character (upper jaw length); nearer rainbow in 3; intermediate in 14 (48%); exceeded both parents in 6; fell below both parents in 5.

**Meristics (12 counts, Table 3):**

*Experiment I:* Half of 12 meristic characters were intermediate between rainbow and Apache parents. Branchiostegal ray counts and caudal fin ray counts were identical across all three groups (rainbow, hybrid, Apache). Scales above and in the lateral line, and pelvic fin ray counts in hybrids, were *lower* than either parental group (transgressive segregation).

*Experiment II:* Only branchiostegals and vertebrae counts were intermediate. Of the remaining 10 meristics: 6 trended toward Apache parent, 2 (scales in lateral series, caudal fin rays) trended toward rainbow parent, and 2 (scales above/in lateral line) showed no clear parental trend.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native range context:** *Salmo apache* is endemic to isolated headwater streams of the White Mountains, east-central Arizona (type locality: East Fork of the White River; broodstock source: Soldier Creek).
- **Threat driver:** Widespread, indiscriminate stocking of non-native rainbow trout throughout the western United States ([[Behnke 1979]]; [[Behnke and Zarn 1976]]) has been implicated in introgressive hybridization with native western *Salmo* lineages, contributing to the near-elimination of pure native trout strains.
- **Habitat degradation:** The authors emphasize that extrinsic factors — stream damming, habitat loss, and degradation — are of **equal importance** to genetic introgression in the decline of native western trouts, cautioning against a genetics-only framing of the conservation problem.
- **Reproductive isolation:** Low hybrid survivorship, high lordosis incidence, and lack of clear hybrid zones in wild White Mountain populations suggest the possibility of prezygotic or weak postzygotic reproductive isolating mechanisms between *S. apache* and *S. gairdneri*, potentially limiting (but not preventing) wild introgression.

## Key Findings

1. **F1 hybrids between *S. apache* and *S. gairdneri* do NOT reliably display intermediate phenotypes** — true morphometric intermediacy occurred in only ~7% of characters, and meristic intermediacy in 17–50% of characters, contradicting the classical diagnostic assumption of hybrid intermediacy ([[Hubbs 1955]]).
2. Results corroborate similar findings by [[Gold et al. 1979]] in rainbow × golden trout (*Salmo aquabonita*) F1 hybrids, where only 8 of 27 characters showed intermediacy.
3. Hybrid rearing success was extremely low (0.6% survival to fingerling stage), with 30% of failed fry showing lordosis — a possible signature of genetic incompatibility/postzygotic isolation.
4. Apache trout from Soldier Creek broodstock displayed substantially **greater natural phenotypic variation** than either the hybrids or rainbow trout parents, underscoring the risk of misinterpreting natural intraspecific variation as evidence of introgression.
5. **Management implication:** The presence of an apparently "intermediate" phenotype in wild-caught trout should NOT be used alone as evidence of hybridization/introgression; doing so risks mismanagement (e.g., unwarranted stream renovation or continued stocking) that could destroy valuable, naturally-occurring genetic variation in isolated native trout populations.
6. Case-by-case genetic and morphological evaluation (including biochemical genetic analysis, per [[Loudenslager et al. In prep]]) is recommended before drawing taxonomic or management conclusions about hybridization status of wild western trout populations.
7. Recovery of the [[Apache Trout]] as a threatened native salmonid requires balanced attention to both intrinsic (genetic/hybridization) and extrinsic (habitat loss, stream degradation) threats, rather than a singular focus on introgression control.
