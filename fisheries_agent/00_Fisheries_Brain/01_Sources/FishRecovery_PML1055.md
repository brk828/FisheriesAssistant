# Endangered Fish, Contaminated Aquifers, and the Cost of Western Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a news-style feature (OCR-scanned, partly garbled) covering two linked western water problems. The first is groundwater contamination and overpumping in the [[Albuquerque Aquifer]] area of the [[Rio Grande Basin]], including the [[San Jose Groundwater Contamination]] site. The second is the decline of native fishes in the [[Colorado River]] basin, where the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] remain listed as endangered. A further section recounts the loss of salmon and steelhead in the [[Columbia River]] system. The article is journalistic rather than a research report, so its main points are the conditions, debates and recovery efforts it describes, not new experimental data.

## Background & Description

The article profiles the four endangered Colorado basin fishes, collectively described as the "four endangered fishes." Native fish species of the upper Colorado River have been shaped by dams, diversions, channelization, and the loss of backwater and nursery habitat. The Colorado Pikeminnow (locally called "squawfish") is the largest of the group. Historically it reached large sizes and made long spawning migrations of hundreds of miles, sometimes in response to spring flows. Present-day adults are scarce, and the article describes wild fish as surviving with "tenuous" prospects.

The document also describes the Columbia River system, where salmon and steelhead runs collapsed after the construction of large dams. Historical accounts mention White Salmon and Chinook runs, and the Pacific salmon fishery at [[Celilo Falls]] before it was inundated by [[The Dalles Dam]]. Sockeye Salmon, Chinook Salmon and [[Steelhead]] populations in the upper Columbia and [[Snake River]] are described as heavily reduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Groundwater (Albuquerque and San Jose).** The Albuquerque subregion of the Rio Grande basin aquifer was estimated by the U.S. Geological Survey in 1975 to hold about 23 billion acre-feet of recoverable fresh groundwater. The article notes that this estimate was later widely questioned as too optimistic. Two 3,000-foot test wells found more clay and less sand than expected, which made deep pumping costly and difficult. The San Jose neighborhood, where about 30 percent of families live at or near the poverty level, lies near a Superfund site that the Environmental Protection Agency had listed eight years earlier, with cleanup stalled by complex negotiations. Proposed new city regulations would restrict certain industrial activities over the aquifer, and the article reports officials' concern that unchecked contamination could cripple the city's groundwater supply.

**Colorado River fishes.** The four endangered fishes are threatened by altered flows, cold-water releases, and non-native competitors and predators. Below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], which was closed in 1963, cold releases suppressed spawning and recruitment of Colorado Pikeminnow. Non-native green sunfish are described as a significant predator of young fish. Outlet modifications made in 1978 to warm the releases were followed by increased spawning success and rebounding population levels. The [[Yampa River]], which is unregulated, retains natural flow variability and is described as providing excellent spawning and nursery conditions, with about 350 miles of good spawning and nursery habitat in the Green and Yampa rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Pikeminnow spawning is limited but detectable.** Field crews at a 15-mile reach near [[Grand Junction]] collected larval fish three to four weeks old. Biologists use the proportion of pikeminnow among collected larvae to estimate spawning levels. Lynn Kaeding, who heads the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] project office, says the news is "not too good" because few young fish survive to grow up.
- **Population estimates are small.** The article gives a wild adult population estimate of roughly 1,000 to 2,000 fish in the upper river. Kaeding also reports that the Green and Yampa rivers may support about ten times the Colorado mainstem population, a comparison based on habitat area. (The exact figures are partly garbled in the scan.)
- **Recovery has been slow.** The U.S. [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] began recovery efforts in 1979, but the article describes early work as largely ignored. A 1992 recovery plan for the four species recommended substantial instream flow changes, which the article presents as a turning point.
- **Columbia River losses are severe.** Dams such as Bonneville and The Dalles interrupted salmon and steelhead migrations. The article states that a number of Snake River and upper Columbia runs are listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and that some tribes and conservation groups argue for dam removal or large-scale flow changes.
- **The Albuquerque debate is unresolved.** The article ends without a final answer on whether proposed aquifer protections will pass, and it presents the Albuquerque and Colorado basin disputes as examples of conflicts over water allocation and environmental protection.
