# Indian River Lagoon Epibenthic Fish Populations and Seagrass Ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #engraulidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document contains three *Florida Scientist* (1983) contributions on the [[Indian River Lagoon]] system in east-central Florida. The main paper, by Mulligan and Snelson, reports the first quantitative summer trawl survey of epibenthic fishes in the Indian River estuary, sampled at 8 fixed stations in June–September 1979 and 1980. Of 105,853 individuals, [[Anchoa Mitchilli]] made up 87%. The study established baseline data for long-term monitoring and found that vegetative cover and salinity were the only measured environmental factors that appeared to influence fish populations. A second fragment contains only the reference list of a pore-water chemistry paper (Carlson et al.), so its findings cannot be summarized. The third paper, by Rice, Trocino and Wells, is a truncated abstract on factors controlling [[Seagrass]] ecology, including epiphyte load, light, temperature and photosynthesis.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** The authors state that estuaries and lagoons make up 80–90% of the Atlantic and Gulf coasts of the United States and are among the most productive habitats in the world (Odum, 1961). Rapid coastal development in the southeastern U.S. threatens them, and surveillance is needed to separate short-term fluctuations from long-term trends (Hellawell, 1977).

**Prior work.** Published fish studies of the Indian River lagoon were qualitative (Evermann and Bean, 1897; Snelson and Williams, 1981) or semiquantitative (Gilmore, 1977). Earlier quantitative data were unpublished. The authors describe their work as the first quantitative summer data on epibenthic fishes in the Indian River estuary, and a continuation of a trawling program begun by Snelson in 1977.

**Study area.** The lagoon system runs 220 km along the Florida east coast, from Ponce de Leon Inlet south to St. Lucie Inlet, and is made up of [[Mosquito Lagoon]], [[Indian River]] and [[Banana River]]. Barrier beaches separate it from the Atlantic, with four inlets linking the two. Average depth is about 1.5 m. Wind, not tide, drives water movement, and the waters are mesohaline. Seagrass beds (*Syringodium filiforme*, *Halodule wrightii*, *Thalassia testudinum*) occupy the shoals, and drifting macroalgae cover the deeper waters. Mangroves (*Laguncularia racemosa*, *Avicennia germinans*) border the shoreline, and mosquito-control impoundments have replaced most native *Spartina* marsh.

**Sampling design.** Eight fixed stations were placed in the deeper central areas and dredged channels, because trawling was ineffective at depths under 2 m. Mosquito Lagoon held stations 1–2, Indian River stations 3–6, and Banana River stations 7–8.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gear and methods.** A 4.9 m semi-balloon otter trawl (3.2 cm wing mesh, 6.2 mm cod-end liner) was towed at about 4 knots during daylight. Each station yielded seven sequential 2-min tows. Fish were identified, measured (standard length), weighed, and assigned to life stages. Larvae, mostly *Anchoa* sp., were excluded.

**Environmental parameters.** Temperature, salinity, pH, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, depth and vegetative cover were measured. Vegetation was scored on a 0–10 tub-fullness index. Statistical tests used ANOVA with Duncan's Multiple Range Test (p < 0.05).

**Gear bias.** The authors note that trawls favor slower swimmers and juveniles, can miss burrowing or bottom-lying fishes, and fail when macroalgae accumulate in the cod end. Repetitive short tows partly offset these biases. An analysis of early, middle and late tows found no significant abundance differences for the 10 most numerous species, which supports averaging the seven tows.

**Environmental gradients.** Salinity fell from north to south. Mosquito Lagoon averaged 33.8 ppt, Indian River 28.9 ppt, and Banana River 25.4 ppt. The authors attribute this to tidal and freshwater differences that are only minor. Turbidity stayed below 5 NTU, and dissolved oxygen was near saturation. Mosquito Lagoon was significantly more turbid than the other two basins. Indian River had the most macroalgae, and that difference was statistically significant.

## Key Findings

**Community composition (Mulligan and Snelson)**
- 57 fish species from 29 families were collected. Eight species from 1979 were absent in 1980, and seven from 1980 were absent in 1979.
- The community was strongly dominated by a few species. The top 15 species made up 99.54% of the 2-year catch. [[Anchoa Mitchilli]] alone comprised 87.47% of 105,853 individuals.
- Five species were restricted to Indian River and Mosquito Lagoon samples, and four were found only in Banana River.
- The 57 species represent about half the total known to inhabit this part of the lagoon system (Snelson, 1982).

**Spatial patterns**
- Indian River produced 22% more fish per tow than Mosquito Lagoon and 62% more than Banana River, largely due to *Anchoa mitchilli*.
- Mosquito Lagoon produced significantly more [[Micropogonias Undulatus]] and [[Leiostomus Xanthurus]] than either other basin. Station 2 alone accounted for 70% of all *L. xanthurus* and 67% of the study's *M. undulatus*.
- Indian River produced significantly more [[Syngnathus Scovelli]] than the other basins. Banana River produced significantly more [[Lagodon Rhomboides]] than Indian River.
- Mosquito Lagoon and Banana River each had more species per station than Indian River.

**Diversity**
- Simpson's index, Shannon-Wiener (H'), redundancy, evenness, richness and relative dominance were all significantly correlated. This is consistent with Livingston (1976).
- Mean species per station was 13, ranging from 6 (stations 3 and 2 in specific months) to 23 (station 7, August 1979).
- Banana River had the highest overall [[Shannon-Wiener Diversity Index]] (H' = 1.85). Its highest monthly value was 3.59, and it held 4 of the 5 highest monthly values. All six lowest monthly values were in Indian River.
- [[Anchoa Mitchilli]] was the first dominant species in 51 of 64 month-station samples.

**Environmental drivers**
- No significant correlation was found between depth, temperature, pH, turbidity, salinity or dissolved oxygen and any diversity or abundance index.
- Abundance of selected species was influenced by vegetative cover and salinity.
- [[Gobiosoma Robustum]] and *S. scovelli* were positively correlated with vegetative cover, which is linked to drift algae.
- *Micropogonias undulatus* and *L. xanthurus* were significantly more abundant at salinities above 34 ppt.
- *Bairdiella chrysoura* showed an abrupt, significant increase in tows with cover indices of 6 or greater.

**Importance Value (IV) index**
- The authors developed an IV index (range 0–300) combining relative frequency, relative abundance and relative biomass per individual. They argue that IV gives a more realistic dominance ranking than abundance alone for long-term monitoring.
- [[Anchoa Mitchilli]] ranked first in both years, with IVs 4 times (1979) and 6 times (1980) greater than the second-ranked species.
- *Lucania parva* was among the 15 most abundant species but fell outside the top 15 by IV because of its small size and infrequent capture. *Cynoscion regalis* and *Sphoeroides nephelus* ranked higher by IV than by abundance.

**Human impacts (Discussion)**
- The authors consider Indian River the most heavily impacted basin. It has four causeways, heavy boat traffic, two fossil-fuel power plants that discharge heated effluent, and waste inputs from Kennedy Space Center, Titusville and industry.
- Mosquito Lagoon and the northern Banana River were less disturbed, and the authors suggest this may explain their higher species counts.

**Seagrass ecology (Rice, Trocino and Wells, truncated abstract)**
- Seagrass beds of *Halophila engelmannii*, *Halodule wrightii* and *Syringodium filiforme* were studied from April 1977 through June 1979 between Cocoa and Grant, Florida.
- Vegetative growth peaked in spring and, to a lesser extent, in fall. Water temperatures ranged from 22 to 30°C.
- Seasonal average epiphyte loads were 850, 615 and 210 mg/gm blade for *Syringodium*, *Halodule* and *Halophila*, respectively.
- Net photosynthesis was highest in spring and fall, at 1185, 1090 and 670 µg C fixed/mg Chl-hr for the same three species, in the order given in the abstract.
- Reduced growth in summer and winter coincided with more blade necrosis, dense epiphytes (over 100 mg/cm blade), low light, and temperatures below 20°C or above 30°C.

**Limitations**
- The Carlson et al. pore-water fragment contains only references, with no data or conclusions.
- The seagrass abstract ends mid-introduction, so its methods and full results are not available.
