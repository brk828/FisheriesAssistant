# Critical Assessment of Levins's *The Strategy of Model Building in Population Biology* (1966)

#population-biology #model-building #theoretical-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Commentary by [[Steven Hecht Orzack]] and [[Elliott Sober]] critically examines [[Richard Levins]]'s influential 1966 article on model building in population biology. Levins argued that model building involves a necessary trade-off among three desiderata: [[Generality]], [[Realism]] and [[Precision]]. He also claimed that truths about nature can be revealed by finding "[[Robust Theorems]]." The authors contend that neither claim should be accepted. They find no necessary trade-off among the three attributes, argue that the [[Type I-III Model Trichotomy]] does not divide models into real types, and conclude that robustness is ill-defined without a clear notion of [[Model Independence]]. The paper does not involve fish or any aquatic taxon; it addresses theoretical method in population and evolutionary biology.

## Background & Description

The commentary was prepared in response to Levins's 1966 article, which the authors say has been widely cited in both the Science Citation Index and the Social Sciences Citation Index. The authors' survey of those citations leads them to believe that Levins's arguments have been widely misinterpreted. The editors invited a reply from Levins, which appears in the same issue.

The authors offer working definitions of the three model attributes:

- **Generality** is comparative: a model is more general if it applies to more real-world systems than another model.
- **Realism** is also comparative: a model is more realistic if it takes account of more independent variables known to have an effect.
- **Precision** is dichotomous: a model is precise if it generates point predictions for output parameters.

Levins's taxonomy has three model types:

- **Type I:** sacrifices generality for realism and precision (a "pure numerical model").
- **Type II:** sacrifices realism for precision and generality (a "frictionless-plane" model, illustrated by [[Fisheries Yield Models]] and [[Heterozygote Superiority Model]] examples).
- **Type III:** sacrifices precision for generality and realism, making only qualitative assumptions and predictions.

The authors note that Levins's trichotomy applies to any subject, not only population biology, so the question has relevance across disciplines.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not study any organism, habitat or ecosystem. Its examples come from population genetics, population ecology and evolutionary biology. These include:

- The [[Hardy-Weinberg Law]], which the authors use to show that generality depends on how a law is phrased.
- A one-locus, two-allele model of viability selection, used to separate mathematical statements from empirical claims.
- Density-dependent population growth models, used to test whether generality and realism vary independently.
- [[Optimality Models]] of sex ratio evolution, including [[MacArthur 1965]] and [[Uyenoyama and Bengtsson 1979]].
- [[Group Selection]], [[Adaptationism]] and the [[Sufficiency of Natural Selection]].
- Consensus phylogenetic methods and the [[Wahlund Principle]].

The paper refers to fisheries work only through [[Watt 1956]], whose fishery models are cited as Type I examples. It does not report any fish or aquatic field data.

## Key Findings

**1. Levins's trade-off claim is rejected.**
- The authors argue that generality, realism and precision need not trade off. Adding an independent variable to a precise model increases realism and generality while the model stays precise.
- Treating precision as a matter of degree (accuracy of predictions) also removes the forced trade-off. Omitting drift and mutation from a population genetics model reduces all three attributes together.
- Generality and realism are tied together for nested uninstantiated models, but not for instantiated ones, where their association depends on how the models are applied.

**2. The trichotomy is not a division of model types.**
- The same model can show Type I, II or III characteristics depending on whether it is instantiated. A one-locus viability selection model is Type III-like when uninstantiated (it predicts a stable polymorphism) and Type II-like when instantiated (it predicts equilibrium genotype frequencies).
- Type III models do exist (the authors give the [[Allopatric Speciation]] and [[Sympatric Speciation]] models as qualitative examples), but Levins's scheme cannot separate mathematical models from one another.

**3. Robustness is an unclear concept.**
- A robust theorem holds across several models. The authors show that robustness is evidence of truth only in a special case, where one of the models is known to be true.
- If all models are false, robustness can reflect convenience rather than truth. The example given is the infinite population size implied by all models of selection as the only force.
- The authors note that [[Levins 1966]] instructs researchers to hold a common biological assumption constant. This practice, they argue, guarantees that the resulting theorems are not independent.
- Logical independence fails for competing models. Statistical independence cannot be applied, because there is no coherent way to assign probabilities to models.
- Robustness within a model (internal robustness, such as the Hardy-Weinberg prediction under Wahlund's principle) is meaningful, but it does not guarantee truth.

**4. Robustness across data sets has its own problems.**
- Robustness across data sets can be a virtue, but only when the data sets are independent. The authors give morphological measurements from left and right forelimbs as an example of non-independent data sets.
- The consensus tree can contradict the best tree for combined data (Barrett et al. 1991), so robustness can conflict with the principle of total evidence.

**5. Levins's claims have been widely misread.**
- Some authors treat the trade-off as a proven necessity. Others claim to have built general, realistic and precise models, and still others cite the "general impossibility" of such models.
- The authors judge that there has been considerable arbitrariness in how biologists place models within the three dimensions, mostly because they rarely name a benchmark model.

**6. Qualitative testing is insufficient.**
- Qualitative tests of models can miss quantitative discrepancies. In the example, an optimality model predicts a sex ratio of 0.95 (proportion female) and an individual produces 0.6, yet a qualitative test would call the model adequate.
- The sufficiency of natural selection, as opposed to its importance, can be assessed only through quantitative testing. The authors say that quantitative testing of optimality models has been done "very rarely."

**7. Recommendations for researchers.**
- Qualitative and quantitative testing are both essential.
- Biologists should not be overly self-conscious about where their models fit Levins's scheme. Most citing authors chose Type III, which the authors see as evidence that Levins's message is read as favoring Type III models.
- Robustness should be treated as having heuristic value, but little substance beyond that.
- Models should be classified by their assumptions and their degree of fit to data. Simplicity (goodness-of-fit against number of parameters) is an extra dimension that Levins did not consider.
