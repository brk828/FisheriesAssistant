# Unusual Fish Standing Crops in Bonita Creek, Graham County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Robert Clarkson and W. L. Minckley (August 1980) measured fish standing crop (biomass and density) in [[Bonita Creek]], a small desert stream in Graham County, Arizona, from November 1977 to December 1978. The goal was to track community trends linked to the variability of a desert stream environment. Bonita Creek is a small tributary of the [[Gila River]] (discharge generally under 0.17 m³/s), lying in the transition zone between the [[Sonoran Desert]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Upstream reaches supported five native fish species and no introduced forms at the time of study. The authors found unusually high biomass, especially at the open-valley site, and compared the values with those from more mesic (non-desert) streams.

## Background & Description

The creek is intermittent and flood-prone, with drainage conditions that vary markedly between its canyon and open-valley sections. The native fauna, the focus of the sampling, included [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarkii*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). The survey recorded five native species, one hybrid combination, and six introduced forms in the broader collections (Table 1, not reproduced). Upstream reaches were free of introduced species.

## Methods

- **Sampling design:** A measured stream area was electrofished downstream into a 4.5 × 1.2 m blocking seine. Large bottom material was vigorously agitated to dislodge fish from crevices and backcurrents so they would float into the net. Each site was sampled three consecutive times.
- **Equipment:** A 115 V AC gasoline generator powering [[Electrofishing]] electrodes.
- **Processing:** Fish were preserved in 10% formalin in the field, stored about one week, rinsed, and transferred to water for about one week. They were then sorted by species, counted, and weighed to the nearest 0.1 g. No corrections were applied to biomass for preservation loss.
- **Storage:** Specimens were transferred to 40% isopropanol or 70% ethanol and deposited in the [[Arizona State University Collection Of Fishes]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Bonita Creek lies in a hot-desert setting where flow regimes and flood frequency shape fish habitat. The study period included several notable disturbances:

- A substantial flood in early 1978 altered habitat quality and diversity throughout the study area, which may have directly caused some fish losses.
- Unusually heavy precipitation through 1978 ended a drought that had persisted in the upper Gila drainage during earlier years.
- Intermittency and human disturbance after the first collection at station III prevented quantitative comparisons with upstream reaches and forced an early end to sampling there.

The authors also cite the [[Eutrophication]] tendency of hot-desert streams (after Minshall 1978) as a possible reason for the high biomass values.

## Key Findings

**Standing crop trends by site**
- **Station I (open valley):** Biomass was initially high through the first three collections, dropped during warmer months, stayed low through October 1978, then rose dramatically. Biomass was generally higher in non-winter months, and winter-period density also rose. The dataset is not expressed as a single fixed number in the text.
- **Station II (narrow canyon):** Biomass was substantially lower than at Station I, changed little during the study, and was generally higher in non-winter months.
- **Station III:** Data were limited by intermittency and disturbance; the site was dropped early.

**Explanations offered for lower canyon biomass**
1. Canyon walls restrict sunlight, limiting [[Primary Production]] and therefore [[Secondary Production]].
2. Floodwaters are more severe in high-gradient, narrow canyons, and flood levels are greater in the lower part of the drainage. Floods of higher frequency and discharge probably reduce fish utilization of canyon reaches.

**Explanations offered for the Station I patterns**
- Concentration by drying: as flow receded, fish may have been concentrated into remaining pools. High early-season and final-collection biomass at Station I may therefore reflect drying rather than true production.
- Increasing perenniality: as flow became more permanent in upstream reaches, previously dry habitat may have become usable, adding fish density and standing biomass.
- Seasonal movement: drawing on [[Stebert]] (1980) studies in [[Aravaipa Creek]], the authors suggest that Sonora Sucker, Gila Mountain Sucker, and Roundtail Chub move into canyon areas in summer to avoid high temperatures, and back to open areas in winter. Minckley's (1980) Aravaipa Creek distribution data supported this interpretation.
- The rather dramatic biomass drop late in the study most likely relates to temperature at the time of the fourth collection, combined with changes in the discharge regime after the 1978 flood.

**Comparison with other streams**
- Bonita Creek biomass values are high compared with streams in more mesic areas (Table 2, not reproduced).
- Maximum values reported in the literature come from [[Tecopa Bore]] (California), [[Eagle Creek]] (Arizona), Aravaipa Creek, and the River Thames (U.K.). Except for the Thames, which is organically enriched, these are all hot-desert sites.
- The Bonita Creek maxima are at least two to three times the maxima reported from other desert streams.
- The average of the three Bonita Creek sites is at or above the maximum limits reported from non-desert climates.
- Mean biomass values for other desert streams are comparable to most maxima reported from mesic areas.

**Conclusion**
The authors propose that the apparent eutrophic character of hot-desert streams is a plausible explanation for the high standing crops and the discrepancies with mesic-area data. The results highlight that fish biomass in desert streams is strongly shaped by flood history, drying, and seasonal refuge use.
