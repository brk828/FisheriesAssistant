# Colorado River Fishes Recovery Program FY 93 Annual Report: Aspinall Unit Studies of Gunnison River Flow Manipulation and Colorado Squawfish Nursery Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ptychocheilus-lucius

## Executive Summary

This FY 93 annual report from the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Program]] (Project 19-C, dated December 10, 1993) evaluates how flow releases from the [[Aspinall Unit]] and experimental flows in the [[Gunnison River]] affect the availability and quality of nursery habitat for [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the [[Colorado River]]. Field sampling of backwater and side-channel habitats was conducted in March, August, and October 1993 in an upper reach near Grand Junction, Colorado and a lower reach at RM 20-30. Squawfish catches were low relative to 1992, with the upper reach producing the fewest fish on every sampling occasion. The report attributes the reduced habitat and fish numbers to higher 1993 flows, which shrank available habitat mainly to large permanent backwaters. A parallel geomorphic study of three backwater/side-channel sites examined scour and fill before, during, and after the 1993 spring runoff. The project was funded at $60,000 and fully expended.

## Background & Description

- **Project:** Recovery Program Project Number 19-C, "Aspinall Unit Studies: Evaluation of Gunnison River flow manipulation upon availability and quality of nursery habitat for Colorado squawfish in the Colorado River."
- **Principal investigators:** Leo Lentsch and Melissa Trammell ([[Utah Division Of Wildlife Resources]]); Frank Pfeifer and Chuck McAda ([[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]]); John Pitlick ([[University Of Colorado]]).
- **Schedule:** Initial year 1992; final year listed as ongoing.
- **Goals:**
  1. Determine current status and reproductive success of Colorado squawfish in the upper mainstem Colorado River and the effect of experimental Gunnison River flows created within the Aspinall Study Plan.
  2. Relate habitat availability at recommended Aspinall Unit releases to (a) habitat actually used by young squawfish and (b) number of young present during the autumn nursery period.
  3. Determine how flow manipulation and habitat availability affect growing season and size of YOY squawfish as they relate to potential overwinter survival.
  4. Coordinate with further [[Aspinall Unit]] operation studies to avoid duplication.
- **Contribution to recovery:** The study offers methods to evaluate the biological response to flow recommendations associated with the Aspinall Unit.
- **FY 93 tasks:**
  - Task 1: Obtain video at each study reach twice per year.
  - Task 2: Sample all backwaters at each station for squawfish use and ground-truth videos.
  - Task 3: Measure physical and biological habitat characteristics of backwaters at each station.
  - Task 4: Determine relationships between backwater area (as measured by video) and (a) number of young squawfish and (b) number of backwaters used by young squawfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species status and threats.** Surveys near Grand Junction, Colorado show that populations of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) are small and continue to decline (Osmundson and Kaeding, 1991). A potentially important factor is reduced available habitat. Backwaters are important to both adult and larval stages.

**Habitat formation.** Backwater morphology depends on the flow regime, which controls scour and fill. Some theories hold that regulated flows have caused a net filling of these areas and a resulting loss of habitat. Deposition at the upper end of a side channel can raise bed elevation and reduce flow entering the channel. Deposition at the lower end can limit the times fish can access the habitat.

**Non-native fishes.** In every sampling trip the majority of fish captured were non-native. In order of prevalence, the report lists [[Red Shiner]], fathead minnow, and sand shiner, with fewer carp, channel catfish, black bullhead, killifish, Gambusia, green sunfish, black crappie, and white sucker. The report notes a small increase in white sucker collections in 1993.

**Native fishes other than squawfish.** Speckled dace, bluehead sucker, roundtail chub, and suckers (many unidentified to species in the field) were collected. Gila were also identified and released.

**Sampling sites.**
- Upper section: near Grand Junction, Colorado.
- Lower section: RM 20-30, including habitat below Moab.

**Summary of field observations**
1. Higher 1993 flows, relative to 1992, reduced available habitat to primarily large permanent backwaters and concentrated squawfish in the remaining habitat.
2. Squawfish were often collected in areas of submerged vegetation. Vegetation is not available as habitat at previously sampled flows, but fish used it when present, much like flooded bottomlands.
3. Possible spawning occurred below Moab. Many more YOY squawfish were collected in the lower section than the upper in October. In August, a few YOY were collected in the lower section and none upstream. The report notes this may be coincidental, and that the difference between 5 fish and none is probably not significant. Fish may move downstream from an upstream spawning area and settle out at the lower site.
4. 1993 was an excellent reproductive year for native suckers. Suckers were released and preserved in large numbers. Some suckers were not identified to species to reduce handling mortality (upper: 686 released in August and 460 released in October; lower: 38 identified in August and 17 identified in October, with others preserved). Gila were identified and released (August: 14 upper, 22 lower; October: 37 upper, 13 lower).

### Thermographs

- In August, thermographs were placed in three backwaters in each section, in the deepest area of the deepest backwaters, with sensors anchored mid-column. Lower section readings were taken at RM 25.0, 21.5, and 20.4. Upper section readings were taken at RM 63.9, 62.5, and 58.0.
- In October, an additional thermograph was placed in one upper-section backwater at RM 62.5 (10/4, 0945) to record surface temperature fluctuations. No thermograph was placed in the lower section because the project ran out of thermographs.

### Backwater and Side-Channel Geomorphology

- **Sites:** Three backwater/side-channel sites were measured.
  - Site 1, RM 162.5: side channel at high flows, backwater at moderate flows, mostly dry at low flows. Six cross-sections surveyed (three upper, three lower). Surveyed March 22, May 14, June 1, July 1, and August 5, 1993.
  - Site 2, RM 159.9: side channel at all but the lowest flows, with a very low-velocity backwater at its mouth at moderate flows. Ten cross-sections surveyed (four upper, six lower). Surveyed March 23, May 25, July 2, and August 6, 1993.
  - Site 3, RM 175.5: side channel at high flows, backwater at all other discharges. Eight cross-sections (three upper, five toward the mouth), all across the side channel. Surveyed May 11, June 9, July 5, and August 7, 1993.
- **Methods:** Bed topography, substrate size, and channel slope were measured across side channels and adjacent main channels. Cross-sections were permanently marked with rebar or metal fence posts. An Electronic Distance Meter (EDM) theodolite was the main survey tool, supplemented by a level for short low-flow sections. Wading depths were measured with a metal probe in water 1.5-2.5 m deep (text reads "1-5-2.5 meters," likely an OCR error). Sonar was used in most cases. Radio communication linked boat and surveyor.
- **Main channel:** Cross-sections were surveyed about every two miles across the 15- and 18-mile reaches to place the study sites in the context of the river as a whole.
- **Planned analysis:** Changes in backwater/side-channel habitat from 1993 flows will be compared graphically and numerically. Relationships among discharge, scour, and fill will be calculated, and the discharges responsible for scour and fill will be estimated from slope and substrate data.
