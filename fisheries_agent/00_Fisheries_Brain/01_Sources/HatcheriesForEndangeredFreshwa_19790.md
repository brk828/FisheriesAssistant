# Hatcheries for Endangered Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, from the volume *Battle Against Extinction: Native Fish Management in the American West* (edited by W. L. Minckley and James E. Deacon, University of Arizona Press, 1991), describes the use of captive propagation and refugia to protect imperiled western North American fishes. It focuses on the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, the longest-running federal endangered fishes program in the region, and compares it with other facilities at [[Devils Hole]], [[Comanche Springs]] and elsewhere. The authors argue that captive maintenance is a legitimate, if temporary, recovery tool, but that it demands special safeguards against disease, hybridization and genetic loss.

## Background & Description

The authors frame captive conservation as an extension of long-standing practice, noting that zoos, botanical gardens and hatcheries already hold wild organisms for education and conservation. They cite the controversy over capturing the last [[California Condor]] as a parallel debate about the trade-offs of captivity: loss of wild contact and behavioral change versus the risk of extinction in the wild.

Fishes are considered well suited to captive work because they can be captured in numbers, bred readily, produce many offspring that can be stocked soon after hatching, mature quickly, and have a long history of sport-fish culture. Early efforts included [[Gila Trout]] rearing in New Mexico (1923–1939), [[Apache Trout]] propagation at Sterling Springs (1960–1974), and attempts with [[Razorback Sucker]] at the Phoenix Zoo (1968).

Three long-term programs began in the 1970s: the [[Devils Hole Pupfish Refugium]] (Nevada, stocked with 27 pupfish in October 1972), the Comanche Springs Pupfish Canal in Texas (1974), and the Dexter NFH endangered species program (from 1974). Dexter NFH was built in the early 1930s to rear warm-water sport fishes such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. It draws water at a constant 18°C from a shallow aquifer and holds sixteen 1360-L concrete tanks, four raceways and forty-eight earthen ponds (0.04–0.7 ha).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Many of the species discussed are endemic and have been extirpated or reduced by water development, flow regulation, piscicides, non-native fishes and habitat loss. Examples include:

- **Colorado squawfish**: the largest native minnow in North America, once up to 1.8 m and 36 kg; extirpated from the lower Colorado basin by about 1970.
- **Bonytail**: the rarest endemic big-river Colorado fish; a remnant population in [[Lake Mohave]] shows little or no recruitment since impoundment in 1954.
- **Woundfin**: lost from most of its historic range; a short Virgin River reach is threatened by red shiners, Asian tapeworm and water development.
- **Pupfishes at Leon and Comanche springs**: threatened by groundwater pumping and by illegal introductions of sheepshead minnow (*C. variegatus*), which hybridizes with native forms.
- **Amistad gambusia**: lost in the wild when Goodenough Spring was flooded by Amistad Reservoir in 1968, and later lost in captivity to contamination by mosquitofish.

Hatchery sites have their own ecological constraints. Big Bend gambusia, which live in thermal springs at 20–34°C, struggle when winter temperatures in hatchery ponds fall below their tolerance limits. Desert pupfish on the Colorado River delta expanded after uncontrolled flows in 1983–1984 and were predicted to contract as deltaic habitats dry under upstream regulation.

## Key Findings

**Dexter NFH program (1974–1989)**
- Twenty-four native fish taxa have been held at Dexter NFH since 1974; twelve were held at the time of the chapter (Table 13-1 lists the full set, with natural ranges across AZ, CA, CO, NM, NV, UT, WY, TX and Mexican states).
- The program has three goals: (1) protect genetic stocks in case wild populations are lost, (2) research threats and their alleviation, and (3) propagate fish for reintroduction within historic ranges.
- Staff at the time included a manager, three fishery biologists, a fisheries researcher and three support personnel.
- Water passes through each pond or system only once, to limit disease spread and mixing of species.
- Priority for space follows the [[Endangered Species Act]]: species most threatened in the wild rank highest, while species with no remaining habitat rank lowest. This "triage" policy led to the loss of the Amistad gambusia.

**Colorado squawfish**
- 122 wild fish were taken for brood stock since 1973, but 60 juveniles did not survive transfer.
- The 1989 brood stock comprised 14 wild fish, 185 F1 fish from the 1974 year class, and 243 F1 fish from 1981.
- In 1987, 31 females were injected with carp pituitary; 25 responded, producing 2,112,681 eggs. About 617,770 hatched, a 29.3% survival to swim-up fry.
- In October 1987, 103,110 fish averaging 7.6 cm TL were stocked into the Salt and Verde rivers, Arizona, where reintroduction began in 1985.
- Feed conversion was 1 kg of fish for every 8 kg of food from fry to 5 cm TL.

**Bonytail**
- Eighteen adults were collected from Lake Mohave (1976–1981). Six females and five males parented the 1,780 F1 brood fish produced in 1981.
- In 1983, twenty-four females produced 1,015–10,348 eggs each (mean 4,990), with 17–38% survival to swim-up.
- Dexter NFH ponds produce 10,000–20,000 young annually; about 110,000 were stocked into Lake Mohave from 1981 to 1987.

**Woundfin**
- About 1,000 fish were brought to Dexter NFH from 1978 to 1987, but many were lost to parasites and disease, especially *Ichthyophthirius multifilis* (ich).
- Production remained low: 464 fry from 157 adults in 1987, and 1,703 fry from 135 adults in 1988. Fecundity is about 200 eggs per female.
- No reintroductions had occurred at the time because of the risk of spreading Asian tapeworm.

**Chihuahua chub**
- Rediscovered in 1975 in Mimbres River tributary after 124 years, with fewer than 100 individuals. After flooding in 1978, ten fish were moved to Dexter NFH.
- The captive population reached 373 fish by 1987.
- USFWS policy is not to supplement established wild populations with captive stock, to avoid introducing disease or genetically inferior fish.

**Pupfishes and poeciliids**
- Leon Springs pupfish were protected by rotenone treatment and selective seining in Leon Creek (1976–1978) after sheepshead minnow hybridization. Only *C. bovinus* phenotypes remained by August 1978.
- In 1988, *C. variegatus* was found in East Sandia Spring, threatening the wild Comanche Springs pupfish population; a stock was moved back into Dexter NFH in 1989.
- Desert pupfish were reintroduced into eight natural or seminatural habitats in Arizona and two outside their historic range.
- Pecos gambusia were removed from the hatchery as sufficiently secure; genetic questions about managing its four populations remained unresolved.
- Gila topminnows were stocked at about 100 habitats in Arizona from 1981, with survival at roughly one-third of sites.

**Management concerns**
- Hatchery stocks risk contamination by hybridization, parasites (*Ichthyophonus* sp., *Lernaea* sp., Asian tapeworm) and mixing of species. Nets and equipment are cleaned between ponds.
- Wastewater drains into enclosed sumps, where evaporation and percolation prevent escape to the Pecos River.
- Experimental, nonessential designation under the ESA reduces protection for reintroduced populations, which the authors note is controversial, particularly for bonytail.

**Conclusion**
- The authors conclude that captive propagation is a means of perpetuating species until habitat can be restored, not an end in itself. Its success depends on genetic management, careful priority-setting, and continued recovery action in the wild.
