# Changes in Walleye Population in Rainy Lake and Factors in Abundance, 1924-75

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Percidae #freshwater-ecology #fisheries-science

## Executive Summary

This study documents a long-term (1924–1975) decline in the [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) population of [[Rainy Lake]], a large boundary lake between Ontario and Minnesota. Annual commercial walleye harvests fell from ~150,000 kg in the 1920s to just 19,000 kg by the early 1970s, and catch-per-unit-effort (CUE) from standardized 102 mm gill nets declined 58% (43.63 to 18.43 kg·km⁻¹·day⁻¹) between 1948 and 1969. A compensatory increase in individual growth rate (1959–1965) combined with sustained heavy exploitation drove a marked reduction in the mean age of the commercial catch, indicating a population increasingly dependent on younger, less resilient age classes. Multiple linear regression identified **spring water levels** and **brood stock abundance** (5-yr lag) as the two dominant predictors of walleye abundance, jointly explaining 65% of the variance in commercial CUE. The findings support water-level management and continued monitoring of exploitation as key levers for walleye population recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Species of interest**: [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*), family [[Percidae]]; also discusses associated species — [[Sauger]] (*Stizostedion canadense*), [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*), [[Cisco]] (*Coregonus artedii*), [[Burbot]] (*Lota lota*), [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*), [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*, introduced 1921), [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*), and [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*, introduced, date unknown).
- **Taxonomic richness**: 36 fish species across 15 families identified in Rainy Lake.
- **Study system**: Rainy Lake straddles the Canada–US border (Ontario/Minnesota) and is divided into three geographically distinct basins: **North Arm**, **East Arm**, and **Redgut Bay**.
- **Anthropogenic context**: Two towns (combined population 20,000), ~2,000 private cottages/homes, and 38 tourist camps (max 1,500 guest capacity) border the lake; domestic pollution has not been a significant problem.
- **Fishery history**:
  - 1885 – first recorded commercial fishery (pound-net [[Lake Sturgeon]] fishery for roe)
  - Turn of the century – extensive trapnet fishery for [[Cisco]]
  - 1904 – first gillnet fishery ([[Lake Whitefish]])
  - Pound nets dominant until 1920s; gill nets became primary gear thereafter, targeting walleye, northern pike, and lake whitefish
  - Minnesota ended its lake sturgeon fishery before 1938
  - 1970 – Ontario raised minimum gillnet mesh size from 102 mm to 108 mm
  - 1972 – Ontario closed East Arm/Redgut Bay commercial fisheries due to mercury contamination in walleye and northern pike (exceeding federal standards); reopened in 1974 after "blending" reduced mercury levels
  - Minnesota transitioning effort from walleye to lake whitefish via mesh-size regulation (102 → 133 mm)
- **Sport fishery**: Based almost entirely on walleye and northern pike. Tagging data (7,315 walleye tagged 1957–74; 1,191 returns within first year) suggest sport and commercial harvests were roughly equal (48% of returns from sport fishery), with evidence of partially discrete population segments exploited differentially by each fishery.

- **Geology**: Precambrian bedrock (Keewatin volcanics, Couchiching sediments, Windigokan sediments, Algoma granite, Animikie sediments, Keeweenawan diabase); Pleistocene glacial deposits (calcareous/noncalcareous till, clays, fluvioglacial sand/gravel). Southwestern shore may have been touched by postglacial [[Lake Agassiz]].
- **Trophic status**: Classified as [[Mesotrophic Lake|mesotrophic]] via the [[Morphoedaphic Index]] (Ryder 1965) — chemically unproductive, consistent with Precambrian Shield origin.
- **Hydrology**: Hydroelectric dam on Rainy River outlet (built 1909) regulates lake levels per International Joint Commission (IJC) rules. Normal pattern: fall/winter drawdown for power, spring/summer buildup. Post-1969 IJC regulations set a minimum May level of 336.93 m and maximum 337.36 m — a range considered only marginally adequate (upper end only) for flooding walleye spawning shoals.
- **Spawning habitat**: Lake-shoal spawning is the dominant walleye reproductive strategy in Rainy Lake, consistent with other populations (Eschmeyer 1950; Johnson 1961; Rawson 1957). Egg surveys: 14/68 North Arm shoreline sections held walleye eggs (1964); 16/30 suspected East Arm spawning shoals utilized (1965). Eggs concentrated on gravel/rubble/shingle substrates in water <0.3 m deep — substrate quality and spring flooding extent govern spawning success (per [[Johnson 1961]]).

## Key Findings

### 1. Commercial Harvest Trends (1924–1975)
- Total (all-species) commercial harvest averaged **374,308 kg/yr**, exceeding the MEI-predicted allowable harvest (258,682 kg) by **45%**.
- Walleye-specific harvest declined from **~150,000 kg (mid-1920s)** to **19,000 kg (early 1970s)**.
- Walleye's contribution to total commercial catch fell from **30% to <10%**.

### 2. Catch Per Unit Effort (CUE) Decline
- Standardized 102 mm gillnet CUE regression: **Y = 44.83 − 1.20X** (P<0.01).
- CUE dropped from **43.63 kg·km⁻¹·day⁻¹ (1948)** to **18.43 kg·km⁻¹·day⁻¹ (1969)** — a **58% decline**, used as the primary abundance index (per [[Ricker 1975]] methodology).

### 3. Compensatory Growth Response
- Mean total length of age IV walleye (North Arm, back-calculated) increased from **37.34 cm (1959)** to **41.60 cm (1965)**; regression: **Y = 36.63 + 0.71X** (P<0.01, r=0.98).
- East Arm age IV walleye were ~20% smaller than North Arm cohorts but showed a similar growth trend.
- Interpreted as a **density-dependent compensatory response** to declining abundance, consistent with patterns in other exploited fish populations (whitefish, pike, blue pike).
- Rainy Lake walleye growth was faster than Red Lakes (MN) and Many Point Lake, similar to Lake of the Woods and West Blue Lake, and slower than Lake Erie and Saginaw Bay populations.

### 4. Age Structure Collapse
- Mean age of North Arm commercial catch declined from **5.33 yr (1957)** to **3.63 yr (1967)**; regression **Y = 5.84 − 0.17X** (P<0.05).
- East Arm: declined from **6.53 yr (1955)** to **4.37 yr (1967)**; regression **Y = 6.71 − 0.18X** (P<0.05); East Arm changes lagged ~5 yr behind North Arm.
- Age III walleye contribution to North Arm catch rose sharply: **43% (1964), 63% (1965), 35% (1967)** — previously negligible pre-1964.
- East Arm: age VII+ fish contributed >50% of catch in the 1950s but <10% by the mid-1960s.
- Mechanistically linked to increased vulnerability at younger ages/smaller sizes to 102 mm gillnet selectivity (per [[Hamley and Regier 1973]]), representing a real leftward shift in the population's age structure, not just a gear artifact — since CUE did not increase concurrently.

### 5. Drivers of Abundance: Spring Water Levels
- Commercial CUE (1948–69) correlated with spring water levels measured 4, 5, and 6 years earlier.
- Correlation coefficients: **r = 0.48 (4-yr), 0.71 (5-yr), 0.47 (6-yr)** — all significant (P<0.05); 5-yr lag most robust.
- Regression: **Y = −93.26 + 16.81X** (water level 5 yr prior → CUE).
- Water levels alone explained **50%** of CUE variance (R²).

### 6. Drivers of Abundance: Brood Stock
- Progeny CUE regressed against brood stock CUE 5 years earlier: **Y = 6.51 + 0.64X** (P<0.01).
- Brood stock abundance alone explained **44%** of variance in walleye abundance 5 years later.

### 7. Combined Model
- Multiple linear regression: **Z = −58.39 + 0.42X + 9.9Y**, where X = spring water level, Y = brood stock abundance, Z = progeny abundance 5 yr later.
- Both coefficients significantly different from zero (P<0.01); each variable's addition significantly improved model fit (P<0.05).
- **Combined R² = 65%** of variance in commercial walleye CUE explained by spring water levels + brood stock abundance.

### 8. Management Implications
- Per Abrosov's model (Abrosov 1969) for stable European percid fisheries, average catch age should be **5.5–6.0 yr**; Rainy Lake walleye catch averaged **<4.5 yr** by the mid-1960s — signaling an unstable, overexploited stock structure.
- Reduced age diversity increases dependence on individual year-classes, lowering population resilience to environmental/biological stress.
- Post-1969 IJC minimum water level regulations judged **insufficient** for optimal walleye spawning habitat inundation.
- Author recommends an adaptive management approach: optimize spring water levels while holding exploitation constant, then reassess the relative role of exploitation based on population response.
- Comparable water-level/spawning-success relationships noted in [[Lake Erie]] walleye (Busch et al. 1975) and Scandinavian pikeperch (*Stizostedion lucioperca*; Svärdson and Molin 1973), supporting the generality of the water-level mechanism across percid populations.
