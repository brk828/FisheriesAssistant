# Conservation Genetics and the Management of Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This *Fisheries* (Vol. 11, No. 1, January–February 1986) synthesis by [[Gary K. Meffe]] argues that recovery programs for [[Endangered Species Act]]-listed fishes must incorporate [[Conservation Genetics]] from the outset. Management of endangered fishes is described as new and largely experimental. Genetic erosion from small population size, [[Genetic Bottleneck|bottlenecks]], [[Genetic Drift]], [[Inbreeding Depression]], [[Artificial Selection]] in captivity and mixing of distinct stocks can reduce fitness. The paper presents theoretical reasoning and five recommended actions, framed as a "best of all possible worlds" reference guide that managers must adapt to budgetary and facility constraints.

## Background & Description

Following the 1973 Endangered Species Act, fisheries scientists have worked to maintain declining populations of rare fishes. These species have been studied in the wild, reared in captivity for restocking, and supported by large investments of money and manpower. The author notes that:

- Only one hatchery facility for endangered fishes existed at the time: the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, established in 1974.
- The bias toward southwestern fishes reflects that about 67% of U.S. fishes originally listed as endangered are endemic to the Southwest (Johnson and Rinne 1982).
- Basic genetic principles were largely derived from other taxa and fields, including aquaculture, sport fisheries, animal husbandry, zoo care and population genetics, because the fish-specific literature was very small.

Meffe's stated purposes are twofold: to alert managers to potential genetic problems, and to propose first-approximation courses of action to minimize genetic deterioration of endangered stocks. The paper explicitly excludes economic, political and social constraints.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Three conservation goals.** Management should maintain (1) viable populations in the short term (extinction avoidance), (2) the capacity of fishes to adapt to changing environments, and (3) the capacity for continued speciation. Since environments are changing faster under human influence, adaptive capacity is the primary focus of conservation genetics.

**The central problem.** Loss of genetic variation erodes evolutionary flexibility and raises extinction probability. Total genetic variation has two components, both of which should be maximized:

- **Within-population variance**: variation within demes on which natural selection acts.
- **Among-population variance**: differences among populations; losing it causes convergence toward one "type" and fewer options for the species.

**Effective population size (Ne).** Census size (N) overstates the genetically effective size, which is nearly always smaller because of:

1. **Sex ratio.** Using Ne = 4NmNf/(Nm + Nf), a census of 100 fish with 50 males and 50 females gives Ne = 100, whereas 10 males and 90 females gives Ne = 36. The balanced population is about 2.8 times larger in genetic terms.
2. **Progeny distribution.** Using Ne = 4N/(2 + σ²), 1000 females with Poisson-distributed offspring (mean and variance of 2) give Ne = 2000. If one female produces 1001 offspring and the rest produce one each, variance rises to 31.6 and Ne falls to 238.
3. **Population fluctuation.** Ne is the harmonic mean of population sizes across generations. Five generations of 100 fish give Ne = 100. The sequence 100, 10, 300, 10, 80 (same arithmetic mean) gives Ne = 22, a 78% reduction.

**Bottlenecks.** A bottleneck samples a few individuals and leaves a remnant with less variation.

- Quantitative variation remaining after one bottleneck is approximately 1 − 1/(2N). Two survivors retain about 75% and ten retain about 95%, so mild bottlenecks do not drastically reduce quantitative variation.
- Rare alleles are lost more critically. In a locus with six alleles (frequencies .90, .02, .02, .02, .02, .02), a bottleneck of 10 causes an average loss of more than three alleles. Repeated over many loci, losses are considerable.

**Genetic drift.** Drift is random gene-frequency change from sampling error, effectively a chronic bottleneck. Retained variation under drift is approximately [1 − 1/(2Ne)]^t, where t is generations.

- A population of 10 retains about 60% of original variance after 10 generations.
- A population of 100 retains 95% after 10 generations, but only 60.6% after 100 generations (100 years in an "annual" fish).
- After 1000 years, variance falls to 0.677%.

In a hatchery stock of Montana westslope cutthroat trout derived about 14 years earlier from roughly 60 wild fish, proportion of polymorphic loci (P) fell 57%, mean alleles per locus (Ma) fell 29%, and heterozygosity (H) fell 21% (Allendorf and Phelps 1980).

**Inbreeding depression.** Inbreeding raises the proportion of homozygous genotypes without directly changing overall variance. Fitness traits (fecundity, fertility, age at maturity, body size, growth, survivorship) are most affected. Data from domesticated animals indicate that an increase in inbreeding (ΔF) of 1% reduces individual reproductive traits by 5–10%, which may amount to about 25% in aggregate reproductive performance across generations.

- Franklin (1980) and Soulé (1980) consider 1% inbreeding tolerable short term, equivalent to Ne = 50.
- Ne above 500 is suggested for long-term viability.

**Captivity and domestication.** Captive environments can select for traits that are advantageous in hatcheries but deleterious in the wild, such as reduced predator avoidance, altered growth or behavior, and increased fecundity at the cost of fitness. Hatchery and wild stocks may therefore diverge quickly.

**Mixing and outbreeding.** Mixing naturally isolated demes can break up coadapted gene complexes, producing hybrids that are robust initially but lose fitness over generations (outbreeding depression). Convergence under common hatchery conditions can also homogenize distinct stocks. Where natural habitats are altered in similar ways, previously isolated demes may need supplemental gene flow to mimic their former large gene pools.

## Key Findings

**Table 1: Recommended actions to maximize long-term genetic health of endangered fishes**

1. Monitor genetic variation in both field and captive populations (effects: reduces erosion of quantitative variation, loss of rare alleles and inbreeding potential).
2. Maintain the largest feasible genetically effective population size in captive stocks.
3. In small captive populations, avoid inbreeding through selective mating.
4. Minimize time in captivity and in hatchery environments (reduces artificial selection and "domestication," and minimizes bottlenecks, drift, inbreeding and catastrophic loss).
5. Maintain separate stocks of distinct populations to preserve population variance.

**Additional conclusions and recommendations**

- Collecting data on population structure and genetic and morphological variation is the most important first step. Without it, decisions about preserving variation are guesswork.
- Detailed genetic analyses should be conducted on any endangered fish to determine how variation is distributed. A Sonoran topminnow study by Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) found that genetic variation was partitioned into within-locality (21%), among localities within drainages (26%) and between drainages (53%), equivalent to subspecies. The author concludes that representatives of all three levels should be preserved, with within-group variation of somewhat lesser importance.
- Genetic management should favor large captive populations with equal sex ratios and balanced progeny contributions. Periodic replacement of stock with wild-caught fish may reduce loss of rare alleles, but must be weighed against outbreeding risk.
- Minimal captive stocks should be kept only as long as necessary, with monitoring of heterozygosity and effective size.
- The author cautions that current conservation genetics knowledge is incomplete and that the theoretical recommendations are a best-available reference rather than a settled prescription. He states that the costs of inaction are likely greater than the costs of applying genetic safeguards.
