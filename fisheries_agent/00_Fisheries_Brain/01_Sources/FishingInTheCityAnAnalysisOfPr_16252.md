# Fishing in the City: Put-and-Take Urban Fishing in Arizona Urban Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #salmonidae #cyprinidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 Arizona Game and Fish Department Research Branch report (Federal Aid in Sportfish Restoration Project F-14-R, authors Gary Edwards and Gene Okamoto) evaluates a one-year [[Put-and-Take Fishing]] program on two small urban lakes, [[Chaparral Lake]] (Scottsdale) and [[Lakeside Lake]] (Tucson). Access was limited to anglers holding a $3.00 adult or $1.00 juvenile [[Urban Waters Fishing Permit]]. The study measured permit sales, stocking outcomes, angler use, catch success, enforcement, and willingness to pay, and it used an economic analysis by the University of Arizona. Permit revenue was $27,520, while the program produced a net monetary loss of more than $36,000 for the department. Including consumer benefits and commercially contracted fish changes the outcome, and the report concludes that urban fishing is worth pursuing, with recommendations for program design.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Rising fuel costs after 1973 made long fishing trips hard for urban anglers, and new rural impoundments were ending. Most urban waters in Arizona lacked the physical or water-quality conditions for a self-sustaining fishery, so regular stocking was judged necessary.
- **Precedent:** A U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service pilot in [[St. Louis Urban Fishing Program]] (1969–1971) generated 78,860 angler days on 21.8 acres at about $0.38 per angler trip. A 1975 national survey found 89% of responding state fisheries agencies involved in urban fishing.
- **Study lakes:**
  - *Chaparral Lake* (Scottsdale): 10⅔ acres (text reads "10%"), 105 acre-feet, mean depth 10 ft (max 17 ft), built 1972–73 for $125,000 as part of the Indian Bend Wash Greenbelt flood-control system. Population within 2 miles was 40,203 (1975).
  - *Lakeside Lake* (Tucson): 12 acres, 44 acre-feet, mean depth 5 ft, in a 50-acre park; completed 1976 at about $74,000 in construction funds. Population within 2 miles was 32,531 (1975). Lakeside is subject to sediment-laden floods from its drainage.
- **Permit rules:** Permits were valid for six-month periods, July–December 1977 and January–June 1978, and were required of all ages. Adults also needed a state license ($4.00 resident plus $3.00 trout stamp).
- **Methods overview:** Weekly random angler interviews (1,016 interviews plus 186 reinterviews, about 9% of permit holders), a yellow direct-question and green [[Five Dollar Auction]] questionnaire, [[Travel Cost Method]] and [[Willingness-to-Pay Approach]] economic analysis, school surveys of 5th graders, a mail survey of non-renewing anglers (1,476 of 4,110 returned, 36%), and enforcement summaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocked species (12-month totals):**
- Common carp: 5,996 (catchable minimum 3 lb)
- Channel catfish: 13,987 (minimum 1 lb)
- Tilapia (*T. mossambica*, *T. zillii*): 6,228 (minimum ½ lb)
- Rainbow trout: 18,000 (minimum ⅓ lb)

**Stocking densities and timing:** Planned warm-water stocking was 750 each of catfish, carp, and Tilapia per lake per month in warm months, and 1,500 trout per lake per month in winter. Overall, 96% of intended fish were stocked at Chaparral and 89% at Lakeside. Lakeside suffered a resident fish die-off in June 1977 and received its first fish on July 18.

**Non-stocked species:** Black crappie, largemouth bass, and bluegill at Chaparral; black bullhead at Lakeside. These made up 22% of Chaparral's catch and 37% of Lakeside's.

**Environmental factors:** Lakeside's turbidity from flood-borne sediment appears to reduce catchability, and the shoal area limits shore access. Summers are hot (mean maximum 38.9°C at Chaparral), and annual precipitation is 7.44 in at Chaparral and 11.05 in at Lakeside.

**Management principle:** The report relies on [[Shupp Stocking Philosophy]], which holds that frequent stockings of smaller numbers maintain stable catch rates and fishing pressure better than large infrequent plantings.

## Key Findings

**Permits and revenue**
- 12,515 permits were sold, generating $27,520 in gross sales ($26,168 net to the department after 5% dealer fees). Adults bought 60% of permits in combined sales table (7,489 adult, 5,026 juvenile).
- Only 20% of first-period buyers purchased a second-period permit. Main reasons were low angling success and lack of time. 71% of respondents said they would buy permits if the program expanded.

**Angler use**
- An estimated 72,409 angler days were provided (Chaparral 42,772; Lakeside 29,637).
- Adults made up about 71% of use. Female anglers accounted for 22% of Chaparral pressure and 19% of Lakeside pressure.
- Permit buyers were mostly Anglo, middle-aged or older males who drove, fished alone, and lived within 4 miles.

**Catch success and harvest**
- Catch rates were 0.31 fish per hour at Chaparral and 0.26 at Lakeside, below the 0.50 traditional put-and-take standard but judged acceptable by urban anglers.
- Estimated harvest: Chaparral 82% of stocked fish (trout 98%, catfish, carp, and Tilapia lower); Lakeside 40%.
- Trout tag returns reached 50% within four days at Chaparral. Tilapia were unsuccessful (5,705 stocked, 169 reported caught). Carp harvest was high (91% at Lakeside), with 81–85% of harvested carp eaten.

**Costs and economics**
- Actual program cost was about $62,608, versus $38,978 if fish were contracted from commercial farms.
- Net monetary loss was $36,439 as conducted, or $12,699 with contracted fish.
- Total consumer benefits were $46,596 (Chaparral $28,470; Lakeside $18,126), and including these the net result became a gain of $7,728 with contracted fish.
- Estimated annual stocking cost for expansion is $2,059 per acre.

**Willingness to pay and pricing**
- Non-discriminating monopolist prices suggested about $3.40–$3.50 for adults at Chaparral and about $2.00 at Lakeside, with $2.00–$3.00 for juveniles. The ex-post maximum collectable revenue was $25,589.
- Willingness-to-sell values exceeded willingness-to-pay values.
- Urban fishing appeared to be an inferior good: income was negatively associated with willingness to pay in about 75% of tests.

**Juvenile and minority use**
- Juvenile use was initially high but declined. More than 50% of juvenile permit holders lived within two miles of the lake.
- Black participation was over ten times the Blacks' share of the Phoenix metropolitan population during the 1977 catfish season at Chaparral. Hispanic use was lower than local demographics at Chaparral.

**Enforcement**
- 394 of 2,480 anglers contacted (15%) were in violation. The most common violation was failure to buy a permit (75%).
- Only 70 violators (18%) were cited; fines totaled $1,009.

**Expansion projection**
- A network of 11 waters (108.5 acres) would require $223,402 per year. Projected revenue was $112,946, covering about 50% of costs.
- Projected permit sales in Phoenix rise to 16,360 (from 5,876), and in Tucson to 11,099 (from 5,244).

**Recommendations**
- Appoint a competent [[Urban Biologist]] with a fisheries background.
- Select sites using population density criteria, water quality, access, and conflict potential.
- Keep permit fees near willingness-to-pay estimates and use a combined urban-plus-statewide license option.
- Contract fish purchases with performance and bonding requirements, and seek funding from parks departments and other agencies.
- Use fishing clinics for youth, and avoid over-strict enforcement that deters target groups.
- Monitor programs using criteria from the New York Department of Environmental Conservation.
