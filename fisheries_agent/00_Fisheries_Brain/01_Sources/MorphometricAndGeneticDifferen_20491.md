# Morphometric and Genetic Differentiation Among Death Valley System Rhinichthys osculus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] under Contract FG0524 by Donald Sada, Hugh B. Britten, and Peter F. Brussard, examines the systematics of [[Speckled Dace]] (*[[Rhinichthys Osculus]]*) in the [[Death Valley System]] (DVS). It combines [[Morphometric Analysis]] of 439 fish with [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] of 311 fish from 11 localities. Results are compared with populations from the [[Lahontan Basin]] and the [[Lower Colorado River]].

The study finds two broad body forms, a deep-bodied "quiet-water" form and a slender "fluvial" form, with notable exceptions. Allozyme data show strong genetic isolation among DVS populations, with gene flow estimated at Nm = 0.19. The authors conclude that each DVS population is unique and that the [[Long Valley Speckled Dace]] may warrant taxonomic recognition.

## Background & Description

The DVS is an endorheic system comprising the Owens, Amargosa, and Mojave river basins. These drained into [[Pleistocene Lake Manly]] (present-day Death Valley) during pluvial periods. Speckled dace occur in isolated habitats in the Amargosa and Owens basins but not in the Mojave basin. Earlier work hypothesized that Amargosa dace derive from lower Colorado River stock and Owens dace from Lahontan stock. Taxonomy has been unsettled: Gilbert (1893) described *R. nevadensis* from [[Ash Meadows]], and later authors variously treated Amargosa populations as *R. o. nevadensis* or as undescribed subspecies.

*Rhinichthys* shows wide morphological variation. Its species count ranges from seven to nine depending on the classification used, and *R. osculus* is possibly the most variable species in the genus. No complete taxonomic analysis of speckled dace had been conducted before this study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat-morphology model.** [[Miller 1984]] proposed that deep bodies, small fins, and reduced meristic counts are associated with quiet-water habitats such as springs, while slender bodies and high meristic counts are associated with fluvial habitats. The DVS data only partly fit this model:

- [[Long Valley]] fish are deep-bodied, typical of quiet water, but their meristic counts and fin sizes are typical of fluvial populations.
- [[Little Lake]] fish have small fins typical of deep-bodied forms, but their meristics and body form fall within the fluvial range.
- Amargosa basin populations are deep-bodied with reduced counts, although only Bradford Spring and Tubbs Ranch offer predictable quiet-water habitat. Sporadic scouring floods in the Amargosa River create more fluvial conditions.
- [[Whitmore Hot Spring]] (quiet water) and [[Hot Creek]] (fluvial) fish look similar in body form.

The authors conclude that morphology in DVS dace results from factors other than environment alone.

**Landscape history.** Until the mid-1900s, the upper [[Owens River]] flowed through Owens Valley into Long Valley. Municipal diversion then dried a 15 km stretch and isolated Long Valley. The presence of native [[Tui Chub]] and [[Mountain Sucker]] in this reach suggests it was not a barrier to fish movement before diversion.

## Key Findings

### Study design and sampling

- Allozyme analysis: 11 localities, 311 fish, 25 isozyme loci assayed (10 polymorphic).
- Morphometric analysis: 13 collections from 12 localities, 439 fish, 18 truss measurements plus 14 additional meristic and mensural characters.
- Only fish longer than 25 mm SL were analyzed.
- The lower Colorado River sample is a single population ([[Beaver Dam Wash]]). The authors had hoped to add an Arizona population but did not receive those fish. They judged that adding them was unlikely to change conclusions.

### Morphology

- Meristic counts differed significantly among populations for all characters (p < 0.001).
- Lateral line scales ranged from fewer than 50 to near 70. Pectoral fin rays ranged from 12 to more than 14. Vertebrae ranged from 36 to nearly 39.
- Groupings from meristics:
  - Amargosa basin and Marble Creek: consistently low lateral line scale and pore counts.
  - Long Valley: high pectoral and pelvic fin ray counts, few vertebrae, no barbels.
  - Beaver Dam Wash: a well-developed frenum, unique among the sampled populations, plus high lateral line scale, pore, and vertebra counts.
  - Owens Valley and Lahontan basin: intermediate values.
- Fin-length data distinguished Amargosa basin populations and Little Lake (reduced fin size) and Beaver Dam Wash (large male pelvic fins).
- Sheared principal components analysis showed that body depth and width account for most body-shape variation. Factor-score plots only weakly matched the meristic groupings, and most populations overlapped strongly.
- Two body forms emerged when overlapping populations were grouped:
  - **Deep-bodied:** Amargosa basin, Long Valley, and Marble Creek.
  - **Slender-bodied:** Lahontan basin, lower Colorado River, and the remaining Owens basin populations.

### Population genetics

- Polymorphism ranged from 4% (Lower Marble Creek, Little Alkali Lake) to 36% (Huntington Creek).
- Mean observed heterozygosity ranged from 0.007 (Little Alkali Lake) to 0.061 (Reese River).
- Hardy-Weinberg departures were few and were considered likely statistical artifacts, except that the Huntington Creek departures may reflect pooling of semi-isolated segments (Wahlund effect).
- Allele frequencies differed significantly among all 11 populations at all polymorphic loci (p < 0.05).
- Hierarchical analysis showed significant differentiation within drainages:
  - Owens basin: six of eight polymorphic loci differed significantly.
  - Amargosa basin: all four polymorphic loci differed significantly.
  - Smoke Creek: differences at two of four loci.
- The two Humboldt drainage populations (Reese River and Huntington Creek) did not differ at any of nine polymorphic loci, suggesting higher gene flow there than among DVS populations.
- Reported F-statistics: population-to-drainage 0.550 and population-to-total 0.558. Roughly half of the variance in allele frequencies lies among populations within drainages and half among drainages.
- The PEPA locus carries a fixed, diagnostic "D" allele found only in Whitmore Hot Spring and Little Alkali Lake, roughly four km apart in Long Valley.

### Genetic affinities

- In the neighbor-joining dendrogram (Nei's unbiased distances), two DVS populations cluster with the lower Colorado population and the rest with Lahontan populations. The Beatty sample is highly divergent, due to fixation of rare alleles.
- Whitmore Hot Spring and Little Alkali Lake cluster closely. Their Nei genetic distance of 0.003 is among the lowest observed in the study.
- Geographic concordance is weak: Owens and Amargosa samples associate with both Lahontan/Humboldt and lower Colorado populations.
- Principal component analysis: the first two components explain 69% of allozyme variance (PC1 51%, PC2 18%). Clustering resembles the dendrogram.
- The consensus cladogram is largely unresolved. Its one well-supported clade is the Long Valley group, which shares the unique PEPA D allele.

### Gene flow and heterozygosity

- Estimated Nm = 0.19, approximately one genetically effective migrant every five generations. This is well below the level thought necessary to prevent divergence under a neutral model.
- Heterozygosity in DVS populations is lower than in the Lahontan and lower Colorado populations. Published means of 0.052 for 183 fish species (Nevo et al. 1984, cited in Crow 1986) and 0.052 for 60 cyprinid species (Buth et al. 1991) suggest the comparison populations are near average, and DVS populations are reduced. The authors attribute this to isolation and [[Genetic Drift]].

### Bishop population

- The [[Bishop Population]] clusters closer to Lahontan dace than to other Owens basin dace. The authors offer two explanations: recent human-mediated transplantation (for example, bait use) or a historically large population that diverged little from its Lahontan ancestors. The Bishop population occupies over 100 km of the Owens River and has the highest expected heterozygosity of the DVS populations.

### Conclusions and recommendations

1. **Two ecotypes** exist among DVS dace (deep-bodied quiet-water and slender fluvial), with exceptions such as Long Valley and Little Lake.
2. **Each DVS population is unique.** Morphology and allozymes both show little gene flow, and the genetic isolation is significant.
3. **Long Valley dace** (Hot Creek, an extinct population; Whitmore Hot Spring; Little Alkali Lake) form a distinct assemblage. The authors suggest subspecies-level recognition may be appropriate, but recommend further analysis with mtDNA and nuclear DNA markers before formal revision.

The authors summarize the data as "much evolutionary 'noise' and only a few faint 'signals.'" Historical biogeographic affinities remain unclear, and the data cannot falsify the hypothesis that Amargosa dace derive from lower Colorado stock and Owens dace from Lahontan stock.
