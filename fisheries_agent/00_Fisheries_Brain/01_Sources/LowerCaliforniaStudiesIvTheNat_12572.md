# The Natural Landscape of the Colorado Delta

#marine-biology #aquatic-ecology #geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This 1932 geographic study by Fred B. Kniffen (University of California Publications in Geography, vol. 5) describes the natural landscape of the [[Colorado Delta]], an alluvial surface built by the [[Colorado River]] where it enters the upper [[Gulf of California]]. The document is primarily about landforms, climate, vegetation and geomorphic history, not about fish. Aquatic organisms appear only incidentally: fish in the river and in ephemeral lakes, salt-water fish reaching the lower river on tides, freshwater and brackish mollusks, and flagellate infusoria that colour the upper gulf. Its main contribution is a relief-based classification of the delta into natural areas and an account of how river, sea, tectonics and climate interact to shape them.

## Background & Description

- **Extent:** The delta runs from the [[Salton Sink]] on the north to Punta San Felipe on the south, and from the [[Sierra Juarez]] on the west to the [[Santa Clara Mesa]] on the east. It is about 150 miles long and about 40 miles wide at its widest.
- **Physiography:** It lies within the Basin Range Province, bounded on the west by the Peninsular Range. Kniffen places it in the Lower Sonoran division of the Lower Austral life zone, and notes that its vegetation and fauna contrast sharply with the surrounding desert.
- **Bounding mountains and features:** The [[Cucopa Mountains]] (Sierra de los Cucopas) lie in the central delta, separated from the Sierra Juarez by the basin of [[Laguna Salada]]. The [[Sierra de las Pintas]] are volcanic and nearly barren. Sierra de las Tinajas is a basalt-capped mesa. Superstition Mountain lies west of Imperial Valley.
- **Three corner stones:** Sykes' term for the isolated masses [[Cerro Prieto]] (an extinct volcano), [[Pilot Knob]] (granite cut by later volcanics, where the Colorado leaves its restricted channel), and Punta San Felipe (a granitic dome marking the southern limit of river alluvium).
- **Natural areas:** Kniffen recognises five: the [[Salton Sink]] (disregarded in detail), the [[Delta Cone]], [[Pattie Basin]], the [[Gulf Plain]], and the desert mountain areas (the Cucopas and Las Pintas ranges).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and the river.** The Colorado drains about 265,000 square miles across five western states. The upper dependable tributaries, above and including the San Juan, supply most of the water. The lower tributaries (Gila, Bill Williams, Virgin, Little Colorado) are unreliable and produce sudden flash floods. Kniffen compares the river's paradoxical position with that of the [[Nile River]], which also rises in a humid area and loses volume in an arid mouth region.

**Discharge at the [[Yuma Gauging Station]] (24-year average):**
- September 30 to March 30: about 13,000 second-feet.
- Average maximum, last week of June: about 81,000 second-feet.
- Flash flood, February 1920 (from the Gila): 164,000 second-feet for one day.
- Absolute maximum: 186,000 second-feet, June 28, 1921.
- Absolute minimum: 1,200 second-feet, September 11, 1924.
- Mean annual run-off: 17,000,000 acre-feet, about one-fourth of the Nile at Cairo.

**Silt load.** An 1892–93 survey gave an average dry-material-to-water ratio of 1 to 277 for the Colorado, compared with 1 to 1900 for the Nile, 1 to 1500 for the Mississippi, and 1 to 3060 for the Danube. A 1904 study by Forbes found 9.62 tons of silt per acre-foot of water, and an annual load above 120,000,000 tons in a below-normal year. The average annual load past Yuma is estimated at about 160,000,000 tons. Silt-laden water changes the mechanics of flow: the river alternates between scouring and deposition, with a critical point near 47,000–50,000 second-feet.

**Tides and the sea.** The narrowing [[Gulf of California]] produces strong tides. At San Felipe, spring tides rise about 20 feet. At Phillip's Point, the spring range is 31.5 feet and the average range is 21.6 feet. Currents reach six knots, with no slack water. The incoming tide forms a [[Tidal Bore]] ("el burro") that normally reaches about four feet at spring tides and up to ten feet in shallow water. Tidal influence reaches far up the Hardy and into the old Colorado channel, and the tide meets flood water to produce overflow in the lower delta.

**Salinity and aquatic life.**
- During floods, river water is potable even at half-tide as far down as Coyote Slough, opposite Montague Island.
- During low water, the Hardy is scarcely potable up to Mayor.
- In one period of continual low water, "small sharks and other salt water fish" ascended nearly to Mayor, and the water could not be drunk even by cattle.
- Stranded fish were found in windrows around the shores of Laguna Salada, where the lake had nearly or entirely disappeared (observed by Anza's 1774 party, MacDougal's party in 1907, and Orcutt in 1890). Kniffen also cites fish and beaver, muskrat, and other animals in the streams of the Delta Cone.
- Fresh or brackish mollusks, identical to living species in desert springs and streams, occur along old beaches and on the desert floor, and are evidence of past freshwater lakes.
- In the upper gulf, patches of red water were caused by "great numbers of flagellate infusoria," not river alluvium.

**Climate.** Yuma (1876–1926) has a January mean of 54.4 °F and a July mean of 91.0 °F. Calexico's July mean maximum is about 12 °F higher. Absolute extremes are 117 °F and 21 °F at Calexico and 120 °F and 22 °F at Yuma. Annual precipitation is 3.10 inches at Calexico and 3.42 inches at Yuma. A single-day storm at Yuma recorded 4.01 inches in August 1909. Annual evaporation from a free water surface at Calexico is 80.66 inches. Relative humidity is low, but early-morning humidity peaks in August and September. Kniffen classifies the climate as BWhh in Russell's modified Köppen scheme.

**Vegetation.** Vegetation follows water availability and soil texture:
- Cottonwood and black willow line the old channel. Arrowweed forms dense overflow stands. Mesquite forms woodland on higher, occasionally flooded ground.
- Tules and rushes occupy permanent water. Salt grass grows on river-mouth islands and brackish springs.
- Mesa vegetation is dominated by [[Creosote Bush]], ocotillo, cholla, and palo verde. Palo fierro, visnaga, and [[Maguey]] occur in higher ranges. Native [[Washingtonia Palms]] mark permanent springs in the Cucopas.
- Kniffen records that Bermuda grass had recently invaded the lower delta.

## Key Findings

1. **Delta origin and structure.** The delta is an alluvial surface built by the Colorado in a closely confined gulf, where strong tidal action produces unusual forms. Its crest runs roughly from Pilot Knob to Cerro Prieto, with a gradient south of the crest of less than two feet per mile.

2. **Tectonic frame.** The Gulf of California and Salton Trough are interpreted as a graben or a geosynclinal depression. Kniffen's relief classification places the trough as a first-order form, followed by the Sierra Juarez–San Pedro Mártir scarp (second order), the Cucopas and Las Pintas (third and fourth orders), and mud volcanoes (fifth order). Lines of weakness include the southward extension of the [[San Jacinto Fault]].

3. **Volcanic and seismic activity.** Mud volcanoes, fumaroles and hot springs are associated with fault lines. Their activity increases with major earthquakes, a link noted as early as 1852.

4. **Secondary deltas and channel change.** Diversion of the Colorado in 1905 created new anastomosing channels in the New and Alamo rivers, and the river has repeatedly shifted between channels (Bee, Pescadero, Hardy). Kniffen describes how the river built a delta cone at the end of a constructed channel, extending by about two miles per year.

5. **Salton Sink history.** Kniffen supports a modified version of Blake's hypothesis: a brief marine phase, a delta barrier closing the sink, a fresh-water lake at the [[Ancient Beach Line]] (up to about 58 feet above sea level), and recent uplift that lowered the sink below sea level. He argues that the desiccation is geologically recent.

6. **Laguna Salada.** Pattie Basin is an enclosed bolson below sea level, filled episodically by river overflow during high water combined with high tides. Recorded lake years include 1884, 1893, 1906, 1914, 1923 and 1928. Lake salinity rises as volume falls.

7. **Dunes and aeolian forms.** Active and fixed dunes (notably the [[Algodones Sand Hills]]) and accretion mounds reflect the strong northwest winds. Kniffen describes desert pavements, caliche cementation, and granite weathering patterns (joint-controlled inverted-V dissection and "socket" weathering).

8. **Human-driven change.** The 1905 diversion and subsequent river shifts altered vegetation, shifted the boundary between the Delta Cone and Gulf Plain, and extended arrowweed and willow at the expense of grassland. Cattle ranching and irrigation in the north are the main cultural modifications.

9. **Overall process balance.** Kniffen concludes that diastrophism and the river work against each other: tectonics creates relief and the river tends to fill and destroy it. The sea and climate contribute to relief reduction, and climatic weathering is less significant than mechanical weathering and denudation.
