# Acid Mine Rehabilitation Problems at High Elevations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #watershed-management

## Executive Summary

This 1975 symposium paper, by Johnston, Brown, and Cravens of the USDA Forest Service, describes a multiagency project rehabilitating [[Acid Mine Drainage]] and disturbed land at the [[McLaren Mine]] and [[Glengary Mine]] on the [[Beartooth Plateau]], Montana. The work addresses acid drainage, slope stability, revegetation, and visual impact in a heavily used recreation area. Research covers revegetation, microclimate, soil-plant-water relations, and water quality, funded through the [[Surface Environment and Mining Program]]. The authors conclude that alpine rehabilitation is especially difficult because harsh climate and poor spoil conditions limit plant establishment, and that any surface disturbance may accelerate acid production.

No fish or aquatic fauna are studied; the aquatic component concerns water chemistry and stream degradation in the [[Upper Stillwater River]] and [[Fisher Creek]].

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Mining on the Beartooth Plateau began in the late 1870s. The study area lies mostly above 3,000 m in an alpine-subalpine transition zone.
- **Climate:** Mean monthly wind speed ranged from 14 km/h (July) to 27 km/h (January). Mean summer daily temperature was 7–8 °C. Annual precipitation was 150–200 cm, mostly as snow, and highly variable due to wind scour and drift.
- **Geology:** A Pre-Cambrian granitic uplift with basalt and acid porphyry intrusions. The ore is a hydrothermal pyritized-copper deposit with gold, silver, and copper values.
- **Mine history:** Shallow open-pit and underground hard-rock mining. The McLaren Mine was largely abandoned by the mid-1950s. The Glengary closed in 1967 after its mill site was destroyed by an avalanche.
- **Project partners:** Custer and Gallatin National Forests, Intermountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, Montana Bureau of Mines and Geology, Montana Fish and Game Department, and the Environmental Protection Agency.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Microenvironment.** Mine spoils reach more extreme and variable conditions than undisturbed tundra. Soil surface temperatures reached nearly 50 °C on spoils, while undisturbed vegetated sites were only slightly above air temperature. Spoils are nearly bare of vegetation and more topographically irregular.

**Soil-plant-water relations.** Low water-holding capacity, very low organic matter, and poor heat and hydraulic conductivity make soil water the main limiting factor. Near-surface water potential on spoils fell to about -20 to -30 bars after a few clear days, versus about -10 bars on undisturbed sites. Leaf water potential of *Poa alpina* fell from -10 bars to about -30 bars by midafternoon on spoil, while on undisturbed ground it did not drop below -18 bars.

**Water quality.** Oxidation of [[Pyrite]] produces acid and releases metals. Mining accelerates this process. Drainage is highly acidic and laden with heavy metals, and streams below the sites are described as essentially sterile for several miles, with iron hydroxide deposits on channel beds.

**Alpine recovery.** Literature cited in the paper suggests plant community recovery may take ten to a thousand times longer than at lower elevations. One cited example noted cutbanks still mostly bare 25 years after road construction in Colorado.

**Frost processes.** Needle ice, solifluction, and hummock formation disturb soil and dislodge young plants, which then desiccate.

## Key Findings

**Revegetation design.** Plots were established in fall 1974 on a southwest slope at 2,950 m at the McLaren Mine. Two 15 × 30 m main plots (overburden alone and overburden with 20 cm of topsoil) each contain 36 subplots of 2 m². Four randomly assigned treatments were replicated nine times: native or introduced seed mixes, each with or without fertilizer. The fertilizer was 16-16-16 at 111 kg N/ha, and seed was broadcast at 56 kg/ha. Native transplants were also established. Assessments in 1975 will measure basal area, plant density, species composition, height, and phenology.

**Microclimate monitoring.** A battery-powered automated data logger was planned for 1975 to measure solar radiation and albedo, air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, precipitation, soil water potential, and soil temperature at paired mine and undisturbed sites.

**Lysimeter water quality (1974 means, Table 1):**

| Parameter | Undisturbed | Mine disturbance |
|---|---|---|
| pH | 4.9 | 2.3 |
| Specific conductance (µmhos/cm) | 61 | 11,828 |
| Copper (mg/L) | <1 | 36 |
| Iron (mg/L) | <1 | 605 |
| Sulfate (SO₄) | 15 | 2,964 |

Acidity and concentrations rose with depth and were highest early in the season, indicating flushing during snowmelt.

**Lysimeter discharge range (Table 2):** pH 1.8–2.9; conductance 4,410–11,150 µmhos/cm; copper 102–3,600 mg/L; iron 42–2,400 mg/L; aluminum 233–323 mg/L; zinc 11–69 mg/L; sulfate 83–254 mg/L. The authors describe this variability as evidence of heterogeneous spoil. They warn that reshaping or ditching would accelerate oxidation.

**Treatments under evaluation.** Six lysimeters (each about 16 m³ of spoil) will test topsoil with revegetation, liming, alumina/silica gel, and surface sealing, with treatments applied in July 1975. Preliminary estimates call for 111–167 kg/ha (100–150 lb/acre) of lime to raise pH to 5.5–6.0.

**Treatment assessments.**
- Chemical water treatment was rejected because of inaccessibility, prolonged snow cover, high cost, and conflicts with revegetation and aesthetic goals.
- Impervious surface sealing is costly, degrades, and conflicts with revegetation.
- Alumina/silica gels may reduce acid production but are untested in alpine climates.
- Any treatment reducing infiltration or concentrating runoff could cause erosion, gullying, higher peak flows, and channel degradation off site.

**Photosynthesis.** The ¹⁴CO₂ method will be used in 1975 to compare photosynthetic efficiency of plants on mine spoil and undisturbed tundra.

**Overall conclusion.** Rehabilitation must rest on sound engineering and revegetation principles, and requires better understanding of the plant-soil-water system in alpine ecosystems.
