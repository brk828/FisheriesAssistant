## Executive Summary

This document comprises two papers from *Freshwater Crayfish* (vol. 3, 1977). The first, by Jay V. Huner of [[Southern University]], updates the status of [[Procambarus Clarkii]] introductions outside its native range and reports new introductions in Africa, Central America, and Europe. The second, by R. S. Lowery and A. J. Mendes of [[Kenyatta University]], documents the biology of the species in [[Lake Naivasha]], [[Kenya]], and its impact on the local gill-net fishery.

The central conclusion is that *P. clarkii* is a highly adaptable species whose introductions have been mostly damaging, as native consumers rarely use it and it can rapidly reach pest densities from very small founder populations. Huner recommends that introductions occur only after an impartial expert panel, working with the [[United Nations]], determines that the species will fill an unfilled niche and be fully exploited.

## Background & Description

*Procambarus clarkii* (Girard), the [[Louisiana Red Swamp Crayfish]], is described as the most economically important crayfish in North America. Most of the harvest in [[Louisiana]] is used for anatomical study or sold live for physiological research, while harvests in other US states reportedly do not exceed five percent of the Louisiana total.

The species is native to the south-central United States and northern Mexico. It is adapted to temporary, lentic habitats and can produce two generations per year in warmer parts of its range. These traits help explain its success in non-native regions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat tolerance:** Huner describes *P. clarkii* as a "very plastic species" able to survive in tropical, subtropical, and temperate climates.
- **Reproduction:** Life history notes from [[Costa Rica]], [[Hawaii]], [[Kenya]], and [[Uganda]] indicate year-round breeding with at least two generations per year in tropical climates. Huner's earlier work in subtropical-temperate Louisiana supports this.
- **Predation and competition:** Population explosions have occurred partly because of limited natural competition and predation.
- **Native diet acceptance:** Introductions have been mostly damaging because native human populations generally did not accept the species as food, so control by harvest was absent.
- **Lake Naivasha conditions:** Lake Naivasha lies in the [[Rift Valley]] just south of the equator. It is a shallow, slightly alkaline lake of approximately 100 km² whose water level fluctuates considerably and erratically. It supports a gill-net and sport fishery.

## Key Findings

### Previously Reported Introductions (Huner 1977)

- **[[Japan]]:** Introduced in 1930 as food for the bullfrog (*Rana catesbeiana*). By 1954 it was established in the southern half of the country, mainly on the Pacific side. Populations in rice fields reached 2000 kg per ha, damaging young rice shoots and destroying levees. By Takeda's 1975 report it was present in all prefectures except [[Hokkaido]]. Despite its abundance, it has little commercial value, which the authors attribute to social prejudice, since Japanese consumers already eat many decapod crustaceans.
- **[[California]]:** Well established in southern and central California by 1954 and still present. It is considered a pest because of crop damage and levee destruction, especially in the [[Sacramento River Delta]] rice fields. It is also harvested and cultured for food and fish bait.
- **[[Florida]]:** A private "crayfish farm" near Hudson, Pasco County, received 700–900 individuals in 1951. Penn doubted success because two endemic species, *Procambarus alleni* and *Procambarus fallax*, occupy similar habitats. The 1951 introduction does not appear to have succeeded.
- **[[Nevada]]:** Well established in southern Nevada by 1954 and still thriving. State law prohibits its sale and transport, possibly from concern about damage to irrigation systems in this arid region.
- **[[Hawaii]]:** Introduced on Oahu in 1934, where it became a significant pest in [[Taro Fields]], damaging irrigation levees and taro roots and corms. By 1954 it was established on Kauai and Maui, and it is now also on Molokai and Hawaii. It has no commercial value but is used locally as food and bait.

### New Introductions Reported (Huner 1977)

- **[[Kenya]]:** The date of the original successful introduction is uncertain. In [[Lake Naivasha]] densities are so high that one person can "catch 100 kilos" in 1–2 hours by scooping blindly with a wire mesh scoop. The animal is not utilized commercially, and export attempts to Europe failed for logistical reasons. It seriously damages gill-netted fish.
- **[[Sudan]]:** Several hundred *P. clarkii* were shipped from Louisiana to the Ministry of Agriculture in Khartoum in late September 1975 to test culture suitability. Stocking in some 10 ha of ponds near Khartoum was reported, but no results were available at writing.
- **[[Uganda]]:** Introduced in 1963–64 using Louisiana stock. The species has spread through major river systems, is well received as food, and commands a high price. No commercial cultivation existed at the time, though it could readily be cultured.
- **[[Costa Rica]]:** Introduced around 1966 into a small reservoir on the Central Plateau near Alajuela, and later reported near San Carlos on the Atlantic Slope. It is not a pest at present, apparently because it has not reached rice-growing areas, and it is not used.
- **[[Nicaragua]]:** A suspected but unconfirmed introduction, based on reports from a local informant.
- **[[Spain]]:** Several shipments from Louisiana were made to southern Spain in 1973 to test culture suitability, with Ministry of Agriculture approval. Stocked in isolated ponds near Seville and Madrid, the species reproduced successfully and supports a limited fishery.

### Discussion and Recommendations (Huner 1977)

- Population explosions have occurred from very small founder numbers, probably no more than 1000 individuals.
- Rapid spread occurred in temperate Japan and central California despite only one generation per year. According to Penn (1954), all Japanese populations descend from twenty animals.
- Huner recommends that introductions be made only after an impartial panel (ecologists, government officials, and interested citizens) with the United Nations concludes that the species will fill an unfilled niche and be fully exploited by local residents.
- The author stresses that a few individuals carried in a container can seriously damage native flora and fauna.
- Despite these risks, the species is now a valued natural resource beyond its native range, especially in food-poor developing nations. Professional astacologists should advise local governments on its control and use.

### Lake Naivasha Biology (Lowery and Mendes 1977)

- *P. clarkii* was introduced into Lake Naivasha in 1971 and is now well established.
- Reproduction occurs year-round, both in burrows and in open water. The breeding pattern is affected by erratic fluctuations in lake level.
- Many crayfish from the shallows and burrows show severely wasted tail muscles of unknown cause.
- The species harms the established gill-net fishery by consuming netted fish and becoming entangled in nets.
