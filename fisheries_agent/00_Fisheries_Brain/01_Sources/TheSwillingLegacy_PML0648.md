# The Swilling Legacy: Irrigation and Early Settlement of the Salt River Valley

#ichthyology #marine-biology #history-of-irrigation

## Executive Summary

This document is a historical account, adapted from a Salt River Project publication, of how irrigated agriculture began in the Salt River Valley of Arizona. It centers on [[Jack Swilling]], a frontiersman, miner and farmer who organized the Swilling Irrigating and Canal Company in November 1867 and dug the first successful modern irrigation ditch in the Valley in 1868. The text also traces his violent, troubled life, his 1878 death in a Yuma jail while accused of a stagecoach robbery, and the debates over who named [[Phoenix, Arizona]] and [[Tempe, Arizona]]. It is a historical narrative rather than research on fish or aquatic ecosystems; the water-related content concerns canals, river flow and irrigation potential, not aquatic species.

## Background & Description

The publication opens by noting that thousands of people move to the Salt River Valley each year without knowing its origins, and argues that understanding how water was developed there can inform future water decisions. Its author, [[Earl Zarbin]], joined The Arizona Republic in 1958 and researched the Valley's water history, producing a series of articles printed in August 1978.

Swilling was born April 1, 1830, in South Carolina, served in the Mexican-American War as a young man, and later worked as a teamster, prospector, mill manager, Confederate soldier (who deserted to the Union side), guide, farmer, mail rider and contractor. He married Trinidad Escalante, reportedly aged 17, in Tucson on April 13, 1864. He was justice of the peace and postmaster of the Phoenix Settlement. Accounts describe him as generous and hospitable when sober but insulting and violent when drunk, often under the influence of morphine taken for old wounds.

The document also covers the surveys of the Valley by William H. Pierce (January 1867) and Wilfred F. Ingalls (March to July 1868), who described rich, level, first-rate soil and river fall of 12 to 15 feet per mile suited to irrigation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Valley's water and land conditions shaped settlement. The Salt River was described as a large stream that never fully dried, with a steep fall making it valuable for irrigation. Vegetation included mesquite, greasewood, cottonwood and willow, with grasses along the river. The summer temperatures sometimes reached 115 degrees or higher, and rattlesnakes, Gila monsters and scorpions were common.

Ancient canals and ruins of former inhabitants were visible when settlers arrived; some mounds rose 20 to 40 feet above ground. Apache raids were the main constraint, delaying serious settlement until about 1866 to 1867. Pre-existing use was limited: the Pima and Maricopa along the Gila River apparently made no serious permanent settlements in the Valley.

The Valley's agriculture was tied to the economy of mining camps. Flour in Prescott cost at least $20 per 100 pounds, and the Army paid for corn and barley, creating demand for local crops.

## Key Findings

- **Origins of the first ditch:** The Swilling Irrigating and Canal Company was organized at Wickenburg on November 16, 1867, with capital stock of $10,000 in 50 shares of $200 each. The first work on the Swilling Ditch was completed March 12, 1868, and the canal reportedly irrigated at least 4,000 acres.
- **Traditional story challenged:** The popular claim that Swilling conceived the canal after visiting John Smith's hay camp in 1867 traces to 1883 Phoenix Daily Herald articles. Historian [[Geoffrey Mawn]] found no contemporary evidence for a Swilling–Smith work relationship.
- **Earlier water claims:** The first water right on the Salt River was claimed June 7, 1867, by four miners, and a second on June 8 by Army officers, both on the north bank near McDowell Crossing, predating the Swilling company.
- **Early crops:** In 1868 Swilling cultivated 100 acres of wheat, barley and corn, and other settlers reported crops from 25 to 100 acres each.
- **Naming of Phoenix:** Competing claims credit either [[Jack Swilling]] or [[Darrell Duppa]]. Early sources, including the Salt River Herald of January 1879 and settler J.E. Wharton's 1884 letter, credit Swilling, who reportedly used an old Webster's dictionary. Other pioneers credited Duppa, and the question was left unresolved by A.F. Banta in 1906.
- **Death and exoneration:** Swilling died August 12, 1878, in a Yuma jail, accused of the April 19, 1878, stagecoach robbery near Wickenburg. Evidence against him was weak, and Maj. J.W. Evans later gave a statement implicating other men. Swilling's last letter, written in jail, says he was persecuted for the reward money.
- **Historical verdict:** The document concludes that Swilling was an estimable pioneer whose agricultural development of the Salt River Valley is a lasting legacy, despite his troubled personal life.
