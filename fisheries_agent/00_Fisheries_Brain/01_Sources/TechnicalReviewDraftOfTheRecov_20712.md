# Chihuahua Chub Draft Recovery Plan (Technical Review)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1985 technical review draft of a [[Recovery Plan]] for the [[Chihuahua Chub]] (*Gila nigrescens*). It was prepared by the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] in cooperation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], based on a management plan by Michael D. Hatch and John P. Hubbard. The species is listed as threatened federally and as endangered by New Mexico. Its only natural U.S. occurrence is a small relict population in the [[Mimbres River]] near [[Bear Canyon Reservoir]]. The plan's prime objective is to secure viable wild populations in New Mexico. The main drivers of decline are habitat loss from flooding, channelization and repeated levee work, with exotic fish and bait harvest as secondary threats. The draft is not an approved government document; comments were requested by May 27, 1985.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species was first described from the Mimbres River as *Gila pulchella* by Baird and Girard (1854). Later names included *Tigoma nigrescens* and *Tigoma pulchra* (Girard 1856) and *Leuciscus nigrescens* (Jordan and Evermann 1896). The present name, *Gila nigrescens*, was adopted after taxonomic revision, with the Janos River, Chihuahua, as type locality. The chub known from the Rio Grande and Pecos drainages was previously assigned the same name but is the distinct [[Rio Grande Chub]] (*Gila pandora*).

**Morphology.** Adults average 5-6 inches at maturity and may reach 12 inches. The dorsal fin origin lies behind the pelvic fin, the dorsal fin ray count is usually 9, and the lateral line has 67-78 scales. Coloration is dusky brown above and whitish below. During breeding, orange-red coloration develops around the mouth and lower fins. Post-larval fish have a spot above the lateral line just before the caudal fin.

**Conservation status.** The species is listed as threatened at the federal level and treated as endangered by New Mexico (N.M.D.G.&F. Reg. No. 599). Deacon et al. (1979) reported that 18 *Gila* species or subspecies were endangered, threatened or of special concern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The species is endemic to the [[Guzman Basin]], ranging from the Mimbres River in New Mexico south to northwestern Chihuahua, Mexico. It was first collected in 1851 from the Rio Mimbres. It was not seen again there until June 5, 1975, when Bill Rogers found about 20 chubs. In Chihuahua it was formerly known from at least 20 streams. A 1979 survey of 18 of these streams found it in only 7, with 3 supporting "healthy" populations. Its status in the Boca Grande and Janos rivers is unknown.

**Habitat and biology.** The chub depends on deep pools with undercut banks, which provide escape cover and foraging sites. It feeds partly like a trout, taking surface insects as well as aquatic invertebrates, and possibly small fish and vegetation. Spawning occurs in late April and May in quiet pools 1-2.5 m deep over matted aquatic vegetation.

**Associated fauna.** Native species in the Guzman Basin include the [[Rio Grande Mountain-Sucker]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], [[Mexican Stoneroller]] and an undescribed pupfish (*Cyprinodon* sp.). The Mexican stoneroller has never been collected in the Mimbres drainage, and the beautiful shiner is extirpated there. Exotic species include [[Common Carp]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Gila Trout]]. Historical accounts (Antisell 1857; Miller 1961) describe a Mimbres River with pools fringed by willows, which is far more habitat than remains today.

**Cultural and historical notes.** Locals once called the chub "Gila trout." Pottery of the ancient Mimbres people depicts fish resembling the species, and suggests that catfish and gar once lived in the river. The record of trout by Emory (1848) is uncertain, and no native salmonid is known from the Guzman Basin.

## Key Findings

- **Primary cause of decline:** loss of habitat. Flooding, then channelization and levee projects that must be repeated each spring near spawning time, were the main causes. Diversions and uneven flows added to the loss. Exotic rainbow trout and longfin dace are considered a minor factor.
- **U.S. population:** remained fixed near Bear Canyon Reservoir and was no more than 30-40 adults until late 1978. Flooding in late 1978 and early 1979 displaced the larger fish downstream and damaged the remaining habitat.
- **Captive insurance:** 10 chubs from the Mimbres River were placed at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] on March 27, 1979. Chubs from the Rio Piedras Verdes, Chihuahua, were also placed there in 1979. Both groups reproduced successfully. The stocks are kept separate because morphological and genetic differences are unknown.
- **Priority restoration sites:** two areas are currently suitable: (1) the Mimbres River in the box canyon near the Cooney Place, and (2) the spring-fed lateral opposite Bear Canyon Reservoir. A third reach needs considerable habitat renovation. Gallina and McKinght creeks are possible future sites.
- **Recovery steps (outline of 4 main objectives):**
  1. *Maintain and enhance* the Mimbres population and habitat. This covers monitoring, reclaiming habitat, protecting the spring-fed lateral through a conservation easement (possibly with The Nature Conservancy), and creating 1-2.5 m pools. Levees must be set back from the channel and built with material trucked in from off-stream.
  2. *Hold and propagate* chubs at Dexter National Fish Hatchery as a reserve gene pool.
  3. *Reestablish* the chub in former range, and monitor reproduction, young-of-year survival and growth of transplants.
  4. *Disseminate information* to local, state, national and professional audiences.
- **Regulatory measures:** ban the introduction of non-native fish, prohibit bait-minnow use across the Mimbres drainage (current state rules allow it in Bear Canyon Reservoir), prohibit taking any fish for bait indefinitely, and replace push-up irrigation diversions with permanent diversions that allow fish passage and do not dewater downstream reaches.
- **Information gaps:** the plan notes uncertainty about the status of the Boca Grande and Janos river populations, and about morphological and genetic differences between the Mimbres and Rio Piedras Verdes stocks.
