# The Radioactivity and Ecology of Aquatic Organisms of the Columbia River

#aquatic-ecology #radioecology #environmental-monitoring #Columbia-River #radioisotopes

## Executive Summary

This document (HW-25021) reports on a limnological study of radiobiological and ecological nature conducted to determine effects of [[Hanford]] pile effluents on organisms in the [[Columbia River]]. The study, initiated in fall 1948 with completion planned for April 1, 1952, examined radioactivity densities in fish, bottom-dwelling plants and animals, and plankton. The research investigated biological relationships to diffusion and transport of radioactive materials, evaluated potential hazards, and determined dispersion patterns of radioactivity in river biota. Organisms concentrated certain radioisotopes (particularly P³²) to levels 100,000-200,000 times water concentrations, though no adverse ecological effects on community composition or population densities were detected.

## Background & Description

The study encompassed multiple aquatic organism groups including fish, benthonic plants and animals (excluding bacteria and protozoa), and plankton. Precursory fish studies began in 1946, with preliminary surveys of other aquatic forms conducted November 1947-April 1948. The major study phase ran from fall 1948 through April 1952, coordinated with companion studies by the [[U.S. Public Health Service]] initiated July 1951.

Sampling focused on littoral zones supporting richest benthonic populations, with sites selected to provide similar hydrographic conditions. Fast water areas with cobblestone bottoms were preferred as they favored large populations of maximal benthonic forms. Collecting sites included:

- Control area at [[Priest Rapids]] (upstream from pile areas)
- Multiple locations within [[Hanford Works Area]] (highest effluent concentrations)
- [[Richland]], [[Pasco]], below [[Snake River]] mouth near Finley
- Seven miles above and below [[McNary Dam]]
- Exploratory surveys to [[Bonneville Dam]]

Collection methods included gill nets and set lines for large fish, hand seines for small fish, scraping for algae, nets and brushing for invertebrates, and a square-foot bottom sampler for quantitative invertebrate samples. Plankton was collected with standard nets, pumps, and flow meters for quantitative sampling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Columbia River]] is characterized as a montane-type stream with variable hydrographic conditions including water velocities, rapids, lagoons, various substrata types, and depth variations. Phosphorus is an essential element in short supply in the river (phosphate concentrations below 0.43 ppm), while sodium is readily available.

Aquatic organisms depend on water to provide essential elements. Some radioactive isotopes discharged from [[Hanford]] piles are of critical importance to aquatic organisms and are concentrated many times above water concentrations. While the greater part of effluent radioactivity comes from short-lived isotopes, organisms concentrate those with longer half-lives.

Radiochemical analyses revealed:

**Fish** (near pile areas): Predominantly P³² with small amounts of Na²⁴ and traces of longer-lived elements

**Aquatic insect larvae**: 15-30% Na²⁴, 70-85% P³², ≤1% long-lived isotopes

**Algae** (28 hours post-collection): 25% Na²⁴, 25% P³², 8% rare earths, 7% Mn⁵⁶, 6% Zn⁶⁵, 4% Cr⁵¹, remainder from arsenic, iron, and zinc-cobalt groups

**Plankton**: Long-lived elements including isotopes of iron, aluminum, chromium, titanium, and rare earths; naturally occurring K⁴⁰ also present

Phosphorus concentration by organisms reaches 100,000-200,000 times water levels due to its essential nature and scarcity. Sodium, though not essential for plants, is accumulated in large quantities, possibly related to absorption and ability to partially replace potassium when adequate supply unavailable.

The aquatic fauna includes herbivores, omnivores, and carnivores. Average activity densities increase progressively through the food chain, with carnivores showing highest values.

## Key Findings

**Radioisotope Accumulation Patterns:**
- Organisms concentrate radioisotopes with longer half-lives despite short-lived isotopes dominating effluent water
- P³² concentrations in organisms reach 100,000-200,000 times water concentrations
- Activity densities increase progressively through food chain: water < plants < herbivores < omnivores < carnivores

**Seasonal Variations:**
- Water activity density inversely related to river flow: maximum in fall/winter (low flow), minimum during spring freshet
- Organism activity patterns more closely related to metabolic rates and life cycles than water activity
- Example: [[Shiners]] show seasonal activity coinciding with water temperature (metabolic rate control)
- Interspecific variations in seasonal patterns attributed to life cycle differences

**Geographical Distribution:**
- Background levels <10⁻² pc/g above [[Hanford Works]]
- Activity increases downstream as additional effluent discharged from each pile area
- Maximum near-shore values occur near [[Hanford]]
- Highest game fish activities: 2.7 × 10⁻⁴ pc/g or less in flesh (safe for human consumption)
- Rapid decrease downstream from [[Hanford]] attributed to radioactivity decay, dispersion, sedimentation removal, assimilation by organisms, and tributary dilution
- September 1951 snail data illustrate downstream activity gradient

**Temporal Trends:**
- Considerable year-to-year increase in radioactivity of all aquatic life forms
- Increases attributed to expanded operations, not accumulation of long-lived isotopes

**Ecological Impact Assessment:**
- Quantitative benthonic samples show extremely variable population densities throughout studied river section
- Variations below piles also occur in control areas
- Population variations attributed to hydrographic conditions: water velocities, rapids, lagoons, substratum types, depth, other montane-stream variables
- Plankton studies show no significant abundance decrease above vs. below pile areas
- **No changes in population densities or community composition attributable to [[Hanford]] pile effluents detected**
- **No abnormalities in aquatic communities attributable to effluent effects observed**

**Human Safety:**
- Maximum game fish flesh activities (2.7 × 10⁻⁴ pc/g) would not make them unsafe for human consumption

The study successfully characterized radioactivity distribution in [[Columbia River]] aquatic ecosystems while finding no detectable adverse ecological effects from [[Hanford]] operations during the study period.
