# The Optomotor Reaction of Schooling Carangid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Carangidae #fish-behavior #schooling-behavior

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by Shaw & Tucker investigates the [[Optomotor Reflex]] in two schooling [[Carangidae|carangid]] species — the [[Bar Jack]] (*Caranx ruber*) and [[Bigeye Scad]] (*Selar crumenophthalmus*) — as a candidate sensory-motor mechanism underlying [[Fish Schooling]] behavior. Using a rotating striped drum apparatus, the researchers tested how individual fish oriented and moved in response to different visual stimuli (fully striped fields, partial striped panels, and plain white controls). The results demonstrate that fish exposed to a fully striped rotating field followed the direction of rotation and increased swimming speed proportionally with drum speed, but did **not** fixate on any single point in the pattern. In contrast, fish exposed to a partial striped panel closely tracked and remained near the panel across the full range of rotational speeds. The authors argue that this optomotor behavior recapitulates three key features of natural schooling — unidirectional movement, coordinated speed changes, and relative repositioning while maintaining parallel orientation — supporting the hypothesis that the [[Optomotor Reflex]] contributes to the maintenance of [[Parallel Orientation]] in [[Fish Schools]].

## Background & Description

- **Species studied:**
  - *Caranx ruber* ([[Bar Jack]]) — 6 to 9 inches in length
  - *Selar crumenophthalmus* ([[Bigeye Scad]]) — 6 to 8 inches in length
- Both species are members of family [[Carangidae]], noted for pelagic, obligate schooling lifestyles.
- **Fragility noted:** Both species proved highly sensitive to handling and captivity; physical contact produced lesions on the body flank leading to death within days.
- **Ocular observation:** The normally transparent cornea occasionally became cloudy/opaque during storage or testing, likely a stress-related pathology — a confound that limited the pool of usable test subjects to only recently-caught, undamaged individuals.
- No detailed anatomical (fin, scale, coloration) descriptions were provided; the paper's focus is behavioral/sensory rather than morphological.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Both species are pelagic marine [[Schooling Fish|schooling fishes]] native to Western Atlantic waters, studied at the [[Lerner Marine Laboratory]] in Bimini, Bahamas.
- **Natural swimming behavior:** Field observations noted that [[Bar Jack]] are markedly faster swimmers than [[Bigeye Scad]] under natural conditions, a distinction that persisted in experimental optomotor swimming speeds (bar jacks completed more revolutions per minute at any given drum speed).
- The study situates its findings within the broader framework of [[Fish Schooling]] theory, building on foundational work by Parr (1927) on parallel orientation and interfish spacing, and connecting to prior schooling behavior reviews (Morrow 1948; Atz 1953; Breder 1959).
- Vision is established in prior literature as the dominant sensory modality mediating schooling coordination, though the precise neuro-behavioral mechanism was previously unknown — this study proposes the [[Optomotor Reflex]] as a mechanistic candidate.
- Comparative note: similar optomotor responses to moving striped fields have been documented in other taxa, including *Carassius* ([[Goldfish]]; Trinckner 1952), *Mugil* ([[Mullet]]; Hortsmann 1959), and *Menidia* ([[Silversides]], observed by the present authors), suggesting the optomotor reflex may be a broadly conserved teleost visual-motor mechanism.

## Key Findings

1. **Optomotor apparatus design:** A cylindrical lucite aquarium was placed inside a concentric motor-driven rotating steel drum (both submerged in a seawater-filled plastic pool). The drum interior was lined with one of three stimuli: (1) fully striped black/white vertical pattern, (2) a partial 60° striped panel with the remainder white, or (3) a plain white control field. Drum rotation speed ranged from 3.5 to 38.5 RPM.

2. **Baseline (stationary drum) behavior:** In the absence of drum movement, fish were either quiescent (long axis radial to the aquarium wall) or circled the tank; they tended to stay near the bottom/center rather than the surface.

3. **Fully striped field response:** Fish exhibited a classic [[Optomotor Reflex|optomotor response]] — continuous, regular movement in the same rotational direction as the drum, oriented normal to the aquarium radius. This matches responses previously documented in goldfish, mullet, and other species (Harden Jones, 1963).

4. **Atypical behaviors observed:** At low speeds, some fish oriented perpendicular to the aquarium wall while pivoting with the drum, exhibiting bilateral head/anterior body movements. This was explicitly distinguished from head nystagmus (as described by Trinckner 1952 in goldfish) since no fast/slow asymmetry in head movement direction was detected.

5. **Partial panel stimulus response:** Fish oriented toward and tracked the striped panel specifically. Synchrony with the panel was closest at intermediate drum speeds — fish swam slightly ahead of the panel at low speeds and slightly behind at high speeds. When the drum stopped, fish immediately ceased circling and remained near the striped panel region, confirming panel-directed orientation rather than incidental circling.

6. **White (control) field response:** With no striping, fish showed no consistent optomotor following; behavior included stillness, erratic vertical movement along the sidewall, or alternating short bursts of clockwise/counter-clockwise swimming — confirming the following response is stripe-dependent, not an artifact of mechanical drum movement (e.g., overhead struts).

7. **Quantitative RPM relationships (Graphs 1–4):**
   - Fully striped field: fish RPM increased regularly with drum RPM in both species; [[Bar Jack]] consistently circled faster than [[Bigeye Scad]] at equivalent drum speeds, mirroring natural swimming speed differences.
   - Partial panel field: fish RPM in both species closely matched drum RPM, reflecting tight panel-tracking behavior.
   - White field: no meaningful following response was observed in either species.

8. **Interpretation of uniform striped field behavior:** Since no single stripe/fixation point is visually distinguishable from any other in a uniformly striped field, fish were proposed to sequentially adopt, lose, and re-adopt different fixation points ("perceptual stopping" of the moving field), rather than tracking one continuous point. This produces an average velocity proportional to drum speed without requiring precise synchrony.

9. **Species-specific swimming velocity:** Natural swimming speed differences (bar jacks faster than bigeye scads) were preserved under experimental conditions, suggesting average optomotor velocity is jointly determined by both stimulus speed and species-typical locomotor tendencies — consistent with species differences reported by Harden Jones (1963).

10. **Schooling parallels:** Three optomotor behaviors were proposed as direct analogs to natural [[Fish Schooling]] dynamics:
    - Fish move in the same direction as the moving visual field (~ schooling fish move in the same direction as neighbors).
    - Fish adjust speed in response to changes in field/stripe speed (~ schooling fish adjust speed to neighbors).
    - Fish shift position relative to the field without losing overall coordination (~ schooling fish reposition while maintaining parallel orientation).

11. **Proposed mechanism:** The authors, building on Baylor & Shaw (1962), suggest the teleost eye's sensitivity to small movements and contrast enhancement may allow individual fish within a school to use neighboring fish as moving visual stimuli analogous to the striped drum, making the [[Optomotor Reflex]] a plausible proximate mechanism for maintaining cohesive, parallel school structure.

12. **Related literature convergence:** Protasov & Altukov (1961) similarly implicated optomotor reactions in schooling fish, juvenile fish, and rheophilic (current-dwelling) river fish, lending cross-study support to the general hypothesis.
