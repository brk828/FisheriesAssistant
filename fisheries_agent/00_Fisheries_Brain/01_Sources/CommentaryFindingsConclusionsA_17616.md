# Review Letter on the ANS Task Force Report on Intentional Introductions of Nonindigenous Aquatic Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-policy

## Executive Summary

This is a 1993 review letter from Walter R. Courtenay, Jr., Chairman of the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] (ASIH) Environmental Quality Committee, to Ms. Sharon Gross, Coordinator of the [[Aquatic Nuisance Species Task Force]] (ANS Task Force) at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. It comments on the proposed report to Congress, *Findings, Conclusions, and Recommendations of the Intentional Introductions Policy Review*. The letter also speaks for the [[Desert Fishes Council]]. The committee commends the report as comprehensive, but argues that it needs stronger wording on Hawaii, a clearer definition of "species," a firmer stance against a "clean list" approach, a more binding federal protocol, and binding interjurisdictional panels. It also reproduces critical comments from Dr. W.L. Minckley on the handling of existing introductions and on the undefined term "ecosystem."

## Background & Description

The ASIH is an international professional society promoting research on fishes, amphibians and reptiles, and their protection. The letter responds to the ANS Task Force's proposed report, which addresses intentional introductions of nonindigenous aquatic species. The committee's ichthyologists agree that the Task Force set its own limits on scope. The letter is signed by Courtenay and copied to Dr. Brooks M. Burr, Dr. Carter R. Gilbert, Dr. David Greenfield, Dr. Roy McDiarmid and Mr. E.P. Pister.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scale of fish diversity.** The letter cites estimates of 22,000 to more than 28,000 living fish species. It states that fish species outnumber all other vertebrates combined. It also notes that in some freshwater areas perhaps more than 25% of fishes remain undescribed.

**Hawaii.** The committee states that Hawaii holds more introduced, established, nonindigenous fishes than any other state, with 34 species in Hawaiian waters that are nonindigenous to any part of the United States.

**Dispersal and watersheds.** Minckley argues that organisms disperse readily, that people (especially sport fishing interests) often aid that dispersal, and that water flows downhill between jurisdictions. As a result, an introduction within one watershed can appear elsewhere, so interjurisdictional problems are predictable.

**Native fish and the Endangered Species Act.** Minckley notes that many introduction and stocking activities could be seen as violating the [[Endangered Species Act]], and that [[Section 7 Consultation]] may often be required. He identifies the problem as partly internal to the agencies involved.

**Madison River example.** The report's treatment of established brown trout in the Madison River illustrates how the word "ecosystem" can be read to exempt ongoing introductions from regulation.

## Key Findings

- **Hawaii should be named** in the Executive Summary alongside Florida and California because of its high number of nonindigenous fishes.
- **"Species" needs clarification.** The committee notes that zoology recognizes subspecies, not populations or stocks. It suggests removing the word "taxonomic" from the definition so that genetically distinct populations (demes) are still covered.
- **Agricultural species are different.** The committee objects that the claim that nonindigenous species provide major benefits applies mainly to domesticated agricultural species. These species have been so altered by selective hybridization that many cannot survive without human care. Feral introductions that need no such care are a separate matter.
- **Federal jurisdiction and transgenics.** The committee supports federal authority over imported species, rejects the view that within-state introductions that spread across borders fall under "states' rights," and welcomes the recognition of transgenic organisms as nonindigenous species.
- **Support for key recommendations.** The committee supports all recommendations overall. It especially supports Recommendation 3A, that interstate introductions (including some in-state "isolated" waters) go through the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) process. Most members want no federal funds used for introductions, under Recommendation 3C.
- **Against a "clean list."** The committee supports the Task Force's rejection of a uniform national approved list. It argues the reason is stronger than the Task Force gives: with so many undescribed fishes, a clean list would be unenforceable. It asks that this be added to Recommendation 3E.
- **Permitting concerns.** The committee supports a federal import permitting system (Recommendation 4A). It warns that the system could hinder scientists importing live fish for research rather than introduction.
- **Weak existing protocol.** The committee says the 1986 [[American Fisheries Society]] protocol (Kohler and Courtenay 1986) is weaker than the AFS 1973 policy. The 1973 policy required staged reviews and a final veto, which were dropped to get the 1986 protocol approved. The committee supports a federal protocol under Recommendation 5 and asks that federal agencies set an example.
- **Interjurisdictional panels.** The committee agrees with Recommendations 6A–6C that interjurisdictional panels should be part of the process. It suggests that federal funds be withheld from states that do not cooperate. It also asks that non-agency biologists be explicitly included on these panels, as stated in Recommendation 6A.
- **Gaps in the report (Minckley).** The report applies only to new introductions and leaves out existing nonindigenous species, sportfish-versus-native-fish management conflicts, and how to balance nonnative and biodiversity values. Minckley asks for a follow-up document on these issues.
- **Undefined "ecosystem."** Minckley argues that the report uses "ecosystem" without defining it. This could let states set their own boundaries and avoid regulation. He proposes that cold tributaries of a warm river could count as separate ecosystems.
