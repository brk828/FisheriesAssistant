# Tagging Experiment in Third Sister Lake, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report, by Robert C. Baur and W. L. Minckley of the [[Michigan Institute for Fisheries Research]], describes a mark-recapture study in [[Third Sister Lake]], a 10-acre lake in Michigan. The original goal was to estimate the fish population from tag returns. That estimate could not be completed, so the work shifted to documenting survival of marked fish, fish movement within a confined habitat, growth of tagged fish, and the effects of tags. Marking ran from May 1939 until the lake was [[Rotenone]]-poisoned on May 6, 1941. Recovery at poisoning, return rates, and movement data are reported for [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Yellow Bullhead]] and [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]]. The authors describe tag returns as low and the movement evidence as limited, and they note that tags caused lasting jaw damage.

## Background & Description

- **Study lake:** Third Sister Lake is oval, has a surface area of 10 acres and a maximum depth of 55 feet, and stratifies thermally and chemically in summer. Its steep bottom limits the [[Littoral Zone]] to a narrow band (about 9 feet deep) that supports dense submerged vegetation. Yellow water lilies border about two-thirds of the shoreline. The lake has no permanent inlet or outlet.
- **Protection:** The lake was closed to fishing and sits inside a [[University of Michigan Forestry Tract]] near a caretaker's lodge, so poaching losses were considered unlikely.
- **Species:** Fifteen species were recovered at poisoning: 4 game, 5 coarse and 6 forage fish. The four study species were the focus.
- **Context:** The work was part of a broader bionomics study directed by [[Institute for Fisheries Research]] staff, including Dr. A. S. Hazzard and Dr. C. J. D. Brown. Earlier results were published in [[Brown and Ball 1943]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling gear:** Four [[Fyke Net]]s were used in 1939–1941, three kept in the lake and one out for repair. Nets were fished from ice-out to ice-in and lifted twice daily. They were moved weekly, mostly to the more favourable habitats. Hook and line supplemented the catch because nets yielded too few usable fish. Seining was limited by soft bottom, dense marginal vegetation and single-person work.
- **Depth limit:** Nets were set no deeper than about 9 feet. A [[Thermocline]] with reduced oxygen lay at that depth. Nets were visible in the clear water, which may have contributed to fish becoming "net shy".
- **Mesh selection:** Fyke net mesh let young-of-the-year bluegills and similar-sized fish escape.
- **Feeding behaviour:** Nearly every bullhead and bass taken in nets was engorged, often with a bluegill nearly as large as the predator could swallow. The authors suggest both species entered nets while searching for food.

## Key Findings

**Tagging and marking methods**
- Most fish received [[Monel Metal Tags]] on the right lower jaw, applied by encircling the mandible and rounding the tag with pliers. A few largemouth bass and large bluegills had tags on the maxillary bone, which caused less irritation in the few recovered fish.
- A total of 96 fingerling tags was used on fish under 110 mm total length. These tags were faulty, often cracked on placement and corroded rapidly, so few probably stayed on for long.
- Thirty-three circular tags of special design were also used.
- From 1940 onward, [[Fin Clipping]] with kitchen shears replaced most tagging, except for largemouth bass, which continued to be tagged. Combinations of clipped fins identified release areas. Bullhead fins regenerated more readily than centrarchid fins, though regenerated fins were recognizable by malformation.
- Handling mortality was judged very low. Boat surveys of the shoreline let the authors check dead fish closely; the few handling deaths floated up within a day or two.

**Numbers tagged and returned (as stated in text)**
- Bluegills: 486 tagged in 1939 (96 with defective fingerling tags, 390 with adult tags); 43 tagged in 1940 (Table 1 gives 45, which conflicts with the text).
- In 1939, 17 of 390 adult-tagged bluegills (4.3%) were returned alive and 4 (1%) were found dead. All live returns came within 50 days. Of the 96 fingerling-tagged fish, 5 were recaptured alive and 3 were found dead; three of the live fish had cracked tags.
- Of 20 bluegills tagged in 1939 and recovered in 1940, 17 were alive and 3 were dead.
- Of the 35 recaptured bluegills released again, only 7 were recovered at poisoning.
- Largemouth bass: 52 tagged in 1939–1940; 7 returned by hook and line or net. Of the 7 returned bass released again, 5 were recovered at poisoning.
- Bullheads: of 50 recaptured and released alive, 24 were recovered at poisoning.
- Pumpkinseed sunfish: none of the recaptured-and-released fish were recovered at poisoning.

**Survival of earlier-tagged fish**
- Of 206 bluegills fin-clipped in 1937, 15 (7.3%) were recovered at poisoning, after about 4 years at liberty.
- Of 162 bluegills tagged in 1933 and 1935 by Dr. D. S. Shetter, 3 (1.8%) were recovered. A further 67 largemouth bass from the same series were tagged; their recovery is not reported here.

**Movement**
- Only fish free in the lake more than 15 days after tagging were included. Release and recapture points were located within 10 yards using 100-foot shoreline sections.
- Bluegills: about 60% of returns were within 30 yards of the capture point. The movement table lists 12 fish recaptured at 0 yards, 4 at 30 yards, 2 at 65 yards, 4 at 65–150 yards and 5 at more than 150 yards.
- Bullheads: more than 50% of recaptures were within 30 yards. Specifically, 39% were within 15 yards and 81% within 100 yards. Every bullhead recaptured more than once was taken at the same spot, even weeks or months apart. Some were recaptured up to six times.
- Largemouth bass: returns were spread across the whole lake, with no tendency to stay in one area. Four of seven were retaken, one four times.
- The authors caution that the sample is small and interpretation is uncertain.

**Growth**
- Length and weight recording was dropped after extra handling raised mid-summer mortality.
- Of the measured fish, 8 bluegills, 17 bullheads and 2 bass were recovered. Bluegill growth ranged from 2 to 20 mm in about 22 months, averaging 9 mm. Bullhead growth ranged from 2 to 67 mm, averaging 22 mm. The two bass grew 5 mm and 11 mm.
- Three pre-study fish showed very slow growth: a bluegill grew 30 mm in 49 months, a bass 27 mm in 68 months, and another bass 46 mm in 91 months.
- The authors reject overcrowding as the cause, because the lake's fish grew at normal rates for southern Michigan lakes. Bass reached the 10-inch legal length in their third or early fourth year, and bluegills reached the 6-inch legal length in their third growing season.

**Tag effects**
- The tags on the three long-term pre-study fish were in good condition and were embedded in a gelatinous secretion from the lower jaw.
- In a bullhead held in captivity from 1941 until its death on March 15, 1943, the jaw tag had nearly eroded through the bone and the sore never healed. The authors note similar, though lesser, sores in nearly all bullheads and some bluegills and bass.

**Overall conclusions**
- The population estimate by tagging failed, so the study's value lies in survival, movement, growth and tag-effect data.
- Low tag return rates were probably partly due to net shyness.
- Bullheads, and to a lesser extent bluegills, showed site fidelity. Bass ranged widely.
- Jaw-tag damage limits the reliability of long-term tagging.
