# Listing of the Yaqui Chub as Endangered and the Beautiful Shiner and Yaqui Catfish as Threatened, with Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a [[Federal Register]] final rule issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on August 31, 1984 (Vol. 49, No. 171), together with a cover letter to the [[Arizona State Clearinghouse]] and a Standard Form 424 (federal assistance application cover sheet). The rule lists three native [[Rio Yaqui Fishes]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]]: the [[Yaqui Chub]] is classified as endangered, and the [[Beautiful Shiner]] and [[Yaqui Catfish]] as threatened. It designates [[Critical Habitat]] on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] in Cochise County, Arizona, and includes a [[Special Rule]] allowing take of the threatened species for educational, scientific, and conservation purposes under Arizona law. The listing is based on severe population reductions from habitat modification and the threat of introduced exotic fishes. The rule took effect October 1, 1984.

## Background & Description

The three species are part of the Rio Yaqui fish fauna. All were first collected and described from San Bernardino Creek, just south of the Arizona–Sonora border, in the latter half of the 19th century.

- **Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*):** Adults inhabit pools and undercut banks in permanent streams.
- **Beautiful shiner (*Notropis formosus*):** Found in a variety of habitats, but the largest populations occur in riffles of small streams. Its biology is thought to resemble that of the [[Red Shiner]].
- **Yaqui catfish (*Ictalurus pricei*):** Usually found in large streams with medium to slow current. Its biology is thought to resemble that of the [[Channel Catfish]].

Little is known about the biology of these fishes beyond basic habitat preferences. In the past they occurred throughout the Rio Yaqui basin and in a few smaller adjacent drainages. The beautiful shiner also formerly inhabited small drainages in the closed [[Guzman Basin]], including the Rio Mimbres in New Mexico and the Casas Grandes, Santa Maria, and Del Carmen rivers in Chihuahua.

**Regulatory history:**
- The Service considered the Yaqui chub for listing in 1960 and 1973, but took no action because its status in Mexico was undetermined.
- A March 1979 list by the [[American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee recommended special concern for the beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish and classed the Yaqui chub as endangered.
- In 1978 the Service contracted biologists from Arizona State University and the University of Michigan to survey fishes of the Rio Yaqui system in Mexico. They found **only one** Yaqui chub specimen after extensive collecting.
- The three species were listed in Category 1 of the December 30, 1982 Vertebrate Notice of Review.
- On April 14 (year partly garbled in scan), the [[Desert Fishes Council]] petitioned the Service to list the Yaqui chub. The Service found the petition warranted and published a proposed rule on July 15, 1983 (48 FR 32527).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss and modification (Factor A):** Habitat destruction is the main reason for the species' decline. The Yaqui chub's remaining U.S. populations are restricted to a few springs on the San Bernardino NWR and to Leslie Creek, both in southeastern Arizona, and are threatened by a gradually dwindling spring flow. The beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish have been extirpated from the United States. Causes include:
- Cattle grazing, which destroyed aquatic habitat at San Bernardino Creek after spring flows diminished.
- Arroyo cutting and stream headwater diversion.
- Construction of impoundments.
- Excessive pumping of underground aquifers.
- Conversion of many lowland Mexican river systems into irrigation canal systems, which destroys pool habitat.

**Geothermal exploration:** The San Bernardino Valley has potential geothermal resources, although it is not a Known Geothermal Resource Area (KGRA). The [[Bureau of Land Management]] has issued geothermal leases on lands adjacent to the refuge. Development could deplete or pollute the aquifers feeding refuge springs, but the rule notes such effects are not expected if exploration is properly designed and regulated.

**Overutilization (Factor B):** The fishes are not used for commercial purposes, and past scientific collecting has had no impact on existing populations.

**Predation and disease (Factor C):** Introduced predators, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Bullhead]], channel catfish, and [[Green Sunfish]], occur in parts of the remaining Rio Yaqui range and probably prey opportunistically on the native fishes. This threat is expected to grow in Mexico but is minor in U.S. habitats.

**Inadequate regulation (Factor D):**
- The Yaqui chub is a Group II species on the Arizona threatened and unique wildlife list, meaning it is endangered of elimination from the state. Arizona law allows take under a scientific collecting permit or a valid angling license.
- The beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish are Group I species, meaning they are extirpated from Arizona but exist elsewhere. Arizona law does not officially protect them or their essential habitat. If reestablished, they would likely be relisted as Group II species.
- The Rio Yaqui fishes receive no protection in Mexico.

**Exotic species (Factor E):** Introduced exotics are the most serious threat to surviving populations. The [[Red Shiner]] is widely established in Arizona, and future releases into the Rio Yaqui system could reduce beautiful shiner populations through competition or genetic swamping. Channel catfish and [[Blue Catfish]] already established in the Rio Yaqui drainage may similarly affect the Yaqui catfish. Earlier work documented the rapid elimination of the native [[Yaqui Topminnow]] after introduction of the [[Western Mosquitofish]]. Exotic introductions in Mexico are expected to increase as the interior of Sonora and Chihuahua develops.

**Public comment:** Five written comments were received from the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the Arizona Department of Water Resources, the Bureau of Land Management, the U.S. Forest Service, and the Mexican Direccion de Flora y Fauna Silvestre. All supported or did not oppose the proposal. No public hearing was requested or held.

## Key Findings

- **Classification:** The Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*) is endangered. The beautiful shiner (*Notropis formosus*) and Yaqui catfish (*Ictalurus pricei*) are threatened.
- **Critical habitat:** All aquatic habitats of San Bernardino NWR in Cochise County, Arizona, specifically Sections 11, 14, and 15, T21S, R30E. The refuge provides habitat for one of the two existing Yaqui chub populations and expansion habitat for it. It is also a prime reintroduction site for the other two species.
- **Physical and biological features (constituent elements):**
  - Clean, small, permanent streams and spring pools free of exotic fishes.
  - Deep pools separated by riffles and flowing areas with moderate current.
  - Backwater areas of streams and springs with overgrown cutbanks and accumulations of detritus, needed for feeding and shelter.
- **Special rule:** Take of the threatened beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish is allowed for educational, scientific, or conservation purposes under Arizona law. Violating state law would also violate the Act.
- **Activities threatening critical habitat:**
  - Lowering the groundwater level enough to reduce spring flow, including groundwater pumping for agriculture and geothermal drilling.
  - Releasing chemical or biological pollutants that alter spring water chemistry.
  - Releasing exotic or nonnative fishes.
- **Federal involvement:** Geothermal exploration is the only identified federal activity that could affect the critical habitat. The designation does not preclude geothermal exploration, which may proceed as long as artesian and surface water supplies at the refuge are adequately protected. Federal agencies must consult with the Service under Section 7 of the Act.
- **Economic impacts:** The Service found that economic or other impacts did not warrant adjusting the proposed critical habitat designation.
- **Conservation status:** The Yaqui chub's Mexican distribution is more restricted, and the rule states that endangered status is "most appropriate." Without listing, the Service concluded that the status of these fishes could continue to decline.
