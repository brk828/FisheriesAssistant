# Taxonomic History and Status of the Gila Chub, *Gila intermedia* (Girard)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae #gila-chub #taxonomy #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary

This technical report by Bruce D. DeMarais (Arizona Game and Fish Department, 1995) reviews the taxonomic history, diagnostic morphology, native distribution, and systematic relationships of the [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), a small cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. The species was historically shuffled between five genera and demoted to a subspecies of [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) for over two decades before being re-elevated to full species status, a designation now widely accepted (Robins et al. 1991). The report emphasizes that despite clear species-level distinction between "pure" *G. intermedia* and *G. robusta* populations, numerous morphologically intermediate populations (historically labeled *G. r. grahami*) complicate taxonomic assignment. Molecular data (allozymes, mtDNA) fail to resolve these problematic populations, leaving morphology as the most reliable — though imperfect — diagnostic tool. The author argues that ancient introgressive hybridization, well-documented elsewhere in the genus *Gila* (e.g., origin of [[Gila Seminuda]]), is a plausible but unconfirmed explanation for these intermediate forms, and recommends that all such populations receive conservation protection regardless of final taxonomic resolution.

## Background & Description

### Diagnostic Morphology (Minckley 1973; Rinne 1976)

*Gila intermedia* is described as a small, thick-bodied, coarse-scaled cyprinid with the following diagnostic suite of characters:

- **Body form:** Chunky; occasional soft, fatty nuchal hump in large females of some populations
- **Scales:** Large, thick, broadly imbricated; basal radii usually present; strongly-inscribed basal radii in 63% of specimens examined (vs. 14.6% weakly developed in *G. robusta*)
- **Lateral-line scales:** Almost always fewer than 80 (97% of 390 specimens had <80; 77% had <75) — contrasts with *G. robusta*, where 80% of 670 specimens had ≥80 scales
- **Dorsal fin-rays:** Usually ≤8 (rarely 9); 94% of specimens had 7–8 rays, vs. 83% of *G. robusta* having ≥9
- **Anal fin-rays:** ≤8 in 98% of specimens (less reliable due to overlap with *G. r. grahami*, ~50% of which also have 8 rays)
- **Pelvic fin-rays:** 8 or 9 (roughly 50:50 split); *G. r. robusta* almost always has 9 (>95%)
- **Morphometrics:** Length of head / depth of caudal peduncle usually ≤3.0 (83% of specimens below 3.0); *G. robusta* is typically >3.0 (87%)
- **Vertebrae:** Total 38–45, usually fewer than 42
- **Pharyngeal teeth:** 2, 5-4, 2 (similar to *G. robusta*)
- **Coloration:** Overall dark, sometimes lighter belly; diffuse lateral band(s) rarely present; no basicaudal spot
- **Breeding males:** Red/orange coloration on lower cheek, posterior lips, paired fin bases, and ventro-lateral surfaces including caudal peduncle

Meristic and morphometric characters are strongly correlated (DeMarais, unpub. data), meaning that one or two characters (e.g., dorsal fin-ray count and/or caudal peduncle depth) are generally sufficient for rapid field identification.

### Taxonomic History

- **1851:** First collected from southern tributaries of the Gila River by J.H. Clark, prior to the Gadsden Purchase (1853)
- **1854:** Originally described as *Gila gibbosa* (Baird and Girard) from the Rio Santa Cruz — later invalidated as an erroneous homonym
- **1856:** Officially described as *Tigoma intermedia* by Charles Girard, based on type specimens from the Rio San Pedro
- **Post-1856:** Placed variously in five genera — *Tigoma, Squalius, Leuciscus, Richardsonius,* and *Gila*
- **1945:** R.R. Miller reclassified the taxon as *Gila robusta intermedia*, a subspecies of [[Roundtail Chub]]
- **1946:** Miller characterized it as an "ecological subspecies" tied to small tributary/brook habitats — this view persisted for >20 years
- **1969–1976:** [[John N. Rinne]] re-elevated the taxon to full species status (*Gila intermedia*) based on univariate morphological/meristic analysis
- **1980:** American Fisheries Society's official name list (Robins et al.) did *not* recognize *G. intermedia* as valid
- **1991:** AFS list revised to recognize *G. intermedia* as a valid species — current consensus

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Native Distribution

*Gila intermedia* is endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico. Its evolution is tied to the long geologic isolation of the ancestral Gila drainage from adjacent systems.

- **Historic range:** Likely encompassed the entire Gila basin except the [[Salt River]] drainage above Roosevelt Lake
- **Current range:** Persists in most major sub-basins — [[Verde River]], [[Agua Fria River]], [[San Pedro River]], and [[Santa Cruz River]]
- **Extirpation:** Apparently lost from the lower Salt River drainage (Fish Creek population)
- **Habitat loss driver:** Natural and anthropogenic draining of [[Cienegas]] (desert wetland/marsh ecosystems) over the past century has eliminated far more populations than direct human activities alone (Hastings and Turner 1965; Hendrickson and Minckley 1985)
- **Potential range extension:** The upper [[Bill Williams River]] drainage may represent a plausible natural range extension via stream capture from the upper Verde headwaters (Williamson and Chino valleys), supported by co-occurrence of other Gila-basin-endemic species: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Gila Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and possibly [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*)

### Habitat Preferences

*Gila intermedia* is characteristic of small streams, low-gradient marshy stretches, and cienegas — in contrast to *G. robusta*, which favors larger, higher-gradient river reaches. This ecological divergence originally led to the "ecological subspecies" framework (Hubbs 1940, 1941; Miller 1946), wherein habitat/morphology gradients within the Colorado River basin *Gila* complex (from large-river *G. elegans* to small-stream *G. intermedia*) were interpreted as intraspecific variation rather than distinct species.

### Sympatry and Comparative Ecology within *Gila*

Six taxa historically recognized in the Colorado River basin *Gila* complex:
- *[[Gila Elegans]]* (Bonytail) — large rivers, slender body, expansive fins, highest meristic counts
- *[[Gila Cypha]]* (Humpback Chub)
- *Gila intermedia* — small streams/marshes, opposite phenotypic extreme from *G. elegans*
- *G. r. robusta* and *G. r. jordani* — intermediate habitat and phenotype
- *[[Gila Seminuda]]* (Virgin River Chub) — intermediate between *G. elegans* and *G. robusta*; confirmed hybrid origin (DeMarais et al. 1992)

Unlike most other *Gila* species (which are typically allopatric, one species per basin), the Colorado River basin supports multiple co-occurring taxa, which fostered — and complicated — early "ecological subspecies" interpretations.

## Key Findings

### Species-Level Distinction from *Gila robusta*

- Rinne (1969, 1976) conducted the first comprehensive univariate analysis, supporting full species recognition and rejecting the "ecological subspecies" model, since both species occupy similar habitats without ever occurring sympatrically
- DeMarais (1986) used multivariate analyses, largely corroborating Rinne's two-species conclusion but **did not support** a phenotypically distinct *G. r. grahami* — instead finding a continuum of variation between *G. intermedia* and *G. r. robusta*
- Contiguous populations of *G. intermedia* and *G. robusta* show **no evidence of ongoing genetic exchange**, supporting their status as evolutionarily independent species

### Phylogenetic Relationships

- Osteological study (Uyeno 1960) suggested *G. robusta* is more closely related to *G. elegans* than to *G. intermedia*, though all were then treated as "ecological subspecies"
- Allozyme cladistic analysis (DeMarais 1992) found strongest support for *G. intermedia* as **sister species to *G. robusta***, with this clade sister to a *G. elegans* + *G. cypha* clade
- mtDNA restriction site analysis (T.E. Dowling, unpub. data) similarly supports a close *G. intermedia*–*G. robusta* relationship, though it **cannot resolve** the two species from one another

### The Hybridization Problem

- Molecular markers (allozymes, mtDNA) provide **no fixed diagnostic differences** between *G. intermedia* and *G. robusta*, despite clear morphological distinction
- *G. intermedia* populations show substantial genetic subdivision; *G. r. robusta* is comparatively homogeneous (DeMarais 1992)
- Confirmed case: [[Gila Seminuda]] (Virgin River Chub) demonstrated to be of **ancient introgressive hybrid origin** between *G. robusta* and *G. elegans* (DeMarais et al. 1992), supporting the plausibility of similar hybrid origins for intermediate Gila River basin populations
- Dowling and DeMarais (1993) documented **widespread ancient hybridization** across multiple *Gila* species in the Colorado River basin and beyond
- Because molecular data can't distinguish parental species, they can **neither confirm nor refute** a hybrid origin for problematic intermediate populations (e.g., "*grahami*")

### Taxonomic Recommendations (Appendix)

- The term **"intergrade"** is inappropriate now that *G. intermedia* and *G. robusta* are recognized as full species
- The term **"hybrid"** is also inappropriate for isolated populations, as it implies ongoing gene flow between sympatric species, which is implausible for geographically isolated stocks
- ***Gila robusta grahami*** is judged **not a valid taxon** — the author recommends dropping the subspecific designation and referring such populations simply to *G. robusta*, while acknowledging ancient hybrid origin remains unproven but plausible
- Identification should not rely solely on locality/stream of capture, since *G. intermedia* and *G. robusta* can co-occur in different reaches of the same stream (e.g., [[Eagle Creek]])

### Case Studies of Problematic Populations

- **[[Oak Creek]] (Yavapai County, AZ):** *G. intermedia* confirmed present in Spring Creek tributary (conforms fully to species diagnostics); absent immediately downstream at the Oak Creek confluence despite no physical barrier — a recurring pattern in lower Colorado basin *Gila*. A self-sustaining population in Oak Creek mainstream itself is considered unlikely, since *G. robusta* also occurs there (the only place *G. robusta* and *G. intermedia* are known to co-occur) and most reaches favor *G. robusta*-type habitat
- **[[Rye Creek]] (Gila County, AZ):** Adjacent Tonto Creek population historically classified as *G. r. grahami*. Limited sampling from Rye Creek revealed fish resembling Tonto Creek "*grahami*" phenotype, but with a higher apparent frequency of 8 dorsal fin-rays (a *G. intermedia*-defining trait) — sample sizes were too small for statistical validation. Further upstream sampling and gene-flow analysis with Tonto Creek recommended

### Conservation Implications

- Given the demonstrated evolutionary importance of hybridization in shaping *Gila* diversity and adaptive potential, the author argues for **protecting the entire species complex**, including morphologically intermediate populations, regardless of their ultimate taxonomic resolution (citing Ryder 1986; Whitham et al. 1991; Jones et al. 1988)
- Reconstructing phylogenies for *Gila* is inherently difficult because standard cladistic methods assume no reticulate evolution (hybridization), which is now known to be prevalent in this genus
- Detecting hybrid origins requires discordance between nuclear and mitochondrial phylogenies; absent this signal, many hybrid-derived lineages may remain permanently cryptic
