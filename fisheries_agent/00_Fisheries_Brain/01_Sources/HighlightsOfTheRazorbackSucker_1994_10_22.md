# Highlights of the Razorback Sucker Genetics Workshop

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary

This document summarizes the outcomes of a 1994 genetics workshop convened to guide conservation genetics management of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Upper Colorado River Basin. Experts from academic institutions, state agencies, and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] reviewed allozyme and mtDNA data, historical population trends, and hybridization risks to define management stocks and broodstock development strategies. Key conclusions include the lack of strong geographic genetic structure among upper basin populations, the necessity of using ecological (rather than purely genetic) criteria to define distinct stocks, and the adoption of a combined broodstock strategy drawing from multiple surviving populations to maximize genetic diversity and local adaptation potential in reintroduction efforts.

## Background & Description

- No detailed external morphological data provided; the paper focuses on population genetics and demography rather than anatomy.
- Growth rates of juveniles stocked in off-channel habitats were notably rapid: **8 to 12 inches (200–300 mm) in one growing season**, a size sufficient to reduce vulnerability to [[Nonnative Fish Predation]] and competition.
- Larvae are **phototactic**, a trait exploited for light-trap sampling; light-trapped larvae showed ~85% survival to 22 days of rearing.
- Recruitment indicators in the Middle Green River included presence of small razorback suckers (<475 mm), signaling some level of natural reproduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Historic habitat: large-river mainstem and connected floodplain/bottomland habitats in the [[Colorado River Basin]], used for spawning, larval drift, and nursery function.
- Habitat degradation drivers: altered natural hydrograph, construction of low irrigation diversion dams and high dams fragmenting riverine connectivity, and dike construction disconnecting floodplain nursery habitats from the main channel.
- Nonnative fish dominance: **≥75% of fish community by number** and **>90% by biomass** in many upper basin reaches, driving predation and competition pressure that limits [[Early Life Stage Survival]].
- Spawning ecology: Razorback sucker spawn on the **ascending limb of the hydrograph**, prior to spawning by most nonnative competitors — a critical timing window exploited in habitat and larval-rescue management strategies.
- Species has demonstrated long-term ecological resilience: documented southward displacement and northward re-expansion approximately **7 times over 1.4 million years** in response to Pleistocene ice advances, with the most recent range shift 10,000–12,000 years ago.
- Documented **introgressive hybridization** with the endemic [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], raising management concerns about human-induced increases in hybridization.
- Analogous conservation approach drawn from [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*) broodstock development at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, informing multi-year larval/juvenile collection strategy for genetic diversity capture.

## Key Findings

### Genetic Structure & Diversity
- Historically greater genetic diversity in the **southern (lower basin)** range compared to the north.
- Most genetic variation occurs **within**, not among, populations/stocks.
- 17 polymorphic characters identified; **no strong geographic genetic structuring** detected via allozyme and mtDNA analyses — genetic data alone cannot differentiate upper basin stocks.
- **Etter Pond** (off-channel gravel pit near Debeque, CO) population is genetically traceable to a **few parent individuals**, showing elevated flannelmouth sucker allele frequency at one locus — flagged as high inbreeding risk and unsuitable as sole broodstock source, though select individuals show value for local adaptability.

### Defined Management Stocks (Upper Basin)
Three ecologically-defined stocks (per spawning site fidelity, distribution, and interaction data), since genetics could not distinguish them:
1. **[[Middle Green/Yampa Rivers]] Stock** — largest breeding aggregation (~350 conservative adult estimate); documented interaction (3 adults) between Green and Yampa spawning sites ~70 km apart, leading to their combination as one stock (managed conservatively).
2. **[[Upper Colorado River]] Stock** — severely declined; historic reports of "several thousand" fish in the 1930s–40s Grand Valley; captures fell from 206 fish (1975) to zero (1989–1992); only 3 wild adults captured in 1993, none in 1994; extirpated from the [[Gunnison River]].
3. **[[Colorado River Arm of Lake Powell]] Stock** (grouped with San Juan River Arm) — population size unknown; 16 adults captured 1980–81, only 1 adult captured 1985–88; DNA-confirmed razorback larvae collected from Colorado River inflow in 1993, suggesting upstream drift-spawning.

### Comparative Lower Basin Model
- [[Lake Mohave]] stock: ~50,000 adults persisted for ~18 years, declining to ~25,000 within 3 years due to old-age mortality — underscoring urgency of short-term extinction-prevention actions.
- Strategy: collect naturally-spawned larvae via light trapping, rear in nonnative-fish-free embayments, restock at ≥300 mm total length once large enough to resist predation.
- Roughly **one-third of wild adult females spawn per year**, requiring a minimum 3-year sampling window to capture full breeding population diversity.

### Broodstock & Stocking Strategy
- Preferred approach for Upper Colorado River broodstock: a **combination strategy** drawing on Upper Colorado River, Colorado River Arm of Lake Powell, San Juan River Arm of Lake Powell, and Middle Green/Yampa River stocks to maximize local adaptation potential.
- Current captive stock (as of workshop): 3 Upper Colorado River fish (1M/2F), 4 Colorado River Arm of Lake Powell fish (2M/2F), 7 San Juan River Arm of Lake Powell fish (3M/4F), and 2 Etter Pond fish (1M/1F, to be outcrossed).
- Target effective population size for broodstock: **50 fish (e.g., 25 breeding pairs)**, calculated to maintain inbreeding depression rate at **≤1%**, considered acceptable for captive propagation.
- Two stocking frameworks:
  - **Augmentation stocking** — for river reaches below physical barriers (e.g., irrigation diversion dams).
  - **Restoration stocking** — for reaches above barriers (e.g., above [[Government Highline Irrigation Diversion Dam]] and [[Redlands Irrigation and Power Diversion Dam]]).
- All stocking requires an approved plan detailing species, source stock, fish size, release timing/location, genetic risk assessment, and evaluation methodology.
- Genetics Management Guidelines prioritize **natural propagation** as the first-line recovery approach, with captive broodstock serving as an "insurance policy" against stock collapse.

### Risk Management Principles
- **Inbreeding depression** identified as one of the greatest genetic risks in reintroduction stocking, especially when broodstocks derive from few or related founders (siblings/half-siblings).
- **"Genetic swamping"** risk flagged for replacement stocking into wild populations (e.g., Middle Green River) — mitigated by stocking limited numbers of progeny from unrelated fish.
- Differences in fitness among family lots may not be detectable through genetic analysis alone, underscoring need for physical conditioning of captive-reared fish prior to release to improve post-stocking survival.
