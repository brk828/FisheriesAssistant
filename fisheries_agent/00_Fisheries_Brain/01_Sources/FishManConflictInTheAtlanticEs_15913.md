# Conflict in the Atlantic Estuaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1967 American Littoral Society guide, written by [[John Clark]], argues that the Atlantic coast's estuaries are the critical nursery and feeding habitat for many commercially and recreationally important salt water fishes, and that poorly planned shoreline development threatens those fish resources. It describes the life histories of 22 estuarine-dependent species, documents marsh loss from dredging, filling, mosquito control, highway construction and water control, reviews conservation law and agency conditions in each of the 14 Atlantic coastal states, and proposes citizen and governmental actions such as land acquisition, zoning and biological review before permits are granted.

## Background & Description

The booklet was published as Special Publication Number 5 of the [[American Littoral Society]] (Highlands, N.J., 1967), with copyright held by Clark. Its stated aim is to supply the public with knowledge about sea fish and the estuarine environment they need, and the author frames it as a guide to "thoughtful use" of the Atlantic estuaries. Conservation is defined as sharing resources across generations. Pollution, shellfish and waterfowl are explicitly excluded to keep the focus on fish, although the text notes that pollution is the major problem in many estuaries.

The estuarine zone is defined broadly to include tidal rivers, marshes, tideflats, lagoons, bays and shallow sounds where salt and fresh water mix. Clark stresses that these are productive habitats, not wastelands. He states that two of every three useful Atlantic fish depend on tidal lands or shallow bays, and that nearly 70 percent of the most valuable Atlantic coast species depend on estuaries at some life stage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Productivity and nursery function.** Estuaries receive fertilizing minerals from land, and sunlight reaches the bottom to support seagrass and marsh growth. The richest coastal marshes are reported to produce 10 tons of plant matter per acre per year, more than six times the world average wheat yield of 1.14 tons per acre (the text gives "114 tons", which appears to be a scan or typographical error). Nutrients are exported by tidal action into coastal waters. Ninety percent of salt water fish are reported to be taken in shallow coastal waters.

**Three patterns of estuarine dependence.** The text classifies dependence as:
- *Residents*: species such as [[Spotted Seatrout]] that live, spawn and die within estuaries.
- *Outsiders*: oceanic spawners, including fluke, bluefish, menhaden and king whiting, whose young drift into estuaries.
- *Insiders*: species that spawn in protected estuarine waters, including weakfish, redfish, mullet and black drum.

**Life-cycle profiles.** Five species are profiled in detail: [[Striped Bass]], [[Atlantic Croaker]], [[Bluefish]], [[Summer Flounder]] (fluke) and [[Spotted Seatrout]]. Striped bass spawn in fresh or nearly fresh water from April through June, with eggs drifting two or three days. Croaker spawn offshore and larvae enter bays in the fall. Bluefish spawn offshore, with larvae reaching the coast at about an inch long. Fluke spawn in October and November over the continental shelf, with larvae drifting south before young enter inlets in February and March.

**Vulnerability.** The critical period is early development, when fish are an inch or less long. [[Spotted Weakfish]] are used as an example of a camouflaged species that depends on grass beds for protection and on grass shrimp as food.

**Table 1** classifies 22 estuarine-dependent Atlantic game and commercial fishes into three categories by adult habitat preference: mostly estuarine (A), partly estuarine (B), and mostly open coast (C). Anadromous species (for example shad, alewife and white perch) are marked separately.

## Key Findings

**Loss of habitat.**
- Dredging and filling is identified as the most damaging activity. Along the North Atlantic coast from Maine to Delaware, 45,000 acres of marsh were destroyed between 1955 and 1964.
- An inventory covering the last five of those years attributed losses to: dredge spoil deposition (34%), housing fill (27%), recreation (15%), bridges, roads, parking and airports (10%), industrial sites (7%), garbage and trash dumps (6%), and other causes (1%).
- The text notes that roughly 8 percent of Atlantic coastal wetlands were eliminated in one decade, and that the same rate sustained for a century would amount to disaster.
- In many areas, shoreline projects have destroyed or disrupted more than 50 percent of the original marsh and tidal habitat.

**Other threats.**
- *Navigation dredging* can alter currents and salinity.
- *Sand and gravel mining* is often disguised as navigation work.
- *Mosquito control* by ditching has mixed effects, while impoundment by dikes converts marshes to fresh water and blocks fish access. Clark states that insecticides are the most dramatic killers.
- *Highway causeways* without culverts can cut marshes off from tidal flow.
- *Water control*, including dams, diversions and freshwater withdrawals, alters salinity and silt loads, and can block anadromous spawning runs.

**Statewide accounts.** The 14 state summaries share common themes: poor ownership records for tidal lands, weak or divided authority, and reliance on public pressure. Notable cases include:
- *Maine*: Clam flats valued at $2,000 to $46,000 per acre per year; state and federal purchase of marshes between Kittery and Portland.
- *New Hampshire*: The Hampton marsh threatened by a planned reclamation of about 1,600 acres.
- *Massachusetts*: About 20 percent of tidemarsh lost; the 1963 Coastal Wetlands Law and 1965 supplement; a 1964 court decision upholding the Director of Marine Fisheries' right to impose conditions on a marina dredging project at Broad Marsh.
- *Rhode Island*: Green Hill and Charlestown Ponds produced 215,710 young winter flounder in one year and were estimated to supply 25 percent of the flounder caught by Rhode Island fishermen, based on [[Saul Saila]]'s study.
- *Connecticut*: 14,839 acres of tidal marsh remained of 23,360 acres in the 1914 inventory; the Water Resources Commission controls only work below mean high water.
- *Long Island, New York*: 29 percent of marsh and shallow habitat was "obliterated" between 1954 and 1964.
- *New Jersey*: 24,600 acres of coastal wetlands (about 9.6%) were lost between 1954 and 1964; the state and federal governments planned to own 150,000 acres of coastal marsh.
- *Delaware*: Canary Creek marsh yields about two-thirds of a ton of detritus per acre per year.
- *Maryland*: Wetland losses near 14 percent in ten years on the Western Shore; a proposed canal dredging of 19,000,000 cubic yards in upper Chesapeake Bay.
- *Virginia*: Back Bay and Currituck Sound degraded by dredging, drainage and silt; a James River dam proposal.
- *North Carolina*: Phosphate mining in the Pamlico River leasing 9,000 acres of river bottom.
- *South Carolina*: Croaker and spot young declined from 177 and 108 (1962) to 33 and 18 (1966) in average catches; the Santee-Cooper diversion caused harbor silting 90 times greater than before.
- *Georgia*: About 45 percent of marsh plant material is exported to adjacent waters; Savannah siltation and diversion concerns.
- *Florida*: Lake Worth's catches collapsed (for example spotted seatrout fell from 336,936 lb in 1950 to 857 lb in 1959); the 1957 Bulkhead Act and 1965 creation of the first marine preserve are cited as signs of progress.

**Economic values.**
- *Commercial catch (1965)*: Table 2 reports nearly one billion pounds of estuarine-dependent species, worth about $28 million to fishermen (about $75 million gross, per the text's multiplier).
- *Sport fishing (1960)*: Table 3 reports 399,430 thousand pounds of estuarine-dependent sport catch. Salt water sport fishing on the Atlantic coast was valued at $346 million in 1960, with more than 4,000,000 sportsmen in 1965.
- *Florida*: The Florida Development Commission estimates 31 percent of tourists visit for fishing, and the text cites a fishing value above $1 billion per year.

**Recommended actions.**
- Biological and fishery studies before any dredging or fill; citizen pressure to keep public officials informed.
- Land acquisition through state, federal or non-profit funds, easements and tax abatements; ownership inventories.
- Zoning of estuarine land and inclusion in community master plans.
- Improved federal authority: the author quotes [[John Gottschalk]] that the Fish and Wildlife Service holds only advisory power, and [[Stanley A. Cain]] calls for equal authority for the Department of the Interior with the Corps of Engineers.
- Bulkhead lines near mean high water and natural shoreline shapes (Florida guidelines).

**Conclusion.** Clark concludes that most problems can be resolved if facts are gathered, that a balance must be struck between essential projects and resource loss, and that action is needed before losses become irreversible.
