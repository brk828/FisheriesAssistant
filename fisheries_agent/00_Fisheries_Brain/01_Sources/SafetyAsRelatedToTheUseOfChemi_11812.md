# Safety as Related to the Use of Chemicals and Electricity in Fishery Management

#fishery-management #safety #toxicology #chemical-control #electrofishing

## Executive Summary

This 1961 special report by Charles L. Sowards of the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife addresses safety protocols for fishery biologists using chemical compounds and electrical equipment in field operations. The document systematically reviews the toxicology of [[Piscicides]], [[Herbicides]], insecticides, and rodenticides commonly employed in fishery management, providing acute toxicity data, lethal dose estimates, and recommended safety precautions. It also covers hazards associated with [[Electrofishing]] equipment and provides first-aid procedures for poisoning and electrical shock. The report emphasizes that modern fishery management increasingly relies on these tools, necessitating comprehensive safety training and adherence to Federal labeling laws (Federal Insecticide, Fungicide and Rodenticide Act of 1947; Miller Pesticide Residue Amendment of 1954).

## Background & Description

The report was prepared as part of the Departmental Management Training Program in April 1961. Data were compiled from five Bureau regional offices, eight state agencies, and technical literature on chemical toxicity and electrical safety. The author notes that toxicity values are empirical and often inconsistent across sources, with considerable individual variation in tolerance to poisons. Similarly, lethal electrical energy levels vary with victim physiology, heart phase, current type, and other factors.

**Chemical Control History**: Ground [[Derris]] root (containing [[Rotenone]]) has been used as a piscicide by tropical peoples for centuries, but synthetic organic pesticides only emerged in the late 1930s. By 1956, U.S. production reached 569,927,000 pounds annually, with sales of $275,000,000 at the manufacturer level.

**Piscicides Covered**: Three active ingredients are examined—[[Rotenone]] (relatively low toxicity), [[Toxaphene]] (extremely poisonous), and [[Sodium Cyanide]] (extremely poisonous). Concentrations used range from 0.005–4.0 ppm depending on compound and application. Maximum quantities reported include 85,000 pounds of rotenone powder and 3,000 gallons of liquid toxaphene by single agencies.

**Herbicides Covered**: Twelve active ingredients are reviewed, including [[Aminotriazole]], [[Ammonium Sulfamate]], [[Arsenic Trioxide]], [[Copper Sulfate]], [[2,4-D]], [[Sodium Arsenite]], [[Dalapon]], and various trichlorophenoxy compounds. Toxicity varies dramatically—the most potent compound is over 1,400 times more toxic than the least potent.

**Electrofishing Equipment**: Types include battery-operated backpack and fly-rod shockers for small streams; portable generators with brails, electric seines, or parallel wires for larger streams; and boat-mounted generators with booms, brails, or trawls for ponds and lakes. Voltage ranges from 50–500V AC, 110–500V DC; amperage from 0.2–28.0A; total energy from 75–3,000 watts. The report notes that pulsed DC units, though not covered in the survey, are considered more efficient.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses freshwater fishery management contexts across the United States, with data from agencies in [[California]], [[Florida]], [[Michigan]], [[Missouri]], [[New York]], [[North Dakota]], [[Texas]], and [[Washington]]. Applications include:

- **Population Control**: Eliminating unwanted fish populations with piscicides is described as "one of the most effective management techniques presently available."
- **Aquatic Plant Management**: Herbicides control nuisance vegetation in lakes, ponds, and streams at concentrations ranging from 0.5–430 pounds per acre or 0.5–10 ppm depending on compound.
- **Fish Sampling**: Electrofishing enables population surveys in diverse habitats from inaccessible headwater streams to large lakes, often conducted at night with artificial lighting.

**Non-Target Effects**: The report briefly mentions that insecticides and rodenticides, while seldom used directly by fishery biologists, require evaluation for effects on fish when applied by other agencies. Toxicity data for 20 insecticides and 4 rodenticides are tabulated, including compounds like [[Aldrin]], [[DDT]], [[Parathion]], and [[Strychnine]].

## Key Findings

**Chemical Toxicity Data**:

1. **Rotenone** (C₂₃H₂₂O₆): Oral LD₅₀ in rats = 132 mg/kg (crystalline), 1000 mg/kg (solid >60 mesh). Chronic exposure causes fatty liver and kidney changes, possible allergies. Safety precautions: wear goggles and respirators during heavy exposure.

2. **Toxaphene** (C₁₀H₁₀Cl₈): Oral LD₅₀ in rats = 69 mg/kg; guinea pigs = 12 mg/kg. May cause liver damage with sublethal exposure. Safety precautions: avoid all ingestion, inhalation, or skin contact; wear rubber gloves, protective clothing, and masks near spray/dust; use non-drip hose connections.

3. **Sodium Cyanide** (NaCN): Estimated lethal oral dose in humans = 50–60 mg per person; inhalation of 150 ppm is potentially toxic. Releases hydrocyanic gas in solution. Chronic exposure causes fatigue, dermatitis, thyroid enlargement. Safety precautions: wear rubber gloves, avoid inhaling dust/fumes, carry amyl nitrite inhalants.

4. **Herbicide Toxicity Range**: From relatively benign (ammonium sulfamate, oral LD₅₀ = 3600 mg/kg in rats) to highly toxic (3-(P-Dichlorophenyl)-1,1-dimethylurea, oral LD₅₀ = 3.5 mg/kg). Arsenic compounds cause fatigue, paralysis, liver/kidney degeneration, and garlic breath odor.

**Basic Safety Rules for Chemicals** (11 rules provided):
- Always read labels before use
- Store only in original labeled containers
- Prevent smoking in application areas
- Avoid inhaling fumes/dusts; wear protective clothing
- Wash immediately if material contacts skin
- Cover food/water containers for livestock and pets
- Dispose of containers safely
- Observe residue limits on edible materials
- Wash hands/face before eating or smoking
- Launder contaminated clothing before reuse
- Restrict access by children, pets, irresponsible adults

**Electrical Safety Findings**:

1. **Accident History**: Several serious accidents and at least one fatality have occurred with electrofishing gear in the United States, indicating need for enhanced safety training.

2. **Causes of Death**: Two major mechanisms—(1) temporary respiratory paralysis, and (2) ventricular fibrillation (heart twitching). At lower voltages (110–480V), loss of normal heart action may occur; at higher voltages (>6 amperes), heart contracts during current flow but may recover if shock is brief, though respiratory paralysis often follows.

3. **Safety Precautions for Electrofishing** (14 recommendations):
   - Instruct all personnel in electrical fundamentals
   - Familiarize crew with equipment operation
   - Ensure equipment is in good condition
   - Follow general electrical safety (touch only one wire/brail at time, wear rubber boots/waders and gloves)
   - Use non-slip footwear
   - Train operators to break circuits by lifting brail or touching opposite-polarity brails together
   - Ensure brail structural integrity can support a man's weight
   - Insulate brail handles with excellent insulators (e.g., 3/4" electrical conduit covered with underground plastic water pipe)
   - Never work alone
   - Display warning signs if public may be endangered
   - Have at least two crew members able to break circuit quickly
   - Train all crew in artificial respiration
   - Use treadle switches on boat shockers to break circuit if operator falls overboard
   - Require constantly buoyant life preservers for all boat crew (victim in electrical field unlikely to retain muscle control for swimming)

**First-Aid Protocols**:

- **Poisoning**: Call doctor immediately; for inhalation, move to open air and provide artificial respiration if needed; for skin contact, wash thoroughly with warm soapy water; for eye contact, flush with clean water; for ingestion, empty stomach with emetics (warm salt or mustard water), then administer demulcents (egg white, gelatin, flour/water, milk). Never give liquids to unconscious persons.

- **Electrical Shock**: Free victim from circuit using non-conductor; start artificial respiration immediately if not breathing; if heart appears stopped, apply chest compressions (60 lbs pressure at 1-second intervals) with mouth-to-mouth respiration (5 seconds every 30 seconds). Continue until natural breathing resumes or rigor mortis sets in.

- **Physical Shock Treatment**: Keep patient lying flat (elevate feet if no head injury/fractures); maintain body warmth without overheating; avoid rough handling, continued bleeding, temperature extremes, or fatigue.

**Regulatory Framework**: Two Federal laws protect public health—the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide and Rodenticide Act (1947) requires precautionary labeling and toxicological data for interstate transport registration; the Miller Pesticide Residue Amendment (1954) regulates residues on food. The report emphasizes that "READING THESE LABELS AND FOLLOWING THE INSTRUCTIONS THEREON ARE THE WISEST PRECAUTIONARY MEASURES."

**Data Sources**: The report includes extensive appendices with piscicide manufacturers (Appendix B), herbicide manufacturers (Appendix C), symbols/abbreviations/definitions (Appendix D), cooperating agencies (Appendix E), and an annotated bibliography of 10 cited references plus additional sources covering pesticide handbooks, toxicology compendia, poison control directories, and safety manuals.
