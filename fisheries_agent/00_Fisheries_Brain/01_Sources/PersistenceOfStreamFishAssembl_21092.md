# Persistence of Stream Fish Assemblages: Effects of Environmental Change

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper (Ross, Matthews and Echelle, *The American Naturalist*, 1985) compares the temporal stability and persistence of fish assemblages in two streams of contrasting physical harshness: [[Brier Creek]], an intermittent prairie-margin stream in southern Oklahoma, and [[Piney Creek]], a permanent, spring-fed Ozark stream in Arkansas. Over roughly one decade (1969–1981), species persistence (presence) was high in both systems. Stability, measured through changes in relative abundance, was lower in the harsh Brier Creek assemblage. The severe 1980 drought and heat death of fishes in Brier Creek had no detectable lasting effect on the whole-creek assemblage, but individual upstream stations that dry regularly showed less consistency. The authors conclude that assessments of assemblage stability should draw on several localities within a watershed.

## Background & Description

The study addresses how environmental perturbation affects community structure and the role of biotic interactions such as [[Competition]] and [[Predation]]. Following [[Connell and Sousa 1983]], stability is defined as constancy in the numbers of organisms over time, and persistence as a qualitative measure of existence. Earlier work suggested that assemblages are more stable in benign than harsh environments, but most studies had examined only one habitat type.

Fish assemblages in both creeks were dominated by small-bodied species, mainly minnows (Cyprinidae), darters (Percidae) and sunfishes (Centrarchidae). The reduced analysis suite (species making up at least 1% of the catch) was dominated by Cyprinidae, which accounted for 66.7% of analysed species in Brier Creek and 85.1% in Piney Creek. Brier Creek held 30 known species and Piney Creek 47.

Key questions were: whether the relative abundance of common species changed significantly between collection periods; whether the magnitude of change differed between streams; whether the 1980 summer left a lasting effect; whether some locations were more variable than others; and how spatial scale affects conclusions about [[Community Stability]] and the relative roles of equilibrial and nonequilibrial processes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Brier Creek (Oklahoma).** A tributary of the Red River arm of [[Lake Texoma]], draining 59.6 km² of agricultural land. Upstream sites 2 and 3 are fed mainly by runoff and dry to isolated pools by midsummer. Station 4 has deeper pools but its riffles usually cease flowing in summer. Stations 5 and 6 have permanent flow in most summers because of gravity-fed seepages. The region has cold winters and hot, dry summers.

**Summer 1980.** June, July and August 1980 rainfall fell below the 95% confidence interval for 1965–1981. Only June 1977 (34.0 mm), July 1972 (1.5 mm) and August 1973 (3.8 mm) were drier in their respective months, and only 1980 had three consecutive months of such low rainfall (58 mm total). Monthly mean maximum temperatures for June to September averaged 34.4, 40.6, 39.9 and 30.0 °C, the highest recorded for the 16-year period. [[Heat Death]] of fishes occurred in July 1980, when flow ceased even at station 5 and water temperatures reached 39 °C.

**Piney Creek (Arkansas).** A medium-sized stream in the Salem Plateau of the Ozark uplift, largely spring-fed, with flow maintained through the driest summers. Pools were at least 80 cm deep at all localities, and no physicochemical stress was detected. Maximum water temperature was 31 °C in July 1972, and midday temperatures on 29 August 1981 ranged from 24.5 to 26.5 °C. The authors judged Piney Creek the less harsh of the two systems.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Fish were collected with small-meshed seines (0.32–0.5 cm mesh) at five stations per creek, sampling all microhabitats for 1.0–1.5 h per station.
- Brier Creek data came from 1969 (Smith and Powell, five to eight collections per site), 1976, 1978 (stations 2, 5 and 6 only, since upper sites were dry) and 1981. Piney Creek data came from July 1972 and August 1981.
- Analyses used [[Spearman Rank Correlation]] for changes in species rank order, [[Kendall Coefficient of Concordance]] for multiple comparisons, [[Morisita Index]] for faunal similarity, and [[UPGMA Cluster Analysis]] for grouping stations.
- Replicate June and July 1981 collections in Brier Creek had a Morisita similarity of 0.83, indicating high agreement over short intervals.

**Persistence (presence data)**
- In Brier Creek, 16 of 17 common species (94%) present in 1969 were collected again in 1981.
- In Piney Creek, all species taken in 1972 were also taken in 1981.
- Both assemblages therefore showed high persistence.

**Stability (abundance data)**
- Brier Creek: rank correlation of species abundances between 1969 and 1981 was not significant (r_s = .29, P > .05), and faunal similarity was low (I_m = .40).
- Piney Creek: rank correlation between 1972 and 1981 was highly significant (r_s = .71, P < .01), with high faunal similarity (I_m = .74).
- Brier Creek therefore showed greater overall compositional change.

**Effect of the 1980 drought**
- Brier Creek total catches were 9,025 (1969), 719 (1976), 1,319 (1978) and 1,925 (1981), with 13,048 fish combined. Piney Creek catches were 1,492 (1972), 1,490 (1981), with 2,982 combined.
- Assemblages were dissimilar between 1969 and 1976 (r_s = .28, P > .05) but highly similar between 1976 and 1981 (r_s = .55, P < .05). Greater change therefore occurred before the drought, which the authors read as contradicting the hypothesis of increased change after 1980.
- Some species were transiently affected, but there was no lasting effect on the overall assemblage.

**Spatial patterns**
- Brier Creek: upstream stations 2 and 3 showed less rank-order consistency than downstream sites, especially station 6. Upstream sites formed a distinct faunal group (cluster A1) in most years. Post-drought assemblages at all sites shifted toward the headwater fauna, particularly at station 4.
- Changes in downstream Brier Creek in 1981 included a decline in [[Bigeye Shiner]] numbers, the loss of [[Spotted Bass]], the appearance of [[Golden Shiner]] and an increase in [[Red Shiner]]. Upstream, [[Bluegill]] and [[Redear Sunfish]] increased.
- Piney Creek showed no longitudinal consistency pattern. Only stations P9 and P2 had significant rank correlations between years, even though the pooled watershed assemblage was significantly correlated. Stations P1, P4 and P5 changed their cluster affinities.

**Mechanisms of stability (discussion)**
The authors propose four mechanisms for the stability and persistence of Brier Creek fishes:
1. Elasticity through high intrinsic rates of increase, allowing rapid repopulation by survivors.
2. Elasticity through high vagility, allowing rapid return to dewatered areas.
3. Resistance through refuge-seeking behaviour during drought.
4. Resistance through physiological tolerance to environmental change.

Species-specific responses matter. For example, [[Harrell 1978]] found that some cyprinids resisted flood disturbance through microhabitat shifts, while another species showed elasticity through higher reproductive output.

**Generation time**
- Most species have life spans of 3–4 years, so the roughly decade-long study covered at least three to four population turnovers.
- Red shiners usually reproduce at age one, and few individuals live beyond 3 years.
- The northern studfish lives about 4–5 years. Sunfishes generally live 5–7 years and bass 8–9 years.

**Scale and interpretation**
- Stations within Piney Creek changed significantly even though the pooled fauna did not. Single-site conclusions would therefore have indicated change, consistent with a stochastic model.
- The authors argue that [[Stochastic Processes]] cannot be assumed to dominate temperate stream fish communities. Evidence from several localities within a watershed is needed, because the true assemblage boundaries and home ranges of most stream fishes are poorly known.

**Summary conclusion**
Persistence was high in both streams. Stability was greater in the benign Piney Creek. The 1980 drought did not produce a lasting change in the Brier Creek assemblage as a whole, but upstream stations that dry annually showed greater variability within a characteristic suite of species. Geographic scale strongly affects assessments of stability and persistence.
