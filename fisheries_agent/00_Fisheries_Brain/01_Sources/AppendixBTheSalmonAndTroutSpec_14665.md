# Monograph of the Genus Salmo: North American Salmon and Trout (Suckley, 1861)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This is a taxonomic monograph by [[George Suckley]], Surgeon, United States Army, written in 1861 and published in the *Report of the Commissioner of Fish and Fisheries* (pp. 92–160). The work was prepared at the request of the [[Smithsonian Institution]], with editorial introduction by [[Spencer F. Baird]], and draws on specimens from the Pacific Railroad Survey, the Northwestern Boundary Survey, and the Hudson Bay collections of [[Robert Kennicott]] and other correspondents. Suckley reviews 43 North American species of *Salmo*, describing each one's synonymy, diagnostic characters, habitat, and life history where known. He proposes a separate subgenus, *Oncorhynchus*, for sea salmon with a permanently hooked bill, and he cautions that the true number of species is probably smaller than 43 because age, sex, and season variations have been mistaken for distinct species.

## Background & Description

**Scope and history.** Suckley's earlier report on the Salmonidae was prepared in 1859 for the Pacific Railroad Reports (vol. xii) and published with [[J. G. Cooper]] in *The Natural History of Washington Territory*. After the Northwestern Boundary Survey returned in 1861, the Commissioner of Fish and Fisheries, Archibald Campbell, entrusted him with a report on the Salmonidae of Washington Territory. At the Smithsonian's request he expanded it to cover all North American species. The memoir was delivered to the Institution in July 1861. The editor notes that Suckley's work is the only available comprehensive treatment and is presented in its original form.

**Classification scheme.** The genus *Salmo* (Linnaeus) is treated as a single genus, except that the sea salmon with permanently hooked bill are placed in *Oncorhynchus* (Suckley). The species are arranged as follows:

- **I. Anadromous salmon** (species 1–24), running up fresh water to spawn:
  - **a.** Males with long, decurved, hooked intermaxillaries (*Oncorhynchus*): species 1–6.
  - **b.** Jaws symmetrical or with the lower jaw point received in a notch: species 7–18 (without red spots, not feeding in fresh water) and 19–24 (spotted with red, feeding freely in fresh water).
- **II. Trout** (species 25–43), not anadromous, living entirely in fresh water or occasionally descending to the sea:
  - **c.** Spotted with red or black, in flowing water, retiring to deep still water in winter: species 25–37.
  - **d.** Trout found in deep rivers or lakes, ascending shallow streams to spawn: species 31–37.
  - **e.** Lake trout, living in deep lakes and never entering running brooks or the sea: species 38–43.

**Diagnostic characters.** Suckley relies on head proportions (length of head expressed as a fraction of total length), fin-ray formulas, branchiostegal ray counts, scale counts along the lateral line, tooth patterns on the vomer, tongue and palatines, tail shape, and spotting patterns. He also notes that sexes differ in jaw shape in fresh-run adults, and that spawning fish develop color changes.

**Methods and sources.** Specimens are referenced by Smithsonian catalogue numbers (e.g., No. 940 for *Salmo gibbsii*, No. 1134 for *Salmo truncatus*). Field observations come from [[Dr. Gairdner]], [[George Gibbs]], [[Dr. Kennerly]], Lt. Beckwith, and Lt. McKibben. Quotations from [[Lewis and Clark]], Richardson, Girard, DeKay, Agassiz, Prescott, and Herbert are included.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pacific anadromous salmon.** Most Pacific species enter rivers in defined runs. Suckley reports that the [[Skowitz]] (*Salmo scouleri*) arrives in the Puget Sound streams about the second week of September and continues to arrive until near Christmas. Its distribution extends up the Columbia to points more than 700 miles from the sea. The [[Dog Salmon]] (*Salmo canis*) enters the Puget Sound streams in autumn, generally between September 15 and October 10. The [[Quinnat]] (*Salmo quinnat*) first arrives in the Columbia River in April. The [[Hump-backed Salmon]] (*Salmo proteus*) enters Puget Sound rivers in August of alternate years.

**Spawning and post-spawning condition.** Several species show marked deterioration after entering fresh water. Dog salmon become "emaciated," with bruised snouts, worn fins, and sores after passing falls and rapids. Skowitz that remain in streams until spring are described as "much emaciated." Many anadromous fish die after spawning. The Indians report that all hump-backed salmon die, and that most dog salmon do not return to sea.

**Freshwater trout habitat.** Trout species are tied to specific water types. The American brook trout (*Salmo fontinalis*) occupies cool, flowing streams and lakes on the Atlantic slope, and it "occasionally repairs to salt or brackish water if within reach." Its color varies with light exposure and substrate: pale fish occur in clear gravelly streams exposed to sun, and dark fish in streams shaded and fed by peat bogs. The Sebago trout (*Salmo sebago*) is restricted to Maine lakes and the Saint Croix drainage. The Oquassa trout (*Salmo oquassa*) moves between Moosemeguntic Lake, the Kenebago River, and Lake Oquassa in a seasonal migration.

**Lake and chiloweyuck habitats.** At Chiloweyuck Lake, Richard's salmon (*Salmo richardii*) and Kennerly's trout (*Salmo kennerlyi*) are present. Dr. Kennerly noted "many dead salmon floating on the lake" on 13 September 1859, which the Indians attributed to "something peculiar in the water."

**Arctic and subarctic habitat.** Ross' Arctic salmon (*Salmo rossii*) and the Coppermine River salmon (*Salmo hearnii*) occur near the mouths of Arctic rivers. The angmaloock (*Salmo nitidus*) and long-finned char (*Salmo alipes*) are found in small lakes in Boothia Felix. Hudson's Bay trout (*Salmo hudsonicus*) occur in Hudson's Bay and its vicinity.

**Human use.** Indians dried large quantities of salmon for winter use. On the Columbia, dip-net fishing at The Dalles was productive: Gibbs reported that a single man could take "twenty or thirty fish in an hour." Puget Sound fisheries took as many as 3,000 hoodii in one seine haul.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** Suckley describes 43 *Salmo* species from North America. He states that the true number is "probably less," because some variations of age, sex, and season have been described as species. He notes that these variations would be easier to reduce "after the publication of this report."
- **Subgenus proposal:** In a notice published June 1861, Suckley proposed *Oncorhynchus* for the sea salmon with a hooked bill. The type species is *Salmo scouleri*. Species assigned to this subgenus in the text are *scouleri*, *proteus*, *cooperi*, *dermatinus*, *consuetus*, and *canis*.
- **Hump-backed salmon (*S. proteus*):** Specimens 1132 and 1133 (male and female) at the Smithsonian are described. The male has "a much more arched" dorsal profile and a prominent adipose hump. The average weight is "about 5 or 6 pounds." Suckley suspects *S. dermatinus* of Richardson is identical to *proteus*.
- **Cooper's salmon (*S. cooperi*):** "The adults rarely exceed 22 inches." The species is abundant in the Okina-kane River. Two typical specimens were collected by George Gibbs on 30 September 1860.
- **Dermatinus (*S. dermatinus*):** Richardson's single specimen measured "about 32.4 inches in total length" and was taken in the Yukon River. It has a toothless tongue, scales that do not overlap, and a strongly cut-out tail.
- **Consuetus (*S. consuetus*):** Richardson's description is from a male "about 23.5 inches in total length" from the Yukon River, ascending as high as the falls of the Porcupine.
- **Quinnat (*S. quinnat*):** Adults can weigh "sixty-four and a half pounds"; two such fish were reported at San Francisco. Fish "of seventy pounds" are reported farther north. Gairdner gives a usual average of "about twenty-five pounds." First arrival dates in the Columbia range from April 10 to April 28, with Lewis and Clark recording April 18, 1806.
- **Skowitz (*S. scouleri*):** The fish begin arriving in Puget Sound in September and continue until near Christmas. In the Columbia it ascends "fully seven hundred miles" from the sea. A Puget Sound haul produced "as many as 3,000 fish."
- **Dog salmon (*S. canis*):** "Enters the streams along Puget Sound in great numbers in autumn." The fish were "in shoals" arriving from September 15 to October 10. In 1856 at Fort Steilacoom, arrivals on October 3 reached "vast numbers" by October 7.
- **Campbell's salmon (*S. campbelli*):** Specimens of 20 to 24 inches in length are described from Puget Sound. Their red-spotted flanks and white dorsal spots separate them from other Pacific trout.
- **Richard's salmon (*S. richardii*):** The fish "runs up Fraser River in great numbers," where it is "the principal summer salmon." Dr. Gairdner notes that *S. paucidens* enters the Columbia with *S. quinnat*, while *richardii* does not ascend the Fraser "until later."
- **Brook trout (*S. fontinalis*):** The "rarely exceeds four pounds" in weight. The species is described as "the favorite game-fish of the Middle States." Forsyth documented three color varieties on Cape Cod, correlated with stream substrate and shading.
- **Lake trout (*S. namaycush*):** Weights are disputed. Herbert reports "none weighed less than 17 or 18 pounds, and many as high as 45"; Richardson cites a 120-pound specimen from Dr. Mitchill; and Suckley asserts that the average is "not four pounds."
- **Siscowet (*S. siscowet*):** Agassiz reports that the fish is "rich, highly flavored, but too fat," and that its average weight "does not exceed four or five pounds."
- **Oquassa trout (*S. oquassa*):** Appears in Moosemeguntic Lake "about October 10," ascends the Kenebago River "in shoals," and returns to Moosemeguntic "after the middle of November."
- **Kennerly's trout (*S. kennerlyi*):** Mature fish "rarely exceed 10 or 11 inches." The species has 25 scale rows above the lateral line and 155 scales on the lateral line in the type specimen.
- **Campbell's and Baird's trout:** *S. bairdii* "attains the weight of ten or twelve pounds." In Chiloweyuck Lake, the chewagh (*S. campbelli*) and related trout reportedly weigh "9 or 10 pounds" when caught.
- **Naming caution:** Suckley argues strongly against naming species from young or partially developed fish, warning that such practice "cannot be too strongly reprobated" and would make classification impossible.
