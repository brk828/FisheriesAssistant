# Temporal and Spatial Relations between the Spawning of Humpback Chub and Roundtail Chub in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 study by Kaeding and colleagues examines whether [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) overlap in spawning and whether they hybridize in the upper [[Colorado River]]. Fieldwork ran from 1983 to 1985 at [[Black Rocks]], Colorado. The authors combined morphometric and meristic analysis, gonad condition, and radio telemetry. Spawning seasons overlapped in 1983 and 1984, and the two species were distinguished morphologically with high reliability. The authors conclude that hybridization is possible but that the evidence does not show it is occurring at a level that threatens humpback chub. They also identify habitat features and flow regimes that favour the endangered humpback chub.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a cyprinid of the Colorado River system and is federally listed as endangered. The roundtail chub is a related species that has been reported to hybridize with it and with other native [[Gila Species]]. Hybridization in cyprinids is widely documented, and interbreeding has been implicated in the decline of native fishes. The authors note that the existence of F1 hybrids does not by itself show that gene exchange occurs in nature. Gene exchange depends on hybrid production, survival, and fitness, and on whether backcross individuals persist.

Before this work, hybrids were thought to be common in some populations. Earlier reports from the Yampa River and elsewhere described intermediate forms, and some of these were considered to be intergrades, variant forms, or possibly pure individuals of one species. The study aimed to:

- Document the timing of spawning in both species and assess temporal overlap.
- Describe spatial and behavioural patterns in radio-tagged fish.
- Assess morphological distinction between the species and identify unclassified fishes.
- Evaluate the habitat requirements of humpback chub and their implications for recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and habitat.** Field work was concentrated at Black Rocks, a turbulent, deep-water reach of the Colorado River with large angular boulders and shoreline rock outcrops. The authors argue that the important habitat feature is not depth but the dynamic flow created when water moves rapidly among boulders and currents shift back and forth across submerged rock formations. Humpback chub at Black Rocks rarely moved from this habitat. The authors suggest this points to a habitat that fully meets their needs during spawning.

**Habitat limitation.** Large riprap used in highway and railroad construction was identified as a habitat type that humpback chub use, and its scarcity may limit population size in the upper basin. This contrasts with [[Bonytail]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], whose declines are attributed mainly to loss of habitat quality and to predation and competition from introduced fishes. Relatively few introduced species have colonized Black Rocks and other unique humpback chub habitats.

**Flow and temperature.** Fish spawned when river discharge was near its seasonal high or was receding, and when water temperatures were 14–24 °C. Record runoff in 1983 and 1984 delayed the seasonal warming of the river compared with earlier observations.

**Habitat in other rivers.** Maximum depths of the [[Little Colorado River]] are only about 3 m, and the deepest humpback chub habitats in the [[Yampa River]] are of similar depth. The authors therefore conclude that depth is not the common factor across humpback chub habitats; the common factor is the dynamic, boulder-rich flow.

## Key Findings

### Morphological and meristic analysis

- The authors examined 597 specimens of *Gila* sp. from Black Rocks, 1983–1985. In the field, 270 were classed as humpback chub (inferred by subtraction, as this count is missing from the scanned text), 269 as roundtail chub, 1 as bonytail, and 57 as unclassified *Gila* sp.
- A [[Principal Component Analysis]] of log-transformed measurements from eight morphological characters explained 85.0% of the variation in the first two components. The two clusters corresponded closely to field identifications.
- Fork length loaded most strongly on the first component (0.940). Depth of the nuchal depression and caudal peduncle depth were the most useful characters for distinguishing the species.
- Only 1 humpback chub (0.4%) and 19 roundtail chub (7.1%) did not cluster with their field-assigned species.
- Of the 57 unclassified specimens, 49 (86%) clustered with humpback chub. Dorsal and anal fin ray counts differed significantly among taxa (p < 0.01), and the unclassified fishes included some roundtail chub and possible backcross individuals with ray-count combinations not seen in either parent species.
- The eight-character analysis separated specimens more effectively than an analysis of similar numbers of largely different measurements used on these species in the Yampa River.

### Seasonal reproduction

- Spawning of humpback chub and roundtail chub overlapped in 1983 and 1984.
- Gonadosomatic indices showed no important differences between years, so data were pooled within species.
- Females of both species with expressible ova were captured from June into late July. A female humpback chub with flowing ova was captured on 28 June 1985.
- In males, the period of expressed milt was generally longer than the period of expressible ova in females.
- Gonadosomatic indices of female roundtail chub rose in early June and declined as spawning progressed in July. Female humpback chub were examined internally beginning in early July 1983, and their indices declined over the following three weeks.

### Movement (radio telemetry)

- The authors implanted 33 humpback chub and 17 roundtail chub with radio transmitters.
- No differences in movement were detected between sexes or among years within species.
- Humpback chub moved relatively little from Black Rocks. Roundtail chub moved extensively, with a maximum displacement of 33.9 km.
- Radio contact could not be made with transmitters at depths greater than 5 m, so some fish in deep water may have been undetected. Three humpback chub and two roundtail chub were never located after release.
- Humpback chub show strong homing ability. Fish released 0.3 km from their capture sites were back within several weeks. One carlin-tagged humpback chub returned to its capture location within one day.
- Two humpback chub displaced from Westwater Canyon to Black Rocks showed relatively large movements, and one humpback chub tagged at Black Rocks was recaptured in Westwater Canyon.

### Hybridization

- Hybridization between bonytail and humpback or roundtail chub was tested in the hatchery. The bonytail × humpback and bonytail × roundtail crosses produced fry, and the self-crosses and four introgressive crosses were attempted.
- A humpback chub × roundtail chub cross could not be attempted because viable humpback chub eggs were not available that year.
- The authors conclude that data strongly suggest postmating reproductive isolating mechanisms exist between humpback and roundtail chub, but that hybrid viability has not been tested directly. They argue that the genotype of the unclassified *Gila* sp. at Black Rocks is a more pressing research question.

### Conservation implications

- Humpback chub is federally listed as endangered. The authors argue that the unusual morphology and specialised habitat of this species may reflect adaptation to unique environments.
- Planned water developments to meet growing demand could alter the aquatic ecosystem of the Colorado River and change the flows that humpback chub depend on.
- The authors suggest that Black Rocks-type habitats are a useful model for habitat assessment, and that artificial riprap may be an important habitat component in recovery planning.
