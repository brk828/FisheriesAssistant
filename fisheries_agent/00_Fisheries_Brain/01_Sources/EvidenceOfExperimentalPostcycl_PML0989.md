# Evidence of Postcyclic Transmission of *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* in Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This research note reports experimental evidence that the invasive Asian fish tapeworm, [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]], can be transmitted [[Postcyclic Transmission]] from one fish to another. Young-of-the-year [[Bonytail Chub]] were exposed to copepods infected with the parasite and then fed to four larger subadult chub. About one week after the last prey fish was consumed, three of the four predator fish were infected with gravid and non-gravid tapeworms. The authors argue that postcyclic transfer may be an important life history feature of this parasite and should be considered when assessing its impact and distribution in the [[Colorado River]] watershed.

## Background & Description

*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* is a pseudophyllidean tapeworm (class [[Cestoda]]) native to China. Its distribution has expanded to five continents, largely through the translocation of cyprinid fishes such as [[Common Carp]] and [[Grass Carp]], as well as [[Mosquitofish]] and baitfish. In the United States it was first recorded in the Colorado River watershed and now infects the federally endangered [[Humpback Chub]], its main host in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in [[Grand Canyon]].

Most pseudophyllideans need a copepod first intermediate host and a fish second intermediate host, where the plerocercoid stage embeds in muscle or viscera. *B. acheilognathi* differs because it needs neither a second intermediate nor a paratenic host. Transmission to the final fish host occurs directly through ingestion of copepods carrying the procercoid stage. Postcyclic transmission, defined by Bozhkov (1969) as the indirect ingestion of an adult parasite by a predator of its definitive host, had not previously been reported for this species, although small fish have been called "carriers" (Hoffman, 1999).

Pathogenicity is well documented. Infections reduced growth in juvenile hatchery-reared carp (Liao and Shih, 1956; Bauer et al., 1973). Hansen et al. (2005) found that infections reduced bonytail chub growth by 9% relative to controls, and infected fish on limited rations had a 62% mortality rate. Those experiments used young fish that eat copepods, so the authors asked whether older fish could become infected through piscivory or cannibalism.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Host feeding ecology:** Juvenile humpback chub feed mainly on zooplankton and smaller invertebrates. With age their feeding becomes generalist and opportunistic, and cannibalism has been reported (Kaeding and Zimmerman, 1983; Stone, 2004; Stone and Gorman, in press). This creates the opportunity for postcyclic transmission.
- **Reservoirs:** Small LCR tributaries are thought to be key parasite reservoirs. They support copepod production and abundant small cyprinids such as [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] (Choudhury et al., 2004).
- **Dispersal:** Larger chub move farther than small cyprinids, including larval and postlarval humpback chub. Infected adults could therefore spread the parasite to other thermally suitable parts of the Colorado River watershed.
- **Life history theory:** Greater transmission success is generally linked to more complex life cycles (Morand et al., 1995), yet this parasite has a simple one. The authors suggest postcyclic transmission may offset the need for a complex cycle, which fits with the parasite's low host specificity (Freeman, 1973; Dove et al., 1997; Hoffman, 1999).
- **Crowding effects:** High parasite density can inhibit cestode growth, maturity, and fecundity (Hoffman, 1971; Kennedy, 1983; Davydov, 1975). Transferring worms to larger fish with more gut area, higher food intake, and lower metabolic rates may relieve these crowding effects.
- **Age-related impacts:** Infection effects on survival and growth are strongest in early developmental stages. Prevalence and intensity decline in older carp, possibly from diet changes or better immunity. Because older humpback chub are piscivorous, they may still carry substantial infections.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Four predator bonytail chub, about 26 months old, were reared in 76 L tanks at the [[National Wildlife Health Center]] and placed in a 4 L exposure tank. Fish came from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Eight young-of-the-year prey chub (approximately 22 mm fork length) were exposed to [[Acanthocyclops Robustus]] copepods infected with *B. acheilognathi*, following Hansen et al. (2005). The age was garbled in the scan.
- Prey were moved into the predator tank 11 days post-exposure. Predators were not fed until all prey were eaten, then given commercial food ad libitum.
- All prey were consumed within 23 days. The last prey fish was eaten 34 days after exposure, at about 23 °C.
- About one week after the last consumption, predators were necropsied.

**Results**
- Three of the four predator chub were infected, with 1, 1, and 36 worms respectively.
- Four of the 36 worms had mature proglottids containing tanned eggs, which indicates gravid worms.
- Predators that ate prey 5 days post-exposure were not infected in unpublished work by Hansen. Those that ate prey 11 days post-exposure were infected, so parasite development time may be critical for successful postcyclic transmission.

**Conclusions and Implications**
- The study provides strong evidence that *B. acheilognathi* can be transmitted postcyclically between fish. It is the first documented route of this kind for the species.
- Gravid worms in a predator fish suggest the parasite could complete its life cycle through this route, although it is unknown whether the gravid worm matured in a prey or predator fish.
- Postcyclic transmission in adult and subadult fish may help spread the parasite beyond LCR tributary reservoirs.
- The authors call for further research on the negative effects of the parasite on older fish and on the role of postcyclic transmission in the parasite's life cycle evolution.
