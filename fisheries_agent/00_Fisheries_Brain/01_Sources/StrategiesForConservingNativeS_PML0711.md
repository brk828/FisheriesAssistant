# Strategies for Conserving Native Salmonid Populations at Risk from Nonnative Fish Invasions: Tradeoffs in Using Barriers to Upstream Movement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service Rocky Mountain Research Station monograph (General Technical Report RMRS-GTR-174, September 2006) reviews the tradeoff managers face when using [[Movement Barriers]] to keep [[Nonnative Salmonids]] out of native salmonid habitat. Native salmonids are increasingly confined to small headwater refugia, where barriers protect them from invasion but can raise their [[Extinction Risk]] by isolating them. The authors synthesize existing knowledge, present a four-question [[Decision Framework]], and illustrate it with two contrasting basins: the [[Coeur D'Alene River]] (Idaho) and the [[Little Snake River]] (Colorado-Wyoming). They conclude that management should be treated as an experiment and carefully monitored, because the risks of barriers and invasions are not fully understood.

## Background & Description

- **Focus taxa:** Native salmonids of the inland western U.S., especially [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Bull Trout]]. The authors note the framework is also relevant to other aquatic vertebrate and invertebrate taxa, but the scope is limited to salmonids.
- **Invaders:** Three commonly introduced salmonids (brook, brown, and rainbow trout) that have established reproducing populations in many regions outside their native ranges.
- **Movement ecology:** Salmonids use dispersed habitats across the [[Riverscape]] for spawning, growth, and refuge. Life histories include [[Anadromy]], [[Fluvial Life History]], [[Adfluvial Life History]], [[Resident Life History]], and [[Lacustrine Life History]], and movement is often critical for persistence in variable environments.
- **Barriers:** The conterminous U.S. has an estimated 77,000 dams at least 2 m high storing at least 50 acre-feet of water (U.S. Army Corps of Engineers 2006), plus smaller dams, diversions, and hundreds of thousands of road culverts that block fish passage (GAO 2001). Natural barriers such as waterfalls also isolate populations.
- **Historical context:** Brook trout were first released in 11 western states between 1872 and 1912 and are still stocked in 7 of them (MacCrimmon and Campbell 1969, as cited).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Isolation effects:** Barriers can extirpate mobile life-history types, restrict populations to habitats inadequate for long-term persistence, and prevent recolonization after catastrophes. Isolated populations are more vulnerable to [[Small Population Phenomena]] (loss of genetic variability, demographic and environmental stochasticity) and to catastrophes such as fire, flood, drought, and debris flows.
- **Persistence thresholds:** Studies of cutthroat and bull trout suggest salmonids need approximately 10 km of suitable stream habitat to persist for about 100 years and maintain genetic diversity, although the amount needed likely varies with climate and basin characteristics. The authors describe this as a first approximation.
- **Invasion impacts:** Brook trout can displace cutthroat trout by reducing survival during the first two years of life. Rainbow trout hybridize with cutthroat trout, and brook trout with bull trout. Invasion success varies: brook trout invasions have stalled in some regions, possibly limited by environmental factors such as temperature, flow regime, and winter flooding.
- **Parasites:** [[Whirling Disease]] (*Myxobolus cerebralis*) can be spread upstream by invading trout and is apparently irreversible once established.
- **Watershed context:** Habitat degradation (mining, logging, grazing, road building, dams, overfishing) fragments streams and compounds the effects of invasion and isolation.
- **Invasion stages:** Transport and spread, establishment (governed by [[Environmental Resistance]] and [[Biotic Resistance]], and by [[Propagule Pressure]]), and impacts (displacement, coexistence, hybridization, parasite transmission).

## Key Findings

1. **Conservation values:** Three classes of value are recognized: evolutionary (distinct, locally adapted populations protected under the U.S. [[Endangered Species Act]]), ecological (roles in food webs and self-sustaining function), and socio-economic (fishing and tourism). These rarely converge in disrupted systems. The authors propose a hierarchy in which irreplaceable values should not be forfeited.
2. **Vulnerability to invasion:** Unauthorized introductions accounted for 90 percent of new introductions of nonnative fishes in five U.S. regions during 1981 to 1999, compared with 15 to 43 percent in earlier periods (Rahel 2004).
3. **Isolation and persistence:** In a study of 52 headwater fragments in Hokkaido, Japan, a minimum watershed area of 2.3 km² gave a 50 percent chance of sustaining charr for 50 years, and about 9 km² gave a 90 percent chance. In Colorado and New Mexico, translocations in watersheds >14.7 km² had a >50 percent chance of establishing a reproducing population.
4. **Population size thresholds:** Populations of 500 and 5000 individuals were proposed as thresholds for maintaining genetic diversity and long-term evolutionary potential, respectively. Half or fewer of extant cutthroat trout populations in various regions occupy streams long enough to support them.
5. **Removal experiments:** Removing brook trout led to recovery of native trout in several studies. For example, Colorado River cutthroat trout survival of age-0 fish rose 13-fold after four years of brook trout removal. A westslope cutthroat trout population increased seven-fold after eight years of removal. Encouraging anglers to remove brook trout in Alberta was ineffective.
6. **Priority framework:** Priorities depend on risk (probability of invasion, effects if invaded, and value lost), representation, redundancy, persistence (viability), and feasibility. Persistence is described as necessary but not sufficient for viability.
7. **Coeur d'Alene River (Idaho):** Large areas remain uninvaded. Barriers could protect genetically pure westslope cutthroat trout, while barrier removal could restore migratory life histories. Existing road culverts are candidates for conversion to intentional barriers or removal. Brook trout are established in some tributaries, and rainbow trout and hybrids pose a more immediate threat.
8. **Little Snake River (Colorado-Wyoming):** Nonnative trout have invaded almost completely. Roughly 160 to 190 km of up to 38 streams hold 15 to 20 distinct populations of [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]. Most populations occupy segments under 3 km, and only four occupy more than 10 km of connected habitat. Recovery depends on removal of nonnative trout, habitat protection, and possible reintroductions.
9. **Decision-space diagram:** Strategic decisions depend on two conditions: the degree of isolation and the degree of invasion threat. Strategies range from preventing invasions and barriers in low-threat basins, to removing barriers and minimizing habitat degradation, to nonnative removal with temporary barriers in nearly complete invasions.
10. **Management as experiment:** Because risks are not fully understood, management should be monitored and evaluated. Barriers should be viewed as calculated, reversible-where-possible risks until research shows isolation effects can be reversed.
11. **Research priorities:** (a) rules of thumb for stream length needed to sustain each species in isolation; (b) environmental and evolutionary factors controlling invasion; (c) whether life-history diversity buffers invasion and enables coexistence.
