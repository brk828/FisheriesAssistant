# Scope of Work: Shortnose and Lost River Sucker Studies Below the Proposed Salt Caves Hydroelectric Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #freshwater-ecology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1986 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) review and comment letter, along with the associated Scope of Work (SOW), addressing proposed research on the endangered [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]] in relation to the proposed **Salt Caves Hydroelectric Project** on the Klamath River. The study, to be conducted by Beak Consultants Incorporated, aims to assess potential hydroelectric project impacts on spawning migration, larval drift, hybridization, and stranding risk for these two endangered catostomids, as well as the related [[Klamath Smallscale Sucker]] and [[Klamath Largescale Sucker]]. The SOW is organized into four sequential research phases spanning literature review, fish collection/radio-tagging, spawning migration tracking, and larval/stranding studies, with a final report due July 1987.

## Background & Description

Identification and differentiation of the four regional catostomid taxa was a central technical challenge due to widespread hybridization. Field and laboratory protocols specified the following diagnostic and data-collection features:

- **Meristic/morphometric measures to be recorded per individual:**
  - Fork length
  - Pre-dorsal length
  - Weight
  - Fin ray counts
  - Gill raker characteristics (count on first arch)
  - Lip form (cleft size and papillae)
  - Head shape
  - Scale counts: lateral line scales, scales before dorsal fin, scales after dorsal fin

- **Key distinguishing characteristics between [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]]** (per Koch et al.):
  - Number of lateral line scales
  - Number of scales anterior to dorsal fin
  - Number of gillrakers on first arch
  - Predorsal length

- Per taxonomic expert **Dr. Gerald R. Smith** (Univ. of Michigan Museum of Zoology), species-level identification could be achieved primarily from:
  - Gillraker structure
  - Lip form
  - Head shape

- **Photographic documentation protocol** recommended by USFWS: high-quality photographs of entire fish (left lateral view), head (lateral), and mouth (ventral) for any individual with morphometric data collected.

- Hybrid identification in the field was noted as **highly difficult**; a standardized field checklist of measurements/counts was recommended to minimize misidentification, with voucher specimens (5–10 individuals minimum, up to 40 per species per Dr. Smith's statistical requirements) preserved for laboratory verification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and Distribution:**
- [[Lost River Sucker]]: Historically documented in the Klamath Lake drainage and Tule Lake since the late 1800s (Coots 1965); unconfirmed historical reports from Lower Klamath Lake and Sheepy Lake. First collected from **Copco Lake** in 1956.
- [[Shortnose Sucker]]: Historical range overlapped with Lost River Sucker, but primary occurrence was in **Upper Klamath Lake** and **Lake of the Woods**. First found in Boles Creek (tributary of Clear Lake) in 1955; first Copco Lake specimen collected in 1956.
- [[Klamath Smallscale Sucker]]: Common in the Klamath and Rogue River drainages, and the Trinity River drainage; abundant in Lower Klamath River reservoirs including Copco Lake, though only two specimens were collected from **Iron Gate Reservoir** (Rogers 1976).

**Spawning Ecology:**
- Both target species were expected to congregate near the upper end of Copco Reservoir or on shallow beaches prior to spawning as water temperatures rose in March–April.
- Spawning migration was hypothesized to be triggered by rising water temperature, with upstream movement into Klamath River tributaries.
- Catostomid incubation periods generally range **10–21 days**, dependent on water temperature, with larval emergence expected shortly after adult out-migration.
- Larval catostomids were noted to primarily **drift at night**, necessitating 24-hour sampling protocols.

**Population Status:**
- A 1986 survey of Upper Klamath Lake (USFWS/ODFW/Klamath Tribal biologists) yielded 190 Lost River Suckers, most estimated to be **>19 years old**, indicating a population suffering from **sustained low recruitment** — a strong warning sign for long-term viability.
- Shortnose Suckers were believed less abundant than Lost River Suckers regionally (Newhouse 1975), though a 1976 Copco Lake collection (Rogers) found numerous Shortnose Suckers versus only three Lost River Suckers, complicating this assumption.

**Interspecific Hybridization:**
- [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]] both hybridize with [[Klamath Largescale Sucker]].
- Hybridization between Lost River and Shortnose Suckers themselves is **less common** than hybridization of either with Klamath Largescale Sucker.
- Hybridization with [[Klamath Smallscale Sucker]] is the **least likely** pairing.
- This hybrid complex has caused persistent unresolved taxonomic difficulties (Koch et al. 1975).

**Anthropogenic Threats (Study Focus):**
- Potential stranding of adult and larval fish due to water level fluctuations from **hydroelectric peaking operations** (proposed high flow: >1500 cfs; low flow: ~350 cfs).
- Electrofishing was flagged as causing **unusually high mortality** in catostomids, especially genus *Chasmistes* (which includes Lost River Sucker), prompting a recommendation to favor **large seines, trap nets, or closely-monitored gill nets** (checked every 3 hours).

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory recommendation**: USFWS endorsed the study as a mechanism to evaluate hydroelectric project impacts, but requested refinements including monitoring both timing *and duration* of spawning runs, correlating flow (not just temperature) data with fish runs, and addressing cumulative effects of powerhouse discharge on migration/spawning.
- **Sampling method concerns**: Electrofishing poses elevated mortality risk to catostomids, particularly *Chasmistes* species; gentler capture methods were mandated.
- **Population age structure red flag**: Preponderance of individuals >19 years old in a 1986 Upper Klamath Lake survey suggests a **recruitment failure**, a critical finding for conservation status assessment.
- **Taxonomic complexity**: Four interbreeding catostomid taxa (Lost River, Shortnose, Klamath Smallscale, Klamath Largescale Suckers) require careful morphometric/meristic documentation and expert laboratory verification (Dr. Gerald R. Smith, University of Michigan) to resolve species identity and hybrid status; a sample of ~40 individuals per species was deemed statistically necessary to detect backcrossing rates.
- **Study design structure**: Four-phase approach:
  1. **Phase I** (Nov 1986) – Literature review and refinement of SOW.
  2. **Phase II** (Dec 1986–Feb 1987) – Fish collection, hybridization sampling, radio tagging (up to 15 individuals/species), winter movement monitoring in Copco Reservoir.
  3. **Phase III** (Mar–May 1987) – Tracking of spawning migration via radio telemetry, weekly surveys of Klamath River and tributaries, remote data logger installed near proposed Salt Caves dam site to record temperature/flow.
  4. **Phase IV** (Apr–Jun 1987) – Larval drift monitoring (0.505 mm stationary drift nets) and stranding surveys under variable flow regimes (>1500 cfs vs. ~350 cfs).
- **Stakeholder involvement**: The Klamath Tribe (via Mr. Craig Bienz) was identified as having conducted independent multi-year studies on both species and was recommended for inclusion in all agency reviews and report distribution.
- **Expert consultation network**: Dr. Gerald R. Smith and Dr. Robert R. Miller (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology), Dr. Carl E. Bond (Oregon State University), and Dr. W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University) were identified as leading authorities on large, long-lived catostomid taxonomy and were consulted or copied on the SOW.
- **Final deliverable**: A comprehensive final report integrating all four phases was due **July 15, 1987**, to be distributed to CDFG, USFWS, and ODFW.
