# Recovery Plan for the Endangered Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans) - Technical Review Draft

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #recovery-planning #Colorado-River-Basin

## Executive Summary

This technical review draft recovery plan for the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) was prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] in 1982. The bonytail chub was listed as endangered on April 23, 1980, without designated critical habitat due to unknown reproducing populations and imminent extinction threat. The species, once abundant throughout the [[Colorado River]] system in mainstem channels and larger tributaries, has declined to extremely rare status. The plan outlines actions to prevent extinction, including intensive population surveys, habitat protection, life history research, captive propagation at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], and reintroduction to historical riverine habitats. Primary recovery sites identified are [[Gray Canyon]] on the [[Green River]] and [[Dinosaur National Monument]] on the [[Yampa River]]. The goal is to establish self-sustaining populations: four for downlisting consideration and six for delisting consideration, with all habitats legally protected.

## Background & Description

The bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) is a large cyprinid originally described by Baird and Girard (1853). Adults commonly reach 30-35 cm (11-14 inches) total length, though larger specimens have been documented from [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. The species evolved in swift waters of the Colorado River system and is closely related to [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), forming an apparent gradation in swift-water adaptation.

Morphologically, bonytail chub possess an elongated, laterally compressed body with a characteristically long, thin caudal peduncle. The head is small with a terminal, somewhat oblique mouth. Adults exhibit a smooth predorsal hump with scales often lacking or embedded on the hump, belly, or caudal peduncle. Dorsal and anal fin rays typically number 10-10 (distinguishing them from roundtail at 9-9 and humpback at 9-10). Gill rakers on the second arch number 18-21 (versus 12-15 in roundtail). Pharyngeal teeth follow the pattern 2,5-4,2, typical of large river chubs.

Adult coloration includes gray or olivaceous dorsum with white, silvery sides and belly. Breeding males develop bright red-orange lateral slashes between paired fins and fine breeding tubercles on head and anterior trunk. Females show more subdued breeding colors and less developed tubercles. Slight orange coloration persists at fin bases in both sexes throughout much of the year.

Taxonomic confusion has complicated identification, particularly for juveniles and potential hybrids. Morphological variation and suspected hybridization between *G. elegans*, *G. cypha*, and *G. robusta* have created identification challenges. Holden and Stalnaker (1970) noted specimens without abrupt nuchal humps but with other humpback characteristics as potential intergrades. Tyus et al. (1982) adopted combined techniques including x-rays and gill raker counts, confirming only one bonytail from 19 suspected specimens collected 1980-1981 from the Green River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical Distribution and Abundance:**
The bonytail chub originally occurred throughout the Colorado River system in main channels and larger tributaries. Historical records document presence in:
- [[Green River]] from [[Flaming Gorge]] basin (southern Wyoming) through [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] to the lower Green River
- [[Colorado River]] mainstem from near the [[San Juan River]] mouth to the [[Gulf of California]]
- [[Gila River]] to Fort Thomas, Arizona, and [[Salt River]] (tributary of Gila) to near [[Verde River]] mouth
- [[Gunnison River]] near Delta, Colorado
- Lower [[Yampa River]]
- [[White River]] of Utah

The species was apparently abundant in the late 1800s. Jordan (1891) seined five specimens in the Green River at Green River, Utah. Kirsch (1888) reported fish "took the hook freely" on the Gila River at Fort Thomas. By the 1950s, bonytails were no longer captured from much of the Gila River system and lower Colorado River. The last known riverine area where bonytails were fairly common was the Green River in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], where Vanicek (1967) collected 91 specimens during 1962-1966.

**Present Status:**
The bonytail chub is presently very rare and close to extinction. In the lower basin, large old adults (primarily females) persist in Lakes Mohave and Havasu, but no reproductive success has been documented. Since 1974, 18 specimens were taken by researchers in Lake Mohave and 10 reported by anglers in Lake Havasu (W.L. Minckley, personal communication).

In the upper basin, few have been reported since Vanicek (1967). Holden and Stalnaker (1975) found 36 adults during 1968-1971 (29 in 1968, three in 1969, four in 1970), with all but two from Green and Yampa rivers in Dinosaur National Monument. No young were found. Subsequent sampling has yielded minimal captures: Holden and Seethaler found two adults in [[Desolation Canyon]] (1974), Holden found one adult near Jensen, Utah and one juvenile in Desolation Canyon (1977), and Holden and Crist found one in lower Yampa River (1979).

**Life History - Riverine:**
Bonytails are considered big-river or mainstem species, seldom caught in smaller tributaries. Vanicek (1967) noted no habitat preference differences between adult bonytail and roundtail; both used pools and eddies rather than moving current.

Growth rates from scale analysis (Vanicek and Kramer 1969) in Dinosaur National Monument showed young reached 55 mm first growing season, 100 mm second season, 158 mm third season. Bonytails grew faster than roundtails after approximately 150 mm. The largest specimen was 388 mm and seven years old.

Diet studies showed young chubs (not separated by species) ate primarily chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs, becoming dependent on surface food as they grew. Larger juveniles consumed diversified diets including terrestrial and aquatic insects. Adults fed on terrestrial insects, plant debris, and filamentous algae, indicating surface feeding. No fish remains were found in stomachs.

Spawning has not been directly observed in rivers. Based on ripe fish collections, spawning in Dinosaur National Monument occurred late June to early July at approximately 18°C water temperature. Ripe bonytails and roundtails were temporally synchronous but spatially separated.

**Life History - Reservoirs:**
In [[Lake Mohave]], bonytails generally occupy lacustrine rather than riverine habitat. Minckley (personal communication) believes cold inflowing Colorado River water precludes use of apparently more favorable habitat. Diet consists primarily of plankton and algae.

Spawning behavior was observed in Lake Mohave (Jonez and Sumner 1954) with approximately 500 fish congregating over gravel bars in up to 9 m depth. Males outnumbered females 2:1. Females were escorted by 3-5 males, and fertilized eggs were deposited randomly without territorial defense. No young have been observed despite spawning observations. Kent Miller observed congregations in [[Lake Powell]] in mid-1960s but no spawning, and no young have been found.

**Life History - Hatchery:**
Six female and five male bonytails from Lake Mohave were artificially crossed at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in 1981 (Hamman 1982b). Spawning began at 20°C water temperature. Hatching success was 90% at 16-17°C (170-269 hours), but only 4% at 12-13°C (334-498 hours). The 96% failure rate at coldest temperatures implicates cold water as a factor in species decline.

In 1980, five female bonytails were hybridized with eight roundtail and 33 humpback males at Willow Beach, with offspring raised at Dexter National Fish Hatchery to aid hybrid identification and resolve taxonomic uncertainty.

**Reasons for Decline:**
The bonytail chub declined in the lower Colorado basin during the first half of the 20th century. Miller (1961) documented that by 1940-42, bonytails became rare in Colorado and Gila rivers near Yuma, Arizona, and were absent by 1950. Contributing factors included:

*Lower Gila River System:*
- Flow depletions from vegetation loss (overgrazing)
- Ground water depletion
- Dams and irrigation
- Mining operations causing siltation and chemical pollution
- Introduction of exotic species
- Near-complete drying of streams in early 1900s with reduced flows, increased flash flooding, and channel cutting

*Lower Colorado River Mainstem:*
- Habitat loss from dam construction
- Loss of natural riverine habitat
- Remnant populations in Lakes Mohave and Havasu show no successful reproduction

*Upper Colorado Basin:*
The decline has been more recent. Documented factors include:
- Construction and operation of reservoirs creating lower summer tailwater temperatures
- Habitat loss in reservoir basins
- Flow reductions below dams
- Competition and predation from exotic species
- Flow depletions from irrigation and consumptive water uses

The [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure (November 1962) exemplifies upper basin impacts. Vanicek et al. (1970) attributed bonytail loss below the dam to "alteration of yearly flow and water temperature patterns." Temperature alterations occurred immediately after closure. Pre-impoundment fish eradication and subsequent riverine habitat loss eliminated bonytails from Flaming Gorge Dam to Yampa River mouth.

Below the Yampa River mouth, bonytails were common in early 1960s. Vanicek (personal communication) regularly caught adults in Dinosaur National Monument (1964-1966). Vanicek and Kramer (1969) documented strong year classes in 1959, 1960, and 1961 for fish above 200 mm. However, reproduction dropped dramatically in 1963-1964, with researchers failing to find young bonytails during or after that period. Growth rates also slowed after 1962, all apparently related to Flaming Gorge Dam closure.

Similar impacts likely occurred with [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closure (1963). Bonytail abundance declined below the dam due to altered flows and cold temperatures, and in the reservoir basin from habitat alteration. Bonytails were fairly common in Lake Powell initially but declined within years, not persisting as in Lakes Mohave and Havasu, probably because fish could emigrate into the Colorado, Green, and San Juan rivers.

Colorado River flows were depleted earlier than Green River flows (Joseph et al. 1977), also affected by major Gunnison River dams in early 1960s. Reduced and unnatural flows likely contributed to bonytail depletion before Green River populations were affected. Water depletions through consumptive use have reduced flows throughout the upper Colorado basin, potentially contributing to decline by reducing essential flow volumes.

Exotic fish species introduction may have contributed through competition, though likely as one of several factors rather than the main cause. Hybridization between bonytails and other chubs, if occurring in relatively large areas as suggested by Holden and Stalnaker (1973), may also factor in decline. The very small remaining population increases hybridization likelihood with more abundant species. Habitat artificialization may reduce deep natural pools in canyon reaches that constitute important reproductive isolating mechanisms. Habitat loss for all *Gila* species may result in severe interspecific competition and niche partitioning.

## Key Findings

**Recovery Goal:**
To restore and maintain the bonytail chub in non-endangered status by establishing self-sustaining populations in natural riverine areas with protected and/or enhanced habitats for all life stages. Primary habitats identified: (1) Green River (Gray Canyon area, Utah); (2) Yampa River (Dinosaur National Monument, Colorado).

**Downlisting Criteria:**
Two additional naturally self-sustaining populations maintained beyond the two primary habitats.

**Delisting Criteria:**
Naturally self-sustaining populations established in two additional areas (to be identified) beyond downlisting requirements, with all habitats legally protected.

**Recovery Strategy - Three-Step Approach:**

*Step 1: Prevent Extirpation*
- Protect two bonytail chub populations (natural or introduced) and their habitats
- Compile historical population data
- Develop taxonomic criteria and improved identification techniques
- Conduct intensive sampling for all age classes in Gray Canyon, Dinosaur National Monument, and other potential areas
- Quantify populations in Lakes Havasu and Mohave (Bureau of Reclamation initiated three-year Lake Mohave study in 1981)
- Enforce all protective laws
- Monitor populations and habitats with designated agencies (Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, Colorado Division of Wildlife, Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service)

*Step 2: Life History Research*
Augment knowledge in critical areas:
- Spawning requirements (vital for preventing extirpation and achieving recovery)
- Movement patterns
- Habitat identification and description (nursery, over-winter, adult)
- Food habits and feeding behavior
- Age distribution and growth rates
- Population dynamics and self-sustaining population definition
- Reasons for hybridization (poses significant threat)
- Parasite and disease occurrence (necessary for hatchery program success)

*Step 3: Population Restoration*
If intensive sampling shows extirpation or imminent danger:
- Determine specific habitat requirements and establish site selection criteria
- Select at least two Green River reaches for reintroduction (initial sites: Gray Canyon area and Dinosaur National Monument Yampa River area)
- Stock 100,000 bonytail per year in selected locations for five successive years
- Develop appropriate stocking procedures with marking experiments
- Develop propagation and holding techniques at Dexter National Fish Hatchery to maximize young production and maintain adult brood stock
- Maintain diversified gene pool (adult brood stock from Lake Mohave obtained March 1981; additional stock from other areas may be needed)
- Monitor reintroduced populations and habitats
- Improve or enhance potential riverine areas for spawning, nursery, adult holding, and winter habitat
- Develop basin-wide management plan once populations identified/restored

**Information and Education:**
- Produce and disseminate I&E materials (leaflets, news releases, Endangered Species Technical Bulletin summaries)
- Provide workshops for public agencies on bonytail identification, critical habitat recognition, management techniques, and endangered species responsibilities
- Include other endemic Colorado River system fishes in workshops

**Status Review Milestones:**
- Consider downlisting recommendation when four self-sustaining riverine populations established with legally protected habitats
- Consider delisting recommendation when six self-sustaining riverine populations established with legally protected habitats
- Continued monitoring required at all stages

**Critical Constraints:**
- Extremely rare current status suggests species may be close to extirpation
- Very few distinct bonytails recently collected or identified from natural riverine habitats
- Identification challenges from morphological variation and potential hybridization with *G. cypha* and *G. robusta*
- Attainment of objectives and fund expenditure contingent upon appropriations, priorities, and budgetary constraints
- Plan subject to modification following review by cooperating agencies and interested parties
