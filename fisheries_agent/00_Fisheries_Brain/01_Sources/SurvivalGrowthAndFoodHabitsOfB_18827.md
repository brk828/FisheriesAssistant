# Survival, Growth, and Food Habits of Brook Trout and F1 Splake Planted in Precambrian Shield Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by J. M. Fraser (Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources) compares [[Brook Trout]] and first-generation [[Splake]] (F1 hybrid of [[Lake Trout]] and brook trout) planted in four small, infertile [[Precambrian Shield]] lakes in southeast [[Algonquin Park]], Ontario, monitored from 1966 to 1978. Splake consistently outperformed domestic brook trout in survival, weight return, and trophy potential. Brook trout returns were largely confined to the year of planting, while splake were recovered over 6-7 years. Growth, condition, and diet varied among lakes according to competitive context and available prey.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), domestic hatchery stock, and F1 splake (*Salvelinus namaycush* x *S. fontinalis*), produced by fertilizing [[Lake Manitou]] lake trout eggs with sperm from [[Hill's Lake Hatchery]] domestic brook trout.
- **Motivation:** Earlier plantings of splake in lakes ranging from small ponds to [[Lake Huron]] had shown variable success. Fraser (1972) found splake yielded greater weight returns than brook trout in Precambrian Shield lakes, and some splake reached trophy size (about 2 kg) within 5-6 years.
- **Study lakes:** Four small headwater lakes draining to the Opeongo River, with areas of 9.3-27.1 ha and maximum depths of 7.3-35.7 m. Waters were slightly acid (pH 6.7-7.0), poorly buffered (alkalinity 7-8 mg/liter), and infertile (total dissolved solids 32-41 mg/liter). Fishing was prohibited by law.
- **Fish communities:** Shallnot Lake held only minnows (four cyprinid species); Chipmunk Lake was dominated by [[White Sucker]]; Rumley Lake by [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Pumpkinseed]]; Presto Lake contained all of these species, but in lower numbers.
- **Methods:** Yearling splake were planted in all four lakes and yearling brook trout in three (not Presto). Age 0+ fish were also planted in some lakes. Fish were recovered by annual June-July [[Gill Netting]] (1968-1978) and stomach contents were analyzed by volume and frequency. Condition was calculated as K = W x 10^5 / L^3.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry and oxygen:** Bottom waters became oxygen-depleted during summer [[Thermal Stratification]], but upper layers stayed suitable for trout. Shallnot Lake could drop to a marginal 3 mg/liter in winter. No physical or chemical changes were detected in the lakes from 1966 onward.
- **Competition gradient:** Lakes were selected to span a range of competitive pressure on planted salmonids. Earlier work (Cooper 1959; Fraser 1972) indicated planted salmonids do best where competition from resident fish is least, a pattern supported here by the Shallnot Lake results.
- **Behaviour after planting:** In laboratory work (Brown 1961), splake sought shelter and spent time on the bottom, whereas domestic brook trout dispersed evenly across the trough. Wild and interstrain hybrid brook trout showed greater longevity than domestic stock in natural ponds (Flick and Webster 1976).
- **Trophic context:** Splake diets reflected lake-specific prey: [[Leeches]] in Shallnot Lake, fish (young yellow perch and pumpkinseed) in Rumley Lake, and [[Crayfish]] in Chipmunk and Presto lakes.

## Key Findings

**Survival and recovery**
- Splake were recovered in greater proportions than brook trout in three lakes and equally in the fourth.
- Mean gill-net recoveries of yearling splake were 15.3% (Chipmunk), 13.2% (Presto), and 8.5% (Rumley). Corresponding brook trout recoveries were 2.9%, 1.2% (age 0+), and 7.2%, respectively.
- In Shallnot Lake, both species had a mean recovery of about 28% (splake 28.3%, brook trout 27.7%). Over 90% of brook trout recovered there were caught in the year of planting, so long-term survival could not be separated from one-season vulnerability.
- Age 0+ splake in Rumley Lake were recovered at 6.2%, close to the 8.5% of yearlings.
- Splake recoveries were spread over 6-7 years; most brook trout were caught in the year of planting or the next.

**Weight return**
- Each kilogram of yearling splake returned 2.5-7.1 kg among lakes. Yearling brook trout returned 0.2-1.3 kg per kilogram planted.
- Age 0+ splake in Rumley Lake returned 4.4 kg per kg versus 2.5 kg for yearlings, suggesting a potentially cheaper management option. The author estimated this could cut management costs by about two-thirds.

**Growth**
- Brook trout and splake planted in the same lake grew at similar rates in the first year at large.
- Splake growth differed significantly among lakes (fastest in Shallnot, then Rumley, Chipmunk, and Presto), and these differences persisted for 5-6 years.
- The largest splake recorded was 61.1 cm and 2.8 kg, from Shallnot Lake.
- Yearling brook trout were larger at planting (15.1 cm vs 11.7 cm for splake) but mortality was high and few survived two years.

**Condition**
- Shallnot splake reached K = 1.23 in year Y+1 and held this for two years before declining.
- Rumley splake steadily improved in condition over six years.
- Slow-growing Chipmunk and Presto splake increased in condition for six years before declining in year seven.

**Maturity**
- Most fast-growing splake in Shallnot and Rumley lakes matured by age 3+, while only 16-52% of slow-growing splake in Chipmunk and Presto did so.
- Some Presto splake did not mature until age 5.

**Food habits**
- Rumley Lake: fish made up 56% (brook trout) and 66% (splake) of the diet, chiefly young-of-the-year perch and pumpkinseed.
- Chipmunk Lake: fish comprised 53% of brook trout diet volume and 20% of splake volume; crayfish (Malacostraca) made up 50% of splake volume.
- Presto Lake: crayfish constituted 68% of splake food volume.
- Shallnot Lake: brook trout ate Odonata nymphs and leeches; splake fed mainly on leeches and Daphnia, but older survivors lost condition on this diet.
- Large splake (>40 cm) depended heavily on one prey type per lake, while small splake (<30 cm) ate a wider variety.

**Conclusions**
- F1 splake yielded much higher returns, especially by weight, than domestic brook trout planted in the same lakes.
- Lakes with less competition gave the highest returns, while the spread-out recoveries in the other three lakes suggest potential for a trophy fishery.
- Splake showed adaptability to different environments in survival, growth, condition, and diet.
