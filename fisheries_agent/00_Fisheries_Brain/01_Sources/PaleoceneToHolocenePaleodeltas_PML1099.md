# Paleocene to Holocene Paleodeltas of the Ancestral Colorado River Offset by the San Andreas Fault System, Southern California

#paleogeography #sedimentology #fluvial-geology

## Executive Summary

This paper reconstructs the early drainage history of the [[Colorado River]] by arguing that Upper Paleocene to Middle Miocene fluvial-deltaic rocks in the [[Los Angeles Basin]] and [[Ventura Basin]] were deposited by an ancestral Colorado river (a "paleoriver") before roughly 300 km of dextral displacement on the [[San Andreas Fault]]. Late Miocene movement on the fault and associated rifting in the [[Salton Trough]] rerouted this paleoriver into a proto-[[Gulf of California]]. Since the Pliocene, a migrating deltaic depocenter has filled the Salton Trough progressively southeastward. The author proposes that the paleoriver joined [[Gila Paleoriver]] and [[Amargosa Paleoriver]] tributaries, and that disruption of the Amargosa system during late Cenozoic tectonism led to the modern course through the lower [[Grand Canyon]]. The model is presented as a constraint on the magnitude of regional Cenozoic displacement.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a long-standing disagreement about the early history of the Colorado River (Elston and Young, 1991). Two factors complicate the record: opinions differ on the origin of some sedimentary units, and pre-late Miocene sedimentary rocks are rarely exposed upstream from the San Andreas fault. Downstream of the fault, large-scale horizontal offsets are recognized (Winker and Kidwell, 1986; Powell, 1993), but their magnitudes and paleogeographic implications remain incompletely understood.

The author reviews chronostratigraphic and palinspastic relations and builds new paleogeographic reconstructions on the hypothesis that a well-developed Cenozoic succession of Colorado paleoriver deposits lies west of the San Andreas fault in southern California. The older rocks are placed in the Los Angeles basin area, while the late Miocene and younger sequence is placed mainly in the Salton Trough. The work is explicitly tied to the [[Suspect Terrane Theory]] debate (Gastil, 1991), since it constrains the magnitude of regional Cenozoic displacement. The term "paleoriver" denotes a drainage ancestral to, and genetically related to, a modern river, not necessarily one with the same geographic shape.

Key methods and tools include:
- [[Palinspastic Reconstruction]] of pre-late Cenozoic paleogeology (Powell, 1993)
- Provenance analysis of clast suites and sand grains (including thin-section matching of quartzite clasts)
- [[Potassium-Argon Dating]] of volcanic units, with ages corrected to new constants (Dalrymple, 1979)
- Chronostratigraphic correlation summarized in Figure 2, which cites 20 supporting sources

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a geological study and contains no biological data (no fish, invertebrate, plankton, or water-chemistry records). Its environmental content concerns depositional settings and drainage character:

- **Fluvial-deltaic systems:** Upper Paleocene to Middle Miocene fluvial-deltaic rocks of the [[Topanga Formation]], [[Sespe Formation]], and related units record the paleoriver's deposits. Eocene conglomerates in the Los Angeles area carry far-traveled clasts (metavolcanic, quartzitic, granitoid), indicating a well-integrated regional drainage.
- **Estuarine embayment:** The [[Bouse Formation]] outlines an embayment along the lower Colorado River. Strontium isotope data suggest its basal limestone formed in a freshwater environment, whereas contemporaneous [[Imperial Formation]] rocks formed under marine conditions. The author interprets this as a salt-wedge (freshwater) estuary, consistent with an [[Estuarine-Deltaic Facies Model]].
- **Lava-dammed drainage:** Volcanism in the Salton Trough (beginning about 35 Ma) and in southwestern Arizona (26 to 20 Ma) may have created lava dams that ponded the ancestral drainage. Torrential floods from breached dams are proposed to explain coarse, exotic detritus in the Sespe Formation and clasts up to 2 m in size in the [[Gypsum Canyon Quarry]] gravel.
- **Modern analog:** Between Parker and Yuma, the present river carries mainly far-traveled detritus despite flanking alluvial fans, illustrating that fanglomerates alone do not prove a throughgoing river.

## Key Findings

**1. Displacement constraints**
- All rock units along the southern San Andreas fault older than 8 to 10 Ma are dextrally displaced by about 300 km (Powell, 1993). Pre-late Miocene Colorado paleodeltas should therefore lie in the Los Angeles basin area, far from Yuma.
- About 60 km of right slip occurred on the San Gabriel-San Andreas fault between 12 and 6 Ma (Powell, 1993).
- The reconstruction of Salton Trough development from Pliocene to Holocene time incorporates 244 km of right slip on the San Andreas fault.

**2. Los Angeles basin paleoriver**
- Cretaceous to lower Paleocene nonmarine conglomerates were deposited by a nonintegrated drainage carrying only local detritus (Kies and Abbott, 1983).
- The upper Paleocene to Middle Miocene fluvial-deltaic succession, reinterpreted to include a boulder conglomerate facies, records the arrival of far-traveled material.
- The Gypsum Canyon facies is interpreted as channel-axis material of the Colorado paleoriver during Topanga time. Its first-cycle origin is indicated by the absence of clasts from the supposed host rock (Sespe Formation). If correct, it marks the last known position of the paleoriver before it was diverted from the Los Angeles basin.

**3. Timing of diversion**
- The river's departure is recorded by an upsection shift to local provenance in the [[Puente Formation]], dated by intercalated volcanic rocks at about 9 Ma.
- Its arrival at the head of the Salton Trough is constrained by 9 to 10 Ma volcanic rocks associated with the Imperial Formation.
- Late Miocene displacement is proposed to have produced a right-lateral offset that eroded the paleovalley later occupied by [[San Gorgonio Pass]].

**4. Salton Trough migration**
- Following [[Crowell Migrating Depocenter Model]] logic (Crowell, 1982; a depocenter migrating along an active strike-slip fault), the early Pliocene Colorado paleoriver lay in Coachella Valley and later filled the Salton Trough with deltaic sediment as it was translated southeastward.
- Pliocene deltaic deposits of the Imperial Formation are generally of Colorado River origin.

**5. Eastern drainage system**
- Little stratigraphic evidence for the paleoriver is exposed east of the San Andreas fault. The only Paleogene basin-axis deposits are far-traveled Eocene(?) to Oligocene conglomerates near Yuma (Lombard, 1993).
- The distribution of the Bouse Formation, with branches into the lower Gila River basin and into Vidal and Cadiz valleys, is taken to represent the drowned mouth of an integrated ancestral Colorado system.
- Progressive lowering of the Amargosa headwaters, beginning when late Miocene rifting initiated [[Death Valley]] about 8 Ma, is proposed to have defeated the Amargosa paleoriver. Pliocene disruption of the pre-existing drainage may have triggered northward headward erosion and the creation of the modern Colorado River and Grand Canyon.

**6. Gila paleoriver**
- The Gila trough formed mainly by early Miocene rifting, but thickness relations suggest an earlier origin as a simple downwarp (Eberly and Stanley, 1978). The stream is antecedent to Basin and Range faults of about 13 Ma, which suggests a pre-late Miocene origin.
- Gila River gravels underlie the 3 Ma Sentinel Basalt, implying a well-integrated paleodrainage by the early Pliocene.

**7. Provenance of exotic clasts**
- Orthoquartzite clasts from a fluvial Eocene conglomerate near Los Angeles were tentatively matched with Tapeats Sandstone and equivalents. The author argues they came from miogeoclinal rocks in the Mojave Desert, not from the cratonal rocks of the lower Colorado basin.
- Clasts that could have come from an ancestral Grand Canyon are excluded because Blue Mountain gravels lie below 15 Ma volcanic rocks, indicating erosion had only reached the Supai Formation before the Middle Miocene.

**8. Regional consistency and base-level changes**
- Paleomagnetic data document a mid-Oligocene unconformity in the Sespe Formation, probably of eustatic origin (Prothero et al., 1996). If the paleoriver drained to the Pacific at that time, this base-level drop may explain the upper Paleogene regional unconformity in Arizona.
- The resumption of volcanism in southwestern Arizona about 35 Ma is proposed to explain calc-alkaline volcanic clasts in Eocene Sespe conglomerates. Cenozoic volcanism in the Los Angeles basin began only about 16 Ma.

**9. Conclusions**
- The [[Los Angeles Basin]] is argued to hold an "invaluable sedimentary record" of Cenozoic tectonic unroofing in the Basin and Range region.
- Abrupt upsection appearance of far-traveled gravels overlapping local fanglomerate may create the illusion of the initial deposits of a throughgoing drainage, so the proposed model depends on basin-axis deposits that are mostly buried in the subsurface.
- The paper states that how much sediment resulted from lava-dam mechanisms versus base-level variations is unknown.
