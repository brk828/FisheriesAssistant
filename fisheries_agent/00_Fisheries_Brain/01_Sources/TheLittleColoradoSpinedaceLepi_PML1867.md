# Little Colorado Spinedace (Lepidomeda vittata) in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review covers the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), a minnow endemic to the upper [[Little Colorado River]] system in Arizona. The species declined sharply between the late 1930s and early 1960s, was restricted to a single stream system ([[East Clear Creek]]), and was considered endangered. Between 1963 and 1966 it showed marked reproductive success and re-invaded most of its former range. The paper adds new localities, abundance data, and information on breeding, fecundity, and food habits. It also flags introduced fishes, especially the [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Red Shiner]], as a serious threat.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The species belongs to the tribe [[Plagopterini]], a group of spiny-rayed minnows endemic to the lower and middle [[Colorado River]] basin of the southwestern United States.
- **Range:** It is restricted to north-flowing tributaries of the upper Little Colorado River in Arizona. It may once have occupied a small part of the basin in New Mexico. Few museum specimens exist. [[Miller and Hubbs]] (1960) plotted three localities, most literature records dated from the mid-1800s, and they restricted the type locality to the Little Colorado River between the mouth of the Zuni River and Sierra Blanca (White Mountain), based on the itinerary of the [[Wheeler Survey]].
- **Survey history:** [[Robert R. Miller]] re-surveyed known sites in 1959–1962 and sampled 17 additional sites. The fish was found only in East Clear Creek, Coconino County. This decline prompted Miller and others to consider the species endangered.
- **Study setting:** Most ecological work by [[Minckley]] and [[Carufel]] was done in East Clear Creek. Collections were also made in headwater creeks, [[Silver Creek]], and the Little Colorado mainstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preference**
- The fish is characteristic of clear, flowing pools of medium depth over fine gravel. It avoids the deepest, heavily shaded pools and shallow open areas.
- Highest concentrations occur in relatively open pools with boulders or undercut banks for cover. There it moves constantly and swiftly in mid-water, rising to the surface for insects and floating debris.
- It is usually solitary but sometimes forms groups of 3 to 8 individuals. When disturbed, it quickly forms aggregated schools. When pursued, it retreats to cover or the deepest water.
- During high discharge and turbidity (snowmelt and summer rain), large adults move to the upstream ends of pools, and young-of-the-year are most abundant on turbulent riffles 10 to 20 centimeters deep.
- During floods, the fish moves laterally to the current. The largest single three-meter seine haul was 38 individuals from a streamside smartweed (*Polygonum*) bed flooded by about 40 centimeters of water.
- Winter collections in December 1964 failed. The fish apparently moved into the deepest pools (2 to 3 meters) under partial ice cover.

**Headwater refugia**
- By autumn 1964 the species was found in brooks at the crest of the [[Mogollon Rim]], above 2,200 meters, where winters include deep snow and sub-zero temperatures. Many of these brooks are less than one meter wide and very shallow, and most are spring-fed.
- In small headwater brooks the fish is usually found at bends and near obstructions in the deepest water. In rocky streams it concentrates in pools that persist through droughts.
- In permanent headwater streams with heavy sedge vegetation and organic silt bottoms, it occurs throughout the channel, both in dense vegetation and in open reaches.
- These headwater populations are thought to persist through summer drought and winter cold, then spread to repopulate drainages when conditions improve. In [[Cienega Canyon]], for example, it appeared in 1966.
- The [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), another headwater species, does not reach as far upstream in East Clear Creek as *L. vittata* does.

**Dispersal constraints**
- In 1963 the fish was abundant only at the "96" crossing in East Clear Creek. It persisted in intermittent pools at least one kilometer upstream, but most upper channels were dry.
- Minckley walked from Potato Lake to near [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] in autumn 1963. He found no water in the channel between Potato Lake and Jones Crossing, and the fish occurred only below Miller Canyon.
- The route the fish would have needed to take was at least six kilometers, and probably 12 kilometers counting its swimming path. It included at least two falls over one meter high, so the authors saw little or no chance that it invaded upstream from East Clear Creek.

**Introduced species and threats**
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) has largely displaced the [[Gila River Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] in the Gila basin. It is established in the Little Colorado system only in [[Concho Reservoir]], and its spread should be prevented.
- [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucus*) is present in Little Colorado streams. It was especially abundant in Upper [[Chevelon Creek]], resisted eradication in 1965, and is being illegally spread by anglers as bait. No *L. vittata* were taken from the Chevelon drainage before, during, or after chemical treatment, even though suitable habitat was present. Golden shiners have been in the basin since the late 1950s.
- The species is rare or absent where exotic fishes other than trout are present, and it has not been taken with any introduced cyprinid.
- Historical decline also involved pollution and fish poisoning. Toxaphene released from [[Lyman Reservoir]] in 1951 is one example. The authors note that pollution may be declining and that piscicide use is now more controlled.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- The species declined from the late 1930s to the early 1960s, then "enjoyed marked reproductive success" and re-invaded most of its former range between 1963 and 1966.
- In 1964 the fish appeared up and downstream from the 96 crossing and was later found in headwater brooks, in Silver Creek, and in the Little Colorado mainstream.
- Specimens from the Little Colorado mainstream near Woodruff (spring 1965) included 28 individuals (ASU 1777), 48 to 80 mm standard length. Seven specimens from Springerville (1966; CSU 22-3-1) were 62 to 69 mm standard length.
- Collecting failures occurred in pools where the fish had previously been abundant. At the 96 crossing, deep pools may provide cover where the fish evade capture.

**Reproduction**
- Breeding consists of a major spawning period in early summer (probably June), followed by sporadic spawning in summer and autumn.
- Young under 20 mm standard length were taken from July through November. Miller (1964) recorded post-larvae as small as 11.3 mm in July.
- Ovary and testis development varied among collections. Females collected in May 1965 had maturing eggs, and several males were tuberculate. Spent or resting ovaries appeared in later collections. Spawning behavior was observed only as a possible male-female pursuit, and no actual spawning was seen.
- The authors infer that the species probably spawns more than once a year, based on the high proportion of mature males at all times and the mix of developmental stages.
- Sexual dimorphism is minimal, apart from male tuberculation.

**Food habits**
- Stomachs of 23 specimens were examined. Twenty contained food, and three were empty or contained unidentified material.
- The diet was dominated by adult aquatic insects and terrestrial insects. Aquatic larvae were sometimes abundant. Chironomid (midge) larvae predominated in fish from silt-bottomed habitats in the Little Colorado mainstream.
- Other items included Trichoptera, Ephemeroptera, Odonata, Coleoptera, Hymenoptera, Hemiptera, and Araneae. Unattached filamentous algae were common, and sand was found in 3 of the 20 stomachs.
- Feeding is consistent with the species' mid-water, active habits.

**Conservation conclusions**
- The species should be considered endangered. Its restricted range, its decline, and its sensitivity to introduced cyprinids make it vulnerable.
- Priorities include preventing the spread of the red and golden shiners, controlling exotic fish introductions, and protecting headwater refugia.
