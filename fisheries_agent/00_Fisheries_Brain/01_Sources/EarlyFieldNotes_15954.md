# Historical Fish Survey Data from Colorado River System (1972-1982)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document comprises field notes from fish surveys conducted in the [[Colorado River]] system, primarily within [[Grand Canyon]] and surrounding areas, spanning 1972-1982. The surveys document native and exotic fish species distributions during a critical period for [[Colorado River Basin]] ichthyofauna. Collections were made by G.H. Clemmer and collaborators (Suttkus, Jones, Deacon, and others) using seine nets at numerous locations along the mainstem Colorado River and tributaries including [[Little Colorado River]], [[Shinumo Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]], and others. The notes provide baseline data on species presence, abundance, habitat conditions, and water quality parameters. Special attention is given to [[Gila cypha]] (Humpback Chub), an endangered native cyprinid, and the spread of exotic species including [[Cyprinus carpio]] (Common Carp) and [[Ictalurus punctatus]] (Channel Catfish).

## Background & Description

The field notes represent systematic ichthyological surveys conducted across multiple years (1972-1982) in the [[Colorado River]] drainage system. Survey methodology consistently employed seine nets (10-50 feet length) deployed in various habitats including backwaters, pools, creek mouths, and along shorelines. Each collection record documents:

- **Location data**: River mile markers, tributary names, county (primarily [[Coconino County]] and [[Mohave County]])
- **Physical parameters**: Water temperature (typically 8-32°C), air temperature, flow conditions, width measurements
- **Habitat characteristics**: Bottom substrate (sand, silt, rubble, boulders, bedrock), vegetation (Tamarisk, Typha, Phragmites), current speed
- **Collection details**: Date, collectors, preservation method (10% formalin), collection numbers (GHE series, ROS series)

The surveys targeted both native species of conservation concern and documented the presence and spread of non-native species. Native species of particular interest included [[Gila cypha]] (Humpback Chub), [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (Razorback Sucker), [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (Flannelmouth Sucker), [[Pantosteus discobolus]] (Bluehead Sucker), and [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (Speckled Dace). Exotic species frequently encountered included [[Cyprinus carpio]], [[Salmo gairdneri]] (Rainbow Trout), [[Ictalurus punctatus]], [[Pimephales promelas]] (Fathead Minnow), and [[Fundulus zebrinus]] (Plains Killifish).

Water conditions varied considerably: "white clear" to "very turbid," with turbidity often noted after flooding events. The [[Little Colorado River]] was frequently described as turbid, particularly after flash floods. Substrate composition ranged from fine silt and mud in backwaters to boulders and bedrock in rapids and tributary mouths.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The surveys document fish communities during a transitional period for the [[Colorado River]] ecosystem, following construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (completed 1963) and during ongoing water management changes. Key ecological observations include:

**Habitat Utilization**: Native suckers ([[Catostomus latipinnis]], [[Pantosteus discobolus]]) were commonly found in pools and along rubble/boulder substrates. [[Gila cypha]] was documented at multiple locations, particularly near the mouth of [[Little Colorado River]] (River Mile 61), in [[Shinumo Creek]] area, and other tributary confluences. The species was observed in pools 3-9 feet deep, often associated with boulder and bedrock substrates.

**Spawning Observations**: Several entries document reproductive activity. At [[Lees Ferry]] (17 June 1982), observers noted suckers "breaking water" and "covering nests" with spawning behavior observed in both [[Colorado River]] and [[Paria River]]. [[Catostomus latipinnis]] specimens showed tuberculation and swollen vents indicating spawning condition. At [[Kanab Creek]] (12 June 1980), extensive spawning activity was documented with adult carp observed, eggs found under rocks, and young-of-year present.

**Temperature Regimes**: Water temperatures varied seasonally and spatially. Mainstem [[Colorado River]] temperatures ranged 8-22°C, while tributaries showed wider variation (7-32°C). The [[Little Colorado River]] consistently showed warmer temperatures than the mainstem Colorado, creating thermal refugia important for native species.

**Exotic Species Impacts**: [[Cyprinus carpio]] was ubiquitous throughout the system, frequently noted as "discarded" after capture, indicating high abundance. The species was observed spawning at multiple locations. [[Salmo gairdneri]] (Rainbow Trout) dominated cold-water reaches below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Other exotics including [[Ictalurus punctatus]], [[Pimephales promelas]], [[Fundulus zebrinus]], [[Dorosoma petenense]], [[Notropis lutrensis]], [[Gambusia affinis]], and [[Micropterus salmoides]] were documented at various locations, particularly in [[Lake Mead]] and lower river reaches.

**Vegetation and Riparian Conditions**: Extensive establishment of [[Tamarisk]] (Tamarix) was noted throughout the system, along with native vegetation including willows, Apache plume, and Chilopsis. Aquatic vegetation included Typha, Potamogeton, and thick algal mats in some locations.

**Flow and Turbidity**: The surveys captured various flow conditions from low flows to high water events. Flash flooding in tributaries (e.g., [[Havasu Creek]], July 1972) created very turbid conditions. The [[Little Colorado River]] was consistently described as more turbid than the mainstem Colorado.

## Key Findings

1. **Gila cypha Distribution**: [[Gila cypha]] (Humpback Chub) was documented at multiple locations during 1976-1982 surveys, with notable collections at:
   - Mouth of [[Little Colorado River]] (River Mile 61): Multiple specimens 5-10 inches, observed in deep pools (3-9 feet) around boulders
   - [[Shinumo Creek]] area (River Mile 108): One specimen 9.5 inches released, photographed
   - [[Colorado River]] at River Mile 65: Specimens collected in turbid conditions
   - The species was described as occurring in pools with boulder/bedrock substrate, often in areas with moderate to fast current

2. **Native Sucker Populations**: [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (Flannelmouth Sucker) and [[Pantosteus discobolus]] (Bluehead Sucker) were widespread and abundant throughout the survey area. Spawning activity documented at [[Lees Ferry]] and [[Kanab Creek]]. Young-of-year and multiple age classes present, indicating successful reproduction.

3. **Xyrauchen texanus Status**: [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (Razorback Sucker) documented in only one collection (April 1973, Paria River at Lees Ferry): "1 specimen - deferred to Suttkus" and noted as a hybrid with [[Catostomus]]. This represents extremely limited occurrence of this endangered species.

4. **Exotic Species Establishment**: Non-native species were pervasive throughout the system:
   - [[Cyprinus carpio]]: Present at virtually all mainstem locations, often in high numbers, spawning documented
   - [[Salmo gairdneri]]: Dominated cold-water reaches, particularly below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]
   - [[Ictalurus punctatus]]: Common in mainstem and lower tributaries
   - [[Pimephales promelas]] and [[Fundulus zebrinus]]: Abundant in backwaters and low-velocity habitats
   - [[Notropis lutrensis]], [[Dorosoma petenense]], [[Gambusia affinis]], [[Micropterus salmoides]]: Present in [[Lake Mead]] and lower river sections

5. **Little Colorado River Importance**: The [[Little Colorado River]] confluence area (River Mile 61) emerged as critical habitat for [[Gila cypha]]. Multiple surveys documented chubs in this area, with observations of fish in pools, around boulders, and in mixing zones where Little Colorado water met Colorado River water. The warmer, more turbid water of the Little Colorado appeared to provide important habitat.

6. **Tributary Habitats**: Tributary streams ([[Shinumo Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Kanab Creek]], etc.) supported diverse fish communities including native [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (Speckled Dace), [[Pantosteus discobolus]], and introduced [[Salmo gairdneri]]. These tributaries provided spawning and rearing habitat.

7. **Seasonal and Flow Variations**: Collections captured seasonal variation in species composition and abundance. High flow events and turbidity influenced catch success. Spring surveys (April-June) documented spawning activity in multiple species.

8. **Historical Baseline**: These 1972-1982 data provide crucial baseline information on species distributions and relative abundances prior to intensive [[Gila cypha]] recovery efforts and before additional water management changes. The notes document a period when native species were still present but under increasing pressure from exotic species and altered flow regimes.

9. **Collection Effort**: Surveys employed consistent methodology (seine nets 10-50 feet) across diverse habitats. Sample sizes varied from single specimens to hundreds of individuals. Many large [[Cyprinus carpio]] and some [[Salmo gairdneri]] were released or discarded, indicating selective retention focused on native species and smaller specimens.

10. **Conservation Context**: The cover letter (28 February 1990) from Nevada Natural Heritage Program to Dr. W.L. Minckley references using these early field notes to "help document the spread of exotics" and appeals to "maintain the integrity of the lower Little Colorado for the sake of G. cypha," underscoring the conservation significance of both the data and the [[Little Colorado River]] habitat.
