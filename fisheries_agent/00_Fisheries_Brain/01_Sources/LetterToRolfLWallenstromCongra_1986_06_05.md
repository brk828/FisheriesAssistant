## Executive Summary
This document is not a primary scientific/morphological study but a set of administrative letters and symposium announcements from the [[Desert Fishes Council]], a conservation-focused organization dedicated to the preservation of desert aquatic ecosystems in North America. The correspondence, authored by Executive Secretary E. P. Pister, congratulates Rolf L. Wallenstrom on his appointment as U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Regional Director and details logistics for the Council's Eighteenth Annual Symposium (November 20–22, 1986, St. George, Utah). While lacking direct morphological or physiological data, the text provides valuable context on the conservation politics, institutional collaborations, and biopolitical challenges surrounding the preservation of desert fish species, particularly in the [[Virgin River]] watershed.

## Key Findings
- The **[[Desert Fishes Council]]**, founded to preserve desert fish ecosystems, had approximately **350 members** as of 1986, including federal/state resource managers, academics, and graduate students.
- The organization has held annual symposia since at least **1972** (Death Valley meeting), reflecting a long-term institutional commitment to desert fish conservation science and policy.
- Leadership transition: **Gail Kobetich** (head of the Sacramento Endangered Species Office) assumed the chairmanship of the Council in November 1986, succeeding prior leadership.
- The **Eighteenth Annual Symposium** (1986) was structured to balance **scientific reporting** and **political advocacy**, reflecting the dual mandate of the Council: disseminating research findings while actively influencing conservation policy.
- Agency reporting sessions (chaired by **Bill Kepner**) were formalized to consolidate interagency communication on desert fish conservation activities, capped at 10–15 minutes per agency to improve efficiency.
- International collaboration was emphasized, with explicit efforts to support Mexican colleagues' participation amid economic hardship (Mexican peso devaluation from ~400 to ~800 per USD between 1985–1986), underscoring the transboundary nature of desert fish conservation.
- A dedicated **field trip to the Virgin River** was included in the symposium agenda, indicating this system's importance as a conservation case study for native desert fish species.
- The Council operated as a **non-profit organization**, primarily funded through membership dues (Student: $5; Regular: $10; Sustaining: $25), which supported publication of proceedings (e.g., the **1984–86 Proceedings**) and symposium logistics.
- Symbolic and educational materials (certificates designed by **Harold Tyus**, pupfish pins) were used to reinforce member identity and public engagement with desert fish conservation causes.
