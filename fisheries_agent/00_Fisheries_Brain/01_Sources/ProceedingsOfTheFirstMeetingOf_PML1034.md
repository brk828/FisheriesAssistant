# Proceedings of the First Meeting of Cuatrociénegas Researchers (2004)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This volume is the proceedings of the first meeting of researchers working in the [[Cuatrociénegas Valley]], Coahuila, Mexico, held 13–14 August 2004 and published online and in print on 30 June 2005. The meeting aimed to improve interactions among researchers, and between researchers, managers (government, NGO and private) and the local community. Papers cover hydrogeology, water chemistry, stromatolites and microbes, genetics of endemic fishes and snails, fish and plant invasive species, vertebrates, archaeology, human ecology and conservation policy. A roundtable closed the meeting with recommendations on water management, invasive species control, tourism regulation, a research station and a proposed biosphere reserve.

## Background & Description

The meeting was organized mainly by [[Dean A. Hendrickson]] of the Texas Natural History Collection, University of Texas at Austin, with [[Francisco García de León]] and [[Susana Moncada]] and staff of the Área Protegida de Flora y Fauna Cuatrociénegas. Sponsors included CONANP, the Instituto Coahuilense de Ecología, The Nature Conservancy, the Desert Fishes Council, the [[Global Environment Facility]], and USAID. The volume is bilingual (Spanish/English) and is hosted on the Desert Fishes Council website. The valley was declared a federal Área de Protección de Flora y Fauna on 7 November 1994.

The conference program was organized into thematic sessions: Stromatolites, Hydrogeology, Ichthyology, Genetics, Biology of Diverse Organisms, Archaeology and History, Sociology, Geographic Information Systems, and a poster session, followed by a field visit to the protected area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The valley is a desert-spring ecosystem with extreme variation in temperature, salinity and chemistry over small spatial scales. The document reports that about 510 water bodies (springs, pozas, lagunas, wells) are estimated in the valley, about 85% of them around the Sierra San Marcos. Of the valley's 84,347 ha, 59% is private property and 41% ejido land. Wetlands cover roughly 210 ha.

Key threats repeatedly identified include:
- Invasive species: [[Tilapia]], the [[Jewel Fish]] (*Hemichromis guttatus*), red crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*), common carp, [[Arundo Donax]] (giant reed), [[Tamarix]] (salt cedar), water lily and the snail *Thiara tuberculata*.
- Water extraction for irrigation, canal construction and declining pozas.
- Tourism pressure, vandalism and solid waste at pozas.
- Land clearing of mesquite and habitat degradation.

The geology section describes a history from the Late Jurassic marine sediments through Cretaceous carbonate platforms and Laramide folding (the Coahuila Folded Belt), with a Tertiary left-lateral fault system isolating the valley. Groundwater is interpreted as partly open and partly closed flow, with recharge from mountain fractures.

## Key Findings

### Ichthyology and Native Fishes
- **Exotic fish eradication (since 1999):** in Churince, San José del Anteojo and Juan Santos, 43,750 individuals of the jewel fish were removed in four years (Churince figure); 18,041 jewel fish were eradicated at Anteojo; 3,159 tilapia were extracted; 11,564 water lily plants were removed; Thiara removal was abandoned after 11,564 individuals were taken in one effort, given its wide distribution.
- **Distribution survey:** 100 localities sampled for fish taxonomy and water chemistry. Species are irregularly distributed; some pozas hold only one or two species, others up to 5–7. Two forms of *Gambusia marshi* and an unidentified *Cyprinodon* form may represent new species or a hybrid.
- **Trophic polymorphism in [[Herichthys minckleyi]]:** an intermediate morph is more abundant than previously reported; random collections contained more than three times as many intermediates. Of 5,060 fish examined, 566 came from 1960–1980 and 494 from 1998–2001. Morph abundance varied spatially by up to 66% and temporally, with intermediates and molariforms increasing up to 19% and 25% and papilliforms decreasing up to 39%.
- **Genetics of H. minckleyi:** two microsatellite loci gave 26 and 10 alleles in 260 individuals; observed heterozygosity was 0.86 and 0.68. Differentiation between Mojarral Este and Oeste was low but significant (FST = 0.051).
- **Hybridization in [[Cyprinodon]]:** hybridization between *C. bifasciatus* and *C. atrorus* is probably very old, with complete replacement of *C. bifasciatus* mitochondrial DNA; reciprocal transplants showed *C. bifasciatus* has a significantly narrower environmental tolerance.
- **Black bass:** 76 *Micropterus* sampled at five localities; introduced [[Florida Bass]] (*M. floridanus*) affects native *M. salmoides*, but pure native populations still exist, prompting a proposed genetic sanctuary.
- **Environmental variance:** in amphipod (*Hyalella azteca*) experiments, low C:P food and stable temperature each increased growth (P < 0.001); amphipods from Churince Spring and Laguna Intermedia did not differ (P = 0.58).

### Microbiology and Stromatolites
- Stromatolites: over a dozen distinct biogenic carbonate forms occur in the basin. Several have been lost or deteriorated from hydrological modifications over recent decades.
- Marine-origin microbes: about 50% of sampled environmental taxa were of marine origin, suggesting entrapped marine pore water from Cretaceous formations roughly 140 million years old.
- N2 fixation in mats and stromatolites was significantly reduced when oxygenic photosynthesis was inhibited, indicating a role for [[Cyanobacteria]] (*Calothrix*); mats fixed more N2 than stromatolites.
- Phage ecology: over 65% of uncultured viral sequences were novel; phage-resistance costs in *E. coli* are modulated by temperature.
- Phosphorus experiments on stromatolites and snail herbivores suggest a stoichiometric "knife-edge" with an optimal C:P ratio for snail growth.

### Hydrogeology and Geology
- Five or more distinct flow paths are proposed (Churince, Garabatal–Becerra–Río Mesquites, Tío Cándido–Hundidos, Santa Tecla, Anteojo).
- Calcium and sulfate dominate water chemistry; evaporation controls salinity.
- Valle del Hundido structures are interpreted as hydraulically connected to Cuatrociénegas through thrust and detachment systems.

### Flora, Fauna and Invasive Plants
- **Arundo donax:** new stands discovered in the Río Mesquites and along canals; the authors judge that control will be costly and unlikely to succeed without massive investment. Arundo is likely responsible for the decline of the endemic darter *Etheostoma segrex*, found at a single known locality.
- **Tamarix biological control:** *Diorhabda* beetles from China and Kazakhstan caused 95–98% defoliation within 300–750 m radius, within three years at five sites north of 38° N.
- **Mammals:** 46 species recorded; *Erethizon dorsatum* is considered exceptional and possibly extirpated.
- **Birds:** 158 species reported; risks include mesquite clearing, water extraction and parasitic cowbirds.
- **Cacti:** 10 threatened species; three (*Ariocarpus fissuratus*, *Astrophytum capricorne*, *Echinomastus mariposensis*) show low densities and reduced distribution.
- **Coahuilan box turtle** (*Terrapene coahuila*): peripheral populations have larger carapace length; core and peripheral populations both should be conserved.
- **Bighorn sheep:** historic records in Sierra de la Madera, Sierra La Fragua and Sierra San Marcos y Pino; extirpated by livestock and unregulated hunting.

### Archaeology, Society and Policy
- Archaeological looting since the early 20th century threatens the record of hunter-gatherer groups active c. 8000 B.C.–A.D. 1600.
- The protected area was built up with support from UANL and NGOs; monitoring of tourism, water and waste is identified as a need.
- **Roundtable recommendations:** declare archaeological monuments zones; oppose a municipal landfill in a sensitive site; control exotic species, tourism and vandalism; send letters to SEMARNAT and CNA; establish a water conservation veda; build a water balance model; produce a LIDAR-based DEM of the valley; create a research station (offered by UANL land); expand a biosphere reserve to include surrounding sierras; publish a bibliography (updated from Minckley 1984, nearly 900 citations) on the web.
