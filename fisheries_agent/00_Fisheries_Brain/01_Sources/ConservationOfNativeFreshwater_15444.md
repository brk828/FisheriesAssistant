# Conservation of Native Freshwater Fishes in California: A Review and Management Framework

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #freshwater-ecology #endangered-species #habitat-management

## Executive Summary

This document consists of correspondence from Dr. Alain J. Crivelli (Scientific Director, Camargue, France) to Dr. W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University) dated March 18, 1993, along with an attached manuscript by Peter B. Moyle reviewing the conservation status of California's native freshwater fish fauna. The manuscript reveals that 63% of California's 115 native fish taxa are extinct or in danger of extinction, representing one of the most severe declines of aquatic fauna globally in regions with [[Mediterranean Climate]]. The review proposes a comprehensive conservation framework centered on [[Aquatic Diversity Management Areas]] (ADMAs) to systematically protect remaining aquatic biodiversity. The correspondence indicates this manuscript was under peer review, with comments from P. Moyle and P. Maitland enclosed for revision.

## Background & Description

California's native fish fauna comprises 115 taxa (63 species further divided into subspecies and distinct spawning runs), representing relatively low diversity given the state's large area (406,000 km²). The fauna falls into three ecological groups: (1) diadromous fishes and stream-resident derivatives (including [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]], [[Steelhead Trout]]), (2) large, long-lived freshwater dispersant fishes, mostly [[Cyprinidae]] (including [[Thicktail Chub]], [[Sacramento Splittail]], various [[Tui Chub]] subspecies), and (3) small freshwater dispersant fishes in isolated habitats such as desert springs (including various [[Pupfish]] species). This assemblage more closely resembles European fish faunas than those of eastern North America.

The state's Mediterranean climate creates harsh conditions: 80% of rainfall occurs November-March, with May-September typically rainless. Rainfall is highly variable year-to-year, with extended droughts common (the 1986-1992 drought lasted six years). These natural extremes are exacerbated by fundamental water allocation conflicts: most precipitation occurs in northern California and at high elevations, while most human water demand is in southern California at low elevations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

California's aquatic ecosystems have been profoundly altered since European settlement began in 1769. Major impacts include:

**Historical alterations (1850-1900)**: Hydraulic mining deposited massive sediment loads, raising river beds 5-7 meters and eliminating [[Chinook Salmon]] runs. Lowland drainage projects eliminated [[Lake Tulare]], the state's largest lake, driving [[Thicktail Chub]] and [[Sacramento Perch]] to extinction or extirpation from native range. Unregulated commercial fisheries rapidly depleted salmon populations (peak catch of 600,000 fish in 1882, declining ever since). Introduction of 51 non-native fish species, including [[Striped Bass]], [[American Shad]], and [[Common Carp]], which became dominant in altered habitats.

**Modern water development (1941-1982)**: Construction of dams and reservoirs on every major stream, with thousands of miles of aqueducts. The [[Central Valley Project]] and [[State Water Project]] fundamentally altered flow regimes. Friant Dam closure in 1945 deliberately extirpated spring-run chinook salmon from the [[San Joaquin River]] (50,000 fish in 1946; last fish attempted migration in 1950). Agriculture consumes 85% of state water, enabling crops like cotton and rice in desert areas.

**Ongoing degradation**: Mechanized logging, expanding agriculture, and intensive livestock grazing have devastated drainage basins. Poor land use practices continue to degrade spawning streams. Introduced species have replaced native fishes in many habitats. The 1986-1992 drought pushed multiple species toward extinction, including drying [[Goose Lake]] completely in 1992.

## Key Findings

**Current conservation status (1992 assessment)**:
- 7% (8 taxa) extinct, including [[High Rock Spring Tui Chub]] (disappeared 1989 after cichlid introduction)
- 13% (15 taxa) formally listed as threatened or endangered under state or federal law
- 23% (27 taxa) qualify for formal endangered listing
- 19% (22 taxa) in decline or may qualify for listing if trends continue
- 37% (41 taxa) appear secure
- Overall: 43% of native taxa are extinct or endangered, up from 24% in 1989

**Species in critical decline**:
- [[Coho Salmon]]: Once present in ~540 coastal streams with ~200,000 spawners annually; now <5,000 wild spawners statewide, many populations <25 fish
- [[Spring-run Chinook Salmon]]: Declined from over 1 million spawners to ~2,500 fish in four populations
- [[Winter-run Chinook Salmon]]: Newly listed as endangered
- [[Delta Smelt]] and [[Longfin Smelt]]: Petitioned for listing by American Fisheries Society
- [[Green Sturgeon]]: Newly recognized as qualifying for listing
- Four endemic [[Goose Lake]] subspecies: Pushed to threatened status when lake dried in 1992

**Six primary causes of decline identified**:
1. Natural factors (drought, temperature extremes)
2. Water development (dams, diversions, flow regulation)
3. Land use practices (logging, agriculture, grazing causing habitat degradation)
4. Introduced species (51 established non-native fish species)
5. Overfishing (historical commercial harvest, ongoing impacts)
6. Pollution and water quality degradation

**Conservation framework proposed**: The manuscript presents a two-component strategy:

**Component 1 - Endangered Species Protection**: Recommends listing species in five regional clusters representing distinct ecosystems:
1. Central California: [[Delta Smelt]], [[Longfin Smelt]], [[Sacramento Splittail]], spring-run chinook, [[Green Sturgeon]], [[Red Hills Roach]]
2. North Coast: coho, [[Chum Salmon]], [[Pink Salmon]], spring-run chinook, summer steelhead, green sturgeon, longfin smelt
3. Interior drainages: [[Eagle Lake Rainbow Trout]], [[McCloud Redband Trout]], [[Goose Lake Redband Trout]], [[Goose Lake Sucker]], [[Goose Lake Tui Chub]], [[Goose Lake Lamprey]], [[Cowhead Lake Tui Chub]]
4. Desert basins: [[Shoshone Pupfish]], [[Saratoga Springs Pupfish]], [[Salt Creek Pupfish]], [[Amargosa Pupfish]], [[Amargosa Canyon Speckled Dace]], [[Owens Speckled Dace]]
5. Southern California: southern steelhead, [[Santa Ana Sucker]], [[Santa Ana Speckled Dace]], [[Tidewater Goby]], [[Shay Creek Stickleback]]

**Component 2 - Aquatic Diversity Management Areas (ADMAs)**: A systematic approach to habitat protection with six design principles:
1. Must contain necessary resources and habitats for species persistence
2. Must be large enough to maintain natural species diversity
3. Must be protected from edge and external threats
4. Should have interior habitat redundancy to buffer localized extinctions
5. Should be paired with at least one other ADMA with similar species but geographically distant
6. Should support populations large enough to avoid demographic and genetic stochasticity

**ADMA classification system** (six classes from best to worst):
- Class 1: Waters most closely resembling unaltered conditions, complete native biota, natural protection (extremely rare)
- Class 2: Modified but restorable to Class 1, mainly native organisms (will form backbone of ADMA system)
- Class 3: Significantly altered, introduced species integral, but refuges for some native biota
- Classes 4-6: Artificial preserves, sources of species for restoration only

**Implementation framework**:
- Habitat classification system with >160 categories (expandable) focusing on regions of endemism
- Ideally, every habitat type protected in two or more ADMAs
- One-page catalog entries for each potential ADMA documenting boundaries, native fishes, status, ownership, protection level, management needs, acquisition priority
- Long-term biological survey program with five goals: comprehensive database development, systematic surveys for new ADMAs, regular monitoring of selected waters, complete taxonomic/genetic studies of all native fishes (achievable in 10-15 years), surveys of other aquatic organisms especially in fishless habitats

**Management approach**:
- Each ADMA assigned to monitoring/management agency
- Interagency committee establishes general philosophy and guidelines
- Local management responsibility essential
- Voluntary citizen management/watchdog groups or individual ADMA Advocates
- Alternative: paid professional stream/lake keepers

**Special considerations for large rivers**: [[Colorado River]], [[Sacramento River]], [[San Joaquin River]], and [[Klamath River]] too modified for traditional ADMA approach. Strategy focuses on managing flows and conditions to enhance native fish populations, particularly during spawning and larval rearing periods. Major re-evaluation of Sacramento River and estuary flow regime underway due to severe biotic decline.

**Legal tools available but insufficient**:
- Federal [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA): Most powerful environmental legislation globally, but slow listing process, underfunded agencies, long backlog
- California endangered species law: Based on federal ESA but with more "take" provisions, requires approval by politically appointed Fish and Game Commission
- California Department of Fish and Game Code Section 5937: Requires viable fish populations below dams
- [[Public Trust Doctrine]]: Government must manage public resources (rivers, fish, wildlife) for greatest public good; successfully applied in [[Mono Lake]] preservation

**Broader implications**: The poor state of California's fish fauna indicates many other endemic aquatic organisms (invertebrates, amphibians, plants) are similarly threatened. Protecting fishes will help protect overall aquatic biodiversity. The ADMA approach provides a systematic framework applicable beyond California to other Mediterranean-climate regions experiencing similar declines. Success depends on overcoming special interests opposing conservation efforts and achieving high local-level involvement alongside government framework and expert guidance.
