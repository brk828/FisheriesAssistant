# Natural Flow Regimes, Nonnative Fishes and Native Fish Persistence in Arid-Land River Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 19-year study (1988–2006) examined how a natural flow regime and [[Nonnative Fishes]] interact to shape [[Native Fish Assemblages]] in the relatively unaltered [[Upper Gila River Drainage]] of southwest [[New Mexico]], USA. Six permanent sites were sampled annually from October to early November. The authors found that native fish assemblages did not decline uniformly. Assemblages at the [[Tularosa River]] and [[San Francisco River]] sites persisted largely intact, and the mainstem [[Gila River]] site showed no native declines. In contrast, the three [[Gila Forks Sites]] (East, Middle and West forks) lost native species, with nonnative predators implicated. The authors conclude that a natural flow regime is essential but not sufficient for native fish conservation. Control of nonnative predators and maintenance of habitat connectivity are equally necessary.

## Background & Description

Growing human demand for water in arid regions has altered most river systems and driven declines of native fishes worldwide. Restoring or maintaining natural flow regimes is widely proposed as a remedy, because native fauna evolved under such conditions. The authors note that a natural flow regime may also make conditions less suitable for establishment of nonnative organisms. However, the study questions whether flow regime alone is enough. It sought to separate environmental drivers from the effects of nonnative fishes, a problem that is difficult to resolve in systems where both are present.

The study had three stated objectives:

1. Test for long-term directional change in fish assemblage structure across the six sites.
2. Characterize the response of native and nonnative fishes to attributes of a natural flow regime.
3. Characterize the effect of nonnative fishes on the persistence of native assemblages.

The authors predicted that native assemblage structure would track annual flow variation, that native assemblages would not change appreciably in a natural flow setting despite nonnative pressure, and that native–nonnative interactions would be most evident in low-flow years.

**Study area:** The six sites ranged from small to medium-sized streams in a landscape with low-impact land use, mostly federal land within the Gila and Aldo Leopold Wildernesses. The Tularosa site (1818 m) was the smallest stream. The San Francisco site (1431 m) lay near Glenwood in a valley used for livestock grazing and irrigated agriculture. The [[Gila Forks Sites]] included the East Fork (1876 m), Middle Fork (1733 m) and West Fork (1737 m). The mainstem Gila site (1359 m) was the most downstream and the most exposed to nonnatives.

**Hydrology:** Discharge was highly variable. Mean daily discharge at the Gila River near Gila gauge ranged from 1 to 8 m³/s (mean 5.17 m³/s). Floods of 50 m³/s or more occurred in 8 of 19 years. Median discharge exceeded the historical median in all but two years between 1988 and 1998, but only in 2001 and 2005 between 1999 and 2006. Comparisons with [[Aravaipa Creek]] and the [[Verde River]] showed similar wet and dry periods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species pool:** Across the six sites the authors recorded seven native and 14 nonnative fish species. Native taxa included [[Longfin Dace]], [[Headwater Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Spikedace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]]. Nonnative taxa included [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]], plus several catfishes and common carp. Most nonnatives were rare, and few were represented by more than a few individuals.

**Sampling:** Seines (3.0 × 1.2 m, 3.2 mm mesh) and backpack electrofishers (12 or 24 V) were used. Crews of three to five people sampled every mesohabitat, working upstream from the downstream terminus of each site. Nonnative fishes were removed and native fishes were enumerated and released. Fewer than 25 individuals of each nonnative species were typically removed per year, which the authors judged unlikely to alter assemblage structure. Discharge at sampling was not associated with fish density across years (P > 0.1).

**Analytical approach:** The authors used multiple methods to reach a consensus. These included Spearman rank correlations for abundance trends, correlation analysis of log-transformed densities, [[Persistence Index]] calculations, [[Mantel Test]]s using Bray-Curtis and Jaccard similarity, [[Principal Components Analysis]] of assemblage structure, [[Redundancy Analysis]] linking assemblages to hydrology, habitat and nonnative densities, and stepwise multiple regression to predict nonnative predator and competitor densities.

## Key Findings

**1. Assemblage change was site-specific.**
- The Tularosa and San Francisco sites had the highest persistence values. Neither Bray-Curtis nor Jaccard indices indicated assemblage-level change at either site.
- At San Francisco, only longfin dace declined in abundance. At Tularosa, loach minnow was possibly extirpated, while speckled dace density increased.
- The [[Gila River Mainstem Site]] showed no assemblage change from 1988 to 2006. All native species present at the start were found in 2006, and none declined in density. This was the only site where no native species declined.

**2. The Gila Forks sites lost native species.**
- At the three forks sites, native species declined almost everywhere. No fork site retained the full native complement present in 1988.
- Exceptions were headwater chub at West Fork, Sonora sucker at Middle and West forks, and desert sucker at West Fork.
- Smallmouth bass increased in density at East and Middle forks. Yellow bullhead was regularly found at Middle Fork.
- Mantel tests showed significant directional change at the forks sites. The strongest time–similarity concordance occurred at Middle Fork.
- Persistence was lower at these sites than at all others.

**3. Nonnative predators were a major factor.**
- Nonnative predator density was positively associated with Sonora sucker and headwater chub, but negatively associated with other native taxa at Middle Fork.
- Densities of nonnative predators were accurately predicted by regression models at five of six sites. The Tularosa site was the only exception.
- Nonnative predators declined at West Fork, which the authors attributed to the cessation of rainbow trout stocking in 1996.
- Contrary to the hypothesis, nonnative predator densities did not rise with consecutive below-median flow years.

**4. Hydrology effects were mixed.**
- Annual discharge was positively related to native abundance at the three forks sites, mainly through high flows in 1991–1993.
- Nonnative densities were generally greatest in low-flow years. Native densities tended to increase with elevated flows.
- The lack of response at East Fork reflected high longfin dace density in 2000, after two consecutive low-flow years.
- Small-bodied native densities at Middle, West and East forks were associated with consecutive years of below-median flows, but the pattern was not significant at all sites.
- No single variable was a strong driver of assemblage structure across all sites.

**5. Life-history traits influenced vulnerability.**
- At Middle Fork, the largest declines were in small-bodied, short-lived (<3 years) natives. Long-lived (>4 years), large-bodied natives persisted only as adults, and their numbers declined over time.
- The authors suggest drought reduced recruitment for species that spawn briefly during snowmelt or storm flows. Combined with stable or increasing predator numbers, this led to elimination of these species at Middle Fork.

**6. Connectivity and conservation implications.**
- The authors describe the basin as fragmented, with limited movement among sites. Loach minnow was apparently extirpated at four of six sites within 10 years or less.
- Irrigation diversion structures protected the San Francisco assemblage from upstream movement of predators, but the isolation may reduce population viability.
- Natural flows alone did not preclude establishment of nonnative predators. Conservation requires active management that removes or excludes problem nonnatives, mitigates barriers to movement, and reverses genetic and demographic effects of isolation through population augmentation.
- Projected aridification in the American Southwest (Seager et al. 2007) would increase drought frequency and intensity, reducing the number of locations where native fishes can persist.
