# Native-Nonnative Fish Interactions in the Lower Salt and Verde Rivers: 1999 Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft progress report (July 1999), prepared by Scott Bryan and Matt Fry of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Research Branch, documents the first field season of a study of native and nonnative fish interactions in the lower [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] in central Arizona. The work covered five objectives: sampling methods, spawning timing and locations, larval rearing habitat, [[Roundtail Chub]] population status, and year-round water temperature. Sucker species dominated native catches and spawning, while [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Red Shiner]] were the most abundant nonnatives. Roundtail chub were tagged in large numbers in the Verde River but were nearly absent from the Salt River. Results are preliminary, and the report states that sample sizes were limited.

## Background & Description

The study was run under the [[Native-Nonnative Fish Interactions]] project. Sampling was conducted by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] with help from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community]]. Flow management was done by [[Salt River Project]], which releases water from [[Stewart Mountain Dam]] (Salt River) and [[Bartlett Dam]] (Verde River).

Study reaches:
- **Lower Salt River:** 21.5 km; three 500-m reaches (Water Users, Blue Point, Phon D. Sutton, Granite Reef Dam, plus other reaches used for specific objectives).
- **Lower Verde River:** 39 km below Bartlett Dam; five 500-m reaches (Bartlett Campground, Needle Rock, Rio Verde Estates, Sycamore Creek, Doka Ranch, Route 87 Bridge, SRP-M Reservation).

Gear used included [[Canoe Electrofishing]], [[Backpack Electrofishing]], [[Trammel Net]]s, [[Minnow Trap]]s, seines, and small-mesh dip nets. Fish were measured, weighed, checked for sexual maturity by gamete extrusion, and examined for health. Abbreviations for reaches, gear and species are listed in the appendix tables.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flows were unusually low.** Dry winter and spring conditions led [[Salt River Project]] to change releases during sampling. Salt River flows were about 350 cfs in January, and about 8 cfs in February and March. Verde flows ranged 350–1,100 cfs in January, about 600 cfs in February, and about 125 cfs in February–March and June. Salt River flow reached about 1,300 cfs in June.
- **Habitat use and availability.** Habitat was measured at transects every 1 km in February and June, using depth, [[Current Velocity]] at 0.6 depth, and substrate. In both rivers, runs made up more of the points during high flows. There was no significant difference in available habitat between high and low flows on the Salt River. There was a significant difference on the Verde River.
- **Temperature and water quality.** [[Water Temperature]] loggers were placed at five Salt River sites and four Verde River sites, recording every two hours. Dissolved oxygen, pH, and conductivity were also measured with Hydrolab and YSI meters. Table 6 reports these values by site and month, but the OCR is partly garbled, so specific values are not reproduced here.
- **Nonnative distribution.** Nonnative fish abundance appeared to rise closer to the confluence, which may be due to warmer water. The authors note this observation was not backed by their limited data.

## Key Findings

**Objective 1: Sampling methods and community composition**
- Fourteen species were collected in the three Salt River reaches and 13 in the five Verde River reaches.
- [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Sonora Sucker]] were the most abundant natives. Largemouth Bass and Red Shiner were the most abundant nonnatives.
- No [[Rainbow Trout]] were collected in the sampling reaches. Two rainbow trout (mean 332 mm, in excellent health) were captured at the Fort McDowell boundary, and a third (about 415 mm) was shocked but not netted. These were excluded from CPUE estimates.
- Canoe electrofishing worked better than trammel nets for larger fish. Backpack electrofishing worked better than seines or minnow traps for smaller fish.

**Objective 2: Spawning timing and location**
- Sucker spawning ran from mid-February through late June. [[Roundtail Chub]] appeared to spawn from late April to early May, and [[Longfin Dace]] in early April.
- Desert and Sonora suckers courted in fast runs above or below riffles and in scour pools. Spawning occurred at pool and run edges or at the upstream ends of riffles over gravel.
- Several males typically spawned with one female. Thrashing in shallow water likely cleaned silt from gravel and was part of the spawning act.
- Spawning stage showed a **longitudinal partitioning**: fish near the dams were in congregation or early courtship, while fish at the confluence were at peak spawning or had finished.

**Objective 3: Larval rearing habitat**
- More than 1,600 points were sampled for larvae from March through June. Of these, 404 contained larval fish, with about 2,500 individuals identified (Table 3 totals 2,492).
- Desert sucker made up about 79% of the catch. Native fishes made up 97%.
- Roundtail chub larvae were found at only seven points, mostly in mid-water and near the bottom. Longfin dace were always collected on the bottom and used faster water than other natives.
- A [[Bonferroni Chi-Square]] comparison found no significant difference between used and available habitat for any native species or macrohabitat type. The authors caution that the low use count (404 use points vs. 1,203 non-use points) produced very wide confidence intervals.
- Runs were the most-used macrohabitat for larval suckers and roundtail chub. Riffles were used more often by longfin dace.

**Objective 4: Roundtail chub population status**
- In April 1999, 180 roundtail chub were [[PIT Tagging]] in the lower Verde River. Of 181 collected, 180 were tagged. The single unmarked fish was 93 mm, while fish as small as 99 mm were tagged.
- Adults (n=161): mean length 379 mm (range 313–482 mm); mean weight 550 g (range 279–1,206 g).
- Juveniles (n=20): mean length 122 mm (range 93–147 mm). Juvenile weight range was 4–30 g.
- The length-frequency histogram had no fish between 150 and 300 mm. The authors suggest a year-class may be missing, but more intensive sampling is needed.
- Most fish were male. Males showed orange breeding colors and tubercles, and some expressed milt. No ripe females were found.
- About half of the fish were parasitized, mostly by *Lernaea*, with black and yellow grubs also present.
- In the Salt River, over 200 roundtail were seen by snorkeling in April, but electrofishing in late April collected none. In early May, only one chub was found. It had been tagged as a ripe male in the Verde River a month earlier, had lost 45 g, and had moved about 4.5 km.

**Objective 5: Year-round temperature**
- Loggers were placed at five Salt River and four Verde River sites. Three Verde loggers went missing, presumably removed by the public after water levels dropped in March.
- Loggers were placed in deep runs and pools to record the coolest temperatures available to fish.
- Downloaded data were to go to AGFD Region VI for trout stocking decisions in August, after which the loggers would be redeployed for the rest of the study.

**Planned next steps**
- Juvenile surveys begin in July.
- Roundtail chub recapture is planned for late September to assess population status.
- Temperature data will be downloaded and redeployed.
