# Spikedace Monitoring Summary: Upper Verde River, Arizona, 1993 Field Season

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 monitoring report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] documents two sampling surveys (April and October 1993) of fish communities along about 36 river miles of the [[Upper Verde River]]. The work was designed to meet monitoring recommendations in the [[Spikedace Recovery Plan]] for the threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a small cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. Seventeen sites were sampled in April and twelve in October, ten of them overlapping. Spikedace were captured at only a few sites, mostly in October, while the catch was dominated by native suckers and [[Longfin Dace]]. Non-native species declined in relative abundance between surveys, and native fish numbers and diversity rose in October, which the authors linked to recovery from winter flooding.

## Background & Description

Spikedace is a small stream-dwelling cyprinid. Adults rarely exceed 75 mm total length and have a bright, silvery side with vertically elongated black dots, an olive-gray to brownish dorsum, and a white venter. Spikedace are visual, midwater carnivores. Adults mainly eat nymphal and adult ephemeropterans, and juveniles eat a wider range of aquatic insects, including larval ephemeropterans, hydropsychid trichopterans, and chironomid dipterans. Spawning occurs from April to June over coarse sand or fine gravel in shallow currents. Individuals reportedly live up to three years.

The species is threatened federally and in Arizona, and endangered in New Mexico. Historic records place it in the Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, Verde, Gila, and Agua Fria rivers. Only remnant populations persist today, in the upper Verde River, the Gila River, Eagle Creek, Aravaipa Creek, and New Mexico's upper Gila system.

Population declines are attributed to:

- Dams and water diversions
- Erosion, overgrazing, and mining
- Groundwater pumping and channelization
- Altered flow regimes
- Competition with introduced exotics, especially the [[Red Shiner]]

Spikedace are adapted to frequent, moderately severe floods. Controlled dam releases alter this pattern and favor non-native species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Spikedace prefer low-gradient, middle-elevation, low-velocity runs with eddy pools and hard bottoms of cobble, gravel, or sand. Adults use shallow (less than one meter deep) flowing shear zones where fast and slow currents meet. Juveniles use backwater zones with silt-sand substrate. Local populations expand and contract with natural environmental variation.

The four monitoring objectives were:

1. Build an expanded database of spikedace distribution and abundance.
2. Monitor known populations and target waterways likely to support spikedace, using consistent, standardized methods.
3. Assess annual changes in aquatic habitat and associated riparian communities.
4. Determine community composition, including the relative abundance of non-native species and their effects on spikedace, using fish stomach dissections.

Non-native fishes such as [[Green Sunfish]] and black bullheads are known to prey on native species and compete for limited habitat.

## Key Findings

**Study area and methods**

- Sampling covered the upper Verde River from the Granite Creek confluence (RM 144.3) to the Sycamore Creek confluence (RM 109.2). April sampling included 17 sites and October sampling included 12 sites.
- Gear included two backpack electrofishers ([[Coffelt Model BP-4]] and [[Smith-Root Model 12 POW]]), aided by dip netters, and 1/8-inch mesh seines.
- Sites were either *permanent* (revisited annually) or *random* (chosen anew each year). Each site was at least 200 m long and divided into subhabitats such as run, riffle, and pool.
- Spikedace were counted, measured for total length, and assigned to age class: juveniles 26–35 mm and adults over 36 mm. Weights were not taken for spikedace. Voucher specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and deposited in the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes.
- Water temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, and conductivity were recorded at permanent sites.

**Species composition**

- October yielded six native and seven exotic species. April yielded six native and three exotic species.
- The October list included spikedace, longfin dace, roundtail chub, Gila robust, Sonora sucker, speckled dace, desert sucker, red shiner, yellow bullhead, channel catfish, green sunfish, fathead minnow, common carp, and smallmouth bass.
- Forty-four razorback suckers were collected from a pool above the Perkinsville Bridge, PIT tagged, and released.

**Spikedace results by site**

| Site | April | October |
|---|---|---|
| [[Burnt Ranch]] Corral (permanent) | Present | 13 individuals, 2.9% of catch (down from 4.3%) |
| [[Perkinsville Bridge]] (permanent) | 16 individuals, 34.1% | 9 individuals, ≤2.2% |
| 0.3 mi below Perkinsville Bridge (random) | 1 individual, 1.6% | 64 individuals, 8.3% |
| [[Railroad Bridge]] (permanent) | None | 18 individuals, 4.6% |
| 0.7 mi below Railroad Bridge (random) | Not reported | 20 individuals, 18.4% |
| Granite Creek, F 638 Crossing, Hell Point, Railroad Draw, and Sycamore Creek sites | None recorded | None recorded |

Spikedace were not recorded at the Granite Creek, 0.5 mi above F 638, F 638 Crossing, 0.5 mi below F 638, or Hell Point sites in either survey.

**Other notable site results**

- At Granite Creek, longfin dace made up 85.2% of the October catch. Exotics fell to 0.44%, down from 10.7% in April.
- At the Sonora-sucker-dominated 0.5 mi above F 638 site, exotics were 16.1% of the catch.
- At Railroad Draw, exotics fell from 9.6% in April to 4.0% in October.
- At the Sycamore Creek Confluence, fish species rose from one in April to six in October.

**Discussion and conclusions**

- Fish numbers and diversity were significantly higher in October than in April.
- April conditions reflected extensive winter flooding, with exposed cobble, downed trees, and debris. By October, new vegetation and algal mats were recovering.
- Native species increased in relative abundance and exotic species declined overall.
- [[Roundtail Chub]] were found at ten sites in October and at two sites in April, among the sites sampled in both months.
- The report's stated objectives include building a multi-year database. The authors recommend continuing standardized monitoring to support future management decisions.
