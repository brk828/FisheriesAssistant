# Review of the State of World Fishery Resources: Part 1, Marine Resources

#ichthyology #marine-biology #multispecies-marine-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Circular (No. 710, Revision 8, Part 1, Rome, 1992, 114 p.) reviews the state of the world's marine fish stocks, based mainly on [[FAO Yearbook Statistics]] through 1990. It covers world catch trends, sixteen regional reviews by FAO statistical area, special sections on environmental issues, and tables of landings and exploitation status for each area. The central message is that global marine landings have plateaued and that many valuable, long-lived stocks are overexploited, while rising catches increasingly depend on low-value, short-lived species and on expanding fleets whose costs exceed their revenues.

## Background & Description

The Circular is a biennial review prepared by the Fishery Resources and Environment Division for FAO's Committee on Fisheries (COFI). It draws on catch and landing data up to 1990, limited 1991 information, fishery commission reports and correspondent input. The revision was split into Part 1 (marine resources) and Part 2 (inland fisheries and aquaculture).

Key global context from the Introduction:

- World marine catch rose about 6% per year until 1970, slowed sharply in the early 1970s (notably with the collapse of the [[Peruvian Anchoveta]] fishery), and grew only about 2.3% per year during 1980-89.
- 1990 marked the first decline in the FAO global series since 1976, a drop of about 3% to 83 million tons.
- The five largest fisheries of the 1980s were low-value small pelagics. They accounted for 13 million tons of the increase in world landings but contributed much less to total economic value.
- About eight species or species groups made up almost 50% of total global value. Finfish contributed 19% of this value and invertebrates 29%.

The Circular is organised as follows:

- **Introduction:** trends, reporting issues and fleet overcapacity.
- **Regional reviews:** 16 FAO statistical areas, each with landings tables.
- **Special boxes:** including the [[Northern Cod Fishery]], [[Multispecies Stock Assessment]], [[Caribbean Spiny Lobster]], [[Orange Roughy]] and [[Black Sea Ecosystem]].
- **Tuna and tuna-like species, whales and dolphins, and environmental issues.**
- **Marine resource tables**, which give catches by species group for each area, with a state-of-exploitation code (unknown, under-exploited, moderately exploited, fully/heavily exploited, over-exploited, depleted, recovering).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Circular stresses that environmental variability is a major driver of stock fluctuations, and that steady-state models are often inadequate:

- **[[El Niño]] events** recur irregularly, at generally two to ten year intervals, and affect eastern Pacific pelagics, the Northwest Pacific and monsoon-linked Indian Ocean fisheries. The 1991-92 event was ongoing when the document was prepared.
- **The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo** was thought to have cooled the global surface by about 0.5°C, with direct solar radiation possibly reduced by 20-30%.
- **Nutrient runoff and [[Eutrophication]]** have increased productivity and landings in semi-enclosed seas such as the [[Mediterranean Sea]] and the [[Black Sea Ecosystem]], but may later cause collapse. Oxygen depletion has affected the North Sea, Skagerrak, Kattegat and Baltic.
- **Upwelling** strongly governs West African small pelagic fisheries, and the [[Benguela Current]] supports high productivity off southern Angola and Namibia.
- **[[Global Climate Change]]:** the IPCC projects an effective doubling of carbon dioxide by 2025-2050 and a global mean temperature rise of 1.5°C to 4.5°C. Projected sea-level rise is about 20 cm by 2030 and 65 cm by 2100. Stock distributions may shift relative to maritime boundaries, as already observed in the Southeast Pacific pelagics.
- **Freshwater and sediment changes:** global water use has risen 4-8% annually in the second half of the 20th century. Sediment loads are being altered by deforestation (Huang He, Ganges/Brahmaputra) and rapid development in African and South American watersheds.

## Key Findings

### Global trends and structural problems

- The growth of landings in the 1980s came largely from a few low-value small pelagic species. Small pelagic stocks fluctuate widely over near-decadal periods, which can mask declines in high-value resources.
- Continued investment has made fishing effort much greater while landings remain roughly constant. The global industrial fleet grew at **double the relative rate of landings** over 1970-89.
- The SOFA analysis estimated that in 1989 the annual operating costs of the global marine fleet exceeded total revenues by **about US$22,000 million**, before capital costs.
- Overfishing (and over-investment) is identified as the main cause of local resource degradation over the last 50 years. Most management systems have failed to contain pressure from technology and new entrants.
- Open-access regimes, inside or outside EEZs, are incompatible with sustainable development.
- Replacement of depleted high-value species by lower-value, shorter-lived species occurred in several ways: new species expanding after depletion of older ones, and formerly discarded predators entering landings.
- Long-lived, slow-growing resources (sea perches, gadoids, snappers and groupers) showed the clearest long-term declines.
- The number of countries fishing rose by about 25% and the number of species reported rose by about 16% across statistical areas, 1970-90. Reporting efficiency is poor, so global landings are probably under-reported.

### Species-group rankings

- Invertebrate resources (e.g., krill) show high rates of increase, and conventional bottom fish show the lowest rankings.
- Shrimp production rose significantly, partly through aquaculture, which consumed "trash" fish as feed.
- Cephalopod catches grew mainly through distant-water fleets focusing on a few outer shelf and oceanic areas.

### Regional highlights

- **Northwest Atlantic (Area 21):** total finfish and invertebrate landings were **3.2 million t in 1990**. Northern cod on the Grand Banks collapsed; peak landings were 800,000 t in 1968. The June 1992 NAFO meeting recommended catches not exceed **50,000 t**. Cod landings in the Gulf of St. Lawrence fell from 106,000 t (1983) to 47,000 t (1989). Georges Bank herring crashed in 1977 after a peak of 374,000 t (1968). Redfish biomass in the Gulf of Maine declined by over 80% from the late 1960s to 1987.
- **Northeast Atlantic (Area 27):** total catch was **9.2 million t in 1990** (8.2 million t finfish, 1.0 million t invertebrates), down from 9.9 million t in 1989.
  - North Sea cod: about 1,000 t in 1990, from about 250,000 t in the 1970s. Spawning stock biomass was 66,000 t in 1990, below the 150,000 t minimum acceptable level.
  - Baltic cod: spawning stock fell from over 800,000 t (1980) to 115,000 t (1992). A 40,000 t TAC was set for 1993, still about 50% above the recommended fishing mortality.
  - Northeast Arctic cod: quotas were halved to 172,000 t in 1990 and 1991.
  - Barents Sea capelin: spawning stock rose from 17,000 t (1987) to 2.6 million t (1990).
  - North Sea herring: spawning stock was 52,000 t in 1977 and has recovered to about 1.3 million t, above the 800,000 t minimum.
  - An epidemic of *Ichthyophonus* began in 1991 in adult herring stocks, with mass mortalities in the Sound. Its population-level impact could not be quantified.
- **Western Central Atlantic (Area 31):** landings fell from 2.6 million t (1984) to 1.7 million t (1990), mostly from declines in Gulf menhaden, American oyster and calico scallop. Most reef fish are overexploited. Conch stocks have been decimated in many areas. Florida spiny lobster shows few recruits surviving each season.
- **Eastern Central Atlantic (Area 34):** catches reached 3.7-4.0 million t in 1989-90. Sardinella landings off Côte d'Ivoire-Ghana-Togo-Benin returned to 80,000-106,000 t/yr for *S. aurita* after a collapse, while *S. maderensis* (20,000-48,000 t/yr) is of concern. The 1988 CECAF review found most large stocks fully fished or overfished. Only about 20 of 43 stocks are updated regularly (about 2.5% of stocks).
- **Mediterranean and Black Sea (Area 37):** combined landings were 1.9-2.1 million t (1982-88), then fell to 1.7 and 1.5 million t in 1989 and 1990. Black Sea catches dropped from **833,000 t (1988) to 428,000 t (1990)**. Small pelagics collapsed around 1988-89, with 1991 anchovy landings estimated below 150,000 t. The exotic ctenophore *Mnemiopsis* is implicated.
- **Southwest Atlantic (Area 41):** catches rose from about 1 million t (mid-1970s) to 2.4 million t (1987), then fell to 2.0 million t (1990). Squid landings peaked at 750,000 t (1989) and fell to 552,000 t (1990).
- **Southeast Atlantic (Area 47):** catches were 2.1-2.7 million t/yr (1980-89), then fell to 1.5 million t in 1990. Namibian waters were free of foreign fleets from 1990 after independence. Hake was over-exploited, while cape horse mackerel and anchovy were fully exploited.
- **Western Indian Ocean (Area 51):** landings were **3.4 million t in 1989 and 1990**, mostly from India (1.6 million t). Omani surveys estimated MSY at 270,000 t of pelagics and 77,000 t of demersal fish. Kuwaiti shrimp landings fell from 5,000 t (1988) to under 2,000 t (1990) following the Gulf War.
- **Eastern Indian Ocean (Area 57):** landings were **2.8 million t in 1990**, with about 120,000 t of shrimp by-catch discarded off east India.
- **Northwest Pacific (Area 61):** landings were **25.7 million t in 1990** (down from 26.7 million t in 1988), about 31% of the world marine catch. The Japanese pilchard catch exceeded 5.4 million t in 1988 and fell to 4.7 million t in 1990. Alaska pollock landings declined from over 5 million t to 4.2 million t (1990).
- **Northeast Pacific (Area 67):** total catch was 3.2-3.5 million t (1986-90), with Alaska pollock accounting for slightly less than half. In August 1992, six parties agreed a two-year moratorium on pollock fishing in the "Donut Hole". Pacific Ocean perch landings are less than one third of early 1970s levels.
- **Western Central Pacific (Area 71):** landings reached a record **7.3 million t in 1990**. Slipmouth, catfish and croakers show over-exploitation in the Gulf of Thailand and Malacca Straits.
- **Eastern Central Pacific (Area 77):** total catches were 1.5 million t (1990), down from 1.8 million t (1989). Californian sardine catches were 440,000-480,000 t (1986-88), rising to 510,000 t (1989) and falling to 400,000 t (1990). Northern anchovy fell from 110,000 t (1989) to 6,000 t (1990).
- **Southwest Pacific (Area 81):** catches have levelled off at around 1 million t (1988-90). Blue grenadier landings rose from 43,000 t (1985) to 392,000 t (1990). Orange roughy is at high risk (see below).
- **Southeast Pacific (Area 87):** total catches have exceeded 10 million t annually since 1985, reaching **15.3 million t (1989)** and falling to **14 million t (1990)**. Peruvian anchoveta catch was 3.7 million t in 1990 (5.4 million t in 1989). Chilean jack mackerel reached a record 3.8 million t in 1990. Sardine catches fell from 5.4 million t (1988) to 4.3 million t (1990).
- **Southern Oceans (Areas 48, 58, 88):** total catch was **429,000 t in 1989/90**, of which krill made up 89%. The Antarctic fisheries are managed by CCAMLR under an ecosystem approach.

### Tuna and tuna-like species

- Total catch rose steadily from about 1.9 million t (early 1970s) to **4.2 million t (1990)**. Principal market tuna catches rose from 1.4 to 2.8 million t.
- Skipjack made up 45% and yellowfin 35% of the 1990 principal market tuna catch.
- Northern bluefin tuna is overfished in the western Atlantic, where adult biomass is now only 10-23% of its 1970 level. Southern bluefin tuna is over-exploited, with parental biomass in continuous decline until 1990.
- Dolphin mortality in purse seines led to restrictions and some canneries refusing tuna caught with dolphins.

### Orange roughy

- Orange roughy may reach 100 years of age and mature at 20-25 years. Maximum sustainable harvest rates are estimated at only 1-5% of virgin biomass.
- Some early spawning concentrations were reduced to 10% of virgin biomass. The St. Helens stock off eastern Tasmania fell to about 50% in five years.
- Present TACs exceed sustainable levels, so the fishery is not sustainable without new stocks.

### Marine mammals

- Unrestrained whaling reduced most large whale stocks. Most large cetaceans are seriously depleted, with minke whale, eastern North Pacific gray whale and Bryde's whale as partial exceptions.
- The Revised Management Procedure adopted in 1992 calls for protection of stocks below **54%** of pre-exploitation levels.
- Management of small cetaceans is weak. Large-scale driftnet fishing has been subject to a UN moratorium recommendation for 30 June 1992.

### Management and assessment

- The **TAC** system is the standard tool in Area 27, yet agreed TACs have consistently exceeded ICES advice, and actual catches have exceeded agreed TACs.
- ICES has moved to advise only where stocks lie below a "minimum biologically acceptable level" (MBAL). Biological reference points such as F0.1 are no longer the basis of advice.
- The **Multi-Species VPA** model for the North Sea found predation mortality on young fish often above 65% per year. Whiting was identified as an important predator, and cod stocking in the Baltic requires about 50,000 t of extra juveniles to offset cannibalism.
- Individual Transferable Quotas (ITQs) were introduced in New Zealand in 1986, and Australia and the USA have used limited entry, ITQs and marine reserves in various fisheries.
- Mesh-size regulation and effort control are the main priorities in the Mediterranean, where a 40 mm minimum mesh is contested.

### Concluding perspective

The Circular argues that the modest increases in global landings are largely offset by declines in individual stocks. Several resources are over-exploited, and the most intensively managed areas show a relatively high proportion of long-term species-group declines. The authors call for effort control, improved assessment data, bio-economic analysis and better reporting. They also urge management that accounts for climate, nutrient runoff and multispecies interactions.
