# Interim Flows for Grand Canyon: Recommendations for Interim Operating Procedures for Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #dam-management #sediment-transport #riparian-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This 1991 report presents scientifically based recommendations for interim operating procedures at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to protect downstream aquatic and riparian resources in [[Grand Canyon National Park]] during the period between research flows (ending July 1991) and implementation of a Record of Decision (anticipated late 1993). The recommendations were developed by the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] Senior Scientist, Program Manager, and scientific experts, reviewed by the Scientific Core Group. The document addresses impacts of dam operations on sediment dynamics (sand bars and beaches), native fishes including the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), introduced species (particularly trout), aquatic food base, riparian vegetation, and secondary attributes including recreation and cultural resources. The recommendations aim to halt degradation of downstream resources by reducing erosion rates, maintaining sediment supply, and protecting critical habitats while recognizing operational constraints of the dam.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations from 1963-1991 caused progressive loss of sand from beaches, decreased beach area, and adverse impacts on aquatic and riparian ecosystems in [[Grand Canyon]]. The dam's flow regime—characterized by daily fluctuations for power generation—accelerates erosion of sand bars, modifies aquatic habitat, affects fish spawning and recruitment, and impacts riparian vegetation communities. The [[Colorado River]] below the dam supports endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), other native fishes (speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker), introduced trout fisheries, and diverse riparian communities including the Old High Water Zone (OHWZ), New High Water Zone (NHWZ), and palustrine marsh zones supporting approximately 5,000 plant and animal species.

The sediment system is fundamentally altered: [[Glen Canyon Dam]] traps upstream sediment, while tributaries (particularly the [[Paria River]] and [[Little Colorado River]]) supply approximately 1.1-2.7 million tons of sand annually to downstream reaches. Sand transport increases logarithmically with discharge—a constant 11,387 cfs transports ~200,000 tons/year, while the same mean flow with daily peaks of 26,000 cfs transports ~1 million tons/year. Beach building requires flows exceeding normal operations by ~10,000 cfs to deposit sand above the operating range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native Fish Ecology**: [[Humpback Chub]] is long-lived (~30 years) and uses backwaters and creek mouths for young-of-year survival. Backwaters are progressively lost as flows decline below 8,000-10,000 cfs, with return channels virtually drained at 5,000 cfs. Daily fluctuations negatively influence access to creek mouths where native fishes spawn. Other native species (flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, speckled dace) similarly depend on backwater habitats and stable creek mouth conditions for reproduction.

**Aquatic Food Base**: The system's productivity depends on *Cladophora glomerata*, a filamentous green alga that colonizes to the minimum discharge elevation (typically 3,000 cfs). *Cladophora* provides essential cover and substrate for attached diatoms and aquatic macroinvertebrates that constitute the food base for fishes. Laboratory studies show 25-75% loss of *Cladophora* biomass after >12 hours exposure. Daily fluctuations from 10,000 to 20,000 cfs negatively affect standing crop at [[Lee's Ferry]]. The aquatic food base supports the trout fishery and wintering [[Bald Eagle]] populations that prey on spawning trout.

**Riparian Vegetation**: Three distinct communities exist: (1) OHWZ—declining due to flood control limiting germination; (2) NHWZ—directly affected by scouring and soil erosion from fluctuations; (3) Riverside marshes—created by tributary sediment settlement in backwaters. These communities stabilize banks, contribute to soil formation, and support exceptional biodiversity.

**Sediment Dynamics**: Daily fluctuations accelerate erosion exponentially with fluctuation magnitude. Ramping rates (rate of discharge change) significantly influence erosion, with down-ramping causing high positive pore pressure in banks, reducing material strength and promoting failure. Mean ramping rates during 1980-1989 ranged from 1,000-2,000 cfs/hr, with extreme events exceeding 19,000 cfs/hr occurring 3-5 times annually. Discharges >20,000-22,000 cfs erode high-elevation sand deposits from the 1983-1986 high-flow years.

## Key Findings

**Maximum and Minimum Discharge Recommendations**:
- Maximum discharge should not exceed 20,000 cfs to prevent erosion of high-elevation sand deposits
- Minimum discharge should be no lower than 5,000 cfs, with average daily discharge ≥8,000 cfs
- Constant 5,000 cfs flow should not exceed 8 hours and occur only between 7 PM and 6 AM to minimize desiccation of aquatic food base

**Daily Fluctuation and Ramping Rates**:
- Daily fluctuation range should not exceed 5,000 cfs
- Up-ramping rate should not exceed 2,000 cfs/hr to reduce beach face calving
- Down-ramping rate should not exceed 1,000 cfs/hr (most critical for preventing seepage-driven erosion and mass wasting)

**Beach Building Flows**:
- Spring season high flow of 31,500 cfs (maximum through turbines) for 6 days to 2 weeks
- Flows elevated gradually over several days, then dropped more rapidly to enhance sand storage on elevated bars
- Timing should avoid enhancement of exotic vegetation recruitment
- Should be implemented as monitored research flow

**Sediment Transport Relationships** (from Grand Canyon gage 1983-1986 data):
- Regression equation: ln(LD) = -14.1 + 3.53 ln(Q), R² = 0.88
- Daily sand transport: 1,500 tons at 15,000 cfs; 4,000 tons at 20,000 cfs; 8,650 tons at 25,000 cfs
- Five-fold increase in annual sand transport occurs with same mean flow when daily peaks reach 26,000 cfs vs. constant discharge

**Monitoring and Adaptive Management**:
- Monitoring program required during interim period to determine impact levels
- Modifications should be made if unanticipated resource impacts occur
- Interim period provides opportunity to continue studies of discharge regime impacts

**Multi-Resource Impacts Summary**: Constant flows with seasonal variation generally benefit beaches, native fish, endangered fish, trout, aquatic food base, and archaeological sites. High daily fluctuations are detrimental to most attributes except potentially enhancing drift for trout feeding and fishing success. Low flows (<8,000 cfs) cause problems for whitewater rafting, while flows of 10,000-20,000 cfs are optimal for this use.
