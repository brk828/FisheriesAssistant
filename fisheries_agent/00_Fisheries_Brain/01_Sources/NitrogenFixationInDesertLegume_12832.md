# Freshwater Islands in a Desert Sand Sea: Pozo Hydrology, Flora, and Phytogeography of the Gran Desierto, with Companion Articles

#botany #wetland-flora #aquatic-ecology #hydrogeology #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This is Volume 9, Number 2 (1988) of [[Desert Plants]], a quarterly journal published by the University of Arizona at the [[Boyce Thompson Southwestern Arboretum]]. Its central research article, by Ezcurra, Felger, Russell and Equihua, examines the [[Adair Bay Pozos]]: small artesian freshwater springs ("pozos") in the saline flats of the [[Gran Desierto]] near the Gulf of California. The authors combine water chemistry, piezometric measurements, clay and sand permeability tests, floristic surveys and species-area analysis. They conclude that the pozos are fed by a confined continental aquifer rather than seawater, and that their flora is a relict of the former [[Colorado River Delta]] wetlands, now largely lost.

The issue also contains an editorial on ethics in science, technology and agriculture; a study of [[Sesbania]]–[[Rhizobium]] specificity and nitrogen fixation; a historical reassessment of [[Piman Indian Agave Cultivation]]; a note on desert legumes; and a book announcement.

## Background & Description

**Pozos.** The pozos are island-like wetlands surrounded by hypersaline clay flats where salt crusts may be several centimetres thick and no vegetation grows. Their name is the Spanish word for "hole" or "well." Their vegetation differs strongly from the surrounding Sonoran Desert in both life-form spectrum and geographic origin. The authors describe them as "island-like relicts" of the Colorado River delta.

**Historical use.** Indigenous peoples used the pozos for millennia. Shell middens (concheros) near the coast reach up to 2 km in extent. Pottery indicates use since at least A.D. 700. The pozos were also on western [[Tohono O'odham]] pilgrimage trails to gather salt, and in 1701 [[Eusebio Kino]] and [[Juan Mateo Manje]] reached one of the pozos (Tres Ojitos).

**Pozo landforms.** Pozos sit within vegetated mounds built from undecomposed roots and blown sand, up to about 1.5 m high. Organic matter in the highest mounds at La Salina ranges from 36% to 45%, compared with less than 0.1% to 0.5% in normal saltflat soils. Coyotes dig and maintain water holes in the mounds, and new pozos can form within a relatively short time.

**Study sites.** Key sites include La Salina (the largest and most diverse oasis), La Soda (Pozos Muerto and Metate), Tres Ojitos, Pozo Zopilote, Laguna Prieta, and [[Quitovac]] and [[Quitobaquito]], which were used for comparison.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrogeology.** The authors propose that a deep aquifer of sand and rounded gravel, probably deposited in ancient river beds and later buried by dunes, is recharged by runoff from the [[Pinacate Volcanic Shield]]. Where it reaches the coast, relatively impermeable clays cause artesian pressure. Pozos appear where clay permeability has increased, possibly through desiccation cracking or flocculation driven by ion exchange, and possibly through plant root activity.

**Permeability.** Under a gradient of 1 m/m, clay from La Soda had a hydraulic conductivity of 0.001 cm/hr, while the aquifer sand had 243 cm/hr. The authors note that water moving 1 mm through the clay would move 2.4 km through the sand. The clays are highly expansible, with mean volume differences of 42% between wet and dry states.

**Water chemistry.** Seventeen water samples were classed as saline, pozo, transitional or well. Multivariate analysis showed saline samples differed significantly from the others (P < 0.001). Pozo and well waters did not differ significantly. Pozo water had chemistry within the normal range for the continental aquifer. Relative ion proportions in Adair Bay mudflats resembled fresh groundwater more than seawater, and no sulfate salts were present in surface crusts. The authors therefore reject the hypothesis of direct seawater intrusion (May, 1973). Surface salts were mainly [[Halite]] (NaCl) and [[Trona]] (sodium carbonate).

**Piezometry.** Two piezometers at each of two La Soda sites reached fresh water at about 3.0 m depth in sand and gravel. The deep piezometers had conductivities of 3.5 and 3.3 mS (about 0.3% TDS), while the shallow ones exceeded 100 mS (more than 8% TDS). Vertical hydraulic gradients were 0.43 m/m at Pozo Muerto and 0.36 m/m at Pozo Metate, indicating strong upward flow.

**Climate and fauna.** The pozos supply essential water for a rich bird fauna and for mammals including coyotes and kit foxes. Lumholtz (1912) noted that horses could not survive the surrounding dunes.

## Key Findings

**Flora and origin**
- The pozo flora contains 26 vascular plant species (the flora section later gives 23 wetland species plus 3–4 dryland species at the margins). Most are wetland plants with temperate affinities. The flora is markedly different from the tropical-subtropical character of the rest of the Sonoran Desert.
- By geographic affinity, 5 species (19%) are cosmopolitan, 4 (15%) are widespread in the Americas, 4 (15%) are widely distributed in North America, 5 (19%) are from western North America, and 8 (31%) are from southwestern North America.
- The authors attribute the flora to dispersal from the lower [[Colorado River]] and [[Gila River]], which once supplied propagules to the delta.
- New records include Apocynum cannabinum (new for Sonora and the Sonoran Desert), Lythrum californicum (new for Sonora), and Sarcobatus vermiculatus (a new generic record for Mexico).
- Only one annual (Polypogon monspeliensis) occurs, while winter-dormant perennials are common. Non-native species are Tamarix ramosissima and P. monspeliensis.

**Pioneer colonization**
- Nitrophila occidentalis is the first plant to colonize places where the aquifer breaks through the clays. It is a reliable indicator of fresh water 20–50 cm below the surface. Saltgrass (Distichlis spicata) is the second colonizer.
- Coyotes often dig at Nitrophila colonies to reach water.

**Delta loss and extinction risk**
- The authors estimate the original Colorado River delta (about 300 km²) supported 200–400 wetland and aquatic species. Model projections gave 192 and 420 species under different dominance assumptions.
- With the delta destroyed, local extinction of pozo wetland species is unlikely to be followed by re-immigration. Weedy invaders such as Tamarix are expected to replace them.

**Species-area relationships**
- Species richness across 11 pozos fits s = kA^z with k = 0.75 and z = 0.368 (95% CI for z: 0.32–0.42; for k: 0.63–0.88; r² = 0.98 and 0.95 for the two fitting methods; P < 0.001).
- The z value is significantly higher than the canonical value of 0.263 (Preston, 1962). It is similar to exponents reported for other island floras, such as the Galápagos and Channel Islands.
- A pozo is expected to hold about three to four times more species than dry terrestrial islands of comparable area.

**Editorial and companion articles**
- The editorial by F. S. Crosswhite and C. D. Crosswhite argues for a "moral element" in science and technology. It advocates arboretums and botanical gardens that preserve endangered species, retain natural communities, explain the plant kingdom, and bring visitors into contact with plants.
- Abdel Magid, Singleton and Tavares tested ten [[Rhizobium]] strains against six [[Sesbania]] accessions in glasshouse acetylene reduction assays. [[Sesbania bispinosa]] (accession BA12) had the highest mean fixation rate (6.63 µmol C₂H₄/plant/h). S. grandiflora (GL 2.02) ranked second (5.35). S. rostrata produced little or no ethylene in the assay, suggesting high specificity, yet it nodulated profusely in potted-soil trials in Saudi Arabia (200 nodules per plant when inoculated).
- Dobyns argues from historic documents, notably Pérez de Ribas (c. 1613), that Piman Agave cultivation and Casa Grande-style architecture persisted later than archaeologists had assumed. He proposes a terminal date for "Classic Hohokam" after A.D. 1613 rather than around A.D. 1450. Melons, peaches and sugar cane later replaced Agave pulp as sweet foods.
- A short article by F. S. Crosswhite and C. D. Crosswhite discusses how legumes supply "captive" nitrogen and could help restore fertility to deserts and cropland.
- A book announcement describes *A Sense of Place: The Life and Work of Forrest Shreve* by Janice Emily Bowers (200 pages, $19.95, The University of Arizona Press).

**Limitations noted by the authors**
- Sampling was limited by pozo accessibility and by flooding of coastal saltflats. Some pozos recorded by May (1973) could not be found.
- The hydrogeological hypothesis of buried fluvial waterways, which would explain why pozos cluster on some saltflats but not others, needs further drilling studies.
