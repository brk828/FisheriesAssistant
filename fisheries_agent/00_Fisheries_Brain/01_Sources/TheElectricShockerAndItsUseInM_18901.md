# The Electric "Shocker" and Its Use in Michigan Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This 1947 article by [[David S. Shetter]] of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]], [[Michigan Department Of Conservation]], describes the [[Electric Shocker]], a portable AC electrofishing unit used to capture stream fish alive, chiefly [[Brook Trout]]. The device was adapted from German work by [[Franz Hager]] and was first used in New York in 1939. Michigan obtained its first unit in September 1941 and began field use on [[Hunt Creek]] in mid-summer 1942. The article covers the equipment, electrode designs, crew procedures, a [[Mark-Recapture Estimation]] example, safety observations, and the device's advantages and drawbacks for research and hatchery work.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** The use of electricity for stream population studies seems to have originated in eastern Germany. Shetter translated and reviewed a short German article by Hager in 1938. That article described the equipment and listed standing crops and their value for certain Bavarian trout streams in three different years (the values themselves are not given in the source).
- **New York:** [[New York State Conservation Department]] personnel assembled and used the shocker in 1939 ([[David C. Haskell]], 1939; Haskell and Zilliox, 1940).
- **Michigan introduction:** Equipment was obtained in September 1941. Advice came from Haskell, with help from Dr. P. I. Tack of Michigan State College and Dr. J. W. Leonard, the first resident biologist at the [[Hunt Creek Fisheries Experiment Station]]. The first unit was operated on Hunt Creek in mid-summer 1942. A second, higher-wattage unit was added the following summer.
- **Power source:** A 1-H.P. gasoline motor drives a generator of 500 watts capacity. Normal current is about 1½ to 2½ amperes, depending on water conductivity and electrode spacing. The unit can be carried by two men or rested in a boat.
- **Circuit:** Current passes through a 2½-ampere fuse or circuit breaker, an optional ammeter, a choke coil (about 180 turns of No. 30 enameled wire on a porcelain tube), and a fixed condenser (0.001 to 0.006 MFD) to one electrode. Electrical current returns through the water to the other electrode and to the generator. The circuit was refined by [[Basil V. Hughes]], resident manager of the state's [[Rifle River Area]] in Ogemaw County.

### Electrode Development

| Electrode type | Construction (as reported) | Operating notes (as reported) |
|---|---|---|
| Hager (German) | 10-inch square copper plates on wooden handles | Original design |
| New York designs | Driven steel stakes plus floating metal screen; later bare copper wire | Screen reduced usable voltage in some soils and was unwieldy in snaggy streams; floating screen created riffle reducing visibility |
| Early Michigan | ⅜-inch mesh hardware cloth soldered to a foot-shaped frame of ¼-inch reinforcing steel, about 16 in long, 6 in high at heel | Satisfactory, but spacing limited to about 8 ft; burned some larger fish |
| Hughes induction-coil | Bare wire on wooden frame, 14 x 6 x 1 in, about 55 ft of #14, 16 or 18 copper or phosphor-bronze wire | Shocked all sizes at 12 to 15 ft apart in water up to 2½ ft deep |
| Triangular | About 95 ft of wire on frame about 2 ft high, 22-inch base, wire spaced ½ inch | Best in water up to 2½ ft; overheats generator in deeper water and gives smaller charged field |
| Long narrow (deep-water) | Dimensions printed as 86 x 6 x 1 in (possibly an OCR error); about 55 ft of wire spaced 1½ in | Shocks more fish in pools 4 to 6 ft deep; charged field narrower but deeper |

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Electrical field and fish response:** The charged field resembles the field of attraction around two magnetic poles. Fish entering it are momentarily stunned and can be netted. The rapid reversal of AC current is thought to affect the nervous system. Large fish succumb readily, but electrodes must be close together to stun fingerlings and fry, which absorb less current.
- **Recovery and mortality:** Stunned fish recover in 30 seconds to five minutes, depending on size and shock severity. Fewer than 1% of handled fish die. Death usually occurs only when a fish stays too long in the field or rests unobserved against an electrode.
- **Stream conditions:** Water conductivity affects current draw. Working upstream against the current avoids roiling the water and carries stunned fish toward the crew. Snags and brushy water limit seine use, and the shocker is preferred there. Stunned fish cannot be observed when rains or floods roil the stream.
- **Hatchery and disease context:** Escaped trout in hatchery waste ditches are described as possible sources of infection and disease for hatchery stock. The waste ditches of the [[Oden Hatchery]] were shocked semi-annually for the previous 18 months to remove them.
- **Rough fish:** The author suggests the device could help control rough fish such as [[Suckers]] and [[Carp]] in natural waters.
- **Safety:** Contact with one hand in the field is only relatively uncomfortable. Two hands in the field at once produce a much more severe shock. A single severe case in Michigan involved a waders tear that let current pass through the wearer during about an hour of work. The author's consensus is that serious injury would occur only if someone were fully immersed under or close to an electrode while power was on.

## Key Findings

1. **Efficiency:** The author concludes that the shocker is "several times more efficient than any previously available" tool for capturing fish alive for stream research. Electricity was found to be more effective than a seine for catching trout.
2. **Adoption:** Michigan's first unit was used on Hunt Creek in mid-summer 1942, and demand from biological staff and hatchery personnel led to a second, higher-wattage unit.
3. **Electrode performance:** The Hughes induction-coil electrode worked at 12 to 15 ft spacing in water up to 2½ ft deep. The triangular model performed best in shallow water. The long narrow model is better for pools 4 to 6 ft deep.
4. **Crew size:** Two people can operate the equipment, but an ideal crew for intensive work is six men. In population studies, each electrode is handled by one man, with assistants netting fish, one person carrying a pail of fresh water, and one tallying fish and recording data.
5. **Mark-recapture method:** Two complete trips are made over the same section, separated by two or three days. Fish caught on the first trip are marked by clipping part of the tail fin. The worked example is:
   - 100 legal brook trout marked on the first run
   - 75 marked and 27 unmarked legal brook trout captured on the second run
   - Solving 75/100 = 27/X gives X = 36 unmarked fish
   - Estimated total population = 100 + 36 = **136 legal brook trout** per mile of stream
6. **Fish survival:** Fewer than 1% of fish handled were killed. Recovery takes 30 seconds to five minutes.
7. **Management goal:** Population studies combined with creel census data on the same trout stream sections are intended to determine how many legal fish per acre or per mile are needed to provide quality sport. The results are expected to guide more accurate and economical stocking of hatchery-reared fish.
8. **Research use:** Wild brook trout used in the organoleptic tests described by [[Baeder, Tack and Hazzard]] were almost all collected with the shocker.
9. **Hatchery use:** The Oden Hatchery waste ditches have been shocked semi-annually for 18 months. The device could also remove unwanted fish from hatchery ponds or natural streams before fingerling stocking.
10. **Safety:** No danger was found with reasonable care. Only one severe shock occurred in Michigan to date, and it involved a leaking pair of waders.
11. **Limitations:** The unit is heavy and bulky, and stunned fish cannot be observed in roiled water after rains or floods.
12. **Outlook:** The author expects the shocker to play an increasingly important role in stream research and in stream survey parties in the future.
