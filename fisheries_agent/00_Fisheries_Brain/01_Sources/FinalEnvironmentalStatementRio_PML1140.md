# Final Environmental Impact Statement: Rio Grande Boundary Preservation, Hudspeth and Presidio Counties, Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This December 1978 Final Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) from the United States Section of the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] (IBWC) evaluates how to restore and preserve the approximately 198.8-mile reach of the [[Rio Grande]] that forms the international boundary between Fort Quitman and Presidio, Texas. The [[1970 Boundary Treaty]] requires the two countries to restore and preserve the river as the boundary. The river channel has filled with sediment and been overgrown by [[Salt Cedar]], and the channel is expected to be obliterated within five to ten years without action.

Four major alternatives were assessed: No Action; Monument the Boundary; Restoration and Preservation of the Channel as the Boundary; and Restoration and Preservation with Wildlife Enhancement. The Environmentally Preferred Alternative is the fourth. The Agency prefers the third option along the existing alignment, with mitigation measures, because it fulfills the 1970 Treaty commitment and falls within the agency's jurisdiction.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The reach runs from about 13 miles downstream of Fort Quitman to a point about 6 miles upstream of Presidio. The river flows through 11 canyon sections totaling 29.3 miles and a series of valleys totaling 169.5 miles. The floodplain covers about 43,800 acres (18,100 acres in the US; 25,700 acres in Mexico). The straight-line distance through the reach is 117 miles.

**Hydrology and climate.** The region is semi-arid Chihuahuan Desert, receiving about 10 inches of rain per year, with evaporation of 90 to 100 inches per year. Upstream reservoirs, including [[Elephant Butte Dam]] and [[Caballo Dam]], regulate flows. Annual flow fell sharply after the late 1940s. Average annual flow was 1,550.2 thousand acre-feet (1889–1914), 1,270.4 (1915–1950), and 138.4 (1951–1976, in the table's units). Fort Quitman recorded zero flow for periods of up to 242 consecutive days.

**Sediment and channel change.** Arroyo flash floods from more than 100 tributaries carry heavy sediment into the channel. Channel cross-sections shrank to 40 percent of their 1935–36 area by 1977 at some measured sites, and in places the channel is three feet or less in width and one foot or less in depth.

**Socio-economics.** Hudspeth County had about 2,400 residents in 1970, and Presidio County about 4,000. The population has declined, and most residents depend on farming and ranching. Many fields are no longer irrigated, and families' incomes are low.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation.** Native cottonwood, willow, and thornyshrub communities are being replaced by salt cedar. Salt cedar covered about 5,800 acres in 1947, 8,700 acres in 1967, and 12,900 acres in 1974. Soil salinity is rising.

**Wildlife.** The study documented 17 mammal species (large mammals), 37 nonpasserine birds, and dozens of passerine species. White-winged doves nest in salt cedar. Reptiles are mostly found in thornyshrub. Mosquito populations are a concern because of disease transmission.

**Fish and aquatic life.** Twelve fish species were identified, and five species (red shiner, common carp, mosquitofish, and others) made up 96.7 percent of the sampled population. Mollusks and crustaceans were surveyed, with two aquatic snail species and four bivalve species recorded. No endangered aquatic species were found.

**Endangered and special-status species.** The American peregrine falcon nests within about 15 to 20 miles of the river. The Mexican duck was delisted from the endangered species list on August 24, 1978. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service found that the preferred alternative would not jeopardize these species.

**Wetlands.** About 1,764 acres of wetlands exist on the US floodplain, 93 percent of which are agricultural fields and salt cedar/mesquite flats.

**Archaeology.** The survey covered about 65 percent of the study area. Sites were located mainly on terraces above the floodplain. No National Register properties are in the study area.

**Air and noise.** Air quality is excellent, and noise levels are acceptable.

## Key Findings

- **Preferred alternative:** Restoration and preservation of the channel along its existing alignment (Alternative 3.a.(1)), plus mitigation. Restoration would excavate the channel to about 5 feet deep, with a 16-foot bottom width and a 35–40-foot top width, only in valley sections. A cleared overflow passageway about 56 feet wide would be kept on each side of the channel, bordered by a 25-foot fringe of trees.
- **Cost:** The initial total cost to the United States for the existing-alignment option is $4.3 million, split equally with Mexico. Mechanical maintenance is estimated at $300,000 to $500,000.
- **Ecological benefit:** The Computer Ecological Impact Evaluation Method projects that channel restoration plus a wildlife management area provides the greatest improvement in ecological health. The No Action alternative shows a decline, with EHUs dropping from 1,580 to 1,520 over 50 years.
- **Mitigation:** Five one-acre ponds with cottonwood plantings, five four-acre ephemeral ponds, and water connections to 295 acres of wetlands. Clearing of about 1,200 acres of salt cedar would be offset by grass seeding.
- **Alignment options:** Four alternatives were studied: existing alignment, minimum realignment, intermediate realignment, and rectification. All meet Treaty requirements, but realignments require complex land exchanges with Mexico.
- **No Action risks:** Loss of the boundary's identity, unresolved disputes, continued habitat decline, and a depopulating economy. The Department of State opposes monumentation because it would require modifying the 1970 Treaty.
- **Wildlife management area:** An optional area of at least 10 percent of the US side, costing about $7.7 million, could be designated by federal or state authorities. It is outside the agency's jurisdiction.
- **Compliance:** The preferred alternative complies with the Clean Water Act (Public Law 95-217). No deposits would enter the channel, and wetlands would not be spoiled.
- **Coordination:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department, and nine environmental associations participated. A public meeting drew 45 objections to the preferred alternative.
