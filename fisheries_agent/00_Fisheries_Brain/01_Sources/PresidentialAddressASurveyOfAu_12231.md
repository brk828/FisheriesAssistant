# A Survey of Australian Ichthyology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #australian-fishes

## Executive Summary

This is the 1964 Presidential Address to the [[Linnean Society of New South Wales]] by G. P. Whitley, a long-serving ichthyologist at the [[Australian Museum]]. It surveys about three and a half centuries of documented knowledge of Australian fishes, from Aboriginal rock art and early European voyage records through the taxonomic work of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Whitley traces the main collectors, expeditions, monographs and institutions, and closes with a call for a fuller synthesis. The document is a historical review rather than original research. It contains no new field data, but it gives useful counts of known species and a bibliographic framework (a subject index, a name-list of about 2,450 species, and a bibliography).

## Background & Description

- **Scope and framing:** Whitley defines ichthyology as the "voice of the fishes" and presents his review as a spokesman's account of the "facts and relations" gathered about Australian fishes over roughly 350 years.
- **Earliest records:** Aboriginal fish paintings and rock engravings, some extending into historical times, are the oldest evidence. Written European records start in 1606, when Prado mentioned albacore and fish eggs in [[Torres Strait]].
- **Early European voyages:** [[Dampier's Voyage to New Holland]] (1703) contained the first published pictures of Australian fishes. [[James Cook]]'s [[Endeavour Voyage]] (1770) and the [[First Fleet]] naturalists added many species, including the [[Port Jackson Shark]] and [[Snapper]].
- **Early taxonomy:** [[Thunberg]]'s *Fauna Novae Hollandiae* (1822) was the first printed list of Australian fishes, though it recorded only two sharks. [[Linnaeus]]' *Systema Naturae* proved insufficient for the new fauna, so later authors added species.
- **Major expeditions:** [[Baudin Expedition]] (1800–1803) with [[François Péron]] and [[Charles Alexandre Le Sueur]], whose hundreds of specimen drawings remain unpublished in Le Havre. Also the [[Beagle Voyage]] (fishes described by Jenyns) and the [[Erebus and Terror Voyage]] (Richardson).
- **Freshwater exploration:** [[Evans]] (1813) first referred to [[Murray Cod]]. [[Oxley]] gave the first detailed account, and [[Mitchell]], [[Sturt]] and others recorded the freshwater fauna of the [[Murray River]] system.
- **Key figures:** [[Krefft]], who described the [[Queensland Lungfish]] in 1870; [[Gunther]], whose eight-volume *Catalogue of the Fishes in the British Museum* (1859–1870) enabled global naming; [[Macleay]], whose *Descriptive Catalogue of Australian Fishes* was a definitive work; [[McCulloch]], who built a card index and a name-list; and [[Waite]], whose illustrated descriptions set a standard.
- **Bibliographic tools:** [[Bashford Dean]]'s *Bibliography of Fishes* (1916–1923) and [[Sherborn]]'s *Index Animalium* (names proposed 1758–1850) were the main indexes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diversity:** Over 40,000 fish species are estimated worldwide, in more than 600 families. Australia had 1,291 known species in 1883 (Macleay) and about 2,450 in 1964. Whitley projected a total of three to four thousand once deep-sea fishes and planktonic larvae are catalogued.
- **Freshwater share:** Only about 7.6% of Australian species live in freshwater. Whitley argues that these fishes deserve conservation alongside koalas, kangaroos and waratahs because of their unique interest.
- **Deep sea and plankton:** Live animals were trawled from 400 fathoms by the [[Erebus and Terror Voyage]] in the Tasman Sea. The [[Dana Expedition]] and the [[Galathea Expedition]] added deep-sea records. Plankton surveys in New South Wales revealed larvae of genera not yet recorded from Australia.
- **Biogeography:** Whitley cites zoogeographic work on the [[Baudinian Region]] and the [[Dampierian-Banksian]] region, and the Great Barrier Reef as a major fish-diversity hotspot.
- **Symbiosis and behaviour:** The appendix lists commensal associations, including anemone fishes, fishes with echinoderms and sponges, pilot fish with sharks, and cormorant-fish interactions.
- **Acclimatization and introductions:** The 1860s "Acclimatization Societies" introduced foreign fishes, "often with dire results in due course."
- **Fisheries:** The [[Royal Commission on the Fisheries of New South Wales]] (1880) provided the first reliable regional data, and the [[CSIR Marine Biological Laboratory]] at Cronulla was a major research centre.

## Key Findings

1. **Knowledge has grown steadily but unevenly.** Early records came from explorers and voyagers. Later work was dominated by routine species description by overseas and local zoologists. Whitley notes that anatomical, physiological and skeletal work on Australian fishes was "almost an untouched field."
2. **Australian species count rose from 1,291 (1883) to about 2,450 (1964),** and the true total may reach three to four thousand.
3. **Large comprehensive works often remained unfinished.** Whitley calls Australian ichthyology "a series of fallen monuments," citing unfinished series by Ogilby, Waite, McCulloch and Munro. Good regional accounts exist for South Australia (Waite 1923; T. D. Scott 1962) and New South Wales (McCulloch 1922).
4. **Synthesis is needed.** Whitley argues that future work should be planned by international committees and symposia, and he notes a new trend toward single-species dossiers (e.g., Thompson 1963 on mullet; Robins 1962 on tuna).
5. **Major 19th-century taxonomic foundations** were laid by [[Gunther]], [[Cuvier]] and [[Valenciennes]] (22 volumes, 650 coloured plates), [[Richardson]], [[Bleeker]] (about 500 papers, roughly 1% on Australian fishes) and [[Macleay]].
6. **The Lungfish discovery** (Krefft, 1870) is highlighted as a "living fossil" milestone; Agassiz reportedly wrote "My fossil sharks are sharks no longer."
7. **Institutional development:** The [[Australian Museum]] (about 50,000 fish specimens, per the appendix), the [[Taronga Zoological Park]] Aquarium (opened 1927) and the CSIR Fisheries Division at Cronulla were central to the field's growth.
8. **Appendices provided:** (A) a subject index of about ninety main headings covering topics such as aborigines, acclimatization, behaviour, blind fishes, classification, coloration, conservation, deep-sea fishes, dentition and distribution; (B) a name-list of about 2,450 species; and (C) a bibliography. The subject index is a practical reference for future students.
