## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by Eville Gorham reports ionic composition analyses of rainwater collected at Rosscahill, County Galway, Ireland, near the boundary between Carboniferous limestone and older Connemara granites/schists/gneisses. Ten rain samples were collected between April 1955 and January 1956 and analyzed for major cations (sodium, magnesium, calcium, potassium) and anions (chloride, bicarbonate, sulphate, nitrate), along with pH. The study uses the sodium:chloride ratio in seawater as a baseline to partition ions in rain into **marine (sea-spray)** and **non-marine (terrestrial dust, biological, or pollution)** sources. Findings are compared against data from the English Lake District, highlighting regional differences driven by proximity to limestone bedrock, sea spray exposure, and atmospheric industrial pollution. The work is foundational to understanding [[Atmospheric Nutrient Deposition]] as an ecological input to nutrient-poor freshwater systems.

- pH values ranged widely across the ten samples, with the August sample notably alkaline (anomalous relative to the rest of the series).
- Overall average pH for the Galway series contrasted with a markedly more acidic average pH of 4.5 recorded in the [[English Lake District]] comparison dataset.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper frames dissolved ions in rain as an important nutrient source for habitats poorly supplied with minerals, including:
- **[[Mountain Lakes]]** — nutrient-poor standing waters reliant on atmospheric ion input.
- **[[Raised Bogs]]** and **[[Blanket Bogs]]** — ombrotrophic peatland systems dependent almost entirely on precipitation for mineral supply.
- **Rock surfaces** — substrates with negligible independent mineral leaching, making atmospheric deposition ecologically significant.
- **Bog pools and lough waters** (referenced from an in-preparation companion study) — peaty-area waters showed the highest non-marine sulphate levels, implicating biological (microbial) processes in local sulphur cycling.

Key ecological/geochemical processes identified:
- **Sea-spray derived ions**: Sodium and chloride predominate among the measured ions and closely track the Na:Cl ratio of seawater, indicating a marine aerosol source consistent with the site's coastal, western Irish location.
- **Terrestrial dust inputs**: Calcium and bicarbonate are overwhelmingly non-marine, attributed to calcium carbonate dust from adjacent Carboniferous limestone and calcareous beach sands of southwest Galway.
- **Non-marine magnesium and potassium**: Partially attributed to dust derived from the older Connemara silicate rocks (granite, schist, gneiss), which are comparatively enriched in these elements relative to limestone.
- **Biogenic sulphate hypothesis**: Non-marine sulphate is proposed to derive from oxidation of hydrogen sulphide released by biological activity in inshore marine muds and/or waterlogged, oxygen-deficient peats — supported by seasonal peaks in sulphate during spring/summer (periods of high biological metabolism) and by elevated non-marine sulphate in peaty bog pools sampled in a related study.
- **Anomalous dry-spell sample**: The August sample, collected after a prolonged dry period, showed an alkaline pH and exceptionally high potassium, sulphate, magnesium, sodium, and bicarbonate — consistent with elevated dust loading during dry conditions rather than typical wet-deposition chemistry.

## Key Findings

- **Cation/anion dominance**: Sodium is the predominant cation; chloride slightly exceeds bicarbonate among anions (excluding the anomalous August sample).
- **Marine origin proportions** (average of ten samples, method: ion:chloride ratio scaled from seawater composition):
  - Sodium: ~80–90% marine-derived
  - Magnesium: ~60–70% marine-derived
  - Potassium: ~20–30% marine-derived
  - Sulphate: ~20–30% marine-derived
  - Calcium and bicarbonate: only a small fraction marine-derived (dominated by non-marine, limestone-dust sources)
- **Total salt budget**: Just over 50% of total dissolved salts in Galway rain are of marine origin, compared to ~70% in the English Lake District.
- **Regional comparison with English Lake District** ([[Gorham 1955]] dataset):
  - Galway rain is richer in most ions overall.
  - Calcium and bicarbonate are strikingly higher in Galway, attributed to the site's proximity to Carboniferous limestone.
  - Sodium, chloride, and magnesium are higher in Galway, reflecting greater sea-spray exposure — though non-marine magnesium supply appears unique to the Irish site.
  - Sulphate and hydrogen ion (acidity) are higher in the Lake District, attributed to greater atmospheric pollution from industrial sulphur compounds oxidizing to sulphuric acid (mean pH 4.5 vs. Galway's less acidic profile).
- **Nitrate**: Consistently low across all samples, comparable to other unpolluted British coastal districts; only a minor fraction attributable to sea spray, with the remaining source unresolved.
- **Sampling/methodological note**: The August sample is identified as a clear statistical and chemical outlier (dry-spell dust effect) and is explicitly excluded from some comparative averages to avoid skewing the marine-origin calculations.
- **Open question**: The mechanism generating non-marine sulphate (biogenic H₂S oxidation from marine muds/peats vs. other pollution sources) remains unresolved and flagged for further investigation.
