# Invertebrates in an Intermittent Stream: Caldwell Hollow, Indiana (1963–1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-invertebrates #asellidae #crangonyctidae

*Note: The document is a 1960s freshwater ecology study of invertebrates rather than fish. Fish are mentioned only briefly. The tag line uses the taxa the study actually covers.*

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Hugh F. Clifford]] examines the invertebrate community of Caldwell Hollow, a small [[Intermittent Stream]] in the Norman Upland of south-central Indiana that is completely dry for part of nearly every summer and autumn. Fieldwork ran from October 1963 to June 1966. The stream hosts a resident aquatic fauna that survives dry periods through life-cycle timing, aestivation, and retreat into [[Subsurface Seepage]] and moist [[Interstitial Habitat]]. The author proposes that such streams are ecologically significant, not merely marginal, and that their faunas differ from those of [[Permanent Stream]] systems.

## Background & Description

- **Study stream:** Caldwell Hollow, mapped as a tributary of Salt Creek, in the Borden Formation of the Norman Upland, about 23 km east of Bloomington, Indiana. Bedrock is shale, sandstone and clay, and glacial outwash is absent from the hollow. The stream bed is mainly non-angular sandstone fragments, and the watershed is wooded.
- **Study reach:** Work concentrated on a sampling area of about 0.82 ha (approximate, as given in the source) containing the last pool to persist before the stream went dry.
- **Sampling:** Monthly collections from October 1963 to June 1966. Methods included spot quantitative samples, emergence and drift traps, and soil and seepage samples. Laboratory work covered reproduction, oxygen consumption, and humidity tolerance.
- **Context:** Intermittent and ephemeral streams make up much of the stream network in south-central North America, yet permanent streams have received most limnological attention.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and temperature.** The stream flowed in spring, formed isolated pools in summer, and was dry in late summer and early autumn. Pools re-formed after heavy autumn rains. Dissolved oxygen fell as leaf litter decomposed. Flow regimes followed the monthly precipitation records kept at the site.

**Dry-period refuges.** Organisms persisted in:
- subsurface seepage beneath the dry bed;
- moist interstitial spaces, where the largest surviving animals were found at the water-table margin;
- moist leaf litter in former pools;
- aestivating larvae or pupae in damp sediment.

**Humidity and soil.** [[Lirceus Fontinalis]] and [[Crangonyx Forbesi]] survived in water-saturated air for long periods, based on laboratory humidity chambers. Laboratory comparisons with Finnish pool crustaceans supplied additional survival data.

**Connectivity and recolonization.** The author found no clear permanent faunal reservoir in the stream itself. He suggested that isolated pools, or interstitial zones beneath former pools, may act as faunal refuges.

## Key Findings

1. **Resident fauna.** Caldwell Hollow hosted a small community of invertebrates, dominated by two malacostracan crustaceans, *L. fontinalis* and *C. forbesi*. Together they made up roughly 80 to 90% of organisms at times, based on the monthly composition figure.
2. **Life-cycle adaptations.** Spring-flowing fauna completed life cycles within about one year. Most were univoltine, and most aquatic insects emerged in spring. Caddisflies such as the *Ironoquia* species showed aestivation, and stoneflies survived dry spells as small nymphs in subsurface seepage.
3. **Isopod reproduction.** *L. fontinalis* showed an annual cycle, with juveniles becoming ovigerous females by late spring. Growth was arrested through the summer, and overwintering adults died by late July. Photoperiod was proposed as a possible factor in resuming growth.
4. **Amphipod reproduction.** *C. forbesi* was univoltine, with breeding from March to May. Males were smaller than females and tended to die first.
5. **Metabolism.** Oxygen consumption of *L. fontinalis* declined in late spring and summer when measured at 5 °C. A winter weight-specific regression coefficient of −0.29 was estimated at 13 °C. Seasonal changes in metabolic rate followed temperature closely but inversely.
6. **Humidity survival.** Interstitial and seepage-associated species survived water-saturated air longer than soil-dwelling forms. Survival time was reported in Table 7 for both Caldwell Hollow and Finnish pool crustaceans.
7. **Comparison with other streams.** The author ranked seven intermittent and permanent streams by permanence, from the Danish River Susaa to Caldwell Hollow. Faunal differences tracked the frequency and length of drying more closely than the flow-state classification alone.
8. **Drought in permanent streams.** Permanent streams in the Salt Creek area showed sparse invertebrate faunas after a 1964 poisoning. Caldwell Hollow, which was dry at the time, helped recolonize the poisoned reaches.
9. **Management implication.** The author argued that intermittent streams should be included in faunal and biological assessments, since they support specialized, life-cycle-adapted faunas and may contribute to the productivity of permanent waters.
