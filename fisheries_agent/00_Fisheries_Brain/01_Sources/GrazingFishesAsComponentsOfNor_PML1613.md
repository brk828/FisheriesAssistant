# Grazing Fishes as Components of North American Stream Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by William J. Matthews, Arthur J. Stewart and Mary E. Power, reviews how abundant algivorous (grazing) minnows of the genus [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma*) shape North American stream ecosystems. Field surveys, enclosure experiments and observations from streams in Oklahoma, Arkansas and the Midwest indicate that the distribution of stonerollers is shaped by predation from [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]], that stonerollers in turn control the standing crop and composition of attached algae ([[Periphyton]]), and that these effects cascade into nutrient cycling, invertebrate communities and the wider stream ecosystem. The authors argue that grazing fishes have been largely ignored in stream ecology and should be studied as major herbivores.

## Background & Description

- **Grazing fishes:** Most stream fishes in North America are predominantly herbivorous or omnivorous, but grazing is seldom studied in relation to ecosystem processes. Earlier reviews of stream ecology mentioned grazing fishes only briefly, and most studies focused on fish feeding behavior rather than ecosystem consequences.
- **Key genus:** Stonerollers (*Campostoma*) are among the most abundant and widespread algivorous minnows in North America. *C. anomalum* is widespread in the Midwest and central North America; *C. oligolepis* occurs in the Mobile Bay and Tennessee River drainages, and a third species occurs on the eastern Gulf coast. Adults commonly exceed 120 mm total length (TL) and can form large schools, sometimes dominating fish biomass.
- **Study sites:** The main site was [[Brier Creek]], a small prairie stream in the Red River drainage of south-central Oklahoma, with 14 sequential pools, where stonerollers and large predatory bass were studied from 1983 to 1984. Comparative surveys were carried out in [[Tyner Creek]], [[War Eagle Creek]], [[Baron Fork]] (Illinois River), and other Ozark and Ouachita streams, including the [[Ozark Uplift]] region.
- **Associated species:** Other algivores mentioned include the redbelly dace (*Phoxinus erythrogaster*) and [[Ozark Minnow]] (*Notropis pilsbryi*), which often co-occurs with stonerollers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predator–prey interactions.** Bass (*Micropterus* spp.) and stonerollers showed strong spatial segregation. In Brier Creek, stonerollers occupied pools lacking bass, and bass occupied pools where stonerollers were absent. Across surveyed Ozark pools, the two groups rarely co-occurred, a pattern interpreted as complementarity. Stonerollers were scarce or absent in Tyner Creek pools where bass and dense algal cover were present. Smallmouth bass ([[Smallmouth Bass]]) in Ozark streams were thought to be less effective predators on large stonerollers than [[Largemouth Bass]] in Brier Creek, because stonerollers of 150 mm and above are not uncommon in Ozark streams. Feeding experiments showed that medium-sized smallmouth bass readily consumed stonerollers as large as typical Brier Creek fish.

**Grazing and algal control.** In experimental pools and enclosures, stonerollers removed the upper algal layers, leaving distinct grazing scars. Stonerollers reduced the standing crop of attached algae to very low levels and favoured the growth of diatoms when grazing pressure was low. In the absence of grazing, blue-green algal felts (*Calothrix*, *Oscillatoria*) became dominant. Bass predation reduced stonerollers' grazing on algae, and algal biomass rose in pools where bass were present.

**Feeding behavior.** Stonerollers fed in large schools and used three grazing methods: nipping (fish nipping at detritus and epiphytes on cobbles), swiping (sharp sideways strikes removing blue-green and diatom felts from rock), and shoveling (pushing the lower jaw along substrate to remove algae). Schools grazed in a "grazing–drifting" sequence: a school grazed an area for 1–2.5 minutes, drifted 3–5 m downstream, then resumed grazing, moving upstream over time.

**Nutrient and carbon dynamics.** Stonerollers' feces accumulated in deeper pools, and nutrients released from feces could support algal growth. Grazed algae often showed higher turnover, and moderate grazing might increase primary productivity per unit algal biomass. Heavy grazing could instead lower productivity and convert nutrients into inaccessible forms. Blue-green algae fix atmospheric nitrogen, so grazing that favours *Calothrix* lawns could alter the nitrogen budget of streams.

**Effects on other stream biota.** Stonerollers were associated with fewer invertebrates in some pools, but sampling scale may have limited detection of differences. Fish communities in reaches dominated by stonerollers had lower diversity of other small fishes. Positive interactions were also possible: [[Ozark Minnow]] often occurred in the same pools, and may have benefited from invertebrates dislodged by stonerollers.

## Key Findings

- **Predation structures distribution:** Bass presence appears to control the spatial distribution of stonerollers; in Brier Creek, stonerollers and bass co-occurred in very few pools, and Ozark surveys showed a strong negative association between the two groups.
- **Grazing controls algae:** Stonerollers can maintain low standing crops of attached algae (reported at about 100–325 g dry weight/m² in grazed Ozark streams versus much higher crops where grazing was absent), and they alter the algal community composition from blue-green felts to diatoms.
- **Grazing scars are extensive:** In Tyner Creek, grazing scars averaged roughly 1,900 per m² in water less than 20 cm deep and exceeded 5,000 per m² in deeper areas, where as much as 28.5% of the surface area of substrate was recently grazed.
- **Herbivory in Ozark streams:** In Baron Fork, stonerollers were extremely abundant, and the standing algal crop was consistently low. The authors conclude that stonerollers are likely the major herbivores in this and many other Ozark streams.
- **Tile experiment:** Substrates incubated in troughs excluding fishes developed diatom-dominated algae, while tiles exposed to stonerollers were colonized by blue-green felts; this demonstrated that grazing alters algal taxonomic composition.
- **Experimental bass removal:** Electrofishing to remove bass led to a rapid increase in stonerollers in the removal side of pools, which reduced attached algae within about two weeks.
- **Cascading effects:** The authors propose that stonerollers initiate a cascade affecting nutrient uptake and release, primary productivity, decomposition, and the spatial heterogeneity of biotic processes, with the effects dependent on flow regime.
- **Research gap:** The authors note that much remains unknown about grazing fish dynamics, algal distributions and the large-scale consequences of grazing, and they call for similar study of other abundant North American stream fishes.
