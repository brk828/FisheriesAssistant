# Competition among Juveniles of Coho Salmon, Brook Trout, and Brown Trout in a Laboratory Stream, and Implications for Great Lakes Tributaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 study by [[Kurt D. Fausch]] and [[Ray J. White]] examines interspecific competition among juvenile [[Coho Salmon]], [[Brook Trout]], and [[Brown Trout]] in streams of the [[Laurentian Great Lakes]]. Field sampling in [[Lake Michigan]] tributaries showed that coho salmon emerged 2-3 weeks earlier and were larger than resident trout. Controlled [[Laboratory Stream Aquarium]] experiments with equal-size pairs showed coho salmon dominated both trout species, and brook trout dominated brown trout. The authors conclude that juvenile coho salmon could exclude juvenile brook and brown trout from profitable stream positions when resources are limiting, although the laboratory results rest on equal-size fish in a simplified, cover-free environment.

## Background & Description

Concern existed in several regions that introduced [[Pacific Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus* spp.) could harm [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], or [[Atlantic Salmon]] when juveniles reared together in streams. Coho and [[Chinook Salmon]] were first introduced to the Great Lakes in 1966-1967, and large numbers of smolts have been stocked since. Brook trout are the only native salmonid among the stream community; the others (steelhead, pink, coho, chinook) are introduced or anadromous.

The authors identified five possible interactions between sympatric salmonids: adult competition for spawning sites, predation by adult trout, redd excavation reducing invertebrate food, pesticide accumulation from eggs, and juvenile competition for food and space. Juvenile competition was judged most likely to have long-term effects on resident trout. Coho salmon were chosen as the focus because they remain in nursery streams about 12 months on average, the longest of the Pacific salmon, and because none of the three species coevolved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream positions:** Juvenile salmonids choose focal points that maximize potential profit (drift energy minus swimming cost), and dominance hierarchies determine access to these positions (see [[Potential Profit]]).
- **Field sites:** Eight sites on six Lake Michigan tributaries were sampled in 1979 at about 3-week intervals from April or May to September. Sampling used backpack [[Electrofishing]] and hand-netting of newly emerged fish. Coho salmon were abundant in several sites, while brook trout were scarce where brown trout or coho were present.
- **Laboratory stream:** A 7.28 m sinuous, recirculating [[Stream Aquarium]] (30 cm wide, sections of 3.64 m with riffles and a pool) was maintained at 15 ± 0.5°C with 100% dissolved oxygen. Water chemistry ranges were alkalinity 104-217 mg/L CaCO3, pH 8.09-8.53, NH3-N 0.01-0.05 mg/L, and NO2-N 2.01-4.44 mg/L. A simulated [[Invertebrate Drift]] of 1-2 mm [[Daphnia]] was delivered at the upstream end.
- **Cover:** The aquarium had little visual isolation beyond four 10-cm rocks per section, a condition the authors later identified as a limit on trout growth.

## Key Findings

**Field emergence and size (Lake Michigan tributaries, 1979)**
- Coho salmon emerged 2-3 weeks earlier than brown and brook trout.
- Emerging coho were 6-7 mm (22-24%) larger than newly emerged brown trout in Pine Creek and Green River, and about 5.5-8.5 mm (18-32%) larger than brook trout at emergence.
- Through the first summer, coho were 7-28% longer than brown trout at Bigelow Creek, 4-54% longer at Pine Creek, and 11-42% longer at Green River. Weight differences reached 0.3-4.1 g.
- Coho were 11-46% longer than brook trout in Sand Creek during summer.
- Brook and brown trout were of nearly equal size and growth in Egypt Creek and in allopatric streams (brook trout only 1-8% longer).

**Laboratory competition (three experiments, equal-size fish)**

| Pair | Dominant species in sympatry | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Brook trout vs. brown trout | Brook trout | Brook trout held positions significantly farther upstream (P = 0.016) |
| Brook trout vs. coho salmon | Coho salmon | Coho held farther upstream positions (P < 0.001) |
| Brown trout vs. coho salmon | Coho salmon | Coho held farther upstream positions (P < 0.002, Mann-Whitney U) |

- Subordinate species shifted upstream to more profitable positions and increased niche breadth when released from competition: brown trout shifted upstream (P = 0.018) and brook trout expanded their range in allopatry.
- Coho salmon positions changed little in allopatry, suggesting intraspecific hierarchy shaped their choices.
- Most coho gained or maintained weight in sympatry, while nearly all brook and brown trout lost weight.
- Coho produced more agonistic acts than trout, mostly directed at other coho. Trout interacted more often with coho than with conspecifics during sympatry.

**Growth rates**
- Laboratory specific growth rates for coho salmon (about 0.010-0.017 per day) matched field rates.
- Laboratory growth of brook and brown trout was much lower than in the field. The authors attributed this to strong intraspecific competition in a cover-poor aquarium, where dominant trout defended large areas.
- Small caveat: all fish were fed about 9% of wet weight daily, and most trout lost weight, which the authors noted as a possible feeding-level effect.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Coho salmon are the dominant competitor over both trout species at equal size. Brook trout appear dominant over brown trout in this setting, although the authors noted that adult brown trout dominated adult brook trout in a Michigan stream and that Swedish work shows dominance may shift with size, age, or temperature.
- Field studies by Taube (1975) and Stauffer (1977) are consistent with these results. Taube found compensatory survival in brown trout, while Stauffer suggested coho depress brook and brown trout numbers.
- Coho salmon produce more young per spawner than the trout species, so their young far outnumbered age-0 brook and brown trout in the study streams.
- The authors urge managers to consider how increases in coho populations might affect resident brook and brown trout in tributary streams, particularly where gravel or cover is limiting.
