# Conservation: Tactics for a Constant Crisis

#conservation-biology #biodiversity #marine-biology

## Executive Summary

This pair of *Science* Perspectives essays from 1991 argues that conservation policy, reserve design, and species listing are failing to keep pace with the global loss of biodiversity. The first piece, "Conservation: Tactics for a Constant Crisis," describes a [[Biospatial Hierarchy]] of conservation targets, six classes of human interference, and seven underlying sources of biotic degradation, and it compares conservation needs in poorer and wealthier nations. It concludes that reserves alone will not suffice and that the United States needs a national biodiversity strategy. The second piece, "An Evolutionary Basis for Conservation Strategies" by [[Terry L. Erwin]], argues that conservation goals should be anchored in evolutionary processes such as [[Speciation]] and that current human impacts threaten those processes.

## Background & Description

The first essay opens by noting that conservation targets are usually chosen at the level of species, populations, or genes, yet most strategies are geographically framed. It proposes five levels in a nested biospatial hierarchy:

1. Whole systems at the landscape or seascape scale
2. Biotic assemblages (associations and communities)
3. [[Species]]
4. Populations
5. Genes

The authors state that ecosystems are the most practical top level, since they match interacting biotic communities to physical topography and drainage. They note that conservation has historically focused on large-bodied or attractive endangered species, and that species-rich regions, coral reefs, and areas with many local endemics are frequently overlooked.

The U.S. listing process, administered by the Fish and Wildlife Service, is described as bogged down. Recovery plans exist for nearly half of the roughly 600 species officially listed as endangered, and the authors say the handful of recovering species is balanced by a similar number that may be extinct. The essay also criticizes the [[Endangered Species Act]] debate, which it says pits "single species" approaches against ecosystem approaches.

The second essay, by Erwin, opens with a historical perspective. Protected areas were originally set aside as hunting grounds for aristocracies, and in the present day, with a human population of 5.3 billion, no more than 4,500 areas are protected globally. The authors estimate this covers only 3.2% of the planet's landmass. Erwin argues that conservation today rests on a perceived impending loss of biodiversity due to [[Tropical Deforestation]], and that inventory data from [[Science]] have lagged behind conservation campaigns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Six classes of human interference** undermine the biospatial hierarchy:

- Loss of habitat
- Habitat fragmentation, with deleterious area, edge, demographic, and genetic effects
- Overexploitation of species
- Spread of exotic species and diseases ([[Exotic Species]])
- Air, soil, and water pollution ([[Pollution]])
- [[Climate Change]]

The essay stresses that the strength of these factors varies by place and time. Tropical species are described as particularly vulnerable to habitat loss and fragmentation because of their vast numbers and small geographic ranges. Introduced predators are described as more damaging on oceanic islands than on continents. Overharvesting of economically important species is described as a greater concern in poorer nations, while pollution and climate are described as major threats in temperate-zone nations.

**Seven fundamental causes** of biotic degradation are listed in a table: [[Population Growth]], poverty, misperception, [[Anthropocentrism]], cultural transitions, economics, and policy implementation. The authors give human population figures of 1 billion around 1800, with projections of 10 billion by 2046 and 12 billion by 2100. They argue that such numbers are incompatible with the persistence of large predators, annual bird migrations, speciation in large organisms, and native biotas.

**Conservation systems** are compared in a table of eight approaches, covering [[In Situ Conservation]], [[Ex Situ Conservation]], restoration, and [[Agroecosystems and Agroforestry]]. Targets range from entire ecosystems and processes to endemic species, population-level diversity, and domesticated varieties. Ownership categories include public, private, and mixed arrangements. The second essay notes that frequent conservation targets are charismatic species, and that managing a small number of indicator species may not protect the wider assemblage.

## Key Findings

- **Hierarchy matters:** Effective conservation requires attention to the nested levels of the [[Biospatial Hierarchy]], because most strategies are geographically framed and species alone are insufficient targets.
- **Protected areas are not enough:** The authors argue that a network of protected areas alone will fail, given the size of the threats. Many reserves in less-developed nations are "paper parks," and reserves in developed nations are threatened by tourism and energy policy.
- **Nations differ:** Conservation tactics must be tailored to location. Tropical nations face habitat loss and overharvesting, while temperate and wealthier nations face pollution and climate impacts.
- **Overexploitation in reserves:** A figure cited in the text is that 95% of tropical reserves report poaching of wildlife.
- **Ex situ tools have limits:** [[Captive Breeding]] and [[Cryopreservation]] are described as important backups, but the authors say they cover only a tiny fraction of threatened species and cannot replace protected habitats.
- **Sustainable use:** Harvesting of nuts, latex, and other non-timber products is presented as a conservation option where populations and ecological conditions support it.
- **Policy gap:** The United States is urged to adopt a national biodiversity strategy and possibly create a new national institute for biodiversity, with restructuring of existing agencies such as the Forest Service and the Bureau of Land Management.
- **Evolutionary framing:** Erwin argues that the speciation process, which creates future biodiversity, is being severely pressured by the fragmentation of continental habitats into island-like patches, and that conservation must address these processes.
- **Adaptive approach:** The authors call for experimentation and humility, noting that currently fashionable conservation systems may be replaced within the coming century, and that conservation scientists must accommodate their objectives to social complexity and scale.
