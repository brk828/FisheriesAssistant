# Evaluation of Tagging Quality and Retention in Bonytail Chub as a Surrogate Species for Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report, submitted to the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]] by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (author: Mike Childs, September 2002), evaluates several marking techniques for use on endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the Little Colorado River. Hatchery-reared [[Bonytail Chub]] served as surrogates. The techniques tested were [[PIT Tag]] (passive integrated transponder) insertion, dorsal and pelvic fin clips, [[Elastomer Dye]] injection, and [[India Ink]] injection. PIT tags provide long-term individual identification, while the other marks serve as short-term batch marks for [[Mark-Recapture]] studies and for estimating PIT-tag loss in the field.

The study recommends PIT-tag needles dipped in India ink as a secondary mark, pelvic fin clips for fish too small to PIT-tag, and further field evaluation of elastomer dye. It also calls for a standardized tagging training session for field biologists.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*), used as a surrogate for the federally endangered Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*).
- **Source of fish:** Bonytail delivered from a Utah national fish hatchery to [[Bubbling Ponds Hatchery]] on June 6, 2002.
- **Holding conditions:** A single outdoor holding tank (described in the source as 1900 L, OCR-uncertain), aerated and supplied with artesian spring water. Fish were fed daily with commercial pellets.
- **Experimental groups:**
  - *PIT-tagging (June 10, 2002):* Biologists each inserted 20 PIT tags, using the hypodermic technique common in field work. Fish were not fed before tagging. Fish were measured (total length and fork length), weighed, and held in a second tank, with a control group held separately.
  - *PIT-tagging (August 13, 2002):* 120 fish were starved overnight to simulate field collection in hoop nets. 60 were tagged with needles dipped in India ink.
  - *Fin clips (June 10, 2002):* 100 fish received a left pelvic fin clip, 100 an upper caudal fin clip, and 100 both. A 100-fish control group received no clip.
  - *Surface marks (August 13, 2002):* 25 fish each received India ink at the base of the dorsal fin, orange elastomer dye at the base of the dorsal fin, or elastomer dye between the eyes. Fish previously PIT-tagged in June (n = 15) allowed evaluation of individual mark retention and of false positives.
- **Monitoring:** Tag and mark retention were checked on July 9 and September 11, 2002. Mortalities were preserved in 10% formalin and necropsied.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses monitoring of an endangered native cyprinid in the Colorado River basin. Reliable marking is needed because mark loss biases population estimates. The report does not present population or habitat data beyond the hatchery setting. Water quality recorded for the holding tank was artesian spring water, aerated, with a temperature of about 18.5 °C and pH of about 7.6 (OCR-uncertain). Mortality was observed only in PIT-tagged fish, and the report links it to handling and tagging technique rather than to environmental conditions.

## Key Findings

**1. PIT-tag mortality and tag loss**
- June-tagged fish (no overnight feeding): low mortality. PIT-tag retention was high at 95.6%, with no further loss between June and September.
- August-tagged fish (fed overnight, then tagged): 18 of 120 died (15.0%). No deaths occurred in the control group.
- Necropsies showed most deaths resulted from intestinal perforation during insertion. Many tags lay anterior to the pectoral fin insertion, suggesting migration after insertion or deep injection.
- Overnight feeding likely made perforation more likely because the intestines were full of food.
- Ten of the 18 August mortalities were from one tagger (about 56%), so the authors recommend a training and discussion session for all field biologists.
- August PIT-tag retention was lower at 82.2%. The authors suspect a faulty battery or power supply in the scanner and plan re-scanning in October-November 2003.

**2. Dorsal fin clip (as secondary mark for PIT-tagged fish)**
- Visible in 83.9% of fish in July and 10.8% in September.
- Judged inadequate as a secondary mark. Many fish had only half or a quarter of the fin removed, and fins regrew.

**3. India ink on PIT-tag needles (secondary mark)**
- Retention of the black scar was high (reported value garbled in scan, approximately 90% or higher) for at least one month.
- Only 10.8% of these fish retained a visible tag scar in September.
- Conclusion: dipping the tag and needle in India ink works adequately as a secondary mark for field evaluation of tag loss.

**4. Fin clip classification (approximately one month after marking)**
- 95 fish had a left pelvic clip, 97 had an upper caudal clip, and 103 had both. Three had no visible clip, and two escaped before examination.
- Overall correct classification was 95.3%, but the authors consider this inflated. All fish were known to be marked, which field conditions would not allow.
- Retention and classification would improve if the entire fin were removed.

**5. Elastomer dye**
- Dorsal fin: 24 of 25 fish retained the mark (96%). Between the eyes: 23 of 25 (92%).
- Marks are highly visible and easy to identify in the field, and appear to last longer than fin clips. Re-evaluation was planned for October 2002 (three months post-injection).

**6. India ink injection below dorsal fin**
- 15 of 25 fish retained the mark (60%), which was much lower than fin clips and elastomer dye.
- Judged not viable as a primary tagging technique.

**7. Recommendations**
- Use PIT-tag needles dipped in India ink as a secondary mark for field tag-loss studies. The method appears to work well, at least as a short-term mark. Longer-term retention should be tested at the hatchery.
- Use pelvic fin clips as the mark for fish too small to PIT-tag.
- Evaluate elastomer dye in the field as a secondary mark for fish too small to PIT-tag.
- Refine specific tagging methods and develop a training program for field biologists who will PIT-tag Humpback Chub.
