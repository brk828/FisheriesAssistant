# Some Merits of Three Kinds of Markers Used in a Fish Tagging Operation on Lake Erie

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #fisheries-management #fish-tagging

## Executive Summary

This study, conducted by the [[Ohio Division of Wildlife]] beginning in 1952, evaluated three tag types—**strap tags**, **streamer-type tags**, and **monel metal ring-type jaw tags**—in a large-scale [[Lake Erie]] fish movement study. Over two years, approximately 60,685 fish across 27 species were tagged and released, with [[Yellow Perch]], [[Walleye]], [[Blue Pike]], [[White Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Sheepshead (Freshwater Drum)]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] comprising over 90% of tagged individuals. The ring-type jaw tag emerged as the most practical marker due to ease of application and durability, despite having a slightly lower recovery rate (1.8%) than the strap tag (2.5%). The study combined field tagging operations with aquarium-based retention experiments to assess tag performance, fish injury, and long-term durability.

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily methodological (tagging technique) rather than a morphological study of the fish themselves, it provides relevant anatomical placement data for each tag type:

- **Strap tag**: Attached to the **gill cover (operculum)**; found awkward to apply due to tag shape and poorly fitting pliers. Appeared to injure highly active fish upon application.
- **Streamer-type tag**: Attached to the **dorsal surface**, secured with nylon thread via specially prepared surgical needles; application was delicate, slow, and required significant preparation.
- **Ring-type jaw tag**: Clamped around the **lower jaw** (with later suggestion that the **premaxillary/upper lip** might be superior); made of **monel metal**, .020 inch by .030 inch width stock, sizes 8 and 10 recommended as adequate for most [[Lake Erie]] fish species. Smaller tags, if not clamped too tightly, caused less irritation and jaw wear than larger tags.

Species-specific retention behavior (from aquarium observations) revealed structural/behavioral differences relevant to tag suitability:
- [[Channel Catfish]] and other catfishes (bullheads, shovelhead catfish) tended to tear off or lose streamer and strap tags, but retained ring-type jaw tags exceptionally well.
- Scaled species (e.g., [[Yellow Perch]], [[Largemouth Bass]]) retained streamer tags longer than catfishes, plausibly due to scale structure providing better anchor points for sutures.
- [[Northern Pike]] (Esox lucius) lost strap tags rapidly (within two months) in aquarium trials.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study covers a broad cross-section of the [[Lake Erie]] fish community, sampled via **fyke nets**, **commercial trap nets**, and **drag seines**. Key ecological/operational notes:

- **Capture and handling stress**: Heavy trap net gear caused high mortality during hot weather; trap net use was **discontinued in July and August** to reduce handling-related mortality.
- **Movement patterns**: Recoveries of jaw-tagged fish indicated movements at least **lake-wide**, suggesting significant mobility for tagged species within [[Lake Erie]].
- **Growth under tagging**: Several species showed continued growth while carrying ring-type jaw tags, though whether observed growth rates were normal or abnormal was not conclusively determined.
- **Fishery interaction**: Commercial trap net fisheries appeared to "overlook" tagged fish more often than hook-and-line anglers, likely due to the high volume of fish processed in commercial operations. Partial data suggested a roughly equal number of returns from commercial and recreational sources despite commercial fisheries harvesting a much greater volume of fish overall — implying a much lower reporting rate per fish caught commercially.
- **Underreporting**: The study estimated that **at least 50%** of tagged fish captured were never reported, despite active publicity efforts (press, radio, conservation agency involvement).

## Key Findings

### Recovery Rates (Two-Year Period)
| Tag Type | Number Applied | Recovery Rate | Cost (per 1,000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strap tag | 274 | 2.5% | ~$22.00 |
| Streamer-type tag | ~1,000 | 1.3% | ~$28.00 (+ nylon thread & needles) |
| Ring-type jaw tag | ~56,411 | 1.8% | ~$22.00–$27.60 |

### [[Strap Tag]]
- Difficult to apply as a gill cover marker due to awkward shape and poorly fitting application pliers.
- Appeared to injure highly active fish during tagging.
- Except in catfishes, a large percentage of tags were lost within several months of release.
- Most recoveries occurred within **five months** post-release.
- Long-term aquarium data (Wascko, 1947–1952) showed variable retention: only ~10% of bass species retained tags for one year; ~30% of [[Bluegill]] and [[Pumpkinseed]] retained tags for one year; ~75% of catfishes retained tags for one year.

### [[Streamer-Type Tag]]
- Delicate, slow to apply, and required considerable pre-tagging preparation.
- Prone to becoming untied, especially in catfishes and under rough water/cold conditions — though the application method was later improved.
- Highly visible; commercial fishermen reported finding shed tags in boats and gear, suggesting good detectability.
- Long-lasting on scaled species (e.g., intact on [[Largemouth Bass]] after 14 months) but poorly retained on catfishes (5 of 8 catfish lost tags within 18 hours in simulated stream conditions due to nylon thread catching on rocks).
- Perch were observed nipping at tags initially but adapted over time.

### [[Ring-Type Jaw Tag]] (Monel Metal)
- Rapidly and easily applied — a four-man crew tagged **800 perch in one hour**.
- Longest recovery time recorded: up to **25 months**, with related work (Clark) showing recoveries up to **~4 years** post-tagging.
- Especially long-lasting on [[Channel Catfish]] (tagged in 1952, still intact and in excellent condition in aquarium).
- Smaller tags (sizes 8 and 10, .020 x .030 inch monel stock) caused less jaw irritation/chafing than larger tags; oversized tags showed a wearing/inflammatory effect on jaw tissue.
- Aquarium trials indicated minimal effect on feeding behavior compared to untagged control fish.
- Suggested improvement: tags clamped around the **premaxillary (upper lip)** rather than lower jaw might improve retention/success further.
- Recoveries from commercial fish markets and inland pay lakes suggest jaw tags are **not easily noticed** during high-volume commercial processing, though some fishermen disputed this, considering them as noticeable as other tag types.

### General Observations
- No formal statistical comparison was made between hook-and-line and commercial recovery rates, but partial data suggest hook-and-line anglers returned a **higher percentage** of tagged fish relative to catch volume than commercial fishermen.
- Tag underreporting is a significant confound, estimated at ≥50% of captured tagged fish.
- Aquarium experimentation (led by [[Harold Wascko]] at the Toledo Zoo Aquarium) was a critical tool for assessing tag retention and fish injury under controlled conditions, complementing field recovery data.
