# Roundtail Chub and Headwater Chub: Public Comments on the 2005 90-Day Petition Finding

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of public comments and a Federal notice concerning a petition to list a [[Distinct Population Segment]] of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) in the Lower Colorado River basin and to list the [[Headwater Chub]] (*Gila nigra*) as endangered or threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]], with critical habitat. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) issued a positive 90-day finding on July 12, 2005 (70 FR 39981–39986), and began a status review. Commenters, including [[Paul Marsh]], [[Sally E. Stefferud]], and the [[Desert Fishes Team]], uniformly argued that the petition was accurate and that federal listing as endangered, with critical habitat, is necessary. The comments also argue that the draft [[Range-Wide Conservation Agreement]] and [[Arizona Statewide Conservation Agreement]] do not meet the [[PECE Criteria]] and therefore cannot substitute for listing.

## Background & Description

**Species description.** Both species are [[Cyprinidae]] minnows with streamlined bodies. Roundtail chub are olive-gray to silvery with a lighter belly and sometimes dark sides blotches; they are generally 25 to 35 cm (9 to 14 in) long and can reach 50 cm (20 in). Headwater chub are dark gray to brown overall with silvery sides and are generally smaller, likely due to the smaller streams they inhabit. Both were recognized as distinct species following taxonomic revision (Minckley and DeMarais 2000; Nelson et al. 2004).

**Historical and current distribution.** Voeltz (2002) estimated historical roundtail chub occupancy in the lower basin at approximately 4,500 km (2,796 mi) of rivers and streams in Arizona and New Mexico. Headwater chub historically occupied about 500 km (312 mi). Current roundtail populations in Arizona include Chevelon and East Clear Creeks, several Bill Williams tributaries, the Salt River system, the Verde River tributaries, and Aravaipa Creek; Headwater chub occur in Fossil Creek, the East Verde River, Tonto Creek tributaries, and parts of the upper Gila River in New Mexico.

**Ecology.** Roundtail chub use deep pools and eddies and are omnivorous. Spawning occurs February through June at roughly 18–22 °C. Headwater chub favor nearshore pools next to swifter riffles and runs over sand and gravel; their diet includes aquatic insects, ostracods, and plant material.

**Regulatory history.** Headwater chub was a Category 2 candidate in 1982, and roundtail chub in 1989. Both remained candidates through 1994, and category 2 status was discontinued in 1996.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats identified in the petition and comments:**
- **Habitat loss and degradation:** livestock grazing, water withdrawal, dams, roads, recreation, mining, urban development, and channelization.
- **Nonnative species:** predatory and competing fishes (e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], flathead catfish, green sunfish) and parasites such as *Lernaea* (anchor worm).
- **Wildfire and drought:** post-fire ash and sediment flows, with the 1990 Dude Fire cited as severely impacting East Verde River fish.
- **Urban growth:** Gila County grew about 20% between 2000 and 2005 (text as given: 2000 to 2008 comparison); increased water use and watershed change in the Verde corridor.
- **Regulatory inadequacy:** rollbacks in environmental protections, declining federal support, and interference with science, according to Stefferud.

**Population status (Voeltz 2002).** Of 40 recently documented roundtail populations in the lower basin, 6 were stable-threatened, 13 were unstable-threatened, 10 were extirpated, and the remainder unknown. Of 19 headwater chub populations, 6 were stable-threatened, 6 unstable-threatened, 1 stable-secure, 3 extirpated, and 3 unknown. Deadman Creek was invaded by green sunfish and was reclassified as stable-threatened.

**Recent conservation actions.** The decommissioning of the Childs/Irving hydropower plant and return of natural flows to Fossil Creek is the principal benefit since 2002. A barrier and nonnative fish removal were completed in 2004; over 15 years and more than $750,000 were spent.

## Key Findings

**Service finding (July 12, 2005).**
- The petition presented substantial scientific and commercial information that listing may be warranted.
- A status review was initiated, expected to take about 9 months.
- Emergency listing was judged not warranted at this time, given the number of extant populations and apparent stability of some.
- Critical habitat would be addressed at the proposed rule stage if listing is found warranted.
- Public comments were due by September 12, 2005.

**Distinct Population Segment analysis.**
- **Discreteness:** Molecular evidence (allozymes and mtDNA) shows that lower-basin and upper-basin populations differ significantly, and Gerber et al. (2001) found lower-basin mtDNA entirely absent from the upper basin.
- **Significance:** Lower-basin populations constitute a majority of the species' range in Arizona and New Mexico and include unique ecological settings.

**Commenters' main arguments:**
- Marsh (Arizona State University) concluded that listing as endangered and critical habitat are "necessary," and that status quo is unacceptable.
- Stefferud (retired Service biologist) reported that the petition is accurate, that the Desert Fishes Recovery Team recommended listing repeatedly from 1992 to 2002, and that the Service's 2005 PECE policy requires certainty of implementation.
- The Desert Fishes Team recommended listing and critical habitat in its October 15, 2003 Report Number 7.

**Assessment of conservation agreements (Stefferud).**
- The draft Arizona Statewide Conservation Agreement and Strategy were judged to fail 13 of 14 PECE criteria.
- Shortcomings include no committed funding, voluntary participation with 60-day withdrawal, unsigned status, a five-year duration, vague objectives, and no implementation schedule.
- The persistence goal was described as inconsistent with Endangered Species Act goals of recovery.
- New Mexico's headwater chub recovery plan was in draft, with final approval scheduled for December 2005.

**Distribution corrections.** Stefferud asked that Eagle Creek, Wilder, Canyon, Cartiza, Cedar, Cibecue, and Corduroy Creeks, and the Black and White Rivers be included as likely roundtail habitat, and that the lower Agua Fria and San Simon Rivers be added to historic range.

**Process points.** The Center for Biological Diversity submitted the petition on April 2, 2003 (received April 14, 2003). The Service's 2005 finding followed a court-ordered settlement.
