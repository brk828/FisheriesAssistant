# Environmental Issues in the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This conference paper, presented by Dale Pontius at the American Bar Association's 16th Annual Water Law Conference (February 19-20, 1998, San Diego, California), reviews environmental conflicts in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It covers the basin's natural ecosystem, the effects of development, and the legal and institutional disputes that shape recovery of [[Endangered Fish]]. The paper focuses on the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), [[Law of the River]] constraints, Indian water rights, the [[Salton Sea]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and the [[Colorado River Delta]]. It closes with recommendations for consolidated recovery planning, a basin-wide coordinating council, dedicated environmental funding, and a binational restoration commission.

## Background & Description

The paper describes the Colorado River as a highly variable system with natural flows of 6.0 to 24 maf/yr. About 70% of flow occurs from May to July, and the river carries roughly 85 million tons of sediment annually. The basin is home to 37 recorded native fish species, 23 of which occur only in the basin, the highest endemic percentage in North America. A vast delta ecosystem was formed by flood flows.

Development has degraded this system through:
- Habitat loss and fragmentation from dams and diversions
- Over 60 maf of storage behind more than 20 major dams
- Diversion of one-third of supply for use outside the basin
- Changed hydrographs and higher water temperatures
- Introduction of exotic species
- Rising salinity and point and non-point source pollution

Three native fish species are extinct, and 17 are listed as threatened or endangered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish recovery programs.** Three separate programs address endangered fish:
- **[[Upper Basin Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP):** Provides instream flows, floodplain and rearing habitat restoration, restocking, genetic conservation, exotic species control, fish passage, and modified flow regimes. It serves as the ESA "reasonable and prudent alternative" (RPA) because it shows "sufficient progress" toward recovery. Open issues include junior-rights instream flows, cost sharing (estimated at about $100 million), and how to measure recovery.
- **[[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]] (SJRRIP):** Created in 1992 after the [[Animas-La Plata Project]] jeopardy opinion. It covers two listed fish and other species of concern. Remaining capital costs are estimated at $15 million, plus operating costs of $800,000 per year. Tribal participation is complicated by competing demands for flows.
- **[[Lower Basin Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP):** Triggered by critical habitat designation for four "big river fish" and a 1997 Biological Opinion (BO). The 50-year plan aims to recover listed species and prevent new listings, with more than 102 species initially identified for study. Its steering committee is now the Ecosystem Conservation & Recovery Implementation Team (ECRIT).

**Litigation and law.**
- In *Southwest Center for Biological Diversity v. U.S. Bureau of Reclamation* (D. Ariz., Aug. 22, 1997), the plaintiffs sought to keep [[Lake Mead]] at or below 1178 feet to protect [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] nesting sites. The District Court denied the injunction and granted summary judgment for the defendants. The case was on appeal to the Ninth Circuit, and rising lake levels later inundated the delta habitat.
- The [[Law of the River]] question is whether the Bureau of Reclamation can manage reservoir levels for non-consumptive purposes such as habitat protection.
- The U.S.-Mexico treaty requires delivery of 1.5 maf annually plus a share of surpluses, with salinity standards. The [[Cienega de Santa Clara]] is now a large wetland supporting the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and [[Desert Pupfish]], and advocates argue these species fall under the ESA.

**Indian water rights.** Tribal rights often have early priority dates, but most remain undeveloped because of funding gaps, off-reservation marketing limits, or incomplete quantification. The Navajo Nation argues that water set aside for endangered species is inequitable and that reserved rights should count as perfected rights in the environmental baseline. The Animas-La Plata Project, authorized in 1968, has been blocked by NEPA and ESA lawsuits. A compromise "ALP Lite" was proposed, but environmental groups still oppose it.

**Other issues.**
- **Salton Sea:** California's largest lake is a key Pacific Flyway stopover. Its salinity is reported as 25% higher than the ocean and rising. Avian botulism and other causes have killed many birds, including endangered Brown Pelicans. Proposed solutions include a new desalter, canals to and from the Gulf of California, and dividing the Sea into two parts. All options are costly.
- **Glen Canyon Dam:** Completed in 1963, it was modified under the Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992 to rebuild beaches, transport sediment, restore back-channel fish habitat, and remove exotic vegetation. A citizens' Environmental Assessment is evaluating a proposal to drain Lake Powell and operate the dam for flood control only. Opposition from water and power users, states, and recreation interests is strong.

## Key Findings

1. **Fragmented recovery governance.** Three sub-basin programs for endangered fish lack a common recovery goal or coordinated funding. The author recommends one multi-species recovery plan with clearly defined goals.
2. **Funding pressure.** Total funding needs are expected to reach hundreds of millions of dollars over the next decade. The author questions why there is not one federal budget for basin recovery.
3. **Section 7 process concerns.** Water users want more public input into RPAs, clearer and more realistic mitigation requirements, and stable decisions. The author suggests adaptive management within reauthorization of the ESA.
4. **Environmental trust fund.** The paper recommends a dedicated fund financed by surcharges on transfers and banked water. It notes that a $5 annual increase for more than 20 million water consumers would raise a substantial amount.
5. **Binational delta initiative.** The paper recommends a Binational Commission to assess delta restoration. It argues that the delta, in the "white spot on the map" context, is a strong opportunity for restoration. It also proposes a comprehensive binational study, possibly using NAFTA processes.
6. **Firm water supply for the Cienega.** A replacement supply is needed if the YDP is operated, because the Cienega currently relies on non-firm Wellton-Mohawk discharge.
7. **Glen Canyon debate.** The paper treats the Glen Canyon debate as a sign of shifting public values toward ecosystem protection. It notes that many early dam proponents later expressed regret.
