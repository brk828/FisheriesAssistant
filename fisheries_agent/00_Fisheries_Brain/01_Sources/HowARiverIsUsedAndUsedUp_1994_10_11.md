## Executive Summary
This 1994 Christian Science Monitor opinion piece uses Garrett Hardin's "Tragedy of the Commons" framework to explain the collapse of [[Pacific Salmon]] populations in the [[Columbia River]] basin. The article is not a primary scientific study but synthesizes commentary from Angus Duncan of the [[Northwest Power Planning Council]] to argue that cumulative, uncoordinated human uses of the river system — dams, pollution, logging, and agriculture — have driven salmon runs to a fraction of historic levels, prompting a $12 million federal disaster aid package for Pacific Northwest salmon fishermen.

## Background & Description
The source contains no direct morphological data on salmon anatomy. It references salmon life-history stages relevant to survival bottlenecks:
- **Juvenile outmigration stage**: young salmon migrate downstream from inland spawning grounds toward the Pacific Ocean, during which they are vulnerable to turbine mortality at dams, disease, and predation in slack water impoundments behind dams.
- **Adult upstream return stage**: mature salmon returning to spawn must navigate fish ladders at each of the dams; many fail to do so successfully.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat/Range**: [[Columbia River]] basin, spanning a ~259,000-square-mile watershed in the Pacific Northwest (USA), draining into the North Pacific Ocean.
- **Migratory life cycle**: Anadromous — salmon are hatched in inland freshwater spawning grounds, migrate to the ocean as juveniles, and return upstream as adults to spawn, making them highly sensitive to barriers and habitat fragmentation along the entire river corridor.
- **Anthropogenic pressures cited**:
  - 211 major dams obstructing migratory access to large portions of the watershed.
  - Hydroelectric turbine mortality and predator/disease exposure in slack-water reservoirs behind dams.
  - Industrial pollution from pulp and paper mills, metals-production facilities, food-manufacturing plants, and mining operations along the Columbia and its tributaries.
  - Cattle grazing along streams and agricultural soil erosion.
  - Logging-related erosion on steep hillsides, exacerbated by US Forest Service road-building (340,000 miles of roads nationally, one-eighth in the Northwest) supporting the timber industry.
- **Indicator species role**: Salmon are described as the ecosystem's "most important indicator of well-being" for the Columbia Basin, reflecting cumulative environmental degradation.
- **Human/cultural dependency**: Native American communities and commercial fishermen are identified as the groups most directly affected by declining salmon runs, given cultural and economic reliance on the species.

## Key Findings
- Historic annual salmon runs on the [[Columbia River]] ranged from **10 million to 16 million** fish; these have declined to approximately **2.5 million** — a reduction of roughly 75-85%.
- **Half** of the original migratory salmon runs in the basin are considered at risk of extinction; **a quarter** are already extinct.
- The U.S. Department of Commerce designated **$12 million** in disaster aid for salmon fishermen in the Pacific Northwest (announced ~October 1994).
- **211 major dams** in the watershed act as the most visible driver of salmon decline, causing mortality at multiple life stages (turbine strikes, reservoir predation/disease, fish ladder failure).
- The decline is attributed not to any single actor but to a cumulative "[[Tragedy of the Commons]]" dynamic: numerous individually rational uses (energy production, industrial discharge, grazing, farming, logging, road-building) collectively "overcommitted" the river's carrying capacity.
- Angus Duncan's assessment: the Columbia River and its watershed are effectively "used up," with consequences extending beyond the salmon population to the livelihoods and cultural values of Columbia Basin residents, particularly Indigenous and commercial fishing communities.
