# Removing Coarse Fish from Bass Lake: Seining Experiment, 1935–1936

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1940 study, by [[William E. Ricker]] and [[John Gottschalk]] (published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, vol. 70), tests whether an overabundance of coarse fish caused the decline of [[Bass Lake]], Indiana. Under the [[Division of Fish and Game]], [[Seining]] removed about 45 tons of [[Common Carp]], 20 tons of [[Quillback]] and 6 tons of [[Buffalo Fish]] in 1935 and 1936. Following removal, water clarity and vegetation recovered and catches of [[Game Fish]] rose sharply in the second half of 1936. The authors conclude that the seining was very probably responsible for the change, while cautioning that the lake's long-term balance cannot be predicted and that seining is not a general cure.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Bass Lake lies in Starke County, near the eastern border of the sandy northwestern section of Indiana. It is shallow (maximum depth 30 ft, average about 10 ft) with a surface area of 1,600 acres at high water, which seasonal fluctuations can reduce considerably.
- **Thermal regime:** The lake is never permanently stratified in summer, so temperatures are high from surface to bottom.
- **Substrate and vegetation:** Sand or stones dominate near shore, with mud farther out. Vegetation (1940) was sparse, with [[Bulrush]] (*Scirpus*) offshore and submerged plants beyond. Spatterdock, white water lilies, *Potamogeton* and *Myriophyllum* occur in protected areas. Overall vegetation is much less profuse than in lakes such as Wawasee, Maxinkuckee and Winona.
- **Fishery history:** [[Striped Bass]] were reportedly introduced around 1915 by the Indiana Fish and Game Commission. Smallmouth bass appeared more numerous than largemouth bass, which is unusual for Indiana lakes.
- **Decline:** By the early 1930s the water was permanently muddy and vegetation was scarce, alongside complaints of poorer fishing. Carp were the main suspect, probably introduced from the [[Yellow River]] during high water in 1915 or 1916.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mechanism hypothesised:** Carp or other coarse fish were thought to muddy the water and uproot vegetation, a pattern also reported elsewhere. The authors cite [[Cahn (1929)]] for a Wisconsin pond dominated by carp and [[Ricker (1932)]] for an Ontario trout pond where removal of suckers (*Catostomus commersonii*) was followed by vegetation regrowth.
- **Alternative explanation:** A natural population cycle was considered, but the authors judged it implausible given the scale of the game-fish increase.
- **Recovery:** The lake became clear and vegetation increased in extent and density in 1936. Recovery persisted to at least 1940, and anglers reported larger catches from 1937 onward.
- **Limits of method:** Seining succeeded only where suitable beaches existed. Most later attempts on other Indiana lakes failed, either because beaches were scarce or because too few coarse fish were caught per haul.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Two seines were used: a large net (1,000 ft × 20 ft) and a small net (800 ft × 8 ft), both with 1¼-inch square mesh (3-inch stretched measure). Hauls were made from a scow with winches.
- Labour came mainly from the Works Progress Administration (W.P.A.), with help from conservation clubs and residents. [[John Metz]] supervised the work from the Bass Lake hatchery.
- Weekly catch records were summarised by semi-monthly period. Locations of individual hauls were not recorded.
- A 75 ft trammel net (1-inch mesh inner web) was sometimes hung behind the seine. Its use after July 1935 is unrecorded.

**Coarse fish removed (1935–1936)**

| Species | Number killed | Average weight (lb) |
|---|---|---|
| Carp | 12,281 (45 tons total) | 7.35 |
| Quillback | 14,456 | 2.70 |
| Buffalo fish | 1,441 | 8.55 |
| Suckers | 65 | 2.03 |
| Gar pike | 215 | 2.21 |

Other coarse species were taken only in negligible numbers.

**Game fish returned to the water**
- Wall-eyed pike: 1,605 (mean 2.81 lb)
- Striped bass: 1,658 (mean 1.01 lb)
- Smallmouth bass: 431 (mean 2.0 lb)
- Largemouth bass: 140 (mean 2.21 lb)
- Crappies: 558 (mean 0.2 lb)
- Bluegill: 1,081 (mean 0.13 lb)
- Common sunfish: 83 (mean 0.06 lb)
- Yellow perch: 1,249 (mean 0.08 lb)
- Rock bass and catfish were each taken only twice.

**Net efficiency**
- In June–July 1936, 26 hauls were made with each net. Overall catch per haul was higher with the small net, but not significantly so.
- Quillback were seven times more abundant in the large net, though this was attributed to haul-to-haul variation.
- Wall-eyed pike catch was higher in the small net, near the margin of significance.
- Because the small net could be hauled more often, it likely took more fish per day.

**Changes in catch per haul, 1935 vs 1936**
- Carp: 36.4 → 24.3
- Quillback: 49.1 → 20.4
- Buffalo: 3.8 → 3.5 (little change)
- Gar pike: 0.1 → 1.2 (increase, resembling game fish)
- Wall-eyed pike: 2.3 → 5.8
- Striped bass: 0.7 → 8.8
- Smallmouth bass: 0.5 → 1.9
- Largemouth bass: 0.1 → 0.6
- Crappies: 0.2 → 1.9
- Bluegill: <0.1 → 6.3
- Common sunfish and yellow perch appeared for the first time in 1936, at 0.5 and 7.4 per haul respectively.

**Authors' conclusions**
- The authors note that 1936 seining may have been more efficient than 1935, so the true declines in coarse fish were probably larger than the catch-per-haul figures suggest. Even if the 1936 game-fish catches were halved, the increase would remain substantial.
- Water clarity improved, with Metz estimating vegetation increased "over 1,000 per cent" and clarity improved by 3 to 1 feet.
- Recovery was judged to be caused by seining. Whether the lake will stay in balance depends on the nature of the original coarse-fish increase, which cannot now be determined. Seining is "not a panacea."
