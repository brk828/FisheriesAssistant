# Effects of Predation on Life-History Evolution in Utah Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Johnson and Belk (Brigham Young University) compared eight isolated populations of [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*) to test whether long-term exposure to a gape-limited predator, [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki*), has shaped life-history evolution. Four populations had co-existed with cutthroat trout since the late Pleistocene and four had not. Predator-sympatric chubs grew more slowly in the Brody coefficient *k* but reached a larger asymptotic length, matured later and at larger sizes, showed lower female reproductive effort, and lived longer. The authors argue these patterns match [[Size-Selective Predation]] theory, in which juvenile mortality is high relative to adult mortality. The study is presented as the first test of predation effects on life-history evolution in a long-lived species, which had previously been studied only in short-lived organisms (life span under 3 years).

## Background & Description

Utah chub is a long-lived [[Cyprinidae]] fish native to the [[Bonneville Basin]]. It is a frequent prey item of cutthroat trout. Predation is known to shape prey morphology, coloration and behavior, and theory predicts that ontogenetic variation in predator-induced mortality can drive prey life-history strategies. Three predictions follow when juvenile mortality is highest:

1. Increased juvenile growth rates.
2. Delayed age at maturity and increased size at maturity.
3. Decreased reproductive effort over an extended reproductive life span.

Previous support for these predictions came from short-lived taxa such as guppies, cladocerans, amphipods and snails. The authors used the Utah chub system as a [[Natural Experiment]]: populations were isolated by the contraction of [[Lake Bonneville]] roughly 10,000 years ago, and some retained cutthroat trout while others did not.

The study addressed two questions: (1) do life-history traits differ among Utah chub populations, and (2) if they do, do the patterns match size-selective predation predictions?

Six traits were measured: growth rate, maximum standard length, age at maturity, length at maturity, size-specific fecundity, and median and maximum age.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical setting.** Lake Bonneville covered about 51,700 km² in the late Pleistocene, and Utah chub and cutthroat trout co-occurred throughout it. Volcanic and tectonic activity around 35,000 years ago diverted the [[Bear River]] into the basin, which then flooded northward and introduced chub into the [[Snake River]] drainage. As the lake contracted, springs and lakes became isolated. Two locality types resulted: "non-predator" sites with chub only, and "predator" sites with both chub and cutthroat trout.

**Sampling sites.** Eight populations were sampled, four in each treatment. Site data from the Figure 1 legend are summarized below.

**Latitude confound.** Predator populations occurred at higher latitudes, so predation and latitude were confounded.

**Other species.** Juvenile cutthroat trout and other native fishes occurred only at predator localities. Exotic game fishes (rainbow trout, white bass, a white bass–striped bass hybrid and largemouth bass) had been introduced to three non-predator sites within the last 100 years.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Chub were collected with experimental [[Gill Netting]] (up to three nets, each four 8 × 2 m panels, mesh 2.5–10 cm) over 24-hour periods, supplemented by minnow traps for small individuals.
- Ages came from thin-sectioned lapillus otoliths (N = 516 aged). Back-calculated lengths used a modified [[Fraser-Lee Back-Calculation]] formula.
- Growth was fitted with the [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Function]] (VBGF) for individuals aged 5 years and older (N = 324). Of these, 319 had r² > 0.90 and 5 had r² > 0.70.
- Age at maturity was estimated three ways (youngest mature fish, probit analysis, and [[Two-Phase Linear Regression]]). All three gave qualitatively the same results.
- Reproductive effort was measured as gonad mass, using a [[Nested Analysis of Covariance]] with body mass as a covariate. Ova diameter was checked first.

**Growth (Table 1)**
- Brody coefficient *k* was higher in non-predator populations: 0.34 (SE 0.02) vs. 0.24 (SE 0.02), F = 16.6, P < 0.01.
- Asymptotic length (L∞) was larger in predator populations: 223.5 mm (SE 23.0) vs. 149.3 mm (SE 20.7), F = 16.2, P < 0.01.
- Predator-site populations were dominated by large size classes (Figure 3).

**Age and size at maturity (Tables 1 and 2)**
- The earliest mature age in non-predator populations was 2 years (Fish Springs, Big Spring, Locomotive Springs). Table 2 lists Rush Lake at 4 years, and it is marked as the youngest age class sampled.
- In predator populations, the youngest mature fish were 3 years (Two Ocean Lake), 5 years (Heart Lake), 6 years (Jackson Lake) and 7 years (Bear Lake). Rush Lake, Bear Lake and Two Ocean Lake were excluded from some estimates because the youngest fish collected were already mature.
- Probit age at 50% maturity was 2.0 years (SE 0.2) in non-predator populations vs. 6.5 years (SE 0.5) in predator populations (F = 18.5, P < 0.03).
- Two-phase regression age at maturity was 3.5 years (SE 0.1) vs. 3.8 years (SE 0.02) (F = 6.0, P < 0.03).
- Size at maturity from probit ages was 60.5 mm (SE 0.5) vs. 202.5 mm (SE 0.5) (F = 40,328, P < 0.01). The authors report these values as given.
- Size at maturity from two-phase regression was 68.2 mm (SE 13.5) vs. 108.2 mm (SE 2.7) (F = 8.5, P < 0.03).

**Reproductive effort (Table 3, Figure 4)**
- Ova diameter did not differ between treatments: non-predator 1.31 mm (SE 0.06), predator 1.29 mm (SE 0.09), F = 0.01, P = 0.76. Gonad mass was therefore used as the measure of reproductive effort.
- Females: gonad mass was significantly lower in predator populations (F = 4.73, P < 0.05). Sites within treatments did not differ.
- Males: no significant difference between treatments (F = 0.21, ns). Gonad mass-body mass slopes varied among sites within treatments (F = 3.39, P < 0.01).

**Longevity (Figure 5)**
- Maximum ages were 7–14 years in non-predator populations and 13–29 years in predator populations.
- Median ages were 12.5 years (Bear Lake) and 14 years (Jackson Lake), versus 4–6 years in the remaining six populations.
- The 95th percentile age was also much higher in Bear and Jackson Lakes.
- Because sample sizes were smallest where the oldest fish occurred, the authors describe the maximum ages from those populations as conservative.

**Alternative explanations**
- *Resource availability*: this was rejected. Higher resources should have produced early maturity and higher reproductive investment, but predator populations matured late and had lower female effort. Only growth was consistent with higher per capita resources, and juvenile cutthroat trout competition may offset that.
- *Latitude/temperature*: this was rejected. Cooler temperatures at higher latitudes should have reduced annual growth, yet predator-site chub grew faster annually.
- *Local phenomena*: the authors noted flooding at Rush Lake (1983), geothermal heating at Heart Lake, and [[Ligula Intestinalis]] parasitism at Two Ocean Lake as possible site-specific influences. They concluded that the overall pattern persisted despite local variation.

**Heritability test**
- Introductions of exotic predators to three non-predator sites (less than 100 years earlier) did not induce predator-like life histories. The authors interpret this as evidence that the life-history differences are heritable rather than plastic, though the evidence is indirect.

**Trade-offs and adaptive longevity**
- Negative life span–reproduction correlations are widespread across taxa, and delayed maturity may extend life span as a by-product.
- An alternative is that longevity is itself adaptive. Fluctuating predation intensity would make juvenile survival unpredictable, favoring extended reproductive life spans. This would spread reproduction across favorable and unfavorable years.

**Conclusions**
- Size-selective predation was the only hypothesis consistent with all the documented patterns.
- Life-history theory developed from short-lived organisms appears to apply to species with much longer life spans, even though selection acts on time scales differing by orders of magnitude.
- The study is a comparative analysis, so predation effects may be confounded with factors such as latitude and resource differences.
