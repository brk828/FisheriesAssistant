# Biological Assessment for the Spikedace (Meda fulgida) - Central Arizona Water Exchange Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #instream-flow #habitat-assessment

## Executive Summary

This biological assessment evaluates potential impacts to the threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) from the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) Water Exchange Project, specifically water diversions from the upper [[Verde River]] system. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] conducted this assessment in March 1988 following the species' listing as threatened in June 1986. The analysis focuses on proposed diversions by the City of [[Prescott]] (7,127 acre-feet annually) and the [[Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community]] (500 acre-feet annually) from the upper Verde River, within known spikedace habitat. Using [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM), the study modeled habitat changes across three river reaches, collecting data on 4,193 adult and juvenile fish and 2,633 larval fish between March 1986 and May 1987. The assessment concludes that proposed diversions may negatively affect spikedace, particularly in Reach 1 where adult habitat would decrease by 20% under maximum diversion scenarios.

## Background & Description

The [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) is a small cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]], representing the sole member of its genus. Most individuals measure less than 65 mm (2.6 inches) in length. The species inhabits "shear zones"—the interface between swift and slow-moving water—typically found at the downstream ends of riffles or in eddies. [[Minckley]] (1973) documented their preference for these transitional habitats.

Spikedace feed primarily on aquatic and terrestrial insects. Spawning occurs from March or April through May, based on studies in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona (Barber et al. 1970). Historically abundant throughout suitable habitats in the [[Aqua Fria River]], [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[San Francisco River]], and [[Gila River]] upstream from Phoenix, the species has been eliminated from approximately 95% of its former range. Current populations persist only in the upper [[Verde River]], [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]] (Arizona), and upper [[Gila River]] (New Mexico).

The species' decline results from multiple anthropogenic factors: introduction of exotic and non-native fish species, damming, stream diversions, watershed deterioration from overgrazing, and groundwater pumping. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] proposed listing the species as threatened in June 1985, with official listing occurring in June 1986. Critical habitat designation was postponed beyond the June 1987 deadline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution in the Verde River**: Spikedace abundance increases upstream, with the species almost entirely confined to reaches upstream of the [[Sycamore Creek]] confluence. During 1986 sampling, 76% of collected spikedace came from the headwaters at river mile 6, where relative abundance was highest. This location supported 95% native fish composition—the highest percentage of any reach studied. Occasional individuals occur downstream depending on stream conditions; one specimen was collected 1.5 miles below the Sycamore Creek confluence.

**Habitat Preferences - Adults and Juveniles**: Adult spikedace occupied shallow depths ranging 0.1 to 1.1 ft (3 to 34 cm); juveniles used slightly broader depth ranges. Water velocities for adults ranged 0.0 to 3.0 ft/sec (0 to 91 cm/sec), with juveniles showing slightly narrower preferences. The species generally associates with sand or gravel substrates, likely reflecting water velocity patterns rather than substrate preference per se. Comparison of June 1986 and January 1987 data from the same Verde River location revealed no significant seasonal shifts in depth or velocity preference (Student's t-test, P>0.05), contrasting with seasonal habitat shifts reported by [[Propst]] et al. (1986) for [[Gila River]] populations in New Mexico.

**Habitat Preferences - Larvae**: In April 1986, 185 larval fish collections yielded 2,633 individuals representing eight species. Larval spikedace comprised approximately 4% (100 larvae) of total samples. Highest larval abundance occurred where the Verde River crosses Forest Service Road 638 (T. 17 N., R. 1W, Sec 4), appearing in 42% of collections at that location. Larval spikedace, along with [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), showed similar habitat preferences for shallow, still water.

**Fish Community Composition**: The instream flow study selected nine representative species: spikedace, [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*), and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Habitat use data were collected on 4,193 adult and juvenile fish and 2,633 larval fish between March 1986 and May 1987, recording stream depth, velocity, substrate, and cover for each individual.

**Hydrology**: The upper Verde River exhibits relatively constant discharge throughout the year with a flat hydrograph and downstream flow accretion. Minimal seasonal variation in base flow characterizes upper reaches due to low water use and low evapotranspiration rates (Owens-Joyce and Bell 1983). [[U.S. Geological Survey]] maintains two gages: USGS gage 5037 (Verde River at Paulden, river mile 7, period of record June 1963-present) and USGS gage 5040 (Verde River near Clarkdale, river mile 34.5, periods of record June 1915-October 1916, June 1917-June 1921, April 1965-present). Median flows were used for analysis, representing flows present 50% of the time.

## Key Findings

**Project Description**: The proposed Prescott/Yavapai-Prescott diversion would extract water from T. 17 N., R. 2 W., SE1/4, Sec. 12, Yavapai County—approximately 2.5 miles downstream from [[Sullivan Lake]], the river's headwaters. An infiltration gallery buried in the riverbed would divert water transported via pipeline to Prescott. Peak demand occurs in June and July, requiring 888 and 881 acre-feet respectively (equivalent to continuous flows of 15.05 and 14.93 cfs). The assessment evaluated both the full proposed diversion and a reduced 1 cfs diversion scenario if only the Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community proceeded.

**IFIM Study Results**: The study divided the upper 33 miles of Verde River containing spikedace into three reaches: Reach 1 (R1, river mile 0-10, includes Paulden Gage), Reach 2 (R2, river mile 10-25, includes Perkins Bridge), and Reach 3 (R3, river mile 25-44, includes Clarkdale Gage). Weighted Useable Area (WUA) was calculated for all spikedace life stages and for red shiner (adult, juvenile, larval) using the PHABSIM computer program. Baseline conditions used median flows: 25 cfs for R1 and R2 (from Paulden Gage data), and 55 cfs for R3 (from Reclamation model).

**Habitat Changes by Reach and Life Stage**:

- **Reach 1**: A 15 cfs diversion would cause an 83% loss of spawning habitat and a 20% reduction in adult spikedace habitat, while increasing adult red shiner habitat by 83%. Juvenile spikedace would experience a 9% habitat loss. This represents the most severe negative impact zone.

- **Reach 2**: A 15 cfs diversion would produce a 238% gain in juvenile spikedace habitat and a 3% increase in adult habitat for both spikedace and red shiner. Spawning habitat would increase 40%.

- **Reach 3**: A 15 cfs diversion would yield an 18% increase in adult spikedace habitat and a 13% increase in adult red shiner habitat. Juvenile spikedace habitat would increase 11%.

**Red Shiner Competition**: [[Red Shiner]] is suspected of causing spikedace population declines (Minckley and Deacon 1968), though whether red shiner actively displaces spikedace or replaces it where habitat has changed remains unclear (Bestgen and Propst 1987). The IFIM analysis included red shiner WUA to assess potential competitive interactions under altered flow regimes.

**Minimal Diversion Scenario**: If only the Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community diverted 1 cfs, overall impacts to spikedace would be minimal, assuming the adult life stage is most limiting (the criterion used in the broader IFIM study).

**Construction Impacts**: The infiltration gallery installation would cause short-term impacts from increased turbidity. However, the buried design would prevent entrainment of all fish life stages. Temperature impacts from flow depletion were considered secondary to flow reduction effects and likely minimal due to spring flow accretion in upper reaches.

**Methodological Limitations**: The assessment acknowledges that while IFIM represents the best available instream flow methodology, it has limitations. Critics (Orth and Maughan 1982; Bleed 1987) note that IFIM primarily considers stream depth, velocity, substrate, and cover, omitting many other parameters affecting fish. [[Rinne]] (1984) argues that southwestern fish declines result from multiple causes, with ecosystem changes from pristine to disturbed conditions setting the stage for native species decline.

**Regulatory Context**: The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (as amended) requires federal agencies authorizing, funding, or directly engaging in construction and operation to conduct biological assessments addressing listed species likely affected by such actions. This assessment fulfills that requirement for spikedace; other endangered species (peregrine falcon, bald eagle) would be addressed separately when additional information became available.

**Conclusion**: Under current project design, flow reduction from both the joint Prescott/Yavapai-Prescott diversion and the Yavapai-Prescott diversion alone may negatively affect spikedace in the Verde River, particularly in Reach 1 where the diversion would occur within crucial habitat and cause substantial spawning habitat loss and adult habitat reduction.
