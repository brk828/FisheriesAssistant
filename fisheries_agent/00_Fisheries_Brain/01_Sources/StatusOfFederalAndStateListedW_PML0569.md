# Status of Federal and State Listed Warm Water Fishes of the Gila River Basin, with Recommendations for Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Desert Fishes Team report (Report Number 1, October 15, 2003) reviews the status of twelve federal and state listed native warm water fishes in the [[Gila River Basin]] and the conservation and recovery actions taken since 1902 by all agencies, organizations, or parties. It provides species accounts, summary tables (historic range, extirpations, and extant populations; [[Transplant and Repatriation Efforts]]; recovery and conservation activities), and recommendations for each species.

The authors conclude that six species are extirpated from the basin, five survive in less than 20% of their original range, and one remains in about 40% of its original range. Distribution and abundance of all extant listed species have declined since listing, and the trend continues. Few successful recovery or conservation actions occurred during the 36-year period assessed. Repatriation has been the primary management tool but has succeeded for only a few species, with limited results.

All federally listed species have existing biologically based recovery plans, but few plan recommendations have been implemented. The authors judge that the control and removal of [[Nonnative Fishes]] is the most urgent and overriding need.

## Background & Description

The native warm water fishes of the Gila River watershed are part of an indigenous fish fauna of the American Southwest that is critically imperiled. The Gila River basin has 20 native fish species; two native trouts are also federal and state listed but were excluded because they are cold water species and game species with separate, more active recovery programs.

The report was produced by the Desert Fishes Team, an independent group of biologists formed to fill the gap left when the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] disbanded its Desert Fishes Recovery Team in 2002. Participants came from the [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[University of Arizona]], [[Arizona State University]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], and independent experts. Information came from published literature, agency documents, and the field and laboratory experience of participants.

The twelve species covered are:

- [[Santa Cruz Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon arcuatus*), extinct
- [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), endangered
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), endangered
- [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), proposed endangered
- [[Headwater Chub]] (*Gila nigra*), state listed, petitioned
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), petitioned, state listed
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), threatened
- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), endangered
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), endangered
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), endangered
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), threatened
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), endangered

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species occupied a range of habitats, from low-elevation springs, cienegas, and backwaters to mid-elevation tributaries and main stem rivers. Documented reasons for decline are well established in the literature and recovery plans. Two main drivers are identified:

- **Nonnative species:** introduction and spread of nonnative aquatic species is a major cause of native species displacement. Examples in the text include green sunfish predation on Gila chub recruitment and predation by ictalurid catfishes and red shiner competition.
- **Habitat destruction:** a variety of human activities, including water development, grazing, road and bridge construction, and mining, has been an equal and interactive factor.

Wildfire also eliminated one reintroduced Gila chub population, and flooding affected downstream fish abundance in one study.

Table 1 of the report documents historic distributions, known extirpated populations, and naturally occupied streams for each species. Table 2 documents transplant and repatriation activity by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] since 1967. Table 3 lists recovery and conservation actions, and Table 4 gives recommendations for replication sites and management actions.

## Key Findings

**Species status**

- **Santa Cruz (Monkey Spring) pupfish:** extinct. The last population disappeared in 1971 after refugium stocks failed to reproduce.
- **Desert pupfish:** no wild populations remain in the basin. It was transplanted to more than a dozen wild locations, and only two remain occupied.
- **Bonytail:** absent from the basin for many decades. No recovery or conservation activities have occurred in the basin.
- **Gila chub:** remains in fewer than 30 small isolated waters, about 45% of its historical range, with two successful transplants known.
- **Headwater chub:** occurs in fewer than 20 wild locations, about 40% of its historical range. Management has been limited to angling regulation changes.
- **Roundtail chub:** resides in fewer than 20 tributary streams, less than 20% of its original range. Fish barriers on Aravaipa Creek are the only management action that directly benefited it.
- **Spikedace:** persists in only seven locations, 10–15% of its original range. It is considered the most vulnerable species in the basin.
- **Woundfin:** extirpated from the basin. Experimental-nonessential stocking was designated but never implemented.
- **Gila topminnow:** extirpated from more than 95% of its historical range and restricted to fewer than a dozen natural sites. Reestablishment at about 175 wild locations produced about two dozen established populations. More than 200 reintroductions or dispersals were recorded, with 17 populations remaining in 1998.
- **Colorado squawfish:** eliminated from the Gila River. Repatriation over nearly 20 years failed; more than 750,000 individuals were stocked in the Salt and Verde rivers with no evidence of long-term survival or reproduction.
- **Loach minnow:** disappeared from more than 85% of its original range and remains in fewer than a dozen locations. No successful transplants have been recorded.
- **Razorback sucker:** wild populations are gone from the basin. Repatriation spanning more than 20 years stocked millions of fish without establishing a population.

**Overall conclusions**

- Six species are extirpated, five survive in under 20% of their original range, and one remains in about 40%.
- Few recovery or conservation actions succeeded during the 36-year period assessed.
- Repatriation was the main management effort but succeeded for only a few species and with limited results.

**Recommendations**

- Implement existing recovery plans for federally listed species, which are considered adequate. Planning for these species is unnecessary.
- Remove nonnative fishes and other aquatic fauna and flora from occupied habitats as the most urgent priority.
- Revoke the experimental-nonessential rules for Colorado squawfish and woundfin, as their implementation would not assist recovery.
- Federally list Gila chub with critical habitat, and up-list spikedace and loach minnow to endangered status.
- Develop biologically based recovery plans for headwater chub, roundtail chub, and Gila chub, which currently lack them.
- Replicate populations into protected and suitable waters, such as Fossil Creek after renovation, Bonita Creek, Tonto Creek, and the San Pedro River.
- Adopt innovative recovery strategies, drawing on new knowledge and data, for species such as razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish.
- Conduct systematic monitoring and comprehensive reporting across all species.
