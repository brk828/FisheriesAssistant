# Molecular Variation in Sonoran Topminnows: Microsatellite and MHC Markers for Species, ESU and MU Identification

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Hedrick]], [[Parker]] and [[Lee]] (Arizona State University, *Molecular Ecology*, 2001) tests whether neutral [[Microsatellite Loci]] and a major histocompatibility gene ([[Major Histocompatibility Complex]], MHC) identify the same population structure in endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] populations. It also examines whether the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] is a separate species. The authors find that the two markers show strongly correlated patterns of variation, that the Gila topminnow forms five genetic groups, and that the Yaqui topminnow differs so strongly from the Gila topminnow that species rather than subspecies status is appropriate. They recommend grouping the Gila populations into two [[Evolutionarily Significant Units]] (ESUs), with one ESU containing four [[Management Units]] (MUs).

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) is a small, live-bearing fish found in Arizona and once widespread in the region. The [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*P. occidentalis sonoriensis*) occurs in Sonora, Mexico. Both are listed as endangered.
- **Conservation rationale:** The authors argue that identifying ESUs and MUs is needed to decide which populations to preserve, and that conserving adaptive variation is central to recovery. Earlier work had used [[Microsatellite Loci]] and allozymes, and [[Mitochondrial DNA]] had been used for phylogeography.
- **Sampling:** The study analysed 13 natural and stocked Gila topminnow populations. Nine samples were new, and four came from an earlier study. Yaqui topminnow samples came from [[Tule Spring]] (n = 20) and [[North Pond]] (n = 19), and a [[Santa Cruz River]] sample was included as a naturally recolonized population.
- **Methods:** Ten microsatellite loci had been identified in earlier work. Five were polymorphic in Gila topminnows, and the study used these together with the MHC locus. Allele frequencies, observed and expected heterozygosity, and allele numbers were calculated, and genetic distances (D_A) were used to build a [[Neighbour-Joining Tree]] with bootstrap support. MHC sequences were analysed for nonsynonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) substitution rates, with sites split into [[Antigen-Binding Site]] (ABS) and non-ABS categories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Populations occupy springs and streams, several of them small and isolated. [[Monkey Spring]] is a constant, warm spring isolated for perhaps 10,000 years by a 10 m high travertine dam. It was historically occupied by an extinct pupfish species and a distinct form of [[Gila Chub]].
- **Population history:** Gila topminnow populations are shaped mainly by nonselective processes: small habitat size, recurring bottlenecks, and extinction and recolonization cycles driven by drought and flood. The [[Bylas Springs]] population, which supports at most a few hundred adults, shows low variation for both marker types.
- **Human-mediated movement:** [[Boyce Thompson Stock]] was drained in the early 1980s to remove non-native [[Gambusia Affinis]] and was then restocked from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Monkey Spring stock). [[Watson Wash]] was later stocked from Boyce Thompson in the late 1980s by the US Bureau of Land Management, according to personal communications cited by the authors.
- **Other threats and tools:** Earlier allozyme and mtDNA studies found low variation in these populations, suggesting recent nonselective losses of diversity.

## Key Findings

**1. Concordance of neutral and MHC variation**
- Correlations between population-level measures for microsatellites and MHC were high: r = 0.75 (H_o), r = 0.73 (H_E) and r = 0.84 (n alleles), all P < 0.01 across the 13 samples.
- The authors conclude that the MHC locus can be used alongside microsatellites for population analyses, and that nonselective factors likely shape much of the MHC variation.

**2. Heterozygote deficiency**
- The MHC locus in [[Sharp Spring]] showed a 43.5% deficiency of heterozygotes relative to expectation. The authors attribute this to a null allele, estimated at 0.180 using [[Brookfield Null Allele Method]] (earlier estimate 0.147).
- A second, duplicated MHC locus was identified in Sharp Spring and five other populations, which changed how genotypes were scored.

**3. Five genetic groups in Gila topminnows**
- **Bylas Springs 1 and 2:** Cluster together (D_A = 0.093), show low genetic variation, and are distinct from other groups.
- **Monkey and Cottonwood Springs:** Cluster together (D_A = 0.300; 73% bootstrap) with intermediate variation.
- **Sonoita Creek drainage** (Sonoita Creek, Coalmine Canyon, Red Rock at Cottonwood Tank, Red Rock Falls): Cluster together (average D_A = 0.112; 89% bootstrap) and are intermediate to variable.
- **[[Cienega Creek]]** and **Sharp Spring:** Form the fourth and fifth groups. They have nearly non-overlapping alleles at two microsatellite loci and share no MHC alleles. Sharp Spring has above-average variation (H_E = 0.202, n = 3.0 across seven shared loci).
- **Monkey Spring:** Has low variation (H_E = 0.138, n = 2.0 across seven shared loci).

**4. Genetic distances and ESU/MU recommendations**
- Monkey Spring is highly differentiated from the Bylas, Cienega and Sharp groups (average D_A = 0.656).
- The Monkey Spring ESU includes Cottonwood Spring, which is genetically similar and lies about five stream km from Monkey Spring via Sonoita Creek.
- The authors recommend two ESUs. The Monkey Spring ESU covers Monkey and Cottonwood Springs. The other ESU has four MUs: Bylas Springs, Cienega Creek, Sharp Spring and the Sonoita Creek group.
- The Bylas and Cienega sites are about 580 km apart along stream channel, much of it dry except during floods. The Sonoita group is highly variable.

**5. Origins of stocked populations**
- Boyce Thompson and Monkey Spring are very similar. The authors find no Bylas ancestry in Boyce Thompson stock, which carries a new MHC allele (Pooc-16) that differs from the common Monkey Spring allele by one amino acid.
- Watson Wash is similar to Boyce Thompson, consistent with the reported stocking history.
- The naturally recolonized [[Santa Cruz River]] sample has the highest heterozygosity of all samples, carries alleles shared with Sharp Spring and two unique MHC alleles, and may contain ancestry from upper Santa Cruz stocks.

**6. Reproductive compatibility**
- Crosses between Bylas Spring 2, Cienega Creek, Monkey Spring and Sharp Spring succeeded and produced F1 and F2 progeny. No reduced growth, fecundity, survival or bilateral asymmetry was found, so molecular differentiation has not caused reproductive isolation (Sheffer et al. 1999).

**7. Gila and Yaqui topminnows are separate species**
- Only 2 of 25 microsatellite alleles in Yaqui topminnows occur in Gila topminnow samples, and several differ strongly in size. For three loci, size ranges do not overlap.
- Every MHC sequence differs between the two taxa. The two taxa also differ by seven restriction sites in mtDNA.
- Tule Spring Yaqui topminnows, used in an earlier fitness study, appear to belong to a different species, so the earlier results need re-evaluation.

**8. MHC diversity and selection**
- Gila and Yaqui topminnows together carry 62 amino acids, of which 26 (41.9%) are variable, and 188 nucleotides, of which 55 (29.3%) are variable.
- Gila topminnow MHC sequences include 12 earlier and five newly identified sequences. Three of the new sequences are attributed to a second, duplicated locus (Pooc-9, Pooc-12, Pooc-13), in an [[Appendix]] argument based on homozygote absence and sequence association.
- Overall dN = 0.238 is significantly greater than dS = 0.100 (dN/dS = 2.38). Thirteen of 20 ABS positions (65%) are variable, compared with 13 of 42 non-ABS positions (31%).
- The authors suggest long-term balancing selection contributes to trans-species polymorphism, but that short-term variation within populations is mainly nonselective.

**9. Conservation implications**
- Monkey Spring is recommended as a distinct ESU because of its isolation, genetic distinctiveness and unique life-history traits.
- Captive stock management should keep Bylas and Cienega stocks separate, and the Boyce Thompson stock should be recognized as Monkey Spring-derived.
- Further work on the MHC duplication, including crosses and gamete sequencing, was planned.
