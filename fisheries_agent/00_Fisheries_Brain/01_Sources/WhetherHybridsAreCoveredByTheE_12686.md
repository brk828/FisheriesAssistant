# Legal Opinion on Endangered Species Act Coverage of Hybrids

#wildlife-law #conservation-policy #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

This 1977 legal memorandum from the U.S. Department of the Interior Office of the Solicitor addresses whether hybrids of endangered or threatened species are protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The Assistant Solicitor concludes that hybrids are covered by the Act based on statutory language, legislative history, and policy considerations regarding commercialization of protected species.

## Background & Description

The memorandum responds to an April 21, 1977 request from the Division of Law Enforcement, [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], seeking clarification on hybrid coverage under 16 U.S.C. §§ 1531 et seq. For purposes of the Act, a hybrid is defined as offspring of two animals or two plants where each parent is from a different species and at least one parent is from an endangered or threatened species. This definition encompasses situations where one parent is listed and the other is not, as well as situations where both parents are from different listed species.

The Act defines "fish or wildlife" to mean "any member of the animal kingdom, including without limitation any mammal, fish, bird... amphibian, reptile, mollusk, crustacean, arthropod or other invertebrate, and includes any part, product, egg or offspring thereof, or the dead body or parts thereof" (16 U.S.C. § 1532(5)). "Plant" is defined as "any member of the plant kingdom, including seeds, roots and other parts thereof" (16 U.S.C. § 1532(9)).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The memorandum notes that plants are considered "even more vulnerable to man's intervention than are animals, since they cannot move to more hospitable circumstances when necessary" (H.R. Rep. No. 93-412). The legislative history characterizes both definitions as "very broadly" and "similarly broad" in scope, indicating Congress intended comprehensive protection.

The opinion addresses a practical conservation concern: large-scale crossbreeding of endangered crocodilians was already occurring in [[Southeast Asia]] at the time. Hybrids are noted to be "almost always the captive offspring of specimens crossbred in captivity," and the Act explicitly covers captive specimens under 16 U.S.C. § 1538(b).

## Key Findings

The Solicitor concludes that hybrids of endangered or threatened species are covered by the [[Endangered Species Act]] based on three lines of reasoning:

**Statutory Language**: The Act's definition of "fish or wildlife" explicitly includes "any offspring without limitation," making the plain meaning of the statute dictate coverage of hybrids of listed animal species.

**Legislative History**: Congressional reports confirm that both "fish or wildlife" and "plant" definitions were intended to be broad and similarly inclusive. The protection of plants was described as "a matter of urgent concern," and it would be illogical for the statute to provide less protection to more vulnerable organisms through narrower definitions.

**Policy Considerations**: A major objective of the Act is to protect endangered and threatened species by reducing commerce therein (16 U.S.C. §§ 1531-1533, 1540(a)(1)). If hybrids were not covered, this goal would be "easily frustrated since dealers can simply crossbreed listed species with similar species." Once demand for hybrid products is stimulated, the drain on protected species from taking for crossbreeding purposes would equal that of allowing commercialization of purebreds.

The memorandum warns that excluding hybrids from Act coverage would "greatly frustrate" the statute's policy of reducing commerce in listed species, as commercial operators could circumvent protections through systematic crossbreeding programs.
