# Endangered Fishes in the National Parks, with Special Emphasis on the Devils Hole Pupfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document, a National Park Service–oriented paper by James E. Deacon and Maxine S. Deacon, reviews research on endangered and native fishes of western national parks, centred on the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*). It links the biology of these fishes to water management in the [[Colorado River]] system and the desert springs of [[Death Valley National Monument]], and it argues that water allocation must increasingly reflect qualitative values such as species preservation alongside economic and population growth. The authors' central quantitative result is that Devils Hole population size tracks water level, sunlight and primary productivity, with a practical management target of roughly 200 to 400 individuals.

## Background & Description

Fishes of the Colorado River show physical adaptations to torrential, shifting, silt-laden waters, including streamlined bodies, large fins, reduced or embedded scales and small eyes. Among the native fishes of [[Grand Canyon National Park]] the most extreme forms are the humpback chub (*Gila cypha*), the roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*), and the Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), which attains the largest size, exceeding eighty pounds. The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) occupies some reservoirs and milder river stretches.

The desert pupfishes of [[Death Valley National Monument]] (genus *Cyprinodon*) live in springs and marshes with extreme salinity and temperature variation. Maximum temperatures reach about 43°C (109°F) in summer, and salinities range from low values to several times that of sea water. The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] is restricted to a single limestone cavern in [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada, and occupies what the authors describe as possibly the smallest habitat in the world holding the entire genome of a vertebrate species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado River regulation.** Large mainstem dams have interrupted native fish migrations needed for spawning and substantially lowered downstream water temperatures. Closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] reduced summer water temperature below the dam from about 10°C in 1968 to about 10°C-range values after reservoir levels rose in [[Lake Powell]]. Introduced fishes (carp, red shiner, green sunfish) were pushed downstream, and by 1975 introduced forms appeared more adversely affected than native forms, which had not shown severe downstream restriction though their abundance had declined.

**Desert springs and thermal limits.** Field and laboratory work at [[Saratoga Springs]] shows that *Cyprinodon nevadensis* is active across a wide temperature range, with activity reduced or stopped at high temperatures. Critical thermal maxima for *C. diabolis* and related populations were determined at about 42-44°C. Eggs of *C. nevadensis* develop between constant temperatures of 20°C and 36°C, and reproductive behaviour ceases above about 38°C.

**Devils Hole habitat.** The water temperature in the cavern is nearly constant at 33°C. Sunlight reaches the water surface only seasonally, so primary productivity varies with season and, after 1969, with water level changes caused by nearby groundwater pumping for irrigated pasture. Population size is concentrated near the surface, although the fish regularly occur down to 26 m.

**Water rights and law.** The authors trace present federal and state water law to competing demands for water in the arid West. They cite the 1974 permanent injunction issued by Federal District Judge Roger Foley (District of Nevada) under the Endangered Species Act, which recognized the public interest in preserving the Devils Hole pupfish.

## Key Findings

- **Population response to water level:** On 20 September 1972 the water level reached a historic minimum of 1.19 m below a USGS reference point. Population crashed between July and September 1972, which the authors attribute to reduced carrying capacity. The population rose to about 250 by January 1973.
- **Court-ordered management:** A permanent injunction was granted on 5 June 1973. Since then the water level has been managed not to fall below 1.01 m, and from 2 July 1976 to remain above 0.91 m. The population reached about 400 individuals by 24 September 1976.
- **Population targets:** The authors recommend a minimum population of 200 individuals and a maximum of about 400.
- **Regression results:** Using maximum population and water-level data for 1967-68 and 1972-76, the correlation coefficient was 0.86 (R² = 0.73, 95% confidence), indicating that about 400 individuals could be expected at a water level of about 0.73 m. Excluding the less accurate 1967-68 data gave r = 0.81 (R² = 0.55).
- **Light and productivity:** Population size increased about two months after sunlight duration and intensity increased (r = 0.76, R² = 0.57). Population also increased about one month after the primary productivity index rose (r = 0.86, R² = 0.75).
- **Productivity target:** A productivity index of 5.47 g O₂/day was estimated to support about 400 individuals. Measured total production ranged from 1.8 to 5.5 g/day between 26 July and 3 September 1976.
- **Habitat area:** At 0.82 m below the reference point, surface area was about 85% and volume about 38% of the original pre-pumping pool. The authors estimate a fluctuation between 200 and 400 individuals at that level.
- **Thermal ecology gaps:** The authors call for research on spawning temperatures, fry thermal tolerance and life-history thermal requirements of the native Grand Canyon fishes, and suggest that managing river temperatures could enhance them.
- **Policy conclusion:** Research in national parks is essential both for its intrinsic value and for informing decisions that express societal values, a point the authors illustrate with the Foley decision.
