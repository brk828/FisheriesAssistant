# Mountain Whitefish Utilization in Montana and a Pulp Mill Waste Fish Kill

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two Montana studies. The first, a utilization study of the [[Mountain Whitefish]], aimed to increase angler use of a common but underused game and food fish by documenting its distribution, capture methods, palatability and preparation. The second reports changes in [[Bottom Fauna]] composition and a [[Fish Kill]] on the [[Clark Fork River]] below a new cardboard pulp mill near [[Missoula]] in 1958. Both are grounded in field observation and, for the whitefish work, interviews and a formal taste panel.

## Background & Description

The [[Mountain Whitefish]] (listed as *Coregonus williamsoni* in the main text and *Prosopium williamsoni* in the literature cited) is native to cold streams and lakes on both sides of the Continental Divide, from Alaska south to the headwaters of the Colorado River. In Montana it is native to the Columbia, Missouri and Saskatchewan drainages. Other native game fish include [[Cutthroat Trout]] (all drainages), [[American Grayling]] (Missouri headwaters) and [[Dolly Varden]] (Columbia drainage).

Non-native fish were introduced into most cold waters, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Eastern Brook Trout]]. [[Kokanee]], [[Lake Trout]] and [[Lake Whitefish]] were introduced into a few lakes that also held mountain whitefish. According to the author, competition from these exotics, heavy fishing pressure and agricultural and industrial development greatly reduced the cutthroat trout, dolly varden and American grayling. The mountain whitefish persisted and was probably the most abundant cold-water game fish in Montana at the time.

Anglers often misidentify the species as American grayling or common sucker. Key distinguishing features include:
- An adipose fin, absent in suckers, [[Columbia River Chub]] and [[Squawfish]].
- A dorsal fin with fewer than 15 rays, smaller than the grayling's.
- More than 70 lateral-line scales, separating it from the [[Pygmy Whitefish]].
- A single skin fold between the anterior and posterior nostrils, a dorsal anterior ray no more than twice the posterior ray, and a pelvic fin origin behind the dorsal fin center, separating it from the [[Lake Whitefish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and abundance.** Information came from fisheries biologists, game wardens and fishermen. The species is most abundant in large rivers and the lower reaches of main tributaries, and less common in secondary tributaries and headwaters. Notable reports include:
- **Missouri drainage:** Common in the [[Missouri River]] between Three Forks and Toston, abundant from Toston to Great Falls. On the [[Yellowstone River]] between Gardiner and Columbus, creel census showed an average catch of about four fish per hour.
- **Madison and Jefferson systems:** Considerable numbers in the [[Madison River]] and its reservoirs, and abundant populations in the Jefferson River. The species was introduced into Cliff Lake, where it became very numerous.
- **Saskatchewan drainage:** Only about 20 miles of the St. Mary River lie within Montana, with very light fishing pressure.
- **Columbia drainage:** Abundant in the Clark Fork from the Idaho-Montana border upstream, and in the [[Bitterroot River]], [[Blackfoot River]] and [[Rock Creek]], which received most of the whitefishing pressure in that area. The [[Flathead Lake]] system also supported populations.

**Angler use.** Winter fishing was growing and was primarily for whitefish, since regulations prohibited trout fishing except in a few streams. Because trout remained abundant, whitefish were often released or discarded, which the author argued gave them a competitive edge. [[Laakso]] (1951) had reported food competition between mountain whitefish and trout.

**Water quality and the pulp mill.** The [[Waldorf Paper Company]] pulp mill, which produced cardboard pulp by the [[Kraft Process]] (about 200 tons of baled pulp per day), began operating in mid-November 1957. It is located about 12 stream miles downstream from Missoula. Improperly treated wastes, measured at about 3,000 gallons per minute, were discharged intermittently from November 1957 to about September 1958. Anglers and sportsmen's clubs reported discoloration and foam on the river during winter and spring 1957-58.

## Key Findings

**Mountain whitefish utilization study**

- **Interview base:** 414 fishermen were interviewed, 276 of them during winter whitefishing. Of more than 100 interviewed in summer, only three were fishing for whitefish.
- **Effort:** More than 300 hours of angling experiments identified effective tackle and techniques.
- **Tackle:** Cane poles up to about 20 feet with guides at least 1/2 inch in diameter resist icing. Nylon monofilament with a breaking strength of at least about 8 pounds is recommended for winter.
- **Hooks and rigs:** Number 10 or 12 short-shank hooks are most effective. A number 5 or 7 split-shot sinker sits about 6 inches above the hook, with a small float (one inch or less) to keep the bait near the bottom.
- **Baits:** Immature stonefly nymphs (hellgrammites or scratchers) were most preferred. Other baits included maggots (especially in winter), earthworms (spring runoff), grasshoppers (late summer) and artificial flies (summer). Flies larger than number 10 missed many strikes.
- **Maggot market:** Eighteen dealers reported that more than four million maggots are sold annually in Montana, at about 50 cents per can of 100 to 200.
- **Fishing methods:** Whitefish school where fast water meets slow shoreline water and in pools below riffles. Bait is drifted along the edge of fast water, and the strike should be allowed to pull the float under before setting the hook. Whitefish often rise to surface insects in summer and can be taken on dry flies, which are often mistaken for trout.

**Palatability experiment**

- Six judges (three men, three women; four with prior food-judging experience) rated 45 fish: 22 whitefish and 23 trout, collected from the Madison River between February 15 and April 18, 1953.
- Whitefish measured 9.6 to 14.3 inches and trout 8.6 to 15.2 inches in total length.
- Analysis of variance showed significant differences in grand averages except for juiciness and intensity of aroma and flavor. Trout scored higher in some categories, but all desirability ratings for whitefish averaged above 4, the threshold for satisfactory.
- Two judges (numbers four and six) found considerable differences between groups; the other four found little or none.

**Preparation, recipes and smoking**

- Fish under 10 inches are usually scaled and pan-fried; larger fish are often filleted, which removes a flavor some people find objectionable. Fish of one pound or more suit baking.
- Recipes tested included pan-fried, baked stuffed, baked creole, loaf, chowder and broiled fillets. More than 99 percent of panel ratings were excellent or good.
- Three smokehouse designs were satisfactory: a 55-gallon drum unit, a four-foot-square asbestos box unit, and a converted refrigerator unit held at about 200°F by a hot plate. Apple wood was preferred, with alder, willow, aspen and chokecherry also effective.
- Curing used either a salt brine (about 1 1/3 cups of salt per gallon of water) or dry salt, for about 24 hours. Iodized salt is not recommended because it imparts an undesirable flavor.
- Smoking was at 180°F for 30 minutes, followed by 10 to 12 hours at about 100°F.
- Custom smokehouse records from 16 operators showed 29,281 fish smoked in winter 1953-54 (largest individual total 4,014; group average 1,829). The author estimated more than 40,000 whitefish are smoked annually in Montana custom plants, at about 10 cents per fish.

**Clark Fork pulp mill study (July 15 to August 15, 1958)**

- **Bottom fauna:** At the Marcure Ranch station, about two miles below the effluent, sensitive (pollution-intolerant) organisms averaged 54 percent over four 1957 collection periods. Samples from July 15 and 24, 1958 averaged 17 percent.
- **Upstream control:** At Deep Creek, about three miles above the discharge, the July 15, 1958 sample was about ten percentage points below the 1957 figure of 76 percent. The authors considered this change much smaller and possibly within sampling error or normal fluctuation.
- **Fish kill:** Reported on the evening of July 31 near Alberton. The kill began immediately below the effluent and extended about 25 miles to the mouth of Fish Creek. Aerial and ground counts located at least 33 concentrations of dead fish, three of which held 112, 116 and 121 fish.
- **Species affected:** Rainbow trout, Rocky Mountain whitefish, redside shiner, Columbia River chub, squawfish, longnose sucker and largescale sucker.
- **Cause:** The State Fish Pathologist found no evidence of parasitic, protozoan or bacterial disease. The syndrome was consistent with death from a toxic substance.
- **Upstream reports:** Unverified reports of dead fish above the discharge were also received.
