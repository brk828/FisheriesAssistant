# Pumped-Storage Generation and Fish Kills at Salt River Dams, Arizona (1973)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

A 1973 newspaper report describes a dispute between the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) over [[Pumped-Storage Hydropower]] units at [[Canyon Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]]. The state agency warned that water exchanges between the lakes and the dams could upset the biological balance by altering lake temperatures, promoting algae blooms, and degrading water quality. SRP called the warning "unfounded." The article reports observed fish kills in the [[Tailrace]] that included [[Walleye]] and [[Threadfin Shad]], and it notes that research by ASU-affiliated scientists was still incomplete. No definitive conclusion is reached.

## Background & Description

- **Technology:** Pumped-storage generators can be reversed. Water drawn from below the dam is pumped back through the turbines, allowing power to be produced and stored without releasing water downstream. Conventional generators are driven only by water released from behind the dam.
- **Installation:** SRP began installing pumped-storage systems in 1968. The federal [[National Environmental Policy Act]] became law the following year. The system had been used in the East but had not previously been used in the Southwest.
- **Infrastructure:** SRP spent about $45 million installing pumped-storage units at Horse Mesa Dam (Apache Lake) and Mormon Flat Dam (Canyon Lake), and upgrading hydroelectric output from the Roosevelt and Saguaro lake generators. The two pumped-storage units began operation in 1972, and according to SRP they were reversed about 100 hours that year.
- **Water system:** The article describes a pump-storage system that circulates water among [[Saguaro Lake]], [[Canyon Lake]], and [[Apache Lake]] through penstocks at Mormon Flat and Horse Mesa dams.
- **Stakeholders:** The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (state fisheries chief Al Essbach), SRP (environmental spokesman J. Phil Clemons), and [[Arizona State University]] researchers, namely botanist [[Milton Sommerfeld]] (hired by SRP) and zoologist [[Wendell L. Minckley]] (hired by the state), all took part.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal stratification:** Officials stated that warm downstream water is forced beneath the [[Thermocline]] behind the dam. The thermocline, where warm surface water gives way to colder, low-oxygen water, lies at a depth of about 20 feet.
- **Thermocline disruption:** Sommerfeld reported that short-term pumped-storage operation broke the thermocline across a large area behind the dams. He said it re-formed after the generator tailrace was aimed downstream.
- **Algae and water quality:** Department spokesmen worried that pumped-storage operation could encourage [[Algal Blooms]], which could hurt fishing and boating and degrade water supplied to the Phoenix Valley.
- **Fish kills:** Essbach reported large kills at Canyon Lake the previous summer during test pumping phases. He personally collected walleye showing broken swim bladders, broken backs, and internal bleeding. He also reported that threadfin shad were drawn through the turbines.
- **Species affected:** Clemons said the fish kills affected species that "congregate in the tailrace," including walleye and threadfin shad. The threadfin shad serves as forage for predatory game fish.
- **Possible benefit:** Essbach noted that pumped storage might open up lake volume that had not previously been occupied by fish.
- **Biological change:** Early data from Sommerfeld and Minckley suggested possible loss of some resident species, which might be replaced by new forms of plankton and aquatic plants. Sommerfeld focused on algae.

## Key Findings

1. **Unresolved scientific dispute.** The state and SRP disagreed on whether pumped-storage operation harms lake life. Clemons said nothing scientifically conclusive had linked the system to the effects the state described. Minckley said the data included some "pretty scary" results, and that if the thermocline breaks, "the result cannot be beneficial... for fish and water quality."
2. **Documented fish mortality.** Fish kills occurred during pumped-storage operations, and SRP acknowledged that fish are killed. The affected fish included walleye and threadfin shad.
3. **Thermocline effects.** Sommerfeld reported that operation broke the thermocline over a large area, which later recovered after the tailrace was aimed downstream.
4. **Research constraints.** Minckley said SRP did not notify him when the Canyon and Apache lake generators would be reversed, which slowed his research. The state said it was waiting for Minckley's conclusions before acting.
5. **Mitigation options discussed.** Proposals included electrically charged weirs to keep fish away from the penstocks and deflectors at the pumpback point. Essbach said he did not know what could be done "at this point," adding, "The Valley needs the electricity and the Valley needs the water."
6. **Potential legal and regulatory escalation.** Essbach predicted a legal fight that might involve the State Water Commission and possibly the Federal Power Commission.
7. **Claimed benefits.** SRP argued that pumped storage helps regulate lake levels, especially in winter when water demand is low, and allows power generation without needless downstream releases.
