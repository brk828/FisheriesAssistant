# No Surprises Policy: Final Rule on Habitat Conservation Plan Assurances (1998)

#endangered-species #habitat-conservation-plan #regulatory-policy

## Executive Summary

This document is a Federal Register final rule (Vol. 63, No. 35, February 23, 1998) issued jointly by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS, Department of the Interior) and the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS, Department of Commerce). It adopts the [[No Surprises Policy]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), which gives non-Federal landowners regulatory certainty when they participate in [[Habitat Conservation Plan]]s (HCPs) that support an [[Incidental Take Permit]] issued under [[Section 10 Permit|ESA Section 10(a)(1)(B)]]. The rule takes effect March 25, 1998. The preamble contains the Services' responses to public comments on the May 29, 1997 proposed rule (62 FR 29091), which drew more than 800 comments, and it sets out the amended regulatory text for 50 CFR Parts 17 and 222.

The core trade-off is that landowners who implement an approved HCP receive assurances that they will not be asked for additional land, water, or money if unforeseen or changed circumstances arise. In exchange, they commit to long-term species conservation.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The ESA's [[Section 9 Take Prohibition]] bars unauthorized "take" of listed species. Section 10(a) lets the Services authorize incidental take in otherwise lawful activities, provided an approved HCP minimizes and mitigates take and avoids jeopardy. The No Surprises Policy was designed to give non-Federal landowners an incentive to join this process.
- **Rationale:** The Services noted that much listed-species habitat lies on non-Federal land. They stated that "more than half of the species listed have 80 percent of their habitat on non-Federal lands." They also reported that although thousands of acres of species habitat were disappearing each year, only a handful of HCPs had been sought and approved since 1982.
- **Policy history:** The draft [[Habitat Conservation Planning Handbook]] was published for comment on December 21, 1994 (59 FR 65782). The final Handbook followed on December 2, 1996 (61 FR 63854).
- **Scope of assurances:** Assurances apply only to incidental take permits issued under section 10(a)(1)(B). They do not extend to Federal agencies with a continuing obligation under [[Section 7 Consultation]] (section 7(a)(1)). They apply only to species "adequately covered" by the conservation plan and only while the plan is being properly implemented.
- **Key regulatory amendments:** The rule adds paragraphs (b)(5) and (b)(6) to 50 CFR 17.22 (endangered species) and 50 CFR 17.32 (threatened species), and adds a new section to Part 222 (NMFS). Definitions of "adequately covered," "changed circumstances," "unforeseen circumstances," "operating conservation program," and "habitat conservation plan" are added to 50 CFR 222.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat functions:** The Services stated that in some situations, such as when an HCP covers most of a species' range or covers severely depleted species, essential habitat functions must be secured for the full permit term. Forest HCPs that include aquatic species may allow significant timber harvest early on, with riparian measures taking "decades" to produce stream conditions that provide essential habitat.
- **Range-based criteria:** For unforeseen-circumstance decisions, the Director or Assistant Administrator considers factors such as the size of the species' current range, the percentage of range adversely affected and conserved by the plan, the ecological significance of the affected portion, the level of biological knowledge, and whether failure to adopt additional conservation measures would appreciably reduce the likelihood of survival and recovery in the wild.
- **Unlisted species:** Unlisted species may be covered in an HCP, and assurances apply to them if they are "adequately covered." Species with significant biological data gaps are not included in the permit unless adaptive management can close those gaps.
- **Migratory birds:** The FWS concluded that species covered by [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]] and [[Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act]] protections will be adequately protected under an HCP that is properly implemented. Assurances can therefore double as special-purpose permits under the MBTA and BGEPA for covered species.

## Key Findings

1. **Assurance structure:** Permittees receive a single level of assurances, which now applies to all HCPs. The earlier "net benefit" tier was eliminated. Assurance duration matches the permit term.
2. **Unforeseen circumstances:** These are defined as events that "could not reasonably have been anticipated by plan developers and the Service" at the time of plan negotiation, such as new species listings or rare natural catastrophic events. Naturally recurring catastrophes in the area, along with reasonably foreseeable changes such as drought, flood, and disease, are expected to be addressed in the HCP.
3. **Burden of proof:** The Director (FWS) or Assistant Administrator (NMFS) must demonstrate, using the best available scientific and commercial data, that unforeseen circumstances exist. Additional measures are limited to modifications within conserved habitat areas or the operating conservation program, and may not require new land, water, or money without the permittee's consent.
4. **Changed circumstances:** These are anticipated in the plan and handled under the operating conservation program.
5. **Adaptive management:** Where biological data gaps are significant, adaptive management is incorporated into the HCP as an integral component. The rule does not negate such provisions. The Services said that when uncertainty is high, they will either deny the permit or build in adaptive management and monitoring.
6. **Funding:** Applicants must ensure "adequate funding" for the plan. Non-guaranteed county or state bonds do not qualify unless a reevaluation mechanism is included. This mechanism was used in the Multiple Species Conservation Program for southwestern [[San Diego County]], California. Assurances lapse if a major component, such as the funding strategy, is never initiated.
7. **Permit-shield removed:** The proposed permit-shield provision was dropped from the final rule after review of [[Shell Oil v. EPA]] (D.C. Cir. 1991). That decision concerned RCRA permits, which have shorter terms and different modification authority. The Services nonetheless said they will continue not to enforce section 9 against a permittee in full compliance with permit terms.
8. **Citizen oversight:** The rule does not restrict citizen suits or public comment. HCP permits, approved management plans, and funding mechanisms form part of the administrative record and are available to the public.
9. **Federal responsibility:** Responsibility for protecting species beyond an HCP's terms rests mainly with the Services. Their tools include land acquisition or exchange (e.g., under the [[Federal Land Policy and Management Act]]), habitat restoration, translocation, section 6 state cooperation, and section 5 land acquisition. Several of these depend on appropriations, while others do not.
10. **Economic and procedural findings:** The Services concluded that the rule imposes no additional costs on non-Federal entities and no significant effect on small entities. It falls below the $100 million Unfunded Mandates threshold. The rule's information collection is covered by existing OMB approval (listed as 1018-0094, expiring February 28, 2001). It was also reviewed under Executive Orders 12866 and 12988 and was categorically excluded from NEPA analysis under 516 DM 2, Appendix 1.
11. **Terminology changes:** "Properly functioning" was replaced with "properly implemented." "Property interests" was replaced with "natural resources." The FWS and NMFS also agreed to use consistent terminology ("unforeseen," not "extraordinary").
12. **Emergency timber salvage precedent:** The Services cited the 1995 emergency salvage rider (Public Law 104-19) as an example of responding to social change. Agencies used a streamlined section 7 process to comply with the ESA while implementing the rider.
