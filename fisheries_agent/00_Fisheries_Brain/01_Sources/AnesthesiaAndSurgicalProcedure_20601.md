# Anesthesia and Surgical Procedures for Implanting Radio Transmitters into Grass Carp

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This methods study, from the [[University of Florida]], evaluates anesthetics and surgical techniques for implanting [[Radio Telemetry]] transmitters into the body cavity of grass carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) weighing 1.6–3.7 kg. The work supports a larger project using [[Grass Carp]] for [[Aquatic Weed Control]] in southern Florida canals, where externally attached tags would foul in dense vegetation. [[Quinaldine]] was effective below about 26 °C but lethal at the concentrations needed for surgery in warm water (above about 29 °C). [[MS-222]] (tricaine methanesulfonate) was suitable at all tested temperatures. Midventral incisions were preferred over lateral incisions for ease of operation and to avoid puncturing ovaries.

## Background & Description

Grass carp were stocked in canals for weed control, and the researchers needed internal, long-term tag placement because the species lacks a well-defined stomach (Hickling 1966), so gastric placement was unsuitable. Initial surgical procedures adapted from [[Flathead Catfish]] tagging (Hart and Summerfelt 1975) were only partly successful. Failures stemmed from suturing problems and post-operative mortality linked to handling stress and the higher quinaldine doses required in warm water (29–33 °C).

Tags were AVM fish modules (105 mm long, 20 mm diameter), disinfected in Amphyl and coated in molten beeswax. Fish were held in 3.7 m diameter outdoor plastic pools at 10–30 fish per pool, supplied with reservoir water at 4–6 L/min, with dissolved oxygen above 4.0 mg/L. Reservoir water quality (Table 1) had a mean dissolved oxygen of 6.2 mg/L (range 4.8–7.3), conductivity of 313 µmhos/cm (305–322), pH 7.2 (6.7–7.6), alkalinity of 145 mg/L as CaCO₃ (142–148), and hardness of 163 mg/L as CaCO₃ (158–167).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water temperature strongly controlled anesthetic performance. Warm-water trials ran at 28–32 °C in summer, while cool-water trials ran at 21–26 °C in October–November. Surgery was performed year-round and under field conditions, so the protocols had to function across this temperature range. Dissolved oxygen fell during anesthetic exposure, for example from 4.2 to 2.4 mg/L in quinaldine treatments and from 3.8 to 2.2 mg/L in MS-222 treatments, which bears on fish welfare during handling. Post-operative holding temperatures were 27–31 °C (surgery series 1) and 25–29 °C (series 2) over 30-day observation periods.

Pathogen pressure was also a concern. A [[Flexibacter Columnaris]] outbreak began 7 days after surgery and killed 13 implanted fish in the lateral-incision group. The authors note that this epizootic cut their trials short.

## Key Findings

**Anesthetic performance**
- **Quinaldine, warm water (30 °C):** 5-minute exposure to 50 mg/L induced anesthesia suitable for surgery. Maintenance at 33 or 50 mg/L kept fish anesthetized for 20 minutes, but all 20 fish died within 40 hours (5 dead and 7 moribund at 18 hours). Gross necropsy of 5 dead fish revealed only enlarged gall bladders.
- **Quinaldine, cool water (22 °C):** 10 mg/L and 50 mg/L both gave adequate anesthesia in 5 minutes. All 24 fish (12 per group) survived.
- **MS-222, warm water (31 °C):** Induction at 100 mg/L followed by maintenance at 75 mg/L (6/6 survived) or 100 mg/L (8/10 survived) was adequate for surgery. Maintenance at 50 mg/L was inadequate, with fish too active for surgery. Two fish died at 18 hours in the 100 mg/L group.

**Surgical technique**
- **Lateral incision:** 18 fish were operated on in September 1980 in about 15 minutes each. Ovaries of mature females under 3.0 kg impeded the procedure. Two surgeries were terminated after the ovary wall was punctured. The Flexibacter outbreak killed 13 of the 18 implanted fish. Sutures held on all dead fish, and 3 survivors healed within 30 days.
- **Midventral incision (preferred):** 18 fish were operated on in October 1980 in about 13 minutes each, with all surviving and healing by 30 days. An additional 44 fish were implanted from November 1980 to September 1981, with no surgical deaths. Of the 56 fish implanted this way, 20% (11 of 56, from the 44-fish series plus the 18-fish October group in total) lost tags through ruptured sutures. The midventral approach avoided ribs (whose contraction complicated lateral surgery), required no removal of large scales, and was easier to suture through the thinner abdominal wall.

**Complications and recommendations**
- One midventral-implanted fish had its tag eroded through the rectum, which the authors attribute to tag mobility in the body cavity.
- Suture failure is attributed to vigorous post-operative activity and tag abrasion. The authors recommend heavier [[Dexon Sutures]] (3-0 polyglycolic acid), anchoring the tag to the body wall with a non-dissolving suture, and further testing of MS-222 above 75 mg/L and above 31 °C.
- The authors conclude that the midventral procedure provides a satisfactory year-round, field-applicable method for implanting tags into grass carp weighing 1.6–3.7 kg.
