# Fish and Fishing in Spear Lake, Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report documents a multi-year creel census and mark-recapture study of the fish community of [[Spear Lake]], a 40-acre glacial moraine lake in Kosciusko County, Indiana. The work was carried out in 1944–45 and again in 1947–49 as part of the [[Indiana Lake and Stream Survey]], run jointly by Indiana University and the Indiana Department of Conservation. The lake was studied under wartime-reduced fishing pressure in 1944, then under increased pressure after 1945, and again after Indiana opened year-round fishing for pan fish in 1948.

The total catch stayed within 44–68 lb per acre per year, and fishing was dominated by cane-pole anglers using live baits. [[Bluegill Sunfish]] made up more than half of the catch by weight. Bluegill abundance and exploitation changed the most: the legal population fell from roughly 600 fish per acre to about 310 per acre, driven chiefly by weaker age III recruitment from 1947 onward. Survival of legal-size bluegills dropped from 29% to 17%.

## Background & Description

The study was designed to measure how a lake's fish populations respond to changes in fishing pressure. Spear Lake was selected in 1944 because it was of convenient size (40 acres), had no lakeside cottages, had a single boat landing that simplified census work, and had a cooperative boat owner, Mr. Jethro Greider. The lake was reported to offer somewhat better than average fishing.

The lake has two broadly joined basins. The maximum depth is 40 feet (13 m) in the south basin, and the average depth is about 15 feet. The bottom is loose deposit with high organic content. Dense [[Spatterdock]] (*Nuphar*) margins the shore across an area of 33 acres inside the dock line. In spring high water, the water area increases by about half.

Field methods combined several elements:
- A summer-long creel census, typically at least 90% complete, in which fish were measured and weighed.
- Fin-clip marking, with recovery from traps and from fishermen's catches.
- Random collection of scales from fishermen's catches for [[Scale Analysis]] of age and growth.

Results were analyzed with the [[Petersen Estimator]] as modified by [[Bailey Formula]] (P = M(H+1)/(Z+1)), which gives a less biased estimate than the simple form, especially when recaptures are few.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishing regime.** Fishing was light in 1944 (about 3,700 pole-hours, or 92 per acre). Hours nearly tripled in 1945, following the end of the war in Europe, then stayed roughly constant through 1949. Cane-pole fishing with small baits (worms, crickets, catalpa worms) made up more than 90% of pole-hours. Fishing was largely restricted to April in the spring of 1944–47 under the May 1–June 15 closed season. Spring fishing was relatively light in 1948 and important in 1949.

**Community composition.** The lake supports a mixed centrarchid-dominated assemblage. Along with the sunfishes and [[Largemouth Bass]], it holds [[Brown Bullhead]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Warmouth]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and small numbers of [[Yellow Perch]], [[Lake Chubsucker]], [[Mud Pickerel]], [[Bowfin]], and [[Spotted Gar]]. Carp were not seen. Golden shiner and green sunfish were recorded in small numbers. Most non-game species were scarce, and the native lake chubsucker was probably the most common of them.

**Regional comparison.** Spear Lake's catch per acre compares well with other Indiana lakes:

| Lake | Catch (lb/acre) | Period |
|---|---|---|
| Spear | 57 | 1944–45, 1947–49 |
| [[Shoe Lake]] | 36 | 1941–43 |
| Muskellunge | 28 | 1942–43 |
| Gordy | 25 | 1950 |
| Yellowwood | 10 | 1948 |

Only the 1941 catch of Shoe Lake (46 lb/acre) overlapped the lower end of the Spear Lake range.

**Regulatory change.** In 1948 Indiana removed the closed season for pan fish, so Spear Lake became a natural monitoring site for the effect of year-round fishing. Bass remained protected for 1.5 months in the spring.

## Key Findings

**Catch and effort**
- Annual catch ranged from 44 to 68 lb per acre, greatest in 1945 and least in 1948. The 1949 catch (59 lb per acre) was close to the 1944 value (58 lb per acre).
- Overall catch was remarkably steady despite a near tripling of fishing effort in 1945 and the opening of spring fishing in 1948.
- Catch per 10 pole-hours fell from 6.3 lb in 1944 to 2.4 lb in 1945, then stayed between 1.9 and 2.4 lb in later years.
- Effort may have been more effective in 1944, when a larger share of anglers were local residents familiar with the lake.

**Bluegill population**
- The legal bluegill population was about 600 fish per acre in 1944–45 and fell to about 310 per acre by 1948–49, or from 113 to 51 lb per acre. Table 12 gives 579, 617, 390, 300 and 320 fish per acre for 1944, 1945, 1947, 1948 and 1949.
- The decline was caused mainly by a drop in the abundance of age III recruits after 1945. The poorer year-classes of 1944–45 came from the large adult stocks of 1944–45, which suggests an inverse relation between spawning stock and recruitment, though the data do not confirm it.
- Exploitation of legal-size bluegills rose from 27% in 1944 to 38–46% in 1945–49. Intermediate-size fish were removed at more than 50% in the later period.
- Year-to-year survival fell from 29% (1944–45) to 17% (1948–49). The instantaneous natural mortality rate was about 0.78.
- The instantaneous fishing rate roughly doubled, from 0.48 to 0.95. Natural mortality did not change significantly.
- Growth of legal age III bluegills ranged from a mean of 155 mm in 1944 down to 135 mm in 1947. Growth did not correlate consistently with year-class strength.

**Other species**
- [[Redear Sunfish]] legal population was about a third of the bluegill population (about 40% by weight in the detailed tables). Redears were caught mainly in spring. Exploitation ranged from 26% (1944) to 40% (1947), and survival was estimated at 0.235 for the wartime period and 0.178 afterward.
- The legal largemouth bass population was 5–7 fish per acre, and about one-third were caught in a year.
- Pumpkinseed exploitation was 46–62% in most years, but the population was small (under 300 fish in four years, with about 900 in 1949).
- Black crappie stock was estimated at 310–1,620 fish. Estimated natural causes accounted for about 5/6 of mortality among legal-size crappies.
- Brown bullhead exploitation was low (6–10%), with a population of 300–400.
- Yellow bullhead exploitation ranged from 9% to 29%, with a population of 190–450.
- Bowfin numbered about 40–50 fish, and the 1944 trap catch of 77 included many repeat captures.
- Lake chubsucker and mud pickerel were scarce and damaged easily in traps. Chubsucker population estimates were poorly constrained, with a figure of up to about 500 individuals in the text and "a few thousand" in the abstract.
- Gar numbers were small but probably underestimated, because traps and angling methods did not suit them.

**Methodological notes**
- Exploitation rates were probably underestimated by about 5% (possibly up to 10%) because of the incomplete census and the way marking was timed.
- Trap-based Petersen estimates were consistently lower than those from fishermen's recaptures, so the authors used fishermen's recoveries as the basis for population estimates.
- Warmouth fishing was light (3.5–20% exploitation), so the authors judged natural mortality to be about the same as for other sunfish.
