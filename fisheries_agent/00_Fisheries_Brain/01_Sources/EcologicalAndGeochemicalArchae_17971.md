# Ecological and Geochemical Archaeology in the Southern Maya Lowlands

#limnology #paleolimnology #paleoecology #maya-lowlands

## Executive Summary

Cowgill and Hutchinson test several hypotheses for the collapse of [[Classic Maya Civilization]] in the southern lowlands of the [[Petén]], Guatemala, using agronomic data, sediment cores, pollen, chloride chemistry and field observations. Their main conclusions are that the central Petén could support a much larger agricultural population under indigenous methods than it does today, that agriculture developed in an originally grassland landscape, that erosion rates changed little during the Classic period, and that climatic change was modest. They conclude that the evidence does not support external ecological causes of the collapse, and that a combination of causes, possibly including demographic factors, is more likely.

## Background & Description

The paper, a summary of research presented in 1963, addresses the depopulation of the Petén, which the authors describe as a special case compared with neighbouring Maya groups that did not suffer permanent depopulation. The authors review hypotheses that blame land misuse or climate:

- **Cook-Bartlett Hypothesis:** Population growth shortened the forest fallow cycle, converting forest into grassland too hard to plant (O. F. Cook, Bartlett).
- **Cooke-Ricketson Hypothesis:** Intensive agriculture caused erosion that silted lakes (Cooke, Ricketson), supported by a pit at [[Uaxactún]] in the Bajo de Joventud.
- **Environmental Determinism:** [[Meggers Environmental Determinism]] argues that tropical settings cannot sustain advanced civilization.
- **Climatic Hypotheses:** Huntington and Russell proposed drier or wetter climates as drivers of decline.

Earlier agronomic work by [[Steggerda Plot Experiments]], Emerson, Kempton and Hester produced conflicting results, and much of it came from northern Yucatán. The authors argue that these findings were extrapolated to all of Middle America without justification.

The study combined several lines of evidence:

- A 1959 agricultural field study ([[Milpa Agriculture]]).
- A 511 cm pit in the [[Bajo de Santa Fé]], a seasonal swamp east of [[Tikal]].
- Two cores from [[Laguna de Petenxil]].
- Pollen and microfossil analysis.
- Chloride geochemistry.
- Field observations of [[Aguada]] basins and [[Chultún]] pits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Agriculture and land use.** Swidden (milpa) cultivation in the central Petén allowed four crops per year: first-year milpa, second-year milpa, San José (dry season, low-lying land) and Yaxkin (wet season, slopes). Estimated carrying capacity was 100 to 200 people per square mile, compared with 60 per square mile estimated by Hester for northern Yucatán. The authors attribute the difference to the longer fallow needed in the north. One milpero could supply food for 12.6 persons. Nutrient analyses showed that burning reduced most soil elements, that yields declined with successive cropping, and that fallow restored nutrients. Grass invasion was judged not to have caused the agricultural decline.

**Bajo sedimentation.** The Bajo de Santa Fé contained 5.11 m of sediment, mainly montmorillonite clay, with organic carbon dated to 11,560 ± 360 years B.P. The clay was derived from dissolved limestone, and the deposit was laid down in a seasonal swamp rather than an open lake. Flint nodules, absence of diatoms and pollen, haematite, and fossilized roots indicate a forest-covered swamp throughout the record. Mean sedimentation was about 40 cm per millennium.

**Lake sediments.** Laguna de Petenxil cores (at most 2.5 m long, covering about four millennia) consist mainly of halloysite with about 5% organic carbon. Sedimentation was 0.7 to 0.9 mm per year until 635 B.P. and 1.4 mm per year above that, and 0.5 mm per year between 3990 and 2050 B.P. in the longer core.

**Vegetation history.** Tsukada's pollen work recorded an early savanna phase with *Quercus*, *Pinus* and grasses, followed by a phase with *Zea* (maize) and an *Ambrosia*-like cultivation weed, and then a later return of tropical forest taxa (Moraceae, *Terminalia*, *Zanthoxylon*) as planting declined after about 1300 B.P. Carbonized grass epidermis supports the view that early agriculture was based on burning grassland.

**Climate indicators.** Chloride was the only geochemical proxy considered. Rainwater averaged 1.57 mg/L chloride. Assuming a mean rainfall of 1800 mm, the authors calculated that about 79.2% of rainfall ran off the Bajo, with the remaining 20.8% retained as chloride. Chloride maxima in the Bajo profile are interpreted as periods of longer wet seasons or intensified transpiration concentration. The Petenxil record suggests lake levels were higher during the Classic period and lower during the post-Classic.

## Key Findings

1. **Carrying capacity was high.** The central Petén could support a much larger indigenous agricultural population than it does at present.

2. **Agricultural decline was real but manageable.** Nutrient depletion from successive cropping explains the observed yield decline. Fallow periods restored soil nutrients, and the modern system was judged the most efficient land use available with its means.

3. **Labour was sufficient.** Non-metallic clearing would still leave close to half the labour supply free, adequate for monumental construction. The authors state that the Classic population cannot be proven to have stayed within slash-and-burn limits, but a settlement-pattern argument makes this unlikely.

4. **Bajo sediments do not support widespread lake silting.** The Bajo de Santa Fé was a seasonal swamp for 11,560 years. Ricketson's pit may have been in a marginal deposit near a stream bed. The authors reject the claim that lakes were destroyed by erosion at Tikal.

5. **Erosion rates changed little.** Bajo erosion increased by at most a factor of 1.5 with human activity. The main erosion increase at Petenxil occurred in post-Classic or post-Columbian times, probably linked to changes in settlement after the rumoured arrival of the Spanish in the 16th century.

6. **Vegetation changed after agriculture.** Agriculture developed in grassland, and forest returned after cultivation declined. A mineral in the Bajo (a variety of [[Crandallite]], formed about the fifth millennium B.C.) may indicate a past vegetation shift involving [[Melastomaceae]], but this remains hypothetical.

7. **Climate changes were small.** Lake levels and chloride data suggest a somewhat wetter period around 2000 B.P. Any drying in the Late and Post-Classic was not catastrophic. Rainfall did not increase during forest re-establishment.

8. **Aguadas are unreliable records.** Most [[Aguada]] basins dry out seasonally, which oxidizes organic and microfossil material. Peccary wallowing has enlarged at least one aguada, complicating interpretation of human enlargement.

9. **Chultunes may be natural in origin.** Uprooted *Sabal* palms leave pit-like holes resembling chultunes. Some were later modified by the Maya.

10. **Demographic factors may contribute.** Female Indian children in the Petén show high mortality, producing an adolescent sex ratio of about 180 males per 100 females (see [[Sex Ratio]]). The authors suggest this may have contributed to depopulation.

11. **Overall conclusion.** The authors invalidate most external ecological explanations of the collapse and propose a multiplicity of causes acting differently across the region.
