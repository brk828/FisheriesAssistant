## Executive Summary
This document is a scholarly book review of **[[The Fishes of Illinois]]** (1979) by Philip W. Smith, evaluated as a successor to the classic **[[Forbes and Richardson]]** (1908) monograph of the same name. The reviewer, Reeve M. Bailey, assesses the volume as an authoritative identification guide for the Illinois freshwater fish fauna, combining practical keys, diagnostic synonymies, distribution maps, and illustrations. Rather than being a primary research study on a single taxon, the work is a comprehensive regional ichthyology documenting **199–200 species** across **28 families**, with a particular emphasis on tracking faunal change (range contraction, extirpation, and range expansion) across roughly seven decades of intensive land use, industrialization, and population growth in Illinois.

## Background & Description
Rather than focusing on the morphology of a single species, the book (and thus this review) addresses fauna-wide diagnostic and illustrative features:

- **Identification tools**: Practical dichotomous keys down to family (28 families) and species level, skeleton synonymies, and diagnoses; extended descriptions were deliberately omitted in favor of succinct variational data with external references.
- **Illustrations**: Each species account includes a distribution map and, for most species, a large drawing (~14–16 cm). Eight new full-page color plates depict 34 species, only two of which (**[[Etheostoma Asprigene]]** and **[[Etheostoma Exile]]**) duplicate plates from the original Forbes and Richardson work. Black-and-white figures are a mix of photocopied color plates and new line drawings of variable quality.
- **Diagnostic dentition**: Smith deemphasized pharyngeal dentition as a diagnostic character in **[[Cyprinidae]]**, omitting tooth formulae from most keys and genus diagnoses except in **[[Nocomis]]** and **[[Notropis]]**. The reviewer notes recorded errors in tooth-count formulae for **[[Notropis Fumeus]]** (typically 2,4-4,2) and **[[Notropis Hubbsi]]** (typically 4-4), and argues that omission of distinctive pharyngeal arch morphology (e.g., in **[[Ctenopharyngodon]]** and **[[Cyprinus]]**) reduces the reference value of the text for tasks such as identifying prey items from stomach contents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The review highlights the book's central ecological contribution: documenting habitat and distributional shifts within Illinois' riverine and lacustrine systems over time.

- **Historical vs. modern distribution**: Smith compared pre-1908 (mostly 1880–1905) distributional records against post-1950 (mostly 1962–72) collection data using distinct map symbols to visualize range change for each species. Notably, the 1908–1950 interval (which included the significant **[[Barnickol and Starrett]]** 1951 Mississippi River survey) was excluded from the mapped comparisons, a point criticized by the reviewer as a methodological gap.
- **Range contraction**: Most species subjected to anthropogenic landscape change, industrialization (especially near Lake Michigan and major rivers), and population growth show contracted ranges, exemplified by **[[Notropis Spilopterus]]**.
- **Range expansion / pollution tolerance**: A minority of species tolerant of water quality degradation have expanded their range and increased in abundance, exemplified by **[[Notropis Lutrensis]]** (red shiner).
- **Drivers of change**: The text situates these shifts within broader habitat degradation, hydrographic modification, and the proliferation of man-made waterways, which also enabled introduction/intrusion of non-native taxa.

## Key Findings
- The Illinois fish fauna, as documented by Smith (1979), comprises **199–200 species** (a 200th species reported on p. 211), a substantial increase from the **150 native species** recognized by Forbes and Richardson in 1908.
- **13 species** are introduced or are aquatic intruders that colonized via man-made waterways.
- **9 (possibly up to 12) species** have been extirpated from Illinois in recent history, with several additional species persisting at critically low levels.
- The increase in known faunal diversity since 1908 is attributed less to new discoveries of rare/peripheral species and more to **taxonomic splitting**, where former single "species" have been resolved into species complexes. Example: **[[Hybognathus]]** went from 2 recognized species (one since reclassified to another genus) to 5 species; **[[Notropis]]** increased from 17 to 28 species despite 2 taxa being removed from the genus.
- Illinois-focused ichthyology texts (Forbes & Richardson 1908/1920 plus Smith 1979) collectively represent one of the most extensive color-plate records of American freshwater fishes, with the 1920 second edition alone depicting 68 species in color.
- The review identifies minor errors (e.g., misspelling of **[[Sciaenidae]]** on p. 298, incorrect pharyngeal tooth formulae) but concludes these are trivial relative to the volume's overall scientific and reference value.
- The work is framed as reinforcing Illinois' status as one of the few U.S. states/Canadian provinces with a "top-rated ichthyology," continuing a lineage of high-quality regional faunal science supported by the **[[Illinois State Natural History Survey]]**.
