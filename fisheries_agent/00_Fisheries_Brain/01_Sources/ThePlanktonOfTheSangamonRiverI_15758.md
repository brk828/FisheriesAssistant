# Plankton of the Sangamon River in the Summer of 1929

#plankton #limnology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin by [[Samuel Eddy]] (published November 1931) examines how a [[Sewage Treatment Plant]] at Springfield and the [[Lake Decatur]] impoundment at Decatur have changed the abundance and composition of [[Plankton]] in the [[Sangamon River]]. Collections were made at nine stations in June, July and September 1929. The study concludes that the lake acts as a plankton reservoir whose organisms are carried downstream, and that the once-polluted lower river now supports a clean-water plankton community that is richer than it was in earlier surveys.

## Background & Description

The Sangamon River is a small central Illinois stream about 237 miles long, draining about 5,390 square miles. Its major tributaries are [[Salt Creek]] (1,940 square miles) and the South Fork (846 square miles). The river flows through glacial till in a well-worn valley, falls about 120 feet in the first 10 miles and about 300 feet over the rest of its course, and has a sand or fine-silt bottom.

Pollution history shaped the river. Before 1923 Decatur discharged all of its sewage below a small dam. During low water the sewage formed the entire flow, and the reach below Decatur was described as devoid of normal aquatic life. Conditions improved after the following changes:

- **1923:** Decatur completed a large dam, creating a lake about one-half mile wide and 12 miles long.
- **1924:** A sewage disposal plant began treating one-third of the city's wastes; in **1928** it was enlarged to treat all of them.
- **1929:** The Springfield sewage treatment plant began operation on July 10, 1929.

The author relied on his own observations from 1923 to 1928 and on earlier work by Jewell (1920) on water quality, along with comparisons to the [[Rock River]], [[Illinois River]] and [[Kaskaskia River]]. The author placed the Sangamon in an intermediate category: plankton is abundant enough to enter fish food chains, but fish yield is not appreciably higher than in the Kaskaskia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Water was dipped from mid-channel with a ten-liter bucket. At each station except Lake Decatur, one 100-liter silk-net collection and one one-liter collection (formalin-preserved, settled and decanted for nannoplankton) were taken. Organisms were counted in a [[Sedgwick-Rafter Slide]] and reported per cubic meter. Lake Decatur was sampled from bottom to surface at [[Lost Bridge]] and averaged.

**Physical conditions.**
- Depth and width: 1–2 ft deep and 25–40 ft wide at Mahomet; 8–10 ft deep and 200–210 ft wide at Chandlerville; 10–18 ft deep in the Lake Decatur channel.
- Current: about 0.5 mph at Mahomet and Monticello; none detectable in the main lake; about 1 mph at Harristown and Illiopolis; too slow to estimate just above the Springfield dam.
- River level: September was about 3¼ feet lower than June and July, while the lake level stayed nearly constant (610.25 ft in June–July; 609.95 ft in September).

**Water chemistry.**
- Temperature: 24–25 °C in June and 25–28 °C in July. The September thermometer broke, so no data were recorded.
- pH: consistently about 7.6 in summer, falling to 7.0 or lower in winter in earlier years.
- Dissolved oxygen (June, cc/L): Mahomet 4.75; Monticello 4.06; Decatur 4.62; Harristown 4.06; Illiopolis 4.62; Riverton 4.25; Petersburg 4.25; Chandlerville 6.47. Springfield data were lost. The author judged supply sufficient for abundant aquatic life throughout.

**Typical taxa.** The plankton below Monticello resembled that of most shallow lakes and large streams in North America. Conspicuous forms included:
- Protozoans: [[Codonella Cratera]] and [[Ceratium Hirundinella]]
- Rotifers: *Brachionus*, *Synchaeta*, *Polyarthra* and *Keratella*
- Cladocerans: [[Moina Affinis]], [[Daphnia Longispina]] and [[Bosmina Longirostris]]
- Copepods: *Diaptomus siciloides* and [[Cyclops Bicuspidatus]]

Bottom diatoms and protozoans were often swept into collections from shallow reaches such as Monticello, Harristown and Illiopolis.

**Lake as reservoir.** Water detention in Lake Decatur was estimated at two weeks to two months, at least twice as long as in the rest of the river. At 0.5 mph, water would take about 20 days to travel from source to mouth. Lake plankton carried over the dam reached Chandlerville about a week later. The author concluded that downstream plankton in June and July largely originated in the lake and thinned out through dilution by tributaries.

## Key Findings

**Plankton distribution by station (cc/m³ and species count, June 25–27 / July 26–28 / September 10–12)**
- Lake Decatur: 15.20 / 9.50 / 9.60 cc/m³; 36 / 47 / 25 species.
- Harristown: 6.00 / 8.00 / 10.00 cc/m³; 22 / 34 / 27 species.
- Illiopolis: 9.00 / 8.00 / 8.40 cc/m³; 23 / 31 / 26 species.
- Riverton: 3.00 / 3.90 / 3.60 cc/m³; 17 / 35 / 35 species.
- Springfield: 12.60 cc/m³ in September; 28 / 36 species in the later months.
- Petersburg: 2.20 / 0.40 / 17.00 cc/m³; 22 / 22 / 39 species.
- Chandlerville: 0.50 / 0.30 / 6.00 cc/m³; 24 / 22 / 30 species.
- Mahomet: no plankton in June, July or September.
- Monticello: very scanty plankton in June and July and somewhat more in September.

**Species totals**
- **June:** 50 species in the whole river; 36 appeared in Lake Decatur and 12 appeared downstream in small numbers.
- **July:** 58 species; 47 first became abundant in Lake Decatur. Only 8 species occurred downstream and not in the lake, and these were usually rare.
- **September:** 66 species; 25 occurred in Lake Decatur and 33 occurred only downstream. With low water, the river below the dam was fed mainly by [[Sewage Treatment Plant]] effluent, and the downstream increase suggests local development in slack pools.

**Taxa with notable patterns**
- Lake-derived species that declined downstream in June and July included *Difflugia lobostoma*, *Codonella cratera*, *Brachionus angularis*, *Keratella cochlearis*, *Moina affinis*, *Daphnia longispina* and *Bosmina longirostris*.
- *Actinastrum hantzschi* was the only alga that increased markedly below the lake in June.
- Flagellates *Trachelomonas volvocina* and *Euglena viridis* declined to Petersburg and then increased.
- *Sphaerotilus natans* was very abundant in the sewage plant but never appeared in river collections.

**Sewage effluent.** The author doubted that many lake species survive the treatment plant, which uses sand filters, tanks and Dorr separators. He also thought significant new plankton development occurs only after treatment is complete.

**Recovery from pollution.** No pollution-indicator organisms were found at Harristown, eight miles below Decatur. Jewell (1920) had reported *Sphaerotilus natans*, nematodes and ciliates there. In 1929 the plankton was typical of clean water, dominated by *Codonella cratera*, *Polyarthra trigla*, *Brachionus*, *Cyclops bicuspidatus*, cladocerans and *Melosira granulata*. Sludge had nearly disappeared, the water was clear, and fish were observed. The author attributed the much larger lower-river plankton population to the creation of the Decatur lake and the removal of the pollution barrier.

**Conclusions**
- Plankton in the lower river is partly carried downstream from Lake Decatur, and partly developed locally when current is slow.
- The lake and the Springfield pool function as plankton-producing reservoirs.
- Plankton abundance in the lower river has increased since the 1920s, largely because of the lake and the end of pollution.
