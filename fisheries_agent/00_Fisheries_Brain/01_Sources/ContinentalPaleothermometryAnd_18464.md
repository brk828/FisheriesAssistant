# Continental Paleothermometry and Seasonality from Fish Otolith Aragonite

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether the oxygen isotope composition (δ¹⁸O) of [[Aragonite]] in [[Fish Otoliths]] can serve as a [[Paleoclimate]] thermometer in freshwater lakes. Paired otolith and water samples from six large, deep, temperate lakes were analysed. The authors derived an empirical [[Fractionation Factor]] calibration across 3.2–30.3 °C, then applied it to a shallow-water eurythermic fish from [[Sandusky Bay]], [[Lake Erie]]. Calculated temperatures agreed with local meteorological records. The approach combines [[Stenothermic Fishes]], which fix the water isotope composition, with [[Eurythermic Fishes]], which record seasonal temperature change.

## Background & Description

**Why seasonality matters.** Most paleoclimate proxies give time-averaged temperatures. The authors argue that intra-annual variation (seasonality) strongly influences the distribution, evolution and extinction of organisms and the surficial processes of temperate regions. Accretionary carbonate structures with rapid growth, such as bivalve shells, corals and fish otoliths, can preserve seasonal oxygen isotope variation in growth bands. Earlier fish-otolith studies mostly resolved only average temperature over a fish's life.

**Otolith structure.** [[Teleost Otoliths]] are aragonitic accretionary structures formed in the [[Inner Ear]] (sacculus). The sagittae are usually the largest pair. Accretion can exceed 1 mm per year in some species and produces visible daily rhythms, which extend into monthly and annual bands. Otolith shape also allows fossil taxa to be identified, linking isotopic records to palaeoecology.

**Growth banding.** Growth stops or slows in response to stress. Discontinuities are of two kinds:
1. Fine-scale daily cycles driven by temperature and nutrition.
2. Less frequent, stronger breaks caused by starvation, spawning or low winter temperature.

Carbonate deposition dominates during the day, and protein-rich layers form at night. Wright (1991) found that accretion tracked metabolic rate more closely than body growth. Umezawa and Tsukamoto (1991) found that otolith growth ceased when food was withheld. Resorption is rare and is compensated by deposition elsewhere on the otolith ([[Mugiya and Uchimura]]).

**Three assumptions of the method.**
1. Growth bands form in isotopic equilibrium with ambient water at the precipitation temperature. Enzyme-catalysed exchange with ear fluids is rapid, on a scale of hours.
2. Diagenetic alteration occurs only when aragonite converts to calcite. If the otolith remains aragonite, its isotopic record is assumed unaltered.
3. [[Vital Effects]] may differ among species or genera and must be evaluated and, if needed, calibrated.

**Paired stenothermic–eurythermic design.** Applying a thermometer to ancient material needs independent knowledge of the water's isotope composition, which is rarely available for lakes. Stenothermic bottom-dwelling fish living at near-constant temperature can reveal the water composition. Once that is known, the δ¹⁸O variation in eurythermic species' growth bands records seasonal surface temperature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study lakes.** Six large, deep, dimictic lakes with isotopically homogeneous water were studied. Bottom-water temperatures were:
- [[Lake Baikal]] (Siberia): about 3.0–3.5 °C below 300 m. The water δ¹⁸O is −15.8 ‰ ± 0.2.
- [[Laurentian Great Lakes]] (Superior, Michigan, Huron, Ontario): roughly 4–5 °C at depth.

The Great Lakes lie between 41° and 47° N and have changed little in level or temperature over about 50 years. Lake Superior levels fluctuated by only 0.6 m, and Lakes Michigan and Huron by 1.5 m, since 1950.

**Species groups.**
- *Cold stenothermic benthic fish* (obligate bottom dwellers that cannot leave the bottom for long): eight Baikal cottids (for example, [[Comephorus Baicalensis]] and [[Limnocottus]] species) and the Great Lakes [[Deepwater Sculpin]] (*Myoxocephalus thompsoni*). Deepwater sculpins range 45–183 m, most abundant at 73–91 m in Lake Michigan.
- *Pelagic and warmer-water species*: [[Cottocomephorus Inermis]], [[Batrachocottus Baicalensis]] and [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), which span wider temperature ranges.
- *Warm stenothermic fish*: [[Lake Chub]] (*Couesius plumbeus*), captured in the Straits of Mackinac in August 1941 at a mean of 21.5 °C, and [[Dace]] (*Leuciscus leuciscus*).
- *Eurythermic fish*: [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) in [[Lake Erie]], which lives in shallow water and grows over a broad temperature range.

**Lake Erie.** The western basin is poorly stratified. Bottom temperatures in 1958 ranged from about 15 °C (May and October) to about 22 °C (July and August), and surface waters were 0–3 °C warmer. Summer surface temperatures in 1959–60 reached 25–27 °C.

**Controlled experiments.** Aquaria were sealed to prevent evaporation. Water isotope composition was measured at the start and end of each ten-day experiment. [[Guppy]] (*Poecilia reticulata*) were held at 30.3 ± 0.29 °C. Hatchery fish (lake trout from Marquette and USFWS) were reared in flow-through groundwater systems, and their source water was sampled.

## Key Findings

**1. Calibrated fractionation equation.** The regression across the 3.2–30.3 °C range is:

10³ ln α = 18.56 (±0.319) × (10³ / T K) − 33.49 (±0.307)

The fit has R = 0.99. Standard errors are given at the 95 % probability level.

**2. Comparison with prior work.** The slope is statistically indistinguishable from [[Grossman and Ku 1986]] (18.07 T⁻¹ − 31.0). The intercept is offset by about +2.5. The authors attribute earlier species-specific differences to experimental error from evaporation or poor temperature control. Figure 2 shows that marine and freshwater data plot on a common relationship, so metabolic or species effects appear insignificant.

**3. Water composition from stenothermic fish.** Deepwater sculpins and Baikal cottids living at about 3.2–4 °C can be used to infer the δ¹⁸O of lake water. The method is set up for this step but the paper does not report a derived water value beyond the Baikal reference of −15.8 ‰.

**4. Seasonal temperature reconstruction.** A 9-year-old freshwater drum (325 mm standard length, collected in 1957 from western Lake Erie) was sampled. Key analytical details:
- Sample paths were 25 µm wide. Each path yielded about 10–40 µm spatial resolution and 10–30 µg of carbonate.
- Mid-summer daily bands are about 4 µm wide, so the record resolves roughly weekly intervals.
- Aragonite accreted over about 140 days early in life.
- Erosional features from winter stress or reproduction create gaps in the record.

The δ¹⁸O record (Figure 5) shows a clear seasonal cycle. Calculated temperatures (Figure 6) match Sandusky Bay weather station air temperatures for 1950–51. The best record is of summer warmth, because fast summer growth gives high resolution while slow winter growth yields little material.

**5. Analytical precision.** Measurements were made by acid reaction at 73 °C on a Finnigan MAT 251 mass spectrometer. Precision is better than ±0.1 ‰ for both carbon and oxygen, checked against NBS-18 and NBS-19. Water δ¹⁸O was measured by CO₂–water equilibration on a Finnigan Delta-S, with reproducibility better than ±0.1 ‰.

**6. Conclusions.** The authors conclude that:
- Fish otoliths are excellent continental paleoclimate proxies.
- Deep temperate lake bottoms stay between about 3.5 and 4.5 °C year-round, so deep-water benthic fish can reveal the δ¹⁸O of ancient lake water.
- Paired stenothermic and eurythermic populations can separate deep-water from surface-water signals and recover seasonal change.
- Future work on large lake systems across time and latitude should clarify continental thermal gradients and meteoric water compositions.
