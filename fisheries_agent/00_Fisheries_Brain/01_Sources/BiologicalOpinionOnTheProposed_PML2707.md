# Biological Opinion on Spillway Repair at the Phelps Dodge Eagle Creek Diversion Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) biological opinion, dated July 2, 1996, responds to a formal consultation request from the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] (received June 24, 1996) under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The proposed action is repair of erosion damage to the spillway of the Phelps Dodge diversion dam on lower [[Eagle Creek]], Greenlee County, Arizona. The listed species of concern are the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and the threatened [[Spikedace]]. The [[Loach Minnow]] was also assessed. The Service concluded that the project is not likely to jeopardize either fish species, and it authorized a limited incidental take statement.

The document also includes a short cover letter noting a backlog of biological opinions, and it lists opinions for several non-fish species (e.g., southwestern willow flycatcher, bald eagle, Mexican spotted owl, peregrine falcon), which the Service judged to be unaffected or not likely to be adversely affected.

## Background & Description

**Species profiles**

- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Listed as endangered in 1991. Grows to over two feet, with a distinctive sharp-edged dorsal ridge. Historically common across the Colorado River basin, it now persists in only about 750 miles of the upper basin. Adults use mainstem and backwater habitats, while larvae and juveniles are thought to use shallow, slow areas. Spawning runs from January to May, over gravel, cobble, or coarse sand. The species can live up to about 50 years. Eagle Creek is not in designated critical habitat, which covers parts of the Gila, Verde, and Salt rivers.
- **Spikedace** (*Meda fulgida*): Listed as threatened on July 1, 1986. Critical habitat was designated March 8, 1994, including Aravaipa Creek and parts of the Gila and Verde rivers, but not Eagle Creek. It is a small silvery fish that lives about two years, with reproduction mainly in one-year-old fish. It spawns March through May and feeds mainly on aquatic and terrestrial insects. It prefers flowing water with slow to moderate velocities over sand, gravel, and cobble.
- **Loach Minnow** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Threatened. The opinion treats it as "may affect, not likely to adversely affect" at the project site.

**Project description**

The Phelps Dodge diversion dam is a concrete structure about 16 feet high and 135 feet wide, built in 1897 and rebuilt several times, most recently after 1993 flooding. The existing apron is about 60 feet long. A scour hole roughly 140 feet long, 180 feet wide, and 10 feet deep eroded downstream during the winter of 1994–1995. The repair would pump water from the scour pool and partially fill it with about 1,200 yards of on-site-batched concrete to form a 260-foot-wide abutment extending about 40 feet downstream at a 3:1 slope. The dam supplies water for Phelps Dodge copper mines and the municipal supply at Morenci and Clifton.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Eagle Creek is intermittent in parts but perennial in the project area, flowing through a deeply incised narrow valley. The median yearly mean flow is 38 cfs. The recorded historic maximum is 36,800 cfs and the historic minimum is 2.9 cfs.
- **Riparian zone:** Sparse, with no significant riparian vegetation near the dam. Upland vegetation is dominated by mesquite, juniper, and shrub live oak.
- **Anthropogenic pressures:** Livestock grazing has been the main land use for about 150 years. Water diversions include a 1945 transfer from the Black River into Willow Creek, which adds about 27 percent to flow in lower Eagle Creek. About 9 percent more is removed at the diversion dam and pumping station. Mining, roads, off-road vehicles, and floodplain development have also contributed to an unstable, braided channel.
- **Non-native fishes:** At least a dozen non-native species have been reported, including black bullhead, common carp, red shiner, smallmouth bass, largemouth bass, rainbow trout, and crayfish. Native fishes still dominate above the dam, but non-natives predominate below it. The long-term trend favors non-natives.
- **Population status:** Razorback sucker was stocked in Eagle Creek between 1981 and 1988, using [[Lake Mohave]] hatchery stock. Only a few individuals have been recorded. Spikedace was common in 1987 but has not been found in several surveys since. The opinion attributes this to possible habitat deterioration or sampling limitations.
- **Genetics:** Eagle Creek spikedace is morphologically intermediate between Verde River/Aravaipa Creek and upper Gila River populations. It is isolated from the nearest population by about 100 river miles.
- **Loach Minnow record:** A 1950 Eagle Creek record, previously questioned, was confirmed by museum specimens at the University of Michigan.

## Key Findings

- **Jeopardy determination:** The repair is "not likely to jeopardize the continued existence" of spikedace or razorback sucker. No critical habitat occurs in the action area.
- **Loach minnow:** The project "may affect, but is not likely to adversely affect" this species, because it is very unlikely to occur in the scour pool.
- **Other species:** No effect was found for the southwestern willow flycatcher, bald eagle, and Mexican spotted owl. The cactus ferruginous pygmy-owl was judged not to be present because habitat is unsuitable. Peregrine falcon: no effect, provided work is not done between March 1 and July 15 because of nesting season.
- **Expected direct effects:** Pumping the pool and filling part of it could kill fish through desiccation, handling, or entrapment in concrete. Any fish present would be salvaged by seining or electrofishing and relocated.
- **Incidental take limits:** Up to 10 spikedace and 2 razorback sucker mortalities, and up to 100 spikedace and 20 razorback sucker captured and relocated. If these levels are exceeded, consultation must be reinitiated immediately.
- **Reasonable and prudent measures:** Minimize direct mortality. Monitor the fish community and habitat. Keep complete records.
- **Terms and conditions:**
  - Minimize heavy equipment use in the stream channel.
  - Keep pollutants out of surface water.
  - Hold a fish biologist on site during pool emptying.
  - Relocate razorback sucker above the dam into the deepest pools, and spikedace below the dam into runs or riffles. Spikedace may not be moved above the dam because of red shiner confusion.
  - Use a holding tank of at least 100 gallons with continuous oxygen.
  - Report any more than 25 dead or dying fish to the Service by telephone.
  - Submit a project report within 60 days of completion.
- **Cumulative effects:** Water manipulation, grazing, and private land use are adverse cumulative impacts. The opinion concludes that the existing baseline is already degraded and that any further habitat loss is especially consequential.
- **Consultation history:** Earlier consultations covered the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest Plan (1986), a Soil Conservation Service channel stabilization project (1994), and a grazing allotment plan (1995). None were found to jeopardize the species.
- **Dam status:** The dam's own effects on listed species have not been assessed under Section 7. The Service recommends a broader consultation on past and future effects of the dam and its periodic repairs.
