# Response of Endangered *Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis* to Introduced *Gambusia affinis* in the Rio Yaqui Drainage, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[David L. Galat]] and [[Ben Robertson]] (Arizona State University, published in *Environmental Biology of Fishes* 33: 249–264, 1992) examines how the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) responds to the introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*[[Gambusia affinis]]*) at [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], Arizona. Fieldwork ran from May 1985 to October 1987 across springs, a spring-fed stream and a flash-flood-prone stream. The authors found that the native topminnow persists with mosquitofish mainly through compensatory reproduction, increased body size where mosquitofish are present, and recolonization from thermal springheads after flooding. The authors conclude that the topminnow shows little difference from the Gila topminnow (*P. o. occidentalis*) in its response to mosquitofish, and that its conservation depends mainly on controlling mosquitofish.

## Background & Description

*Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (Gila topminnow, Poeciliidae) was once widespread in streams, springs and marshes of the Sonoran Desert in the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. It has declined sharply because of [[Habitat Alteration]], competition with and predation by introduced fishes, and [[Flash Flooding]] effects. The species was federally listed as endangered in 1967 (U.S. Department of the Interior 1980). Two subspecies are recognized: *P. o. occidentalis* in the United States, and *P. o. sonoriensis*, which extends into the Rio Yaqui basin in Sonora, Mexico. *P. o. sonoriensis* is restricted within Arizona to the Rio Yaqui basin headwaters.

*Gambusia affinis* was first collected in Arizona in 1926 and reached the Rio Yaqui drainage by 1979 (Hendrickson et al. 1980). Its introduction is a classic example of the displacement of native fishes by an exotic species.

Subspecies identification relied on keys from Minckley (1973), and the authors classified fish by gonopodium development and breeding coloration. Reproductive effort (RE) was calculated as the percentage of total body weight invested in reproduction, and fecundity was measured as the number of stage 2–4 ova and embryos carried (instantaneous fecundity).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites (San Bernardino NWR):**
- **West Border Spring (WBS)**: a limnocrene where *P. o. sonoriensis* and *G. affinis* are syntopic.
- **Bunting and Cottonwood springs**: limnocrenes with *P. o. sonoriensis* only (no *Gambusia*).
- **Black Draw-Border**: a flash-flood-prone stream where the two species coexist; *Gambusia* dominates.
- **Black Draw-Source**: a thermal springhead where *Poeciliopsis* was reintroduced in 1985 (about 200 females and 50 males from North Pond) and where *Gambusia* was later able to gain access.
- **Leslie Creek**: a stream with *Poeciliopsis* but no *Gambusia*.

Water temperature was 27–29 °C at the limnocrenes, 19–21 °C at Black Draw-Source and 12–16 °C at Black Draw-Border. Total alkalinity was 7.2 meq l⁻¹ at Black Draw-Source, versus 4.6 meq l⁻¹ at Black Draw-Border and 4.9 meq l⁻¹ at WBS (Fisher & Grimm 1983). Other native species, including [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Gila Chub]], co-occur at these sites. Native fishes such as [[Agosia]] and [[Gila Topminnow]] are also present.

## Key Findings

1. **Size and reproduction where mosquitofish are present:** At WBS, female *P. o. sonoriensis* were significantly heavier than female *G. affinis* of the same standard length. Caudal fin damage was 76% (mean severity 0.76), versus 0.06 and 0.00 at Cottonwood and Bunting springs. Fecundity and reproductive effort were higher at WBS than at the springs without mosquitofish.
2. **Compensatory reproduction:** The authors interpret these results as evidence of compensatory reproduction in response to [[Size-Selective Predation]] by *Gambusia*. Smaller *Poeciliopsis* appear to be removed selectively by predation, and surviving fish produce larger broods.
3. **Recolonization dynamics at Black Draw-Source:** Mean body wet weight of reproductively active females fell 43% between March 1986 and March 1987, and mean ovary wet weight fell 72%. Reproductive effort fell by nearly 40%. Standard length decreased over time, which the authors attribute to population growth reducing growth rates.
4. **Decline at Black Draw-Border:** *Poeciliopsis* made up 1.4–12.4% of poeciliids at WBS (mean 4.8 ± 4.4%), with no significant trend. At Black Draw-Border, the proportion declined about 7% per month, with none recovered in July 1987 (R² = 0.68, p = 0.012).
5. **Flood-driven recovery:** After flash flooding in August 1987 (about 2.4 cm of precipitation in the first two weeks of the month; flooding up to about 1.5 m deep), the proportion of *Poeciliopsis* at Black Draw-Border increased dramatically. The authors argue this supports the [[Differential Selection by Flooding]] hypothesis (Minckley & Meffe 1987).
6. **Photoperiod and temperature:** Reproduction in October, November and January was infrequent even in constant-temperature habitats. Photoperiod therefore appears to control reproductive activity more than water temperature. Temperature still directly affects reproductive onset: over 90% of females at thermal Black Draw-Source carried broods in March, versus only one of 48 mosquitofish and no *Poeciliopsis* at non-thermal Black Draw-Border.
7. **Conservation implications:** The authors state that the native habitat lies largely within the National Wildlife Refuge System, so conservation depends mainly on controlling *Gambusia*. They recommend three strategies: physically preventing reinvasion from Mexico, eradicating existing mosquitofish populations in those habitats, and public education about the value of native poeciliids as mosquito control agents.
8. **Conclusions:** The authors found no biotic mechanism by which *P. o. sonoriensis* resists severe population reduction by *Gambusia*. Persistence depends instead on recurrent flooding and uniform-temperature springhead refugia, which are also needed to maintain healthy populations.
