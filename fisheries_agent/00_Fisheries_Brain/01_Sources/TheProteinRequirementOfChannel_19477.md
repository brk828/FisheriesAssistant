# Protein Requirement of Channel Catfish (Ictalurus punctatus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by [[Lam Nam Muse]] tested the dietary protein level needed for optimum growth of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). Eight purified diets, combining four protein levels (6.3, 15.8, 25.3 and 34.8 percent) with two [[Carbohydrate Levels]] (9.3 and 18.6 percent), were fed to fish in 40 [[Stainless Steel Troughs]] at [[Auburn University]]'s [[Farm Ponds Laboratory]]. The authors concluded that 25.3 percent protein gave optimum growth and protein deposition, that maintenance protein requirements were 0.079 g and 0.029 g per 100 g of fish per day at the two carbohydrate levels, and that carbohydrate exerted a [[Protein-Sparing Effect]] equivalent to 0.05 g of protein. The paper is an experimental research article, not a proposal, budget, or correspondence.

## Background & Description

- **Subject species:** [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus* Rafinesque), a freshwater warm-water fish.
- **Research question:** The level of dietary protein needed by channel catfish for optimum growth.
- **Context:** The introduction notes that protein requirements of higher animals had been studied for over 100 years, whereas nutritional studies on fish had been conducted for a relatively short period and were limited, especially for warm-water fish.
- **Source material:** Fish were 1960-spawn channel catfish from the Farm Ponds Project of the Agricultural Experiment Station of Auburn University. Roughly 1,200 fish were brought into the laboratory on May 15, 1961, and 1,000 were randomly selected for the trials at 25 fish per trough. The paper was drawn from a thesis presented to the Graduate Faculty of Auburn University. The author was then District Fishery Biologist with the [[Alabama Department of Conservation]].
- **Experimental setup:** Forty stainless steel troughs (7' x 1' x 0.8') were set in groups of four, each with individual aeration and water supply and a drain. Water was filtered, chlorinated municipal supply passed through an [[Activated-Charcoal Filter]]. Water temperature ranged from 25°C to 28°C. Lighting was provided by 100-watt and 300-watt bulbs.
- **Diet preparation:** Diets were formulated with [[Casein]] (68.0 percent protein), [[Dextrin]], [[Corn Oil]], [[Mineral Mix]], [[Alphacel]] (filler), a vitamin fortification mixture (20 g per 1,000 g of diet), and agar (5 percent solution). Diets were pelleted by meat grinder and stored at 10°C. An acclimation diet was fed before the trial.
- **Protocol:** Fish were fed 2.5 percent of body weight per day and re-weighed every 14 to 21 days. The trial ran from June 22 to September 3, 1961.
- **Analytical methods:** Carcass protein was determined by the [[Kjeldahl Method]] and fat by [[Continuous Ether Extraction]]. Growth comparisons used [[Analysis Of Variance]] with orthogonal comparisons (Steel and Torrie, 1960).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** All work was conducted in controlled laboratory troughs, not in natural waters. The fish were reared under conditions intended to mimic farm pond culture.
- **Water conditions:** Chlorine was removed by activated charcoal. Water flow was regulated for equal flow through all troughs. Temperature was 25°C to 28°C.
- **Husbandry and sanitation:** Individual cleaning brushes and scouring pads were assigned to each trough group to limit the spread of parasites and disease organisms. Troughs were cleaned every 2 to 3 days to prevent fecal accumulation and minimize algal growth.
- **Feeding behaviour:** Fish took 5 to 6 days to begin eating the diet. Within 10 days all fish accepted it readily and consumed their daily ration within 1 to 2 minutes.
- **Comparative context:** The author compared protein needs with those of chicks, pigs, guinea pigs, foxes, [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Chinook Salmon]]. The paper also reviewed carbohydrate utilization in trout and salmon.
- **Trial termination:** The experiment ended early because of a water supply failure that killed large numbers of the larger fish. No mortality occurred in any treatment during the trial.

## Key Findings

**Growth and optimum protein level**
- All troughs gained weight, except that some troughs on Diet No. 1 (6.3 percent protein, 9.3 percent carbohydrate) lost or made no gain at the first weighing.
- At alpha = 0.05, there were significant differences between protein levels and between carbohydrate levels. There was no significant interaction.
- Orthogonal comparisons showed that 25.3 percent protein was significantly better than 6.3 and 15.8 percent, but not significantly different from 34.8 percent.
- **Conclusion:** 25.3 percent dietary protein is the optimum level tested for channel catfish growth.
- The author notes this is higher than the requirement of most warm-blooded animals (for example, 18 percent for chicks and growing pigs), but the paper states that the Phillips et al. (1957) work suggests trout requirements could be reduced by attention to dietary calorie balance.

**Protein deposition and carcass composition**
- Carcass protein ranged from 12 to 16 percent, with most fish around 14 percent.
- Grams of protein deposited indicated that the 25.3 percent diets gave optimum protein deposition.
- Protein conversion rates did not differ significantly among protein levels (alpha = 0.05), but did differ among carbohydrate levels. The author attributes the lack of significance to the small number of fish per diet and large variation among fish treated alike.

**Maintenance requirements**
- Because all troughs gained weight and there were no mortalities, the author assumed all fish received protein in excess of maintenance needs.
- Estimated maintenance protein requirements were **0.079 g per 100 g of fish per day** on the 9.3 percent carbohydrate diets and **0.029 g per 100 g of fish per day** on the 18.6 percent carbohydrate diets. The author describes these as speculative, based on extrapolation of [[Figure 1]].
- The average percentage gain for the 6.3 percent protein troughs was 21.29 percent at 9.3 percent carbohydrate and 37.13 percent at 18.6 percent carbohydrate.

**Carbohydrate utilization**
- Fish on the 18.6 percent carbohydrate diets gave the best growth. Livers of 32 fish from the experimental diets were examined and all appeared normal.
- The author suggests channel catfish can use 18.6 percent carbohydrate well and that higher levels may be usable, but this requires further investigation.

**Protein-sparing effect of carbohydrate**
- A difference of **0.23 g of carbohydrate per 100 g of fish per day** existed between the 9.3 and 18.6 percent carbohydrate diets.
- The Summary section states that 0.25 g of carbohydrate spared 0.05 g of protein, whereas the Results section gives 0.23 g. This is a discrepancy within the source.
- Growth on 0.05 g less protein was equivalent to growth on the higher-carbohydrate diet.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Small numbers of fish per diet and large variation among fish treated alike.
- Early termination of the trial.
- Carbohydrate-sparing mechanism not fully known. The author cites [[Munro 1951]] on non-essential amino acid formation and [[Geiger et al. 1950]] on hepatic portal carbohydrate function as possible explanations.
