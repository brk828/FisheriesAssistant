# Spring Pickerel census, 1953

## Executive Summary
This report presents the findings of a 1953 spring creel census conducted by the New Jersey Fisheries Laboratory across eight lakes in Morris and Sussex Counties: [[Cranberry Lake]], [[Bear Pond]], [[Lackawanna Lake]], [[Big Swartswood Lake]], [[Lake Hopatcong]], [[Musconetcong Lake]], [[Budd Lake]], and [[Culvers Lake]] (newly added in 1953). The study evaluated angler success and assessed the effectiveness of a **15-inch minimum size limit** on [[Chain Pickerel]]. A total of 2,414 fish were reported by 1,013 anglers, with [[Chain Pickerel]] and panfish (bluegill/pumpkinseed) forming the bulk of the harvest. Overall angler effort and catch rates declined slightly compared to 1952, largely attributed to poor weather, though card return rates improved.

## Background & Description
The 1953 census followed the same postcard-based voluntary reporting methodology used in 1952, distributed through cooperating boat liveries on each lake. Anglers recorded catch data which was either mailed in or collected directly by fisheries laboratory staff. The study period ran from **May 20 to June 14, 1953**, capturing the early open-water fishing season.

Two lake categories were distinguished based on regulatory history:
- **12-inch minimum size lakes**: e.g., [[Bear Pond]], [[Budd Lake]], [[Musconetcong Lake]]
- **15-inch minimum size lakes**: e.g., [[Culvers Lake]], [[Lake Hopatcong]], [[Cranberry Lake]], [[Big Swartswood Lake]]

This distinction allowed comparative analysis of the **15-inch minimum size limit's** effect on legal retention rates of [[Chain Pickerel]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Weather effects**: More than half the weekends during the census period had rain and strong winds, suppressing angler turnout and total catch relative to 1952.
- **Weed growth**: Extensive aquatic vegetation in [[Lackawanna Lake]] and [[Musconetcong Lake]] reduced fishing access and pressure after early June, and likely functions as important habitat structure affecting pickerel and panfish distribution.
- **Habitat use by pickerel**: In [[Lake Hopatcong]], larger [[Chain Pickerel]] were found concentrated in deep water; anglers fishing shallow water caught fewer/smaller fish, suggesting depth-based habitat partitioning by size class.
- **Species interactions/first stocking**: [[Lake Hopatcong]] received its first-ever trout stocking in 1953 (previously stocked with salmon), and trout comprised 9% of the total catch there — indicating successful establishment in a lake historically dominated by [[Chain Pickerel]] and [[Yellow Perch]].
- **Population dynamics**: A dominant year-class of 4-year-old fish (noted from Project F-3-R-1, per Jules Marron Jr.) in [[Bear Pond]] may have reached legal/retainable size by spring 1953, explaining a jump in kept [[Yellow Perch]] (73% kept vs. 18% in 1952).
- **Recruitment signal**: Low numbers of sublegal "throwback" pickerel in [[Budd Lake]] during spring 1953 correctly predicted another poor ice-fishing season in 1954, indicating recruitment failure or a weak year class.

## Key Findings

### Overall Catch Composition (1953, all lakes combined)
- Total catch: **2,414 fish** by **1,013 anglers** (avg. 2.4 fish/angler, down slightly from 2.5 in 1952)
- **Sunfish (bluegill + pumpkinseed)**: 36.2% of total catch (slight decrease from 1952)
- **Chain Pickerel**: 595 kept, 24.6% of total harvest (down from 27% in 1952)
- **Yellow Perch**: 24.2% of catch (up substantially from 19% in 1952)
- Remaining catch: calico bass (black crappie), catfish, and trout (trout limited to [[Lake Hopatcong]] only, ~9.1% of that lake's catch)

### Lake-by-Lake Highlights

**1. [[Bear Pond]]**
- 88.9% of pickerel caught were retained (up from prior year), but overall pickerel harvest low (only 8 fish kept by 114 anglers)
- Yellow perch retention jumped from 18% kept (1952) to 73% kept (1953), suggesting increased average size due to a maturing year-class

**2. [[Budd Lake]]**
- Sharp decline in pickerel catch: average dropped from 0.490 to 0.012 fish/angler — largest measured decline in the study
- Only 28 pickerel caught in spring census; 24 retained
- Low throwback numbers correctly predicted continued poor ice-fishing season in 1954
- Panfishing (yellow perch, bluegill, calico bass) remained comparable to 1952

**3. [[Cranberry Lake]]**
- Highest total pickerel catch and highest number of "throwbacks" among all lakes
- Release rate: 90.3% released in 1953 vs. 92.7% in 1952 (slight decrease)
- Highest combined catch rate of all lakes: **3.6 fish/angler/day**

**4. [[Culvers Lake]]** (new to census in 1953)
- 133 anglers caught 123 pickerel; 72 retained (58.5%) — highest legal retention rate among 15-inch lakes
- Good yellow perch fishing including large individuals; sunfish/catfish catch minimal

**5. [[Lake Hopatcong]]**
- Marked drop in angler numbers and pickerel harvest vs. 1952
- Pickerel + yellow perch: only 56.2% of catch in 1953 (down from 76.5% in 1952)
- First-ever trout stocking (previously stocked with salmon) — trout comprised 9% of catch
- Increased catches of catfish, calico bass, and trout offset the pickerel/perch decline
- Sunfish only 5.8% of total catch (notably low compared to other lakes)

**6. [[Lackawanna Lake]]**
- Highest pickerel yield recorded in any open-water census to date: avg. **2.2 pickerel/angler** (2.7/angler in first week)
- Significant increase from 1.660 (1952) to 2.208 (1953) pickerel/angler
- Pickerel comprised ~71% of total catch; sunfish made up most of the remainder
- Weed growth curtailed fishing after first week of June

**7. [[Musconetcong Lake]]**
- Pickerel comprised 81% of total catch, averaging 1.2 pickerel/angler
- Significant decline from 1.690 (1952) to 1.187 (1953) pickerel/angler
- Only 10.6% of 658 pickerel caught were retained (mostly sublegal)
- Low panfish catch attributed to angler focus on pickerel rather than species scarcity

**8. [[Big Swartswood Lake]]** (referred to as "Swartswood Lake")
- Substantial increase in sublegal ("throwback") pickerel in 1953, raising angler concern
- Subsequent unpublished 1954 ice census data showed decline in throwbacks

### Size Limit Comparison (12" vs. 15" Lakes)
- Among **12-inch lakes**, [[Lackawanna Lake]] had the best pickerel fishing; [[Musconetcong Lake]] had the highest percent of "keepers"
- Among **15-inch lakes**, [[Culvers Lake]] had the best pickerel fishing, with [[Lake Hopatcong]] a close second

### Gear Trends
- Average rods used per angler ranged from **1.2–1.4**, well below the U.S. average of 2.3 rods/angler
- Attributed to increasing popularity of spinning tackle, which is typically fished with a single rod

## Conclusions (Author-Stated)
1. Card return rates improved in 1953 vs. 1952.
2. Fewer anglers fished during the early season in 1953, attributed to poor weather.
3. [[Chain Pickerel]] remained second to sunfish in total harvest across all lakes, though its share declined slightly.
4. Sunfish and calico bass catch percentages dropped slightly; yellow perch and catfish catches increased notably.
5. Sublegal pickerel numbers decreased slightly in [[Cranberry Lake]] but increased in [[Big Swartswood Lake]].
6. Calico bass (black crappie) is an important early-season panfish in some lakes.
7. Trout in [[Lake Hopatcong]] contributed significantly to total catch following first-time stocking.
8. Overall fish-per-angler rates were statistically unchanged from 1952 to 1953 across all lakes.
9. Statistically significant *decreases* in average pickerel/angler occurred at [[Budd Lake]] and [[Musconetcong Lake]]; a significant *increase* occurred at [[Lackawanna Lake]].
10. Lines/rods used per angler remain below national averages, attributed to widespread adoption of spinning tackle.
