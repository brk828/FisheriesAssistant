# Evolution of Reproductive Behaviors in Percid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This review reconstructs the evolution of spawning behavior in percid fishes, a group that arose as freshwater derivatives of a marine perciform and diversified strongly in North America. The paper recognizes six egg-deposition behaviors (broadcasting, stranding, burying, attaching, clumping and clustering) and argues that they form an evolutionary sequence in which each step conferred a survival advantage over its precursor. Reproductive behavior is treated both as a management-relevant trait and as a phylogenetic character useful for resolving relationships among species, particularly within the darter genus [[Etheostoma]].

## Background & Description

- **Origin and diversity:** Percids are freshwater derivatives of a marine perciform, possibly an anadromous serranid ([[McCully 1962]]; [[Collette and Banarescu 1977]]). Most diversification is thought to have occurred during the Pliocene or later.
- **North American radiation:** About 151 species in five genera occur in North America. Only minnows ([[Cyprinidae]], about 225 species) are more diverse among North American freshwater fishes. Together, percids and minnows make up almost half of the temperate North American freshwater fish fauna.
- **Eurasian distribution:** Percids occur elsewhere only in temperate Eurasia, with 14 species in six genera. Two genera, [[Stizostedion]] and [[Perca]], occur on both continents.
- **Taxonomic table (Table 1):** Subfamilies Etheostomatinae (tribes Luciopercini, Etheostomatini, Romanichthyini) and Percinae. Notable counts include [[Etheostoma]] (ca. 103 species, Nearctic), [[Percina]] (ca. 38, Nearctic), [[Ammocrypta]] (7, Nearctic), [[Zingel]] (3, Palearctic), [[Gymnocephalus]] (4, Palearctic) and [[Perca]] (3, Holarctic).
- **Rationale for study:** Reproductive habits are interesting in themselves, underpin population persistence (relevant to protection and management), and carry phylogenetic information. Percids are valuable because transitions among reproductive types remain visible in living species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning substrate and flow:** Broadcasters need coarse substrate (rocks, plants) and water movement to ventilate eggs. Buriers are restricted to flowing water with coarse substrates, so stream modifications such as [[Impoundments]] and [[Channelization]] prevent successful spawning.
- **Oxygen and siltation:** Male [[Sauger]] and [[Walleye]] pikeperch fan eggs, likely to prevent silt cover and anoxia. Stranded perch eggs in midwater avoid siltation and gain ventilation.
- **Predation:** Egg strategies are driven by predation pressure. Burying, nest sites under stones and guarded clusters all reduce exposure of eggs to predators such as birds, fishes, crayfishes and caddisflies.
- **Habitat specificity:** Species in slow-flowing or standing water (for example [[Boleichthys]] darters) benefit from attaching eggs, because burying would expose eggs to oxygen-poor substrates. Bedrock-pool species face similar constraints.
- **Conservation concern:** [[Etheostoma sellare]] (Maryland darter) is described as on the verge of extinction, with a single extant population whose spawning sites may be limiting.

## Key Findings

**Six reproductive behavior types (76 species with known spawning behavior):**

1. **Broadcasting** (primitive). Eggs and sperm are released in large numbers during group frenzies over coarse substrate, with no further parental provision. Group A spawns over open substrate ([[Stizostedion vitreum]], [[Stizostedion canadense]], [[Stizostedion marinum]], [[Percarina demidoffi]]). Group B spawns over a male-built pit ([[Stizostedion lucioperca]]), guarded and fanned by the male, and may be monogamous.
2. **Stranding.** Females extrude eggs in long gelatinous, hollow strands, which may reach 2.4 m long and 10 cm wide. Documented in [[Perca flavescens]], [[Perca fluviatilis]] and possibly [[Gymnocephalus cernua]]. Stranding is probably derived from broadcasting.
3. **Burying.** Eggs are released just below the substrate surface, with no parental care. Characteristic of all observed [[Percina]] species and many [[Etheostoma]] species. Burying is the primitive darter condition.
4. **Attaching.** A derived behavior found only in *Etheostoma*. One to three adhesive eggs are attached per spawning act to plants or rocks and abandoned. About 35 species are listed as attachers, with [[Etheostoma blennioides]] as a representative. Attaching derives directly from burying.
5. **Clumping.** Male-guarded eggs wedged between a stone and gravel, known only in subgenus *Nothonotus* (three species: *E. maculatum*, *E. aquali*, *E. microlepidum*). Derived from burying.
6. **Clustering.** Single-layer egg clusters on the underside of a stone (or log in *Boleosoma*), guarded by the male until hatching. Documented in 16 species across subgenera *Catonotus* and *Boleosoma*. Every egg is exposed and individually tended, which the author regards as a "perfected" form.

**Clustering grades:**
- Group A: female and male briefly invert for each release (e.g., *E. squamiceps*, *E. olivaceum*, *E. neopterum*).
- Group B: female remains inverted for prolonged periods while male inverts briefly (e.g., *E. flabellare*, *E. kennicotti*, *E. smithi*).
- Group C: both sexes remain inverted for extended periods (*Boleosoma* species, e.g., *E. olmstedi*).

**Phylogenetic conclusions:**
- Stranding, the most derived broadcast form, and the derived attaching/clustering lineages are placed in a single hypothesized sequence (Fig. 15): broadcasting → burying → attaching → clumping → clustering (alpha, beta and gamma grades), with pit-broadcasting as a specialized broadcast side branch.
- Darters are supported as derivatives of, and probably the sister group to, [[Stizostedion]]. This disagrees with Collette (1963), who based the conclusion on osteology, but agrees with Hubbs (1971), who drew on intergroup percid hybrids.
- Egg attaching evolved independently at least twice within *Ozarka* (e.g., *E. boschungi* and *E. trisella*), weakening a presumed close relationship between them.
- Each transition is considered to have arisen possibly more than once, and all alpha and beta precursors of *Boleosoma* are believed extinct.
- The author proposes that the reproductive characters of *E. sellare* could be significant for its conservation.

**Taxonomic recommendation:** *Etheostoma sanguifluum* should be elevated from subspecies to species status, because its male dorsal fin pigmentation is shared with *E. aquali* and not with *E. maculatum*. *E. maculatum vulneratum* should accordingly be named *E. sanguifluum vulneratum*.

**Summary of the paper's position:** Reproductive behavior is a reliable and informative phylogenetic and conservation trait in percids, and the sequence of breeding strategies shows survival advantages at each evolutionary step.
