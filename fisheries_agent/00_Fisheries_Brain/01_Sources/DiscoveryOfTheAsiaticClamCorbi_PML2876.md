# Discovery of the Asiatic Clam, Corbicula fluminea, in Lake Michigan

#malacology #freshwater-ecology #cyrenidae #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This short research note reports the first detection of the invasive [[Asiatic Clam]] (*[[Corbicula Fluminea]]*) in [[Lake Michigan]]. Ten live individuals were collected from benthic pump samples on 22 November 1983 offshore from the [[J. H. Campbell Power Plant]], a site adjacent to power-plant intake-discharge riprap. The authors infer a very recent introduction, since earlier benthic surveys at the same site (1977 to October 1981) found no clams. The note also places the discovery within the species' North American spread and warns that a breeding population could cause biofouling of water-intake systems across the [[Great Lakes]].

## Background & Description

*Corbicula fluminea* is a small freshwater bivalve native to Asia. Its North American range expanded through several dispersal events:

- **Columbia River, Washington:** first live specimens collected in the late 1930s (Burch 1944).
- **Western United States and Texas:** by the early 1970s the species had spread through California and east across Texas to southern Oklahoma.
- **[[Lower Ohio River]]:** a second dispersal epicenter formed in the late 1950s, giving rise to populations in the Mississippi, Tennessee, Illinois, and Wabash rivers.
- **[[Lake Erie]]:** the first Great Lakes record came from specimens collected in 1980 in western Lake Erie (Clarke 1981). The authors note that growth and reproduction there were later examined alongside the [[Maumee River]] population (Scott-Wasilk et al. 1983).

Although the species has strong natural and human-assisted dispersal powers and occurred in drainages less than 100 km from the Great Lakes, it did not cross that gap for more than 20 years. Researchers had assumed that Great Lakes temperatures were too cold in winter for survival or too cold in summer for successful reproduction.

The note states it is the second Great Lakes record and the first from Lake Michigan. Its authors are from the University of Michigan Great Lakes Research Division.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling site.** The collection site is 7.6 m deep, with coarse sand and gravel substrate, beside the riprap of the power plant's intake-discharge structures. Water temperature at collection was 7.2 °C. The plant's thermal plumes were considered a possible refuge for populations in colder lakes.

**Prior surveys.** The lake bottom around the J. H. Campbell plant was sampled extensively from 1977 through October 1981 and no *Corbicula* were found (Jude et al. 1978; Winnell and Jude 1982). These sampling programs were part of the same lake-bottom work that [[Lake Trout]] spawning-site surveys built upon.

**Why it matters for infrastructure.** A breeding population in Lake Michigan, or elsewhere in the Great Lakes, could create biofouling problems for water-intake systems. Earlier literature documented clam-fouled condensers and flow blockage (McMahon 1977; Britton et al. 1979; USNRC 1981). A Detroit Edison response to an NRC bulletin reported flow blockage of cooling water to safety systems by *Corbicula* at the Enrico Fermi plant (Detroit Edison 1981).

## Key Findings

- **Detection:** 10 live *Corbicula fluminea* were collected on 22 November 1983 in benthic pump samples offshore from the J. H. Campbell Power Plant, southeastern Lake Michigan. This is the first Lake Michigan record.
- **Density:** The sampled area was slightly more than 3 m², giving a density of 3.0 clams m⁻².
- **Size structure:** Two clams were under 2 mm long, and eight measured 3.4 to 5.1 mm. Individual shell measurements are tabulated in the original note but are partly illegible in the scanned text.
- **Age classes:** The note states that all clams fell within the age-class 0 category defined by Scott-Wasilk et al. (1983).
- **Introduction timing:** The absence of clams in the 1977 to October 1981 surveys, together with the small size range, indicates a very recent introduction. The authors suggest a summer 1983 recruitment event.
- **Great Lakes status:** The species is now recorded from Lake Erie (1980) and Lake Michigan (1983). The authors consider it uncertain whether deeper, colder Lakes Huron, Michigan, and Superior can sustain populations outside thermal plumes.
- **Research priorities:**
  - Determine whether the Lake Michigan population survives the winter.
  - Check for reproduction in 1984.
  - Assess whether any Great Lakes population is breeding.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Researchers sampling nearshore areas of the Great Lakes should be aware of *Corbicula*.
  - Lake Michigan water users, especially operators of water-intake systems, should be alerted to potential biofouling.
- **Other lakes:** The authors suggest that specimens may already exist in Lake Huron near power-plant thermal discharges, or that colonization there is more probable than previously assumed.
