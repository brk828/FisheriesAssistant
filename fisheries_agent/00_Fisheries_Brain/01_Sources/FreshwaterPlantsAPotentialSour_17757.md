# Fresh-water Plants: A Potential Source of Protein

#aquatic-botany #hydrophytes #economic-botany

## Executive Summary

This 1968 *Economic Botany* paper, by [[Boyd]] of [[Auburn University]], is a preliminary study assessing whether freshwater aquatic plants, usually regarded as nuisance [[Aquatic Weeds]], could serve as a food source. The work measured the chemical composition of dried plants as potential roughage, the extractability of [[Leaf Protein]] from several species, and the amino acid quality of that protein. The author concludes that several species are rich in [[Crude Protein]], that some yield substantial extractable protein, and that further feeding trials and exploratory research in protein-short nations are warranted. The paper is a feasibility assessment, not a field program.

## Background & Description

Aquatic weeds form large stands in natural and impounded waters and often interfere with water use, so control measures are routinely needed. Earlier work on lake mineral metabolism had produced data on aquatic plant mineral content, and US workers had reported higher crude protein and lower fiber in aquatic plants than in [[Forage Plants]]. Some submersed weeds had high [[Xanthophyll]] content and colored poultry yolks, and European and Asian workers reported limited use of water weeds as fodder, but no nutritive data had been published. The author frames the study as the first phase of a program on the food value of aquatic plants.

Plants were selected for availability near Auburn, Alabama, though most genera are cosmopolitan. [[Water Hyacinth]] (*Eichhornia crassipes*), described as among the worst aquatic pest plants in tropical nations, was reserved for a separate report. The work was supported by a [[Rockefeller Foundation]] grant (RF-65061), with manuscript preparation aided by a US Atomic Energy Commission contract.

**Methods in brief:**
- **Sampling:** Plants were collected from one to three stands per species on a single spring date, with some species sampled monthly at selected stands. Emergent plants were cut to leaves or aerial shoots, submersed plants to shoots, and algae hand-picked from dense mats.
- **Proximate analysis:** Dry matter (65 °C, 72 hr), [[Kjeldahl Nitrogen Analysis]] (crude protein = N × 6.25), ash (550 °C, 6 hr), cellulose, tannin (colorimetric), crude fat (continuous ether extraction) and caloric value (bomb calorimeter).
- **Leaf protein extraction:** Based on the [[Byers Extraction Method]]. Fresh leaves were pulped, pressed, re-extracted, and protein was precipitated by heating to 80 °C. Amino acids were measured after 6N HCl hydrolysis on a [[Beckman Amino Acid Analyzer]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Growth form matters:** The plants were grouped as (a) completely submersed vascular plants, (b) vascular plants with emergent leaves, and (c) non-planktonic algae.
- **Stand characteristics:** Submersed and emergent species and algae often form monospecific stands covering hundreds of hectares. Standing crop for submersed plants and algae usually ranged from 1,000 to 4,000 kg dry weight/ha, and emergent plants equaled or exceeded many crop plants.
- **Fertility and age:** The author notes that water fertility influences nutritive quality as much as plant age. All samples came from relatively fertile sites, so values may vary widely.
- **Mineral deposits:** Calcium carbonate encrustation ("marl") on [[Myriophyllum Spicatum]], [[Pithophora Sp.]] and [[Chara Sp.]] raised ash content. Ash levels above 50% have been reported for *Pithophora* and *Chara*.
- **Harvest logistics:** Harvesting machinery is costly, but manual labor is inexpensive in many tropical countries.

## Key Findings

**Leaf protein extractability**
- Extractable protein nitrogen ranged from 9.7% to 61.0% of pulp nitrogen.
- Using the [[Byers Classification]] (above 40%, 20–40%, below 20%), values above 40% occurred in young *Justicia americana* and *Alternanthera philoxeroides*, all [[Orontium Aquaticum]] samples, and all but one [[Nymphaea Odorata]] extraction. Twelve species were intermediate and four gave less than 20%.
- Protein extractability fell with age in *J. americana* even though extractable protein was higher in May, when standing crop was low.
- Many species with poor extractability contained large amounts of mucilage. Nitrogen content was therefore not a reliable predictor of extractability.
- Areal extractable crude protein yields ranged from 17 to 590 kg/ha. *J. americana*, [[Sagittaria Latifolia]] and *A. philoxeroides* gave yields similar to reported crop-plant leaf protein.
- Laboratory figures represent ideal conditions; the author expects large-scale machinery to recover less.

**Leaf protein quality**
- Dried protein isolates contained less than 9% nitrogen, a level typical of legumes. Isolates from *Orontium*, all but two *J. americana* preparations and one *S. latifolia* sample were between 7% and 9%. Several were below 5%.
- Leaf protein was high in fat and low in fiber. Its caloric value was fairly high because of the fat content.
- Compared with meat proteins, the amino acid profile was somewhat low in [[Lysine]] and [[Methionine]]. Phenylalanine was very low in *A. philoxeroides* and *S. latifolia*. Otherwise levels resembled meat and crop-plant leaf proteins, so the author judged the protein useful as a dietary supplement.
- About half the pulp dry matter remained as fiber after extraction. It contained enough crude protein to be useful as feed, mainly as ruminant roughage because cellulose was high.
- The juice after protein precipitation contains amino acids, nucleotides and soluble carbohydrate, and the author suggests it could support microorganism culture for animal feed.

**Dried plant composition**
- Dry matter ranged from 5.2% to 15.8% (submersed), 10.3% to 23.9% (emergent) and 3.9% to 14.9% (algae). Roughage crops had much higher dry matter than water weeds.
- Crude protein ranged from 8.5% (*Myriophyllum heterophyllum*) to 31.3% (the cyanophycean alga *Lyngbya* sp.). Of 40 species analyzed, 9 exceeded 20% crude protein.
- Algal means were all above 16.7% crude protein, higher than vascular plants and most high-quality roughages.
- Protein fell rapidly with maturity in *J. americana*, *A. philoxeroides* and *Pithophora* sp. Monthly harvestable crude protein in *J. americana* was 1,142, 1,198, 1,265 and 1,150 kg/ha (May to August), so the author recommends harvest in May or June.
- Most submersed plants and algae contained less than 4% crude fat. About nine emergent species (almost half) exceeded 4%.
- Vascular plants generally contained 20–30% cellulose, some about 40%. Algae ranged from 10.0% to 40.9%.
- Mineral content: submersed plants and algae had about twice the ash of roughages. Calcium was elevated by marl deposits, while iron and manganese exceeded terrestrial levels.
- Caloric values ranged from 2.58 to 4.30 kcal/g, with most above 3.25 kcal/g. Caloric value fell as ash rose, but ash-free values clustered near 4.30 kcal/g.

**Tannin**
- Digestibility declines when tannin exceeds about 6–7% (dry weight). Eleven vascular species exceeded 6%, while algae contained very little.
- *Nymphaea odorata* (15.00%) and *Nuphar advena* (6.47%) were the exceptions among high-protein species. All species with crude protein above 15% had less than 5% tannin.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Several species could serve as high-protein roughages, and some contain notable trace minerals.
- Feeding trials are required to establish biological value for dried plants and leaf protein.
- Air drying could be developed, since mechanical dewatering would be prohibitive. High moisture is advantageous for extraction.
- Hand harvesting is probably economical in tropical regions.
- Water weeds could help relieve local protein shortages in developing nations, but are unlikely to contribute greatly to any nation's total food supply.
- Exploratory research into native aquatic flora should begin in nations facing severe protein shortages.
