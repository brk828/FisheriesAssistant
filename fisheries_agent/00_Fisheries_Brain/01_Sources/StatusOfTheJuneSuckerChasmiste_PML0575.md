# June Sucker (*Chasmistes liorus*) Status and Recovery in Utah Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a status and recovery review of the [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*), a midwater planktivorous sucker endemic to [[Utah Lake]], Utah. Its opening page is the abstract of a separate paper by Robert J. Behnke on flow management for endangered fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. That abstract argues that no solid evidence yet supports flow recommendations for endangered fishes and proposes a federal-state "water budget" that accounts for fishery values.

The main text, by Randy L. Radant (Utah Division of Wildlife Resources) and Terry J. Hickman (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service), documents a steep population decline driven by commercial harvest, habitat loss, water diversion and nonnative species. The June sucker is now rare and reproduces mainly in the [[Provo River]]. The authors judge recovery very difficult and say that a coordinated, multi-agency effort is needed within several years.

## Background & Description

*Chasmistes* are lake-dwelling midwater planktivores with numerous gill rakers and a terminal mouth. Fossil records indicate the genus once occurred widely across western North America. Only three species survive:

- **June sucker** (*C. liorus*): endemic to Utah Lake, and the focus of this document.
- **Cui-ui sucker** (*C. cujus*): Lahontan Basin, Nevada. It is federally listed as endangered.
- **Shortnose sucker** (*C. brevirostris*): Klamath River Basin, Oregon and California.

The cui-ui is federally listed as endangered, and the other two were under consideration for listing. The June sucker is a remnant of [[Lake Bonneville]], and Utah Lake is slightly saline, turbid and highly eutrophic. The document gives a mean depth of "2.9 m" (OCR-garbled in source) and a maximum depth of 4.2 m. The Provo and Spanish Fork rivers are its largest tributaries, and the Jordan River is its only natural outflow.

**Taxonomy.** The June sucker is distinguished from the [[Utah Sucker]] (*Catostomus ardens*) by a subterminal mouth, relatively smooth divided lips, a broad skull and more gill rakers. Its spawning also occurs in June, while the Utah sucker spawns in early April. Miller and Smith (1981) proposed that hybridization with the Utah sucker during the 1912–1915 drought produced the present-day June sucker population. They assigned the 1880s specimens to *C. liorus* and specimens collected after 1979 to a second named form, "nictus" (as printed). Earlier reports of three sucker species in Utah Lake are therefore considered mistaken.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical abundance.** When pioneers arrived in 1848, Utah Lake supported abundant cutthroat trout, Utah chub and suckers. Fish from the lake supplied early settlers. Jordan (1891) reported "millions" of suckers and called the lake "the greatest sucker pond in the Universe." In the late 1890s, about 1.56 t of spawning suckers were killed when the Provo River was dewatered. Other mass mortalities occurred from dewatering and from crowding and freezing when irrigation drained the lake (Tanner 1936).

**Drivers of decline.** The document names several causes:
- Commercial fishing (162 t harvested annually, 1901–1905). Sucker catches were still high in 1951, when 1,750 could be taken in one day of seining.
- Irrigation withdrawals and the degradation of water quality.
- Introduction and expansion of nonnative fishes, especially [[White Bass]] and [[Walleye]] in the mid-1950s.
- Changes in lake level, degraded spawning habitat, and predation and competition from nonnative species.

**Current fish community.** The dominant species in Utah Lake are [[Common Carp]], [[White Bass]], [[Walleye]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. Cutthroat trout were extirpated by 1976, and the Utah chub is considered extirpated.

**Spawning habitat.** Sucker spawning is restricted mostly to the Provo River, with limited spawning also in the Spanish Fork River. Adults travel over 6 km upstream to a diversion barrier, though few pass it. Spawning occurs at mean river temperatures of 11–15 °C. Water velocities at spawning sites averaged 0.37 m/s on the bottom (range 0.06–0.98 m/s). Depths ranged from 0.10 to 0.86 m, with a mean of 0.51 m. The substrate is mainly coarse gravel to small cobble.

**Life history.** Eggs are pale yellow, demersal and weakly adhesive. They hatch in four days at 21.1 °C and in ten days at 10.6 °C. Larvae stay quiescent on the bottom for ten days, then move to shallow, low-current backwaters. Most young-of-the-year remain in the river through August, and most have left by October. A few overwinter in the river. Research on June sucker in the [[Mona Reservoir]] is planned to establish a second population.

**Management pressures.** The Central Utah Project (CUP), costing several billion dollars, will reduce and alter flows in the Provo River and isolate parts of Utah Lake behind dikes. Impacts could begin as early as 1984 or 1985.

## Key Findings

**Population status**

- Gill-net catch rates fell from 0.16 suckers per net hour in 1959 to 0.01 in 1970. No suckers were caught in 1978–1979 gillnetting.
- A 1978–1979 inventory produced 2,097 separate net collections and recorded 341292 adult fish as printed. This figure may be an OCR error for 34,292. Only 102 (0.1%) were June suckers, and 18 were Utah suckers. No young-of-the-year suckers were taken.
- No survival or young-of-the-year June suckers have been recorded in Utah Lake in recent years.

**Biology**

- Biological data were nonexistent before 1978 and remain generally lacking.
- Spawning begins in the second or third week of June and is usually completed within 5–8 days.
- Spawning is a group process: a female is flanked by two males, and eggs and milt are released in brief bursts.

**Listing and regulation**

- The [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] lists the June sucker as a protected species.
- The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) Salt Lake City office recommended a listing proposal in 1982. A December 1982 *Federal Register* notice included the species among those under review.
- In March 1983 a recommendation to prepare an endangered-species listing proposal was forwarded to the USFWS Washington Office. In 1983 the USFWS received a petition from the Desert Fishes Council to list the species. A finding was published on 14 June 1983 concluding the petition warranted further action.
- A listing proposal was expected in March or April 1984. Once published, the Service would have up to one year to decide on a final listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Recovery plan**

The authors list the main recovery steps:
1. Obtain life-history data on preferred lake habitat, food, and competition and predation by nonnative fishes.
2. Coordinate Utah Lake fisheries management to reduce competition and predation by nonnative species. Future introductions should be fully evaluated.
3. Develop a hatchery program, modeled on [[Cui-Ui Sucker]] recovery, to increase the size and numbers of June suckers entering Utah Lake. A second lake may be needed for propagation and security. An established 526-ha water body about 19 km south of Utah Lake is being considered for stocking.
4. Rehabilitate the Spanish Fork River so June suckers can spawn successfully there.
5. Monitor CUP developments and resolve negative impacts to ensure recovery efforts are not hampered. Flows in the Provo River must be adequate for spawning.

**Conclusion.** The authors doubt the June sucker will ever again reach its 1880s abundance. However, they state that if the species is to avoid extinction, an intense coordinated effort by state, federal and private entities must reverse the decline within the next several years.
