# Studies of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker Use of the 15-Mile Reach of the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report (July 1989, Colorado River Fishery Project) evaluates the relative abundance, movement, habitat use and spawning of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]] in the 15-mile reach of the Colorado River in the Grand Valley, Colorado, and adjacent reaches. Data were collected from May 1986 through December 1988 using electrofishing, [[Radiotelemetry]], larval seining and young-of-the-year (YOY) seining. The 15-mile reach is found to be an integral part of a larger Grand Valley habitat complex: adults move among three adjacent reaches to meet season-specific needs, and loss of the 15-mile reach would be especially significant to both species. Successful reproduction in the reach appears very limited. The document also includes an August 1989 errata letter correcting misprinted Figure 21 pages.

## Background & Description

- **Status:** The range of the Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker has declined by about 75% since the turn of the century. Only the squawfish is federally listed as endangered; the razorback is considered rarer and may be near extirpation.
- **Distribution:** Both fishes are largely restricted to the upper Colorado River basin above [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. A remnant population of old razorback adults persists in [[Lake Mohave]], a lower-basin reservoir.
- **Threats:** Dams and diversions (e.g., [[Grand Valley Diversion]], [[Redlands Diversion]], [[Price Stub Diversion]]) have altered natural flow and temperature regimes and partitioned habitat.
- **Study area:** The "15-mile reach" runs from the Grand Valley Diversion (RM 185.1) to the Gunnison confluence (RM 171.0), now 14.2 miles long after the confluence shifted following the 1983-84 floods. Adjacent areas include the 18-mile reach and the lower 2.2 miles of the [[Gunnison River]].
- **Funding:** U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, under an agreement tied to [[Green Mountain Reservoir]] and [[Ruedi Reservoir]] water sales.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and habitat.** Irrigation withdrawals during August-October further reduce already low natural summer flows, raising concern the reach could become uninhabitable.

**Habitat use by season (radio-tagged adults, 1986-88):**
- Winter (Nov-Feb): restricted movement; pools most used, then low-velocity runs; ice cover forms over low-velocity areas in January.
- Spring (Apr-Jun): spring runoff; flooded [[Gravel Pit Ponds]] and large backwaters peak in use in May-June.
- Summer (Jul-Sep): runs predominantly used, with some riffles and rapids; flows lowest.
- October: transition, pool use increases.

**Depth and velocity.** Squawfish used deep water (>4.5 ft) most in winter and shallow water (<3 ft) most in April, May and August. Mean column velocity at squawfish sites was usually below 0.35 ft/s except July-October. Bottom velocities were always lower than mean column velocities.

**Substrate.** Substrate type correlates with velocity: silt/sand at low-velocity sites, rubble/boulder/bedrock at swift sites.

**Razorback habitat.** Razorbacks showed a stronger preference for deep water (mean depth >6.0 ft November-April) and used sand/silt substrates in winter-spring.

**Temperature.** Squawfish at times selected warmer habitats (backwaters and gravel pits up to 10.5 °C warmer than the main channel). Spawning-minimum temperatures (20 °C) were reached in the 15-mile reach, though often not until mid-summer.

**Water clarity.** Secchi visibility ranged widely in 1987-88. Depth at fish locations correlated weakly but significantly with clarity (r = 0.29, P < 0.01, n = 204).

**Other species.** At least 16 fish species inhabit the rearing areas of young squawfish. Six dominated YOY samples: native [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]], and introduced [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Sand Shiner]] and [[Red Shiner]].

## Key Findings

**Relative abundance**
- Standardized surveys (1979-81) captured 22 adult squawfish (>450 mm TL) from seven upper-basin reaches, 188 miles total. The Grand Valley reach yielded 3 adults (0.10/mile), about average.
- Of 70 razorbacks captured river-wide in 1979-85, 53 (76%) came from the Grand Valley.
- Electrofishing in the lower Gunnison (1987-88) caught few squawfish; catch per hour was 0.00-1.50 depending on month.

**Movement**
- Adult squawfish showed a range of patterns: some resident in the 15-mile reach year-round; others present only in winter/spring or during spawning; some used the Gunnison River, [[Price Stub Diversion]] plunge pool or Redlands Diversion plunge pool, and some moved as far as RM 130.
- Of 34 squawfish tagged 1982-85, 15 (44%) occupied the 15-mile reach at some time during May-October.
- Razorbacks showed directed spawning migrations; one fish moved upstream to RM 180.0 in May, and others moved downstream 10-12 miles after spawning.

**Spawning**
- Squawfish spawning was estimated at 25 July-5 August in 1986, and about 4 July in 1987 and 1988.
- No squawfish larvae were found in the 15-mile reach in 1986-88. Only 2 larvae (<25 mm TL) and no YOY have been collected from the reach since 1982.
- Larvae and YOY were found in the downstream 18-mile reach: 8 larvae in 1986 (catch 0.14/sample), 1 in 1987 and 1 in 1988. 1986 was considered a considerably better reproductive year.
- A 105-mm yearling squawfish was seined from the 15-mile reach in 1985.
- Razorbacks spawn in May-June. No larval or YOY razorback was collected in any year. Ripe razorbacks were caught in gravel-pit ponds: 50 of 57 razorbacks (88%) captured 1979-88 came from flooded gravel-pit ponds.

**Native vs. introduced fish trends (1986-88)**
- Young of bluehead sucker, speckled dace and (partly) roundtail chub declined; roundtail chub larvae were most abundant in 1987.
- Introduced fathead minnows, sand shiners and red shiners increased sharply, particularly in the 15-mile reach.
- The authors do not know whether these trends will continue or reflect short-term flow fluctuations (peak flows declined from 1986 wet year to 1988 low-flow year).

**Limiting factors identified**
- Summer flows and availability of deep water; contaminants (unknown levels); water clarity increasing predation risk; winter ice cover; introduced predators such as [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]; angling mortality; and loss of gravel-pit and backwater habitat after floods.
- Razorback recruitment appears to fail at egg/early-life stages; carp predation on eggs is a suspected factor in the lower basin.

**Management recommendations**
- Maintain the 15-mile reach as habitat for both species, with adequate flows, especially during summer low flow.
- Consider temperature augmentation to promote earlier spawning and growth, and high spring flushing flows to control introduced fishes.
- Earlier recommendations (Valdez and Wick 1982; Osmundson 1987) to seal gravel pits from the river, and to excavate backwaters open at all flows, remain untested.
