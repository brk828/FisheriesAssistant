# Ecology of an Invasive Pest Bivalve: *Corbicula fluminea*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by Robert F. McMahon in volume 6 of *The Mollusca* (Ecology, edited by W. D. Russell-Hunter, 1983), reviews the ecology of the Asian clam [[Corbicula Fluminea]], a freshwater bivalve that has become one of the most important mollusc pests ever introduced into the United States. It synthesizes taxonomy, introduction history, pest impacts, physiological ecology, life-history traits, control measures and potential beneficial uses. Although the document is a book chapter rather than a fish study, it covers aquatic ecosystem components relevant to freshwater benthic communities.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The genus *Corbicula* belongs to the order Veneroida and family Corbiculidae (Sphaeriacea group). Native to freshwater and estuarine habitats of Southeast Asia, Africa, the Indian subcontinent, Pacific Islands and South America. Taxonomic confusion has led to several names being applied to North American populations (e.g., *C. manilensis*, *C. leana*, *C. fluminalis*); the author argues North American clams are *C. fluminea*, closely allied to the freshwater Asian form rather than the estuarine *C. fluminalis*.
- **Morphology:** Shell with large concentric ridges, a very large muscular foot adapted for burrowing in unstable sand-gravel habitats, and short inhalant and exhalant siphons fringed with tentacles (Fig. 1). Typical shell lengths in North America are around 40–45 mm, occasionally up to 60 mm.
- **Life history (North American populations):** Lifespan of 2–3 years, two reproductive periods per year, simultaneous hermaphroditism, and a non-swimming benthic juvenile released at about 0.2–0.22 mm.
- **Introduction history:** First reported as empty shells in 1924 at Nanaimo, Vancouver Island, British Columbia, and from the Columbia River, Washington, in 1938. Rapid spread followed (see [[Invasion History of Corbicula]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preference:** Primarily lotic, well-oxygenated, shallow waters with coarse sand, gravel or sand-gravel substrata; almost always absent from reducing mud and silt with high organic content.
- **Salinity:** Strictly freshwater in North America, with tolerance to salinities of roughly 3–8‰ and short-term shock exposure of 10–14‰; populations rarely persist above about 5‰.
- **Oxygen:** Poor ability to regulate oxygen uptake under hypoxia; metabolic rate falls quickly as oxygen decreases.
- **Temperature:** Long-term upper lethal limit about 34–35 °C; reduced spawning and filtration above 25–30 °C; very low tolerance to winter temperatures below 2 °C, which restricts northern distribution to heated-effluent areas.
- **Aerial exposure:** Survives in air for up to about 38 days at high humidity by sealing valves with hardened mucus and periodically exposing mantle edges.
- **Human-mediated spread:** Several anomalous records (Ohio River, Lake Overholser) indicate transport by humans, such as via bilge water, bait or aquarium trade.

## Key Findings

- **Pest impacts:** Fouls irrigation canals and pipes, water treatment intakes, municipal water systems, and especially steam condensers and emergency cooling systems of power stations, including a nuclear plant near Russellville, Arkansas. Shell accumulation can restrict flow in irrigation canals; densities of more than 1,000 clams/m² are common, with a maximum of 131,000 clams/m² reported in the Delta-Mendota Canal, California.
- **Competition:** Native unionid and sphaeriid bivalves may decline where *Corbicula* invades, but the author notes that evidence is mixed and that *C. fluminea* is less tolerant of high temperature, hypoxia and desiccation than many natives. Impacts are most likely in larger navigable rivers with management-driven disturbance.
- **Physiology:** Strong Na⁺ uptake capacity, high filtration and locomotion rates, and greater osmoregulatory ability than unionids, but less developed than estuarine bivalves. Physiology is largely similar to estuarine ancestors.
- **Life-history strategy:** Early maturity, short lifespan, high fecundity, two broods a year, incubatory reproduction and a benthic juvenile stage that adheres by a byssus thread, favouring colonisation of unstable habitats. The author describes this as a shift from the r-selected strategy typical of invasive species.
- **Genetics:** Electrophoretic work (M. H. Smith et al., 1979) found North American populations nearly monomorphic (genetic variability of 0, similarity index of 1), indicating a single founder introduction, probably near northwestern Washington State.
- **Likely origin:** Southeast Asia, probably China, with introduction in the early 1920s most likely via Chinese immigrants carrying the clams as food.
- **Control:** Natural-environment control is considered impractical because the clams close their valves against biocides such as chlorination. Facility controls include continuous or periodic chlorination (e.g., 0.5–1.0 µg Cl/L continuous residual, or 500 µg Cl/L for about 100 h), screening, flushing and mechanical removal. EPA rules restrict chlorination in recirculated systems to 2 h in every 24 h, which is too short to be effective. Closed-loop cooling and heat-shock using heated effluent (above 35 °C for 20–30 days, or 43 °C for 30 min) are proposed.
- **Beneficial uses:** Potential fish and waterfowl food, game-fish bait, protein source for livestock feed, aquaculture or tertiary sewage clarification (though temperature and hypoxia limit this), bioassay and bioindicator organism, and a classroom substitute for endangered unionids.
- **Research agenda:** The chapter identifies gaps including the reproductive cycle and hermaphroditism, competition with natives, dispersal, control methods and comparative life-history studies. The author suggests *C. fluminea* could become the freshwater counterpart of the marine model *Mytilus edulis*.
