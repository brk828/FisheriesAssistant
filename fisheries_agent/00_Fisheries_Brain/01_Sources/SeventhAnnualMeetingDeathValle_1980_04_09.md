## Executive Summary

This document is the report of the **Seventh Annual Meeting of the [[Death Valley System Committee]]** of the **[[Desert Fishes Council]]**, held February 22, 1980, at Furnace Creek, Death Valley. The meeting addressed conservation status, threats, and management actions for the endemic fish fauna of the **[[Death Valley System]]** — a network of isolated desert springs, remnant Pleistocene lake outlets, and intermittent rivers in the Mojave Desert/Great Basin region hosting numerous highly range-restricted cyprinodontid, cyprinid, and catostomid species. Key concerns discussed include the **[[Ash Meadows]]** land acquisition crisis following the **Devils Hole Supreme Court decision**, the existential threat posed by the proposed **[[MX Missile System]]** deployment across the Great Basin, ongoing recovery efforts for multiple pupfish and chub taxa, and habitat rehabilitation projects (e.g., **[[Fish Slough]]**, **[[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]]**, **[[Scruggs Springs]]**). The report catalogs population statuses for 15 taxa, most of which are single-spring or single-drainage endemics facing threats from groundwater decline, exotic species introduction (notably *Gambusia* and mollies), and habitat loss.

## Background & Description

The source is a policy/status report rather than a morphological study, so detailed anatomical data are absent. However, taxonomic identifications establish key morphological/systematic distinctions:

- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) — smallest-range pupfish, confined to a single limestone cavern pool; distinct dwarfed morphology (not described in text but implied by isolation).
- **[[Pahrump Killifish]]** (*Empetrichthys latos*) — a goodeid-affiliated livebearer-like killifish lineage endemic to the Pahrump Valley springs; taxonomically distinct from *Cyprinodon*.
- Multiple **[[Rhinichthys osculus]]** (speckled dace) subspecies are under active taxonomic review to determine differentiation between geographically isolated Death Valley System populations (Ash Meadows, Owens River, Amargosa River near Beatty, Amargosa Gorge below Tecopa).
- **[[Gila bicolor]]** subspecies (Owens chub *G. b. snyderi*, Mohave chub *G. b. mohavensis*) represent divergent chub lineages isolated in separate Pleistocene lake relict habitats.
- **[[Catostomus fumeiventris]]** (Owens sucker) is the system's sole large-bodied catostomid, abundant in the Long Valley Reservoir (Crowley Lake) area.
- **[[Xyrauchen texanus]]** (razorback sucker) is mentioned only regarding behavioral radio-telemetry, not morphology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Death Valley System comprises isolated relict aquatic habitats — spring pools, spring-fed marshes, intermittent river reaches, and artesian outflows — remnants of Pleistocene-era lakes and rivers in an otherwise arid hydrographic basin. Each taxon typically occupies a single spring, spring complex, or short river reach, making populations extremely vulnerable to stochastic and anthropogenic disturbance:

- **[[Pahrump Killifish]]**: extirpated from native Manse Spring; survives only in translocated populations at Corn Creek (~2,500 individuals) and Shoshone Ponds (~2,500 individuals). Threatened by invasive bullfrogs (*Rana catesbeiana*) and proposed MX Missile infrastructure.
- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]**: confined to Devils Hole, a single geothermal fissure pool; winter population ~200–253 individuals (Feb 21 count = 253), with seasonal rise expected. Supplemental populations maintained at an Amargosa pupfish station (transplant in progress) and Hoover Dam (~60 fish).
- **[[Ash Meadows Speckled Dace]]** (*R. o. nevadensis*): intermittent Amargosa River reaches near Springdale and Beatty, plus Ash Meadows springs; fluctuating populations.
- **[[Ash Meadows Pupfish]]** (*C. n. mionectes*): established across ten springs in Ash Meadows; threatened by exotic *Gambusia* and mollies and long-term groundwater decline.
- **[[Warm Springs Pupfish]]** (*C. n. pectoralis*): ~500 individuals at School Spring, ~5,400 across all habitats including Scruggs Springs; *Gambusia* eradication a recovery priority.
- **[[Owens Chub]]** (*G. b. snyderi*): single population below Long Valley (Crowley Lake) Dam; population size unknown — potentially the most endangered fish in the system.
- **[[Owens Dace]]**: small populations in lower Bishop Creek tributaries of the Owens River; stable but small.
- **[[Mohave Chub]]** (*G. b. mohavensis*): thriving at Fort Soda (BLM refugium, enlarged 50%), Lark Seep Lagoon (China Lake Naval Weapons Center, aided by artificial shelters), Barstow Way Station, and Hinckley school property.
- **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (*C. radiosus*): managed within the Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary (enlarged 1980); exotic removal completed; restocking planned from BLM Spring (Fish Slough) and Warm Springs refugium near Big Pine. Habitat integrity threatened by 202 acres of private land within Fish Slough.
- **[[Tecopa Pupfish]]** (*C. n. calidae*): likely extinct; possible relict habitat search planned; threatened further by geothermal prospecting near Tecopa.
- **[[Cottonball Marsh Pupfish]]** (*C. milleri*): stable, isolated in Death Valley wilderness area, benefiting from wilderness management status.
- **[[Saratoga Springs Pupfish]]** (*C. n. nevadensis*): stable; potential talc mining and Fort Irwin expansion impacts monitored.
- **[[Amargosa Pupfish]]** (*C. n. amargosae*): stable in Amargosa River near/below Tecopa; secondary introduced population in River Springs, Adobe Valley, Mono County (introduced by Robert R. Miller, Aug 31, 1940).
- **[[Salt Creek Pupfish]]** (*C. salinus*): stable; supports a successful public interpretive facility at Salt Creek.
- **[[Owens Sucker]]** (*C. fumeiventris*): abundant and stable, especially in Long Valley Reservoir (Crowley Lake) area.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*C. macularius*): highly endangered from habitat loss and exotic species; under State/Federal listing review; Salton Sea-area jojoba bean cultivation could reduce agricultural water demand.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): behavioral studies via radio telemetry at Senator Wash Reservoir near Yuma, AZ.

## Key Findings

- **Ash Meadows land crisis**: Nature Conservancy (Steve McCormick) negotiating with Preferred Equities for land purchase; original NDOW/USFWS asking price was $3.2 million. GAO Los Angeles office formally tasked (per Sen. Alan Cranston letter, March 27, 1980) with a comprehensive Desert Pupfish study beginning ~May 1, 1980.
- **MX Missile System** identified as the single greatest environmental threat to the Great Basin; HDR Sciences Division commissioned to assess aquatic resource impacts (initial reports due July 1980); contrasted starkly with the ~$3–4 million sought to protect Ash Meadows versus >$60 billion proposed MX program cost.
- **17 resolutions** were passed by the Council during 1979 (available from the Secretary upon request).
- **Proceedings publication**: 1978 Proceedings in press (mailing expected May 1980); 1979 Proceedings draft nearing completion (expected mid-summer 1980).
- **Delisting procedures** deemed "complicated"; inquiries directed to the Endangered Species Office.
- **1980 Symposium** tentatively scheduled for Monterrey, Mexico, November 12–14, with a post-symposium field trip to **[[Cuatro Ciénegas]]**.
- **Death Valley System Recovery Team** formation deemed increasingly likely per USFWS Portland Office (reported by Don Sada).
- **National Fisheries Recognition System** (proposed by USFWS Assistant Secretary for Fish and Wildlife and Parks) would provide non-ownership-based protective recognition analogous to the Natural Landmarks Program; Council drafting a letter of support to Assistant Secretary Herbst.
- Multiple taxa remain critically vulnerable due to **exotic species introductions** (*Gambusia affinis*, mollies), **groundwater depletion**, and **habitat fragmentation**, underscoring the fragility of single-locality endemic desert fish populations across the Death Valley System.
- The **[[Owens Chub]]** is flagged as potentially the most endangered fish in the entire Death Valley System due to lack of basic population data.
- **[[Little Lake]]** (Inyo County) identified by Phil Pister as a promising new native fish refugium site due to abundant artesian water and location in the Pleistocene outlet of Owens Lake.
- The **California Desert Plan Draft EIS** was released for public review, with Council comment coordination led by Phil Pister.
