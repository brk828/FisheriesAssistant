# The Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) in the Grand Canyon Area of the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Tulane University Museum of Natural History occasional paper (No. 12, 1977) by [[Royal D. Suttkus]] and [[Glenn H. Clemmer]] redefines the [[Humpback Chub]], *Gila cypha*, from specimens taken in the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Canyon]]. The authors examined 97 specimens ranging from 24.6 to 320 mm standard length (SL) and documented growth, development, sexual dimorphism, breeding tubercles, coloration, reproduction, and meristic and morphometric data. They concluded that all Grand Canyon material belongs to a single, highly variable *G. cypha*, and that the species' future in the canyon is uncertain because of altered flow and temperature regimes below [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

## Background & Description

**Historical records.** The earliest known observation of the species in the Grand Canyon is attributed to the Kolb brothers (1911), who described fish with "bony tails" near the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]]. The authors interpret these as humpback chub rather than [[Bonytail]], which was the species the Kolbs were more likely describing. The holotype was collected from the Colorado River near the mouth of [[Bright Angel Creek]] before fall 1942 (Miller, 1946). By 1973, [[Minckley]] noted that almost nothing was known of the species' biology because of collecting difficulties and its rarity in collections.

**Taxonomic history.** Earlier authors had emphasized the nuchal hump as a diagnostic feature. The authors regard the hump as highly variable and rely instead on a combination of characters, including a small eye and an inferior to nearly horizontal mouth, to identify juveniles and adults. Early in the study they suspected some slender-bodied fish were [[Gila Elegans]] or hybrids, but accumulating material convinced them of a single variable form.

**Methods.** Fin rays, scales, and body proportions followed Hubbs and Lagler (1964), with modifications. Measurements were taken with dial calipers to the nearest 0.1 mm. Collections included the authors' own sampling (15 float trips by September 1976), four adults from the Little Colorado River mouth (June 1976), two salvaged adults from Powell Reservoir, specimens from below Glen Canyon Dam (1967), and four loaned specimens from the Museum of Northern Arizona. Preservation damage limited measurements on the older series. The study was supported by National Park Service Contract CX821060006.

**Size range and sampling gaps.** Specimens from 26.4 to 110 mm SL were taken with a ten-foot seine. No specimens were available between 110 and 164 mm SL, or between 164 and 204 mm SL. The largest specimen is a 320 mm female salvaged from Powell Reservoir.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and habitat.** The authors presume spawning occurs in June and July in the Grand Canyon area. The 24.6 mm and two 24.7 mm individuals, taken on 22 September, are presumed young-of-the-year. The lower [[Little Colorado River]] is considered the major spawning area for the surviving population. A single adult was released at the mouth of [[Shinumo Creek]], where three young were taken, and the authors consider that site marginal for spawning, mainly because of its small size.

**Flow and temperature.** The authors describe the population's future as questionable. They suggest a cold-water strain may persist near the Little Colorado River. Under average to maximum releases from Glen Canyon Dam, the volume of very cold water overwhelms the warming effect of the Little Colorado River. The authors view extreme man-manipulated flow patterns on the main Colorado River as detrimental to survival. Low summer flows (1977 spawning season) might temporarily benefit the species by allowing warmer Little Colorado River water to raise mainstem temperatures.

**Localities.** Powell Reservoir specimens lack precise collection sites; some came from Kane County, Utah, and some from Coconino County, Arizona. All Grand Canyon specimens are from Coconino County. Other material came from the Colorado River at multiple river miles and from the [[Green River]] at the town of Green River, Utah.

## Key Findings

**1. Taxonomic conclusion.** All Grand Canyon material is referable to *Gila cypha*. Scattergram patterns and comparisons with specimens of [[Gila Elegans]] and [[Gila Robusta]] (Green River juveniles, 70–78 mm SL) supported this. The nuchal hump is highly variable and is not a reliable standalone character.

**2. Growth and development.** Young fish under 10 mm SL have fewer than ten lateral line tubes and no scales. Pectoral fin rays are not fully developed at 24.6 mm, and specimens up to 28 mm may lack a full complement. Unusually, [[Lateral Line Scales]] develop from anterior to posterior. A full lateral line series is not present until about 50 mm SL. Scale size and exposure decrease dorsally and ventrally from the lateral line, and the largest specimens are fully scaled. Allometric regressions (Figures 1–8) were fitted to 24 proportions, with n = 97 for most.

**3. Meristic counts.** Of 96 specimens examined for principal caudal rays, 95 had 19 and one had 18. This contrasts with Miller's (1946) count of 20 for the holotype. Of 20 pharyngeal arches examined, 17 had the typical dental formula 2,5–4,2. Dorsal and anal fin-ray and vertebral counts were not diagnostic. Lateral line scale counts were difficult, with an accuracy of about ±2 scales. The fish have a two-chambered swim bladder.

**4. Sexual dimorphism.** Some males have decidedly longer pectoral and pelvic fins than most females, though some females also have long paired fins. The tubular end of the digestive tract is longer in females, and the urogenital papilla is hidden in females but projects ventrally in males.

**5. Breeding tubercles.** Nuptial males carry more extensive [[Breeding Tubercles]] on the head, hump, body, and fins than nuptial females. Tubercles occur on pectoral and pelvic rays, along the margins of breast-patch scales, and on lateral scales. Females have few or no tubercles. One June 1976 adult male from the Little Colorado River represents an early stage of nuptial development. The Powell Reservoir salvage specimens showed breeding coloration, including orange lower body and fin bases in the male, even after freezing.

**6. Reproduction.** The 320 mm female from Powell Reservoir had two size groups of ova. Larger ova ranged from 1.3 to 2.2 mm in diameter, and smaller ova from 0.9 to 1.3 mm. The authors measured preserved ova only, so these values reflect preservation shrinkage. Female 2740 (305 mm SL), with a larger-ova range of 1.6–2.2 mm, was probably ripe at capture.

**7. Conservation outlook.** The authors judge that the species' survival in the Grand Canyon is questionable. Warm water from the Little Colorado River is the key factor in maintaining the population, and mainstem flow management is a primary concern. The paper ends by describing the species' rarity and the need for better understanding of its biology.
