# Conservation Genetics of North American Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Robert Vrijenhoek]] reviews how [[Desert Fish Conservation]] depends on the interplay of physico-chemical and biological processes in arid aquatic habitats. It argues that small, fragmented populations lose genetic diversity through [[Genetic Drift]] and [[Inbreeding Depression]], that this loss can reduce fitness, and that management must match the cause of fragmentation. Natural postpluvial drying favors keeping populations isolated, while human-caused fragmentation may justify restoring gene flow. The chapter illustrates these points with case studies of the [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Sonoran Topminnow]], and the unisexual [[Poeciliopsis Monacha]] complex. The document opens with several pages of unrelated billfish and tuna references, which are not part of the chapter's content.

## Background & Description

North American desert fishes persist in the Great Basin, Mohave, and Sonoran deserts. These fish are remnants of wetter pluvial periods, and natural drying over the last 8,000 to 12,000 years has isolated them in small springs and disconnected stream segments (Miller, 1958). The chapter notes that of 224 fish species listed as threatened or endangered in the United States, approximately 50% live west of the Continental Divide, and more than 20 western species have gone extinct in the past 100 years.

The desert fishes are geologically young and lack specialized adaptations such as aerial respiration or desiccation-resistant embryos seen in other arid regions. Key taxa discussed include:

- **Poeciliopsis monacha** and its hybrid-derived clonal relatives, including the all-female biotype [[Poeciliopsis Monacha-Lucida]], which reproduce by hybridogenesis or gynogenesis.
- **Cyprinodon nevadensis** species complex (Death Valley pupfishes), including the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]] (*C. diabolis*), which may be the most geographically restricted vertebrate in the world.
- **Xyrauchen texanus** (razorback sucker) of the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Poeciliopsis occidentalis** (Sonoran topminnow), once abundant in the Gila River basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonality and habitat stability.** In the Sonoran Desert, a long dry season is followed by intense summer rains, and El Niño can bring midwinter storms. Rainfall drops sharply northward, and Death Valley may receive only four cm annually. Rainy seasons cause flash floods that temporarily reconnect isolated habitats, but these habitats dry again. Residual dry-season pools can be harsh, with crowding, lethal daytime temperatures, nighttime oxygen depletion, and wide swings in salinity and hardness. Springs, by contrast, can be very stable. Some Death Valley springs are fed by aquifers 10,000 to 20,000 years old.

**Pleistocene legacy.** Lake Manly, which at its height was 160 km long, 10–17 km wide, and 185 m deep, is now the salt-encrusted floor of Death Valley. Its springs still hold remnant cyprinodont and minnow populations. The Great Salt Lake has become hypersaline and no longer supports freshwater fish, while Pyramid Lake still does.

**Anthropogenic threats.** Groundwater pumping, surface diversion, dams, exotic species, and hatchery practices all fragment or degrade habitat:

- Pumping in Ash Meadows lowered aquifers, threatening Devil's Hole and eliminating the Pahrump killifish (*Empetrichthys latos pahrump*) once Pahrump Valley springs dried.
- Dams on the Colorado River altered flow and temperature. About 40% of Colorado River fish are native, while nearly 30 species are introduced (Richardson, 1976).
- [[African Tilapia]] thrive in impoundments and can replace native cyprinids and poeciliids when they escape.
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia* spp.) prey on larval native fish and can carry or transmit exotic parasites such as *Gyrodactylus turnbulli*, which is lethal to one *P. monacha* clone.
- Hatchery-reared [[Rainbow Trout]] have replaced or introgressed native [[Cutthroat Trout]] in the Rockies, and sheepshead minnows used as bait hybridized with the Pecos River pupfish.

## Key Findings

**1. Hatchery conservation and genetic monitoring.** The Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center (DNFHTC) in New Mexico maintains 14 endangered fish taxa for restoration (Table 12.1). The chapter stresses that hatchery programs need geneticists and that mass strip-spawning can drastically reduce effective population size. Bartley et al. (1992) found that mass strip-spawning of 2,000 rainbow trout was genetically equivalent to only 88.5 breeders, and 10,000 Chinook salmon to only 132.5.

**2. Effective population size.** The chapter distinguishes inbreeding, variance, and extinction effective sizes (Box 12.3). Sex-ratio imbalance, population fluctuation, skewed family reproduction, and non-random mating all reduce effective size below the count of breeding adults.

**3. Genetic diversity of desert fishes.** Echelle (1991) reviewed 27 threatened or endangered taxa, finding mean within-population heterozygosity (H_s) of 0.04 (range 0.01–0.07). This is lower than in marine fishes but typical of other freshwater fishes. Desert *Gambusia* from the Chihuahuan Desert had significantly lower heterozygosity (mean H_s = 0.016) than 14 species from less arid regions (mean H_s = 0.047), which Echelle attributed to bottlenecks and geographic isolation.

**4. Partitioning diversity among populations.** Genetic diversity partitions into within-population and among-population components. Within-population shares were:
- Rainbow trout (migratory): 85.0%, with 7.7% among samples within drainages and 7.3% among drainages.
- Yellowstone cutthroat trout (nonmigratory): 67.6%, with 15.7% and 16.7%.
- *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*: 21.3%, with 25.5% among samples within drainages and 53.2% among drainages.

**5. Metapopulations and gene flow.** Desert fish fit the [[Metapopulation]] model, with subpopulations linked by colonization, dispersal, and turnover. The island model links gene flow (Nm) to divergence: Nm below 1 allows substantial divergence, while Nm above 1 counters it. Nm is a compound parameter, so the same value can arise from large populations with little migration or small populations with much migration (Box 12.4).

**6. Two management models.**
- **Death Valley model:** Pupfish isolated by natural drying should stay in their refugia. Translocation could erase local adaptations and cause outbreeding depression. Conservation should focus on protecting water supplies and removing exotic predators and competitors.
- **Stream hierarchy model:** Dendritic river fish that were fragmented by human activity may need restored connectivity through fish ladders or translocations, guided by historical gene-flow patterns. Multiple independent loci are needed to test for consensus.

**7. Razorback sucker case study.** Reproductive recruitment has been limited or nonexistent since dam construction, and natural populations are mostly adults born before 1954. A sample of 48 Lake Mohave fish showed 32 mtDNA haplotypes (h = 0.98). The hatchery stock declined to four haplotypes (h = 0.71) in the 1990 year-class, produced from only 17 females. The authors recommend discontinuing mass stripping, using paired matings, and adding wild Lake Mohave fish to the hatchery stock. They also recommend predator-free rearing at protected sites, since natural hatchlings disappear to non-native predators.

**8. Sonoran topminnow case study.** The Monkey Spring (MS) source stock lacked variation at 25 protein loci. Experiments with 10 wild females per stock compared MS (H = 0.000), Sharp Spring (SS; H = 0.037), and Tule Spring (TS; H = 0.015). MS showed the highest mortality, slowest growth, poorest fecundity, and weakest developmental stability. SS performed best for growth, fecundity, and developmental stability. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service adopted the Sharp Spring recommendation in its Gila Topminnow Recovery Plan. The 1984 recovery plan had called for stocking 191 sites, and by 1990 a severe drought left only nine natural populations.

**9. Fitness loss and recovery in *Poeciliopsis monacha*.** In the Arroyo de los Platanos, *P. monacha* made up 76% of fish in Heart Pool in 1975. A 1976 drought eliminated the population, and the founders recolonized with essentially no genetic diversity. By 1978 the clonal MML made up 95% of fish. The homozygous founder population showed greater developmental instability, poorer survival under hypoxia, and higher parasite loads. Translocating 30 genetically variable females from the Arroyo de Jaguari in 1983 restored diversity and competitive ability, with *P. monacha* reaching nearly 80% of fish in Heart Pool by 1985. Spread over roughly 30 generations, the transplant equaled an Nm of about 1 per generation. Box 12.6 reports that heterozygotes never had the worst survival under opposing hypoxic-heat and cold stresses, suggesting balancing selection in a heterogeneous environment.

**10. Summary of recommendations.** The chapter concludes that remnant genetic variation within and among desert fish populations is a precious part of biodiversity. Management should address the cause of fragmentation, restore natural dispersal where human action caused isolation, and monitor captive stocks with genetic designs. Diversity loss in small populations can carry real fitness costs, so the chapter rejects the argument that loss of neutral diversity is unimportant.
