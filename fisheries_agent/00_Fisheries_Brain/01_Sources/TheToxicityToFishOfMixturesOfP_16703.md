# Toxicity to Fish of Mixtures of Poisons II: Copper-Ammonia and Zinc-Phenol Mixtures

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmo-gairdnerii

## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by D. W. M. Herbert and Jennifer M. Vandyke, published in *Annals of Applied Biology* (vol. 53), tests whether the toxic threshold of a two-poison mixture to [[Rainbow Trout]] can be predicted by summing each poison's concentration as a fraction of its individual threshold. The authors examined [[Ammonium Chloride]] with [[Copper Sulphate]] and [[Phenol]] with [[Zinc Sulphate]]. For 50% mortality at 48 hours, the threshold occurred where the summed fractions equalled about 1, supporting the working hypothesis. For the copper-ammonia mixture, the method became less reliable at lower percentage mortalities (25% and 10%), where it tended to over-predict toxicity. Both mixtures were far more toxic than [[Independent Joint Action]] models predicted.

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** Hatchery-reared [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii* Richardson, as cited). Copper-ammonia trials used yearling fish averaging 12.5 cm; phenol-zinc trials used fish 3–4 months old averaging 5.9 cm.
- **Central hypothesis:** The threshold concentration of a mixture of poisons P and Q is reached when (P_S/P_50 + Q_S/Q_50) = 1, where the subscript S is the solution concentration and 50 is the [[Median Lethal Concentration]] (here termed the threshold, or 48 h TL_m) for a single poison.
- **Prior work:** The authors built on [[Lloyd]] (1961, zinc-copper), [[Herbert]] (1962, ammonia-phenol), and [[Herbert and Shurben]] (1964, ammonia-zinc). Similar additivity had been reported for heavy metals on micro-organisms and for odour and taste thresholds of organic chemicals.
- **Methods overview:** Tests used fixed-volume solutions, following the procedure of the first paper in the series. Ten fish were exposed to each concentration for up to 48 hours, after 2 days' acclimation at 17.0 °C and pH 7.8. Concentrations followed a geometric progression, and the two poisons were mixed in a 1:1 ratio of their threshold fractions. Dissolved oxygen was held at air saturation. Ten control fish per series did not die.
- **Analysis:** Probit analysis was applied to log median survival time and log concentration data, using [[Probit Analysis]] with the summed fraction as the dose metameter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water quality as a variable:** The authors emphasised that poisons in mixtures may interact chemically before reaching the fish. Zinc and ammonia can form zinc-ammonium complexes, but less than 1% of total zinc was complexed at the test concentrations. Zinc-phenol association was considered insignificant.
- **Copper speciation:** Copper and ammonia have a high affinity for each other. In the copper-ammonia mixture where the summed fraction was 1, about 75% of added copper existed as [[Cuprammonium Ions]], mainly Cu(NH3)4²⁺, while only about 0.3% of added ammonia was bound in this form. The authors concluded that cuprammonium ions are equal, or nearly equal, in toxicity to free copper ions.
- **Comparison with cyanide complexes:** [[Doudoroff]] (1956) showed that copper-cyanide complexes in moderately hard water are less toxic to fish than equivalent metal or cyanide concentrations. The authors attributed the difference to complex stability. The copper-ammonia complex is less stable than the copper-cyanide complex, so it dissociates more readily to release copper at the gill surface. Copper's affinity for sulphydryl groups in proteins, as in the amino acid cysteine, was also discussed in the context of [[Metal Toxicity Mechanisms]], following [[Shaw and Grushkin]] (1957).
- **Biological degradation of phenol:** Phenol is oxidised by micro-organisms that grow quickly in test aquaria. To keep concentrations within 4% of the target throughout 48-hour tests, fish were moved to freshly prepared solutions every 6 hours.

## Key Findings

**1. Ammonia-copper mixture (50% mortality)**
- Single-poison 48 h TL_m values: ammonia (as N) = 26.4 p.p.m.; copper (as Cu) = 0.27 p.p.m.
- Mixture 48 h TL_m = 0.92 (95% fiducial limits 0.75–1.12), not significantly different from the predicted value of 1.
- Experimental points fell between the single-poison curves and approached a threshold near 1.

**2. Phenol-zinc mixture (50% mortality)**
- Single-poison 48 h TL_m values: phenol = 9.78 p.p.m.; zinc (as Zn) = 2.46 p.p.m.
- Mixture 48 h TL_m = 1.07. Fiducial limits could not be calculated because only one degree of freedom was available. Visual inspection of the probit line suggested the value did not depart significantly from 1.

**3. Lower percentage mortalities (copper-ammonia)**
- Using 25% mortality single-poison concentrations, the mixture produced 25% mortality at 0.82 (95% limits 0.55–1.02).
- For 10% mortality, the value was 0.69 (95% limits 0.35–0.90).
- The predictive method therefore over-predicted toxicity at lower mortality levels for this mixture.
- Lower-percentage adequacy could not be tested for phenol-zinc because the data did not support fiducial limits.

**4. Departure from independent joint action**
- Under [[Bliss Independent Action Model]] and [[Plackett and Hewlett]] models, the copper-ammonia mixture at its 50% concentration should have produced only about 0.1% mortality. The observed effect was much greater.
- For phenol-zinc at its 50% concentration, predicted mortality was 18% under independent action with no correlation in susceptibility, and 20% under negative correlation. The authors considered these values probably not significantly different from the experimental data.
- The authors concluded that independent joint action mechanisms cannot explain the zinc-ammonia and copper-ammonia results, although they may apply to phenol-zinc.

**5. Overall conclusion**
- Across all tested pairs (zinc-copper, zinc-ammonia, copper-ammonia, phenol-ammonia, phenol-zinc), the 50% threshold concentration could be predicted by summing individual poison fractions. This was taken as evidence for a general, though empirical, additive model of [[Toxicity Additivity]] for these mixtures.

**6. Practical implication**
- For water pollution assessment, the findings support using the summed-fraction rule to estimate the threshold for mixed effluents. The authors note that the rule's reliability falls at lower mortality levels for some combinations, so it should not be applied uncritically to sublethal or low-effect thresholds.
