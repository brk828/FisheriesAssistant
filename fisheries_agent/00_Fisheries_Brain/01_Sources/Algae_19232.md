# Algae: Versatile Plants in Aquatic Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phytoplankton #limnology #aquatic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This educational article from the New York State Conservationist examines the ecological importance of algae in freshwater systems, their role in aquatic food chains, and management considerations for controlling excessive algal growth. The document addresses common concerns about algae control while emphasizing their fundamental importance to fish production and aquatic ecosystems. Written by Senior Aquatic Biologist C. W. Greene, it provides practical guidance on pond fertilization techniques and the cautious use of copper sulphate for algae control.

## Background & Description

Algae represent a diverse assemblage of pigmented protista that have evolved plantlike characteristics. The term "algae" is not included in the Linnean system of plant classification but encompasses five to nine classes of plants depending on taxonomic authority. Smith's "Fresh-water Algae of the United States" characterizes them as "the series of pigmented protista (one-celled organisms) in which definite plantlike representatives have been evolved."

This evolution has produced more than 25,000 species with astonishing variety. Algae include [[Diatoms]], blue-green algae, and green algae. Even complex forms like [[Stoneworts]] ([[Chara]] and [[Nitella]]) with their resemblance to higher plants are typically grouped with algae. Most algae occur as single cells or as aggregations (colonies) of single cells, where each colonial cell can perform all life functions independently if separated from the colony.

Algae exhibit remarkable versatility in coloration and habitat selection. Different species display green, blue-green, yellow-green, brown, and red pigmentation. While most live in water and all require moisture, they inhabit diverse environments including soil, rocks, animal hair, hot springs, and even snow. Some species in hard water lakes produce almost pure lime deposits, sometimes forming marl beds in quantities practical for mining.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Algae occupy a position in aquatic biotic communities analogous to grass and weeds on land. They perform the critical function of transforming raw materials (mineral salts and carbon dioxide plus solar energy) into foods usable by animals (carbohydrates, fats, and proteins). A single algal cell typically carries out all essential photosynthetic functions within itself.

Algae are among the most cosmopolitan organisms, dispersed primarily by birds, winds, and streams. Newly created ponds, including "sky" ponds receiving water only from surface drainage, normally become inoculated with algal cultures almost immediately upon filling with water. This widespread distribution reflects their efficient dispersal mechanisms and reproductive capabilities under favorable conditions.

The basic importance of algae in the fish food chain is recognized in pond fertilization techniques. Fish production depends primarily on encouraging abundant algal growth. Given a heavy crop of this basic forage, smaller forms of animal life upon which fish feed become abundant, and fish yields increase correspondingly. Pond fertilization consists of furnishing basic nutrients needed by algae or supplementing naturally deficient nutritive materials.

Successful pond fertilization serves dual functions: increasing fish-food production and potentially balancing fish populations favorably. Dense algal crops shut out sunlight necessary for rooted plant growth, limiting their development to very shallow water or preventing it entirely. Species like [[Bluegill Sunfish]] use rooted plants for shelter and often become overcrowded in weedy situations, preventing good growth of themselves and associated species. When used with [[Largemouth Bass]] in fertilized ponds, bluegills may be kept under control because abundant algae retards development of their natural shelter; bass grow well on a diet of bluegills, in turn thinning the bluegill population so they grow well too.

Lakes typically experience two principal blooming periods: spring and early fall. These blooms result from combinations of conditions favorable for algal growth and reproduction. Different species are favored by different conditions, so blooms occurring at different seasons are generally dominated by different algal species. Some algae species, unless controlled, lend bad taste or odor to drinking water, and decomposing algae after heavy blooms may produce strong odors. Blooms are also associated with poor fishing, supported by fishing experience and the logic that visibility is drastically reduced during such events.

## Key Findings

**Fundamental Ecological Role**: Fishes and other aquatic animals depend directly or indirectly upon algae, at least in some stage of their life history, and could not subsist without this plant type. Most aquatic animals consume algae rather than leaves of higher rooted plants.

**Pond Management Applications**: Fertilization of farm fish ponds successfully increases fish production by encouraging abundant algal growth. Since even new ponds apparently contain cultures of requisite algae species, the fish production problem hinges primarily on encouraging algae to produce abundantly.

**Population Balance Benefits**: Dense algal crops in fertilized ponds can prevent rooted plant development, helping maintain favorable fish population balance. In [[Largemouth Bass]]-[[Bluegill Sunfish]] systems, this prevents bluegill overcrowding by eliminating their shelter habitat.

**Copper Sulphate Control Considerations**: [[Copper Sulphate]] is most commonly used for algae control because it is readily available, reasonable in cost, and relatively easy and safe to use. Laboratory work demonstrates that some algae species can be killed by copper sulphate concentrations not lethal to fish. However, several considerations suggest overoptimism regarding its effectiveness:

- Tolerance ranges from 0.12 to 10.00 parts per million among different algae species
- Toxicity varies with temperature, water hardness, organic matter content, carbon dioxide levels, and algae species/forms present
- Sudden kill-off of large algae quantities may cause fish kills through oxygen depletion during decomposition
- Concentration and decay of dead algae may cause worse odors than if the bloom had run its natural course with gradual mortality

**Management Recommendations**: Active control measures should be considered carefully and usually attempted only as a last resort in fishing waters. When attempted, the objective should be preventing blooms rather than destroying enormous algal concentrations after bloom development.

**Future Potential**: Recent research into algae productivity indicates promising future values directly to humanity. Under optimum conditions, certain algae may produce roughly 60 times as much protein as soybeans, one of the present top producers. This productivity may allow expansion of food resources and other essential raw materials.

**Symbiotic Relationships**: Certain algae species occur only in association with specific animals, including the South American three-toed sloth (where greenish coloration may provide protective camouflage), turtles, and microscopic crustaceans. In some instances these relationships benefit both organisms; in others the algae are parasitic, being nourished by the host animal.
