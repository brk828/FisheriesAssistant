# Black-Footed Ferret Reintroduction in Aubrey Valley, Arizona

#mammalogy #conservation-biology #mustelidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines a 1994 Environmental Assessment (EA), a November 15, 1995 Federal Register proposed rule, and a November 20, 1995 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service cover letter. Together they propose reintroducing captive-raised [[Black-Footed Ferret]]s into the [[Aubrey Valley]] Management Area in northwestern Arizona. The reintroduction would be a joint effort of the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Service) and the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AGFD). The reintroduced population would be designated a [[Nonessential Experimental Population]] under [[Section 10(j) Experimental Population]] provisions of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The EA evaluates three alternatives: No Action, the Proposed Action, and a Delayed Action. The Proposed Action would release up to 50 ferrets, with a target of a free-ranging population of at least 30 breeding adults. The document also records landowner concerns and the public comment process (hearing on December 12, 1995; comments due January 2, 1996).

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Black-Footed Ferret]] (*Mustela nigripes*) is listed as endangered under the federal Endangered Species Act and as endangered by the Arizona state list. It is the only ferret species native to North America. Adults are nearly 60 cm long and weigh up to about 1.1 kg (2.5 lb). They have a black face mask and a black-tipped tail.
- **Dependence on prairie dogs:** Ferrets depend almost exclusively on prairie dogs for food and shelter and breed only within prairie dog colonies. Specimen records come from three prairie dog species: [[Black-Tailed Prairie Dog]], [[White-Tailed Prairie Dog]], and [[Gunnison's Prairie Dog]]. In Arizona, ferrets were associated with Gunnison's prairie dog colonies north of the Mogollon Rim.
- **Historic range:** Records include 12 U.S. states and two Canadian provinces (Alberta and Saskatchewan). Four Arizona specimens date from the early 1900s. The nearest documented occurrence to Aubrey Valley is a 1929 collection from "Red Lake," about 45 miles away.
- **Decline:** Poisoning and cultivation sharply reduced prairie dog populations. [[Sylvatic Plague]] further decimated them. The ferret decline followed, and may also reflect secondary poisoning and susceptibility to [[Canine Distemper]].
- **Recovery history:** The last known wild population, near Meeteetse, Wyoming, collapsed from canine distemper in 1985–1986. Eighteen survivors were taken into captivity in 1986–1987. The 1988 [[Recovery Plan]] set goals of 200 captive breeding adults by 1991 (achieved), and 1,500 free-ranging breeding adults in 10 or more populations, with at least 30 breeding adults in each, by 2010. Downlisting to threatened would follow if these goals were met.
- **Captive breeding:** The [[Captive Breeding Program]] grew from 18 animals in 1986 to about 300 in 1991, and about 400 by 1995 (figures vary between sections). Seven separate captive subpopulations were established across the United States and Canada to guard against a single catastrophic event.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The [[Aubrey Valley Management Area]] is a flat to gently rolling short-grass plains grassland, about 40 km (25 mi) long and 20 km (12 mi) wide. Its valley floor covers about 220 km² (137 mi²). Elevations range from about 1,900 m at the north end to about 1,600 m to the south-southeast. Annual precipitation is 25–30 cm (10–12 in). The valley is bounded on the east by the Aubrey Cliffs, which rise about 300 m, and pinyon-juniper hills surround it on the north, south, and west. Dominant grasses include blue grama. The management area covers 44,134 ha (109,055 acres): 25,598 ha of deeded land (58%) and 18,536 ha of state trust land (42%).

**Land ownership.** The primary landowner is the Navajo Nation. Other owners are the State of Arizona, and private ranches (including the Campbell holding). Nearly all lands are grazed by cattle.

**Prairie dog habitat.** Surveys in 1992 found about 6,969 ha (17,196 acres) of prairie dog towns in the proposed experimental area. Using the [[Ferret Family Rating]] index (Biggins et al. 1989), the site rated 35, meaning it could potentially support about 35 black-footed ferrets. Prairie dog habitat is managed as a [[Prairie Dog Complex]], defined using the "7 Kilometer Rule" (towns less than 7 km apart).

**Survey effort.** Ten black-footed ferret surveys totaling over 600 person-hours found no evidence of wild ferrets. The Service concluded that wild ferrets were highly improbable at the site, so any reintroduced population would be separate from others.

**Wildlife.** The EA lists federal threatened, endangered, and candidate species that may occur in the area (e.g., bald eagle, peregrine falcon, Mexican spotted owl and other raptors, and sensitive plants). It also identifies more than 100 other wildlife species, including mammals, birds, reptiles, and amphibians (Appendix 3). Ferret reintroduction would add predation pressure on prairie dogs from coyotes, badgers, and raptors. The EA expects this to have no significant impact on prairie dog populations.

**Recreation and land use.** Dispersed recreation includes big game hunting (mainly pronghorn), small game and jackrabbit hunting, furbearer trapping, and prairie dog shooting, which is very common. Restrictions on these uses would be voluntary and temporary.

## Key Findings

- **Alternatives compared:**
  - *No Action:* No reintroduction, and no nonessential experimental designation. Predator control and distemper surveys would not occur.
  - *Proposed Action:* Release up to 50 captive-raised ferrets in 1995 (or later, if habitat or animal supply is unsuitable), designated as nonessential experimental.
  - *Delayed Action:* Same as the Proposed Action, but postponed by a reported distemper case, prairie dog decline, animal shortage, or funding limits.
- **Alternatives dismissed:** An endangered (non-experimental) designation was rejected because the land is private or state-owned, and landowners would oppose the stricter protections. An essential experimental designation was also rejected for the same reason. The Little Colorado River Basin (BLM land) and the Coconino Plateau were rejected for smaller, scattered prairie dog towns, higher plague incidence, or insufficient colony density.
- **Release methods:** The release uses a "soft" approach, with ferrets held for about 10 days in above-ground cages with underground nest boxes and supplied with food. Animals are marked with Personal Identification Tags (PIT). Individual releases are spaced 200 m apart and occur over a period of several weeks. Releases may be surrounded by electric fencing. Tracking would use radiotelemetry, spotlight surveys, and snow tracking.
- **Expected mortality:** Up to 90% mortality is expected in the first year, highest in the first month. A realistic goal is for some ferrets to survive the first month and at least 10% to survive winter. The draft biological opinion anticipates incidental take of about 12% of reintroduced ferrets and their offspring.
- **Population goal:** A free-ranging population of at least 30 breeding adults. The EA sets a target of 1998, while the proposed rule sets 2000. The EA projects a wild population within about five years.
- **Criteria for reevaluation or termination** include: fewer than 30 breeding-adult habitat capacity over a long term; failure to obtain the nonessential designation; discovery of wild ferrets before the first breeding season; a canine distemper case within six months before release; fewer than 20 ferrets available for the first release; or lack of funding.
- **Predator and disease management:** At least 10 coyotes (potential distemper carriers) would be removed and tested, and ferrets would be vaccinated against canine distemper once a usable vaccine exists. Predator control is short-term, and no predator management is planned after the release phase.
- **Legal status:** The nonessential experimental designation lets the Service apply special rules. Incidental take is authorized if unintentional and not due to negligence. Knowing take is referred for prosecution. Federal agencies must confer with the Service only on actions likely to jeopardize the population. The designation does not supersede private landowners' rights, and critical habitat would not be designated.
- **Landowner concerns (scoping, May 1990–September 1993):** Concerns included uncertainty over prairie dog control, the source of reintroduction funds, possible reclassification to "essential," restrictions on land use, and prairie dog shooting limits. Most landowners had no objection to ferrets on their land, but did have problems with prairie dogs.
- **Impacts:** The EA finds no significant impacts to threatened or endangered species, livestock grazing, recreation, or other wildlife. The Proposed Action is expected to benefit the ferret and species associated with prairie dog ecosystems (burrowing owl, golden eagle, and others).
- **Consultation and status:** A draft biological opinion concluded the action is not likely to jeopardize the species. A final opinion would accompany the final rule.
- **Outreach:** Public comments are solicited, a public hearing is set for December 12, 1995 (Seligman, Arizona), and the Service contact is Bill Austin. Reports of injured or dead ferrets go to the State Supervisor in Phoenix, (602) 640-2720.
