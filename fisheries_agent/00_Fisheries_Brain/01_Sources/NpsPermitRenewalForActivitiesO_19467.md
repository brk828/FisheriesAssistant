# NPS permit renewal for activities on Lake Mead and Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary
This document set comprises National Park Service (NPS) permit applications and Investigator's Annual Reports submitted by Dr. Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) and Dr. W.L. Minckley (ASU Department of Zoology) covering multi-year (1980–projected 2015) research and management activities targeting two federally significant Colorado River fishes: the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]] within the Lake Mead National Recreation Area. The project, ongoing since August 1967, involves population assessment, broodstock acquisition, augmentation stocking, larval rearing in backwater "grow-out" coves, and fish community monitoring, conducted in cooperation with the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Biological Survey, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and Nevada Department of Wildlife under the auspices of the Native Fish Work Group.

## Background & Description
The source material is administrative/permit-based rather than a morphological study, and does not provide detailed anatomical descriptions. However, the following taxon-specific notes are implicit in the collection and tagging protocols:

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*, family [[Catostomidae]]): Adults are large-bodied enough to be captured and fitted with PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tags, indicating a body size sufficient for internal tag implantation (typically >300 mm total length in this species). Larval razorback suckers were also targeted for study of nutritional requirements, implying a distinct and vulnerable early life-history stage.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*, family [[Cyprinidae]]): Also large enough for adult capture and PIT tagging; treated as broodstock source, indicating reproductively mature adults were targeted.
- **[[Striped Bass]]** (*Morone saxatilis*, non-native, family Moronidae): Noted as an expanding population in Lake Mohave; no morphological data given, but its presence is ecologically significant as a potential predator/competitor of native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Both target species inhabit the lentic (reservoir) environments of Lake Mead and Lake Mohave, impoundments of the lower [[Colorado River]]. Backwater coves (e.g., Yuma Cove, Davis Cove) are specifically used as protected grow-out/nursery habitats for larval and juvenile razorback sucker and bonytail chub before release into the open lake.
- **Life History Stages Targeted**: Adult population assessment (via capture-tag-recapture using PIT tags), broodstock collection for captive propagation, wild larval and fertilized ova collection for grow-out, and juvenile release/augmentation stocking.
- **Community Interactions**: The 1992 report explicitly notes an expanding population of non-native [[Striped Bass]] in Lake Mohave, raising concerns about predation/competition pressure on native catostomid and cyprinid populations — a key ecological threat driving the augmentation program.
- **Conservation Status Context**: Both *Xyrauchen texanus* and *Gila elegans* are federally listed endangered species (collection authorized under USFWS permit PRT-676811), reflecting their critically reduced wild populations in the Colorado River system.

## Key Findings
- **Population Assessment (1992 field season)**: Nearly 200 adult razorback suckers were captured and PIT-tagged across March, May, and November field trips; 3 adult bonytail chub were also captured and tagged.
- **Augmentation Success**: Approximately 300 young razorback suckers reared in Yuma Cove were released into Lake Mohave or transferred to Davis Cove, demonstrating feasibility of on-site backwater grow-out rearing for augmentation stocking.
- **Genetic Studies**: Genetic characterization of the Lake Mead fish populations was in process (cooperating with geneticists Thomas E. Dowling, Michael E. Douglas, Philip Hedrick), suggesting concern over genetic diversity/stock identity in remnant populations.
- **Threat Identification**: The non-native [[Striped Bass]] population was noted as expanding in Lake Mohave during the reporting period, a potential predation/competition concern for native fish recovery efforts.
- **Research Continuity**: The project represents one of the longest-running native fish conservation monitoring efforts in the region (est. start 08/1967), with consistent objectives across at least three consecutive annual reports (1992–1994): population assessment, broodstock acquisition, community status assessment, and cove-based rearing/augmentation.
- **Publications/Products**: Referenced ongoing publications on light-trap sampling methodology and odonate (dragonfly nymph) predation on larval fishes, plus a conference presentation (G. Mueller) at a Desert Fishes Council meeting.
- **Specimen Disposition**: Collected specimens were deposited in the ASU Museum Collection of Fishes (voucher specimens) or, in the case of live bonytail, transferred to USFWS facilities — consistent with permit-mandated specimen handling requirements.
- **Interagency Cooperation**: Research was jointly funded and logistically supported by U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Lower Colorado Region), U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and National Biological Survey, in cooperation with Arizona Game and Fish Department and Nevada Department of Wildlife — illustrating a multi-agency native fish recovery framework under the [[Native Fish Work Group]].
