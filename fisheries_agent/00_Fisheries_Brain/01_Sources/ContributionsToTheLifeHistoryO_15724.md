# Contributions to the Life History of the Piute Sculpin, Cottus beldingii Eigenmann and Eigenmann, in Lake Tahoe

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cottidae #benthic-fish #Lake-Tahoe

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive life history study of the [[Piute Sculpin]] (*Cottus beldingii*) in [[Lake Tahoe]] examined 6,424 specimens collected from September 1963 through September 1964. The research focused on diet composition, age and growth patterns, reproductive biology, and parasite loads. Sculpins were collected primarily using otter and sled trawls at depths ranging from 100 to 400 feet, with additional specimens from littoral rotenone treatments. The study revealed that [[Piute Sculpin]] are opportunistic benthic feeders consuming a diverse array of invertebrates and detritus, with diet varying by fish size, season, depth, and location. Age determination via otolith annuli identified five age groups (0-IV), with fish reaching approximately 69 mm by age IV. Spawning occurs primarily in May and June, with females producing an average of 123 eggs. Three parasite species were documented, with infection rates varying by location and depth. As a key forage species for [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) and other piscivores, the [[Piute Sculpin]] plays a critical role in the [[Lake Tahoe]] food web by converting benthic invertebrates and detritus into biomass available to game fishes.

## Background & Description

The [[Piute Sculpin]] (*Cottus beldingii*) is a small benthic fish inhabiting [[Lake Tahoe]], a large oligotrophic lake straddling the California-Nevada border. This study represents the first published comprehensive life history investigation of this species, though three unpublished theses (Dietsch 1950, Miller 1951, Jones 1954) previously contributed knowledge about the species. An unpublished manuscript by Phillip H. Baker describes distribution, size composition, and relative abundance.

[[Lake Tahoe]] is characterized by extreme depth (maximum ~1,600 ft), cold temperatures, and oligotrophic conditions. The lake supports extensive deepwater plant beds (Chara, mosses, filamentous algae) at depths from 200 to 350 feet. The [[Piute Sculpin]] is closely associated with the substrate throughout its life cycle and occurs from shallow littoral zones to depths exceeding 700 feet.

Physical characteristics: Total length ranges from approximately 22 mm (young-of-year) to 127 mm (maximum observed). Sexual dimorphism becomes apparent at approximately 65 mm total length (age II), with mature males developing broader, heavier bodies than the more streamlined females. The species lacks scales, requiring age determination via otolith or vertebrae examination or length-frequency analysis.

The length-weight relationship follows the equation: log W = -5.244 + 3.166 log L (where W = weight in grams, L = total length in mm). Mean coefficient of condition was 1.05 for males (n=417) and 0.99 for females (n=382), with condition varying seasonally and with body size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Piute Sculpin]] occupies a critical ecological niche in [[Lake Tahoe]] as the primary forage fish for piscivorous game species. Stomach content analysis of predators revealed sculpins in approximately 30% of [[Lake Trout]] stomachs containing food, with particularly high importance (56% by volume) for lake trout in the 5.0-9.9 inch size class. Sculpins also occurred in 37.9% of [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) stomachs but only 1.6% of [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) stomachs.

**Habitat utilization**: Sculpins were collected across a broad depth gradient. Approximately 89% of study specimens came from depths of 100-400 feet via bottom trawls, with the remainder from rubble areas in the littoral zone. One specimen was captured at 700 feet depth. The species shows strong association with vegetated flats at intermediate depths (200-350 ft) where aquatic plant beds reach maximum density.

**Spawning habitat**: Nesting occurs in wave-swept littoral zones at depths of 30-40 inches. Nests consist of cave-like apertures under rocks free from the substrate, with floors composed of gravel and sand. Eggs are attached in clusters to nest ceilings. Miller (1951) documented 18 nests in a limited area at the south end of the lake. Additional spawning areas occur at the mouths of [[Griff Creek]] and [[Tunnel Creek]].

**Food web position**: As omnivorous benthic feeders, sculpins convert diverse bottom-dwelling invertebrates and detritus into biomass available to piscivorous fishes. The most common food items by frequency of occurrence were ostracods (50% of stomachs), green filamentous algae (40-45%), chironomid larvae (35%), plecopterans (32-38%), amphipods (27-28%), and cladocerans (16-26%). Nearly 50% of total stomach volume consisted of detritus, suggesting an important role in energy transfer from organic bottom material.

**Invertebrate prey**: The deepwater amphipod *Stygobromus* comprised approximately 7% of total stomach volume and was numerically abundant in stomachs containing them. The ostracod *Candona tahoensis* (described by Ferguson 1966) was the only free-living ostracod species found in bottom fauna collections and occurred most frequently in sculpin diets. Apterous plecopterans of the genus *Capnia* were common prey items.

## Key Findings

**Diet and Feeding Ecology**:
- Analysis of 851 stomachs revealed highly diverse, opportunistic feeding behavior
- Most common foods by frequency: ostracods (50%), green filamentous algae (43-45%), chironomid larvae (35%), plecopterans (32-38%), amphipods (*Stygobromus* 27-28%), cladocerans (16-26%)
- Detritus comprised 45-59% of total stomach volume across all analyses, suggesting important role in benthic energy transfer
- Diet varied significantly with depth: cladocerans decreased with depth; *Stygobromus* and oligochaetes increased with depth; ostracods, plecopterans, moss capsules, and algae peaked at intermediate depths (200-300 ft) corresponding to deepwater plant bed distribution
- Seasonal variation: lowest food consumption in fall/winter, highest in spring/summer; ostracods, chironomids, plecopterans, amphipods, cladocerans, oligochaetes, and moss capsules all peaked in summer
- Size-related differences: larger fish consumed greater variety (12-14 items vs. 8 items in fish <40 mm); ostracods dominated diet of smallest fish; larger prey items (*Stygobromus*, gastropods) more important in larger fish
- Geographic differences: south end sculpins showed higher frequency of cladocerans, gastropods, and *Hyallela*; north end fish consumed more moss capsules and oligochaetes
- Cannibalism rare: only occasional sculpin remains found, comprising 4.8-5.4% of total volume when present
- No fish eggs found in any stomachs examined

**Age and Growth**:
- Five age groups identified (0, I, II, III, IV) based on otolith annuli examination of 92 fish (November-December 1963) and 61 fish (April-June 1964)
- Calculated mean total lengths at end of each year: 33.3 mm (age I), 48.9 mm (age II), 64.7 mm (age III), 69.0 mm (age IV)
- Body length-otolith radius relationship: L = 13.40 + 1.55R (linear regression)
- Lee's phenomenon observed: progressive decrease in back-calculated lengths in successively older fish
- Annulus formation occurs early in spring
- Most annual growth occurs in spring and early summer: age 0 fish grew from 35.5 mm (Nov-Dec) to 48.4 mm (Apr-Jun); age I from 53.7 to 65.1 mm; age II from 67.6 to 75.5 mm
- Growth rates similar to [[Sagehen Creek]] populations, though Tahoe fish slightly larger at most ages
- Length-frequency analysis inadequate for aging individual specimens except age 0 fish due to wide length ranges within age groups

**Length-Weight and Condition**:
- Length-weight relationship: log W = -5.244 + 3.166 log L
- Mean coefficient of condition: 1.05 for males, 0.99 for females (difference not statistically significant overall)
- Condition varies directly with body length in both sexes, but relationship differs significantly between sexes (P<0.01)
- Males: K = 0.650 + 0.0063X; Females: K = 0.804 + 0.0031X (where X = length midpoint)
- Sexual dimorphism in body form becomes significant at ~65 mm (age II): males become broader and heavier-bodied
- Seasonal fluctuation in condition: females lowest in July, highest April-June (related to gonadal development); males lowest late summer through early winter, highest March-July (possibly related to food supply rather than reproduction)
- No significant differences in condition between north and south shore populations

**Reproduction**:
- Spawning period: primarily May and June based on gonosomatic ratios, egg diameters, and field observations
- Mean gonosomatic index peaked at >13% for females in April-June; only 0.86% for males in April
- Mean egg diameter increased from 0.397 mm (September minimum) to 2.104 mm (May maximum), then dropped to 1.503 mm in June
- Mean fecundity: 123.4 eggs per female (range 11-387 eggs)
- Highest monthly mean fecundity: 217.3 eggs in December; lowest 76 eggs in September
- Minimum fecundity: 11 eggs in 67 mm female; maximum 387 eggs in 92 mm female
- Sexual maturity: females >50 mm contained mature-sized eggs; all age I females (with one exception) had eggs too small to ripen that year, indicating spawning begins in second year of life
- Sex ratio: 417 males to 382 females overall (not significantly different from 1:1)
- Nesting behavior: eggs attached to ceiling of cave-like apertures under rocks in wave-swept littoral zone at 30-40 inch depths
- Nests found from May 7 to July 4 (Miller 1951); one nest observed June 21, 1965 in present study
- Spawning areas documented at south end near [[Taylor Creek]], at [[Sugar Pine Point]], and at mouths of [[Griff Creek]] and [[Tunnel Creek]]

**Parasitology**:
- Three parasite species documented in 851 fish examined:
  1. *Plistophora* sp. (Cnidosporidia: Microsporida): white spindle-shaped cysts (0.4-0.9 mm) in abdominal wall; 6.5% overall infection rate; similar intensity at north (7.5%) and south (5.7%) shores; absent from west shore/Emerald Bay samples
  2. *Ligula* sp. (Cestoidea: Diphyllobothridae): large plerocercoid larvae in abdominal cavity, coiled around digestive tract, length 2/3 to 3/4 host length; 1.2% overall infection; marked difference by area: 12.0% in shallow water (west shore/Emerald Bay) vs. ~1% in deep water collections (north and south shores); higher shallow-water infection possibly related to fish-eating bird presence
  3. Strigeoid trematode metacercaria: small cysts in liver; 10.6% overall infection; dramatic geographic difference: 24.0% at south shore vs. 0.2% at north shore; higher south shore infection possibly related to extensive marshes at mouths of [[Upper Truckee River]] and [[Taylor Creek]]
- Infection patterns suggest depth and habitat differences influence parasite loads

**Ecological Significance**:
- [[Piute Sculpin]] identified as key forage species in [[Lake Tahoe]] food web
- Occurred in ~30% of [[Lake Trout]] stomachs containing food across all lake trout sizes >5.0 inches FL
- Particularly important for small lake trout: 56.0% of stomach volume in 5.0-9.9 inch fish, declining to 4.8% in fish ≥20 inches
- Also important for [[Brown Trout]] (37.9% frequency) but not [[Rainbow Trout]] (1.6% frequency)
- Widespread distribution and abundance throughout lake (Phillip H. Baker, MS)
- Omnivorous feeding converts diverse benthic invertebrates and detritus into biomass available to piscivorous game fishes
- Critical role supporting [[Lake Tahoe]] sport fishery, which depends primarily on [[Lake Trout]]

**Comparative Notes**:
- Diet differs markedly from [[Sagehen Creek]] populations: lake fish consume more ostracods, gastropods, amphipods, plant material; creek fish consume more immature insects
- Diet also differs from Miller's (1951) Lake Tahoe study: present study found more plant remains, ostracods, gastropods, amphipods, greater diversity, presence of plecopterans, oligochaetes, pelecypods, sculpins, copepods, fewer empty stomachs; Miller reported trichopterans, fish eggs, branchipods not found in present study
- Differences attributed to depth of capture: Miller's specimens primarily from shallow rubble areas; present study primarily from 100-400 ft vegetated flats
- Growth rates similar to [[Sagehen Creek]] populations (Jones 1954, Dietsch 1950), with Tahoe fish generally slightly larger at given ages
