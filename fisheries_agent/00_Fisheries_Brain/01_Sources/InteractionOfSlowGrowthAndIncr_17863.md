# Interaction of Slow Growth and Increased Early-Life Mortality: Decline of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study proposes that the decline of the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the principal native piscivore of the [[Colorado River Basin]], is driven in large part by the interaction of slow first-year growth and elevated early-life mortality in the upper basin. The authors link a growth-versus-temperature relation from [[Black and Bulkley Growth Studies]] to river temperature regimes, show a strong positive relation between temperature suitability and age-0 fish length, and use population simulations to show that slow-growing stocks are far more vulnerable to early-life mortality. The authors recommend water management to enhance growth temperatures and control of introduced predatory fishes.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish was once widespread and abundant in large rivers and major tributaries of the Colorado River basin. It now occurs naturally only in the upstream portion of its historic range and is threatened with extinction. Its decline is generally attributed to water-resources development, introduced non-native fishes, and poor land-use practices, but the factors limiting remaining populations were less clearly understood.

Piscivores often show rapid early-life growth, which reduces the period when fish are vulnerable to predation and allows a wider prey range. Examples cited are [[Northern Pike]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Colorado squawfish lack this rapid early growth in the upper basin. In a pond study by Osmundson (1987), fish stocked at 50–75 mm grew to an average of 226 mm within one year (largest 304 mm), indicating that growth in upper-basin rivers is suboptimal rather than intrinsically slow.

Growth in fishes depends largely on the interaction of water temperature and food availability. Laboratory work by Black and Bulkley found growth optimal at 25 °C, with growth at 15, 20 and 30 °C equal to 18%, 54% and 51% of optimum. Growth appeared to cease below about 13 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature regimes:** Using USGS records, the authors compared the Green River (near Green River, Utah) and the upper Colorado River (near the Colorado–Utah border) with historic data from the lower Colorado River near Yuma, Arizona (1917–1924) and the middle Colorado River near Grand Canyon, Arizona. Growing seasons were under 6 months in upper-basin reaches, versus 7–9 months in historic reaches.
- **Suitability indices:** Relative suitability for growth was estimated at 3.2 (Green River), 2.4 (upper Colorado), 3.9 (middle Colorado) and 4.9 (lower Colorado). A pond regime had a suitability of 3.6.
- **Climate correction:** Historic and present temperature records were not concurrent. Climate records showed historic lower-river years averaged 0.8 °C cooler than the long-term mean, while historic middle-river years averaged 0.8 °C warmer, so the comparisons carry uncertainty.
- **Dams:** Dam operation was found not to have reduced temperatures in most Colorado and Green river reaches still inhabited by the species, which the authors argue implies that other factors drove the decline there.
- **Introduced fishes:** Channel catfish, green sunfish and largemouth bass have colonized occupied reaches. [[Green Sunfish]] can strongly suppress native cyprinids.
- **Spawning:** Spawning begins at 20–22 °C, normally in July or August in the upper basin, with embryos hatching 4–5 days later. Historically in the lower river, spawning temperatures were reached in early May.

## Key Findings

1. **Growth-temperature suitability predicts early growth.** Mean total length of fall age-0 fish from the Green and Colorado rivers correlated strongly and positively with the suitability index (r = 0.95, p < 0.01; R² = 0.91, meaning 91% of length variation was explained by suitability).
2. **Slow growth amplifies mortality effects.** Simulations starting from 1000 female larvae showed that the advantage of fast-growing stocks increased as early-life mortality rose. At 99% early-life mortality, the production of mature females was 1200% higher in the fast-growing stock (Table 1). The fast-growing stock also had a much higher finite rate of increase (f = 1.837 versus 1.121 for slow-growing stock).
3. **Present populations likely cannot sustain losses.** The net reproductive rate of the upper-basin population is estimated at no more than 1, which the authors describe as worse than even the simulated slow-growing stock.
4. **Small age-0 size may limit recruitment.** Overwinter survival of young fish is size-dependent, and larger age-0 fish dominate recruitment in related species. The authors suggest that the greater occurrence of large age-0 fish in the Green River may partly explain its larger adult stock.
5. **Historic longitudinal effect.** Warmer historic middle and lower river temperatures likely supported faster growth and a longer season, although food availability and growth rates there were never measured.
6. **Compensation is limited.** Because optimal temperatures are a resource independent of population density, density-dependent compensation (faster growth and higher fecundity among survivors) would not be expected to offset losses.
7. **Behnke and Benson hypothesis questioned.** The authors judge the hypothesis that replacement of native prey by smaller introduced fishes reduced food and growth to be less plausible, since prey of varied sizes remain abundant.
8. **Management recommendations:**
   - Operate dams and reservoirs to lengthen the growing season and increase suitable temperatures, while cautiously weighing benefits to undesirable species.
   - Avoid water development that reduces temperatures.
   - Test the relation between age-0 size and overwinter survival.
   - Prevent further introductions of non-native fishes, and identify which non-natives most harm the squawfish, since complete removal is impracticable in the Colorado River.
