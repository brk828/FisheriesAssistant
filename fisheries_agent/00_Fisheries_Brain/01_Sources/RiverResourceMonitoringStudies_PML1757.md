# River Resource Monitoring Studies, Grand Canyon: Humpback, Colorado and Bonytail Chubs (Gila)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Part II, April 1980) from the [[Grand Canyon National Park]] River Resource Monitoring Project covers three endangered or rare [[Gila]] chubs in the Colorado River system: the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), the [[Colorado Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The Colorado and bonytail chubs are considered extirpated or exceedingly rare within the canyon. The humpback chub is the focus of the study, with data on distribution, food, reproduction, age and growth, relative density, parasitism and the effects of dam operations, all collected largely during 1977–1979. The report concludes that dam-regulated flows, cold tailwaters and daily water fluctuations are the main threats, and that the species' survival in Arizona depends on continued management effort.

## Background & Description

The genus *Gila* is widespread in the American Southwest, represented in Arizona by seven species and four subspecies. Three Gila chubs were recorded from the Grand Canyon region:

- **Colorado Chub (*G. robusta*)**: First collected from [[Glen Canyon]] in 1950, before impoundment. Later collections by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] came from the Glen Canyon damsite (1961–1963) and below Glen Canyon Dam (1966). Few records exist, so the species is considered extirpated or certainly exceedingly rare in the park. It is relatively common elsewhere in Arizona and is listed in Group IV of the state's Threatened and Unique Wildlife list.
- **Bonytail Chub (*G. elegans*)**: Once occurred throughout the mainstream Colorado River. Grand Canyon records are few: a fossilized-era specimen from Stanton's Cave (about 4,000 years old, approximately 30 miles below Lee Ferry), angler captures at Bright Angel and Phantom creeks (1942, 1944), a report from the base of Grand Falls, and one fish near Spencer Creek (1955). The species persists in [[Lake Mohave]] and is proposed for endangered status by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. It is listed in Group II of Arizona's list.
- **Humpback Chub (*G. cypha*)**: Endemic to the Colorado River Basin and first described from the Grand Canyon by [[Miller (1946)]]. It was not reported before the 1940s, probably because its canyon habitat was inaccessible to early researchers. The [[Humpback Chub Recovery Plan]] (1979) notes that populations may have been lost to human alteration in the Lower Basin, such as near [[Hoover Dam]], before their existence was known.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical and current distribution.** Before the 1950s, the humpback chub was known mainly from the Grand Canyon. Surveys in the 1950s and 1960s found substantial numbers in the upper Green River and in the Colorado River above and below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Historically it likely occupied larger swift-water canyons of the Colorado and Green rivers above Lake Mead, plus the Yampa and White rivers. Its abundance was usually limited, though locally common in one or two spots.

Major population changes include:
- Elimination from the Green River above the mouth of the Yampa River after [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] became operational in 1962.
- Collapse in [[Lake Powell]] after closure in the 1960s; fish were common in early samples but absent in recent years.
- Major reductions in [[Marble Canyon]] and Grand Canyon, attributed to cold tailwaters from Glen Canyon Dam.

Present distribution in the canyon ranges from River Mile 27 to River Mile 108, with a concentration in and near the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]]. A downstream extension to River Mile 194 (86 miles beyond the earlier record) was documented during 1977–1978 surveys (Table 1). Distribution across all areas is sporadic.

**Food habits.** Stomach contents from three juvenile fish that died during density studies showed that the main food items were benthic insect larvae and organic detritus, indicating foraging on or near the substrate. Identified taxa included midge larvae (Chironomidae), a biting midge larva (Ceratopogonidae) and a long-legged fly larva (Dolichopodidae). Sample size was very small, so the authors describe the findings as cursory.

**Reproduction.** Spawning was not observed. Its timing and habitat were inferred from related species (bonytail and Colorado chub), which spawn at about 18 °C. Spawning in the humpback chub is thought to occur in March and extend through June and probably July, based on collections of ripe adults in March and April and young-of-the-year fish in June and July. Water temperatures at collection times ranged from about 16 °C to 20 °C (value reported in OCR-affected text). The Little Colorado River substrate is a fine, compacted mud, unlikely for spawning. Young-of-the-year were found over gravel-cobble at Shinumo Creek, and mainstream reproduction was suspected to occur sporadically.

**Parasitism.** The most common parasite of native fishes in the canyon was the external copepod *Lernaea cyprinaceae*. It was first recorded in October 1978 on humpback chubs from the Little Colorado River, with a very high infestation rate. The rate decreased markedly over the next year but persisted through October 1979. The Little Colorado River is an important nursery and the main known spawning area of the species in the lower Colorado system, so infestation there is a serious concern. The parasite's introduction route is uncertain; possibilities include Kanab Creek, the Little Colorado River, [[Lake Mead]] (via carp) and [[Lee Ferry]] (via infested hatchery trout). Its thermophilic nature would restrict reproduction to warm habitats such as the Little Colorado River and Kanab Creek.

**Effects of dams and water fluctuation.** Large mainstream dams have altered flow regimes and water temperatures, destroying backwater areas, blocking spawning runs and keeping temperatures below spawning requirements. Daily hydroelectric fluctuations, with peak releases in summer, have had a major impact on tributary fauna. For example, the Paria River confluence changes from about 35 m wide and 2 m deep at high water to a mud flat with a 1 m wide, 4 cm deep channel at low water (estimated). Such fluctuations can desiccate spawn, expose shoreline cover, increase predation on juveniles and push young fish into colder, faster mainstream water. Carothers and Minckley (1980) and Minckley (1978) also argue that flow levels control access of spawners to tributaries such as [[Bright Angel Creek]]. The authors state that dam construction on the Little Colorado River would be devastating and lead to the species' extinction.

## Key Findings

**Status**
- The Colorado Chub is considered extirpated or exceedingly rare in Grand Canyon National Park.
- The Bonytail Chub is considered extinct or exceedingly rare in the canyon, persisting in Lake Mohave.
- The Humpback Chub persists at low levels in the canyon and is listed as endangered; its continued existence depends on agency management.

**Distribution**
- Humpback chub distribution spans River Mile 27 to River Mile 108 with a concentration near the Little Colorado River mouth, and one record extending to River Mile 194.
- Mainstream captures were rare downstream of the Little Colorado River confluence. Localized concentrations occurred near Tiger Wash (River Mile 27.5).

**Age and growth (opercle method, n = 10 mortalities, four age classes)**
- Opercle radius correlated with total length (r = 0.956, p < 0.01), indicating isometric growth; the Dahl-Lea back-calculation method was considered valid.
- Mean first-year growth was about 86.0 mm total length; second-year growth about 39 mm; growth fell steadily to 18.6 mm in the ninth year.
- The largest fish analyzed was nine years old and about 350 mm total length.

**Relative density (catch per unit effort)**
- Mainstream catches ranged from a low of 0.5 fish/h in winter.
- In the Little Colorado River, gill nets averaged 35.2 fish/h and standard trammel nets up to 70 fish/h. Drift nets gave 5.3 fish/h, angling 4.3 fish/h, and electrofishing was largely unsuccessful.
- Seining estimates were 71 fish/ha in spring, 2,473 fish/ha in summer (0.1 km upstream), 990 fish/ha in fall, and 2,300–6,364 fish/ha at 8 km upstream in summer.

**Threats and management**
- Lake Powell's cold tailwaters and daily hydroelectric fluctuations are major causes of decline.
- Parasitism by *Lernaea cyprinaceae* on juveniles in the Little Colorado River poses a serious new threat to a critical nursery area.
- Future dam construction or large fluctuations in the Little Colorado River confluence would threaten spawning, eggs, fry and nursery areas.
- The authors state that the extinction of the humpback chub within Arizona is unacceptable, and that the impacts of ongoing power and water demands can be reduced through active management.

**Limitations**
- Sample sizes are small for growth and diet analyses, and the authors caution against rigorous statistical inference.
- Spawning was not directly observed, and reproductive timing is inferred from related species and field collections.
