# San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area Monitoring Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecology #conservation-management #fisheries-monitoring

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive monitoring plan establishes protocols for the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] (RNCA), established by Public Law 100-696 (Arizona-Idaho Conservation Act of 1988). The 56,430-acre conservation area protects one of the most extensive remaining broadleaf riparian ecosystems in the desert Southwest. The plan encompasses hydrological monitoring, vegetation assessment, fisheries surveys, reptile and amphibian inventories, avian censuses, mammal monitoring, and cultural resource documentation. The monitoring framework aims to track changes in riparian quality and ensure consistency with the primary conservation purposes: protecting and enhancing the riparian ecosystem and its associated aquatic, wildlife, archaeological, paleontological, scientific, cultural, educational, and recreational resources. A fifteen-year moratorium on livestock grazing was instituted to allow ecosystem recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[San Pedro River]] flows year-round through the conservation area, a rare occurrence in the modern Southwest. The cottonwood/willow gallery forest and associated upland habitats harbor over 350 bird species, including 25 species of diurnal raptors. The area supports approximately 30% of nesting [[Gray Hawk]] populations in the United States. Riparian obligate species include [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], vermillion flycatcher, common yellowthroat, yellow-breasted chat, and yellow-rumped warbler. Over 82 mammal species have been recorded, though larger species like Mexican gray wolf and grizzly bear have been extirpated. Approximately 24 bat species occur, including the endangered [[Sanborn's Long-nosed Bat]].

Forty-three reptile, turtle, and amphibian species inhabit the RNCA, including the [[Mexican Garter Snake]], a wetland obligate reptile and candidate for Arizona's Threatened Species List. Twelve fish species utilize aquatic habitats; only two native species persist: [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]]. These provide important prey for herons, egrets, kingfishers, and other piscivorous species.

The area contains over 120 prehistoric and historic sites and nine fossil sites, including the famous Clovis sites at [[Murray Springs]] and [[Lehner Ranch]] (dating to 11,000 years ago). The Lehner Mammoth Kill Site was designated a National Landmark in 1967. At the northern end, a rare cienega represents remnant marsh habitat formerly widespread in southern Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The upper [[San Pedro River]] watershed encompasses multiple vegetation associations classified using Brown et al. (1979) system: cottonwood/willow association (224.531), Chihuahua mixed scrub (153.2), mesquite bosque (223.231), mesquite-sacaton (143.142), tobosa grassland (143.12), mixed grass-scrub (143.155), creosote-tarbush (153.213), and giant sacaton (143.141). The riparian corridor's health depends critically on maintaining surface flows and protecting the alluvial aquifer from groundwater withdrawal for irrigation.

Hydrological monitoring focuses on three aquifer systems: the floodplain/alluvial aquifer (younger alluvium), basin fill (regional aquifer/older alluvium), and their interconnections. The Sierra Vista cone of depression poses a potential threat to baseflow. Seven stream gaging sites were established during initial BLM assessment, supplemented by USGS stations at Palominas (#094705) and Charleston (#094710). Precipitation monitoring utilizes five BLM clear plastic wedge gauges and a recording gauge at Fairbank with 10-day chart capacity.

Stream habitat classification follows McCain et al. (1988) methodology, delineating 22 discrete habitat types within three main categories: riffles (differentiated by gradient), pools (differentiated by position and scour cause), and flat water. Subhabitat types include high gradient riffle, cascade, low gradient riffle, secondary channel pool, various backwater formations, trench pool, edgewater, plunge pool, lateral scour pools, dammed pool, pocketwater, glides, runs, step runs, corner pools, main channel pools, and confluence pools.

## Key Findings

**Monitoring Framework Structure:**

The plan establishes integrated monitoring across seven major resource categories:

1. **Hydrological Monitoring** includes: complete inventory of water sources with GIS database; watershed condition assessment via photogrammetric techniques; precipitation monitoring at multiple stations; bi-weekly flow measurements at seven stream gaging sites; weekly groundwater monitoring in riparian zone wells (2-5 years to establish patterns, then 3-4 times yearly); basin fill aquifer monitoring coordinated with USGS, ADWR, and City of Sierra Vista; water quality sampling combining baseline, cause-and-effect, and compliance efforts; sediment sampling coordinated with USGS geomorphological study; and geomorphological analysis using historical aerial photography.

2. **Vegetation Monitoring** protocols: 46 photo points (61 aspect views) established in 1987, primarily in riparian zones, to be rephotographed every 2 years (cottonwood/willow) or 3 years (other associations) for first cycle; historic photo point investigation and rephotography; 43 pace frequency transects (200 plots each, 40cm x 40cm frames) in upland habitats, repeated every 3 years for first 9-year cycle; combined point intercept/line intercept transects perpendicular to stream channel (100 feet each side), repeated yearly for first 3 years then every third year; color infrared photography at 1:6000 scale flown in April/May 1990, 1995, then every 10 years; and 15 natural color aerial photography transects from helicopter at 350 and 500 feet, repeated at 3-year intervals for first 9 years.

3. **Fisheries Monitoring** employs: October fish surveys using depletion sampling with consecutive seine hauls (15-foot seine) at two key locations containing multiple subhabitats (run, riffle, pool, connected backwater, isolated backwater); population estimates calculated using two-step removal formula; stream habitat classification and quantification from international boundary north to Fairbank, measuring lengths, widths, depths of 22 habitat types; and correlation of fish populations to flow regimes, precipitation, flood frequency, water quality parameters.

4. **Reptile and Amphibian Monitoring** utilizes: twelve pit-fall arrays (four 18.9-liter buckets per array with three 25-foot aluminum drift barriers at 120° angles) installed across eight vegetation types; daily checks from mid-May to mid-September; calculation of Shannon diversity index (H') and relative density (individuals per trap night); and correlation of changes to foliage height diversity (FHD) and ground cover measurements.

5. **Avian Monitoring** includes: summer raptor surveys mapping all known raptor nesting sites, with intensive focus on [[Gray Hawk]] territories during last week of June and first week of July to determine nesting success and fledgling numbers; and 18 permanent Emlen strip-transect censuses measuring foliage height diversity, patchiness, and tree counts per 40 hectares, with bird inventories three times per month across four vegetation types (cottonwood-willow, mesquite, mesquite-grassland, upland), calculating densities as individuals per 40 hectares.

6. **Mammal Monitoring** encompasses: small mammal sampling at 87 sites using snap trap grids (two parallel lines 50 feet apart, 15 stations per line, two mouse traps and one rat trap per station, plus 10 wire live traps) for three consecutive nights, expressing relative densities as catch per 300 trap nights; desert shrews captured in reptile pit-fall arrays; FHD measurements and tree counts on three-year cycle; game mammal monitoring via cooperative post-hunt count transects with Arizona Game and Fish Department for mule deer, javelina, white-tailed deer; and mountain lion surveys using track comparison method coordinated with AGFD.

7. **Cultural and Paleontological Resources** monitoring: monthly checks of all known cultural sites to document vandalism or unauthorized artifact collection; enforcement of Antiquities Act and Archaeological Resource Protection Act (PL96-95) as top priority; and periodic checks of high-potential paleontological sites for exposed fossils.

**Management Actions and Priorities:**

Water rights research, updating, and perfection for all sources is crucial to protect the riverine ecosystem. Administrative consumptive uses will be quantified including fire suppression requirements. Irrigation wells will be pumped only to protect potential water rights if the San Pedro Watershed is designated an Active Management Area, or on emergency basis for imperiled resources (such as fish populations in drought). Unnecessary wells will be closed and capped.

Watershed rehabilitation will target areas with apparent problems identified via aerial photography and field inspection. Preliminary cost-benefit estimates will determine expenditure priorities. Dikes or berms adjacent to abandoned farm fields will be removed to reestablish natural drainage patterns. Erosion control structures will be built in side drainages to protect resource values.

Wildlife management includes preparation of Habitat Management Plan; special management guidance for important raptor and waterfowl areas; evaluation of hunting impacts in cooperation with AGFD; minimization of human disturbance around important wildlife areas; experimental planting of one abandoned farm field with preferred forage species; prescribed fire to improve terrestrial habitat; development of ponds and marshes for aquatic and wetland-obligate wildlife; planting of native trees along riparian corridor; provision of wildlife waters in xeric upland areas; and evaluation of exotic fish removal from existing ponds.

**Data Management and Analysis:**

All inventory data will be resident on Geographic Information System (GIS) with microcomputer terminal at San Pedro Project Office for data entry and manipulation, backed up on floppy disks. Statistical analyses will test for significant differences between initial inventory and subsequent monitoring efforts using ANOVA and linear regression. Correlations will be explored between: vegetation profile board obscuration index and animal abundance indices; plant species diversity and animal species diversity; canopy cover changes and white-tailed deer abundance; FHD changes and reptile relative densities; precipitation/climatological data and all biological parameters.

**Monitoring Cycles and Adaptive Management:**

The plan establishes variable monitoring frequencies based on parameter sensitivity and funding levels. Maximum effort involves resampling all sites; minimum effort focuses on key sites showing greatest change, with at least one site per vegetation association. Negative changes in baseline parameters outside normal fluctuations may signal riparian quality decline, triggering investigation of causes and potential remedial action. The monitoring framework allows evaluation of recreational development impacts on riparian quality and effectiveness of mitigation measures.

Visitor use monitoring will be implemented at all developed facilities using sign-in forms in weather-proof boxes to voluntarily collect information on party size, visit purpose, and comments. This index of visitor use may be correlated to changes in bird species diversity, raptor nesting success, and other animal abundance indices to assess recreation impacts.

The comprehensive five-year avian inventory (ending April 1991) will be followed by spot-check summer monitoring using the same methods to determine population changes. An ending report will document five continuous years of data collection, with management decisions based on bird species richness per habitat type, sensitivity of certain species, and population status changes as vegetation changes. College-level or higher studies on [[Gray Hawk]] population dynamics, riparian obligates, and bird population responses to vegetative changes will be encouraged.
