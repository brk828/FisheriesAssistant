# Radio Telemetry Monitoring of Stocked Gila elegans in the Green River, Utah - 1988 Annual Report

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #telemetry

## Executive Summary

This 1988 annual report documents the first year of a two-year radio telemetry study monitoring hatchery-reared [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) released into the [[Green River]], Utah within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The project aimed to assess dispersal, behavior, habitat use, survival, and movement patterns of reintroduced fish to inform recovery efforts for this endangered cyprinid. A total of 39 seven-year-old bonytails from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] were implanted with radio transmitters and released on four occasions (March, May, June, July 1988) at two primary sites: Island Park (RM 213.8-212.9) and Chew Bridge (RM 196.4). Survival was extremely poor, with only four fish (10.3%) outliving their transmitters. The study investigated various pre-release procedures including transport methods, holding times, and surgical recovery periods to identify factors contributing to high mortality. Extended tracking sessions revealed nocturnal activity patterns and habitat preferences for runs and eddies in low-flow areas near main currents.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) is an endangered cyprinid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. This project represents a critical component of the Bonytail Recovery Plan, initiated in February 1988 to evaluate reintroduction strategies. All fish used were seven-year-old adults reared in earthen ponds at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico. Fish averaged 419.5 mm total length and 587 g weight across all releases (n=39, SD=23.4 mm and 103.3 g respectively).

Two types of radio transmitters were employed: cylindrical external antenna units (10g, 55mm x 12mm, Advanced Telemetry Systems, 40.600-40.650 MHz) and internal antenna padlock-shaped units (7g, AVM company). Surgical implantation followed established protocols with fish anesthetized in MS-222 solution (8 mg/l). All individuals received large orange [[Carlin Tag]]s. The shielded-needle technique was used for external antenna implantation with modifications: incisions were made slightly lateral to mid-ventral line, and antenna exit-holes were not sutured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Release timing corresponded with varying hydrological and thermal conditions in the [[Green River]]. The March 22 release (n=10) occurred during rising flows and declining temperatures (water temperature dropped from 12.5°C to 4°C overnight). May 18 release (n=4) preceded the major hydrographic peak of 14,100 CFS recorded at the Jensen, Utah USGS gage. June 18 release (n=10) occurred during high flows (10,200-11,200 CFS). July 28-29 releases (n=15 total) took place during declining flows and warmer temperatures (18-24°C).

Habitat preference analysis (174 daytime contacts from May-July releases, 27 fish) revealed bonytails primarily utilized main channel (102 contacts) and side channel habitats. Secondary habitat types most frequently used were runs and eddies, with fish often positioned on the "eddy-fence" between these habitat types. Specific habitat measurements (n=31 daytime observations) indicated fish selected low-flow areas (mean bottom velocity 0.32 ft/sec, 0.2 depth velocity 0.97 ft/sec, 0.6 depth velocity 0.83 ft/sec) in close association with main currents. Nocturnal locations (n=7) were slightly shallower and further removed from main currents.

The study area extended from the lower terminus of [[Whirlpool Canyon]] downstream to Jensen Bridge (RM 181.9), encompassing [[Rainbow Park]], [[Split Mountain Canyon]], and various backwater and side channel complexes.

## Key Findings

**Survival and Mortality**: Survival was catastrophically low. Of 39 released fish, only 4 (10.3%) survived beyond transmitter life (108 days maximum). The March release experienced complete mortality within 2 days, attributed to thermal shock (temperature drop from 12.5°C to 4°C) and inadequate pre-release procedures. Fish that died averaged 12.2 days post-release survival. Thirty-three fish were tracked to ultimate (death) locations; two transmitters malfunctioned immediately post-release.

**Pre-Release Procedures**: The study systematically tested multiple pre-release strategies to identify optimal protocols:
- March: immediate implantation and release following air transport resulted in 100% mortality
- May: 4-day holding at [[Ouray Wildlife Refuge]] prior to implant, immediate release; 50% survival
- June: three treatment groups with varying hold times (20-36 days pre-implant, 1-14 days post-implant); 0% survival, average 12.9 days
- July (Dexter): fresh transport, immediate implant and release; 10% survival, average 10.8 days
- July (Ouray): 46-76 days holding pre-implant; 40% survival

Results were contradictory, with the two longest-surviving fish from July (Ouray) having been held 46 and 76 days respectively, contrary to initial hypotheses favoring minimal handling.

**Movement Patterns**: Fish that died (n=33, excluding March) were contacted an average of 6.4 times and drifted an average of 26.9 river miles downstream (range: 2.1-74.5 miles). Suspected passive drift averaged 22.2 miles over 12.6 days. Only 6 of 23 fish (May-July releases) displayed upstream movements (0.2-0.9 miles), typically immediately post-release.

The four surviving fish exhibited distinct movement patterns:
- Freq. #635 (May): remained in Island/Rainbow Parks 20 days, relocated 30.8 miles downstream to Brush Creek area, then migrated 11.7 miles upstream over 13 days; last contact at RM 197.2 after 68 days
- Freq. #645 (May): confined to upper Island Park for 20 days, relocated 30 miles downstream; last contact at RM 183.6 after 61 days
- Freq. #598 (July-Ouray): moved gradually 0.4 miles upstream over 61 days to RM 213.3, remained sedentary 47 days; last contact after 108 days
- Freq. #627 (July-Ouray): remained near Chew Bridge release 31 days, relocated 8.6 miles downstream, moved 0.7 miles upstream to final location (RM 188.5) where it remained 56 days; last contact after 108 days

**Activity Patterns**: Extended tracking sessions (n=12, including 24-hour, 12-hour nocturnal, and crepuscular tracks) revealed distinct diel periodicity. Nocturnal hours (1900-0659) showed greater activity: 42% of observations (n=124) recorded movement versus 33% during daytime (0700-1859, n=85). Peak activity occurred during crepuscular and early nighttime hours (sunset to 2300 hrs), presumably related to feeding behavior. Fish typically occupied low-flow habitats near main currents during day, moved into main currents during evening activity periods, then selected shallower, low-flow locations further from current at night, returning to daytime sites at dawn.

Activity declined markedly in autumn as water temperatures dropped below 11-13°C. By November (water temperature 0-9°C), both surviving fish (#598 and #627) became virtually sedentary, attributed to thermal effects on metabolism and feeding cessation.

**Passage Through Split Mountain Canyon**: Of 20 fish released at Island Park (excluding March), 17 passed through the 9-mile [[Split Mountain Canyon]] section. Only 2 fish were confirmed alive below the canyon. This raised questions about whether canyon passage contributed to mortality, though 3 of 4 fish released below the canyon (Chew Bridge) also died, suggesting canyon passage was not the primary mortality factor.

**Pen Study**: An auxiliary study (September 8 - December 6, 1988) held 13 small bonytails in wire mesh pens to investigate mortality factors. Fish were divided into four treatments: dummy transmitter implant (n=3), incision only (n=5), handled (n=3), and control (n=2). Five fish died over 90 days. Key observations:
- Incisions healed slowly in cold water (<10°C) and with fish in starved condition (off feed 3+ months)
- Fish in flowing water developed severe fin and snout abrasions
- Fungal infections occurred secondary to physical trauma and handling stress
- No fish fed on natural foods available in the river
- Transmitters remained properly positioned in body cavity; no migration toward exit holes
- Teflon-coated antennas remained clean while Carlin tags accumulated algal growth

**Transmitter Recovery**: Fourteen transmitters were recovered; four were still in carcasses or associated with remains, four were found above high water lines (indicating scavenger activity). This pattern suggested minimal transmitter expulsion. Three transmitters from March release were found buried in shallow substrate.

**Visual Observations**: Three visual sightings occurred of released fish in atypical, vulnerable habitats (shallow riffles, surface backwaters) shortly before those individuals died, suggesting behavioral abnormalities in stressed fish.

**Recommendations for 1989**: Based on 1988 results, the project proposed: (1) holding fish in Green River pens rather than Ouray Wildlife Refuge to provide acclimation to ambient conditions; (2) minimum one-week holding both pre- and post-surgery; (3) increased sample size from 35 to 59 fish; (4) greater use of internal antenna transmitters; (5) more equal distribution of releases above and below Split Mountain Canyon to further test canyon passage effects.
