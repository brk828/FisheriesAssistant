# Effects of Nutrient Enhancement on Juvenile Sockeye Salmon Growth

## Executive Summary
This study investigated whether artificial nutrient enrichment could enhance the growth of juvenile [[Sockeye Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) — a strategy proposed to aid recovery of the endangered Snake River sockeye population — while evaluating tradeoffs with lake water clarity. Researchers conducted large-scale limnocorral (mesocosm) fertilization experiments in [[Redfish Lake]] and [[Pettit Lake]] in the Sawtooth Valley, Idaho. Because sockeye were endangered, surrogate [[Kokanee Salmon]] (a resident form of *O. nerka*) were used in Redfish Lake, while [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius* sp.) were attempted as surrogates in Pettit Lake but did not survive handling. Results showed that nutrient additions increased chlorophyll *a*, decreased water clarity (Secchi depth), and increased zooplankton egg production (a proxy for reproduction), but did **not** produce a detectable increase in fish growth. Instead, fish growth was strongly and inversely correlated with final fish density within corrals, suggesting that competition/planktivory pressure — not nutrient supply — was the primary limiting factor on growth in this experimental system. The study concludes that a combined strategy of nutrient enhancement *and* competitor (kokanee) reduction may offer the best pathway to support endangered sockeye recovery, and that metalimnetic (versus epilimnetic) nutrient additions may allow productivity gains with less loss of water transparency.

## Background & Description
This paper is primarily physiological/ecological rather than morphometric, but it reports the following biometric data collected on study organisms:

- **Juvenile Kokanee Salmon (*O. nerka*)** — collected shortly after emergence from gravel in Fishhook Creek (a [[Redfish Lake]] inflow):
  - Initial total length and wet/dry weight measured on a subsample prior to stocking (25 fish/corral).
  - Mid-experiment subsample (~13 fish removed 31 July 1993): total length, wet weight (g), preserved for stomach content analysis.
  - Final sampling (13 September 1993): all fish removed via dry ice/SCUBA, measured for total length, wet weight, stomach contents pumped, then frozen for dry weight analysis.
  - Final wet weight varied by treatment (control/low/high nutrient) — see Fig. 6 — but no consistent growth enhancement with nutrient level was found.
  - Growth rate metric used: **g growth / g intermediate weight / day**, inversely related to final fish density (r² = 0.688–0.691).

- **Age-0 [[Redside Shiner]]** (*Richardsonius* sp., surrogate species in Pettit Lake):
  - Initial mass: 0.3–0.5 g; 15 fish stocked per corral.
  - All fish died due to extreme sensitivity to handling stress — data unusable for growth analysis.

- **Zooplankton (*Bosmina*, *Daphnia*)**:
  - Egg-ratio (eggs per adult female) used as a production metric.
  - *Bosmina* egg production highest in high-nutrient treatment, lowest in controls (Redfish).
  - *Daphnia* egg production increased with both epilimnetic and metalimnetic additions (Pettit).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: High-elevation (6,800 ft / ~2,073 m) oligotrophic lakes within the Sawtooth National Recreation Area, central Idaho (44°N, 115°W). Lake morphometry varied: max depth 26 m (Stanley) to 91 m ([[Redfish Lake]]); surface area 73 ha (Yellow Belly) to 615 ha (Redfish). High water transparency (Secchi depths 6–16 m); mean chlorophyll *a* 0.5–2 µg/L; zooplankton biomass 5–80 µg/L.
- **Life history**: Snake River sockeye historically migrated >1,400 km from the Pacific Ocean to five Sawtooth Valley nursery lakes (Redfish, Pettit, Alturas, Stanley, Yellow Belly) which served as both spawning and juvenile rearing habitat.
- **Trophic dynamics**: Juvenile sockeye/kokanee are planktivorous, relying heavily on zooplankton (e.g., *Bosmina*, *Daphnia*) as prey. High fish density suppressed zooplankton biomass regardless of nutrient input, indicating strong top-down (planktivory) control overriding bottom-up (nutrient) effects.
- **Vertical habitat use**: Juvenile sockeye reportedly spend considerable time below the thermocline, supporting relevance of metalimnetic nutrient delivery as an enrichment strategy that avoids surface water clarity loss.
- **Interspecific competition**: [[Kokanee Salmon]] are believed to strongly compete with juvenile sockeye for diet and habitat resources due to high dietary/habitat overlap, positioning competitor reduction as a complementary management lever alongside nutrient enhancement.
- **Endangered species context**: The Snake River sockeye salmon stock was declared endangered in 1991, linked to reduced marine-derived nutrient input from declining numbers of spawning adult carcasses (a self-reinforcing decline mechanism).

## Key Findings
1. **No direct growth benefit from nutrient enrichment**: Despite significant increases in chlorophyll *a* and reduced water clarity, juvenile kokanee salmon growth showed no positive response to low or high nutrient additions in Redfish Lake limnocorrals (Fig. 6).
2. **Density-dependent growth control**: Fish growth rate (g/g/day) was inversely correlated with final fish density per limnocorral (r² = 0.688–0.691; slope y = –0.004x + 0.059), indicating competition for zooplankton prey — not nutrient supply — was growth-limiting.
3. **Water clarity tradeoffs**: 
   - Redfish low-nutrient treatment reduced Secchi depth by 2–3 m relative to controls (likely publicly tolerable).
   - Redfish high-nutrient treatment reduced Secchi depth by 4–6 m (likely unacceptable to recreational users).
   - Statistically significant treatment effects on Secchi depth confirmed via repeated-measures ANOVA (Redfish: P = 0.00244; Pettit: P = 0.00794).
4. **Metalimnetic vs. epilimnetic nutrient delivery**: In Pettit Lake, metalimnetic nutrient additions caused less reduction in water clarity than epilimnetic additions, while achieving similar increases in zooplankton (*Daphnia*) egg production — suggesting metalimnetic dosing could enhance productivity with reduced aesthetic/clarity costs.
5. **Zooplankton production vs. biomass decoupling**: Cladoceran (*Bosmina*, *Daphnia*) egg production increased with nutrient additions in both lakes, but total zooplankton biomass did not increase — attributed to strong grazing pressure (planktivory) by stocked fish overwhelming the bottom-up nutrient signal.
6. **Surrogate species limitations**: Redside shiners were unsuitable surrogates for sockeye in Pettit Lake mesocosm experiments due to handling mortality, limiting direct fish-growth conclusions from that lake.
7. **Management implication**: The authors recommend a combined strategy of **competitor (kokanee) reduction plus nutrient enhancement** — potentially via metalimnetic nutrient delivery — as the most promising approach to support recovery of endangered Snake River sockeye salmon while minimizing conflict with recreational water-clarity values.
