# Rare and Endangered Species: The Rio Grande Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 report by Robert Behnke of the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit]] reviews the status, taxonomy and distribution of the native cutthroat trout of the [[Rio Grande Basin]] (*Salmo clarkii virginalis*). Although the trout is listed as endangered by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, the author argues that "status unknown" may be more accurate, because original distribution, taxonomic characters and population variability are poorly documented. The report synthesizes historical literature, anecdotal reports and specimen data from 68 specimens. It concludes that populations in the upper Rio Grande (Rito Seco, Indian Creek in Colorado, and Rio Chiquito in New Mexico) are pure native stock, while the Mescalero population is distinct, and that the status of trout in the [[Pecos River]] and [[Canadian River Basin]] remains unresolved.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** The first valid scientific name for the Rio Grande trout is *virginalis*, given by Girard (1856) from specimens collected in 1853 at [[Ute Creek]], near [[Fort Massachusetts]], Colorado. Cope (1872) described a second species, *Salmo spilurus*, which led to a period of confusion in which *spilurus* was applied to Rio Grande trout and *virginalis* to Bonneville basin trout. Jordan (1891) examined two Rio Grande specimens from Del Norte, Colorado, and his comments became the basis for nearly all later taxonomic descriptions. The author notes that the taxonomic foundation rests on very few specimens and that the type-era concept of "typological" species encouraged the naming of invalid forms among highly variable trout.

**Subspecific status.** No contention exists that the Rio Grande trout belongs to the widely ranging *Salmo clarkii* species. If it is distinct enough to warrant subspecific rank, the name *Salmo clarkii virginalis* should be used.

**Key diagnostic characters (from the 1958 and 1966 samples):**
- An adipose fin with a black border, the most obvious distinguishing feature.
- Minute basibranchial teeth, visible only under magnification.
- Medium-sized spots concentrated posteriorly, with smaller spots anterior to the dorsal fin.
- Distinct parr marks on all specimens measuring 5 to 10 inches.
- A pelvic appendage less than one-fifth the length of the first pelvic ray in northern samples.

Meristic counts (gillrakers, scales, vertebrae, pyloric caeca) were judged of little value for separating the upper Rio Grande trout from other interior cutthroat trout. Jordan's scale-count distinction (155–160 in Rio Grande specimens versus 185–190 in Colorado specimens) was based on only two fish, yet it is still widely quoted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical distribution.** Reports of trout in the Rio Grande system are among the earliest records of trout in the New World. Coronado's 1539 expedition noted "very good trout in the upper Pecos River" (Rostlund, 1952). Cope (1886) reported two cutthroat-like trout with basibranchial teeth from the Sierra Madre of southern Chihuahua, Mexico, but these specimens have not been located, and no cutthroat trout have been collected in Mexico since that note. The author suggests that cutthroat trout may have ranged throughout the Rio Grande system into Mexico during colder Pleistocene periods and may persist in isolated colonies in the [[Rio Conchos]].

**Anecdotal reports from Texas and New Mexico.** Nineteenth-century letters to *Forest and Stream* describe trout in Texas and New Mexico, including the Devil's River, Limpia River, and Rio Bonito, during the Civil War era. These accounts are considered unreliable, and some correspondents, such as Johnson (1880), doubted that trout existed in Texas. [[Hubbs]] (1957) found no authentic records of native trout in Texas. The only self-reproducing trout population noted there is introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] in McKittrick Creek, Guadalupe Mountains.

**Canadian River system.** A New Mexico Department of Fish and Game survey (1953–1956) found introduced [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]] and rainbow trout in most headwater streams, with "native cutthroat" still dominant in the uppermost headwaters. However, the survey did not distinguish Yellowstone cutthroat from Rio Grande cutthroat, so native status is unconfirmed. The author notes the strong possibility that trout were native to the Canadian system through headwater stream transfer with Rio Grande tributaries in the [[Sangre de Cristo Mountains]].

**Isolated desert basins.** The [[Tularosa Valley]], a desiccating basin with no connection to the Rio Grande for several thousand years, contains the southernmost known Rio Grande cutthroat record. The author argues that cold spring-fed headwater tributaries of isolated desert basins may yield further native populations.

**Related taxa.** The [[Golden Trout]] of Pacific drainages in Chihuahua (*Salmo chrysogaster*) and the [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) are not closely related to the Rio Grande cutthroat. Trout from the Rio Casas Grandes and Rio Yaqui appear intermediate between these forms but are not cutthroat trout.

## Key Findings

- **Conservation status is uncertain.** The trout is listed as endangered, but the author considers "status unknown" more appropriate because data are scarce (few museum specimens, from a restricted area).
- **Original range is poorly documented.** Evidence suggests populations once occurred south into Chihuahua, but this rests on a single 1886 note. Texas records are unverified.
- **Upper Rio Grande populations are pure native stock.** Samples from the [[Rito Seco]], [[Indian Creek]] (Costilla County, Colorado), and the [[Rio Chiquito]] (New Mexico) showed high homogeneity, sharing the black-bordered adipose fin. The author concludes they diverged little from the ancestral cutthroat and may represent the type material for *virginalis* and *spilurus*.
- **No evidence of hybridization** with rainbow trout or [[Yellowstone Lake Cutthroat Trout]] was found in the upper Rio Grande samples.
- **Yellowstone stock differs.** Yellowstone Lake trout, the source of most western introductions, differ in adipose fin marking, gillraker development and basibranchial teeth, so comparisons with that stock are required to test purity.
- **The Mescalero population is distinct.** Eight specimens from Indian Creek on the [[Mescalero Apache Indian Reservation]] (collected November 1966 by Terrence Merkel) have larger, fewer spots concentrated near the caudal peduncle, lack the black adipose border, and have a pelvic appendage one-third to one-half the length of the first pelvic ray. The author considers them not derived from Yellowstone Lake stock. Whether they are native or introduced from the Pecos basin remains unresolved.
- **Specimen base is small.** The report draws on the following collections: six Jordan specimens (1889); 30 University of Michigan specimens from the 1958 Colorado collections, with 5 retained at Colorado State University; and 20 Rio Chiquito specimens (2 collected in May and 18 in June 1966, by Dean and Henderson).
- **Pecos and Canadian basins are unstudied.** No published taxonomic accounts or museum specimens of native trout from the Pecos River division exist. The author calls for collections there and in the Canadian system to clarify variation within the Rio Grande trout group.
- **Call for further study.** The report aims to stimulate collection and research, echoing Evermann and Kendall (1895), who wrote that the distribution of Rio Grande basin trout "furnishes very interesting and proper subject for investigation."
