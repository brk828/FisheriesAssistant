# Feeding Ecology of Ambassis Species in Natal Estuaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ambassidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by Martin and Blaber, published in the *South African Journal of Zoology*, examines the feeding ecology of three glassy perchlet species ([[Ambassis Productus]], [[Ambassis Natalensis]] and [[Ambassis Gymnocephalus]]) across six [[Natal Estuaries]]. Diets were quantified using four stomach content methods, and feeding periodicity was measured by dry mass and points methods. The authors conclude that all three species are predominantly zooplanktivorous and carnivorous, that ecological separation among them is achieved largely through diet, and that *Ambassis* may exert a significant piscivorous predatory effect on estuarine fish communities, particularly through egg and larval predation.

## Background & Description

*Ambassis* (glassy perchlets) are small, abundant members of the family Ambassidae, widely distributed in tropical and subtropical coastal waters and estuaries of the Indo-Pacific. Prior to this work, biology of the family was poorly known in the western Indian Ocean. Earlier studies cited include larval development in *A. gymnocephalus* ([[Chua 1973]]), growth from length frequencies ([[Raman et al. 1975]]), feeding behaviour in *A. gymnocephalus* ([[Natarajan and Patnaik 1968]]), prey–predator relationships in *A. commersoni* ([[Nair and Nair 1981]]), and digestive tract histology of two freshwater species ([[Moitra and Ray 1979]]).

The study forms part of a series on *Ambassis* in Natal estuaries, aiming to provide comparative data on distribution, habitat preference and diet, and to identify factors that may minimize interspecific competition where the three species co-occur.

Methods included:
- Stomach analysis of fish collected March 1980 to February 1982, using four methods: percentage frequency of occurrence, percentage numerical occurrence, percentage calorific (energy) contribution, and Ricker's (1968) percentage "points" of fullness.
- Fish were grouped as immature/adult (> 30 mm S.L.) or fry (< 30 mm S.L.).
- Feeding periodicity was assessed by netting fish at 3-hour intervals over 24-hour periods at [[Nhlange Estuary]] and [[St Lucia Estuary]], using dry mass and points methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Diet varied among estuaries because faunal composition shifts with physical conditions. The study sites span a wide salinity range:
- **Kosi system:** from almost fresh water (1‰) at Nhlange to marine (34‰) at the mouth.
- **St Lucia:** high salinities, becoming hypersaline (43‰) in dry seasons.
- **Mdloti:** closed from the sea for long periods and at times fresh (0‰).

Distribution differed among species. *A. natalensis* was the most widespread and most dietarily varied. *A. productus* occurred mainly in low salinities (< 10‰) except at St Lucia. *A. gymnocephalus* was restricted to permanently open estuary mouths and bays with salinities above 30‰.

Other zooplanktivores, including the filter feeders [[Gilchristella Aestuarius]], *Hilsa kelee* and *Thryssa vitrirostris*, were judged unlikely to compete strongly with *Ambassis*, because they feed mainly offshore and by day, whereas *Ambassis* feeds along shallow margins mainly at night.

Energy values used for dietary calculations (J mg⁻¹) were: planktonic crustaceans 15.33; insects 22.16; fish larvae and fry 24.05; fish eggs 41.90; miscellaneous food 16.55.

## Key Findings

**Diet and feeding strategy**
- All three *Ambassis* species are predominantly carnivorous, feeding mainly on zooplanktonic crustaceans, with *Pseudodiaptomus stuhlmanni* the most important prey at many sites.
- Dietary niche breadth: *A. productus* and *A. natalensis* are euryphagic (varied diets); *A. gymnocephalus* is stenophagic (few food types), feeding almost entirely on zooplankton.
- *A. natalensis* has the widest distribution and most varied diet. *A. productus* is the most piscivorous, deriving almost as much energy from fish as from crustaceans (fish reached 153 mm S.L.).
- Crustacean dominance by site: *A. productus* at St Lucia (61.55% crustaceans, 25.51% fish); *A. gymnocephalus* at Durban Bay (85.92% crustaceans). At Kosi, *A. productus* consumed mainly insects (66.93%).

**Seasonal shifts at St Lucia**
- Throughout the year, crustaceans made up more than 70% by volume for both species, except in winter.
- In winter (June–August), *A. gymnocephalus* fed on fish eggs (47.64%) and crustaceans (48.6%). Fish eggs occurred in over 79% of its stomachs.
- The authors tentatively attribute these eggs to mullet (Mugilidae), citing the egg dimensions (0.68–0.76 mm) and winter spawning of *Liza macrolepis* and *L. dumerili* at St Lucia.

**Fry diet**
- *A. productus* fry at Kosi fed mainly on copepod nauplii (60.66% crustaceans) and filamentous algae (34.18%).
- *A. natalensis* fry at Kosi were mainly insectivorous (61.81%); at St Lucia they fed chiefly on crustaceans (60.45%) and polychaete larvae (25.19%).
- *A. gymnocephalus* fry at St Lucia consumed almost entirely crustaceans (92.11%), mostly copepod nauplii.

**Feeding periodicity**
- *A. productus* fed mainly at night, peaking before dawn (03h00) in summer at Nhlange, and fed little by 15h00.
- *A. natalensis* showed bimodal feeding by dry mass (early evening and early morning), but points analysis gave a different pattern, indicating the two methods can yield different peak times.
- Feeding peaks coincided with disturbance from returning boat anglers around 18h00–21h00, which reduced ingested food volumes.

**Water-column position**
- Over 83% of the diet of all three species was suspended food.
- *A. natalensis* took surface prey (36.21%), notably stranded terrestrial insects such as hymenopterans and termites.
- *A. productus* took benthic prey (35.57%).

**Niche partitioning**
- Species separated mainly in secondary and tertiary food: *A. productus* fed on fish fry; *A. gymnocephalus* relied on fish eggs; *A. natalensis* fed on aquatic and terrestrial insects.
- The authors conclude that interspecific competition is minimized by prey abundance, feeding periodicity, water-column level and prey size.

**Ecological significance**
- The authors suggest that *Ambassis* may be the dominant zooplanktivore after the filter feeders and, through predation on recruiting fish fry and larvae, could significantly affect estuarine fish communities.
- Egg predation by *A. gymnocephalus* during winter may affect mullet recruitment at St Lucia, though the actual impact is unknown.
- Comparative data from [[Natarajan and Patnaik 1968]] (Chilka Lake, India) and [[Nair and Nair 1981]] (Kerala) show similar piscivorous and egg-eating behaviour, including a potential threat to farmed fish and prawn juveniles in culture systems with estuary access.
