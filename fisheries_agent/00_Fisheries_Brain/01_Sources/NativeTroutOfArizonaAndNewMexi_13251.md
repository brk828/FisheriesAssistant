# Native Trout of Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #salmonidae #native-trout #conservation #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service document (1987) describes three native trout species of the southwestern United States: [[Gila Trout]], [[Apache Trout]], and [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]. All three species experienced dramatic population declines due to competition and hybridization with introduced non-native trout species ([[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]]), habitat degradation from land use changes, and overfishing. By the mid-twentieth century, pure populations survived only in remote headwaters protected by natural barriers. The document outlines the conservation status, physical characteristics, distribution, and management efforts for each species.

## Background & Description

### Definition of Native Trout

A native trout is a species that naturally occupies a lake or stream without human assistance. Many resulted from sea-run trout that migrated up river systems, became landlocked, and evolved over thousands of years. In contrast, commonly caught species like [[Rainbow Trout]] (from [[Sierra Nevada Mountains]], California), [[Brook Trout]] (from southeastern Canada and eastern United States), and [[Brown Trout]] (from Europe) were introduced to the Southwest in the late 1800s.

### Historical Context

Early settlers found streams in mountain regions of [[New Mexico]] and east central [[Arizona]] teeming with native trout, which became important food sources and provided sportfishing opportunities. As human populations grew and accessible streams became depleted, state and federal agencies established hatcheries. However, they preferentially cultured non-native species that had been domesticated and were easier to produce than wild natives.

### Species Descriptions

**[[Gila Trout]] (Oncorhynchus gilae)**
- Native to headwaters of the [[Gila River]]
- Occupies streams in [[Gila Wilderness]] and [[Aldo Leopold Wilderness]] areas of southwestern New Mexico
- Dense pattern of small, irregularly-shaped spots located mainly on upper body
- Males more brightly colored with iridescent gold on sides, blending to olive-green on upper body
- Faint pink band may be present along sides of adults
- Fins tipped with yellow and white; yellow cutthroat mark present
- Growth potential: 13-14 inches with adequate food and space; potential to reach 16-20 inches or more
- Largest known specimen: 21.75 inches long

**[[Apache Trout]] (Oncorhynchus apache)**
- Native to upper tributaries of [[Salt River]] and [[Little Colorado River]]
- Inhabits streams primarily on [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] and [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] in east central Arizona
- Iridescent gold on sides and gill covers blending to olive-green on top
- Small, uniformly rounded spots sparse on front, increasing in density toward tail
- Spots toward front typically exhibit light halo
- No pink band (unlike [[Gila Trout]])
- Yellow cutthroat mark and white or yellow tipped fins present
- Displays largest dorsal and adipose fins of any American trout
- Size in small streams: females average 9-10 inches, males 12-14 inches
- Growth potential in larger waters: current Arizona state record 3 lbs. 10 oz.; research specimens exceeded 4 pounds in less than 3 years

**[[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] (Oncorhynchus clarkii virginalis)**
- Native to headwater streams of [[Rio Grande]] and [[Pecos River]] in northern New Mexico and southern Colorado
- Unverified reports suggest possible historic presence in Texas and Mexico tributaries
- Large, pronounced spots on tail; sparse to absent on upper forward body region
- More colorful than [[Apache Trout]] or [[Gila Trout]]
- Light rose to red-orange pastel hues on sides; pink or yellowish orange underneath
- Colors brighter on adult fish, particularly breeding males
- Lower fins tinted with orange or red; distinct red or orange cutthroat mark
- Reports of specimens reaching 24-30 inches in length
- Known as one of the easiest trout to catch

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Threats and Population Decline

Prior to the 1900s, native trout were widespread throughout their respective river systems. By the 1960s, [[Gila Trout]] was reduced to less than 5% of its former range. [[Apache Trout]] populations were so drastically reduced by the mid-1900s that original distribution became impossible to determine with certainty.

**Primary Threats:**
1. **Competition**: Non-native trout species competed for available food
2. **Hybridization**: Interbreeding with introduced species destroyed pure populations
3. **Habitat Degradation**: Changing land uses (grazing, timber harvest, mining) decreased water quality and impaired natural reproduction
4. **Overfishing**: Accessible streams became depleted
5. **Hatchery Practices**: Millions of non-native trout stocked, which depended less on natural reproduction

### Refugia

By mid-twentieth century, the only remaining pure populations were located in remote headwaters where natural waterfalls served as barriers to upstream migration of foreign trout.

## Key Findings

### Conservation Status (as of 1987)

**[[Gila Trout]]:**
- Listed as "endangered with extinction" under [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 and [[New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act]] of 1975
- Status expected to change from endangered to threatened due to range expansion efforts
- Collaborative efforts by [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]], [[New Mexico State University]], [[U.S. Forest Service]], and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]

**[[Apache Trout]]:**
- Originally listed as "endangered with extinction" in 1967
- Status changed to "threatened" in 1975 to simplify management and restoration
- Collaborative efforts by [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]], [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]
- Goal: restore 30 populations to historic streams
- Successful hatchery rearing program at [[Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]]

**[[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]:**
- Recognized as species of special concern
- Never afforded full protection under [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973
- Successful restoration programs in New Mexico and Colorado established approximately 90 populations
- Most populations open to controlled sport fishing

### Management Goals

1. **[[Gila Trout]]**: Continued renewal of populations within historic range; eventual removal from protected status for management as sport fish
2. **[[Apache Trout]]**: Potential removal from threatened/endangered species list; development as important southwestern gamefish
3. **[[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]**: Continued management to ensure availability for future generations; maintenance of controlled sport fishing opportunities

### Restoration Success

State and federal agencies successfully implemented restoration programs that expanded ranges and established new populations, demonstrating that native trout recovery is achievable through coordinated management, hatchery programs, and habitat protection.
