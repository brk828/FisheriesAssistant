# Impact of Copper Mining on Desert Intermittent Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study, by Michael A. Lewis and Ronald Burraychak (Arizona State University and U.S. Forest Service), examines how copper mine wastes affected the biota of [[Pinto Creek]], an intermittent desert stream in Gila County, Arizona. Data were collected at 13 sampling stations during 1975–1976. Mine effluents made up to 90% of total flow in Pinto Creek at times and strongly altered water quality, sediment regime and biological communities. Sedimentation and suspended solids were judged the primary detrimental factor, while heavy metals were diluted, buffered and partly bound to sediments. The stream still supported fish and invertebrates ten kilometres downstream at the close of the study, but the authors warned that rising sediment loads and future spills threatened the system.

## Background & Description

Copper mining is a major part of U.S. domestic mineral production and is concentrated in Arizona. The document notes that Arizona mine wastes often include tailings, runoff and effluents carrying copper, zinc, lead and cadmium. Desert streams are typically intermittent, with low flows and droughts that intensify the effect of pollutants. Prior documentation of copper effects on desert streams was described as scarce, which motivated the study.

Methods combined field sampling of fish, invertebrates and vegetation with water chemistry analyses. Metals were measured by atomic absorption spectrophotometry (Perkin Elmer 403). Acute 96-hour toxicity tests were run with [[Agosia chrysogaster]] at the Arizona Department of Health laboratory. Fish and invertebrates were sampled with a Surber sampler at each station. Comparison streams included [[Lynx Creek]], [[Mineral Creek]], Salt River, Tonto Creek and Seven Springs Wash.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Pinto Creek contained a total species pool of 64, slightly below the 65–300 species typical of permanent streams. Six fish species were recorded, of which only three were abundant in the intermittent reach before 1975: Agosia chrysogaster, Pantosteus clarki and Gambusia affinis. Fish and macroinvertebrate abundance increased with distance from the mine and with duration of surface flow.

Water quality varied widely. Mean pH ranged from 7.6 to 8.5 over the study. Sulfate was the dominant anion. Mean water quality frequently exceeded Arizona standards for sulfate (250 mg/l) and periodically exceeded copper (0.5 mg/l) and zinc (5 mg/l) standards. Metals were diluted and buffered by the stream and by high hardness, and were adsorbed to clays and organic particles.

## Key Findings

- **Fish community:** Pantosteus clarki was eliminated from intermittent areas by 1976. Gambusia affinis populations were reduced in 1975. Agosia chrysogaster appeared highly resistant.
- **Invertebrates:** Aquatic insects with long life cycles (Baetis sp. and Corydalus cornutus) were eliminated by 1976. Heavy metals had no observable effect on aquatic insect survival.
- **Suspended solids and sedimentation:** Deposition reached up to 50 cm (mean 15 cm) in intermittent areas. Sediment cover increased approximately 300% over 1975 levels. Reduced depth and habitat loss were more damaging to Pantosteus clarki than to Agosia chrysogaster.
- **Toxicity:** Zinc was the most lethal metal in toxicity tests (96-h LC50 = 0.21 mg/l copper and 0.28 mg/l zinc for Agosia chrysogaster). Copper and zinc exceeded estimated lethal levels in 25% of water samples, yet a chronically toxic exposure did not significantly affect length-weight relationships or condition of Agosia.
- **Fish kills:** Two fish kills including Agosia occurred in 1975 after large discharges raised zinc to 10 mg/l and copper to 9 mg/l. An estimated 300,000 Agosia were killed at times of high waste discharge from nearby mines.
- **Bioaccumulation:** Agosia in Pinto Creek concentrated 2× more zinc and 6× more copper than Agosia in Seven Springs Wash. Benthic vegetation accumulated 5× more zinc and 60× more copper than reference samples.
- **Primary production:** Gross primary production was reduced in the polluted reach, attributed to scouring, burial of producers and possibly toxic metal ions. Heterotrophy increased, and the stream was becoming more dependent on allochthonous material.
- **Conclusions:** Sedimentation was the primary detrimental effect. Metal inputs were largely diluted, though spills and sudden discharges could still produce toxic pulses.
- **Recommendations:** Coat or vegetate tailings ponds and dams, increase pond storage capacity, control erosion from spoil piles and roads, and monitor the [[Pinto Creek Aquifer]] for changes in surface flow.
