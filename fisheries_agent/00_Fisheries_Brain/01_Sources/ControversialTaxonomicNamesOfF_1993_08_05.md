## Key Findings

- **Core Issue**: A draft memorandum from the Phoenix Ecological Services office contained a paragraph addressing a taxonomic naming controversy; this paragraph was recommended for deletion by the Regional Fisheries & Federal Aid office.
- **Policy Position**: The Fish and Wildlife Service should avoid engaging in "academic" taxonomic disputes over scientific names that have **no direct bearing** on species listing or recovery efforts, as such involvement wastes staff resources and does not advance conservation outcomes.
- **Standard Reference Authority**: The accepted standard for fish nomenclature is designated as **"[[Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada]]"**, Fifth Edition (1991), published by the [[American Fisheries Society]] (Special Publication 20, Bethesda, MD), authored by Robins, C. R., R. M. Bailey, C. E. Bond, J. R. Brooker, E. A. Lachner, R. N. Lea, and W. B. Scott.
- **Update Cycle**: This reference publication is updated approximately every **10 years**, meaning taxonomic nomenclature used by the Service may lag behind the most current academic taxonomic proposals.
- **Dispute Context**: The [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] apparently sought to revert a scientific name to a historically used designation, contrary to the name change reflected in the current (5th) edition of the standard reference.
- **Resolution Protocol**: Per Service policy, until the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] formally convinces the broader taxonomic community (via peer-reviewed taxonomic literature) that the name change is unwarranted, the **currently published name remains valid** and should be used by Service biologists and the threatened/endangered species community.
- **Institutional Stance on Expertise**: The memo explicitly notes that most Service biologists are **not professional taxonomists** and should therefore refrain from taking professional positions in taxonomic disputes, deferring instead to the established peer-reviewed literature and standard nomenclature publications.
- **Broader Implication**: This memo illustrates a recurring tension in conservation biology between **taxonomic stability** (as codified in standard references) and **evolving scientific consensus**, particularly for imperiled or narrowly distributed species such as those managed under the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] program, which historically has included taxa like desert pupfish and chub species in the [[Southwestern United States]].
