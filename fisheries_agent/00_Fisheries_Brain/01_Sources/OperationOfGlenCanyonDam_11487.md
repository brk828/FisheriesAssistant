# Operation of Glen Canyon Dam: Draft Environmental Impact Statement Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #regulated-rivers

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation summary covers the Draft Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) on the operation of [[Glen Canyon Dam]], part of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] in northern Arizona. The EIS reevaluates how the dam releases water so as to reduce downstream impacts on environmental, cultural, and Native American resources in Glen and Grand Canyons while still producing [[Hydropower]]. It evaluates nine alternatives, from unrestricted fluctuating flows to steady flows, and names the [[Modified Low Fluctuating Flow Alternative]] as the preferred option. The document states that no alternative can restore predam conditions.

The EIS was mandated by the Secretary of the Interior in 1989 and was shaped by the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992, which required the EIS be completed by October 30, 1994. Written comments on the draft were due April 11, 1994.

## Background & Description

**Dam and project.** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963 as the key feature of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (CRSP). It stores water in [[Lake Powell]], which has an active capacity of almost 25 million acre-feet (maf). The dam's authorized purposes include river regulation, water storage, reclamation of arid lands, flood control, and hydroelectric generation. The dam predates NEPA, so no EIS was filed for its construction or operation.

**Operating history.** The powerplant has been used mainly for peaking power. Daily fluctuating releases raised concern among resource agencies, anglers, rafters, and environmental groups. A 1987 uprate and rewind raised potential capacity to 33,200 cfs, but maximum releases have been held to 31,500 cfs pending a comprehensive study. The multiagency [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) began in 1982, with Phase I completed in 1988 and Phase II started in June 1988.

**Interim operations.** Testing began August 1, 1991, and interim criteria took effect November 1, 1991. They set peak releases at 20,000 cfs, minimum releases at 5,000 cfs at night and 8,000 cfs during the day, daily fluctuations of 5,000 to 8,000 cfs, and ramp rates of 2,500 cfs/hr up and 1,500 cfs/hr down.

**Study area.** The corridor runs from Lake Powell through Glen and Grand Canyons to the headwaters of [[Lake Mead]]. The uppermost 15 miles lie within the Glen Canyon National Recreation Area, and the remaining 278 miles lie within Grand Canyon National Park. Several tribes have land interests in the area, including the Navajo Nation, Hopi Tribe, Havasupai Tribe, and Hualapai Tribe.

**Public involvement.** Scoping drew more than 17,000 comments. Information reached about 20,000 people through meetings and newsletters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document treats the canyon as a linked ecosystem. Water release patterns and sediment transport drive physical, biological, cultural, recreational, and hydropower resources.

**Aquatic food chain.** Cold, clear, nutrient-rich dam releases allow the non-native alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]] to flourish. It supports diatoms and insect larvae, and the non-native crustacean [[Gammarus Lacustris]] feeds on the diatoms and uses the alga as refuge. Together these form the food base for [[Rainbow Trout]], which support a blue ribbon sport fishery in the Glen Canyon reach.

**Native fish.** Eight native fish species inhabited the river before the dam. Now several face survival problems, and only three remain relatively common in tributaries and some river sections. Habitat change and non-native fish are the principal known causes. Cold water confines native reproduction largely to tributaries such as the [[Little Colorado River]]. The only known successful spawning habitat for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] is the Little Colorado River. The [[Razorback Sucker]] is rare in the study area with no known reproduction, and the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] is a candidate for listing but is relatively abundant.

**Sediment and terrestrial links.** Sediment stabilizes archeological sites and camping beaches, builds backwater fish habitat, and supports [[Riparian Vegetation]], which provides habitat for wildlife. Over half of the bird species nesting along the corridor nest in riparian vegetation. One of the highest known densities of [[Peregrine Falcon]] in North America occurs in Grand Canyon, where the falcons feed on swallows, swifts, and bats. The endangered [[Bald Eagle]] feeds on spawning and stranded trout in winter.

**Physical changes.** Predam annual flows ranged from spring peaks sometimes above 100,000 cfs to winter lows of 1,000 to 3,000 cfs. Predam water temperature varied from about 80 °F to near freezing. Released water now averages 46 °F year-round. Lake Powell traps sediment, so the river is clear except when tributaries add sediment. Sand supply now depends mainly on the Paria River, the Little Colorado River, Kanab Creek, and side canyons.

**Power and air.** The powerplant has eight generating units with a combined capacity of 1,356 MW and supplies about 75 percent of CRSP power. Western sells to more than 180 wholesale customers across a six-state area. Navajo Generating Station, independent of the dam, is the major source of sulfates in Grand Canyon haze.

## Key Findings

### Alternatives and preferred option
- Nine alternatives were analyzed: two unrestricted (No Action and Maximum Powerplant Capacity), four restricted fluctuating (High, Moderate, [[Modified Low Fluctuating Flow Alternative]] (preferred), and Interim Low), and three steady (Existing Monthly Volume, Seasonally Adjusted, and Year-Round).
- The preferred alternative sets minimum releases of 8,000 cfs from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. and 5,000 cfs at night, a maximum of 20,000 cfs during fluctuating releases, and ramp rates of 2,500 cfs/hr up and 1,500 cfs/hr down. It matches the interim criteria plus [[Habitat Maintenance Flows]], endangered fish research, and the common elements.
- The No Action baseline allows releases up to 31,500 cfs, a minimum of 1,000 cfs from Labor Day to Easter, and 3,000 cfs from Easter to Labor Day.

### Common elements
- [[Adaptive Management]] would use an Adaptive Management Work Group, a technical work group, and an independent scientific review panel.
- Flood frequency reduction aims to limit unscheduled floods above 45,000 cfs to no more than once in 100 years on a long-term average, down from about once in 40 years. Options are raising the spillway gates 4.5 feet for about 0.75 maf of storage (about $3 million), or reserving 1 maf of space January through June.
- [[Beach Habitat Building Flows]] are assumed at a frequency of 1 in 5 years, with magnitudes at least 10,000 cfs above the minimum-release-year peak but not above 45,000 cfs. A single test at about 45,000 cfs is planned.

### Endangered fish and selective withdrawal
- The draft [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] biological opinion said the preferred alternative would likely not jeopardize the bald eagle, peregrine falcon, or Kanab ambersnail. It would likely jeopardize the humpback chub and razorback sucker.
- Endangered fish research flows would run between 8,000 and 20,000 cfs and may take up to 5 low-release years. Research could likely be completed within 10 years.
- Reclamation would make every effort to establish at least one more humpback chub population in a tributary below the dam.
- [[Selective Withdrawal]] through multilevel intakes on the eight penstocks could raise downstream temperatures 5 to 18 °F (54 to 69 °F from May to October). The estimated cost is $60 million. The temperature would still be 7 to 16 °F cooler than predam summers.

### Hydropower and economics
- Restricted alternatives would reduce power flexibility and raise costs. Household electric bills would rise $6 to $40 per year (0.9 to 6.4 percent) under restricted fluctuating alternatives, and $51 to $69 per year (8 to 11 percent) under steady alternatives.
- National economic impacts range from a gain of $2 million per year (Maximum Powerplant Capacity) to a cost of $119 million per year (Seasonally Adjusted Steady Flow).
- Recreation benefits rise by $3.74 million (Modified Low) and $4.76 million (Seasonally Adjusted Steady Flow) in equivalent annual terms.

### Resources and impacts
- **Sediment:** The Modified Low alternative has a higher probability of net riverbed sand gain than the No Action alternative, but steady flows are somewhat better. Only the Moderate and Modified Low alternatives, combined with habitat maintenance flows, build sandbars roughly as high as the alternatives with greater fluctuations.
- **Vegetation and wildlife:** Reduced maximum flows allow riparian vegetation to expand. Woody plant area could rise from 0 to 40 percent (Moderate) up to 94 percent (Year-Round Steady) above no action.
- **Cultural resources:** The affected area is a 255-mile section of the corridor. The document lists 475 prehistoric and historic sites, of which 313 are eligible for the [[National Register of Historic Places]]. Alternatives with positive net sand balance, including Moderate, Modified Low, and Seasonally Adjusted, are favored for cultural preservation.
- **Recreation:** Steady alternatives essentially eliminate river stage changes, a major improvement in angler safety. Maximum Powerplant Capacity keeps upstream fishing access at no-action levels.
- **Air quality:** Total SO2 and NOx emission changes would be less than plus or minus 1 percent under any alternative over the 50-year analysis.

### Other conclusions
- No permits are needed immediately for any alternative. Future Clean Water Act sections 402 and 404 permits may be needed depending on monitoring results.
- Run-of-the-River and Historic Pattern alternatives were eliminated because sediment augmentation would take 15 to 20 years. A [[Reregulation Dam]] alternative was eliminated because it would significantly harm Glen Canyon reach resources.
- No adverse impacts to Indian trust assets are anticipated under the preferred alternative.
- Peaking power loss is unavoidable under any restricted fluctuating or steady alternative.
