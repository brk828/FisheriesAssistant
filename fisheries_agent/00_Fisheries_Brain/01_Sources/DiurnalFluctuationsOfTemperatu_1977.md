# Diurnal Fluctuations of Temperature Near the Bottom of Shallow Water Bodies as Affected by Solar Radiation, Bottom Color and Water Circulation

## Executive Summary
This 1977 *Hydrobiologia* study by Dale & Gillespie investigates the physical microhabitat conditions at the sediment–water interface in shallow freshwater systems, focusing on how **substrate color**, **solar radiation**, and **wind-driven water circulation** interact to produce diurnal temperature fluctuations. While the paper is fundamentally a physical limnology study, it has direct relevance to [[Ichthyology]] because substrate temperature and light regimes at the [[Benthic Zone|lake bottom]] influence fish nesting behavior, egg incubation microclimates, and habitat selection in species such as [[Bluegill Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and juvenile [[Pacific Salmon]]. Field measurements at Lake Opinicon (Ontario) and controlled tank experiments at the University of Guelph demonstrated that dark substrates (e.g., organic detritus, black-painted disks) heat and cool more rapidly and to a greater extreme than light substrates (sand, white disks), with measured temperature differentials of 0.8–4.0 °C depending on setting, wind speed, and cloud cover.

## Background & Description
*Note: This paper is not organism-morphology focused; it is a physical/environmental study. The following relates to substrate/microhabitat "morphology" rather than fish anatomy.*

- **Substrate types studied**: light-colored sand, dark organic detritus, and four painted wooden disks (black, white, yellow [7.5YR 7/11], green [10G 5/8]) used as standardized artificial substrates.
- **Disk specifications**: 46.8 cm diameter × 1.4 cm thick, painted with boat enamel, submerged at 11.5 cm or 21.5 cm depth in 77 cm × 47 cm cylindrical tanks.
- **Fish nest morphology (field observation)**: [[Bluegill Sunfish]] nests in Lake Opinicon exhibited exposed light-colored sand substrate contrasted against surrounding dark organic detritus — a result of the fish's fanning behavior during nest construction, which alters local substrate color and thus local thermal properties.
- **Albedo values** (proxy for surface "reflective morphology"):
  - Black: 0.031
  - Green: 0.094
  - Yellow: 0.29
  - White: 0.34
  - Ratio ≈ 1 : 3 : 9 : 11 (black:green:yellow:white)

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: Shallow littoral zones of lakes and ponds (0.5–3 m depth), specifically the upper few centimeters of substrate — a microhabitat supporting [[Saprobes]], detritivores, and rooted [[Macrophytes]] such as *Utricularia resupinata*, *Vallisneria americana*, and *Potamogeton* spp.
- **Thermal microhabitat and fish behavior**:
  - [[Hoar Et Al 1957]] documented that juvenile [[Pacific Salmon]] select differing light intensities, a behavior intertwined with substrate-driven thermal gradients.
  - [[Robbins 1975]] found that potamodromous [[Smallmouth Bass]] change bottom-color preference (black vs. white) with age/ontogeny, suggesting an ontogenetic shift in microhabitat thermal/light preference.
  - [[Bluegill Sunfish]] nest-site substrate manipulation (exposing sand vs. retaining dark detritus) creates localized temperature mosaics (~0.8–0.9 °C difference), which may have adaptive significance for egg incubation temperature control.
- **Substrate-water energy exchange**: The paper frames four main determinants of near-bottom substrate temperature: (1) biological/microbial heating, (2) geological heat exchange, (3) water-substrate energy exchange (circulation-dependent), and (4) radiant solar energy — the last being the primary diurnal driver in unshaded, unvegetated shallow zones.
- **Vegetative shading effect**: Aquatic macrophyte and algal canopies can mask/dampen these diurnal substrate temperature fluctuations (cf. [[Dale And Gillespie 1976]] on floating plant mats).

## Key Findings
- **Field (Lake Opinicon)**:
  - Sunfish nest surface (sand) was consistently **0.8 °C cooler** than surrounding dark detritus (20.1° vs 20.9°, n=5 readings).
  - Sand substrate temperature at 1 cm depth was poorly correlated with overlying air or water temperature, indicating substrate-specific energy dynamics dominate over ambient conditions.
- **Tank experiments (Guelph, May 1975)**:
  - Black disk surface temperatures rose fastest and reached highest maxima (up to 28.0°C); white disk rose slowest and reached lowest maxima (24.0°C) — a **4 °C maximum difference** between color extremes.
  - Water 20 cm below disks showed minimal color-dependent variation (<1 °C), with thermal maxima delayed ~4.3 hours relative to surface disk maxima — evidence of decoupling between surface "boundary layer" heating and deeper water thermal regime.
  - A **14% reduction in net solar radiation** (cloud cover, May 29 vs. May 28) reduced the diurnal temperature range at the black disk surface by **5 °C**.
  - Comparable radiation but differing wind speeds (0.9 m/s vs. 3.6 m/s) produced a similarly large **5 °C difference** in diurnal range at the black disk surface, demonstrating that **wind-driven water circulation** is as influential as radiation intensity in controlling near-bottom thermal fluctuation.
  - Water depth over disks (11.5 cm vs 21.5 cm) had negligible effect on surface temperature response, underscoring that color and circulation—not depth per se—are the dominant controlling variables in this shallow range.
- **Broader ecological implication**: On clear, calm days in early spring, dark unshaded substrates can generate significantly elevated near-bottom temperatures relative to open water, potentially creating localized thermal refugia or accelerated development conditions for [[Benthic Invertebrates]], detritivores, and fish embryos — conditions not predictable from standard open-water temperature monitoring.
- **Methodological note**: Painted wooden disks can simulate substrate color/albedo but cannot replicate the heat-conduction properties of natural, porous lake sediments; thus tank-based thermal gradients likely underestimate the complexity of natural substrate–water heat exchange.
