# Year-to-Year Variation in the Aquatic Macroinvertebrate Fauna of a Northern California Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Chironomidae

Note on scope: this document is a benthic macroinvertebrate study. It does not address fish, so species-level fish fields are recorded as N/A and the taxon tag above reflects the dominant aquatic insect group.

## Executive Summary

McElravy, Lamberti and Resh (1989, *Journal of the North American Benthological Society*) examined the benthic macroinvertebrate community of [[Big Sulphur Creek]], a third-order coastal stream at [[The Geysers]] in northern California, across seven years (1977–1983). Sampling was done twice yearly, in mid May (end of the wet season) and late August (near the end of the dry season), under a [[Mediterranean Climate]] regime with wet winters and nearly rainless summers. Rainfall and stream discharge varied strongly among years, ranging from a drought year to an extremely wet year. The authors show that wet-season hydrology strongly shapes the May community, while the August community was far less variable from year to year. The work is framed as a demonstration that long-term data and abiotic predictors help separate human-caused perturbation from natural variability.

## Background & Description

Most stream macroinvertebrate studies come from continental temperate regions with year-round rainfall. In the central Pacific Coast region, streams flow westward from steep coastal mountains, and more than 80% of annual precipitation falls between November and March. Storm-driven "washout" events (Siegfried and Knight 1977) can remove benthic fauna catastrophically several times each winter.

The authors note that most aquatic insect studies are short. In a review of 266 articles from 1980–1987, 69% covered one year or less and only 12% covered three years or more. The study therefore uses a long-term dataset collected at The Geysers, originally designed for impact monitoring (Resh et al. 1984) and not for year-to-year analysis.

Study site: a roughly 100 m reach, about 13 km below the headwaters, at approximately 415 m elevation. Habitat ranges from pools to swift riffles and from boulders over 1 m in diameter to fine sand and silt. Stream width is 10–25 m, with a gradient of about 25 m/km. Terrain is unstable and highly erodible, with 70–75% of the basin having slopes above 30%.

Methods in brief:
- Four Surber samples (0.093 m² each) per sampling date, two from [[Riffle Habitat]] and two from [[Pool Habitat]].
- Specimens identified to the lowest feasible level, usually genus, with species separated as operational taxonomic units (OTUs).
- Community metrics: taxon richness, macroinvertebrate density, relative abundance of Chironomidae, and [[Simpson's Diversity Index]] (inverse concentration form).
- Storm severity was linked to physical stress: shear stress was calculated from depth and slope, and a washout threshold was reached at a discharge of 3.35 m³/s at Pine Flat.
- Statistics included one-way ANOVA with a Bonferroni-type adjusted alpha (0.013), Student-Newman-Keuls tests, linear and second-order polynomial regression, and cluster analysis (unweighted average linkage on Euclidean distances).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Precipitation at The Geysers averaged 145 ± 38.3 cm/yr over 1951–1975 (wet season, September–May). Annual totals during the study ranged from 30% of the mean (1977 drought) to 173% (1983, extremely wet). Years were classed as wet (1978, 1982), dry (1979, 1981), extremely wet (1983), drought (1977), or average (1980).

Storm and discharge patterns followed rainfall:
- In the 1977 drought year, discharge never exceeded the 3.35 m³/s disturbance threshold.
- Wet and extremely wet years (1978, 1982, 1983) produced 40 spates above threshold combined, causing frequent scour.
- Mean August discharge tracked previous wet-season rainfall (r² = 0.94, p < 0.001), but wet years had only slightly higher summer flow than the near-average year.
- Mean August water temperature was relatively constant, 22.4–23.6 °C, across the years.

The authors describe mid-May as the starting point of community development after washout, and late August as the end product of summer colonization, growth and reproduction. Drought effects are discussed in the context of [[Flow Reduction]] and [[Stream Drying]]: reduced flow tends to lower rheophilic and filter-feeding taxa while favouring silt-tolerant and detritivorous forms, and complete drying leaves mainly resistant eggs and recolonizing adults.

## Key Findings

- **Dominant groups:** Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Diptera made up 93% of individuals and 62% of 81 taxa in mid May, and 96% of individuals and 64% of 69 taxa in late August.
- **Sample sizes:** 26,414 individuals in 81 taxa in mid May; 92,636 individuals in 69 taxa in late August (more than three times the May count).
- **Drought effect:** In the 1977 drought, Trichoptera dominated because of large populations of the caddisfly [[Gumaga Nigricula]] and [[Helicopsyche Borealis]]. The drought was associated with a proliferation of a few tolerant taxa.
- **Mid-May richness and diversity:** Richness was significantly reduced in the drought year and in high-rainfall years compared with the average or dry years (ANOVA, p < 0.001). Simpson's diversity was also reduced in those years.
- **Density and Chironomidae:** Macroinvertebrate density decreased as wet-season rainfall increased (r² = 0.38–0.39, p < 0.013). Chironomidae relative abundance rose with rainfall, from 39.9% in 1979 to 90.6% in 1982.
- **Peak discharge:** Higher peak discharge was linked to lower density and higher Chironomidae proportion. Longer storm-free periods were linked to higher richness.
- **Riffle versus pool:** Year-to-year differences were clearer in riffles (richness, diversity and Chironomidae all significant). In pools, only Simpson's diversity differed significantly, with the average year (1980) highest.
- **Clustering:** In mid May, the three wet and extremely wet years plus the average year formed one cluster. Riffle samples split into wet, normal/dry and drought clusters.
- **August stability:** No significant annual differences in density, richness, diversity or Chironomidae were detected in riffle-plus-pool samples, apparently due to high within-year variability. Riffle richness did differ, and pool density rose significantly in 1982. The authors suggest that August community structure is governed by "predictable, interactive succession" (Hildrew and Townsend 1987) and near-constant summer temperature.
- **Gumaga nigricula indicator:** The caddisfly was abundant in 1977 (34.4% of May individuals, 57.9% of August individuals), but was nearly absent after the 1982 and 1978 wet seasons, suggesting that even mild disturbance in 1979 and 1981 prevented it from dominating.
- **Predictive relationship:** With the 1977 drought excluded, wet-season rainfall was highly correlated with richness and [[Hydropsyche]] density in mid-May riffle samples (r² = 0.82–0.89, n = 12).
- **Monitoring implications:** Abiotic predictors can help separate perturbation effects from natural variability, especially in before-after monitoring where no control sites exist. Resh et al. (1988b) used these relationships to design sequential decision plans for geothermal monitoring.
