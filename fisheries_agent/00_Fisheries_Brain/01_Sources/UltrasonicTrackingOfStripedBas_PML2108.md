# Ultrasonic Tracking of Striped Bass Movements in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 report by W.L. Pelle and Larry J. Paulson (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) with Jon Sjoberg (Nevada Department of Wildlife) describes an ultrasonic telemetry study of [[Striped Bass]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. The study tested whether striped bass movements and habitat use were influenced by stocking of [[Rainbow Trout]], which were suspected to be preyed upon by large striped bass and linked to a decline in the trout fishery. Seventeen tagged fish were released in November 1992 and tracked until May 1993. Tag and mortality losses were high, and only four fish survived the full study. Striped bass detections were concentrated in USFWS stocking areas, but stocking frequency did not correlate with bass occurrence, and bass were never observed following the stocking barge. The authors conclude that trout stocking may concentrate bass only locally and temporarily, and that continuous stocking near the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] may foster large bass growth.

## Background & Description

- **Striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*)** were first found in Lake Mohave in 1981. They were never stocked there and are thought to have entered from [[Lake Mead]] through the upper intake towers and/or over the spillway. Their share of the total catch has increased since the early 1980s, and trophy-sized fish have been caught between Hoover Dam and Eldorado Canyon in recent years.
- **Rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*)** have been stocked below Hoover Dam since 1935 and do not reproduce naturally. The Lake Mohave trout fishery, once known for trophies (an Arizona state record of 9.2 kg was caught near Willow Beach in 1966), has declined since 1980. Reported catch-rate declines ranged from 42% (Bushman 1992) to 55% (Burrell 1991).
- **Stocking program:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) stocks seven regular locations by barge, and the [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] (NDOW) stocks four by truck. Black Canyon is stocked year-round due to stable low water temperatures; other areas are stocked January to May and November to December.
- **Telemetry method:** Fish were surgically implanted with Sonotronics CT-82-2 transmitters and tracked with a Sonotronics USR-4D receiver, using [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]].
- **Prior predation hypothesis:** Striped bass predation was previously suggested as the cause of a trout fishery crash in Lake Mead (Allen and Roden 1978) and as a possible cause of the Lake Mohave decline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and physical setting:** Lake Mohave, the third mainstream impoundment on the Colorado River (about 108 km long), was formed by Davis Dam in 1950. It has two parts: [[Black Canyon]] (upper 32 km), which is cold (11.0-12.5 °C) and fast-flowing (maximum 2.1 m/s), with limited pool and backwater habitat; and the slower, basin-rich Lake Mohave proper, beginning at El Dorado Canyon (river mile 43).
- **Water quality:** Dissolved oxygen was generally high (about 7 mg/l), dropping below 4 mg/l only in late summer near Davis Dam bottom water, and never below 3 mg/l. Surface temperatures reached 29 °C in Little Basin, but hypolimnetic temperatures never exceeded 17 °C. The lake becomes isothermal in winter.
- **Known behavioral drivers:** Earlier studies linked striped bass seasonal migration to temperature (about 22 °C optimum) and dissolved oxygen (minimum about 2 mg/l), with prey availability and inherent migratory behavior also implicated.
- **Study area constraints:** Tracking focused on the main channel, where most fish were found, and the area south of Cottonwood Basin was not searched until the third week.

## Key Findings

**Survival and tag loss**
- Mortality was estimated at 41% and tag loss at 29% of initial implants during the first three weeks. By January 12, 1993, only four fish were considered alive with transmitters.
- Possible explanations for apparent mortality include transmitter shedding, carcass drift to shore, and fish dying under cover. Transintestinal shedding was judged unlikely for this study because several days to a week is not long enough for encapsulation.

**Movement patterns**
- All fish moved rapidly downstream in the first two days after release.
- Average movement between tracking sessions dropped below 1 km/day through winter and rose slightly in early spring, with high variation throughout.
- Of the four survivors, fish #258 and #456 ranged between river mile 25 (Arizona Bay) and mile 35 (Aztec Wash) in winter, then moved upstream in spring. Fish #456 traveled at least 37 miles in one month to river mile 63 near Hoover Dam. Fish #276 and #97 stayed near the Willow Beach hatchery outflow (river mile 52.75) through winter, and #97 moved back downstream to river mile 26.75 in spring.
- The study found the bass did not follow the seasonal migration patterns reported by others, possibly because no part of the lake was thermally or oxygen-limited during the study.

**Habitat preference and stocking**
- USFWS regular stocking areas covered about 22% of the river/lake, NDOW areas about 13%, with about 5% overlap.
- Of 115 total detections, 41% were in USFWS regular stocking areas, significantly more than expected (χ² = 23.86, p < .005, df = 1). Detections in NDOW areas matched expectation (13%). Only 6% of detections were in recently stocked NDOW areas, significantly fewer than expected (χ² = 4.91, 0.025 < p < .05).
- Stocking frequency did not correlate significantly with striped bass occurrence (Spearman, R = .643, alpha = 0.05). The highest stocking count (177) occurred at area 7, which includes the Willow Beach hatchery outflow, and this area also had the most bass detections (38).
- No tagged bass were detected following the USFWS stocking barge, although on five tracked trips the barge passed nine tagged fish en route to stocking areas.
- Authors note that 55% of USFWS-area detections were near Willow Beach, largely from two fish (#276 and #97), which may reflect homing or a pre-existing preference, since these fish were caught near that site.

**Physiology and performance**
- Fish weights ranged from 3,180 g to 9,530 g (mean 6,082 g, SD 1,787); mean total length was 78 cm (SD 9); mean condition factor (K) was 1.2 (SD 0.1).
- Longevity ranged from 1 to 172 days. Movement index ranged from 0.5 to 31.8 km/day in the table data.
- Size and condition were not significantly correlated with longevity or distance traveled; the highest correlation was between longevity and condition (r = .195).

**Management implications**
- Trout stocking likely concentrates striped bass locally and only while trout remain abundant. Long-term concentration would require near-continuous stocking, as at Willow Beach.
- The authors suggest avoiding re-stocking of areas still holding trout from prior plants to reduce predation, but they also suggest Willow Beach may be the best site if augmenting the striped bass fishery is desired. They argue that large bass at Willow Beach may be grown there rather than drawn there.
- Future handling should use anesthesia and iodine-based disinfectant; size did not affect results and need not be a selection criterion.
