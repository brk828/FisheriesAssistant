# Environmental Research in the Klamath Basin, Oregon: 1992 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation annual report (Denver Office, September 1993, edited by S. G. Campbell) compiles multi-agency research on the Klamath Basin, Oregon, aimed at a basin-wide management plan for aquatic resources. Its focus is the Agency Lake sub-basin of [[Upper Klamath Lake]], where hypertrophic conditions, [[Harmful Algal Blooms]] dominated by [[Aphanizomenon Flos-Aquae]], and extreme pH and dissolved oxygen swings are considered likely contributors to the decline of two endangered catostomid fishes, the [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]]. The report covers hydrology and water quality of the [[Wood River]], Agency Lake limnology, nutrient and zooplankton enclosure experiments, wetland water quality, a literature review and fish faunal survey, and laboratory bioassays on juvenile suckers. The data are described as preliminary.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Upper Klamath Lake]] is a large, shallow lake (maximum surface area 36,000 ha; mean depth about 2.4 m) on the eastern slopes of the Cascades. It is split into the northern [[Agency Lake]] basin and the main basin, joined by a narrow channel. The region is semi-arid (mean annual rainfall 35 cm).
- **Species status:** Both sucker species were listed as endangered in July 1988 (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 1988). Catch data from the mid-1980s found most adult suckers were 18+ years old and juveniles were captured in very low numbers.
- **Trophic state:** The lake was historically eutrophic and is now classified as hypereutrophic. Each summer, blue-green algal blooms produce rank, odorous scums.
- **Management approach:** The project, "Basin-Wide Optimum Aquatic Resource Management" (WATER program), is planned in five phases from FY91 to FY99, moving from field research to modeling, pilot management projects, implementation, and a final management manual. Options include stream corridor fencing, irrigation conservation, detention ponds and strip wetlands, and limiting cattle access.
- **Participants:** [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[Klamath Tribes]], [[Oregon State University]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Fishery Contaminants Research Unit), and the U.S. Geological Survey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Eutrophication and algae.** Blooms are associated with high pH, low dissolved oxygen, high ammonia, and toxin production by *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*, which may contribute to sucker decline and to fish kills over the past two decades. Lake restoration by dredging or chemical treatment is considered impractical given the lake's size.

**Wood River inflow.** The Wood River is weakly buffered (bicarbonate alkalinity below 50 mg/L) with low ionic strength, so it is unlikely to lower lakewide pH in Agency Lake at the measured inflow. Localized pH reductions near the mouth could occur. Irrigation diversions and the 1992 drought strongly reduced flow.

**Drought.** 1992 was a below-average inflow year, comparable to the 1930s drought. Agency Lake depths were below average all year.

**Phytoplankton and zooplankton succession.** The spring bloom of diatoms and flagellates is followed in early June by an *Aphanizomenon* bloom, with *Daphnia pulicaria* (reported as *D. schodleri* in the 1992 annual data) as the dominant cladoceran. Peak Daphnia biomass coincides with the summer blue-green blooms.

**Fish fauna.** The survey and literature review cover native species (including lampreys, sturgeon, trouts, minnows, suckers, sculpins) and exotic fishes such as sunfish, perch, and catfish. The blue chub (*Gila coerulea*) was used as a zooplanktivorous fish in enclosure experiments.

## Key Findings

**Hydrology and water quality (Chapter 2, Campbell, Ehinger, Kann)**
- Wood River water temperature ranged 4.7–7.4 °C at the Dixon Road headwater and 2.3–16.8 °C at the Agency Dike Road mouth.
- Dissolved oxygen was 9.8–12.7 mg/L at the headwater and 8.9–10.8 mg/L at the mouth.
- Mean pH at the mouth was about 7.6. The Wood River inflow in the summer of 1992 averaged 493,709 m³/day, which would take 138 days to fill an empty Agency Lake basin.
- Orthophosphate averaged 74 µg/L at the headwater and 102 µg/L at the mouth. TKN averaged 40 µg/L and 203 µg/L respectively.
- Iron at the mouth ranged 131–295 µg/L, versus <5.0–40.9 µg/L at the headwater.
- Irrigation-season discharge at Weed Road was about 54% lower than in the non-irrigation season. The 1992 channel was almost completely dewatered in June and August at that site.
- Irrigation-season flows in 1991 and 1992 were about 50–60% below those recorded in the mid-1960s.

**Wood River nutrient export (Ehinger)**
- A 705-hectare (1742-acre) flood-irrigated pasture was sampled for net mass export over eight sampling dates between September 1991 and September 1992. Results were preliminary and presented without discussion pending recalculation.

**Agency Lake limnology, 1992 (Chapter 3, Kann)**
- Mean June–September pH was 8.78 at AS and 8.94 at AN. Water column pH peaked at 10.21 (AS) in mid-June.
- Late-afternoon dissolved oxygen reached 13–15 mg/L (180–200% saturation). Values fell below 5 mg/L for about 12-hour periods on numerous occasions.
- Mean June–September water temperature was 21.6 °C at AS and 21.2 °C at AN.
- Chlorophyll a at AS averaged 157 µg/L in June–September, inflated by a single 986 µg/L surface scum on June 17. July–September values were mostly below 50 µg/L.
- Total phosphorus was significantly related to chlorophyll a at AS (r² = 0.82, p < 0.0001).
- Summer SRP was lower in 1992 (AS June–September mean 61 µg/L) than in 1990 (99 µg/L) or 1991 (162 µg/L).
- *Aphanizomenon* dominated only from late May through June. Euglenophyta, Chlorophyta, Pyrophyta, and unidentified flagellates made up as much as 75% of biovolume in July–August.
- Zooplankton community: cladocerans were substantially lower in 1992 than in 1990 or 1991.

**Enclosure experiments (Chapter 4, Ehinger)**
- Six 4-L cubitainer experiments (1990–1991) tested nitrogen/phosphorus additions and *Daphnia pulicaria* gradients (5–125 individuals/L).
- Nitrogen limitation of chlorophyll a was seen three times, phosphorus twice, and both once.
- Of taxa analyzed, 40% were unaffected by Daphnia, 20% decreased, 33% responded unimodally, and 8% increased.
- Increasing growth rates involved colonial blue-greens (*Microcystis aeruginosa*, *Gloeotrichia echinulata*), with ammonia and soluble reactive phosphorus positively related to Daphnia biomass.
- The author concludes that nutrient limitation and grazing vary seasonally and that external phosphorus load reduction may be effective for lake restoration.
- A separate 1991 biomanipulation test found that Agency Lake is not a likely candidate for biomanipulation, given its hypertrophic state.

**Wetlands and marsh tributaries (Chapter 5, Sartoris, Sisneros, Campbell)**
- Water quality was measured in two irrigation return canals (7-Mile and 4-Mile) and two marsh tributaries (Crystal and Thomason Creeks) draining the [[National Wildlife Refuge]] on the west side of Agency Lake.
- Summary: details are presented in figures and appendices (benthic invertebrates, zooplankton, and Hanks Marsh surveys) and were not reproduced here.

**Literature review and fish survey (Chapters 6–7, Logan and Markle)**
- The literature review covers Upper Klamath Lake fish families from lampreys to sculpins, plus fish kills and stocking history.
- The 1992 faunal survey used plankton drift nets, beach seines, electrofishers, trammel nets, trap nets, upstream migrant traps, and underwater observations. Weight-length regressions were computed for multiple species.

**Sucker bioassays (Chapter 8, Monda and Saiki)**
- Juvenile Lost River and shortnose suckers were tested in single-variable tests (pH, un-ionized ammonia, temperature, dissolved oxygen) and multiple-variable tests combining high pH, temperature, and low oxygen.
- Results are reported as 96-h LC50 values and as observed versus expected mortalities. The full data tables were not reproduced here.
- The study was designed to identify lethal limits for conditions common during algal blooms.

**Conclusions and plans**
- Data are preliminary and subject to change. Each author is responsible for their own chapter.
- Future work includes statistical analysis of phytoplankton and limnological trends and a 1994 final report.
- The management strategy is to demonstrate options in the Agency Lake sub-basin before extrapolating to the whole Klamath Basin.
