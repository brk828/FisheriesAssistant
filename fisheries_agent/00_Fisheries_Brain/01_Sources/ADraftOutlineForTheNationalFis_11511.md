# National Fisheries Plan: Draft Outline (NMFS, August 1974)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This document is an interim discussion draft of a proposed [[National Fisheries Plan]] (NFP), issued by the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS), part of NOAA, in August 1974. It is not a final plan. It identifies major U.S. fisheries problems, sets out goals, and proposes numerous options for public review. Reviews were requested by January 15, 1975. The outline covers 23 major issues and 47 "application" summaries for specific fisheries, plus aquaculture and development proposals.

The core diagnosis is that U.S. fisheries are depleted and poorly managed. Management is fragmented among States, Federal authority beyond the territorial sea is unclear, and rising foreign fishing has eroded domestic harvests. The plan frames its mission as optimizing the economic, social and aesthetic values of fisheries while keeping stocks productive for the future.

## Background & Description

The NFP was developed in response to a recommendation by the [[National Advisory Committee on Oceans and Atmosphere]]. Its stated purpose is to coordinate existing State, Federal, industry and international programs into one plan of action. It excludes inland recreational and Great Lakes fisheries, which fall under separate Department of the Interior planning.

The mission is stated as: "To optimize the economic, social and aesthetic values of fisheries to the Nation consistent with maintaining fisheries resources for the future." Four goals follow:

1. Restore and maintain fish stocks of interest to the United States.
2. Develop and maintain a healthy commercial and recreational fishing industry.
3. Improve the contribution of marine resources to recreation and other social benefits.
4. Increase the supply of wholesome, competitively priced fisheries products.

Key baseline data from the document:

- U.S. tidal shoreline: 88,633 miles.
- Potential annual marine fish yield: 40.7 billion lb (18.5 MMT), over eight times the present catch, though not all is economically harvestable.
- Total catch (U.S. plus foreign fleets) rose from about 4.4 billion lb (2.0 MMT) in 1948 to 11.1 billion lb (5.0 MMT) in 1972.
- Over 2,000 fish species are recognized in U.S. and Canadian waters. About 170 finfish and 50 shellfish species are landed commercially; about 50 finfish species supply over 90% of finfish landings by weight.
- Commercial landings in 1973: 2.33 billion lb of food fish and 2.40 billion lb of industrial fish, valued at $907 million dockside.
- Marine anglers: 4.5 million (1955) to 9.6 million (1970). Over 30% are under 24.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The plan identifies two main threats to stocks. The first is overfishing, worsened by distant-water fleets, "pulse fishing" by foreign vessels, and the lack of effective management. The second is habitat loss from pollution, dredging, bulkheading, filling and coastal development.

Notable examples cited:

- **California sardine:** Landings averaged 1.13 billion lb (1934-1945), peaked at 1.5 billion lb (1936), fell to 9.5 million lb (1953), and effectively ceased by 1962, attributed largely to overfishing.
- **Haddock:** U.S. catches fell from 134 million lb (1966) to 26.8 million lb (1970) after foreign fleets arrived off New England.
- **Yellowfin sole (Bering Sea):** Rapidly depleted by intensive fishing and still at low levels.

The document estimates that stocks of over twenty major U.S. fisheries species are depleted to some degree, and it notes this may be an underestimate because data are often inadequate.

Estuaries are described as essential habitat for at least two-thirds of the U.S. commercial fish and shellfish harvest at some life stage, and nearly all sport-caught fish. The document states that roughly 60% of the 1973 U.S. catch by weight came from within 3 miles of shore.

## Key Findings

**Status and problems**

- Management is split: States regulate waters to 3 miles (and some extend control to 12 miles through landing laws), while the Federal role beyond the territorial sea is limited.
- Foreign fleets caught 6.16 billion lb of fish off U.S. coasts in 1972 (worth $487 million dockside).
- Productivity of the fishing industry has fallen 4-5% per year, while cattle and broiler productivity rose 5-8% per year. New England fishermen's real wages fell 26% from their 1966 peak.
- Employment in fishing, processing and wholesaling exceeded 220,000 (about 0.24% of the U.S. workforce). In Gloucester, Massachusetts, an estimated 21.1% of the 1972 workforce was engaged in fishing and related work.
- The fleet numbered 87,161 craft in 1973, of which 13,591 exceeded 5 tons.
- Insurance costs average 5-7% of vessel operating costs (excluding depreciation). The Jones Act and foreign-built vessel rules raise fleet costs.
- Spoilage may reach 5% of supply. On a $5.7 billion retail value, that is about $285 million per year.
- Only about 2% of fish processing plants participate in the voluntary federal inspection program.

**Recreation**

- Recreational anglers caught over 666 million fish in 1970 (about 1.58 billion lb), nearly one-third of the U.S. commercial catch.
- Anglers are projected to increase 60-100% by 1985.
- Recreational expenditures were at least $1.2 billion in 1970.
- Only 23% of U.S. shoreline (excluding Alaska) is publicly owned, limiting access.

**Economics and programs**

- The NMFS FY1974 budget was $60.2 million, about half for fisheries management. Sea Grant totaled $19.5 million, with about $6 million for living resources and aquaculture. Federal grants to states were $5.8 million. States were estimated to spend over $50 million annually.
- Marine fishing supports an estimated $7 billion per year.
- Recommended target: add 3 billion lb of U.S. harvest and 60-100% more recreational activity by 1985.

**Management options (Section 5.1)**

- **Management organization (5.1.1):** Eight options range from uniform state laws and interstate compacts to federal-regional councils, cooperative State-Federal management, and federal preemption. The document concludes a single uniform system is unlikely to suit every fishery.
- **International fisheries (5.1.2):** Extended jurisdiction to 200 miles threatens U.S. salmon, shrimp and tuna interests. About 23 million salmon could be taken by a Japanese mothership fleet in a 60-day spring period, versus an annual past Japanese harvest of about 2.5 million North American salmon. Only about 10% of the U.S. tuna catch is taken within 200 miles.
- **Anadromous species (5.1.3):** Pacific salmon management is complicated by mixed stocks, multiple jurisdictions and overcapitalization. Over 85% of the salmon catch comes from net fisheries.
- **Coastal fisheries (5.1.4):** Five options, including extending State-Federal programs and creating a national fisheries management council.
- **Scientific basis (5.1.5):** Calls for systematic resource assessment, noting stocks can now be overfished within two years of a fishery starting. Programs cited include MARMAP and MESA.
- **Allocation (5.1.6):** Four procedures are compared, from maintaining the status quo to shared decisions by user groups.
- **Funding (5.1.7):** Options include appropriated funds, fees, a fee on foreign entry, and a marine recreational license. Licensing in all coastal states could raise up to $47 million per year (9.5 million adult anglers at $5 each). Only 8 of 24 coastal states had such a license at that time.
- **Habitat (5.1.8-5.1.10):** Five options work through coastal zone plans, Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act reviews (about 40,000 federal actions per year, projected to rise to 61,000 in FY1975), NEPA impact statements (1,207 received in the year before May 31, 1973), and water resources planning. Habitat protection tools include marine sanctuaries and protected areas.
- **Economic regulation (5.1.11):** Limited entry, through license limitation, taxes and fees, or fishermen quotas, is presented as the main way to restore profitability. Canada, Alaska and Washington have already applied it to salmon.
- **Productivity (5.1.12):** Proposes improved product quality, mechanical deboning, shucking and crab picking R&D, cutting vessel search time, and reducing insurance costs. Eliminating net tariffs could cut costs 3-4%.
- **Marketing (5.1.13):** Options include marketing orders, centralized electronic listings, price support programs, cold storage loans, and cooperatives. The document also proposes a standardized naming advisory committee, given FDA labeling rules.
- **Recreational support industries (5.1.14):** Proposes a statistical survey of marine anglers (about $450K per year), an industry economic survey ($100K), and liaison offices.
- **Access (5.1.15):** Options include an access coordinator (about $75,000 per year), developing public shorelines (about $20,000 per site), and private rights-of-way (about $500,000).

**Application summaries (Section 5.2)**

The outline lists 47 species-specific proposals in five groups: management and recreational (8), aquaculture (7), fisheries development (32, including Northern anchovy, snow crab, Pacific hake and squid), and related items. Examples include maintaining billfish and trophy fisheries, rehabilitating Atlantic and Gulf menhaden, restoring river herring, and developing aquaculture of salmon, oysters and shrimp.

**Caveats**

The document states it is a first cut with limited headquarters review. Many cost estimates are marked "To be added," and no references are included yet. Options are not NMFS endorsements.
