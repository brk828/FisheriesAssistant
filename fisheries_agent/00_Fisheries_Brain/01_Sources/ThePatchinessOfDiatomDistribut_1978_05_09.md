## Executive Summary

This study investigates whether the visually apparent spatial "patchiness" of benthic biotic assemblages in a desert stream (Sycamore Creek, Arizona) corresponds to real differences in the underlying microbial community — using diatoms as a representative and ecologically important microorganism group. Seven macroscopically distinct patch types (ranging from exposed rock to bare sand to algae covered in brown gelatinous material) were sampled and their diatom assemblages compared using multivariate discriminant analysis, MANOVA, and Shannon diversity indices. The results demonstrate that macroscopic visual patchiness is a meaningful predictor of microscale diatom community composition, though most patch-type differences manifest as shifts in *relative abundance* rather than presence/absence of taxa. A single taxon, *Nitzschia fonticola*, was found to dominate and homogenize assemblages on multiple substrate types, reducing between-habitat diversity and blurring epilithic/epiphytic/epipelic distinctions where it proliferated.

## Background & Description

While this is a community-ecology paper rather than a species-morphology study, taxonomic and structural distinctions among diatoms are used to classify ecological guilds:

- **Total taxonomic richness**: 50 diatom taxa across 20 genera identified from 49 samples (7 replicates × 7 patch types), comparable to richness reported in other small Arizona streams.
- **Dominant taxon**: *Nitzschia fonticola* — the most abundant taxon overall, ranging from ~30% relative abundance (patch types A, B) up to 60.4% in patch type G (sand covered with golden-brown gelatinous material). Its frustule mass is directly linked to the visually distinct "brown gelatinous/flocculent material" patch types.
- **Habitat-restricted taxon**: *Cocconeis pediculus* — essentially confined to the *Cladophora glomerata* mat (patch type B, 7.5% relative abundance), consistent with prior reports of this species' affinity for attachment to aquatic macrophytes; nearly absent elsewhere (0% or trace).
- **Functional/morphological guild classification** (based on attachment mode and substrate affinity):
  - **Epilithic** (rock-attached): *Achnanthes* spp., *Amphora veneta*, *Gomphonema angustatum*, *G. tenellum*
  - **Epiphytic** (plant-attached): *Cocconeis* spp., *Gomphonema angustatum*, *G. parvulum*, *G. tenellum*, *Melosira varians*
  - **Epipelic** (free-living on sediment): *Gomphonema olivaceum*, *Navicula* spp., *Nitzschia* spp.
- Diatoms were cleaned via nitric acid digestion and mounted in Hyrax resin; relative abundances determined via frustule counts (500 per sample) at 1000× magnification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System**: Sycamore Creek is an intermittent desert stream draining the Mazatzal Mountains into the [[Verde River]], with median discharge of only 0.01 m³/s but flash-flood peaks exceeding 400 m³/s. During low flow, surface water is restricted to "spring" reaches where shallow alluvium overlies bedrock — the study reach in Round Valley is unshaded, sand-dominated, with cobble and exposed bedrock patches.
- **Patch types sampled**:
  - A — exposed rock
  - B — bright green *Cladophora glomerata* mat
  - C — *Cladophora* entangled with coarse brown material
  - D — *Cladophora* fully covered in brown gelatinous material
  - E — apparently bare sand
  - F — sand covered with brown flocculent material
  - G — sand covered with golden-brown gelatinous material
- **Substrate-guild correspondence**: Rock (A), *Cladophora* (B, C), and sand (E, F, G) substrates broadly map onto epilithic, epiphytic, and epipelic diatom guilds, respectively — supporting the classic substrate-based niche partitioning framework of [[Hodgkiss and Tai 1976]].
- **Successional/community convergence dynamic**: Patch types D and G (both dominated by *N. fonticola*-rich gelatinous overgrowth) converge in composition despite differing substrates (*Cladophora* vs. sand), suggesting a temporal overgrowth process that homogenizes community structure across substrate types as diatom biomass accumulates.
- **Highly autotrophic, unshaded desert stream** context is emphasized as the rationale for diatoms' presumed ecological importance as primary producers in this system.
- **Disturbance regime**: The stream's extreme discharge variability (flash flooding) is implicated in shaping an "unstable environment" hypothesis, where evenness of taxon distribution is more variable than species richness — linking to broader theory on diversity in disturbance-prone systems ([[Tramer 1975]]).

## Key Findings

- **Visual patchiness reflects real community structure**: MANOVA on the 20 most frequent taxa found significant differences among patch types (approximate F = 6.1; df 140, 145), despite substantial within-patch variation (median coefficient of variation ~25% for individual taxa). Canonical discriminant analysis showed 73% of among-group variance captured in the first two canonical variables, with all patch types distinct except D and G, which overlapped.
- **Differences are driven by relative abundance, not richness**: Nearly all 50 taxa occurred in all seven patch types (exceptions: *Cocconeis pediculus* and rare taxa <1% abundance). This indicates that desert stream diatom patchiness is primarily an **equitability/evenness** phenomenon rather than a species-presence phenomenon — a pattern the author links to instability in desert stream environments.
- **Nitzschia fonticola as a homogenizing dominant**: This taxon's abundance directly tracks the macroscopically visible "brown material" overgrowth on both *Cladophora* and sand substrates, and its dominance drives convergence between otherwise distinct epiphytic and epipelic assemblages (patch types D and G).
- **Diversity metrics**:
  - Mean within-patch-type diversity (Shannon H′) ranged from 1.55 (patch G) to 2.20 (patch E), with ANOVA confirming significant inequality among patch types (F = 11.5, df 6,42), attributable to the *N. fonticola*-dominated patches (D, F, G) having depressed diversity.
  - Total stream diversity (49 samples pooled): H′ = 2.21
  - Mean within-habitat diversity: H′ = 2.08
  - **Between-habitat diversity** = 6% of total diversity — notably lower than the 2–50% range reported for terrestrial vegetation by [[Pielou 1966]], attributed to high taxonomic similarity among patch types in this stream.
- **Practical/methodological implication**: Because visual patch types correspond to real compositional differences, a **stratified sampling design** is recommended over random sampling for efficiently characterizing stream microbial community structure.
- **Successional hypothesis**: The authors propose that diatom overgrowth over time (mass increase dominated by a single taxon like *N. fonticola*) may cause both within- and between-habitat diversity to *decline* with successional time in desert streams — a pattern contrary to classical ecological succession theory predicting increasing diversity over time ([[Margalef 1963]]; [[Odum 1969]]).
- **Broader relevance**: Findings support using diatoms as an efficient proxy for assessing microscale heterogeneity of periphyton/microbial assemblages in lotic systems, with implications for how "patchiness" indices could be standardized for comparative stream studies.
