# A guide to approved chemicals in fish production and fishery resource management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #fishery-management

## Executive Summary

This extension publication, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[National Fisheries Research Laboratory]] (La Crosse, Wisconsin) and the [[University of Arkansas Cooperative Extension Service]], is a reference guide to chemicals approved for use in [[Aquaculture]] and fishery resource management. It explains why chemical use requires care: a product that works may still kill non-target organisms, leave prohibited residues in fish flesh, soil, or water, or pose health hazards. The guide summarizes the U.S. regulatory framework administered by the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA) and the [[U.S. Food and Drug Administration]] (FDA), defines key regulatory terms, and provides six tables of registered or approved products. The listings reflect known approved compounds as of October 1, 1984. The authors state that recommendations become invalid if a registration is cancelled.

## Background & Description

Aquaculture is the production of aquatic animals in rearing units such as ponds, raceways, and tanks. It is one of the most intensive animal husbandry industries. Hundreds of thousands, and sometimes millions, of animals may be confined in one unit, so stress is common and disease spreads easily. Uncontrolled mild diseases can reach epizootic proportions and cause severe economic losses.

Chemicals serve several purposes:
- **Therapeutic uses:** [[Anesthetics]] reduce handling trauma; [[Disinfectants]] remove pathogens from gear and facilities; antiseptics, antibacterials, viricides, fungicides, and parasiticides control specific disease organisms.
- **Water and habitat management:** chemicals improve water quality, control algal blooms and vegetation, and remove nuisance organisms in pond and fishery systems.

Chemicals used for these purposes must not contaminate natural ecosystems. Treated water should be held until the compound degrades to nontoxic substances, is detoxified, or is removed by filtration.

**Regulatory framework:**
- Congress assigned EPA control of pesticides and FDA control of drugs; both agencies issue the rules.
- Control covers manufacture, bulk shipment, formulation, labeling, interstate shipment, applicators, application rates, use patterns, and permitted species. Only uses on the label, at the listed rates, are legal; both over- and under-application are illegal.
- FDA classifies species as "food fish" (e.g., trout, catfish, bass, bluegill), for which tolerances and residue data are required, or "nonfood fish" (bait and ornamental species such as [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]).
- FDA permits (Forms FD 1800 and 2656) are required for on-farm medicated feed production.
- Discharges from fish culture facilities are regulated under the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES).

**Key terms defined in the guide:**
- *Tolerance*: permitted residue level in human food.
- *Withdrawal time*: waiting period after treatment before an animal can be consumed.
- *GRAS* (Generally Recognized As Safe): substances FDA has found safe for human food.
- *NADA* (New Animal Drug Application): a sponsor's petition for FDA approval of a drug use.
- *Data Call-In Program*: requires pesticide registrants to supply EPA data.
- *Registration Standards* and *Special Review*: EPA processes for reassessing pesticides, which can end in reregistration, cancellation, restricted use, or hearings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guide stresses that many chemicals kill desirable organisms and alter water chemistry. Several recurring safety factors appear across the tables:
- **Water hardness and alkalinity:** Many copper and diquat products note that fish toxicity generally decreases as water hardness increases. Several warn against use in waters containing trout when [[Carbonate Hardness]] is below 50 ppm. Rotenone guidance is tied to total alkalinity bands (0–40 ppm, 40–60 ppm, 60–90 ppm, 90–200 ppm, above 200 ppm).
- **Oxygen depletion:** Decaying algae or vegetation can drop dissolved oxygen to lethal levels. Labels commonly require treating only one-third to one-half of a water body at a time, with 10–14 days between treatments.
- **Water-use restrictions:** Many products restrict use of treated water for irrigation, livestock, drinking, or swimming for set periods, commonly 7, 10, or 14 days.
- **Sensitive sites:** Several products are not for streams, running waters, public waters without agency consultation, or aquatic sites in Florida.
- **Non-target species:** Products used for mosquito control may be toxic to fish. Options include [[BT]] (*Bacillus thuringiensis* var. *israelensis*) and [[Methoprene]], which may be used on other aquatic pests under specified conditions.

## Key Findings

1. **Regulation requires strict label compliance.** Off-label use, unapproved compounds, or generic products that avoid registration costs can lead to disciplinary action, product removal, and environmental harm. Inspections by EPA and FDA have increased.

2. **Approved products are organized into six tables** (as of October 1, 1984):
   - **Table 1, Therapeutants (about 8 products):** includes [[Formalin]] (1,000–2,000 ppm for 1–10 min as a parasiticide, and 25 ppm in ponds), [[Oxytetracycline]] (Terramycin, for furunculosis in salmonids), [[Sulfadimethoxine-Ormetoprim]] (Romet-30, with a 0.1 ppm tolerance in salmonids and catfish), [[Sulfamerazine]], [[Trichlorfon]] (Masoten), and salt (sodium chloride) as an osmoregulatory enhancer.
   - **Table 2, Anesthetics (3 products):** carbon dioxide (200–400 ppm for 4 min), [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222, Finquel, with a 21-day withdrawal before harvest for food fish), and sodium bicarbonate (142–642 ppm for 5 min).
   - **Table 3, Disinfectants (2 products):** didecyl dimethyl ammonium chloride (Net-Dip) and calcium hypochlorite (Olin HTH Dry Chlorinator), used for equipment disinfection and for algae/bacteria control in ponds.
   - **Table 4, Water treatment compounds (4 products):** fluorescein sodium and rhodamine B/WT (flow-tracing dyes), lime (pond sterilant, about 1,338 lb/A quicklime), and potassium permanganate (oxidizer, 2 ppm).
   - **Table 5, Herbicides and algicides (about 60 products):** dominated by [[Copper Sulfate]] and elemental copper formulations (Cutrine, Algimycin, AV-70, Komeen, and others), [[Diquat Dibromide]] products (Aquathol, Reward-type diquat herbicides, Weed-O-Cide and many others), [[2,4-D]] formulations, [[Endothall]] (Hydrothol 191, Aquathol), [[Amitrole]] (Amitrol-T, Herbizole), simazine (Aquazine), and dichlone (Dichlone 50 WP). Amitrole is under EPA Special Review because it causes cancer in laboratory animals. Its use is limited to nonfood fish.
   - **Table 6, Fish control agents (about 25 products):** rotenone formulations (Chem-Fish, Noxfish, Prentox, Security Powdered Cube, and others), [[Antimycin]] (Fintrol-Concentrate), [[TFM]] (3-trifluoromethyl-4-nitrophenol) sea lamprey larvicides, and [[Bayluscide]] (niclosamide) for sea lampreys. Most are restricted to nonfood fish, and several require certified applicators or permits.

3. **Insecticides for mosquito control:** Approved options that are generally safe for fish include BT, methoprene (Altosid), mineral and petroleum oils, naled, and parathion. Products known to be toxic to fish include chlorpyrifos (Dursban), DDVP, fenthion, lindane, malathion, methoxychlor, pyrethroids, and temephos.

4. **Hazard warnings:** Sodium cyanide and Thanite may be used by state and federal biologists for collecting fish, but they must be used with extreme caution, never near domestic water supplies, and always in ventilated areas under supervision.

5. **Responsible agencies and resources:** Registration research is coordinated by the National Fishery Research Laboratory in La Crosse, Wisconsin, with work also at the National Fish Health Research Laboratory (Leetown, West Virginia) and the Fish Farming Experimental Station (Stuttgart, Arkansas). USDA's IR-4 project and state Cooperative Extension Services also participate. Technical questions go to the National Fishery Research Laboratory, and use patterns and water-chemistry effects are available from state Extension services.

6. **Conclusion:** The publication is a practical guide for fish farmers, hatchery managers, and fishery biologists. It emphasizes that a product's effectiveness does not establish that it is safe or legal, and that users must read labels carefully, observe withdrawal periods and water-use restrictions, and consult state fish and game agencies before treating public waters.
