# Desert Pupfish (Cyprinodon macularius) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan, prepared by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and Donald W. Sada for the Arizona Game and Fish Department (August 1992), addresses the [[Desert Pupfish]], listed as endangered throughout its range. The taxon comprises two subspecies: the [[Colorado River Pupfish]] (*C. m. macularius*) and the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*). The Quitobaquito form persists in a single native habitat, [[Quitobaquito Spring]] in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona.

The recovery objective is downlisting of the Colorado River subspecies from endangered to threatened. Recovery criteria require securing, maintaining and replicating all extant natural populations in a three-tier hierarchy, with downlisting expected in 2009 if criteria are met. The total estimated cost is US$1,272 thousand (excluding acquisition costs), and recovery is expected to take about 15 years after approval.

## Background & Description

The desert pupfish was described by [[Baird and Girard]] (1853) from specimens collected in the San Pedro River, Arizona. Historically it was widespread and abundant across southern Arizona, southeastern California, northern Baja California and Sonora, occupying cienegas, springs, streams and lake or river margins.

- **Morphology:** Body thickened and chubby or compressed; superior, protractile mouth with tricuspid jaw teeth; breeding males show orange-yellow to red caudal and anal fins and a dark ocellus on the dorsal fin.
- **Taxonomy:** Synonymies include *Cyprinodon m. californiensis* and *Lucania browni*, both now considered synonyms of *C. m. macularius*. The Quitobaquito form is distinguished by males with a longer, wider and deeper head and body, and by reduced scale size.
- **Genetics:** Allozyme analysis (Turner 1983) found heterozygosity within the range reported for the [[Killifish]] *Aphanius dispar*; some differentiation was detected among geographic areas and among the four Salton Sink populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Historically the species occupied the Gila River basin below about 500 m elevation, the lower Colorado River, the Salton Sink, the Rio Sonoyta and the Laguna Salada basin. Today natural populations persist in at least a dozen localities in the United States and Mexico, and at least 20 to 24 transplanted non-aquarium populations are extant.

**Habitat tolerance.** Desert pupfish tolerate water temperatures to 45°C, dissolved oxygen of 0.4 to 1.0 mg/L, and salinities about twice that of seawater (58 g/L), conditions that exceed the tolerances of nearly all other freshwater fishes.

**Reproduction.** Males are territorial during the breeding season (roughly spring to winter, when water exceeds about 20°C), defending territories of 4.5 to 6.0 m² in water usually less than 1 m deep. Females lay single eggs (about 2 mm) in random deposits within male territories, with no parental care. Consort-pair breeding occurs where territoriality is less advantageous.

**Growth and lifespan.** Sexual maturity can be reached at about six weeks at 1.5 cm total length. Maximum length is about 7.5 cm. Lifespan ranges from under one year to about three years.

**Diet.** Wild fish are opportunistic omnivores, feeding on algae, plants, small invertebrates and detritus; adults take ostracods, copepods, insects and [[Pit Digging]] for food.

**Co-occurring fishes.** The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) was a common co-habitant in cienegas and springs. Other native species such as the [[Gila Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]] usually inhabited deeper water with little interaction.

**Reasons for decline.** Principal threats include habitat loss (groundwater pumping, channel erosion, impoundment and diversion), livestock grazing, pollution, and especially introduced non-native fishes such as [[Mosquitofish]], [[Sailfin Molly]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Tilapia]] and [[African Cichlids]]. The [[American Bullfrog]] preys on pupfish; a population at Arizona State University was nearly eliminated by fewer than 20 adult bullfrogs over about a year. Aerial pesticide drift near Quitobaquito Spring and mercury in fish tissue near Cerro Prieto were also noted. Introduced [[Tamarisk]] may reduce water at critical times.

## Key Findings

**Status and objectives**
- Listed as endangered; downlisting of the Colorado River subspecies is the objective. Delisting is not considered feasible in the foreseeable future, and the Quitobaquito subspecies is not expected to be downlisted or delisted because of its limited range.

**Downlisting criteria**
- Natural populations in the United States and Mexico are secure at 12 known localities, grouped into five metapopulations (Salton Sink, Rio Sonoyta, El Doctor/Santa Clara Slough, Laguna Salada, Cerro Prieto).
- Populations are re-established and secured within probable historic range.
- A protocol for genetic exchange is implemented.
- Population and genetic monitoring plans are in place.
- A viable population is defined as at least 500 overwintering adults (or existing numbers, whichever is greater) with natural reproduction and recruitment.

**Action plan (Tasks 1–7)**
1. Protect natural populations: identify landowners, acquire habitat and water rights, and remove non-native fishes.
2. Re-establish populations in a three-tier system: Tier 1 (natural populations), Tier 2 (re-established in natural habitats), and Tier 3 (re-established in natural or quasi-natural refugia).
3. Establish a refugium population of the Quitobaquito form, using at least 500 fish from Quitobaquito Spring.
4. Monitor populations, with genetic sampling of about 50 fish per population at five-year intervals.
5. Determine factors affecting persistence and acquire life history and habitat criteria.
6. Inform the public through education, displays and training.

**Transplant history**
- More than 100 transplants of the Colorado River subspecies have occurred. Of 20 populations whose failure was documented in 1989, eight were due to desiccation, two to exotic fishes, one was renovated, and nine failed for unknown reasons.
- The Quitobaquito form has been stocked or transferred to nearly 30 other localities.

**Cost and timeline**
- Total cost: US$1,272 thousand (excluding acquisition costs). Downlisting is expected in 2009 if criteria are met, and about 15 years are estimated after approval.
