# Tidal Control and Wave Adaptation in Gulf Grunion Spawning, with Notes on Deep-Sea Rattail Life History

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atherinidae #macrouridae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt contains two fish studies. The main paper analyzes how tides and waves shape the spawning runs of the [[Gulf Grunion]] (*[[Leuresthes Sardina]]*) in the northern Gulf of California. The second, truncated paper (abstract and introduction only) describes the life history of the deep-sea rattail *[[Coryphaenoides Carapinus]]* in the North Atlantic. The excerpt also begins with a fragment of bibliography from an unrelated Blenniidae paper.

The grunion study concludes that Gulf grunion runs are driven mainly by tidal amplitude rather than by moon phase per se, and that the fast spawning act is an adaptation to short-period, low-amplitude waves rather than to avian predation.

## Background & Description

**Gulf grunion (*Leuresthes sardina*)** is one of only two grunion species. It is geographically isolated and morphologically distinct from the **[[California Grunion]] (*[[Leuresthes Tenuis]]*)**. Both species spawn out of the water, depositing eggs under beach sand after a high tide. The Gulf grunion differs in one striking way: it spawns in daylight as well as at night, whereas the California grunion spawns only at night.

Earlier work by [[Walker Egg Maturation Hypothesis|Walker (1949)]] proposed that California grunion runs follow an egg maturation period of 17.9 days, set by the second preceding moon phase. The authors note that no published work on grunion run periodicity had appeared since then. Their 1968–1973 study aimed to test whether these factors also explain Gulf grunion timing, in a different tidal and wave regime.

**Deep-sea rattail (*Coryphaenoides carapinus*)** belongs to the subgenus *[[Lionurus Subgenus]]* of a speciose genus. The authors note that published natural history for rattails is scarce, and that recent deep benthic trawling is only now providing the material needed for life-history work.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and tides.** Runs were observed on beaches of El Golfo de Santa Clara, Sonora, in the northern Gulf of California. Because local tide data were unavailable, runs were compared with predictions for [[Puerto Pefiasco]], which the authors found to be nearly synchronous with El Golfo tides but slightly lower in amplitude. The northern Gulf has a mixed semidiurnal tide regime, so the higher high tide shifts through the season.

**Seasonal and geographic range.** Gulf grunion runs occur from January to May, while California grunion runs usually run from early March to August. Fortnightly Gulf grunion runs are consistent around San Felipe and El Golfo de Santa Clara. Runs elsewhere along the upper Gulf (Bahía Concepción, Guaymas) are sporadic.

**Wave regime.** The protected northern Gulf has sea waves with periods of about 2–4 seconds (Gayman, 1969). The Pacific coast surf has longer periods, which carry California grunion far up the beach.

**Predators.** Brown pelicans, cormorants and several gull species feed on adult grunion near shore. Gulls feed most actively at the start of a run, when males are the main fish on the beach, and take little during peak spawning. Sanderlings and western sandpipers eat eggs after a run.

**Deep-sea habitat.** *Coryphaenoides carapinus* lives between about 1200 and 2800 m off southern New England. Its diet is dominated by benthic invertebrates, with selective feeding on the brittle star *Ophiura liungmani*. The authors suggest that the subgenera *Nematonurus*, *Chalinura* and *Lionurus* occupy overlapping but distinct adaptive zones, separated by prey and foraging area.

## Key Findings

### Grunion spawning and tides

- Analysis of **53 spawning runs** (1968–1973) in the northern Gulf of California suggests that run initiation depends strongly on tidal amplitude.
- Runs occur fortnightly from January to May, following a descending series of higher high tides after new and full moons. They begin about 3.5 days after full moon and about 4 days after new moon.
- The mid-season shift in higher high spring tides, from early morning (0300–0500) to late afternoon (1500–1700), coincides with the switch from night-only runs to day-and-night and then day-only runs.
- Runs were often observed on successive high tides while the height difference between consecutive highs was under 0.5 m, which the authors interpret as grunion "comparing" tide heights during a run series.
- Runs occurred mostly at tide heights of +4 to +3.4 m, and occasionally at extremes of +4.4 to +2.2 m.

### Moon phase versus tide height

- Under [[Walker Egg Maturation Hypothesis|Walker's]] scheme, full-moon-linked runs would be expected to lag new-moon runs, but the Gulf pattern is roughly the reverse. Full-moon runs began earlier.
- Days from moon phase to the preceding high tide were **4.06 ± 0.70** (new moon, N = 27) versus **3.36 ± 0.64** (full moon, N = 26), P < 0.001.
- Mean run intervals were **19.5 days** after full moon and **17.5 days** after new moon. The run-series midpoint averaged about **18.5 days** after either phase (range 17.4–19.8 days), close to Walker's 17.9 days.
- New moon tides were significantly higher than full moon tides (HHW maximum 5.34 ± 0.28 m vs 4.86 ± 0.19 m, P < 0.001).
- Tide heights at the start and end of runs were similar between new and full moon runs (differences of 0.21 m and 0.11 m), and showed no significant difference between night and day runs.
- The authors conclude that grunion respond directly to a descending high tide of the proper amplitude, not to moon phase, while not ruling out a lunar rhythm in gamete maturation.

### Spawning act and waves

- The spawning act in *L. sardina* lasts **3–7 seconds**, compared with about **30 seconds** in *L. tenuis*.
- Waves timed during Gulf runs had periods of **2.9–4 seconds**. When waves interrupted spawning, completion took a maximum of 7 seconds, usually 3–4 seconds.
- Storm waves sometimes prevented an expected run altogether.
- Run duration did not differ significantly between night (82.52 min, N = 21) and day (79.65 min, N = 17) runs.
- Predation pressure is judged an unlikely main driver of the rapid spawning act. The authors instead argue that it is primarily a response to the short-period, low-amplitude waves of the upper Gulf, where interruptions by waves would otherwise wash out eggs.

### Deep-sea rattail (*Coryphaenoides carapinus*)

- The species is most abundant at about **2.4 individuals per 1000 m²** in the transition zone from slope to rise.
- Larger individuals, mostly female, tend to live at greater depths.
- Winter spawning is indicated, with **50,000 to 220,000** 0.5-mm eggs produced per female.
- Of **108 stomachs** examined, **84 contained food**. Benthic invertebrates were the most important prey, and diet changed little with growth.
