## Executive Summary

This document is not a conventional species-level scientific paper but a 1993 internal memorandum authored by W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University, Department of Zoology) addressing the institutional and policy failures underlying native fish recovery efforts in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. Minckley critiques the fragmented, agency-driven approach to endangered species conservation under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA), and [[Clean Water Act]] (CWA), arguing that redundant, non-peer-reviewed agency science has failed to produce meaningful biodiversity outcomes. He proposes shifting from species-specific or ecosystem-scale conservation toward a **community-based conservation framework**, using multivariate statistical analysis of perennial stream data to identify priority conservation areas, conflict zones, and compromise areas. The memo directly references applying [[Peter Moyle]]'s natural-waters classification schemes and cites [[Aravaipa Creek]] as an exemplar "good" stream community.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although no specific species' ecology is detailed quantitatively, the memo articulates a conceptual ecological framework centered on **community-level indicators of habitat and stream integrity**:

- **Community persistence as an indicator of ecosystem health**: Minckley argues that the presence of a high proportion of expected native fish species (relative to historical/scientific baselines) in a stream reach indicates "good" ecological condition, while their absence signals degradation. [[Aravaipa Creek]] is cited as a benchmark "good" stream due to retention of much of its expected native fish assemblage.

- **Elevation- and stream-type-specific expectations**: Proposed naturalness metrics are explicitly relativized — the number of native species persisting should be divided by the number *expected* for a stream of a given type and elevation, rather than using raw native species counts.

- **Riparian-aquatic linkage**: The framework incorporates riparian value indices (proposed in collaboration with Dunc Patton and [[Julie Stromberg]]) alongside fish community metrics, recognizing riparian condition as integral to aquatic habitat quality.

- **Community vs. species vs. ecosystem conservation units**: The memo explicitly contrasts three conservation units — individual endangered species (politically contentious, narrow), full ecosystems (jurisdictionally unwieldy, hard to bound), and biological communities (flexible, cross-jurisdictional, less politically charged) — advocating the community as the most tractable unit for proactive management.

- **Multi-use conflict mapping**: Ecological value (endangered species presence/potential, riparian index) is proposed to be statistically compared against competing human-use values (recreational fishery indices from AZGF, hydropower/agricultural water use via SRP indices) to classify stream reaches into conservation priority categories.

## Key Findings

- **Institutional fragmentation** among USFWS, USBR, USBLM, USFS, and state agencies has produced redundant, poorly synthesized, and rarely peer-reviewed environmental data, undermining sound policy formulation for endangered species recovery.
- **Regional inconsistency within USFWS** is noted, with Region 6 (Denver) criticized more strongly than Region 2 (Albuquerque) for prioritizing stakeholder interests over species/habitat recovery mandates.
- **Career and institutional inertia** within agencies pushes trained scientists into non-scientific administrative roles, degrading the scientific rigor of environmental management ("a continuing compilation of data but no synthesis").
- **Proposed solution**: Adopt a **community conservation approach** (analogous to the U.S. Forest Service's "Bring Back the Natives" program) rather than pursuing single-species (ESA-driven) or full-ecosystem (CWA/NEPA-driven) strategies, as communities are argued to be more resistant to jurisdictional conflict and stakeholder backlash.
- **Proposed methodology**: Use an existing compilation of Arizona perennial streams (Brown and Carmony map) scored across five weighted criteria — (a) native fish community naturalness index, (b) endangered species presence/recovery potential, (c) riparian value index, (d) recreational fishery value, (e) agricultural/power water-use value — subjected to multivariate statistical analysis to classify stream reaches into: **no-conflict conservation priorities**, **high-conflict "war zones,"** and **intermediate compromise areas**.
- **Comparative validation** proposed against [[Peter Moyle]]'s aquatic preserve classification criteria to test the quantitative approach's validity.
- **Practical/funding note**: The author recommends seeking seed funding independent of any single agency to preserve programmatic neutrality, referencing constraints tied to a 1993 work-study hiring deadline (16 August 1993) and associated budget figures (~$1344 for two semesters at $7.00/hour, plus 5% administrative overhead).
- **Underlying philosophy**: Minckley asserts that true habitat "naturalness" in the American West is no longer attainable without active, informed human intervention — passive "set-aside" preserves alone will not sustain native fish biodiversity in a heavily altered landscape.
