# San Pedro River Groundwater Depletion and the Expansion of Fort Huachuca: A 1994 Public Comment Dossier

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a September 3, 1994 public comment letter from the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]] (SWCBD) to the U.S. Army Garrison at [[Fort Huachuca]], Arizona. It responds to the Federal Register notice of May 19, 1994 announcing a [[Programmatic Draft Environmental Impact Statement]] (PDEIS) for the Master Plan Update at the U.S. Army Intelligence Center and Fort Huachuca. The letter argues that groundwater pumping for the fort and the surrounding [[Sierra Vista]] area is dewatering the [[San Pedro River]], threatening its riparian ecosystem and federally listed fishes. It requests that a detailed chronology of supporting studies, agency statements, court rulings and legislation be included in the official EIS. Most of the content is a dated chronology (1967–1994) of scientific reports, agency correspondence, congressional records and litigation notices. The letter's conclusions are the author's advocacy; the quoted sources are the evidence presented.

## Background & Description

The San Pedro River is described as one of the last relatively intact riparian ecosystems in the Southwest. The letter cites 489 species of birds, mammals, fish, amphibians and reptiles, 24 of which need federal or state protection. It highlights the [[Cottonwood-Willow Riparian Forest]], described as the most extensive surviving example of its forest type in North America.

Fishes are a central concern. A [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) report by W.L. Minckley (1987) states that of 18 native fish species known from the [[Gila River Basin]], one is extinct and 10 are listed as threatened, endangered or of special concern. The San Pedro supported at least 13 of these fishes historically, and eight persist as remnant populations. Listed species named in the document include:

- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): listed endangered, March 11, 1967
- [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*): listed endangered, March 11, 1967
- [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*): listed endangered, March 31, 1967
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*): listed threatened, July 1, 1986; proposed for reclassification to endangered, July 11, 1994
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*): listed threatened, 1986; proposed for reclassification to endangered, July 11, 1994
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): listed endangered, October 23, 1991

The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*), a bird, was described as endangered in 1987 by Philip Unitt and was proposed for federal listing in July 1993. The 1993 proposal included about 87 km (54 miles) of the San Pedro from Hereford Bridge to Benson as critical habitat. The letter notes that as much as 90 percent of Arizona's lowland riparian habitat has been lost.

The [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] (SPRNCA) was established by the Arizona-Idaho Conservation Act of 1988 (Public Law 100-696, November 18, 1988) to protect the riparian, aquatic and wildlife resources along about 31 miles of river. The area covers 56,431 acres of BLM land, largely acquired from Tenneco in 1986.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and groundwater.** The sources describe the San Pedro as dependent on groundwater discharge. A 1974 U.S. Army Corps of Engineers report states that groundwater emerges as base flow and maintains a perennial reach near Charleston. The river is perennial between Hereford and Fairbank, always perennial near Charleston and intermittent elsewhere. Base flows are very low, as little as 0.5 cubic feet per second in some months (BLM, 1987).

**Aquifer and recharge figures.** Reported values include:

- A 1978 steady-state model (Freethey, USGS Open-File Report 82-752, 1982) estimated total recharge and discharge of about 16,500 acre-feet per year, with 75 percent of recharge from mountain runoff and 19 percent from underflow from Mexico.
- In the 1977 model, 10,500 acre-feet of pumpage was simulated, of which about 5,600 acre-feet (about 53 percent) came from storage.
- The 1985 model update estimated about 18,000 acre-feet pumped, of which 10,700 acre-feet (about 59 percent) came from storage.
- A 1991 Fort Huachuca EIS section cited groundwater reserves of 36 million acre-feet and recharge of 35,000 acre-feet per year.

**Cones of depression.** The Sierra Vista–Fort Huachuca cone of depression was about 4 miles long and 1.5 miles wide in 1974 and grew to about 7.5 square miles by 1986 (ADWR, 1988). Average decline near Sierra Vista was 1.4 feet per year from 1968 to 1986, with maxima of 3.7–3.9 feet per year locally. A projection to 2000 estimated an additional 208,000 acre-feet mined from the regional aquifer, with a maximum decline of about 80 feet.

**Stream–aquifer interaction.** A 1992 University of Arizona report found that streamflow increased about 26 percent between Hereford and Lewis Springs and about 71 percent between Lewis Springs and Charleston Bridge, with model baseflow gains of about 80 percent between Hereford and Charleston. The report also estimated a net loss of 15,669 acre-feet per year from the regional aquifer. Dr. Maddock's model predicted that 37 percent of water from the Sierra Vista well systems comes from the San Pedro. A Charleston stream gage's future funding was reported as uncertain in July 1994.

**Fort Huachuca water use.** In the 1992 Final Supplemental EIS, the installation pumped about 2.90 million gallons per day (about 3,250 acre-feet per year) from eight high-capacity wells. The BRAC 91 realignment was projected to raise consumption by 20 percent, to 3.5 mgd. The EIS concluded this would not significantly affect groundwater. Projected drawdown in 2038 ranged from 72 feet (Well No. 8) to 223 feet (Wells 1 and 2), according to the Arizona Department of Water Resources (ADWR) 1991 report.

**Wildlife.** Reported figures include about 150 breeding or migrant bird species (1984), over 200 bird species and at least 20 raptor species (Rep. Udall, 1987), about 40 pairs of gray hawk in the United States (11 nesting along the San Pedro), and the widest mammal diversity in the country (Rosenzweig, 1984). The Fort Huachuca cantonment area reportedly holds 450 acres of wetlands (BRAC 93 assessment), though earlier BRAC assessments described wetland information as unavailable.

## Key Findings

- **Groundwater overdraft is reducing river flow.** Multiple agencies and studies (USDA 1977, USGS 1982, BLM 1987, ADWR 1988, University of Arizona 1992) document hydrologic connection between basin-fill pumping, the floodplain aquifer and river flow. The 1994 documents cite the [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] hydrologist Kraeger-Rovey (December 20, 1993) that "there is no sustainable pumping rate" for Sierra Vista and Fort Huachuca wells that would avoid removing water from storage.
- **Agency warnings were issued repeatedly.** Warnings from the U.S. Department of the Interior Regional Environmental Officer (July 27, 1992), the Interior Field Solicitor (November 17, 1993, citing "catastrophic effect" on the river) and a Technical Review Committee (July 5, 1994, stating that "overdraft is already diminishing the flow of the San Pedro River") are quoted.
- **Legal ruling on subflow.** In *In re the General Adjudication of All Rights to Use Water in the Gila River System and Source* (Superior Court, June 30, 1994), Judge Goodfarb defined the "subflow" zone as the saturated floodplain Holocene alluvium. The court held that wells outside this zone that pump from the stream or its subflow should be included in the adjudication. It also held that cones of depression can have long-term effects after pumping stops.
- **Policy shift criticized.** The letter criticizes ADWR's September 29, 1993 change in water-adequacy policy for subdivisions, saying the supporting documents showed no scientific or hydrological basis. A 1984 ADWR refusal to certify a 100-year water supply for the Tenneco project is cited as precedent.
- **Environmental review criticized as inadequate.** The letter argues that the August 1992 Final Supplemental EIS found "no significant environmental impacts," failed to address cumulative effects, and that the Master Plan EIS promised for 1993 was delayed. It states that the Army has "endeavored to avoid" examination of these effects.
- **Recommended alternative.** SWCBD proposes that, with eventual downsizing or closure of Fort Huachuca, the SPRNCA be expanded to include the present cantonment area, creating a corridor between the Huachuca Mountains and the San Pedro lowlands. The letter also recommends that the BLM restore groundwater, streamflow and precipitation monitoring in the riparian corridor, which the TRC says was discontinued.
- **Legal action.** SWCBD filed a lawsuit against the Department of Defense and Fort Huachuca on August 8, 1994, seeking a programmatic EIS. Notices of litigation under the [[Endangered Species Act]] were filed on July 30, 1994 (against the Interior, Fish and Wildlife Service and Bureau of Reclamation over the [[Central Arizona Project]]) and on August 16, 1994 (against the Defense Department, Interior, HUD, FHA and VA over dewatering of the river). These notices cite spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, desert pupfish, razorback sucker and southwestern willow flycatcher.
- **Military and congressional context.** The document reproduces BRAC 89, 91 and 93 Army summaries, which state the environmental impacts of realignment were minimal, and congressional statements praising the San Pedro's values. These are included as evidence that the Army's assessments did not address the cumulative effects of groundwater depletion.
