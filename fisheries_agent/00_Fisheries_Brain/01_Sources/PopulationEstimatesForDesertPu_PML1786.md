# Desert Pupfish Refugia Population Estimates, 2005

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service report by C.O. Minckley (Arizona Fishery Resources Office-Parker, September 2005) gives mark-recapture population estimates for two constructed refuge populations of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]], *Cyprinodon macularius macularius*, sampled in July 2005. The estimates were about 1,086 to 1,096 fish (>25 mm) at [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] (CNWR) and 137 fish (>25 mm) at [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] (INWR). Water quality was judged adequate, fish condition was good, and the refugia are to be maintained with annual summer surveys.

## Background & Description

**Species status.** The desert pupfish was once widespread and abundant in southern Arizona. Habitat destruction and exotic fishes caused the extirpation of all natural Arizona populations by the mid-1960s. Remaining natural populations occur near the [[Salton Sea]] in California and at several localities on the lower [[Colorado River]] in Mexico. The species was listed as endangered in 1986, and the [[Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan]] (1992) calls for securing extant populations and re-establishing replicate populations in natural habitats within the probable historic range.

**Source stock.** The refuge stock originated from the [[El Doctor Marshes]] in Sonora, Mexico, collected in the early 1970s by W.L. Minckley of Arizona State University. The marshes are reached south of Yuma, Arizona, and are recognized by cottonwood and tamarisk trees growing beside the springs.

**Cibola refuge pond (CNWR).** The pond is 10 m × 5 m, concrete-lined, and 0.5–1 m deep. It has river gravel and piles of large rocks for cover and is supplied with unchlorinated water from the refuge system. On 17 September 1999, 37 pupfish were moved from the cheetah moat at the [[Phoenix Zoo]] to CNWR; the moat population has since been lost.

**Imperial refuge pond (INWR).** The pond is 5 m × 5 m, plastic-lined, and up to 1 m deep, about 100 m from refuge headquarters. It has a river gravel bottom, perimeter [[Palo Verde]] and catclaw acacia, and cattails. It is supplied with unchlorinated water. On 6 March 2000, 23 pupfish from CNWR were introduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Fish were sampled with 12 unbaited [[Minnow Traps]] set in a 4 × 3 grid in each pond, fished from 0600 to 1200 hrs. INWR was sampled on 28–29 July and CNWR on 26–27 July 2005. On day 1, fish were counted, measured for total length, marked by clipping the anal fin, and released. On day 2, fish were recaptured, counted, checked for fin clips, and released. Estimates used the [[Seber Estimator]] (Seber 1982), and 95% confidence limits assumed a normal distribution.

**Water quality.** [[Water Quality]] was measured with a Quanta Hydrolab in the morning and afternoon, at the surface and at 1 m depth.

**Historical and comparative context.**
- The 2005 estimates are the third set for these populations (previous reports: Minckley 2002, 2004). The first CNWR estimate (October 2001) was 689 fish; INWR trapping in October 2001 produced no marked fish, so no estimate was possible.
- In 2004, estimates for fish >30 mm were 2,578 at CNWR and 135 at INWR.
- The only natural Arizona population is at [[Quitobaquito Spring]], Organ Pipe National Monument, home of the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon eremus*). A one-day census over nine years found 1,033 to 5,252 fish (Tibbets 2003). Douglas et al. (2001) estimated 9,556 fish for 1998–1999.
- The CNWR estimate falls within the range reported for Quitobaquito. The author notes that no direct comparison is possible.

- The text gives SE 39 and 95% CI 1,109–1,163 for CNWR. The table gives SE 20 and CI ±400 for the same estimate. The CNWR interval in the text does not contain the stated estimate, so the value should be verified against the original data.
- For INWR, the text gives SE 11 and 95% CI 116–157. The table gives SE 10 and CI ±20.
- Hundreds of individuals under 25 mm were seen visually at both sites but were not counted.

**Fish condition and size.** Fish looked healthy on visual inspection. INWR fish averaged larger, while the smallest fish at both sites were similar in size. The largest fish measured was 62 mm TL at CNWR.

**Water quality.**
- INWR dissolved oxygen was low in the morning (about 1–1.5 ppm) and rose by afternoon (text reads "@.14.7ppm," likely 4.1–4.7 ppm).
- CNWR morning oxygen was higher (about 4.5–5.1 ppm) and rose to 7.2–7.5 ppm in the afternoon.
- Water temperatures were elevated (about 30.5–35.8 °C) but within the range pupfish tolerate.
- Reported pH was about 9.0–9.4, and conductivity about 2.1–3.4 (units not stated). Table 2 is partly garbled in the source.

**Conclusions and management.**
- The author concludes that water quality and fish condition were good, and that both refugia will be maintained.
- Fish from one or both sites, and possibly Dexter NFHTC, will be used to establish future refuges.
- An annual summer population estimate will be made at each site.
- If wild fish can be obtained from Mexico, they will be mixed with these and other populations to maintain genetic heterogeneity.
- A possible future step is to compare artificial habitats to identify what makes a successful refuge.
