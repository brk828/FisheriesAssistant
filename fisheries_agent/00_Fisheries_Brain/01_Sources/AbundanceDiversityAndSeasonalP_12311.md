# Abundance, Diversity and Seasonal Patterns of Estuarine Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-fish-assemblage

## Executive Summary

This 1973 study by McErlean, O'Connor, Mihursky and Gibson, published in *Estuarine and Coastal Marine Science*, analyzes four years of quarterly fish collections from the middle [[Patuxent Estuary]] in Maryland. Three gears were used ([[Haul Seine]], [[Beam Trawl]] and [[Otter Trawl]]) at 50 stations. The authors tested whether heated effluent from the [[Chalk Point Steam Electric Station]] altered fish assemblages, and tracked the trends of several community indices over time.

No marked effect of the power station was detected, although occasional temporal shifts occurred. All indices showed strong seasonal cycles tied to the estuary's nursery function and migration. However, regression trends for species number, abundance-based indices and diversity measures all declined over the study period. The authors interpret this as a real loss of species and a shift in population structure, and warn that the system may become less buffered and more prone to population "crashes and booms."

## Background & Description

The study was motivated by growing estuarine use, particularly as a receiving medium for waste heat from coastal power plants. Earlier fish surveys in Virginia, Delaware, Florida, Long Island Sound and New England were reviewed. Most had limited temporal coverage (a single seasonal cycle or one gear), with exceptions such as [[Massmann et al. 1952]] (three years, several gears) and Gunter (1938, two and a half years).

Methodologically, the authors combined several community indices:

- **Number of species (S)** and **log abundance (log N)**, corrected to a common effort unit.
- **Species richness index (S-1)**, after [[Margalef Index]] (1951), which emphasizes species number over abundance.
- **Information theory index (H)**, a Shannon–Weaver measure that weights species by their relative abundance and reflects population structure.
- **Evenness (J)**, the ratio of observed H to its theoretical maximum, after [[Pielou Evenness]] (1966).

Sampling used 50 ft haul seines (three 60 ft hauls per sample), a 10 x 2 ft shallow-water beam trawl (two hauls per station) and a 40 ft otter trawl with approximately 1320 ft tows. Catches were standardized to estimated square feet sampled. Data were displayed as three-dimensional grids (five West–East by ten South–North coordinates, with the parameter as the vertical axis). Of 70 plots prepared, four seasonal representatives were shown in the report.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nursery function.** The authors emphasize that estuarine shore and shelf zones serve as nurseries, providing food and protection for juveniles. Resident species such as [[Atlantic Silverside]] and [[Killifish]] (*Fundulus* and *Menidia*) dominated shore collections, while seasonal migrants added to the spring and summer peaks.

**Seasonal cycle.** Warm-season collections had higher species numbers and richness, with highest values on the shores. Cold-season collections shifted toward the channel and up-river areas. Winter fish appeared to concentrate in deeper holes, although the authors noted that this tendency was flexible and that fish could instead accept warmer effluent water.

**Thermal effects.** The authors discuss the risks of fish entering intake structures, residing in effluent canals, and suffering cold shock if the station suspended operation. They also note that winter abundance near the effluent was linked to over-wintering resident species, including [[White Perch]], as documented by a creel census (Moore & Frisbie).

**Possible drivers of decline.** The authors suggested sedimentation, organic enrichment, drought cycles, habitat alteration and other human pressures, singly or combined, as candidate causes of the diversity decline. They did not identify a single factor.

## Key Findings

1. **Sampling scale:** Four years of quarterly sampling, three gears, 50 stations; 70 three-dimensional plots prepared (14 per parameter).
2. **Power plant effect:** No marked or consistent effect of the [[Chalk Point Steam Electric Station]] on S, log N, S-1, H or J. Occasional depressions or peaks near the effluent were noted, but these were irregular and inconsistent between years. Winter data (1967 H and some abundance patterns) hinted at possible effects, but the evidence was marginal.
3. **Strong seasonality:** All parameters showed regular annual cycles, with warm-season peaks in species number, richness and H, and fall/winter lows. The authors describe these cycles as potentially useful for prediction.
4. **Downward trends:** Regression trends declined for number of species, species richness (S-1), information diversity (H) and evenness (J). Log abundance showed only a slight downward tendency. Evenness also showed an increasing seasonal amplitude over time, mainly from lower cold-season values.
5. **Dominance shifts:** The dominant species, white perch and hogchoker, remained high or were increasing. Losses therefore appear to involve migrants and less common species, consistent with a shift toward dominance by residents.
6. **Interpretation:** The authors conclude that a real loss of species and diversity has occurred, independent of the power station. They describe this as "overwhelming" evidence of time-related losses, and warn of reduced structural complexity, shortened food chains and greater risk of population crashes and booms.
7. **Methodological caveats:** Log transformation was used to reduce the influence of extreme numerical variation. The authors acknowledged that diversity analysis had been criticized (Hurlbert 1971) and that the normal range of H values for estuarine fish was unknown. The dataset was described as the only baseline for self-comparison, with raw data available on request.
8. **Recommendations:** Continued monitoring to separate natural fluctuation from man-induced change; biomass-per-species analysis to refine abundance trends; and further research on migratory behavior to inform plant operating schedules and prevent fish kills.
