# West Turkey Creek Watershed Restoration and Enhancement: Habitat Conservation Plan and Project Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #ichthyology

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of materials for a West Turkey Creek (WTC) watershed project in Cochise County, Arizona. It contains (1) a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Environmental Assessment/Habitat Conservation Plan (EA/HCP) for a 25-year [[Section 10(a)(1)(B) Incidental Take Permit]] covering incidental take of [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Topminnow]] and other [[Rio Yaqui Fishes]] during ranching on [[El Coronado Ranch]] and associated [[USFS Turkey Creek Allotment]]; (2) the applicants' narrative proposal to the [[Arizona Water Protection Fund]] (AWPF) for a two-year watershed restoration, monitoring and GIS mapping program centered on rock erosion-control structures (gabions); (3) supporting forms, budgets, a land management plan, a 1995 USFS grazing plan and a newspaper article on the 1994 [[Rattlesnake Fire]].

The proposed (preferred) alternative is direct issuance of the permit with an HCP of habitat maintenance, water-level management, grazing adjustments, watershed protection and access for monitoring. The EA concludes that the net effect on affected fishes is positive, with some individual losses expected. The AWPF request totals $84,700 against a project budget of $141,100 over two years.

## Background & Description

**Applicants and setting.** Josiah (Joe) and Valer Austin own El Coronado Ranch (ECR), a cow/calf operation of 15,193 acres. Of this, 13,278 acres are Coronado National Forest land and 1,920 acres are deeded ranch land. The ranch covers the entire Turkey Creek watershed plus part of the upper Rock Creek drainage. Elevation ranges from 5,420 ft (where Turkey Creek leaves private land) to 9,600 ft on Chiricahua Peak. The average annual precipitation is 19.0 inches. The project area is roughly 8 miles of mainstem stream, with an upstream extent of 9,000 ft and a downstream extent of 5,200 ft.

**Fish species of concern.** The EA identifies primary and secondary concerns:
- Primary: [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*), both federally listed as endangered; [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), whose Rio Yaqui form is the only known stock persisting in the Sulphur Springs Valley.
- Potential additions: [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*), [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*) and [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*). The document states four of the six species (chub, shiner, catfish, topminnow) are already listed as threatened or endangered; the text does not state which species beyond chub and topminnow are listed.
- Non-indigenous fishes that threaten native fish: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Also noted: [[Bullfrog]] and [[Tiger Salamander]].

**Vegetation and land use.** Vegetation grades from desert grassland at low elevation, through Madrean evergreen woodland and chaparral (the dominant upslope association in WTC), to pine and mixed-conifer forest at high elevations. Grazing has reduced ground cover and promoted arroyo cutting. The ranch is managed under a "Best Pasture Management" rotation. The USFS Turkey Creek Allotment is permitted for 66 cattle (3/01–2/28) and 25 cattle (9/18–12/15), totaling 867 animal months (AUMs). Recreation includes a trailhead, two developed campgrounds and fourteen cabins under special-use permits.

**Legal framework.** The EA is prepared under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, section 10(a)(1)(B), implemented by 50 CFR 17.22. The HCP has a 25-year permit term. Its terms are enforceable under section 11(b) after permit expiration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Aquatic scarcity.** Bedrock lies near the surface, so runoff is rapid with little groundwater storage. Most channels are ephemeral, and the mainstem WTC becomes intermittent during drought. Aquatic diversity is far lower than terrestrial diversity.
- **Sky island biogeography.** The Chiricahua Mountains support many rare or imperiled species because of their proximity to Mexico and habitat diversity. Table 1 of the EA lists sensitive species for Cochise County (not reproduced in the source text in full).
- **Rattlesnake Fire (1994).** A monthlong lightning-sparked fire in summer 1994 burned 27,500 acres in the upper watershed on USFS land. Subsequent ash, soot and sediment runoff "essentially eliminated fishes and most other aquatic organisms" from the stream and ponds. Native fishes reinvaded from unidentified refugia and are now reproducing.
- **Wetlands.** Section 404 jurisdiction covers areas at or below the ordinary high-water mark. The HCP requires no new construction within the waterway.
- **Cultural resources.** No National Register properties or known archaeological sites exist on ECR or the allotments. The SHPO certification (dated 27 July 1995) is signed for the activity.
- **Erosion-control structures (gabions).** Hundreds of rock retaining structures have been built across ephemeral tributaries since 1987. The Rattlesnake Fire led to 243 loose-rock check dams being built in Saulsberry Canyon on USFS land in the following August.

## Key Findings

### Proposed Action and HCP
- **Preferred alternative:** Direct issuance of a section 10(a)(1)(B) permit with a habitat-oriented HCP. It is expected to allow continued ranching while offsetting impacts to species of concern.
- **Rejected alternatives:**
  - *Removal of chubs and topminnows from ranch:* reduces habitat for recovery and is unacceptable.
  - *Petition to downlist or delist:* unlikely to be accepted, because downlisting criteria are unmet and delisting is not foreseeable given the species' limited U.S. range.
  - *Await a Regional permit:* the Service has no HCP scheduled for WTC, so delay is unreasonable.
  - *No action:* results in unreasonable restrictions on conservation and land use.
- **HCP mitigation measures:**
  - Routine maintenance of diversions, canals, ponds and outflow structures.
  - Maintaining pond water levels for oxygen and water supply.
  - Grazing plan to minimize impacts on riparian and aquatic habitat, developed with USFS, AZGFD and USFWS.
  - Prohibiting adverse watershed modifications.
  - Access for resource personnel for monitoring and fish translocation.
  - Annual review meetings and an amendment procedure.

### Take Projections
- Tens of individuals of all three species may die during routine operations from injury, canal diversion and habitat loss.
- Hundreds may be lost when canals or ponds are dried for maintenance.
- Thousands could be lost in catastrophe (pond failure, sustained drought, extensive fire, or predation and competition from non-indigenous species).
- Refugium stocks are expected to persist throughout the 25-year HCP.
- The EA states that the Yaqui chub has proven highly resilient, rebounding "from a few tens to thousands of individuals in a matter of weeks or months." Recent surveys confirmed recruitment of dace and chubs in WTC ponds soon after the Rattlesnake Fire.

### Environmental Consequences
- **Onsite:** net effect on listed fishes is positive. Water quality is enhanced by sediment trapping, and stream reach of permanent flow has lengthened over the past decade.
- **Offsite:** population-level impacts are expected to be neutral or beneficial, though individual fish may be negatively affected by downstream movement and loss during drought. Recreational take may increase, and limits on soaps, chemicals and waste discharge may be necessary.
- **Cumulative:** the project is judged to have a net positive cumulative impact on the Yaqui chub, topminnow, and WTC watershed. No other present or future projects known to the applicant are expected to contribute losses.

### AWPF Narrative Proposal
- **Goal:** restore and enhance the WTC watershed to sustain ranching and native biota, testing whether erosion-control gabions improve water retention and stream flow.
- **Objectives:**
  1. GIS-based map and narrative of erosion-control and riparian activities since the project began in 1987.
  2. Establish permanent sampling stations for controls and repeated measurements.
  3. Sample and monitor streamflow, water quality, soil moisture/water table and resident biota, comparing modified and unmodified sub-watersheds.
- **Design:** the project will sample at least 5 modified and 5 unmodified sub-watersheds over 2 years. Table 1 lists monitoring sites: precipitation 7, temperature 3, baseflow volumes 12, flow duration 12, floodflow volumes 7, quarterly photographic 10, annual photographic 20, soil moisture/groundwater 12, water quality 12, fish and invertebrate ecology 5, vegetation/faunal change 10.
- **Methods:** fishes sampled by seines, traps and electrofishing; aquatic invertebrates by Surber sampler, dredge, sweep nets and light traps. Soil moisture is measured by gravimetric drying at 100 °C. A minimum of 10 PVC sampling wells will be installed, and 10 ephemeral tributaries (5 modified, 5 unmodified) will be instrumented with wells.
- **Deliverables:** an interim report (end of Year 1), a final report, a database, and a biological inventory supporting a future HCP.
- **Personnel:** Josiah and Valer Austin (owners); Wendell L. Minckley, Ph.D., Arizona State University (research oversight, Desert Fishes Recovery Team); Dale Pontius (legal and endangered-species consultant); a graduate-student research assistant (not yet named); and student field and lab assistants.

### Budget and Timeline
- **AWPF request:** $45,400 (Year 1) and $39,300 (Year 2), totaling $84,700.
- **Applicant cash:** $23,850 (Year 1) and $1,700 (Year 2), totaling $25,550.
- **Applicant in-kind/services:** $14,400 (Year 1) and $16,450 (Year 2), totaling $30,850.
- **Total project budget:** $83,650 (Year 1), $57,450 (Year 2), total $141,100.
- **Major costs:** GIS base maps $17,000 (first six months); gabion construction $17,000 over two years (an estimated 250–300 additional gabions); electrofisher $2,600; V-notch weirs $2,800; weather-station equipment $800.
- **Administration:** $2,400, stated as 2.8% of the AWPF request (within the 5% limit).
- **Excluded costs:** salaries, wages, travel and consultant costs ($70,800) are listed separately.

### Stated Limitations
- The authors acknowledge that the Arizona climate makes conclusive results from a two-year study "highly unlikely." Positive, equivocal or negative results will not confirm that gabions work as intended.
- Ecosystem-wide effects require long-term study.

### Regulatory and Community Context
- Compliance checklist identifies the Endangered Species Act, Arizona Native Plant Laws and Section 404 as applicable. Floodplain, water-quality certification, and related permits were also reviewed.
- Dr. Minckley and associates hold federal and state permits for Yaqui chub work, plus a state permit for longfin dace.
- The applicants received the 1993 "Conservationist of the Year" award from the Willcox–San Simon Natural Resource Conservation District.

### USFS Context and Critique
- Critics attribute the severity of the Rattlesnake Fire and erosion to decades of fire-suppression policy (suppression since the 1930s). The USFS District Ranger states that about $8.5 million was spent on firefighting and about $100,000 on post-fire restoration.
- The USFS says the fire-suppression issue "will take time to modify," and a draft federal fire policy recognizes fire as a "critical natural process."
