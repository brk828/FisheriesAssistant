# Use of M.S. 222 in Anesthetizing and Transporting the Sand Shrimp

#ichthyology #marine-biology #crangonidae #crustacea

## Executive Summary
This 1966 study by Frank J. Schwartz (Chesapeake Biological Laboratory) investigates the use of **M.S. 222 (tricaine methanesulfonate)**, a common fish anesthetic, for anesthetizing and transporting the [[Sand Shrimp]] (*Crangon septemspinosa*) — a species for which crustacean-specific anesthesia data were previously lacking (prior work only existed for lobsters, with discouraging results). The research was motivated by a practical need: transporting 6,000–7,000 egg-bearing adult shrimp via a 7-hour flight from Chesapeake Bay, Maryland to three alkaline, saline lakes in North Dakota for introduction/stocking purposes. A series of four laboratory anesthesia trials established survival thresholds for varying M.S. 222 concentrations and exposure durations, which informed a large-scale field shipment of 3,240 shrimp. The shipment achieved roughly 80% survival through air transport but only 40–60% survival through subsequent truck transport under harsh winter conditions, demonstrating M.S. 222's viability as a bulk anesthetic for sand shrimp for up to 7 hours.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Crangon septemspinosa* Say (Sand Shrimp), family Crangonidae, as taxonomically defined by [[Holtuis 1951]].
- **Size range at capture**:
  - Males: 45–53 mm total length
  - Females: 47–70 mm total length
- All specimens used were **adult, egg-bearing (ovigerous) females and mature males**.
- Physiological anesthesia indicator: gills observed as **white and fluffy, not contracted** in successfully anesthetized (non-recovering) specimens — a possible sign of lethal over-exposure.
- Eggs of heavily gravid females became **opaque and non-viable (dead)** following prolonged anesthetic exposure (Experiment 1).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native habitat**: Benthic zones of Chesapeake Bay, captured via 30-foot balloon otter trawl at ~120 feet depth near Buoy 16D.
- **Salinity tolerance**: [[Sand Shrimp]] noted by [[Price 1962]] to occur across a wide salinity range, consistent with the species' euryhaline biology; experimental seawater salinities ranged 14.0–18.0 p.p.t.
- **Temperature range during experiments**: 0.6°C to 2.2°C (winter conditions).
- **Introduction sites**: Three alkaline, saline North Dakota lakes:
  - **Round Lake** (McKinley County) — supports [[Sticklebacks]] (*Eucalia*) and Sacramento perch; rooted vegetation (*Potamogeton pectinatus*, *Ruppia maritima*, *Scirpus americanus*).
  - **Moon Lake** (Barnes County) — harbors rainbow trout, tiger salamanders, and crustaceans.
  - **Lake George** (Kidder County) — most barren; only unidentified *Cladophora* algae present.
- **Target lake water chemistry**: pH 8.9–9.5; alkalinity 1,150–1,872 p.p.m.; Na⁺ 900–1,198 p.p.m.; Cl⁻ 180–2,600 p.p.m.; CO₃ 252–952 p.p.m.; HCO₃ 830–920 p.p.m. — representing an extreme departure from the shrimp's native marine/estuarine chemistry, testing its physiological plasticity.
- **Diet in captivity**: fed live soft-shell clams (*Mya arenaria*) prior to shipment.
- **Post-1965 note (Addendum)**: [[Brown Lake]] and Moon Lake were confirmed by the author (July 1965) to support established *Crangon* populations, suggesting successful colonization following the transplant.

## Key Findings
- **Experiment 1** (0.5 g M.S. 222 / 250 cc seawater, 1.7°C, 17.9 ppt): Full anesthesia took up to 1 hour; **no survivors** after 4 hours of exposure; only 1 of 12 shrimp recovered (in 14 hours) after 7 hours of exposure — indicating this concentration was too strong for extended use.
- **Experiment 2** (same concentration, 2.2°C, 17.5 ppt): At 1–3 hour exposures, recovery rates varied by sex — **females recovered more readily and quickly than males**, which frequently appeared dead immediately post-exposure before some recovered. Best result: after 2 hours exposure, 10/12 females fully recovered and all 12 males became active by 5:30 p.m.
- **Experiment 3** (weaker solution: 0.5 g M.S. 222 / 1,000 cc seawater, 1.1°C, 17.3 ppt): Markedly better outcomes — shrimp recovered immediately after 1–2 hours of exposure; some mortality emerged only after 3–4 hour exposures (e.g., 4 males dead after 3 hrs + 3 hrs recovery).
- **Experiment 4** (weakest solution: 0.5 g M.S. 222 / 2,000 cc seawater, 1.7°C, 18.0 ppt; larger test lots of 24+24): Best overall survival — full recovery after 1–2 hour exposures; only minor mortality (1 male, 1 female) at 4 hours; after **7 hours** of anesthesia and 7 hours recovery, only 3 males and 3 females died, with the majority surviving — establishing this as the **most viable dilution for extended (7-hour) bulk transport**.
- **Field shipment (Feb 27, 1964)**: 3,240 shrimp shipped in 4 boxes (A–D) using a 2 g M.S. 222 / 8,000 cc seawater solution in sealed plastic bags with ice, inside styrofoam containers.
  - Transport chain: truck to Dulles Airport (2.5 hrs) → flight to Minneapolis-St. Paul (7 hrs total anesthesia) → truck to Jamestown, ND (total distance 1,170 miles) → introduction into lakes same evening.
  - Shrimp experienced a **17°C temperature differential** between plane transport water and final lake water.
  - **~80% survival** through the full air shipment and 7-hour anesthetic exposure.
  - **40–60% survival** through subsequent tank truck transport, hampered by attempts to prevent water freezing in −10°C, windy, snowy conditions.
  - Shrimp directly transferred to 1.1°C lake water were observed to survive at least 31 hours before weather ended monitoring.
- **Overall conclusion**: M.S. 222 is an effective anesthetic for **bulk handling and long-duration transport (up to 7 hours) of sand shrimp**, marking the first successful documented application of this fish anesthetic to a crustacean species (in contrast to prior unsuccessful lobster trials by the [[Fisheries Research Board of Canada]], 1964).
- Findings support broader use of M.S. 222 for live crustacean logistics in fisheries management and species introduction programs.
