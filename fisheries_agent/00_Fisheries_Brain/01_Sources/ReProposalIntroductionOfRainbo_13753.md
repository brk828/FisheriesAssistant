# Critical Review of Proposed Rainbow Smelt Introduction to Lake Powell

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reservoir-ecology #conservation-biology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal letter from Professor W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University, Department of Zoology) dated February 4, 1983, responding to a proposal by the Utah Division of Wildlife to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]]. Minckley expresses strong reservations about the proposed introduction, citing concerns about ecosystem stability, downstream invasion risks, impacts on endangered native species, and the irreversibility of species introductions. The letter critiques the supporting documentation's interpretation of fishery trends and questions the long-term benefits of introducing another prey species to support the [[Striped Bass]] fishery.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Powell]] is a large man-made reservoir in the [[Colorado River]] basin that took several years to fill, topped out, and spilled in 1983. The reservoir has experienced progressive addition of fish species through both planned introductions and unplanned colonization. Key species mentioned include:

- [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*): Introduced and developed a fishery by 1979
- [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*): Appeared without introduction, described as not planned or wanted
- [[Kokanee]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi*): Stocked with 600,000 in 1983 and 35,000 in 1984, but did not thrive
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*): Showed population declines after 1977
- [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*): Also declined after 1977
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*): Introduced in the 1980s
- Threadfin shad: Introduced as prolific prey species

The proposal under review sought to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] as forage enhancement for game fish, particularly [[Striped Bass]]. Average fishing success rate reported as 0.4 fish/hour over more than two decades.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Minckley describes [[Lake Powell]] fishery development as following a pattern widely documented in North America: initial explosion of prey and predator species, overshoot of predator populations, and fluctuating downward trends as predators balance with prey base. The reservoir had not achieved ecological stabilization at the time of writing, with no more than a few years available for stabilization at any stage.

Angling pressure showed almost-level trends from 1973-1975, with 50% increase from 1975-1979 in the expanding reservoir. Population declines in [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] after 1977 coincided with the expanding [[Striped Bass]] population, raising questions about habitat effects versus competition and predation.

Endangered and threatened native species in the system include:
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): Concentrated in [[Little Colorado River]], largest known reproducing population
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*): Persists as large adults in [[Lake Mohave]], subject to active population augmentation program using fish from Dexter National Fish Hatchery
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Candidate species, numerous large adults in [[Lake Mohave]], minimal recruitment despite decade-long effort to solve recruitment failure

## Key Findings

**Critique of Management Approach:**
- Lack of synthesis of patterns in western American reservoir management, with bits and pieces of data not pertinent to whole systems
- Vast interpretations based on relatively short-term data
- Undocumented generalizations detract from assessment quality
- Qualitative rather than quantitative interpretations of species success (e.g., "did well," "would not thrive")
- No spectacular increase in angling pressure or success rates to document [[Striped Bass]] fishery "success"

**Downstream Invasion Risks:**
- Proposal acknowledges smelt will invade downstream into [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]]
- No comparable data on trophic structure and thermal ecology collected from downstream reservoirs
- Question of liability: Is Utah willing to take responsibility for correcting or compensating other states for problems?
- Evidence that fishes do not "read the books" - examples include [[Striped Bass]] spawning in reservoirs, walleye appearance in [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mead]], Tilapia persistence in unsuitable thermal conditions
- Winter put-and-take trout fisheries established in low-desert habitats demonstrate fish ability to find suitable thermal refugia
- Smelt could survive in creek mouth inflows despite summer die-off, impacting early life stages of other organisms

**Upstream Invasion Risks:**
- Smelt likely to move into [[Colorado River]] and [[San Juan River]] above [[Lake Powell]]
- Flowing water likely more important to sexually-oriented fish than turbidity levels
- Smelt swept into [[Grand Canyon]] may find quiet waters of creek mouths suitable habitat
- Mouths of [[Little Colorado River]] and Havasu Creek include semi-lentic habitat suitable for smelt at certain river discharges

**Impacts on Endangered Species:**
- [[Humpback Chub]] in [[Grand Canyon]]: Young smelt would compete with and feed on young chubs; adults would also prey on chubs - deemed "unacceptable"
- [[Bonytail Chub]] in [[Lake Mohave]]: Epilimnetic, feed on zooplankton, travel near or into hypolimnetic waters in summer - interactions with smelt anticipated
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: Larval razorback common in early spring in [[Lake Mohave]], occur as ichthyoplankton; pelagic, larvivorous and planktivorous predator spawning in winter-late spring adds "another problem" - also "unacceptable"
- Potential contact with other endangered species (e.g., Colorado pikeminnow) and native forms that may be considered for Endangered Species Act inclusion

**Regulatory Requirements:**
- Introduction will likely jeopardize listed species, requiring consultation with U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service
- Probable need for Environmental Impact Statement preparation
- Federal funding used in proposal development, plus federal responsibility in Lake Powell creation and operation, dictates need for consultation

**Alternative Management Recommendations:**
- Question whether short-term gain of enhanced [[Striped Bass]] fishery justifies risks
- "Too many" striped bass now, will be "too many again"
- Layered fishery (cold-water trouts deep, warm-water species epilimnetic) cannot be maintained
- Recommend public education, opening fishery to commercial exploitation, and other attempts to limit quantity and increase quality of individual [[Striped Bass]]
- Non-selective gill nets in open water and use of chum would selectively take [[Striped Bass]] and largely exclude other game species
- Conflict resolution through education and careful regulation

**Fundamental Concerns:**
- Each introduced and established species is there for perpetuity - action is irreversible
- Introduction of new prey species may cause minor variation in pattern but likely little effect on overall yield pattern in long run
- 0.4 fish/hour average likely to continue as sustained yield regardless of prey base manipulation
- Utah actions will influence and dictate management options for neighboring states and other entities
- From perspective of native fish researcher and manager, "the risks are unacceptable"
