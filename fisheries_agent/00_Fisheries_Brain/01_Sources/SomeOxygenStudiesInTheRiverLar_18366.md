# Some Oxygen Studies in the River Lark

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by M. Owens and R. W. Edwards of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] (Stevenage), studies the oxygen balance of two stretches of the [[River Lark]] in Suffolk, carried out with the Great Ouse River Board. The river was studied before and after the closure of the old [[Bury St Edmunds Sewage Works]] (January 1962), and shortly after a new works with full biological treatment opened further upstream.

The central finding is that the river's dissolved oxygen (DO) was driven far below levels predicted from [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] (BOD) and surface reaeration alone. The missing demand was largely attributed to oxygen consumed by river-bed muds, especially the [[Chironomus Riparius]] larvae living in them. Including mud respiration brought calculated and observed profiles into much closer agreement. Caged [[Rainbow Trout]] died when DO fell below about 1.5 ppm. The paper also quantifies [[Weir Aeration]] and gives a plant-community oxygen budget for one reach.

## Background & Description

**Context and aims.** The authors note that DO is a key water-quality index. In deep rivers, DO distribution can be predicted from BOD and surface aeration. In shallow lowland streams, bottom deposits, attached algae and rooted plants matter much more because low velocities allow fine organic material to settle.

**Study sites.**
- **Reach 1 (Lackford Bridge to Marston's Mill, Stations F1, F2, 1, 3, 11, 14–17):** The reach once supported good fishing but had deteriorated; stocking with trout had failed. Until January 1962 it received effluent from the old Bury St Edmunds works, which used primary settlement and short lagoon retention. The effluent was discharged at night, when the lagoons were emptied.
- **Reach 2 (Ducksluice Farm Weir to Lock Weir, Stations A–D):** Located about 1,300 yd below the outfall of the new works, and studied shortly after it opened.

**Methods (summarised).**
- Fish survival: rainbow trout held in floating cages, with deaths recorded at intervals.
- Water sampling: samples taken from a boat moving at the mean water velocity, following a body of water labelled with radioactive ammonium bromide (Br-82).
- Respiration: dark-bottle BOD measurements, and laboratory [[Mud Respiration]] measurements on cores from the reach using a mud respirometer.
- Flow: the radiochemical "total count" (gulp) method with Br-82.
- Reaeration: the exchange coefficient was determined by night-time DO changes, and by [[Sulphite Deoxygenation]] runs with a cobalt catalyst.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Oxygen budget framework.** Changes in DO between stations depend on photosynthetic release, respiratory uptake by plants, animals, bacteria and direct chemical oxidation, and air–water exchange (governed by saturation deficit). At night only respiration and diffusion act, so the exchange coefficient can be derived from the rate of DO change at different saturation deficits.

**Effects of sewage and oxygen on fish.**
- DO was below 5 ppm at Station F1 and about 1 ppm at Station F2 before high effluent concentrations (about 26 per cent) arrived at 2230 and 0430 hours respectively.
- No trout died while DO was above 2 ppm. Fish died rapidly below 1.5 ppm.
- Trout caged before DO fell were acclimatised to low but non-lethal levels and survived longer than fish moved directly into very low oxygen. The authors conclude that no trout could survive the night-time DO levels in this stretch.

**Benthic (bed) processes.** Mud from Reach 1 had very high oxygen consumption, about 0.95 g O2/m² of mud surface per hour at 20°C. The abundance of around 140,000 midge larvae per m² was estimated to account for about 0.3 g O2/m²/h of this. Mud was calculated to be responsible for more than 50 per cent of total oxygen consumption in a river less than 2.5 ft deep, and about 20 per cent in a river 10 ft deep (BOD 1.25 ppm/h case).

**Nitrogen transformations.** Inorganic nitrogen was lost (about 0.3 ppm/h) as water moved downstream, and nitrification was not detected. The authors suggest [[Denitrification]] in the mud, since nitrate loss occurred while overlying water still held several ppm DO. Laboratory losses were about 240 mg nitrate N/m²/day at 10°C.

**Plant productivity (Reach 14–17).** Plants and algae contributed to the oxygen budget. Weed-cutting by the River Board reduced total respiratory demand from 24.4 to 19.6 g O2/m²/day. The text also gives photosynthetic production of about 14 and 13 g O2/m²/day. The authors note that this reach has large mud deposits that consume most of the oxygen.

**Comparison with another river.** In the unpolluted [[River Ivel]] (a chalk stream), summer average community respiration was about 14 g O2/m²/day, lower than in the Lark, while photosynthesis (10–17 g O2/m²/day) was similar.

## Key Findings

1. **Oxygen collapse below old works outfall.** Before the effluent arrived, DO had already fallen to low values. In the 9–10 August 1961 run, it dropped from 11.6 ppm at Station 3 to under 4 ppm at Station 11, even though the sampled water held little effluent. Above the outfall, DO did not fall below 6.6 ppm during the September 1961 run.

2. **Fish mortality thresholds.** Trout mortality began when DO fell below about 1.5 to 2 ppm. Acclimatisation extended survival.

3. **Model failure in shallow streams.** Profiles predicted from BOD (0.21 ppm/h) and surface aeration (3–4 cm/h ppm deficit) differed markedly from observations. The authors state that this approach works for large systems such as the Thames estuary but over-simplifies the processes in the 2–3 ft deep Lark.

4. **Mud is a major sink.** Including mud respiration (0.95 g O2/m²/h at 20°C, and a later 0.14 g O2/m²/h at 20°C in 1962) gave much closer agreement with observed profiles in 1961.

5. **Improvement after closure.** Six and nine months after the old works closed (May and September 1962), mud consumption had fallen from 0.95 to 0.14 g O2/m²/h. Short-term BOD was undetectable, and a [[Dissolved Oxygen Recorder]] about 2 miles downstream confirmed a marked improvement.

6. **Residual discrepancy.** Some discrepancy remained after mud respiration was included. The authors suggested possible causes: difficulty measuring the water volume used in respirometry (especially in midge-rich muds), mud heterogeneity, and uncertainty in the exchange coefficient.

7. **Weir aeration.** The deficit-ratio equation predicted weir performance reasonably well:
   - Lock Weir (2 ft fall): observed 1.25 and 1.33; calculated 1.35.
   - Ducksluice Farm Weir (2 ft 2 in fall): observed 1.49 and 1.25; calculated 1.37.
   - Marston's Mill (movable board, about 1.5 ft free fall): observed 3.05; calculated 2.02, attributed to splashing over a concrete sill.

8. **Weir versus surface reaeration.** In a worked example (10,220 yd² stretch, flow 20 ft³/s, exchange coefficient 6 cm/h, initial DO 3.00 ppm, saturation 10.00 ppm), surface aeration raised DO to 4.4 ppm. A weir with a deficit ratio of 1.25 gave the same result, while one with 3.05 raised DO to 7.7 ppm. Weirs can therefore strongly increase reaeration. However, upstream velocity falls, which encourages fine sediment accumulation.

9. **Management implications.** The authors stress that spot daytime sampling would have missed the night-time DO minimum, so automatic sampling and recording are needed. They also argue that night-time discharge and bed deposits must be considered when deciding on treatment and on whether to build weirs, alongside fishery and abstraction interests.

10. **Discussion points.** Discussants raised several issues:
    - Professor Odum's finding that in systems under 3 m deep, bottom oxygen consumption usually exceeds that of the water.
    - Dr Southgate's note that grass filtration of effluent reduces suspended solids.
    - Denitrification in reservoirs and rivers, with Mr Wheatland and Mr Brandon both linking nitrate loss to gas production.
    - Mr Edwards' finding that algal-covered mud removed about 1 g nitric N/m²/day at 20°C (10 ppm nitrate in the overlying water) with no equivalent ammonia or nitrite produced, probably taken up by algae.
