# The Pleistocene Amphibians and Reptiles of Texas

#paleoherpetology #pleistocene #texas #amphibians #reptiles #fossil-fauna

## Executive Summary

This monograph, published by the [[Museum of Zoology, Michigan State University]] (Biological Series, Vol. 4, No. 5, pp. 161–192, 4 June 1969), by J. Alan Holman, synthesizes the known fossil amphibian and reptile records from Pleistocene localities in Texas. It compiles an annotated list of taxa from [[Pleistocene Localities in Texas]] grouped into Late Kansan, Illinoian, Sangamon, Wisconsin, and Late Wisconsin deposits, plus a few undated or poorly dated sites. The paper emphasizes faunal analysis over single-taxon description and uses fossil assemblages to infer past climate: milder winters and moister or cooler summers than today, with range shifts correlated with climatic change. The author notes that the only conclusively extinct herpetological species are giant tortoises of the genera [[Geochelone]] and [[Gopherus]]; extinct subspecies are few.

## Background & Description

The Pleistocene vertebrate record of Texas was sparse before the late 1950s, consisting mainly of turtle and alligator records from scattered localities with poor stratigraphic control (Hay, 1924). Methodological change, particularly [[Microvertebrate Fossil Collecting]] techniques (Hibbard, 1949), shifted the field toward systematic faunal study. Age determination was improved by [[Carbon-14 Dating]], though age interpretations of several sites differ among authors. The author followed the authorities he considered most familiar with each site.

Nomenclature has changed in the literature; the paper provides a concordance of more than 20 names (for example, *Testudo* now *Geochelone* or *Gopherus*; *Coluber* now *Masticophis*; *Natrix sipedon confluens* now *N. fasciata confluens*). A dagger marks extinct taxa. Tortoises were excluded from the final list because they were under revision at the time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Texas lies across several [[Biotic Provinces of Texas]] (Blair, 1950), including the Kansan, Balconian (limestone cave region of southern Texas), Tamaulipan, and Chihuahuan provinces. Many Pleistocene fossil sites fall outside the modern ranges of their taxa, which the author uses as evidence for past climatic and distributional shifts:

- Late Kansan (Seymour formation, Knox and Briscoe counties) faunas indicate a frost-free, mesothermal, moist, subhumid, maritime climate (Hibbard and Dalquest, 1966), with tortoises, *Alligator* sp., and *Syrrhophus marnocki* far north of their modern ranges.
- The Yarmouth box turtle *Terrapene carolina putnami* suggests a milder, moister climate in Lubbock County.
- The Illinoian Slaton local fauna contains the box turtle and alligator, which today occur east and south of the site; Dalquest (1967) attributes this to a possibly milder early Illinoian winter.
- Sangamon faunas combine southern and eastern intrusives with northern forms. Slaughter (pers. comm.) proposed a more equitable year-round climate: mild winters for southern forms and tortoises, cool moist summers for northern and eastern forms.
- Late Wisconsin faunas resemble modern Texas faunas, but suggest milder winters and cooler, moister summers, supported by eastern amphibians and northern mammals (Patton, 1963).

Large non-burrowing tortoises apparently could not survive the freezing temperatures accompanying the onset of the modern climate.

## Key Findings

**Overall content**
- The annotated list covers the Pleistocene amphibians and reptiles of Texas, excluding tortoises pending revision; it includes roughly 100 taxa or taxon identifications across the amphibian, turtle, crocodilian, lizard, and snake lineages, many identified only to genus or as indeterminate.
- The author notes that most extant Texas herpetofauna appear to have been present in the Pleistocene.

**Extinct taxa and subspecies**
- The only conclusively extinct species are the tortoises of [[Geochelone]] and [[Gopherus]]. Among these, *Gopherus hexagonata* has a shell nearly a meter long.
- Extinct subspecies: *Pseudemys scripta bisornata* (late Kansan), *Terrapene carolina putnami* (Yarmouth), and *Bufo woodhousei bexarensis* (late Wisconsin, Friesenhahn Cave; larger than the modern subspecies).
- Giant tortoises of *Geochelone* (subgenus *Caudochelys*) apparently became extinct by late Wisconsin times; the small *Geochelone wilsoni* persisted.

**Box turtle intergrades**
- Fossils identified as *Terrapene carolina putnami* × *T. c. triunguis* are considered temporally intermediate forms, found from Sangamon through late Wisconsin deposits, and many were very large. One specimen from the Lewisville site was about eight times the bulk of living *T. c. putnami*.

**Range anomalies**
- *Ambystoma texanum* from Groesbeck Creek (Hardeman County) lies well within the Kansan biotic province, about 100 miles west of the modern Texan-province limit.
- *Pseudacris streckeri* from Groesbeck Creek lies 60–70 miles west of its modern western limit.
- *Arizona elegans* from Clear Creek lies a few miles east of its modern easternmost range.
- *Amphiuma means* represents only the second fossil record of its monotypic family; the other is from the Late Pleistocene of Vero, Florida.
- *Plethodon glutinosus* from Cave Without a Name (Kendall County) belongs to the Balconian isolate *P. g. albagula* today.

**Climate interpretation**
- Intrusive eastern and southern forms, a northern form, and large non-burrowing tortoises indicate milder winters and moister, perhaps cooler, summers until Recent times, in line with mammalian evidence.
- The summary concludes that by Illinoian, and possibly late Kansan, times the herpetofauna conformed to the distributional patterns outlined by Blair (1950).
- With the onset of the modern climate, the last fossil tortoises became extinct and several smaller species withdrew east and south.

**Sampling notes**
- Several identifications rest on single bones or fragmentary material (e.g., one ilium at Vera and Easley Ranch for *Syrrhophus marnocki*; two bones for *Scaphiopus* sp.), and many taxa are identified only to genus or as "indeterminate."
- *Rana pipiens* was the most abundant anuran at many localities.
- The *Plethodon glutinosus* fossil comprised hundreds of bones, but its subspecific status was not determined.
